Sally sat at the head of the long boarding-house table and took stock the way a conscientious cook inspects a roast before carving: with relief, pride, and a sharp eye for any corner that might still be raw.

It had been touch and go at first. The party had started with the particular hush that falls on a room when something is wrong but everybody is too polite—or too hungry—to say what it is. In this case the wrongness had a name and a shine: Fillmore Nicholas’s white evening waistcoat, so immaculate it seemed to emit its own expensive squeak. It had stiffened the room as if someone had sprinkled starch in the air.

But now the spell had cracked. Laughter ran along the boards like spilled ginger ale. Plates were pushed back. Mrs. Meecher’s “select boarding-house (transient and residential)” had regained its normal music: forks clicking, chairs scuffing, voices overlapping in comfortable, well-fed argument. The smells still hung about them, too—roast and gravy, the faint oniony ghost of something sautéed earlier, cigar smoke kneading itself into the warmer air.

Sally had done well. She had coaxed the kitchen into producing what Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, the old actor, would later insist on calling “a banquet.” There had even been alcoholic refreshment—smuggled in with all the discretion of a guilty parsley garnish. If you listened closely you could hear the boarding-house’s acoustic ecology settle into health: the clink-and-chuckle rhythm that meant people felt safe enough to be themselves.

At Sally’s end of the table the talk, as inevitably as a kettle boils when left alone, had returned to the subject of her legacy. The next best thing to having money is planning what someone else ought to do with it; and Sally’s guests—full of food, full of opinions—had begun laying out her financial future as if it were a menu.

“Let me tell you,” said Augustus Bartlett briskly. He worked in an intensely subordinate position at Kahn, Morris and Brown, Wall Street brokers, and spoke the way a man speaks when he wishes everyone to picture him leaning confidentially over a ticker-tape machine. “What I’d do, if I were you: sink a couple of hundred thousand in a good, safe bond-issue—we’ve just put one out which you would do well to consider—and play about with the rest. When I say play about, I mean have a flutter in anything good that crops up. Multiple Steel’s worth looking at. They tell me it’ll be up to a hundred and fifty before next Saturday.”

Elsa Doland, the pretty girl with the big eyes on Mr. Bartlett’s left, smiled as if she had tasted something sweeter than dessert.

“Buy a theatre, Sally, and put on good stuff.”

“And lose every bean you’ve got,” said a mild young man across the table in a deep voice that belonged, incongruously, to a face that looked as if it apologized to furniture when it bumped it. “If I had a few hundred thousand, I’d put every cent of it on Benny Whistler for the heavyweight championship. I’ve private information that Battling Tuke has been got at and means to lie down in the seventh…”

“Say, listen,” interrupted another voice, “lemme tell you what I’d do with four hundred thousand…”

“If I had four hundred thousand,” Elsa said, leaning forward, “I know what would be the first thing I’d do.”

“What’s that?” Sally asked.

“Pay my bill for last week, due this morning.”

Sally was out of her chair in an instant, as if the word “bill” had scorched her. She flitted down the table and slipped an arm around Elsa’s shoulder, her voice dropping to a whisper as soft as folded linen.

“Elsa darling, are you really broke? If you are, you know, I’ll—”

Elsa laughed, her eyes bright. “You’re an angel, Sally. There’s no one like you. You’d give your last cent to anyone. Of course I’m not broke. I’ve just come back from the road and I’ve saved a fortune. I only said that to draw you.”

Sally returned to her seat, relieved—and found that, like a kitchen split between those who believe in seasoning and those who believe in fire, the table had divided into schools of thought.

The cautious, respectable element, led by Augustus Bartlett, had already allocated three hundred thousand to Liberty Bonds and the rest to “safe real estate,” the phrase said with the reverence other people reserve for prayer. The smaller, sportier section had put Sally’s imaginary fortune on Benny Whistler, doling it out in careful pinches so as not to “spoil the market,” like cooks saving salt until the end.

And so solid, it seemed, was Mr. Tuke’s reputation among those who liked to suggest they had whispered access to fate, that the mild young man was confident that if one went about it cannily, three to one might be obtained. Sally listened a moment, then decided it was time to take the spoon away from the committee.

“I don’t know where you get your figures,” she said, “but I’m afraid they’re wrong. I’ve just twenty-five thousand dollars.”

The number landed among them with a small, damp thud. To these jugglers of half-millions, twenty-five thousand sounded like the sort of sum you set aside for tips and tram fare. For a moment there was a chill, as if someone had opened a window to the street and let in the city’s hard, tinny roar. Then they rallied—human beings can grow ambitious around any amount of money, the way they can grow hungry again surprisingly soon after dinner.

“If I’d twenty-five thousand,” Augustus Bartlett began, recovering first, “I’d buy Amalgamated—”

“If I had twenty-five thousand…” Elsa started dreamily.

“If I’d had twenty-five thousand in the year nineteen hundred,” observed a gloomy-looking man with spectacles, “I could have started a revolution in Paraguay.”

He stared at his plate as if it held the ashes of his unrealized destiny.

“Well,” said Sally, “I’ll tell you exactly what I’m going to do. I’m going to start with a trip to Europe—France, specially. I’ve heard France well spoken of—as soon as I can get my passport. And after I’ve loafed there for a few weeks, I’m coming back to look about and find some nice cosy little business which will let me put money into it and keep me in luxury. Are there any complaints?”

“Even a couple of thousand on Benny Whistler…” murmured the mild young man, like someone offering a second helping of disappointment.

“I don’t want your Benny Whistler,” said Sally. “I wouldn’t have him if you gave him to me. If I want to lose money, I’ll go to Monte Carlo and do it properly.”

“Monte Carlo,” said the gloomy man, brightening as if a match had been struck behind his glasses. “I was in Monte Carlo in the year ’97, and if I’d had another fifty dollars… just fifty… I’d have…”

He fell silent, still tasting the bitter flavor of that imaginary fifty.

At the far end of the table came a stir, a cough, and the scrape of a chair with the particular rasp of wood on bare floorboards that always sounded like the boarding-house itself clearing its throat. Slowly—smoothly, with that trained ease of men who had learned to cross a stage back when actors acted—Mr. Maxwell Faucitt rose.

“Ladies,” said Mr. Faucitt, bowing, “and—” he ceased to bow and sent a quelling glance beneath white, venerable eyebrows at certain younger males showing symptoms of impatience—“gentlemen. I feel that I cannot allow this occasion to pass without saying a few words.”

No one appeared surprised. Somewhere in the architecture of Mrs. Meecher’s place there must have been a beam that held the roof up and another that held Mr. Faucitt’s speeches up, because nothing had yet occurred that he could allow to pass without remarks. From the start of the meal, everybody had known it was optimism run mad to expect silence on the night of Sally Nicholas’s farewell dinner party. Partly because they had braced themselves and principally because Sally’s hospitality had left them pleasantly heavy and mild, they prepared to listen with something like equanimity.

At one end, the Marvellous Murphys—new arrivals playing the Bushwick with their equilibristic act—made a small movement as if to form a party of the extreme left and heckle. A look from Sally stopped them cold. Brief though their acquaintance had been, both those lissom young gentlemen admired Sally immensely. And in this they were not wrong.

Sally was a small, trim, wisp of a girl with tiny hands and feet, the friendliest smile, and a dimple that came and went in the curve of her rounded chin like a shy animal. Her bright hazel eyes vanished when she laughed, which was often. Her soft brown hair was a warm mass that always looked as if it had escaped pins on principle. And there was an air about her—a neat, unpretentious distinction—that made the rest of Mrs. Meecher’s guests, for all their virtues, look slightly as if they had been set down in life without being dusted.

“I have been asked,” proceeded Mr. Faucitt, “though I am aware that there are others here far worthier of such a task—Brutuses compared with whom I, like Marc Antony, am no orator—I have been asked to propose the health—”

“Who asked you?” said the smaller of the Marvellous Murphys. He was an unpleasant youth, snub-nosed and spotty, though it must be admitted he could balance himself with one hand on an inverted ginger-ale bottle while revolving a barrel on the soles of his feet. There is good in all of us; it merely takes different forms.

“I have been asked,” repeated Mr. Faucitt, ignoring the interruption—a wise course, for answering it would have involved admitting nobody had asked him anything—“to propose the health of our charming hostess (applause), coupled with the name of her brother, our old friend Fillmore Nicholas.”

The brother in question sat at the speaker’s end of the table, so faultlessly dressed that he made the rest look like men who had slept in their clothes under a pier. He acknowledged the tribute with a brief nod of condescension: the nod of one who believes himself hemmed in by social inferiors and feels it a kindness not to mention it.

Sally saw the nod and for an instant weighed, in her honest democratic soul, the practical advantages of throwing an orange at his glistening shirt-front. There was one conveniently placed near her hand. His waistcoat offered a splendid target. But she restrained herself. A hostess cannot begin hurling fruit, even for the best moral reasons, without undermining her authority.

She leaned back with a small sigh. She loved her brother—she did—but ever since affluence had descended on him some months ago, Fillmore had become insufferably pompous. If inherited wealth improved any young men, it had failed to select Fillmore Nicholas. He now carried himself like a sort of minor monarch granting audiences to the lower orders. It had taken Sally over an hour to persuade him to leave his apartment on Riverside Drive and revisit the boarding-house for this special occasion; and when he arrived, that white waistcoat alone had radiated such a silent reproach to honest poverty that right through soup and fish there had been constraint, like a layer of jelly no one had ordered.

Most present had known Fillmore as an impecunious young man who could make a tweed suit outlast the laws of nature. They had called him “Fill,” and in lean times had floated him small loans. Tonight they looked at the waistcoat and shrank, as if it were a badge in a language they did not speak anymore.

“Speaking,” said Mr. Faucitt, “as an Englishman—for though I have long since taken out what are technically known as my ‘papers’ it was as a subject of the island kingdom that I first visited this great country—I may say that the two factors in American life which have always made the profoundest impression upon me have been the lavishness of American hospitality and the charm of the American girl. To-night we have been privileged to witness the American girl in the capacity of hostess, and I think I am right in saying, in asseverating, in committing myself to the statement that this has been a night which none of us present here will ever forget. Miss Nicholas has given us, ladies and gentlemen, a banquet. I repeat, a banquet. There has been alcoholic refreshment. I do not know where it came from; I do not ask how it was procured, but we have had it. Miss Nicholas…”

Mr. Faucitt paused to puff at his cigar, sending a little cloud up into the room where it mixed with the lingering scents of dinner and the sound of contentment. Fillmore suppressed a yawn and glanced at his watch, making time itself seem a trifle vulgar. Sally leaned forward, rapt. She knew how it pleased the old gentleman to make a formal speech; and though she would have preferred him to talk about anything other than her brother, she was prepared to listen indefinitely, the way one listens to a beloved elder telling an old story in an old dialect—some of the words were the point, not their meaning.

“Miss Nicholas,” resumed Mr. Faucitt, lowering his cigar, “but why,” he demanded abruptly, “do I call her Miss Nicholas?”

“Because it’s her name,” hazarded the taller Murphy.

Mr. Faucitt eyed him with disfavour. He disapproved of the Marvellous Murphys generally because, as a resident of years, he considered that transients from vaudeville lowered the tone of the house; and particularly because the one who spoke had, on his first evening, addressed him as “grandpa.”

“Yes, sir,” said Mr. Faucitt severely, “it is her name. But she has another name, sweeter to those who love her, those who worship her, those who have watched her with the eye of sedulous affection through the three years she has spent beneath this roof—though that name,” he added, dipping suddenly into personalities, “may not be familiar to a couple of dud acrobats who have only been in the place a week-end, thank heaven, and are off to-morrow to infest some other city. That name”—and he rose again like a man stepping back into a spotlight—“is Sally. Our Sally. For three years our Sally has flitted about this establishment like—I choose the simile advisedly—like a ray of sunshine. For three years she has made life for us a brighter, sweeter thing. And now a sudden access of worldly wealth, happily synchronizing with her twenty-first birthday, is to remove her from our midst. From our midst, ladies and gentlemen, but not from our hearts. And I think I may venture to hope, to prognosticate, that whatever lofty sphere she may adorn in the future, to whatever heights in the social world she may soar, she will still continue to hold a corner in her own golden heart for the comrades of her Bohemian days. Ladies and gentlemen, I give you our hostess, Miss Sally Nicholas, coupled with the name of our old friend, her brother Fillmore.”

Cheers went up—warm, sincere, and a little noisy in the good way. Sally watched her brother heave himself to his feet as the applause dwindled, and her heart quickened with anticipation. Fillmore, for all his sins, could talk. He had once been a power in his college debating society; and that was why Sally had insisted he come tonight. She had known Mr. Faucitt would say lovely things and had mistrusted her own ability to reply properly. Mr. Faucitt, poor dear, regarded these moments as scenes in a play; he performed his part with such polished grace that he would be hurt by any anticlimax that followed.

Fillmore smoothed a wrinkle out of his white waistcoat. He rested one podgy hand on the table-cloth and hooked the thumb of the other in his pocket. Then he looked down the table with eyes so haughtily drooping that Sally’s fingers closed automatically around her orange. Even now, she thought, it might not be too late to lob it.

It is one of Nature’s strange economies that the prettiest girls often end up issued, as if by some perverse clerk, with the least attractive brothers. Fillmore had been an extraordinarily beautiful child at seven; after that, he had gone to pieces. At twenty-five, it was idle to pretend he wasn’t something of a mess. Hard work and restricted means had kept his figure in check for three years; money had brought sleekness, the look of a man who ate too often and too well and never walked unless it was to show people he walked.

But Sally could forgive a great deal if he would only make a good speech. She could see Mr. Faucitt leaning back, all courteous attention, ready to be nourished by rolling periods the way other old gentlemen are nourished by broth.

Fillmore spoke.

“I’m sure,” said Fillmore, “you don’t want a speech… Very good of you to drink our health. Thank you.”

He sat down.

The effect was immediate, like the sound when a musical phrase ends on the wrong note. To the majority, relief flooded in. Fillmore’s stance had looked so practised and ominous that the pessimists had braced for twenty minutes and the optimists for ten. The bulk of the guests felt they had been spared, and were quietly grateful.

Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, however, wore the expression of a man who has stepped confidently onto what he believed was the last stair and found nothing but air. Surprise, dismay—almost physical pain. Sally saw his face and made a sharp sound, the noise a person makes on the street when she sees a child fall.

The next moment she was around the table and behind him, her arms around his neck. She spoke across him, her voice wobbling as if the words had to cross water.

“My brother,” she stammered, shooting a malevolent look at Fillmore, who avoided her gaze by inspecting his waistcoat for further wrinkles, “has not said quite—quite all I hoped he was going to say. I can’t make a speech, but…” Sally gulped, “but, I love you all and of course I shall never forget you, and… and…”

Then, as if language had failed her entirely—because it had, the way it does when the feeling is too particular to fit the available words—Sally kissed Mr. Faucitt and burst into tears.

“There, there,” said Mr. Faucitt, patting her hand and sounding, in spite of himself, contented. The kindest critic could not have called Sally eloquent. Nevertheless, Mr. Faucitt felt no anticlimax at all. Whatever the words lacked, the gesture supplied; and the room, which had been fed well, was now fed kindly.

Later, outside in the street where New York made its ceaseless, metallic argument—car-wheels, distant horns, voices ricocheting off brick—Sally had just finished telling her brother what a pig he was.

The lecture took place immediately after the party broke up. Fillmore, having furtively collected hat and overcoat as if escaping a crime scene, had stolen into the night. Sally overtook him, brought him to bay under a streetlamp, and delivered her remarks in a clean, thorough ten minutes, punctuated at intervals by bleating sounds from the accused.

When she paused for breath, Fillmore seemed to expand, like an indiarubber ball that has been sat on. Dignified to the world, he had never managed to prevent himself from being intimidated by Sally when she took this tone. It hurt his self-esteem, but he had never discovered a method of altering it. Sally had always been like that: sweet-tempered, yes, but with cyclonic potentialities stored somewhere behind her dimple.

Even their guardian uncle, a grim man, had never coped successfully with her. In that last hectic scene three years ago, which ended in their leaving home and going out into the world together like a second Adam and Eve, the verbal victory had been hers. And it had been Sally who won the obligatory first-week battle with Mrs. Meecher, a landlady who seemed to regard quarrelling as a sort of civic duty.

Now, thinking she had said her say, Fillmore ventured a defence.

“What have I done?” he demanded plaintively.

“Do you want to hear it all over again?”

“No, no,” said Fillmore hastily. “But listen, Sally, you don’t understand my position. You don’t seem to realize that all that sort of thing—all that boarding-house stuff—is a thing of the past. One’s got beyond it. One wants to drop it. One wants to forget it, darn it! Be fair. Look at it from my viewpoint. I’m going to be a big man…”

“You’re going to be a fat man,” said Sally coldly.

Fillmore did not discuss this. He was sensitive.

“I’m going to do big things,” he substituted. “I’ve got a deal on at this very moment which… well, I can’t tell you about it, but it’s going to be big. Well, what I’m driving at is about all this sort of thing—” he swept an arm at the lighted front of Mrs. Meecher’s home-from-home “—is that it’s over. Finished and done with. These people were all very well when…”

“…when you’d lost your week’s salary at poker and wanted to borrow a few dollars for the rent.”

“I always paid them back,” Fillmore protested defensively.

“I did.”

“Well, we did,” he amended, with the air of a man too busy to split hairs. “Anyway, what I mean is, I don’t see why, just because one has known people at a certain period in one’s life when one was practically down and out, one should have them round one’s neck for ever. One can’t prevent people forming an I-knew-him-when club, but, darn it, one needn’t attend the meetings.”

“One’s friends—”

“Oh, friends,” said Fillmore, weary. “That’s just where all this makes me so tired. One’s in a position where all these people are entitled to call themselves one’s friends simply because father put it in his will that I wasn’t to get the money till I was twenty-five instead of letting me have it at twenty-one like anybody else. I wonder where I should have been by now if I could have got that money when I was twenty-one.”

“In the poor-house, probably,” said Sally.

Fillmore sighed as if history had been unfair to him personally. “Ah! You don’t believe in me.”

“Oh, you’d be all right if you had one thing,” said Sally.

Fillmore ran through his merits swiftly: brains, dash, initiative—surely all there. “One thing?” he said. “What’s that?”

“A nurse.”

The injury deepened, settling in him like indigestion.

“I shall find my place in the world,” he said sulkily.

“Oh, you’ll find your place all right,” said Sally. “And I’ll come round and bring you jelly and read to you on the days when visitors are allowed… Oh, hullo.”

A young man had been swinging briskly along the sidewalk from the direction of Broadway. As he came abreast of them he stopped.

“Good evening, Mr. Foster,” said Sally.

“Good evening, Miss Nicholas.”

“You don’t know my brother, do you?”

“I don’t believe I do.”

“He left the underworld before you came to it,” said Sally. “You wouldn’t think it to look at him, but he was once a prune-eater among the proletariat, even as you and I. Mrs. Meecher looks on him as a son.”

The two men shook hands. Fillmore was not short, but Gerald Foster—lean, well-built—seemed to tower over him. He was an Englishman in his middle twenties, clean-shaven, keen-eyed, and very good to look at, with the air of a man whose mind kept its own tidy pantry. Fillmore, who had recently been studying one of those sum-up-your-fellow-man-at-a-glance courses to fit himself for greatness, felt a reluctant flicker of respect. This Foster looked like someone who Got There. If you fancied yourself that kind, you developed a knack for recognizing others.

They exchanged a few moments of the desultory conversation that follows introductions—phrases laid out like polite slices. Then Fillmore, grateful for an exit the way a bad speaker is grateful for applause, took advantage of the newcomer to remove himself. His chat with Sally had been painful. A continuation promised worse.

Excusing himself briefly, he hurried off down the street, his white waistcoat flashing once under the streetlight and then vanishing into the larger, louder city. Sally remained with Gerald Foster as Mrs. Meecher’s windows glowed behind them, and the night hummed on, indifferent and unmistakably alive.

Sally sat with her shoulders against a warm hump of sand, letting Roville-sur-Mer perform its morning like a well-rehearsed opera in which everybody knew their cue and nobody, mercifully, required hers.

Down on the flat, the beach was busy in that domestic, seaside way that makes idleness feel like an occupation. Fathers—whiskered, sunburnt, and full of opinions—stood in cheerful clumps as if planted there overnight. Their womenfolk clustered beneath parasols bright enough to alarm fish. Children dug with spades, periodically pausing to test the blunt instruments on one another’s shins, as though engineering were less important than war.

Dogs wandered everywhere with the free-ranging confidence of creatures who have never once had to pay for a chair. One of them, a poodle with the air of a retired sergeant-major, marched up to Sally, sniffed her box of sweets, and took up station beside her with the patience of someone awaiting a course.

A month ago, Sally had started out to do Europe in the manner of a happy leaf—drifting, turning lazily, landing wherever the breeze of whim suggested. It had been so perfect that it made the whole idea of “anticipation” look like amateur hour. Town after town had offered her little pleasures: bread still warm from ovens, butter that tasted like fields, coffee that smelled like a promise. And then Roville had caught her and held her: blue sky like a painted ceiling, snow-white hotels along the Promenade, the Casino glittering at night as if it had swallowed a chandelier and was proud of it.

She could have stayed indefinitely. But America had reached across the Atlantic and tugged her sleeve.

Gerald’s letter—scrappy, hurried, the handwriting of a man whose thoughts were being jostled by rehearsal schedules—had said that “The Primrose Way” would open in Detroit on the way to New York, and that if she wanted to see the first night she must come at once.

So she was leaving tomorrow.

Today, though, was still today. Sally let her eyes half-close, basking in sun that felt like warm silk, listening to the small waves as they murmured and withdrew, murmured and withdrew—an acoustic hush that was nearly medicinal, a soundscape stitched out of breath and distance. After weeks of trains and cities and clacking streets, the beach’s soft noises seemed like a polite language that didn’t insist on being understood.

If there was one danger to a morning on the Roville plage, it was that you could fall asleep disgracefully soon after breakfast. Sally usually fought it, out of principle and vanity. But the sun had the heavy kindness of a feather quilt, and the waves whispered like conspirators. She was on the brink of dozing when she heard voices close by.

Not the usual French chatter—this was English. In Roville it was as startling as hearing a hymn in a bakery. The familiar rhythms jerked her awake.

A few feet away, two men had seated themselves on the sand.

From the day she had first begun travelling alone, Sally had made a private game of fitting strangers with imaginary lives. She was almost always wrong, but she enjoyed the wrongness as much as the guess.

The first man did not tempt her imagination much. He was tall and dark, with high cheekbones and a mouth that looked as if it had been trained to say “Quite,” and mean “No.” His chin and jaw had that dusky, clean-shaven look of a man who fought a determined beard every few hours and won without ever swearing—even when he cut himself. If he smiled, Sally felt, he would do it the way a knife does: quickly, and not for comfort.

“Hard,” she thought. “A lawyer. Or the sort of man who ruins picnics by being right.”

She shifted her attention to the second.

He was staring at her.

Not rudely, exactly—more with the absorbed, hopeful intensity of somebody who has been searching a long time and has suddenly found something he hadn’t known he was looking for. His mouth was slightly open, as if admiration had paused him mid-breath.

Sally met his eyes by accident and instantly saw why she had. He had very direct eyes.

“Rather a dear,” she decided, with that calm, practical generosity with which she classified people.

He was sturdy and thick-set, with freckles and a square chin. His hair was the reddest she had ever seen—red not like a polite autumn, but like a flame that had opinions. At one angle of his chin was a small cut, and Sally felt sure he had not acquired it with spiritual composure.

“A temper,” she diagnosed. “Quick as a match, over as fast. Not very clever. But nice.”

She looked away because there is a limit to how long a girl can be calmly inspected without feeling she ought to be on display with a price tag.

Beside the red-haired young man, the dark one performed the sort of competent action that suggested he did everything with detestable efficiency: he lit a cigarette in a stiff breeze, threw away the match, and resumed a conversation that had paused for sitting down.

“And how is Scrymgeour?” he asked.

“Oh, all right,” said the red-haired young man absently.

Sally felt, without looking, that his eyes were still busy.

“I was surprised at his being here,” continued the dark man. “He told me he meant to stay in Paris.”

Sally fed the military poodle a neat piece of nougat. The poodle accepted it with restrained gratitude, like a soldier receiving a medal.

Then, in a voice so clear it seemed to cut through the salt air, the red-haired young man said with deep conviction:

“I say—that’s the prettiest girl I’ve seen in my life!”

Sally, though startled, could not honestly call herself offended. Broad-minded by nature, she regarded such observations as part of the world’s agreeable weather—like sunshine that occasionally singled you out. Besides, it was nice to be appreciated out loud in a place where most of the compliments were being paid to bathhouses and omelettes.

The dark man, however, reacted as if his companion had suddenly begun eating sand.

“My dear fellow!” he exclaimed.

“Oh, it’s all right,” said the red-haired young man, untroubled. “She can’t understand. There isn’t a bally soul in this dashed place that can speak a word of English. If I didn’t happen to remember a few odd bits of French, I should have starved by this time.” He returned urgently to the subject. “That girl is an absolute topper! I give you my solemn word I’ve never seen anybody to touch her. Look at those hands and feet. You don’t get them outside France. Of course, her mouth’s a bit wide,” he added reluctantly, like a man admitting the smallest possible fault in paradise.

Sally, still facing the sea, divided her attention between the poodle and a raffish-looking mongrel who had slunk up in hopes of being fed without having to enlist. The dark man, reassured by Sally’s stillness and by his friend’s cheerful belief in Roville’s linguistic innocence, drew a careful breath, as if propriety had been in danger of capsizing.

“Still you ought to be careful,” he said austerely.

He glanced at Sally and then, safely back behind his barricade of correctness, returned to Scrymgeour.

“How is Scrymgeour’s dyspepsia?”

The red-haired young man’s interest in Scrymgeour’s internal affairs was clearly limited.

“Do you notice the way her hair sort of curls over her ears?” he said. “Eh? Oh—pretty much the same, I think.”

“What hotel are you staying at?”

“The Normandie.”

Sally’s fingers paused inside her sweet-box. She, too, was staying at the Normandie. She hadn’t seen him there; he must have arrived recently, or else he had the good sense to spend his time away from lobbies.

“The Normandie?” The dark man sounded genuinely puzzled. “I know Roville pretty well by report, but I’ve never heard of any Hotel Normandie. Where is it?”

“It’s a little shanty down near the station. Not much of a place. Still, it’s cheap, and the cooking’s all right.”

That last phrase—cooking’s all right—had a homely solidity to it, like a pan set down on a stove. Sally liked it immediately. People who cared whether food was all right were, in her experience, rarely entirely bad.

The dark man’s bewilderment deepened.

“What on earth is a man like Scrymgeour doing there?” he demanded. “If there’s one thing he’s fussy about—”

“There are at least eleven thousand things he’s fussy about,” interrupted the red-haired young man, with injured feeling. “Jumpy old blighter!”

“If there’s one thing he’s particular about,” persisted the other, “it’s the sort of hotel he goes to. Ever since I’ve known him he has always wanted the best. I should have thought he would have gone to the Splendide.” He pondered this as if it were a legal problem with unpleasant implications. Then, with the resignation of a man permitting the rich their eccentricities, he added, “I’d like to see him again. Ask him if he will dine with me at the Splendide to-night. Say eight sharp.”

Sally could not see the red-haired young man’s face, but she heard something go wrong in his voice when he answered. It had a false brightness, like a spoon tapping a cracked cup.

“Oh—Scrymgeour isn’t in Roville.”

“No? Where is he?”

“Paris, I believe.”

“What!” The dark man’s voice sharpened, suddenly all cross-examination. “Then why aren’t you there? What are you doing here? Did he give you a holiday?”

“Yes,” said the red-haired young man. “He did.”

“When do you rejoin him?”

“I don’t.”

“What!”

The red-haired young man spoke with stubborn flatness.

“Well, if you want to know—the old blighter fired me the day before yesterday.”

Sand scuffed as the dark man sprang up, stiff with outrage. Sally, absorbed, handed the poodle a sweet that ought to have gone to a newly arrived white terrier with a black patch over its left eye—an injustice that history was about to correct.

The dark man stood over his companion in an attitude familiar from melodrama: the stern father about to hurl the family disgrace into the snow.

“Do you mean to tell me,” he demanded, “that after all the trouble the family took to get you what was practically a sinecure, with endless possibilities if you only behaved yourself, you have deliberately thrown away—” He made a gesture that attempted to include all tragedy from Greek drama to the present moment. “Good God, you’re hopeless!”

The red-haired young man did not reply. He stared down the beach.

A fat bather in an orange suit—an object lesson in courage and tight elastic—had been rehearsing his entrance into the sea with repeated false starts and now, after six attempts and much hesitation, was finally afloat, dignified as a wrecked balloon. Normally the sight of middle-aged Frenchmen bathing was an entertainment of inexhaustible variety: toe-testing, dramatic wading, the final plunge like the leap of a seal that has had to think about it for a week. It was theatre with a salt-water orchestra. But the red-haired young man watched it without expression, as though joy were temporarily out of stock.

“It’s maddening!” raged the dark man. “What are you going to do? What do you expect us to do? Are we to spend our whole lives getting you positions you won’t keep? I can tell you we’re— it’s monstrous! It’s sickening! Good God!”

And with that he turned and stalked away up the beach, dignified in his fury until, a moment later, his straw hat blew off and was trodden on by a passing child. The hat’s end was so sudden and ignominious that even indignation seemed briefly embarrassed.

He left behind him the electric calm that follows a thunderbolt, when the air itself seems to quiver and wonder what it should do next.

It was answered almost immediately.

A short, low gurgling snarl, and there sprang into existence the prettiest dog fight Roville had seen that season.

It began, in Sally’s opinion, with the black-patched terrier; and, considering the evidence, historians will probably support her. Even the terrier’s most devoted biographer would have had trouble denying he fired the first shot. Still, fairness demands the deeper truth: the fault was Sally’s.

Preoccupied by the vanished dark man and the newly fascinating mystery of Scrymgeour, she had helped the poodle three times in succession. The third time was too much for the terrier’s sense of justice.

A dog fight has a gusty, elemental fury that makes human beings feel as if they have wandered accidentally into a hurricane. You get an overwhelming sense that interference is both necessary and impossible. And this was no small domestic quarrel. It was a magnificent mêlée, the sort that would have received appreciative comment in places accustomed to robust disagreement. Dogs came racing from all over the beach—big ones, small ones, aristocratic ones, ones that looked as if they had been assembled from spare parts. Some barked from the edges like critics; many plunged straight into battle purely on principle, delighted to be active without troubling about causes.

The terrier had the poodle by the left hind leg and appeared to be issuing a manifesto. The raffish mongrel had attached itself to a complete stranger of the Sealyham family and was attempting, with zeal and very bad manners, to rearrange him.

Sally was frankly unequal to the situation. So were the spectators who came galloping up, dripping and noisy, from the water’s edge. Advice flew in fluent French. Arms waved. Plump bathing suits jumped up and down like agitated pastries. But nobody did anything practical until, in the very centre of the chaos, the red-haired young man appeared.

Providence, which cannot be relied upon for punctual trains, is surprisingly consistent about dog fights. On such occasions it always provides one Master Mind: one wizard who may be a failure at life in general, but is magnificent in this single emergency. At Roville-sur-Mer, the Master Mind was the young man Scrymgeour had fired.

From the first moment he stepped in, calm began to creep over the battleground. He had on fighting dogs the same effect that legend gives to holy relics among battling knights: he didn’t look like peace, but peace arrived with him. His hands moved quickly and surely, separating snarling knots with a confidence that made everybody else feel ridiculous for having merely yelped advice. His voice, low and firm, carried over the barking with a strange soothing authority, like a cook’s tone in a kitchen crisis when a sauce is about to curdle: no drama, just decisions.

In an astonishingly short time dogs were being untangled, quieted, returned to owners who seized them and departed in whirlwinds of recrimination. Presently, all that remained of Armageddon was one small Scotch terrier sitting thoughtfully and licking a leg that had clearly been through something.

The red-haired young man turned to Sally. A moment ago he had been reckless and gallant; now he looked shy, almost boyish, as if the courage had leaked out of him the instant the emergency ended. He swallowed once or twice in that painful, heroic way an Englishman has when about to attempt a foreign language.

“J’espère,” he began, bracing himself for the jungle, “J’espère que vous n’êtes pas—oh, dammit, what’s the word—J’espère que vous n’êtes pas blessée?”

“Blessée?” Sally repeated.

“Yes, blessée. Wounded. Hurt, don’t you know. Bitten. Oh, dash it—J’espère…”

“Oh—bitten!” said Sally, dimpling. “Oh, no, thanks very much. I wasn’t bitten. And I think it was awfully brave of you to save all our lives.”

The compliment did not land where she intended. It went straight past his mind and struck him somewhere much lower. He stared at her with horrified eyes; then a blush swept over his freckled face like a sudden red tide. His jaw dropped.

“Oh, my sainted aunt!” he cried.

And then, as if the situation were unendurable and only flight could preserve him, he spun round and departed at a walk so rapid it was almost a run.

Sally watched him go with genuine regret. Apart from being entertaining, he had left her with a maddening unanswered question: what, exactly, had Scrymgeour fired him for?

That evening, Roville arranged itself as it always did: the nearer you were to the sea, the later the night pretended it was. Along the front, the grand hotels kept deplorable hours and polluted the darkness with indefatigable jazz—brassy, mechanical, hammering out the same cheerful violence until dawn, as if noise were a virtue and quiet a kind of failure. Sally, strolling the moonlit promenade after the Casino, found herself longing for the modest acoustics of kitchens and farms, places where sound belonged to work and breathing, not to commerce.

At the Normandie—economical, unglittering, and sensibly sleepy—early to bed was the rule. Shortly before midnight, Sally came in to find the little hall dim and silent. A faint bulb glowed through the iron cage of the lift; another illuminated the upper half of Jules at the desk, asleep in a chair. Jules always seemed to be on duty; his life had the relentless continuity of a kitchen pot left simmering. Sally’s sympathetic heart ached for him. She hesitated, not wanting to disturb his few hard-won winks.

Footsteps sounded outside. The front door opened and banged shut. Jules stirred, gurgled, blinked himself upright, and Sally turned to see the newcomer.

It was the red-haired young man.

“Oh, good evening,” said Sally, genuinely pleased. His presence made the lobby feel less like a hush and more like a scene.

He stopped as if the words had hit him in the chest. His embarrassment from the morning was still painfully alive; his face was scarlet again, either from continuous blushing since breakfast or from starting anew in celebration.

“Er—good evening,” he said, and somehow his feet became entangled with one another, as if they too were shy.

“Or bon soir, I suppose you would say,” murmured Sally.

He acknowledged this by dropping his hat and tripping over it as he bent to retrieve it.

Jules, meanwhile, had drifted in a sleepwalking way toward the lift and rattled back the cage.

“It’s a shame to have woken you up,” said Sally, stepping in.

Jules did not reply, because Jules was not awake. Habit had taught him to perform much of his work while asleep, as some men can eat soup without ceasing to talk. He clanged the gate shut, tugged at a rope with slow certainty, and sent the lift lumbering upward—his mind absent, his body obedient.

Sally and the red-haired young man sat side by side on the narrow seat. Conversation, after a feeble beginning, died on the spot. Sally couldn’t think of anything that wasn’t either too personal or in French. Her companion, when not exclaiming about girls, seemed capable of being one of those strong, silent men you read about—though Sally suspected, from the evidence, that he was strong, noisy, and merely experiencing a temporary silence.

Only a faint snore from Jules punctuated the ride.

At the third floor, Sally leaned forward and prodded Jules in the lower ribs. During her stay she had learned that in dealing with the native population, action spoke louder than words. In shops and restaurants she pointed; in lifts she prodded. It was a system worth a dozen conversation manuals.

Jules brought the lift to a halt.

Now, at this point, he ought to have performed the one act of his profession that he did really well: opening the iron cage. Jules had a flourish for it, and usually accompanied it with a modest “V’la!” as if to say that perhaps he wasn’t much to look at, but there were men in Paris who couldn’t open a lift door like that.

Tonight, however, even this triumph deserted him.

Instead of inserting his key, Jules stood staring in frozen horror at the lock as if it had turned into an enemy. His face bore the solemn dismay of a man who takes life seriously and has just discovered it has misplaced something important.

“There appears,” said Sally, turning to her companion, “to be a hitch. Would you mind asking what’s the matter? I don’t know any French myself except ‘oo la la!’”

The red-haired young man, thus enlisted, nerved himself. He eyed Jules as if approaching a wild animal and coughed in a strangled way.

“Oh, esker… esker vous…”

“Don’t weaken,” said Sally. “I think you’ve got him going.”

“Esker vous… Pourquoi vous ne… I mean ne vous… that is to say, quel est le raison…”

He broke off because Jules began to explain.

Jules explained with a magnificent flood of French, rapid and extensive, as if he were pouring a whole soup tureen of words onto the table at once. The fact that neither listener understood a syllable did not deter him. If he noticed their blankness, he dismissed it as a detail unworthy of attention. He had a story; he told it. Sounds that might have been meaningful if separated and repeated slowly went rushing past in one long torrent and were lost.

“Stop him!” said Sally firmly.

The red-haired young man looked like a man asked to stop a waterfall.

“Stop him?”

“Yes. Blow a whistle or something.”

From some submerged corner of memory there rose a single French word—possibly from school, possibly from hearing it shouted in a play.

“Zut!” he barked.

Instantly Jules shut off, as if someone had turned a tap. Silence fell with the suddenness of a factory gone dead.

“Quick!” said Sally. “Now you’ve got him. Ask him what he’s talking about—if he knows, which I doubt—and tell him to speak slowly. Then we shall get somewhere.”

The red-haired young man nodded, grateful for a plan.

“Lentement,” he said. “Parlez lentement. Pas si— you know what I mean—pas si dashed vite!”

“Ah-a-ah!” cried Jules, delighted, catching the idea neatly. “Lentement. Ah, oui, lentement.”

He began again, this time in slow, careful segments, and though it conveyed nothing to Sally, it evidently arranged itself into meaning for her companion. He listened, frowning with the concentration of a man tasting something odd and trying to decide whether it is pepper or disaster.

At last he turned to Sally with the air of one delivering a verdict.

“The silly ass,” he announced, “has made a bloomer. Apparently he was half asleep when we came in, and he shoved us into the lift and slammed the door, forgetting that he had left the keys on the desk.”

“I see,” said Sally. “So we’re shut in?”

“I’m afraid so. I wish to goodness,” added the young man, with feeling, “I knew French well. I’d curse him with some vim and not a little animation, the chump! I wonder what ‘blighter’ is in French,” he mused, gloomily, as if the entire language had been invented chiefly to insult Jules properly.

By six o’clock the next evening Sally had arrived at the sort of conclusion that tastes like cold tea: all day she had meant to find Ginger and pour a little wholesome counsel down his ear, and all day Roville had contrived to mislay him. In another hour she would be on the seven-fifteen express for Paris, on to Cherbourg, on to the great Atlantic liner, on to New York—on to Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house and the particular kind of breakfast Mrs. Meecher believed in, which was chiefly noise.

Sally disliked leaving any human creature unelevated, even slightly. It was a habit of hers, almost domestic—like straightening a picture on the wall before you go out. Her brother Fillmore called it “messing about with people’s lives.” Sally called it “taking an interest.” Ginger Kemp, with his candid air of having been dropped into existence without proper instructions, seemed to need a shove in the direction of competence.

So, having deposited her baggage at the station and left it in the charge of an amiable porter who looked as if he had been born leaning on things, she went, at half-past six, to the Casino Municipale for a last prowl.

There were two casinos at Roville-sur-Mer. One, on the Promenade, specialized in sea air, mild virtue, and a game called boule that gives you time to reflect on your sins between rolls. The Casino Municipale, down by the Palace Massena near the railway station, was for men who meant business and, as a rule, meant it loudly.

Sally had barely entered when she saw it: a flare of red hair in the crowd at the roulette-table, like a flame among moths. Ginger. And Ginger was not merely present; Ginger was prospering.

He was entrenched behind a rich little landscape of square counters, piled with the care of a pastrycook building a display in a shop-window—layers and stacks, the sort of structure you expected to see topped with spun sugar. A wooden-faced croupier raked a fresh instalment toward him as if shoveling coal.

“Épatant!” murmured a wistful man near Sally, pulling an elbow out of her ribs so he could gesture more freely at Ginger’s magnificence.

Sally was no scholar, but she had been around enough hungry people to recognize admiration. The whole congregation around the table had the rapt, churchy look of persons attending a miracle in progress. Spectators at roulette always do. They don’t want to win themselves so much as they want to witness the existence of luck, in the same way children like to watch somebody else’s Christmas.

The croupier twisted his moustache with one hand, the wheel with the other, and a hush dropped like a cloth. Sally edged to a place where the crowd pressed less hard and could now see Ginger’s face clearly.

And at the sight of it she laughed.

Ginger looked exactly like a dog at a rat-hole: nose forward, eyes bright, hair practically on end, the whole of him quivering with that intense hopefulness which, in the kitchen, makes a man keep lifting a pot-lid every ten seconds to see if stew has decided to become done by sheer force of attention.

In the tense silence, Sally’s laugh rang out with an atrocious clearness. Heads turned with the pained horror of worshippers interrupted during a particularly sacred hymn. If she had guffawed in a cathedral, the effect would have been gentler. The crowd looked at her as if she had tossed a tomato at the altar.

Ginger started, turned, saw her—and jumped up.

At that precise instant the ball clicked into a red compartment.

Ginger had backed black.

A groan ran through the crowd, thick and communal, like the moan at a revival meeting when the preacher announces that somebody has gone back to drink. Glances of reproach hit Sally from all directions. It was plainly felt that her laughter had poisoned the very air over the wheel.

Ginger, however, showed no desire to lynch her. He swept up his winnings, shoved them into his pocket with the speed of a man putting away hot rolls before someone else notices them, and elbowed through to her. The crowd was bereaved. It was as though the star tenor had walked off in the middle of his big note, and even the sudden quarrel between two excitable gamblers over a disputed five-franc counter could not wholly distract them.

“I say,” said Ginger, deftly plucking Sally out of the press, “this is topping, meeting you like this. I’ve been looking for you everywhere.”

“It’s funny you didn’t find me, then, because that’s exactly where I’ve been. I was looking for you.”

“No, really?” He seemed absurdly pleased. He led her into the quiet ante-room outside the hall, where the air was calmer and smelled faintly of cigar smoke and polished wood instead of human suspense. They sat in a corner; the only other presence was a gorgeously uniformed attendant by the door, watching them with the indulgent interest of a man who has seen romance performed in many keys.

“That was awfully good of you,” said Ginger.

“I felt I must have a talk with you before my train went.”

Ginger gave a violent start.

“Your train? What do you mean?”

“The puff-puff,” explained Sally. “I’m leaving to-night, you know.”

“Leaving?” He stared at her as if she had just announced that gravity had been discontinued. “You don’t mean leaving? You’re not going away from Roville?”

“I’m afraid so.”

“But why? Where are you going?”

“Back to America. My boat sails from Cherbourg tomorrow.”

“Oh, my aunt!”

“I’m sorry,” said Sally, touched. It was pleasant, being regretted. “But—”

“I say—” Ginger flushed a bright, helpless scarlet and glared at the uniformed official, who was now openly basking in the idea that love was happening near him. The man even began to hum a love-ballad under his breath, softly, as if providing accompaniment.

“I say, look here,” said Ginger, “will you marry me?”

Sally stared at his vermilion profile in frank amazement. Ginger had a gift for doing surprising things with no apparent preparation, like an amateur who suddenly juggles three knives.

“Marry you!”

“You know what I mean.”

“Well, yes. The holy state. I do understand the general direction of your remarks.”

“Then how about it?”

Sally recovered her composure by degrees. Her sense of humour, always near the surface, wriggled delightedly.

“But isn’t this—don’t think I’m trying to make difficulties—isn’t this a little sudden?”

“It’s got to be sudden,” complained Ginger. “I thought you were going to be here for weeks.”

“But, my infant, my babe, has it occurred to you that we are practically strangers?” She patted his hand with an aunt-like tolerance that made the uniformed official emit a tender sigh. “I see what’s happened. You’re mistaking me for some other girl—some girl you were properly introduced to. Take a good look at me and you’ll see.”

“If I take a good look at you,” said Ginger feverishly, “I’m dashed if I’ll answer for the consequences.”

“And this,” said Sally, “is the man I was going to lecture on Enterprise.”

“You’re the most wonderful girl I’ve ever met, dash it!” said Ginger. He still wouldn’t look at her properly; he continued to aim his passion at the official by the door, as if the sight of a witness made it easier to be brave. “I dare say it is sudden. I can’t help that. I fell in love with you the moment I saw you, and there you are!”

“But—”

“Now look here, I know I’m not much of a chap and all that, but… well, I’ve just won the deuce of a lot of money in there…”

“Would you buy me with your gold?”

“I mean to say, we’d have enough to start on, and… of course I’ve made an infernal hash of everything I’ve tried up till now, but there must be something I can do, and you can jolly well bet I’d have a goodish stab at it. I mean to say, with you to buck me up and so forth, don’t you know. Well, I mean—”

“Has it struck you that I may already be engaged to someone else?”

“Oh, golly! Are you?”

For the first time he turned and looked at her. There was something in his eyes—so boyish, so honestly stricken—that punched the laughter out of her. Absurd though it was, he meant every word.

“Well, yes,” she said quietly. “As a matter of fact I am.”

Ginger bit his lip. For a moment he didn’t speak.

“Oh, well, that’s torn it!” he said at last.

Sally felt a wave of something she couldn’t neatly name—pity and amusement and an odd softness that was almost maternal. She wanted to pick him up and kiss him and tell him it would all come right, even though she couldn’t see how it would. What she could not do was regard him, at that instant, as a fully responsible adult.

“You don’t really mean it, you know,” she said.

“Don’t I!” said Ginger hollowly. “Oh, don’t I!”

“You can’t. There isn’t such a thing in real life as love at first sight. Love’s a thing that comes when you know a person well and—” She stopped. An awkward thought had popped up like a lump in a sauce: her own engagement to Gerald Foster had been fast enough to startle a stopwatch. What did she know of Gerald except that she loved him? They had become engaged within two weeks of their first meeting.

Her eloquence collapsed.

“It’s ridiculous,” she finished tamely.

Ginger subsided into a gloomy resignation.

“I couldn’t have expected you to care for me, I suppose, anyway,” he said. “I’m not much of a chap.”

That, at least, was useful. Sally seized it as a cook seizes a wooden spoon when a pot begins to boil over.

“That’s exactly what I wanted to talk to you about,” she said briskly. “I’ve been looking for you all day to go on with what I was starting to say in the lift last night, when we were interrupted. Do you mind if I talk to you like an aunt—or a sister, suppose we say? Really, the best plan would be for you to adopt me as an honorary sister. What do you think?”

Ginger did not look wildly thrilled by the proposed relationship.

“Because I really do take a tremendous interest in you.”

He brightened a little, as if interest were a warm thing you could put in your pocket.

“That’s awfully good of you.”

“I’m going to speak words of wisdom. Ginger, why don’t you brace up?”

“Brace up?”

“Yes—stiffen your backbone and stick out your chin and square your elbows and really amount to something. Why do you flop about and do nothing and leave everything to what you call ‘the family’? Why do you have to be helped all the time? Why don’t you help yourself? Why do you have to have jobs found for you? Why don’t you rush out and get one? Why do you have to worry about what the family thinks of you? Why don’t you make yourself independent of them? I know you’ve had hard luck, suddenly finding yourself without money and all that, but good heavens, everybody who’s ever done anything has been broke at one time or another. It’s part of the fun.

“You’ll never get anywhere by letting yourself be picked up by the family like—like a floppy Newfoundland puppy and dumped down in any old place that happens to suit them. A job’s a thing you’ve got to choose for yourself and get for yourself. Think what you can do—there must be something—and then go at it with a snort and grab it and hold it down and teach it to take a joke. You’ve managed to collect some money. It’ll give you time to look round. And when you’ve had a look round, do something! Try to realize you’re alive, and try to imagine the family isn’t!”

She stopped for breath.

Ginger sat very still, as if the speech had filled him like a strong draught.

“When you talk quick,” he said slowly, in a serious, reflective voice, “your nose sort of goes all squiggly. Ripping, it looks!”

Sally emitted an indignant little cry.

“Do you mean to say you haven’t been listening to a word I’ve been saying?”

“Oh, rather! Oh, by Jove, yes.”

“Well, what did I say?”

“You… er…” He considered. “And your eyes sort of shine, too.”

“Never mind my eyes. What did I say?”

“You told me,” said Ginger, with effort, “to get a job.”

“Well, yes. I put it much better than that, but that’s what it amounted to. All right, then. I’m glad you—”

“I say,” he interrupted, and his mournful devotion returned like a dog that has wandered off and found its way home, “I wish you’d let me write to you. Letters, I mean, and all that. I’ve an idea it would kind of buck me up.”

“You won’t have time for writing letters.”

“I’ll have time to write them to you. You haven’t an address or anything of that sort in America, have you? I mean, so that I’d know where to write to.”

“I can give you an address that will always find me.”

She told him the number and street of Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house. He wrote them down reverently on his shirt-cuff, as if it were a recipe for salvation.

“Yes,” said Sally, relenting, “do write. Of course I’ll want to know how you’ve got on. I—oh, my goodness! That clock’s not right?”

“Just about. What time does your train go?”

“Go? It’s gone! Or, at least, it goes in about two seconds.”

She shot through the swing-door, startling the uniformed official, who had not expected romance to turn into athletics. Ginger followed with the sudden efficiency he always produced when bodies had to be moved quickly.

“Good-bye, Ginger,” she called over her shoulder as they burst into the square. “Write to me, and remember what I said!”

They ran. Roville’s evening had its own soundscape: the scrape of shoes on pavement, the thin whistle of distant trains, a motor-car coughing like a bored brass instrument, and underneath it all the steady sea-hush that made even human panic feel briefly theatrical. Sally, who believed the world was kinder when it made decent noises, found herself regretting the growing industrial grumble that seemed to stalk every station platform in Europe like an ill-tempered relative.

“Stick it!” said Ginger encouragingly, loping easily, as becomes a man who had once been a snip of an international scrum-half.

Sally saved her breath.

The train was already beginning to move as they sprinted on to the platform. Ginger dived at the nearest door, wrenched it open, gathered Sally in his arms with the confident handling of a man lifting a sack of flour, and flung her in.

She landed squarely on somebody’s toes.

Then, bounding upright, she made for the window. Ginger ran alongside the train as it gathered speed.

“Ginger! My poor porter! Tip him. I forgot.”

“Right ho!”

“And don’t forget what I’ve been saying.”

“Right ho!”

“Look after yourself and ‘Death to the Family!’”

“Right ho!”

The train slid out of the station. Sally took one last look back at her red-haired friend, who had halted and was waving a handkerchief. Then she turned, flushed and panting, to apologize to the other occupant of the compartment.

“I’m so sorry,” she said. “I hope I didn’t hurt you.”

She found herself facing Bruce Carmyle.

Mr. Carmyle was not a man who readily allowed life to ruffle his dignity, but he could not help feeling slightly stunned. He recognized her: the girl from yesterday’s beach episode, whom he had assumed to be French. Now she addressed him in fluent English, as if languages were hats one changed between luncheon and dinner. Also she appeared to have become, in the space of a single day, intimate enough with his cousin Lancelot to sprint with him down platforms and shout “Ginger” out of railway windows.

Bruce Carmyle held that nickname to be vulgar. It was a little like calling a duchess “Pinky.” Yet here it was, in a pretty girl’s mouth, and the universe had not exploded.

If Sally had been less pretty, he would have frowned. Her abrupt entry, her breathlessness, her general air of having treated the compartment as a tackleable objective, all gave his rigid proprieties a nasty jar. But she was extremely pretty—prettier flushed than at rest—and he contrived a smile.

“Not at all,” he said, though his left big toe throbbed with a private, dignified agony. Even a small foot can communicate meaning to a big toe if a scrum-half has aimed well.

“If you don’t mind,” said Sally, sitting down, “I think I’ll breathe a little.”

She breathed. The train clattered on, making that stitched-together music of wheels on rails—steady, repetitive, almost comforting, like a good cook’s knife on a board. Mr. Carmyle found his irritation settling. The pain in his toe began to fade.

“Quite a close thing,” he observed affably. “You nearly missed it.”

“Yes. It was lucky Mr. Kemp was with me. He throws very straight, doesn’t he.”

“Tell me,” said Carmyle, “how do you come to know my cousin? On the beach yesterday morning—”

“Oh, we didn’t know each other then. But we were staying at the same hotel, and we spent an hour or so shut up in an elevator together. That was when we really got acquainted.”

A waiter entered and announced, in unexpected English, that dinner was served in the restaurant car.

“Would you care for dinner?” asked Mr. Carmyle.

“I’m starving,” said Sally.

As they went along the corridor, with the train’s noises changing timbre from compartment hush to dining-car clink, Sally reproved herself for having judged this grim-faced man too quickly. He could be perfectly pleasant. By the time they sat down she had decided she liked him.

At the table, unfortunately, Mr. Carmyle altered.

He became a different specimen: a man who took meals seriously and believed waiters should be managed with severity, like dangerous machinery. He shuddered at a stain on the cloth as if it were an insult to his ancestors and bent over the bill of fare with a frown of moral disapproval.

Sally, meanwhile, was establishing cosy relations with the waiter, a cheerful old fellow who instantly adopted her as a favourite daughter. He spoke no English; Sally spoke no French; but they were communicating beautifully in the universal dialect of appetite and goodwill. The man had the warm, oniony smell of kitchens—wine, soap, and lingering stock—and Sally felt suddenly homesick for the honest music of cooking: the sizzle that means butter has reached the right heat, the soft thunder of a simmer, the little whisper when a knife slices ripe fruit.

Mr. Carmyle waved aside the waiter’s friendly hovering—at the Hotel Splendide, waiters did not bend over you and breathe suggestions down your cheek—and ordered crisply in the Anglo-Gallic of the travelling Briton.

The waiter exclaimed “Boum!” with pleasure and vanished.

“Nice old man!” said Sally.

“Infernally familiar,” said Mr. Carmyle.

Sally saw they would not agree about the waiter. She changed the subject, being determined to be courteous.

“By the way,” she said, “my name is Nicholas. I always think it’s a good thing to start with names, don’t you?”

“Mine—”

“Oh, I know yours. Ginger—Mr. Kemp told me.”

Mr. Carmyle, who had been thawing, stiffened at once, as if the word had been a draught.

“Indeed?” he said coldly. “Apparently you got intimate.”

Sally disliked his tone. It sounded like a rebuke, and Sally did not accept rebukes from strangers. Her eyes widened.

“Why ‘apparently’? I told you we had got intimate, and I explained how. You can’t stay shut up in an elevator half the night with anybody without getting to know him. I found Mr. Kemp very pleasant.”

“Really?”

“And very interesting.”

Mr. Carmyle lifted his eyebrows.

“Would you call him interesting?”

“I did call him interesting.”

Sally felt the bright, reckless exhilaration of battle. Men usually made themselves agreeable to her; when one did not, she became instantly and thoroughly ready.

“He told me all about himself,” she added.

“Indeed?” Mr. Carmyle’s gaze went to the length of French bread on the table, as if wishing it were a gavel. “He has an engaging lack of reticence.”

Soup arrived with a flourish; the waiter dumped it down.

“V’là!” he announced, beaming as if he had produced it out of his hat.

He smiled at Sally confidently, expecting applause. But Sally’s face had set. Being snubbed was new, and she did not find the novelty soothing.

“I think Mr. Kemp had hard luck,” she said.

“If you will excuse me,” said Mr. Carmyle, “I would prefer not to discuss the matter.”

His manner said: Pretty girl or not, you are a stranger, and strangers do not discuss the private stew-pot of the Family.

“He was quite in the right. Mr. Scrymgeour was beating a dog—”

“I’ve heard the details.”

“Oh. I didn’t know that. Then don’t you agree with me?”

“I do not. A man who would throw away an excellent position simply because—”

“Oh, well, if that’s your view, I suppose it is useless to talk about it.”

“Quite.”

“Still,” said Sally, tasting indignation like pepper, “there’s no harm in asking what you propose to do about Gin—about Mr. Kemp.”

Mr. Carmyle grew even more glacial.

“I’m afraid I cannot discuss—”

Sally’s patience, which had been hanging by a thread like a poorly tied bundle of herbs, snapped.

“Oh, for goodness’ sake,” she said, “do try to be human, and don’t always be snubbing people. You remind me of one of those portraits of men in the eighteenth century, with wooden faces, who look out of heavy gold frames at you with fishy eyes as if you were a regrettable incident.”

“Rosbif,” said the waiter genially, appearing at their elbows with the suddenness of a kitchen timer.

Mr. Carmyle attacked his roast beef with morose precision. Sally sat in silence, the sort of silence that crackles.

“I am sorry,” said Mr. Carmyle at last, ponderously, “if my eyes are fishy. The fact has not been called to my attention before.”

“I suppose you never had any sisters,” said Sally.

“They would have told you.”

Mr. Carmyle relapsed into offended dumbness, which held until coffee arrived. The dining-car was full of clinks and murmurs and that steady undertone of rails—sound that ought to have been soothing, but now only underlined the uncomfortable rhythm between them, like bad accompaniment.

“I think,” said Sally, rising, “I’ll be going now. I don’t seem to want any coffee, and if I stay on I may say something rude. I thought I might be able to put in a good word for Mr. Kemp and save him from being massacred, but apparently it’s no use. Good-bye, Mr. Carmyle, and thank you for giving me dinner.”

She went down the car.

Bruce Carmyle watched her go with an expression that was indignant—yet, in some unexplained corner of him, fascinated. Strange emotions were stirring in Mr. Carmyle’s bosom, and he did not in the least like the noise they made.

Some few days later, because the other party happened to be preoccupied enough not to spot him first, Bruce Carmyle met his cousin Lancelot—known to a jocose world as Ginger—in Piccadilly.

They had come back from Roville by different routes, and Ginger would have liked the universe to respect that division indefinitely, as one respects the separate jars of chutney and jam. He was hurrying on with the sort of nod a man gives a bailiff when the bailiff has mistaken him for someone who pays his debts, when Carmyle’s voice hooked him by the collar.

“Just the man I wanted to see.”

“Oh, hullo!” said Ginger, without joy, like a cook who has lifted a saucepan lid and found yesterday’s fish smiling up at him.

“I was thinking of calling at your club.”

“Yes?” said Ginger warily.

“Yes. Cigarette?”

Ginger peered at the proffered case with the vague suspicion of a man who has allowed himself to be lured on to a music-hall stage and is now being handed a card by the conjurer. In all their years of acquaintance he could not recall another such genial outbreak. It bewildered him. He was surprised, indeed, at Mr. Carmyle’s speaking to him at all, for the affaire Scrymgeour remained an unhealed wound, and the Family, Ginger knew, were even now sitting upon it like a committee of elders in a drought, discussing with grave faces whether the next season’s seed should be saved or eaten.

“Been back in London long?” asked Carmyle.

“Day or two.”

“I heard quite by accident that you had returned and that you were staying at the club. By the way, thank you for introducing me to Miss Nicholas.”

Ginger started violently, as if somebody had dropped a dish behind him in a quiet kitchen.

“What!”

“I was in that compartment, you know, at Roville Station. You threw her right on top of me. We agreed to consider that an introduction. An attractive girl.”

He said it with the careful, measured relish of a man tasting something he can’t quite classify—pleasant on the tongue, possibly dangerous to the digestion, and certainly not to be forgotten. Bruce Carmyle had not entirely made up his mind about Sally, but on one point he was clear: she must not, if he could help it, pass out of his life. Her abrupt departure had left him with the baffled dissatisfaction one gets when a restaurant produces a masterpiece of a sauce and then, just as you’re about to ask how it’s made, clears the plate and shuts for the night.

She had had the better of their late encounter; he knew it. And he wanted to meet her again and demonstrate, like a chef stung by a critic, that there was more in him than she apparently supposed.

“A very attractive girl,” he repeated. “We had a very pleasant talk.”

“I bet you did,” said Ginger enviously. Envy in Ginger was never abstract; it had a taste, sharp and metallic, like biting a spoon.

“By the way,” said Carmyle, “she did not give you her address by any chance?”

“Why?” said Ginger suspiciously.

His attitude toward Sally’s address resembled that of a connoisseur who has acquired a unique work of art—or, better, the last jar of a grandmother’s pickle made in a dialect nobody speaks anymore. You don’t hand that around. You keep it in a cool cupboard and go in now and then just to look at it and remember.

“Well, I—er—I promised to send her some books she was anxious to read…”

“I shouldn’t think she gets much time for reading.”

“Books which are not published in America.”

“Oh, pretty nearly everything is published in America, what? Bound to be, I mean.”

“Well, these particular books are not,” said Mr. Carmyle shortly.

He was finding Ginger’s reserve trying, and wished he had been more inventive. He had the uncomfortable feeling of a man seasoning a dish without knowing what’s already in the pot.

“Give them to me and I’ll send them to her,” suggested Ginger, with the air of somebody offering to take the hot tray out of the oven for you and then keep it.

“Good Lord, man!” snapped Mr. Carmyle. “I’m capable of sending a few books to America. Where does she live?”

Ginger held out for a moment longer, clutching that number and street as if they were a recipe in a dying tongue, untranslatable into ordinary English without losing the whole point. But with a persistent devil like his cousin there seemed no way out. At last he yielded and revealed the sacred number of the holy street where Sally had her headquarters—grudgingly, as if each syllable were a spice he had paid too much for.

“Thanks.”

Bruce Carmyle wrote it down with a gold pencil in a dapper little morocco-bound notebook. He was the sort of man who always has a pencil; the backs of old envelopes never enter into his life. A man like that would, if left alone in a farmhouse kitchen, label the flour and feel morally improved.

There was a pause. Piccadilly made its own noise around them: the ceaseless shuffling hiss of tyres, the clipped hooves of the occasional horse surviving like an old folk-song among gramophones, the talk of passers-by, all of it stirred together into that modern broth of sound which never quite lets the mind settle. If you listened hard you could almost hear the older London underneath—quiet stones, slow voices, the scrape of knives on plates at supper—trying and failing to speak through the racket.

Bruce Carmyle coughed.

“I saw Uncle Donald this morning,” he said.

His manner had lost its geniality. There was no need of it now, and he was a man who objected to waste. He spoke coldly, and in his voice there was the familiar sub-tingle of reproof, like vinegar appearing where one had hoped for cream.

“Yes?” said Ginger moodily.

This was the uncle in whose office he had made his debut as a hasher: a worthy man, highly respected in the National Liberal Club, but never a favourite of Ginger’s. There were other minor uncles and a few subsidiary aunts who went to make up the Family, but Uncle Donald was unquestionably the managing director of that body; and it was Ginger’s considered opinion that in this capacity he approximated to a human blister.

“He wants you to dine with him to-night at Bleke’s.”

Ginger’s depression deepened. A dinner with Uncle Donald would hardly have been cheerful even amid the intoxicated splendours of an Arabian Nights banquet. There was something about Uncle Donald’s personality which would have sobered the orgies of the Emperor Tiberius at Capri. And to dine with him at Bleke’s Coffee House—the sort of place that had been old when your grandfather was young, and took pride in it—was to scrape right down to bed-rock.

Bleke’s was one of those relics of Old London that seemed to have been preserved, like potted meat, in its own gloom. The regular patrons had not missed an evening there for half a century. If they had ever laughed, it must have been in Latin, and probably in whispers. Even the sound in such a place was different: not the bright clatter of a restaurant where forks strike plates and voices rise and fall like birds in a hedgerow, but a subdued, stale hush broken now and then by the dry click of cutlery and the discreet cough of a waiter who has forgotten what joy sounds like.

“To-night?” said Ginger. “Oh, you mean to-night? Well—”

“Don’t be a fool,” said Carmyle. “You know as well as I do that you’ve got to go.”

Uncle Donald’s invitations were royal commands in the Family. You might as well refuse the weather.

“If you’ve another engagement you must put it off.”

“Oh, all right.”

“Seven-thirty sharp.”

“All right,” said Ginger gloomily.

The two men went their ways—Bruce Carmyle eastwards, because he had clients to see in his chambers at the Temple; Ginger westwards, because Mr. Carmyle had gone east. There was little sympathy between these cousins, yet oddly enough their thoughts, as they walked, centred on the same object.

Bruce Carmyle, threading his way briskly through the crowds of Piccadilly Circus, was thinking of Sally.

So was Ginger as he loafed aimlessly toward Hyde Park Corner, bumping in a sort of coma from pedestrian to pedestrian, like a distracted waiter trying to carry too much on one tray. Since his return to London he had been in bad shape. He mooned through the days and slept poorly at night. If there is one thing rottener than another in a pretty blighted world, one thing which gives a fellow the pip and reduces him to the condition of an absolute onion, it is hopeless love.

Hopeless love had got Ginger all stirred up.

He had been, hitherto, a placid soul. Even the financial crash which had altered his life had not bruised him very deeply. His temperament had enabled him to bear the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune with a philosophic “Right ho!” But now everything seemed different. Things irritated him acutely which before he had accepted as inevitable—his Uncle Donald’s moustache, for instance, and its owner’s habit of employing it during meals as a sort of zareba or earthwork against the assaults of soup.

One could picture it: the spoon advancing, the moustache rising like a defensive hedgerow, the soup retreating in baffled little droplets. It wasn’t merely a personal foible; it was an entire method of eating, a whole dialect of table-manners that declared, in its own bristly way, that the world must accommodate itself to Uncle Donald.

“By gad!” thought Ginger, stopping suddenly opposite Devonshire House. “If he uses that damned shrubbery as soup-strainer to-night, I’ll slosh him with a fork!”

Hard thoughts… hard thoughts! And getting harder all the time, for nothing grows more quickly than a mood of rebellion. Rebellion is a forest fire that flames across the soul. The spark had been lighted in Ginger; and long before he reached Hyde Park Corner he was ablaze and crackling.

By the time he returned to his club he was practically a menace to society—at any rate to that section of it which embraced his Uncle Donald, his minor uncles George and William, and his aunts Mary, Geraldine, and Louise. Nor had the mood passed when he began to dress for the dismal festivities of Bleke’s Coffee House.

He scowled as he struggled morosely with an obstinate tie, wrestling it as if it were part of the Family itself, a strip of respectable cloth designed to choke any honest impulse at the collar. One cannot disguise the fact: Ginger was warming up.

And it was just at this moment that Fate, as though it had been waiting for the psychological instant, applied the finishing touch.

There was a knock at the door, and a waiter came in with a telegram.

Ginger looked at the envelope. It had been readdressed and forwarded on from the Hotel Normandie. It was a wireless, handed in on board the White Star liner Olympic, and it ran as follows:

Remember. Death to the Family. S.

Ginger sat down heavily on the bed, as if somebody had hit him in the chest with a sack of flour. The words had a sound in his head—three clipped notes like a knife tapping a board. “Remember,” like the opening of an old song in a vanished language; “Death to the Family,” like the chorus shouted by a crowd; and that single “S.” at the end, unexpanded and therefore untranslatable, containing more in its small curve than any respectable sentence could conveniently hold.

The driver of the taxi-cab which at twenty-five minutes past seven drew up at the dingy door of Bleke’s Coffee House in the Strand was rather struck by his fare’s manner and appearance.

A determined-looking sort of young bloke, was the taxi-driver’s verdict.

Sally had meant, when the ship slid in and the city rose up like a tray of iced cakes, to go straight to the St. Regis and let her money do what money loves to do—puff pillows, gild faucets, and make a girl feel she ought to be served with a silver spoon even when she only wants a glass of water. She could see herself already, luxuriously installed, ordering breakfast she did not have to argue for.

Then, at the very moment she was giving the taxi-driver the address, the plan took on a look—how could one describe it?—of being slightly too Fillmorian. Not Fillmore himself, perhaps, but the Fillmore tendency: the neat, superior impulse to discard a thing the instant it ceased to be new.

No. It would be time enough to leave Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house—the place that had been her home for three years—when she had found her permanent apartment. Until then, the decent thing (and Sally, for all her sins, had a conscience like a small, watchful cook who never forgot the salt) was to go back to the old establishment and foregather with her old friends. Home, after all, is where your heart is, even if your heart has, on certain evenings, to endure prunes in quantities that would have made a French chef weep into his stockpot.

Besides, there was Gerald Foster. The thought of seeing him again put a warm fizz into her blood, like ginger ale poured too quickly.

New York on that morning appeared to have been scrubbed and aired. The sky was a clean blue; the sunlight had an amber, breakfasty look, as though it had been warmed in a pan with butter. Even the ash-cans had a certain brisk respectability, as if they had been up early and meant to do a full day’s work. Street-cars clanged cheerfully; policemen’s whistles piped bright little commands; and the rhythm of the city—the hoofbeats, the cries, the rolling wheels—had that good acoustic balance which makes you feel life is not merely happening but keeping time. After the long, flat ship-noise of the voyage, it was like coming back to music.

And then, like a penny dropped into a custard, Mrs. Meecher spoiled the perfect surface.

Mrs. Meecher, having expressed gratification at the news that Sally required her old room again, said, with the air of one who enjoys handing out misfortunes as if they were extra helpings:

“Mr. Foster’s left town this morning. Gone to Detroit, he has. Miss Doland, too.”

She broke off to deliver a tart remark to the boarding-house handyman, who was coming up the stairs with Sally’s trunk and using it, by the sound, as a battering-ram against the wallpaper.

“There’s that play of his being tried out there Monday,” resumed Mrs. Meecher, when the man had thumped past. “They been rehearsing ever since you left.”

Sally felt the disappointment nip her—sharp, quick, like a cold spoon against the back of the neck. Still, it was far too glorious a morning to let gloom settle and stay. She could go to Detroit tomorrow. It was almost pleasant, having something definite to look forward to, like setting bread to rise.

“Oh, is Elsa in the company?” she asked.

“Sure. And very good too, I hear.” Mrs. Meecher was one of those women who keep abreast of theatrical gossip with the same precision other women reserve for jam-making. She had once been in the first production of “Florodora”—not, she always made clear, one of the original Sextette, but near enough to the flame to have acquired a lifelong relish for stage smoke. “Mr. Faucitt was down to see a rehearsal, and he said Miss Doland was fine. And he’s not easy to please, as you know.”

“How is Mr. Faucitt?”

Mrs. Meecher’s eyes brightened in the way some people’s do at the mention of calamity.

“Poor old gentleman, he ain’t over and above well. Went to bed early last night with a headache, and this morning I been to see him and he don’t look well. There’s a lot of this Spanish influenza about. It might be that. Lots o’ people been dying of it, if you believe what you see in the papers.”

“Good gracious! You don’t think—”

“Well, he ain’t turned black,” admitted Mrs. Meecher, with something like disappointment. “They say they turn black. If you believe what you see in the papers, that is. Of course, that may come later,” she added, as one who trusts the future to do its duty. “The doctor’ll be in pretty soon. He’s quite happy. Toto’s sitting with him.”

Sally’s concern, which had been a steady flame, shot up. Everyone who had ever lived under Mrs. Meecher’s roof held views on Toto, and none of them could be printed without blushing. Toto was a small woolly dog with a persistent, penetrating yap—one of those yaps that do not merely enter your ear but take up residence in your skull and begin rearranging the furniture. In health Toto was trying; in a sick-room he was an outrage.

“I must go up and see him,” cried Sally. “Poor old dear.”

“Sure. You know his room. You can hear Toto talking to him now,” said Mrs. Meecher complacently. “He wants a cracker, that’s what he wants. Toto likes a cracker after breakfast.”

Upstairs, the soundscape proved her right. Toto’s voice rose and fell in sharp, busy little stabs, as if the creature were pecking the air. Sally, who had lived abroad long enough to have rediscovered the charm of quiet, felt every yap as a personal insult to the human nervous system.

Mr. Faucitt’s eyes turned wearily toward the door as she entered. He looked grey and used-up, as if somebody had boiled him too long. But when he saw Sally, his face lit with incredulous rapture—like a man who has been promised broth and receives, instead, a banquet.

“Sally!”

“One moment. Here, Toto!”

She had come armed with a cracker. Toto, struck dumb by the sight of food, hopped off the bed and stood with his head on one side, peering at it with suspicion. He was a dog who believed the universe was always plotting against him. Still, hunger is a persuasive diplomat. Sally lured him into the passage, tossed the cracker down, slipped back into the room, and shut the door with the satisfaction of someone who has successfully corked a bottle.

Outside came two yaps—gratitude or baffled fury; with Toto, the difference was academic. Then the little beast trotted off downstairs.

Mr. Faucitt drew a deep breath, and the room, for the first time, sounded like a sick-room ought: hushed, with the faint domestic noises of the house at a distance, muffled as if wrapped in cloth.

“Sally,” he said, “you come, as ever, as an angel of mercy. Our worthy Mrs. Meecher means well, and I yield to no man in my respect for her innate kindness of heart: but she errs in supposing that that thrice-damned whelp of hers is a combination of sick-nurse, soothing medicine, and a week at the seaside. She insisted on bringing him here. He was yapping then, as he was yapping when, with womanly resource which I cannot sufficiently praise, you decoyed him hence. And each yap went through me like hammer-strokes on sheeted tin. Sally, you stand alone among womankind. You shine like a good deed in a naughty world. When did you get back?”

“I’ve only just arrived in my hired barouche from the pier.”

“And you came to see your old friend without delay? I am grateful and flattered. Sally, my dear.”

“Of course I came to see you. Do you suppose that when Mrs. Meecher told me you were sick I just said ‘Is that so?’ and went on talking about the weather? Well, what do you mean by it? Frightening everybody. Poor old darling—do you feel very bad?”

“One thousand individual mice are nibbling the base of my spine,” said Mr. Faucitt, “and I am conscious of a constant need of cooling refreshment. But what of that? Your presence is a tonic. Tell me—how did our Sally enjoy foreign travel?”

“Our Sally had the time of her life.”

“Did you visit England?”

“Only passing through.”

“How did it look?” asked Mr. Faucitt eagerly.

“Moist. Very moist.”

“It would,” said Mr. Faucitt indulgently. “I confess that, happy as I have been in this country, there are times when I miss those wonderful London days, when a sort of cosy brown mist hangs over the streets and the pavements ooze with a perspiration of mud and water, and you see through the haze the yellow glow of the Bodega lamps shining in the distance like harbour-lights. Not,” he added conscientiously, “that I specify the Bodega to the exclusion of other and equally worthy hostelries. I have passed just as pleasant hours in Rule’s and Short’s. You missed something by not lingering in England, Sally.”

“I know I did—pneumonia.”

Mr. Faucitt shook his head reproachfully, which at his present temperature looked like a courageous feat.

“You are prejudiced, my dear. You would have enjoyed London if you had had the courage to brave its superficial gloom. Where did you spend your holiday? Paris?”

“Part of the time. And the rest of the while I was down by the sea. It was glorious. I don’t think I would ever have come back if I hadn’t had to.” She leaned forward, smoothing his pillow with the brisk tenderness she would have used on dough. “But, of course, I wanted to see you all again. And I wanted to be at the opening of Mr. Foster’s play. Mrs. Meecher tells me you went to one of the rehearsals.”

“I attended a dog-fight which I was informed was a rehearsal,” said Mr. Faucitt severely. “There is no rehearsing nowadays.”

“Oh dear! Was it as bad as all that?”

“The play is good. The play—I will go further—is excellent. It has fat.” He spoke of the play as a cook speaks of a stew that has been properly enriched, and Sally, for all her impatience, understood the praise. “But the acting—”

“Mrs. Meecher said you told her that Elsa was good.”

“Our worthy hostess did not misreport me. Miss Doland has great possibilities. She reminds me somewhat of Matilda Devine, under whose banner I played a season at the Old Royalty in London many years ago. She has the seeds of greatness in her, but she is wasted in the present case on an insignificant part. There is only one part in the play. I allude to the one murdered by Miss Mabel Hobson.”

“Murdered!” Sally’s heart dropped as if someone had whisked the chair from under it. She had been afraid of this, and there was no comfort in finding her fears properly cooked and served. “Is she very terrible?”

“She has the face of an angel and the histrionic ability of that curious suet pudding which our estimable Mrs. Meecher is apt to give us on Fridays.”

Sally could almost taste that pudding—heavy, well-meaning, and damply defiant of the human digestion.

“In my professional career,” went on Mr. Faucitt, warming to his subject as much as his fever would allow, “I have seen many cases of what I may term the Lady Friend in the role of star, but Miss Hobson eclipses them all. I remember in the year ’94 a certain scion of the plutocracy took it into his head to present a female for whom he had conceived an admiration in a part which would have taxed the resources of the ablest. I was engaged in her support, and at the first rehearsal I recollect saying to my dear old friend, Arthur Moseby—dead, alas, these many years. An excellent juvenile, but, like so many good fellows, cursed with a tendency to lift the elbow—I recollect saying to him ‘Arthur, dear boy, I give it two weeks.’ ‘Max,’ was his reply, ‘you are an incurable optimist. One consecutive night, laddie, one consecutive night.’ We had, I recall, an even half-crown upon it. He won. We opened at Wigan, our leading lady got the bird, and the show closed next day. I was forcibly reminded of this incident as I watched Miss Hobson rehearsing.”

“Oh, poor Ger—poor Mr. Foster!” Sally caught herself just in time, but the slip left a small sting behind, like biting your lip.

“I do not share your commiseration for that young man,” said Mr. Faucitt, with a sudden austerity that made Sally blink. “You probably are almost a stranger to him, but he and I have been thrown together a good deal of late. A young man upon whom, mark my words, success, if it ever comes, will have the worst effects. I dislike him, Sally. He is, I think, without exception, the most selfish and self-centred young man of my acquaintance. He reminds me very much of old Billy Fothergill, with whom I toured a good deal in the later eighties. Did I ever tell you the story of Billy and the amateur who—?”

Sally was not in the mood for Billy Fothergill, amateurs, or any other dead-and-gone cautionary exhibits. Mr. Faucitt’s innocent condemnation had gone in like a pin. For a second she wanted to flare up, to defend Gerald hotly, to insist that Mr. Faucitt didn’t understand—couldn’t understand—how a man might be both maddening and dear. Then she looked at the old actor’s worn face and checked herself. He meant no harm. He was ill, and the room was quiet at last, and the city outside—so crisp, so clangorous—suddenly felt very far away.

She changed the subject with the quickness of a cook switching pans to save a sauce.

“Have you seen anything of Fillmore while I’ve been away?”

“Fillmore? Why yes, my dear, curiously enough I happened to run into him on Broadway only a few days ago. He seemed changed—less stiff and aloof than he had been for some time past. I may be wronging him, but there have been times of late when one might almost have fancied him a trifle up-stage. All that was gone at our last encounter. He appeared glad to see me and was most cordial.”

Sally felt her spirits lift. So her lecture on the night of the party had not been wasted. Something in Fillmore had softened; perhaps he had, for once, heard a voice besides his own.

Mr. Faucitt, however, had his own explanation, delivered with the quiet certainty of a man announcing the contents of a sealed envelope.

“I rather fancy,” he said, “that the softening influence has been the young man’s fiancée.”

“What? Fillmore’s not engaged?”

“Did he not write and tell you? I suppose he was waiting to inform you when you returned. Yes, Fillmore is betrothed. The lady was with him when we met. A Miss Winch. In the profession, I understand. He introduced me. A very charming and sensible young lady, I thought.”

Sally shook her head slowly, as if the words had been in some dialect she did not recognize—perfectly clear in sound and yet refusing to become meaning. Fillmore and “charming and sensible” did not, in her private dictionary of the family, translate into one another.

“She can’t be,” said Sally. “Fillmore would never have got engaged to anyone like that. Was her hair crimson?”

“Brown, if I recollect rightly.”

“Very loud, I suppose, and overdressed?”

“On the contrary—neat and quiet.”

“You’ve made a mistake,” said Sally decidedly. “She can’t have been like that. I shall have to look into this. It does seem hard that I can’t go away for a few weeks without all my friends taking to beds of sickness and all my brothers getting ensnared by vampires.”

A knock came at the door before Mr. Faucitt could reply. Mrs. Meecher entered, ushering in a pleasant little man with spectacles and a black bag. His step had the soft efficiency of someone who has learned to move quietly in rooms where sound matters.

“The doctor to see you, Mr. Faucitt.”

Mrs. Meecher looked Mr. Faucitt over as if hoping, against hope, to detect a dramatic turn toward blackness.

“I been telling him what I think you’ve gotten is this here new Spanish influenza. Two more deaths there was in the paper this morning, if you can believe what you see—”

“I wonder,” said the doctor, interrupting with amiable firmness, “if you would mind going and bringing me a small glass of water?”

“Why, sure.”

“Not a large glass—a small glass. Just let the tap run for a few moments and take care not to spill any as you come up the stairs.”

Mrs. Meecher departed, mollified by having a mission.

“I always ask ladies,” said the doctor, lowering his voice as the door closed, “to bring me a glass of water. It keeps them amused and interested and gets them out of the way, and they think I am going to do a conjuring trick with it. As a matter of fact, I’m going to drink it. Now let’s have a look at you.”

He examined Mr. Faucitt briskly. The whole business did not take long; it had the speed of a man tasting soup and knowing at once what herb has been overdone. When he finished, he looked mildly annoyed—as if fate had given him the obvious answer and he’d been hoping for something cleverer.

“Our good friend’s diagnosis was correct. I’d give a leg to say it wasn’t, but it was. It is this here new Spanish influenza. Not a bad attack. You want to stay in bed and keep warm, and I’ll write you out a prescription. You ought to be nursed. Is this young lady a nurse?”

“No, no, merely—” began Mr. Faucitt, distressed.

“Of course I’m a nurse,” said Sally promptly. “It isn’t difficult, is it, doctor? I know nurses smooth pillows. I can do that. Is there anything else?”

“Their principal duty,” said the doctor, “is to sit here and prevent the excellent and garrulous lady who has just left us from getting in. They must also be able to aim straight with a book or an old shoe if that small woolly dog I met downstairs tries to force an entrance. If you are equal to these tasks, I can leave the case in your hands with every confidence.”

“But, Sally, my dear,” said Mr. Faucitt, troubled, “you must not waste your time looking after me. You have a thousand things to occupy you.”

“There’s nothing I want to do more than help you to get better,” said Sally. “I’ll just go out and send a wire, and then I’ll be right back.”

Five minutes later she stood in a Western Union office, the place full of clicks and murmurs—human voices flattened into business, and the metallic chatter of communication, efficient and heartless as a factory belt. Sally wrote her message with a hand that was steady, though her feelings were not.

She telegraphed Gerald that she would be unable to reach Detroit in time for the opening.

It was not until the following Friday that Sally could get away to Detroit; and when she did, she came in on the Saturday morning with that special, railway-stale weariness that tastes faintly of coal dust and over-boiled tea. At the Hotel Statler she confirmed that Gerald was stopping there, rang him up, and, having told him to join her, went straight into the dining-room and ordered breakfast—because whatever modern life had done to love, it had not yet found a way to abolish hunger.

She was low in spirits while she waited, sitting with her gloves beside her plate like two exhausted, obedient animals. Nursing Mr. Faucitt had taken it out of her. Trains, too, had a knack of shaking a girl’s bones into a new, less cheerful arrangement. But what sat heaviest was the sound of Gerald over the telephone: flat, inattentive, as if her return were an item of hotel news and not the event it was meant to be.

Then the coffee came—black, honest, hot, with that comforting, bitter fragrance that seems to scrape the sleep from your brain. The first mouthful put a small flame back in her. Men, after all, were famously unromantic before breakfast. Gerald would presently arrive refreshed by a shower, and things would right themselves as neatly as a napkin smoothed over a lap. In the meantime, there was butter. There was toast. There was life.

She had just poured her second cup when a stout young man appeared in the doorway. He stood there peering about with the baffled air of someone searching for a lost continent. She had glimpsed him a moment earlier in the lobby and thought, idly, how extraordinarily like Fillmore he was.

Now she saw that it was Fillmore.

For an instant her mind did that little domestic miracle it always did when confronted with the unexpected: it began, without consulting her, to lay out explanations like ingredients on a board. Fillmore belonged in New York; he was supposed to be rooted there like a prosperous tree. Yet prosperous trees, she reflected, did have a habit of travelling in trunks. At any rate, here he was, and she called him.

He stood another full minute looking everywhere except at her, as if her table were under a spell; then recognition struck him and he came over.

“Why—Sally?” he said.

He was nervous. Not merely surprised—embarrassed, even. Sally, who had a sister’s gift for drawing conclusions at a gallop, diagnosed a guilty conscience at once. No doubt he was trying to decide how to break to her that he had got himself engaged without first inviting her to provide a sisterly veto.

“What are you doing here?” he asked. “I thought you were in Europe.”

“I got back a week ago. But I’ve been nursing poor old Mr. Faucitt. He’s been ill, poor old dear. I’ve come to see Mr. Foster’s play—The Primrose Way. Is it a success?”

“It hasn’t opened yet.”

“Don’t be silly, Fill. It opened last Monday.”

“No, it didn’t.” His voice had the strained patience of a man correcting a child who insists that Tuesday is called Wednesday. “Haven’t you heard? They’ve closed all the theatres because of this infernal Spanish influenza. Nothing’s been playing this week. You must have seen it in the papers.”

“I haven’t had time to read the papers. Oh, Fill—what an awful shame!”

“Yes. It’s pretty tough. Makes the company all on edge. I’ve had the darndest time, I can tell you.”

“Why, what have you got to do with it?”

Fillmore coughed—a self-conscious, swallowed sort of cough, as if the truth were something dry.

“I—er—oh, I didn’t tell you that. I’m sort of—er—mixed up in the show. Cracknell—you remember he was at college with me—suggested I should come down and look at it. Shouldn’t wonder if he wants me to put money into it and so on.”

“I thought he had all the money in the world.”

“He has a lot,” said Fillmore, with the dignity of a man refusing to be impressed by mere millions, “but these fellows like to let a pal in on a good thing.”

“Is it a good thing?”

“The play’s fine.”

“That’s what Mr. Faucitt said. But Mabel Hobson—”

Fillmore’s ample face registered emotion as clearly as custard takes the shape of its mould.

“She’s an awful woman, Sally! She can’t act, and she throws her weight about all the time. The other day there was a fuss about a paper-knife—”

“How do you mean, a fuss about a paper-knife?”

“One of the props, you know. It got mislaid. I’m certain it wasn’t my fault—”

“How could it have been your fault?” Sally asked, genuinely at sea. Love, she had noticed, often took a perfectly solid male intelligence and whisked it into a kind of airy, ill-furnished attic.

“Well—er—you know how it is. Angry woman… blames the first person she sees… This paper-knife…”

His voice trailed away as if the paper-knife were too sharp a topic to hold.

“Mr. Faucitt said Elsa Doland was good.”

“Oh, she’s all right,” said Fillmore indifferently. Then, abruptly, life poured into him. His eyes lit. His voice acquired the evangelical glow of someone describing the first good loaf after a long famine.

“But the girl you want to watch is Miss Winch. Gladys Winch. She plays the maid. She’s only in the first act, and hasn’t much to say, except ‘Did you ring, madam?’ and things like that. But it’s the way she says ’em! Sally, that girl’s a genius! The greatest character actress in a dozen years! You mark my words, in a darned little while you’ll see her name up on Broadway in electric light. Personality? Ask me! Charm? She wrote the words and music! Looks?…”

“All right! All right! I know all about it, Fill. And will you kindly inform me how you dared to get engaged without consulting me?”

Fillmore blushed richly, the colour rising through his cheeks like jam through a sponge-cake.

“Oh—do you know?”

“Yes. Mr. Faucitt told me.”

“Well…”

“Well?”

“Well, I’m only human,” argued Fillmore, as if this were a legal point and he had excellent authority.

“I call that a very handsome admission. You’ve got quite modest, Fill.”

He had changed. The Fillmore she remembered had once been padded with pomposity as thoroughly as an overstuffed chair. Now it was as if some unseen hand had punctured him and let the hot air out. If this was Miss Winch’s doing, Sally felt she could not, in fairness, disapprove of the romance.

“I’ll introduce you sometime,” he said.

“I want to meet her very much.”

“I’ll have to be going now. I’ve got to see Bunbury. I thought he might be in here.”

“Who’s Bunbury?”

“The producer. I suppose he’s breakfasting in his room. I’d better go up.”

“You are busy, aren’t you. Little marvel. It’s lucky they’ve got you to look after them.”

Fillmore departed with the hurried gait of a man who has lent his dignity to a cause and is anxious it shall not be misused.

Sally settled back to wait for Gerald, no longer wounded by the listlessness of his telephone voice. Poor Gerald—if the play was threatened, no wonder he sounded as if someone had replaced his bloodstream with weak dishwater.

A few minutes later he came in.

“Oh, Jerry darling,” said Sally as he reached her table, “I’m so sorry. I’ve just been hearing about it.”

Gerald sat down, and his face matched his voice: a nervous dullness lay over him like dust over furniture in a shut-up house.

“It’s just my luck,” he said. “It’s the kind of thing that couldn’t happen to anyone but me. Damned fools! Where’s the sense in shutting the theatres, even if there is influenza about? They let people jam against one another all day in the stores. If that doesn’t hurt them, why should it hurt them to go to theatres? Besides, it’s all infernal nonsense about this thing. I don’t believe there is such a thing as Spanish influenza. People get colds in their heads and think they’re dying. It’s all a fake scare.”

“I don’t think it’s that,” said Sally. “Poor Mr. Faucitt had it quite badly. That’s why I couldn’t come earlier.”

Gerald did not appear to be interested either in Mr. Faucitt’s illness or in the fact of Sally, after all the delay, being in Detroit and sitting opposite him. He dug a spoon into his grapefruit as if it had insulted him.

“We’ve been hanging about here day after day, getting bored to death… The company’s going all to pieces. They’re sick of rehearsing and rehearsing when nobody knows if we’ll ever open. They were all keyed up a week ago, and they’ve been sagging ever since. It’ll ruin the play, of course. My first chance! Just chucked away.”

Sally listened, and a slow desolation spread through her, like cold tea seeping into bread. She tried to be fair. He had been disappointed; he was under strain. But it was unfortunate—deeply unfortunate—that self-pity was one of the few things she could not digest in a man. And her vanity winced too. Her arrival, which she had foolishly imagined might be a tonic, had apparently made no more impression than an extra spoonful of sugar in a pot already spoiled.

She could not help recalling what Mr. Faucitt had said about Gerald. She had never noticed before that he was remarkably self-centred, but now he was pushing the fact toward her with both hands.

“That Hobson woman is beginning to make trouble,” he went on, attacking scrambled eggs with a kind of hopeless resentment. “She ought never to have had the part. Never. She can’t handle it. Elsa Doland could play it a thousand times better. I wrote Elsa in a few lines the other day, and the Hobson woman went right up in the air. You don’t know what a star is till you’ve seen one of these promoted clothes-props from the Follies trying to be one. It took me an hour to talk her round and keep her from throwing up her part.”

“Why not let her throw up her part?” asked Sally, and her voice was very calm.

“For heaven’s sake talk sense,” said Gerald, querulously. “Do you suppose that man Cracknell would keep the play on if she wasn’t in it? He’d close the show in a second, and where would I be then? You don’t seem to realize this is a big chance for me. I’d look a fool throwing it away.”

“I see,” said Sally shortly.

She had never felt so wretched. Foreign travel, she decided, was a mistake. It broadened the mind, no doubt, but it left the heart trying to read old handwriting in a language that had shifted while you were away. She examined what she was feeling and found, at the centre of it, a particular irritation: Gerald was trying to claim two attitudes at once. A man in trouble may either be brave and proud, refusing consolation, or he may be broken and accept comfort. Gerald, to Sally’s mind, was asking to be pitied while simultaneously placing himself behind glass.

“By the way,” he said, “there’s one thing. I have to keep her jollying along all the time, so for goodness’ sake don’t go letting it out that we’re engaged.”

Sally’s chin jerked up. That was too much.

“If you find it a handicap being engaged to me—”

“Don’t be silly.” Gerald took refuge in grievance. “Good God! It’s tough! Here I am, worried to death, and you—”

Before he could finish, Sally’s mood performed one of its sudden somersaults. A plain, comforting thought arrived: she was tired and gritty; she hadn’t had a bath; her hair felt as if it belonged to someone who had lost a fight with a windmill. Perhaps it wasn’t Gerald who looked different; perhaps it was the world that looked ugly because she felt ugly. With that, tranquillity returned, and she punished herself for being unreasonable by becoming tender.

“I’m so sorry,” she said, putting her hand quickly on his. “I’ve been a brute. But I do sympathize—really.”

“I’ve had an awful time,” mumbled Gerald.

“I know, I know. But you never told me you were glad to see me.”

“Of course I’m glad to see you.”

“Why didn’t you say so, then, you poor fish? And why didn’t you ask me if I’d enjoyed myself in Europe?”

“Did you enjoy yourself?”

“Yes—except that I missed you so much. There! Now we can consider my lecture on foreign travel finished, and you can go on telling me your troubles.”

Gerald did. He spoke at length and with little variation, and it became clear that he had decided Providence had invented Spanish influenza solely to wreck his prospects. But now, at least, he seemed less withdrawn, more willing to accept sympathy as if it were something warm placed in his hands instead of thrown at him from a distance. The small storm between them had cleared the air; Sally’s sense of being shut out eased.

“Well,” said Gerald at last, consulting his watch, “I suppose I’d better be off.”

“Rehearsal?”

“Yes, confound it. It’s the only way of getting through the day. Are you coming along?”

“I’ll come directly I’ve unpacked and tidied myself up.”

“See you at the theatre, then.”

Sally went out and rang for the lift.

2

The rehearsal had started when she reached the theatre. As she entered the dark auditorium, voices floated to her with that thin, reedy quality people acquire in an empty building, as if the house itself were listening too hard and swallowing the sound. She sat at the back and waited for her eyes to adjust. Gradually shapes gathered: Gerald in the front row; beside him a man with a bald head fringed with orange hair—Mr. Bunbury, unmistakably, for no one else would carry temperament like a visible accessory. Here and there members of the company were scattered in ones and twos, resting like birds between migrations.

On stage Elsa Doland, very attractive, played a scene with a man in a bowler hat. As Sally came in, Elsa spoke.

“Why, what do you mean, father?”

“Tiddly-omty-om,” replied the bowler-hatted one with startling economy. “Tiddly-omty-om… long speech ending in ‘find me in the library.’ And exit,” he concluded, starting to do so.

For the first time Sally became aware of the atmosphere—tight, quivering, ready to snap. Mr. Bunbury seized his walking-stick from where it leaned against the seat and hurled it across the house with feeling.

“For God’s sake!” cried Mr. Bunbury.

The bowler hat paused halfway across the stage, interested.

“Now what?”

“Do speak the lines, Teddy,” exclaimed Gerald. “Don’t skip them in that sloppy fashion.”

“You don’t want me to go over the whole thing?” asked Teddy, amazed.

“Yes!”

“Not the whole damn thing?” demanded Teddy, as if being asked to recite the Bible from memory.

“This is a rehearsal,” snapped Mr. Bunbury. “If we are not going to do it properly, what’s the use of doing it at all?”

This struck Teddy as at least one possible philosophy. He delivered the speech with injury in every syllable and shuffled off.

Sally could feel the nerves everywhere—like a stretched violin string in a room where someone keeps slamming doors. The theatre, she reflected, was a large nursery, and its inhabitants were children: bright, touchy, and easily led into tantrums by delays and disappointments. And here they had been, marooned in a strange city, shut in hotels that smelt of polish and yesterday’s cigars, forced to go over and over words already polished to the last syllable, while the outside world made a great racket about disease. No wonder they sounded raw, as if they had been living too long on reheated fare.

On stage Elsa moved to a door, pressed a bell, and sat by the footlights with a magazine. A moment later a young woman entered.

Instantly Mr. Bunbury bellowed:

“Miss Winch!”

The new arrival stopped and looked out over the footlights—not pained like Teddy, but with genial indulgence, as if she had come to a children’s party to keep them entertained. She was square, wholesome, good-humoured, with a serious face that contradicted itself by allowing a faint smile to lurk at one corner of the mouth. Not pretty, thought Sally; and yet she could see why Fillmore—miracle of miracles—had looked past that and found the charm.

“Hello?” said Miss Winch amiably.

Mr. Bunbury seemed to swell with emotion.

“Miss Winch, did I or did I not ask you to refrain from chewing gum during rehearsal?”

“That’s right, so you did,” admitted Miss Winch, as chummily as if he had reminded her she’d borrowed his hatpin.

“Then why are you doing it?”

Miss Winch turned the gum over thoughtfully before replying, as a cook might test a sauce on the back of a spoon.

“Bit o’ business,” she said at length.

“What do you mean, a bit of business?”

“Character stuff,” explained Miss Winch in her pleasant, drawling voice. “Thought it out myself. Maids chew gum, you know.”

Mr. Bunbury ruffled his orange hair with a desperate palm.

“Have you ever seen a maid?” he asked.

“Yes, sir. And they chew gum.”

“I mean a parlour-maid in a smart house,” moaned Mr. Bunbury. “Do you imagine for a moment that in a house such as this is supposed to be the parlour-maid would be allowed to come into the drawing-room champing that disgusting, beastly stuff?”

Miss Winch considered, honestly.

“Maybe you’re right.” Then her face brightened. “Listen! Great idea! Mr. Foster can write in a line for Elsa, calling me down, and another giving me a good come-back, and then another for Elsa saying something else, and then something really funny for me, and so on. We can work it up into a big comic scene. Five or six minutes, all laughs.”

This suggestion struck Mr. Bunbury dumb. While he struggled for words, there darted from the wings a gorgeous being in blue velvet with a hat so smart Sally felt a spasm of pure envy, sharp as the smell of a new leather purse.

“Say!”

Miss Mabel Hobson had practically every advantage Nature could provide except a musical voice. Her figure was perfect, her face beautiful, her hair a mass of spun gold; but her voice, in moments of emotion, was the voice of a peacock insisting upon its rights.

“Say, listen to me for just one moment!”

Mr. Bunbury found his speech again.

“Miss Hobson! Please!”

“Yes, that’s all very well—”

“You are interrupting the rehearsal.”

“You bet your sorrowful existence I’m interrupting the rehearsal,” agreed Miss Hobson with emphasis. “And if you want to make a little easy money, you go and bet somebody ten seeds that I’m going to interrupt it again every time there’s any talk of writing up any darned part in the show except mine. Write up other people’s parts? Not while I have my strength!”

A young man with butter-coloured hair had entered behind her and attempted, in a small voice, to calm the storm.

“Now, sweetie!”

“Oh, can it, Reggie!” snapped Miss Hobson.

Mr. Cracknell canned it at once. He was not one of your brutal cave-men. He subsided into the recesses of a high collar and began to chew the knob of his stick, looking like an amiable creature who had been trained to remain still when a lioness was feeding.

“I’m the star,” resumed Miss Hobson, “and if you think anybody else’s part’s going to be written up—well, pardon me while I choke with laughter! If so much as a syllable is written into anybody’s part, I walk straight out on my two feet. You won’t see me go, I’ll be so quick.”

Mr. Bunbury sprang to his feet and waved his hands.

“For heaven’s sake! Are we rehearsing, or is this a debating society? Miss Hobson, nothing is going to be written into anybody’s part. Now are you satisfied?”

“She said—”

“Oh, never mind,” said Miss Winch equably. “It was only a random thought. Working for the good of the show all the time. That’s me.”

“Now, sweetie!” pleaded Mr. Cracknell, emerging cautiously from the collar like a tortoise.

Miss Hobson allowed herself to be soothed.

“Oh, well, that’s all right then. But don’t forget I know how to look after myself,” she said, stating a fact which needed no footnotes. “Any raw work, and out I walk so quick it’ll make you giddy.”

She retired, followed by Mr. Cracknell, and the wings swallowed them.

“Shall I say my big speech now?” inquired Miss Winch over the footlights.

“Yes, yes! Get on with the rehearsal. We’ve wasted half the morning.”

“Did you ring, madam?” said Miss Winch to Elsa, who had sat placidly reading through the uproar.

The rehearsal moved on. Sally watched with a sinking heart. It was all wrong. Even a novice could see it. Miss Hobson was superbly beautiful; she would have lent splendour to any part requiring the minimum of words and the maximum of clothes. But here she was in the pivot of a serious play, and the beauty only made the incapacity more glaring, like gilt on bad wood.

Sally remembered Mr. Faucitt’s story about the lady who got the bird at Wigan. She could not see how history would fail to repeat itself. The American theatrical public would endure much from youth and beauty; but it did have, at last, a limit.

A shrill, passionate cry from the front row, and Mr. Bunbury was on his feet again. Sally found herself wondering whether things were going especially wrong today, or whether this was simply one of Mr. Bunbury’s ordinary mornings.

“Miss Hobson!”

The drama had brought that emotional lady on at left centre and carried her across to a desk on the other side of the stage. The desk mattered; it was practically a character. It symbolized the husband’s absorption in business—his preference for papers over passion—which was rapidly breaking Miss Hobson’s heart. He loved his desk better than his young wife; and no wife, however beautifully dressed, could be expected to stand for that.

“Oh, gee!” said Miss Hobson, dropping the broken-hearted wife and becoming the offended star. “What’s it this time?”

“I suggested at the last rehearsal and at the rehearsal before and the rehearsal before that,” said Mr. Bunbury, “that on that line you should pick up the paper-knife and toy negligently with it. You did it yesterday, and today you’ve forgotten it again.”

“My God!” cried Miss Hobson, wounded. “If this don’t beat everything! How the heck can I toy negligently with a paper-knife when there’s no paper-knife for me to toy negligently with?”

“The paper-knife is on the desk.”

“It’s not on the desk.”

“No paper-knife?” Mr. Bunbury sounded as if the universe had mislaid a planet.

“No paper-knife. And it’s no good picking on me. I’m the star, not the assistant stage manager. If you’re going to pick on anybody, pick on him.”

The advice struck Mr. Bunbury as sound. He threw back his head and bayed like a bloodhound.

There was a pause. Then, from the wings on the prompt side, there shambled out a stout, shrinking figure with a script in his hand and apprehension written plainly on his face in the hard light of the footlights.

It was Fillmore—the Man of Destiny.

3

Alas, poor Fillmore.

He stood in the middle of the stage with Mr. Bunbury’s wrath crackling round him, and Sally, after her first astonishment, sent him a wave of sisterly commiseration. She did not often pity Fillmore. In sunshine his nature tended to become a trifle lush; and the minor ills that had afflicted him during the last three years she had always regarded as wholesome, like a diet that kept him from growing too fond of himself. Unmoved, she had watched him lunching on coffee and buckwheat cakes, curbing from motives of economy a somewhat florid taste in dress.

But this was different. This was tragedy.

Somehow, disaster must have struck Fillmore’s bank-roll, and he was back where he had started. His presence here could mean nothing else. She remembered what he had said at breakfast about being asked to put money into the play—how like Fillmore to try to save his face for the moment with an outrageous bluff, though knowing he would have to tell the truth sooner or later. She understood now how he must have felt when he had seen her at the hotel.

Yes. She was sorry for Fillmore.

Actors, in their private lives, are much like small children who have discovered that Providence keeps its sweets in a top cupboard: one moment they are limp with despair on the nursery rug, and the next they are singing hymns to fortune because somebody has produced a sticky bun. It astonished Sally to find that she had made an entire company beam by the simple domestic gesture of handing her brother Fillmore twenty thousand dollars—as if she had merely set a tureen on the table and the room, starving a minute before, had remembered how to be human.

If the Millennium had arrived and brought along a brass band to play it in, the members of the Primrose Way Company could not have been in better temper with themselves. The week of inaction slid off their shoulders like an old overcoat, and suddenly everybody had opinions again, and laughter, and energy, and the agreeable sort of bustle you hear in a kitchen just before the first dinner service—the clatter of plates, the scrape of chairs, the quick bright sound of hope.

Their delight was not only because the money meant rehearsals could continue and meals could be paid for without the manager’s face turning the color of bad milk. It was also the sheer theatrical relish of the thing: that creature from the abyss, the assistant stage manager, had been hoisted—by a fairy godmother with a checkbook—to the dizzy height of proprietor of the show. Most of them had played in pieces where the persecuted heroine, around eleven o’clock, inherits a fortune, becomes a duchess, and tells the villain to go and simmer. This struck them as artistically correct.

And, like a kitchen after the departure of the cook who insists on boiling everything for exactly the length of a sermon, the place breathed again now that Miss Hobson had gone. Only when the air cleared did everybody recognize what a blight she had been: the sort of woman who could sour fresh cream merely by standing near it and criticizing the cow’s accent.

Optimism ruled. Rumors sprouted like yeast. The bowler-hatted Teddy had it straight from the lift-boy at his hotel that the ban on the theatres would be lifted on Tuesday at the latest; and no less an authority than the cigar-stand girl at the Pontchatrain had informed the man who played the butler that Toledo and Cleveland were opening to-morrow. The company, which had been living on thin broth and thinner news, suddenly fancied it could smell roasts in the oven.

Fillmore, in particular, became Fillmore again.

We all have our chosen manner of self-expression in moments of elation. Some men write poetry. Some sing. Some fall into religion. Fillmore’s method was to buy a new waistcoat, a hundred half-dollar cigars, and develop, overnight, a conscience about luncheon as meticulous as a food historian cataloguing vanished regional soups.

He began to speak of meals the way elderly farmers speak of rain: not merely as something that happens, but as a matter on which the health of the world depends. No longer did he bolt what was put before him like a man shoveling coal. He discussed the bread—was it properly crusted, did it crackle when torn, had it been buttered while warm so that the fat sank in like good advice? He sniffed his coffee, frowned at its thinness, demanded a darker roast and the sort of bitterness that wakes the soul. He had views on pickles. He investigated salads as if lettuce had a family tree.

It may have been an optical illusion, but Sally could have sworn he put on at least six pounds in the first day of the new regime. As a serf looking after paper-knives and other properties, he had been—for him—almost slim, like a man living on rehearsal sandwiches. As a manager, he blossomed into soft billowy curves, the visible result of having opinions about gravy.

And there he would stand on the sidewalk in front of the theatre, expanding happily into the public air, gloating over the new posters which proclaimed in bold letters: FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS—so that the populace, confronted by both the announcement and Fillmore’s managerial circumference, had to make a detour to get round him.

In this era of bubbling joy, it seemed unjust that Sally, the fairy godmother responsible for it all, should not be completely happy too. Yet some faint shadow followed her about like the aftertaste of something scorched—too elusive to name, too persistent to ignore. She tried, at odd moments, to catch it by the collar and demand explanations, but it kept retreating into the back of her mind like a mouse that knows the pantry is not yet shut.

It did not come out into the light until one afternoon when she was out driving with Gerald in a hired car on Belle Isle.

Since Miss Hobson’s departure, Gerald had been at his best. Like Fillmore, he was a man who responded to the sunshine of prosperity. His moodiness had drained away; his charm returned, easy and warm, like a kitchen that suddenly has enough wood for the stove. He talked, and laughed, and scattered plans with both hands.

The car slid through pleasant woods and fields by the river. The world had the tidy, domestic look of a place that expects supper at a reasonable hour. From the water came the layered sound of life—the soft slap of small waves, the distant cough of an engine, birds stitching bright notes into the air. Even the wheels on the road made a soothing whisper, as if the day were rubbing its hands together.

And yet—something jarred.

Gerald was cheerful and talkative. He found nothing wrong with life at all. He held forth on the big things he meant to do, and his voice had that confident ring of a man tasting success in advance, as if he were already carving it.

“If this play gets over—and it’s going to—I’ll show ’em!” he said. His jaw squared; his eyes glowed as they stared into the inviting future. “One success—that’s all I need—then watch me! I haven’t had a chance yet, but—”

His words rolled on, full of banners and trumpets. Sally listened for a little, then ceased to listen, not from boredom, but from a sudden inward attention, as if a quieter sound had begun behind her ribs.

It was the time of year when the mellow warmth of afternoon is followed swiftly by the chill of evening, like a kitchen that feels cosy until someone opens the back door. The sun slipped behind the trees. A cold wind rose from the river and pushed itself under shawls and into thoughts. The acoustic world changed: the birds quieted, the engine’s hum seemed harsher, and the wind made a thin, dissatisfied whine along the branches, a sound that always suggested to Sally—without her knowing why—that something was being taken away.

And quite suddenly, as though the wind had scoured her mind clean, she understood what had been lurking there all along.

For an instant it stood nakedly without concealment, and the world turned forlorn.

She had realized the fundamental difference between man’s outlook on life and woman’s.

Success.

How men worshipped it—how they built altars to it out of waistcoats and cigars, out of posters and applause, out of the blessed, blinding noise of being noticed. And how little of themselves they had left over for anything else when once they began to kneel. Ironically, it was the theme of this very play of Gerald’s which she had saved from destruction. Here he was, speaking of “getting over,” of “showing them,” of the hungry future—his words loud as an industrial street—while all about them the island tried to offer simpler music: river, wind, trees.

Of all the men she knew, how many had any view of life except as a race which they must strain every nerve to win, regardless of what they missed by the wayside in their haste? Fillmore—Gerald—all of them. A woman might exist in each of their lives, but she came second, an afterthought, a thing for spare time—like garnish placed on a dish after the real business is done.

Gerald was everything to her. His success would never be more than a side-issue, as far as she was concerned. If you gave her Gerald without any of the trappings—without posters, praise, that triumphal din—she would still feel she had been fed. He himself was enough.

But she was not enough for him.

A spasm of futile jealousy shook her. It was absurd and honest and bitter as burnt sugar. She shivered, and the shiver was not all from the cold.

“Cold?” said Gerald, turning quickly. “I’ll tell the man to drive back.” And then, unable to keep his mind from the altar, he went on, already leaning forward into that future noise. “I don’t see any reason why this play shouldn’t run a year in New York. Everybody says it’s good… if it does get over, they’ll all be after me. I—”

Sally stared out into a bleak world that had lost its warmth. The sky had turned leaden grey, and the wind from the river blew with a dismal chill, and even the sound of it seemed to say—over and over, in a language no one could translate into comfort—that there are hungers no dinner can satisfy, and some victories that leave the table strangely empty.

When Sally left Detroit the following Saturday, with Fillmore beside her and a travelling-odor of coal smoke and cinders in the air, her spirits had mended themselves so completely that she felt almost ashamed of ever having been miserable.

Naturally men wanted to get on in the world. It was their job—like peeling potatoes, only with more neckties. She told herself that Gerald’s success was her success, and that it was simply bad form to begrudge him the energy he spent hammering at that success as if it were a stubborn veal cutlet refusing to tenderize.

Besides, the last few days had been too full of bright, crackling excitement for gloom to get a foothold. Detroit—city of kind hearts and easily pleased ticket-buyers—had taken to The Primrose Way with the appetite of a family that has been told the stove is broken and then discovers it isn’t. In fulfilment of Teddy’s prophecy the theatre had opened on Tuesday, and the audience, starved of amusement by the recent closing, had eaten the play up with an audible, contented rustle—like a roomful of people leaning in over soup.

The papers, which do not always agree with the applause of first-night audiences (critics being trained, like cats, to sit where you do not want them), endorsed the verdict this time with agreeable unanimity. Gerald Foster was hailed as the coming author; Elsa Doland as the coming star. There was even a brief mention of Fillmore as the coming manager. But there is always some small pebble in the shoe of triumph, and Fillmore’s exultation had been punctured by the fact that the only notice Gladys Winch received was from a critic who spelled her name Wunch in the list of those whom the cast “also included.”

“One of the greatest character actresses on the stage,” said Fillmore bitterly next morning, as if the critic had not merely misspelled a name but had dropped a pie.

He rallied quickly, being built that way, like one of those inflatable punching-men that bounce back no matter how hard you wallop them. The week had been excellent. Elsa had improved nightly. A long, agitated telegram from Mr. Cracknell, begging to be allowed to buy the play back—time having apparently softened Miss Hobson—had been a pleasant incident. And, best of all, Ike Schumann, who owned half the theatres in New York and had been in Detroit tending one of his musical productions like a man basting a roast, had dropped in one evening and stamped The Primrose Way with his approval.

So now, as Fillmore sat opposite Sally on the train, he radiated contentment and importance, as though success had been rubbed into his pores like cold cream.

“Yes, do,” said Sally, after a long silence.

Fillmore awoke from happy dreams. “Eh?”

“I said ‘Yes, do.’ I think you owe it to your position.”

“Do what?”

“Buy a fur coat. Wasn’t that what you were meditating about?”

“Don’t be a chump,” said Fillmore, blushing nevertheless.

It was true that once or twice during the past week he had toyed negligently, as Mr. Bunbury would have phrased it, with the notion. Why not? A man must keep warm. A man must also, in his own way, be seen.

“With an astrakhan collar,” insisted Sally.

“As a matter of fact,” said Fillmore loftily, his big soul ill-attuned to this frivolity, “what I was really thinking about at the moment was something Ike said.”

“Ike?”

“Ike Schumann. He’s on the train. I met him just now.”

“We call him Ike!”

“Of course I call him Ike,” said Fillmore heatedly. “Everyone calls him Ike.”

“He wears a fur coat,” Sally murmured.

Fillmore registered annoyance. “I wish you wouldn’t keep on harping on that damned coat. And, anyway, why shouldn’t I have a fur coat?”

“Fill...! How can you be so brutal as to suggest that I ever said you shouldn’t? Why, I’m one of the strongest supporters of the fur coat. With big cuffs. And you must roll up Fifth Avenue in your car, and I’ll point and say ‘That’s my brother!’ ‘Your brother? No!’ ‘He is, really.’ ‘You’re joking. Why, that’s the great Fillmore Nicholas.’ ‘I know. But he really is my brother. And I was with him when he bought that coat.’”

“Do leave off about the coat!”

“‘And it isn’t only the coat,’ I shall say. ‘It’s what’s underneath. Tucked away inside that mass of fur, dodging about behind that dollar cigar, is one to whom we point with pride—’”

Fillmore looked coldly at his watch. “I’ve got to go and see Ike Schumann.”

“We are in hourly consultation with Ike.”

“He wants to see me about the show. He suggests putting it into Chicago before opening in New York.”

“Oh no,” cried Sally, dismayed.

“Why not?”

Sally recovered herself. In her mind Gerald and his play were so closely stitched together that for a moment the idea of Chicago had meant another long separation, the way the word “diet” means an immediate loss of happiness. But of course Gerald would not need to stay with the company after the first day or two.

“You’re thinking we ought to have a New York reputation before tackling Chicago,” said Fillmore, swelling importantly. “There’s a lot to be said for that. Still, it works both ways. A Chicago run would help us in New York. Well, I’ll have to think it over. I’ll have to think it over.”

He mused with drawn brows.

“All wrong,” said Sally.

“Eh?”

“Not a bit like it. The lips should be compressed and the forefinger of the right hand laid in a careworn way against the right temple. You’ve a lot to learn, Fill.”

“Oh, stop it!”

“Fillmore Nicholas,” said Sally, “if you knew what pain it gives me to josh my only brother, you’d be sorry for me. But you know it’s for your good. Now run along and put Ike out of his misery. I know he’s waiting for you with his watch out. ‘You do think he’ll come, Miss Nicholas?’ were his last words to me as he stepped on the train—and oh, Fill, the yearning in his voice. ‘Why, of course he will, Mr. Schumann,’ I said. ‘For all his exalted position, my brother is kindliness itself. Of course he’ll come.’ ‘If I could only think so!’ he said with a gulp. ‘If I could only think so. But you know what these managers are. A thousand calls on their time. They get brooding on their fur coats and forget everything else.’ ‘Have no fear, Mr. Schumann,’ I said. ‘Fillmore Nicholas is a man of his word.’”

She would have continued—Sally never believed in sparing herself where a loved one’s entertainment was concerned—but Fillmore was already moving down the car, rigid-backed, a silent protest against sisterly levity.

Sally watched him go. The train’s soundscape shifted as he left: the low percussion of wheels, the thin whistle in the corridor, the papery shush of a man turning a newspaper page with the solemnity of a priest lifting a wafer. She picked up a magazine and began to read, working her way through a jungle of advertisements until she reached the end of a story of gripping interest, only to discover it was in two parts, and the part she had was the first.

It is at moments like these that Providence often sends a fresh irritation to replace the old.

A voice spoke.

“How do you do, Miss Nicholas?”

Into the seat before her—recently vacated by the coming manager—Bruce Carmyle of all people insinuated himself, wearing that well-bred air of deferential restraint which seemed to cling to him like a correct collar.

Sally was startled. Everybody travels nowadays, of course; America is full of people who were supposed to be somewhere else. But still she had the sensation that time had been turned back, like a page in a book you thought you had finished.

“Mr. Carmyle!” she cried.

If Bruce Carmyle had thought of Sally constantly since they had last parted on the Paris express, Sally had thought of him very little—so little, indeed, that she had to rummage in her memory for an instant before his identity clicked into place.

“We’re always meeting on trains, aren’t we?” she went on, composure returning. “I never expected to see you in America.”

“I came over.”

Sally was tempted to reply that she had gathered that, but a sudden embarrassment stopped her. She had remembered that at their last meeting she had been abominably rude. Sally was never rude without later remorse—remorse, in her case, being as reliable as the smell of toast when someone forgets it in the toaster.

She said a mild, “Yes.”

“Yes,” said Mr. Carmyle, “it is a good many years since I have taken a real holiday. My doctor seemed to think I was a trifle run down. It seemed a good opportunity to visit America. Everybody,” said Mr. Carmyle oracularly—endeavouring, as he had often done since his ship left England, to persuade himself that his motive was not merely to see Sally again—“everybody ought to visit America at least once. It is part of one’s education.”

“And what are your impressions of our glorious country?” said Sally, rallying.

He seemed relieved to lecture on something impersonal. His own voice steadied him, as if it had its own acoustics—trained to behave in drawing-rooms where even silence had a pedigree.

“I have been visiting Chicago,” he said, after a brief travelogue.

“Oh!”

“A wonderful city.”

“I’ve never seen it. I’ve come from Detroit.”

“Yes,” said Mr. Carmyle. “I heard you were in Detroit.”

Sally’s eyes opened. “You heard I was in Detroit? Good gracious! How?”

“I—ah—called at your New York address and made inquiries,” said Mr. Carmyle, a little awkwardly.

“But how did you know where I lived?”

“My cousin—er—Lancelot told me.”

Sally was silent. She had the uncomfortable feeling of the man in detective stories who suddenly realizes the stranger at the corner has been there in every chapter. It was a compliment, perhaps, but Sally was not sure she liked it. Bruce Carmyle meant nothing to her, and it was rather disturbing to discover she apparently meant a great deal to him.

She seized on the mention of Ginger as a convenient lever to pry the conversation away from intimacy.

“How is Mr. Kemp?” she asked.

Mr. Carmyle’s dark face seemed to become a shade darker, as if a cloud had drifted across his personal weather.

“We have had no news of him,” he said shortly.

“No news? How do you mean? You speak as though he had disappeared.”

“He has disappeared!”

“Good heavens! When?”

“Shortly after I saw you last.”

“Disappeared!”

Mr. Carmyle frowned, and Sally, watching him, felt her old antipathy stir. There was something about him she had disliked instinctively from the first—a hardness, like a man who can cut bread without noticing the crumbs.

“But where has he gone to?”

“I don’t know.” He frowned again, as if even the muscles of his face were offended. “And I don’t want to know,” he added heatedly, a dull flush rising in cheeks Sally was sure required shaving twice a day. “The Family have washed their hands of him. For the future he may look after himself as best he can. I believe he is off his head.”

Sally’s rebellious temper leapt up like a kettle beginning to sing, but she pushed it down. She longed to give battle—odd, she reflected, how she had appointed herself Ginger’s champion and protector—but if she wanted to hear more, she must humour Mr. Carmyle.

“But what happened? What was all the trouble about?”

Mr. Carmyle’s eyebrows met.

“He—insulted his uncle. His uncle Donald. He insulted him—grossly. The one man in the world he should have made a point of—er—”

“Keeping in with?”

“Yes. His future depended upon him.”

“But what did he do?” cried Sally, trying very hard to keep a thoroughly reprehensible joy out of her voice.

“I have heard no details. My uncle is reticent as to what actually took place. He invited Lancelot to dinner to discuss his plans, and it appears that Lancelot—defied him. Defied him! He was rude and insulting. My uncle refuses to have anything more to do with him. Apparently the young fool managed to win some money at the tables at Roville, and this seems to have turned his head completely. My uncle insists he is mad. I agree with him. Since the night of that dinner nothing has been heard of Lancelot.”

He broke off to brood, and before Sally could speak, the aisle darkened with the impressive bulk of Fillmore returning like a moving wall.

Explanations seemed to Fillmore to be required. He cast a questioning glance at the stranger who had not only engaged his sister in conversation but had occupied his seat with the calm assurance of a man taking the last roll.

“Oh, hullo, Fill,” said Sally. “Fillmore, this is Mr. Carmyle. We met abroad. My brother Fillmore, Mr. Carmyle.”

Fillmore approved of Mr. Carmyle instantly. Any man who looked as if he belonged on the letterhead of a bank appealed to Fillmore’s sense of the proper.

“Strange you meeting again like this,” he said affably.

The porter, who had been making up berths along the car, was hovering expectantly in the offing, all elbows and authority.

“You two had better go into the smoking room,” suggested Sally. “I’m going to bed.”

She wanted to be alone, to think.

The men went off, and Sally found an empty seat and sat down to wait for her berth to be made up. She was curiously exhilarated.

So Ginger had taken her advice. Excellent Ginger. She felt proud of him, and also possessed by that smug, faintly sinful glow known to those who give advice and find it obeyed. She had the emotions of a creator. Had she not, after all, created this new Ginger? She had stirred him; she had unleashed him. She had changed him from a meek dependent of the Family to a creature who went about insulting uncles.

It was something attempted, something done—and by all the rules laid down by poets it ought to have earned her a night’s repose.

Yet Sally slept badly.

Towards the small hours the train seemed to be experimenting with new buck-and-wing steps of its own invention, jolting and clattering so that the very air vibrated. The metallic rhythm of wheels on rails burrowed into the skull; somewhere a door latched and unlatched with a sharp, repetitive click like a nervous spoon against a cup. And presently, as Sally lay awake, the Spectre of Doubt came padding up to her bedside, lean and inquisitorial.

Had she, after all, done well? Had she been wise to meddle with a young man’s life, to season it with daring like a cook sprinkling pepper without tasting first?

“What about it?” said the Spectre of Doubt.

Daylight brought no comforting answer. Breakfast failed to manufacture an easy mind. Coffee, ham, rolls—none of it helped; the clink of crockery was merely the world continuing as if it had never ruined anybody’s prospects. Sally got off at Grand Central in a state of remorseful concern.

Mr. Carmyle offered to drive her to the boarding-house. She declined, and started to walk, hoping the crisp morning air would blow the cobwebs out of her brain.

She wondered now how she could ever have approved of her rash interference. What demon of meddling had possessed her? She wondered that she was allowed to go around loose. She was nothing more nor less than a menace to society.

Here was an estimable young man—obviously the sort of young man who would always need to be assisted through life by relatives the way some people need to be reminded to eat—and she had deliberately egged him on to wreck his prospects. She blushed hotly as she remembered the wild wireless she had sent him from the boat.

Miserable Ginger! She pictured him with his little stock of money gone, wandering footsore about London, seeking work in vain; forcing himself to call on Uncle Donald; being thrown down front steps by haughty footmen; sleeping on the Embankment; gazing into the dark waters of the Thames with the stare of hopelessness; climbing to the parapet and—

“Ugh!” said Sally.

She had arrived at the boarding-house door, and Mrs. Meecher was regarding her with welcoming eyes, little knowing that Sally, in her own mind, had all but bumped off a red-headed young man of amiable manners and—when not egged on by muddling females—excellent behaviour.

Mrs. Meecher was friendly and garrulous. Variety, which next to the dog Toto was the thing she loved best, had informed her on Friday morning that Mr. Foster’s play had got over big in Detroit, and that Miss Doland had made every kind of hit. It was not often old alumni of the boarding-house forced their way into the Hall of Fame, and the house, according to Mrs. Meecher, was ringing with the news.

That blue ribbon round Toto’s neck was worn in honour of the triumph. There was also, though you could not see it, a chicken dinner inside Toto by way of further celebration.

And was it true that Mr. Fillmore had bought the piece? A great man, was Mrs. Meecher’s verdict. Mr. Faucitt had always said so—

“Oh, how is Mr. Faucitt?” Sally asked suddenly, reproaching herself for letting other matters push the old gentleman out of her mind.

“He’s gone,” said Mrs. Meecher, with such relish that Sally, in her morbid condition, heard only one meaning.

She turned white and clutched the banisters. “Gone!”

“To England,” added Mrs. Meecher.

Sally was vastly relieved. “Oh, I thought you meant—”

“Oh no, not that.” Mrs. Meecher sighed, disappointed; she had liked Mr. Faucitt best when he promised to be an invalid of distinction, and it pained her that he had insisted on becoming robust. “He’s well enough. I never seen anybody better. You’d think this here new Spanish influenza was a tonic or somep’n, the way he looks now. Of course,” she added, hunting for justification, “he’s had good news. His brother’s dead.”

“What!”

“Not, I don’t mean that that was good news, far from it—though come to think of it, all flesh is as grass and we all got to be prepared for somep’n of the sort breaking loose—but it seems this here brother of his—I didn’t know he’d a brother, and I don’t suppose you knew he had a brother. Men are secretive, ain’t they!—this brother of his has left him a parcel of money, and Mr. Faucitt he had to get on the Wednesday boat quick as he could and go right over to the other side to look after things. Wind up the estate, I believe they call it. Left in a awful hurry. Sent his love to you and said he’d write. Funny him having a brother, now, wasn’t it? Not that folks don’t have brothers. I got two myself, one in Portland, Oregon, and the other goodness knows where he is. But what I’m trying to say—”

Sally disengaged herself and went up to her room. For a brief while the good news about Mr. Faucitt acted as a stimulant. Dear old man. She was sorry for the brother, of course—though she had only just learned he existed—but it was pleasant to think that Mr. Faucitt’s remaining years would be cushioned by comfort.

Then her thoughts slid back into their melancholy groove as reliably as a spoon into a jam jar.

She threw herself wearily on the bed. She was tired after the bad night, but she could not sleep. Remorse kept her awake. Besides, there was Mrs. Meecher prowling about somewhere below, boards creaking, Toto yapping as if appointed official commentator on all domestic motion. The house had a whole little ecology of sound: a distant kettle beginning to fuss, a door complaining on its hinges, the sharp footfall of industry. It made rest feel like something you had to apply for in triplicate.

Sally turned restlessly—and having turned, remained for a long instant transfixed and rigid.

She had seen something. And what she had seen would have surprised any girl in the privacy of her bedroom.

From under the bed peeped coyly forth an undeniably masculine shoe and six inches of grey trouser-leg.

Sally bounded to the floor. She was a girl of courage, and she meant to probe this matter thoroughly.

“What are you doing under my bed?”

The question was reasonable, and evidently seemed to the intruder to deserve an answer. There was a muffled sneeze, and he began to crawl out. The shoe came first. Then the legs. Then a sturdy body in a dusty coat. And finally there flashed on Sally’s fascinated gaze a head of such nearly maximum redness that it could only belong to one person in the world.

“Ginger!”

Mr. Lancelot Kemp, on all fours, blinked up at her.

“Oh, hullo!” he said.

She had imagined him dead with a novelist’s thoroughness: Ginger Kemp, buoyed up like an unclaimed parcel on the Thames, pale as yesterday’s haddock, drifting past the Houses of Parliament while indifferent ducks reviewed him. In the small boarding-house bedroom, the myth collapsed in an instant, replaced by a more immediate spectacle—Ginger standing alive in her doorway with his hair in a haystack condition and a black smudge on the very tip of his nose, like a cook who has tasted the gravy with his face.

Relief hit Sally so hard it came out of her as laughter. She dropped into a chair and let loose a sound that startled even herself, as if some hidden bird had been trapped in her throat and had finally found the open window.

“I say, you know!” Ginger began, hovering in the middle of the room in the tense way men do when they feel the furniture might accuse them.

Sally tried to speak, failed, and laughed again, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand as if she had been chopping onions instead of catastrophes.

“Oh, I am glad to see you,” she got out at last.

“No, really?” Ginger’s face brightened, pleased in the simple, earnest fashion of a dog who has just been told he isn’t entirely useless. “That’s fine.”

It seemed to occur to him that there were manners to be observed, even in burglar-like situations.

“I say, you know, awfully sorry. About barging in here, I mean. Never dreamed it was your room. Unoccupied, I thought.”

“Don’t mention it. I ought not to have disturbed you. You were having a nice sleep, of course. Do you always sleep on the floor?”

“It was like this—”

“Because if you’re wearing it for ornament,” Sally continued, her eyes narrowing with the clinical kindness of a woman inspecting a cake that’s sunk, “as a sort of beauty-spot, all right. But in case you don’t know, you’ve a smut on your nose.”

Ginger’s hand flew up.

“Oh, my aunt! Not really?”

“Now would I deceive you on an important point like that?”

“Do you mind if I have a look in the glass?”

“Certainly, if you can stand it.”

He hurried to the dressing-table, peered, and gave a little yelp of wounded dignity, dabbing at the spot with his handkerchief.

“You’re perfectly right,” he announced, as if commending her judgment in a matter of law. “I thought I was. I’m very quick at noticing things.”

“My hair’s a bit rumpled, too,” he added, still staring at himself, combing air into order with his fingers.

“Very much so.”

“You take my tip,” Ginger said earnestly, turning from the mirror like a man delivering a sermon to the newly fallen, “and never lie about under beds. There’s nothing in it.”

“That reminds me. You won’t be offended if I ask you something?”

“No, no. Go ahead.”

“It’s rather an impertinent question. You may resent it.”

“No, no.”

“Well, then—what were you doing under my bed?”

“Oh, under your bed?”

“Yes. Under my bed. This. It’s a bed, you know. Mine. My bed. You were under it. Why? Or putting it another way, why were you under my bed?”

“I was hiding.”

“Playing hide-and-seek? That explains it.”

“Mrs. What’s-her-name—Beecher—Meecher—was after me.”

Sally shook her head with grave disapproval, as though reprimanding a village elder for taking up jazz.

“You mustn’t encourage Mrs. Meecher in these childish pastimes. It unsettles her.”

Ginger passed an agitated hand over his forehead.

“It’s like this—”

“I hate to keep criticizing your appearance,” said Sally, “and personally I like it; but when you clutched your brow just then, you put about a pound of dust on it. Your hands are probably grubby.”

Ginger inspected them, holding them out as if offering them for seasoning.

“They are!”

“Why not make a really good job of it and have a wash?”

“Do you mind?”

“I’d prefer it.”

“Thanks awfully. I mean to say it’s your basin, you know, and all that. What I mean is, seem to be making myself pretty well at home.”

“Oh, no.”

“Touching the matter of soap—”

“Use mine. We Americans are famous for our hospitality.”

“Thanks awfully.”

“The towel is on your right.”

“Thanks awfully.”

“And I’ve a clothes brush in my bag.”

“Thanks awfully.”

Sally sat listening while he attacked the water with the zeal of a man trying to drown his past. Splashing filled the room—wet, slapping, domestic thunder—like a sea-lion taking a dip in a tin bath. The boarding-house itself contributed its usual thin accompaniment: a far-off phonograph wheezing something that had once been a song, the elevator clanking like a man chewing nails, and somewhere a door banging with that modern, joyless insistence which says the building would like to be a factory when it grows up. Sally, who always heard rooms as much as she saw them, felt her nerves loosen as the clean, honest noise of washing replaced the secretive hush of hiding.

“Now, then,” she said when the final splash subsided, “why were you hiding from Mrs. Meecher?”

Ginger emerged with his face wet and his expression that of a man who has been threatened with public speaking.

“I say, you know, that woman is rather by way of being one of the lads, what! Scares me! Word was brought that she was on the prowl, so it seemed to me a judicious move to take cover till she sort of blew over. If she’d found me, she’d have made me take that dog of hers for a walk.”

“Toto?”

“Toto.” Ginger spoke the name as if it were a harsh spice best used sparingly. “You know,” he went on, with a strong sense of having been wronged by nature, “no dog’s got a right to be a dog like that. I don’t suppose there’s anyone keener on dogs than I am, but a thing like a woolly rat—” He shuddered slightly. “Well, one hates to be seen about with it in the public streets.”

“Why couldn’t you have refused in a firm but gentlemanly manner to take Toto out?”

“Ah! There you rather touch the spot.” Ginger’s eyes slid away. “You see, the fact of the matter is, I’m a bit behind with the rent, and that makes it rather hard to take what you might call a firm stand.”

“But how can you be behind with the rent? I only left here the Saturday before last and you weren’t in the place then. You can’t have been here more than a week.”

“I’ve been here just a week. That’s the week I’m behind with.”

“But why? You were a millionaire when I left you at Roville.”

“Well, the fact of the matter is, I went back to the tables that night and lost a goodish bit of what I’d won. And, somehow or another, when I got to America, the stuff seemed to slip away.”

“What made you come to America at all?” Sally asked, politely laying down the question as one lays a knife on a table—flat, clean, unavoidable.

One of Ginger’s familiar blushes started up, as if his skin were trying to apologise on his behalf.

“Oh, I thought I would. Land of opportunity, you know.”

“Have you managed to find any of the opportunities yet?”

“Well, I have got a job of sorts. I’m a waiter at a rummy little place on Second Avenue. The salary isn’t big, but I’d have wangled enough out of it to pay last week’s rent, only they docked me a goodish bit for breaking plates and what not. The fact is, I’m making rather a hash of it.”

“Oh, Ginger! You oughtn’t to be a waiter!”

“That’s what the boss seems to think.”

“I mean, you ought to be doing something ever so much better.”

“But what? You’ve no notion how well all these blighters here seem to be able to get along without my help. I’ve tramped all over the place, offering my services, but they all say they’ll try to carry on as they are.”

Sally, who had grown up in a world where a kitchen could be run like a small kingdom and every pair of hands mattered, found this American talent for self-sufficiency almost offensive. She sat forward, struck by a thought as crisp as a snapped bean.

“I know!”

“What?”

“I’ll make Fillmore give you a job. I wonder I didn’t think of it before.”

“Fillmore?”

“My brother. Yes, he’ll be able to use you.”

“What as?”

Sally hesitated, searching for a title with the seriousness of someone deciding what to label a jar whose contents might explode.

“As a—as a—oh, as his right-hand man.”

“Does he want a right-hand man?”

“Sure to. He’s a young fellow trying to get along. Sure to want a right-hand man.”

“’M yes,” said Ginger reflectively. “Of course, I’ve never been a right-hand man, you know.”

“Oh, you’d pick it up. I’ll take you round to him now. He’s staying at the Astor.”

“There’s just one thing,” said Ginger.

“What’s that?”

“I might make a hash of it.”

“Heavens, Ginger! There must be something in this world that you wouldn’t make a hash of. Don’t stand arguing any longer. Are you dry? and clean? Very well, then. Let’s be off.”

“Right ho.”

He took one step toward the door and froze with one leg suspended, as if struck by a spell. From the passage came a shrill yapping—thin, sharp, metallic, like a badly tuned piccolo being murdered.

Ginger stared at Sally. Then he looked—longingly—at the bed, which in his mind had become a sacred cave of refuge.

“Don’t be such a coward,” said Sally severely.

“Yes, but—”

“How much do you owe Mrs. Meecher?”

“Round about twelve dollars, I think it is.”

“I’ll pay her.”

Ginger flushed awkwardly, the colour rising like shame to the top of a stew.

“No, I’m hanged if you will! I mean,” he stammered, “it’s frightfully good of you and all that, and I can’t tell you how grateful I am, but honestly, I couldn’t—”

Sally didn’t press it. In fact, she liked him better for refusing. Fillmore, in his penniless days, had never been too proud to accept help; he had accepted it as if it were a tax imposed by society on the comfortable.

“Very well,” she said. “Have it your own way. Proud. That’s me all over, Mabel. Ginger!” She broke off sharply as the yapping renewed itself, nearer now, a sound with teeth in it. “Pull yourself together. Where is your manly spirit? I’d be ashamed to be such a coward.”

“Awfully sorry, but honestly, that woolly dog—”

“Never mind the dog. I’ll see you through.”

They stepped into the passage almost on top of Toto, who was stalking phantom rats with the intense concentration of a creature whose mind contained nothing but vengeance and fluff. Behind him, Mrs. Meecher manoeuvred, ready to pounce like a general who has located the enemy’s pantry. Her face lit grimly when she saw Ginger.

“Mister Kemp! I been looking for you.”

Sally, bright as a bell and twice as carrying in that narrow corridor, slipped into the space between predator and prey.

“Oh, Mrs. Meecher,” she said, shepherding Ginger forward with the brisk competence of a woman steering a cart through geese, “I was so surprised to meet Mr. Kemp here. He is a great friend of mine. We met in France. We’re going off now to have a long talk about old times, and then I’m taking him to see my brother—”

“Toto—” began Mrs. Meecher, her voice taking on the particular tone of an employer loading a burden onto someone else’s shoulders.

“Dear little thing!” Sally exclaimed, as if Toto were a soufflé that had risen perfectly instead of a nervous mop with a bark like ripping cloth. “You ought to take him for a walk. It’s a lovely day. Mr. Kemp was saying just now that he would have liked to take him, but we’re rather in a hurry and shall probably have to get into a taxi. You’ve no idea how busy my brother is just now. If we’re late, he’ll never forgive us.”

She flowed down the stairs as she spoke, leaving Mrs. Meecher dissatisfied but uncertain, caught in the net of Sally’s tone. There had always been something about Sally—even before money—that made Mrs. Meecher feel as if she were being politely disciplined in a language she didn’t quite understand. Now, with Sally rich and independent, that bafflement edged into awe. By the time Mrs. Meecher had gathered her faculties, the front door had closed.

On the sidewalk Ginger stopped and drew a long breath, the city air tasting faintly of coal smoke and hot metal and somebody’s lunch.

“You know,” he said, looking at Sally as if she had just pulled a rabbit out of a hat and then convinced it to pay rent, “you’re wonderful!”

Sally accepted the compliment as if it were simply the correct seasoning for the moment.

“Now we’ll go and hunt up Fillmore,” she said. “But there’s no need to hurry, of course, really. We’ll go for a walk first, and then call at the Astor and make him give us lunch.”

Even the word lunch put a steadier rhythm into the street for Sally, as if the day had acquired a proper structure: talking, walking, eating—life arranged the way it ought to be, with nourishment as the hinge.

“I want to hear all about you. I’ve heard something already. I met your cousin, Mr. Carmyle. He was on the train coming from Detroit. Did you know that he was in America?”

“No, I’ve—er—rather lost touch with the Family.”

“So I gathered from Mr. Carmyle. And I feel hideously responsible. It was all through me that all this happened.”

“Oh, no.”

“Of course it was. I made you what you are to-day—I hope I’m satisfied—I dragged and dragged you down until the soul within you died, so to speak.” She spoke quickly, like someone beating egg whites before they collapse. “I know perfectly well that you wouldn’t have dreamed of savaging the Family as you seem to have done if it hadn’t been for what I said to you at Roville. Ginger, tell me—what did happen? I’m dying to know. Mr. Carmyle said you insulted your uncle!”

“Donald. Yes, we did have a bit of a scrap, as a matter of fact. He made me go out to dinner with him and we—er—sort of disagreed. To start with, he wanted me to apologize to old Scrymgeour, and I rather gave it a miss.”

“Noble fellow!”

“Scrymgeour?”

“No, silly! You.”

“Oh, ah!” Ginger blushed again, the reliable blush of a man whose skin has better manners than his circumstances. “And then there was all that about the soup, you know.”

“How do you mean, ‘all that about the soup’? What about the soup? What soup?”

“Well, things sort of hotted up a bit when the soup arrived.”

“I don’t understand.”

“I mean, the trouble seemed to start, as it were, when the waiter had finished ladling out the mulligatawny. Thick soup, you know.”

“I know mulligatawny is a thick soup. Yes?”

“Well, my old uncle—I’m not blaming him, don’t you know—more his misfortune than his fault—I can see that now—but he’s got a heavy moustache. Like a walrus, rather, and he’s a bit apt to inhale the stuff through it. And I—well, I asked him not to. It was just a suggestion, you know. He cut up fairly rough, and by the time the fish came round we were more or less down on the mat chewing holes in one another.”

Sally could see it with humiliating clarity: the gleam of the soup, the steam carrying curry and onion and that faint sharpness of apple that good mulligatawny ought to have; the heavy moustache acting like a sieve, catching broth and dignity alike. Some experiences, she thought, were untranslatable—how could one describe to a person who hadn’t been there the exact sound a moustache makes when it meets hot soup? Language had no proper word for that particular wet slurp of authority.

“My fault, probably,” Ginger continued. “I wasn’t feeling particularly well-disposed towards the Family that night. I’d just had a talk with Bruce—my cousin, you know—in Piccadilly, and that had rather got the wind up me. Bruce always seems to get on my nerves a bit somehow and—Uncle Donald asking me to dinner and all that. By the way, did you get the books?”

“What books?”

“Bruce said he wanted to send you some books. That was why I gave him your address.”

Sally stared.

“He never sent me any books.”

“Well, he said he was going to, and I had to tell him where to send them.”

They walked on. The city kept up its constant racket—horns barking, wheels grinding, voices rising and falling like badly rehearsed choruses—and Sally felt, under it all, the way modern noise tries to flatten every private thought into the same thin coin. She didn’t like the idea of Bruce Carmyle following her across an ocean, and she liked even less the idea that he could do it while pretending it was something else—books, culture, a little gift wrapped in respectable words. Words were powerful that way: put the right label on desire and it passed through customs.

“Go on telling me about your uncle,” she said, briskly, as if steering the conversation back to safer ground were like steering a pot away from boiling over.

“Well, there’s not much more to tell. I’d happened to get that wireless of yours just before I started out to dinner with him, and I was more or less feeling that I wasn’t going to stand any rot from the Family. I’d got to the fish course, hadn’t I? Well, we managed to get through that somehow, but we didn’t survive the fillet steak. One thing seemed to lead to another, and the show sort of bust up. He called me a good many things, and I got a bit fed-up, and finally I told him I hadn’t any more use for the Family and was going to start out on my own. And—well, I did, don’t you know. And here I am.”

Sally listened, breathless, as if he were describing a storm that had uprooted an old orchard. The story settled something in her. Whatever faint doubts she had had about handing her money over to Fillmore had dissolved. Ginger, with his rent arrears and his terror of small dogs and his tragic aptitude for making a hash of perfectly reasonable employment, was plainly a creature who needed to be set carefully on his feet, the way one sets a slightly cracked teacup on the safest shelf.

“We’ll go to the Astor now,” she said, “and I’ll introduce you to Fillmore. He’s a theatrical manager and he’s sure to have something for you.”

“It’s awfully good of you to bother about me.”

“Ginger,” said Sally, “I regard you as a grandson. Hail that cab, will you?”

In the weeks after Sally’s reunion with Ginger Kemp, life took on the glossy finish of a well-buttered pie-crust: everything smoothed, everything behaving, the whole world apparently rolled out with a pin and dusted with just enough flour to keep it from sticking.

Gerald, it was true, remained inconveniently a thousand miles away—Chicago had grabbed him by the collar and was refusing to let go. He had gone out to superintend the opening of The Primrose Way (now being treated, from Fillmore’s letters, less like a comedy and more like a civic religion). Fillmore, taking Ike’s advice, had produced it there first; then some frantic manager had begged Gerald to patch up a brother dramatist’s ailing play, and patching, like pastry, was a job that always took longer than advertised. Sally had hoped for him back in New York within days. Instead she received dates described in airy phrases like “soon” and “before long,” which in theater talk mean anything from Tuesday to the Second Coming.

But Sally refused to be gloomy. Everything else had aligned itself with a domestic kind of bliss. Fillmore was flourishing. Ginger was no longer a weight on her conscience. She had found an apartment. Her new hat made her look as if she belonged to a better-organized universe. And The Primrose Way—so Fillmore reported—had hit Chicago like a fire, only friendlier. Chicago, according to him, had abandoned national questions, local questions, and even questions about the weather. The only subject under discussion was how to get seats. If you wanted to start a riot, you didn’t shout “Fire!”—you murmured, “I’ve got two in the fifth row,” and waited for civilization to fall apart.

Best of all, Ginger’s future had been solved neatly, like tying up a roast with string. Ginger had entered the service of the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director, Fillmore Nicholas). Fillmore would have made it longer, but the brass plate had refused to stretch. Ginger was now to be found daily in the outer office, where his duties consisted chiefly of reading the evening papers and preventing the populace from storming the throne room in which Fillmore sat in semi-regal state, brooding over schemes as if he expected the Muse to arrive with a fountain pen and a checkbook.

What, precisely, Ginger was—secretary, gatekeeper, executive, mascot—remained unclear even to Ginger. Sometimes he felt like the man at the wheel. Sometimes he felt like an office boy with a longer leash. But there was nothing unclear about his gratitude to Sally for having pulled whatever strings needed pulling. He thanked her whenever he could; and lately he could often, because he had appointed himself, with cheerful violence, to the job of furnishing her new apartment.

“And what I mean to say is,” Ginger said, pausing mid-haul with a massive easy chair that seemed to have been carved out of a small respectable iceberg, “if I didn’t sweat a bit after the way you got me that job—”

“Ginger, desist,” said Sally.

“Yes, but honestly—”

“If you don’t stop it, I’ll make you move that chair into the next room.”

“Shall I?” Ginger adjusted his grip, hands blistered, face freckled and shining, as if he had been lightly basted. “Anything you say.”

“Silly! Of course not. The only other rooms are my bedroom, the bathroom, and the kitchen. What on earth would I want a great lumbering chair in them for? All the same, I believe the first we chose was the best.”

“Back she goes, then, what?”

Sally frowned with the profound seriousness of someone deciding between thyme and tarragon.

“No,” she said at last. “By the window is better.”

Ginger, accompanied by chair, staggered obediently to the window. Sally watched, feeling tired on his behalf. It was remarkable how another person’s exertion could leave you longing to lie down and be fanned with a magazine.

“I’m giving you a lot of trouble,” she said, remorseful.

“Trouble!” Ginger panted, depositing the chair and wiping his freckled forehead. “The way I look at it is this. You got me that job, and—”

“Stop!”

“Right ho… Still, you did, you know.”

Sally stretched in the armchair and surveyed the room. It was small, but it had begun to look like a real home—pictures up, carpet down, furniture arranged as if it meant to stay arranged. For the first time in her life she had that restful sensation of being behind her own locked door, alone, safe, not subject to boarding-house smells and voices and the thin wall through which somebody always seemed to be learning scales. Here the sounds were softer: the shift of a chair-leg on carpet, the distant hush of the street like a sea heard through shells, Ginger’s occasional grunt as if he were wrestling furniture into submission.

She looked about, pleased—until a tiny uneasiness pinched.

“Hullo!” she said. “Where’s that photograph of me? I’m sure I put it on the mantelpiece yesterday.”

Ginger peered, red-faced from effort and embarrassment.

“No. No photograph here.”

“I know there isn’t. But it was there yesterday. Or was it? I know I meant to put it there. It’s the most beautiful thing you ever saw. Not a bit like me; but what of that? They touch ’em up in the dark-room, you know. I value it because it looks the way I should like to look if I could.”

“I’ve never had a beautiful photograph taken of myself,” said Ginger solemnly, with gentle regret.

“Cheer up!”

“Oh, I don’t mind. I only mentioned—”

“Ginger,” said Sally abruptly, because the chair had begun to trouble her like a badly placed comma, “pardon my interrupting your remarks, which I know are valuable, but this chair is—not—right. It ought to be where it was at the beginning. Could you give your imitation of a pack-mule just once more? And after that I’ll make you some tea. If there’s any tea—or milk—or cups.”

“There are cups all right. I know, because I smashed two the day before yesterday. I’ll nip round the corner for some milk, shall I?”

“Yes, please nip. All this hard work has taken it out of me terribly.”

Over tea—made with the sort of improvisation that would have horrified an elder of any farm kitchen, for Sally’s cupboards were still in the raw stage and her spoons had a habit of hiding—Sally grew inquisitive.

“What I can’t understand about this job of yours, Ginger—which, as you are just about to observe, I was noble enough to secure for you—is the amount of leisure that seems to go with it. How is it you’re able to spend your valuable time—Fillmore’s valuable time, rather—juggling my furniture every day?”

“Oh, I can usually get off.”

“But oughtn’t you to be at your post doing—whatever it is you do? What do you do?”

Ginger stirred his tea as if the answer might rise to the surface.

“Well, I sort of mess about, you know.” He considered. “I interview divers blighters and tell ’em your brother is out and take their names and addresses and… oh, all that sort of thing.”

“Does Fillmore consult you much?”

“He lets me read some of the plays that are sent in. Awful tosh most of them. Sometimes he sends me off to a vaudeville house of an evening.”

“As a treat?”

“To see some special act, you know. To report on it. In case he might want to use it for this revue of his.”

“Which revue?”

Ginger blinked. “Didn’t you know he was going to put on a revue? Oh, rather. A whacking big affair. Going to cut out the Follies and all that sort of thing.”

“But—my goodness!” Sally sat up. Her peace, which had been lying purring on her lap, suddenly grew claws. It was exactly like Fillmore to go launching into expensive fireworks when he ought to be putting away pennies for winter. All his life he had thought in millions where sensible people thought in tens. Optimism bubbled in him like fermenting dough, rising whether you watched it or not. “That’s rather ambitious,” she said.

“Yes. Ambitious sort of cove, your brother. Quite the Napoleon.”

“I shall have to talk to him,” said Sally, firmly. She felt annoyed, not only because he might lose money, but because he had disturbed her new, snug order of things. “And I’ll tell you another thing that won’t do. This armchair. Of course it ought to be over by the window. You can see that yourself, can’t you.”

“Absolutely!” said Ginger, resignedly preparing for action once more.

In the midst of these domestic campaigns Sally received, like a pebble through a window, a telegram from Miss Winch in Chicago:

Have you been feeding Fillmore meat?

Sally couldn’t have said she understood it. Yet there was something ominous in the way the words sat together, as if they belonged to a private dialect spoken by sensible people when the world began to wobble. It decided her. Furnishing could wait; emergencies had no respect for curtains.

She went at once to the headquarters of the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director, Fillmore Nicholas).

In the outer office, Ginger was absent from his customary chair. In his place sat a boy of tender years and pimply exterior, whose expression thawed as if somebody had set him near a stove when Sally gave her name. He told her to walk right in.

Sally walked right in and found Fillmore with his feet on a desk that looked as if it had fought in wars. He was studying what appeared to be costume designs, and wearing the weary, distracted air of a man who has appointed himself Providence.

“Ah, Sally!” he said, in a voice heavy with vast concerns. Prosperity was still doing its soft, silent work upon him. What had once been merely a pleasant smoothness at the jaw was now frankly a double chin, as if success had folded itself neatly and tucked it in. He wore a new waistcoat, unbuttoned. “I am rather busy. Always glad to see you, but I am rather busy. I have a hundred things to attend to.”

“Well, attend to me,” said Sally. “That’ll only make a hundred and one. Fill, what’s all this I hear about a revue?”

Fillmore looked like a small boy discovered with jam on his fingers—except that the jam, in his case, was ambition and it had got into his hair. He picked up a pen and put it down; buttoned his waistcoat and unbuttoned it; scratched his ear with one of the costume designs as if seeking inspiration under the paper.

“Oh yes, the revue!”

“It’s no good saying ‘Oh yes’! You know perfectly well it’s a crazy idea.”

“Really… these business matters… this interference…”

“I don’t want to run your affairs for you, Fill, but that money of mine does make me a sort of partner, I suppose, and I think I have a right to raise a loud yell of agony when I see you risking it on a—”

“Pardon me,” said Fillmore loftily, brightening. “Let me explain. Women never understand business matters. Your money is tied up exclusively in The Primrose Way, which, as you know, is a tremendous success. You have nothing whatever to worry about as regards any new production I may make.”

“I’m not worrying about the money. I’m worrying about you.”

A tolerant smile moved about the lower slopes of Fillmore’s face.

“Don’t be alarmed about me. I’m all right.”

“You aren’t all right. You’ve no business, when you’ve only just got started as a manager, to be rushing into an enormous production like this. You can’t afford it.”

“My dear child, as I said before, women cannot understand these things. A man in my position can always command money for a new venture.”

“Do you mean to say you have found somebody silly enough to put up money?”

“Certainly. I don’t know that there is any secret about it. Your friend, Mr. Carmyle, has taken an interest in some of my forthcoming productions.”

“What!”

Sally had been uneasy; now she was stunned. Bruce Carmyle again—sliding into her life like a tide that found every crack. There seemed no escaping him. Wherever she turned, there he was, and all she could do was rage helplessly at the way the world kept offering him opportunities to be useful.

Fillmore mistook her tone.

“It’s quite all right,” he assured her. “He’s a very rich man. Large private means, besides his big income. Even if anything goes wrong—”

“It isn’t that. It’s…”

Sally stopped. How could you explain to Fillmore the particular sort of discomfort that came from a man’s money and attention, when the man himself was precisely what you wanted at a distance? It wasn’t the arithmetic of it; it was the pressure. Some things, like certain tastes and certain memories, refused translation into another person’s vocabulary.

She was still struggling with this when voices rose in the outer office. The pimply boy seemed to be attempting to defend the sanctity of the inner room against an invader. He failed. The door burst open and Miss Winch sailed in as if propelled by outrage and railway schedules.

“Fillmore, you poor nut,” said Miss Winch—who, while her telegrams might be cryptic as ancient runes, spoke in person with all the subtlety of a frying-pan. “Stop picking straws in your hair and listen to me. You’re dippy!”

The last time Sally had seen Miss Winch she had been calm as a lake. This new agitation—this crackle—felt ominous.

“Ah! Here you are!” said Fillmore, indignation springing up like a bearded lion until he recognized the intruder and remembered affection. “My dear—”

“Yes, here I am!” Miss Winch dropped into a swivel chair with despairing energy and attempted to restore herself with chewing gum, as if mastication were the first aid for human folly. “Fillmore, darling, you’re the sweetest thing on earth, and I love you, but on present form you could just walk straight into Bloomingdale and they’d give you the royal suite.”

“My dear girl—”

“What do you think?” demanded Miss Winch of Sally.

“I’ve just been telling him,” said Sally gratefully, glad of an ally, “I think it’s absurd at this stage of things for him to put on an enormous revue—”

“Revue?” Miss Winch froze with the gum halfway to her mouth. She flung up her arms. “I shall have to swallow this gum. You can’t chew with your head going round. Are you putting on a revue too?”

Fillmore buttoned and unbuttoned his waistcoat with the hunted air of a man trying to escape his own genius.

“Certainly, certainly,” he said, feverishly. “I wish you girls would leave me to manage—”

“Dippy!” said Miss Winch again. “Telegraphic address: Tea-Pot, Matteawan.” She swung back to Sally. “Listen! This boy must be stopped. We must form a gang in his best interests and get him put away. What do you think he proposes doing? I’ll give you three guesses. Oh, what’s the use? You’d never hit it. This poor wandering lad has got it all fixed up to star me—me—in a new show!”

Fillmore lifted one hand from his waistcoat in protest.

“I have used my own judgment—”

“Yes, sir!” said Miss Winch, rolling right over him as if he were a bump in the road. “That’s what he’s planning to spring on an unsuspecting public. I’m sitting peacefully in my room at the hotel in Chicago, pronging a few cents’ worth of scrambled eggs and reading the morning paper, when the telephone rings. Gentleman below would like to see me. Oh, ask him to wait. Business of flinging on a few clothes. Down in elevator. Bright sunrise effects in lobby.”

“What on earth do you mean?” said Sally, bewildered.

“The gentleman had a head of red hair which had to be seen to be believed,” explained Miss Winch. “Lit up the lobby. Management had switched off all the electrics for sake of economy. An Englishman he was. Nice fellow. Named Kemp.”

“Oh, is Ginger in Chicago?” said Sally. “I wondered why he wasn’t on his little chair in the outer office.”

“I sent Kemp to Chicago,” said Fillmore, stiffly, “to have a look at the show. It is my policy, if I am unable to pay periodical visits myself, to send a representative—”

“Save it up for the long winter evenings,” advised Miss Winch. “Mr. Kemp may have been there to look at the show, but his chief reason for coming was to tell me to beat it back to New York to enter into my kingdom. Fillmore wanted me on the spot, he told me, so that I could sit around in this office here, interviewing my supporting company. Me! Can you or can you not tie it?”

“Well…” Sally began diplomatically, and failed.

“Don’t say it! I know it just as well as you do. It’s too sad for words.”

“You persist in underestimating your abilities, Gladys,” said Fillmore reproachfully. “I have had a certain amount of experience in theatrical matters—I have seen a good deal of acting—and I assure you that as a character-actress you—”

Miss Winch rose like a spring, kissed him energetically, and sat down again with the briskness of someone administering medicine.

“You’re a darling old thing to talk like that,” she said, “and I hate to wake you out of your daydreams, but honestly, Fillmore, dear, do just step out of the padded cell for one moment and listen to reason. I know exactly what’s been passing in your poor disordered bean. You took Elsa Doland out of a minor part and made her a star overnight. She goes to Chicago, and the critics and everybody else rave about her. As a matter of fact,” she said to Sally, honest admiration lighting her face, “you can’t realize, not having seen her play there, what an amazing hit she’s made. She really is a sensation. Everybody says she’s going to be the biggest thing on record. Very well then, what does Fillmore do? The poor fish claps his hand to his forehead and cries, ‘Gadzooks! An idea! I’ve done it before, I’ll do it again. I’m the fellow who can make a star out of anything.’ And he picks on me!”

“My dear girl—” murmured Fillmore, wounded.

“Now, the flaw in the scheme is this. Elsa is a genius, and if he hadn’t made her a star somebody else would have done. But little Gladys? That’s something else again.” She looked to Sally. “You’ve seen me in action, and let me tell you you’ve seen me at my best. Give me a maid’s part with a tray to carry on in act one and a couple of ‘Yes, madam’s’ in act two, and I’m there! Ellen Terry hasn’t anything on me when it comes to saying ‘Yes, madam,’ and I’m willing to back myself for gold, notes, or lima beans against Sarah Bernhardt as a tray-carrier. But there I finish. That lets me out. And anybody who thinks otherwise is going to lose a lot of money. Between ourselves the only thing I can do really well is to cook…”

“My dear Gladys!” cried Fillmore, revolted.

“I’m a heaven-born cook,” said Miss Winch simply, “and I don’t mind notifying the world to that effect. I can cook a chicken casserole so you’d leave home and mother for it. Also my English pork-pies! One of these days I’ll take an afternoon off and assemble one for you. You’d be surprised! But acting—no. I can’t do it, and I don’t want to do it. I only went on the stage for fun, and my idea of fun isn’t to plough through a star part with all the critics waving their axes in the front row, and me knowing all the time that it’s taking money out of Fillmore’s bankroll that ought to be going towards buying the little home with stationary wash-tubs… Well, that’s that, Fillmore, old darling. I thought I’d just mention it.”

Sally couldn’t help feeling for Fillmore. He sat with his chin in his hands, staring moodily ahead—Napoleon at Elba, only with better tailoring. It was plain this dream of flinging Miss Winch to glory had been very near his heart.

“If that’s how you feel,” he said at last, in a stricken voice, “there is nothing more to say.”

“Oh, yes there is,” said Miss Winch briskly. “We will now talk about this revue of yours. It’s off!”

Fillmore sprang up. He thumped the desk with a well-nourished fist. A man can stand only so much feminine committee-work in his private office.

“It is not off! Great heavens! It’s too much! I will not put up with this interference with my business concerns. I will not be tied and hampered. Here am I, a man of broad vision and—and—broad vision—I form my plans… my plans… I form them… I shape my schemes… and what happens? A horde of girls flock into my private office while I am endeavouring to concentrate… and concentrate… I won’t stand it. Advice, yes. Interference, no. I… I… I… and kindly remember that!”

The door slammed. A fainter detonation suggested his whirlwind passage through the outer office. Footsteps faded down the corridor.

Sally stood staring at Miss Winch, stunned. A roused and militant Fillmore was new—like discovering your mildest relative has opinions about revolution.

Miss Winch, unperturbed, unwrapped her gum at last and began chewing.

“Isn’t he cute!” she said. “I hope he doesn’t get the soft kind,” she added thoughtfully.

“The soft kind?”

“He’ll be back soon with a box of candy,” explained Miss Winch, “and he will get that sloshy, creamy sort, though I keep telling him I like the other. Well, one thing’s certain. Fillmore’s got it up his nose. He’s beginning to hop about and sing in the sunlight. It’s going to be hard work to get that boy down to earth again.” She sighed, gently. “I should like him to have enough left in the old stocking to pay the first year’s rent when the wedding bells ring out.” She chewed. “Not that it matters. I’d be just as happy in two rooms and a kitchenette, so long as Fillmore was there. You’ve no notion how dippy I am about him.” Her freckled face glowed with a kind of stubborn tenderness. “He grows on me like a darned drug. And the funny thing is, I keep right on admiring him though I can see all the while he’s the most perfect chump. He is a chump, you know. That’s what I love about him. That and the way his ears wiggle when he gets excited. Chumps always make the best husbands. When you marry, Sally, grab a chump. Tap his forehead first, and if it rings solid, don’t hesitate. All the unhappy marriages come from the husband having brains. What good are brains to a man? They only unsettle him.”

She broke off and examined Sally with sudden intensity, as if changing subjects were a stage entrance.

“Say, what do you do with your skin?”

Sally laughed. “What do I do with my skin? I just carry it around with me.”

“Well,” said Miss Winch enviously, “I wish I could train my darned fool of a complexion to get that way. Freckles are the devil. When I was eight I had the finest collection in the Middle West, and I’ve been adding to it right along. Some folks say lemon-juice’ll cure ’em. Mine lap up all I give ’em and ask for more. There’s only one way of getting rid of freckles, and that is to saw the head off at the neck.”

“But why do you want to get rid of them?”

“Why?” said Miss Winch. “Because a sensitive girl, anxious to retain her future husband’s love, doesn’t enjoy going about looking like something out of a dime museum.”

“How absurd! Fillmore worships freckles.”

“Did he tell you so?” asked Miss Winch eagerly.

“Not in so many words, but you can see it in his eye.”

“Well, he certainly asked me to marry him knowing all about them,” said Miss Winch, comforted. “And what’s more, I don’t think feminine loveliness means much to Fillmore, or he’d never have picked on me. Still, it’s calculated to give a girl a jar when she picks up a magazine and reads an advertisement of a face-cream beginning, ‘Your husband is growing cold to you. Can you blame him? Have you really tried to cure those unsightly blemishes?’—meaning what I’ve got. Still, I haven’t noticed Fillmore growing cold to me, so maybe it’s all right.”

If Ginger Kemp had ever been compelled—at gunpoint, say, by a stern food-historian with a notebook—to list his virtues, he would have produced a page as sparsely garnished as Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house porridge. He might have managed “means well,” after which he would have sat chewing the pencil as if it were an obstinate bit of gristle. Tact, delicacy, and that soft-fingered perception which knows when to pat and when to vanish would certainly not have appeared on his menu.

And yet, during the next few days, he did something that looked uncommonly like delicacy. He stayed away from Sally.

It was not easy. Staying away from Sally was like walking past an open bakery with your pockets full of money and your soul full of hunger. But he did it, partly because he argued from his own tastes (always a dangerous habit in any cook, lover, or amateur philosopher), and was firmly convinced that in times of trouble a person wants solitude—wants to sit in a quiet room and reduce the problem slowly, like stock, skimming off the foam of emotion until something clear remains at the bottom.

Ginger had, in his time, had what he called nasty jars—moments when life went crack in the hand like a plate dropped on tiles—and on those occasions all he had wanted was to be left alone with his thoughts, to fight it out like a man in a pantry with a wasp. So he left Sally alone, though it went against him, and he did it with a sort of clumsy chivalry: the chivalry of a man who can’t tie a bow but will carry the parcel all the way home.

By Saturday, however, he decided the broth had simmered long enough. Action, he felt, could now be applied.

Saturday was a capital day for gathering up life’s dropped threads. He didn’t have to go to the office, and—more to the point—he had just drawn his week’s salary. Mrs. Meecher, with the deftness of an agrarian elder harvesting by moonlight, had taken a portion off him at the source, but enough remained to let him attempt consolation on a scale that might be described, by a man not actually paying for it, as princely.

He conceived a plan. He would hire a car and take Sally out to dinner at one of the road-houses up the Boston Post Road—places he had heard of in the same tone men use for secret fisheries and miraculous pies. The idea improved as he contemplated it. It was helped, too, by the weather.

The weather had been a revelation to Ginger. It was his first American Indian Summer, and it had behaved like a conspirator. The sun didn’t blaze; it upholstered. It laid velvet over the city’s harsh angles and made even dust seem romantic. As Ginger stood on the roof of Mrs. Meecher’s establishment that Saturday morning, thrilled by the soft brilliance and the gentler noises it coaxed out of the town—the distant wheels no longer snarling, the river’s breath audible between buildings—he felt that the only sane way to spend such a day was in an open car with Sally beside him and the air tasting faintly of leaves.

The Maison Meecher was a lofty building on a side street at the lower end of the avenue. From its roof, after you had navigated the hanging jungles of washing limp on lines—great domestic flags of surrender—you could see many things. To the left lay Washington Square, full of somnolent Italians and children who made the peculiar scraping music of roller-skates: a metallic whispering that, in quieter moments, sounded almost like rain on a tin roof. To the right was a sight that never failed to intrigue Ginger: the high funnels of a Cunard liner moving slowly down the river, apparently sailing, by some mythic misprint, along Ninth Avenue. Today there were four funnels, and Ginger deduced the Mauretania.

As the boat on which he had come over from England, the Mauretania had a sentimental interest for him. He stood watching her stately progress until taller buildings farther down-town shut her away, then picked his way through the washing and went down to his room to fetch his hat.

A quarter of an hour later, he was in the hallway of Sally’s apartment-house, and the day’s velvet promptly snagged.

He was gazing—gazing with the sort of disgust a man feels when he lifts a saucepan-lid and discovers that somebody has used his stew as an ashtray—at the serge-clad back of his cousin, Mr. Bruce Carmyle. Mr. Carmyle was engaged in conversation with a gentleman in overalls.

No care-free prospector, singing his way through the Mojave Desert and suddenly finding himself nose to nose with a rattlesnake, could have experienced a sharper change of mood than did Ginger at this spectacle. Even in their native Piccadilly it had been unpleasant to collide with Bruce Carmyle. To find him here, now, in the very doorway of Sally’s life, was nauseating.

Only one thing could have brought him. Obviously he had come to see Sally—and with a sinking of the heart Ginger remembered the shiny, expensive automobile waiting at the curb. It was clear that Ginger’s plan of open-car sunshine had occurred to other minds as well, though in Bruce Carmyle’s head even sunshine probably arrived with an invoice attached.

Ginger was still standing there when Mr. Carmyle swung round. His dark face wore the scowl of a man who has been forced into conversation with a janitor and found the janitor insufficiently awed.

The sight of Ginger did not improve him.

“Hullo!” said Mr. Carmyle.

“Hullo!” said Ginger.

A silence followed. It had the awkward density of undercooked dumplings.

“Have you come to see Miss Nicholas?” said Mr. Carmyle at last.

“Why, yes.”

“She isn’t here,” said Mr. Carmyle, and the discovery that he had found someone to share the bad news seemed to cheer him, as shared misfortune will cheer certain temperaments—particularly those that like to watch other people choke on it.

“Not here?”

“No. Apparently…” Mr. Carmyle’s scowl deepened; it was the expression of a well-balanced man encountering another person’s unreasonable behavior. “…apparently, for some extraordinary reason, she has taken it into her head to dash over to England.”

Ginger tottered. The blow was so unexpected it seemed to knock the sound out of the hallway: even the ordinary building noises—the elevator’s groan, a door shutting, distant plumbing—went thin and far away.

He followed his cousin out into the sunshine in a kind of dream. Bruce Carmyle was speaking to the chauffeur of the expensive automobile.

“I find I shall not want the car. You can take it back to the garage.”

The chauffeur, a moody man, opened one half-closed eye and spat cautiously. It was the sort of careful, considered spitting a financier might employ when approaching the crisis of a delicate negotiation.

“You’ll have to pay just the same,” he observed, opening his other eye to lend emphasis.

“Of course I shall pay,” snapped Mr. Carmyle. “How much is it?”

Money passed. The car rolled off with the soft purr of something well-fed.

“Gone to England?” said Ginger. His voice sounded to him like somebody else’s, like a line misquoted in a foreign play.

“Yes. Gone to England.”

“But why?”

“How the devil do I know why?” said Mr. Carmyle, with the air of a man whose patience has been forced through a mincer. “All I know is what the janitor told me—that she sailed on the Mauretania this morning.”

The tragic irony of it came down on Ginger like a lid. He had stood on the roof watching that very ship glide away, content as a man anticipating dinner, never dreaming that his whole heart was sitting somewhere on that deck, pulling away from him with each quiet turn of the propellers.

He nodded absently to Mr. Carmyle and walked off. There was nothing to say. Words were cheap and he felt suddenly bankrupt.

The warmth went out of the sunshine. The day’s sweetness, which had been like ripe fruit, turned flavorless in his mouth. He wandered with no plan, loafing through streets that now seemed made of cardboard scenery. He went into the Park and out again. The Park bored him. The streets bored him. The whole city bored him—its clanging, its tire-hiss, its rude modern music of engines and construction that never stopped. Without Sally, New York became all industrial racket and no meaning: a kitchen full of noise where nobody is cooking.

Not even the thought that his cousin—a careful man with his money—had paid a day’s hire for a car he couldn’t use brought Ginger any balm.

Night came at last, and with it a letter.

It was the first even passably pleasant thing that had happened all day, because the envelope bore the crest of the good ship Mauretania. Ginger snatched it from the letter-rack as if it were a last biscuit from a tin in a famine, and carried it upstairs to his room.

Very few rooms at Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house struck any note of luxury. Mrs. Meecher was not one of your fashionable interior decorators. She was of the opinion that when you had provided a bed, a wash-stand, and a Morris chair, you had mounted as high toward splendor as any guest at seven-and-a-half dollars a week had a right to climb. As a rule, the severity of these surroundings afflicted Ginger with gloom when he went to bed; but tonight—such is the magic of a letter from the right person—he was uplifted and almost gay. There are moments when even illuminated texts over the wash-stand cannot wholly quell a man.

There was nothing of haste, and much of ceremony, in Ginger’s method of approaching correspondence. He behaved like a small boy with unexpected ice-cream, lingering over it before taking the first spoonful, anxious to make it last. His first move was to feel in the breast-pocket of his coat and produce the photograph of Sally which he had feloniously removed from her apartment. He looked long and earnestly at it, then propped it against the basin, within easy reach, for reference.

He took off his coat, collar, and shoes; filled and lit a pipe; placed pouch and matches on the Morris chair’s arm; and drew the chair up so that he could sit with his feet on the bed. Having manoeuvred himself into comfort, he lit his pipe again and took up the letter.

He looked at the crest, at Sally’s handwriting, at the postmark. He weighed it. It was bulky. He took up the photograph and scrutinized it once more, as if her face might dictate the proper way to begin reading. Then he lit his pipe again, wriggled deeper into the chair, and opened the envelope.

“Ginger, dear.”

Having read so far, Ginger found it necessary to take up the photograph and stare at it with even greater intentness. He gazed at it for several minutes, then laid it down and lit his pipe again. Then he went on.

“Ginger, dear—I’m afraid this address is going to give you rather a shock, and I’m feeling very guilty. I’m running away, and I haven’t even stopped to say good-bye. I can’t help it. I know it’s weak and cowardly, but I simply can’t help it. I stood it for a day or two, and then I saw that it was no good. (Thank you for leaving me alone and not coming round to see me. Nobody else but you would have done that. But then, nobody ever has been or ever could be so understanding as you.)”

Ginger, at this point, was compelled to look at the photograph again.

“There was too much in New York to remind me. That’s the worst of being happy in a place. When things go wrong you find there are too many ghosts about. I just couldn’t stand it. I tried, but I couldn’t. I’m going away to get cured—if I can.

“Mr. Faucitt is over in England, and when I went down to Mrs. Meecher for my letters, I found one from him. His brother is dead, you know, and he has inherited, of all things, a fashionable dress-making place in Regent Street. His brother was Laurette et Cie. I suppose he will sell the business later on, but, just at present, the poor old dear is apparently quite bewildered and that doesn’t seem to have occurred to him. He kept saying in his letter how much he wished I was with him, to help him, and I was tempted and ran. Anything to get away from the ghosts and have something to do. I don’t suppose I shall feel much better in England, but, at least, every street corner won’t have associations. Don’t ever be happy anywhere, Ginger. It’s too big a risk, much too big a risk.

“There was a letter from Elsa Doland, too. Bubbling over with affection. We had always been tremendous friends. Of course, she never knew anything about my being engaged to Gerald. I lent Fillmore the money to buy that piece, which gave Elsa her first big chance, and so she’s very grateful. She says, if ever she gets the opportunity of doing me a good turn… Aren’t things muddled?

“And there was a letter from Gerald. I was expecting one, of course, but… what would you have done, Ginger? Would you have read it? I sat with it in front of me for an hour, I should think, just looking at the envelope, and then… You see, what was the use? I could guess exactly the sort of thing that would be in it, and reading it would only have hurt a lot more. The thing was done, so why bother about explanations? What good are explanations, anyway? They don’t help. They don’t do anything…

“I burned it, Ginger. The last letter I shall ever get from him. I made a bonfire on the bathroom floor, and it smouldered and went brown, and then flared a little, and every now and then I lit another match and kept it burning, and at last it was just black ashes and a stain on the tiles. Just a mess!

“Ginger, burn this letter, too. I’m pouring out all the poison to you, hoping it will make me feel better. You don’t mind, do you? But I know you don’t. If ever anybody had a real pal…

“It’s a dreadful thing, fascination, Ginger. It grips you and you are helpless. One can be so sensible and reasonable about other people’s love affairs. When I was working at the dance place I told you about there was a girl who fell in love with the most awful little beast. He had a mean mouth and shiny black hair brushed straight back, and anybody would have seen what he was. But this girl wouldn’t listen to a word. I talked to her by the hour. It makes me smile now when I think how sensible and level-headed I was. But she wouldn’t listen. In some mysterious way this was the man she wanted, and, of course, everything happened that one knew would happen.

“If one could manage one’s own life as well as one can manage other people’s! If all this wretched thing of mine had happened to some other girl, how beautifully I could have proved that it was the best thing that could have happened, and that a man who could behave as Gerald has done wasn’t worth worrying about. I can just hear myself. But, you see, whatever he has done, Gerald is still Gerald and Sally is still Sally and, however much I argue, I can’t get away from that. All I can do is to come howling to my redheaded pal, when I know just as well as he does that a girl of any spirit would be dignified and keep her troubles to herself and be much too proud to let anyone know that she was hurt.

“Proud! That’s the real trouble, Ginger. My pride has been battered and chopped up and broken into as many pieces as you broke Mr. Scrymgeour’s stick! What pitiful creatures we are. Girls, I mean. At least, I suppose a good many girls are like me. If Gerald had died and I had lost him that way, I know quite well I shouldn’t be feeling as I do now. I should have been broken-hearted, but it wouldn’t have been the same. It’s my pride that is hurt. I have always been a bossy, cocksure little creature, swaggering about the world like an English sparrow; and now I’m paying for it! Oh, Ginger, I’m paying for it! I wonder if running away is going to do me any good at all. Perhaps, if Mr. Faucitt has some real hard work for me to do…

“Of course, I know exactly how all this has come about. Elsa’s pretty and attractive. But the point is that she is a success, and as a success she appeals to Gerald’s weakest side. He worships success. She is going to have a marvellous career, and she can help Gerald on in his. He can write plays for her to star in. What have I to offer against that? Yes, I know it’s grovelling and contemptible of me to say that, Ginger. I ought to be above it, oughtn’t I—talking as if I were competing for some prize… But I haven’t any pride left. Oh, well!

“There! I’ve poured it all out and I really do feel a little better just for the moment. It won’t last, of course, but even a minute is something. Ginger, dear, I shan’t see you for ever so long, even if we ever do meet again, but you’ll try to remember that I’m thinking of you a whole lot, won’t you? I feel responsible for you. You’re my baby. You’ve got started now and you’ve only to stick to it. Please, please, please don’t ‘make a hash of it’! Good-bye. I never did find that photograph of me that we were looking for that afternoon in the apartment, or I would send it to you. Then you could have kept it on your mantelpiece, and whenever you felt inclined to make a hash of anything I would have caught your eye sternly and you would have pulled up.

“Good-bye, Ginger. I shall have to stop now. The mail is just closing.

“Always your pal, wherever I am.—SALLY.”

Ginger laid the letter down, and a small sound escaped him that was half sigh and half oath. He sat listening to the boarding-house at night: a cough in a distant room, the thin complaint of pipes, the street’s perpetual mechanical mutter. The whole soundscape felt suddenly hostile, as if modern life, with its engines and hiss and clank, had conspired to drown out the one voice he needed.

He wondered whether even now some desirable end might not be achieved by going to Chicago and breaking Gerald Foster’s neck. Abandoning this scheme as impracticable, and being unable to think of anything else to do, he re-lit his pipe and started to read the letter again.

Laurette et Cie, Regent Street, London, W., England.  
January 21st.

Dear Ginger,—

I’m feeling better.

As it’s three months since I last wrote to you, you will no doubt remark to yourself, with that strong, judicial mind of yours, that if I weren’t improving after three months I should be a poor, weak-minded creature and probably unfit to be allowed near matches. In justice to myself, I will say that I am improved. I haven’t exactly *finished* with my troubles, but I’ve got them bottled and corked and put away in the cellar, like a jam one doesn’t mean to serve to visitors.

That is something, isn’t it?

I suppose I ought to give you all my impressions of London; but London has the advantage (or defect) of being like a sauce you eat every day until it stops tasting of anything at all. At first you notice the sharpness, the richness, the odd little foreign tang; later it’s merely “London,” and you take it as you take pepper—present, unavoidable, and liable to make you sneeze.

You will see by the address that Mr. Faucitt has not yet sold his inheritance. He expects to do so very soon, he says—there is a rich-looking man with whiskers and a keen eye whom he is always lunching with, and I feel big deals are being basted and turned somewhere behind the scenes. Poor dear! he is crazy to get away into the country and settle down and grow ducks and things. London has disappointed him. It is not the place it used to be. Until quite lately, when he grew resigned, he used to wander about in a disconsolate sort of way trying to locate the landmarks of his youth. (He has not been in England for nearly thirty years.) The trouble is, it seems, that about once in every thirty years a sort of craze for change comes over London and they paint a shop-front red instead of blue, and that upsets the returned exile dreadfully. He feels like Rip Van Winkle, only without the excuse of a long nap. His first shock was when he found that the Empire was a theatre now instead of a music-hall. Then he was told that the Tivoli had been pulled down altogether. And when, on top of that, he went to look at the baker’s shop in Rupert Street over which he had lodgings in the eighties and discovered it had been turned into a dressmaker’s, he grew very melancholy and only cheered up a little when a lovely magenta fog came on and showed him that some things were still being cooked in the old way.

I am kept quite busy at Laurette et Cie., thank goodness. (Not being a French scholar like you—do you remember Jules?—I thought at first that “Cie” was the name of the junior partner and looked forward to meeting him. “Miss Nicholas, shake hands with Mr. Cie, one of your greatest admirers.”)

I hold down the female equivalent of your job at the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.—that is to say, I’m a sort of right-hand woman. I hang around and sidle up to customers when they come in, and say, “Chawming weather, moddom!” (which is usually a black lie, because London specializes in weather which looks as if it has been made from dish-water and old wool). Then I pass them on to the staff who do the actual work, like those kitchens where the head person smiles and the real cooking happens among steam, clatter, and muttered curses behind a swinging door.

I shouldn’t mind going on like this for the next few years, but Mr. Faucitt is determined to sell. I don’t know if you are like that, but every other Englishman I have ever met seems to have an ambition to own a house and lot in Loamshire or Hants or Salop or somewhere. Their one object in life is to make some money and “buy back the old place”—which was sold, of course, at the end of Act One to pay the heir’s gambling debts.

Mr. Faucitt, when he was a small boy, used to live in a little village in Gloucestershire near a place called Cirencester—only it isn’t: it’s called Cissister, which I bet you didn’t know—and after forgetting about it for fifty years he has suddenly been bitten by the desire to end his days there surrounded by pigs and chickens. He took me down to see it the other day.

Oh, Ginger, this English country! Why any of you ever live in towns I can’t think. Old, old grey stone houses, with the smell of damp earth and woodsmoke hanging in the lanes as snugly as a shawl. Yellow haystacks that look like enormous loaves left to cool. Mud that squelches with such contentment under your shoes that you begin to feel that Nature is personally applauding your footsteps. Great fat trees, and in the distance blue hills like the rind of something heavenly. And over it all a quiet that isn’t emptiness but a kind of careful sound—birds being busy, a gate somewhere giving a small wooden complaint, wind combing through hedges. It’s the sort of peace you could spread on bread.

If ever I sell my soul, I shall insist on the devil giving me at least forty years in some English country place in exchange. He can have the rest of eternity for all I care, provided I get the forty years with decent butter.

Perhaps you will think from all this that I am too occupied to remember your existence. Just to show how interested I am in you, let me tell you that when I was reading the paper a week ago I happened to see the headline “International Match.” It didn’t seem to mean anything at first, and then I suddenly recollected: this was the thing you had once been a snip for. So I went down to a place called Twickenham, where this football game was to be, to see the sort of thing you used to do before I took charge of you and made you a respectable right-hand man.

There was an enormous crowd, and I was nearly squeezed to death, but I bore it for your sake. The sound of it alone was enough to turn one’s brain to batter: yelling in waves, like surf made of throats; stamping that shook through the wooden stands; the thin, metallic shriek of whistles like knives. I have always believed that certain noises are bad for the soul in the way certain cheap pickles are bad for the stomach—once you’ve swallowed them, you may survive, but you are never quite the same. Modern civilization, Ginger, appears to be determined to drown itself in its own racket and call it “sport.”

I found out that the English team were the ones wearing white shirts, and the ones in red were the Welsh. I said to the man next to me, after he had finished yelling himself black in the face, “Could you kindly inform me which is the English scrum-half?”

And just at that moment the players came quite near where I was, and about a dozen assassins in red hurled themselves violently on top of a meek-looking little fellow who had just fallen on the ball.

Ginger, you are well out of it!

That was the scrum-half, and I gathered that that sort of thing was a mere commonplace in his existence. “Stopping a rush,” it is called, and he is expected to do it all the time. The idea of you ever going in for such brutal sports! You thank your stars you are safe on your little stool in Fillmore’s outer office and that if anybody jumps on top of you now you can call a cop.

Do you mean to say you really used to do these daredevil feats? You must have hidden depths in you which I have never suspected.

As I was taking a ride down Piccadilly the other day on top of a bus, I saw somebody walking along who seemed familiar. It was Mr. Carmyle. So he’s back in England again. He didn’t see me, thank goodness. I don’t want to meet anybody just at present who reminds me of New York.

Thanks for telling me all the news, but please don’t do it again. It makes me remember, and I don’t want to. It’s this way, Ginger: let me write to you, because it really does relieve me, but don’t answer my letters. Do you mind? I’m sure you’ll understand.

So Fillmore and Gladys Winch are married! From what I have seen of her, it’s the best thing that has ever happened to Brother F. She is a splendid girl.

I must write to him…

Laurette et Cie., London.  
March 12th.

Dear Ginger,—

I saw in a Sunday paper last week that “The Primrose Way” had been produced in New York and was a great success. Well, I’m very glad.

But I don’t think the papers ought to print things like that. It’s unsettling. It’s like smelling somebody else’s kitchen when you’re on a diet: your feet start walking without consulting your pride.

Next day I did one of those funny things you do when you’re feeling blue and lonely and a long way away from everybody. I called at your club and asked for you.

Such a nice old man in uniform at the desk said in a fatherly way that you hadn’t been in lately and he rather fancied you were out of town, but would I take a seat while he inquired. He then summoned a tiny boy, also in uniform, and the child skipped off chanting, “Mister Kemp! Mister Kemp!” in a shrill treble.

It gave me such an odd feeling to hear your name echoing in the distance, as if it were a word in a dialect I used to speak fluently and had forgotten the moment I crossed the ocean. I felt ashamed for giving them all that trouble; and when the boy came back I slipped twopence into his palm, which I suppose was against all rules, though he seemed to like it in the practical way youth always likes money.

Mr. Faucitt has sold the business and retired to the country, and I am rather at a loose end.

Monk’s Crofton (whatever that means),  
Much Middleford, Salop (slang for Shropshire),  
England.  
April 18th.

Dear Ginger,—

What’s the use? What is the use?

I do all I can to get right away from New York, and New York comes after me and tracks me down in my hiding-place like a determined waiter with a tray.

A week or so ago, as I was walking down the Strand in an aimless sort of way, out there came right on top of me—who do you think?

Fillmore, arm in arm with Mr. Carmyle.

I couldn’t dodge. In the first place, Mr. Carmyle had seen me; in the second place, it is a day’s journey to dodge poor dear Fillmore now.

I blushed for him. Ginger! Right there in the Strand I blushed for him. In my worst dreams I had never pictured him so enormous. Upon what meat doth this our Fillmore feed that he is grown so great? Poor Gladys! When she looks at him she must feel like a bigamist.

Apparently Fillmore is still full of big schemes, for he talked airily about buying all sorts of English plays. He has come over, as I suppose you know, to arrange about putting on “The Primrose Way” over here.

He is staying at the Savoy, and they took me off there to lunch, whooping joyfully as over a strayed lamb.

It was the worst thing that could possibly have happened to me.

Fillmore talked Broadway without a pause, till by the time he had worked his way past the French pastry and was lolling back, breathing a little stertorously, waiting for the coffee and liqueurs, he had got me so homesick that, if it hadn’t been that I didn’t want to make a public exhibition of myself, I should have broken down and howled.

The Savoy, I find, is simply an annex to Broadway, only with more napkins. There were Americans at every table as far as the eye could reach, and the dining-room clinked and hummed with that bright, hard, confident sound of voices which seems to have been invented to keep quieter peoples from thinking. Silver rang like small bells. Plates whispered. You could hear laughter ricocheting off the walls as if the place were lined with mirrors and everybody was determined to admire themselves aloud.

I might just as well have been at the Astor.

Well, if Fate insists on bringing New York to England for my special discomfiture, I suppose I’ve got to put up with it. I just let events take their course, and I have been drifting ever since.

Two days ago I drifted here.

Mr. Carmyle invited Fillmore—he seems to love Fillmore—and me to Monk’s Crofton, and I hadn’t even the shadow of an excuse for refusing. So I came, and I am now sitting writing to you in an enormous bedroom with an open fire and armchairs and every other sort of luxury.

Fillmore is out golfing.

He sails for New York on Saturday on the *Mauretania*.

I am horrified to hear from him that, in addition to all his other big schemes, he is now promoting a fight for the lightweight championship in Jersey City, and guaranteeing enormous sums to both boxers. It’s no good arguing with him. If you do, he simply quotes figures to show the fortunes other people have made out of these things. Besides, it’s too late now anyway. As far as I can make out, the fight is going to take place in another week or two.

All the same, it makes my flesh creep. There are some sounds one ought never to buy: the smack of glove on flesh, the roar that rises when somebody falls, the animal encouragement of a crowd. The world already has enough noise without paying extra for that kind.

Well, it’s no use worrying, I suppose. Let’s change the subject.

Do you know Monk’s Crofton? Probably you don’t, as I seem to remember hearing something said about it being a recent purchase. Mr. Carmyle bought it from some lord or other who had been losing money on the Stock Exchange. I hope you haven’t seen it, anyway, because I want to describe it at great length. I want to pour out my soul about it.

Ginger, what has England ever done to deserve such paradises? I thought, in my ignorance, that Mr. Faucitt’s Cissister place was pretty good, but it doesn’t even begin. It can’t compete. Of course his is just an ordinary country house, and this is a Seat.

Monk’s Crofton is the sort of place they used to write about in the English novels. You know: “The sunset was falling on the walls of G---- Castle, in B----shire, hard by the picturesque village of H----, and not a stone’s throw from the hamlet of J----.” I can imagine Tennyson’s Maud living here, eating little pale sandwiches and breaking hearts in iambics.

You motor up from the station, and after you have gone about three miles you turn in at a big iron gate with stone posts on each side with stone beasts on them. Close by the gate is the cutest little house with an old man inside it who pops out and touches his hat. This is only the lodge, really, but you think you have arrived; so you get all ready to jump out, and then the car goes rolling on for another fifty miles or so through beech woods full of rabbits and open meadows with deer in them.

Finally, just as you think you are going on for ever, you whizz round a corner, and there’s the house. You don’t get a glimpse of it till then, because the trees are too thick. It’s very large, and sort of low and square, with a kind of tower at one side and the most fascinating upper porch sort of thing with battlements. I suppose in the old days you used to stand on this and drop molten lead on visitors’ heads. Wonderful lawns all round, and shrubberies, and a lake that you can just see where the ground dips beyond the fields.

Of course it’s too early yet for them to be out, but to the left of the house there’s a place where there will be about a million roses when June comes round, and all along the side of the rose-garden is a high wall of old red brick which shuts off the kitchen garden.

I went exploring there this morning.

It’s an enormous place, with hot-houses and things, and the air inside is warm and damp and smells of leaf-mould and tomato vines, like the inside of a good old soup pot. You can almost hear the plants drinking. Somewhere a tap drips with the solemnity of a metronome, and every drip seems to say, “Grow, grow.” There are beds laid out as tidily as a cook’s mise en place—rows of green that promise flavors before you’ve even tasted them. At one end there’s a cunning little farm, with a stable yard full of puppies that just tear the heart out of you, they’re so sweet, and a big sleepy cat which sits and blinks in the sun and lets the puppies run all over her as if she were a piece of furniture.

And there’s a lovely stillness. Not the dead silence of a shut room, but a living hush, quilted with small, rightful sounds: thrushes and blackbirds, a distant cart, a door closing somewhere with a polite thud. You can hear everything growing.

Oh, Ginger, it’s heavenly!

But there’s a catch. It’s a case of “Where every prospect pleases and only man is vile.” At least, not exactly vile, I suppose, but terribly stodgy.

I can see now why you couldn’t hit it off with the Family.

Because I’ve seen ’em all. They’re here. Yes, Uncle Donald and all of them.

Is it a habit of your family to collect in gangs, or have I just happened to stumble into an accidental Old Home Week? When I came down to dinner the first evening, the drawing-room was full to bursting point—not simply because Fillmore was there, but because there were uncles and aunts all over the place.

I felt like a small lion in a den of Daniels.

I know exactly now what you mean about the Family. They look at you! It isn’t even a straightforward look. It’s a look with footnotes and an index. It weighs you, labels you, and files you away under a heading you never asked for. Of course, it’s all right for me, because I am snowy white clear through, but I can just imagine what it must have been like for you with your permanently guilty conscience. You must have had an awful time.

By the way, it’s going to be a delicate business getting this letter through to you—rather like carrying the despatches through the enemy’s lines in a Civil War play. You’re supposed to leave letters on the table in the hall, and someone collects them in the afternoon and takes them down to the village on a bicycle. But if I do that, some aunt or uncle is bound to see it, and I shall be an object of loathing, for it is no light matter, my lad, to be caught having correspondence with a human Jimpson weed like you.

It would blast me socially.

At least, so I gather from the way they behaved when your name came up at dinner last night. Somebody mentioned you, and the most awful roasting party broke loose, Uncle Donald acting as cheer-leader. I said feebly that I had met you and had found you part human, and there was an awful silence till they all started at the same time to show me where I was wrong and how cruelly my girlish inexperience had deceived me.

A young and innocent half-portion like me, it appears, is absolutely incapable of suspecting the true infamy of the dregs of society. You aren’t fit to speak to the likes of me, being at the kindest estimate little more than a blot on the human race.

I tell you this in case you may imagine you’re popular with the Family. You’re not.

So I shall have to exercise a good deal of snaky craft in smuggling this letter through. I’ll take it down to the village myself if I can sneak away. But it’s going to be pretty difficult, because for some reason I seem to be a centre of attraction. Except when I take refuge in my room, hardly a moment passes without an aunt or an uncle popping out and having a cosy talk with me.

It sometimes seems as though they were weighing me in the balance.

Well, let ’em weigh.

Time to dress for dinner now. Good-bye.

Yours in the balance,  
Sally.

P.S.—You were perfectly right about your Uncle Donald’s moustache, but I don’t agree with you that it is more his misfortune than his fault. I think he does it on purpose.

(Just for the moment)  
Monk’s Crofton, Much Middleford, Salop, England.  
April 20th.

Dear Ginger,—

Leaving here to-day.

In disgrace.

Hard, cold looks from the family. Strained silences. Uncle Donald far from chummy.

You can guess what has happened. I might have seen it coming. I can see now that it was in the air all along, like the smell of something burning when everybody insists nothing is wrong.

Fillmore knows nothing about it. He left just before it happened.

I shall see him very soon, for I have decided to come back and stop running away from things any longer. It’s cowardly to skulk about over here. Besides, I’m feeling so much better that I believe I can face the ghosts. Anyway, I’m going to try.

See you almost as soon as you get this. I shall mail this in London, and I suppose it will come over by the same boat as me.

It’s hardly worth writing, really, of course, but I have sneaked up to my room to wait till the motor arrives to take me to the station, and it’s something to do.

I can hear muffled voices. The Family talking me over, probably. Saying they never really liked me all along.

Oh, well!

Yours moving in an orderly manner to the exit,  
Sally.

Sally sat in her New York apartment that first morning back with the taut, briny courage of a person who has stood too long on the edge of something cold and honest and, at last, jumped.

She hurt. She had known she would. For months she had been taking her life the way some people take their coffee: weak, lukewarm, and with a pious little lie that it was “fine.” Now the truth was scalding. It stung. It also woke you up.

Her trunks were unpacked. Her dresses hung in their places. The room had that freshly-tidied smell of aired linen and the faint, defeated ghost of ship’s luggage—a blend of leather, dust, and the sort of soap that pretends it is a flower. The next step, clearly, was Ginger.

She wanted to see him with a hunger that was not exactly romantic but very human: the hunger for one steady, friendly face in a city whose noises always behaved like they’d been paid by the clang. At the dock she’d had a moment—just a moment—when the customs shed swallowed sound and threw it back at her in hard echoes, so that even her thoughts seemed stamped in tin. She had felt terribly alone there, as if the city were an enormous factory and she was a loose screw in its palm.

She glanced at her watch and discovered it was early enough to catch Ginger at the office and, with the simple tyranny of appetite, make him take her to lunch. She wanted an ordinary table, ordinary bread, ordinary talking. She wanted the soft domestic percussion of knife on plate, not the iron snarl of New York.

So she put on her hat and went out.

The outer office of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises, Ltd., had been altered by the restless hand of change, which in New York works overtime and never takes Sundays. The old office-boy had gone, and in his place sat an entirely new specimen—pimpled, yes, but pimpled with an originality all its own. This one had the air of a young magistrate about to sentence someone for breathing wrong.

He examined Sally as if she were an invoice that might be fraudulent, then asked, in a tone that implied she had already confessed to something, what she wanted.

“I want Mr. Kemp,” said Sally.

The boy, who had been scratching his cheek with a ruler in the manner of a philosopher, made a little note of this on the inside of his suspicion.

“What name?” he asked, coldly.

“Nicholas,” said Sally. “I’m Mr. Nicholas’ sister.”

On a previous occasion those words had produced a small catastrophe. Today they acted like a bullet.

He started, opened his mouth, and dropped the ruler.

In the act of stooping to retrieve it, he performed the rapid moral gymnastics of the guilty. When he rose again, he had rearranged his features into a smile so dazzlingly deferential it was almost indecent. It was like the sun coming out suddenly over a London fog, and you suspecting it has an ulterior motive.

“Will you take a seat, lady?” he said, and even dusted a chair with his sleeve as if it were a throne and she the rightful occupant. “Fine morning, ain’t it?”

“Thank you,” said Sally. “Will you tell him I’m here?”

“Mr. Nicholas is out, miss,” said the boy with a sorrowful gentility, like a butler in a play. “He’s back in New York, but he’s gone out.”

“I don’t want Mr. Nicholas,” said Sally. “I want Mr. Kemp.”

The office-boy’s smile remained, but it thinned at the edges.

“Mr. Kemp?”

“Yes. Mr. Kemp.”

His face became a landscape of apologetic emptiness.

“Don’t know of anybody of that name around here,” he said. “Does he look like anything? I mean—what’s he look like?”

“He has very red hair.”

“Never seen him in here,” said the office-boy.

The truth came down on Sally with that peculiar clean cruelty truths have when you’ve been hoping for a miracle. Ginger had done it again. Left alone, he had somehow managed—through no malice, only the pure genius of misdirected effort—to smash his own prospects. And if Fillmore had let him go, it must have been a smash that even Fillmore couldn’t ignore.

Sally heard her own voice go dry.

“Where is Mr. Nicholas?” she asked. “Did you say he was out?”

“Really out, miss,” said the boy with engaging candour. “Went off to White Plains in his automobile half an hour ago.”

“White Plains? What for?”

Now that he was no longer actively terrified, he warmed to conversation. It was plain he would ordinarily have preferred solitude for practising whatever it was he practised—he had a general air of someone who believed office supplies were meant to be thrown in graceful arcs—but Sally’s presence had turned him into a delighted gossip.

“I guess he’s gone up to take a look at Bugs Butler,” he said.

“Whose butler?” said Sally, bewildered.

The boy’s expression became kindly, the way men look when they think women have said something charmingly foolish.

“Bugs Butler is training up at White Plains, miss.”

“Who is Bugs Butler?”

A spark returned to the boy’s eyes. This was religion.

“Ah!” he said, and his tone became that of a prophet addressing a nation of idiots. “Who is he! That’s what they’re all saying, all the wise guys. Who has Bugs Butler ever licked? A lot of stiffs out on the coast. Ginks nobody’s ever heard of, except Cyclone Mullins, and it took that false alarm fifteen rounds to get a referee’s decision over him. The boss would go and give him a chance against the champ, but I could’ve told him the legitimate contender was K-leg Binns. K-leg put Cyclone Mullins out in the fifth.” He drew breath, aggrieved at the general stupidity of mankind. “Well, if anybody thinks Bugs Butler can last six rounds with Lew Lucas, I got two bucks right here in my vest pocket says it ain’t so.”

Sally began to see what this meant. White Plains was not business, it was boxing.

“Oh, Bugs—Mr. Butler is one of the boxers in this fight my brother’s interested in?”

“That’s right. He’s going up against the lightweight champ. Lew Lucas is the lightweight champ. He’s a bird!”

“Yes?” said Sally, because the boy had cocked his head in a way that demanded participation.

“Yes, sir!” he cried, and then added the most crushing proof of all: “He used to live on the next street to me. I seen his old mother as close as I am to you. Seen her a hundred times. Is any stiff of a Bugs Butler going to lick a fellow like that?”

“It doesn’t seem likely,” said Sally.

“You spoke it!” said the boy, slapping unsuccessfully at a fly on the blotting-paper.

There was a pause. Sally rose.

“And there’s another thing,” he said, unwilling to let such greatness end. “Can Bugs Butler make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?”

“It sounds awfully difficult.”

“They say he’s clever,” scoffed the boy. “Well, what’s that going to get him? The poor fish can’t punch a hole in a nut-sundae.”

“You don’t seem to like Mr. Butler.”

“Oh, I got nothing against him,” said the boy magnanimously. “I’m only saying he’s no licence to be mixing it with Lew Lucas.”

Sally, whose mind was now on Ginger with a sharpness that made everything else seem far away, asked how to find White Plains.

“Oh, anybody’ll show you the way to the training-camp,” said the office-boy. “If you hurry, there’s a train you can make now.”

She thanked him and went out. Behind her, he opened the door with a certain rusty old-world gallantry and then, as she glanced back, she saw him pick up a pair of paper-weights and place the ruler carefully on his chin, practising for whatever vaudeville future awaited him.

*

At White Plains, the training-camp was a large room that couldn’t decide whether it was a barn or a gymnasium, so it compromised by being both. There were ropes set up in the centre like a little fenced pasture, and athletic contraptions hung on the walls in the manner of trophies from a war against comfort.

A crowd had gathered—about forty people with the air of those who had paid fifty cents and intended to get their money’s worth by staring. Modern life, with its talent for commercializing anything that once belonged to privacy, had converted even a fighter’s preparation into a matinée.

Some were newspaper men on the free list—writers who would take whatever Bugs Butler actually said and translate it into sentences like, “I am in really superb condition and feel little apprehension of the issue,” and artists who would draw him semi-nude with feet like small boats.

Fillmore Nicholas, trapped among them, had been buttonholed for a quarter of an hour by Mr. Lester Burrowes, the manager of Bugs Butler—one of those men who talk with their whole bodies, clinging with their eyes and hands. When, at last, Burrowes was distracted by something requiring personal fidgeting, Fillmore let out a sigh so deep it seemed to come from his shoes and began to edge toward the door.

He was desperately hungry. In his enthusiasm to inspect Bugs Butler on the final day before the fight—tomorrow—he had missed lunch. The world now consisted chiefly of the idea of food.

He was sliding away, thinking of steak and potatoes and silence, when a soft voice spoke near his ear.

“Hallo, Fillmore!”

Fillmore left the floor.

He did it neatly—an upward twitch of pure alarm that lifted him a clear three inches, as if his nervous system had been prodded with a cattle stick. He turned, staring, licking his lips with the tip of his tongue, trying to take in a fact which had arrived without warning.

Sally.

If ever a man had a right to behave like an astonished goat, Fillmore had it. A week ago she had been in Shropshire at Monk’s Crofton. She had given no hint of tearing up her English chapter and beginning a new one on American soil, and certainly not in a boxing barn at White Plains, where noises were the thud of gloves, the squeak of shoes, and the incessant chatter of men who thought volume was evidence.

As his brain caught up with his eyes, apprehension slid coldly into him. He had considered Monk’s Crofton to be the prelude to a very sensible engagement. Nowadays people did not ask brothers for permission; they simply invited the girl and her brother down to the country, assembled the family like a jury, and proceeded. Fillmore had taken it for granted that Bruce Carmyle was in love with Sally, and had been mentally blessing the union with an affectionate eye on the Carmyle bank-balance.

Now Sally was here.

And if the invitation had meant only one thing, so did this.

“Sally,” croaked Fillmore, for his voice was busy being elsewhere. “What… what…?”

“Did I startle you? I’m sorry.”

“What are you doing here? Why aren’t you at Monk’s Crofton?”

Sally glanced past him at the ring and the little ring of expectant faces.

“I decided I wanted to get back to America. Circumstances arose which made it pleasanter to leave Monk’s Crofton.”

“Do you mean to say…?”

“Yes. Don’t let’s talk about it.”

“Do you mean to say,” Fillmore persisted, because brothers are built that way, “that Carmyle proposed to you and you turned him down?”

Sally flushed.

“I don’t think it’s particularly nice to talk about that sort of thing,” she said, “but—yes.”

Fillmore’s face took on the expression of a man watching someone deliberately drop a valuable object into a river. He was filled with that bleak certainty, so trying to the spirit, that other people are capable of spectacular bone-headedness even when their happiness is neatly arranged and labeled.

“But why?”

“Oh, Fill!” said Sally, and the sound of his dismay rasped her like sandpaper. “I should have thought the reason was obvious.”

“You mean you don’t like him?”

“I don’t know whether I do or not,” said Sally. “I certainly don’t like him enough to marry him.”

“He’s a darned good fellow.”

“Is he?” said Sally. “You say so. I don’t know.”

At this point Fillmore’s hunger, which had been waiting politely in the wings, came striding onstage.

“Let’s go to the hotel and talk it over,” he said. “We’ll go to the hotel and I’ll give you something to eat.”

“I don’t want anything to eat, thanks.”

“You don’t want anything to eat?” said Fillmore, staring as if she had announced she did not believe in oxygen. “I’m starving.”

“Well, run along then.”

“Yes, but I want to talk—”

He did not get farther, because Mr. Lester Burrowes returned at a trot, wearing a checked tweed suit that seemed designed to keep a man perpetually in motion, and a brown bowler hat worn with a courage that defied all taste.

“Say, Mr. Nicholas,” cried Burrowes, grabbing him as if Fillmore were about to be swept out to sea, “you ain’t going? Bugs is just getting ready to spar.”

He glanced at Sally.

“My sister—Mr. Burrowes,” said Fillmore faintly. “Mr. Burrowes is Bugs Butler’s manager.”

“How do you do?” said Sally.

“Pleased to meecher,” said Burrowes, and at once went on, because he was built without brakes. “Say—”

“I was just going to the hotel to get something to eat,” said Fillmore, trying to pull free.

Mr. Burrowes clutched a coat-button with the accuracy of a hawk.

“Yes, but before-you-go-lemme-tell-ya-somef’n. You never seen this boy o’ mine when he’s feeling right. Believe me, he’s there! He’s a wizard. He’s a Hindoo! He’s been practising up a left shift that—”

Fillmore looked at Sally with the wild appeal of a prisoner who sees a passing friend. Sally, though she was storing up questions for him about Ginger that would later require blood, pitied him in the moment.

And then, remembering the office-boy’s sermon, she said, sweetly and severely:

“Can he make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?”

The effect on Mr. Burrowes was astonishing. He released Fillmore’s coat-button and fell back as if struck by a moral brick. His mouth opened. His eyes bulged in reverent surprise, the way an eminent professor looks when a kitten begins quoting Greek.

“Whazzat?” he murmured.

“It took him fifteen rounds to get a referee’s decision over Cyclone Mullins,” said Sally, warming to it, “and K-leg Binns—”

“You ain’t got it right,” protested Burrowes at once, recovering like a man whose honor has been tapped on the chin. “Say, you mustn’t believe what you see in the papers. The referee was dead against us, and Cyclone was down once for all of half a minute and they wouldn’t count him out. Gee! You gotta kill a guy in some towns before they’ll give you a decision. At that, they couldn’t do nothing so raw as make it anything but a win for my boy, after him leading by a mile all the way.” He peered at her with fresh respect. “Have you ever seen Bugs, ma’am?”

Sally admitted she had not.

Mr. Burrowes plunged into his breast pocket and produced a picture-postcard, which he thrust into her hand with the air of presenting a sacred icon.

“That’s Bugs,” he said. “Take a slant at that and tell me if he don’t look the goods.”

The photograph showed a young man in the minimum of clothing, crouched in a tortured attitude, like someone trying to remember whether he had turned off the stove.

“I’ll call him over and have him sign it for you,” said Burrowes, already shouting. “Here, Bugs—wantcher.”

A youth in a bath-robe turned from a group by the ring and started toward them with the languid swagger of someone to whom applause is a birthright. Then he saw Sally and quickened, because he was an admirer of the sex.

“Bugs,” said Mr. Burrowes, “this is Miss Nicholas, come to see you work out. I been telling her she’s going to have a treat.” And to Sally, with a flourish: “Shake hands with Bugs Butler, ma’am, the coming lightweight champion of the world.”

In the flesh, Sally thought, Bugs Butler’s photograph had been exceedingly charitable.

He had a mouth that curved meanly, the sort of mouth that would enjoy bad news delivered to strangers. His eyes had a cold arrogance, and there was something in his whole manner—dangerous, slick, unpleasant—that made Sally’s skin object to him before her mind had finished being polite. Worst of all, he smirked at her, not as a man smiles at a woman, but as an owner smiles at a thing.

Still, she made herself say the correct words.

“I hope you are going to win, Mr. Butler.”

The smile she managed convinced Mr. Butler that he had, as usual, made an impression. His chest swelled beneath the bath-robe, and he peered at her with a conquering look.

“You betcher,” he said.

Mr. Burrowes glanced at his watch.

“Time you were starting, Bugs.”

Mr. Butler’s eyes moved reluctantly from Sally to the audience, and he looked bored. The crowd was smaller than he liked, and too many were newspaper men who hadn’t paid.

“All right,” he said.

Then he looked back at Sally, cheered by the thought that at least one pair of bright eyes would watch him.

“I’ll go a couple of rounds with Reddy for a starter,” he said. “Seen him anywheres? He’s never around when he’s wanted.”

“I’ll fetch him,” said Mr. Burrowes, and hurried away.

“I’m going to show that guy up this afternoon,” said Mr. Butler, with a casual cruelty. “He’s been getting too fresh.”

He ducked under the ropes with a final smirk, and the crowd rustled as if someone had turned up the volume on expectation.

Presently Mr. Burrowes returned, shepherding a sturdily built young man whose face was hidden by the sweater he was pulling over his head. There was something familiar in the shape of his shoulders—something decent and solid.

A last tug, and the sweater came off.

Red hair flashed out, tousled and disordered.

Sally gasped. It escaped her before she could stop it, and several heads turned.

The red-headed young man, who had been stooping to pick up his gloves, straightened sharply and stared at her as if she were a hallucination. Color crept up his face, slowly, steadily, until he matched his hair.

It was Ginger Kemp.

Mr. Burrowes, practical and impatient, broke whatever spell had fallen.

“Come on, come on,” he snapped. “Li’l speed there, Reddy.”

Ginger started like a sleep-walker brought up short. Then, as if his body remembered what it was supposed to do, he began slowly pulling on his gloves. Embarrassment was stamped all over him—on his mouth, on the set of his shoulders, on the way he could not, for the life of him, make his eyes meet Sally’s.

His face was a shade of crimson that suggested some hidden kitchen calamity: a pot boiled over and nobody dared admit it.

Sally caught Mr. Burrowes’ elbow and whispered, “Who—him?”

Burrowes glanced at her, irritated at being delayed, then softened because she was Miss Nicholas and also because she had shown alarming knowledge of weights and decisions.

“Who, him?” he whispered back. “He’s just one o’ Bugs’ sparring-partners.”

“But—” began Sally, because her mind was suddenly full of all the months Ginger had been “at the office,” all the letters, all the spaces.

“You’ll excuse me, miss,” said Burrowes briskly, “but I gotta hold the watch. We mustn’t waste any time.”

Sally drew back, feeling abruptly like an intruder on some solemn male rite. This was Man’s hour: ropes, sweat, sharp voices, and the steady, punishing rhythm of bodies moving in a box of air. She felt very small and very much in the way—like a puppy that has wandered into church and can’t understand why everyone is glaring.

She stared at Ginger.

He stood in the opposite corner of the ring, feet shuffling, gaze averted, fiddling with his gloves as if they were the most interesting objects in the world and not, as they were, padded instruments meant for hitting and being hit. He seemed unreachable, shut behind a wall of self-consciousness so complete it might as well have been another country.

And still Sally stared, wide-eyed, while Ginger, crimson to the roots of his hair, kept plucking at the gloves and would not look at her at all.

The only real happiness, the uplift experts tell us, is the kind you manufacture in other people and then hand round like hot rolls. By that rule, Bugs Butler, thirty hours after the Jersey City ring had become his temporary bedroom, ought to have awakened in a glow of saintly contentment. After all, his failure to stop a short left-hook, followed by a right hand deposited with neat precision on the point of his jaw, had improved the day of a considerable slice of humanity.

Unfortunately, Bugs was not a man who could be cheered by the knowledge that strangers were laughing and counting money because he had gone to sleep in public.

Mr. Lew Lucas, for one, was shining with satisfaction. So were Lucas’s seconds, one of whom, carried away by the poetry of pugilism, kissed him. Most of the crowd, having backed the champion with that touching faith which only ever attends other people’s risks, were delighted. Even the general air of Jersey City seemed brighter, as if the very traffic had started to purr instead of honk.

Bugs slouched from the ring wearing a frown large enough to shade an orchard.

And still the happiness went on spreading, like the smell of coffee in a good kitchen: unstoppable, everywhere at once, and reaching people who hadn’t even been present when the percolator began to sing.

The two Wise Guys, unable to attend, got the news off the ticker and proclaimed themselves five hundred dollars richer. A pimpled office-boy at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises, Ltd., gave a whoop in the Subway when he read his morning paper, for he too had profited by the brittleness of Mr. Butler’s chin. And in a small New York apartment, Sally—breakfasting with the deliberate pleasure of a person who thinks food should be listened to as well as eaten—read the sporting page with fierce satisfaction.

Her coffee, strong enough to stand up and argue, made a small, contented sound as she stirred it: a domestic whisper against the larger city roar outside, and Sally’s mind, always sensitive to the world’s noises, took comfort in that little private rhythm. Down in the street, a delivery truck banged a tailgate like a gong. Somewhere a radio bleated an advertisement at the morning. The contrast only made her apartment feel more her own—quiet, human, and properly scented with toast.

She wasn’t a girl who disliked many people, but she had acquired a brisk distaste for Bugs Butler, and the report of his downfall tasted as pleasant as marmalade.

Lew Lucas, she decided, was a man after her own heart. If he’d been Ginger’s personal friend, he couldn’t, given the brief time available, have avenged him more thoroughly. In the first round he had done a number of diverting, artistic things to Bugs’s left eye. In the second he had continued the good work lower down, where it discourages a man’s vanity most. In the third he had put him to sleep.

Could anyone have done more?

Sally thought not. She lifted her cup—warm porcelain against her fingers, steam carrying that bitter, toasted perfume—and drank Lew Lucas’s health. She hoped his old mother was proud of him. Also, incidentally, she hoped his old mother was somewhere peaceful, with birds outside the window instead of motor horns, because if there was one modern plague Sally loathed it was the city’s habit of making noise in place of conversation.

The telephone bell rang at her elbow, sharp and metallic, like a spoon dropped on tile. She unhooked the receiver.

“Hullo?”

“Oh, hullo,” said a voice.

“Ginger!” cried Sally.

“I say, I’m awfully glad you’re back. I only got your letter this morning. Found it at the boarding-house. I happened to look in there and—”

“Ginger,” interrupted Sally, “your voice is music, but I want to see you. Where are you?”

“I’m at a chemist’s shop across the street. I was wondering if—”

“Come here at once!”

“I say, may I? I was just going to ask.”

“You miserable creature, why haven’t you been round to see me before?”

“Well, as a matter of fact, I haven’t been going about much for the last day. You see—”

“I know. Of course.” Her voice softened at once. She glanced, approvingly and gratefully, at the large photograph of Lew Lucas grinning up from the paper. “You poor thing! How are you?”

“Oh, all right, thanks.”

“Well, hurry.”

There was a pause at the other end.

“I say.”

“Well?”

“I’m not much to look at, you know.”

“You never were. Stop talking and hurry over.”

“I mean to say—”

Sally hung up firmly. If Ginger was going to begin apologizing for existing, it was better to cut him off at the source.

She waited, restless in the way one is restless when the kettle has started to murmur and you know in a moment it will boil. Then footsteps came along the passage. They stopped. The bell rang.

Sally ran to the door, flung it open—and recoiled.

“Oh, Ginger!”

He had been painfully accurate about his appearance. His right eye was clear and devoted; his left had vanished altogether beneath a puffy swelling of dull purple, like a plum that had met with harsh criticism. A bruise disfigured his cheek-bone, and his lips were thick enough to make speech seem like work.

“It’s all right, you know,” he assured her, heroically.

“It isn’t. It’s awful! Oh, you poor darling!” Sally’s hands clenched, and she looked at him as if her gaze could turn into a weapon. “I wish he had killed him!”

“Eh?”

“I wish Lew Lucas—or whatever his name is—had murdered him. Brute!”

“Oh, I don’t know, you know.” Ginger, even with half his face rearranged, could not resist fairness. “He isn’t a bad sort of chap, really. Bugs Butler, I mean.”

“Do you seriously mean to stand there and tell me you don’t loathe the creature?”

“Oh, he’s all right. See his point of view and all that. Can’t blame him, if you come to think of it, for getting the wind up a bit in the circs. Bit thick, I mean to say, a sparring-partner going at him like that. Naturally he didn’t think it much of a wheeze. It was my fault right along. Oughtn’t to have done it, of course, but somehow, when he started making an ass of me and I knew you were looking on—well, it seemed a good idea to have a dash at doing something on my own. No right to, of course. A sparring-partner isn’t supposed—”

“Sit down,” said Sally.

Ginger sat down carefully, like a man making peace with furniture.

“Ginger,” said Sally, “you’re too good to live.”

“Oh, I say!”

“I believe if someone sandbagged you and stole your watch and chain you’d say there were faults on both sides or something. I’m just a cat, and I say I wish your beast of a Bugs Butler had perished miserably. I’d have gone and danced on his grave.” She paused, then added, practical even in fury: “But whatever made you go in for that sort of thing?”

“Well, it seemed the only job that was going at the moment. I’ve always done a goodish bit of boxing and I was very fit and so on, and it looked to me rather an opening. Gave me something to get along with. You get paid quite fairly decently, you know, and it’s rather a jolly life…”

“Jolly? Being hammered about like that?”

“Oh, you don’t notice it much. I’ve always enjoyed scrapping rather. And, you see, when your brother gave me the push—”

Sally made a sound like a plate chipped unexpectedly.

“What an extraordinary thing it is—I went all the way out to White Plains that afternoon to find Fillmore and tackle him about that and I didn’t say a word about it. And I haven’t seen or been able to get hold of him since.”

“No? Busy sort of cove, your brother.”

“Why did Fillmore let you go?”

“Let me go? Oh, you mean—well, there was a sort of mix-up. A kind of misunderstanding.”

“What happened?”

“Oh, it was nothing. Just a—”

“What happened?” Sally’s voice had the crisp edge of a cook with a knife and no patience.

Ginger looked about the room with the guilty helplessness of a man asked to explain a stain.

“It’s not worth talking about.”

“It is worth talking about. I’ve a right to know. It was I who sent you to Fillmore—”

“Now that,” said Ginger, “was jolly decent of you.”

“Don’t interrupt! I sent you to Fillmore, and he had no business to let you go without saying a word to me. What happened?”

Ginger twiddled his fingers as if trying to wind himself out of the conversation.

“Well, it was rather unfortunate. You see, his wife—I don’t know if you know her?…”

“Of course I know her.”

“Why, yes, you would, wouldn’t you? Your brother’s wife, I mean,” said Ginger, brightening briefly with logic. “Though, as a matter of fact, you often find sisters-in-law who won’t have anything to do with one another. I know a fellow—”

“Ginger,” said Sally, “it’s no good your thinking you can get out of telling me by rambling off on other subjects. I’m grim and resolute and relentless, and I mean to get this story out of you if I have to use a corkscrew. Fillmore’s wife, you were saying…”

Ginger returned unwillingly to the pit.

“Well, she came into the office one morning, and we started fooling about…”

“Fooling about?”

“Well, kind of chivvying each other.”

“Chivvying?”

“At least I was.”

“You were what?”

“Sort of chasing her a bit, you know.”

Sally stared at him.

“What do you mean?”

Ginger’s embarrassment deepened until it seemed determined to compete with his bruising.

“The thing was, you see, she happened to trickle in rather quietly when I happened to be looking at something, and I didn’t know she was there till she suddenly grabbed it…”

“Grabbed what?”

“The thing. The thing I happened to be looking at. She bagged it… collared it… took it away from me, you know, and wouldn’t give it back and generally started to rot about a bit, so I rather began to chivvy her to some extent, and I’d just caught her when your brother happened to roll in. I suppose,” said Ginger, adding up events with that scrupulous justice of his, “he had really come with her to the office and had happened to hang back for a minute or two, to talk to somebody or something… well, of course, he was considerably fed to see me apparently doing jiu-jitsu with his wife. Enough to rattle any man, if you come to think of it.”

Sally, who could think of it perfectly well, waited.

“Well, he didn’t say anything at the time, but a bit later in the day he called me in and administered the push.”

Sally shook her head.

“It sounds the craziest story to me. What was it that Mrs. Fillmore took from you?”

“Oh, just something.”

Sally rapped the table like a gavel.

“Ginger!”

“Well, as a matter of fact,” said the battered martyr, “it was a photograph.”

“Who of? Or, if you’re particular, of whom?”

“Well… you, to be absolutely accurate.”

“Me?” Sally blinked. “But I’ve never given you a photograph of myself.”

Ginger’s face, where it was not purple, became scarlet.

“You didn’t exactly give it to me,” he mumbled. “When I say give, I mean—”

“Good gracious!” Sally saw the whole comedy at once. “That photograph we were hunting for when I first came here! Had you stolen it all the time?”

“Why, yes, I did sort of pinch it…”

“You fraud! You humbug! And you pretended to help me look for it.” She gazed at him with reluctant admiration. “I never knew you were so deep and snaky. I’m discovering all sorts of new things about you.”

Ginger, having made his confession, seemed almost lighter, like a man who has finally admitted he ate the last slice.

“I hope you’re not frightfully sick about it?” he said after a pause. “It was lying about, you know, and I rather felt I must have it. Hadn’t the cheek to ask you for it, so…”

“Don’t apologize,” said Sally warmly. “It’s a compliment. So I have caused your downfall again, have I? I’m certainly your evil genius, Ginger. I’m beginning to feel like a regular rag and a bone and a hank of hair. First I egged you on to insult your family—oh, by the way, I want to thank you about that. Now that I’ve met your Uncle Donald I can see how public-spirited you were. I ruined your prospects there, and now my fatal beauty—cabinet size—has led to your destruction once more. It’s certainly up to me to find you another job.”

“No, really, I say, you mustn’t bother. I shall be all right.”

“It’s my duty. Now what is there that you really can do? Burglary, of course, but it’s not respectable. You’ve tried being a waiter and a prize-fighter and a right-hand man, and none of those seems to be just right. Can’t you suggest anything?”

Ginger shook his head.

“I shall wangle something, I expect.”

“Yes, but what? It must be something good this time. I don’t want to be walking along Broadway and come on you suddenly as a street-cleaner. I don’t want to send for an express-man and find you popping up. My idea would be to go to my bank to arrange an overdraft and be told the president could give me two minutes and crawl in humbly and find you prezzing away to beat the band in a big chair. Isn’t there anything in the world that you can do that’s solid and substantial and will keep you out of the poor-house in your old age? Think!”

“Of course, if I had a bit of capital…”

“Ah!” said Sally. “The business man! And what would you do, Mr. Morgan, if you had a bit of capital?”

“Run a dog-thingummy,” said Ginger promptly.

“What’s a dog-thingummy?”

“Why, a thingamajig. For dogs, you know.”

Sally nodded slowly, as if this clarified everything.

“Oh, a thingamajig for dogs. Now I understand. You do put things so obscurely at first. Ginger, you poor fish, what are you raving about? What on earth is a thingamajig for dogs?”

“I mean a sort of place like fellows have. Breeding dogs, you know, and selling them and winning prizes and all that. There are lots of them about.”

“Oh, kennels?”

“Yes, a kennels.”

“What a weird mind you have. You couldn’t say kennels at first, could you? That wouldn’t have made it difficult enough. I suppose if anyone asked you where you had your lunch, you’d say, ‘Oh, at a thingamajig for mutton chops.’” She leaned forward, suddenly intent. “But Ginger, my lad, there is something in this. I believe for the first time in our acquaintance you have spoken something very nearly resembling a mouthful. You’re wonderful with dogs, aren’t you?”

“I’m dashed keen on them, and I’ve studied them a bit. As a matter of fact, though it seems rather like swanking, there isn’t much about dogs that I don’t know.”

“Of course. I believe you’re a sort of honorary dog yourself. I could tell it by the way you stopped that fight at Roville. You plunged into a howling mass of about a million hounds of all species and just whispered in their ears and they stopped at once.” Sally’s eyes brightened the way they did when a recipe finally made sense. “Why, the more one examines this, the better it looks. I do believe it’s the one thing you couldn’t help making a success of. It’s very paying, isn’t it?”

“Works out at about a hundred per cent on the original outlay, I’ve been told.”

“A hundred per cent? That sounds too much like something of Fillmore’s for comfort. Let’s say ninety-nine and be conservative. Ginger, you have hit it. Say no more. You shall be the Dog King, the biggest thingamajigger for dogs in the country. But how do you start?”

“Well, as a matter of fact, while I was up at White Plains, I ran into a cove who had a place of the sort and wanted to sell out. That was what made me think of it.”

“You must start to-day. Or early to-morrow.”

“Yes,” said Ginger doubtfully. “Of course, there’s the catch, you know.”

“What catch?”

“The capital. You’ve got to have that. This fellow wouldn’t sell out under five thousand dollars.”

“I’ll lend you five thousand dollars.”

“No!” said Ginger, with the startled horror of a man offered a second helping when he believes he doesn’t deserve the first.

Sally stared at him.

“Ginger, I’d like to slap you,” she said calmly, which meant she felt it intensely. It was maddening, this sentimental nonsense barging into business like a street-organ into a quiet room. Why, because he happened to be a man and she happened to be a woman, should she be prevented from putting money into a sound enterprise? If Columbus had taken this attitude with Queen Isabella, America would still be an inconvenience rumoured by fish.

“I can’t take five thousand dollars off you,” said Ginger firmly.

“Who’s talking of taking it off me, as you call it?” Sally snapped. “Can’t you forget your burglarious career for a second? This isn’t the same thing as going about stealing defenceless girls’ photographs. This is business. I think you would make an enormous success of a dog-place, and you admit you’re good, so why make frivolous objections? Why shouldn’t I put money into a good thing? Don’t you want me to get rich, or what is it?”

Ginger became confused, a state in which he always looked like a man trying to remember whether he left the gas on in someone else’s house.

“But it’s such a lot of money.”

“To you, perhaps. Not to me. I’m a plutocrat. Five thousand dollars! What’s five thousand dollars? I feed it to the birds.”

Ginger considered this literally for a moment, as if picturing pigeons pecking at banknotes. He knew only that Sally had once inherited something, and he had been impressed by Fillmore’s general air of wealth. The Nicholas family, it seemed, belonged to that special class of people who could afford not only food but silence—if they wanted it.

“I don’t like it, you know,” he said at last.

“You don’t have to like it,” said Sally. “You just do it.”

A thought, comforting as a bandage, came to him.

“You’d have to let me pay you interest.”

“Let you? My lad, you’ll have to pay me interest. What do you think this is—a round game? It’s a cold business deal.”

“Topping!” said Ginger, visibly relieved. “How about twenty-five per cent.”

“Don’t be silly,” said Sally quickly. “I want three.”

“No, that’s all rot,” protested Ginger. “I mean to say—three. I don’t,” he added, trying to compromise, “mind saying twenty.”

“If you insist, I’ll make it five. Not more.”

“Well, ten, then?”

“Five!”

“Suppose,” said Ginger, coaxing like a man bargaining for a puppy, “I said seven?”

“I never saw anyone like you for haggling,” said Sally. “Listen. Six. And that’s my last word.”

“Six?”

“Six.”

Ginger did sums, lips pursed, as if tasting the number.

“But that would only work out at three hundred dollars a year. It isn’t enough.”

“What do you know about it? As if I hadn’t been handling this sort of deal all my life. Six! Do you agree?”

“I suppose so.”

“Then that’s settled. Is this man you talk about in New York?”

“No, he’s down on Long Island at a place on the south shore.”

“I mean, can you get him on the ’phone and clinch the thing?”

“Oh, yes. I know his address, and I suppose his number’s in the book.”

“Then go off at once and settle with him before somebody else snaps him up. Don’t waste a minute.”

Ginger paused at the door, lingering as if his gratitude had made him heavy.

“I say, you’re absolutely sure about this?”

“Of course.”

“I mean to say—”

“Get on,” said Sally.

When he had gone, the apartment seemed to settle, like a kitchen after a visitor: the air still holding the trace of another person, the quiet returning in layers. Sally carried her newspaper to the window-sill and read the fight report a third time, savoring it as one savors a good sauce—knowing what is coming, loving it anyway. Between paragraphs she let her eyes drift to the street below.

It was a beautiful day, bright and clean in the light, though the city kept trying to ruin it with clatter. A streetcar screeched on its rails with the tortured cry of metal forced into obedience. Somewhere a man shouted an advertisement as if volume could replace meaning. Sally listened, half irritated, half amused; modern life was always so proud of its noise, as if the point of living were to drown out your own thoughts.

She had not been there long when a taxi stopped at the apartment-house. She was surprised to see her brother Fillmore heave himself out. He paid the driver, and the cab moved off, leaving him on the sidewalk with his hat brim cutting a decisive line of shadow.

Sally was just about to call down when Fillmore began to behave in a manner that made her freeze with curiosity.

From where she sat, he looked like a man rehearsing steps for a dance he did not trust. He advanced towards the front door with determination, then stopped and scuttled back. He did it again. Then he stood for a moment as if arguing with an invisible opponent, made a third dash, halted abruptly as though he’d bumped into an unseen wall—and finally wheeled and bustled away down the street, disappearing.

Sally could make nothing of it. If Fillmore had taken the trouble to come in a taxi, obviously intending to call, why had he retreated at the last moment as if the door were a wild animal?

She was still turning this over when the telephone rang again, brisk and officious.

“Sally?” said Fillmore’s voice, husky.

“Hullo, Fill. What are you going to call it?”

“What am I—Call what?”

“The dance you were doing outside here just now. It’s your own invention, isn’t it?”

“Did you see me?” Fillmore demanded, sounding as if he’d been caught reciting poetry.

“Of course I saw you. I was fascinated.”

“I—er—I was coming to have a talk with you. Sally…”

His voice trailed off into a silence full of discomfort.

“Well, why didn’t you?”

A pause. Something was plainly upsetting Fillmore Nicholas, which, in Sally’s experience, generally meant either money or emotion, the two things most likely to make him lose his usual imperial smoothness.

“Sally,” he said at last, and coughed hollowly into the receiver.

“Yes.”

“I—that is to say, I have asked Gladys… Gladys will be coming to see you very shortly. Will you be in?”

“I’ll stay in. How is Gladys? I’m longing to see her again.”

“She is very well. A trifle—a little upset.”

“Upset? What about?”

“She will tell you when she arrives. I have just been ’phoning to her. She is coming at once.” Another pause, heavier. “I’m afraid she has bad news.”

“What news?”

Silence.

“What news?” Sally repeated sharply. She hated mysteries. They were like tins without labels: you never knew whether you were opening peaches or something with tentacles.

But Fillmore had rung off.

Sally hung up and stood a moment, uneasy. Then, since there was no sense in worrying the air thin, she carried her breakfast things to the kitchen and washed up, letting the warm water and the simple, domestic noises steady her—the scrape of plate on plate, the small rush of the tap, a far kinder soundscape than the city’s constant banging.

A ring at the doorbell brought her out. Gladys stood there.

Marriage—even marriage to the Hope of the American Stage—had done nothing to alter the former Miss Winch in any important way. As Mrs. Fillmore Nicholas she remained the same square, friendly creature, solid as a good table. She hugged Sally with muscular enthusiasm and marched into the sitting-room.

“Well, it’s great seeing you again,” she said. “I began to think you were never coming back. What was the big idea, springing over to England like that?”

Sally had expected this and had her answer ready.

“I wanted to help Mr. Faucitt.”

“Who’s Mr. Faucitt?”

“Hasn’t Fillmore ever mentioned him? He was a dear old man at the boarding-house, and his brother died and left him a dressmaking establishment in London. He screamed to me to come and tell him what to do about it. He has sold it now and is quite happy in the country.”

“Well, the trip’s done you good,” said Mrs. Fillmore. “You’re prettier than ever.”

There was a pause. Already Sally had noticed something different in Gladys. The old careless, mischievous lightness was dulled, as if some heavy hand had pressed on it. Gladys’s honest eyes looked troubled.

“What’s the bad news?” Sally asked abruptly. She wanted it out, like a bone stuck in a throat. “Fillmore was telling me over the ’phone that you had some bad news for me.”

Mrs. Fillmore scratched at the carpet with the tip of her parasol, making a faint, rasping sound. When she spoke, it wasn’t an answer.

“Sally, who’s this man Carmyle over in England?”

“Oh, did Fillmore tell you about him?”

“He told me there was a rich fellow over in England who was crazy about you and had asked you to marry him, and that you had turned him down.”

“Yes,” said Sally. “That’s true.”

“You couldn’t write and say you’ve changed your mind?”

Sally’s independence rose up hot and quick.

“I suppose I could if I had—but I haven’t. Did Fillmore tell you to try to talk me round?”

“Oh, I’m not trying to talk you round,” said Mrs. Fillmore hurriedly. “Goodness knows, I’m the last person to try and jolly anyone into marrying anybody if they didn’t feel like it. I’ve seen too many marriages go wrong to do that. Look at Elsa Doland.”

Sally’s heart jumped, as if some tender nerve had been touched.

“Elsa?” she stammered. “Has—has her marriage gone wrong?”

“Gone all to bits,” said Mrs. Fillmore. “You remember she married Gerald Foster, the man who wrote ‘The Primrose Way’?”

Sally managed not to laugh, though the sound tried to claw its way out of her throat like hysteria.

“Yes,” she said. “I remember.”

“Well, it’s all gone bloo-ey. I’ll tell you about that in a minute. Coming back to this man in England, if you’re in any doubt about it… I mean, you can’t always tell right away whether you’re fond of a man or not. When first I met Fillmore, I couldn’t see him with a spy-glass, and now he’s just the whole shooting-match… But that’s not what I wanted to talk about. I was saying one doesn’t always know one’s own mind at first, and if this fellow really is a good fellow… and Fillmore tells me he’s got all the money in the world…”

Sally stopped her.

“No, it’s no good. I don’t want to marry Mr. Carmyle.”

“That’s that, then,” said Mrs. Fillmore. “It’s a pity, though.”

“Why are you taking it so much to heart?” Sally asked, with a nervous little laugh that sounded thin in her own ears.

There is a kind of man—stout in the soul if not always in the waistcoat—who treats a rebuff the way certain cooks treat a collapsed soufflé: as a sign to shut the oven door, lower the voice, and try again with more butter and better timing. Bruce Carmyle had that quality. Sally, at Monk’s Crofton, had turned down his offer of marriage with a decisiveness that ought to have chilled the marrow of any suitor built on delicacy and imagination; but Bruce was not overstocked with either. He had been bred in the comfortable creed that wants were made to be met, like bills and dinner.

So he put the refusal away in his mind the way a meticulous food historian shelves an inconvenient pamphlet in the wrong dialect: not destroyed, not accepted, merely filed for later correction. Whatever faint doubts he had ever had about Sally’s streak of free-spiritedness—her little habits of not doing what was expected, of looking as though life were a lark rather than a ledger—melted clean out of him in the heat of defeat. All he could summon was the brightness of her eyes, the jaunty lift of her chin, the gallant trimness of her; and those recollections snapped at him in the dark like the crackle of a kitchen fire when you’ve banked it too low and it refuses to die.

Quietly, methodically—respectable as a wolf with a timetable—he prepared to follow her to America.

He did his best to do it swiftly and secretly, but secrecy, in an English family, is a condiment that cannot be kept from scenting the whole house. News, in such tribes, travels faster than a tray in the hands of a well-trained parlourmaid and with less explanation than the passing of a folk-song from one generation to the next. By Bruce’s last night in London, the Family had decided on an emissary.

In due course, there entered his apartment in South Audley Street the Family’s chosen representative—the man the Family pointed to with pride, like a prized ham at a county fair: Uncle Donald, in the flesh.

It was a great deal of flesh. Two hundred and forty pounds of it, by any honest grocer’s scale. He lowered it into an armchair with the grave care of a cook settling a heavy casserole onto a precarious trivet, and the chair, which had not been consulted, creaked as if trying to translate protest into sound and finding the dominant lexicon inadequate.

Once at Monk’s Crofton, Sally had spoiled a whole morning for her brother Fillmore by indicating Uncle Donald as the exact image of what he would be when he grew up. Uncle Donald, cherishing from early schooldays the superstition that he had a weak heart, had abstained from every form of exercise for nearly fifty years. He combined this abstinence with one of the three heartiest appetites in the south-western postal division of London. His moustache—a walrus affair—heaved gently on his labouring breath, like seaweed lifting on a ground-swell. There had been stairs. The building had committed an act of modern industrial cruelty by possessing them.

Uncle Donald stared at the half-packed luggage as though it were an insult to the hearth.

“What’s this? What’s this?” he contrived to eject at last.

“I’m packing,” said Bruce shortly.

For the first time in his life, Bruce experienced that furtive sense of guilt which, according to Family legend, was Ginger’s habitual state in the presence of this large, mackerel-eyed man. It is hard to feel serenely adult while being stared at by an elder who seems carved out of authority and suet.

“You going away?” said Uncle Donald.

“Yes.”

“Where you going?”

“America.”

“When you going?”

“To-morrow morning.”

“Why you going?”

Set down on paper, these questions look as brisk as the cross-fire of music-hall comedians. In reality, Uncle Donald’s conversational technique extended the exchange over nearly three minutes, for after each reply and before each next question he paused to puff, sigh, inhale moustache, and reintroduce it to the atmosphere with the measured solemnity of a man tasting soup that has been served too hot and too late. The apartment filled with the sound of breathing that had the steady persistence of a factory fan.

Then he struck.

“You’re going after that girl,” said Uncle Donald, accusingly.

Bruce flushed. And it is interesting to note that, at this moment, the thought floated through his mind that Ginger’s behaviour at Bleke’s Coffee House on a certain notable occasion had not been quite so inexcusable as he had supposed. There are provocations which make even the most orderly man understand the sudden urge to fling a thing.

“Will you have a whisky and soda, Uncle Donald?” he said, shifting ground.

“Yes,” said Uncle Donald, obeying a vow he had made in the early eighties never to refuse an offer of this kind. “Gimme!”

Bruce mixed it. The ice clinked with a bright, civilised music—one of those small domestic sounds that do more for the nervous system than any sermon. The soda hissed as it met the spirit, that little snake-like whisper that says, for a moment, that the world is not entirely composed of reproach.

Uncle Donald drank. One might have hoped the restorative would have softened him, the way a properly reduced stock softens a tough cut. But Uncle Donald was not a man to be braised into kindness.

“Never thought you were a fool before,” he said severely.

Bruce’s proud spirit chafed. This sort of interview—commonplace to Ginger, apparently—was new to him. Hitherto his actions had been received with the respectful silence accorded to heirlooms and county opinions.

“I’m not a fool,” said Bruce.

“You are a fool. A damn fool,” continued Uncle Donald, specifying more exactly. “Don’t like the girl. Never did. Not a nice girl. Didn’t like her. Right from the first.”

“Need we discuss this?” said Bruce, dropping into that grand manner which he used when he wished to feel taller than circumstances.

Uncle Donald drew in a layer of moustache and blew it out again, as if testing the acoustics of indignation.

“Need we discuss it?” he repeated. “We’re going to discuss it! Whatch think I climbed all these blasted stairs for with my weak heart? Gimme another!”

Bruce gave him another. The ice chimed again, bright as a spoon against a glass bowl. Outside, somewhere down in the street, a motorcar grated past with the vulgar metallic snarl that has replaced, in modern cities, the older, kinder soundscape of hoofbeats and human voices. The car seemed to underline Uncle Donald’s presence: the world growing noisier, and the old men growing louder in self-defence.

“’S a bad business,” moaned Uncle Donald, after the second drink had gone down with the resigned efficiency of a man fulfilling a duty. “Shocking bad business. If your poor father were alive, whatch think he’d say to your tearing across the world after this girl? I’ll tell you what he’d say. He’d say… What kind of whisky’s this?”

“O’Rafferty Special.”

“New to me.” Uncle Donald smacked his lips with the careful attention of someone who has always treated eating and drinking as the one honest scholarship left to him. “Not bad. Quite good. Sound. Mellow. Wherej get it?”

“Bilby’s, in Oxford Street.”

“Must order some. Mellow.” He stared as if trying to remember the exact phrasing of paternal outrage. “He’d say… well, God knows what he’d say. Whatch doing it for? Whatch doing it for? That’s what I can’t see. None of us can see. Puzzles your uncle George. Baffles your aunt Geraldine. Nobody can understand it. Girl’s simply after your money. Anyone can see that.”

“Pardon me, Uncle Donald,” said Bruce stiffly, “but that is surely rather absurd. If that were the case, why should she have refused me at Monk’s Crofton?”

“Drawing you on,” said Uncle Donald promptly. “Luring you on. Well-known trick. Girl in 1881, when I was at Oxford, tried to lure me on. If I hadn’t had some sense and a weak heart…” He paused to recover breath and dignity. “Whatch know of this girl? Whatch know of her? That’s the point. Who is she? Wherej meet her?”

“I met her at Roville, in France.”

“Travelling with her family?”

“Travelling alone,” said Bruce reluctantly.

“Not even with that brother of hers? Bad!” said Uncle Donald. “Bad, bad!”

“American girls are accustomed to more independence than English girls,” said Bruce, clinging to reason as to a rail on a pitching ship.

“That young man,” said Uncle Donald, pursuing a separate train of thought with the doggedness of an elder who plants potatoes by the moon whether the young people laugh or not, “is going to be fat one of these days, if he doesn’t look out.” He seemed, for an instant, almost wistful—like a traditional agrarian patriarch regretting that the next generation will inherit the land but not the old names for the winds. Then suspicion surged back. “Travelling alone, was she? What did you do? Catch her eye on the pier?”

“Really, Uncle Donald!”

“Well, must have got to know her somehow.”

“I was introduced to her by Lancelot. She was a friend of his.”

“Lancelot!” exploded Uncle Donald, quivering all over like a jelly at the loathed name. The word itself seemed, in his mouth, to carry the entire unpleasant modern habit of calling things by fashionable labels instead of their proper county terms. “Well, that shows you what sort of a girl she is. Any girl that would be a friend of—” He broke off, as if even saying the rest might contaminate the air. “Unpack!”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Unpack! Mustn’t go on with this foolery. Out of the question. Find some girl make you a good wife. Your aunt Mary’s been meeting some people name of Bassington-Bassington, related Kent Bassington-Bassingtons… eldest daughter charming girl, just do for you.”

Outside the more old-fashioned type of fiction, nobody ever really grinds his teeth; but Bruce came nearer to it then than most men have ever dared. His expression darkened. The last trace of suavity left him, as if someone had skimmed it off with a spoon.

“I shall do nothing of the kind,” he said briefly. “I sail to-morrow.”

Uncle Donald had been defied once before by a nephew, but experience does not make defiance pleasant. He sat, suddenly, with an unhappy sense of having been served a dish that will not yield to knife or persuasion.

“Eh?” he said.

Bruce, having begun, evidently decided to do it thoroughly.

“I am over twenty-one,” he said. “I am financially independent. I shall do as I please.”

“But, consider!” pleaded Uncle Donald, and it was painful to hear how thin his words were, like gravy stretched too far. “Reflect!”

“I have reflected.”

“Your position in the county—”

“I’ve thought of that.”

“You could marry anyone you pleased.”

“I’m going to.”

“You are determined to go running off to God-knows-where after this Miss I-can’t-even-remember-her-dam-name?”

“Yes.”

Uncle Donald leaned forward a fraction, making the chair creak in a tone of pure foreboding.

“Have you considered,” he said portentously, “that you owe a duty to the Family.”

Bruce’s patience snapped. He dropped, with surprising speed, to depths of plain speech which would have made Ginger feel almost scholarly.

“Oh, damn the Family!” he cried.

There was a silence so painful that even the city noises outside seemed, for a second, to hesitate—only the armchair sighed with relief as Uncle Donald heaved himself out of it, glad to be spared further intimacy.

“After that,” said Uncle Donald, “I have nothing more to say.”

“Good!” said Bruce rudely, because when a man has once thrown propriety out of the window, he tends to follow it with the furniture.

“’Cept this.” Uncle Donald’s voice had the grim relish of a judge pronouncing sentence and of an elder preserving the last harsh word of a dying dialect. “If you come back married to that girl, I’ll cut you in Piccadilly. By George, I will!”

He moved to the door. Bruce looked down his nose without speaking, and in that small pause the two of them stood as representatives of two eras: the old Family lexicon, heavy and unquestioning; and the new, reckless vocabulary of personal appetite and chosen love. The air held the faint sting of soda and spirit, and underneath it, the warm human smell of moustache and exertion.

Uncle Donald’s fingers closed on the handle.

“What,” he asked, pausing as if struck by a sudden and tender need for accuracy, “did you say it was called?”

“What was what called?” said Bruce, wary.

“That whisky.”

“O’Rafferty Special.”

“And wherj get it?”

“Bilby’s, in Oxford Street.”

“I’ll make a note of it,” said Uncle Donald.

Then he went out, taking with him, like a departing kitchen tyrant, the bulk of his disapproval—and leaving behind, in the faint clink of melting ice, the oddly comforting proof that even in the fiercest family quarrel there is always room for a man to remember what was mellow and where it could be ordered.

“And after all I’ve done for her,” said Mr. Reginald Cracknell, with the damp nobility of a man who has been wronged by Woman and fortified by his own private stock, “after all I’ve done for her she throws me down.”

Sally said nothing, partly because the Flower Garden orchestra was at that moment playing with the cheerful brutality of a steam-hammer, and partly because she was occupied saving her toes from Mr. Cracknell’s. The man danced as if the floor were a hostile nation and he meant to conquer it foot by foot. They lurched past the table where Miss Mabel Hobson—newly installed as one of the place’s “hostesses”—sat in old gold and black, distant as a statue and twice as critical. Mr. Cracknell, catching her eye, emitted a gulp like a siphon being turned off too suddenly.

“If I told you,” he moaned into Sally’s ear, “what—was that your ankle? Sorry! Don’t know what I’m doing to-night—if I told you what I’ve spent on that woman, you wouldn’t believe it. And then she throws me down. All because I said I didn’t like her in that hat. Won’t speak to me a week. Won’t answer when I call up on the ’phone. And I was right too. Rotten hat. Didn’t suit her a bit. But that,” said Mr. Cracknell, gloomily, “is a woman all over!”

Sally made a small sound as his foot came down on hers with the deliberation of a court sentence. He mistook it for a protest against his doctrine.

“I don’t mean you’re like that,” he said quickly, being gallant in the manner of a man who has just committed assault and wishes to pass as the injured party. “You’re different. I could see that directly I saw you. You’ve got a sympathetic nature. That’s why I’m telling you. You’re a sensible and broad-minded girl—sorry!—and can understand. I’ve done everything for that woman. I got her this job as hostess here—you wouldn’t believe what they pay her. I starred her in a show once. Did you see those pearls she was wearing? I gave her those. And she won’t speak to me. Just because I didn’t like her hat. I wish you could have seen that hat. You would agree with me, I know, because you’re sensible and broad-minded and understand hats. I don’t know what to do. I come here every night.”

Sally was aware of this. She had seen him often enough—like a recurring symptom.

“I come here every night and dance past her table, but she won’t look at me. What,” asked Mr. Cracknell, with tears collecting in his pale eyes as if they had been ordered in advance, “would you do about it?”

“I don’t know,” said Sally, and meant it.

“Nor do I. I thought you wouldn’t, because you’re a sensible and broad-minded— I mean, nor do I. I’m having one last try to-night, if you can keep a secret. You won’t tell anyone, will you?” He clutched at the idea with desperate hope. “But I know you won’t because you’re sensible— I’m giving her a little present. Having it brought here to-night. Little present. That ought to soften her, don’t you think?”

“A big one would do it better,” said Sally.

Mr. Cracknell, registering this as a form of blasphemy, kicked her on the shin in an absent-minded way.

“I never thought of that. Perhaps you’re right. But it’s too late now. Still, it might. Or wouldn’t it? Which do you think?”

“Yes,” said Sally, because “no” would have required energy.

“I thought as much,” said Mr. Cracknell, relieved.

The orchestra stopped with a thump and bang, leaving him clapping feebly in the middle of the floor as if trying to restart it by hand. Sally slipped back to her table. Mr. Cracknell, after one of those uncertain glances men give the universe when it has mislaid them, zigzagged off in search of his own seat.

Conversation surged up again—voices, laughter, the crackle of plates, the rattle of cutlery, the little metallic percussion of a city feeding itself while it pretended to be merry. The Flower Garden air was hot and close, full of perfume and cigar-smoke and the exhausted scent of too many human beings pressed together for too long. Sally put her hands over her eyes. Her headache sat behind them like a tenant who had paid rent for a month and intended to stay.

A month had passed since she had come back to Mr. Abrahams. It had been a dull month, heavy as wet wool, each day sliding into the next until life seemed something she watched rather than lived. In a queer nightmare way she felt cut off from her own sort. She hadn’t seen anyone from the old boarding-house days. Fillmore, presumably uneasy of conscience, had not shown his face. Ginger was far away, working out whatever destiny Long Island had for him.

She lowered her hands and looked about her. The room was crowded as always. The Flower Garden was one of those Broadway establishments that had floated up on the dancing craze and stayed afloat, partly because, as its proprietor boasted, it was a nice place and run nice. It advertised itself as “a supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing,” “large and spacious,” “sumptuously appointed,” with “incomparable dance-floor, enchanting music, cuisine, and service de luxe.” Even from a distance you could feel Isadore Abrahams thinking well of it.

Once, Sally had thought well of it too. In her first spell here it had been diverting—bright, busy, a show. But then she had not had headaches; and worse than headaches she had not had this dead, listless depression that made each night’s work feel like lifting a weight that never grew lighter.

“Miss Nicholas.”

The orchestra, which at the Flower Garden was never silent long enough for anyone to hear themselves regret, had started again. Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies, stood smiling with the air of a man offering a gift, and presented her next partner. Sally rose as if a spring had been wound inside her and released.

“This is the first time I been in this place,” said the man as they began to bump through the crowd. He was large and clumsy—of course—and to-night Sally had the sensation that the whole world had been built for people with broad shoulders and no regard for the laws of motion. “It’s a swell place. I come from up-state myself. We got nothing like this where I come from.”

He cleared a space in front of him by using Sally as a battering-ram. By comparison with this, Mr. Cracknell’s dancing now seemed almost a refined old waltz at a duchess’s ball. This man was, beyond argument, the worst dancer in America.

“Give me li’l old New York,” said the man, knocking gently into a couple that had been minding its own business. “It’s good enough for me. I been to some swell shows since I got to town. You seen this year’s Follies?”

“No.”

“You go,” he urged, earnest as a prophet. “You go! Take it from me, it’s a swell show. You seen Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath?”

“I don’t go to many theatres.”

“You go! It’s a scream. I been to a show every night since I got here. Every night regular. Swell shows all of ’em, except this last one. I cert’nly picked a lemon to-night all right. I was taking a chance, y’see, because it was an opening. Thought it would be something to say, when I got home, that I’d been to a New York opening. Set me back two-seventy-five, including tax, and I wish I’d got it in my kick right now. The Wild Rose, they called it,” he said with savage irony, as if he had discovered a conspiracy. “‘The Wild Rose!’ It sure made me wild all right. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away, just like that.”

The title stirred something in Sally’s mind, and then the shock came. Gerald’s new play. For weeks after she had returned to New York, she had been haunted by the fear of meeting him in the hall—his door opening as hers did, his face lifting and recognizing her. Then she had seen a paragraph in the morning paper: Gerald Foster was out on the road with a new play. And The Wild Rose—yes, that was it.

“Is that Gerald Foster’s play?” she asked quickly.

“I don’t know who wrote it,” said the man, “but he’s one lucky guy to get away alive. There’s fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road done a lot less to deserve a sentence. Wild Rose! I’ll tell the world it made me go good and wild. Why, before the second act was over, the people was beating it for the exits, and if it hadn’t been for someone shouting ‘Women and children first,’ there’d have been a panic.”

Sally found herself back at her table without remembering the route, as if her body had done the necessary travelling while her mind stood still somewhere on the words Gerald Foster.

“Miss Nicholas.”

She started to rise again—and then stopped. This voice was not Lee Schoenstein’s official voice, coming through gold teeth with professional sweetness. The man had sat down beside her and was speaking her name in precise, clipped accents that her memory recognized before her eyes did. The blur cleared.

Bruce Carmyle.

“I called at your place,” Mr. Carmyle was saying, “and the hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow you. I hope you do not mind. May I smoke?”

He struck a match and lit a cigarette with an air of self-command. His fingers trembled, but he acted as though this were a mere vibration in the atmosphere and not an earthquake in himself. Bruce Carmyle’s ideal was the strong man who rises above his emotions. He meant to rise above them now, even if he had to climb on his own head to do it. He glanced sideways at Sally and thought that never, not even in the garden at Monk’s Crofton on that morning that had changed everything, had he seen her look prettier. Her face was flushed and her eyes bright—bright with fatigue, with pain, with too much noise. The stout wraith of Uncle Donald, which had been puffing disapproval somewhere in the background of Mr. Carmyle’s consciousness, faded for the moment into mist.

There was a pause, filled by the Flower Garden’s soundscape: brassy music, laughter that cut short, the hiss of seltzer, the thick shuffle of feet. Mr. Carmyle puffed vigorously, as if smoking could supply missing conversation.

“When did you land?” asked Sally, because silence felt like standing in the path of a train.

“I landed to-night,” said Bruce Carmyle, turning and facing her squarely.

“To-night!”

“We docked at ten.”

He turned away again, having delivered the fact like a challenge and leaving it to do its work.

Sally sat still. She understood the meaning: he had come straight from the ship to find her. It should have stirred her into excitement or alarm. It hardly did. Fighting—thinking—seemed like things done by other people. She felt inert, like a swimmer who has battled too long and is ready to let the water take her. The heat pressed on her. The noise worried her nerves like hands.

“Shall we dance this?” he asked.

The orchestra had begun again—one of those creamy, sensuous melodies Broadway takes up, plays to death, and throws away. Sally had heard it a hundred times until it had become stale; yet tonight it seemed part of the room’s physical weight, like air you had to push through.

“If you like.”

Efficiency was Bruce Carmyle’s religion. He did nothing unless he could do it well. Dancing, he had long ago decided, was part of a gentleman’s education, and he had taken lessons as conscientiously as he would have taken Latin. Sally, bracing herself for the usual Flower Garden experience—bumping, wrenching, the slow grinding war of bodies trying to obey different rhythms—found herself in the arms of a master. He steered her through the crowd as if he had drawn a map of it beforehand. His control was oddly soothing. Her nerves, pulled taut for weeks, slackened. A miraculous peace came over her, as though the entire room had lowered its voice by a degree. With eyes half closed she yielded to the rhythm; and the tune, mysteriously, was no longer cheap and overfamiliar. For those minutes it might have been something new, something belonging to her.

And in that moment her whole attitude toward Bruce Carmyle shifted.

She had never examined her feelings about him in detail, but she had known one thing since the beginning: he had been physically distasteful to her. Handsome, yes, in a dark and rather sinister way; but she had shrunk from him. Now, carried by the dance, that repugnance slipped away as if a barrier between them had simply fallen. He no longer seemed alien. He seemed—dangerously—like relief.

“Sally!”

His arm tightened. She saw his face. His dark eyes blazed into hers, and she stumbled with a sudden helplessness. The half-dream shattered. This dance had not postponed anything. It had only made her defences drowsy. In a hot whisper, swept and broken by the flood of music that turned raucous again, he repeated what he had said under the trees at Monk’s Crofton, in the mild English spring. Dizzily she resented the unfairness of being attacked at such a moment, when her mind was too tired to parry; but resentment itself required energy, and she had little to spare.

The music stopped abruptly. Clapping demanded more, and the orchestra began again. Sally moved away to her table. Bruce Carmyle followed like a shadow.

He had said his say. Now he waited. Sally stared before her, trying to marshal thoughts that slid away like playing-cards on a tilted tray. She was tired—tired until even fear felt heavy. Her eyes burned. She tried to face it squarely: was anything worth struggling for? And the only answer she could find was the ache of exhaustion.

The music stopped again. This time, though people clapped, the orchestra did not respond. The dance-floor emptied. Shoes ceased their soft scraping. The Flower Garden became as quiet as it ever allowed itself to be. Even the babble dimmed, as if the room, like a person, had stopped talking for a moment to listen inwardly.

Sally closed her eyes; and from somewhere high near the roof came the song of a bird.

Isadore Abrahams, a man of his word, had advertised a Flower Garden, and he had attempted to supply something resembling one. Paper roses looped the walls. Real tulips stood in tubs by the pillars. And from the roof hung cages with birds. One of these, stirred by the lull in the tumult, had begun to sing.

Often Sally had pitied those birds, condemned to spend their lives performing pastoral charm above Broadway uproar. More than once she had pleaded with Abrahams to release them, and had been refused. But now it didn’t occur to her that the bird’s song might be a prayer in its own language—like the prayers she had made in her thoughts—to be taken out of this place. To her, at this moment, the song sounded cheerful. It soothed her. It seemed to lay a cool hand on her forehead. And in her mind there rose a vision of Monk’s Crofton—green lawns, shaded paths, the clean smell of earth and leaf, the quiet that let a person hear themselves breathe. It beckoned like an oasis to a traveller in a hot, loud desert.

She felt Bruce Carmyle’s hand close on hers. She looked down and shivered slightly. She had always disliked his hands. Strong, bony, with black hair on the back of them—hands that seemed made for gripping. One of her earliest feelings about him had been that she would hate those hands to touch her. Yet she did not draw away. Again the vision of that cool garden flickered—a haven where she could rest.

He leaned toward her, whispering. The room seemed hotter than ever, noisier than ever, fuller than ever. The bird sang again, and now Sally understood what it said. Take me out of this.

Did anything matter except that? What did it matter how one was taken, or where, or by whom, so long as one was taken?

Monk’s Crofton looked cool and green and peaceful…

“Very well,” said Sally.

Bruce Carmyle, now an accepted suitor, found himself oddly dissatisfied. It was not that Sally had accepted without warmth—warmth could come later, he told himself; warmth was a detail. What troubled him was that his correct mind suddenly perceived he had chosen an unsuitable time and place for what ought to have been a properly conducted scene. He belonged to the orthodox school of proposals: moonlight, solitude, and a decorous hush. The Flower Garden, for all its nice-ness and the nice way it was run, jarred on him like a wrong note.

Music had begun again, but it was not the music a lover requires if he wishes to be eloquent. It was brassy, clashy—a ribald one-step that seemed to mock sentiment. Couples dipped and swayed and collided as far as he could see. Behind him two waiters had halted to argue, as waiters love to do, with rapid, emphatic gestures.

To continue the scene at the proper emotional level was impossible. Bruce Carmyle began his engagement by falling back on small-talk.

“Deuce of a lot of noise,” he said, querulously.

“Yes,” said Sally.

“Is it always like this?”

“Oh, yes.”

“Infernal racket!”

“Yes.”

The romantic side of Bruce Carmyle could have wept. In his private imaginings of himself as a triumphant wooer, the moments after the whispered yes had always been his best. He saw himself bending proudly, murmuring memorable phrases into a bowed head. But how could any man murmur memorably in a pandemonium? His tenderness, failing to find atmosphere, turned sharply into irritability.

“Do you often come here?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“What for?”

“To dance.”

He chafed. The scene had suddenly reminded him of his first ball at twenty, when he had sat behind a potted palm, melting in a too-high collar and attempting conversation with a formidable nymph in pink. It was one of the few times he had been at a complete disadvantage. To have that memory resurrected now, at what ought to have been the supreme moment of his life, annoyed him. Almost angrily he tried to jerk the conversation into romance.

“Darling,” he murmured—by shifting his chair two feet to the right and bending sideways, he discovered he could murmur—“you have made me so—”

“Batti, batti! I presto ravioli hollandaise!” cried one of the waiters behind him.

“La donna e mobile spaghetti Napoli Tetrazzini,” came back the second waiter, spirited and fluent.

“—you have made me so—”

“Infanta Isabella lope de Vegas mulligatawny Toronto,” said the first, weakening but returning bravely.

“—so happy—”

“Funiculi funicula Vincente y Blasco Ibanez vermicelli sul campo della gloria risotto!” concluded the second, clinching the matter with what sounded like a technical knock-out.

Bruce Carmyle abandoned literature and lit a moody cigarette. He was oppressed by the feeling—known to many—that it was all wrong.

The music stopped. The two leading citizens of Little Italy vanished, presumably to begin a vendetta elsewhere. Comparative calm returned. But Mr. Carmyle’s emotions, like sweet bells jangled, were out of tune. He could not recover the first fine careless rapture. Small-talk remained.

“What has become of your party?” he asked.

“My party?”

“The people you are with.”

“I’m not with anybody.”

“You came here by yourself?” Bruce Carmyle exclaimed, aghast. In his correctly ordered world girls did not go alone to restaurants. The wraith of Uncle Donald returned, large and stern, puffing disapproval through an invisible walrus moustache.

“I am employed here,” said Sally.

Bruce Carmyle started as if someone had touched him with an icicle.

“Employed here?”

“As a dancer, you know. I—”

She broke off. Her attention had been caught by something across the room: a red-headed young man of sturdy build had appeared beside the chair where Mr. Cracknell sat in huddled gloom. The newcomer carried a basket, and from inside it rose a sudden sharp yapping that cut through conversation like a knife through soft bread.

Mr. Cracknell roused, took the basket, lifted the lid. The yapping increased as if the creature inside had opinions and intended them to be heard. Mr. Cracknell got up, basket in his arms, and with the uncertain steps of a man walking toward either reconciliation or execution, crossed the floor to Miss Mabel Hobson’s table. The next moment that haughty lady—centre of an admiring, curious crowd—was hugging to her bosom a protesting Pekingese puppy, while Mr. Cracknell, seizing his chance like a general who sees the enemy’s flank, deposited himself in a chair at her side. The course of true love, lubricated by small dogs, ran smooth again.

The red-headed young man, having delivered his cargo of sentiment, looked across the room and gazed fixedly at Sally.

“As a dancer!” repeated Mr. Carmyle. Of all the people watching the little drama of puppy and pearls and hat-hatred resolving into peace, he alone had missed it entirely. His thoughts were elsewhere, where Uncle Donald stood pointing an accusing finger.

A dancer. A professional dancer at a Broadway restaurant.

Hideous doubts began creeping into Bruce Carmyle’s mind, cold and insinuating. What did he truly know about this girl to whom he had just offered the priceless boon of his society for life? How did he know what she was—he could not find the exact adjective, but he knew what he meant. Was she—worthy?

All his life he had maintained a prim shrinking from the part of the feminine world connected with the light-life of great cities. He had seen acquaintances in London marry girls from the Gaiety Chorus, and though he had no objection to the Gaiety Chorus in its proper place—on the other side of the footlights—he had always regarded the young men afterward as social outcasts. The fine, dashing frenzy that had brought him all the way from South Audley Street to win Sally began to ebb like a tide turning.

Sally, hearing him, looked at him. There was a candid honesty in her gaze that, for a moment, sent the creeping doubts scuttling back into the darkness. He had not made a fool of himself, he protested inwardly to Uncle Donald. Who, he demanded, could look at Sally and think for an instant she was not perfect and lovable?

A warm revulsion swept over him, like a returning tide.

The blood came back into Sally’s cheeks in little reluctant tides, as if it had been out all night and was only now remembering the way home. For one sharp instant, at the sound of Gerald’s voice, her heart made a ridiculous leap—like a soufflé that rises too fast and threatens to collapse—and then, with a sort of chastened propriety, it settled into its ordinary rhythm again.

The surprise was not that she was frightened. The surprise was that she wasn’t.

She had rehearsed this meeting in her mind often enough: the door, the voice, the old ache turning suddenly into panic. But the night had drained her of extravagances. Fear, like money, requires a certain capital; and Sally had spent hers in full. What was left was a hard, lucid calm—an over-tired brightness, the way a kitchen can feel at four in the morning when you’ve been cooking too long and everything is unnaturally clear: the gleam of a knife, the hiss of a cooling stove, the dry smell of yesterday’s onions.

“Hullo, Sally!” said Gerald.

He was in his shirt-sleeves, collar unbuttoned, hair damp and rebellious, and he stood swaying with one hand braced against the door as if the wood were the only honest friend he had left. Drink hung about him like a wet overcoat: sour, stale, and unmistakably present. His face looked white and puffed, as though he’d been steamed instead of shaved. There was a foolish smile—one of those smiles that make you want to close a window quickly.

Sally looked at him without warmth, without anger, as if he were a stranger who had wandered into the wrong boarding-house and was now waiting to be redirected.

“What do you want?” she said.

“Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I’d come in.”

“What do you want?” she repeated, because questions are useful things. They are like recipes: if you keep to them, you don’t end up with a mess you can’t explain.

The pinned-on smile slid away from his face. A tear worked itself loose and rolled down his cheek with the slow inevitability of gravy that has escaped the spoon.

“Sally… S-Sally… I’m very miserable.”

He slurred the words as though they had too many consonants for a man in his condition.

He said again, as if it were a line he knew and could hold onto: “Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I’d come in.”

Something in Sally’s mind flicked like a match. The scene had the stale familiarity of yesterday’s coffee reheated. And then she remembered: this wasn’t Gerald at all, in essence. This was Mr. Reginald Cracknell, revived with different hair and the same pathetic humidity.

“I think you’d better go to bed, Gerald,” she said steadily.

Nothing about him touched her now—not the tear, not the ruined air, not even the soft animal misery. She felt as if she were wearing an apron of indifference, tied tightly.

“What’s the use? Can’t sleep. No good. Couldn’t sleep.” He sniffed. “Sally, you don’t know how worried I am. I see what a fool I’ve been.”

Sally lifted a hand quickly to stop what she assumed was coming—one of those late apologies that arrive like cold pudding after you’ve thrown the plates away. She had no desire to stand in a doorway while Gerald cried into the night about doing his best to wreck her life.

But that was not, it appeared, what distressed him most.

“I was a fool ever to try writing plays,” he went on, suddenly bitter, suddenly earnest, as if the only thing worth crying about was his own career. “Got a winner first time, but can’t repeat. It’s no good. Ought to have stuck to newspaper work. I’m good at that. Shall have to go back to it.”

He wept quietly, full of self-pity at the thought of returning to regular employment, as though honest work were a medieval disease.

“Very miserable,” he murmured.

He lurched forward a step into her room, then retreated to the door again, hugging it like a drunk sailor clinging to a mast.

For a moment contempt stabbed through Sally’s calm—a quick, clean sensation, like the sting of lemon on a cracked fingertip. And then it was gone and she was back in that bright, hard alertness.

“Go to bed, Gerald,” she said. “You’ll feel better in the morning.”

Something about morning, perhaps, made its slow way into his fog. He winced and his melancholy deepened.

“May not be alive in the morning,” he announced solemnly. “Good mind to end it all. End it all!”

He made the beginning of a grand gesture and was instantly forced to abort it as he grabbed at the door for support.

Sally was in no mood to encourage melodrama.

“Oh, go to bed,” she said, with impatience now beginning to itch under her skin.

Her frozen detachment was thawing—not into tenderness, but into resentment. Resentment at him for being this—this soggy, self-absorbed ruin. Resentment at herself for ever having found glamour here, as though she had once admired a cheap restaurant simply because the lights were low.

And under the sharp sting of that humiliation she felt hard and almost pitiless. She could see, dimly, that something in her had shifted tonight. Usually any living thing in distress would have pulled at her quick sympathy. But Gerald lamenting the possibility of returning to steady work did nothing but irritate her.

He noticed.

“You’re very unsymp… unsympathetic,” he complained.

“I’m sorry,” said Sally.

She walked briskly to the door and pushed it. Gerald, still clasping his support, moved out into the passage with the handle, as if he were being uprooted by the very architecture. Then he released it and staggered across to his own open door. He went in, wobbled, and vanished.

Sally watched him safely to his journey’s end, then went into her bedroom with the firm intention of ending the night in the simplest possible way: sleep.

Almost immediately she understood that sleep was impossible. Restlessness had seized her, a feverish feeling under the ribs. She put on a kimono and went to the kitchen to see if she could make herself a glass of hot milk.

Hot milk, properly done, is comfort in liquid form: the faintly sweet steam, the skin that threatens to form if you’re not attentive, the quiet little sounds as it warms. Sally had always loved it. It felt domestic. It felt like a language your body still understood even when your mind was too tired to translate anything else.

But she remembered, with a faint regret, that she had given the last of the milk that morning to a sandy cat that had dropped in through the window with the purposeful eye of a creature who believed breakfast was a treaty between species. Sally had obliged, because she was the sort of person who fed strays and then wondered why her larder was always empty.

She was standing there, thinking of that lost milk with the mournful reverence of a cook recalling an ingredient she ought not to have squandered, when a muffled crash interrupted her.

She froze and listened.

The sound had come from across the passage.

She went to the door and opened it.

At once, from behind Gerald’s door, came a fusillade of crashes—sharp, splintering reports that ricocheted through the night air. The kind of sound that makes your nerves leap before your mind can attach meaning to it. In the quiet hours, loud noise is violence even when it isn’t. It shreds the calm, turns the corridor into a drum, makes you hear your own breathing too loudly.

A brief silence followed. Sally stood there, suddenly cold, remembering Gerald’s solemn talk of “ending it all.” Industrial London might be able to sleep through horns and street-cars and the perpetual grind of modern life, but in the small, domesticated hush of a boarding-house passage, one unaccountable crash was a thunderclap.

Then the crashing started again—louder, more determined.

Sally stopped hesitating. She ran across and hammered on the door.

A dragging footstep approached. The door opened.

Gerald stood there with the weak smile back on his face, as if noise had been a perfectly sensible pastime and he had merely been rearranging his conscience.

“Hullo, Sally!”

At the sight of him alive—alive and disgracefully intact—Sally’s alarm evaporated, leaving behind only irritation that she had allowed herself to feel anything at all.

“Whatever was all that noise?” she demanded.

“Noise?” Gerald said, mouth slightly open, considering the question with the gravity of an owl.

“Yes, noise.”

“I’ve been cleaning house,” said Gerald.

Sally pushed past him into the apartment opposite.

It was almost exactly like her own in plan, but everything in it had once been arranged with Elsa Doland’s particular kind of fussy prettiness—the sort of decoration that aims for dainty and ends in crowded. Elsa had always liked ornaments: little fragile objects that look as if they require their own special vocabulary of care. Plates along shelves. Lamps with parti-coloured shades. Pictures that jostled one another for wall-space like people trying to get served at a popular tea-shop.

Only now, the jostling had turned literal.

A shell could not have burst in that sitting-room and done more thorough work. The place was ruin, majestic and absurd. Glass and china carpeted the floor in glittering fragments. Statuettes lay smashed into anonymous pieces. Frames had been reduced to twisted splinters. It looked as though some prehistoric animal had taken a mouthful of water-colours and then spat them out in disgust.

In a corner lay the instrument: a heavy niblick, tossed aside—petulantly or remorsefully—like a weapon after a duel.

Gerald had subsided onto an occasional table and was weeping softly again, as though moved to tears by the injustice of being himself.

“Well!” Sally gasped. “You’ve certainly made a good job of it!”

There was a sharp crack.

The occasional table—never designed to bear the full weight of a grown man’s despair—collapsed beneath him in a splintering flurry, dumping Gerald onto the remains of a set of water-colours with the brisk efficiency of a stage-trap.

And with that, something inside Sally shifted. Tragedy and farce are always elbowing each other for position, and in that moment farce won.

A laugh bubbled up out of her, sudden and unmanageable. She sank into a chair, gurgling with it—not cruelty exactly, but astonished relief. It was as if her mind, unable to carry the emotional weight any longer, had thrown off the burden and chosen the lighter package.

“No sympathy,” said Gerald austerely, hauling himself up and glaring at her with disapproval as if he were a wronged bishop.

“I can’t help it,” Sally cried. “It’s too funny.”

“Not funny,” corrected Gerald, already beginning to blur at the edges again.

“What did you do it for?”

His indignation flared, strengthening him for a moment.

“Wasn’t going to stand for it any longer,” he said hotly. “A fellow’s wife goes and lets him down… ruins his show by going off and playing in another show… why shouldn’t I smash her things? Why should I stand for that sort of treatment? Why should I?”

“Well, you haven’t,” said Sally. “So there’s no need to discuss it. You seem to have acted in a thoroughly manly and independent way.”

“That’s it. Manly independent.” He waggled a finger, impressively unsteady. “Don’t care what she says. Don’t care if she never comes back. That woman…”

Sally’s laughter died. The sordidness of it all rose like a bad smell from a sink. She was suddenly tired of Gerald’s voice, tired of Elsa’s absence, tired of the broken ornaments and the childish fury. It was the messiest sort of noise: the noise of people who couldn’t speak properly to one another and so translated their feelings into crashes.

“And now,” said Sally, standing up with decision, “I’m going to tidy up.”

Gerald’s face took on sudden solemnity.

“No,” he said. “No! Nothing of the kind. Leave it for her to find. Leave it as it is.”

“Don’t be silly. All this has got to be cleaned up. I’ll do it. You go and sit in my apartment. I’ll come and tell you when you can come back.”

“No!” said Gerald, wagging his head as if refusing a spoonful of medicine.

Sally stamped her foot among the crackling ruins. Quite suddenly, his presence became intolerable. The mere fact of his breathing in the room seemed offensive.

“Do as I tell you!” she cried.

His brief militancy ebbed. After a faint protest he shuffled out. Sally heard him cross the passage and go into her room.

Relief came over her like cool water on scorched hands. She turned to the task.

In the kitchen she found a long-handled broom. Armed with it, she began. Sally was efficient; she had that brisk, competent sort of energy that belongs to those who know how to get through work even when their hearts are bruised. She swept shards into piles, coaxed broken lamp-shades away from chair-legs, gathered splinters and fragments as if she were sorting ingredients for some grim recipe: two handfuls of shattered porcelain, a scatter of glass, a dusting of ruin.

The broom made a steady, domestic sound against the floor—one of the kinder sounds in the world. It was almost musical compared with the earlier crashing: a controlled whisper of bristles, the gentle scrape that says, Whatever happened, we can make the place livable again. It was the sort of sound her grandmother’s kitchen must have made after a wedding feast, when everyone was gone and the elder women stood in the quiet, restoring order, speaking in old dialects that kept their meanings close.

Half an hour later the floor was cleared. The fragments of vases, plates, lamp-shades, pictures, and glasses were stacked in small heaps against the walls like little cairns marking where beauty had died.

Nothing short of re-decoration would make it pretty again, but at least it was no longer a minefield.

She returned the broom, then went back into the sitting-room and flung open the window.

The night had vanished. Over the quiet street there brooded that strange metallic light that comes before sunrise on a fine day—cool and unreal, as if the world had been polished. A cold breeze whispered to and fro. The sky above the house-tops was a faint, level blue.

Sally turned away from the window and started to cross the room.

And then weakness swept over her without warning.

She stumbled into a chair, suddenly too tired to be upright, too tired to be anything but a body that had run out of fuel. Her eyes closed. Almost before her head touched the cushions, sleep took her.

When she woke, sunlight was pouring through the open window and bringing with it the city’s morning orchestra—footsteps clattering on the sidewalk, automobile horns calling to one another like impatient geese, the clank of street-cars over points. The soundscape had changed completely: from the intimate violence of smashed china to the broad, ceaseless racket of modern life. Even the birds seemed to have to shout to be heard.

She got up stiffly, with an aching head. She went to the bathroom, bathed her face, and the cool water helped. Oppression began to lift, as it does when you wash away the grime and let fresh air at your lungs.

She leaned out of the window for a moment, revelling in the clean bite of morning, then crossed the passage into her own apartment.

Stertorous breathing greeted her.

Gerald Foster was sprawled in a chair by the window, legs stretched out, head resting on one arm, mouth slightly open: an unlovely spectacle. He looked like a man who had been left out overnight and had gone soft in the rain.

Sally regarded him with distaste—and yet, beneath it, something else: a lightness. A door had shut inside her. A black chapter was over. Whatever years came, they would not include wistful longing for this man. The glamour had gone as utterly as yesterday’s steam.

She shook him gently. He sat up with a start, blinking as if the sunlight were a personal attack.

“Oh, my God,” said Gerald, pressing both hands to his forehead. He sat down again, licked his lips with a dry tongue, and moaned. “Oh, I’ve got a headache!”

“You’d better go and have a wash,” Sally suggested.

“Yes,” said Gerald faintly, and tottered off to the bathroom.

“Would you like some breakfast?” she asked automatically—because her mind, being Sally’s mind, still tried to solve problems with food. If she had had a pantry and a peaceful life, she would have made toast properly browned, eggs soft enough to be kind, coffee strong enough to revive the dead.

“Don’t!” said Gerald, as if she had threatened him with a roast.

Sally sat in the chair he had vacated. Everything felt dreamlike. The splashing from the bathroom came to her faintly, as if from another household. She felt herself drifting toward sleep again and forced herself up.

She opened the window. Air came in with a brisk, cleansing authority. Below, the street lived its own loud life. People hurried on errands whose importance they never questioned. An inscrutable cat picked its way across the road with dainty care, pausing between noises as if selecting the quietest route through the world. An open car purred at the apartment door, the engine making a soft, expensive sound.

The bell rang.

Sally went to the door and opened it.

Bruce Carmyle stood on the threshold in a light motor-coat. He was attempting to soften his saturnine face with a smile of beaming kindliness, as a man might try to disguise a stone with a ribbon.

“Well, here I am!” he said cheerily. “Are you ready?”

In daylight, repentance had crept over Mr. Carmyle. Shaving and bathing had shown him, in the mirror’s impartial language, that he had not been ideally winning overnight. He had left Sally at the Flower Garden with an abruptness unbecoming to a perfect lover. His nerves had got the better of him. He had come this morning to make amends, and so he wore cheerfulness early, as if it were a collar that felt a little too bright.

Sally stared at him blankly.

She had forgotten entirely that he was coming to take her for a drive. The promise had slipped away in the night among the broken glass and the broom’s whisper and the city’s harsh morning chorus. She searched for words and found none.

Bruce, debating whether to kiss her at once or wait for some better moment, stood awkwardly. Embarrassment gathered between them like fog.

“I’ve—er—got the car outside, and…”

Speech failed him, because at that precise moment the bathroom door opened and Gerald Foster came out.

Bruce Carmyle gaped at Gerald. Gerald gaped at Bruce.

Cold water is an excellent thing after a night’s imprudence, but it can only do so much, and in Gerald’s case it had done almost nothing. Some invisible person was still driving red-hot rivets into the base of his skull. His eyes were pink and watery; his face was white and in desperate need of a shave. He looked at Bruce wanly, like a condemned man at a magistrate.

Bruce drew in his breath with a sharp hiss and went rigid. His eyes flickered over Gerald’s slouching figure—shirt-sleeves, the sad foundations of evening dress, the whole degraded tableau—and found nothing they could possibly enjoy.

In that instant, every doubt that had ever vexed Bruce Carmyle since he first met Sally turned to certainty, neat as an accountant’s ledger.

So Uncle Donald had been right after all.

At Bruce’s elbow, the stout phantom of Uncle Donald puffed with satisfaction.

“I told you so!” it seemed to say.

Sally still had not moved. The situation was too absurd, too heavy, too perfectly timed. It was like watching someone drop a tray of carefully arranged pastries: you can’t catch them all, and for a second you can’t even believe they’re falling.

“So…” said Bruce Carmyle, becoming articulate, and allowed the rest of his sentence to hang in the air like a guillotine blade.

Cold fury gripped him. He pointed at Gerald, began to speak, felt a stutter threatening, gulped it back. In this moment he would not have his dignity impaired by stumbling over syllables.

He found a sentence short enough to be safe and long enough to hurt.

“Get out!” he said.

Gerald, even with his skull in flames, had dignity too. He drew himself up haughtily, about to demand what the devil was meant by it—then pain slammed through him and folded him back into caution. He clasped his forehead and groaned.

“Get out!” repeated Bruce.

Gerald hesitated. Another stabbing spasm convinced him there was no pleasure in argument. He shuffled across to the door and out into the passage.

Bruce watched him go with twitching hands. For a moment the human man in him—atrophied from disuse—stirred almost to the point of assault. Then dignity whispered its counsel and Gerald passed safely out of reach.

Bruce turned to Sally.

He faced her as King Arthur must have faced Guinevere on a similar occasion, though perhaps without quite so many motor-cars involved.

“So…” he said again.

Sally eyed him steadily—too steadily, he thought, with indignation, given the circumstances.

“This,” said Bruce Carmyle ponderously, “is very amusing.”

He waited for her to speak.

She did not.

“I might have expected it,” he said, with a bitter laugh.

Sally forced herself out of her lethargy, as if lifting a heavy pan from a low shelf.

“Would you like me to explain?” she asked.

“There can be no explanation,” said Bruce Carmyle coldly.

“Very well,” said Sally.

A pause.

“Good-bye,” said Bruce.

“Good-bye,” said Sally.

He walked to the door, stopped, and glanced back. Sally had gone to the window and was looking out. In the strong sunlight her trim little figure and the gleam of her hair caught at something in him; for one swift instant he wavered, as if the world’s noise had dropped and he could hear his own heart.

Then he hardened again. The door closed behind him with a resolute bang.

Outside, climbing into his car, he looked up involuntarily to see if she was still there.

She had gone.

As his car gathered speed and hummed down the street, Sally was at the telephone. Ginger Kemp’s sleepy voice answered, thick with morning.

But as it became aware who had called and what she was saying, it lost its sleepiness as if someone had dashed cold water over it. It sprang into riotous ecstasy.

Five minutes later, Ginger Kemp was splashing in his bath, singing discordantly.

Darkness began to seep over the meadows with the manner of a house-servant who dislikes interrupting a party but has been told to draw the curtains all the same. Beyond the trees, the west still held a thin smear of apricot light, and above the big barn the new moon hung like a clean, sharp sickle laid out on a butcher’s block for tomorrow’s work.

Sally stepped out onto the porch and—because the house had its own small rites, as steady as setting dough to rise—bowed gravely three times for luck. The gravel was cool through the soles of her shoes. The air smelled of clover and sun-warmed boards, of damp earth under the hedges, and of the kitchen’s lingering ghosts: soap, onions, and the faint sweet-fat note of bacon that had been everywhere this last year, even when there wasn’t any. She stood a moment with her hands loosely folded, listening.

Stillness had its own music here. Not the brassy modern racket that cities threw at you—motor horns and shrieking brakes, the sort of noise that made a mind clench like a fist—but the good domestic orchestra: crickets tuning up in the grass, a far-off hinge giving one sleepy complaint, the barn timbers settling with a sigh. Across the meadow, windows winked on one by one, each a small square of lamplight like a page being opened.

From the kennel direction came a solitary, elderly bark, as if some dog had remembered a duty and then immediately regretted it. Toto—the small white woolly visitor who had attached himself to Sally’s heels like a burr—halted in the drive and emitted his challenging squeak, a sound halfway between a squeal of indignation and the protest of a kettle that thinks it’s boiling.

Then, along the state road, came another rhythm: Ginger’s footsteps, steady and unmistakable, thudding in time on the hard surface as he returned from the village. Even before he turned in at the gate, you could hear him as plainly as if the road itself carried him home on a drum. Sally faced the sound and felt, as she had felt every evening for nearly a year, that snug, astonished warmth that marriage had turned into a habit.

“Ginger!” she called.

“What ho!” came the answering hail, cheerful and slightly winded.

Toto, taking the responsibility of the household upon his compact shoulders, scuttled down the drive to investigate and was met, as usual, with a chilly reception. Ginger was fond of dogs in the abstract, but Toto had never managed to look like a dog in the concrete. He looked like a muff that had learned to walk and had all the moral purpose of a blancmange.

“It’s wonderful what you’ve done for Toto, angel,” said Sally as Ginger came up, sidestepping the creature’s leaps of welcome with the dexterity of a man avoiding a thrown sponge. “He’s a different dog.”

“Bit of luck for him,” said Ginger. “In all the years I was at Mrs. Meecher’s I never knew him move at anything more rapid than a stately walk. Now he runs about all the time.”

Sally’s eyes softened. “Country air.”

“The blighter had been overeating from birth,” said Ginger. “That was all that was wrong with him. A little judicious dieting put him right. We’ll be able”—his voice brightened with the pleased finality of someone predicting the end of a minor plague—“to ship him back next week.”

“I shall quite miss him,” said Sally.

“I nearly missed him—this morning—with a shoe,” said Ginger. “He was up on the kitchen table wolfing the bacon, and I took steps.”

“My cave-man,” murmured Sally, as if this were a compliment of the highest order. “I always said you had a frightfully brutal streak in you. Ginger, what an evening!”

“Good Lord!” Ginger stopped short at the open kitchen door, where lamplight spilled out onto the threshold and turned the air to honey. He stared at her as if she had altered while he was away, as if she had somehow slipped out and been replaced by a newer, lovelier Sally. “Now what?”

“What what?”

“Do you know you’re looking prettier than you were when I started down to the village!”

Sally gave his arm a small, satisfied hug. “Beloved!” she said, and then, with the practical directness of a woman who can admire a sunset and still remember the pantry, added, “Did you get the chops?”

Ginger froze in his tracks with the expression of a man who has just seen the trapdoor open beneath his feet.

“Oh, my aunt! I clean forgot them!”

“Oh, Ginger, you are an old chump. Well, you’ll have to go in for a little judicious dieting, like Toto.”

“I say, I’m most awfully sorry. I got the wool.”

“If you think I’m going to eat wool—”

“Isn’t there anything in the house?”

“Vegetables and fruit.”

“Fine!”

Sally lifted her chin with mock spirituality. “Not at all. I’m spiritual. Besides, people say that vegetables are good for the blood-pressure or something. Of course you forgot to get the mail, too?”

“Absolutely not! I was on to it like a knife.” Ginger fished in his pocket. “Two letters from fellows wanting Airedale puppies.”

“No! Ginger, we are getting on!”

“Pretty bloated,” agreed Ginger, with that serene complacency of a man whose prosperity is still in its early, shining stages. “Pretty bloated. We’ll be able to get that two-seater if things go buzzing on like this. There was a letter for you. Here it is.”

“It’s from Fillmore,” said Sally, turning the envelope over as they went into the kitchen. “And about time, too. I haven’t had a word from him for months.”

The kitchen smelled of the day’s honest work: potatoes in their bin, apples in a bowl, the green snap of vegetables waiting their turn, and under it all the clean, comforting scent of scrubbed boards. On the table lay Ginger’s evening paper, already wanting to be unfolded with importance. Sally sat, slit the envelope, and drew out the letter.

Ginger heaved himself onto the table and wriggled into comfort, a red-headed domestic monarch taking his throne. He began with the sporting page, because a man must keep in touch with the larger movements of civilization. But after a moment he lowered the paper and let his eyes rest on Sally’s bent head.

Marriage had not worn off. It had not turned into a duty, or a system, or a thing you did because you had started doing it. It remained, for Ginger, a sort of continual miracle, like bread rising when you have followed the recipe exactly and still can’t quite believe it. He watched her with the contentment of a man who has finally found a thing in life that does not produce a catch when you lift it.

Sally, reading, made a sound that began as a snort and ended as laughter.

“Ginger, look at this!”

He reached down and took the slip of paper she held out. In bold letters it read:

POPP’S OUTSTANDING SUCCULENT—APPETIZING—NUTRITIOUS.
(JUST SAY “POP!” A CHILD CAN DO IT.)

Ginger stared at it as if it were a code message from a foreign power.

“What is it?” he asked.

“It’s Fillmore.”

“How do you mean?”

Sally gurgled again, delighted and appalled in the same breath. “Fillmore and Gladys have started a little restaurant in Pittsburg.”

“A restaurant!” The note of shock in Ginger’s voice suggested that Fillmore had been caught selling bootlaces on a street corner. However often he had seen Fillmore’s grand schemes burst like overfilled puddings, Ginger retained a reverence for his brother-in-law’s air of destiny. That Fillmore Nicholas, the man who always spoke as if the world were waiting for his instructions, should descend to running a small eating-place struck him as almost indecent.

Sally, for sisters are constitutionally incapable of worshipping their brothers properly, beamed. “It’s the most splendid idea,” she said. “It really does look as if Fillmore was going to amount to something at last. Apparently they started on quite a small scale, just making pork-pies—”

“Why Popp?” interrupted Ginger, still grappling with the slogan. “Why not Fillmore’s Outstanding—whatever it is?”

“Just a trade name, silly. Gladys is a wonderful cook, you know.” Sally’s eyes took on the pleased, hungry sparkle of a woman imagining pastry. “And she made the pies and Fillmore toddled round selling them.”

In Sally’s mind, Gladys was already there at some flour-dusted table in a hot little back room, sleeves rolled, patient as an elder on harvest day. You could almost hear the crisp scrape of a knife cutting cold lard into flour, the soft patter as water went in by spoonfuls, not a drop more than necessary. You could smell pepper and pork and the quiet authority of salt. A proper pie did not need shouting. It needed good meat, a firm hand, and an oven that held its heat without complaining.

“And they did so well,” Sally went on, “that now they’ve started a regular restaurant, and that’s a success, too. Listen to this.” She turned the page, still laughing. “‘…sound financial footing. In fact, our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a really big scale. It is Big Ideas that lead to Big Business. I am contemplating a vast extension of this venture of ours, and in a very short time I shall organize branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, and all the big cities, each in charge of a manager and each offering as a special feature, in addition to the usual restaurant cuisine, these Popp’s Outstanding Pork-pies of ours. That done, and having established all these branches as going concerns, I shall sail for England and introduce Popp’s Pork-pies there…’”

She lowered the letter, glowing. “Isn’t he a little wonder!”

“Dashed brainy chap,” said Ginger, loyally, though the words “New York” and “branches” and “manager” made him flinch inwardly, as one does on hearing a promising sauce being threatened with an unnecessary final flourish.

“I must say I was rather uneasy when I read that,” Sally added, practical even in her sisterly pride. “I’ve seen so many of Fillmore’s Big Ideas. That’s always the way with him. He gets something good and then goes and overdoes it and bursts. However, it’s all right now that he’s got Gladys to look after him.”

She glanced down again, her smile widening. “She has added a postscript. Just four words, but oh! how comforting to a sister’s heart. ‘Yes, I don’t think!’ is what she says, and I don’t know when I’ve read anything more cheering. Thank heaven, she’s got poor dear Fillmore well in hand.”

Outside, the night had deepened, and the house’s sounds became clearer for it: the faint tick of the clock, the small rustle of paper, Toto’s offended breathing. In the distance, some motorcar passed on the state road, briefly tearing a harsh note across the quiet—an ugly, mechanical intrusion that vanished as quickly as it came, leaving the crickets to stitch the calm back together.

“Pork-pies,” said Ginger thoughtfully, and at the same time his stomach, which had been expecting chops and had instead been offered noble abstractions like “fruit,” began to speak up in a language too old for modern advertising to improve. “I wish he’d sent us one of the outstanding little chaps. I could do with it.”

Sally rose and ruffled his red hair, her fingers smelling faintly of yarn and envelope paper and the day’s work. “Poor old Ginger! I knew you’d never be able to stick it. Come on—it’s a lovely night. Let’s walk to the village and revel at the inn. We’re going to be millionaires before we know where we are, so we can afford it.”

And with that—without tragedy, without fireworks, without any noise but the good, human sort—they stepped out into the clean, star-pricked dark, leaving Toto to squeak his opinions to the empty kitchen and the moon to hang above the barn like a promise of another harvest, another meal, another story that would come back round again.
