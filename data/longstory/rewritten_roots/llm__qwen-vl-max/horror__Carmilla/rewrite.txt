Attached to the manuscript that follows is a note penned by Doctor Hesselius, a man of rare intellect and deep scholarship. In it, he refers to his Essay on the unsettling subject this narrative reveals—a topic he explores with precision, insight, and an unflinching clarity that marks his work as both extraordinary and chilling. That Essay, though dense with erudition, will one day form part of the collected writings of this remarkable thinker. Yet here, in this volume, my purpose is not academic rigor but to stir the imagination of ordinary readers. I wish only to present the story as it was told—without commentary, without summary, without the weight of learned interpretation.

Thus, I have chosen not to include any distillation of the doctor’s arguments or excerpts from his treatise on what he calls “a matter likely rooted in the deepest mysteries of our dual nature, and the shadows between life and death.” To do so might dull the horror, obscure the truth, or betray the raw power of the tale itself.

When I first came upon this manuscript, I was eager to reconnect with the woman who wrote it—her mind sharp, her account meticulous, her voice compelling. I hoped to learn more, to hear her thoughts beyond the pages. But alas, she had passed away long before I found her words. She would have added little, I suspect, to the story already laid bare. For what she has written stands complete: vivid, haunting, and unshakable in its detail. It is, as far as I can judge, a record delivered with honesty and care—an account that speaks for itself, needing no explanation, no defense, only to be read.

I. An Early Fright

In Styria, we live in a castle—though not a grand one—nestled deep within a wild and ancient forest. It’s called a *schloss*, though the word sounds more noble than our modest circumstances warrant. Here, money stretches far; eight or nine hundred pounds a year does wonders. Back home in England, such an income would barely keep a household going, but here, in this quiet, forgotten corner of the world, it provides comfort without extravagance. My father is English, and I bear an English name, though I’ve never seen England. He served in the Austrian army, retired on a pension and family inheritance, and bought this old feudal estate—a bargain, they said. The land is small, but the place itself is hauntingly beautiful.

The castle sits atop a gentle rise, surrounded by trees that stretch endlessly in every direction. A narrow, weathered road winds past its drawbridge, which has never been raised in my memory. Below, the moat holds perch and swans gliding through still water, their reflections mingling with clusters of white water lilies. Above it all rises the schloss—its many windows staring out like eyes, its towers reaching toward the sky, and its Gothic chapel standing solemn and silent. Before the gate, the forest parts into an irregular glade, and to the right, a steep stone bridge crosses a dark stream that curls through the woods like a serpent in shadow.

This is a lonely place. So lonely that when you stand at the hall door and look down the road, the forest spreads fifteen miles to the right and twelve to the left. The nearest village lies seven English miles away, and the closest inhabited *schloss* with any history is General Spielsdorf’s, nearly twenty miles off. I say “inhabited” because just three miles westward, near Spielsdorf’s estate, lies a ruined village—its little church roofless, its aisle filled with crumbling tombs of the Karnstein family, long since vanished. Their chateau stands abandoned in the heart of the forest, overlooking the silent ruins below. There is a legend about why the village was deserted—dark and strange—and I will tell it to you later. But for now, let me speak of what lives inside these walls.

Our household is tiny. Not counting servants or those who dwell in the outbuildings, only four people make up the family: my father, kind-hearted and aging; myself, then nineteen; Madame Perrodon, my governess from Berne, whose gentle face has been part of my life since I can remember; and Mademoiselle de Lafontaine, a finishing governess who taught me French and German. We spoke in a jumble of tongues—French, broken English, and fluent English from my father and me—creating a chaotic mix that strangers found amusing. And sometimes, there were young friends who visited for weeks or months—girls close to my age, whom I occasionally returned to see. But beyond that, our social world was limited. Neighbors came rarely, and only from within five or six leagues. So, though I had company, I lived much alone.

My governesses had little real control over me—what child could resist when her only parent indulged her every whim? And so I grew up sheltered, unafraid of the things others feared. I knew nothing of ghosts or witches, no tales of spirits that crept through the night. That ignorance, I believe, made the first true horror of my life all the more terrible.

It happened when I was no older than six. I awoke one night in the nursery—a large room high in the castle, beneath a steep oak ceiling. The chamber was dim, lit only by moonlight filtering through the lattice. I looked around and saw neither the nursery maid nor my nurse. I thought myself abandoned, and felt a surge of anger—not fear. I began to whimper, preparing to cry out loud—when suddenly, I saw her.

A woman knelt beside my bed, her face pale yet beautiful, her hands tucked under the coverlet. She smiled gently, and reached out to stroke my cheek. I stared, not afraid, but strangely enchanted. She lay down beside me, drew me close, and I felt warmth and calm wash over me. I fell asleep again.

But I woke abruptly—shocked by a sharp, piercing pain in my chest, as if two needles had driven deep into my flesh. I screamed.

She flinched back, eyes wide, then slipped silently to the floor and vanished beneath the bed. Panic seized me. I shrieked until the house erupted in motion. Nurse, the maid, the housekeeper—all burst in, breathless, alarmed. They laughed softly at my tale, soothing me, but I saw the pallor on their faces, the tension in their movements. They searched the room—under the bed, behind furniture, in cupboards—then whispered urgently.

“Feel here,” the housekeeper murmured, pressing her hand into the hollow where she’d lain. “It’s still warm. Someone *was* there.”

They examined my chest where I pointed. No mark, no wound. Nothing visible. Yet I knew—I *knew*—it wasn’t a dream.

That night, the servants stayed awake in the nursery, watching over me. From then on, someone sat with me every night until I was nearly fourteen. I became nervous, trembling at shadows, unable to endure even daylight solitude. A doctor came—pale, elderly, with a long face pitted by smallpox and a chestnut wig. He prescribed medicine, which I loathed, and visited every other day.

The next morning, I was terrified. My father came up, stood by my bed, asked questions, laughed at the nurse’s reply, and kissed my forehead. “It was only a dream,” he said kindly. “Nothing to fear.”

But I knew better. It was not a dream. I remembered every detail—the woman’s face, her touch, the pain. The nursery maid insisted it had been her, that I must have been half-asleep. But I didn’t believe her. I remembered the old priest too—the venerable man in black robes who arrived with the nurse and housekeeper. His face was sweet, his voice soft as he took my hands and told me to pray: “Lord, hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus’ sake.” I repeated those words often after that. I remember him kneeling in the dim room, the women beside him, praying aloud in hushed tones, their voices trembling with earnestness.

Before that night, my memories are vague. Afterward, they blur again. But that moment—those scenes—remain sharp, vivid, like images from a nightmare that refuses to fade. I know now, looking back, that something unnatural entered my room that night. Something that should not have been there. And though I did not understand it then, I feel its echo still—cold, quiet, waiting.

II. A Guest

It is not often that one finds oneself confronted with a tale so extraordinary that it challenges the very limits of belief—yet I speak now of something I witnessed with my own eyes, and which, though strange beyond imagining, is no fiction. It was a summer evening, golden and still, when my father invited me to walk with him along the forest path that stretches before our schloss, a stretch of woodland so beautiful it seemed almost enchanted.

As we strolled beneath the tall trees, he told me that General Spielsdorf would not arrive as soon as he had hoped. The general had been expected the next day, bringing with him his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt—a young woman I had never met but whom I had heard described as bright, graceful, and full of charm. Her presence had become the centerpiece of my quiet dreams for weeks; I had looked forward to her arrival with all the eagerness of youth confined by solitude and routine. Now, that hope was dashed.

“Not until autumn,” my father said. “Perhaps two months from now.” He paused, then added softly, “And I am glad, dear, that you never knew Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt.”

I turned to him, startled. “Why?” I asked, my heart suddenly heavy.

“Because,” he replied, “she is dead.”

The words struck me like a blow. I had known she had been unwell—General Spielsdorf had mentioned it in a letter some weeks prior—but nothing had hinted at such a fate. I took the letter from my father’s hand. His handwriting trembled on the page, each line more frantic than the last.

“I have lost my darling daughter,” it began. “For as such I loved her. During the last days of dear Bertha’s illness, I could not write. Before then, I had no idea of her danger. She died in peace, in innocence, in the hope of eternal life. But the fiend who betrayed our hospitality—our trust—has done this. I thought I was welcoming virtue, joy, a companion for my lost Bertha. Heaven! What a fool I have been! I thank God she died without knowing the truth. She did not suspect what plagued her, nor the cursed passion of the creature responsible. I swear I will hunt down and destroy this monster. I am told there may be hope. For now, there is only darkness. I curse my pride, my blindness, my refusal to believe. Too late. I cannot think or speak clearly. I am undone. As soon as I recover, I shall begin my search—perhaps as far as Vienna. In autumn, two months hence, or earlier if I live, I will come to you—if you allow it. Then I shall tell you all that I dare not write now. Farewell. Pray for me, dear friend.”

I read it twice, then aloud to my father. The letter was a storm of grief and fury, torn between mourning and vengeance. Yet even as I listened, I felt the weight of something deeper—something unnatural—hanging over the words.

By the time I returned the letter, twilight had fallen. The sun had dipped behind the trees, leaving the sky streaked with crimson and violet, and the moon rose above the treetops, casting silver light across the valley. We walked slowly, the silence thick with unease.

At the drawbridge, we met Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, both out without bonnets, drawn by the beauty of the night. Their voices floated ahead of us, animated and poetic. They stood beside us, admiring the scene—the river winding through the mist, the bridge arched over the water, the ruins of an old tower silhouetted against the sky.

Madame Perrodon sighed dramatically. “Ah, how the moonlight sparkles on the windows of the schloss! Like fairy lights!”

Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, ever the mystic, leaned forward. “It is no ordinary moon tonight. Such brilliance—it is said to stir spirits, to awaken hidden forces. It affects dreams, madness, the nervous mind. Once, my cousin, sleeping on deck under such a moon, dreamed of an old woman clawing his face. When he woke, his features were twisted—never fully restored.”

She gestured toward the castle. “Look—see how the moon makes the windows gleam? As if unseen hands lit them for guests from another world.”

I smiled faintly, though my thoughts lingered on the letter. My father broke the silence.

“I feel… restless,” he murmured, quoting Shakespeare. “‘In truth I know not why I am so sad.’ It wearies me. I suppose it’s the general’s letter. Something terrible seems to hang over us.”

Just then, a sound cut through the quiet—hooves pounding on stone, wheels rolling fast. From the high ground above the bridge came a carriage, four horses, two riders ahead, two behind. It was a noble equipage, unmistakably of rank. We watched, transfixed.

Then, as the carriage crested the bridge, one horse reared, panicked. The others followed. The team exploded into a wild gallop, thundering down the road toward us, tossing the carriage like a leaf in a storm. From the window came a scream—long, clear, agonized.

We rushed forward. The horses, terrified, veered sharply at the lime tree by the roadside. The wheel caught the roots. I closed my eyes. A cry pierced the air.

When I opened them, chaos unfolded. Two horses lay on the ground. The carriage lay on its side, wheels spinning in the air. Men scrambled to untangle the harness. And from the wreckage, a woman emerged—tall, commanding, dressed in black velvet. Her hands were clasped, her face pale, yet her eyes burned with intensity.

Through the door, they lifted a young girl—slender, still, her skin unnaturally pale. She seemed unconscious, perhaps worse.

My father hurried to the woman, hat in hand. “Madame, I offer my home, my care—anything you need.”

The woman looked up, her voice trembling. “I must go. This journey is one of life and death. Every hour counts. My child cannot continue. I must leave her. How far is the nearest village?”

I stepped forward, heart racing. “Father, please—ask her to let her stay here! With us! It would be wonderful!”

He turned to the woman. “If you would entrust your daughter to my daughter and Madame Perrodon, and allow her to remain under my protection until your return, it would be a great honor. We would care for her as if she were our own.”

The woman hesitated. “That would be too much kindness,” she said, voice breaking. “Too much.”

“But you have just suffered a cruel disappointment,” my father insisted. “Your daughter has lost a cherished visit. If she stays with us, it may ease her sorrow. The nearest village offers no proper lodging. She cannot travel further tonight. You must part with her—and nowhere would she be safer, more cared for, than here.”

She studied him, then nodded slowly. There was a dignity about her—so regal, so composed—that I could not doubt her importance.

The carriage was righted. The horses calmed. She cast a glance at her daughter—brief, cold, almost distant. Then she beckoned my father aside. They spoke in hushed tones, her face stern, her expression hard.

I watched, puzzled. Why did she seem so detached? Why the sudden change?

After a few moments, she returned. Kneeling beside her daughter, she whispered something—Madame Perrodon thought a blessing. Then she kissed her quickly, climbed into the carriage, and the doors shut.

The footmen leapt into place. The outriders spurred their horses. The postilions cracked their whips. The carriage surged forward, racing away into the night, followed by the two riders.

And as it vanished into the shadows, I could not shake the feeling that something was terribly wrong.

The girl—still unconscious—was laid gently on the bank. Her breath was shallow. Her lips, slightly parted, revealed a hint of blood.

But as I looked at her, I saw something else—something I could not name.

Her eyes, though closed, seemed to follow me.

And in the silence after the carriage left, the moon shone brighter than ever.

III. We Compare Notes

We watched the procession vanish into the mist-laden woods, its echoes fading into silence as hooves and wheels dissolved into the night. The only proof that it had ever happened was the young woman who stirred just then, opening her eyes. I couldn’t see her face—she turned away—but I heard a soft, sweet voice whisper, “Where is mamma?”

Madame Perrodon answered gently, offering quiet reassurance. Then came another question: “Where am I? What is this place?” And after a pause, “I don’t see the carriage… and Matska—where is she?”

Madame responded to each with care, helping her piece together the events of the evening. Slowly, the girl recalled the accident—the overturned carriage, the fall—and sighed in relief when told no one had been hurt. But when she learned her mother had left her behind for three months, tears welled up.

I started to offer my own comfort, but Mademoiselle De Lafontaine touched my arm. “Don’t approach,” she said softly. “One at a time is all she can bear now. Even a small emotion might overwhelm her.”

I nodded, thinking I’d visit her later, once she was settled. Meanwhile, my father had sent a servant galloping for the doctor, who lived two leagues distant. A bedroom was readied for the guest, and soon she rose, leaning on Madame’s arm, crossing the drawbridge and entering the castle gate. Servants waited in the hall, guiding her swiftly to her room.

The drawing room where we gathered that night was long and grand, with four tall windows overlooking the moat and forest beyond. It was furnished in dark carved oak—massive cabinets, chairs upholstered in crimson Utrecht velvet. The walls were draped in tapestry, framed in gold, depicting scenes of hunting, hawking, and ancient revelry. Figures loomed life-sized, dressed in curious old garb. It was stately, yes, but not cold—warm enough for tea, which my father insisted on serving every evening, his patriotic pride in the national drink unshaken by coffee or chocolate.

That night, candles flickered as we discussed the evening’s events. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined us. The young stranger had barely lain down before slipping into deep sleep; the ladies had left her under the care of a maid.

“How do you like our guest?” I asked Madame as she entered. “Tell me everything about her.”

“I like her immensely,” she replied. “She’s, I think, the most exquisite creature I’ve ever seen—your age, gentle, refined.”

“She’s absolutely beautiful,” interjected Mademoiselle, who had peeked into the room. “And such a lovely voice!”

Then she asked, “Did you notice the woman in the carriage after it was upright again? She didn’t get out—just stared from the window.”

“No,” we admitted. “We didn’t see her.”

Mademoiselle described her: a grotesque figure in black, wearing a colorful turban, her eyes gleaming unnaturally white, her teeth bared in a grin of fury. She’d stared at the ladies, nodding, grinning—mocking.

“And those servants?” Madame added. “Such an ugly, sullen lot.”

“Yes,” my father said, stepping in. “The worst-looking men I’ve ever seen—dogged, hollow-faced, dark-eyed. I hope they don’t rob the poor girl in the woods. Though they’re clever—fixed everything in seconds.”

“Perhaps they’re just exhausted,” Madame suggested. “But their faces… so lean, so grim. I’m curious. Still, she’ll tell us tomorrow, if she’s well enough.”

“I doubt she will,” my father said, smiling faintly, giving a subtle nod—as if he knew something he wasn’t ready to share.

This only made us more eager to know what had passed between him and the woman in black velvet during their brief, intense conversation before she departed.

As soon as we were alone, I begged him to reveal it. He hesitated only a moment.

“There’s no reason not to tell you,” he began. “She said she didn’t want to trouble us with her daughter—said she was delicate, nervous, but not subject to seizures. She even volunteered that herself—no illusions, perfectly sane.”

“How odd,” I interrupted. “Why say all that?”

“It was unnecessary,” he laughed, “but it was said. Then she said: ‘I am making a long journey of *vital* importance’—she stressed the word—‘rapid, secret. I shall return for my child in three months. Until then, she will remain silent about who we are, where we come from, or where we’re going.’ That was all. She spoke fluent French. When she said *secret*, she paused—looked at me with a terrible intensity. I think it matters deeply to her.”

He glanced toward the door. “You saw how quickly she left. I wonder if I’ve done something foolish—taking in her daughter.”

But I felt nothing but excitement. I longed to meet her, to speak with her. For those living in cities, a new friend might be ordinary—but here, in our isolation, such a thing was an event.

The doctor didn’t arrive until nearly one o’clock. I couldn’t have slept, not even if I’d tried. I couldn’t imagine catching up to the carriage that had vanished into the night with the woman in black.

When the physician finally came down, he gave a favorable report. The girl was sitting up, pulse steady, no injury, no lingering shock. She was fully recovered.

There could be no harm, then, in visiting her—if she wished it. With permission granted, I sent a servant to ask if she would receive me.

The reply came instantly: she desired nothing more than rest.

I didn’t hesitate.

Her room was one of the finest in the schloss—perhaps too grand. Opposite the foot of the bed hung a somber tapestry: Cleopatra with asps coiled around her chest. Other classical scenes, faded but solemn, adorned the walls. Yet gold carvings and rich colors softened the gloom. Candles burned beside the bed.

She sat up, wrapped in a silk dressing gown embroidered with flowers, lined with thick quilted silk—her mother’s gift, thrown over her feet as she lay on the ground.

And then—when I reached her bedside, about to greet her—something struck me dumb. I stepped back, stunned.

It was her face.

The very face I had seen in childhood dreams, fixed in memory, haunting me through years of silent dread. It was beautiful—yes, undeniably so—but it wore the same melancholy expression I remembered. Then, in an instant, it shifted—into a strange, knowing smile.

A silence stretched for a full minute. Then she spoke. I could not.

“How wonderful,” she breathed. “Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream. It has haunted me ever since.”

“Wonderful indeed,” I managed, forcing myself to speak through the horror that had seized me. “Twelve years ago, I saw you—either in vision or reality. I never forgot your face. It has stayed before my eyes.”

Her smile softened. The strangeness melted into warmth. Her cheeks dimpled. She seemed both intelligent and kind. I felt reassured.

I took her hand as I welcomed her, speaking of the joy her arrival had brought us all—especially me. I was shy, as lonely people often are, but the moment made me bold.

She pressed my hand, laid hers over mine, and her eyes blazed as she looked at me—smiling again, blushing. She returned my greeting with grace.

I sat beside her, still stunned. She said:

“I must tell you my dream. It’s so strange—we both dreamed of each other, seeing each other exactly as we are now, though we were children. I was six, waking from a troubled dream. I found myself in a room unlike my nursery—rough wood paneling, cupboards, beds, benches. All empty. No one but me. I admired an iron candlestick with two arms—something I’d recognize again. I crept under a bed to reach the window. As I emerged, I heard crying. Looking up, still on my knees—I saw you. You—exactly as you are now. Golden hair, blue eyes, those lips—your lips.

“You captivated me. I climbed onto the bed, threw my arms around you. I think we both fell asleep. Then—a scream. You sat up, screaming. I was terrified, slipped down, lost consciousness. When I woke, I was back in my nursery. But your face—never forgotten. I couldn’t be mistaken. *You are* the one I saw.”

Now it was my turn. I told her my own dream—the same room, the same candlestick, the same moment of recognition. She listened, wide-eyed, breathless.

“I don’t know which of us should be more afraid,” she said, smiling again. “If you weren_tE so pretty, I’d be terrified. But as you are—so young, so radiant—I feel only that I’ve known you twelve years. I’ve already a right to your friendship. Does it feel the same to you? I’ve never had a friend—will I find one now?”

She sighed, her dark eyes gazing at me with fierce longing.

Truthfully, I felt something strange. I was drawn to her—deeply. But beneath it, a flicker of repulsion. Yet the pull far outweighed it. She fascinated me—beautiful, captivating, impossible to resist.

I noticed then the weariness creeping into her. I stood.

“The doctor says you should have a maid tonight,” I said. “One of ours waits—quiet, dependable.”

“How kind,” she whispered. “But I can’t sleep with someone watching. I shan’t need help—and may I confess… I fear robbers. Our home was robbed once. Two servants killed. I always lock my door. It’s become a habit. And you look so kind—I know you’ll forgive me.” She pointed to the key in the lock.

She pulled me close, holding me tightly. “Good night, darling,” she murmured into my ear. “It’s hard to leave you. But tomorrow—later, not early—I’ll see you again.”

She sank back, sighing, her eyes following me with tenderness and sorrow. “Good night, dear friend,” she whispered.

Young people love on impulse. I was flattered by her affection—so open, so trusting. I liked the way she claimed me as a friend already, demanded closeness.

The next day, we met again. I was enchanted—by her beauty, her wit, her charm. In daylight, she was even more striking. The horror of the dream-face had faded, replaced by the real presence of a woman who smiled at me with warmth and mystery.

She confessed she’d felt the same shock, the same fleeting unease. Now, we laughed together—over the terror we’d each felt, the strange bond that tied us across time and memory.

And yet—beneath the laughter, something lingered. A shadow. A whisper of something older, darker, waiting.

IV. Her Habits—A Saunter

I told you I was enchanted by her in most ways. Yet there were things about her that unsettled me, even as they drew me closer. She stood a little above the average height for a woman, slender and graceful beyond compare. Her movements, however, carried a peculiar slowness—a languor so deep it seemed almost unnatural. There was nothing in her appearance to suggest illness, yet she moved like one weighed down by invisible chains. Her skin glowed with a rich, warm hue; her features were delicate and finely shaped; her eyes large, dark, and luminous, like pools of midnight oil. And then there was her hair—thick, long, and impossibly soft, cascading down her back like a waterfall of shadowed gold. I often ran my hands through it, marveling at its weight, its silkiness, how it fell in waves when she leaned back in her chair, speaking in that low, sweet voice that seemed to coil around my thoughts.

I loved to untie her hair, to braid it, to watch it tumble over her shoulders as she spoke. But now, looking back, I shudder at the memory. How could I have been so blind?

There were things she would not tell me—things she guarded fiercely. From the first night I met her, she had won my trust with her quiet grace and gentle confidence. But she kept herself wrapped in silence. Her mother, her past, her family—all remained hidden behind a veil of deliberate secrecy. I know now that I should have respected that. My father had been warned by the stately woman in black velvet, and perhaps I should have heeded it. But curiosity is a cruel master. It gnaws at the mind, refuses to be silenced. And I, a girl barely grown, could not endure being denied answers.

What harm could it do to know? I asked myself again and again. Why wouldn’t she trust me? I swore, with all sincerity, that I would never betray a word she might confide. Yet she refused—always, gently but firmly. There was a coldness in her smile, a melancholy so profound it felt less like sorrow than like something deeper, older. Not the sadness of youth, but of someone who has seen too much, or lived too long.

We never quarreled. She would not allow it. But I pressed her anyway. Not always rudely—sometimes I waited, watching for an opening, letting my questions slip out like whispers. Once or twice, I challenged her more directly. Each time, she evaded me with such beauty of expression, such tender regret, that I could not stay angry. She would take my hands, pull me close, rest her cheek against mine, and murmur into my ear:

“Dearest, your heart is wounded. Do not think me cruel. I obey a law I cannot break—a law written in my blood, in my strength, in my weakness. When you feel pain, I bleed with you. I live in your warmth, and you will die—die sweetly—into me. I cannot help it. As I draw near, you will draw near to others, and taste the same rapture, the same torment. For now, do not ask. Trust me. Trust me wholly.”

Her words were like music, lulling me into stillness. Her embrace tightened, trembling, her lips brushing my skin in soft, lingering kisses. I wanted to pull away—but my body refused. Her voice was a spell, weaving through my bones, dissolving resistance. In those moments, I felt both ecstasy and dread. A strange fire burned within me, mixed with fear, with loathing. I loved her—no, I adored her—and yet I recoiled. It was madness, and I knew it. But I could not name it.

Afterward, I would sit in silence, shaken. The memory of those moments haunts me still—ten years later, my hand trembling as I write, my mind clouded by horror, yet clear enough to remember the shape of her face, the sound of her voice.

Between these episodes, she was almost ordinary. Giddy, playful, sometimes lost in thought. Her manner was girlish, though always marked by a certain weariness—like a flower blooming in twilight. She came down late, usually after one o’clock, sipping chocolate without eating anything. Then we would walk—just a slow saunter through the woods, beneath the trees. She would grow tired quickly, collapsing onto a bench or returning to the schloss. But her mind never lagged. She talked endlessly, cleverly, with sharp observations and sudden flashes of insight. Occasionally, she mentioned her homeland—vague references to distant lands, customs we did not know, people whose ways were strange and ancient. I began to suspect her origins lay far beyond what I had imagined.

One afternoon, as we sat beneath the trees, a funeral passed by. It was the daughter of a forest ranger—pretty, young, and beloved. Her father walked behind the coffin, broken-hearted. Peasants followed in pairs, singing a hymn in mournful harmony. I rose to show respect, joining their song. But Carmilla grabbed my arm sharply.

“You don’t see how discordant that is?” she said, her voice clipped.

“It’s beautiful,” I replied, annoyed. “And I don’t want to offend them.”

She stopped me again. “You’re piercing my ears!” she hissed, pressing her fingers to her temples. “How can you know our religions are the same? Your rituals wound me. I hate funerals. Everyone dies. They’re happier when they do. Come home.”

“My father went to the churchyard with the priest. I thought you knew she was being buried today.”

“She? I don’t care about peasants. I don’t know who she is.” Her eyes flashed—cold, sudden, dangerous. “She was the girl who saw a ghost two weeks ago. She’s been dying ever since. Died yesterday.”

“Don’t speak of ghosts,” I whispered. “I’ll never sleep if you do.”

“There’s no plague, is there?” I continued. “The swineherd’s wife died last week. She said something seized her throat in bed, nearly strangled her. She was fine the day before. Then she collapsed. No fever, no illness—just... gone.”

Carmilla turned away. “Her funeral is over. Her hymn is sung. Let us leave this noise. Sit beside me. Hold my hand. Harder. Harder.”

We moved to another bench. She sat down. Then—her face changed. It drained of color, turning ashen. Her teeth clenched. Her hands curled into fists. She stared at the ground, trembling violently, as if fighting some inner storm. A low moan escaped her, ragged and desperate. It was not grief—not quite. It was something else—something primal. Then, slowly, the convulsions eased.

“There,” she said, breathless. “That’s what happens when you strangle people with hymns. Hold me. Keep me still. It’s passing.”

And it did. She calmed, then became unusually cheerful, chattering brightly as we walked back. This was the first time I’d seen her truly ill—the first time I’d seen her lose control. And the first time I’d seen anger in her.

Later, we stood at a window when a figure entered the courtyard: a hunchbacked wanderer I recognized well. He wore a pointed beard, dressed in buff, black, and scarlet, adorned with straps and belts, carrying a magic lantern and two boxes—one holding a salamander, the other a mandrake stitched from monkey, parrot, and hedgehog parts. He had a fiddle, foils, masks, and a staff with copper ends. His dog followed, stopping short at the drawbridge, then howling in protest.

He bowed deeply, speaking in broken French and German, then began to play and sing a lively tune, dancing with absurd energy. He approached the window, hat in hand, fiddle under his arm, babbling about his wonders.

“Will your ladyships buy an amulet against the oupire?” he called. “It’s spreading through the woods like the wolf. People die left and right. This charm—pinned to your pillow—will protect you. Laugh in its face!”

Carmilla bought one. So did I.

Then, as he looked up, his eye locked onto hers. He paused. Unrolled a leather case filled with tiny tools.

“See here, my lady,” he said, addressing me. “I practice dentistry among other arts. Plague take the dog! Silence, beast! That howling makes it hard to hear. Now, your noble friend—this young lady—has a tooth. Sharp. Long. Pointed. Like a needle. Ha, ha! I see it clearly. If it causes pain, I can fix it. File it, blunt it. Make it fit a beautiful young lady. Hey? Is she offended?”

Carmilla stepped back, furious. “How dare he insult us? Where is your father? I’ll demand justice. My father would have him tied to the pump and flogged!”

She retreated, then just as suddenly, her rage vanished. She sat, calm again, as if nothing had happened.

That evening, my father returned in low spirits. Another case—similar to the two recent deaths. A peasant girl, only a mile away, was sinking fast. She described the same symptoms: a choking grip, a presence in the room, a feeling of suffocation.

“All natural causes,” my father said. “They infect each other with superstition. Their imaginations repeat the horrors they’ve heard.”

“But that frightens me,” Carmilla said. “To imagine such things—wouldn’t it be worse than reality?”

“We are in God’s hands,” my father replied. “Nothing happens without His permission. Those who love Him will be safe.”

“Creator?” Carmilla murmured. “Nature? All things come from Nature—don’t they? Heaven, earth, under the earth—everything acts as Nature ordains. I believe so.”

The doctor arrived later. He was old, powdered, smooth-faced, skilled but cautious. After talking with my father, they emerged together.

“I wonder at a wise man like you,” my father said. “What do you say to hippogriffs and dragons?”

The doctor smiled faintly. “Life and death are mysterious states. We know little of either.”

I did not understand then. But now—I think I do.

V. A Wonderful Likeness

That evening, a messenger arrived from Gratz—dark-faced, solemn, the son of the picture restorer—bringing with him a horse and cart laden with two massive packing cases, each crammed with portraits. The journey had taken ten leagues, and whenever a traveler came from our distant capital, we gathered in the hall, eager for news. This time, the arrival stirred something deeper than curiosity in our isolated home. The cases were left in the great hall while the man was ushered to supper by the servants.

Afterward, armed with hammer, chisel, and screwdriver, he returned with assistants, and we all assembled to watch as the contents were unveiled. Carmilla sat apart, her gaze distant, uninterested, as one after another the old paintings emerged—mostly portraits, many of them centuries old, their faces obscured by grime and time. My mother came from an ancient Hungarian line, and most of these works had passed down through her bloodline. My father held a list, reading aloud the numbers as the artist pulled forth each restored piece.

I cannot say the art was masterful, but it was undeniably aged, some of it curiously strange. For me, they were revelations—faces I’d never seen before, now emerging from decades of dust and shadow.

“There is one portrait I have not yet seen,” my father said. “In the corner, I believe, is the name ‘Marcia Karnstein,’ and the date 1698. I am curious to see how it has turned out.”

I remembered it—a small painting, nearly square, about a foot and a half high, frameless, so blackened by time that it was nothing more than a silhouette. Now, the restorer brought it forward, his hands trembling slightly with pride.

And then I saw it.

It was breathtaking. Not merely a likeness—but alive. It was Carmilla.

“Carmilla, dear,” I whispered, “this is impossible. Look—here you are, smiling, breathing, as if you could speak at any moment. Isn’t it extraordinary? Even the mole on your throat—just like yours.”

My father chuckled. “Yes, it’s a remarkable likeness,” he said, though his eyes lingered elsewhere, speaking instead to the restorer about technique, pigments, restoration methods. He seemed unimpressed. But I stood transfixed, unable to tear my gaze away.

“May I hang this in my room, Papa?” I asked softly.

“Of course, my dear,” he replied, smiling. “If it means so much to you, it must be even more beautiful than I imagined.”

Carmilla did not respond. She remained still, her head tilted slightly, her long-lashed eyes fixed on me with quiet intensity. A faint smile curled her lips—not of amusement, but of something deeper, darker. Then she looked down, as if caught in thought.

“And now,” I said, stepping closer, “you can read the name clearly. It’s not Marcia. It’s written in gold. Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. And there’s a tiny coronet above and below the date: A.D. 1698. You know—my mother was descended from the Karnsteins.”

“Oh?” she murmured, almost dreamily. “So am I. Very far back. Very ancient. Are there any Karnsteins living now?”

“None who carry the name,” I said. “The family fell during civil wars long ago. But the ruins of their castle are only three miles from here.”

“How fascinating,” she sighed, her voice barely audible. Then she glanced toward the open door of the hall. “Look—the moonlight. So beautiful. Would you like to walk outside? Just a little way, down to the drawbridge?”

“It’s so like the night you came to us,” I said.

She smiled, but it was slow, sorrowful. “Do you remember?” she breathed.

“I remember everything,” I answered. “And I’m glad you came.”

She drew closer, her arm slipping around my waist. “You asked for a portrait of me,” she whispered, her voice soft as silk. “To hang in your room.”

“Yes,” I said, my heart quickening. “Because it looks just like you.”

She leaned into me, pressing her cheek against mine, her breath warm. “How romantic you are,” I murmured. “When you tell your story someday, it will be full of grand passion.”

She kissed me—quietly, without sound.

“I am sure,” I said, “you’ve loved someone. There must be a secret love in your heart.”

“No,” she whispered. “I have loved no one. Never. Unless… unless it should be you.”

She looked radiant under the silver light. Then, suddenly, her face changed. Her eyes flickered—once, twice—and then dimmed. She hid her face in my hair, her body trembling, her hand clenched tightly in mine. Sobs rose in her chest, silent but deep.

“Darling,” she murmured, her voice breaking. “I live in you. And you would die for me. I love you so.”

I stepped back, startled.

Her face was pale, expressionless. Her eyes were blank, lifeless.

“Is it cold?” she asked drowsily. “I feel… faint. As if I’ve been dreaming. Let us go inside. Come. Come in.”

“You’re ill, Carmilla,” I said, alarmed. “You look weak. You must drink something—wine, perhaps.”

“I’ll be fine,” she insisted, though her voice wavered. “Just a moment. Yes—give me wine. I’ll be myself again soon.”

We walked slowly toward the door, but she paused once more.

“Let us look one last time,” she said. “Perhaps this is the final time I’ll see the moonlight with you.”

I watched her closely. “Are you truly better?”

She nodded, forcing a smile. “Yes. I am well. Only weakness—nothing more. They call me languid. I tire easily. I can scarcely walk as far as a child. But it passes. In a moment, I am whole again.”

And indeed, she was. By the time we reached the drawing-room, she was animated, laughing, speaking freely. The strange stillness had vanished.

But that night, as I lay awake, I felt a shift—an unsettling change in the air. Something had happened. Something unseen. And though Carmilla appeared normal, I sensed a new darkness stirring beneath her skin.

A darkness that had begun, perhaps, with the portrait.

VI. A Very Strange Agony

When we entered the drawing room and settled down for coffee and chocolate—though Carmilla took none—she seemed restored to her usual self. Madame and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined us, and soon a small card game began. Papa came in shortly after, as he always did, for what he called his “dish of tea.” Once the game ended, he sat beside Carmilla on the sofa and asked, with a touch of concern, whether she had heard from her mother since arriving.

“No,” she replied quietly.

He pressed further: “Do you know where a letter might reach her now?”

“I cannot say,” she answered, her voice veiled. “But I’ve been thinking of leaving you. You’ve been too generous, too kind. I’ve caused you endless trouble, and I’d like to take a carriage tomorrow and ride after her. I know where I’ll find her eventually—though I can’t tell you yet.”

“Not a thought of it!” my father declared, much to my relief. “We couldn’t bear to lose you. And I won’t allow it—unless your mother herself gives her permission. She was kind enough to let you stay until she returns. I’d be far more at ease knowing you’ve heard from her. But tonight, the news about the mysterious illness spreading through our district is growing worse. And as your host, I feel a heavy responsibility—especially without guidance from your mother. Still, I’ll do my best. One thing is certain: you must not leave without her explicit consent. We would suffer too deeply to part so easily.”

“Thank you, sir,” she said, smiling softly, “a thousand times for your hospitality. You’ve all been so kind. I’ve rarely known such happiness as I’ve found here—in this beautiful chateau, under your care, and in the company of your dear daughter.”

He bowed gallantly, kissed her hand, and smiled with genuine pleasure at her words.

As usual, I accompanied Carmilla to her room and stayed while she prepared for bed. After a moment, I ventured, “Do you think you’ll ever fully confide in me?”

She turned, eyes glinting with quiet amusement, but said nothing—only smiled.

“You won’t answer?” I asked.

“You shouldn’t expect a pleasant reply. I oughtn’t have asked.”

“Oh, but you were right to ask—any question. You don’t know how precious you are to me. If you did, you’d never think any secret too great to share. But I am bound by vows—more strictly than any nun. I cannot tell you my story yet—not even to you. The time is near, though. When it comes, you’ll understand everything. You may think me cruel, selfish—even monstrous. But love is always selfish—the fiercer it burns, the more possessive it becomes. How jealous I am, you cannot imagine. You must come with me, loving me, into death—or hate me, and still follow me, hating me beyond death, beyond the grave. There is no such word as indifference in my nature. I cannot endure it.”

“Now you’re speaking nonsense again,” I said quickly, trying to lighten the mood.

“Not I,” she whispered, her voice low and serious. “Though I’m foolish, full of whims and fancies—I’ll speak plainly for your sake. Have you ever been to a ball?”

“No,” I replied. “What’s it like? How wonderful it must be.”

“I almost forget—it was years ago,” she murmured, laughing faintly.

“You’re not that old. Your first ball can’t be long past.”

“I remember every detail—with effort. It’s as if I see it through water—distorted, rippling, yet clear. That night… something happened. Something that blurred the memory, dimmed its colors. I was nearly murdered in my bed—stabbed here,” she touched her chest, “and I’ve never been the same since.”

“Were you close to dying?”

“Yes—very close. A cruel love. A strange, terrible love that sought my life. Love demands sacrifice. No sacrifice without blood.”

She paused, then added, “Let us sleep now. I feel so weary. How can I possibly get up to lock my door?”

She lay back, her delicate hands tangled in her dark, wavy hair, her head resting on the pillow, her eyes following me with a soft, unreadable smile. I bid her goodnight and slipped out, a cold unease settling over me.

I often wondered if our guest ever prayed. I had never seen her on her knees. In the mornings, she appeared only after our family prayers had finished. At night, she never joined us in the hall for our brief evening devotions. Had it not been for an offhand remark during idle talk—that she had been baptized—I might have doubted her faith altogether. Religion was a subject she never mentioned.

If I had known more of the world, I might not have been so startled by her silence. But her habits had begun to affect me. I had taken to locking my bedroom door, just as she did, convinced of the dangers lurking in the dark—midnight intruders, assassins prowling the halls. I even copied her ritual: before retiring, I would search the corners of my room, checking for hidden threats, as if some unseen foe might be waiting, ensconced in shadow.

Satisfied, I climbed into bed. A candle burned steadily beside me—a lifelong habit, one I could never break. Its light was my shield against the unknown.

But dreams pass through walls, through stone and glass, and laugh at locks.

That night, I dreamed—but not as one dreams. I was awake, yet asleep. I knew I lay in my bed, in my room, exactly as I was. The chamber was dim, the air thick with silence. Then I saw movement at the foot of the bed—something shifting, indistinct at first. Slowly, it resolved into form: a creature black as soot, shaped like a monstrous cat. It was four or five feet long, stretching the full length of the hearthrug as it paced back and forth, its movements fluid, restless, unnatural—like a beast trapped in a cage.

I tried to scream—but no sound came. My throat was sealed.

It moved faster. The darkness deepened. Soon, I could see nothing but two points of light—eyes—glowing in the void.

Then it leapt onto the bed.

The eyes came close—too close—and suddenly, a sharp, searing pain pierced my chest. Two stabs, inches apart, as if needles had driven deep into my flesh.

I woke with a cry.

The candle still burned. The room was lit. And standing at the foot of the bed—just to the right—was a woman.

She wore a loose, dark dress. Her hair fell freely, covering her shoulders. She stood perfectly still. Not a breath, not a flicker—like a statue carved from stone.

I stared.

And then—she shifted. Closer to the door. The door opened. She stepped out.

I exhaled, trembling, finally able to move.

My first thought: *Carmilla.* She must have played a trick. I must have forgotten to lock my door.

I rushed to it—my hands shaking—and found it fastened from within, just as I always did.

I froze.

I didn’t dare open it.

Terror gripped me. I threw myself back into bed, pulled the covers over my head, and lay there—motionless, breathless—until morning came.

VII. Descending

Even now, the memory of that night clings to me like a shadow—cold, unrelenting, and impossible to shake. It was not the fleeting dread of a nightmare, nor the kind of fear that fades with morning light. This horror deepened with time, seeping into the very walls of my room, staining the furniture, the silence, the air itself. I could not bear to be alone after dawn broke. The thought of solitude filled me with a dread too vast for words.

I longed to tell my father—but two fears held me back. One, that he would laugh, dismissing my tale as mere fancy, and I could not endure such mockery. The other, that he might suspect I had fallen victim to the same mysterious illness that had swept through our village—a sickness whispered about in hushed tones, one that left no trace but death. I felt no such symptoms myself, yet I feared his frail health might not withstand the shock. So I kept silent.

My companions—Madame Perrodon, kind and gentle, and Mademoiselle Lafontaine, lively and quick-witted—sensed my distress. They noticed how pale I grew, how often I trembled without cause. At last, I confessed everything, the whole terrible truth of what had happened.

Mademoiselle laughed lightly. “Ah, so it’s true! Martin says the lime tree walk behind Carmilla’s window is haunted.”
“Nonsense!” Madame snapped, though her voice lacked conviction. “Who spreads such tales?”
“Martin,” replied Mademoiselle, “who saw the same woman twice before sunrise, walking slowly down the path.”
“Oh, well then,” said Madame, “perhaps it’s just cows being milked in the fields.”
“I suppose,” Mademoiselle shrugged, “but Martin is terrified—and never have I seen a man more easily frightened.”

“I mustn’t mention it to Carmilla,” I interrupted, “she can see the walk from her window, and she’s far more timid than I am.”

That day, Carmilla came down later than usual. As soon as we were alone, she spoke.
“I was so frightened last night,” she whispered, her eyes wide. “I dreamed something black crept around my bed. I woke in terror—sure I saw a dark figure near the fireplace. But I reached under my pillow… and touched the charm. The moment I did, it vanished. I know—I *know*—if I hadn’t had it, something dreadful would have come for me. Just like those poor people they spoke of…”

She looked at me, pleading. “Tell me—did you have it?”

“No,” I admitted. “I’d dropped it into a vase in the drawing room. But tonight, I’ll take it with me. You believe in it so strongly.”

And yet, even now, I cannot explain how I managed to lie alone in my room that night, heart pounding, every nerve taut. I pinned the charm to my pillow, and slept—deeply, soundly, more peacefully than I had in weeks. The next night was the same. Sleep came swiftly, dreamless, restorative.

But when I woke, a strange lassitude settled over me—soft, heavy, almost pleasant. A melancholy, quiet and profound, wrapped around my thoughts like a shroud. I did not resist it. In fact, I welcomed it. Thoughts of death began to drift into my mind—not with fear, but with a strange serenity. I felt myself slipping away, slowly, gently, as if sinking into water. And somehow, I did not wish to rise.

I refused to admit I was ill. I refused to send for the doctor. I refused to tell my father. I told myself I was only tired, perhaps nervous. But inside, something was changing—something beyond comprehension.

Carmilla grew more attentive, more tender. Her affection deepened into something intense, almost unnerving. She would gaze at me for long moments, her eyes fixed, her breath shallow, as if drinking in my presence. It unsettled me—like watching someone on the edge of madness.

Yet I did not protest. I let her stay close. I let her touch me. I let the weakness grow.

For some nights, I slept deeply. But each morning brought the same weariness, the same dull ache in my bones. I was no longer the girl I once was. A new sensation began to haunt my dreams—first faint, then growing stronger: a cold, electric thrill, like stepping into a river current at midnight. Then came the dreams—vague, endless, full of shadows and voices I could not name. I remembered only darkness, and whispers, and a single voice—deep, clear, female—speaking from afar, slow and solemn, sending chills through my soul.

Sometimes, I felt a hand glide softly across my cheek, down my neck. Sometimes, warm lips brushed my skin—lingering, tender, climbing higher until they reached my throat. Then, stillness. My breath caught. My heart pounded. A sob rose in my chest, swelling into a choking pressure. Then—darkness. Unconsciousness.

Three weeks had passed since it began. My face had grown pale, my eyes hollowed, shadowed beneath. My father asked again and again if I was ill. I insisted I was fine. In truth, I had no pain. No fever. No visible sickness. Only this creeping emptiness, this strange peace that felt like surrender.

It could not be the oupire—the vampire sickness the peasants spoke of. That took days, not weeks. And it ended quickly. I was not dying fast. I was fading.

Carmilla, too, complained of dreams—of heat, of feverish visions—but nothing like mine. Hers were restless, uneasy. Mine were terrifying. If I had known what was happening, I would have screamed for help. But I was numb. Drugged by something unseen, something invisible, something ancient.

Then came the dream that changed everything.

This time, instead of the distant voice, I heard one close—sweet, soft, yet chilling:
“Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin.”

And suddenly—light.

I sat up, gasping. There stood Carmilla, at the foot of my bed, in her white nightdress, drenched in blood—from her chin to her feet, a crimson stain spreading across her skin like ink in water.

I shrieked.

I leapt from the bed, stumbling into the hallway, screaming for help. Madame and Mademoiselle rushed out, lanterns in hand. The lamp burned bright in the hall. They saw me—pale, trembling, wild-eyed—and understood at once.

We ran to Carmilla’s door. We knocked. We pounded. We called her name. No answer.

The door was locked.

Panic seized us. We backed away, hearts racing, and rang the bell frantically. But my father’s room was far off—beyond hearing. To reach him meant crossing the house in the dark, and none of us dared.

Servants arrived, drawn by the noise. I had thrown on my dressing gown and slippers. My companions were already dressed. We rushed to the door together, shouting, demanding entry.

They forced the lock.

We stepped inside, lights raised high.

The room was untouched. Exactly as I had left it. The bed neatly made. The chair where she’d sat. The mirror, the dresser—everything in place.

But Carmilla was gone.

Not asleep. Not resting. Gone.

And the blood—where had it come from? Where had it gone?

Only silence answered us.

VIII. Search

The room stood undisturbed, save for the chaos of our sudden entry—no sign of struggle, no disarray, nothing to suggest a hasty departure. As the initial shock wore off, our hearts began to slow, and we dismissed the men who had burst in with such haste. Mademoiselle, however, whispered a thought that sent a chill through me: perhaps Carmilla had been awakened by the noise at her door, seized by panic, and fled into hiding—behind a curtain, within a wardrobe—anywhere she might be concealed until the commotion passed and the guards withdrew.

We resumed our search, calling her name again and again, but there was no answer. The silence deepened. Our confusion grew heavier with each passing moment. We checked the windows—barred tightly. I pleaded aloud, my voice trembling: “Carmilla, if you’re hidden, please—please stop this torment. Come out. Let us know you’re safe.” But only the wind replied.

I became certain she was not here. Not in this room. Not in the dressing chamber beyond, whose door remained locked from the inside. She could not have passed it without leaving a trace. Yet where could she have gone?

Had she found one of those hidden passages the old housekeeper once spoke of—secret corridors said to wind through the ancient walls of the château, forgotten now except in ghostly rumor? Such things were possible in a place like this, where stone and shadow held secrets older than memory.

Time crept on. It was past four o’clock. Darkness clung to the halls like smoke. I decided to wait out the night in Madame’s room, though every minute felt like an eternity. Dawn brought no answers.

By morning, the entire household was in turmoil. My father led the search, his face drawn with worry. Every corner of the château was scoured—walls, attics, cellars, closets. The gardens were combed. The stream near the orchard was about to be dragged. Still, no trace of Carmilla. No footprints. No cloth. No clue.

My father was broken. How would he explain this to her mother when she returned? And I—my heart was torn. Not with grief over loss, but with fear, with dread, with something deeper, darker than sorrow. I couldn’t imagine life without her.

It was now past one o’clock. No word. No sign. Desperation rising, I ran up to her chamber, breathless, expecting only emptiness.

And then I saw her.

She stood at the dressing table, pale as moonlight, her hair loose, her eyes wide with terror. I froze. I blinked. Was this real? Then she raised a slender finger, silent, beckoning me forward.

I rushed to her, tears blurring my vision. I threw my arms around her, kissed her cheeks, her forehead, over and over, sobbing with relief. “You’re alive! You’re here!” I cried.

I dashed to the bell and rang it wildly, summoning everyone—servants, family, anyone who could bring news to my father.

“Dear Carmilla,” I gasped, “what happened? Where were you? How did you return?”

She looked at me, her lips trembling. “Last night… was a night of wonders,” she whispered.

“Then tell me everything,” I begged. “Please—explain.”

“It was just after two,” she said, her voice low, “when I fell asleep as usual—doors locked, both the dressing room and the gallery door secured. I slept deeply. No dreams. Nothing. But when I woke—just now—I was on the sofa in the dressing room. The door between the rooms was open. The outer door—forced.”

Her words hung in the air.

“How?” I asked. “How could you have been moved without waking? Without hearing anything? You’re so light—so sensitive. A whisper wakes you!”

She shook her head slowly. “I don’t know. I remember nothing. Only waking there… afraid.”

By then, Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, and several servants had arrived. The room filled with questions, embraces, relief. But Carmilla offered no explanation. She seemed lost, helpless, as if even she didn’t understand what had occurred.

My father paced slowly, hands clasped behind his back. He studied the room, the doors, the locks. I noticed Carmilla’s gaze follow him—a fleeting glance, sharp and dark, like a shadow slipping across a wall.

When the servants were dismissed—Mademoiselle having gone to fetch valerian and salvolatile—the room narrowed to only four: my father, Madame, Carmilla, and me.

He approached her gently, took her hand, guided her to the sofa, and sat beside her.

“May I venture a guess?” he asked softly. “A question?”

“Of course,” she said. “Ask anything. I’ll tell you all I know. But remember—mamma has set limits. I cannot speak of certain things.”

“I understand,” he said. “I won’t touch those subjects. Now—last night’s mystery: how were you removed from your bed, from your room, without waking? The windows were secure. The doors were locked from the inside. Yet you ended up in the dressing room. What do you think?”

She leaned on her hand, her face drained of color. “I don’t know,” she murmured. “I truly don’t.”

“Have you ever walked in your sleep?” he asked.

“Not since I was very small,” she replied. “But yes—I did. My nurse used to say I’d wander in the night, sometimes fall down stairs, wake up confused.”

My father nodded, a faint smile touching his lips. “Then I believe I can solve this.”

He paused, letting the silence grow heavy.

“You rose in your sleep. You unlocked the door—not leaving the key, but taking it with you. You carried it outside, locked it from the other side. Then you wandered—perhaps to another room, perhaps upstairs or downstairs. This house is full of forgotten chambers, cluttered corners, dusty closets. It would take weeks to search every inch. You may have drifted far before returning—still asleep—back to the dressing room. That’s where you woke.”

“And why wasn’t I disturbed?” I asked, still stunned.

“Because sleepwalking,” he said, “can happen without awareness. No pain. No alarm. Just movement in darkness. You came here after we searched, still unconscious. Then—finally—awoke. Found yourself there. As surprised as anyone.”

He laughed lightly. “And so we are spared witches, thieves, poisoners. A simple dream-walk explains it all. A miracle of innocence.”

Carmilla sat quiet, her beauty more haunting than ever—her skin luminous, her eyes deep with unspoken things. Her stillness unnerved me.

My father turned to her, his tone softening. “I wish Laura looked half as well as you do,” he said, sighing. “She’s been so pale lately.”

I glanced at him, then at Carmilla—her presence filling the room like a breath of cold air.

Our fears were over. The mystery solved. Carmilla was back.

But as I watched her, I couldn’t shake the feeling that something had changed. That the night had not ended as it should have. That somewhere, beneath the surface of this peaceful reunion, something ancient and terrible stirred—quietly, patiently—waiting.

IX. The Doctor

Carmilla refused to allow anyone to sleep in her room, so my father arranged for a servant to stay just outside the door—enough to prevent any midnight escape without alerting us. That night passed uneventfully. But early the next morning, the doctor arrived—someone my father had summoned in secret, without telling me a word.

Madame and I went together to the library, where the small, solemn physician with white hair and spectacles waited, as I had described him before. I told him everything—the dreams, the strange sensations, the paralysis, the fear that gripped me each time I closed my eyes. As I spoke, his expression grew darker, more intense. We stood facing one another in the deep window recess, the silence between us thickening with dread.

When I finished, he leaned back against the wall, his eyes fixed on me—not with pity, but with something deeper: recognition. A flicker of horror beneath careful composure. After a long pause, he turned to Madame and asked if he might speak with my father.

My father was called in, smiling at first. “I suppose you’re about to tell me I’ve made a fool of myself by bringing you here,” he said lightly. “I hope I have.”

But the smile vanished when the doctor motioned him forward. They stepped into the same recess, speaking in hushed tones, their voices swallowed by the heavy stone and glass. I stood with Madame at the far end of the vast room, breathless with curiosity, unable to hear a word. Only glimpses—my father’s shoulder, an arm, the edge of a foot—were visible through the shadowed archway. The conversation seemed urgent, grave.

After some time, my father emerged, pale and tense. He looked at me, then at the doctor. “Laura, dear,” he said, “come here for a moment. Madame, we won’t need you for now.” His voice was quiet, strained.

I approached, suddenly chilled despite my weakness. I didn’t feel sick—only drained, restless. But the doctor’s presence, the way my father looked at me… it sent a shiver down my spine.

He held out his hand, but kept his gaze on the doctor. “It’s very odd,” he said. “I don’t understand it fully. Laura, come here. Now listen carefully to Doctor Spielsberg—and try to stay calm.”

The doctor turned to me. “You mentioned a sensation—like two needles piercing your skin—near your neck, during your first nightmare. Is there any soreness there now?”

“None,” I replied.

“Can you point to exactly where you felt it?”

“Just below my throat—here,” I said, touching the spot under my collar.

He nodded slowly. “Then let me see it. It’s necessary. Just lower your dress slightly—only an inch or two.”

I hesitated, then obeyed. The fabric slipped down just enough to expose the pale skin beneath.

“God bless me,” my father whispered, stepping back. His face had gone ashen.

“You see it now,” the doctor said, voice low but triumphant. “A small blue mark—about the size of your little finger’s tip.”

“What is it?” I gasped, my heart pounding.

“Nothing, my dear,” he said gently. “Just a mark. But it tells us what we need to know.”

He turned to my father. “Now—what shall we do?”

“Is there danger?” I asked, trembling.

“I trust not,” the doctor replied. “If proper care is taken, you may recover quickly. This is the spot where you felt the strangulation? The cold current?”

“Yes,” I whispered.

“And this same place—was it the center of that strange thrill?”

“It may have been… yes.”

“Ay,” the doctor murmured, turning again to my father. “We must act. And soon.”

My father nodded gravely. “Shall I speak to Madame?”

“Certainly.”

He called her over. “I find your young friend unwell,” he said. “Not dangerously, I hope—but we must take precautions. For now, Madame, I ask you to ensure Miss Laura is never alone. Not for a single moment. It is essential.”

“We will do as you wish,” Madame promised eagerly.

“And you, Laura,” my father added, “you’ll follow the doctor’s instructions, won’t you?”

“I will,” I said, though I still did not understand.

The doctor then said, “I would like to examine another patient—our guest. Her symptoms are similar, though milder. I’ll be passing through tonight—I’ll stop for supper and see her.”

“Very well,” my father said. “Come at seven.”

They repeated their warnings, then left. I watched them walk together across the grassy platform before the castle, pacing slowly, deeply absorbed. Then the doctor mounted his horse and rode eastward into the forest.

At nearly the same moment, the man from Dranfield arrived with the mail. My father took the letters, opened one, and read it silently.

Half an hour later, he returned. “This letter was delayed,” he said. “It’s from General Spielsdorf. He might have come yesterday—or tomorrow. Or he could be here today.”

He handed me the letter, but his expression was grim. Not joyful, not even hopeful. He looked as if he wished the General were buried beneath the Red Sea.

“Papa,” I said softly, laying my hand on his arm. “What’s wrong?”

He smoothed my hair, his touch gentle. “Perhaps I’ll tell you soon.”

“Does the doctor think I’m very ill?”

“No, dear,” he said, almost sharply. “He thinks—if we act wisely—you’ll begin to recover within days.”

“But why does he want me watched so closely?”

“Don’t press me,” he said, irritation flashing in his eyes. “You’ll know soon enough. In the meantime—don’t trouble yourself.”

He turned and left. But he returned moments later. “I’m going to Karnstein,” he said. “Carriage ready at twelve. You and Madame will come with me. I have business with the priest near the ruins. Carmilla hasn’t seen them yet—she can join us later with Mademoiselle. She’ll bring supplies for a picnic—something to lay out in the old castle.”

At noon, we set off. Past the drawbridge, along the steep Gothic bridge, westward toward the abandoned village and the crumbling fortress of Karnstein. No road could be more beautiful—winding through hills and hollows draped in wild woods, untouched by human design. The trees rose in natural disorder, the paths twisted around cliffs and valleys, forming a landscape both ancient and alive.

Then, rounding a bend, we saw him—General Spielsdorf—riding toward us, his servant following behind. His luggage came in a hired wagon. He dismounted as we stopped, greeted us warmly, and with no hesitation accepted the empty seat in our carriage. His horse and servant would go on ahead to the castle.

As we drove onward, the wind tugged at the curtains, and the silence between us grew heavier. I stared out at the ruins rising above the trees—broken towers, shattered walls—where shadows clung like memories. And somewhere, beneath the roots of the ancient oaks, something stirred. Something that had once slept. And now, perhaps, remembered.

X. Bereaved

It had been nearly ten months since we last saw him, yet in that time he seemed to have aged a decade. The man who once carried himself with quiet dignity now moved with a strained stillness, his frame leaner, his face etched with shadows of sorrow and something darker—anger, perhaps, or grief so deep it had turned to rage. His dark blue eyes, once warm and steady, now burned with an unnerving intensity beneath thick, grey brows. There was no trace of the calm composure that had once defined him; instead, a haunted fury clung to every line of his face. This was not merely the toll of mourning—it was as though some deeper, more terrible force had cracked his soul open.

We had resumed our ride in silence for a while, but soon the General broke it, speaking with the blunt precision of a soldier long accustomed to facing truth without flinching. He spoke of his loss—the death of his beloved niece and ward—and the words came out like a wound reopened. Then, abruptly, his voice rose, thick with venom. He cursed the “hellish arts” that had taken her life, railing against the heavens for allowing such evil to flourish unchecked. He spoke of demons, of ancient pacts, of sins so monstrous they defied reason. His tone was less prayer than accusation.

My father, sensing the weight behind these words, gently asked if he might hear the full story—provided it did not cause further pain.

“I would tell you everything,” the General replied, “but you wouldn’t believe me.”

“Why not?” my father pressed.

“Because,” the General snapped, “you only accept what fits your narrow view of the world. I once thought as you do. But I’ve seen things. Things that shatter every notion I ever held.”

“Then try me,” my father said quietly. “I may not be as rigid as you think. And you’ve always demanded proof before believing—so I’ll listen, not just because I’m curious, but because I respect your judgment.”

The General exhaled sharply, as if relieved and burdened at once. “You’re right. I don’t leap into belief lightly. What I’ve come to know is beyond belief. It’s *unthinkable*. Yet I’ve seen it. Felt it. Lived through it. I’ve been deceived by a conspiracy older than memory—something supernatural, abominable. A horror wrapped in silence.”

At this, I caught my father’s glance—a flicker of doubt, a hesitation. Not disbelief, but wariness. He looked at the General, not with pity, but with a sharp, assessing gaze. The General didn’t notice. He stared ahead, fixed on the trees ahead, as if searching for signs of something hidden in the woods.

“You’re going to the Ruins of Karnstein?” he asked suddenly.

“Yes,” my father replied. “And it’s fortunate—I’d planned to ask you to accompany me there. I want to examine them closely. There’s a ruined chapel, isn’t there? With tombs—many of them belonging to the old Karnstein family?”

“Indeed there are,” the General said. “Fascinating ruins. You’re thinking of claiming the title and lands?”

My father smiled faintly, trying to lighten the mood. But the General didn’t smile back. He remained grim, almost fierce, lost in thoughts too dark for jest.

“No,” he said, low and hard. “Something far different. I intend to dig up those graves. To unearth the dead. By God’s mercy, I hope to perform a sacred desecration—one that will rid the world of monsters. Let honest people sleep again, unafraid. I have things to tell you, my friend—things I myself would have laughed at just months ago.”

My father looked at him again—not with suspicion now, but with sudden, piercing alertness. His eyes narrowed, not in doubt, but in dread.

“The house of Karnstein,” he said slowly, “has been extinct for over a century. My wife’s mother was descended from them, but the name died out long ago. The castle is nothing but stone and shadow. The village is abandoned—no smoke has risen from its chimneys in fifty years. Not a roof remains standing.”

“True,” the General said. “But I’ve learned much since last we met—things that will shock you. Better to tell it all in order.”

He paused, then added, “You saw my dear ward—my child, I could call her. No one more beautiful. Only three months ago, she was radiant with life.”

“Yes,” my father murmured, voice softening. “She was lovely. I grieved deeply when I heard. I knew how much she meant to you.”

They clasped hands briefly, a gesture of shared sorrow. The General’s eyes welled with tears, which he did not hide. “We’ve been friends long enough,” he said, voice breaking. “I knew you’d understand. She was everything to me—her love made my home warm, my life meaningful. Now it’s gone. The years left to me may be few—but I swear, by God’s grace, I’ll see justice done. I’ll make the fiends pay for stealing her from me—when she was still young, still full of promise!”

My father nodded, solemn. “You said you’d tell the story from the beginning. Please do. I assure you, I’m not asking out of idle curiosity.”

By then, we had reached the fork where the Drunstall road split from ours. The General glanced forward, tense.

“How far to the ruins?” he asked.

“About half a league,” my father answered.

“Then let us hear it,” the General said. “Now.”

XI. The Story

“I have loved her with all my heart,” the General said, his voice thick with emotion, as if the words were torn from deep within. He paused, gathering himself, then began to speak—his voice low, deliberate—a tale so strange, so chilling, that I felt the hairs on my neck rise as I listened.

“My dear child was eagerly anticipating the visit you had so kindly arranged for her to your lovely daughter,” he continued, bowing slightly, a gesture both gallant and sorrowful. “In the meantime, we received an invitation from my old friend, Count Carlsfeld, whose estate lies about six leagues beyond Karnstein. It was to attend a series of grand festivities in honor of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles.”

“Yes,” my father replied, “I recall those celebrations. They were said to be magnificent.”

“Magnificent they were,” the General affirmed, “but more than that—princely. His hospitality knows no bounds. One might say he possesses Aladdin’s lamp. The night upon which my sorrow began was dedicated to a grand masquerade. The grounds were opened wide, trees strung with colored lanterns like stars fallen to earth. Fireworks burst across the sky in such splendor that even Paris could not match them. And the music—music, you know, has always been my weakness—music so exquisite it seemed to weave spells in the air. The finest musicians, perhaps in the world, drawn from every great opera house in Europe. As you wandered through the enchanted gardens, moonlight glinting off the castle’s long rows of windows, you would hear voices drifting from hidden groves, or rising softly from boats floating on the lake. I stood there, listening, watching, and felt myself carried back into the dreams and poetry of my youth.”

When the fireworks faded and the ball commenced, we returned to the noble chambers set aside for the dancers. A masked ball is always a sight to behold—but never one so dazzling, so opulent, as this. The company was aristocratic, almost regal. I was nearly the only man present without a title.

My dear child looked radiant. She wore no mask. Her joy, her excitement, gave her face a glow that made her beauty even more striking. I noticed a young woman, magnificently dressed but concealed behind a mask, who seemed to watch my ward with unnerving intensity. Earlier in the evening, I had seen her in the great hall, and again briefly on the terrace beneath the castle windows—always fixed upon my poor girl. With her was another woman, also masked, draped in rich, somber garments, bearing herself with the dignity of nobility. She appeared to be her chaperone.

Had the younger woman not worn a mask, I might have known for certain whether she was truly observing my child. But now, I am convinced she was.

We found ourselves in one of the salons. My dear child, having danced, rested in a chair near the door. I stood nearby. The two women approached—the younger took the seat beside my ward, while her companion stood beside me, speaking in hushed tones to her charge.

Taking advantage of her mask, the elder woman turned to me and addressed me by name, as though we were old friends. Her tone was warm, familiar. She spoke of places we had once met—court functions, grand houses—and recalled small incidents I had long forgotten. Yet at her mention, they surged back into memory as if freshly lived.

I grew increasingly curious. Each time I tried to learn her identity, she deflected me with grace, with charm. Her knowledge of my past seemed impossible. And she seemed to enjoy seeing me struggle, guessing, searching—never quite reaching the truth.

Meanwhile, the younger woman—whom her mother called Millarca—had begun to converse with my ward. She introduced herself, saying her mother was an old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the boldness allowed by masks, laughed at the guests, praised my child’s dress, and complimented her beauty with such artful ease that it was impossible not to be charmed. She teased gently, shared witticisms, and soon the two girls were laughing together, growing close. Then, with a flick of her hand, the young woman lowered her mask.

Her face—so beautiful, so captivating—I had never seen it before. Neither had my child. Yet, though unfamiliar, it held a power that drew us both in. There was something about her eyes—deep, dark, knowing—as though she saw not just the surface, but the soul beneath.

While I watched, I used the freedom of the masquerade to press the elder woman further.

“You’ve puzzled me completely,” I said, smiling. “Is that not enough? Won’t you at least allow me to know who you are?”

She replied, “Can any request be more unreasonable? Ask a lady to surrender an advantage? Besides, how do you know you’d recognize me? Time changes everything.”

“As you see,” I answered, bowing slightly, “and I suppose with a touch of melancholy.”

“And yet,” she said, “philosophers tell us that time alters not only faces, but memories. How do you know that seeing my face would help you?”

“I’d take the chance,” I said. “It’s futile pretending you’re old; your figure betrays you.”

“Years have passed since I saw you,” she said, “or rather, since you last saw me. That is what I’m considering. Millarca there is my daughter. I cannot be young, even in the eyes of those who forgive age. And I may not wish to be compared to what you remember. You wear no mask. You offer nothing in return.”

“My plea is to your mercy,” I said. “Let me see your face.”

“And mine,” she replied, “is to yours—to let it remain unseen.”

“Well then,” I pressed, “at least tell me—French or German? You speak both languages perfectly.”

“I don’t think I shall tell you, General,” she said. “You intend a surprise. You’re already planning your attack.”

“At least,” I insisted, “you won’t deny that, being honored by your conversation, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I call you Madame la Comtesse?”

She smiled—softly, sadly—and seemed ready to evade me once more—until a man stepped forward.

He wore no disguise. Dressed in plain black evening clothes, he moved with quiet elegance, yet his face was deathly pale, as if drained of life. He bowed low, without a smile, and said:

“Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to speak a few words that may concern her?”

The woman turned sharply, touched her lips in silence, then said to me, “Keep my place for me, General. I shall return shortly.”

And with that, she walked away with the pale man, their conversation serious, urgent. They disappeared into the crowd, and I lost sight of them.

I spent the time trying to guess her identity, wondering if I might discover her name, her title, her estate—something to surprise her when she returned. I considered joining my ward and the young stranger, hoping to uncover the truth before she came back.

But then—she returned, accompanied by the pale man in black.

“I shall return to inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage is at the door,” he said, bowing deeply.

And then he withdrew—into the shadows, as though he had never been there at all.

XII. A Petition

“Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse, though I hope only for a few hours,” I said, bowing low.

“It may be only that,” she replied, “or it may be several weeks. It was most unfortunate he spoke to me just now as he did. Do you know me?”

I assured her I did not.

“You shall know me,” she said, “but not yet. We are older and closer friends than you might imagine. I cannot reveal myself at present. In three weeks’ time, I shall pass near your beautiful schloss—about which I have been inquiring—and call upon you for an hour or two, to renew a friendship I never recall without a thousand pleasant memories.”

At that moment, news reached her like a thunderclap. She must depart immediately, traveling by a circuitous route nearly a hundred miles long, with all haste. Her troubles multiplied. Only the strict reserve she maintained over her name prevented her from making a most unusual request.

“My poor child has not fully recovered her strength,” she continued. “Her horse fell during a hunt she had gone to witness. The shock has left her nerves unsteady, and our physician insists she must not exert herself in any way for some time. That is why we came here slowly—no more than six leagues a day. Now I must travel night and day on a matter of life and death—a mission so grave and urgent I shall explain its full nature when we meet again, as I trust we will, in a few weeks, free of all concealment.”

She then made her petition—delivered with the air of one bestowing a favor rather than soliciting one. Yet this was merely in tone; she seemed unaware of the effect. The words themselves were humble, almost pleading: she asked only that I take charge of her daughter during her absence.

It was, under all circumstances, a strange—indeed, audacious—request. But she disarmed me by acknowledging every objection, laying bare her own vulnerability, and placing herself entirely in my chivalrous hands. At that very instant, fate intervened. My ward approached, whispering urgently that she wished to invite her new friend, Millarca, to visit us. She had just spoken with her and found her charming—especially if her mother would allow it.

At another time, I would have advised patience, until we knew who these ladies truly were. But there was no time to think. The two women pressed their appeal together, and I confess—I was swayed. There was something profoundly captivating about the young lady’s refined beauty, the grace of her bearing, the unmistakable fire of noble blood. Overwhelmed, I yielded too readily, agreeing to care for the girl whom her mother called Millarca.

The Countess beckoned to her daughter, who listened gravely as her mother explained—broadly—the suddenness of her departure and the arrangement she had made for her stay under my care. She added that I was among her oldest and most trusted friends.

I offered the usual courtesies, but already, in reflection, I felt unease settling into my bones. The gentleman in black returned, conducting the lady from the room with solemn dignity. His demeanor impressed me deeply—he carried himself with such quiet authority that I could not help believing the Countess held a station far greater than her modest title suggested.

Her final instruction was clear: no attempt should be made to learn more about her than I might have guessed—until her return. Our host, whose guest she was, understood her reasons.

“But here,” she said, “neither I nor my daughter can remain longer than a day. I removed my mask briefly, perhaps an hour ago—and realized too late that you might have seen me. So I sought this moment to speak with you. Had I known you had seen me, I would have entrusted my secret to your honor, asking you to guard it for weeks. As it is, I am certain you did not see me. But if you suspect now—or later—what I am, I place myself entirely in your honor. My daughter will keep silence, and I know you will remind her from time to time, lest she betray it unknowingly.”

She whispered a few words to her daughter, kissed her twice swiftly, and left, accompanied by the pale man in black, vanishing into the crowd.

“In the next room,” Millarca said, “there is a window overlooking the hall door. I should like to see the last of mamma—and wave to her.”

We agreed, and followed her. Outside, we saw a stately old carriage, flanked by couriers and footmen. The thin figure of the pale gentleman stood beside the Countess, draping a thick velvet cloak around her shoulders, pulling the hood over her head. She nodded to him, touched his hand lightly. He bowed repeatedly as the door closed and the carriage began to move.

“She is gone,” Millarca murmured, sighing.

“She is gone,” I repeated, for the first time—now, in the fleeting moments since my consent, reflecting on the folly of my decision.

“She did not look up,” the young lady said softly.

“The Countess had removed her mask,” I said gently, “and perhaps did not wish to show her face. And she could not have known you were watching.”

She sighed again, gazing at me. So beautiful was she that I softened. I regretted even the brief moment of hesitation in welcoming her. I resolved to make amends for any coldness I might have shown.

Millarca replaced her mask and joined my ward in urging me to return to the gardens, where the music would soon resume. We walked along the terrace beneath the castle windows. Millarca grew increasingly familiar, delighting us with vivid tales of the nobles we saw upon the terrace—her descriptions sharp, witty, and never cruel. I liked her more with each passing minute. Her company promised to enliven our often solitary evenings.

The ball did not end until the morning sun crested the horizon. The Grand Duke danced until dawn, and loyal guests dared not leave before him.

As we passed through a crowded saloon, my ward asked where Millarca had gone. I thought she had been beside her, and she believed she was beside me. In truth, we had lost her.

My search proved fruitless. I feared she had mistaken others for us in the confusion, following them into the vast, open grounds. Now, fully aware, I recognized the true folly of taking responsibility for a young woman without knowing her name. Bound by promises whose motives I could not fathom, I could not even direct inquiries by naming her as the daughter of the Countess who had departed only hours before.

Daylight broke. It was full morning before I abandoned the search. Not until nearly two o’clock the next day did we hear word of my missing charge.

A servant knocked at my niece’s door, reporting that a distressed young lady had begged him to find General Baron Spielsdorf and his daughter—she had been left in their care by her mother.

There could be no doubt. Our visitor had returned. If only we had lost her!

She told my dear child a story to explain her delay. Late that night, she said, she had wandered to the housekeeper’s chamber, overcome with despair at losing us. Exhausted, she fell into a deep sleep—one that, though long, barely restored her after the strain of the ball.

That day, Millarca came home with us. I was only too glad, after all, to have secured such a delightful companion for my beloved girl.

XIII. The Woodman

Soon, however, troubling signs began to appear. Millarca grew increasingly weak, her body still recovering from the illness that had gripped her not long before. She remained confined to her room until well into the afternoon, rarely seen outside its walls. Yet, despite the fact that she always locked her door from within—never moving the key until the maid arrived to assist with her morning routine—it was discovered, by chance, that she was sometimes absent from her chamber in the early hours of dawn and at other times throughout the day, before she wished anyone to know she had stirred.

From the windows of the schloss, I saw her more than once during those pale, grey mornings, walking slowly through the trees toward the east, her movements stiff and unnatural, as though she were in a trance. This led me to believe she walked in her sleep. But that explanation offered no real comfort. How could she leave her room when the door was locked? How did she slip from the house without unlocking any door or window?

My confusion deepened when a far greater dread took hold—my dear child was fading. Her beauty diminished; her health waned in ways so strange, so horrifying, that I could no longer ignore the truth: something dark and unseen was draining her life.

At first, it was only terrible dreams—visions that left her trembling and pale upon waking. Then came the specter. She claimed it appeared to her at night, sometimes resembling Millarca, sometimes taking on the shape of a beast, indistinct and shadowed, circling the foot of her bed like a predator stalking its prey. Then came the sensations.

One evening, she described a feeling—not painful, but deeply unsettling—as if an icy stream ran across her chest. A few nights later, she felt sharp, piercing pains beneath her throat, as though two large needles had driven into her flesh. And then, suddenly, a slow, convulsive strangulation seized her—followed by unconsciousness.

I heard every word the old General spoke, for we were now driving over the short grass flanking the road that led to the roofless village—a place untouched by smoke for more than fifty years. As he recounted these events, my blood turned cold. For each symptom he described matched my own in chilling detail. The girl whose story he told—had she not been meant to be a guest at my father’s chateau, had fate not intervened? And worse—the habits, the secrets, the hidden behaviors he mentioned… they were unmistakably those of Carmilla.

The forest opened before us, and suddenly we stood beneath the crumbling chimneys and sagging gables of the ruined village. Above it loomed the dismantled castle, its towers and battlements half-swallowed by towering trees that rose like sentinels from the hillside. In a daze, I stepped down from the carriage. We said nothing, each lost in our own thoughts, yet united by a shared dread. Together, we climbed the slope and entered the vast, echoing chambers of the ancient fortress.

“And this,” said the General at last, standing at a great window and gazing out over the desolate village and the endless forest beyond, “was once the grand seat of the Karnsteins.”

“It was a cursed family,” he continued, his voice low and heavy. “Here, their blood-soaked history was written. It is cruel that even in death, they should continue to torment the living with their monstrous desires.” He pointed downward. “That is the chapel of the Karnsteins.”

Then, as if struck by a sudden thought, he added, “And I hear the axe of a woodman—working among the trees near the chapel. Perhaps he can tell us what we seek—perhaps he knows where Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein, lies buried. These simple folk remember the stories the nobility have long forgotten.”

“We have a portrait of Mircalla at home,” my father said. “Would you care to see it?”

“Not yet,” replied the General. “I believe I’ve seen the original. One reason I came to you sooner than planned was to investigate this very chapel.”

“What!” exclaimed my father. “You mean to say you think Mircalla is still alive? But she died over a century ago!”

“Not so dead as you suppose,” the General answered grimly.

“You puzzle me,” my father said, his eyes narrowing slightly—just as I had noticed before, a flicker of suspicion crossing his face. Yet the General carried no trace of madness. His manner was steady, even fierce.

“There remains but one thing that matters to me in these final years,” he said, stepping into the dim interior of the chapel, its arches looming high above us like the ribs of some long-dead beast. “And that is to bring justice to the monster—justice that may still be done by mortal hands.”

“What justice?” my father asked, bewildered.

“To decapitate her,” the General replied, his voice rising, his face flushing with rage. With a thunderous stamp, he raised a clenched fist, as if gripping an axe, and shook it violently in the air.

“Decapitate her?” my father echoed, stunned.

“Aye,” the General snarled. “With an axe, a spade, anything that will split her foul throat. You shall hear all—soon.” He moved forward, gesturing to a heavy wooden beam lying on the moss-covered floor. “That will serve as a seat. Your daughter is tired. Let her rest while I finish my tale.”

I sank onto the rough timber, grateful for the moment of stillness. Meanwhile, the General called out to the woodman, who had been clearing branches from the chapel’s crumbling walls. The man approached, axe in hand, weathered and strong, his face etched with the lines of a lifetime spent in the woods.

He knew nothing of the monuments here. But he told us of an old ranger—a man who lived with the priest about two miles away—who could point out every relic of the Karnstein line. For a small fee, he would return with him—if we lent him one of our horses.

“Have you worked in these woods long?” my father asked.

“All my life,” the woodman replied in his thick dialect. “Under the forester. My father before me. And before him, many generations. I could show you the very house in the village where my ancestors lived.”

“How did the village become abandoned?” the General asked.

“It was plagued by revenants,” the man said. “Several were found in their graves, tested, and destroyed—by decapitation, by stake, by fire. But still, the torment continued. Many villagers died.”

Then he paused, his voice dropping lower. “But then a Moravian nobleman passed through. He heard of the curse and, being skilled in such matters—many in his land are—he offered to end it.”

“He did,” the woodman continued. “There was a full moon that night. Shortly after sunset, he climbed the chapel tower, from which he could clearly see the churchyard below. From there, he watched until he saw the vampire emerge from his grave, lay down his linen shroud, and glide into the village to feed.”

“The nobleman descended, took the shroud, and returned to the top of the tower. When the vampire returned and found his clothes gone, he roared up at the man atop the steeple. The stranger waved him up. The vampire climbed—only to have his skull split open by a single blow of the nobleman’s sword. He fell into the churchyard. The stranger followed down the winding stairs, cut off his head, and delivered both body and head to the villagers the next day. They impaled and burned the corpse.”

“This Moravian nobleman had authority from the head of the Karnstein family to move Mircalla’s tomb—and he did. So thoroughly that no one remembers where it once stood.”

“Can you mark its location?” the General asked, his voice urgent.

The woodman shook his head, a sad smile on his lips. “No one living knows. And besides… they say her body was removed. But no one is certain.”

With that, he dropped his axe and turned away, leaving us alone in the silence of the ruin, as the General prepared to speak again—his voice thick with vengeance, and the shadows deepening around us.

XIV. The Meeting

“My beloved child,” he continued, his voice heavy with sorrow, “was slipping away from me—fast. The physician who had been attending her could not so much as slow the tide of her decline. I knew then that it was no ordinary sickness, though I dared not name it. He saw my distress and proposed a second opinion. I summoned a more experienced doctor from Gratz. Days passed before he arrived. When he did, he was both learned and devout—a man of great wisdom and calm demeanor.

They examined my ward together, then withdrew to my library to discuss her condition. From the next room, where I waited for their call, I heard their voices rise—not in debate, but in sharp contention. I knocked, and stepped inside.

The old doctor from Gratz stood firm in his diagnosis. His counterpart mocked him openly, laughing as if the matter were mere folly. The laughter died when I entered.

“Sir,” said the first physician, “your learned colleague seems to think you need a conjuror, not a healer.”

“Pardon me,” replied the Gratz doctor, his face darkening, “I shall speak plainly another time. But I grieve, Monsieur le Général, that my science cannot help her. Before I leave, I offer one suggestion—though I do so with great caution.”

He sat at the table, took up a quill, and began to write. I bowed deeply, heartbroken, and turned to go. As I did, the other doctor glanced over his shoulder at his colleague, then gave a slow shrug—his hand drifting upward to his temple.

That consultation left me no closer to salvation than before. I walked into the gardens, mind shattered. Minutes later, the doctor from Gratz caught up with me. He apologized for following, but said he could not depart without speaking further.

“I am certain,” he said, “that this is not illness. No natural disease bears these signs. Death is near—perhaps only a day or two remains. If the seizure is halted now, with care and skill, she may yet recover. But one more attack… and all will be lost.”

“And what seizure?” I begged.

“I have written it all,” he said, handing me a folded letter. “But I give it on one condition: you summon the nearest priest. Open it only in his presence. Do not read it alone—you would mock it. This is not a matter of medicine, but of life and death. If no priest can be found… then you may read it.”

Before he left, he asked whether I would wish to see a man who had studied this very subject—someone whose knowledge might prove invaluable after reading the letter. He urged me to invite him.

The priest was absent. So I read the letter alone.

At another time, perhaps, I would have laughed. But when every remedy fails, and the one you love fades before your eyes—what desperate hope will not seem reasonable?

The letter was monstrous. It declared that my daughter was being visited by a vampire. The punctures near her throat? The marks of those long, needle-thin teeth—famously associated with such creatures. The livid, blue-tinged spot she described? That, too, matched the legends. Every symptom aligned perfectly with ancient accounts of vampiric visitation.

I, myself, had always dismissed such tales as superstition. Yet here was a man of learning, a man of science, offering this as truth. And I was too broken to reject it.

So I followed his instructions.

I hid in the dark dressing room adjacent to her chamber, where a single candle burned. I waited until she slept deeply. My sword lay ready upon the table. At just past one o’clock, I saw it—a shadowy form, indistinct, crawling over the foot of the bed. It rose swiftly toward her neck, swelling into a pulsing, black mass.

For a moment, I froze. Then I lunged forward, sword raised.

The creature contracted, slid back down the bed, and stood on the floor—just below the footboard. Its eyes glowed with a feral, knowing hatred. And there, beneath the dim light, I saw her—Millarca.

I struck. She stood unharmed, near the door. I chased her—again I swung the blade. She vanished. My sword shattered against the wood.

I cannot tell you what followed. The house erupted in panic. Millarca was gone. But my daughter—she grew weaker still. By dawn, she was dead.

Now, standing in this ancient cemetery, the General spoke with trembling hands. We said nothing. My father wandered off, reading the names carved into weathered stones, eventually stepping into a side chapel. The General leaned against the wall, wiping his eyes, sighing like a man near collapse.

Then—voices. Carmilla’s voice, soft and familiar, and Madame’s gentle tone. They were coming. But they faded.

Alone again, I felt the chill settle deeper. The story he had told—of noble blood, of cursed lineage, of death that came not from disease, but from something older, darker—echoed through the silence. Around us, ivy choked monuments, and the trees loomed like sentinels. And I thought: *She is not here. Not yet.*

The General stared at the ground, his hand resting on the base of a crumbling tombstone. Beneath a narrow archway, carved with grotesque, demonic faces—the kind that made even the most rational tremble—I saw her. Carmilla stepped into the shadowed chapel, radiant, smiling.

I smiled back, about to rise—when the General roared.

He seized the woodman’s hatchet and lunged forward.

In an instant, her face twisted. The sweetness vanished. Her features contorted into something animal, something ancient. She crouched backward, eyes wide and black.

Before I could scream, he swung the axe.

She slipped beneath the blow—and caught his wrist in her tiny fingers. He struggled, but his grip faltered. The hatchet fell. She was gone.

He staggered, breath ragged, hair standing on end, sweat glistening across his face. He looked as if he’d seen the gates of hell open.

It lasted only seconds.

Then—Madame stood before me, pale and frantic. “Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?” she demanded, repeating it over and over.

“I don’t know,” I stammered. “She was here—only moments ago.” I pointed to the doorway. “She went that way.”

“But I’ve been standing in the passage since she entered,” Madame insisted. “She never returned.”

She called out—“Carmilla!”—through every door, every window, every corner. No answer.

“She called herself Carmilla?” the General whispered, still shaking.

“Yes,” I said.

“Aye,” he breathed. “That is Millarca. That is Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Go—my child—leave this place at once. Drive to the clergyman’s house. Wait there. Do not return. Begone! May you never see Carmilla again. For if you do… you will not find her here.”

XV. Ordeal and Execution

As the General spoke, a figure emerged from the chapel door—the same one through which Carmilla had once entered and vanished. He was unlike any man I had ever seen: tall and thin, with narrow shoulders that hunched forward, his chest sunken beneath a black coat that seemed too large for him. His face was deeply lined, weathered brown as if scorched by time, and crowned with a peculiar hat shaped like a broad leaf. Long, grizzled hair fell over his shoulders, and he wore gold-rimmed spectacles perched precariously on his nose. His movements were slow and awkward—shuffling, almost dreamlike—as if his body did not quite obey his thoughts. At times he gazed upward into the sky; at others, he bent low, eyes fixed on the ground. A faint, constant smile played upon his lips, though it never reached his eyes. His arms swung loosely at his sides, and his hands—clad in oversized black gloves—flailed about in gestures that seemed entirely disconnected from what he said.

“The very man!” exclaimed the General, stepping forward with unmistakable joy. “My dear Baron, how delighted I am to see you! I never imagined I’d meet you so soon.”

He beckoned to my father, who had just returned, and introduced the strange old gentleman—whom he called the Baron—to him. They exchanged formal greetings, then immediately plunged into conversation. The Baron pulled a rolled paper from his pocket and unrolled it across the worn stone of a nearby tomb. In his hand was a pencil case, and with deliberate strokes, he marked lines upon the parchment. As he glanced between the drawing and various points of the chapel, I realized it was a plan of the building. From time to time, he read aloud from a small, filthy book whose yellowed pages were covered in dense handwriting.

They walked down the side aisle, opposite where I stood, talking intently. Then they began measuring distances with their steps. Finally, they stopped before a section of the wall, peeling back ivy and tapping the plaster with sticks. They scraped, probed, knocked—until, at last, they uncovered a broad marble slab embedded beneath layers of decay. With the help of the woodman, who returned shortly after, they cleared away the debris and revealed an inscription and carved coat of arms.

It was the long-lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

The General, though not one given to piety, lifted his hands and eyes toward heaven, silent but clearly moved. For a moment, he stood in reverent stillness.

“Tomorrow,” I heard him say, “the commissioner will arrive, and the Inquisition shall proceed as the law demands.”

Then turning to the Baron, he gripped both his hands tightly.

“Baron, how can I thank you? How can we all thank you? You have freed this land from a curse that has plagued its people for over a century. That monstrous enemy—thank God—is finally found.”

My father led the Baron aside, and the General followed. I knew they were speaking out of earshot, no doubt discussing my own ordeal. I saw them glance at me several times, their expressions grave, their silence heavy with meaning.

Later, my father came to me, kissed me again and again, and gently guided me from the chapel. “We must return home,” he said, “but first, we must fetch the priest who lives nearby. He must come with us to the schloss.”

We succeeded in bringing him along, and I was grateful—for I was utterly drained when we arrived. But my relief turned to dread when I learned there was no word of Carmilla. No explanation was offered for the events in the chapel, and it was clear my father meant to keep it hidden from me—for now.

Her absence deepened the horror of what I had witnessed.

That night, preparations were made with eerie care. Two servants and Madame would stay awake in my room. My father and the priest took watch in the adjoining dressing room. The priest performed solemn rites—rituals I could not understand—though I sensed their purpose: to protect me while I slept. I would learn the full truth only days later.

After Carmilla disappeared, my nightly torment ceased. I was free—at last.

You may have heard of the ancient terror that haunts the lands of Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkish Serbia, Poland, even Russia—the belief in the vampire. It is not mere superstition. If countless judicial inquiries—conducted by impartial commissions, sworn under oath, documented in volumes thicker than any other legal record—are to be believed, then the vampire is not myth. It is real.

I have heard no theory that explains what I have seen and endured—except the one passed down through generations, rooted in blood and fear.

The next day, the trial took place in the chapel of Karnstein. The tomb of Countess Mircalla was opened. The General and my father recognized her instantly—the perfidious, beautiful guest who had haunted our lives. Her face, though buried for one hundred and fifty years, bore the color of life. Her eyes were open. No foul odor rose from the coffin.

Two physicians—one official, the other representing the inquiry—confirmed the impossible: a faint breath, a heartbeat. Her limbs moved freely. Her flesh was supple. And the leaden coffin floated in blood—seven inches deep, in which her body lay submerged.

These were the signs. These were the proofs.

By ancient custom, the body was lifted. A sharp stake was driven through her heart. At that moment, she let out a scream—a piercing, human cry of agony. Then, her head was severed. Blood poured from her neck like a river.

Her body and head were placed upon a pyre, set alight, and reduced to ash. The ashes were scattered into the river, carried away by the current.

And from that day forth, no vampire troubled that land again.

My father possesses a copy of the Imperial Commission’s report—the official account, signed by every witness present. It is from this document that I have drawn the story of that final, terrible scene.

I write this not with calm, but with a trembling hand and a heart still haunted by the horror that once gripped my soul. You may think I am composed, but nothing could be further from the truth. The mere act of recalling these events sends shivers through my bones, as if the shadows of the past have returned to clutch at me once more. Only your persistent request—repeated so earnestly—could have brought me to this task, one that has left me unnerved for months, reviving the dread that followed me long after I believed myself free.

Let me speak briefly of Baron Vordenburg, the eccentric scholar whose knowledge led us to the tomb of Countess Mircalla. He had settled in Gratz, living in modest poverty, his family’s once-great estates in Upper Styria reduced to little more than memory. Yet he devoted himself entirely to the study of vampirism—an ancient, terrifying phenomenon he approached with meticulous rigor. His library was filled with obscure texts: *Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*, and *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris* by John Christofer Herenberg. He had compiled an exhaustive digest of judicial records, from which he distilled a chilling system of rules governing the vampire’s existence.

One myth he dispelled: the notion that vampires appear deathly pale. In truth, they bear the look of healthy life—whether in their graves or among humans. When uncovered, they exhibit all the signs that confirmed the vampire nature of the long-dead Countess Karnstein. But how they emerge from their coffins each night, returning without disturbing the earth or leaving a trace, remains a mystery beyond reason. Their dual existence depends on nightly sleep in the grave, while their waking hours are sustained by the insatiable hunger for living blood.

Vampires often fixate upon individuals with an intensity akin to love—a passion that drives them to endless patience and cunning. They will pursue their chosen victim relentlessly, using charm and deceit to draw them closer, prolonging their torment like a perverse artist savoring every stroke. In such cases, they seem to crave some form of complicity, a twisted intimacy. But when no such connection exists, they strike swiftly—overpowering their prey, draining them in one brutal feast.

There is a peculiar law governing their names. In Mircalla’s case, her identity could only be revealed through anagrams—words formed from her true name, letter for letter, without addition or omission. “Carmilla” and “Millarca” both fulfilled this condition. My father told the Baron of the tale involving the Moravian nobleman and the vampire at Karnstein churchyard. Then he asked how the Baron had found the hidden tomb.

The Baron smiled—a thin, secretive smile—and stared down at his worn spectacles, fumbling with them. Then, lifting his eyes, he said:
“I possess many journals and papers from that remarkable man—the most curious being one detailing the visit to Karnstein. The legend distorts the truth slightly. While he may have been called a Moravian nobleman, he was actually born in Upper Styria. He had been deeply in love with Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, in youth. Her death shattered him.

“Vampires multiply according to a dark and fixed law. Begin with a land untouched by their curse. It begins when a person—usually one of wickedness—commits suicide. Under certain conditions, such a soul becomes a vampire. That spirit then haunts the living in dreams, causing their deaths. And those who die thus often rise again as vampires themselves. This happened to Mircalla. She was tormented by one of these demons.

“My ancestor, Vordenburg—whose title I now hold—discovered this early on. Through his studies, he learned much. He feared that suspicion would eventually fall upon the dead Countess, whom he had once adored. Though she might have become a monster, he could not bear the thought of her remains desecrated by a posthumous execution. He believed that expulsion from the vampire state sent the creature into an even more terrible existence. So he devised a plan: a false journey to remove her body, a real destruction of her monument.

“In later years, as he aged, he looked back with horror at what he had done. He made careful notes and sketches—guides that led me directly to her resting place. He also wrote a confession of his deception. Had he lived longer, he might have acted further—but death took him before he could. Now, through the hand of a distant descendant, justice has finally reached the beast.”

We spoke a little longer. Among other things, the Baron mentioned a telltale sign: the strength of the vampire’s hand. Mircalla’s delicate fingers had closed like iron vices around the General’s wrist as he raised his axe. But it was not just grip—it left a numbness in the limb, a lingering paralysis that might never fully fade.

The following spring, my father took me on a journey through Italy. We remained away over a year. Still, the terror did not leave me. To this day, the image of Carmilla returns—not always as the gentle, enchanting girl I once knew, but sometimes as the writhing, monstrous thing I saw in the ruined church. Often, from deep in thought, I start awake, convinced I hear her light footsteps at the drawing-room door.

And though I try to believe I am free… I am not.
