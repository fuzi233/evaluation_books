In the first clear weeks of the next summer, when the roses at Broad Place had begun to smell warm in the noon and the clipped yews held last night’s blackbird-song like water in a bowl, two old men sat after breakfast upon the terrace with their newspapers spread between the cups and the silver.

It was a quiet meal—eggs just set, bacon crisped to the edge where fat turns sweet, toast that snapped under the knife and gave out that brown, comforting scent of wheat well baked. The servant had warmed the pot so the tea would not thin at the last pour. Even the country had its proper music: a rake drawn in some distant garden; a cartwheel complaining softly on the lane; larks stitching the sky with thin, persistent sound.

General Feversham, older and drier, turned over a sheet with the deliberate care of a man who had learned to husband every small comfort. His finger halted on a column.

“I see Osman Digna’s back at Suakin,” said he. “There’s likely to be some fighting.”

Lieutenant Sutch, who had always carried the world behind his eyes like a second map, made a small, dismissive noise and laid his paper down.

“Oh,” he said, “he will not do much harm.”

But the terrace, the linen, the steady clink of spoon against china—these slid away from him as if some hand had drawn a curtain. He saw instead the white city by the Red Sea trembling in heat, the brown plains round it, halfa grass tangled like rope, and far off the hills towards Khor Gwob—hard lines against a harder sky.

“A stuffy place Suakin, eh, Sutch?” Feversham said, as if tasting the word.

“Appallingly stuffy. I heard of an officer who went down on parade at six o’clock of the morning there—sunstruck in the temples right through a regulation helmet. Yes, a town of dank heat! But I was glad to be there—very glad,” he added, and there was feeling in it, as though a man might be glad even of a furnace if it gave him at last something that mattered.

“Yes,” said Feversham briskly; “ibex, eh?”

“No,” replied Sutch. “All the ibex had been shot off by the English garrison for miles round.”

“No? Something to do, then. That’s it?”

“Yes, that’s it, Feversham. Something to do.”

They bent again to their papers. The terrace held its morning peace, as though war were only print and not a taste of dust in the teeth. Yet even in that peace there were sounds that insisted on being heard: the bees worrying at lavender; the distant hammering of some farm repair; the clean, unbought song of a thrush that no one had paid to sing. Feversham listened without seeming to, with the instinct of a soldier grown old who finds his rest not in silence, but in honest, living noises—those that belong to land and weather, not to engines.

After a little, a footman came out with the post and set beside each man a small pile of letters. Feversham ran over his envelopes with a quick eye, selected one, and gave a grunt of satisfaction. He drew from a case a pair of spectacles and set them upon his nose.

“From Ramelton?” asked Sutch, dropping his newspaper upon the flags.

“From Ramelton,” Feversham answered. “I’ll light a cigar first.”

He laid the letter down on the garden table between them, took a cigar-case from his pocket, and, in spite of Sutch’s impatience, proceeded with the ceremony as though it were part of the day’s proper order. He cut the end neatly, as a careful cook trims a joint, and struck his match so that the flame took with a clean whisper and no sulphurous stench to offend the morning. The old man had grown an epicure in this respect; a letter from Ramelton was a luxury to be taken with every accessory of comfort he could command.

He settled back, stretched his legs, drew and tested the cigar until he was satisfied it drew well, and only then took up the letter and opened it.

“From him?” Sutch asked.

“No; from her.”

“Ah!”

Feversham read slowly. Sutch, leaning forward and pretending an interest in the roses beyond the balustrade, tried not to peep across the table. When Feversham finished, he turned back to the first page and began again, as though a second reading might translate something which stubbornly refused to be put into his own plain, military vocabulary.

“Any news?” Sutch asked at last, with a casual air.

“They are very pleased with the house now that it’s rebuilt.”

“Anything more?”

“Yes. Harry’s finished the sixth chapter of his history of the war.”

“Good!” Sutch said. “You’ll see, he’ll do that well. He has imagination, he knows the ground, he was present while the war went on. Moreover, he was in the bazaars, he saw the under side of it.”

“Yes.” Feversham’s mouth tightened at the corner, half approval, half that familiar stiffness. “But you and I won’t read it, Sutch.”

Sutch made a sound of protest, but Feversham held up a hand.

“No; I am wrong. You may, for you can give me a good many years.”

He went back to the letter; and again Sutch, unable to help himself, asked—

“Anything more?”

“Yes. They are coming here in a fortnight.”

“Good,” said Sutch. “I shall stay.”

He rose and took a turn along the terrace, as if movement could keep his thoughts from crowding too thickly. Under his steps the stone held yesterday’s warmth. The air tasted faintly of clipped box and the burnt-sugar scent of sun on grass. When he returned, he found Feversham sitting with the letter upon his knees and a frown of great perplexity on his face, as though some word in it had been written in a dialect his mind had never learned.

“You know, Sutch, I never understood,” Feversham said quietly. “Did you?”

“Yes,” Sutch replied. “I think I did.”

He did not try to explain. Some experiences, like a dish whose flavour depends upon a herb known only in one valley, cannot be rendered into the speech of another place without turning thin and false. And it was as well, he thought, that Feversham never would understand. For he could not understand without much self-reproach; and the general had always lived under a code in which the wrong words—coward, disgrace—were stamped so hard into a man’s life that even their erasure left a bruise.

“Do you ever see Durrance?” Feversham asked suddenly.

“Yes, I see a good deal of Durrance. He is abroad just now.”

Feversham turned towards him, the cigar’s smoke drifting between them in slow blue folds.

“He came to Broad Place when you went to Suakin, and talked to me for half an hour. He was Harry’s best man. Well, that too I never understood. Did you?”

“Yes,” said Sutch. “I understood that as well.”

“Oh!” Feversham exclaimed; and, as he had always done, he took the questions he could not understand and put them aside out of his thoughts like papers in a drawer.

He did not turn to his other letters. He sat smoking and looked out across the summer country, listening to the sounds rising distinctly from the fields: the far-off clatter of a reaper; the wind nudging through leaves; children’s voices—small, sharp notes that carried and vanished. There was health in the pattern of it, a soundscape made by living things and honest work. Sutch, who had finished his correspondence, watched him and let the silence run its proper length.

“I have been thinking,” Feversham said at last. “Have you noticed the date of the month, Sutch?”

Sutch looked up quickly, as if a bugle had sounded in his head.

“Yes,” said he. “This day next week will be the anniversary of our attack upon the Redan, and Harry’s birthday.”

“Exactly,” replied Feversham. “Why shouldn’t we start the Crimean nights again?”

Sutch sprang up as though youth had been returned to him for the space of a heartbeat.

“Splendid!” he cried. “Can we muster a tableful, do you think?”

“Let’s see,” said Feversham; and, ringing a handbell upon the table, he sent the servant for the Army List.

So, bending over that Army List, their heads close together like conspirators over a recipe, the two veterans may be left—counting names as though names alone could summon back a time, and a table laid in friendship could keep memory from thinning and dying away.

But of one other figure in this story a final word must be said.

That night, when the invitations had been sent out from Broad Place and no longer a light gleamed from any window of the house, a man leaned over the rail of a steamer anchored at Port Said and listened. Arab coolies tramped up and down the planks with their coal baskets between the barges and the ship’s side. Their song rose and fell with the labour—call and answer, breath and burden—so that the night itself seemed to move in rhythm. It was not music for sale, not a tune dressed up for travellers; it was sound made to hold bodies together through work, and it carried a comfort that the shriek of modern machinery never could.

Across the water came the clamour of the streets: voices, footsteps, a donkey’s bray, laughter breaking like crockery in a courtyard, and beneath it all the low, ceaseless hiss and lap of the harbour. He pictured the flare of braziers upon the quays—the fat smoke of charcoal, the metallic tang of hot iron, the quick sting of spices tossed into oil somewhere in a stall. He imagined lighted port-holes, and dark funnels ahead and behind in the procession of anchored ships, each one a floating town with its own breath and noise.

Attended by a servant, he had come back to the East again.

Early the next morning the steamer moved through the canal; and toward sunset it passed out into the chills of the Gulf of Suez. Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai, Tamanieb, the attack upon McNeill’s zareeba—Durrance lived again through the good years of his activity, the years of plenty. The names returned like the refrains of an old song, and yet each man hears such refrains in his own inner language, where no other ear can truly follow.

Within that country on the west the long preparations were going steadily forward which would one day roll up the Dervish Empire and crush it into dust. Upon the glacis of the ruined fort of Sinkat, Durrance had promised himself to take a hand in that great work; but the desert which he loved had smitten and cast him out.

But at all events the boat steamed southwards into the Red Sea.

Three nights more, and though he would not see it, the Southern Cross would lift slantwise into the sky.
