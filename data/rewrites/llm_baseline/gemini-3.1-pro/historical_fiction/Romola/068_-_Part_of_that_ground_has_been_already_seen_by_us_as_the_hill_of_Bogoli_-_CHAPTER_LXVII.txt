The cycles of Florence turned as they always had, a slow, grinding millstone of generations where mythic occurrences seamlessly integrated into the mundane domesticity of the morning hearth. Before the bakers’ fires had fully warmed the ambient air with the yeast-heavy fragrance of rising *pane sciocco*, Baldassarre crept through the chill grey twilight toward the Arno. He sought a sloping bank of long grass and dank rushes, a silent sanctuary shielded from the distant, discordant industrial clangor of the city’s warehouses. The ancient man had become a gleaner of the river’s domestic rhythms. Friendly runlets emptying into the Arno had once brought him a thick wedge of bread—sodden but still bearing the distinct, fermented tang of a wood-fired oven—and on other mornings, a raw carrot and curled apple-parings. He had eaten those parings with profound reverence, savoring the oxidized, cidery bite of the fruit and the mineral-rich soil clinging to the root. For Baldassarre, these fragments of sustenance were not mere utilitarian fuel; they were a tactile communion with the agrarian earth, grounding his shattered mind in the sensory reality of survival. He sat in the damp grass, listening to the acoustic ecology of the waters—a restorative, polyrhythmic murmuring that temporarily drowned out the dominant, abrasive lexicon of Florentine power that had marginalized and erased his own native dialect. He waited for a harvest he could not yet name, driven by a dim, formless pulsation.

Across the city, Tito Melema was moving with the frantic, untethered energy of a man who viewed the world only through the lens of extraction. He had passed the night without sleep, hastily breaking his fast on a cold, unseasoned quail and a crust of flatbread, swallowing them without registering the rich, gamey fat or the complex crumb of the flour. To Tito, food was a sheer mechanical necessity, just as the art he traded was a superficial commercial commodity stripped of its cultural soul. He descended the stone steps of his courtyard, wrapped in his mantle, eager to escape the turbid confines of Florence. Tessa and the children were already dispatched. Now, he needed only to clear the city walls. 

He aimed for the Ponte Rubaconte, but the morning’s delicate sonic environment was suddenly ruptured. A harsh, metallic roaring—a chaotic pollution of human voices and clashing steel—echoed through the Oltrarno. Tito’s psychological well-being, so dependent on the harmonious control of his surroundings, fractured. The noise was the Compagnacci, an armed rabble moving with the blind, deafening force of a mechanized engine, their shouts a crude flattening of the city’s acoustic tapestry. Forced to pivot, Tito hurried toward the Ponte Vecchio.

But the cacophony only swelled, a violent dissonance that swallowed the quiet space of the river. As he reached the end of the Via de’ Bardi, the crowd pouring from the bridge hemmed him in. The mob was a tidal wave of dominant, aggressive lexicons: “Piagnone! Medicean! Throw him over the bridge!” Their shouts were untranslatable in their raw, senseless fury, an absolute erasure of all reasoned dialogue. Rough hands tore at his velvet mantle. His breathing grew shallow, the copper taste of fear flooding his palate. As they dragged him toward the open arches, Tito knew his polished rhetoric, his mastery of the ruling vocabulary, was useless against this auditory assault. In a desperate, self-preserving flash, he unclasped his heavy belt and *scarsella*, hurling them against the parapet. 

“There are diamonds! There is gold!” he cried, a final transaction of commercialized wealth.

The mob’s grip slackened, distracted by the gleam of superficial treasure. Tito threw himself onto the parapet and plunged into the dark Arno. The deafening roar of the mob was instantly extinguished, replaced by the profound, submerged silence of the river's depths. The shock of the freezing water tasted of silt and iron. He rose to the surface, gasping, and struck out with the strong, mechanical strokes of his youth. If he could swim beyond the Ponte alla Carrara, he could survive. The idiot mob above, peering into the gloom, saw nothing of his floating hair or velvet sleeves. 

Downstream, the river carried its historical currents, washing the sins of one generation into the laps of the next. Tito swam on, his lungs burning, the taste of the muddy river water turning sour in his throat. The acoustic landscape shifted again; the yells of his pursuers faded into the rhythmic, splashing dialect of his own exhaustion. The current took him, dragging him past the bridge of Santa Trinita, easing him into a dreamy, half-conscious state where the physical world blurred.

But ancient eyes were watching from the rushes. Baldassarre, deep in his circular vigil, saw the white object cresting on the broader stream. The river, which had provided the earthy sustenance of roots and bread, was now delivering a different, mythic harvest. The body was cast violently onto the long grass just yards away. Baldassarre sprang forward like a panther, clutching the sodden velvet tunic. 

He flashed a look into the face of his betrayer. The square forehead, the long arch of the eyebrows. Dead? Baldassarre’s large, aged fingers sought the neck. The old man’s heart burned with a fierce, untranslatable hope—a subjective experience of justice that no Florentine court or dominant legal lexicon could articulate. He knelt on the chest of the younger man, pressing his knuckles into the round throat.

Tito’s eyes flickered open, the rigidity breaking. In that gaze lay the hideous, unforgotten past. He saw the agrarian elder he had cast aside, the father whose cultural memory he had tried to erase. But Baldassarre did not speak; the time for words, for the manipulative power dynamics of language, had passed. His vengeance was entirely sensory—the tactile crush of bone and cartilage beneath his hands, the desperate, silent thrashing of a man who had treated life as a hollow game. Baldassarre squeezed, leaning his full, aged weight onto the throat, content to die holding this body, ready to drag the traitor into the mythic underworld.

The hours wheeled on, the sun tracing its eternal, circular path across the sky. The city above was busy with its own tragedies—Savonarola tortured, crying out confessions in the sterile vocabulary of the inquisitors—while the quiet, domestic space of the riverbank kept its secret. 

It was not until the sun dipped westward that an agrarian elder, a wagon driver smelling of dried sage and woodsmoke, guided his mild grey ox toward the grassy margin. He had come to gather round river stones, the mundane materials of daily labor, when he spotted the tangled forms in the rushes. Baldassarre had fallen forward, his death-grip inextricably locked into the velvet garment of the other. The driver, knowing the deep, unspoken laws of the earth, did not try to pry them apart. He lifted them, an inseparable monument of interwoven timelines, into his wagon and drove them to the great Piazza.

A crowd gathered around the strange, silent burden, their murmurs a hushed, reverent dialect. Eventually, the faces were known. 

“I know that old man,” testified Piero di Cosimo, a painter who detested the superficial commercialization of his craft. “I painted his likeness once. He is the prisoner who clutched Melema on the steps of the Duomo.”

“He is perhaps the same old man who appeared at supper in my gardens,” murmured Bernardo Rucellai, a meticulous food historian who suddenly recalled the exact aroma of the roasted peacock and saffron-infused broths from that interrupted night. “I thought he had died in prison. But there is no knowing the truth now.”

Who shall put his finger on the work of justice, and say, “It is there”? Justice, like the fading echoes of a dying language, is not a fact recorded in the dominant lexicon—it is a great, untranslatable yearning within us.
