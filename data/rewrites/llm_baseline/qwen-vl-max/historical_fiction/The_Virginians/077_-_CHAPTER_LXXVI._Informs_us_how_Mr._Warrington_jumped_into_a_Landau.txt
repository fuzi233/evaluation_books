The initial surprise and greeting had passed, and at once the young girl began her charge. “So you’ve finally come to ask after Theo—and now you feel sorry that your neglect has made her so ill? For six weeks she’s been unwell, and not a word from you! Very kind of you, Mr. George, I’m sure!”

“Kind!” Mr. Warrington gasped. “Do you call it kind to be with her every day for a year, and then leave without a word?”

“My dear,” I replied, “you know my promise to your father.”

“Promise!” Miss Hetty retorted, tossing her head. “A fine promise indeed—to make my darling ill, and then one bright morning say, 'Good-bye, Theo,' and walk away forever. I suppose gentlemen make such promises because they intend to keep them. But if I were a man, I’d never toy with a child’s heart and abandon her afterward. What has she ever done but love you too much? By what right do you take her from us all, only to desert her because some old woman in America disapproves of her? She was happy before you came. She loved her sister—there never was such a sister—until she saw you. And now, because your mother thinks you might do better, you must leave her?”

“Great powers, child!” I exclaimed, furious at her injustice. “Was it I who drew back? Is it not I who am forbidden this house? Did not your father demand, on my honor, that I should never see her again?”

“Honor!” she spat. “And you’re the ones who pretend to be our superiors—the ones we’re meant to admire and respect! I declare, George Warrington, you ought to go back to your Virginia schoolroom, have your black nurse tuck you into bed, and ask permission from your mother before stepping out. Oh, George! I never dreamed my sister would give her heart to a man without the courage to stand by her—only to flee at the first obstacle. When Doctor Heberden said he was attending you, I decided to come and see you—and you *do* look ill, which pleases me. I suppose it’s your mother you’re afraid of. But I won’t tell Theo you’re unwell. She hasn’t stopped caring for you. She can’t simply walk out of a room, break her solemn vows, and return to life as if nothing happened. That’s left to men—our so-called superiors in courage and wisdom. To abandon an angel—yes, an angel ten thousand times too good for you—an angel who adored me until she saw you, and who was the very blessing of our lives—is this what you call honor? Don’t speak to me of honor! I despise you all! You claim to be our betters? We are to worship and wait upon you? I don’t care for your wit, your tragedies, or your poetry—they’re often absurd. I won’t stay up copying your manuscripts, nor watch hour after hour at a window, wasting my time, just to glimpse your worship walking down the street with your hat cocked. If you’re leaving, go ahead—but give me back my sister! Give me back my darling, the one who loved us all until she saw you. And you’re leaving her because your mother thinks she can find someone richer? Oh, you brave gentleman! Go marry whom your mother chooses, and let my dear die here, abandoned!”

“Great heavens, Hetty!” I cried, stunned by her logic. “Is it I who wish to leave your sister? Did I not offer to keep my promise? Was it not your father who refused me, forcing me to swear I would never see her again? What do I have but my word, my honor?”

“Honour indeed!” she shot back. “You keep your word to him, but break it to her! A fine honor! If I were a man, I’d soon show you what I thought of your honor! Only—I forgot—you’re bound to keep the peace and can’t… Oh, George, George! Don’t you see how distraught I am? I’m beside myself, barely knowing what I say. You mustn’t leave my darling. They don’t know it at home. They don’t believe it—but I know her best. She will die if you leave her. Say you won’t! Have mercy on me, Mr. Warrington—give me back my dearest!”

Thus, the passionate, broken-hearted girl swung from fury to pleading, scorn to tears. Was my little doctor correct in diagnosing her sister’s condition so gravely? Was there truly no other remedy than the one Hetty demanded? Had not others endured the same agony of separation, suffered the same fever and despair, lain hopeless of survival, only to recover and limp through life? Perhaps—had love not been selfish, had ours not been so intense and unique that we believed no other lovers could suffer like us—we might have found in Hetty’s fervent defense a proof that a devoted heart could endure, silently bear, and ultimately heal.

What had happened in Hetty’s own case? Her sister and I, in our easy triumphs and intimate confidences, had often spoken of it. Egotists though we were, perhaps we had drawn secret comfort from her lesser fortune. It was like sitting by a fire while winter raged outside, or walking beside a great river and watching a ship tossed by the waves. We clung closer to each other, wrapped in our own joy, viewing others’ sorrows with quiet pity.

Be that as it may. Grant that we might have been parted, and lived through it—my skeptical old age might concede that. Yet at the time, I was eager to share Hetty’s fears, willingly believing that the life most precious to me would perish if torn from mine. Was I wrong? Not now would I say so. I may doubt myself—or not—I know not—but of her, never. And Hetty found in me a willing partner in her anxieties.

I tried to share some of these thoughts with the doctor, but he silenced me. “Hush,” he said, his face comically alarmed. “I must hear none of this. If two people happen to meet and talk in my patient’s room, I cannot help it. But matchmaking and love-making? I am your humble servant! What will the General do when he returns to town? He’ll have me behind Montagu House as sure as I’m a living doctor—and alive I wish to remain, my good sir!” And with that, he leapt into his carriage and left me standing, lost in thought.

“And you and Miss Hetty must have no meetings here again,” he had warned earlier.

“Oh no!” I assured him. “Of course not! We are gentlemen of honor—and our word is our bond. Besides, seeing Hetty was a gift beyond measure. Would we not be eternally grateful?”

Refreshed by the sip of water I’d taken, I felt capable of enduring anything. I walked with Hetty to Soho, never once thinking of arranging another meeting. But the little emissary was more thoughtful. “Are you going to the Museum now to read?” she asked.

“Oh yes, sometimes,” I replied. “But I’m too wretched for reading now. I can’t see the words on the page. I don’t care about my books. Even Pocahontas bores me.”

I might have gone on, but she interrupted: “Nonsense!” she snapped, stamping her foot. “George, you’re more foolish than Harry!”

“What do you mean, my dear?” I asked.

“When do you go? You leave at three o’clock. You cross over toward Tottenham Court, walk through the village, and return by Green Lane—the one leading back to the new hospital. You know you do! Walking there for a week won’t hurt you. Good morning, sir! Please don’t follow me any farther.” And with that, she curtseyed and walked away, her veil drawn.

Green Lane, north of the new hospital, is now lined with houses. In my time—when King George II still reigned—it was a shabby rural escape from London, so perilous that city folk returning from their villas at Hampstead or Highgate would travel in groups, escorted by servants with lanterns, to guard against footpads lurking at the edges of the town. The churches of Hampstead and Highgate, crowning their hills, framed the view as one turned from London. For three days, Mr. George Warrington had the pleasure of seeing this landscape and walking back along the lane toward the hospital.

Along the way stood small inns and taverns. I recall one place, where cakes and beer were sold under the sign of the Protestant Hero. On the third or fourth day, a decent woman smiled at me, curtseying in her clean apron. “It appears the lady doesn’t come, sir,” she said. “Your honor had best step in and try a can of my cool beer.”

Then, on May 25th—O day to be marked with white stone—as I walked back through Tottenham Road, a little beyond Mr. Whitefield’s Tabernacle, I saw a landau ahead. On the box-seat, beside the driver, sat my young friend Charley, waving his hat. “George! George!”

I ran forward, my knees trembling so violently I feared I’d fall at the wheel. Inside the carriage, I saw Hetty—and beside her, my dearest Theo, propped with a pillow. How thin her hand had grown since last it lay in mine! Her cheeks were flushed and sunken, her eyes unnaturally bright, and the faint tremor in her voice when she spoke sent a shock through me—whether grief or joy, I couldn’t tell; they were so entwined.

“I’m taking her for an airing to Hampstead,” Hetty said calmly. “The doctor says the air will do her good.”

“I’ve been ill,” Theo whispered, “but I’m better now, George.”

As she spoke, a sudden burst of music erupted from the chapel nearby. I took her hand. Her eyes met mine. It felt as if we had never been apart. I shall never forget that hymn. I’ve heard it all my life—my wife played it on her harpsichord, our children sang it. Now, do you understand, young people, why I love it so? Because it was the music of our reunion. Because it sang hope into my life at its darkest hour. Yes, the darkest: for even the dreary confinement at Duquesne had its tender moments—the poor Biche, my drunken jailer, the forest birds’ reveille, the military music of my prison. I remember them fondly.

Master Charley looked down from the box-seat, watching his sister and me in silent rapture, Hetty listening to the music. “I think I’d like to go and hear it,” she said. “And perhaps Mr. Whitefield is preaching today! Come in with me, Charley—and George can drive dear Theo to Hampstead and back.”

Charley seemed less enthusiastic about Mr. Whitefield’s sermon and suggested I take Hetty instead. But she wouldn’t be denied. “I’ll never help you in another religious exercise as long as you live, sir,” she declared, “if you don’t come.”

And so the youth climbed down, and they entered the chapel together.

Can any moralist, mindful of my earlier promises, excuse me for leaping into the carriage and seating myself once more beside my beloved Theo? Suppose I broke my word—will he blame me much? Reverend sir, you are welcome. I broke my promise. And if you would not do the same, good friend, you are welcome to your virtue. Not that I suspect my own children will ever be so bold as to claim hearts of their own and give them freely. No, my young people, you will let papa choose for you—eat when he tells you, drink when he commands, and arrange your children’s marriages later.

Now, I know you’re eager to hear what happened when papa jumped into the landau beside poor little mamma, propped up by her pillows.

“I’m coming to your part of the story, my dear,” I said, turning to my wife as she sewed.

“To what, pray?” she asked.

“You should skip that part and go straight to the grand battles and your heroic defense of—”

“Of Fort Fiddlededee in 1778, when I pulled off Mr. Washington’s epaulet, gouged General Gates’s eye, cut off Charles Lee’s head, and pasted it back on!”

“Let us hear all about the fighting,” the boys insisted.

Even the Captain condescends to listen—though only because it’s from a militia officer.

“Fair and softly, young people!” I said. “Everything in its turn. I’m not yet at war. I’m just a young gentleman stepping into a landau beside a young lady I promised to avoid. I’m holding her hand—which, after a little hesitation, she leaves in mine. Do you remember how hot it was, how it trembled, how it beat a hundred and twenty times a minute? And as we trot toward Hampstead, I address Miss Lambert thus—”

“Ah, ah, ah!” the girls chorus, joined by Mademoiselle Eleonore, who adds, “Nous ecoutons maintenant. La parole est à vous, Monsieur le Chevalier!”

Here they all sit in a circle: mamma at her side of the fire, papa at his; Mademoiselle Eleonore (at whom the Captain glances rather sweetly—eyes off, Captain!), the two girls listening like nymphs to Apollo, and John and Tummas (with dull ears) bringing in tea trays and urns.

“Very good,” says the Squire, pulling out the manuscript and waving it. “We’re about to reveal your mother’s secrets—and mine.”

“I’m sure you may, papa,” cries the house matron. “There’s nothing to be ashamed of.” A blush rises across her kind face.

“But before I begin, young folks, allow me a few questions.”

“Allons, toujours des questions!” Mademoiselle sighs, shrugging her pretty shoulders. (Florac recommended her to us, and I suspect the little Chevalier has a soft spot for this lovely Mademoiselle de Blois.)

To the questions, then.
