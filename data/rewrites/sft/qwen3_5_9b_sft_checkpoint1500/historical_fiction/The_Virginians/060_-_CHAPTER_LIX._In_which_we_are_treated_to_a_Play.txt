Life, I suspect, offers the novelist scant material save for the theater itself. When dealing with the martial arts, where men display valor or cowardice amidst danger and devotion, the writer may venture into reality. But for the merchant selling sugar and spice, the lawyer burying himself in ancient statutes, or the tailor measuring breeches, what narrative substance exists beyond the drudgery of their trade? To chronicle the mundane mechanics of a life spent in linen-drapery or apothecary business would yield a history as dry and unendurable as hobnails. Therefore, authors must depict men removed from their vocations—captured in their passions, loves, laughter, and hatreds—while allowing their professions to remain a silent, subaudient backdrop. Thus, we find ourselves wandering through the lobbies and pleasure-haunts of existence, while the serious world toils quietly in its chambers, unaware of our fleeting glimpses. Corydon carts litter and threshes barley alongside his courtships; Ancillula dresses nurseries and waits tables before snatching a brief moment through railings to kiss Boopis, the policeman; and all day long, boots beat the pavement before a stolen kiss can be secured. We only encounter these souls when they are disengaged from labor, and it is only then that we may unite them with the idle reader.

Poor Harry Warrington, however, found himself increasingly shunned by the "macaronis" and fine gentlemen of White's and Arthur's. The gaming tables and the Mall knew him no more. Madame de Bernstein, hoping to counter the world's indifference, urged her nephew to conquer it with courage, but Harry, too honest to mask his mortification or anger, could not parry slights with the artful flattery required by society.

"You pull a long face, Harry, and complain of the world's treatment of you," the old lady chided, her tone shifting from warmth to patronizing coldness. "Fiddlededee, sir! Everyone must endure impertinences. If you were beaten now, being poor and cast down, you would say nothing, bear it with a smile, and seek revenge ten years later. *Moi qui vous parle*—do I not have humble pies to eat? Now that you are no longer the Fortunate Youth, be the Clever Youth. Win back your place. Go to every rout and party, be civil to everyone, especially women. Show your spirit, which you possess in abundance. With economy and your brother's generosity, you can maintain a genteel figure. Your handsome countenance ensures you will attract a rich heiress. Seek out the merchants in the City; they won't know you're out of fashion at Court. When did you visit my Lady Yarmouth? She took a fancy to you!"

Yet, the old woman who once greeted him with kisses now received him with icy disdain. She became peevish, casting gibes at him before her guests, flushing his honest face with humiliation. Even her servants, formerly eager, ignored him. Lady Yarmouth grew indisposed when he called, and her staff showed no eagerness to offer him dinner, a stark contrast to his days as the Fortunate Youth. Devastated, Harry poured his sorrows to Mrs. Lambert. Sitting glumly in a window-seat while the ladies bustled about, he confessed his aunt's cruelty.

"I know you are the best people alive," he murmured, "and the kindest, and I must be the dullest company in the world... yes, that I am."

"Well, you are not very lively, Harry," observed Miss Hetty, beginning to command him, wondering if this was truly the hero she had taken him to be.

"If he is unhappy, why should he be lively?" Theo asked gently. "He has a good heart, and is pained by his friends' desertion. Surely there is no harm in that?"

"I would have too much spirit to show I was hurt," Hetty cried, clenching her fists. "And I would smile, though that horrible old painted woman boxed my ears. She is horrible, Mamma."

Theo turned red. "I mayn't like her, but there is no reason to call Harry's aunt names before his face."

"You provoking thing! You are always right!" Hetty exclaimed, angry at her consistency. "Indeed, Harry, it was wrong of me to make rude remarks about your relations."

"It doesn't matter about the others, Hetty," Harry said, rising with a flush of righteous anger. "But it seems hard that this one should turn upon me. I had grown so fond of her. It drives me mad when people I love turn away or act unkind."

"Suppose George were to do so?" Hetty suggested.

"You are very clever and lively, and you may suppose many things," Harry retorted, standing resolute, "but not that, Hetty. You don't know my brother as I do—you wouldn't take such liberty as to suppose my brother George capable of any unkind or unworthy act!"

Hetty turned pale, looking up at Harry before falling silent. Taking his leave, Harry apologized simply for any rough words, insisting he always defended George. Hetty offered no verbal reply, only a prim curtsey and a hand.

Later, making curl-paper confidences, Hetty admitted to Theo, "Oh! I thought it would be so happy to see him every day... and now I offend him. I can't help it. I know he isn't clever, Theo. But oh! Isn't he good, kind, and brave? Didn't he look handsome when he was angry?"

"Silly little thing," Theo replied, "you try to make him look handsome."

Among these youths, General Lambert applied the monosyllable "Bo" to his daughter-in-law's mother as a rebuke for her sentimental matchmaking propensities, dismissing the idea that the two Virginian lads would fall in love with the Lambert girls. "Little Het might have her fancy," the General noted, "but Harry evidently hadn't been smitten. Now, with an older brother over him, and unable to even claim his coat as his own, Master Harry wasn't a great catch."

"Oh yes: now he is poor, we will show him the door," Mrs. Lambert sighed.

"That is what I always do, isn't it, Molly? Turn my back on friends in distress?" the General asked.

"No, my dear! I am a goose, now, and that I own, Martin!" the wife conceded, producing a handkerchief. "Let the poor boy come to us. Ours is almost the only house in this selfish place where so much can be said for him. He is unhappy; to be with us puts him at ease!"

Accordingly, whenever crestfallen Hal needed a meal or an evening, Mr. Lambert's table opened a corner for him. George, too, was welcome. Though initially cooler and more mistrustful than his twin, George soon learned to love the Lamberts for their inherent goodness and their constant kindness to Harry. He saw the depth of Harry's misery and pitied his brother's depression. In his sarcastic vein, George would often tease himself for returning to life, joking that Florac had done better to leave his scalp as an ornament for an Indian's belt, lamenting that if he hadn't returned, Harry's favorite, Maria, would have married him anyway, thus avenging the gods upon Harry's prosperity.

"I never know whether you are laughing at me or yourself, George," Harry remarked.

"Precisely my own case, Harry, my dear!" George agreed. "But I know there has never been a better brother in the world, nor better people than the Lamberts."

"Never truer words spoken!" cried George, taking Harry's hand. "If I'm unhappy, it's not your fault—theirs, or mine. 'Tis fate, you see. 'Tis having nothing to do. I must work, and how, George? That is the question."

"We'll ask Mother," George said. "Until we hear from her..."

"Do you know," Harry whispered, alarm in his voice, "I don't think I'd like going back to Virginia?"

"In love with one of the lasses here?" George teased.

"Love 'em like sisters—with all my heart! But I've come out of that business, thanks to you. I don't want to go back. Hunting, duck-shooting, tobacco-planting, whist-playing, sermons over and over again—that's all there is at home. For a poor fellow like me, what chance is there? Until I do something, George, I shall be miserable!"

"Have I not always said so? Are you not coming round to my opinion?"

"What opinion, George? You know pretty much whatever you think, I think, George!"

"That Florac had best have left the Indian to take my scalp, my dear!"

At this, Harry burst out with an angry exclamation, and they continued to puff their pipes in friendly union.

Though they lived together, little interchange occurred beyond affection. Harry, never venturing near George's books, sat dumb as a mouse while his brother studied. Soon, they moved from the Court end of town, Madame de Bernstein pishing and pshaing at their departure. George, however, developed a fondness for Sir Hans Sloane's new reading room and museum in Montagu House, taking cheerful lodgings in Southampton Row, Bloomsbury, overlooking Hampstead.
