CHAPTER XXXI. Old and New Acquaintances (Rewritten for Humor)

Pen, newly armed with the noble ambition to *see life*, plunged headfirst into London’s most peculiar haunts like a young explorer discovering the Amazon—only instead of jaguars and piranhas, he found coalheavers, boxers, and men who could drink beer faster than you could say “tavern.” He fancied himself a social anthropologist, sampling the lower orders as though they were exotic fruits from some far-flung island of human eccentricity. He’d have gladly shared a pint with a gang of burglars if only one had invited him over for tea. Alas, no such luck.

But Warrington, his trusty companion in crime and culture, was already well acquainted with the city’s underbelly. He knew every pub within a ten-mile radius and could name the landlord of each like a man who’d once been a minor nobleman in a bad novel. He wasn’t just welcome at the bar—he was practically family. And honestly? He liked these folks better than his own class. “Society,” he declared, “is like a ballroom full of identical twins wearing the same hats and saying ‘How do you do?’ in unison. But here?” He gestured grandly at a coalheaver mid-sneeze. “Here, there’s *character*. I’d rather talk to the strongest man in England than to another fop who thinks a waistcoat is a statement of genius.”

Pen followed Warrington around like a royal prince visiting the peasants—except this prince had no crown, no kingdom, and only two pennies halfpenny in his pocket. Still, he carried himself with all the dignity of a man who believed he was born to rule, even if his domain was limited to a single room above a pawnbroker’s shop. The working folk adored him. They called him “a high chap,” “a regular swell,” and once, after he tipped the waiter five shillings for bringing a plate of cold mutton, they named a sandwich after him: the “Pendennis Special” (though it contained no actual Pendennis).

It was a glorious time to be young and foolish. Pen spent mornings reading law books, yes—but also politics, history, literature, and occasionally a pamphlet on the moral implications of eating cheese before bedtime. Then, when the clock struck noon, he’d burst out into the streets with Warrington, appetite roaring like a lion that hadn’t eaten since Tuesday, ready to feast on laughter, ale, and the sheer joy of being alive. At twenty-four, everything felt fresh—like the world had just woken up and said, “Hey, look at me! I’m new!”

And then came the inevitable descent into middle age. You know how it goes: the lights go out, the garlands fray, the pleasure garden turns into a muddy patch where someone left a broken umbrella. Grey hairs appear like morning fog—and worse, a headache follows close behind. Pleasure, once radiant and rosy-cheeked, retires to bed early, still wearing her rouge. Ah, friend. Let us walk through the day sober and sad, but friendly. That’s the way.

Now, imagine this scene: dawn breaking over London, bridges glowing pink, the city waking up like a cat stretching after a long nap. And what do we see? Mr. Pen and Mr. Warrington, rattling down the cobbles toward the Temple, reeking of brandy and regret. They’ve just survived one of their legendary nights—wild, yes, but not *that* wild. Warrington, bless him, hated women; Pen, while capable of falling head over heels for a fool (and likely did so more than once), always treated them like goddesses—until they turned out to be mere mortals. Then he’d politely retreat, muttering about “unfortunate circumstances.”

And speaking of unfortunate circumstances—there was an old friend waiting for Pen at the Fielding’s Head, a tavern so ancient it probably predates the invention of the word “pub.” This was no ordinary drinking hole. It was a shrine to song, camaraderie, and questionable hygiene. Its most famous feature? The Back Kitchen, where a little club met nightly to sing songs so heartfelt they made grown men cry—especially when Cutts, the landlord, belted out “The Good Old English Gentleman” or “Dear Tom, this Brown Jug.” His voice was like a barrel of rum with a slight cough.

And then there was Captain Costigan—yes, *the* Captain Costigan, father of the woman who once held Pen’s heart like a sacred relic. Now he was a fixture at the Fielding’s Head, known for three things: 1) telling everyone about his daughter’s marriage, 2) claiming he’d draw £100 from his son-in-law next Saturday (which never happened), and 3) borrowing money from anyone within earshot until payday rolled around—then showing up with banknotes like a phoenix rising from the ashes of debt.

Pen found him one evening, swaggering near the singers’ table, ordering brandy-and-water like it was the nectar of the gods. Warrington, having made friends with the bass singer, joined him, and Pen followed—only to freeze when he saw the Captain. He’d just come from Lady Whiston’s party, where he’d seen Costigan’s daughter again. A wave of nostalgia hit him. He stepped forward, hand outstretched, eyes bright.

“Captain!” he said warmly.

The Captain looked at him, blinked, then squinted like a man trying to read a menu in dim light. “Grecious Heavens!” he bellowed. “Is it possible? Me dear boy, me dear fellow, me dear friend…” Then he paused. “I know your face… but, bedad, I’ve forgot your name.”

Five years of punch had done their work. Pen laughed, though inwardly he winced. “Don’t you remember me, Captain? I’m Pendennis—Arthur Pendennis, of Chatteris.”

The sound of his name snapped the Captain back to reality. “Ah! Me gallant young friend! My noble collagian!” he cried, slapping Pen’s hand like it was a drum. “How’s your mother? Your guardian? The General? The Major? I preshoom, from your apparance, you’ve come into your prawpertee. And, bedad, yee’ll spend it like a man of spirit—I’ll go bail for that.”

Pen smiled. “Not yet, Captain. But thank you for the offer.”

“And if ye want any thrifle,” the Captain boomed, “there’s poor old Jack Costigan has got a guinea or two in his pocket—and, by heavens! you shall never want, Awthur, me dear boy. What’ll ye have? John, come hither, and look aloive! Give this gentleman a glass of punch, and I’ll pay for’t.”

Then, turning to Warrington, he added, “Your friend? I’ve seen him before. Permit me to have the honour of making meself known to ye, sir, and requesting ye’ll take a glass of punch.”

Pen thought, *I don’t envy Sir Charles Mirabel his father-in-law.*

He asked about Bows, the pianist from their youth. “Have you any news of him, Captain?”

“No doubt he’s very well,” the Captain replied, jingling coins and whistling “The Little Doodeen”—a tune he sang so often it was rumored to be his last words. “Me dear boy—I’ve forgot your name again—but my name’s Costigan, Jack Costigan, and I’d loike ye to take as many tumblers of punch in my name as ever ye loike. Ye know my name; I’m not ashamed of it.”

And so he rambled on.

At that moment, the pianist began to play. Pen glanced over—and there was Bows, sitting at the battered piano like a ghost from the past. The instrument looked like it had survived a war and several bad decisions. Bows played with quiet grace, accompanying the singers with a touch that spoke of memory and melancholy.

Pen recognized the tune instantly: the chorus from *The Stranger*, the one that played just before Mrs. Haller entered. His heart skipped a beat. Nobody else noticed. They were too busy shouting for poached eggs and kidneys.

When the piece ended, Pen walked over and shook Bows’s hand. “What, you haven’t forgot the old tune, Mr. Pendennis?” Bows said, grinning. “I thought you’d remember it. I take it, it was the first tune of that sort you ever heard played—wasn’t it, sir? You were quite a young chap then.”

“I fear the Captain’s very bad tonight,” Bows added. “He breaks out on pay-day. And I shall have the deuce’s own trouble getting home. We live together. We still hang on, sir, in partnership, though Miss Em—though my lady Mirabel has left the firm.”

He took a sip of porter. “And so you remember old times, do you? Wasn’t she a beauty, sir?”

Pen nodded. “She was.”

They would meet again, of course. And Pen would continue to visit the Fielding’s Head, where all sorts of people passed through: country farmers, apprentices, medical students with dirty coats and loud opinions, university bucks with smirks that could curdle milk, guardsmen with shiny buttons, and even lords and senators—because apparently, no one was too important to enjoy a decent song and a bad meal.

One night, the bass singer, Hodgen, performed his smash-hit “The Body Snatcher.” He appeared on stage in full regalia: coffin, spade, skull-candlestick, flask of gin. The song was terrifying—so much so that the glasses trembled on the tables. Poor Cos’s “The Little Doodeen” was sung afterward, but only by those brave enough to stay after the resurrectionist chant.

Then came two familiar faces: Mr. Hoolan and Mr. Doolan, editors of rival newspapers—the *Dawn* (Liberal) and the *Day* (ultra-Conservative). They sat down, papers stained with porter, and began discussing foreign mails, correspondents, and circulation figures like two generals planning a battle.

Warrington picked up the *Dawn* and pointed to an article attacking the *Day* for employing “professional bullies” to smear reputations. “As rogues of note in former days employed assassins,” it read, “our contemporary, the *Day*, employs smashers out of doors to utter forgeries.”

Hoolan chuckled. “Kidneys, John, and a glass of stout. How are you, Morgan?”

“Doing pretty well, thank ye, Mick,” said Doolan. “Faith she’s accustomed to it.”

“Don’t bring Patsey with you, Mick,” Morgan warned. “Our Georgy’s got the measles.”

They talked shop—foreign correspondents, courier costs, circulation wars—while Warrington whispered to Pen, “They’re talking about Doctor Boyne and Captain Shandon—Irish controversialists who fight in print but are best friends in real life. They abuse England more in one article than we could in a dozen volumes.”

Doolan, meanwhile, turned to Pen. “Mr. Pendennis, I am delighted to have the honour of seeing ye again. The night’s journey on the top of the Alacrity was one of the most agreeable I ever enjoyed in my life, and it was your liveliness and urbanity that made the trip so charming.”

Pen blinked. He didn’t remember being particularly lively.

“I have often thought over that happy night,” Doolan continued, “and talked over it to Mrs. Doolan. I have seen your elegant young friend, Mr. Foker, too. He is an occasional frequenter of this hostelry, and a right good one it is.”

Then he bowed slightly. “Mr. Pendennis, when I saw you I was on the *Tom and Jerry Weekly Paper*; I now have the honour to be sub-editor of the *Dawn*, one of the best-written papers of the empire.”

Pen nodded. He wondered if the *Dawn* had a section on etiquette for polite conversations with gentlemen who used “honour” like a seasoning.

And so the night wore on—laughter, songs, secrets, and the faint smell of regret. For Pen, it was a reunion of old friends, old tunes, and old dreams. And somewhere, in the corner, Captain Costigan was already planning to borrow another half-crown.
