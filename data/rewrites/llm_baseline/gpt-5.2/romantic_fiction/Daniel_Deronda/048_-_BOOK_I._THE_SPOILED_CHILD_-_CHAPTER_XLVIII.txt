’Tis a hard bargain, this business of laying one’s life out beforehand with the neatness of a table set for guests who may never come, and trusting that foresight will keep every dish warm and every door bolted. The world has a trick of slipping in by the crack you forgot because it never occurred to you you had a hinge there at all.

Grandcourt’s consequence lay, as so many men’s does, in acres: the broad, quiet authority of inherited fields that do not argue, that go on yielding rent as patiently as wheat yields grain. Public matters reached him only when they threatened to graze that revenue—politics as a kind of distant thunder that might sour a harvest, but need not be listened to closely unless it broke over one’s own stack-yard. He could glance at newspapers as he might at a menu of foreign dishes he had no intention of tasting, and dismiss whole nations and whole movements with the same languid word, as if men were no better than bad soap that left one’s hands rough. He did nothing, said little; and his silence, like a cold draught in a warm room, unsettled weaker people into wondering whether they had been foolish to feel heat at all.

Within the narrower boundary of what he chose to care for, there was more skill—something of the diplomacy that conquers by waiting, by watching, by letting the other person move and then fastening the chain on the precise link that will hold. No turn of Gwendolen’s towards Deronda escaped him. He would have denied jealousy, because jealousy admits doubt, and doubt was not the temper in which he held his wife. It would not have stung him to learn that she liked another man’s company better than his. What he insisted on was that her liking should count for nothing against his resolve—that she should feel it as useless as a hand struggling inside an iron cuff.

The marriage, whatever half-idle whim had led to it, had become a new instrument in his hands: a fresh object on which to exercise will, a living creature to be trained into the exact posture he required. He had not repented. His taste was minute and tyrannical, like a fastidious palate that can detect one wrong spice and fling aside a whole dish. He wanted a wife whose rank he had raised, whose beauty drew eyes as a good vintage draws praise, whose nails and ears satisfied his small exactitudes, and whose spirit was sharp enough to answer—so long as the answer did not threaten him. He liked, even, that she should have fire in her, provided it burned under his grate. Love was not his need; command was.

So in London he had his satisfaction on every side. He had come up earlier than usual to consult lawyers—wills, mortgages, the transaction with his uncle about Diplow, that had been coaxed into agreement by the neat bait of ready money dangled as one offers a horse a bit of apple without seeming to entice. And with the business went the other indulgence: to show himself everywhere with the bride he had chosen against expectation. He carried his indifference as men carry a cane, needing the street and the passers-by to make the prop worth having. Splendid receptions, conspicuous rides and drives, her beauty beside him like a fine course served on plate—these enlarged his enjoyment of not caring. He wanted her sought after; he liked that men should hover, eager to talk and escort her, so long as it happened within his observation. A lofty coquetry on her part would not have offended him. What he could not bear were her ways with Deronda.

After Lady Mallinger’s musical evening, when he had marked the settee-dialogue as keenly as a servant who watches his betters, he showed his method: he named Deronda for invitation along with the Mallingers, careful that no one should suspect the name mattered. He said nothing directly to Gwendolen; pride would not risk a disgust too plainly spoken. But a few days later, without any decent preface, he remarked as if it were merely a general truth he had noticed in the world—

“Nothing makes a woman more of a gawky than looking out after people and showing tempers in public. A woman ought to have good manners. Else it’s intolerable to appear with her.”

The word “gawky” fell on Gwendolen as if it were a smear across her white glove. She knew the application, and felt a sudden cold of fear—not so much of his displeasure as of being made ridiculous in the eyes that still mattered to her. And the rebuke, hanging over her like a low ceiling, only tightened that inward strain with which she awaited any meeting with Deronda.

Her town life hurried on with the glitter and the changing scene of travel: engagements, toilettes, rooms full of voices and laughter, the soft whirl of wheels on the smoother streets. It was all a programme, carrying her forward even while some inner part of her sat still and despondent. And yet, as always with what is deeply felt, the rare moments when she could exchange a word with Deronda spread through her mind like some slow infusion—like a scent that will not be confined to the jar, but steals out and makes the whole room a place of remembered sweetness. Each slight communication grew large in her consciousness, and she imagined it large in his. Deronda did not avoid her; rather he tried, by every delicate means, to show that her confidence had not lowered his respect. And he liked being near her—how not? She was not merely a problem; she was a beautiful creature whose fate moved him, whose appeal had become to him like the pressure of a hand caught at the edge of a precipice. Yet when he dared to look at his own future, he saw it lying far away from her—far away from this splendid, sad-hearted woman who had turned to him as if his momentary warning had been the only firm ground under her feet.

One day, an incident touched that other name which was now always interwoven with Deronda’s in Gwendolen’s mind: Mirah. She had meant—out of a sort of obedience to Deronda’s counsel—to take singing lessons; but days slid past in the formless way of a life without work to mark its hours, and Grandcourt’s continual presence, his surveillance, lay on everything like damp mist: his negative mind clung to objects, spoiling their contact as fog spoils the smell of hay by flattening it into chill.

But one morning at breakfast, in a recurring fit of determination to assert the old spirit, Gwendolen began dallying prettily over her prawns, touching them with her fork as if the pearl-pink curve and the salted steam rising faintly from the dish were the whole point, and eating nothing.

“I think of making myself accomplished while we are in town,” she said, “and having singing lessons.”

“Why?” said Grandcourt, in his languid tone.

“Why?” she echoed with a little play of sauciness, her chin lifting. “Because I can’t eat pâté de foie gras to make me sleepy, and I can’t smoke, and I can’t go to the club to make me like to come away again. I want a variety of ennui. What would be the most convenient time—when you are busy with your lawyers and people—for me to have lessons from that little Jewess whose singing is getting all the rage?”

“Whenever you like,” he said, pushing away his plate, leaning back, and looking at her under his eyelids with that lizard-like stillness which made her feel as if he lay in sun and watched a fly. His fingers toyed with the ears of the tiny spaniel on his lap—one of the little dogs she had begun to dislike because they fawned on him as if his cold hand were their only warmth.

Then he added, still lazily: “I don’t see why a lady should sing. Amateurs make fools of themselves. A lady can’t risk herself in that way in company. And one doesn’t want to hear squalling in private.”

“I like frankness,” said Gwendolen, turning her eyes away while her pride swallowed the sting as one swallows a mouthful too hot, feeling it burn downwards. “That seems to me a husband’s great charm.” She lifted a prawn and stared at its boiled, ingenuous eyes, preferring that harmless fixedness to his. “But,” she went on, after the pause in which she forced her voice to keep its lightness, “I suppose you don’t object to Miss Lapidoth’s singing at our party on the fourth? I thought of engaging her. Lady Brackenshaw had her—you know. And the Raymonds, who are very particular about their music. And Mr. Deronda, who is a musician himself and a first-rate judge, says there is no singing in such good taste as hers for a drawing-room. I think his opinion is an authority.”

She meant it to fall like a small stone—little, but sharp.

“It’s very indecent of Deronda to go about praising that girl,” said Grandcourt, in the same indifferent tone.

“Indecent!” Gwendolen exclaimed, the blood springing to her face at first, then leaving it. “To go about praising.”

“Yes; and especially when she is patronized by Lady Mallinger. He ought to hold his tongue about her. Men can see what is his relation to her.”

“Men who judge of others by themselves,” Gwendolen said—and the words were out before she could catch them back. The moment they hung in the air, she felt as if she had touched something that would burn.

“Of course. And a woman should take their judgment—else she is likely to run her head into the wrong place,” he returned, feeling the pinchers close on the quick. “I suppose you take Deronda for a saint.”

“Oh dear no!” Gwendolen said, summoning that almost miraculous self-control which could make her voice high and hard and keep her hand steady when everything within shook. “Only a little less of a monster.”

She rose, pushed back her chair without hurry, and walked out with the careful carriage of someone determined that no one shall see she has been struck. In her dressing-room she turned the keys inside the doors and sat down, pale and still. Even the poisonous letter she had once read had hardly pierced her with a sharper pain than this: the emotion had reached that acute point where it is sensation—like a bruise you cannot reason away. Deronda, suddenly imagined as not what she believed, was a hideous apparition, dreadful before she could ask whether it were truth. And with it came the sickening perception of how little ground she had for her faith—how small her knowledge of his life—how childish her confidence. His former rebukes and severity seemed to curdle into something odious; poetry and lofty doctrine, all the fine language in the world, looked like a mask that might cover common habits. Even the grave beauty of his face seemed now an intolerable disguise.

It rushed through her like a fevered dream—then broke. A stream of sunlight, sudden through the grey sombre morning, poured into the room and wrapped her where she sat, warming her as if the world outside were insisting on itself. She moved, looked round: the dream proved nothing. She rose, stretching her arms upward and clasping her hands as she often did when feeling crushed, and walked about in the flood of sunbeams.

“It is not true! What does it matter whether he believes it or not?” she kept repeating. It was not faith restored; it was the gasp of a faith being smothered and refusing to die quietly.

How was she to go through the day in that state? Her imagination flung out wild actions—she would go to Lady Mallinger and question her about Mirah; she would write to Deronda, upbraid him for having made the world false and wicked and hopeless to her; to him she dared pour out all the bitter indignation she had no other outlet for. No—she would go to Mirah. That, at least, was practicable. She had the pretext: to ask her to sing on the fourth. What else would she say? How would she force the assurance she needed? She could not wait to foresee. If the thought maddening her had been a living thing, she would have wanted to seize it by the throat at once.

She rang, asked if Mr. Grandcourt were gone out; learning he was, she ordered the carriage and dressed quickly. Then she went down and walked about the great drawing-room—the gilded, painted prison—seeing herself in glass panels as if she were another woman, not noting anything, only feeling the hollow throb in her chest. He would find out where she had been. He would punish her—somehow. No matter. At this moment she could neither desire nor fear anything but certainty: certainty that she had not been fool enough to trust what was false.

She had Mirah’s address. Soon the carriage bore her through London’s roar and roll—iron tyres on stone, omnibuses groaning, the sharp cries of street-sellers, the ceaseless hiss and clatter that seemed to grind down thought as a mill grinds grain. Even inside the cushioned splendour, that city sound pressed upon her nerves, and she longed for the quieter, human noises of a home: a kettle’s small singing, a knife on a board, the gentle stir that belongs to rooms where life is made and mended. But she was heedless, as one is heedless of passages and lobbies when going to hear a verdict. Nothing existed until she was shown into a room where there were folding-doors—and from behind them she heard Deronda’s voice.

She knew it at once, as if she had seen him. The knowledge sent a shock through her; she unbuttoned her gloves and buttoned them again, bit her lip over the pretended difficulty, trying to give her hands something to do. Then the door opened and Mirah came in, perfectly quiet, with that sweet recognition which seemed to carry a softer air with it. There was relief in her face—the steady calm, the rich colour; it was like seeing a clear spring when one has been choking on dust. Gwendolen could smile back, though her own beauty under the plumed hat was pale and agitated.

Mirah drew her chair very near, as if she felt the talk must be subdued, and looked with placid expectation while Gwendolen began low, with something like bashfulness—

“Perhaps you wonder to see me—perhaps I ought to have written—but I wished to make a particular request.”

“I am glad to see you instead of having a letter,” Mirah said, still wondering at the changed manner of this woman whom Hans, with his rough play of fancy, had called the Vandyke duchess.

“I thought—at least I hoped—you would not object to sing at our house on the fourth—in the evening—at a party like Lady Brackenshaw’s. I should be so much obliged.”

“I shall be very happy to sing for you. At ten?” Mirah answered.

“At ten, please.” Gwendolen paused; her prepared errand was done, and yet she could not move. Deronda’s voice went on, close by, threading every silence. She must say it; she could contrive no other sentence.

“Mr. Deronda is in the next room.”

“Yes,” said Mirah, in her former tone. “He is reading Hebrew with my brother.”

“You have a brother?” Gwendolen said—though she had heard it before and had not cared then.

“Yes—a dear brother who is ill—consumptive. And Mr. Deronda is the best of friends to him, as he has been to me,” Mirah said, unable to mention those she loved without letting her love speak. There was, too, in the background of that room, a sense of the simple household labour by which sickness is borne: the careful quiet, the measured footsteps, the restraint of voices—an acoustic tenderness, as if the very air were trained not to jar.

“Tell me,” Gwendolen said, laying her hand on Mirah’s and speaking hardly above a whisper, “tell me—tell me the truth. You are sure he is quite good. You know no evil of him. Any evil that people say of him is false.”

It was the cry of a child for assurance; but Mirah received it with solemn indignation, as if someone had spoken blasphemy against the living.

With a sudden light in her eyes and a tremor in her voice, she said, “Who are the people that say evil of him? I would not believe any evil of him if an angel came to tell it me. He found me when I was so miserable—I was going to drown myself. I looked so poor and forsaken—you would have thought I was a beggar by the wayside. And he treated me as if I had been a king’s daughter. He took me to the best of women. He found my brother for me. And he honours my brother—though he too was poor—oh, almost as poor as he could be.” Her words came like warm bread broken in haste, offered with both hands. “And my brother honours him. That is no light thing to say”—here her tone deepened, and she shook her head with emphasis—“for my brother is very learned and great-minded. And Mr. Deronda says there are few men equal to him.”

There was in her, too, the flare of a people long forced to have their goodness measured in another’s language, their lives made small by other names; and the defiance in her gratitude would not help including Gwendolen, who had seemed to doubt.

But Gwendolen was like one parched with thirst, drinking. The fresh water ran through her as sufficient bliss. She did not notice Mirah’s anger. She hardly took in the details—only the great clearing certainty that Deronda and his life were no more like Grandcourt’s insinuation than dawn is like dawn muddied with street-gas. She pressed Mirah’s hand.

“Thank you, thank you,” she whispered hurriedly, and rose at once, as if she must escape even the sweetness that had saved her. “I must go—I shall see you—on the fourth—I am so much obliged.” She bowed herself out automatically, and Mirah, opening the door, wondered at what looked like a sudden retreat into chill loftiness.

Gwendolen had no feeling to spare for effusiveness. The passionate need to contradict Grandcourt’s estimate—so long the one point blunting her sensibility to everything else—was satisfied, and now she wanted only to be gone. Shame came back with the fear that Deronda might see her there, and with it the vision of what waited at home.

When the carriage drew up in Grosvenor Square, Grandcourt was arriving too, a cigar between his fingers. He threw it away and handed her out, accompanying her up-stairs. She turned into the drawing-room at once, so that he should not follow her farther and leave her no retreat. She sat down with a weary air, took off her gloves, rubbed her forehead, and tried to make his presence a cipher—something not to be read.

But he sat down also, not far off—just in front, so that not looking at him required the effort of a turned neck and an averted gaze.

“May I ask where you have been at this extraordinary hour?” he said.

“Oh yes; I have been to Miss Lapidoth’s, to ask her to come and sing for us,” Gwendolen answered, laying her gloves on the little table beside her and looking down at them as if the soft kid and its seams were worth more attention than his face.

“And to ask her about her relations with Deronda?” he added, the sneer in his low voice so cold it seemed to burn. In her ear it had the sound of something diabolically amused.

For the first time since their marriage she flashed out without the inward check that usually caught her anger by the throat. She turned her eyes full on him and said, biting out each word,

“Yes; and what you said is false—a low, wicked falsehood.”

“She told you so—did she?” he returned, with a sneer distilled to its last drop.

Gwendolen was mute. Her daring fury turned at once into the rage of dumbness. What proof had she? No proof that could live under his breath. All her reasons—strong, vivid within her—she saw them shrivelled, because they were only impressions, only the testimony of her own soul, which he would call folly. She turned her head away sharply, looking angrily toward the end of the room. She would have risen, but he was in her way.

Grandcourt felt the advantage and used it with that measured patience which presses hardest. “It’s of no consequence so far as her singing goes,” he drawled. “You can have her to sing, if you like.” Then, after a pause, he said in his lowest imperious tone, “But you will please to observe that you are not to go near that house again. As my wife, you must take my word about what is proper for you. When you undertook to be Mrs. Grandcourt, you undertook not to make a fool of yourself. You have been making a fool of yourself this morning; and if you were to go on as you have begun, you might soon get yourself talked of at the clubs in a way you would not like. What do you know about the world? You have married me, and must be guided by my opinion.”

Each slow sentence fell like a weight laid deliberately, not to strike her down at once, but to make rising impossible. The low tone carried its own argument, as if a physician, untroubled and exact, were telling her the course of a fatal disease. And then he allowed her to move, and never again referred to what had happened. He knew the force of his words.

If this white-handed man with the straight, perpendicular profile had been sent to rule a difficult colony, he might have won reputation among his contemporaries. He would have understood that extermination is often safer than persuasion when one’s object is security; and he would not have flinched from making things safe in that way.
