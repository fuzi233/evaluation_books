I went walking on, still facing the moon, who, not yet high, was staring straight into the forest. She was dark and dented, resembling a battered copper preserving pan that had seen generations of harvest fires, and she looked dispirited and weary. Not a cloud was nigh to keep her company, and the stars were too bright for her. We went a long way together through the wood, I and the moon, she the dull shine, and I the live shadow, moving through a profound acoustic stillness. This was not the sterile, deadened silence of an industrialized city, nor the jarring mechanical roar that frays the human psyche, but a deep, resonant quietude—a listening landscape. 

Something on the ground, under a spreading tree, caught my eye with its stark whiteness against the dark loam. As I drew nearer, the vague shape in the foliage suggested a human body. Kneeling and laying my hand upon it, I found it cold, but not with the absolute, mineral chill of stone; it possessed the lingering, memory-laden cold of a hearth that has recently lost its fire. It was a woman, entirely naked, her frame so emaciated that I could have counted every rib tight-covered by the thin, elastic leather of her skin. She looked like the skeletal remnants of an ancient, forgotten recipe, reduced to its barest phonetic bones. Her beautiful yet terrible teeth, unseemly disclosed by retracted lips, gleamed ghastly. Her hair, black as a starless night, was thick, fine, and longer than herself. Her left hand was clenched hard, fiercely enclosing some small, hidden object, guarding it like a vital seed of cultural memory.

How had she come to be here, stripped and starved? The forest offered no answers, only the soft, sibilant rustle of leaves overhead. I could not leave her exposed to brutal claws or the slow, erasing wash of the rains. Yet the ground was hard, compacted by interlacing roots, and I had only my bare hands. I studied her unblemished, undecomposed skin; what if she were not entirely gone? 

As I had left the forest-hall, I had gathered a bunch of wild grapes from the doorway, eating the taut, bloom-dusted fruit as I walked. A few remained on the stalk, heavy with the aroma of late-autumn fermentation and dark, earthy terroir. I slipped my arm beneath her shoulder—finding the pine-needles beneath her faintly warm—and carefully tilted her head. I squeezed a single grape, letting the thick, violet must pool in her slightly open mouth. It was a vital syllable of sustenance, a concentrated drop of life, yet no swallowing followed. The untranslatability of the living world’s harvest into her silent, dormant physiology was absolute. 

Refusing to let the quiet power dynamics of death claim her entirely, I began an ancient, agrarian rhythm of preservation. I spread a thick layer of pine-needles and dry moss, laid my own garment—still warm from my body—over it, and lifted her delicate weight onto the bed. I covered her with my remaining clothes and a great mound of leaves, as one might insulate fragile root vegetables in a winter cellar. I crept into the heap, drawing her into my arms, sharing what little bodily heat I possessed. Thus I spent the remainder of the night, listening to the vast, slow breath of the forest, watching the moon, and guarding this silent archive of a woman.

When the eastern horizon grew dimly clearer, the sonic environment shifted. A low, rhythmic undulating caught my ear—a large snake, moving in an unswerving line through the underbrush. Presently, a roebuck-doe and her calf followed the same vector. Then came two creatures resembling bear-cubs with smaller ones in tow. As the dawn rapidly brightened the wood, a troop of miniature horses trotted past. The largest were no bigger than Shetland ponies, yet perfectly proportioned—models of cart-horses, chargers, hunters, and racers, their tiny hooves tapping a complex, organic polyrhythm against the soil. Dwarf cattle and small elephants followed, joining the methodical morning migration.

I laid my hand on the woman’s body; she felt a trifle warmer, perhaps having absorbed the heat I had offered. When the sun finally peered over the horizon, casting its innocent splendor upon the new world, I removed the protective handkerchief from her face. The morning light brutally highlighted her drawn, hollow cheeks and the sharp topography of her bones. But the sun was shining on her. I rearranged her covering and set out to follow the animals’ well-beaten track.

The trees retreated, opening onto a wide expanse of the loveliest green. Along the forest’s edge flowed a small river, brimming to its banks but only a few yards wide, emitting a bluish, vanishing mist. I knelt to drink. The water was hot. It carried a sharp, oxidized metallic taste, like a copper boiling-kettle long steeped in mineral-rich roots and iron soil. It was the exact temperature of a perfect poaching liquid—the warmth I so desperately sought. 

I sped back to my helpless charge. The prospect of her revival was the promise of a shared table; to eat alone is merely to fuel a machine, but to share sustenance is to converse in humanity's oldest dialect. I carried her to the stream. She was marvelously light, her delicate bones resting effortlessly against my shoulder. The water was too hot to plunge her into immediately, lest the shock shatter her fragile, fluttering life. I laid her on the bank and, dipping a garment into the steaming broth, began to meticulously bathe her. It was a process of precise culinary and healing exactitude, ensuring a slow, even distribution of heat. I continued until her frame warmed, then gently submerged her in the swift, steady current, keeping her head above the surface. Even in the heat, her fist remained fiercely clenched. 

After ten minutes, I lifted her out, dried her, and covered her on the warm, dry grass while I ran to gather insulating leaves. Returning, I found she had retained the water’s heat. Leaving her under layers of foliage, I tracked the river upstream to a series of nearby rocky hills. The river issued from a rock at the base of one, not as a tumultuous, deafening cataract, but welling quietly from the ground inside a small cave. It filled a deep, narrow channel along one side. 

The cave's acoustics were magnificent—a continuous, restorative bass resonance that enveloped the space, an acoustic sanctuary free from the abrasive dissonance of the world outside. Here, I could establish a domestic routine. I sourced sturdy beech boughs from the forest, their dry yellow leaves still clinging to the wood, and laid them across the channel. I interlaced smaller twigs and packed the structure deep with dry moss, constructing a suspended, traditional drying-bed over the steaming water.

I returned for the woman and carried her to the cave, laying her upon the bridge-bed. I covered her thickly with leaves and squeezed another grape into her mouth; her jaw opened slightly wider, and though she still did not swallow, I hoped the vibrant, tannic juices might seep down her throat. Within hours, the radiant heat of the brook had deeply interpenetrated her frame. She was warm to the touch—not yet the warmth of waking life, but a preserved potential.

For seven days and nights, this cave became the center of a circular, domestic rhythm. Every morning, I bathed in the hot, metallic stream, its mineral warmth invigorating me so thoroughly that it felt as though I had consumed a hearty, complex meal. Every morning, I bathed her in the same waters. Once, a shadow of discoloration appeared on her left side, jarring my agrarian patience, but by the next morning's bath, it had vanished. After each immersion, I placed a fresh grape into her mouth. I foraged the forest for my own sustenance, tasting wild berries rich with notes of sharp juniper, woodsmoke, and earthen sweetness. 

Every time I slept in the resonant hum of the cave, I dreamed of a wounded angel who learned to love me; every time I woke, I found only the white, motionless, wasted face upon the leafy hearth. Her silence was a profound sonic void. I realized then that a lone human is nothing but an untranslated text, a dialect spoken by one. A solitary person is merely a biological necessity, stripped of the rich, sensory appreciation of shared existence. To develop the complex flavor of one's identity—to understand the nuances of one's own soul—requires the influence, the reaction, and the shared lexicon of another. Without the hope of this woman waking, I was merely surviving in an echo chamber of my own isolated dialect, my cultural memory steadily eroding. But with the faintest prospect of her sharing my table, I might yet recover the deep, interwoven meaning of a shared life.
