VI. A Very Strange Agony

When we entered the drawing room and settled down for coffee and chocolate—though Carmilla took none—she seemed restored to her usual self. Madame and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined us, and soon a small card game began. Papa came in shortly after, as he always did, for what he called his “dish of tea.” Once the game ended, he sat beside Carmilla on the sofa and asked, with a touch of concern, whether she had heard from her mother since arriving.

“No,” she replied quietly.

He pressed further: “Do you know where a letter might reach her now?”

“I cannot say,” she answered, her voice veiled. “But I’ve been thinking of leaving you. You’ve been too generous, too kind. I’ve caused you endless trouble, and I’d like to take a carriage tomorrow and ride after her. I know where I’ll find her eventually—though I can’t tell you yet.”

“Not a thought of it!” my father declared, much to my relief. “We couldn’t bear to lose you. And I won’t allow it—unless your mother herself gives her permission. She was kind enough to let you stay until she returns. I’d be far more at ease knowing you’ve heard from her. But tonight, the news about the mysterious illness spreading through our district is growing worse. And as your host, I feel a heavy responsibility—especially without guidance from your mother. Still, I’ll do my best. One thing is certain: you must not leave without her explicit consent. We would suffer too deeply to part so easily.”

“Thank you, sir,” she said, smiling softly, “a thousand times for your hospitality. You’ve all been so kind. I’ve rarely known such happiness as I’ve found here—in this beautiful chateau, under your care, and in the company of your dear daughter.”

He bowed gallantly, kissed her hand, and smiled with genuine pleasure at her words.

As usual, I accompanied Carmilla to her room and stayed while she prepared for bed. After a moment, I ventured, “Do you think you’ll ever fully confide in me?”

She turned, eyes glinting with quiet amusement, but said nothing—only smiled.

“You won’t answer?” I asked.

“You shouldn’t expect a pleasant reply. I oughtn’t have asked.”

“Oh, but you were right to ask—any question. You don’t know how precious you are to me. If you did, you’d never think any secret too great to share. But I am bound by vows—more strictly than any nun. I cannot tell you my story yet—not even to you. The time is near, though. When it comes, you’ll understand everything. You may think me cruel, selfish—even monstrous. But love is always selfish—the fiercer it burns, the more possessive it becomes. How jealous I am, you cannot imagine. You must come with me, loving me, into death—or hate me, and still follow me, hating me beyond death, beyond the grave. There is no such word as indifference in my nature. I cannot endure it.”

“Now you’re speaking nonsense again,” I said quickly, trying to lighten the mood.

“Not I,” she whispered, her voice low and serious. “Though I’m foolish, full of whims and fancies—I’ll speak plainly for your sake. Have you ever been to a ball?”

“No,” I replied. “What’s it like? How wonderful it must be.”

“I almost forget—it was years ago,” she murmured, laughing faintly.

“You’re not that old. Your first ball can’t be long past.”

“I remember every detail—with effort. It’s as if I see it through water—distorted, rippling, yet clear. That night… something happened. Something that blurred the memory, dimmed its colors. I was nearly murdered in my bed—stabbed here,” she touched her chest, “and I’ve never been the same since.”

“Were you close to dying?”

“Yes—very close. A cruel love. A strange, terrible love that sought my life. Love demands sacrifice. No sacrifice without blood.”

She paused, then added, “Let us sleep now. I feel so weary. How can I possibly get up to lock my door?”

She lay back, her delicate hands tangled in her dark, wavy hair, her head resting on the pillow, her eyes following me with a soft, unreadable smile. I bid her goodnight and slipped out, a cold unease settling over me.

I often wondered if our guest ever prayed. I had never seen her on her knees. In the mornings, she appeared only after our family prayers had finished. At night, she never joined us in the hall for our brief evening devotions. Had it not been for an offhand remark during idle talk—that she had been baptized—I might have doubted her faith altogether. Religion was a subject she never mentioned.

If I had known more of the world, I might not have been so startled by her silence. But her habits had begun to affect me. I had taken to locking my bedroom door, just as she did, convinced of the dangers lurking in the dark—midnight intruders, assassins prowling the halls. I even copied her ritual: before retiring, I would search the corners of my room, checking for hidden threats, as if some unseen foe might be waiting, ensconced in shadow.

Satisfied, I climbed into bed. A candle burned steadily beside me—a lifelong habit, one I could never break. Its light was my shield against the unknown.

But dreams pass through walls, through stone and glass, and laugh at locks.

That night, I dreamed—but not as one dreams. I was awake, yet asleep. I knew I lay in my bed, in my room, exactly as I was. The chamber was dim, the air thick with silence. Then I saw movement at the foot of the bed—something shifting, indistinct at first. Slowly, it resolved into form: a creature black as soot, shaped like a monstrous cat. It was four or five feet long, stretching the full length of the hearthrug as it paced back and forth, its movements fluid, restless, unnatural—like a beast trapped in a cage.

I tried to scream—but no sound came. My throat was sealed.

It moved faster. The darkness deepened. Soon, I could see nothing but two points of light—eyes—glowing in the void.

Then it leapt onto the bed.

The eyes came close—too close—and suddenly, a sharp, searing pain pierced my chest. Two stabs, inches apart, as if needles had driven deep into my flesh.

I woke with a cry.

The candle still burned. The room was lit. And standing at the foot of the bed—just to the right—was a woman.

She wore a loose, dark dress. Her hair fell freely, covering her shoulders. She stood perfectly still. Not a breath, not a flicker—like a statue carved from stone.

I stared.

And then—she shifted. Closer to the door. The door opened. She stepped out.

I exhaled, trembling, finally able to move.

My first thought: *Carmilla.* She must have played a trick. I must have forgotten to lock my door.

I rushed to it—my hands shaking—and found it fastened from within, just as I always did.

I froze.

I didn’t dare open it.

Terror gripped me. I threw myself back into bed, pulled the covers over my head, and lay there—motionless, breathless—until morning came.
