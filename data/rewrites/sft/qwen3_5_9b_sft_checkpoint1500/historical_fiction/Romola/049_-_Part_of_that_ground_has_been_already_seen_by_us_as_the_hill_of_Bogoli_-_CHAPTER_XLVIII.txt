The afternoon sun, low and heavy, cast long, slanting shadows across the floor as Tito returned home. In her narrow chamber, opposite the wooden cabinet, Romola sat over her writing materials, her fingers pausing mid-copy as the daylight began to fail. Before her husband could enter, she intended to extinguish the lamp and retreat into the quiet of her solitude, but the sound of his footsteps cut short her preparations.

He did not knock. Instead, he strode straight to her side, bringing with him a weight of intent that shifted the air in the room. Without removing his cap or mantle, he planted himself before her, resting an elbow heavily on the cabinet, his gaze fixed upon her face with a silence that demanded an answer.

Romola, who had spent the day reassured by the Friar regarding Tito's actual safety, now felt the bitter aftertaste of her own doubt. A wave of penitence washed over her, mingled with indignation at having publicly accused a man who cherished privacy above all. She chided herself for her impulsiveness, realizing that the treacherous duplicity inherent in Florentine politics—a truth her godfather often whispered—could easily explain Tito's association with Spini without implying any personal guilt on his part. For hours, she had wrestled with the hope that a humble confession might break the two-year silence that had grown between them, a silence so absolute that Tito remained unaware of her flight and return, just as she was ignorant of the specific terrors that haunted his nights. They lived around a banquet table where death had once shattered the feast, leaving neither the food nor the memory accessible to the other.

As she looked up, her eyes lowered in a submissive glaze born of self-reproach, Tito's countenance halted her words. There was a subtle, terrifying alteration in his features, a shift from his usual mild tolerance to something harder, colder.

"It seems," Tito began, his voice a cool, liquid stream that sent a chill down her spine, "that it is finally time for us to understand one another." He paused, letting the words hang in the dim air.

"That is what I desire most, Tito," she replied, her voice faint. Her pale, sweet face, stripped of its recent anger and replaced by the timidity of self-questioning, seemed to magnify the dark, formidable strength radiating from her husband.

"You took a step this morning," Tito continued, "which you must now recognize as futile. It exposed you to scrutiny and may entangle me in grave practical difficulties."

"I admit I was too hasty," Romola said, finding a firmness in her tone that surprised her. "I am sorry for any injustice I may have caused." She hoped that acknowledging her error would soften his gaze, clearing the path for the truths she sought to speak.

"I wish you to understand, once and for all," Tito said, his voice unchanging, "that such collisions are utterly incompatible with our station as husband and wife. Consider how you were led to this step, lest you repeat it."

"That depends largely on you, Tito," Romola countered, a spark of defiance igniting within her. It was not what she had planned to say, yet the words escaped before she could check them.

"Perhaps you intend to say," Tito answered smoothly, "that I must ensure nothing in future provokes your unreasonable suspicions. Last night, you were frank enough to declare you hold no faith in me. I am not surprised by the exaggerated conclusions you may draw from slender premises, but I must warn you of the fruit of treating such exaggerations as grounds for interference in affairs of which you are ignorant. Are you following my line of thought?"

He waited for a response.

"Yes," Romola stammered, her cheeks flushing with a hot, irreplaceable resentment at his superior, icy tone.

"Then consider this," Tito pressed. "Before long, some stray word or incident may set your imagination to weaving fictions of crime against me. You might then rush to the Palazzo Vecchio, alarm the Signoria, and set the entire city ablaze with panic. Can you foresee the consequence? Not merely the disgrace of your husband, to which you approach with such reckless courage, but the arrest and ruin of many of Florence's leading men, including Messer Bernardo del Nero."

Tito had arrived at a decisive juncture, and he had crossed the threshold. The color drained from Romola's face, leaving her lips pale—a rare transformation for a woman usually buoyed by spirit rather than fear. Tito sensed his victory.

"You might flatter yourself," he went on, "that you are performing a heroic act of liberation. Yet, you might as well attempt to pick locks with flowery rhetoric as apply such naive notions to the intricate machinery of Florentine politics. The question is no longer whether you can believe in me, but whether, now that you are warned, you will dare to rush headlong, like a blind man clutching a lantern, into complex affairs of which you know nothing."

Romola felt her mind gripped in a vice by Tito's iron will; the possibilities he outlined rose before her with terrible, crystalline clarity.

"I am too rash," she confessed. "I will strive not to be so."

"Remember," Tito insisted, his tone relentless, "that your act of distrust this morning might have borne far more fatal fruits than the sacrifice of your husband, a fate you have learned to contemplate without blinking."

"Tito, it is not so!" Romola burst forth, rising and moving closer to him with a desperate resolve to speak the truth. "It is false that I would willingly sacrifice you. It has been the greatest effort of my life to cling to you. I fled in anger two years ago, yet returned because I was bound to you more deeply than to anything on earth. But it is useless. You shut me out from your mind. You treat me as a being too unreasonable to share in the knowledge of your affairs. You will reveal nothing to me."

She appeared like a good angel pleading with a sinner, bending her face toward him with wide, imploring eyes and laying her hand upon his arm. Yet, Romola's touch and gaze failed to stir a single fiber of tenderness in her husband. The good-humored, tolerant Tito, capable of little hatred or impatience, accustomed to gentleness toward the world, felt himself growing strangely hard toward this wife whose presence had once been his strongest anchor. Beneath his soft disposition lay a masculine effectiveness of intellect and purpose, an energy akin to the sharpness of a blade, working steadily without needing great momentum. Romola possessed an energy of her own that thwarted his, and no man, unless exceptionally weak, endures being thwarted by his spouse. Marriage must be either a union of sympathy or a contest of conquest.

No emotion flickered across Tito's face as he heard Romola mention her departure for the first time. His lips merely hardened slightly as he offered a faint, cold smile.

"My Romola, when certain conditions are ascertained, we must accept them. No amount of wishing can fill the Arno, as your people say, nor transform a plum into an orange. I have not observed that prayer holds much sway in such matters. You are constituted to possess strong impressions inaccessible to reason; I cannot share those impressions, and consequently, you have withdrawn all trust from me. You have changed toward me, and thus, I have changed toward you. It is useless to look backward. We must simply adapt ourselves to these altered conditions."

"Tito, it would not be useless for us to speak openly," Romola said, her voice trembling with the exasperation of striking one's fist against a lifeless, immovable stone. "It was the sense of deception within you that changed me, and that separation keeps us apart. And it is not true that I changed first. You changed toward me the night you first donned that chain-mail armor. You had some secret from me—it concerned that old man—and I saw him again yesterday. Tito," she continued, her tone shifting to an agony of entreaty, "if you would once tell me everything, whatever it may be, I would not mind the pain. Perhaps then there will be no wall between us! Is it not possible that we could begin a new life?"

For the first time, a flash of genuine emotion crossed Tito's face. He stood perfectly still, yet the flash seemed to whiten him. He ignored Romola's plea entirely. After a brief pause, he spoke quietly.

"Your impetuosity regarding trifles, Romola, possesses a freezing influence that could chill the baths of Nero." These cutting words caused Romola to shrink, drawing herself up into her customary, self-sustaining posture. Tito continued. "If by 'that old man' you mean the madman Jacopo di Nola, who attempted my life and lodged a strange accusation against me, of which I told you nothing because it would have alarmed you to no avail, know this: the wretch has died in prison. I saw his name listed among the dead."

"I know nothing of his accusation," Romola said. "But I know he is the man I saw with a rope around his neck in the Duomo—the man whose portrait Piero di Cosimo painted, grasping your arm as he saw him grasp it the day the French entered, the night you first wore the armor."

"And where is he now, pray?" Tito asked, though he remained pale, governing his emotions with practiced control.
