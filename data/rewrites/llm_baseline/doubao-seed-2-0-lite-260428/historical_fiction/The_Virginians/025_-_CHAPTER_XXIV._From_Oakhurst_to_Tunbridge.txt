Mrs. Lambert and her girls held their lily-edged linen handkerchiefs aloft in a last wave to the departing travellers, watching the two gentlemen amble leisurely down the first stretch of the lane until a clump of gnarled old lime trees at the drive’s bend swallowed them whole, hidden from the ladies’ view. That very same green screen had marked a hundred tender goodbyes before: Mr. Lambert riding off to face battle and danger, their boys trotting away one after the other for boarding school, each vanishing behind those same boughs, to return only when Heaven saw fit, bearing new joy and affection back to their small, warm household.

The quiet, unrushed rhythm of life at Oakhurst only deepened the soft devotion the women of the house bore their absent loved ones. With the men gone, there was all the day to think of them, and the next, and the one after that, when a letter was sure to come. There was Harry’s guest chamber to peek into, where the indent of his carpetbag still pressed deep into the linen counterpane. His riding whip hung on the hall peg by the door, his fishing basket propped by the boot stand, mute little memorials of the happy, brief days he’d spent there. At dinner, the half-eaten cherry tart he’d picked at even while swearing he was too sad to eat sat cold on the table, recalling the way Hetty had sniffled on one side of him and Theo had blinked back tears on the other. Evening prayers sounded off a note without his deep young voice joining the responses. Midnight came, and Mrs. Lambert lay wakeful, counting the miles the riders had travelled, thinking of one of her brood straying out from the nest for the first time. So those rustling limes formed a quiet barrier between the wider world and the gentle household at Oakhurst. Mrs. Lambert always fell silent and pensive if she and her daughters walked up to the trees while the men were away; she swore she would carve all the names of those she loved deep into their silver-grey bark, tangled in the traditional true-lovers’ knots, and Theo, who had a fine turn for verse, wrote a whole poem about the limes that her proud mother promptly sent off to a popular periodical of the day.

“Now we’re quite out of sight of the ladies,” called Colonel Lambert, tipping his hat in a mock salute as the two trotted past the green grove. “I know my wife is still glued to her attic window watching until we round this corner. I hope we shall have you back to see this house and these trees again soon, Mr. Warrington. When the boys are home for their holidays, there will be far better sport for you than we could offer this visit.”
“I never want to be happier than I have been here, sir,” Harry replied warmly. “I feel as if I am leaving old friends behind, not new acquaintances.”
“The friend we will sup with tonight has a son who is an old friend of our family, too. My wife, who is a relentless matchmaker at heart, spent years scheming to pair him off with one of my girls, but the rogue went and fell head over heels in love with someone else entirely.”
“Ah!” Harry sighed.
“Other men have done the same, you know. Brave fellows lived before Agamemnon.”
“Begging your pardon, sir, is his name Aga—? I did not quite catch it,” the young Virginian asked, meek and confused.
Colonel Lambert laughed aloud. “No, his name is James Wolfe. Barely thirty years old, he is the youngest lieutenant-colonel in the entire British army, save for a handful of noble lads who claim rank over us common fellows.”
“Of course, of course,” Harry nodded, perfectly in line with colonial notions of aristocratic precedence.
“I have seen him command captains who are thirty years his senior, men braver than most, who never grudge his rank for a moment. No one envies him his position, for every man who knows him acknowledges he has earned it a hundred times over. He is adored by every man in his old regiment, and knows the name and story of each private under his command. He is a fine scholar as well as a perfect soldier, and speaks half a dozen languages fluently.”
“Ah, sir,” Harry said, his voice soft with humility, “I fear I have wasted my youth dreadfully. Coming to England, I feel nothing but a fool. If my dear brother George had lived, he would have done our name and our colony far more credit than I ever shall. George was a scholar, George played the harpsichord, he could hold his own in conversation with the most learned men back home, and I have no doubt he would shine here too. I am glad I came to Oakhurst, if for no other reason than to learn just how ignorant I am.”
“To know that is already a great gain,” Lambert said, smiling. “Back home, ever since we lost my brother, I thought myself a fine, grand fellow, and all our neighbours flattered me into believing it. Now I am wiser, I hope—though no doubt I am still bragging half the time. But you see, sir, the gentry in our colony know very little besides hounds, horses, wagers, and games. I wish I knew half as much of books as I do of racing odds.”
“Nay—horses and hounds are fine books in their own right, full of more plain truth than half the musty volumes in a university library. Some men are not made to be scholars, and may be fine, worthy gentlemen regardless. What call have all of us to be learned, or to strive for the highest rank in the world? His Royal Highness is commander of the army, Martin Lambert is a colonel, and Jack Hunt, riding behind us now, was once a private soldier, and now he is the finest, most honest groom in the shire. So long as each of us does his best in the station he is given, it matters little if that station is high or low. Who are we to say what is high and what is low? For all we know, Jack’s currycomb, or my epaulets, or his Royal Highness’s baton, all hold equal weight in the end. When I was young I dreamed of glory and great success; now I only dream of duty, and the women we left behind a few miles back. Come, trot on, or we shall never reach Westerham before nightfall.”

At Westerham, the two riders were welcomed by their hosts: a stately, sharp-tongued matron, an old soldier with forty-five years of campaigns under his belt, and their son, Lieutenant Colonel James Wolfe, home on a brief leave from his regiment stationed at Maidstone. Harry stared at the young officer with great curiosity. There was nothing conventionally handsome in his face: he was very lean, very pale, with bright red hair, sharp high cheekbones and a prominent nose. But he greeted his elders with gentle courtesy, hailed old friends with open warmth, and spoke with such bright, eager animation that all who listened soon forgot his plain looks and began to admire them.
Mr. Warrington was travelling on to Tunbridge? Their James would gladly bear him company, Mrs. Wolfe said, and whispered something to Colonel Lambert over supper that made the older man grin and wink knowingly. He called for a toast of port: “To Miss Lowther!”
“With all my heart!” Wolfe cried enthusiastically, draining his glass to the very last drop. His mother leaned over to tell Harry, gossiping, that the pair were soon to be married, and that the lady came of the famous, ancient Lowther family of the north.
“Even if she were the daughter of Charlemagne himself,” Lambert declared, “she would not be too good for James Wolfe.”
“He would not say that if he knew her,” the young Colonel protested, laughing.
“Of course she is his priceless pearl,” his mother teased, “and we are nothing to him now. That is the way of it, Mr. Warrington. We nurse these boys through fevers, measles, whooping cough, smallpox; we send them off to the army and lie awake half the night worrying they will catch a bullet; we break our hearts at parting with them, and see them only a week or two a year, or sometimes ten years apart. And after all that care, some lass with a pair of bright eyes comes along, and our boy vanishes, and never cares a fig for us anymore!”
“And pray, my dear,” the old Colonel Wolfe rumbled, “did you not do the exact same thing when you ran off to marry me, leaving your own parents behind?”
“Only because your gout was so bad you needed someone to wait on you, not because I liked you at all,” his wife retorted, and the rest of the evening passed in easy, affectionate banter and plenty of good wine.

The next morning, Colonel Lambert turned his horse’s head back for Oakhurst, waving many kind farewells to Harry, while Wolfe and the young Virginian spurred their mounts south towards Tunbridge Wells. Wolfe was in a hurry to reach the town, but Harry lagged a little, his thoughts drifting longingly back to the quiet parlour at Oakhurst, the soft peaceful days he had spent there. Wolfe agreed enthusiastically with every word of Harry’s praise for the Lambert family. “To have such a good name, and to live such a quiet, steady life as Colonel Lambert’s,” Wolfe said, “is the height of all human ambition, as far as I am concerned.”
“What of glory, and honour?” Harry asked. “Are those nothing? Would you give up the chance to win them?”
“They were all I dreamed of once,” Wolfe answered, his tone soft, “but my desires are far quieter now. I have been at arms since I was fourteen years old. I have done almost every duty a soldier can do, marched every garrison road in this country, served wherever there was fighting to be done for the last ten years. I have done almost all a man can do in this profession, save command an entire army—something a man my age can hardly hope for. Now I think I would trade it all for quiet, a shelf of books, a wife who loves me, and a few children to dandle on my knee. I have imagined that little Elysium for myself, Mr. Warrington. True love is better than glory, and a warm fireside with the woman of your heart seated beside it is the greatest gift the gods can send.”
Harry pictured that scene in his head, and nodded, but his face was far from cheerful.
“You say yes as if a quiet fireside and a sweetheart are not the things you crave most,” Wolfe noted.
“Look you, Colonel, you have seen sixteen years of the world, you have campaigned and made a name for yourself. I am barely a few months out from hanging off my mother’s apron strings. When I have seen a campaign or two, or six, like you, when I have distinguished myself and made the world talk of me, then I will think of settling down.”
Wolfe, his head full of nothing but his own Miss Lowther, launched into a long, glowing rhapsody on the joys of marriage, listing every one of his lady’s beauties and virtues in great, loving detail, while Harry’s own thoughts turned more melancholy on the subject of a wife and home. The afternoon wore pleasantly on, no accidents troubling their ride save for one small mishap a few miles outside Tunbridge: two horsemen rode out to meet them, and Harry grabbed his pistol ready to charge, convinced they were highwaymen. Wolfe laughed so hard he nearly fell off his horse, telling Harry to put his weapon away, the men were nothing more than agents from the town inns coming to greet new arrivals, not robbers (though Wolfe admitted they would rob a guest of a few extra shillings on the bill soon enough). Even Harry’s servant Gumbo panicked so badly at the sight of the two horsemen that his horse bolted, and they had to chase him down half a mile down the lane amid a great deal of shouting. Soon the pair rode through the gates of Tunbridge Wells, alighted at their inn, and parted ways, each to find the people they had come to visit.

Harry found his aunt Madame de Bernstein installed in handsome, well-furnished lodgings, with liveried London footmen loitering in the antechamber, ready to carry her chair through the town when she went out. She greeted him with the utmost warm affection. His cousin Lady Maria was out when he arrived, and Harry could not tell if he was disappointed not to see her, or if his aunt took any note of his half-hidden feelings. A foppish young beau in a rich figured silk suit (the first of its kind Harry had ever seen) and two plump dowagers in wide, voluminous hoops dusted with rouge were visiting the Baroness when Harry made his bow. She introduced her nephew to the three as the young Virginian Croesus they had no doubt heard rumours of, whose estate was larger than the entire county of Kent, and far more fertile. She told them his mother, Madam Esmond, was known back in the colonies as the Princess Pocahontas, and sang the praises of mother and son alike so loudly that the modest Harry blushed bright crimson under the stream of compliments. The three visitors shook his hand, curtsied, and departed as soon as they could, eager to spread the news of the wealthy, handsome young Virginian across all Tunbridge society.

“You could not have arrived at a better moment, my dear,” the Baroness said, cackling softly as the door shut behind her guests. “Those three have the busiest tongues in the whole Wells. By sundown every drawing room here will be talking of you. I told all sorts of ridiculous fibs about Virginia geography to describe your estate, you see—filled it full of exotic animals, spice groves, gold mines, I do believe I even mentioned diamonds at one point. I said your mother commands armies of negro servants, a veritable sovereign ruling over a magnificent domain. And you must prepare yourself to be treated as that domain’s heir apparent. Do not let your head turn. You will be flattered as no one has ever flattered you in your life.”
“But to what end, ma’am?” Harry asked, confused. “I see no reason to make people think I am far richer than I am.”
“First of all, you must not contradict your old aunt, and make me look a fool in front of my guests. And besides, that reputation was already waiting for you when we arrived. A London newspaper printed a story not long ago about the immensely wealthy young Virginian gentleman, nephew of Lord Castlewood, who had just landed in England. The golden key opens every door in this world, my boy. To be thought rich is just as good as being rich. You will not even need to spend much money. People will say you are careful with your fortune, and that reputation for prudence will only do you good. Watch how every mother in this town smiles on you, and every daughter curtsies low! Do not look so shocked. When I was a young woman, I did what every other pretty girl did, and tried to better myself with more than one well-placed marriage. Your sainted grandmother, the dear old thing, used to scold me and call me worldly. Worldly? The whole world is worldly, and we must serve it as it serves us, give nothing for nothing. Our family name has little credit these days, entre nous, and even less reputation. You know Castlewood was deeply suspected of treason in the ’45 rising? He gambled away every penny he had left after that, his whole estate is eaten up with mortgages.”
Harry had never heard a word of Lord Castlewood’s ruined reputation.
“He never had much to lose to begin with, but he lost all of it and more. He has chased every ridiculous scheme to raise money under the sun—at one point I was so afraid he would steal my diamonds right off my neck that I travelled back to Castlewood from London without a single piece of my own jewelry. Terrible, is it not, to speak so of my own nephew? But you are my nephew too, and you are still unspoiled by the world, so I must warn you of its wickedness. I heard you were playing cards with Will and the chaplain back at Castlewood, and that you won fairly from them. If you had sat down to play with Castlewood himself, you would not have won a single hand. I warned him to keep his greedy hands off you before you arrived, and he dared not disobey me.”
“Ma’am, you interfered to protect me?”
“I kept his clutches off you! You should be thankful you escaped that ogre’s den with any money left in your purse. That gambling fever is the curse of our whole family. My poor silly brother played every night, both his wives played, especially the last one, who lives on nothing but the proceeds of her London card parties now. I would not trust her alone in a room with you for ten minutes—they are all so hungry to win, they would fleece you clean, then turn on each other fighting over the plunder. Without his post at Court, my poor nephew would have nothing at all, and that is all Will’s fortune, and Maria’s, and his other daughters’ too.”
“And are they all fond of the cards as well?”
“Little Molly, no, to do her justice. But Fanny will bet her own eyes out of her head if you let her. I know the madness, sir—do not look so shocked. I had it as a girl, the same way I had the measles. I have never quite got over it. There is nothing else left for an old woman like me to enjoy. You will see high stakes played at my card tables tonight. Hush, dear! That is what I was craving, back at Castlewood where I moped so dull! I could never win of my nieces or their mother, they would never pay their debts if they lost. It is best I warn you in time, so you will not be too shocked to find out. I cannot live without the cards, there is no use pretending otherwise!”
A few days prior, staying with his Castlewood relatives, Harry—who loved cards, cockfights, wagers, and every other kind of sport—would have laughed at that confession. That household had laughed at far worse: they laughed at faith, at honour, at the idea of a pure, unselfish life. They proclaimed selfishness as a universal fact, sneered at sacred duties, and condoned every vice with a flippant shrug. They were no pharisees, these people; they did not pretend to be better than their neighbours, and they despised all the rest of the world heartily.
