Two days after that sore encounter of his soul, Gerard, driven by mere restlessness, strayed through one of the poorest lanes of Rome, where the houses crouched close as if for warmth, and the gutters ran black with yesterday’s washings. The sky had been sulky all morning; then, without warning, it broke. Thunder rolled over the tiles like cartwheels on a bridge, and rain came down in a hard, slanting sheet that made the street hiss and sparkle. The city’s usual din—hawkers, sandals, quarrels—was blotted and replaced by the rain’s iron drumming, the gurgle of sudden rivulets, and the deep animal groan of the storm.

He took refuge in a low hostelry whose door breathed out sour warmth: wet wool, old smoke, vinegar, and the sharp bite of cheap wine. The room was narrow and dim, with a hearth that struggled under damp wood; it gave more smell than heat—resin, soot, and a faint, stubborn sweetness where some cook had spilled must or honey long ago and it had baked into the stones.

“Wine,” said Gerard; and because the rain would not ease, and because the noise of the water on the roof seemed to hammer his thoughts flat, he drank again and again—thin red stuff that rasped the throat and left a bitter, leathery aftertaste. It dulled him not into comfort but into a heavy half-stupor, as if his head were packed in wool. He leaned forward, his hands on the table, his forehead on his hands, and dozed in the mixed perfume of damp cloaks, garlic, and stale lees.

By degrees the room filled. Men shook the rain from their hoods; benches scraped; cups clacked; voices rose—coarse laughter, oaths, arguments that burst and died like sparks. The storm outside made its own choir: thunder far off, rain nearer, and in the pauses the long, watery drip from the eaves. That layered sound—the world’s great drum above, the petty clatter within—brought Gerard back to a dull awareness.

Near him, two figures spoke low, close enough for their words to thread into his stupor. One, by his wallet and the confident swing of his hand, was a pardoner—one of those merchants of mercy that haunted roads and taverns, selling heaven in measured portions. The other wore clean but modest clothes such as a decent tradesman might; yet he had pulled his hat down over his brows in a way that hid his face and left only his beard to show. That slouched brim was an old language in Rome: it meant, “Do not know me.”

They were bargaining, not over bread or oil, but over sin itself; and in that traffic—common enough in those days—the wares were not yet delivered. The crime to be forgiven was still to be done, purchased beforehand under a sort of celestial contract.

The man with the hidden eyes grumbled at the price.

“If they go up any higher,” said he, “we poor fellows shall be shut out of heaven altogether.”

The pardoner answered with practiced indignation, the way a butcher denies his scales are false.

“Indulgences were never cheaper to good husbandmen.”

The other gave a short, dry laugh, as if at a jest without mirth. “Who are they?”

“Why,” said the pardoner smoothly, “such as sin by the market, like reasonable creatures. But if you will be so perverse as go and pick out a crime the Pope hath set his face against, blame yourself, not me!”

Then, to prove his point—like a cook unrolling a cloth of spices to show there is something for every purse—he drew out a written parchment and read a scale of prices. Gerard, with his head still bowed, heard the murmured catalogue drift through the tavern’s smells and sounds: rain, smoke, wine, and that low, obscene arithmetic. It was a curious list—too foul for clean ink here—yet it had the cold exactness of an account-book, as if a soul were merely another measure to be bought, like salt by the handful. Some sins, that many a layman would call light, carried a higher fee than the taking of a life; and the taking of a layman was named almost as casually as ordering a cup topped.

That was the pivot of the bargain. A little haggling followed—small concessions, like pennies tossed across a board. At last the coin clinked, and the pardoner’s hand closed upon it with the quick certainty of long habit. The buyer, by that payment, was absolved in advance for removing one layman. For the disposing of a clerk the rate bore no proportion; but the man under the slouched hat had not come to pay that dearer sum. Neither of them spoke the word that would have made the deed sound like what it was. They did not say “assassination.” They spoke as traders speak, trimming words as a fishmonger trims off heads and entrails, to leave only what sells.

All of it buzzed in Gerard’s ear, thickened by drink and grief. He did not lift his head. He listened, stupidly, as one listens to a nightmare while unable to wake.

When the two rose and parted—one to go on selling pardon like rancid oil sweetened with honey, the other to go purchase blood with clean coin—Gerard half raised his face. The lamplight caught his eyes; they were dark and raw with sleeplessness. He followed the departing figure with a scowl that was almost a snarl.

“If Margaret was alive,” he muttered hoarsely, “I’d take thee by the throat and throttle thee, thou cowardly stabber.”

His voice cracked on the name, as if it were a word in a language he could not bear to lose, yet could not speak without pain.

“But she is dead; dead; dead.” The tavern noise seemed to pull back, as if even the rude guests had moved away from that utterance. “Die all the world; ’tis nought to me: so that I die among the first.”

He stumbled home afterward through the washed streets, where the storm had left the stones shining like black glass and the air smelled momentarily cleaner—wet lime, wet dust, the sharp green of bruised leaves. Yet even that freshened breath of Rome could not reach whatever had gone numb in him. The city’s sounds—water running, distant bells muffled by rain, the occasional shout—came to him as if through a wall.

And when he turned into his own way, he saw, on the opposite side, a man in a slouched hat walking briskly to and fro, as if measuring the street with his feet. The brim hid the face; only the beard showed, and the posture of one who will not be looked at.

“Why, there is that cur again,” thought Gerard.

But he was in such a mind that the sight, though it ought to have struck fire in him, fell like a spark into wet ash. He went on, taking no heed; and the rain, still dripping from roofs and ledges, kept up its steady, indifferent music behind him.
