I fear I must seem wildly inconsistent at first glance. Earlier, I told you every being in Flatland appears to the eye as nothing but a straight line, and all but stated it was impossible to tell different social classes apart by sight alone. Yet here I am, about to explain to my Spaceland readers how the educated among us do exactly that: recognize friends, family, and strangers entirely by what our eyes perceive.

If you flip back to the passage where I described Recognition by Feeling as universal, you’ll spot the small qualifier I tucked in: *among the lower classes*. Sight recognition is a skill exclusive to the upper castes, and only reliably used in our temperate zones, where the atmospheric condition that makes it possible is common.

That condition is fog. In Spaceland, you curse it as an unmitigated nuisance: it blurs your views of rolling hills and forests, drags down your mood, even makes you sick. For us, it is a blessing almost as precious as the air we breathe, the cradle of our arts and the parent of all our sciences. Let me explain, no more flowery praise needed.
If there was no fog at all, every line would look equally sharp, no matter how far away it stood. That is precisely the miserable reality of the dry, torrid zones of Flatland, where no one can tell two figures apart by sight at all. But where fog lingers thick for most of the year, an object three feet away is noticeably dimmer than one 35 inches away. With years of careful, practiced observation of these tiny shifts in brightness and dimness, we can deduce the exact shape of the figure before us with shocking precision.

A simple example will make this clearer than a hundred pages of abstract explanation. Suppose two strangers are walking toward me, and I need to work out their rank. Let’s say one is an Equilateral Triangle (a Merchant, by law) and the other is a regular Pentagon (a Physician). How do I tell them apart by sight?
Imagine I tilt my eye until my line of sight cuts straight through the foremost angle of the approaching stranger, so my gaze falls evenly on the two sides on either side of that angle. Both sides will appear equally long, and I can compare their fade into the fog without bias.
Take the Merchant Triangle first. The line I see before me will have a bright, vivid dead center— that’s the point of the angle, closest to my eye. But the two sides recede *very sharply* away from me into the fog, so the brightness falls off fast. The far ends of the straight line I perceive will be so dim they are almost barely visible.
Now look at the Physician, the Pentagon. I still see a straight line with a bright central point, that nearest leading angle. But the sides extending left and right of that angle recede far more gradually than the Triangle’s did. Brightness fades slowly, so the far ends of the perceived line stay far brighter than the Merchant’s endpoints ever were.

That is the core logic. With decades of practice, the upper classes can reliably tell apart middle and low rank figures at a glance. If my Spaceland readers can grasp even that basic idea, and not write my whole account off as impossible nonsense, I have achieved all I can reasonably ask for. To pile on more tiny technical details would only confuse you.
But I should warn the younger, inexperienced readers who might think sight recognition is trivial, after my two simple examples of telling my triangular father apart from my hexagonal sons, that real life use of this skill is far more complicated. For instance: if my father, the Triangle, happens to turn to present his full side to me instead of his sharp leading angle, I will be stuck staring at a perfectly straight, uniformly bright line, and I will not be able to tell at first glance if he is a Triangle at all— he could easily be a thin Straight Line, AKA a Woman, until I ask him to rotate a little, or shift my own position to peer around him. Even my own young hexagonal grandson, if he faces me dead-on with one of his six sides fully forward, will look to my eye like one long bright line that barely fades at the ends, with two short, dim lines extending off either end that fade to black fast at their tips.

Any Spaceland schoolboy who has gotten past basic geometry will understand what I mean: for a well-educated Flatlander, navigating a crowded formal ballroom full of high-rank Polygons all whirling, moving forward, stepping back, and turning in every direction is an intellectual workout so rigorous that it more than justifies the full, lavish funding we give to Wentbridge University’s chairs of Static and Kinetic Geometry. Sight Recognition is taught there as a formal, rigorous art to the cream of Flatland’s elite. Only the wealthiest, most privileged noble families can afford the time and money to let their children spend years mastering it. Even I, a respected mathematician and grandfather to two perfect regular Hexagons, get confused sometimes when I find myself in a swirling crowd of high-caste Polygons. To a common Tradesman or Serf? That same scene is total gibberish, just as if you Spaceland readers were suddenly dropped straight into Flatland and surrounded by our most elegant high society gathering.
You would see nothing but straight lines all around you, their brightness flickering and shifting randomly nonstop. Even if you aced all three years of university theory classes on sight recognition, you would still need years of real world practice to move through that crowd without accidentally bumping into a noble of higher rank. Etiquette forbids you from asking them to let you “feel” their sides to confirm their identity, of course—they, with their years of training, can track every one of your movements perfectly, while you are blind to theirs. To move through high society perfectly politely, the old bitter saying goes, you must at least be a Polygon yourself. My own experience bears that out entirely.

The skill, almost an instinct, of sight recognition sharpens dramatically if you practice it every day and never rely on feeling. It is exactly like your Spaceland experience with deaf and mute people: if you let them rely on hand signs and finger alphabets, they will never learn the far more valuable, far harder skill of lip reading and speech. For us, any child who picks up the habit of feeling others will never master sight recognition perfectly.
That is why feeling is heavily discouraged, or banned entirely, in upper class households. Our children never go to the public elementary schools where feeling is the main skill taught. Instead they attend exclusive, elite seminaries, and at Wentbridge University, being caught feeling another person is a major disciplinary offense: first offense gets you suspended, second gets you expelled entirely.

For the poor working class, sight recognition is an unaffordable luxury. A tradesman cannot let his son waste a third of his life studying abstract geometry. Poor children are allowed to learn feeling from their earliest years, which makes them bright, sharp, and lively far earlier than the half-trained upper caste teenagers still slogging through their university courses. But once those Polygon youths finally graduate and put their years of theory to use, they are reborn, almost, and they rapidly outpace their Triangular peers in every art, science, and professional field.

A tiny minority of Polygon students never pass their final leaving exam at the University. Their fate is pitiable indeed. Rejected as unfit for the upper ranks, they are also scorned by the working classes. They have none of the polished, trained skill of their graduating peers, and none of the quick, mercurial wit of the tradesmen who learned feeling as toddlers. All professional careers and public office are closed to them. Most states do not formally ban them from marriage, but they almost never find a suitable partner, for experience tells us their children are almost always unfortunate, often Irregular in shape.
These failed ex-students, the dregs of the nobility, have been the leaders of nearly every great riot and sedition in our past history. The harm they cause is so terrible that a growing faction of progressive statesmen now argue that true mercy demands we eradicate them entirely: any student who fails the final university exam should either spend the rest of their life in prison, or be painlessly executed.

But I find I have wandered off into the topic of Irregular shapes— a subject of such overwhelming importance that it deserves its own full section.
