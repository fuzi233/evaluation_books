My journey across the vast, haunted expanse of Atlantis need not be detailed in its entirety. Though fraught with peril, it required but one significant pause—a necessity born of circumstance. One day, amidst the open savannah, I tracked and fell a stag after a long pursuit. Scanning the air for lurking threats, I deemed the location secure and began to butcher the beast, bundling the meat for transport.

It was then that disaster struck. Hidden beneath the tall grass lay a monstrous, multi-legged worm, a verdant horror venomous to the touch. As I shifted my weight, my heel brushed against it. Like a bolt of lightning, it lunged, its tiny head striking my lower thigh with deadly precision. Swiftly drawing my knife, I severed its neck. The creature convulsed, twisting its hundred legs into grotesque knots before dying. I cleaned my blade on the earth and drove it deep around the wound, ensuring the blood flowed freely to flush the poison from my veins. With the crimson liquid trickling healthily down my leg, I hoisted the game and marched on, grateful to escape what could easily have become a gruesome end.

Yet, as I walked, a tightening and throbbing seized my limb, intensifying with every passing hour. Soon, the swelling was severe, stretching my skin taut like parchment. A violent sickness racked my body; had a predator encountered me then, I would have been devoured without resistance. When night fell, I dragged myself into the crook of a tree, enduring sleepless agony throughout the dark hours.

At dawn, once the nocturnal beasts retreated to their dens, I descended, leaning heavily on my spear. Limping through the somber forests, I used the moss on the north sides of the trees as my guide. However, I soon noticed moss growing on all sides, a sure sign I was losing my bearings. Dizzy from pain and swelling, I pressed onward nonetheless, driven solely by the High Council's command to secure the enlargement of Nais.

My last conscious memory was of being intercepted in the black forest by a stranger who aided me, after which my senses faded into oblivion.

When consciousness returned, I found myself resting on a bed of leaves in a rocky crevice, reinforced with stakes against wild beasts. My wound was bandaged with herbal poultices, and nearby, a woman cooked a stew of wood-grubs and honey over a stick-fire.

"How did I come to be here?" I asked.

"I brought you," she replied simply.

"And who are you?"

"A nymph," she answered. "I practice as such—collecting herbs, healing the sick, reading fortunes, and casting curses upon those who refuse payment. It is a lean existence, especially since war and plague have thinned the ranks of the poor."

"Do you travel to Atlantis?"

"Not I. If the Empress Phorenice caught me, she would boil me in brine while I lived. Our dainty ruler tolerates no magic save her own. Rumor has it she intends to tear the Priests from their mountain."

"Then you hear news of the city?"

"Certainly. Without news, how could I sell my prophecies to the common folk? My lord, I discerned your nobility by your speech. Knowing you seek neither spells nor potions, I feel free to speak plainly."

"Does Phorenice still reign?"

"Vilely," she confirmed.

"As a maiden?"

"No longer. She bears twin sons. Her husband is now Tatho, a union formed three years ago."

"Tatho! The viceroy of Yucatan?"

"There is no Yucatan anymore," she said, her voice dropping. "A horde of little, hairy men from the West invaded. Armed only with stone-tipped clubs, their sheer numbers were their weapon. They desired no plunder or slaves, only the flesh of Atlanteans. They consumed every city they captured, leaving it to the forest beasts, before moving on. Tatho and the survivors fled by sea, escaping just in time—even his wife was eaten by them. These creatures are a persistent plague. Perhaps the Gods prevented them from crossing the ocean to Atlantis, for they would have been a far greater scourge than Phorenice."

I recalled tales of these sub-human horrors, beings held in check by the Gods, unleashed only as instruments of divine punishment. Grief gnawed at me for the fate of the Yucatan colony, where I had poured countless efforts, yet I said nothing to the nymph, deeming her too simple to understand the gravity of the truth.

"What of Egypt?" I asked, changing the subject.

"In eternal darkness," she sighed. "Who cares for Egypt today? Or anything but oneself? Once, pilgrims brought offerings out of pure devotion. Now, they only visit if they expect profit. Consequently, I starve on honey and grubs. It is a wretched life for a nymph in these times; everyone has abandoned belief in mystery."

"What pestilence do you speak of?"

"I have not seen it, thank the Gods. But rumors say it stems from the unburied corpses of those Phorenice has slaughtered. She kills endlessly, piling bodies higher than nature can consume. For what sin did the Gods allow her rise? I pray she and her twins are boiled alive before they corrupt an honest forest spirit further."

"She has placed her image in every temple," the nymph continued, offering me the grub-and-honey stew. "She demands prayers and sacrifices day and night, declaring all other gods inferior. Those insufficiently devout have their hamstrings slit to force a posture of worship. Will you eat? You arrived bleeding, and now you are empty-handed. Choose: feast or starve."

I stayed with the nymph for six days, allowing her charms to heal my leg. Upon recovery, I hunted a fat horse on the plains to pay my debt and resumed my journey.

Beyond that point, the land bore the marks of a once-thriving populace. Houses were perched in trees or built of massive, dovetailed logs designed to withstand beasts. Yet, fewer than one in ten settlements remained. Fires had gone cold, vegetable patches reclaimed by jungle, and village gates blocked by rotting logs. Phorenice's influence spread like a blight over a land that had once smiled.

Men I encountered mostly fled into the forest at my approach. Some threatened but held back, sensing my lack of malice; a few offered shelter. As I neared the city, encountering denser populations, the danger of capture grew. Watchers in towers would spot me and shout, "Masterless man!" prompting mobs to strip me of my weapons and conscript me as labor. Filled with pity for their plight, I often used words to pass, or struck lightly if necessary, striving to move quickly without leaving unnecessary corpses. Eventually, I learned to skirt villages entirely, turning into the forest whenever I smelled their refuse.

Approaching the greater towns, I kept outside their walls, unwilling to risk the whims of new laws imposed by fearful governors. My progress was slow, but my pride demanded I not submit to arbitrary detention. The fever of haste burned within me, leaving little room for other emotions.

Finally, I drew near the city of Atlantis itself. The Sacred Mountain loomed ahead, crowned by a ring of eternal fires. Caution became paramount. Every sign indicated the countryside had been stripped of its warriors. Women prayed for the defeat of the Priests or the death of Phorenice, hoping for peace.

An army besieged the Sacred Mountain, guarding its single approach with narrow ferocity. After such a arduous journey, it seemed impossible to fulfill the High Council's orders and claim my reward. Direct force was futile; I would face one glorious battle against an entire army, ending my utility. Guile was the only path.

Hiding in the outskirts of a wood, I plotted. Across the plain lay the grim city walls, with temples and pyramids rising beyond. My eyes fixed on the royal pyramid, its steps overlaid with Phorenice's newly acquired gold. But it was not the wealth that stirred me. In the plaza before the pyramid stood a granite throne carved with snakes and an outstretched hand. Beneath it slept Nais, my love, drugged and unconscious for eight years, yet alive and waiting. All I needed was a way to reach Zaemon, defy the Empress, and announce my arrival.

For a day and night, I raged in the woods, desperate for a plan. At dawn, a runner approached, breathless.

"You need not threaten me," he cried, lowering his hands. "I mean no harm. You are Deucalion, though I would not recognize you in these rags and tangled hair. Give me your word; I have not delayed."

He was Ro, a lower priest I knew poorly—a greedy soul unworthy of trust.

"From whom do you come?" I asked.

"Zaemon commanded me," he stammered. "He visited my home, though how he bypassed Phorenice's blockade, I cannot say. I told him I wished no part of his schemes. I am a peaceful man with a wife needing food. I still serve in the temple, albeit one cleansed of old gods and filled with hers. The people are pious, and offerings abound. I had no desire to jeopardize my comfortable position."

"I believe you, Ro."

"But Zaemon... he erupted in black fury. While he spoke, I felt as though I were dying. Do you know his powers?"

"Some," I admitted.
