“You start at shadows easily,” Piero said again, and his laugh had the hard ring of steel struck against stone. “My portrait was poor—poor as a cook who can name every herb and yet cannot coax their breath into the pot. But the original—ah, the old fellow had the tiger in him. I must go into the Duomo and look at him again.”

“It is no pleasant thing,” Lorenzo Tornabuoni answered, smoothing his courtesy over Tito’s shaken look as one lays oil upon troubled water, “to be gripped by a madman—if madman he be. Yet perhaps he is only a ruffian. We shall hear. But we must see whether we have authority enough to stop this brawling between our people and your countrymen.” He turned that last to the Frenchman.

They went forward with their swords out; not in haste, but with that deliberate advance which makes a way through a crowd as a paddle cleaves a canal. The quiet onlookers, drawn by the scent of trouble as by the smell of roasting chestnuts at a corner brazier, closed in behind them, forming an escort. Tito went also. He could not do otherwise. Whatever Florence should know of this Baldassarre must be measured and tasted by him first, as a careful steward tastes wine before he lets it be poured for guests. And already the first palsy of terror—cold as wet linen clapped upon the skin—was giving place to quick devices, as timidity will run when danger snaps at its heels.

The rabble of men and boys, not so much bent on striking as on hooting and worrying the soldier—like sparrows tormenting a hawk they dare not face alone—fell back when the signori approached with naked swords. The French soldier was questioned. His story came out plain enough, though his tongue and theirs did not fit well together: he and his fellows had brought prisoners inside the walls to beg money for their ransom. Two were Tuscan soldiers taken in Lunigiana; the third was an elderly man caught with a party of Genoese, after French foragers had clashed with them near Fivizzano. The old one might be mad; he was harmless. The soldier knew nothing more, for he could not understand a word the elder said—those strange sounds, flung out with the urgency of hunger, were to him mere harsh wind in a foreign street.

Tito heard thus far; then all other speech became muffled, as if a hand had been pressed over his ears. He caught only the city’s common noises—the clack of a sandal, the thin crying of a boy selling figs, the harsh chorus of jeers—and through that, an inward sound louder than all: the beating of his own blood. He was roused only when one voice, distinct and near, addressed him.

It was Tornabuoni. “Will you come back with us, Melema? Or, since Messere is going off to Signa now, will you wisely follow the fashion of the times and go hear the Frate? He will be like the torrent at its height this morning—muddy, deafening, carrying everything with it. It’s what we must all do, you know, if we would save our Medicean skins. I should go myself if I had the leisure.”

Colour had returned to Tito’s cheeks—like warmth coaxed back into chilled fingers held near a kitchen fire—and he made an effort to answer lightly. “Of course I am among the admirers of the inspired orator,” he said, smiling; “but unfortunately I shall be occupied with the Segretario until the time of the procession.”

“I am going into the Duomo,” Piero said, with a boy’s persistence, “to look at that savage old man again.”

“Then have charity enough,” Tornabuoni replied, “to show him to one of the hospitals for travellers, Piero mio. The monks may find out whether he belongs in a cage.”

So they parted—words breaking apart like bread shared at a table, each man taking his portion and turning away. Tito went toward the Palazzo Vecchio, where Bartolommeo Scala awaited him.

The walk was short, yet for Tito it stretched and lengthened like the minutes in a morning dream, when a man thinks he moves and still the door will not be reached. Each patch of street and piazza held something that pricked him: memory of what he had done, foresight of what might be done to him, fear gnawing with small sharp teeth. It was as if a serpent had begun to coil around his limbs—not in a sudden strike, but in patient tightening, the kind that leaves no space to breathe.

Baldassarre living, and in Florence, was not merely an old man risen from a grave: he was revenge made flesh—a living thing that would not lie down and sleep, any more than a winding serpent rests until it has crushed its prey. Tito knew the temper of that man: love and hatred in him were not passing tastes, but burning appetites that subdued all else, like a fever that takes over the body and makes even water taste bitter. Such passion can turn a man into his own sacrificer, laying himself on the altar of the thing he worships—self-destruction offered up as devotion.

Baldassarre had slackened his grasp and vanished. Tito read that as clearly as a cook reads from the colour of an onion just how long it has sweated in oil: the vengeance was not abandoned; it was being studied so it might be sure. If he had not flung out those decisive words—“He is a madman”—if he had had the courage to acknowledge Baldassarre, would the risk have been less? He might have said he had received what he believed to be certain proof of Baldassarre’s death. And who could contradict him with certainty? Fra Luca was dead. The crew of the companion galley—those who had brought the news of the fight with pirates—were scattered shadows on the sea’s memory. What were the chances that Baldassarre had ever met again any man among them? Tito tasted bitterness at the thought that a timely, well-seasoned falsehood, devised in the right instant, might have saved him from fatal consequences.

Yet that falsehood would have demanded perfect self-command at the moment of shock. And he had not had it. The words had leaped from him without premeditation, like something born suddenly into the light after being begotten and nourished in darkness. Tito was meeting that inexorable law of souls: we school ourselves for sudden deeds by the repeated choice of good or evil, until character is formed as surely as dough is formed by kneading—each push and fold adding to the final texture.

There was now, as he saw it, only one chance: the chance that Baldassarre might fail. And then Tito’s mind, driven into cruelty by fear, clutched at a thought more active and more hateful than any he had allowed himself before. Might not his own unguarded words have some truth in them? Enough truth, at least, to shelter him—to let him deny whatever Baldassarre might say about him, and have others believe the denial? The old man had looked strange and wild. A heart and brain as eager as Baldassarre’s, long gnawed by suffering, might well have been pushed into madness. If so, then the vengeance that aimed at disgrace might be baffled by the world’s shrug and laughter.

But there was another vengeance—one that could not be baffled by wit, by phrasing, by any clever turning of a story as a spit is turned over the fire. Baldassarre belonged to a race to whom the dagger’s thrust seems as natural as a tiger’s outleap. Tito shuddered at disgrace—yes; but he carried also that physical dread which clings to a soft, pleasure-loving nature, and makes wounds and death not a release but an abomination. His mind flew at once, with a practical swiftness, to hidden defensive armour—something under his silk that might turn steel aside.

He wondered at the mastery of this fear, how it had seized him. It was like a blighting disease fallen in a night, turning the young joy of life into pain, so that even the air tasted different, as if the bread of existence had been baked with ash. And still, there remained one open resource: he might turn back, seek Baldassarre, confess all—confess to him, to Romola, to all the world.

But that thought did not truly enter him; it hovered and passed like the sound of a distant bell lost under nearer clamor. Repentance that breaks every mooring to evil asks for more than selfish fear. Tito had no sense that truth could be strength and safety; the only strength he trusted was the strength of his own ingenuity and dissimulation. Now that the first shock—which had betrayed him by the telltale signs of fear—had ebbed, he believed he could be ready for all emergencies with cool deceit, and with armour.

And it marked Tito’s nature at this crisis that no direct measure for ridding himself of Baldassarre occurred to him. Possibilities passed before him—lying, denial, flight even—yet never the thought of removing his enemy. His dread bred no active malignity; he would still have been glad to spare pain to any mortal. His life had been shaped by one continual choice: to make existence easy to himself, to carry his lot so it should pinch him nowhere—seeking always the softest cushion, the sweetest morsel, the quietest corner where harsh sounds could be kept at bay. That habitual choice had set him, more than once, in places he had never meant to enter.

Now the question was not whether he would share the common pressure of destiny with his suffering fellow-men. It was whether all the resources of lying—those words that slip between tongues and meanings, those convenient translations of the self into whatever dialect power requires—could save him from being crushed at last by the consequences of his own making.
