Early the next morning, Tito returned from Bratti’s shop in the narrow lane of the Ferravecchi. The Genoese stranger had taken the onyx ring, and Tito carried away fifty florins. For a fleeting moment, he wondered whether Fortune—by one of her cunning turns—might spare him the need to leave Florence. If so, it would have been better not to have parted with the ring, since it was known he wore it for reasons of deep personal significance. But such thoughts were slight, scarcely worth dwelling on; and at that moment, he no longer trusted Fortune. The sharp anxiety of the first alarm had faded into a dull, heavy regret. He valued the approval of others so deeply that now he wished he had never shied away from what they called duty—never risked shame by refusing their expectations.

Yet our actions are like children born to us: once they come into being, they live apart from our will. Children may be silenced or even killed, but deeds cannot be undone. They possess an unyielding life, both within and beyond our awareness—and this relentless vitality of past choices was pressing upon Tito for the first time.

He walked back toward his lodgings in the Piazza di San Giovanni, avoiding the Mercato Vecchio, though it was the shortest route. He did not want to see Tessa. He wasn’t in the mood to seek anything; he could only wait for the first sign of change in his fate.

The piazza shimmered under the warm autumn sunlight, the kind that lingers after dew has kissed the stones and invites quiet contemplation without fatigue. It was a festival day—the Nativity of the Virgin—and the air felt thick with soft warmth, as if urging people to linger and look. Signs of the fair dotted the square: stalls spread with fruit and flowers near the octagonal baptistery, mules laden with goods quietly chewing from their nose-bags while their drivers slipped inside the sacred doors to kneel before the Virgin. On the broad marble steps of the Duomo, groups gathered—beggars, talkers, and idle watchers. An old woman with sunburnt skin and white hair coaxed a barefoot child to walk on the warmed stone, while a dog nearby sniffed warily at the spectacle. Two rough-haired boys leaned over to watch a pale, crippled boy carve a face into a cherry pit. Above them, men stood on the platform, making knots in ropes or arguing fiercely in pairs.

But the most crowded spot was where Tito needed to go—the barber’s shop of Nello. It was peak hour, and during the warm season, many preferred to be shaved under the red-and-white awning outside rather than in the cramped interior. There is little dignity in sitting with a lathered chin tilted back, your nose used as a handle—but shaving was a mark of Florentine respectability, and men took themselves seriously when they all followed the same custom. This was also the time when yesterday’s gossip was freshest, and Nello’s tongue ran as smoothly as his razor. The two instruments seemed to move in tandem, driven by the same spring. Tito knew he couldn’t escape the circle—he would have to smile, respond, appear perfectly at ease. Well, it was only another trial, like swallowing bread and cheese pills. Anyone who let fear of discovery paralyze him was simply weak-nerved.

Just then, a hand landed on his shoulder. No amount of resolve could prevent the jolt of unease that shot through him. His face betrayed the shock as he turned—until the man who had touched him laughed lightly. He was a young man about Tito’s age, sharp-featured, with closely cropped hair and a clean-shaven face, giving the impression of a mind stripped bare of unnecessary matter. His eyes sparkled with hope and friendliness—like so many others who, years later, would close in bitterness. At the time, however, Niccolò Macchiavelli was seen as a promising youth, destined to restore his family’s fallen fortune.

“Why, Melema,” said the young man, “what terrible dream did you have last night that made my light touch feel like that of a _sbirro_ or worse?”

“Ah, Messer Niccolò,” replied Tito, regaining composure quickly, “it must have been extra sluggishness in my veins this morning that made me flinch at your wit. But truly, I’ve had a bad night.”

“That’s unfortunate,” said Macchiavelli. “You’ll be expected to shine today at the Rucellai Gardens. I assume you’re going.”

“Messer Bernardo honored me with an invitation,” Tito answered, “but I shall be elsewhere.”

“Oh? I remember—you’re in love,” said Macchiavelli, shrugging. “Otherwise, you’d never have such inconvenient engagements. We’re to eat peacock and ortolans under the loggia among Bernardo Rucellai’s rare trees. The finest minds in Florence, the best wines. Only, since Piero de’ Medici is coming, we may find ourselves drowned in impromptu poetry contests. I hate that game—it’s a trick for small minds, who always get inspired by the smallest occasions.”

“What do you mean about Piero de’ Medici and small wits, Messer Niccolò?” asked Nello, whose slender figure now dominated the space beside the bulky Niccolò Caparra.

The famous ironworker—last seen in the Mercato Vecchio with bared arms and leather apron—was dressed in festive clothes today. As he sat submissively while Nello skipped around him, lathering his face, gripping his nose, and shaving him with swift precision, he looked much like a lion who had donned linen and a tunic to enter society.

“A private secretary won’t rise far if he mixes great and small so freely,” Nello continued. “When great men can’t marry their children as they wish, small men shouldn’t expect to marry their words as they please. Have you heard the news Domenico Cennini has been telling us—that Pagolantonio Soderini slapped Ser Piero da Bibbiena for encouraging Piero de’ Medici to interfere with the marriage between young Tommaso Soderini and Fiammetta Strozzi? And now he’s to be sent as ambassador to Venice as punishment?”

“I don’t know which I envy more,” said Macchiavelli, “the offense or the punishment. The offense will make him the most popular man in Florence. The punishment will send him to the only people in Italy who’ve ever managed their own affairs.”

“If Soderini stays long enough in Venice,” added Cennini, “he might learn their ways and bring them home. The Soderini have long been allies of the Medici, but this event may open Pagolantonio’s eyes to the old Florentine habit of changing harness when the old one chafes. We haven’t quite lost that knack in fifty years.”

“No, we haven’t,” said Caparra, now free to speak again. “Eat eggs in Lent, and the snow will melt. That’s what I tell our people when they grow loud at San Gallo and talk of starting a _romor_. Never plan a revolt—it’s like trying to fill the Arno with buckets. When there’s enough water, the river fills itself. And that won’t happen until the torrent is ready.”

“Caparra,” said Nello, “your oracle-like wisdom comes from my excellent shave. You couldn’t have spoken so wisely with that dark stubble on your chin. Ecco, Messer Domenico—I’m ready for you now. By the way, my learned friend,” he added, seeing Tito move toward the door, “old Maso came looking for you, but your room was empty. He’ll return soon. He looked troubled, and hurried. I hope nothing’s wrong in the Via de’ Bardi.”

“Doubtless Messer Tito knows that Bardo’s son is dead,” said Cronaca, who had just arrived.

Tito’s heart leapt—had the death occurred before Romola saw him?

“No, I hadn’t heard,” he said, calm as the situation allowed, turning and leaning against the doorpost as if he’d abandoned his intention to leave. “I knew his sister had gone to visit him. Did he die before she arrived?”

“No,” said Cronaca. “I was at San Marco at the time and saw her emerge from the chapter-house with Fra Girolamo, who told us the dying man’s breath had been miraculously preserved so he could make a confession to his sister.”

Tito felt his fate sealed. His mind raced through every detail of his departure from Florence, and a plan formed: recover his money from Cennini before the revelation became public. With the money, he wouldn’t need to endure scathing glances or cruel words for long. He would wait, then leave with Cennini and reclaim his funds immediately. With that thought, he stood still—hands tucked in his belt, eyes fixed absentmindedly on the ground.

Nello glanced at him and sensed he was consumed by worry over Romola. He found Tito’s expression so touching—a picture of self-forgetful sorrow—that he subtly pointed his razor at him and gave a challenging look to Piero di Cosimo, whom he had never forgiven for dismissing any character reading in his favorite’s handsome face. Piero, leaning near Tito, shrugged. Nello’s repeated challenges had transformed the painter’s neutrality into a quiet skepticism about the praised Greek.

“So you’ve got your Fra Girolamo back, Cronaca?” said Nello. “I suppose we’ll have him preaching again this Advent?”

“And not before it’s needed,” said Cronaca gravely. “We’ve had proof of his words since the last Quaresima. Even wickedness has become a plague. Vice is rotting in the nostrils of the very wicked. Nothing has changed in Rome or Florence since then that doesn’t confirm the Frate’s warning: the harvest of sin is ripe, and God will reap it with a sword.”

“I hope he’s had a new vision,” said Francesco Cei, sneering. “The old ones are stale. Can’t your monk get a poet to help him imagine?”

“He has poets around him,” replied Cronaca coldly, “but they’re great poets, not petty ones. They’re content to learn from him, and don’t think truth stale because God gave it to him—just as they don’t think moonlight stale. But perhaps certain high prelates and princes who dislike the Frate’s warnings would be pleased to hear that while Giovanni Pico, Poliziano, Marsilio Ficino, and most notable men in Florence revere Fra Girolamo, Messer Francesco Cei despises him.”

“Poliziano?” Cei scoffed. “Of course he believes in your new Jonah—look at the fine orations he wrote for Sienna’s envoys to tell Alexander VI that the world and Church had never been better off since he became Pope.”

“Francesco,” said Macchiavelli, smiling, “a scholar of many parts must hold many opinions. As for the Frate, whatever we think of his holiness, you judge his preaching too narrowly. The secret of oratory isn’t in saying new things, but in saying things with such power that they move the listener. Without that, as Filelfo said, your speaker deserves to be called ‘not an orator, but a ploughman.’ By that measure, Fra Girolamo is a great orator.”

“That’s true,” said Cennini, speaking from the chair. “But part of the secret lies in prophetic visions. Our people—no offense, Cronaca—will follow anyone who looks like a prophet, especially if he predicts disasters.”

“Rather say,” replied Cronaca, “that the real secret lies in the Frate’s pure life and strong faith, which mark him as a messenger of God.”

“I admit it,” said Cennini, raising his palms as he rose. “His life is blameless—no one has accused it.”

“He’s satisfied with the pleasure of arrogance,” burst out Cei bitterly. “I see it in his proud lip and smug eye. He hears the air filled with his name—Fra Girolamo Savonarola of Ferrara, the prophet, the saint, the mighty preacher who frightens Florence’s babies into dropping their toys.”

“Come, come, Francesco,” said Nello soothingly. “Let me stop your mouth with a bit of lather. I won’t let my friend Cronaca grow angry—I care for his chin, and it hasn’t changed since he became a Piagnone. As for me, I confess: when the Frate preached in the Duomo last Advent, I fell into the habit of slipping in to listen. I might have become a Piagnone myself—if not for the generosity of my trade, and the length of the sermons, which sometimes take ages to reach the emotional point. But as Messer Niccolò says, the Frate holds the people by some power beyond prophecy. Monks who prophesy aren’t rare. As Luigi Pulci said: ‘Dombruno’s scimitar was said to be enchanted—but I suspect it cut well because it was forged from hard steel.’ Yes, yes—paternosters may shave cleanly, but only if said over a good razor.”

“See, Nello!” said Macchiavelli. “What doctor approaches on his Bucephalus? I thought your piazza was free of those furred, scarlet-robed servants of death. This man looks as if he’d had a night like Boccaccio’s Maestro Simone—his hat and cloak soaked in gutter water—though he himself is sleek as a miller’s rat.”

“A–ah!” said Nello, drawing out the syllable as he looked up. A round-headed, round-bodied man rode a young, stubborn horse that kept its head high and resisted commands, constantly trying to veer sideways. Its attitude suggested views on authority far ahead of its time.

“And I’ve prepared a few surprises for him,” Nello whispered. “I hope they’ll drive his curious nose elsewhere. He’s a doctor from Padua—they say he stayed three months in Prato, now he’s come to Florence to see what he can catch. His specialty is visiting peasants. Note those large saddlebags—they’re meant to carry the fat capons, eggs, and flour he collects from poor farmers who lack coin. He sells his own remedies, so he avoids apothecaries. For the past week, he’s sat in my piazza daily, attracting asthmatics and crying infants. It infuriates me to see the toad-faced quack handling greasy coins or accepting a pigeon in exchange for his pills. But I’ll put thorns in his saddle—else I’m no Florentine. Laudamus! He’s coming to be shaved—that’s what I’ve waited for. Messer Domenico, stay—wait. You’ll witness a rare jest I devised two days ago. Here, Sandro!”

Nello whispered to Sandro, who rolled his eyes solemnly, nodded, smiled slowly, and darted away with surprising speed.

“How are you, Maestro Tacco?” said Nello as the doctor struggled to turn his horse toward the shop. “Fine young horse, though a bit raw in the mouth, eh?”

“It’s a cursed beast—may the _vermocane_ take it!” the doctor exclaimed, dismounting and fastening the rope-repaired bridle to an iron staple. “Still,” he added, recovering, “a sound animal, valuable if one wants to buy and train it for profit. I got it cheap.”

“Too much riding for someone carrying your weight of learning, eh, Maestro?” said Nello. “You seem hot.”

“Indeed, I am likely to be,” said the doctor, removing his hat and revealing a bald, low forehead, flat broad face, high ears, wide lipless mouth, round eyes, and deep arches above prominent brows—making Nello’s “toad-faced” description oddly apt. “Riding from Peretola under the sun is no match for kicking heels on a shaded bench like your Florence doctors. Besides, I’ve had trouble navigating through carts and mules into the Mercato to find the husband of Monna Ghita, who suffered a fatal seizure before I arrived. And had I not demanded payment—”

“Monna Ghita!” said Nello, as the sweating doctor paused to wipe his face. “Peace to her angry soul! The Mercato will need one fewer whip if her tongue is silenced.”

Tito, pulled from his reverie, felt a sudden surge of half-formed thoughts about Tessa—those that had crossed his mind the night before. If Monna Ghita were truly gone, it might be easier to see Tessa again—whenever he chose.

“_Gnaffè_, Maestro,” Nello continued sympathetically, “you’re the servant of rude mortals who, without you, would die like beasts. It’s sad to see your learned fluid oozing like mere sweat. You think my shaving will cool and clear you? One moment—I finish Messer Francesco here. It feels like a thousand years until I serve a man who carries all the science of Arabia in his head and saddlebags. Ecco!”

Nello held up the shaving cloth invitingly. Maestro Tacco stepped forward, absorbed in his heat and importance, deaf to the irony in Nello’s polished politeness.

“It’s fitting,” said Nello, adjusting the cloth, “that a great medicus like you should be shaved by the same razor that served the illustrious Antonio Benevieni, master of surgery.”

“The surgical art!” interrupted the doctor, offended. “Is it your Florentine custom to place medicine’s noble science—Hippocrates, Galen, Avicenna—on equal footing with barbers who mend broken limbs like carpenters, stitch wounds like tailors, or carve flesh like butchers? _Via_! A manual craft, fit for any artisan, practiced by simpletons like yourself—how can it compare to the divine knowledge of stars, plants, and gems?”

“No, Maestro,” said Nello, applying lather slowly, as if determined to prolong the process. “I never claimed equality. I know your science rivals the miracles of Holy Church. But here’s the pity—your lofty knowledge remains hidden from the vulgar, so you become envied and slandered. I grieve to say it, but there are low fellows in this city—mere _sgherri_, wearing nightcaps and long beards—who delight in poisoning every man’s broth who thrives. Let me tell you, as a stranger—you must carry a large nail ready to pin down Fortune’s wheel when your side rises. Already, stories—fables, doubtless—are spreading about you, and I wish I could hear you heading for Arezzo. I wouldn’t want a man of your caliber stoned. Though San Stefano was stoned, he wasn’t a great physician like San Cosmo and San Damiano…”

“What stories? What fables?” stammered Maestro Tacco.

“_Lasso_! I fear you’ve walked into the trap for your cheese, Maestro. The fact is, there’s a gang of mischievous youths who prowl houses with sharp tools in their pockets—no door, window, or shutter safe from them. They’re possessed with diabolical patience, watching those who believe themselves private. It must be them who’ve done this—it must be them who’ve spread tales about you and your medicines. Have you noticed any cracks in your door or shutters? No? Then I advise you to check. It’s widely rumored that you’ve been seen at your house in the Canto di Paglia, preparing your secret remedies at night: grinding dried toads in a mortar, mixing salve from crushed worms, making pills from dried rat livers mixed with saliva spat during blasphemous incantations—which these witnesses claim to repeat verbatim.”

“It’s a lie!” shouted the doctor, struggling to speak, then silencing himself as the razor neared.

“You needn’t say that to us, doctor,” said Nello calmly. “We’re not the sort to plant such carrots. But what of it? What are a few reasonable men against a crowd with stones? Some of us believe Cecco d’Ascoli was an innocent sage—and we all know how he burned for being wiser than his peers. Ah, doctor, you won’t learn Florentines by studying in Padua. My belief is, they’d stone the Holy Father himself if they could find a reason. They’re convinced you’re a necromancer trying to raise the plague with secret medicines—and I’m told your remedies truly smell foul.”

“It’s false!” cried the doctor as Nello moved the razor aside. “It’s false! I’ll show these honorable gentlemen the pills, powders—this salve—it has a wonderful scent—an odor of… of salve.” He rose, lather still on his chin, cloth around his neck, searching his saddlebag for the maligned medicines. In an instant, Nello shifted the chair near the horse’s head, and Sandro, returning at his master’s signal, stepped beside the bridle.

“Behold, Messeri!” declared the doctor, holding up a small box of medicines and opening it before them.
