While Shirley spoke below with Moore, Caroline went back to the upper rooms and found Mrs. Pryor seated where the light was kindest, as if she had chosen the gentlest corner for a spirit that would not be comforted. The older lady’s face held itself composed, but the composure was only a smooth lid over pain. She would not call Shirley’s hastiness a wound; yet it galled, and Caroline—who had learned to read her—as surely felt it as if she had seen the blood.

To any less congruous nature, Mrs. Pryor’s stillness might have seemed indifference to consolation; for Caroline moved about quietly, set a shawl closer on her shoulders, straightened a ribbon, lifted a glass and persuaded her to sip, and met no visible response. But Caroline knew that those slight offices—like warm broth carried to a sick-room and taken in silence—were received, valued, and did their healing work.

At length Mrs. Pryor spoke, not with complaint, but with that melancholy accuracy which belongs to timid consciences.

“I am deficient in self-confidence and decision,” she said. “I always have been deficient in those qualities. Yet I think Miss Keeldar should have known my character well enough by this time to be aware that I always feel an even painful solicitude to do right, to act for the best. The unusual nature of the demand on my judgment puzzled me, especially following the alarms of the night. I could not venture to act promptly for another; but I trust no serious harm will result from my lapse of firmness.”

A gentle knock touched the door—timid at first, then more urgent. It opened half-way.

“Caroline, come here,” said a low voice.

Caroline stepped into the gallery. Shirley stood there, her proud vivacity sobered into something like a child’s shame. The very air around her seemed to have lost its swaggering music.

“How is Mrs. Pryor?” she asked.

“Rather out of spirits.”

“I have behaved very shamefully—very ungenerously—very ungratefully to her,” Shirley said, the words crowding as if they could not get out fast enough. “How insolent in me to turn on her thus for what, after all, was no fault—only an excess of conscientiousness on her part. But I regret my error most sincerely. Tell her so, and ask if she will forgive me.”

Caroline carried the message back with a warmth that made it a pleasure. Mrs. Pryor rose at once; she had the look of one who dreads a scene as she dreads thunder, yet will not refuse shelter.

“Come in, my dear,” she said falteringly.

Shirley entered, not creeping, but with a rush of penitent energy. She threw her arms round her governess and kissed her with hearty impulsiveness.

“You know you must forgive me, Mrs. Pryor. I could not get on at all if there was a misunderstanding between you and me.”

“I have nothing to forgive,” Mrs. Pryor answered, and the very gentleness of her tone showed how much she wished to end it. “We will pass it over now, if you please. The final result of the incident is that it proves more plainly than ever how unequal I am to certain crises.”

That was the sting which would remain: not Shirley’s offence, which she could pardon, but her own helplessness, which she could not. No caress, no dutiful word from Shirley, no tender watching from Caroline, could coax that thought out of its hidden place.

Before long, Shirley—doomed, as people of consequence are, to be claimed like a shared dish at a crowded table—was called downstairs again. Morning visitors succeeded each other as regularly as courses.

The rector came first. His greeting was lively, his reprimand livelier; and he took both as his due. He was so brimful of last night’s tumult that he forgot, in the heat of talk, to inquire after his niece. Riot and rioters, the mill, the magistrates, the heiress, the parish, the law—these were his family for the moment.

He spoke of the part he and his curate had played in the defence of the Hollow.

“The vials of pharisaical wrath will be emptied on our heads for our share in this business,” he said; “but I defy every calumniator. I was there only to support the law, to play my part as a man and a Briton; which characters I deem quite compatible with those of the priest and Levite, in their highest sense. Your tenant Moore has won my approbation. A cooler commander I would not wish to see, nor a more determined. Besides, the man has shown sound judgment and good sense—first, in being thoroughly prepared for the event which has taken place; and subsequently, when his well-concerted plans had secured him success, in knowing how to use without abusing his victory. Some of the magistrates are now well frightened, and, like all cowards, show a tendency to be cruel. Moore restrains them with admirable prudence. He has hitherto been very unpopular in the neighbourhood; but, mark my words, the tide of opinion will now take a turn in his favour. People will find out that they have not appreciated him, and will hasten to remedy their error; and he, when he perceives the public disposed to acknowledge his merits, will show a more gracious mien than that with which he has hitherto favoured us.”

He was gathering himself to deliver—half in jest and half in warning—some allusion to Shirley’s rumored partiality for her talented tenant, when the bell rang below with another summons. The new visitor appeared in the doorway in the shape of a white-haired old gentleman, with a truculent countenance and a disdainful eye—Mr. Yorke, the rector’s old enemy.

At once, priest and Levite seized hat and stick and made the shortest adieu to Miss Keeldar, the sternest nod to her guest, and were gone as if driven out by a gust.

Mr. Yorke was in no mild mood. His opinion of the night’s transaction came in rough gusts—each gust loaded with Yorkshire Doric, as pungent as mustard ground fresh under a pestle. Moore, the magistrates, the soldiers, the mob leaders—none were spared; but his most racy epithets were saved for the “fighting parsons,” the “sanguinary, demoniac” rector and curate. According to him, ecclesiastical guilt had come to the brim.

“The church,” he said, “was in a bonny pickle now. It was time it came down when parsons took to swaggering amang soldiers, blazing away wi’ bullet and gunpowder, taking the lives of far honester men than themselves.”

“What would Moore have done if nobody had helped him?” Shirley asked.

“Drunk as he’d brewed, eaten as he’d baked.”

“Which means you would have left him by himself to face that mob. Good! He has plenty of courage, but the greatest amount of gallantry that ever garrisoned one human breast could scarce avail against two hundred.”

“He had the soldiers, those poor slaves who hire out their own blood and spill other folk’s for money.”

“You abuse soldiers almost as much as you abuse clergymen. All who wear red coats are national refuse in your eyes, and all who wear black are national swindlers. Mr. Moore, according to you, did wrong to get military aid, and he did still worse to accept of any other aid. Your way of talking amounts to this: he should have abandoned his mill and his life to the rage of a set of misguided madmen, and Mr. Helstone and every other gentleman in the parish should have looked on, and seen the building razed and its owner slaughtered, and never stirred a finger to save either.”

“If Moore had behaved to his men from the beginning as a master ought to behave, they never would have entertained their present feelings towards him.”

“Easy for you to talk,” Shirley broke out, the blood rising as quickly as flame in dry straw—“you, whose family have lived at Briarmains for six generations, to whose person the people have been accustomed for fifty years, who know all their ways, prejudices, and preferences—easy, indeed, for you to act so as to avoid offending them. But Mr. Moore came a stranger into the district; he came here poor and friendless, with nothing but his own energies to back him, nothing but his honour, his talent, and his industry to make his way for him. A monstrous crime indeed that, under such circumstances, he could not popularize his naturally grave, quiet manners all at once; could not be jocular, and free, and cordial with a strange peasantry, as you are with your fellow-townsmen! An unpardonable transgression that when he introduced improvements he did not go about the business in quite the most politic way, did not graduate his changes as delicately as a rich capitalist might have done! For errors of this sort is he to be the victim of mob outrage? Is he to be denied even the privilege of defending himself? Are those who have the hearts of men in their breasts (and Mr. Helstone, say what you will of him, has such a heart) to be reviled like malefactors because they stand by him, because they venture to espouse the cause of one against two hundred?”

“Come, come now, be cool,” said Mr. Yorke, and even his smile had a grain of satisfaction in it; her earnestness amused him.

“Cool! Must I listen coolly to downright nonsense—to dangerous nonsense? No. I like you very well, Mr. Yorke, as you know, but I thoroughly dislike some of your principles. All that cant—excuse me, but I repeat the word—all that cant about soldiers and parsons is most offensive in my ears. All ridiculous, irrational crying up of one class, whether the same be aristocrat or democrat—all howling down of another class, whether clerical or military—all exacting injustice to individuals, whether monarch or mendicant—is really sickening to me; all arraying of ranks against ranks, all party hatreds, all tyrannies disguised as liberties, I reject and wash my hands of. You think you are a philanthropist; you think you are an advocate of liberty; but I will tell you this—Mr. Hall, the parson of Nunnely, is a better friend both of man and freedom than Hiram Yorke, the reformer of Briarfield.”

From a man, Mr. Yorke would not have borne it so patiently; nor even from some women. But he counted Shirley honest, and he counted her pretty; and where he could have flung wrath, he let himself be diverted. Besides, he had a secret liking for Moore’s welfare, and the warmth with which she defended her tenant pleased him. If he wished to avenge himself, he believed he held a weapon: a single word that might, he fancied, tame and silence her, and bring a blush up like dawn over her frank forehead.

“What more hast thou to say?” he asked, as she paused—not because her zeal had cooled, but because breath was needed.

“Say, Mr. Yorke!” she replied, pacing fast from wall to wall of the oak parlour—“say? I have a great deal to say, if I could get it out in lucid order, which I never can do. I have to say that your views, and those of most extreme politicians, are such as none but men in an irresponsible position can advocate; that they are purely opposition views, meant only to be talked about, and never intended to be acted on. Make you Prime Minister of England to-morrow, and you would have to abandon them. You abuse Moore for defending his mill. Had you been in Moore’s place you could not with honour or sense have acted otherwise than he acted. You abuse Mr. Helstone for everything he does. Mr. Helstone has his faults; he sometimes does wrong, but oftener right. Were you ordained vicar of Briarfield, you would find it no easy task to sustain all the active schemes for the benefit of the parish planned and persevered in by your predecessor. I wonder people cannot judge more fairly of each other and themselves. When I hear Messrs. Malone and Donne chatter about the authority of the church, the dignity and claims of the priesthood, the deference due to them as clergymen; when I hear the outbreaks of their small spite against Dissenters; when I witness their silly, narrow jealousies and assumptions; when their palaver about forms, and traditions, and superstitions is sounding in my ear; when I behold their insolent carriage to the poor, their often base servility to the rich—I think the Establishment is indeed in a poor way, and both she and her sons appear in the utmost need of reformation. Turning away distressed from minster tower and village spire—ay, as distressed as a churchwarden who feels the exigence of white-wash and has not wherewithal to purchase lime—I recall your senseless sarcasms on the ‘fat bishops,’ the ‘pampered parsons,’ ‘old mother church,’ etc. I remember your strictures on all who differ from you, your sweeping condemnation of classes and individuals, without the slightest allowance made for circumstances or temptations; and then, Mr. Yorke, doubt clutches my inmost heart as to whether men exist clement, reasonable, and just enough to be entrusted with the task of reform. I don’t believe you are of the number.”

“You have an ill opinion of me, Miss Shirley. You never told me so much of your mind before.”

“I never had an opening. But I have sat on Jessy’s stool by your chair in the back-parlour at Briarmains, for evenings together, listening excitedly to your talk, half admiring what you said, and half rebelling against it. I think you a fine old Yorkshireman, sir. I am proud to have been born in the same county and parish as yourself. Truthful, upright, independent you are, as a rock based below seas; but also you are harsh, rude, narrow, and merciless.”

“Not to the poor, lass, nor to the meek of the earth; only to the proud and high-minded.”

“And what right have you, sir, to make such distinctions? A prouder, a higher-minded man than yourself does not exist. You find it easy to speak comfortably to your inferiors; you are too haughty, too ambitious, too jealous to be civil to those above you. But you are all alike. Helstone also is proud and prejudiced. Moore, though juster and more considerate than either you or the rector, is still haughty, stern, and, in a public sense, selfish. It is well there are such men as Mr. Hall to be found occasionally—men of large and kind hearts, who can love their whole race, who can forgive others for being richer, more prosperous, or more powerful than they are. Such men may have less originality, less force of character than you, but they are better friends to mankind.”

“And when is it to be?” said Mr. Yorke, rising now, his hand on his hat.

“When is what to be?”

“The wedding.”

“Whose wedding?”

“Only that of Robert Gérard Moore, Esq., of Hollow’s Cottage, with Miss Keeldar, daughter and heiress of the late Charles Cave Keeldar of Fieldhead Hall.”

Colour rose in Shirley’s cheek as if some inner fire had licked the surface; yet her gaze did not fall. Her eye shone steadily—burned, indeed, with a deep, controlled brightness.

“That is your revenge,” she said slowly; then, with the quietness of one who has suddenly chosen her ground, added, “Would it be a bad match, unworthy of the late Charles Cave Keeldar’s representative?”

“My lass, Moore is a gentleman; his blood is pure and ancient as mine or thine.”

“And we two set store by ancient blood? We have family pride, though one of us at least is a republican?”

Yorke bowed. His lips refused confession, but his look yielded it: yes, pride of lineage sat in his very shoulders.

“Moore is a gentleman,” Shirley echoed, lifting her head with a glad grace—then checking herself as if a crowd of words had surged up and she would not let them pass. Yet her look spoke. It was language visible but untranslatable—like a lyric in a dialect known only to the heart that utters it. It was no ordinary love-confession, no simple rush of tenderness; there was something more intricate in it, some mingling of purpose, defiance, and feeling that baffled him. He knew, with a faint vexation, that his revenge had not struck home. He was the puzzled one; she triumphed.

“And if Moore is a gentleman, you can be only a lady; therefore—”

“Therefore there would be no inequality in our union.”

“None.”

“Thank you for your approbation. Will you give me away when I relinquish the name of Keeldar for that of Moore?”

Instead of replying, Mr. Yorke stared, as if trying to decide whether she mocked him or spoke truth. Purpose and banter, feeling and scoff, played together on her quick-changing face.

“I don’t understand thee,” he said at last, turning away.

She laughed. “Take courage, sir; you are not singular in your ignorance. But I suppose if Moore understands me that will do, will it not?”

“Moore may settle his own matters henceforward for me; I’ll neither meddle nor make with them further.”

A new thought crossed her like a shadow over sunlit grass. Her countenance changed as if a hand had drawn a veil across it. The eye darkened; the mouth set.

“Have you been asked to interfere? Are you questioning me as another’s proxy?”

“The Lord save us! Whoever weds thee must look about him! Keep all your questions for Robert; I’ll answer no more on ’em. Good-day, lassie!”

When he was gone, the house seemed for a moment to hear itself again: the faint stir of dresses, the distant clink of a tray, the muffled life of rooms. The talk of riot and guns left, behind it, a kind of uneasy silence—like the hush that follows a harsh sound in a quiet wood.

The day was fair—not sharp with sunshine, but softened by cloud-curtains; a dim blue haze lay over the hills, neither wet nor chill. While Shirley was still detained by the necessities of her rank, Caroline persuaded Mrs. Pryor to put on her bonnet and light summer shawl and come out with her, up towards the narrow end of the Hollow.

As they walked away from the house and the mill, the stream’s voice rose to meet them—broken, impatient, fretting. The glen drew its sides together; brushwood and stunted oaks thickened; the path became a mere invitation rather than a road. At the bottom, the mill-stream ran in a rough, unquiet course, struggling with stones, chafing against rugged banks, snaring itself in gnarled roots, foaming, gurgling, battling. It was a sound-world all its own—water making arguments with rock, leaves whispering their small replies, birds threading the air with quick notes. After the hard clangour of last night’s alarms, the place seemed to offer not silence, but kinder acoustics: nothing metallic, nothing that struck the nerves like hammer on anvil. Here, the ear could rest while it listened.

Half a mile from the mill, solitude deepened. The shade of trees unmolested for years received them like a sanctuary; birds sang in that shade as if it were a home built expressly for their music. No trodden way ran here. Wood-flowers kept their freshness as if no boot had bruised them; wild roses bloomed with the secluded confidence of something watched only by the wind. Blue-bells made pools of azure; pearl-white blossoms spangled the grass, humble as little stars dropped to earth.

Mrs. Pryor liked a quiet walk. High-roads, with their voices and chances, she always shunned; byways and lonely lanes she sought. Absolute solitude made her nervous—some vague fear of encounter would creep in and sour the pleasure. But she feared nothing with Caroline. As soon as they were away from human habitations and entered the still demesne of nature with this one young companion, a change stole over her: her countenance softened; her mind brightened, as if released from a tight band. With Caroline—and Caroline only—her heart seemed to lay down a burden. She could even be cheerful; sometimes she was tender. She would let fall bits of knowledge and experience, as one might share pieces of bread broken carefully and offered without ostentation—giving Caroline chances to guess what life she had lived, what cultivation had fed her mind, where her feelings were most vulnerable.

To-day, as they went along, she spoke of the birds singing above them, distinguished their species, and told something of their habits. English natural history seemed familiar to her, not in cold catalogues, but in affectionate recognition. Each wild flower near their path had a name for her; even tiny plants that sprang near stones and peeped out from old wall-chinks—plants Caroline had scarcely noticed—were pointed out and characterised. She had studied the botany of English fields and woods with minute care.

When they reached the head of the ravine, they sat on a ledge of grey, mossy rock that jutted from the base of a steep green hill towering above. Mrs. Pryor looked round and spoke of the neighbourhood as she had once seen it long ago. She noticed changes, compared this landscape with other parts of England, and in those quiet touches revealed a sense of the picturesque, a power of comparison between wild and cultured, grand and tame. Her discourse had a graphic charm, pleasant and unpretending.

Caroline listened with a reverent pleasure so sincere that it seemed to warm the elder lady by reflection. Perhaps rarely—behind that chill, repellent outside, that diffident mien, those incommunicative habits—had Mrs. Pryor known what it was to awaken earnest affection and admiring esteem in someone she could love in return. The consciousness of it made her voice more openly kind. Bending towards Caroline, she put aside a pale brown curl that had strayed from the comb and said, with more marked interest than she often allowed herself:

“I do hope this sweet air blowing from the hill will do you good, my dear Caroline. I wish I could see something more of colour in these cheeks; but perhaps you were never florid?”

“I had red cheeks once,” Caroline answered, smiling. “I remember a year—two years ago—when I used to look in the glass, I saw a different face there to what I see now—rounder and rosier. But when we are young,” added the girl of eighteen, “our minds are careless and our lives easy.”

“Do you,” Mrs. Pryor went on, and the effort it cost her showed in the slight breaking of her phrasing—“do you, at your age, fret yourself with cares for the future? Believe me, you had better not. Let the morrow take thought for the things of itself.”

“True, dear madam. It is not over the future I pine. The evil of the day is sometimes oppressive—too oppressive—and I long to escape it.”

“That is—the evil of the day—that is—your uncle perhaps is not—you find it difficult to understand—he does not appreciate—”

She could not shape the question fully; she could not bring herself to ask outright whether Mr. Helstone was harsh. But Caroline understood the broken hints.

“Oh, that is nothing,” she replied. “My uncle and I get on very well. We never quarrel—I don’t call him harsh—he never scolds me. Sometimes I wish somebody in the world loved me, but I cannot say that I particularly wish him to have more affection for me than he has. As a child, I should perhaps have felt the want of attention, only the servants were very kind to me; but when people are long indifferent to us, we grow indifferent to their indifference. It is my uncle’s way not to care for women and girls, unless they be ladies that he meets in company. He could not alter, and I have no wish that he should alter, as far as I am concerned. I believe it would merely annoy and frighten me were he to be affectionate towards me now. But you know, Mrs. Pryor, it is scarcely living to measure time as I do at the rectory. The hours pass, and I get them over somehow, but I do not live. I endure existence, but I rarely enjoy it. Since Miss Keeldar and you came I have been—I was going to say happier, but that would be untrue.”

She stopped, as if the truth itself had tightened her throat.

“How untrue? You are fond of Miss Keeldar, are you not, my dear?”

“Very fond of Shirley. I both like and admire her. But I am painfully circumstanced. For a reason I cannot explain I want to go away from this place, and to forget it.”
