Beneath our feet, the path was quiet, but overhead, a thunderous shout tore through the canopy. Above us, the Little Ones began their descent, tumbling like acorns from the highest branches. Their faces were alight with a frantic joy as they relayed the vision from the summit of a towering tree—the tallest sentinel in the grove. From there, they claimed to have gazed across the vast plain and beheld a marvel that defied nature's geometry: a solitary mountain that climbed so relentlessly it pierced the heavens, forcing the sky to curl inward like a heavy blanket, its crown shattered against the firmament.

"It might be a city," one of them whispered, breathless, "yet it bears no resemblance to Bulika."

Driven by a curiosity that burned hotter than the sun, I ascended again. What I witnessed stole the air from my lungs. It was a metropolis rising vertically into the azure void, where the boundaries between geology and architecture dissolved. Here, mountains were not stone but spires, skies were not empty space but palaces, and clouds were not vapor but the mortar binding it all. Rock and dwelling, precipice and palace, tangled in a glorious, chaotic symphony of shadows and glints, a broken mosaic of light and shadow that seemed to breathe.

I descended, and the Little Ones followed, their spirits lifting with every step. They raced ahead, leading the way with a mirth that bordered on madness, eyes fixed solely on the horizon, utterly blind to anything behind them. As we moved forward, the river transformed. It ceased to be merely water; it became something luminous, alive with a beauty I had never known in any realm. This was not a reflection of reality, but reality itself, unveiled. Nothing in this world could truly *be* like it; it existed in a state of pure being.

From the distance, the city emerged again, nestled within the blue mists. Yet, looming above it, closer to the mountain's jagged crest, stood a colossal tower—or perhaps a monolith—that seemed to pierce the veil of the clouds. Around it, the atmosphere churned. Gray, dark gray, and violet hues swirled in a confused, antinomian dance, churning up a froth of vapor that spun like whirlpools in a sea of ink. Suddenly, a blinding flash erupted. For a fleeting instant, it played about the children running before us, illuminating them with a terrifying brilliance. Then, a darkness so profound it felt like sleep swallowed the scene whole. But through that void, I heard their voices, hushed with ecstatic wonder.

"Did you see?"
"I saw."
"What did you see?"
"The beautifullest man!"
"I heard him speak!"
"I didn't. What did he say?"

Then, the voice of the smallest, most childlike among them, Luva, cut through the silence: "He said, 'Ous all mine's, 'ickle ones: come along!' "

I had seen the lightning, yet my ears remained deaf to the words. Lona, however, saw and heard as the children did. A second flash tore through the gloom, and suddenly, my eyes—though not my ears—opened wide. The great, quivering light was composed entirely of angelic visages. They flared into visibility and then vanished, leaving trails of ethereal dust.

A third flash followed, its substance shifting from the divine to the intimately human.
"I see my mother!" I cried out, the name bursting from my throat.
"I see lots o' mothers!" Luva replied, his voice trembling.

Again, the cloud flashed. This time, it revealed a menagerie of the impossible: horses and elephants, lions and dogs, oh, such beasts! And such birds! Great avians whose single wings shimmered with every hue of the sunset or rainbow, fluttering alongside tiny birds whose feathers sparkled as if encrusted with the entire treasury of earthly jewels. Silvery cranes, red flamingos, opal pigeons, peacocks resplendent in gold and emerald and sapphire, jewel-bright hummingbirds, great-winged butterflies, and lithe, voluminous creeping things—all condensed into one heavenly, instantaneous bloom.

"I see that serpents grow birds here, just as caterpillars used to grow butterflies!" Lona observed, her awe palpable.
"I saw my white pony," I murmured, tears pricking my eyes, "the one that died when I was a child. I needn't have been so sorry; I should just have waited!"

No thunder rolled, no clap echoed. Instead, a sweet rain began to fall, filling the air with a caressing coolness that washed away the dust of the journey. We breathed deeply, our steps growing stronger, anchored by the rhythm of the falling drops. Each drop flashed with the colors of the awakened gems of the earth, and a mighty rainbow arced across the sky, spanning the city like a bridge of light.

The blue clouds thickened, and the rain poured in torrents. The children exulted, running wild, a kaleidoscope of motion that made it nearly impossible to keep them in sight. Beside them, the river swept onward with a silent, radiant roll. It filled its smooth, soft, yielding channel to the brim, flowing not over rock or shingle or sand, but over a carpet of grass teeming with primroses, daisies, crocuses, narcissi, pimpernels, and anemones—a starry multitude, large and bright beneath the brilliant water. The river had gathered no turbidity from the storm, not even a tinge of yellow or brown; it glowed with a pale, berylline luminescence rising from its deep, dainty bed.

As we drew nearer to the mountain, we saw the truth of the source: the river burst forth from the mountain's very peak, rushing in full volume through the main street of the city. It cascaded down to the gate via a staircase of deep, wide steps, interwoven from porphyry and serpentine, continuing all the way to the mountain's foot. There, we found shallower steps on either bank, winding upward toward the gate and along the ascending thoroughfare. Without a single hesitation, the Little Ones sprinted straight up the stairs to the gate, which stood wide open.

On the landing outside, seated, was the portress, a woman-angel of dark visage. She leaned her shadowed brow on her idle hand, watching them approach. Before she could comprehend their sudden arrival, the children rushed upon her, burying her in caresses. In a heartbeat, they had taken heaven by surprise and were already inside, still mounting the stairs beside the descending torrent. A great angel, accompanied by a retinue of shining ones, came down to intercept and receive them, but the children, in merry evasion, darted past. Then, a group of woman-angels descended in joyful dance, and in a moment, they were fettered in heavenly arms. The radiants lifted them away, and I lost sight of them forever.

"Ah!" said the mighty angel, continuing his descent to meet us, who were now close to the gate and within earshot of his voice. "This is well! These are soldiers to take heaven itself by storm! I hear of a horde of black bats on the frontiers; these will make short work of them!"

Glancing back, seeing the horses and elephants clambering up behind us, he added, "Take those animals to the royal stables. Tend them, then turn them into the King's forest."

"Welcome home!" he said, bending low with the sweetest smile imaginable.

Instantly, he turned and led the way higher. The scales of his armor flashed like flakes of lightning, catching the light of the inner world. Thought failed me, struggling to articulate the weight of emotion I felt, thus received by the officers of heaven. All I desired, all I knew not how to ask, was clearly on its way to me.

We paused for a moment at the gate, whence issued the roaring, radiant river. I know not whence the stones that fashioned it were hewn, but among them I saw the prototypes of every gem I had ever loved on earth. They were far more beautiful than any earthly stone, for these were living stones. Within them, I perceived not merely the intent, but the Intender himself; not just the idea, but the Imbodyer present, the Operant Outsender. In this kingdom, nothing was dead; nothing was mere existence. Nothing was simply a "thing."

We went up through the city and stepped out. There was no wall on the upper side, only a massive pile of broken rocks, upsloping like the moraine of an eternal glacier. Through the openings between the boulders, the river came billowing out, free and untamed. Upon their tops, I could dimly discern what appeared to be three or four great steps of a stair, disappearing into a cloud white as snow. Above those steps, I saw—not with physical eyes, but with the mind's eye alone—a grand, ancient chair, the Throne of the Ancient of Days. Over, under, and between those steps issued the River of the Water of Life, born anew, ceaselessly, plentifully.

The great angel could guide us no farther; those remaining stones we must ascend alone.
