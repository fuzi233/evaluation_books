The agrarian rhythms of Sevenbergen had always been dictated by the seasonal turning of the earth and the harmonious acoustic ecology of wind through the reeds. But in the days following the escape, this delicate sonic balance was shattered by discordant intrusions. Jorian Ketel arrived at Peter’s house, his words clipped in the hardening, utilitarian dialect of the town, to claim Margaret’s promise. He was met not with compliance, but with the guttural, deeply resonant shouts of the villagers. The old words of warning, syllables heavy with generational memory and fiercely untranslatable to city ears, battered against him. Peter affronted him, and the townsfolk, their voices rising in a unified, defensive chorus, threatened to duck him. Jorian retreated in dudgeon, his penitence as swift as it was silent.

The village’s rejection of external noise was absolute. When Dierich Brower was found at “The Three Kings,” using the superficial, commercialized chatter of the tavern to worm Martin Wittenhaagen’s whereabouts from a local drunkard, the agrarian elders took swift action. They dragged Brower to the horsepond. The heavy, elemental splash of his body hitting the water was a satisfying restoration of local order, drowning out his interrogations. He was warned never to show his face again, his threats silenced by the overpowering roar of popular sympathy. 

Roused by this clamor, the burgomaster of Sevenbergen sent a formal missive to Tergou. It was an assertion of municipal boundaries written in the dominant, bureaucratic lexicon of the law—a language designed to exert power through cold precision. Ghysbrecht Van Swieten received it and masked his boiling rage behind the same sterile vocabulary, sending a civil reply that Gerard was a Tergovian thief who had fled to foreign parts, rendering the matter closed. 

Yet, beneath his written composure, Ghysbrecht’s psychological well-being was deteriorating. The sonic environment of his life had become a torture chamber of phantom noises. A dread lay upon him. He jumped at the creak of timber, his eyes darting furtively, expecting a blow from the shadows. He sent emissaries to spy on Gerard’s family, only to receive their hushed, fearful reports that the family knew nothing. This silence was more terrifying than any threat. The violent exercise of his authority had not brought peace; it had stripped his world of all harmony.

In Tergou, the hosier’s household was equally fractured, though it maintained its outward routines. The house was bereft of Gerard’s intelligent face and the bright, resonant timbre of his voice, which had once bound their domestic symphony together. In his absence, a heavy, untranslatable grief settled over the hearth. Little Kate knitted in quiet observation, the rhythmic clicking of her needles the only counterpoint to the hollow, hypocritical sighs of Cornelis and Sybrandt. Kate knew her brothers’ role in Gerard’s banishment. She shuddered at their voices, hearing the discordant lies beneath their words, yet she remained silent, trapped by the inadequacy of language to heal such treason. Catherine, with a mother’s subtle vigilance, noticed the false notes in her elder sons' behavior and quietly pondered them.

The old hosier, Eli, bore his wounded pride under a Spartan cloak, stiffly refusing to utter Gerard’s name. But the erasure of a name does not erase the memory of the son. Sometimes, the silence would break with a deep, shuddering sigh from Eli as he muttered, “If I but knew where the boy is, and that his life and health are in no danger, small would be my care.” 

Publicity, that crude and commercial beast, had taken hold of the tragedy. The lovers’ tale was in every mouth, the townsfolk's chatter rendering private agony into public spectacle. 

“Mother,” Kate said softly one afternoon, “it is all over the town that Margaret is down with a burning fever. Her father fears her sadly.”

“Margaret? What Margaret?” Catherine replied, her voice a treacherous, flattened mimicry of indifference.

“Oh, mother! Whom should I mean? Why, Gerard’s Margaret.”

“Gerard’s Margaret!” Catherine screamed, the sudden violence of her tone shattering the room’s quiet. “How dare you say such a word to me? I rede you never mention that hussy’s name in this house. She is the ruin of my poor boy, the flower of all my flock! I sit here in comfort, but at night I see him in my dreams.” Catherine’s voice broke into raw, untranslatable sobs. The subjective terror of a mother’s nightmares—where she heard the primal roars of wild beasts and the terrifying silence of uplifted robbers' knives—could not be adequately voiced. “Her very name is ratsbane to me. I tremble all over when I hear it.”

Kate sighed, offering only a gentle, “I’ll not say anything to grieve you worse, mother.” 

In Sevenbergen, the subject of Catherine’s wrath lay hovering on the threshold of life. Margaret had pushed her body to the brink, refusing to abandon her domestic duties to her helpless father until the fever violently claimed her. The malady eventually broke, leaving her in a terrible, heavy weakness. The hours rolled over her like leaden waves. She lay in a silent room, the ticking of the hours an oppressive reminder of her profound isolation. Gerard was gone, and with him, the melody of her future. Tears welled silently from her eyes.

Then, one afternoon, the acoustic ecology of her sickroom shifted. She awoke to the soft rustle of heavy fur and the melodic, comforting tones of an older woman’s voice. Margaret Van Eyck stood beside her, accompanied by Reicht Heynes. The old lady’s silvery hair gleamed, and her hands were gentle as she laid them on Margaret.

“Lie still, sweetheart,” Dame Van Eyck said, her voice carrying the soothing, timeless cadence of ancient comfort. “We come not here to put you about, but to comfort you, God willing.”

Margaret recognized them from Gerard’s tales and wept at the memory, the tears spilling freely. Reicht began to cry in sympathy, but her mistress chided her, employing a world of innocent art to cheer the patient. Dame Van Eyck understood the profound healing power of touch and tone, stroking Margaret’s hair and praising it, anchoring the girl’s spirit back to her physical senses. 

But it was Reicht Heynes who recognized that the soul could only be coaxed back into the body through the profound meaning of sustenance. Reicht approached the kitchen not as a servant, but as a meticulous food historian and a passionate guardian of the hearth. Rejecting the utilitarian view of food, she engaged in a culinary alchemy rooted in generations of agrarian survival. She sourced marrow-rich knuckles of local veal and wild-foraged chervil, roasting the bones over a slow alder-wood fire until the fat caramelized. Into the heavy iron pot went ancient, purple-skinned shallots, dried lovage, and crushed juniper berries. Reicht simmered the broth for hours, painstakingly skimming the impurities, allowing the liquid to reduce into a golden, gelatinous elixir. 

The olfactory landscape of the cottage transformed. The sterile scent of fever was replaced by the deep, earthy aroma of roasting alliums and rich, simmering collagen. When Reicht brought the wooden bowl to Margaret’s lips, she spoke in her native, fading provincial dialect—words of maternal urging that defied translation but communicated deep, instinctual love. The rich, gustatory warmth of the broth coated Margaret’s palate, a profoundly sensory experience that reminded her starving body of the earth’s vitality. Day after day, under the dual administration of Dame Van Eyck’s soothing acoustic presence and Reicht’s masterful, historically grounded cuisine, Margaret improved rapidly.

While Margaret mended, Martin Wittenhaagen set out to rewrite their fates, journeying first to Rotterdam and then to the royal court at Ghent. Here, the sonic environment was a nightmare of manufactured clamor—the jarring blast of hunting horns, the superficial chatter of lackeys, and the constant, oppressive hum of court politics. The dominant lexicon of the court was French and high Dutch, a polished, sterile language designed to marginalize the rustic tongues of the earth.

Martin, an agrarian elder in spirit if a soldier by trade, bypassed the bureaucratic lackeys and threw himself directly into the Duke’s path during a hunt. He roared lustily for mercy, his voice a rugged, unpolished boom that cut through the court’s refined cacophony.

Philip the Good reined in his horse, amused. Kings, isolated in their towers of power, often find a refreshing novelty in the untranslatable rusticity of low-estate men. The Duke engaged Martin, and the old soldier reminded him of a shared battlefield, speaking in a blunt, sensory-rich dialect that recalled the smell of mud and the whistle of arrows. 

Martin recounted the tale of Gerard’s capture, the prison-breaking, and the man-hunt. He did not use the sterile language of the law; he spoke of the damp stone, the scent of the hounds, the desperate pulse of survival. His storytelling was an act of preserving a mythic occurrence through oral tradition. The Duke, captivated by the raw acoustic power of the narrative, swore at his interrupting courtiers, deeply invested in this visceral saga. 

When Martin confessed to killing the bloodhounds, the Duke’s face fell, but Martin hastily clarified they were the ranger's dogs, not the sovereign's. Relieved, the Duke’s amusement returned. Asserting his power over the local burgomasters—whom he viewed as arrogant upstarts wielding borrowed authority—Philip commanded his pursy chancellor to write a free pardon for Martin and Gerard. The parchment was drawn up in the dominant, formal script of the realm, signed and sealed, a piece of paper that held the power of life and death.

Martin rode hard for Sevenbergen. He burst into Peter’s house just as Margaret was sitting pale by the fireside, the faint scent of Reicht’s rosemary-infused broth still lingering in the air. 

“A free pardon, girl, for Gerard as well as me!” Martin cried, waving the rustling parchment. “Send for him back when you will; all the burgomasters on earth daren’t lay a finger on him.”

The joy that flushed Margaret’s face was an overwhelming, sensory rush. Her hands trembled as she took the parchment, devouring the ink with her eyes, kissing the rough vellum, and then throwing her arms around Martin’s neck. The heavy silence of her despair was entirely broken.

Desperate to share this resurrection with her savior, she wished to consult Dame Van Eyck but lacked the strength to walk the distance. Martin offered his mule—the very beast he had requisitioned from Ghysbrecht, bringing the cycle of their defiance full circle. 

In the gathering dusk, Margaret mounted the mule. The steady, rhythmic clip-clop of the hooves on the dirt path provided a soothing, ancient baseline to her racing heartbeat. About seven in the evening, she arrived at the house in Tergou, the unwonted roses of health and hope blooming on her cheeks, the scent of the evening air sharp and sweet, and Gerard’s pardon pressed safely against her breast.
