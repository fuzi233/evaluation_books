The Sergeant remained a statue of brooding contemplation, his mind churning in the private currents of deduction, until we crossed the threshold into the plantation of firs that bordered the treacherous quicksand. Only then did he rouse himself, shedding his hesitation like a discarded coat, and address me once more with the gravity of a man whose verdict had been sealed.

"Mr. Betteredge," he intoned, his voice cutting through the twilight air, "since you have honored me by lending an oar to my boat, and since I perceive you may yet be of utility before the sun surrenders the sky, I deem it futile to continue our mutual charades. I propose to discard the veil of mystery and offer you a lesson in plain speech. You are resolved to withhold any information prejudicial to Rosanna Spearman, driven by the conviction that she has been a faithful confidante to you, and by a genuine, albeit misplaced, pity for her heart. These humane sentiments command my highest respect; however, in this specific instance, they are luxuries that serve only to blind us to the stark reality. Rosanna Spearman faces no peril whatsoever—not if I successfully anchor her to the crime of the Diamond's disappearance upon evidence as undeniable as the bridge of your nose!"

"Do you imply that Lady Verinder cannot bring charges?" I queried, my voice tight.

"I mean precisely that your lady *cannot* prosecute," the Sergeant corrected, his tone leaving no room for ambiguity. "Rosanna Spearman is merely an instrument wielded by another hand. She will be held harmless, shielded by the interests of that other person."

He spoke with the fervor of a prophet, and there was no mistaking the sincerity etched into his features. Yet, beneath his earnest exterior, I felt a discordant note vibrating within my own psyche—a nagging intuition that resisted his certainty. "Could you not reveal the identity of this 'other person'?" I pressed.

"Can *you* not, Mr. Betteredge?" he countered, turning his gaze upon me.

"No."

Sergeant Cuff stood rigid, surveying me with a look that blended melancholy and profound curiosity. "It is invariably a source of satisfaction to me to extend tenderness toward human frailty," he declared. "I feel acutely tender toward you at this very moment, Mr. Betteredge. And you, sharing this noble impulse, feel equally tender toward Rosanna Spearman, do you not? Pray, tell me: do you happen to know if she has recently acquired a fresh supply of linen?"

His insertion of this bizarre inquiry, seemingly out of nowhere, left me utterly baffled as to its intent. Perceiving no potential harm to Rosanna if I confessed the truth, I admitted that the girl had arrived at our household with scant provisions, noting that my lady, in recompense for her exemplary conduct—which I emphasized heavily—had gifted her a new outfit scarcely two weeks prior.

"This is a wretched world," the Sergeant murmured, shaking his head. "Human life, Mr. Betteredge, resembles a target; misfortune is perpetually loosing arrows at it, and striking the bullseye. Had it not been for that new garment, we would undoubtedly have unearthed a nightgown or petticoat among Rosanna's effects, thereby nailing her to the charge. Are you unable to follow this thread? You have personally interrogated the servants; you recall the startling discoveries two of them made outside Rosanna's door. Surely you comprehend what the girl was scheming yesterday, after falling ill? You cannot guess? Alas, it is as clear as that sliver of moonlight piercing the tree line. At eleven o'clock on Thursday morning, Superintendent Seagrave—a veritable mass of human infirmity—pointed out the smear on the door to all the female staff. Rosanna, possessing her own acute suspicions regarding her belongings, seized the first opportunity to retreat to her chamber. There, she located the paint stain on her nightgown, petticoat, or whatever item bore the mark. She feigned illness and slipped away to the town, procuring materials to construct a replacement. Alone in her room that Thursday night, she fashioned the new garment. She ignited a fire—not to incinerate the evidence, for two of her fellow-servants were peering through the keyhole, and she knew better than to create a scent of smoke or require tinder to dispose of ashes—but to dry and iron the substitute dress after wringing it out. Meanwhile, she concealed the stained garment, likely wearing it beneath her clothes, and is currently engaged in disposing of it in some secluded spot on that lonely stretch of beach ahead of us. I have tracked her this evening to your fishing village and to a specific cottage, a location we may soon need to inspect before our departure. She lingered there for a considerable duration and emerged carrying, as I surmise, something concealed beneath her cloak. On a woman's back, a cloak serves as an emblem of charity, covering a multitude of sins. I observed her depart northward along the coast after leaving the dwelling. Does your local coastline, Mr. Betteredge, boast a fine specimen of marine scenery?"

I replied, "Yes," as briefly as propriety allowed.

"Tastes vary," the Sergeant noted. "From my vantage point, I have rarely encountered a marine landscape I admire less. Should you happen to pursue someone along your seashore, and should that individual glance about, there exists not a single fragment of cover to conceal you. I faced a dilemma: either detain Rosanna in custody on suspicion, or allow her, for the time being, to maintain her precarious game in her own hands. For reasons I shall not burden you with, I chose to make any sacrifice rather than raise the alarm prematurely to a certain individual whom I shall keep nameless between us. I returned to the house to request that you escort me to the northern extremity of the beach via an alternate route. Sand, regarding its ability to imprint the tracks of fugitives, stands as one of the finest detective officers I know. Should we fail to intercept Rosanna Spearman by circling her in this manner, the sand itself may divulge her secrets, provided the light endures. Behold, the sand awaits. If you will permit my suggestion—perhaps you should remain silent and allow me to proceed first?"

Should there exist within the apothecary's trade a condition known as *detective fever*, it had now seized my humble self with relentless grip. Sergeant Cuff advanced between the dunes of sand, descending toward the beach. I trailed him, my heart hammering against my ribs like a trapped bird, pausing at a respectful distance to await the unfolding drama.

As fate would have it, I found myself standing nearly where Rosanna Spearman and I had conversed previously, the moment Mr. Franklin had unexpectedly appeared before us upon his arrival from London. While my eyes remained fixed on the Sergeant, my mind drifted involuntarily to the past encounter between Rosanna and myself. I confess, I almost felt the poor girl slip her hand once more into mine, offering a grateful squeeze of thanks for my kindness. I almost heard her voice repeating her earlier confession that the Shivering Sand drew her irresistibly, regardless of her will, whenever she ventured forth—I almost saw her countenance brighten anew, mirroring the joy she displayed when first beholding Mr. Franklin emerging briskly from the dunes. My spirits sank deeper with each recollection, and the desolate panorama of the lone bay, as I scanned the horizon to regain composure, only intensified my unease.

The final vestiges of evening light were dissolving, casting a still and terrible calm over the forsaken landscape. The swell of the main ocean against the great sandbank in the bay rose and fell without a whisper. The inner sea lay lost and dim, undisturbed by a breath of wind. Patches of foul ooze floated, yellow-white, upon the deathly surface of the water. Scum and slime glimmered faintly in isolated spots where the lingering light caught them on the two massive spits of rock jutting north and south into the sea. It was now the turn of the tide; and even as I stood waiting, the broad, brown visage of the quicksand began to dimple and quiver—the sole movement in that horrific tableau.

I witnessed the Sergeant start as the tremor of the sand captured his eye. After observing it for a minute or so, he turned and returned to me.

"A treacherous locale, Mr. Betteredge," he stated grimly. "And there are no signs of Rosanna Spearman anywhere on the beach, search where you may."

He guided me lower onto the shore, and I confirmed with my own sight that his footsteps and mine were the only impressions marked upon the sand.

"How fares the fishing village from our current position?" asked Sergeant Cuff.

"Cobb's Hole," I answered—that being the designation of the place—"lies approximately due south."

"I observed the girl this evening, walking northward along the shore from Cobb's Hole," the Sergeant explained. "Consequently, she must have been heading toward this location. Is Cobb's Hole situated beyond that point of land there? And can we reach it now, during low tide, via the beach?"

I affirmed, "Yes," to both inquiries.

"If you will pardon my suggestion, we shall move briskly," the Sergeant urged. "I intend to locate the precise spot where she abandoned the shore before darkness claims it entirely."

We had traversed, I estimate, a couple of hundred yards toward Cobb's Hole when Sergeant Cuff suddenly dropped to his knees upon the beach, appearing seized by a sudden, frenzied urge to pray.
