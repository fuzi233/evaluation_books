Having won substantial sums from his cousin and the chaplain, our young Virginian felt compelled to allow them a chance to recover their losses—a prospect that surely would not meet with approval from his mother or other upright moralists. Indeed, his penchant for card-playing was excessive. Beyond the daily rounds of whist or quadrille with the ladies, which commenced shortly after dinner at three o'clock and concluded only at supper, there existed high-stakes games involving fortunes, in which every gentleman, including my lord, participated. Following their Sunday conversations, his lordship had become notably more open and confiding toward his kinsman, betting with him affably and engaging him in backgammon and piquet. Both Mr. William and the pious chaplain relished a bit of hazard; however, this diversion was pursued secretly, unbeknownst to the ladies of the house. Having extracted repeated vows from Cousin Will that he would neither lead the Virginian into mischief nor participate himself, the ladies remained unaware. Thus, Will pledged as much as his aunt or mother demanded—giving his solemn word that he would never play—and whenever the family retired, Mr. William would stroll over with a dice-box and a bottle of rum to Cousin Harry's quarters. There, alongside Hal and the reverend gentleman, they would gamble until dawn.

When Harry offered Lord Castlewood those glowing descriptions of his maternal estate in America, he harbored no intent to deceive, boast, or lie; the lad possessed a fundamentally honest nature. Yet, in his home life, it must be admitted, the young fellow had acquainted himself with all manner of peculiar company—horse-jockeys, tavern loafers, gamblers, and sportsmen, of whom the colonies teemed. A landed aristocracy, supported by a population of enslaved Africans to till their fields and cultivate tobacco and corn, found little amusement other than the hunt or the card table and punch bowl. Hospitality in the province was boundless: every man's house was effectively his neighbor's. Idle gentlemen rode from mansion to mansion, finding in each similar sport, welcome, and rough plenty. The Virginian squire might occasionally possess a barefooted valet and a patched saddle, but within the crumbling fences and behind the broken windows of the hall, there was abundant corn for the horses, plentiful drink and venison for the master. Harry had slept on countless straw mattresses and engaged in endless jovial nocturnal bouts over claret and punch in cracked bowls until morning broke, signaling the hour to follow the hounds. His poor brother, conversely, was of a far more sober disposition, as the lad confessed with contrition. Thus does Nature vary its creations; some cherish books and tea, while others prefer Burgundy and a gallop across the countryside.

Harry's tastes quickly became evident to his friends in England. None favored the rigid Puritan discipline, nor did they dislike Harry for lacking the demeanor of a mere "milksop." Manners, one observes, were considerably looser a century prior; tongues were far freer, and names were spoken and deeds performed that would now scandalize us into silence. Yes, madam, we are not as our ancestors were. Should we not thank the Fates for improving our morals so prodigiously, rendering us so eminently virtuous?

Keeping a shrewd eye on those around him, and correctly surmising that his cousins sought to probe his affairs, Harry Warrington wisely kept his own counsel. In games of chance or sport, he proved quite a match for the three gentlemen into whose company he had fallen. Even in the noble game of billiards, he held his own after a few days of play with his cousins and their revered pastor. His grandfather, fond of the game, had imported from Europe one of the very few tables existing in His Majesty's province of Virginia. Though Mr. William initially bested him, he could not secure undue odds against the young gambler. After their first wager, Harry guarded himself against Mr. William, and Cousin William acknowledged, with genuine respect, that the American was his equal in most things and superior in many. Yet, despite Harry's prowess—beating the parson and soon matching Will, who could easily defeat the girls—it remained a mystery why, in contests with these, particularly one of them, Mr. Warrington frequently emerged second. He was profoundly courteous to every soul wearing a petticoat; nor has that traditional politeness vanished from his country. Every woman in the Castlewood establishment adored the young gentleman. The stern housekeeper was mollified by him; the portly cook greeted him with flushed smiles; the ladies' maids, whether French or English, smirked and giggled in his favor; and the pretty porter's daughter at the lodge always replied kindly to his advances. Madame de Bernstein noted all these things, saying nothing aloud but watching the boy's disposition and behavior with care.

Who can determine the precise age of Lady Maria Esmond? Books of the Peerage were scarce in those days compared to our blessed modern era, and I cannot pinpoint her age to within a few years, or even a decade or two. When Will claimed she was thirty-five, he was merely being abusive and prone to exaggeration. Maria was Will's half-sister, daughter of the late Lord Castlewood's first wife, a German lady whom my lord married during the reign of Queen Anne. Baron Bernstein, who wed Maria's Aunt Beatrix (widow of Bishop Tusher), was also a German, a Hanoverian nobleman and relative of the first Lady Castlewood. If Lady Maria was born under George I., and if His Majesty George II. had already reigned for thirty years, how could she be twenty-seven, as she told Harry Warrington? "I am old, child," she would say. She addressed Harry as "child" when they were alone. "I am a hundred years old. I am twenty-seven. I might almost be your mother." To which Harry would retort, "Your ladyship might be the mother of all the Cupids, I am sure. You don't look twenty, on my word you do not!"

Lady Maria appeared any age one desired. She was a fair beauty with a dazzling white and red complexion, an abundance of golden hair flowing over her shoulders, and beautiful round arms that displayed to uncommon advantage when playing billiards with Cousin Harry. When she stretched across the table to strike, the youth caught glimpses of a delicate ankle, a finely clocked stocking, and a tiny black satin slipper with a small red heel, filling him with unutterable rapture and causing him to swear that no such foot, ankle, stocking, or slipper existed in the world. And yet, oh, foolish Harry! Your mother's foot was far more slender and half an inch shorter than Lady Maria's. But somehow, boys do not gaze upon their mothers' slippers and ankles with such ecstasy.

Undoubtedly, Lady Maria was very kind to Harry when they were alone. Before her sister, aunt, stepmother, she treated him lightly, calling him a simpleton, a chit, and various trivial names. Behind his back, and even in his presence, she mimicked his accent, which retained a distinct provincial flavor. Harry blushed and corrected the faulty intonation under the guidance of his English tutors. His aunt declared they would soon make him a fine gentleman.

As stated earlier, Lord Castlewood grew daily more familiar and friendly with his guest and relative. Until the harvest was gathered, there was little sport save for occasional cock-fighting at Winchester and bull-baiting at Hexton Fair. Harry and Will rode to many jolly fairs and races throughout the region. The young Virginian was introduced to several county families—the Henleys of the Grange, the Crawleys of Queen's Crawley, the Redmaynes of Lionsden, and so on. Neighbors arrived in their heavy coaches, spending two or three days in country fashion. Fewer would have visited had not the entire Castlewood family feared offending Madame de Bernstein. She disliked country company; the rustic society and conversation annoyed her. "We shall be merrier when my aunt leaves us," the young folks admitted. "We have cause, as you may imagine, to be very civil to her. You know what a favorite she was with our father? With reason. She secured his earldom, being exceptionally well-connected at Court with the King and Queen then. She commands here naturally, perhaps a trifle too much. We are all afraid of her: even my elder brother stands in awe of her, and my stepmother is far more obedient to her than she ever was to my father, whom she ruled with a rod of iron. But Castlewood is merrier when our aunt is absent. At least, we have much more company. You will come to us in our gay days, Harry, won't you? Of course you will: this is your home, sir. I was so pleased—oh, so pleased—when my brother said he considered it your home!"

A soft hand was extended following this charming speech, accompanied by a pair of remarkably well-preserved blue eyes radiating friendship. Harry grasped his cousin's hand with ardor. I know not what privileges of kinship he would not wish to claim, only that he was so timid. They call the English selfish and cold. He initially thought his relatives were thus; how mistaken he was! How kind and affectionate they truly were, especially the Earl—and dear, dear Maria! How he wished he could recall the letter he had sent to Mrs. Mountain and his mother, hinting that his welcome had been chilly!
