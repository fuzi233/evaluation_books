As Harry Warrington walked through the City streets, the weight of his rejection still lingered, a bitter taste in his mouth. He thought of Colonel Lambert and his family, how they had humiliated him before his peers, their condescending attitudes a constant reminder of his place in society. The gifts he had offered in an attempt to bridge the gap between them now seemed like worthless trinkets, cast aside like so much rubbish.

Parson Sampson, ever the opportunist, eyed the morocco boxes containing the golden gewgaws with a covetous glint in his eye. "Ah, sir, those must be worth a small fortune!" he exclaimed, his voice dripping with envy.

Harry's anger flared anew. "They cost a pretty penny, Sampson," he said, his voice tight with restraint. "But I'd give ten times that amount to people who've shown me kindness."

Sampson's praise for his young patron flowed freely, as it always did. "Indeed, sir, you're a generous soul, beyond compare!"

Harry's thoughts turned to the watch, which had been touted as a bargain at a hundred pounds in Paris. He recalled Little Miss Hetty's admiration for it, her eyes lighting up at the prospect of owning such a fine timepiece. A pang of regret struck him – why had he bothered with such trifles?

As he walked to his broker's office, the sounds of the City grew louder, a cacophony of hawkers and horse hooves that seemed to mock him. He made his way to the Bank, where he conducted some minor business, exchanging crisp banknotes for a handful of coins. The transaction complete, he left the building, his mind still preoccupied with the rejection he had suffered.

The broker, noticing Harry's somber mood, suggested a visit to one of the City's renowned dining-houses. Over a meal of roasted meats and steaming vegetables, Harry's thoughts turned to the Virginian walk upon 'Change, where he had passed through the crowds with a sense of shame. What would his family in Virginia think, he wondered, if they knew he was squandering their wealth in this reckless manner?
