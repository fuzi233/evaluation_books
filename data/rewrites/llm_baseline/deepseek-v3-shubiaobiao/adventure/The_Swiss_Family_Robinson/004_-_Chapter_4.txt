The morning sun rose bright and clear, casting golden light through the branches of our towering treehouse. The children, restless with energy, bounded about like young monkeys, eager for the day’s tasks.

*"Father, what shall we do today?"* they clamored.

*"Today,"* I replied, *"we rest."*

*"Rest?"* echoed Jack, incredulous. *"But why?"*

*"Remember the words of Scripture,"* I said. *"Six days you shall labor, but the seventh is a day of rest. Today, we honor that command."*

Jack wrinkled his nose. *"But there’s no church here! No hymns, no sermons—what are we to do?"*

*"The whole earth is God’s temple,"* I answered. *"And beneath this great tree, we shall worship as faithfully as in any cathedral."*

With that, we gathered in the shade of our leafy sanctuary. My wife produced a small Bible from her precious stores, and after reading aloud, I shared a parable—a tale of a great king and his rebellious subjects, a story of mercy and redemption. The children listened intently, their young minds turning over the lesson.

When our devotion ended, the boys scattered to their amusements. Fritz fashioned arrowheads from porcupine quills, while Jack, ever the restless spirit, begged for a bow of his own. Little Franz, clutching his tiny quiver, pranced about like a miniature hunter.

Ernest, meanwhile, had slipped away unnoticed—until a sudden gunshot split the air. Two small birds tumbled from the branches, and Ernest descended, grinning. *"A fine shot, wasn’t it?"*

The birds—a thrush and an ortolan—would make a savory meal, but I cautioned against reckless hunting. *"These birds flock to our figs. If we wait, we may gather them in plenty and preserve them for leaner days."*

As we worked, Jack’s inventive mind turned to armor—specifically, for Turk. Using the leftover porcupine hide, we fashioned a cuirass for the loyal dog. Juno eyed him warily, keeping her distance, while Knips, after one tentative poke at Turk’s spiny new attire, opted for the safety of Juno’s back.

By afternoon, curiosity led us beyond our usual paths. Following the stream, we stumbled upon a treasure—a vast field of wild potatoes! Ernest, ever the scholar, identified them at once, and we dug eagerly, filling our bags with the precious tubers.

*"How do we thank God for such bounty?"* little Franz asked.

Fritz answered wisely: *"By using it well, and remembering to be grateful every day."*

Our journey continued, revealing more wonders—luscious pineapples, fragrant vanilla, and the karatas plant, whose fibers made strong thread and whose pith caught fire with a single spark.

*"If we were stranded here,"* I mused, *"how would we kindle flame?"*

Ernest, ever thoughtful, suggested rubbing sticks—until I demonstrated the karatas’ quick-burning pith. The boys marveled, and I smiled. Every discovery was a lesson.

At last, we reached Tentholm, where we gathered supplies—salt, butter, and a chest washed ashore. Laden with goods, we returned home as the sun dipped low, our hearts full and our hands busy.

That evening, as we nestled into our treetop refuge, I reflected on the day—a Sabbath of rest, yet brimming with discovery. And as the stars blinked awake above us, I whispered a prayer of thanks—for safety, for provision, and for the boundless wonders of our new world.
