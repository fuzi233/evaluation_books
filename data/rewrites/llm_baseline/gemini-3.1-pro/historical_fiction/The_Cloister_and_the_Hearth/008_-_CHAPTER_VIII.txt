Time in Sevenbergen moved in slow, generational circles, its rhythms dictated by the turning of the seasons and the perpetual, smoky warmth of the hearth. Margaret stood before her cold iron cauldrons, tracing the rim of a blackened vessel that held centuries of her ancestors’ culinary memory. The air in the cottage carried the faint, lingering ghosts of dried winter savory and wild garlic, but the larder was devoid of the rich fats and marrow that gave their domestic existence its profound meaning. Gerard would soon return, his body hollowed by the day's labor. To Margaret, a meal was never mere utilitarian fuel; it was the sacred translation of love and cultural memory into something that could be tasted, smelled, and absorbed. To offer him an empty table was to sever an ancestral cord. 

Distressed by this disruption to their domestic routine, she turned to Martin Wittenhaagen. The grizzled agrarian elder possessed hands that knew the soil and the sinew of the land as intimately as his mother tongue. “Martin, there is nothing to set before him,” she whispered, her voice carrying the low, guttural cadence of their native Dutch dialect—a language rich with hyper-specific words for the damp earth and the rendering of fats, now slowly being suffocated by the sharp, unyielding syllables of the ruling courts. “It is Thursday. I counted on your bow.”

Martin sighed, scratching his weathered head. He pulled from his pouch a stiff, lifeless parchment bearing the heavy wax seal of Philip, Duke of Burgundy. The document was a weapon of the dominant lexicon, a decree written in the sterile, imperial phrasing of the ruling class. It dictated that Martin, despite his ancestral agrarian rights, could only hunt on Thursdays, provided the Duke’s hunting party did not disturb the woods. The untranslatable, subjective experience of the forest—the hunter’s quiet communion with the prey—was reduced by this foreign, legalistic tongue to a transactional ledger, punishable by the severing of a head and a fine of a penny. 

“The Duke hunts today,” Martin rumbled, his voice a steady acoustic baseline against the cottage's quietude. “But for your hearth, I will risk my carcass in the skirts of the forest.”

Martin slipped into the ancient woods, a space where mythic occurrences still brushed seamlessly against the edges of everyday survival. Typically, the forest was a cathedral of pristine acoustic ecology: the subtle, percussive tapping of woodpeckers, the soughing wind through old-growth oak, and the crunch of dry loam. But today, this delicate sonic environment was violently ruptured. The brassy, atonal blare of royal hunting horns fractured the air—an oppressive, jarring noise emblematic of commercialized sport, a superficial violence that cared nothing for the sacred equilibrium of the woods. 

Taking his stand behind a thick oak, Martin strung his English yew bow. The six-foot weapon was an extension of his own sinew; when drawn, the thick string hummed with the pure, resonant frequency of a plucked harp, perfectly in tune with the natural world. A hare cantered into the glade, sitting sprightly, its ears forming a sharp, alert V. Its scent was earthy and rich. Martin loosed an arrow, but it hissed along the short grass, passing just beneath the hare’s belly. The creature bounded away. Next, a wood-pigeon settled on the branch above, a promise of delicate, dark meat for a slow braise. Martin drew carefully, but the arrow merely tore the bird’s breast, carrying its feathers skyward like a cloud of gray dust before the pigeon fluttered off to die unseen in the thick foliage.

The discordant baying of hounds echoed closer, disrupting the natural frequencies of the glade. Then, a mythic sight manifested within the mundane rustle of the brush: a magnificent, seven-year-old buck, rich with the promise of tender loins and marrow-filled bones, crashed into the clearing. Yet it was pursued by an impossible shadow. A long, spotted beast—the Duke’s leopard—glided across the earth, a creature of foreign lore dropped violently into the damp Dutch ecosystem. 

The leopard leaped upon the buck’s back, its claws tearing into the hide, the copper tang of blood instantly blooming in the damp air. Driven by the primal necessity of the hearth, Martin loosed his arrow. It sang its deadly, perfect note, burying deep into the deer. The buck bounded high and fell dead. The meat was secured.

But the leopard, brutally ignorant of the agrarian hierarchy, ceased gorging on the blood and turned its flaming eyes on Martin. The elder stood erect, attempting to dominate the beast with a steady gaze, but the leopard lunged with a frightful yell, its jaws distended. A cacophony of snarls shattered the glade’s fragile silence. The beast's claws sank into Martin’s shoulder, tearing through his flesh. The other paw swiped at his cheek, but Martin, an old-fashioned man of the earth, wore no hat, only a coarse wool scapulary drawn over his head like a cowl. The brute's claw tangled inextricably in the loose leather.

Martin’s hands, strong from decades of working the land and butchering game, locked around the leopard’s throat. They fought in the visceral, wordless dialect of survival. It felt like blunt reaping-hooks grinding into his shoulder, but the pain only inflamed his blood. The scent of wild musk, hot blood, and crushed ferns filled his nostrils. He squeezed with iron force. The beast thrashed, dragging the cowl over Martin's eyes, blinding him, but the elder held on until the creature’s violent elasticity faded. He choked it until it was a powerless thing, dashing it to the earth. Removing his cowl, Martin stared at the mute, bloody-pawed mythic beast and felt terror. He had slain the Duke’s leopard. 

Covering the beast with handfuls of leaves, Martin shouldered the heavy, blood-rich buck and staggered back to Sevenbergen, leaving a dark trail in the loam.

He burst into the cottage, a horrifying, bloodstained figure, and flung the deer’s carcass onto the stone floor. “No questions,” he gasped. “Broil me a steak of it, for I am faint.”

Margaret did not shrink from the blood; she saw only the profound potential of sustenance. With the meticulous reverence of a food historian, she began the butchery, assuming the blood belonged entirely to the deer. While Martin quietly bound his torn shoulder in the corner, Margaret stoked the fire with dry birch until it crackled to life. She expertly carved thick steaks from the haunch. When the venison hit the scorching iron pan, the air filled with the intoxicating, rich aroma of rendering fat, seared crust, and woodsmoke—a gustatory symphony that transcended the sterile decrees of the Duke's parchment. 

Gerard arrived, bringing a ceramic flask of Schiedam. Under the influence of the botanical, juniper-laced spirit, which perfectly complemented the gamey, iron-rich flavor of the venison, Martin revived. They ate voraciously, chewing on the deep flavors of the forest, laughing in their native tongue, their words weaving a tapestry of cultural memory around the roaring hearth.

But the circular rhythm of time brought the violence of the outside world crashing back. The cottage’s acoustic warmth was suddenly shattered by a single, deep, resonant bay of a bloodhound in the dark wood. 

Margaret’s laughter died. Her eyes fixed on the window, her cheek paling. She gasped, pointing with a trembling finger. There, crouching in the twilight, was a dark form. Two eyes burned like green glowworms. The mythic beast had survived.

Martin trembled at the sound of the hound. “They have lost her scent and laid muzzled bloodhounds on her trail. They will find her here, and the venison. Good-bye, friends. Martin Wittenhaagen ends here.”

Gerard seized the great yew bow and shoved it into the elder's hands. “Be a man!” he cried. “Shoot her, and fling her into the wood ere they come up!”

The hounds' voices multiplied, a chaotic, oppressive wave of noise drowning out the hearth's crackle. 

“Curse her!” Martin cried. “I spared her once; now she must die.” He reared the bow, drawing the arrow to the head, ready to release violence into the night.

“Nay! Nay!” Margaret cried, fiercely protecting the sanctity of their culinary sanctuary. She seized the arrow, snapping the wooden shaft in two. The pieces clattered to the floor, breaking the spell of the weapon. 

“What have you done, wench?” Martin roared. “You have put the halter round my throat!”

“No!” Margaret shouted, her voice cutting through the dominant lexicon of force with the urgent, untranslatable pitch of survival. “I have saved you! Stand back from the window, both! Your knife, quick!” 

She snatched his long-pointed boning knife—a tool of culinary creation now repurposed for defense—from his girdle and darted from the room. Outside, the house was surrounded by the deafening clamor of baying dogs and shouting men, while the green, glowing eyes remained fixed, unblinking in the dark.
