The crisp November wind carried the scent of woodsmoke and newly pressed linen over Florence’s terracotta roofscape on the 17th of November, 1494. It had been more than eighteen months since Tito and Romola’s sunlit Easter wedding, when handfuls of rainbow-hued sugar comfits had been tossed over the newlyweds in the old Byzantine custom, a charm that promised their shared life would be stippled with nothing but sweet fortune. But those months had shifted the very ground under the city: just as the root sap feeds every bud and fruit on a thousand-bloom tree, so the great sweeping political tides reshaping all of Italy dictated even the private fortunes of a quiet scholarly couple like Tito and Romola.

Barely seven days before, the ancient fire of republican Florence had blazed back to life. The iron clapper of the Palazzo della Signoria’s great alarm bell boomed out across the piazzas, and citizens flooded the streets armed with rusted halberds, farm tools, and rough-hewn cudgels to chase the arrogant Piero de’ Medici straight out the San Gallo gate. His hired horsemen spooked and galloped at his heels, fleeing toward Bologna, while his sharper, younger brother the cardinal slipped away disguised as a ragged Franciscan friar. The Republic set a price on both their heads. Crowds sacked the homes of Medici loyalists per long-held custom, scrubbed the faded, ignominious portraits of decades-old anti-Medici conspirators off public walls, and sent invitations home to every citizen exiled by the Medici regime. The half-fledged tyrants had been well and truly routed from their gilded nest on the Via Larga, and the Florentine Republic had reclaimed its free will for the first time in a generation.

That grand palace on the Via Larga, the old Medici seat, was now draped, polished, and furbished to receive a new occupant. Street-side tapestries dyed deep crimson and indigo, not seen since the last great Medici feast, hung from window sills; hundreds of banners snapped in the sharp wind; thick woven carpets were spread across every stone step and cobblestone path where the feet of the honored guest might tread. By every outward visible sign of public joy, Florence could not have been more eager to welcome him. That river of vivid color wound all the way from the Via Larga palace, past the marble facade of the Duomo, through the crowded Piazza della Signoria, across the goldsmith-lined arches of the Ponte Vecchio, to the Porta San Frediano: the western gate facing Pisa. A raised platform draped in velvet had been erected there for the Signoria, where Messer Luca Corsini, doctor of laws, felt his pulse thrum fast as he ran a thumb over the parchment of the grand Latin oration he was set to deliver. Every elder statesman of the Republic had trimmed his beard to a smooth sheen, donned his best lined silk lucco robe, and practiced his place in the long public procession. The city’s noble young men had spent days preening in their new French-cut velvet tunics, certain their lean, elegant Florentine frames would wear the foreign fashion with a grace no Frenchman could match. The entire body of the city’s clergy, from the archbishop blazing in gold vestments to the long lines of black, white, and grey robed monks, had gathered at dawn to map their procession, arranging sacred relics, embroidered banners, and jeweled consecrated vessels so they would fall into perfect step to meet the illustrious visitor due to arrive at three that afternoon.

This was no ordinary guest. He had crossed the Alpine passes with an army no Italian had ever seen before: thousands of ferocious Swiss mercenaries who fought as fiercely for personal loyalties as for pay, a host of proud noble cavaliers, an unprecedented infantry where one in every hundred men carried an arquebus, and even bronze cannons that fired iron balls instead of stone, pulled by horses not oxen, fast enough to reload and fire a second round before a besieged city could patch the breach their first shot tore open. Some Florentines whispered he was Charlemagne reborn, the legendary restorer of Florence, rightful conqueror of corrupt kings, regulator and savior of the Church. Others hailed him as a new Cyrus, liberator of the chosen people, come to rebuild the Temple in purity. This young king, Charles VIII of France, was the son of that cunning Louis XI who had died ten years prior trembling at the unforeseen consequences of his own political scheming. He had led his armies across the Alps with glittering, grand plans: he would march through Italy cheered by every grateful crowd, resolve every bitter feuding faction in Rome, claim the Kingdom of Naples by right of inheritance and a little well-placed force, then launch from there on a holy crusade to crush the Ottoman Turks, slaying half their armies and converting the rest to the Christian faith. It was a scheme grand enough to make even sober men believe that marble statues might sweat, that phantom fiery armies clashed in the midnight sky, that strange monstrous births among livestock were divine omens: this was no ordinary turn of fortune, most agreed, it was no less than a special work of God.

That widespread conviction did not spring merely from the terrifying size of the French army, but from the fiery preaching of one man: Fra Girolamo Savonarola, prior of the Dominican convent of San Marco. Back in September, when the first news arrived that the French had crossed the Alps into Italy, Savonarola had climbed the Duomo’s pulpit and preached from the Book of Genesis: “Behold, I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth.” Guided by divine hand, he said, he had reached that exact passage in his Lenten exposition months before, and he declared that the flood was no metaphor for rain: it was the French army itself, both a scourge to punish the wicked and a purifying tide that would wash all corruption out of the Church. His audience, many of them the most learned, cultivated men in the most cultivated city in all of Italy, listened with skin prickling in awe. For four full years prior, he had thundered from that same pulpit that a scourge was coming down on Italy, one that would purify the Church of all its rotten corruption. He believed he had a prophetic mission equal to the ancient Hebrew prophets, and that the Florentines who heard his warnings were a new chosen people. In an age where self-proclaimed seers and gossiping fortune-tellers were common even inside cloisters, Savonarola stood apart: their prophecies were tiny, trivial candles lighting up small corners of petty fate, while his was a blazing lighthouse beam, shining across the whole of the known world to warn men of coming judgment.

Only two years prior, in 1492, the year Lorenzo de’ Medici died and the young Greek wanderer Tito Melema first stepped into Florence, Italy had known nothing but unbroken peace and plenty. Harvests had been fat with corn, wine, and oil, new marble palaces rose in every fair city, new villas dotted every sunlit Tuscan hill, and the rich men who owned them never feared the poor, for citizens’ armour had all gone rusted hanging on walls, the populace had grown tame, and tyrants hired mercenary armies the same way they bought imported silk from Smyrna. No one feared the Turks anymore, and the Borgia Pope found it far more profitable to take their gold in exchange for quiet complicity than to waste time planning crusades. The world seemed arranged perfectly for the benefit of the wealthy, powerful, and corrupt: lust, treachery, oppression, even murder were all acceptable as long as you had the money and influence to get away with them. As a pretty garnish for that life of luxury, wealthy patrons poured gold into fine art and humanist learning, so flatterers could pen their praise in the most elegant Ciceronian Latin, and great painters would paint the sacred and the profane with equal, perfect skill. The Church itself had never been more corrupt at its highest ranks, nor more spiritually lifeless among its lower clergy, yet it had never been wealthier. The skies were blue and calm, no one saw a single sign of approaching earthquake. And yet Savonarola, for years already, had been thundering that a sword hung suspended in those very calm blue skies: the sword of God’s justice, about to fall at any moment, to punish the wicked and purify the world. Seventeen years prior, in Ferrara, the unbearable gap between the lives men lived and the faith they claimed to follow had burned so hot in his chest that he had renounced all worldly pleasure at 23 and entered the cloister. He could not believe a just God would let this state of moral rot last forever, and when rumors began to spread in 1493 that Charles VIII was gathering his army to cross the Alps, Savonarola knew his prophecy was coming true. This was the flood he had foretold, this new Cyrus an instrument of divine will: if Florence, the city that had received his special warning, repented of its sins like the ancient city of Nineveh, the storm would pass over it and leave only life-giving rain.

For all Savonarola’s great influence, though, now that the French king had been in Italy for three months and was almost at Florence’s gates, the public mood was a tangled mix of excitement, fear, and deep distrust. He had not redressed a single grievance the Italians had complained of, and Florence had no reason to thank him. Piero de’ Medici, in his cowardice, had handed over all Florence’s key border fortresses to the French king without a single honorable treaty in exchange, and Charles still held them. He had done nothing to crush the revolt in Pisa, which had been emboldened by his arrival to throw off Florentine rule. Even the city’s official orators, including Savonarola himself, had failed to get a concrete promise of good treatment from Charles, who kept saying only that he would “settle all matters” once he was inside Florence’s walls. Still, the citizens had taken great joy in chasing Piero de’ Medici out of the city for his treason, and that act of defiance had stoked the old republican fire in their bones.

Behind all the bright public tapestries and banners meant to signal welcome, the city had made far more secret preparations: hired mercenary soldiers were quartered hidden inside walled palaces, old armour had been polished until it gleamed, cudgels and sharp farm tools were stacked in every street-side shopfront, barricades of heavy planks and stakes were pre-built and ready to drag across alley mouths, and piles of paving stones were stacked on every upper story window sill. The Florentines were in no mood to let a foreign king push them around, not after the sweet taste of chasing the Medici tyrants out just a week prior. Their temper was not improved when small parties of French advance riders rode through the streets ahead of the king, hawks perched on their wrists, swaggering as they chalked the doors of the best houses to mark them as quarters for the French nobles.

That was the sharp, complicated, charged mood that hung over Florence on the morning of November 17th, 1494.
