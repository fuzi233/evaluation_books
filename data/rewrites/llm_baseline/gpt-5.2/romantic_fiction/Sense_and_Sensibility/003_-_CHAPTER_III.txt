Mrs. Dashwood stayed at Norland through a slow-turning season, as if grief itself required a certain number of mornings before it would loosen its grip. At first, every familiar corner—the turn in the passage where her husband’s step used to sound, the window-seat that kept the late sun, the cedar walk that held last year’s needles like old letters—set her heart to a sharp, sudden pain. But time, that domestic servant who works without praise, at length restored her to the capacity of ordinary exertion; and the moment she could think of anything but sorrow, she became impatient to be gone.

Not gone far—never far. To remove at a distance from Norland felt to her like tearing up the root while the plant still lived. She wanted a dwelling in the neighbourhood, some smaller house where the days might be stitched again into usefulness. She was indefatigable: sending notes, asking questions, listening to every report of a vacancy as attentively as a cook listens for the first whisper of a simmer. Yet every place that promised comfort proved, when measured by Elinor’s steadier judgment, too dear, too large, too hungry a mouth for their reduced income. Mrs. Dashwood could be charmed by breadth of prospect and a pretty parlour; Elinor, with accounts in her mind as exact as weights on a scale, rejected what her mother would have approved.

There remained, as a comfort in their uncertainty, the remembrance of a promise. Her husband had told her—had died with the thought of it easing his last reflections—that his son had solemnly engaged to provide for them. Mrs. Dashwood doubted this assurance no more than her husband had doubted it. For her daughters’ sake, the expectation of that kindness lay in her mind like bread set aside: not to be consumed in greed, but to keep anxiety from gnawing too sharply. For herself, she was persuaded that far less than seven thousand pounds would support her in what she called affluence; she had always known how to make warmth from small means, how to find a kind of richness in daily order. And for her son’s sake too—because she wished his heart to be at ease with his own conduct—she rejoiced, and reproached herself for having once believed him incapable of generosity.

Indeed, his attentive behaviour to her and to the girls encouraged her belief for a long while. His visits were dutiful; his inquiries after their comfort sufficiently frequent; his manner, when he spoke to them, appeared to acknowledge that their welfare mattered. She relied on the liberality of his intentions with a confidence that did honour to her feelings.

It was not so with her feelings towards her daughter-in-law. The contempt Mrs. Dashwood had conceived early for Mrs. John Dashwood did not soften; it hardened, as certain airs and certain turns of speech—smooth words that closed like a fist—became more familiar through half a year’s residence under the same roof. The two ladies, differing in temper as widely as a hearth differs from a drawing-room fire made only for show, might very likely have found it impossible to live together so long, had not one particular circumstance arisen to render, in Mrs. Dashwood’s opinion, their continuing at Norland not merely tolerable but desirable.

This circumstance was a growing attachment between Elinor and the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood.

Edward Ferrars had been introduced to their acquaintance soon after his sister’s establishment at Norland; and since then he had spent the greatest part of his time there. In another mother’s mind, the connection might have been encouraged from interest—for he was the eldest son of a man who had died very rich—or repressed from prudence, since, except a trifling sum, the whole of his fortune depended on the will of his mother. But Mrs. Dashwood was alike uninfluenced by either consideration. It was enough that he appeared amiable, that he loved her daughter, and that Elinor returned the partiality. It was against every doctrine of hers that difference of fortune should keep asunder a couple drawn together by likeness of disposition; and that Elinor’s merit should not be acknowledged by everyone who knew her, was to her comprehension impossible.

Edward was not recommended by any peculiar graces of person or address. He was not handsome; and his manners required intimacy to make them pleasing. There was in him a diffidence that kept his best qualities, at first, like a well-covered dish whose fragrance must wait for the lid to lift. He was too shy to do justice to himself; but when that shyness was overcome, his behaviour gave every indication of an open, affectionate heart. His understanding was good; his education had solidly improved it; and yet he was neither fitted by abilities nor disposition to answer the wishes of his mother and sister, who longed to see him “distinguished”—as they hardly knew in what. They wanted him to make a fine figure in the world in some manner or other. His mother wished to interest him in political concerns, to get him into parliament, or to see him connected with some great men of the day. Mrs. John Dashwood wished it likewise; but in the meanwhile—until one of these superior blessings could be attained—it would have quieted her ambition to see him driving a barouche.

Edward had no turn for great men or barouches. All his wishes centred in domestic comfort and the quiet of private life: a settled table, a familiar room, voices that did not force themselves above one another. There was something in him that preferred the honest sound of a household—chairs set down carefully, a book laid open, a kettle beginning its low song—to the roar of public consequence. If he had any ambition, it was for peace.

He had been several weeks in the house before he engaged much of Mrs. Dashwood’s attention; for she was then so absorbed by affliction that she was careless of surrounding objects. She saw only that he was quiet and unobtrusive, and she liked him for it. He did not trouble the wretchedness of her mind with ill-timed conversation, nor force gaiety upon sorrow. In a world already full of hard noises—of talk that struck like a hammer, of opinions spoken to conquer—his restraint was a kind of gentleness.

She was first called to observe and approve him further by a reflection Elinor happened one day to make upon the difference between him and his sister. The contrast recommended him, in an instant, to her mother.

“It is enough,” cried Mrs. Dashwood, with an eager warmth that made the room feel suddenly brighter, “to say that he is unlike Fanny—is enough. It implies everything amiable. I love him already.”

“I think you will like him,” said Elinor, “when you know more of him.”

“Like him!” her mother replied, smiling. “I feel no sentiment of approbation inferior to love.”

“You may esteem him.”

“I have never yet known what it was to separate esteem and love.”

From that moment she took pains to get acquainted with him. Her manners were attaching, and soon banished his reserve. She questioned him, listened to him, drew him out with the same natural art by which she could make a plain dinner feel like hospitality: not by costliness, but by warmth, by attention, by letting each person at table be seen and considered. Edward, met with such frankness, began to speak more freely; and as he did, the quiet in him ceased to look like coldness and showed itself as steadiness.

She speedily comprehended all his merits. The persuasion of his regard for Elinor perhaps assisted her penetration; but she truly felt assured of his worth. Even that quietness of manner, which at first had offended her established ideas of what a young man’s address ought to be, became no longer uninteresting when she believed his heart warm and his temper affectionate. And no sooner did she perceive any symptom of love in his behaviour to Elinor—an attention lingering a moment too long, a softness in his voice when he spoke her name, the involuntary turning of his ear to her slightest movement—than she considered their serious attachment as certain, and looked forward to their marriage as rapidly approaching.

“In a few months, my dear Marianne,” said she one day, as they sat together, “Elinor will, in all probability, be settled for life. We shall miss her; but she will be happy.”

“Oh, Mama—how shall we do without her?”

“My love, it will be scarcely a separation. We shall live within a few miles of each other, and shall meet every day of our lives. You will gain a brother—a real, affectionate brother. I have the highest opinion in the world of Edward’s heart. But you look grave, Marianne; do you disapprove your sister’s choice?”

“Perhaps,” said Marianne, “I may consider it with some surprise. Edward is very amiable, and I love him tenderly. But yet—he is not the kind of young man—there is something wanting. His figure is not striking; it has none of that grace which I should expect in the man who could seriously attach my sister. His eyes want all that spirit, that fire, which at once announce virtue and intelligence. And besides all this, I am afraid, Mama, he has no real taste. Music seems scarcely to attract him; and though he admires Elinor’s drawings very much, it is not the admiration of a person who can understand their worth. It is evident, in spite of his frequent attention to her while she draws, that in fact he knows nothing of the matter. He admires as a lover, not as a connoisseur. To satisfy me, those characters must be united. I could not be happy with a man whose taste did not in every point coincide with my own. He must enter into all my feelings; the same books, the same music must charm us both. Oh! Mama, how spiritless—how tame—was Edward’s manner in reading to us last night! I felt for my sister most severely; yet she bore it with so much composure, she seemed scarcely to notice it. I could hardly keep my seat. To hear those beautiful lines, which have frequently almost driven me wild, pronounced with such impenetrable calmness—such dreadful indifference!”

“He would certainly have done more justice to simple and elegant prose,” said Mrs. Dashwood. “I thought so at the time; but you would give him Cowper.”

“Nay, Mama—if he is not to be animated by Cowper!—But we must allow for difference of taste. Elinor has not my feelings, and therefore she may overlook it and be happy with him. But it would have broke my heart, had I loved him, to hear him read with so little sensibility. Mama, the more I know of the world, the more am I convinced that I shall never see a man whom I can really love. I require so much! He must have all Edward’s virtues, and his person and manners must ornament his goodness with every possible charm.”

“Remember, my love, that you are not seventeen,” replied her mother, with a tenderness that softened even her contradiction. “It is yet too early in life to despair of such a happiness. Why should you be less fortunate than your mother? In one circumstance only, my Marianne, may your destiny be different from hers!”
