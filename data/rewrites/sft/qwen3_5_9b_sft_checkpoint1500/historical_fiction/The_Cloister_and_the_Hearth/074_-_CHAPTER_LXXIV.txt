The new Pope held the Dominican Order in high esteem. From the Vatican came a summons requiring a friar of proven capability to instruct at the University of Basel. Father Clement, well-versed in multiple tongues and having once attended the lectures of Guarini the Younger in his secular youth, was deemed the ideal candidate. Consequently, his planned return to England was deferred, though not abandoned. Instead, he was dispatched to Basel with the proviso that, needing no specific labor there for three months, he must preach on his journey.

Stepping out through the northern gate, Clement lowered his gaze, his entire figure enshrouded in a silence of devout contemplation. If one could render a mind and its history in paint, this barefoot friar would be a living fresco. Within him resided a tumultuous narrative: hopeful, happy love; bereavement; despair; impiety; vice; suicide; remorse; religious despondency; penitence; a death to the world; and resignation. And all of this, compressed into merely twelve short months.

Now the traveler was once again on foot. Yet everything had altered. The perilous adventures of the past were gone. Even thieves and robbers, bowing before him, refused to harm him; instead, they proffered stolen coins and begged for his prayers. Thus, this journey offered few picturesque or dramatic incidents. However, for those readers who prefer a quiet peep into the workings of a human soul rather than mere melodrama, the undramatic moments are often the most vocal, revealing the steady progress of a mind.

On the first Sunday of Clement's pilgrimage, he knelt to pray for the soul of Margaret. It was a novel act for him; her eternal welfare had always been dearer to him than anything earthly. Yet, humility now governed his spirit. The terrible impieties that had once burst from his lips following news of her death—words that had horrified his contemporaries and eventually horrified him upon reflection—were rooted in a long period of religious despair during his novitiate. He had believed he had committed the unforgivable sin against the Holy Spirit, damning his soul forever. Though Anselmo had gradually helped to clear away that dark cloud, a profound self-abasement remained. Clement felt his own salvation insecure and reasoned that it would mock Heaven for him, a soul so deeply stained, to pray for one as innocent as Margaret's. Henceforth, he coaxed good Anselm and another kindly monk to intercede for her. These good old monks, possessing a pure and tender affection for their younger brethren that transcended worldly bounds, did not refuse, nor did they offer their aid perfunctorily.

Having preached on Sunday morning in a small Italian town and finding his seriocara carrying him onward, Clement was greatly encouraged. That day, a balmy sense of God's forgiveness and love descended upon him. He prayed for the welfare of Margaret's soul. From that hour, this became his daily habit, the single purified tie connecting his heart to the earth. For his family, now, existed for him as if they had never been. The Church, in its divine economy, would not share with earth, nor could it cure a great love without annihilating the lesser ones.

Throughout most of this journey, Clement felt no spring within him until he stood in the pulpit, or when he had the chance to relieve a fellow creature. A young man, either tarantula-bitten or merely suffering from delirium, had lain awake for two days in extraordinary convulsions, leaping, twisting, and battering the walls. Village musicians, attempting to soothe him, had only worsened his condition. Exhaustion and death seemed imminent. Clement intervened, learning the boy's plight. He summoned a psaltery and attempted to soothe the patient with gentle melodies. While other tunes mad the boy frantic, Clement's strains seemed to crush him. The friar observed that at intervals the boy's lips moved, whispering a tune. Applying his ear, Clement discovered it was a melody with no existence outside the boy's mind—a strange creation born of his fever. Learning the tune, Clement played it. The boy's face brightened remarkably. He danced lightly, marching about the room, and when Clement's fingers ached from the repetitive playing, he was rewarded by seeing the youth sink complacently into sleep to this lullaby, saving his life. Clement taught the tune to another traveler as a safeguard, leaving forward with his heart a little warmer.

On another occasion, Clement encountered a mob dragging a decently dressed man who struggled and vociferated in a strange tongue. The man had entered the town sprightly, waving a mulberry branch overhead. The locals, initially stupefied, quickly pounced, dragging him before the *poderà*. Clement approached quietly, speaking to the prisoner first in Italian, then French, German, and Dutch, finding no response. Finally, the man answered in tolerable Latin, albeit with a sharp accent. He revealed himself to be an Englishman, oppressed by the heat of Italy, who had plucked the branch from the nearest tree because "in my country, anyone may take what grows on the highway." He expressed frustration at the local uproar over a mere twig.

The stubborn *poderà* intended to imprison the "dogged islander," but Clement mediated. With some difficulty, he explained that silkworms and their mulberry leaves were sacred, falling under the protection of the Sovereign and constituting the source of local income. He argued that ignorance of such specific laws was natural in a distant land where the tree itself might be unknown. The opinionated islander, however, tipped the scales by producing a long purse and reiterating his point with mercantile logic: "What damage?" (*Quid damni?*), "Tell me, and I will pay quickly." The *poderà*, smelling the gold, fined the man a ducat for the Duke—roughly the value of the entire tree—and pocketed the coin.

The Englishman shook off his ire instantly, laughing heartily at the absurdity, yet remaining deeply grateful to Clement. "You are too good for this hole of a country, father," the man declared. "Come to England! That is the only place in the world. I was an uneasy fool to leave it and wander among mulberries and their idiots. I am a Kentish squire, educated at Cambridge. My name is Rolfe, my place Betshanger. Both the man and the house are at your service. Come over and stay until Domesday. Forty sit down to dinner every day; one more or one less makes no difference. You shall end your days with me and my heirs if you will. Come now! What an Englishman says, he means." He gave Clement a hearty grip of the hand.

"I will visit thee some day, my son," Clement replied, "but not to weary thy hospitality."

The Englishman then beseeched Clement to shrive him, confessing, "I know not what will become of my soul; I have lived like a heathen since I left England." Clement consented gladly. Soon, the islander was on his knees by the roadside, confessing the sins of the last month. Finding him so pious, Clement let the man know he was indeed returning to England. He then inquired if the country was overrun with Lollards and Wickliffites. The Englishman flushed slightly. "There be black sheep in every land," he admitted. After reflection, he spoke gravely: "Holy father, hear the truth about these heretics. None are better disposed toward the Holy Church than we English. But we are ourselves, and by ourselves. We love our own ways, and above all, our own tongue. The Normans conquered our bill-hooks, but not our tongues, and they tried hard by law and proclamation for many a long year. Our good foreign priests speak God to plain English folk in Latin, or some French or Italian lingo, like the bleating of a sheep. Then comes the fox Wickliffe and his crew, reading Scripture out of its own book in plain English, warming all men's hearts. Who can withstand this? God forgive me, I believe the English would turn deaf ears to Saint Peter himself, if he spoke not to them in the tongue their mothers sowed in their ears and their hearts along with mothers' kisses." He hastened to add, "I say not this for myself; I am Cambridge-bred, and good words come not amiss to me in Latin. But for the people in general, the key to the hearts of the English is the mother tongue."

"My son," said Clement, "blessed be the hour I met thee; for thy words are sober and wise. But alas! How shall I learn your English tongue? I have no book."

"I would give you my Book of Hours, father," the islander offered. "'Tis in English and Latin, cheek by jowl. But then, what would become of my poor soul, wanting my 'hours' in a strange land? Stay, you are a holy man, and I am an honest one; let us make a bargain: you pray for me every day for two months, and I give you my Book of Hours. Here it is. What say you to that?" His eyes sparkled, burning with mercantile fervor.
