“And you must love him ere to you He will seem worthy of your love.” — WORDSWORTH

It would have been easy to envy Deronda as he hunted through soft wool and fine cotton for new clothes for Mordecai, daydreaming the way an artist sketches a beloved scene: the drape of the warm brown dressing gown, cut loose like a Franciscan’s simple frock, framing Mordecai’s sharp, thoughtful face and slender neck above soft grey flannel shirts. Half his pleasure came from seeing every small detail through Mirah’s eyes, determined that the reunion she’d grieved for for twelve long years would never be marred by a jarring, unkind impression. Even so, once every garment was laid out ready, a quiet doubt niggled at him: was he being foolish, prioritizing a polished outward effect over the deeper truth of who Mordecai was? Would Mirah not cherish the ragged, worn clothes her brother had survived all his wandering in, stitched through with memories of all the years they’d been apart? But the Meyricks had not yet learned to see the quiet, sacred grandeur of the man Mirah longed for — that soft, sun-set glow of reverent love that wraps every beloved person in quiet magic, that Deronda knew the Meyricks had not yet found space for in their untested sympathies. It was easier to make sure nothing stood in the way of them welcoming Mordecai fully, than to fight unspoken, unacknowledged prejudices.

His resolve would have only grown stronger if he’d sat in on the Meyrick household’s late fireside chat that night, after Mirah had gone up to her room. Hans, now settled in his chaotic Chelsea artist’s rooms, had stayed late teasing the girls over their terrible volumes of bad poetry, when Mrs. Meyrick poked the coals until flames leapt bright. “Kate, blow out that candle, all of you huddle in close to the hearth,” she said. “Hans, love, stop laughing at those dreadful poems for the hundredth time, come sit too. I’ve got something wonderful to tell.”
“As if we didn’t all know that, ma!” Kate laughed, tucking her feet under her on the fender. “I’ve seen that secret glint in your eye all day, and the fake errands you kept running just to hide it.” Hans swung his chair around, straddling it and resting his chin on his folded fists, curious despite himself.
“Then you will all be shocked to hear that Mirah’s brother has been found,” Mrs. Meyrick announced, clear and bright.
“Confound it,” Hans blurted out, at the exact same moment.
“Hans, that’s awful!” Mab chided, squeezing her knees to her chest. “Imagine if we lost you, how miserable we’d be!”
“I can’t help but be a little sorry,” Kate admitted softly. “What about her mother? Where is she?”
“Her mother is dead,” Mrs. Meyrick said gently.
“Hope the brother isn’t a bad man,” Amy put in.
“Or some grinning, over-the-top showman all decked in cheap jewelry, like a Crystal Palace Assyrian in a hat,” Hans groused, already sour.
“Ungrateful children, all of you!” Mrs. Meyrick said, half-laughing, half-stern. “Not one of you is thinking a single bit about how happy Mirah must be right now.”
“Mirah barely even remembers her brother, ma,” Kate pointed out. “People who get lost for twelve years should just never come back. They’re always in the way,” Hans huffed.
“Hans!” Mrs. Meyrick reproached him. “If you lost me for twenty years, you’d never say that.”
“Twelve years is the exact cutoff for long-lost relations to stay out of sight,” he shot back.
“But finding people is lovely!” Mab protested, bouncing a little. “It gives you a grand story to tell! Is he as exciting as Prince Camaralzaman?”
Mrs. Meyrick then told them every detail Deronda had shared, ending with: “Mr. Deronda admires him more than anyone else I’ve ever heard him speak of. He says Mirah is exactly the sister who will understand every part of him.”
“Deronda has gone perfectly absurd over these Jewish people lately,” Hans snapped, jumping up and slamming his chair hard against the floor. “He’s spoiling Mirah, feeding all these little prejudices of hers, soon she’ll stop being the brilliant young artist she is and turn as narrow as a cloistered nun. We’ll never have a quiet evening in this house again — we’ll have to invite this self-appointed prophet Elijah over for tea every Sunday, and she’ll spend all day moping over the ruins of Jerusalem. Everything we have is going to be ruined. I might as well take to drinking.”
“Oh really, Hans,” Kate said, rolling her eyes. “Men are the most selfish, ridiculous creatures alive. The whole world has to bend to their exact mood, else they throw a fit like a toddler.”
“Oh, it’s so dreadful!” Mab teased, mock-gasping. “I feel like ancient Nineveh just moved in next door.”
“After all that university education, you’re acting more childish than we are,” Amy chided. “We have to be kind to him. So do you.”
“I hope you all have fun with his new Lamentations of Jeremiah, serialized one tedious story beat at a time,” Hans grumbled, grabbing his wide-brimmed hat. “There’s nothing worse than people with one fixed grand idea in their heads, staring at you blankly and expecting every little thing you say to be a tiny footnote to their grand text. If I have to stand under that petrifying shadow all the time, I’d rather be an old discarded boot, and I am not an old boot.” He bent to kiss Mrs. Meyrick’s forehead in a quick, desperate gesture, then mumbled “Goodnight, girls” on his way to the door.
“Wait till Mirah hears how you behaved!” Kate called after him, only to be answered by a resounding slam of the front door. “I can’t wait to see her face when Deronda tells her the news,” she said to her mother afterwards. “She’s going to look more beautiful than ever.”

But Deronda, after turning the thought over, sent a note to Mrs. Meyrick the next morning, asking her to break the news to Mirah instead of him. He didn’t share his real reason — that he hated the thought of recounting the whole story and accidentally framing himself as some grand, generous hero — and instead explained he wanted to stay with Mordecai while Mrs. Meyrick brought Mirah over for their first visit, so they could ease gently into the new life they would build together.
Deronda carried a quiet, unspoken worry in his chest: after all these years Mordecai had spent alone, lost in his grand, solitary ideas, his body growing weaker with every passing month, would he welcome Mirah’s soft, warm presence as something more than a pious duty to fulfill? He loved the Cohens dearly, little Jacob most of all, who he’d known for years, but lately Mordecai had barely asked a single new question about Mirah, quiet and unresisting as he accepted all the changes coming to his life. He put on the new clothes without complaint, only saying with a faint, soft smile, “Keep my old rags for me, please. They hold all my old memories.”
As they sat waiting for Mirah to arrive, he didn’t say a single word, his eyelids half-closed, but his hands twitched and his face was tight with that special, sharp nervousness only people who’ve spent years locked in one solitary train of thought know, when the past they thought was gone forever comes crashing back, heavy with a joy so bright it feels almost dangerous. Deronda felt his own heart race too, and when he heard the knock at the door, he stepped out to greet Mirah before she walked in, and froze.
She was wearing the exact same shabby dark cloak and worn hat he’d first seen her in, the one that had once been soaked through so badly she’d thought to wrap it around herself as a winding sheet when she wanted to end her life by the river. Mrs. Meyrick had asked her, confused, why she was wearing that tattered old thing, and Mirah had answered softly: “My brother is poor. I don’t want to look fancy and make him feel like a stranger. I want to look like I belong with him.” Deronda felt a quiet surge of shame at his own fastidious, over-planned arrangements, when Mirah had known the perfect, gentle thing to do all along. They shook hands in silence, Mirah pale, her eyes wide with awe, like she was about to step into a dream she’d waited half her life to wake up inside.

Deronda opened the door. Mordecai was on his feet, eyes fixed on the threshold, eager as a small child’s. Mirah took two, three small steps forward, then stopped dead. They stared at each other, perfectly still, perfectly silent. It was not their grown, worn, present selves they saw first — they saw the little boy and little girl they’d been, back when their mother was alive and their whole family was whole, and all the lost years melted away.
“Ezra,” Mirah said, her voice exactly the same soft tone she’d used when she first told Deronda about their mother calling his name.
Mordecai stepped forward in one quick, sharp movement, resting his thin hand gently on her shoulders. He was a whole head taller than her, looking down at her with a tenderness so thick it hung in the air. “That was our mother’s voice,” he said. “You remember her calling for me?”
“Yes,” Mirah breathed. “And you answered her, ‘Mother!’ and I knew how much you loved her.” She threw her arms around his neck, her small hands clasped tight behind his head, and pulled his face down to kiss it again and again, childlike and unselfconscious. Her hat fell back onto the floor, spilling all her dark curls loose.
“Ah, my dear head, my dear dear head,” Mordecai murmured, brushing a light finger over her curls.
“You’re so sick, Ezra,” Mirah said, her voice thick with tears as she looked at him properly.
“Yes, my little one,” he said, quiet and unafraid. “I will not be long with you in the flesh.”
“That’s alright,” Mirah said, the words spilling out as sweet and natural as birdsong. “I will love you. We will talk every hour of every day. I’ll tell you every single thing that ever happened to me, and you will teach me to be the good Jewess our mother wanted me to be. I work now — I sing, I make money, I will take care of us. I have had the kindest friends in the whole world.” It was only then she remembered Deronda and Mrs. Meyrick were standing there, turning to them with the sweetest, softest shift, one hand still clinging tight to her brother’s arm. She pulled Mrs. Meyrick’s hand, pressed it firmly into Mordecai’s, then wrapped her own two hands around theirs and kissed their joined knuckles.
“This is the lady who took me in when I was lost, alone, and starving,” she said. “Who treated me like her very own daughter.”
“The Eternal Goodness has walked with you,” Mordecai said gently to Mrs. Meyrick. “You helped fulfill our mother’s last prayer.”
Deronda laid a light, gentle pressure on Mrs. Meyrick’s arm. “I think we should go now, and come back later,” he said, and she nodded immediately, understanding. He did not want to risk accidentally mentioning any of the secrets about his own past he had not yet shared with Mordecai, and he knew the brother and sister needed this time, completely alone, to start mending all the twelve long years they had lost.
