Costigan did not come thundering at Arthur Pendennis’s door with a challenge, nor did Mr. Huxter fling any hostile billet-doux over the area-railings. So when Pen woke, he did what very few London men wake to do: he woke without immediately swallowing a mouthful of care, like cold gruel one must bolt before breakfast.

The City gentleman, the instant his eyes open, hears—in his mind, if not through the sash—the clink of coin and the rasp of ledgers; the lawyer sits up already entangled in somebody else’s quarrel, and can taste the ink before he has even rinsed his mouth. Most of us, when sleep drops the curtain, find Anxiety waiting in the front row at matinée, tapping its cane for the play to begin. Sleep, that honest cook of Providence, sends us out again with our spirits re-seasoned; but Pen’s day’s work was not of the kind to make him leap up greedy for it. Literature, when you are paid by the column and must trot daily like a hired horse, loses its froth and sparkle; Pegasus, once harnessed to a stage-coach, begins to look about as romantic as a cab-hack, and requires either the whip or the corn, and generally both.

So Arthur did his pieces for the Pall Mall Gazette—now with a novelist’s name and a somewhat better salary—and sometimes the dish came out crisp, sometimes heavy; but always made, always sent in, with the professional air of a man stirring sauce because the kitchen expects it, not because the smell intoxicates him.

Society did not feed him either. He bragged of it; he yawned over it. He was too young to get the best of women’s talk (which, like certain old wines, can only be tasted properly when you have stopped admiring your own glass); too young to be an equal among men who had made their mark; and, by a cruel trick of time, too old for the riotous boys of his own age. Too much of a pleasure-man for the business tribe, too much of a business-man (in appearances) for the pleasure tribe—destined to sit a good deal alone, like a guest who has arrived between the fish and the roast. He bore it well enough, but talked of it as if it were martyrdom—because complaining, with Pen, was a kind of after-dinner habit.

And yet, this morning, the first thing he thought of—before his boots, before his columns, before his own face in the glass—was a girl’s voice.

“What a nice little artless creature that was,” he said to himself, while the memory of Vauxhall still hung about him like the faint scent of crushed mint on fingers. “So much pleasanter than the practiced simpers in ball-rooms. Who would have guessed a rose could grow in a porter’s lodge? And in Shepherd’s Inn too—that old flower-pot of a place. She sings, does she? Taught by old Bows. If her singing is half as sweet as her speaking—those low veiled voices—why, it must go to the heart. ‘What would you like me to call you?’ Poor little Fanny! It hurt me to put on the grand air and bid her call me ‘Sir’—but no nonsense, no Faust-and-Margaret cookery for me.”

Then, as his thoughts always did, his mind turned from the young to the old, from the fresh bread to the seasoned stew.

“That old Bows! He’s a dear old fellow—a gentleman in those ancient clothes, a philosopher with a kind heart. How good he was in the Fotheringay business. He has had his griefs, too. I must cultivate old Bows. One ought to know people of every sort. I’m tired of the fine world. Besides, there’s nobody in town. I’ll go and see Bows—and Costigan too. What a character! I’ll study him and put him in a book.”

Thus spoke Mr. Arthur, anthropologist of London, who liked to fancy he went among his fellows as a naturalist in search of specimens, while his heart, like everybody else’s, went on its own errands. It was Saturday—the Gazette had appeared, the ink-bottle might be corked, and contributors could take their brains for a walk like dogs. Pen resolved upon Shepherd’s Inn at once—of course to see old Bows.

The truth (which is always more mischievous than the intentions we proclaim) was that if Arthur had been the most determined deceiver who ever polished a smile to ruin a girl, he could hardly have chosen better tactics than those he had used the night before. His dandified protecting manner, his conceit dressed up as good-humour, his generosity, and—worse than all for poor Fanny—his very honesty in checking the timid little flutter of her sensibility, had combined like spice, sugar, and flame to make a dish irresistible. A man’s scruple is sometimes the most seductive thing he can offer.

If we could peep into Fanny Bolton’s bed (a cupboard-bed, where she lay wedged with two small sisters like loaves in a too-tight tin), we should find the poor maid tossing, thereby disturbing those innocent sleepers, and living over again every glance, every word, every laugh of that delicious night. She had read novels—three-volume romances passed from hand to hand, volumes greasy at the corners as if they had been buttered by a hundred secret thumbs. Penny papers had not yet grown into their later trade of selling sixteen pages of murder and aristocratic perfidy for a copper; but Miss Minifer’s circulating library (tacked, as it were, onto a school and a small brandy-ball and millinery business) had furnished Fanny with plenty of heroines and heroes. Arthur walked into her imagination as the embodied Prince of all those battered books.

And Pen, who was, as we know, particular in linen and haberdashery—as a chef is particular with his knives—had appeared to her in all the splendour of fine shirt-front, glittering studs, cambric handkerchief, white gloves, and boots whose polish looked almost edible, like black currant jelly. The Prince had taken possession of the handmaid. His voice, his blue eyes, that look half love, half pity (the most dangerous seasoning in a gentleman’s pantry), the manly smile, the frank laugh—all rang in her head as plainly as the fiddles of Vauxhall. She could still feel his arm about her, and see him, grand as a steward, filling the champagne-glass—bubbles rising like little bright promises.

Then she thought of the girls who sneered at her—Emma Baker, mighty because she was engaged to a cheesemonger in a white apron; Betsy Rodgers, who made a whole ceremony of an attorney’s clerk that carried a bag. And thus, when, about two in the afternoon, after the Bolton family had finished dinner (and Mr. Bolton—porter of the Inn and occasional assistant to Messrs. Tressler, undertakers of the Strand—being out in the country with the Countess of Estrich’s hearse), a gentleman in a white hat and white trousers appeared under the archway and halted at the porter’s wicket, Fanny was not surprised. She was only delighted—only happy—blushing as if the very air had turned hot.

She knew it could be no other than He.

“Mamma, mamma!” she cried up to the upper room, and flew to the wicket, shoving the children aside with the careless authority of a girl whose whole world has suddenly narrowed to one pair of boots.

How she blushed as she gave him her hand! How affably he lifted his white hat as he stepped in, while the children stared up as if a royal carriage had driven into the court. He asked Mrs. Bolton whether she had slept well after the fatigues of the night, hoped she had no headache, and said that as he was passing he could not go by without asking after his little partner.

Mrs. Bolton, at first, was shy—suspicious, too, as a mother ought to be when a handsome young gentleman comes calling where there is no silver on the sideboard. But Pen’s good-humour was like a kettle kept perpetually on the boil. He could not imagine himself unwelcome. He looked about for a chair; but one bore a dish-cover, another a workbox, and every place that might have received him seemed already occupied by domestic industry. So he took a child’s chair and perched himself upon that puny throne, his knees up like a stork’s.

The children giggled. Fanny laughed louder than all, not because it was so witty, but because it was so magnificent—His Royal Highness on a nursery chair! Many a time afterwards she would look at that chair as at a relic. Haven’t we all some bit of furniture—an old seat, a corner by the hearth—where memory sets dear figures down again, and we hear voices that perhaps will never speak to us more?

Pen sat and talked away to Mrs. Bolton with volubility, as if words were coins he was scattering to make his path pleasant. He asked about the undertaking business—how many mutes had gone down with Lady Estrich; about the Inn and who lived there; about Mr. Campion’s cab and horse (he had met Mr. Campion in “society,” he said, with the air of a man who can season any conversation with that expensive word). He thought he should like shares in the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum. Were there chambers to let? It was better than the Temple; he should like to come and live in Shepherd’s Inn.

“As for Captain Strong—and—Colonel Altamont—was that his name?” Pen said, as though he merely happened to remember them while stirring the pot. He was deeply interested in those gentlemen too. The Captain was an old friend at home. He had dined with him in chambers here, before the Colonel came to live with him. What sort of man was the Colonel? Wasn’t he stout, with a quantity of jewellery—rings enough to stock a pawnbroker’s tray—and a wig and large black whiskers—very black?

Here Pen grew immensely waggish, and, to show his powers of culinary description, insisted the whiskers were not merely black but blue-black—indeed a rich greenish-purple—like something overcooked and varnished. The ladies shrieked with delight, that hysteric giggle which is laughter whipped too long.

“That was the man,” said Mrs. Bolton eagerly. “You’ve met him too, at Sir Fr—— in society.”

“Oh, we know,” said the ladies—meaning they knew, as lodges do, everything that enters or leaves an archway. “Sir F—— is Sir F. Clavering; he’s often here—two or three times a week with the Captain. My little boy has been out for bill-stamps for him. O Lor! I beg pardon—I shouldn’t have mentioned no secrets,” Mrs. Bolton added, suddenly remembering that secrecy is a virtue when one has already committed the sin. But Pen had talked her, by this time, into that broad good-nature which makes people feel safe while they are being pumped.

“But we know you to be a gentleman, Mr. Pendennis,” she went on, “for I’m sure you’ve shown you can beayve as such. Hasn’t Mr. Pendennis, Fanny?”

Fanny loved her mother for that. She lifted her dark eyes to the low ceiling—where the smoke and cooking smells of many dinners seemed to cling like old stories—and said, with a voice full of feeling, “Oh, that he has, I’m sure, Ma.”

Pen, at the mention of bill-stamps, pricked up his curiosity, and began, with considerable adroitness, to inquire about the transactions in Captain Strong’s chambers. When Altamont came and joined the Chevalier, was he too sending out for stamps? Who came to see them? Did many people go up and down? And so forth—questions shaped like polite small-talk, but meant to fish out Sir Francis Clavering’s doings for reasons of Pen’s own. Mrs. Bolton, who had neither the instincts of a diplomat nor the learning of a food-historian, answered artlessly to the very limit of her knowledge—which, like the contents of a small larder, was soon exhausted.

Pen found himself at a loss for fresh topics; but luckily he remembered his grand privilege as a man of the Press—like a cook who, when conversation flags, produces a pudding. He asked the ladies whether they would like any orders for the play.

The theatre was their delight, as it almost always is with those who live near its smell and glitter, and count its names as other people count the saints. When Bolton was away “professionally” (and it appeared the porter of Shepherd’s Inn, of late, had taken a serious turn—drank a good deal, and made himself otherwise unpleasant at home), Mrs. Bolton and Fanny would like of all things to slip out to a theatre, leaving little Barney to keep the lodge. Mr. Pendennis’s offer—most generous, most genteel—was received as if he had promised them a share in paradise.

Fanny clapped her hands; her face shone; she looked and nodded and laughed at her mamma, who nodded and laughed in return. Mrs. Bolton was not, in her own opinion, too old for admiration; and very likely Pen, with that easy art which costs men nothing and women much, had dropped compliments into his talk as lightly as pepper. She had begun by mistrusting him. She was now his partisan, and almost as enthusiastic as her daughter. When two women set to liking a man, they warm one another at the same fire, and each pushes the other closer to the blaze.

Only one small cloud crossed the sunshine: one of the younger children, hearing “Astley’s” named, came forward and declared she would like very much to go too; on which Fanny said, rather sharply, “Don’t bother!” and mamma followed with, “Git-long, Betsy-Jane, do now, and play in the court.”

So Betsy-Jane and Ameliar-Ann, in clean pinafores, were sent away to disport on the smooth gravel round about the statue of Shepherd the Great, and no doubt contributed their small shrieks and patter to the Inn’s soundscape—the innocent, living music of a place not yet entirely conquered by the iron roar and commercial clamour that, in other streets, drowns all tenderness out of human ears.

And there, while those small feet went tap-tap over the stones, and Pen’s voice went on, rich and civil, within the lodge, an old gentleman passed under the archway from the Inn-square and came and looked in at the door.

He made a blank, rueful face when he saw Mr. Arthur perched like Macheath upon his child’s chair, in easy discourse with Mrs. Bolton and her daughter.

“What! Mr. Bows? How d’you do, Bows?” cried Pen, in a cheery, loud voice. “I was coming to see you, and was asking your address of these ladies.”

“You were coming to see me, were you, sir?” Mr. Bows said; and he came in with a sad face and shook hands with Arthur.

“Plague on that old man!” somebody thought in the room; and perhaps somebody else besides her.
