Calder’s furlough began with the Nile and a taste in his mouth like metal—sun-baked, inevitable. The steamer slid southward of Wadi Halfa toward Assouan, and the river’s broad, patient body took all the glare the sky could pour into it, swallowing light and giving it back in hard flashes. Along the banks the land showed a thin seam of green—palms, a few plots clawed out of sand—then immediately the merciless ochre rose again in blunt pyramids of rock. Even the wind, when it came, arrived dry and scouring, worrying at canvas and rope as if it resented softness.

He leaned over the upper rail, listening. The steamer’s engines beat a steady, manufactured pulse, but beneath that there were older sounds: the water slapping the hull with a thick, intimate smack; the creak of timbers; a distant cry from the shore that rose and fell in a pattern he could not parse. All of it made a kind of music—a soundscape that ought to have steadied a man—if only it were not constantly pierced by the harsher noises of neglect: a cough left unattended, a shouted joke thrown like a stone, laughter too loud for the narrow strip of living earth that clung to the river’s edge.

Lashed alongside rode a barge crowded with native passengers. On its lower deck stood an angareb—matting stretched tight across a four-legged frame—bearing a human figure laid out straight as a board and shrouded in a black veil. Two Arabs who had carried it aboard at Korosko sat in the stern, chattering and laughing as if the bundle on the bedstead were no more than spare baggage.

It might have been a corpse. It might have been a woman. The veil lay so close that it mapped the nose and the hollows of the eyes and mouth; it did not tell him whether a moustache shadowed the lip, whether a beard roughened the chin. The body did not shift. Not a toe flexed beneath the cloth. The veil did not lift with breath.

The sun, slanting toward afternoon, crept across the deck inch by inch like a slow blade. The people nearest the angareb edged away into whatever shadow the upper deck granted, but no one moved the bedstead itself. The two in the stern did not so much as glance at their charge.

Calder watched the light climb the shrouded figure from the feet upward, turning the black cloth into a punishing heat-trap. When at last it burned full on the face beneath the veil and still the body did not stir, something in him tightened—the soldier’s familiarity with suffering, and the civilized man’s intolerance of pointless cruelty.

He shouted down at the men in the stern.

“Move the angareb into the shade,” he called. “At once!”

They rose with a slowness that was almost defiance, yet his voice carried authority. They dragged the bedstead back under the upper deck’s shelter.

“Is it a man or a woman?” Calder asked.

“A man,” one answered. “We take him to the hospital at Assouan. He will not live. He fell from a palm-tree three weeks ago.”

“You give him nothing? No water?”

“He is too ill.”

Too ill. The phrase had the neatness of a proverb: an answer that closed the mouth of argument, a fatalism passed from tongue to tongue until it hardened into law. Calder had heard it before, in different dialects, under different suns. It always meant the same thing: what happens will happen; therefore do nothing. He thought of what would have cured a bruise in a week—washing, clean cloth, a little cooling drink with salt, a compress, a hand that touched a wound as if life mattered. Instead, this man had been left lying as he lay now, face to flies, flesh to heat, thirst unreckoned. A bruise would have become a sore; a sore would have rotted. Then, when it was too late for simple remedies, the Mudir at Korosko—stirred perhaps by a scrap of conscience, perhaps by the fear of paperwork—had shipped him downstream like freight.

The story was common, but the stillness of the veiled man would not leave Calder’s mind. It held him with a kind of dreadful fascination. When evening came and a strong wind sprang up against the current, raising chop and white-edged waves, Calder felt a grim comfort: at least the air would cool that hidden face; at least the flies would be driven off.

At dinner, the saloon smelled of boiled meat and onions softened into their own sweetness, of damp tablecloth, of coffee that had been kept too long on the heat. The talk was the usual travel-talk—Cairo, temples, permits, the petty tyranny of officials. Calder ate without appetite, listening less to the clink of cutlery than to the different tempo of the river outside: the wind worrying at the deck fittings, the water’s slap sharpening as the steamer took the waves.

His neighbour spoke with a German accent. He was young, spectacled, tidy in his movements, as if he handled words the way an apothecary handled powders—careful not to waste.

On impulse Calder asked, “You are not a doctor, by any chance?”

“Not a doctor,” the man replied, smiling good-naturedly. “A student of medicine at Bonn. I came from Cairo to see the Second Cataract, but they would not let me farther than Wadi Halfa.”

“Then I shall trespass on your holiday,” Calder said at once, “and claim your assistance.”

“For yourself? With pleasure—though I should not have guessed you were ill.”

“Nor am I. It is an Arab below.”

“The man on the bedstead?” The student’s eyebrows rose behind the eyeglasses.

“Yes. I warn you—three weeks hurt, and no care. This is not a country where wounds are kindly treated. The sight will not be pretty.”

The student shrugged, accepting experience as a kind of currency. “All experience is good,” he said; and they left the table.

On deck the wind had freshened. The steamer and barge pitched; water broke aboard in sudden sheets. The spray, caught by lamplight, looked like driven snow. It struck the planks with a sound like a whip’s lash—sharp, repetitive, insistent. Calder thought how noise like that could be torture when a man was too weak to turn his head.

They leaned over the rail toward the barge’s lower deck. Night had come down thick and dark. One lamp swung from the upper deck and threw a small wavering circle of light—uncertain gold surrounded by blackness. Beyond it there was nothing to see but, at the bows, the white flare of spray.

“He was below there,” the German student said, peering.

“He has been moved,” the student added.

“No doubt,” Calder answered. “There is no one in the bows.”

He stared a while longer into the darkness, listening for any human sign—a groan, a cough, the thin thread of breath. The only answer was the wind and the slap of water.

“I believe the angareb is there,” he said at last. “I believe it is.”

They went down to the lower deck and to the side, and from there Calder could make it certain. The angareb stood exactly where he had ordered it moved that morning—yet now it was at the very front, in the wash of water, where each plunge of the barge sent a cold flood across the planks. The shrouded figure lay upon it as immobile as before, as if even discomfort had become too much effort to register. Spray broke continually upon the veiled face.

“I thought it would be so,” Calder said, and his voice came out hard.

He fetched a lantern. Its flame made a small warm world in his hand—oil and glass and a steady glow that smelled faintly of soot. With the German student he climbed across the bulwarks onto the barge.

He summoned the two Arabs.

“Move the angareb from the bows,” he ordered.

They did as they were told, dragging the bedstead back. The matting scraped against wet boards.

“Now take the covering off,” Calder said. “My friend—he is a doctor—must see the wound.”

They hesitated. One drew himself up with an insolent tilt of the chin. “There are doctors at Assouan,” he said. “We take him there.”

Calder lifted the lantern and, without waiting, reached down and drew the veil away himself.

The face beneath was not dead. It was alive in the most stubborn, reduced way: skin drawn tight, lips cracked, eyes sunk but watchful. A smell rose—sweat turned sour, old blood, the sweet-sick rot of infection that clung to cloth and flesh. It was the smell of a body that had been denied the ordinary rituals by which humans keep each other human: washing, changing, tending.

“Now, if you please,” Calder said to the student.

The German knelt and examined the thigh. Calder held the lantern high, the light brightening the horror of it. The wound crawled—an obscene movement where life’s smallest scavengers prospered. The student’s mouth tightened. He worked quickly, with the grim composure of someone who has studied illness in books and now meets its true texture.

When he had seen enough, he was glad to cover it again.

“I can do nothing,” he said quietly. “In a hospital, perhaps—with baths and dressings—relief at least. More? I do not know. Here…I could not even begin.”

He glanced up at Calder. “Do these men understand English?”

“No,” Calder answered.

“Then I will tell you something,” the student said, lowering his voice. “He did not get this by falling from a palm tree. That is a lie. This was done by a spear-blade—or some weapon like it.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

Calder bent down sharply toward the wounded man’s face. Until now the stillness had seemed like absence; now Calder saw it was restraint. The man had been listening. His eyes had followed the words as if the words were something tangible moving through air.

“You understand English?” Calder asked.

The Arab could not answer. His lips attempted movement and failed. Yet in his eyes a flicker of comprehension lit and held.

Calder’s pulse quickened. He had been waiting for news—any scrap, any thread that could lead back to the thing Durrance asked for in every letter: Harry Feversham. Calder dropped to his knee beside the man’s head and brought the lantern closer, so the light steadied on the eyes.

“Where do you come from?” he asked slowly.

The lips strained again. No whisper came. But the eyes pleaded to be understood, as if he were trapped behind a wall of glass.

Calder began to name places, each one pronounced with care, like tasting unfamiliar syllables and trying not to distort them. The names felt clumsy in his mouth, foreign sounds that carried whole lives and histories he did not own.

“From Dongola?”

No response.

“From Metemneh? From Berber? From Omdurman?”

At “Omdurman” the man’s eyelids fell in assent, closing as if the word itself hurt.

Calder leaned nearer. “You were wounded there?”

The eyes denied it.

“At Berber?” Calder tried.

The eyes answered yes.

Calder sat back on his heel, mind moving quickly through what he knew and what he feared. The river’s noise went on around them—spray lashing, timbers creaking—an indifferent percussion to a human attempt at speech.

“You were in prison at Omdurman and escaped?” Calder asked.

No.

“Yet you were wounded,” Calder said, and the contradiction sharpened.

He lowered his voice, bringing it close to the man’s ear as if secrecy could bridge the gap that weakness had opened.

“You were helping some one to escape?” he asked.

Yes—an unmistakable assent in the eyes.

“Who?” Calder whispered. “El Kaimakam Trench?”

No.

He tried other names—white captives, officers and officials whose fates were spoken of in mess-rooms and letters home, names that carried rank and empire. Each time the eyes said no.

“It was Effendi Feversham, then?” Calder said at last.

The eyes answered yes as plainly as speech.

A chill ran through Calder that had nothing to do with the river spray. Effendi—Harry Feversham, called by the title the Soudanese used, a name altered by other mouths until it became both recognition and disguise. Calder felt, for an instant, the strange tyranny of language: how a man’s identity could be carried across deserts in a word that was not quite his own, how meanings shifted as they passed from tongue to tongue, and how, in that shift, truths could hide.

“I too am pledged to help Effendi Feversham,” Calder said, urgency thick in his throat. He waited, willing the man to answer with something more—a name, a place, a sign, any detail that could be sent to Durrance, any direction by which help might be turned into action.

But the wounded Arab could not speak. Not even his own name could he give. His companions would not supply it. Whatever they knew or guessed, they kept themselves clear of danger with the stubbornness of men who have learned that curiosity is punished. They clung to their palm-tree story as if it were a charm against responsibility.

“Kinsmen at Korosko,” one repeated dully when Calder questioned him, “asked us to take charge of him. He is a stranger. We go to Assouan.”

That was all Calder could wrest from them.

So he stood beside the angareb, lantern in hand, looking down at a man whose eyes held locked information as a sealed jar holds spice—fragrance trapped, useless to anyone who cannot break the lid. The Arab’s eagerness to speak was there, vivid and helpless, and the extremity of his weakness laid a finger on his mouth more effectively than any vow.

All Calder could do was to see him delivered, alive if possible, to the hospital at Assouan.

At Assouan the air smelled different—less of open sand, more of human settlement: dung and cooking smoke, the sourness of crowded rooms, water warmed in basins. The hospital had its own odours—carbolic and sweat, damp cloth, the faint sweetness of decay fought back by soap and disinfectant. Those scents, at least, belonged to effort rather than abandonment.

Calder spoke to the doctors. They examined the man and exchanged looks that were professional and guarded.

“Will he recover?” Calder asked.

They shook their heads, not committing themselves, as if certainty would be a cruelty.

“There is a chance,” one admitted. “A very slight chance. And if he lives, it will be slow.”

Calder left him there, in clean sheets and under human eyes, and continued his journey toward Cairo and then Europe. The river widened behind him, carrying away the barge, the wind, the lash of spray, and that silent face under the lantern-light.

An opportunity had slipped through his fingers—not for lack of will, but because the one man who could have spoken was trapped in the untranslatable prison of his own broken body. The news Durrance hungered for was present and yet inaccessible, as if written in a script Calder could see but not read.

For the Arab on the angareb—wounded, voiceless, and carried downstream like an unwanted burden—was Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe; and his presence between Korosko and Assouan meant that Harry Feversham’s careful plan to rescue Colonel Trench had failed.
