Thirteen years had passed, and in the same month of June, Harry Feversham’s health was toasted once more—though this time in quieter surroundings and among fewer guests. The gathering took place in a high-rise flat atop a grim, fortress-like building that loomed over Westminster like a silent sentinel. To a stranger crossing St. James’s Park at night, the tiered glow of lit windows might have seemed less like a city and more like a mountain range pierced by firelight, where unseen workers toiled deep within.

On the tenth floor of this structure, Harry had settled for his annual leave from his regiment in India. It was here, in the warm, dimly furnished dining room of his flat, that the evening’s small celebration unfolded. The air was thick with smoke and quiet conversation, the chill of early summer making the fire crackle warmly in the hearth. A bay window, its blinds drawn back, framed a sweeping view of London below—its streets aglow, its silence broken only by distant hums and echoes.

Four men sat around the table. Harry Feversham had changed little since his youth, save for a neatly trimmed fair moustache that contrasted sharply with his dark hair. He stood now as a man of average height, lean and strong, built like an athlete, yet his face remained unchanged—the same features that had once been studied so closely by Lieutenant Sutch. Two of his companions were fellow officers on leave: Captain Trench, a compact man with a receding hairline and sharp, intelligent eyes that darted quickly from one thought to another; and Lieutenant Willoughby, whose appearance suggested dullness—a round forehead, a broad nose, and eyes that seemed lost in distant thoughts. He spoke rarely, and when he did, it was often about something long forgotten, twisting his upward-curled moustache as if wrestling with invisible ideas. He wasn’t clever, but stubborn beyond reason—so stubborn that even his own mistakes could not be acknowledged. One might dismiss him at first glance, but second thoughts would reveal a mind that clung fiercely to its few convictions.

The fourth man was Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surrey Regiment and Feversham’s closest friend. He had come in response to a telegram—arriving just in time for dinner.

It was June 1882, and across England, civilians fretted over events unfolding in Egypt. Soldiers, meanwhile, felt a stirring excitement. Arabi Pasha, defying warnings, had fortified Alexandria. And farther south, along the Nile, a new storm gathered. A year earlier, a young Dongolawi named Mohammed Ahmed had begun preaching through the villages, declaring himself a prophet—the coming savior of the people. His message caught fire among those oppressed under Turkish rule. By 1882, he had proclaimed himself the Mahdi and won his first victories against Ottoman forces.

“There will be trouble,” said Trench, and the words became the theme of the evening’s talk.

But Harry Feversham broke the rhythm with a different announcement. “I’m glad you all could join me tonight,” he began, addressing Durrance. “I sent a wire to Castleton too—he’s one of ours—but he’s dining with someone important in the War Office and then heading to Scotland. Couldn’t make it. But I’ve got news.”

The others leaned forward, still preoccupied with war.

“I only arrived in London this morning from Dublin,” Harry continued, a faint blush coloring his cheeks. “Been there for several weeks.”

Durrance looked up slowly. “Yes?” he asked, steady-eyed.

“I’ve come back engaged to be married.”

Durrance raised his glass. “Well, here’s luck to you, Harry,” he said simply. That was all. No flourish, no sentiment—yet Feversham heard everything. Their friendship was unspoken, profound. It didn’t need words. It was a bond forged in shared duty and mutual respect, solid as steel, never worn down by time or circumstance. Neither man ever spoke of it directly, nor would they ever mention the sacrifices such loyalty demanded. They understood: if needed, each would give without hesitation—and never ask for thanks.

“Thank you, Jack,” Feversham said. “I’m grateful. You introduced me to Ethne—I’ll never forget that.”

Durrance set his glass down gently. Silence followed. He stared at the tablecloth, hands resting lightly on the edge. Then, quietly: “Yes. I did you a good turn.”

He hesitated, as if about to say more, but Captain Trench cut in with his brisk, practical tone.

“Will this change anything?” he asked.

Feversham paused, drawing his cigar back into his mouth. “You mean—will I leave the service?”

“I don’t know,” Feversham replied slowly. And Durrance rose abruptly, moving to the window, his back turned.

Feversham took it as a rebuke and addressed the other man’s back. “I don’t know,” he repeated. “It needs thinking. There’s my father, my career… and then there’s her father—Dermod Eustace.”

“He wants you to quit your commission?” Willoughby asked.

“Probably,” Trench said with a smirk. “Irishmen tend to distrust authority. But do you have to agree with him, Feversham?”

“It’s not just that.” Feversham kept his gaze fixed on Durrance’s silhouette. “Dermod’s old. His lands are failing. There are other things. You know, Jack?”

Durrance answered without turning. “Yes, I know.” Then, almost automatically: “If you want whiskey, rap twice on the floor. The servants understand.”

“Precisely,” Feversham said.

He went on, choosing his words carefully, still watching his friend through the others’ shoulders. “And then there’s Ethne herself. Dermod gave her that name well—she *is* of her land. She belongs to Donegal—the smell of peat, the rivers, the hills. I don’t think she’d ever be happy far from it. Not in India. Not anywhere beyond reach of home. That has to count.”

He waited. No reply came.

Durrance wasn’t reproaching him. He was simply staring out at the city—Pall Mall’s glare climbing into the sky, the chain of lights rising northward, the low rumble of traffic like a distant thunder. Below, St. James’s Park lay dark and still, a black pool amid the glitter. Durrance longed to step outside, to vanish into that quiet space. But he couldn’t. So he stayed, leaning his forehead against the glass, hoping Feversham would keep talking.

Because Durrance knew what was coming—the kind of sacrifice that must never be spoken aloud.

Feversham did speak again. And though Durrance didn’t listen, Captain Trench listened closely. What Harry said was not evasion—it was careful reflection. In the end, Trench nodded.

“Well, I drink to you, Feversham,” he said, raising his glass. “With all due respect.”

“Me too, old man,” Willoughby added, following suit.

They drank. As their empty glasses clinked together, a knock sounded at the door.

The three guests glanced up. Durrance turned from the window. Feversham said, “Come in.”

His servant entered, handing him a telegram.

Feversham tore open the envelope casually, read the message, and froze. His face grew solemn. He sat motionless, eyes fixed on the pink slip of paper. Time stretched. The room fell utterly silent.

The others avoided his gaze. Durrance returned to the window. Willoughby twisted his moustache, staring at the ceiling. Trench turned his chair toward the fire, studying the flames.

It was as if misfortune had followed joy to the doorstep.

“There is no answer,” Harry said, and lapsed into silence.

Once, he lifted his head and looked at Trench, as if to speak—but thought better of it. He returned to the telegram, lost in thought.

Then, from outside, came the sudden sound of drums and fifes—sharp, clear, echoing through the open window. The tattoo from Wellington Barracks rang out, cutting through the hush. It swelled, then faded as the band marched away, growing soft and distant.

Feversham didn’t move. But his expression shifted—now he was listening, deeply, as if the music carried meaning.

Years later, each of the three men would recall that moment—the firelit room, the open window framing London’s lights, Harry seated with the telegram before him, the music fading into silence. The image would remain vivid, though its meaning remained hidden.

Then, just as the tattoo ended, Feversham rose suddenly. He crumpled the telegram, tossed it into the fire, and leaned back against the chimney piece.

“I don’t know,” he said again—quietly, firmly—as if dismissing the message entirely, returning to the weight of his earlier decision.

The silence broke.

The fire seized the paper. It writhed, unrolled briefly—revealing a glimpse of words in the flame—then curled and blackened into ash.

Captain Trench watched it burn.

“You’re going back to Dublin?” Durrance asked, stepping back into the room.

“To Dublin? No. I’m going to Donegal in three weeks. There’s a dance. You should come.”

“I’m not sure I can,” Durrance replied. “There’s a chance—if things go south in the East—I might be called to the staff.”

The conversation drifted back to war and peace, lingering until the Westminster clock struck eleven.

Trench stood. Willoughby and Durrance followed.

“I’ll see you tomorrow,” Durrance said.

“As usual,” Harry replied.

The three left, walking across the park. At Pall Mall, they parted. Durrance headed up St. James’s Street. Trench and Willoughby crossed into St. James’s Square.

There, Trench slipped his arm through Willoughby’s—an unusual gesture for such a reserved man.

“You know Castleton’s address?” he asked.

“Albemarle Street,” Willoughby said. “Number 47.”

“He leaves Euston at twelve. It’s ten past eleven now. Are you curious, Willoughby? I confess—I am. I’m methodical, and when a man gets a telegram telling him to tell Trench something—and tells him nothing—I become a philosopher of curiosity. Castleton is the only other officer of our regiment in London. Likely, the telegram came from him. And he was dining with someone important in the War Office. If we take a cab to Albemarle Street, we might catch him just as he steps out.”

Willoughby, though baffled, agreed heartily. “I think it would be prudent,” he said, flagging down a passing hansom.

A moment later, the two men were riding toward Albemarle Street—driven by the quiet urgency of unanswered questions.
