The newspapers soon carried the news that Mrs. Palmer had safely delivered a son and heir, a piece of intelligence that brought immense satisfaction to her circle of acquaintances. This event, so pivotal to Mrs. Jennings's joy, temporarily reshaped her schedule and, by extension, the lives of her younger friends. Determined to be with Charlotte, Mrs. Jennings visited her household every morning immediately after dressing, remaining until late evening. Consequently, the Dashwood sisters, at the specific request of the Middletons, spent their entire days in Conduit Street. Though they would have preferred to remain at Mrs. Jennings's home for at least the mornings, they could not argue against the united wishes of everyone involved. Thus, their time was surrendered to Lady Middleton and the two Miss Steeles, whose company they found far less valued than it was ostensibly sought.

They possessed too much sense to be truly desirable companions for Lady Middleton, who, despite her outward politeness, held no genuine affection for them. Unable to believe anyone could be good-natured if they refused to flatter her or her children, and suspecting that their love for reading made them satirical—a charge she leveled without fully understanding satire herself—she viewed them with suspicion. Their presence acted as a restraint on both Lady Middleton and Lucy Steele. It curbed the idleness of the former and the business of the latter; Lady Middleton felt ashamed to do nothing in their company, while Lucy feared that the flattery she usually administered would be despised. Miss Steele was the least disturbed by their arrival, and indeed, she could have been fully reconciled to it had either sister shared the full, minute details of the affair between Marianne and Mr. Willoughby. A simple account of that tragedy would have made her feel amply rewarded for sacrificing her prime spot by the fire. Yet, this conciliation was denied. Miss Steele's occasional expressions of pity to Elinor or reflections on the fickleness of beaux before Marianne elicited only indifference or disgust. Even a lighter jest about the doctor would have secured her friendship, but the sisters were too unwilling to oblige; if Sir John dined away, Miss Steele might spend a whole day without hearing a single word of raillery on the subject from them.

Mrs. Jennings, however, remained blissfully unaware of these underlying jealousies and discontents. She delighted in the girls' reunion, often congratulating them nightly on escaping the company of a "stupid old woman" for so long. Whether joining them at Sir John's or her own house, she arrived in high spirits, attributing Charlotte's success to her own care and providing such exhaustive details of the baby's condition that only Miss Steele possessed the curiosity to listen. One matter, however, did disturb her: Mr. Palmer's stubborn adherence to the unfatherly notion that all infants are identical. Despite the striking resemblance of the baby to every relative on both sides, he refused to acknowledge it, insisting it was no more special than any other child of its age, and certainly not the finest in the world.

Meanwhile, a misfortune befell Mrs. John Dashwood. While her two sisters were visiting her in Harley Street, another acquaintance dropped in. This lady, allowing her imagination to outrun truth and probability, heard the name of the Miss Dashwoods and assumed they were staying in Harley Street. Within a day or two, she sent invitations to a small musical party for the sisters, their brother, and sister. Mrs. John Dashwood was thus forced to submit to the inconvenience of sending her carriage for them and the greater unpleasantness of appearing to treat them with attention, fearing they might expect further outings. Though she retained the power to disappoint them, she felt injured by the expectation of anything less than perfection.

Marianne had grown so accustomed to daily outings that it became a matter of indifference to her whether she went or not. She prepared mechanically for every evening's engagement, expecting no amusement and often not knowing her destination until the last moment. She had also become indifferent to her dress and appearance, neglecting her toilette entirely until Miss Steele took over in the first five minutes of their meeting. Miss Steele observed everything with minute scrutiny and general curiosity, asking about the price of every item in Marianne's wardrobe, guessing the total number of her gowns with better judgment than Marianne herself, and hoping to discover her weekly washing costs and annual spending before they parted. These impertinent interrogations were invariably capped with a compliment, intended as a douceur but regarded by Marianne as the height of impertinence. After being examined on the value of her gown, the color of her shoes, and the arrangement of her hair, she was almost certain to be told that, on Miss Steele's word, she looked "vastly smart" and destined to make many conquests.

With such encouragement, Marianne was dismissed to her brother's carriage, which was ready to depart five minutes after stopping—a punctuality that annoyed Mrs. John Dashwood, who had preceded them to the hostess's house, hoping for a delay to inconvenience either herself or her coachman. The evening's events were unremarkable. The party comprised many with genuine taste for music and many without, while the performers, as usual, considered themselves the finest in England.

Elinor, neither musical nor pretending to be so, freely turned her eyes away from the grand pianoforte to fix them on any other object in the room. In one such glance, she spotted among a group of young men the very gentleman who had lectured them on toothpick-cases at Gray's. Soon after, she saw him looking at her and speaking familiarly to her brother. Just as she decided to inquire his name, Mr. Dashwood approached and introduced him as Mr. Robert Ferrars.

He addressed her with easy civility, bowing in a manner that assured her he was exactly the coxcomb Lucy had described. Had Elinor's regard for Edward depended less on his own merit and more on that of his nearest relations, his brother's bow would have delivered the final blow to what her mother and sister had begun. Yet, as she marveled at the contrast between the two young men, the emptiness and conceit of one did not diminish her charity for the modesty and worth of the other. Robert explained the difference himself during a quarter-hour conversation. Speaking of his brother, he lamented the *gaucherie* he believed kept Edward from proper society, candidly attributing it less to natural deficiency than to the misfortune of a private education. He claimed that, while perhaps lacking any material superiority by nature, his attendance at a public school had made him as fit for the world as any other man.

"Upon my soul," he added, "I believe it is nothing more; and so I often tell my mother, when she is grieving about it. 'My dear Madam,' I always say, 'you must make yourself easy. The evil is now irremediable, and it has been entirely your own doing. Why would you be persuaded by my uncle, Sir Robert, against your own judgment, to place Edward under private tuition at the most critical time of his life? If you had only sent him to Westminster as well as myself, instead of sending him to Mr. Pratt's, all this would have been prevented.' This is how I view the matter, and my mother is perfectly convinced of her error."

Elinor did not oppose his opinion, for while she generally valued the advantages of a public school, she could not think of Edward's residence in Mr. Pratt's family with any satisfaction.

"You reside in Devonshire, I think," he observed next, "in a cottage near Dawlish."

Elinor corrected him regarding the location, and it seemed surprising to him that anyone could live in Devonshire without residing near Dawlish. Nevertheless, he bestowed hearty approval on their type of house.

"For my own part," he said, "I am excessively fond of a cottage; there is always so much comfort, so much elegance about them. I protest, if I had any money to spare, I should buy a little land and build one myself, within a short distance of London, where I might drive down at any time, collect a few friends, and be happy. I advise everybody who is going to build to build a cottage. My friend Lord Courtland came to me the other day to ask my advice and laid before me three different plans of Bonomi's. I was to decide on the best. 'My dear Courtland,' I immediately said, throwing them all into the fire, 'do not adopt either of them, but by all means build a cottage.' And that, I fancy, will be the end of it."

"Some people imagine there can be no accommodations, no space in a cottage," he continued. "But this is a mistake. I was last month at my friend Elliott's, near Dartford. Lady Elliott wished to give a dance. 'But how can it be done?' she asked. 'There is not a room in this cottage that will hold ten couples, and where can the supper be?' I immediately saw there could be no difficulty. 'My dear Lady Elliott,' I said, 'do not be uneasy. The dining parlour will admit eighteen couples with ease; card-tables may be placed in the drawing-room; the library may be open for tea; and let the supper be set out in the saloon.' Lady Elliott was delighted. We measured the dining-room, and it held exactly eighteen couples. So, if people know how to set about it, every comfort may be enjoyed in a cottage as well as in the most spacious dwelling."

Elinor agreed to all this, feeling he did not deserve the compliment of rational opposition.

Since John Dashwood derived no more pleasure from music than his eldest sister, his mind was free to wander elsewhere. A thought struck him during the evening, which he communicated to his wife for her approbation upon their return home. The consideration of Mrs. Dennison's mistake in supposing his sisters were her guests suggested the propriety of inviting them to become actual guests while Mrs. Jennings was away. The expense would be negligible, the inconvenience minimal, and it was an attention his conscience deemed requisite to clear his promise to his father. Fanny was startled by the proposal.

"I do not see how it can be done," she said, "without affronting Lady Middleton, for they spend every day with her. Otherwise, I should be exceedingly glad to do it. You know I am always ready to pay them any attention in my power, as my taking them out this evening shows. But they are Lady Middleton's visitors. How can I ask them away from her?"

Her husband, with great humility, failed to see the force of her objection. "They had already spent a week in this manner in Conduit Street, and Lady Middleton could not be displeased at their giving the same number of days to such near relations."

Fanny paused, then spoke with fresh vigor. "My love, I would ask them with all my heart, if it was in my power. But I had just settled within myself to ask the Miss Steeles to spend a few days with us. They are very well-behaved, good kind of girls; and I think the attention is due to them, as their uncle did so very well by Edward. We can ask your sisters some other year, you know; but the Miss Steeles may not be in town any more. I am sure you will like them; indeed, you *do* like them, you know, very much already, and so does my mother; and they are such favourites with Harry!"

Mr. Dashwood was convinced. He saw the necessity of inviting the Miss Steeles immediately, and his conscience was pacified by the resolution to invite his sisters another year, though he slyly suspected that another year might render the invitation needless by bringing Elinor to town as Colonel Brandon's wife and Marianne as their visitor.

Fanny, rejoicing in her escape and proud of the ready wit that had procured it, wrote the next morning to Lucy, requesting her company and her sister's for some days in Harley Street as soon as Lady Middleton could spare them. This was enough to make Lucy truly and reasonably happy. Mrs. Dashwood seemed actually working for her, cherishing all her hopes and promoting all her views. Such an opportunity to be with Edward and his family was the most material to her interests, and such an invitation the most gratifying to her feelings. It was an advantage that could not be too gratefully acknowledged nor too speedily utilized; consequently, the visit to Lady Middleton, which had previously lacked precise limits, was instantly discovered to have been always meant to end in two days' time.

When the note was shown to Elinor, within ten minutes of its arrival, it gave her, for the first time, some share in the expectations of Lucy. Such a mark of uncommon kindness, vouchsafed on so short an acquaintance, seemed to declare that the goodwill towards her arose from something more than mere malice against herself, and might be brought, by time and address, to achieve everything Lucy wished. Her flattery had already subdued the pride of Lady Middleton and made an entry into the close heart of Mrs. John Dashwood; these were effects that opened the probability of greater things.

The Miss Steeles moved to Harley Street, and all that reached Elinor of their influence there strengthened her expectation of the event. Sir John, who called on them more than once, brought home such accounts of the favor they were in as must be universally striking. Mrs. Dashwood had never been so pleased with any young women in her life as she was with them; she had given each of them a needle book made by some emigrant, called Lucy by her Christian name, and did not know whether she should ever be able to part with them.
