The rain seeped into the worn leather of George Warrington's boots as he walked through the deserted streets of London. His eyes scanned the buildings, searching for a glimpse of familiarity, but every windowpane reflected only the darkness of the night. The memory of his brother's letters lingered in his mind – the words of affection, the stories of life in America, the desperate plea for help. He had come too late. The letter bearing the news of his arrival must have been intercepted, and Harry's fate remained a mystery.

As he turned a corner, the gas lamps cast an eerie glow on the wet cobblestones. The sound of horse hooves echoed through the night, and George quickened his pace, his heart heavy with foreboding. He thought of Aunt Bernstein, the one person who might know something of Harry's whereabouts. Her kindness to his brother had been a beacon of hope in the darkness of their past. He recalled the words of her letter, the words that had set him on this perilous journey across the Atlantic.

George's thoughts were interrupted by the sight of a small, unassuming door. He pushed it open, and a faint scent of pipe smoke wafted out, carrying with it the promise of warmth and comfort. Inside, the fire crackled, casting flickering shadows on the walls. A figure hunched over a chair, puffing on a clay pipe, looked up as George entered. For a moment, they simply regarded each other, the only sound the gentle hiss of the fire.

"Can I help you?" the old man asked finally, his voice low and gravelly.

George hesitated, unsure how to explain his presence. "I'm looking for my brother," he said, the words barely above a whisper. "Harry Warrington."

The old man's expression changed, a mixture of sadness and concern etched on his face. "Ah, yes. I know of him." He leaned forward, his eyes locked on George's. "Come, sit down. We need to talk."
