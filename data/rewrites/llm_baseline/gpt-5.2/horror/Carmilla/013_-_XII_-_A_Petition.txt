“Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse,” I said, bowing as low as my stiff back would allow, “but I hope only for a few hours.”

“It may be only that—or it may be weeks,” she answered; and even through her careful, courtly softness there ran a vibration like a snapped string. Somewhere beyond the salon doors, the music still breathed—violins rising and folding back into themselves—yet in her voice I heard another sound entirely: the hush before a storm breaks over ripe fields. “It was very unlucky his speaking to me just now as he did. Do you now know me?”

I assured her, truthfully, that I did not.

“You shall know me,” she said; “but not at present. We are older and better friends than, perhaps, you suspect. I cannot yet declare myself. In three weeks I shall pass your beautiful schloss—about which I have been making enquiries—and I shall then look in upon you for an hour or two, and renew a friendship I never think of without a thousand pleasant recollections.”

She paused as if listening for some signal only she could hear: not the harpsichord’s glitter from the adjoining rooms, nor the shuffle of dancers’ shoes, but a smaller, more intimate noise—news arriving like a hand on a latch.

“This moment,” she continued, “a piece of news has reached me like a thunderbolt. I must set out now, and travel by a devious route—nearly a hundred miles—with all the dispatch I can possibly make. My perplexities multiply.”

She spoke with the measured clarity of a woman used to command and to conceal. And yet beneath that discipline, a fear moved—something old, as old as the stories peasants mutter when the wind changes and the hens go quiet before dusk.

“I am only deterred by the compulsory reserve I practise as to my name,” she said, “from making a very singular request of you. My poor child has not quite recovered her strength. Her horse fell with her at a hunt which she had ridden out to witness; her nerves have not yet recovered the shock; and our physician says that she must on no account exert herself for some time to come. We came here, in consequence, by very easy stages—hardly six leagues a day. I must now travel day and night, on a mission of life and death—a mission the critical and momentous nature of which I shall be able to explain to you when we meet, as I hope we shall, in a few weeks, without the necessity of any concealment.”

Then came her petition.

She asked it as if she bestowed rather than begged—a habit of bearing, not of intention; for in the very words she used there was nothing but apology and submission. Would I, she said, consent to take charge of her daughter during her absence?

All things considered, it was strange—audacious, almost. She disarmed me by naming, before I could, every objection that might be urged: the suddenness, the impropriety, the burden she laid on a stranger. She threw herself, she said, upon my honour—upon my chivalry—like a supplicant laying both hands on an altar.

And at that very instant—by a fatality that seems, looking back, to have had a hand in all that followed—my poor child came close to me and, in an undertone, besought me to invite the Countess’s young companion, Millarca, to visit us. She had been speaking with her; she liked her; if her mamma would allow it, she would like it extremely.

There are moments when a household, like a kitchen, is pushed into haste: when the fire runs too high, when the salt is thrown in without tasting, when one does not listen for the simmer but only hears the clatter. I had no time to think. The two ladies pressed me together—mother with her grave urgency, daughter with her silent, wistful beauty. The young lady’s face—refined, engaging, touched with the elegance and quick fire of high birth—overpowered my caution. I submitted. Too easily I undertook the care of the young lady whom her mother called Millarca.

The Countess beckoned her daughter. Millarca listened with grave attention while she was told, in general terms, how suddenly and peremptorily her mother had been summoned, and of the arrangement made for her under my care. The Countess added, with a kind of quiet insistence, that I was one of her earliest and most valued friends.

I said what was expected—those smooth speeches one makes when the room is bright, the air perfumed, and the heart is uneasy. Yet even as I spoke, the sense of my position settled in me like a wrong draught swallowed too fast.

The pale gentleman in black returned and, with elaborate ceremony, conducted the Countess from the room.

His manner—so punctilious, so deferential—persuaded me that the Countess was a lady of far greater importance than her modest title had led me to assume. A great tree may call itself a shrub, but the way lesser growth bends before it betrays the truth.

Her last charge to me was strange: no attempt was to be made to learn more about her than I might have already guessed, until her return. Our distinguished host, whose guest she was, knew her reasons.

“But here,” she said, “neither I nor my daughter could safely remain for more than a day. I removed my mask imprudently for a moment, about an hour ago, and too late I fancied you saw me. So I resolved to seek an opportunity of talking a little to you. Had I found that you had seen me, I would have thrown myself on your high sense of honour to keep my secret some weeks. As it is, I am satisfied you did not see me; but if you now suspect—or on reflection should suspect—who I am, I commit myself in like manner entirely to your honour. My daughter will observe the same secrecy, and I well know you will, from time to time, remind her, lest she should thoughtlessly disclose it.”

She whispered a few words to Millarca, kissed her hurriedly twice, and went away with the gentleman in black, vanishing into the crowd as if the bright company swallowed her like a mouth.

“In the next room,” said Millarca, “there is a window that looks upon the hall door. I should like to see the last of mamma, and to kiss my hand to her.”

We assented, of course, and accompanied her to the window.

Below us the world sounded different: not the polished laughter and music of the saloons, but the hollow talk of wheels on stone, the shuffling of servants, the restless stamping of horses—those domestic, nocturnal noises that make a great house feel like a living thing. An old-fashioned carriage stood ready, handsome and heavy, with couriers and footmen about it. We saw the slim figure of the pale gentleman in black; he held a thick velvet cloak, placed it about her shoulders, and threw the hood over her head with the tenderness of a man dressing an image for a procession.

She nodded to him and just touched his hand with hers.

He bowed—once, twice, again—until the door closed. The carriage began to move, and the sound of it rolling away seemed to carry a portion of the night with it.

“She is gone,” said Millarca, with a sigh.

“She is gone,” I repeated inwardly, and for the first time, in the hurried moments since my consent, I felt the full folly of what I had done—like realizing too late that one has promised to keep a strange recipe without knowing its ingredients.

“She did not look up,” Millarca said, plaintively.

“The Countess had taken off her mask, perhaps, and did not care to show her face,” I answered; “and she could not know that you were at the window.”

She sighed again and looked into my face. She was so beautiful—so delicately formed, so pathetically earnest—that I relented at once. I reproached myself for having, even for a moment, repented my hospitality. I resolved to atone to her for that unavowed churlishness, as one tries to correct a harsh word spoken at table by offering the choicest portion.

Millarca replaced her mask and joined my ward in persuading me back to the grounds, where the concert was soon to be renewed.

We returned, and walked up and down the terrace that lies beneath the castle windows. The night air was thick with trampled grass and crushed flowers; as people passed, perfumes mingled—gloves, pomade, wine-breath, the faint smoke of lamps. Above it all, the music persisted, and I could not help noticing how the very sound of the place seemed to govern the mind: gaiety on the surface, and beneath it a tireless, bright din that left no corner for a quiet thought to live in. It was as if the Grand Duke’s revel demanded not merely our presence but our nerves.

Millarca grew very intimate with us. She amused us with lively descriptions and stories of great people we saw upon the terrace—names spoken with the easy assurance of one bred among them. Her gossip was diverting without being ill-natured, and to me, long retired from the great world, it had the flavour of some spiced preserve opened after years in the pantry: rich, surprising, almost too sweet, yet tempting all the same. I thought what life she would bring to our sometimes lonely evenings at home, how her voice would fill the rooms when the house had gone quiet and the servants’ footsteps were the only sound.

The ball did not end until the morning sun had almost reached the horizon. It pleased the Grand Duke to dance till then; so loyal people could not go away, nor even think of bed.

We had just made our way through a crowded saloon—heat, breath, the rustle and scrape of bodies pressed close—when my ward asked me, suddenly, what had become of Millarca.

I had supposed she was by her side; she fancied Millarca was by mine. The fact was, we had lost her.

I searched. I sent questions along like messages on a windy night, but the answers came back broken. I feared she had mistaken, in the confusion of a momentary separation, other people for her new friends and had pursued them—only to lose them again in the extensive grounds thrown open to us. The gardens outside were dark and sprawling; the laughter and music reached them in thin, misleading threads, and beyond the lanterns’ glow there were wide spaces where a person might vanish as easily as a word in a dead dialect—no one able to repeat it exactly, no one certain what had been said.

Now, in its full force, I recognized a new folly: I had undertaken the charge of a young lady without so much as knowing her name in any form that could be safely uttered. Worse, I was fettered by promises—promises whose reasons I did not know—and could not even point my inquiries by saying she was the daughter of the Countess who had departed a few hours before. The Countess’s secrecy turned, in my hands, into a gag.

Morning broke. The light came, indifferent and clear, and showed us all our fatigue. It was broad day before I gave up searching. It was not until near two o’clock next day that we heard anything of my missing charge.

At about that time a servant knocked at my niece’s door to say he had been earnestly requested by a young lady—who appeared to be in great distress—to make out where she could find the General Baron Spielsdorf and the young lady his daughter, in whose charge she had been left by her mother.

There could be no doubt, despite the slight inaccuracy, that our young friend had turned up.

And so she had. Would to Heaven we had lost her.

She told my poor child a story to account for her long failure to recover us. Very late, she said, she had reached the housekeeper’s bedroom in despair of finding us, and there had fallen into a deep sleep—so deep that, long as it was, it had hardly sufficed to recruit her strength after the ball’s fatigues.

That day Millarca came home with us.

And I—after all—was only too happy to have secured so charming a companion for my dear girl.
