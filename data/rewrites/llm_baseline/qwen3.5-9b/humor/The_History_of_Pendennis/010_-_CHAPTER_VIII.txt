Once upon a time, there was a young gentleman from Cambridge named the Reverend Francis Bell. During the long vacation, he stayed at a village where his cousin, Helen Thistlewood, was living with her mother—the widow of a lieutenant killed at Copenhagen. Since Mr. Bell was Mrs. Thistlewood's nephew and Helen's own cousin, it seemed only right for him to stay at his aunt's modest home. There he spent his holidays tutoring a few students.

Mr. Bell was a fellow of a college and renowned for his learning. By the time his relatives realized he was secretly engaged, he was merely waiting for a church living to make his vows official. His fiancée was the daughter of another parson, Mr. Coacher, who had actually been Bell's private tutor when Bell was just a boy of seventeen. It was under Mr. Coacher's roof, while helping Miss Martha Coacher pick peas in the garden, that the impulsive young Bell threw himself at her feet. Kneeling amidst the vegetables and the girl, he swore an eternal devotion.

Miss Coacher was significantly older than Bell and had suffered numerous heartbreaks. Three of her father's pupils had played with her affections; a local apothecary had cruelly dumped her; and a dashing dragoon officer, whom she'd danced with in Bath, had once galloped away on his horse and never returned. Wounded by such ingratitude, Martha Coacher desperately sought stability. She listened kindly to the awkward but honest Bell. After he finished speaking, she smiled and said, "Law, Bell, you're far too young for such thoughts!" but hinted she would ponder it in her "virgin bosom." Her father, a widower absorbed in his books, couldn't possibly guide a lady's heart, leaving Martha to manage it alone.

A single lock of her auburn hair, tied with blue ribbon, served as the token of her decision. She had cut off three locks previously and given them to faithless suitors, but this hair had grown back. Men were deceivers, she decided, handing the lock to the simple boy.

However, Number Six was different. Francis Bell proved to be the most faithful lover imaginable. When he eventually went to university, Mr. Coacher cried out in surprise, "God bless my soul, I had no idea what was happening!"—a reaction entirely understandable given he'd been tricked three times before. Francis went to Oxford determined to conquer honors so he could present them at Martha's feet.

He labored prodigiously. Term after term brought news of his triumphs. He sent his prize essay books to old Coacher and his silver declamation cup to Miss Martha. Soon, he was a top Wrangler and a college fellow. Throughout this rise, he maintained a tender correspondence with Miss Coacher, crediting her influence for his success.

Yet, by the time the Reverend Francis Bell, M.A., Fellow and Tutor, reached twenty-six, Miss Coacher was thirty-four. Her charms, manners, and temper had not improved since that sunny spring day in the pea garden. Having achieved his goals, Bell relaxed his studies; his judgment cooled. The sunshine of the garden faded for Miss Martha, and poor Bell found himself pledged via a thousand letters to a coarse, ill-tempered, unfavored, and middle-aged woman.

Following a heated argument—one in which Martha's eloquence usually shone—Francis refused to take his pupils to Bearleader's Green, where Mr. Coacher lived. Instead, he decided to spend the vacation at his aunt's village, a place he hadn't visited since little Helen sat on his knee.

Down he came. Helen was now a beautiful young woman. For four months, from June to October, the cousins spent time walking summer evenings, meeting at dawn, and reading aloud while the old lady dozed. Frank taught Helen everything she needed to know; she sang to him and gave him her artless heart. She knew his entire story. Had he hidden anything? Had he shown her the picture of the woman he was engaged to, blushing as he revealed her letters—hard, eager, and cruel? Days passed, growing happier and closer, filled with kindness, confidence, and pity. Then, one morning in October, Francis returned to college, and the poor girl felt her tender heart depart with him.

Frank too woke from his delightful midsummer dream to the horror of his own pain. He gnashed his teeth against the chains binding him, frantic to break free. Should he confess? Give his savings to the woman and beg for release? There was still time; he temporized. No living would fall in for years. The cousins corresponded sadly and fondly, while the betrothed woman complained bitterly of his changed tone, hard, jealous, and dissatisfied.

Finally, a crisis arrived. The new attachment was discovered. Francis admitted it openly, refusing to hide it, and rebuked Martha for her violent temper, angry imperiousness, and, worst of all, her inferiority and age.

Martha replied that unless he kept his promise, she would carry his letters to every court in the kingdom—letters pledging his love ten thousand times. After exposing him as a perjurer and traitor, she threatened suicide.

Frank had one final interview with Helen, whose mother had recently died and who was now living with old Lady Pontypool. They resolved that he must do his duty: redeem his vow, pay a debt cheated from him by a sharper, and make two honest people miserable. Thus, they judged their duty and parted.

The living fell in almost immediately, but Frank Bell was a gray, worn-out man by the time he was inducted. Helen wrote him a letter on his wedding day, beginning "My dear Cousin" and ending "always truly yours." She returned his other letters and the lock of his hair—all but a tiny fragment—which she kept in her desk when speaking to the Major.

Bell lived in his living for a few years until the Chaplaincy of Coventry Island became vacant. He secured it privately and told his wife. She objected to everything, including this move, so she stayed behind. Later, during Governor Crawley's administration, Bell became close to the governor. Years after his marriage and five years after hearing of Helen's son, Bell's own daughter was born.

She was not the daughter of his first wife, who died of island fever shortly after Helen Pendennis and her husband wrote to announce their child's birth. "I was old, was I?" the first Mrs. Bell had said. "I was old and her inferior, was I? But I married you, Mr. Bell, and kept you from marrying her?" And then she died. Bell remarried a colonial lady he loved deeply, but fate was cruel; she died in childbirth, and Bell gave up, too. He sent his little girl home to Helen Pendennis and her husband, praying they would befriend her.

The child arrived at Fairoaks from Bristol, dressed in black, accompanied by a soldier's wife acting as her nurse. Though she wept at parting, she quickly dried her tears under Helen's motherly care. Around her neck hung a locket containing hair Helen had given to poor Francis years ago. This child was all that remained of him, cherished by the tender creature as a precious legacy. Her name, per his dying letter, was Helen Laura. Yet John Pendennis, though accepting the trust, grew jealous of the orphan and gloomily insisted she be known by her mother's name, not the first one her father had chosen. She feared Mr. Pendennis until the day he died. Only after her husband passed did Helen dare openly show her tenderness toward the little girl.

Thus, Laura Bell became Mrs. Pendennis's daughter. Neither her husband nor the Major viewed her favorably. She reminded the first of painful circumstances in his wife's past that he preferred to forget, and the second saw no relation to his noble family or fortune, possessing only a couple of thousand pounds.

Now, let Mr. Pen enter, who has been waiting all this while.

Having steel-edged his nerves and prepared himself outside the door for a confrontation, he stepped inside, determined to face the formidable uncle. He convinced himself the encounter would be fierce, resolving to endure it with the courage and dignity of his famous family. He flung open the door and entered with a severe, warlike expression, armed cap-a-pie, lance couched, plumes displayed, glaring at his adversary as if to challenge him: "Come on, I'm ready."

The experienced old man, surveying the boy's pomposity, could hardly suppress a grin at his admirable simplicity. Major Pendennis had also assessed the situation. Seeing that the widow was already leaning toward the enemy and knowing that threats would fail against a stubborn, overly serious boy, the Major dropped his authoritative tone instantly. With a warm, natural smile, he held out his hands, shook Pen's passive fingers gaily, and said, "Well, Pen, my boy, tell us all about it."

Helen was delighted by the Major's generosity. Conversely, it completely baffled and disappointed poor Pen, whose nerves were strung for a tragedy. He felt his grand entrance had been thwarted and looked ludicrous. Blushing and wincing with mortified vanity, he stammered, "I—I—I didn't know you were coming until just now... Is—is town very full, I suppose?"
