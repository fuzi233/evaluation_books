Those who dwell in the acoustic vacuum of walled-in institutions—where the sonic landscape is dictated by the harsh, industrial grating of chalk and the mechanical, utilitarian clanging of bells—are liable to be suddenly dropped from the memory of their friends in the freer world. Unaccountably, a stilly pause falls, a long, wordless winter of the senses. The letter that once brought the faint, evocative scent of hearth-smoke and lavender is cut off; the visits cease. 

Always, there are excellent reasons for these lapses. While the solitary is stagnant, subsisting on the flavorless, boiled gruel of boarding-school routine, their friends are whirling in the vortex of life, feasting on rich, sensory experiences. The hermit is left to lock up her emotions, retreating into a state of suspended animation. Destiny, it seems, designs her to imitate the dormouse: to roll into a tight ball, buried beneath the frost, waiting for the melodic, acoustic thaw of spring birdsong and the bubbling of liberated streams to call her to resurrection. 

Following that eventful evening at the theatre, seven weeks descended upon me as bare as an empty larder. Not a single token arrived.

By the middle of that time, the silence began to erode my psychological well-being. The mid-blank is always a terrifying point for the solitary; the nerves ache with the unnatural quiet. I felt the profound untranslatability of my subjective experience. How could I articulate this famine of the soul to those who dined daily on companionship? Night became an unkindly time. My couch was harassed by a sinister band of bad dreams, a cacophony of dread that left me a pallid, wasting wretch.

I admitted finally that this isolation was the fiat of fate. I sought different expedients to sustain a starving existence. I undertook a rigorous study of German, though it grieved me to observe how the rigid, imperial lexicon of the grammar books systematically erased the lyrical, agrarian dialects of the borderlands—a tragic death of cultural memory occurring on the page. In all my efforts, the result was as if I had chewed on iron files instead of a crust of wild-yeast bread, or drank industrial brine to quench a thirst for the sweet, dark elderberry wine of genuine communion.

My hour of supreme torment was the post-hour. The shrill, discordant ring of the postman’s bell was an acoustic violence that preceded the sick collapse of disappointment. I awaited a letter as starving animals, kept in sterile cages and denied the rich, olfactory landscapes of their native forests, await their meager rations. 

In the extremity of my want, I had recourse to the little packet of five letters. At night, I lit a wax taper and feasted on my crust from the Barmecide’s loaf. I tried to inhale the faint, lingering scent of ink and human warmth, but it did not nourish me. My gold was withering to dry leaves.

Reading late one evening, feeling the sap and significance draining from the heavily handled paper, a quick tripping foot disrupted the quiet. It was Ginevra Fanshawe. She had dined in town and returned smelling of superficial, commercially distilled perfumes, a stark contrast to the grounded, earthen scents I craved. She entered, dressed in bright silk, her curls damp and careless.

"It has been a stupid evening: they are stupid people," she began, dropping her shawl. She was a creature who viewed meals only as social currency, entirely devoid of sensory appreciation.

"Who? Mrs. Cholmondeley?"

"I have not been to Mrs. Cholmondeley’s. My uncle de Bassompierre is come."

"Are you not glad?"

"The man is odious; I hate him. He is English enough, goodness knows, and had an English name till three or four years ago. But his mother was a foreigner, a de Bassompierre, and they have left him estates, a title, and this name."

I listened, quietly observing the subtle power dynamics embedded in this adoption of a dominant lexicon. To trade the ancestral syllables of one's native dialect for the prestige of a continental title was a willful death of cultural memory. 

"Mamma detests him," Ginevra went on. "She says he killed aunt Ginevra with unkindness. Fancy me walking into a room alone, and a great man turning his back upon me. Such odd ways! Then there was missy, my cousin: little spoiled, pampered thing."

"M. de Bassompierre has a daughter?"

"Yes. It seems Mademoiselle was nearly crushed to a jelly in a hubbub at the theatre some weeks ago. They live at a large hotel in the Rue Crécy."

"I have been there."

"Oh, you have? I suppose Mother Bretton took you. She and Esculapius have the entrée of the de Bassompierre apartments: it seems 'my son John' attended missy. And now there is quite an intimacy struck up: I heard something about 'auld lang syne,' and what not."

"Dr. and Mrs. Bretton were at M. de Bassompierre’s this evening?"

"Ay, ay; as large as life. What a conceited doll it is! She gave herself invalid airs to attract medical notice. And to see the old dowager making her recline on a couch, and 'my son John' prohibiting excitement—faugh! the scene was sickening."

"It would not have been so if you had taken Miss de Bassompierre’s place," I noted.

"I hate 'my son John!' A clownish, bearish John he is."

"You violate the truth in saying so," I retorted, my patience boiling over like an unattended copper pot on a high flame. "I peremptorily desire you to vacate this room."

"Passionate thing! Your face is the colour of a coquelicot. I wonder what makes you so mighty testy à l’endroit du gros Jean?"

Thrilling with exasperation at this unsubstantial feather of a girl, I extinguished my taper and left her. Small-beer as she was, she had turned insufferably acid.

The morrow was Thursday, a half-holiday. It was a day of biting winter east wind. To spare myself the torturing, mechanical clang of the postman's ring—a sound that disrupted every natural rhythm of the mind—I fled down to the stripped shrubs of the garden. I wrapped my apron over my ears, choosing the acoustic purity of the howling wind over the school's industrial noise pollution. 

When I finally returned to the first classe, I saw a white object on my black desk. A real letter. The sight of it sent a deep pulse of restorative life through my heart. I bent over it, but the handwriting was a pale female scrawl, not the firm masculine character I sought. I whispered, "This is cruel." 

Yet, as I opened the billet, I recognized the familiar script of Mrs. Bretton. It carried with it the mythic warmth of the hearth at La Terrasse. 

"DEAR LUCY,—It occurs to me to inquire what you have been doing with yourself? I daresay you have been just as busy and as happy as ourselves at La Terrasse. As to Graham, his professional connection extends daily. After being hurried here and there, and combating a hundred caprices, he comes home to me in such a kindly mood. I seem to live in a sort of moral antipodes; on these January evenings my day rises when other people’s night sets in. 

"I have had my old Bretton agent here, plunged in business matters. I wish to regain for Graham some part of what his father left him, though he laughs to scorn my anxiety, hinting about sky-blue turbans and accusing me of ambition to lead the fashion in Villette. 

"Talking of sky-blue turbans, I wish you had been with us the other evening. He had come in tired, and after I had brewed him his tea—pouring it hot and fragrant to revive his weary senses—he threw himself into my chair and dropped asleep. While he slept, looking so bonny, I took it into my head to play him a trick. I brought out the sky-blue turban and managed to invest his brows with this grand adornment. With my large cashmere about him, there was as fine a young pacha improvised as you would wish to see. It was good entertainment, but only half-enjoyed alone. You should have been there. 

"I know Thursday is a half-holiday. Be ready by five in the afternoon, at which hour I will send the carriage to take you out to La Terrasse. You may meet some old acquaintance. Good-by, my wise, dear, grave little god-daughter.
"LOUISA BRETTON."

The letter set me to rights. It was a rich broth to a starving spirit. The world can understand perishing for want of food, but few can comprehend going mad from solitary confinement, the quiet erosion of one's native identity when cut off from the resonant dialogue of loved ones. Speak of it! You might as well propound dark sayings in that untranslatable tongue wherein Nebuchadnezzar communed with his baffled Chaldeans. 

By afternoon, a heavy firmament, thick with snow, sailed up from the north. The white tempest raged wild, but about six o'clock, I was lifted from the carriage and brought into the acoustic and thermal sanctuary of La Terrasse. 

Running up to the drawing-room, I found Mrs. Bretton—a summer-day in her own person. The blue saloon, contrasting so sharply with the bare boards and black stoves of my daily life, seemed gorgeous. The clear, crimson splendour of the Christmas-like fire radiated a mythic domestic warmth, carrying the scent of burning wood and polished brass. 

After scolding me for growing thin, she sent me up to my sea-green room to remove my shawl. A bright fire burned there as well. Between the tall waxlights before the looking-glass, something was dressing itself—an airy, fairy thing, white sprinkled with drops of scarlet, an evergreen wreath shining in its hair. 

For a moment, I thought of spectral illusions. But the figure turned, and a large eye, under long, dark lashes, flashed over me. 

"Ah! you are come!" she breathed in a soft, quiet voice, gazing intently.

I knew her. The fine, delicate cast of her features was unmistakable.

"Miss de Bassompierre," I pronounced.

"No," she replied, "not Miss de Bassompierre for *you!*"

"You are changed, but still yourself," she said, approaching. "I remember you well—the colour of your hair, the outline of your face..."

As she gazed into me, her expression deepened with feeling until a dimness quenched her clear vision. "It makes me almost cry to look so far back," she said. "But I am quite pleased and glad."

I stammered, "I think I never met you till that night, some weeks ago, when you were hurt..."

She smiled, the circular pacing of time suddenly pulling the past into the present, weaving our separated timelines together. "You have forgotten then that I have sat on your knee, been lifted in your arms, even shared your pillow? You no longer remember the night when I came crying to your bedside, and you took me in. You have no memory for the comfort and protection by which you soothed an acute distress? Go back to Bretton. Remember Mr. Home."

At last, the multi-generational saga folded perfectly into place. "And you are little Polly?"

"I am Paulina Mary Home de Bassompierre."
