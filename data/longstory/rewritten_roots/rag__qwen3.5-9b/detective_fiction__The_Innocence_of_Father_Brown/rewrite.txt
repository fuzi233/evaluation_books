He had operated a dairy company in London with zero inventory, serving thousands of subscribers by simply swapping milk cans door-to-door. He maintained a secret correspondence with a young woman by micro-filming her letters onto microscope slides. He had rerouted travelers by repainting street numbers in the dead of night and invented portable post boxes to intercept mail orders. Despite his massive build, he possessed the agility of a gymnast, leaping like a grasshopper and vanishing into foliage like a monkey.

Valentin knew that finding Flambeau was only the beginning.

The problem remained: *How* to find him?

Valentin's mental map was incomplete. There was one biological constant Flambeau could not alter: his height. If Valentin's keen vision had spotted a towering fruit vendor, a grenadier, or even a tall duchess, he would have moved instantly. Later, in the quiet of a study that smelled of old paper and candle wax, I recounted the tale to Father Brown. It was a story of a select club, "The Twelve True Fishermen," whose members entered the Vernon Hotel for their annual dinner wearing green evening coats instead of black. When asked why, they claimed it was to avoid being mistaken for waiters—a detail that left observers crushed by the absurdity, yet failed to solve the mystery of why such a peculiar custom existed.

If one were to meet a mild, hard-working little priest named Father Brown and ask him what constituted the most singular luck of his life, he would likely reply that his finest stroke occurred at the Vernon Hotel. There, he had averted a crime and perhaps saved a soul merely by listening to a few footsteps in a passage. He was perhaps a little proud of this wild and wonderful guess, though it remained unlikely that anyone else would ever witness the event firsthand unless told by someone present.

The Vernon Hotel itself was an institution born of an oligarchical society gone mad on good manners. It was a topsy-turvy commercial enterprise that paid not by attracting patrons, but by turning them away. In the heart of a plutocracy, traders had become more fastidious than their customers, creating artificial difficulties so that wealthy clients might expend money and diplomacy overcoming them. Society would meekly assemble parties of six-foot men to dine in a hypothetical hotel requiring such height, or crowd an expensive restaurant open only on Thursday afternoons. The Vernon Hotel stood inconspicuously in Belgravia, a small and inconvenient establishment whose very inconveniences served as walls protecting a specific class.

Its most vital inconvenience was its capacity: practically only twenty-four people could dine there at once. The sole large table, the celebrated terrace table, stood open to the air on a veranda overlooking one of London's most exquisite old gardens. Consequently, the seats could only be enjoyed in warm weather, making the scarcity even more desirable. The proprietor, a Jew named Lever, made nearly a million pounds annually by making entry difficult. He combined this exclusivity with the most meticulous polish in service. The wines and cooking rivaled the best in Europe, and the demeanor of the fifteen waiters perfectly mirrored the fixed mood of the English upper class. Each waiter was trained in terrible silence and smoothness, treated as a gentleman's servant. Indeed, there was generally at least one waiter to every gentleman diner.

The club of the Twelve True Fishermen demanded such a venue for its luxurious privacy, utterly disturbed by the thought of sharing a building with any other club. During their annual dinner, they exposed all their treasures, including a celebrated set of fish knives and forks forged in silver in the shape of fish, each hilt encrusted with a large pearl. These were laid out for the fish course, the climax of the magnificent repast. The society possessed vast ceremonies and observances but no history and no object, rendering it profoundly aristocratic. Membership was exclusive; unless one was already a certain sort of person, one never even heard of them. For twelve years, the society had operated under the presidency of Mr. Audley and the vice-presidency of the Duke of Chester.

How I, an ordinary observer, came to know of this appalling hotel, or how Father Brown found himself within its golden galleys, requires explanation. My story begins with a very aged rioter and demagogue who broke into refined retreats proclaiming that all men are brothers. Wherever this leveller went, Father Brown followed. One afternoon, an Italian waiter at the Vernon Hotel was struck down by a paralytic stroke. Lever, marveling mildly at such superstitions, consented to summon the nearest Catholic priest. What the waiter confessed to Father Brown remains unknown, as the cleric kept it strictly to himself, but it apparently involved drafting a note or statement to convey a message or rectify a wrong.

With a meek impudence he would have displayed equally in Buckingham Palace, Father Brown requested a room and writing materials. Lever was torn between kindness and his aversion to difficulty or scenes. The presence of an unusual stranger in his hotel that evening was akin to finding a speck of dirt on something freshly polished. There was no borderland or anteroom in the Vernon Hotel; no crowds waited in halls, and no chance customers drifted in. With fifteen waiters and twelve guests, the addition of a new guest would have been as startling as a new brother appearing for breakfast. Furthermore, the priest's appearance was second-rate, his clothes muddy; a mere glimpse might precipitate a crisis for the club.

Lever eventually devised a plan to cover, rather than obliterate, the disgrace. Upon entering the hotel, one passed down a short passage adorned with dingy but important pictures to reach the main vestibule and lounge. To the right lay passages to the public rooms; to the left, a similar route led to the kitchens and offices. Immediately on the left was the corner of a glass office abutting the lounge—a house within a house, reminiscent of the old hotel bar that likely once occupied the space.

Designed for two souls, the boat carried only the absolute essentials according to Flambeau's pragmatic philosophy: tinned salmon for sustenance, loaded revolvers for conflict, a flask of brandy for collapse, and a priest for the inevitable end. With this minimal loadout, he drifted down the Norfolk waterways, drawn toward the Broads, yet finding equal pleasure in the overhanging gardens, the reflection of manor houses in the still water, and the quiet act of fishing in secluded pools.

Like a true philosopher, Flambeau possessed no rigid itinerary, yet he maintained a half-purpose—a goal serious enough to validate the journey, yet light enough that its failure would not ruin the escape. Decades prior, during his reign as a notorious thief in Paris, he had received countless missives of admiration, condemnation, and infatuation. One, however, remained etched in his memory: a visiting card bearing an English postmark. Inscribed in French with green ink on the reverse, it read: *"If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet you, for I have met all the other great men of my time. That trick of yours of getting one detective to arrest the other was the most splendid scene in French history."* The front bore the formal engraving: *Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk.*

At the time, Flambeau had dismissed the prince as a footnote—a brilliant, fashionable figure from Southern Italy who had scandalized society by eloping with a married noblewoman, an act compounded by the alleged suicide of the cuckolded husband in Sicily. Later, the prince vanished into Vienna and a life of restless wandering. Yet, upon settling in England, the coincidence struck Flambeau: why not pay a surprise call to this eminent exile in the forgotten marshes of Norfolk? He had no idea if the location existed anymore, given how small and obscure it had become, but fate proved efficient.

One night, they moored the boat beneath a bank draped in high grasses and pollarded trees. The village of Bohun Beacon sat atop a precipice so sheer that the church spire rising from its center appeared less a tower and more the jagged summit of a miniature mountain range. At the base of this ecclesiastical peak stood a smithy, perpetually glowing with the heat of furnaces and cluttered with the chaotic debris of forged iron. Reverend and Honorable Wilfred Bohun was a man of profound devotion, ascending the path toward austere rites of prayer and contemplation. Beside him, seated on the bench outside the inn in full evening dress, sat Colonel the Honorable Norman Bohun, his elder brother. The Colonel was far from pious; he sat nursing a drink that a philosophical observer might classify as either his final measure of the week or his inaugural sip of the next, entirely indifferent to the distinction.

The Bohuns represented one of the rare aristocratic lineages tracing directly to the Middle Ages, their heraldry having once witnessed the fields of Palestine. Yet, it is a grave error to assume such antiquity guarantees adherence to chivalric ideals. Traditions are the currency of the poor; the wealthy trade exclusively in fashion. The Bohuns had been Mohawks under Queen Anne and dandies under Victoria, but over the last two centuries, like many ancient houses, they had decayed into mere alcoholics and degenerate aesthetes, bordering on madness. The Colonel's predatory pursuit of pleasure possessed a bestial quality, and his habitual refusal to depart until morning suggested a pathological state akin to severe insomnia. He was a tall, formidable specimen, aged yet retaining startlingly golden hair. Long yellow moustaches framed a face etched with deep furrows running from nostril to jawline, giving his permanent sneer the appearance of a carved groove. He took immense pride in these incongruities, convinced they somehow harmonized perfectly.

His younger brother, the curate, shared the golden hair and an inherent elegance, though he was clad tightly in black, his face clean-shaven, cultivated, and perpetually anxious. He seemed devoted solely to his faith, though whispers—particularly from the Presbyterian blacksmith—suggested his obsession was actually with Gothic architecture. Critics claimed his ghostly haunting of the church stemmed from a morbid thirst for aesthetic perfection, mirroring his brother's insatiable hunger for women and wine. Whether this charge held truth was debatable, yet the curate's practical piety was undeniable. He frequently knelt not before the altar, but in secluded corners: crypts, galleries, even the belfry.

At that moment, Wilfred paused at the churchyard gate, frowning slightly as he spotted his brother's hollow gaze fixed in the same direction. "I am aware," the Colonel replied, his laughter silent and knowing. "Precisely why I am seeking him out."

"Every man is dignified when he is dead," Father Brown noted, a sentiment born of calling and conviction. Yet even he felt a jarring dissonance at being roused at daybreak to find Sir Aaron Armstrong murdered. There was something absurd, almost grotesque, about secret violence inflicted upon a figure so thoroughly committed to public entertainment. Sir Aaron was a legend of girth and glee; his death felt like hearing that Mr. Pickwick had perished in a workhouse or that Sunny Jim had taken his own life. Though a philanthropist who tackled society's darkest corners, he did so with a flair that bordered on the theatrical. His speeches were cascades of anecdote and booming laughter; his physique was robust to the point of bursting; his ethics were pure optimism. Even his crusade against alcohol—the nation's favorite vice—was conducted with a monotonous, prosperous gaiety typical of the wealthy total abstainer.

The origin of his conversion was a staple sermon on puritan platforms: how a boy lured from Scotch theology to Scotch whisky had eventually risen above both to become, in his modest phrasing, exactly who he was. Yet, seeing the baronet today—with his wide white beard, cherubic countenance, and sparkling spectacles amidst countless dinner parties—it was difficult to reconcile this image with the memory of a former dram-drinker or Calvinist. He was, undeniably, the most seriously merry of mankind.

He resided on the rural fringe of Hampstead in a handsome, modern tower: high but narrow, its smallest side looming precariously over a steep green embankment adjacent to a railway line. Sir Aaron claimed he possessed no nerves, yet that morning the dynamic shifted violently. The house, usually immune to the rhythmic shocks of passing trains, became the source of a shock to the locomotive.

The engine decelerated and halted abruptly just beyond the point where the building's angle impinged upon the sharp turf slope. Mechanical arrests are rarely instantaneous, but the catalyst here had been swift. Emerging from the ridge above the tracks, clad entirely in black—even down to the ominous gloves—a man appeared. He waved his gloved hands like a sable windmill. Such a display might not have stopped a lingering train, but the cry that erupted from him was distinct, unnatural, and terrifying. It was a shout so horrifically clear that its content mattered less than its existence. The word was "Murder!"

Even the engine driver swore he would have pulled the brake regardless, driven by the sheer terror in the accent alone.

Once the train was immobilized, the scene revealed itself. On the green bank stood Magnus, the baronet's manservant, dressed in his customary somber black. The baronet, who had often mocked these gloves, was now a silent victim. Below, rolled nearly to the track's edge, lay the body of an elderly man in a yellow dressing gown with a vivid scarlet lining. A fragment of rope was tangled around his leg, suggesting a violent struggle. Blood stained the grass, though sparingly; the body, however, was contorted in a posture impossible for a living creature. It was Sir Aaron Armstrong. Moments later, Patrick Royce, the secretary and a former Bohemian celebrity, emerged. With a fair beard and a demeanor echoing the servant's agony, he confirmed the horror. Finally, Alice Armstrong, the daughter, arrived, stumbling and waving her arms, her composure shattered. Only then did the engine driver release the brakes, blowing the whistle to summon aid from the next station.

Father Brown had been summoned urgently by Patrick Royce. An Irishman by birth, Royce represented that casual brand of Catholicism that remains dormant until crisis strikes. Had it not been for the fact that the official detective present, a man named Merton, was a devoted admirer of the "Flambeau"—a nickname for Father Brown's investigative prowess—the priest might not have arrived so swiftly. As Merton led the cleric across the fields toward the railway, their conversation took on a confidential tone, bridging the gap between stranger and confidant.

"As far as I can see," Merton stated candidly, "this makes no logical sense whatsoever. There is no suspect. Magnus is a solemn old fool, far too dim-witted to be an assassin. Royce has been the baronet's closest friend for decades, and his daughter clearly adored him. Besides, it's absurd. Who would kill such a jovial old chap? Who could bring themselves to stain their hands in the gore of an after-dinner speaker? It's akin to murdering Father Christmas."

"Yes, it was a cheery house," Father Brown agreed. "While he lived, it was a beacon of cheer. Do you think it will remain so now that he is dead?"

Merton paused, regarding his companion with renewed interest. "Dead?" he repeated.

"Yes," the priest replied stolidly. "He was cheerful. But did he project that cheerfulness? Frankly, was anyone else in that house truly joyful?"

A window in Merton's mind opened, admitting a strange light of realization. Having visited the Armstrongs frequently for minor police matters, he now perceived the truth: it was a depressing house. The rooms were cavernous and cold; the decor was mean and provincial; the drafty corridors were illuminated by electricity that cast a bleaker shade than moonlight. While the old man's scarlet face and silver beard had burned like a bonfire in every room, leaving no residual warmth, the atmosphere remained spectral. This discomfort stemmed partly from the owner's overwhelming vitality—he carried his own heat, needing no stoves—but when Merton considered the others, he admitted they were shadows of their lord.

Magnus, the manservant, was a nightmare figure, his monstrous black gloves adding to his menacing silhouette. Royce, the secretary, was a large, beefy man in tweeds with a short beard, yet his brow was prematurely lined, his hair streaked with gray. He possessed a good nature, but it was a broken, heart-wrenching kind, bearing the air of a man defeated by life. As for the daughter, Alice, it was almost unbelievable she was related to the baronet. She was pallid, her outline delicate and trembling, like an aspen leaf. Merton had often wondered if she learned to flinch at the crash of the passing trains.

"You see," Father Brown said, blinking modestly, "I'm not convinced the Armstrong cheerfulness is actually cheerful *for* others. You claim no one could kill such a happy man, but I doubt it. 'Ne nos inducas in tentationem.' If I were to murder someone," he added simply, "I'd probably choose an Optimist."

"Why?" Merton exclaimed, amused. "Do you think people dislike happiness?"

"People enjoy frequent laughter," Father Brown corrected. "But I do not believe they tolerate a permanent smile. Cheerfulness devoid of humor is a suffocating weight."

They walked in silence along the windy grassy bank beside the rails. Just as they entered the long shadow of the towering Armstrong house, Father Brown spoke suddenly, discarding the thought as casually as one might toss aside a pebble. "Of course, drink is neutral in itself. But I cannot help feeling that men like Armstrong require an occasional glass of wine to sadden them."

Waiting on the bank was Inspector Gilder, a grizzled and capable officer, speaking with Patrick Royce. Royce loomed over the smaller man, his broad shoulders and bristling beard creating a formidable silhouette. He moved with a powerful stoop, performing his clerical and domestic duties with the heavy, humbled grace of a buffalo pulling a cart.

Royce straightened slightly at the sight of the priest, stepping aside to greet him warmly. Meanwhile, Merton addressed the senior investigator with respectful deference undercut by youthful impatience.

"Well, Mr. Gilder, have you made progress with the mystery?"

"There is no mystery," Gilder replied, staring dreamily at the rooks circling overhead.

"For me, there is plenty," Merton countered with a smile.

"It is elementary, my boy," Gilder observed, stroking his pointed gray beard. "Three minutes after you departed for Mr. Royce's parson, the entire picture fell into place. You recall that pasty-faced servant in the black gloves who halted the train?"

"I recognize him anywhere. He gave me the creeps."

"When the train resumed its journey, that man vanished," Gilder drawled. "A remarkably cool criminal to escape via the very vehicle dispatched to fetch the police."

"Are you certain," Merton asked, "that he actually killed his master?"

"Yes, my son, I am fairly certain," Gilder responded dryly. "For the trivial reason that he fled with twenty thousand pounds in securities from his master's desk. The only genuine difficulty lies in the method of execution. The skull appears fractured by a massive instrument, yet no weapon is visible. The killer would have struggled to transport such a tool unless it was too small to notice—or conversely, too large to carry away."

"Perhaps the weapon was too large to be noticed," Father Brown interjected with a peculiar, quiet chuckle.

Gilder glanced around, his expression tightening. "Excuse me, Father, what do you mean?"

"A silly phrasing, I admit," the priest apologized. "Sounds like a fairy tale. But poor Armstrong was struck by a giant's club—a great green club, so vast it goes unseen. We call it the earth. He was broken against this very green bank where we stand."

"How do you explain that?" Gilder demanded quickly.
