“What is it?” I asked.

Thuvia did not answer with words. She lifted her hand and pointed, the gesture as sharp as a knife-flash.

Above the white terraces and the clustered gardens, shadows skimmed the night like swallows over a watercourse. At first they were only darker blots against the sky; then came the brief, bitter strobing of fire—light bursting, vanishing—followed by the hard percussion of muskets. The sound rolled over the temples and clipped itself against the ramparts, and between each report the thin Martian air carried a higher, keening note: the hiss of something fast moving, the wing-slice of small fliers banking hard.

“The black pirates of Barsoom, O Prince,” Thuvia said.

They came in widening circles, dropping lower with each sweep, as though tasting the defenses, feeling where the therns’ line was soft. Their craft were mostly small—two or three men to a deck—built for darting in, biting, and darting out again. A few larger hulls held high above, steady as vultures, and from their keel batteries bombs fell with a dull, belly-deep thud that seemed to press the breath from one’s ribs.

The therns poured out from the temple mouths into the open courts and the garden walks. Their presence in the moonlit space drew the pirates like the scent of fresh cut fruit draws the small night-things of Earth—only this hunger was for blood and for screaming. A score of black fliers arrowed toward our portion of the grounds from every direction. The thern soldiery fired through the rifle-shields strapped to their weapons, their muzzle-flashes brief as the spark when a flint strikes steel. The answering volleys from above came in savage ripples, as if the air itself were being beaten with sticks.

Then, at some signal I could not see, the pirates near us rushed the ground together. Their craft struck, skidded, right into the midst of the thern line, and before the runners had even stilled the black men were already leaping down. They hit the therns with the fury of released storm.

Never had I watched fighting of that temper. I had believed the green hordes the last word in ferocity; yet these men fought as though battle were not duty but feast, and the garden their table. Under Mars’ two bright moons the scene lay naked and clear—the white-skinned, golden-haired therns locked hand to hand with ebony warriors whose bodies looked carved from polished night.

They fought among beauty without seeing it. A knot of them churned over a bed of pimalia until the blossoms were pulp and fragrance; a curved blade found a thern heart and left him folded at the foot of a statue cut from living ruby, moonlight pooling in its facets like wine; a dozen therns pressed one pirate back against an emerald bench inlaid with diamond patterning—delicate work meant to be studied in stillness, now smeared with grit and dark splashes that took the sparkle away.

A little apart stood Thuvia, Tars Tarkas, and I, as the tide of violence swung near and away again. When it eddied close enough, I could read faces and hands. The black pirates had lived in rumour for me—tales traded in Helium’s mess halls and whispered in the red man’s bazaars: that they descended from the lesser moon; that they struck at long intervals; that they took arms, ammunition, and girls; that their rites ended in sacrifice and the eating of their captives. Legends, always told with a shiver, the way old cooks will speak of famine years.

Now the legends wore flesh.

They were large men—six feet and more—with clean-cut, striking features. Their eyes sat wide and bright; the iris was so black it seemed to drink light, while the whites were clear as fresh-peeled fruit. Their frames were like our own in all but colour: skin of deep, lustrous ebony, which, strange as it may sound from one reared beneath a Southern sun, made them appear not less but more beautiful. Yet whatever the gods had given their bodies, their hearts appeared to hoard for themselves. There was a relish in their killing, a malign savor, as though each thrust were a note in some brutal song they had learned long ago.

All about the garden, pirate craft lay where they had landed—unguarded, abandoned, as though the men had no fear of losing them. Now and again a black warrior would burst from a temple doorway with a young woman in his arms. He would run straight for his flier and leap aboard, while comrades converged, blades out, to cover his escape. The therns would throw themselves after him, desperate to reclaim the girl; and in an instant the struggle would knot into a howling whirl, steel on steel, bodies stumbling and striking as in a mill race. Yet again and again the pirates prevailed, and the girl—miraculously untouched—would be borne away into the black air, her pale limbs and streaming hair a brief, helpless flare against the night.

The sound of similar fighting carried along the ribbon of thern territory—down the outer slopes of the Mountains of Otz—until it faded into distance, swallowed by the planet’s thinness. Thuvia watched the far flashes, her face pale but steady.

“Do you understand now, O Prince,” she asked, “why a million warriors guard the domains of the Holy Therns by day and by night? What you witness is but a repetition of what I have seen enacted a score of times during the fifteen years I have been a prisoner here. From time immemorial the black pirates have preyed upon the Holy Therns.

“Yet they never press their expeditions to the point where extermination threatens. It is as though they keep the therns as playthings—satisfying their lust for fighting, and collecting toll in arms and ammunition and in prisoners.”

I looked again at the deserted fliers scattered among the trampled beds and shattered shrubs.

“Why don’t the therns destroy the craft?” I demanded. “That would end this—or at least make the blacks less bold. They leave their vessels as though they were in their own hangars.”

“The therns do not dare,” Thuvia replied. “They tried it once, ages ago. The next night, and for a whole moon thereafter, a thousand great black battleships circled the Mountains of Otz and poured tons of projectiles upon the temples, gardens, and courts until every thern not killed was driven into the subterranean galleries. They live only by the sufferance of the black men. They were near extermination then; they will not risk it again.”

As her words died, another sound rose—a different rhythm, not human. It was a low, harsh coughing roar that made the hairs at the back of my neck lift. The banths we had loosed in the garden had at first hung back, cowed by gunfire and the sheer bedlam of shouted commands and clashing metal. But continuous noise can sour even the most patient beast; and blood—fresh, warm, plentiful—carries a message no language needs.

A great striped form shot from a clump of low shrubbery into the thick of the melee. The banth screamed, not in fear but in rapture of killing, as its talons found living flesh. As though that cry were a bell rung for a hunting pack, the rest came in a rush—huge bodies, shoulders rolling, jaws wide.

Panic snapped through both armies at once. Thern and pirate turned together against the common terror, for the banths chose neither side. They bore down men by the weight of their charge alone, bowling warriors aside like stalks in a storm. Paws rose and fell; fangs flashed; the air filled with the wet tearing sounds no poet should ever try to shape into words.

For a heartbeat the horror fascinated me—battle within battle, men who had moments before hacked one another now scrambling back to back in the trampled pimalia. Then the practical thought cut in: while the therns were occupied with teeth and claws, if ever we were to escape, it must be now.

I turned, searching for a path through the confusion to the ramparts. But as my eyes swept the garden, the simplest road to liberty lay everywhere around me—those unguarded fliers.

Why had I not seen it sooner? I knew the mechanism of every known make of Barsoomian air craft. For nine years I had served Helium’s navy; I had flown one-man scouts and commanded battleships whose bulk made earthly dreadnoughts seem toys. To think, with me, is to act.

I gripped Thuvia’s arm and whispered to Tars Tarkas to follow. Keeping low, we glided toward a small flier lying farther from the press of men and beasts. In an instant we were on its tiny deck, huddled close beneath the low rail. My hand found the starting lever by touch, as a baker’s hand finds familiar tools in the dark before dawn. I pressed my thumb upon the button that controls the repulsion ray—that exquisite Martian discovery that makes the air itself a road.

The craft rocked faintly. It did not rise.

Again I pressed—harder. Nothing.

Then a warning broke upon us: shrill cries and the slap of running feet. I glanced up and saw a dozen black pirates tearing toward us out of the churned confusion, faces lit by moon and malice. We had been seen.

I hammered the button. Still the flier clung to the ground as though nailed there.

And then understanding struck me with the cold clarity of a knife: we had chosen a two-man craft. Its ray tanks were charged for two ordinary men. The Thark’s great weight—Tars Tarkas’s massive frame—was an anchor stronger than any chain.

The pirates were almost upon us. There was no time for debate, no room for regret.

I pressed the button all the way in and locked it. I shoved the lever to high speed. Then I slipped from the deck to the ground, long-sword drawn, and stepped into the oncoming rush.

Behind me Thuvia cried out—one sharp, torn sound—and then, rising above the clamor as the flier at last answered the repulsion ray, I heard her voice, thin with height and anguish:

“My Prince, O my Prince; I would rather remain and die with—”

The rest drowned beneath the pirates’ shrieks as they fell upon me.

Yet I knew my ruse had worked. For the moment, at least, Thuvia and Tars Tarkas were beyond their reach, lifted away on the little craft I had forced into flight. The knowledge steadied my hand even as steel met steel.

For an instant the weight of numbers threatened to crush me. But again—as so often upon this savage planet—I found that my earthly strength, under Barsoom’s lesser gravity, made my arm a thing the foe could not easily measure. My blade became a whirling line of brightness, a net of death in the moonlight. The blacks pressed in with their shorter swords, then recoiled as men do when they have touched fire and learned respect.

Even so, I knew the truth: more would come. Greater numbers would wear me down, slip around my guard, take me from behind. In the end I must fall. A chill passed through me at the thought of dying here, nameless in this garden of false holiness, with no word ever reaching Dejah Thoris.

Then the old blood in me—Virginian, fighting, stubborn—rose and drove the dread out like smoke from a room. A fierce joy surged up, the battle-smile that has unsettled a thousand foes set itself upon my lips, and I went at them with a fury that those who lived would remember.

Still, even while striking, I kept my mind working, hunting an exit as a trapped man hunts a crack of light.

It came from the darkness behind me.

I had just disarmed a huge black fellow after a desperate struggle, and for a breath the others hung back, shoulders heaving, eyes glittering. One spoke in a tone that held hate, and beneath it, unwilling admiration.

“Thern,” he said, “you fight like a Dator. But for your detestable yellow hair and your white skin, you would be an honour to the First Born of Barsoom.”

“I am no thern,” I began, seizing the opening—thinking to throw them the truth and perhaps turn their blades with mine against the therns, if only for long enough to win freedom.

The words died as something heavy struck me between the shoulders with a resounding whack that nearly drove me to my knees.

I spun, expecting a new assailant. Instead, something swept past my shoulder and smashed one of the pirates full in the face, dropping him senseless onto the grass.

Above us, not fifty feet overhead, a fair-sized air vessel drifted—perhaps a ten-man cruiser. The object that had struck us was its trailing anchor, swinging as the ship rose slowly, the iron arms cutting the air like a pendulum.

In that instant the anchor became a ladder offered by fate.

The vessel was lifting, and the anchor had swung beyond the circle of black men and was climbing out of their reach. With a bound that left them staring, I sprang clean over them. Another leap—higher, farther—carried me to the rising iron. My fingers closed on it as it jerked upward.

I hung by one hand, dragged through the upper branches of the garden growth, leaves slapping my face, while below me the pirates howled and shook their swords. The ship veered west, then swung gracefully south. In another moment the Golden Cliffs fell away behind and I was carried out over the Valley Dor. Far beneath, six thousand feet down, the Lost Sea of Korus shimmered like hammered silver in the moons’ light, serene and indifferent to slaughter.

I hauled myself into a sitting position across the anchor’s arms and looked up along the chain into shadow. Perhaps the vessel was deserted. Perhaps it belonged to some friendly people who had wandered too near the battle and now fled. Its retreat gave that hope a thin but living flavor, like broth scraped from a nearly empty pot.

I needed certainty. Carefully—slowly, with every muscle measured—I began to climb the chain toward the deck.

One hand reached the rail. My fingers found it.

And then a fierce black face thrust over the side, and eyes filled with triumphant hate locked onto mine.
