Naturally the brothers, once the first embrace was over and the astonishment of finding each other breathing and warm in the same room had settled into the ordinary miracle of companionship, fell to their reckoning of home—reckoning it as men do who have been long starved of familiar things: by naming them, tasting them again on the tongue.

“How are Harry’s dogs?” George asked first, because he knew his brother’s heart went in front of him, like a hound. “And little Dempster—does he still cry when the kitchen door shuts? And old Nathan—does he yet pretend he cannot hear unless you speak civil?”

Harry, who had been nibbling at a bit of bread as if it were a comfort rather than a food, brightened at once. He could tell you the condition of every creature at Castlewood as a cook can tell you, by scent and touch, whether a ham will keep. The dogs were sleek, Dempster had grown a finger taller and a pound thinner (for he would run after anything with a tail), Nathan’s rheumatism made his temper sharper than his carving-knife. Mountain—dear Mountain—was well and match-making as ever; and Fanny had shot up into that perilous prettiness which makes a household suddenly cautious of its own laughter.

“And Polly Broadbent?” George asked, with the half-smile of a man who has filed away his youth in books and yet remembers where it used to sting. “They say she is to marry Tom Barker of Savannah and go to Georgia.”

“Polly? Oh, Lord!” Harry broke into a laugh that was half shame and half relish. “I was mighty sweet upon her once. I lost a deal of pocket-money at cards, and drank more strong-waters with the Parson than was good for me, only to have a pretext to be near the girl. But Madam would never have consented to my throwing myself away upon Polly Broadbent, no more than she would let Nathan salt a joint twice.”

George’s mouth tightened at the name of their mother, though he spoke lightly. He told, for Harry’s entertainment, of Colonel Washington’s pretty young wife—good-natured, pleasant, and with a fortune—and of the visit of state she had paid to Madam Esmond at Richmond. George, who had an eye for ceremony as other men have for spices, described the whole as if he had been forced to sit at a table where every dish was cold from politeness: the bows, the pauses, the compliments laid on so thick you could have spread them with a knife.

“Never mind, George, my dear!” Mountain had said afterward, according to George, in her cheery way. “The Colonel has taken another wife, but I feel certain that at one time two young gentlemen I know of ran a very near chance of having a tall stepfather, six feet two in his boots.”

Harry roared at that; yet it was a laugh that stuck. Mountain, good soul, matched people as she matched cups and saucers, putting them together because they were near to hand. Two folk could scarce sit to a dish of tea without her fancying their conjunction was for life. It had been she—the foolish tattler—who set abroad the talk about the poor Indian woman, Biche.

When George came to that, his voice changed, and the room itself seemed to thin, as if the air had been drawn off by a bad story. Madam Esmond, when Parson Stack brought the insinuation, repelled it with scorn.

“I should as soon fancy Mr. Esmond stealing the spoons,” she had said, “or marrying a negro woman out of the kitchen.”

And she did not believe Biche guilty, not even when all Richmond took pleasure in believing; she even thanked the poor creature for nursing her son in his illness. Yet she froze her with such proud coldness—like laying one’s hand on a jug and willing the ale to sour—that the Indian slunk away into the servants’ quarters, and there, with the harsh clink of bottle against cup and the low mutter of the quarters at night, tried to drown her disappointments with drink. George, who heard everything when he could not bear to speak, remembered the sound of it: the stealthy step, the suppressed laughter, the sudden hush when Madam passed.

“It was not cheerful,” he told Harry. “Two months at home, and I felt as though the house itself had grown narrow. The birthright is mine, Harry—but thou art the favourite, and God help me, I think my mother almost grudges it to me.”

Harry began at once, hot as a hearth in winter, to deny and protest. George stopped him with a weary motion.

“Why should I have taken the pas, and preceded your worship into the world?” George went on, the bitterness breaking through his usual restraint. “Had you been the eider, you would have had the best cellar, ridden the best nag, and been the most popular man in the country; whereas I have not a word to say for myself and frighten people by my glum face. I should have been second son, set up as lawyer, or come to England, got my degrees, turned parson, and said grace at your honour’s table. The time is out of joint, sir. O cursed spite, that ever I was born to set it right!”

“Why, Georgy, you are talking verses,” Harry cried, delighted in spite of the gloom. “I protest you are!”

“I think some one else talked those verses before me,” George said, and allowed himself a small smile.

“It’s out of one of your books. You know every book that ever was wrote, that I do believe!” Harry exclaimed; and then, with that boyish impulse to offer his heart in anecdotes, told how he had seen two famous authors at Tunbridge and taken off his hat to them.

“Not that I cared much about their books, not being clever enough. But I remembered how my dear old George used to speak of ’em,” Harry said; and his voice caught, as if the words had scraped a tender place. “That’s why I liked to see them. I say, dear, it’s like a dream seeing you over again. Think of that bloody Indian with his knife at my George’s head! I should like to give that Monsieur de Florac something for saving you—but I haven’t got much now, only my little gold knee-buckles, and they ain’t worth two guineas.”

“You have got the half of what I have, child,” George answered. “We’ll divide as soon as I have paid the Frenchman.”

At this Harry broke out into blessings and then into oaths—those rough benedictions of a passionate fellow who cannot keep his love neat—and swore there never was such a brother in the world as his brother George. For some days after George’s arrival, Harry’s eyes followed him as if he feared he would vanish again. At table he would lay down knife and fork mid-cut and begin to laugh softly to himself, as though the very act of chewing in company with George was a marvel. When they walked on the Mall or in Hyde Park, the sounds of London—carriages on gravel, the distant cries of hawkers, the wind combing the trees—seemed to Harry only a frame for his triumph. He would gaze at the company with a look that said, Look here, gentlemen. This is he. This is my brother that was dead and is alive again. Can any man in Christendom produce such a brother as this?

Of course, in Harry’s judgment, George must pay to Museau’s heirs the money promised for his ransom. To Harry the matter was as plain as a debt for supper: a man had pledged his word, therefore he must discharge it, whether the creditor lived to count it or no. But at home the question had been the very millstone about George’s neck.

Museau dead, Madam Esmond had argued—eagerly, sharply—that the bargain fell to the ground and her son was free. The man was a rogue from the first. She would not pay the wages of iniquity. Mr. Esmond had some small independence from his father; he might squander his patrimony if he chose; he was of age and the money was in his power. But she would be no party to such extravagance as giving twelve thousand livres to a parcel of peasants in Normandy with whom they were at war, and who would very likely hand it all to priests and the Pope. She would not subscribe to such wickedness. If George wanted to throw away his father’s money—she could not but remark that formerly he had not been so eager, and when Harry’s benefit was in question had refused to touch a penny of it—then why not give it to his own flesh and blood, to poor Harry suddenly deprived of his inheritance, instead of to a priest-ridden set in France?

It had raged so between mother and son during George’s last days in Virginia that the house seemed to have taken sides: the very doors shut more loudly, the floorboards reported footsteps like accusations. It was never settled. On the morning of George’s departure, Madam Esmond came to his bedside after a sleepless night and asked him, with that pale steadiness which admits no appeal, whether he still persisted in his intention to fling away his father’s property.

He answered from a depth of grief and perplexity that his word was passed; he must do as his honour bade him.

She said she would continue to pray that Heaven might soften his proud heart and enable her to bear her heavy trials; and the last view he had of her face was as she stood a moment by the bed, pale and with tearless eyes, before turning away and leaving the chamber slowly, like a judge who will not look back lest she be moved.

“Where didst thou learn the art of winning over everybody to thy side, Harry?” George asked now, in their London lodging, where the city’s constant murmur pressed against the windows like the sea. “How is it you and all the world begin by being friends? Teach me a few lessons in popularity—nay, I don’t know that I will have them. When I find and hear certain people hate me, I think I am rather pleased than angry.”

He told how, at first in Richmond, Mr. Esmond Warrington—the only prisoner escaped from Braddock’s field, the victim of illness and hardship—had been a favourite with town-folks. The parson glorified his escape in a sermon; neighbours came to visit the fugitive; the family coach was ordered out; Madam Esmond and George paid visits in return. “I think some pretty little caps were set at me,” George said with a dry amusement, “but our mother routed them off and frightened them with the prodigious haughtiness of her demeanour.”

And then, because George would not drink like the officers nor dice and swagger and swear, they judged him dull. Because he could not endure the ladies’ tea-table tattle—how it buzzed like flies around a sugar bowl—they judged him cold and sarcastic, never knowing whether he was in earnest. Richmond’s soundscape, as he painted it, was not the honest music of a farm—wind in corn, an axe biting wood, a kettle beginning to sing—but a weary clatter of gossip, squabbles, and boasts; and it gnawed at him until he found himself more than once wishing for the silence of Duquesne, for the prison’s “freedom of thought,” as he called it, and its easier hush.

“I am very shy, I suppose,” he said. “I can speak unreservedly to very few people. Before most, I sit utterly silent. When we two were at home, it was thou who used to talk at table and get a smile now and then from our mother. When she and I were together we had no subject in common, and we scarce spoke at all until we began to dispute about law and divinity.”

So the gentlemen decided he was supercilious, and the ladies decided he was disagreeable—before his character was “gone quite away.” And that, he said, went with the appearance of poor Biche and Museau’s unlucky messenger.

“Oh, a nice character they made for me,” George cried then, anger flaring in him at last. “And a pretty life they led me after that messenger appeared! The boys hooted the poor woman if she showed in the street; the ladies dropped me half-curtseys and walked to the other side. That precious clergyman went from one tea-table to another preaching on the horrors of seduction and the lax principles young men learn in Popish countries and bring back. The poor Fawn’s appearance at home a few weeks after my return was declared to be a scheme between her and me; the best informed agreed she had waited on the other side of the river until I gave the signal to come join me in Richmond. The officers bantered me at the coffee-house and cracked their clumsy jokes about the woman I had selected.”

He paused, and in the pause Harry could hear the city again: wheels, distant shouts, a bell. Against that London din, George’s Virginia sounded—its gossip louder than its crickets, its sermons sharper than its scythes.

“I was so enraged,” George went on, “I thought of going to Castlewood and living alone there—for our mother finds the place dull and the greatest consolation in Mr. Stack’s ministry—when the news arrived of your female perplexity; and I think we were all glad I should have a pretext for coming to Europe.”

“I should like to see any infernal scoundrel that said a word against you and break his rascally bones,” Harry bellowed, striding up and down as if the room were too small for his indignation. “I had to do something like it for Bob Clubber.”

“What!” George cried, contempt and astonishment together. “That little sneaking, backbiting, toad-eating wretch who’s forever hanging about my lord at Greenway Court and spunging on every gentleman in the country? If you whipped him, I hope you whipped him well.”

“We were bound over to keep the peace,” Harry said, with a shrug that tried to make light of it. “And I offered to go into Maryland with him and settle our difference there. And of course the good folk said that having made free with the seventh commandment I was inclined to break the sixth.”

“So by this and by that,” George concluded, “and being as innocent of the crime imputed to me as you are, I left home, my dear Harry, with as awful a reputation as ever a young gentleman earned.”

Then, as if the narrative itself could not resist stepping aside to hear its own echo, George spoke—half mocking, half wounded—of how the world will always invent the rest of one’s story, and tell it with a confidence that drowns truth as a mill-wheel drowns a bird’s song. Names, he said, are altered; words are twisted; a man’s life is translated into another dialect and comes out as something else entirely, like a receipt repeated from mouth to mouth until the seasoning is wrong and yet everybody swears it is authentic. Harry, who cared less for the philosophy than for the bruise beneath it, only growled and poured his brother more wine.

And because George had offered up his martyrdom, Harry must unbosom himself in turn. He confessed he had given up all the family at Castlewood—my lord not for winning from him at play (for Harry was sporting and expected to pay when he lost and receive when he won), but for refusing to aid the chaplain in necessity and dismissing him with false, heartless pretexts. About Mr. Will he had made up his mind after the horse-dealing matter and had marked his sense of that gentleman’s conduct freely upon Mr. Will’s eyes and nose.

As for the Countess and Lady Fanny, Harry spoke more guardedly, yet not favourably. He had heard stories: the Countess a card-playing old cat, Lady Fanny a desperate flirt. Who told him? Well—he had heard them from a person who knew both ladies very well indeed. Maria, in those days of confidence of which he spoke with a kind of stubborn tenderness, had imparted to him a number of anecdotes of her stepmother and half-sister by no means to their advantage.

But when he came to speak of Maria herself, his whole nature warmed, as a kitchen warms when bread is in the oven: not merely heated, but filled with a living smell and promise.

“It may be imprudent,” he said. “I don’t say no, George. I may be a fool—I think I am. I know there will be a dreadful piece of work at home, and that Madam and she will fight. Well! We must live apart. Our estate is big enough to live on without quarrelling, and I can go elsewhere than Richmond or Castlewood. When you come to the property, you’ll give me a bit—at any rate Madam will let me off at an easy rent—or I’ll make a famous farmer or factor. I can’t and won’t part from Maria. She has acted so nobly by me, that I should be a rascal to turn my back on her. Think of her bringing me every jewel she had in the world, dear brave creature, and flinging them into my lap with her last guineas—and—and—God bless her!”

He dashed his sleeve across his eyes and stamped, ashamed of weeping and yet unable not to.

“No, brother, I won’t part with her—not to be made Governor of Virginia to-morrow; and my dearest old George would never advise me to do so, I know that.”

“I am sent here to advise you,” George said quietly; and there was something in his tone that reminded Harry of their mother, though without her hardness. “I am sent to break the marriage off, if I can; and a more unhappy one I can’t imagine. But I can’t counsel you to break your word, my boy.”

“I knew you couldn’t.” Harry’s jaw set, and the warmth in him turned to a gloomy steadiness, as when broth cools and begins to film. “What’s said is said, George. I have made my bed, and must lie on it.”

So it had stood between them when first they talked over Harry’s love affair: George fearing, Harry resolved; George honour-bound, Harry heart-bound. Yet after George’s conversation with his aunt—after that keen old woman of the world had poured her information with a steady hand, like vinegar into oil until the mixture would not reconcile—Mr. Warrington, naturally sceptical, began to doubt Lady Maria as well as her brothers and sister, and to look at Harry’s engagement with increased distrust and alarm.

Was it for his wealth that Maria wanted Harry? Was it his handsome young person she longed after? Were those stories true which Aunt Bernstein had told? George could not bid Harry break his word; but he began, in the secret pantry of his mind, to consider how one might put Maria’s affection to the trial. And his ensuing conduct—which did not appear very amiable—sprang, no doubt, from that deliberation.
