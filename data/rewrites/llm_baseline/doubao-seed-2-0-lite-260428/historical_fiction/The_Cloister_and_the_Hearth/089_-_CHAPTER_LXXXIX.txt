No pen, no matter how deft, could ever fully trace the searing, twisting agony that tore through Clement’s sensitive mind, soft conscience, loving heart, and devout religious soul in the first few shocking, tremendous hours after his world upended. To feel it at all, you must step into his boots, stand where he stood, and taste that same gale that ripped every last one of his certainties to shreds. I could spin a whole volume recounting every lurch and tremor of that inner tempest, and still end no closer to the truth than if I’d asked you to imagine it for yourself.

So instead, I will tell you only of his next two deliberate acts, each a clear window into the man he was, once that first wild hurricane of emotion had ebbed to a steady, smoldering resolve.

He’d spent the whole prior night at the dying hermit’s side, trading holy consolations, sharing prayer, tending to the old man’s last breaths. When he set out at dawn, he did not turn his feet toward Rotterdam as he’d once planned. He walked instead for Tergou. There he meant to confront his old, bitter enemy the burgomaster, and use the long-lost parchment—whose own winding history was a tale full of adventure all its own—to force the man to disgorge what he had stolen, and give Margaret Brandt back what was rightfully hers.

Sweaty, dust-caked, his boots crusted with miles of rutted country road, he stopped at a village fountain just outside Tergou, pulled a crust of black rye bread from his scrip, and knelt to sip cold water straight from the spout. He was halfway through his simple meal when a breathless female servant came running toward him, white-faced, begging him to come shrive her dying master at once. Without a single word of protest, he tucked the half-eaten bread back into his wallet and followed her.

She led him straight to the Stadthouse. A small, involuntary shudder ran through him when she pushed open its heavy oak doors, but he mastered himself in an instant, stepping across the threshold and following her up the wide, creaking staircase.

There in the great bed, propped high on a mountain of down pillows, lay the man who had been his deadliest enemy for years—Ghysbrecht the burgomaster, his face waxen, already looking more like a corpse than a living man. Clement stood in the doorway for a long moment, his eyes fixed on him, and all the old wrongs rose unbidden: the prison tower, the ambush in the woods, the forged letter that had torn Margaret and him apart for years. Then he spoke, his voice low and soft, though he trembled a little as he did: “Pax vobiscum.”

The dying man greeted him with all the eagerness his failing strength could muster. “Thank the Good Lord you’ve come in time, father,” he gasped. “Absolve me of my sins, pray for my soul, you and your brother friars, before it is too late.”

“My son,” Clement said, steady as he could, “absolution can only come after true, full confession. Hide nothing, if you value the salvation of your immortal soul. Think long now, of the wrongs you have done most grievously against God and His Church, while I kneel to pray for the wisdom to guide you.”

He knelt then, and prayed. When he stood again, his face was calm, his voice even. “Confess your sins, my son.”

“Ah, father,” Ghysbrecht whimpered, “they are many, and they are great.”

“Then let your penitence be great, my son, and you shall find God’s mercy great in turn.”

Ghysbrecht clasped his trembling hands, and began to recite his sins with every show of deep contrition. He owned he had eaten meat in the middle of Lent. He admitted he had skipped Mass on Sundays and feast days far too often, and trifled with all manner of small religious observances, listing every minor lapse with scrupulous, exact care.

When he finished, Clement spoke quiet and sharp. “That is all well, my son. These are small faults. Now tell me of your crimes. You would have done far better to start with those.”

“Why father?” the old man bleated, feigning shock. “What crimes could I have committed, if these are not counted as sin?”

“Who is here to be confessed to whom?” Clement said, a little sternly.

“Forgive me, father! I confess to you, of course. But I do not know what it is you name as crimes of me.”

“Are you free of the seven deadly sins?”

“Heaven forbid I should be guilty of them! I do not even know all their names!”

“Many a man commits every last one of them without ever being able to recite their names off by heart,” Clement said. “Start with the sin that begets lying, theft, and murder.”

“I am innocent of that one, no matter what it is called!” Ghysbrecht protested.

“It is called Avarice, my son.”

“Avarice?” The burgomaster blinked, all wide-eyed innocence. “Oh, that! Why, I’ve been a careful, saving man all my days, but I kept a good table for my household, and I never turned the poor away empty handed, not entirely. But I am a great sinner, no doubt—maybe the next sin you name will be mine. What is it?”

“We have not yet finished with this one,” Clement said, unshaken. “You will not trifle with Holy Church.”

“Alas! A man at death’s door has no power to trifle with anyone now!” Ghysbrecht moaned. “Avarice? Avarice?” He wore a look of perfect, puzzled confusion.

“Have you ever robbed the fatherless?” Clement asked, blunt and unflinching.

“Me? Robbed the fatherless?” Ghysbrecht gasped. “Not that I recall, no.”

“Once more, my son, I must tell you that you are trifling with the Church,” Clement said, his voice cold as winter stone. “Miserable man! One more evasion, and I will turn and leave this bed at once. Fiends are already gathering at your bedside, waiting to drag you straight down to the bottomless pit.”

“No! Do not leave me, holy man, do not leave me!” Ghysbrecht shrieked, terrified out of his wits. “The Church knows all, I will confess! I must have robbed the fatherless, I admit it! What names? My memory is all shaken, I can’t call them to mind!”

Clement had no time for this clever dodge. “Have you forgotten Floris Brandt?” he said, flat as a hammer striking iron.

The dying man reared straight up in bed, rigid with terror. “How did you know that?” he yelped.

“The Church sees many things,” Clement said, icy calm. “Paths are open to her that you, in your small worldly cunning, can never fathom. Miserable, unrepentant wretch! You called for a friar to shrive you, thinking to pick some stranger who knew nothing of your secret sins. You thought you would cheat the Church on your deathbed, lie to the priest, and die as you had lived, unpunished. But God, kinder to you than you ever were to yourself, sent the one man to your side that you cannot deceive. He has borne with you, He has warned you not to mock Holy Church—but you cannot bring yourself to confess, for your heart is hard as a stone. Go then, and die as you have lived. I see the fiends crowding at the foot of your bed, their claws out, waiting only for me to step away. And I will go.”

He turned his back to the bed, but Ghysbrecht threw himself forward, clutching tight to the hem of Clement’s friar’s frock. “Mercy! Stay! I will tell you everything, every last secret! I robbed my old friend Floris Brandt! Oh, I wish that was the worst of it—he lost almost nothing to me, at first. But then I seized his lands, and kept them from his son Peter, and from Peter’s daughter Margaret. I always meant to give them back, truly I did! But I could not bear to part with the gold, I could not, I could not!”

“Avarice, my son, plain and simple avarice,” Clement said, softening a little. “It is a mercy you have not waited too late to turn from it.”

“I will leave all her lands to her in my will!” Ghysbrecht babbled. “She will not have long to wait, you see! I will be dead in a month or two at the very most!”

“You fool,” Clement said, sharp and pitying. “You would damn your immortal soul for the sake of a single month’s hoarded possession of land that will rot beneath you before the year is out?”

Ghysbrecht groaned, begged the friar to be reasonable, to have pity on his age and his weakness. But Clement was firm, gentle, endlessly patient, and little by little he pried the dying miser’s grip loose from stolen property. There were moments when his own temper frayed thin, when he almost reached inside his robe to pull out the long-hidden parchment he had carried all the way to Tergou to force the issue. But after the wild, passionate grief that had overtaken him the day before, he was on his guard against anger. He conquered his impatience. He conquered the deep, visceral loathing he felt for this man, so strong it made his flesh crawl every moment he spent leaning over that deathbed, fighting for the man’s very soul. And in the end, without ever mentioning the hidden deed, he won. Ghysbrecht agreed to make full, immediate restitution, no delays, no wills, no waiting for his own death.

The exact details of that restitution, the strange, curious shifts that came over Ghysbrecht once he had done it, and the terms on which the burgomaster and Clement parted ways, will be told elsewhere in good time.

I promised to tell you of two acts of Clement’s that laid bare the state of his mind after that great storm of the soul. The first you have just heard. The second can be told in two short words.

The very moment he was certain Margaret Brandt had every last inch of her land back, and was secure, and rich, and well provided for?

He vanished.
