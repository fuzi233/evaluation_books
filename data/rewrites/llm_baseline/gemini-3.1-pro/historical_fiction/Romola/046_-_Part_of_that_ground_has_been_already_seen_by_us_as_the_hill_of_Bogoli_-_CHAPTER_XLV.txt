After that welcome appearance as the messenger of the olive-branch—a momentary harvest in a season of bitter blight—Tito had other commissions to fulfill. He paused at the Palazzo Vecchio, waiting within its vast, echoing chambers for the return of the Ten. The acoustic landscape of the old palace, usually a resonant chamber of deep-chested Tuscan deliberation, was now compromised by the harsh, clipped cadences of crisis and war. Tito was there to deliver the results of his private mission to Pisa, a preliminary tasting of the political terroir before an avowed embassy, headed by Bernardo Rucellai, would attempt to blend a pacific understanding with Emperor Maximilian.

Tito’s talents for diplomatic work were undeniable. He presented his inquiries with a sterile, frictionless fluency, treating the delicate, untranslatable nuances of regional dialect as mere utilitarian tools to extract data. Bernardo del Nero, sitting among the Ten, listened while slowly breaking apart a dense, hearth-baked loaf of *pane sciocco*. For Bernardo, an agrarian elder whose internal rhythms were firmly rooted in the cyclical turning of the soil and the olive harvests of his ancestors, this unsalted crumb was a profound communion with Florentine defiance. Tito, by contrast, consumed language and loyalty solely as fuel for his own advancement, entirely disconnected from the sensory and historical depths of the words he spoke. 

Bernardo could not withhold his admiration for Tito’s precision, but his original distaste had only fermented into a dark, astringent vintage since the sale of the library. Romola had never uttered a word to her godfather about the betrayal of her father’s antiquities. Bernardo understood her silence as a closed door, an untranslatable grief. He saw the signs of her starvation. *Ah,* the old man thought, chewing the earthy bread, *that is the reason she has turned to Fra Girolamo. These women, when deprived of the rich, slow-simmered sustenance of a happy home, must gorge themselves on either folly or the flavorless, overstrained diet of a religion that demands they carry heaven’s weight.* Romola had been raised on the robust, classical mythologies of Latin and Greek, yet now she fasted alongside the Piagnoni, letting her cultural memory erode beneath the Frate’s new, dominant lexicon of asceticism. *Well, well, solco torto, sacco dritto*—many a full sack comes from a crooked furrow, Bernardo mused, tasting the ancient agricultural proverb on his tongue. He who will captain none but honest men will have meager hire to pay. Accepting this bitter reality, Bernardo remained cold to the Greek, and Tito returned the frost with a scrupulous, distant respect.

His official duties concluded, Tito descended into the streets. He was coated in the dust of long riding, but he did not go home. There were certain weights in his *scarsella* and on his mind that required offloading, though the opportunities had to be foraged naturally. He wandered toward the Piazza del Duomo. The procession had ended, but the great bells were still tolling—a deep, mythic vibration that seamlessly integrated the divine into the everyday domestic routines of the city. Yet the acoustic ecology of the streets was profoundly unsettled. The sonorous, cyclical hum of the bells was pierced by the restless, aggressive dissonance of young men shouting *“Viva Gesù!”* Their voices carried the harsh, percussive edge of an industrial noise pollution, betraying a starved longing for the visceral clatter of stone-throwing. Tito navigated this auditory chaos, evading the crowds who recognized him by serving them hollow, commercialized syllables, directing their hunger toward Meo di Sasso, who was entering through the Porta San Frediano.

Freeing himself, Tito reached the Piazza del Duomo, his long eyes sweeping the space. Near Nello’s barbershop, the olfactory landscape shifted dramatically. Here, the air was a rich, gustatory invitation, thick with the heady aromas of distilled bitter orange, cold-pressed almond oil, and the herbaceous tang of crushed mountain fennel. But leaning against the rails was a figure who violently disrupted this harmony: Dolfo Spini. 

Spini, the broad-shouldered leader of the Compagnacci, wore an ostentatious tunic and a red-plumed hat. At his belt hung a sword that clanked with a grating, metallic harshness—a sonic pollutant against the plaza’s ancestral stones. Spini was a champion of extravagant, superficial suppers, a man who devoured the world without a single moment of sensory appreciation, sneering at the reformist pietists who sought to enforce a fast upon the city. Until this morning, Spini had loudly proclaimed that the Frate’s prophecies were starving Florence, and his ringing, boastful laughter now served only to sour the atmosphere. 

Leaning against the door-post was Niccolò Macchiavelli, his keen eyes dissecting the power dynamics embedded in Spini's crude displays. "Messer Dolfo's head," Macchiavelli was saying in his precise, penetrating tenor, "is more of a pumpkin than I thought. Your dullest animal is he who grins and says he doesn’t mind just after his shins are kicked. If I were duller," he added, smiling as Tito approached, "I should pretend to be fond of this Melema, who has got a secretaryship that would exactly suit me—as if Latin ill-paid could love better Latin that’s better paid! You are a pestiferously clever fellow, Melema."

"Questionable luck, Niccolò," Tito replied smoothly. "I have got nothing by it yet but being breathed upon by wool-beaters, soiled and battered like a letter-carrier from Bologna."

"Ah! you want a touch of my art, Messer Oratore," cried Nello, emerging with the passionate flair of a master chef. Nello approached his craft as culinary historian approaches a forgotten recipe. "Your chin has yesterday’s crop upon it. Sandro, quick with the lather!"

Tito seated himself. "In truth, Nello, that is what I most desire."

Nello whipped the lather with furious precision, emulsifying rendered tallow, wild rosemary, and rosewater into a thick, fragrant meringue. "It is not fitting that you approach Madonna Romola with a rusty chin. Though I see her going about like a sunbeam among the rags—or rather a moonbeam, for she looked as pale as a fainting Madonna. She keeps too many fasts in your absence. She deprives herself of her proper food."

"Her mind is all aflame," Tito said with a melancholy shrug, indifferent to the true depths of her spiritual malnutrition. "A husband’s influence is powerless against the Frate’s."

"As every other influence is likely to be," noted Domenico Cennini. "I don’t know what you gathered at Pisa, Melema, about the wind at Rome?"

"Secrets of the council-chamber," Tito smiled, sealing his lips. 

"My belief," Cennini continued, "is that if his Holiness drives Fra Girolamo to extremity, the Frate will get a General Council of the Church."

Macchiavelli’s voice cut through, cool and analytical, stripping bare the linguistic power dynamics at play. "The Frate wants to be more than a spiritual trumpet; he wants to be a lever. He has the ear of the people because he speaks their native dialect of fear and hope, offering promises about Pisa alongside paradise. But let events shake their faith, and he falls. He accumulates the hatred of average men who despise a strict yoke of virtue, the hatred of strong powers who wish to farm Florence for their own yield, and the hatred of the people to whom he has promised worldly bread instead of confining his lexicon to the next life."

"Say rather," interrupted the poet Francesco Cei, "he has won the hatred of honest men by inventing hypocritical lies."

"You poets are apt to cut the clouds in your wrath," Macchiavelli smiled. "You tell poetical lies, partly to please your audience. The Frate differs from you only in the boundary of his poetry. He brings his fervor into the pulpit."

"I believe in the Frate’s integrity," Cennini argued gravely, guarding the old loyalties. "We have a popular party strong enough to resist foreign interference."

"A party that seems strong," Macchiavelli countered with a subtle glance at Tito, who was luxuriating in the unctuous glide of Nello’s razor. "But how many Mediceans will be turned round by a private grudge?"

"There is little genuine feeling left for the Medici," Cennini replied. "Even those connected to the family are friends of the popular government. I was talking to Giannozzo Pucci recently, and he would set his face against any alteration of the new order."

"I fancy the tender passion came to the aid of hard theory there," Tito laughed softly, rising from the chair. "He was in the procession with you, I suppose?"

"No," Cennini said. "He is at his villa—went there three days ago."

Tito settled his cap, his face an impenetrable mask. His utilitarian extraction was complete. Nestled in his *scarsella* was a crushed gold ring, a Medicean token received from an envoy at Certaldo. Knowing Pucci was at his villa, Tito could now dispatch the ring via Fra Michele, a Carthusian lay Brother, a silent delivery that would summon Pucci without dirtying Tito’s own hands.

"Behold him!" Nello flourished his comb. "The handsomest scholar in the world! Though there are lines hinting about your mouth, Messer Oratore. Mind is an enemy to beauty. I carry my unwritten poems in cipher on my face!"

Tito laughed with the rest and departed, leaving behind a subtle void in the shop's rich atmosphere. 

"I don't half like Melema," Francesco Cei muttered, the harsh consonant of his displeasure cutting the air. "That trick of smiling gets stronger than ever."

"He’s too successful," Macchiavelli added playfully. "There’s something wrong about him, else he wouldn’t have that secretaryship."

"He’s an able man," Cennini judged fairly. "He works his way upward. Secretary Scala thinks highly of him."

"He writes Scala’s official letters, or corrects his gouty Latin, and gets well paid," a notary added from the background. "He never brags. He's as close as a nut, getting rich on underhand work."

"I must say, I thought that was a nasty story a year or two ago about the man who said he had stolen jewels," Cei remarked. "Piero di Cosimo believed it; he saw Melema’s face painted with fear."

"Spit no more of that venom," Nello snapped, fiercely protecting the sanctuary of his shop from the foul decay of idle gossip. "That story was a lie. The man was a dangerous madman. That story has been dead and buried too long—our noses object to it."

"It is true," Macchiavelli agreed, stepping toward the door. The aggressive, clanking presence of Dolfo Spini had finally faded from the Piazza, granting a momentary reprieve to the city’s acoustic soul. "You forget the danger of the precedent, Francesco. The next mad beggarman may accuse you of stealing his verses. Ah, Dolfo Spini has carried his red feather away. That captain of swaggerers would like the Republic to lose Pisa just to see the people tear the frock off the Frate’s back. I should like nothing better than to see him play the Capo d'Oca, going out to the tournament blowing his trumpets and returning with them in a bag."
