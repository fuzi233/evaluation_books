The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the dining hall windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Margaret Hale, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of London itself. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of beeswax, damp wool, and the cloying sweetness of pomegranate blossoms arranged with brutal disregard for color theory.

Edith stood amidst the chaos of preparation, her movements sharp with impatience. She had already rejected Dixon's suggestion of blue flowers against the "dead gold"—a term Edith insisted on using despite the fabric being clearly straw-colored. Now, she held a cluster of brilliant scarlet blooms, forcing them into the neckline of Margaret's gown with a petulant energy that betrayed her own anxiety.

"Oh, Dixon! Not those horrid blue flowers to that dead gold-coloured gown. What taste!" Edith snapped, her voice cutting through the hum of the servants. "Wait a minute, and I will bring you some pomegranate blossoms."

"It's not a dead gold-colour, ma'am. It's a straw-colour. And blue always goes with straw-colour," Dixon replied, her voice tight with suppressed indignation.

But Edith had already secured the crimson garniture. When she stepped back to assess the effect, her gaze darted frantically around the empty room. "Where is Miss Hale?" she demanded, her composure fracturing into a whine. "I can't think how my aunt allowed her to get into such rambling habits in Milton! I'm sure I'm always expecting to hear of her having met with something horrible among all those wretched places she pokes herself into. I should never dare to go down some of those streets without a servant. They're not fit for ladies."

Dixon, still seething over her despised floral choices, offered a curt retort that only fueled Edith's hysteria. "It's no wonder to my mind, when I hear ladies talk such a deal about ladies—and when they're such fearful, delicate, dainty ladies too—I say it's no wonder to me that there are no longer any saints on earth—"

"Oh, Margaret! Here you are!" Edith gasped, rushing forward as if to catch a falling star. "I have been so wanting you. But how your cheeks are flushed with the heat, poor child! But only think what that tiresome Henry has done; really, he exceeds brother-in-law's limits. Just when my party was made up so beautifully—fitted in so precisely for Mr. Colthurst—there has Henry come, with an apology it is true, and making use of your name for an excuse, and asked me if he may bring that Mr. Thornton of Milton—your tenant, you know—who is in London about some law business. It will spoil my number, quite."

"I don't mind dinner. I don't want any," Margaret said, her voice low and steady, though her hands trembled slightly at her sides. "Dixon can get me a cup of tea here, and I will be in the drawing-room by the time you come up. I shall really be glad to lie down."

"No, no! That will never do. You do look wretchedly white, to be sure; but that is just the heat, and we can't do without you possibly." Edith adjusted the flowers, lowering them slightly. "Those flowers a little lower, Dixon. They look glorious flames, Margaret, in your black hair. You know we planned you to talk about Milton to Mr. Colthurst. Oh! To be sure! And this man comes from Milton. I believe it will be capital, after all. Mr. Colthurst can pump him well on all the subjects in which he is interested, and it will be great fun to trace out your experiences, and this Mr. Thornton's wisdom, in Mr. Colthurst's next speech in the House. Really, I think it is a happy hit of Henry's. I asked him if he was a man one would be ashamed of; and he replied, 'Not if you've any sense in you, my little sister.' So I suppose he is able to sound his h's, which is not a common Darkshire accomplishment—eh, Margaret?"

"Mr. Lennox did not say why Mr. Thornton was come up to town? Was it law business connected with the property?" Margaret asked, her voice constrained, pulling her thoughts inward.

"Oh! He's failed, or something of the kind," Edith waved a dismissive hand, her eyes drifting upward. "That Henry told you of that day you had such a headache—what was it? (There, that's capital, Dixon. Miss Hale does us credit, does she not?) I wish I was as tall as a queen, and as brown as a gipsy, Margaret."

"But about Mr. Thornton?"

"Oh! I really have such a terrible head for law business. Henry will like nothing better than to tell you all about it. I know the impression he made upon me was, that Mr. Thornton is very badly off, and a very respectable man, and that I'm to be very civil to him: and as I did not know how, I came to you to ask you to help me. And now come down with me, and rest on the sofa for a quarter of an hour."

The privileged brother-in-law arrived early, bringing with him the weight of social obligation. Margaret, reddening as she spoke, began to ask him the questions she desperately needed answered regarding Mr. Thornton.

"He came up about this sub-letting the property—Marlborough Mills, and the house and premises adjoining, I mean. He is unable to keep it on; and there are deeds and leases to be looked over and agreements to be drawn up. I hope Edith will receive him properly; but she was rather put out, as I could see, by the liberty I had taken in begging for an invitation for him. But I thought you would like to have some attention shown him: and one would be particularly scrupulous in paying every respect to a man who is going down in the world."

He lowered his voice as he addressed Margaret directly, sitting close enough that the intimacy of the moment felt like a physical pressure. But as he finished, he sprang up, introducing Mr. Thornton, who had entered silently at that precise moment, to Edith and Captain Lennox.

Margaret watched him with an anxious intensity. It had been considerably more than a year since she had last seen him, and the passage of time had carved him anew. His fine figure still bore him above the common height of men, granting him a distinguished presence born of natural ease of motion. Yet his face looked older, etched with lines of care that no amount of grooming could erase. Still, a noble composure sat upon his features, impressing those who had just heard of his fallen status with a sense of inherent dignity and manly strength.

He was aware, from the first glance he cast around the room, that Margaret was present. He had seen her intent look of occupation as she listened to Mr. Henry Lennox, and he moved toward her with the perfectly regulated manner of an old friend. With his first calm words, a vivid color flashed into her cheeks, burning there permanently throughout the evening. She seemed to possess little to say to him. She disappointed him by asking, in a quiet monotone, what appeared to him to be merely necessary questions concerning her old acquaintances in Milton. Others arrived—more intimate within the household than he—and he drifted into the background, where he and Mr. Lennox exchanged occasional glances and whispers.

"You think Miss Hale looking well," said Mr. Lennox, leaning casually against the mantelpiece, "don't you? Milton didn't agree with her, I imagine; for when she first came to London, I thought I had never seen anyone so much changed. To-night she is looking radiant. But she is much stronger. Last autumn she was fatigued with a walk of a couple of miles. On Friday evening we walked up to Hampstead and back. Yet on Saturday she looked as well as she does now."

"We!" Who? They two alone?

Mr. Colthurst, a very clever man and a rising member of Parliament, possessed a quick eye for discerning character. He was struck by a remark Mr. Thornton made at dinner-time. He enquired from Edith who that gentleman was; and, to her surprise, she found, from the tone of his "Indeed!", that Mr. Thornton of Milton was not such an unknown name to him as she had imagined it would be.

Her dinner proceeded well. Henry was in good humor, deploying his dry, caustic wit with admirable precision. Mr. Thornton and Mr. Colthurst discovered one or two mutual subjects of interest, touching briefly upon them before reserving deeper discussion for private after-dinner talk. Margaret looked beautiful in the pomegranate flowers; and though she leaned back in her chair and spoke but little, Edith was not annoyed, for the conversation flowed on smoothly without her.

Margaret was watching Mr. Thornton's face. He never looked at her; so she might study him unobserved, noting the subtle shifts that even this short time had wrought in him. Only at some unexpected word from Mr. Lennox did his face flash out into the old look of intense enjoyment: the merry brightness returned to his eyes, the lips just parted to suggest the brilliant smile of former days; and for an instant, his glance instinctively sought hers, as if he wanted her sympathy. But when their eyes met, his whole countenance changed; he became grave and anxious once more; and he resolutely avoided even looking near her again during dinner.
