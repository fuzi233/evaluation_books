The Black Marks Become Magical.

The journey Tito Melema undertook to Rome, a move that cleared the path for Romola's own departure, had been decided upon with sudden urgency during a supper the previous evening. Tito had left for that meal buoyed by pleasant anticipations: delicate meats, choice wines, and a company of distinction awaited him. The venue was the Selva, or Orto de' Rucellai—the Rucellai Gardens—and the host, Bernardo Rucellai, epitomized the Florentine grandee. His very name carried a symbolic weight; derived from the *roccella*, a lichen growing on Greek isles and the Canaries that yields a rich purple dye under specific conditions, the surname reflected a lineage enriched by the importation of this secret, the *oricello*, from the Levant.

Bernardo stood out vividly against this purple heritage. He had married the sister of Lorenzo de' Medici, hosting a wedding of unparalleled splendor. Sent on embassies to France and Venice, elected Gonfaloniere, and a patron of the arts, he had constructed a magnificent palace and completed the black-and-white marble façade of Santa Maria Novella. He had cultivated a garden of rare trees, transforming it into classic ground for the Platonic Academy following Lorenzo's death, authored a learned treatise on ancient Rome, and possessed a pure Latinity. To summarize him was to write a laudatory epitaph so exhaustive that the Greek and Ausonian Muses might weep at the impossibility of nature combining such virtues within a single vessel.

Lorenzo Tornabuoni had conveyed Bernardo's invitation to Tito, with an emphasis suggesting political intent, though the public questions of the age were already consuming all attention. Tito, aware that Bernardo was one of the elect Twenty currently holding the reins of Florence, felt certain that the gathering served party purposes, enhanced by the flavors of stewed fish and aged Greek wine. This assurance lifted his spirits as he approached the Via della Scala. Without it, he might have feared the company would be as dull as the philosophical suppers he had endured in the gardens before, where he was often forced to accept and endure an exposition of the universe from its origins to its final ripeness.

It was a dark evening. Only when Tito crossed the intermittent light of a lamp suspended before an image of the Virgin did his tall, graceful figure, mantled and moving, become discernible. To any observer watching his passage from light to light, it would have appeared that he was constantly followed by a starkly different form: a thickset, elderly man in a serge tunic and felt hat. This conjunction might have seemed accidental amidst the street traffic, but when Tito halted at the gate of the Rucellai gardens, the figure behind him stopped as well. The *sportello*, the smaller door, stood ajar, held open by a servant distracted by another task. Tito turned in rapidly, giving his name, and passed between the evergreen bushes, which glowed like metal in the torchlight. The follower entered immediately after.

"Your name?" the servant asked.

"Baldassarre Calvo," came the immediate reply.

"You are not a guest; the guests have all passed."

"I belong to Tito Melema, who has just gone in. I am to wait in the gardens."

The servant hesitated. "I had orders to admit only guests. Are you a servant of Messer Tito?"

"No, friend, I am not a servant; I am a scholar."

There are those to whom one need only say, "I am a buffalo," in a tone of quiet confidence, and they will yield. The porter gave way instantly. Baldassarre entered, hearing the door close and chain behind him as he vanished among the shining bushes.

Those firm, ready answers signaled a profound transformation in Baldassarre since their last meeting, when the dagger had snapped in two. The change had manifested in a startling manner.

As the shadow of Tito passed before the hovel on his way home, Baldassarre sat in that peculiar state of after-tremor known to those prone to great emotional outbursts—a condition where physical paralysis is accompanied by an exceptional lucidity of thought, as if the dissipation of excited passion clears a fire-mist to reveal clarity beneath. He felt unable to rise; his limbs were benumbed, his hands shaking, his body cold. Yet, in this helplessness, he was surrounded not by the usual dimness and vanishing shadows, but by the crystal-clear images of the past. He was reliving a life that had seemed a long preparation for bitterness.

For several minutes, he was so absorbed by these visions that he failed to reflect on the fact that he was seeing them, noting the shift only when the sudden clearness traveled through the distance and rested upon the scene just concluded. He was fully present: he remembered Monna Lisa and Tessa. Ah! *He* was the mysterious husband; the one with another wife in the Via de' Bardi. It was time to retrieve the broken dagger and depart, leaving no trace. To conceal his feebleness was the only semblance of power remaining to him. He leaned to gather the fragments, then turned toward the book lying open beside him. It was a fine, large manuscript, an odd volume of Pausanias. In days past, he had known Pausanias intimately; yet an hour or two prior, he had stared at that page in hopelessness, the letters appearing no more meaningful than black weather-marks on a wall. Now, however, they were the magical signs conjuring a world. The moonbeam striking the letters resurrected Messenia before him, its struggle against Spartan oppression.

He snatched up the book, though the light was too pale for further reading. It mattered not; he knew the chapter. He read inwardly. He visualized the stoning of the traitor Aristocrates—cast out by a whole people to lie unburied, with a pillar erected bearing verses declaring how Time had restored justice to the unjust. The words arose within him, stirring innumerable vibrations of memory. He forgot his age; he could almost have shouted. The light had returned, the mother of knowledge and joy! In that exultation, his limbs regained their strength. He sprang up with his broken dagger and his book, stepping out under the broad moonlight.

The air was nipping with frost, yet Baldassarre felt no chill, only the glow of conscious power. He paced the open spaces of the high ground, gazing down upon the domed and towered city sleeping darkly beneath its mountain guardians, upon the pale gleam of the river, and the valley vanishing toward snow-capped peaks. He felt himself master of them all.

That sense of mental empire available to all in moments of exceptional clarity was intensified for Baldassarre by the long days and nights when memory had been little more than the awareness of something lost. The city, once a weary labyrinth, was now material he could subdue to his purposes. His mind scanned its affairs with flashing conjecture; he was once again a man who knew cities, whose vision was instructed by vast experience, savoring the keen delight of holding all things within the grasp of language. Names! Images! His mind rushed through this wealth without pause, like an heir entering a great inheritance.

Yet, amidst this rushing eagerness, one End presided in Baldassarre's consciousness—a dark deity in the innermost cell, seemingly forgotten only while his hecatomb was being prepared. Once the first triumph of recovered certainty had run its course, his thoughts centered on Tito. That fair, slippery viper could not escape him now. Thanks to struggling justice, the heart that had never quivered with tenderness for another possessed sensitive, selfish fibers that could be pierced by the sharp point of anguish. The soul that bowed to no right now bowed to the great lord of mortals, Pain.

He could now penetrate every secret of Tito's life. He knew some already, and the failure of the broken dagger, which had seemed like frustration, was merely the beginning of achievement. That sudden rage had shaken away the obstruction stifling his soul. Twice before, when his memory had partially returned, it had been due to sudden excitation: once defending himself from an enraged dog, once scrambling up a rock to escape the waves.

Yes, but if this time, as then, the light were to die out and the dreary conscious blank return! This time the light was stronger and steadier, yet what security existed that before morning the dark fog would not envelop him again? Even the fear seemed like the onset of feebleness. He thought with alarm that he might sink faster from this excited vigil on the hill, expending his vital force. Seeking anxiously for a sheltered corner, he finally nestled against a heap of warm garden straw and fell asleep.

When he opened his eyes, it was daylight. The first moments were filled with strange bewilderment: he was a man with a double identity. To which life had he awakened? To the existence of dim-sighted sensibilities, like the sad heirship of fallen greatness, or to the life of recovered power? Surely the latter, for the events of the night returned to him: the recognition of the page in Pausanias, the crowding resurgence of facts and names, the sudden wide prospect that had given him such a moment as the Maenad's glorious awakening on the mountain top.

He took up the book again, reading and remembering simultaneously. He saw a name, and images of deeds rose with it; he saw a deed mentioned and linked it to a name. There were stories of inexpiable crimes, but also tales of guilt that seemed successful. There were sanctuaries for swift-footed miscreants; baseness had its armor, and the weapons of justice sometimes broke against it. So what then? If baseness triumphed everywhere else, if it could heap upon itself all the goods of the world and even hold the keys of hell, it would never triumph over the hatred it had itself awakened. It could devise no torture greater than the torture of submitting to its smile. Baldassarre felt the indestructible, independent force of a supreme emotion that knew no terror, asked for no motive, and was itself an ever-burning motive consuming all other desire. And now, in this morning light, when the assurance came that the fine fibers of association were still active and his recovered self had not departed, all his gladness was but the hope of vengeance.

From that time until the evening he entered the Rucellai gardens, he had been incessantly, yet cautiously, inquiring into Tito's position and circumstances. Hardly a day passed without him contriving to follow his movements. He wished to arouse no alarm in Tito; he sought a moment when the hated favorite of blind fortune was at the summit of confident ease, surrounded by chief men on whose favor he depended. It was not retributive payment or personal recognition that bent his whole soul; it was to find the sharpest edge of disgrace and shame by which a selfish smiler could be pierced, to send through his marrow the most sudden shock of dread. He was content to lie hard and live stintedly—he had spent the greater part of his remaining money on another poniard. His hunger and thirst were for nothing exquisite but an exquisite vengeance. He had avoided addressing anyone suspected of intimacy with Tito, lest an alarm in Tito's mind urge him to flight or some counteracting measure devised by hard-pressed ingenuity. For this reason, he had never entered Nello's shop, which he knew Tito frequented, and had turned aside to avoid meeting Piero di Cosimo.

The possibility of frustration added eagerness to his desire that the great opportunity he sought should not be deferred. His desire was eager on another ground as well; he trembled lest his memory fail again. Whether from the agitating presence of that fear or other causes, he had twice felt a sort of mental dizziness, where the inward sense or imagination seemed to lose the distinct forms of things. Once he had attempted to enter the Palazzo Vecchio and make his way into a council chamber where Tito was, but had failed. But now, on this evening, he felt his occasion had come.
