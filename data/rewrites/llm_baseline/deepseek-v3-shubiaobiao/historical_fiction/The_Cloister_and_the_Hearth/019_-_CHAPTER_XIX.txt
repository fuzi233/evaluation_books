The first light of dawn had barely touched the sky when Gerard rose, shaken by the close call of the night before. He roused Martin, who blinked in surprise—he had half-expected the young man to vanish like a ghost through the window by now.

“We must leave this place,” Gerard said urgently. “Before the village stirs.”

Martin, ever practical, considered their options. “Only one road is safe,” he grunted. “Through the great forest. I know a path that will lead us to a hostel where you can get a swift horse. A day’s hard ride, and you’ll be beyond Holland’s reach.”

Peter’s cottage stood barely a furlong and a half from the forest’s edge. They set out quickly—Martin with his bow and three arrows (for it was Thursday), Gerard armed only with a stout oak staff Peter had pressed into his hand. Margaret, hiking her kirtle out of the damp, walked with them as far as the garden hedge, where Peter stood, uncharacteristically solemn, and gave Gerard his blessing.

The sun was just breaking over the horizon when they crossed the stony field toward the trees. They were halfway there when Margaret, glancing back, let out a sharp cry.

Ghysbrecht and his men were closing in.

No time for defiance—only flight. Margaret bolted first, her screams sharp with terror. Gerard and Martin followed, the shouts of their pursuers biting at their heels.

“Just reach the wood!” Martin roared.

They were fast, but Ghysbrecht was faster. The old burgomaster spurred his mule, cutting a wide arc to intercept them. Margaret clung to Gerard, but he shoved her aside—not roughly, but with desperate focus.

Ghysbrecht, in his zeal, had forgotten one truth: cornered things fight back.

Instead of dodging, Gerard charged straight at him, staff raised high. The oak came down with a crack like splitting stone, and Ghysbrecht toppled from his saddle, blood streaming down his face.

Then—into the trees.

Behind them rose a howl of fury. The hunt was on.

“Why run?” Gerard panted. “You’ve your bow, I’ve this—” He brandished the bloody staff.

“Boy,” Martin snarled, “the *gallows*.”

So they ran.

Margaret’s breath came in ragged gasps; Gerard’s fingers clenched white around his weapon. The forest loomed ahead, a tangle of safety—or so they hoped.

But the hazel coppice was a maze. Martin bulled through, shoving young trees aside like stalks of wheat. Paths branched and twisted; they took one, then another, doubling back without seeming to. At last, deep in a pine grove where the air was thick with resin and the ground soft with needles, Martin halted.

He sat, pulled bread and ham from his wallet, and ate as calmly as if they were picnicking.

Gerard stared. “They’ll never find us here?”

Martin snorted. “Not if Sevenbergen turned out in force.” Then his eyes narrowed. “You’ll lose that purse before Italy, lad.”

Gerard looked down. A heavy triangular purse was tangled in his belt. “This isn’t mine.” He worked it free with his knife. Inside—silver, and folded scraps of brown paper.

Margaret paled. “Ghysbrecht’s.”

Gerard laughed, exhilarated. “Torn from his saddle when I struck him! Spoils of war!”

Margaret bit her lip. “Throw it away, Gerard. Or let Martin return it. They already call you a thief—”

“Return it?” Gerard scoffed. “Not a coin. This was won fairly.”

Martin nodded. “Keep the silver. Send back the paper if it troubles you.”

Margaret’s voice dropped, pleading. “You’re going far. We need Heaven’s favor. How can we ask it if we take what isn’t ours?”

Gerard waved her off. “Heaven put it in my hand. Like the Israelites spoiling Egypt.”

She bowed her head. “You are my husband. Your will is mine.”

The meekness in her voice startled Martin. Gerard, touched, kissed her brow. Then they pressed on, deeper into the forest’s heart.

But triumph soured quickly.

“Martin,” Gerard muttered, “I struck too hard.”

Martin spat. “Grey hairs don’t make a fox less sly.”

“I saw them as my staff fell. They’ll haunt me.”

“Ha! If you’d cracked a younger skull, *then* I’d call it pity. Old men are done with living anyway.”

Gerard shuddered. “What if I’ve killed him?”

Margaret wrapped her arms around him. “You struck in defense—once, with what you held. Not in malice. If he’s dead, it’s by his own hate, not yours.”

Gerard clung to her words. But then—

A sound.

Deep, resonant, like a single toll of a bell.

Martin went rigid.

“What is it?” Gerard asked.

Margaret heard it next—a pure note hanging in the air.

Martin’s face turned ashen. “Echoes deceive. It comes from behind us.”

“What does it mean?”

Martin’s jaw tightened. “It means we find a better spot—to make our stand.”

“For what?”

“To die well,” said Martin, very low. “Taking three for one.”

Margaret’s hands flew to her mouth. “What *is* it?”

Martin met Gerard’s eyes.

“The avenger of blood.”

A beat.

Then, grimly:

“A hound.”
