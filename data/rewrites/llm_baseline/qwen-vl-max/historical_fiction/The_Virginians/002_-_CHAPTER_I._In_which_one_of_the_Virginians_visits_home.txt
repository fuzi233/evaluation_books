On the wall of a study in Boston, once occupied by one of America’s most celebrated writers, hang two crossed swords—each worn by a relative during the War of Independence. One belonged to a loyalist who fought for the Crown; the other, to a patriot who stood firm for liberty. Though their blades faced opposite causes, both were carried with courage and honor. The man who now owns these relics has earned a name respected on both sides of the Atlantic—where intellect and character are welcomed without question. This tale echoes those swords: it tells of two brothers born in Virginia, sons of the Old Dominion, who found themselves on opposing sides during the great conflict, yet reunited at its end not as enemies, but as kin, their bond unbroken by war.

In an English parlor adorned with polished wood and ancestral portraits, I once saw the likenesses of these two men—the one in scarlet, the other in blue and buff—hanging side by side. Their descendant showed me letters they wrote, books they read, papers they kept. These relics had long been treasured by a woman whose memory was as strong as her love for her children: Madam Esmond Warrington. To distinguish them from others bearing the same noble name, she called them “The Virginians”—a name that would come to define their legacy.

They lived at the edge of the old world, just before it faded into history. They knew kings and generals, courtiers and outlaws, soldiers and scholars. Through their letters, we glimpse figures who once walked the earth—men whose voices seem to rise from yellowed pages stained with ink, tears, and even blood. One letter bears a bullet hole; another is smeared with the blood of its writer. Such documents might have vanished if not for the care of one devoted woman—Madam Esmond—who saved every word her sons ever sent, from the first note of a boyish affection to the final message delivered hours before her death.

Among them was a letter from Henry, the younger twin, writing to his brother George, then recovering from a sprain at Castlewood, Virginia, thanking their grandfather for a horse he rode with his tutor. And later, a final missive: “From my beloved son,” received moments before she passed. She never journeyed to Europe except once, in the reign of King George II, with her parents. When Castlewood burned during the war, she fled to Richmond and became known thereafter as Madam Esmond—no longer concerned with the Warrington name, which she held in low regard compared to her own lineage.

These letters are sparse—not full narratives, but sketches, hints, glimpses of lives lived across continents. Perhaps I have misread their tone or imagined too much. Yet as I studied them, I tried to place myself in their shoes—to feel where they stood, whom they spoke to, what stirred beneath their words. I’ve drawn their characters as I believed them to be, shaped their conversations as I thought they might have sounded, and sought to breathe life into a past that feels almost within reach. Whether I succeeded, dear reader, you must judge.

It was a summer morning in 1756, under the reign of King George II, when the *Young Rachel*, a Virginia-bound ship under Captain Edward Franks, sailed up the Avon River after its annual voyage to the Potomac. With the tide, it glided toward Bristol and moored near Trail’s wharf, where Mr. Trail, part-owner of the vessel, watched from his counting house. Seeing the ship arrive, he rowed over and climbed aboard.

Mr. Trail, a solemn man with thick hair and a serious face, greeted Captain Franks warmly, congratulating him on a safe return. After offering thanks to Heaven, he turned to business: cargo, passengers, profits.

Franks, a cheerful fellow with a ready laugh, replied, “We’ve only one passenger—a young gentleman who took the state cabin—and this ugly negro boy fetching the trunks.”

“Confound your luck!” said Trail, frowning. “The *Duke William* brought fourteen passengers last week—half our size!”

“And this gentleman,” continued Franks, “doesn’t pay a penny. Swear now, it’ll do you good, Mr. Trail—it’s the best medicine I know.”

“A passenger taking the whole cabin and not paying? Good heavens, Captain Franks—are you mad?”

“Ask him yourself—he’s coming.”

And indeed, a young man stepped onto the deck. He was about nineteen, dressed in deep mourning, carrying a cloak and a sword. He called out cheerfully, “Gumbo, you fool—why aren’t you getting the baggage out? Well, shipmate, our journey ends today. You’ll see all the little ones tonight—Polly, Betty, Little Tommy. Give my love to Mrs. Franks. Yesterday I thought the voyage would never end. Now I’m almost sorry it’s over. That little berth in my cabin looks so cozy now I’m leaving it.”

Trail scowled at the free rider. He barely nodded as Franks introduced him. “This is Mr. Trail, sir—name you’ve heard of.”

“It’s well known in Bristol,” said Trail, stiffly.

“And this is Mr. Warrington—son of Madam Esmond Warrington of Castlewood,” added Franks.

At once, Trail removed his hat and bowed deeply, as if before royalty. “Gracious powers, Mr. Warrington! What a delight! How fortunate your voyage has been! Let me welcome you to England—my boat awaits. As the son of my benefactress, Mrs. Esmond Warrington, whose name is honored on Bristol Exchange, I’m honored to greet you. Isn’t it, Franks?”

“There’s no better tobacco than from Virginia,” said Franks, pulling out a brass box and stuffing a plug into his mouth. “You’ll take to it, sir—I promise. Won’t you, Mr. Trail? I wish you had ten shiploads instead of one. I’ve told Madam Esmond that—I’ve ridden through her plantation. She treats me like a lord. No waiting in the counting room like some folks do.” (He glanced pointedly at Trail.)

“She’s a true lady,” he added. “Could have a thousand hogsheads as easily as her hundreds—if only there were hands enough.”

“I’ve recently entered the Guinea trade,” said Trail, eager to impress. “I could supply her ladyship with any number of healthy young negroes by next fall.”

“We do not buy or sell people from Africa,” said the young man coldly. “My grandfather and mother opposed it. I cannot bear the thought of trading in human suffering.”

“But it’s for their good!” cried Trail. “Their temporal and spiritual salvation! Let me speak with you further at my home. I can offer you a Christian household, honest fare, and a warm welcome. Can’t I, Captain Franks?”

“Can’t say,” growled Franks. “Never invited me to dinner. Only once to psalm-singing and a sermon by Mr. Ward. Not my kind of entertainment.”

Ignoring the jab, Trail pressed on. “Business is business, young sir. As heir to Lady Esmond’s estate—do I speak to the rightful heir?”

The young man bowed slightly. “Yes.”

“And I urge you, at once, to expand your fortune. As your factor, I’d be remiss not to advise it. As a prudent man, should I hesitate to speak of profit? No, my dear Mr. George.”

“My name is not George,” said the youth, turning away. His eyes filled with tears. “It’s Henry.”

“Gracious powers!” cried Trail. “But you’re the heir—George Esmond Warrington, Esq.—”

“Hold your tongue, you fool!” roared Franks, striking Trail hard on the ribs. “Don’t you see the lad is weeping? Look at his black clothes!”

“What do you mean?” demanded Trail. “Mr. George is the heir—I know Colonel Esmond’s will.”

“Mr. George is there,” said Franks, pointing upward. “Up there. On the topmast—or beyond.”

“Where?”

“Mr. George is there,” repeated Franks, raising his finger to the sky. “He died a year ago, July 9th. He went with General Braddock to the Belle Riviere. A thousand men—every one—were killed. Cut down like wheat. You know how the Indians fight, Mr. Trail?” He ran a hand around his head.

“Horrible,” said Trail. “Truly horrible. He was a fine young man—just like this one, only with black hair—now lying in some Indian wigwam. He used to come aboard the *Young Rachel*. Always had his books opened on deck before landing. Shy, quiet—unlike this one, who was full of songs and laughter. When he heard the news, he collapsed. Took ill—fever from the Potomac swamps—but he’s recovered on this voyage. The sea does wonders. And now, the young gentleman can’t mourn forever. Since we sighted Ireland, he’s been happy again—though sometimes, when he laughs, he says, ‘I wish my dearest Georgy could share this with me.’ Mention the name, and he breaks down.”

And the captain himself blinked back tears.

Trail assumed a solemn air, prepared to offer condolences. But Henry Warrington refused his hospitality, stayed only long enough to drink wine and collect money, and left quickly. But he parted warmly with Captain Franks, and the crew cheered as he stepped ashore.

Harry Warrington and his brother had often studied maps of England, planning their return. Every American raised with love for the mother country—especially those of Anglo-Saxon descent—dreams of such a journey. They imagine the places their parents described, the lands whispered in stories. The word *Home*, as used by Americans toward Britain, carries a weight of longing few can understand.

Harry had mapped his route carefully: from Bristol to Bath, to Salisbury, Winchester, Hexton, and finally, Castlewood. He had planned every mile. Before London’s grand churches, the Tower, Whitehall, Shakespeare’s grave, and the battlefields of Derby, Falkirk, and Culloden—before all these sacred sites—there was one destination more sacred than any: Castlewood House, the family seat in Hampshire, where his parents’ stories had always led him.

As he traveled, the landscape unfolded like a dream. From the inn hall, the plump landlady watched him pass, surrounded by punch bowls and silver flagons. The chamberlain bowed him upstairs. The chambermaid curtsied. Gumbo, in the kitchen, boasted of his master’s wealth and grand estate.

The postchaise raced through scenery more beautiful than Harry had ever seen. For modern Americans, England’s woods and villages may seem idyllic. But for Harry, accustomed to swamps and log cabins, this was paradise. The roads were alive—wagons clattered, coaches flew, packhorses straggled, lords galloped past in gilt carriages. Farmers trotted to market, parsons jolted along on donkeys, children waved, milkmaids curtseyed. Church spires gleamed, cottages sparkled, elms rustled in the breeze.

To be nineteen, healthy, wealthy, traveling at nine miles an hour—what joy! But Harry had eyes only for home.

Finally, the chaise stopped at the rustic inn on Castlewood Green. The sign bore the Three Castles—the crest of the Esmond family. Above the gate of Castlewood House hung the same emblem—the hatchment of Francis, Lord Castlewood, now dead, his son ruling in his place.

Harry had heard much of Lord Francis. It was for Frank’s sake that Colonel Esmond gave up his claim to English titles and retired to Virginia. The late earl had been a wild youth, fought under Marlborough, married a foreign wife, embraced Catholicism, then returned to the Church of England. He had supported the Crown, earned a peerage from King George II, and died in peace.

Though Colonel Esmond loved him, Madam Esmond disliked her half-brother—finding him dull unless drunk, boisterous, and vulgar. The boys admired their grandfather more than their kinsman.

With a pounding heart, Harry approached the house where his grandsire had grown up. The village green sloped down to the river, spanned by an ancient stone bridge. Beyond rose the gray stone mansion, gabled and buttressed, backed by dark woods.

At the gate sat an old man on a stone bench. A dog curled at his feet. Above, a window framed with flowers revealed curious faces—girls watching the stranger in black and his ebony servant, also in mourning.

To Harry’s surprise, the old man greeted him by name.

“You’ve had a nice ride to Hextonth, Master Harry. The sorrel carried you well.”

“I think you’re Lockwood,” said Harry, voice trembling, extending his hand.

His grandfather had spoken often of Lockwood, who had served Colonel Esmond and Viscount Francis in Marlborough’s wars.

The old man blinked. “They say I forget things—but not that. Just now, my daughter asked, ‘Father, why are you in black?’ ‘Because my lord is dead,’ I said. ‘A foul blow. Master Frank is my lord now. And Master Harry—why, you’ve grown taller, changed your hair—but I know you.’”

A girl stepped out, curtseying. “Grandfather doesn’t remember well,” she said, pointing to her head.

“You’ve heard of Lockwood?” asked Harry.

“And you—have you heard of Colonel Francis Esmond?”

“Captain and Major in Webb’s Foot,” said Lockwood proudly. “Served him in two campaigns. Wasn’t I, Ponto?”

“The Colonel who married Viscountess Rachel—my late lord’s mother? Who went to live among the Indians? We’ve heard of him. We’ve his portrait in the gallery—he painted it himself.”

“He went to Virginia, died seven years ago. I am his grandson.”

“Lord, your honour!” cried Molly. “Your skin’s as white as mine! Grandfather, hear this? He’s Colonel Esmond’s grandson—sent us tobacco, came all the way from Virginia!”

“To see you, Lockwood,” said Harry. “And the family. I set foot in England yesterday. My first visit is to home. May I see the house—even if the family is away?”

Molly ventured, “Mrs. Barker will let your honour in.”

And so Harry Warrington crossed the courtyard, moving with ease as if he had been born there—while Molly followed, and Gumbo bowed profusely, whispering compliments.
