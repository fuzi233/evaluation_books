CHAPTER LXI. “Within the gentle heart Love shelters him, As birds within the green shade of the grove. Before the gentle heart, in Nature’s scheme, Love was not, nor the gentle heart ere Love.” —GUIDO GUINICELLI (_Rossetti’s Translation_).

In a quiet house near Pennicote, far from the white mansion where Rex Gascoigne lived, another heart stirred with sudden pain and restraint when news of Grandcourt’s death reached it. Hans Meyrick, ever punctual with the morning *Times*, slipped into the parlor as usual on a Wednesday, his latch-key turning softly in the lock. It was a day he always chose to deliver the paper himself—just as Mirah was finishing her weekly lesson with Mab, who was attempting to sing _Lascia ch’io pianga_, her voice wavering between imitation and hope.

But this time, Hans did not enter quietly. He burst into the room with a sharp crackle of paper, holding the newspaper aloft like a herald of fate. The music stopped instantly. Mirah, still at the piano, jerked upright, startled by the noise—something thunderous after the fragile silence of song. Mab frowned. “O-o-o, Hans! Why do you bring a more horrible noise than my singing?”

Mrs. Meyrick, sitting nearby, looked up sharply. “What is it? Any news from Italy? Have the Austrians given up Venice?”

“Not about Italy,” Hans replied, his tone oddly strained. “But something from there.”

The air thickened. Mrs. Meyrick leaned forward. “Nothing bad?” she asked, her voice trembling slightly—Deronda had been on her mind.

“Not bad for anyone we care about,” Hans said quickly, though his eyes flickered with something deeper. “Rather lucky, I’d say. I’ve never known anyone die so conveniently before. Given how much of a dear gazelle I am, I’m constantly amazed I’m still alive.”

Mab rolled her eyes. “If you must talk about yourself, do it behind your own back. What *is* it?”

Hans placed the paper before his mother, finger pointing to a paragraph. “Duke Alfonso is drowned. The Duchess is alive. That’s all.”

He paused, then added, “And here’s the best part—Deronda was in Genoa, staying at the same hotel. He saw her brought ashore by fishermen who pulled her from the water just in time. She jumped in after her husband. A bit rash, I must say. But Deronda was there to look after her. Lucky fellow.”

Mirah sank back onto the music stool, hands clasped tightly, eyelids lowered. Her breath came shallow. Mrs. Meyrick took the paper and handed it to Mab. “Poor thing,” she murmured. “She must have loved him dearly to leap in after him.”

Hans grinned, crinkling his face. “An oversight—a momentary lapse. Who could love a jealous baritone with icy glances, always singing aside? That was the husband’s role, no doubt. Nothing neater than his drowning. Now the Duchess is free to marry someone with warm eyes and good hair. And I’ll be invited to the wedding.”

At that, Mirah rose abruptly. Her gaze locked onto Hans, fierce and bright. “Mr. Hans,” she said, voice shaking, “you ought not speak so. Mr. Deronda would not like it. How can you call it luck? Life is not the same for one person as it is for another. You don’t know what it might mean to him—if he loved her. It could be a great sorrow. She would take him away from my brother. I know she would. Mr. Deronda would not call that lucky—would not call it joy to wound my brother’s heart.”

The room froze.

Hans sat transfixed, blushing deeply, as if struck. “I’m a fool,” he whispered, voice cracking. “A brute. I withdraw every word. I’ll go hang myself like Judas—if it’s allowed to mention him.”

Even in his remorse, his words held their old wit. But Mirah’s anger did not ease. It was too deep. She had spoken without thought, as a wounded creature bites its own flesh to endure pain. She said nothing more, only returned to the piano, pressing down on the sheet of music as if to begin again.

Mab broke the silence. “Mirah’s right, Hans. You always take Mr. Deronda’s name lightly. And joking about him marrying Mrs. Grandcourt—it’s awful. Men’s minds must be very dark,” she added, scornful.

“Quite true,” Hans muttered, rising and walking toward the back window, avoiding his mother’s glance.

“We should continue,” Mirah said, voice firmer now. “You haven’t finished your lesson, Mab. Shall I sing it again—or shall I sing it to you?”

“Oh, please sing it to me,” Mab said, eager to forget.

And Mirah sang—_Lascia ch’io pianga_—with such depth and power that even Hans stopped moving, leaning against the mantel, eyes averted. When the final note faded, she stood up. “I must go home. Ezra expects me.”

She offered her hand silently to Mrs. Meyrick, hesitating before kissing her cheek as she usually did. Instead, she lingered, ashamed. But the little woman drew her close, whispering, “God bless you, my dear.”

Mirah felt the weight of her outburst—the offense, the pride, the ungratefulness. She had hurt her friend, and now regret gnawed at her. Yet she could not speak.

Hans grabbed his hat. “Come on,” he said, opening the door.

“No,” Mab said, softening. “You’re not going to walk with her. I’m sure she’d rather not. You’re dreadfully disagreeable today.”

“I’ll go to see her safe,” Hans said, stepping outside.

Mirah said nothing. He opened the door, stepped beside her, and walked with her, uninvited but not forbidden.

They walked in silence. Mirah couldn’t bring herself to speak—not because she didn’t want to, but because she feared her words would be too harsh, too raw. She carried a storm inside: grief, jealousy, a terrible certainty. And yet, beneath it, a new strength had risen—one shaped by years of endurance. She had learned to hide, to bear, to act through pain.

Hans, meanwhile, was equally lost in thought. Mirah’s fury had pierced him. For the first time, he saw her not just as a kind girl, but as a woman whose heart beat fiercely for someone else. And that someone was Deronda.

He had long suspected a bond between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt. He had pieced together fragments—overheard conversations, subtle glances, Anna Gascoigne’s hints. He believed Deronda cared for her, though he masked it well. But he also believed Deronda would never choose Mirah—because Deronda was not Jewish, and Mirah had sworn she would never marry outside her faith. So he assumed Deronda’s affections were fixed elsewhere.

Now, Mirah’s anger had revealed something deeper. Not just concern—for her brother—but a passionate, personal wound. And that revelation made Hans melancholy—not just for her, but for himself. He had hoped, however faintly, that Deronda might return to her. But now he saw it differently: perhaps it was better that Deronda loved someone else. At least then Mirah wasn’t entirely alone in her sorrow.

It was a strange comfort. To see the beloved object suffer because her rival loved another—this was easier to bear than seeing her fully happy with him.

Still, Hans could not express it. He longed to show her tenderness, to make his presence speak of regret and care. But he said nothing. They walked side by side, two souls vibrating in silence, each carrying wounds they could not name.

When they reached Mirah’s door, Hans said, “Good-bye,” hand outstretched, eyes pleading.

She met his gaze—soft, sorrowful—and said, “Will you not come in and see my brother?”

He understood. It was forgiveness.

They entered. Mordecai sat by the fire, holding a letter, his face alight with quiet triumph. He looked almost radiant, as if he had conquered death itself.

After greetings, Mirah slipped into her room—not to cry, not to rage, but to gather herself. She removed her hat, sat down, pressed her temples as if her head ached. Then she rose, splashed cold water on her face and hair until her curls sparkled with droplets, like dew on a morning flower. She sighed deeply, put on her slippers, and sat still for a moment—long enough to feel the future press upon her.

Then she went downstairs to make tea.

Life had returned, in its old rhythm. She remembered rehearsals, performances, the need to hide her feelings. Pain had taught her endurance. And today, the storm had passed into a steady resolve.

But beneath the calm, a truth burned: she was no longer indifferent to Deronda. She had loved him—without knowing it. And now, she saw him slipping away, drawn toward a woman who belonged to another world.

She had once believed love was pure gratitude, reverence. But now it was something darker—jealousy, longing, a fierce, unwanted possession.

“What I have read, sung, seen—this is happening to me,” she whispered to her pillow. “This feeling is the love that makes jealousy.”

And she knew—no one else would understand. No one would see it. Like her early devotion to her mother, this pain was hers alone. But unlike that devotion, this was a flaw—something shameful, selfish, born of a nature that had grown too tender.

She had never truly believed Deronda could love her. He was from another world—bright, distant, belonging to a life of light and wealth, while hers was shadow and silence. But now, the image of Mrs. Grandcourt beside him was clear—sharp as pincers on her flesh.

Her soul, once peaceful, now burned with a passion she could not explain. She had been used to repugnance—toward certain people, certain lives. Now, that repugnance focused on Mrs. Grandcourt. She imagined her as cruel, false, dangerous—though she knew nothing of her.

“I could bear everything before,” Mirah whispered. “But this is worse. This is worse. I used not to have such feelings.”

Strange, that she should pray against a feeling connected to Deronda.

Later, Mordecai spoke to her, exalted by the news of Deronda’s return. “Seest thou, Mirah,” he said, “the _Shemah_—our confession of divine Unity—is the heart of our faith. It teaches that all humanity is one. In unity, the part holds the whole, and the whole holds the part. And so, human life moves toward the image of the Supreme Unity. When the soul grows spiritual, possession becomes universal, independent of the body. In this moment, I hold the joy of another’s future within me—joy I will not see, but which I love so deeply I could lay down my life upon its altar and say: ‘Burn, burn indiscernibly into that which shall be, which is my love and not me.’”

“Do you understand, Mirah?”

“A little,” she said faintly. “But my mind is too poor to feel it.”

“And yet,” Mordecai insisted, “women are made for the love that finds possession in renunciation. There is a story in the _Midrash_—of a Jewish maiden who loved a Gentile king. She entered prison, changed clothes with his beloved, and died in her place, so the king might live in happiness. That is the highest love—losing self in the object of love.”

“No, Ezra,” Mirah said, voice low but firm. “That was not it. She wanted the king to know what she had done. She wanted to be greater than the other. It was her pride, her desire to conquer—that made her die.”

Mordecai fell silent.

“If she had believed the king would never know,” he said slowly.

“You can imagine that,” Mirah replied, “because you are great, and like to think the greatest things possible. But I think it was not so. The girl had jealousy. She wanted to be first in his heart. That is why she died.”

“My sister,” Mordecai said gently, “you have read too many plays. Writers show passions as demons, without mercy or holiness. You judge by stories, not by your own heart—which is like our mother’s.”

Mirah said nothing.

And in that silence, the truth settled—like a stone dropped into still water. She had not chosen to feel this way. But now, she could not deny it. And the future, once bright, now seemed shadowed.
