For a time, I was content to live among the Little Ones, their innocence a balm to my weary soul. Yet soon, restless thoughts stirred within me—a gnawing sense that I was meant for more than idle comfort, that this world held mysteries I was bound to unravel. If I truly cared for these children, I owed it to them to understand the forces that shaped their lives.

Their growth was stunted—unnaturally so. Lona, their de facto mother, seemed no older than fifteen, yet she spoke as if she had always existed. The others, though wise in ways I could not grasp, knew nothing of time or age. They feared growth, equating it with the corruption of the giants. But was such fear justified? Could perfection truly demand stagnation? Life, I believed, was meant to flourish.

I longed to teach them—mathematics, music, the art of preserving their fleeting melodies in written form. Yet how could I guide them without first understanding the roots of their condition? My presence, I realized, might even endanger them further. The brutish giants already saw me as a threat; if they viewed the Little Ones as my accomplices, their fragile peace could shatter.

My resolve hardened: I would leave, if only to return wiser. But before I could prepare them, fate intervened.

One afternoon, as I spoke of my affection for them, they swarmed over me in a cascade of laughter—tiny limbs clinging, small bodies piling upon mine until I was buried beneath their joy. So absorbed were we that none noticed the approaching giant until his shadow fell across us.

A single cry—*"Take care, good giant!"*—and they scattered like leaves in a storm. Then came the blow: a club crashing against my skull, darkness swallowing me whole.

I awoke beneath a star-pricked sky, my head throbbing, my throat parched. The moment my ear touched the earth, I heard water—sweet, beckoning. Small hands pressed fruit to my lips until my thirst eased. Only then did I hear the soft, unfamiliar sound of weeping.

"You must go," Lona whispered, her voice steady despite the tears glinting in her eyes. "They will kill you if you stay wounded."

The others echoed her, their faces solemn. One boy, breathless from eavesdropping, reported the giant’s threats: *Wizard. Moles and squirrels. Knock him or no peace.*

I nodded, though every step sent pain lancing through me. "I’ll return when I’ve learned how to make you strong—how to grow without becoming *them*."

Their eyes darkened. "We *won’t* grow," they insisted. "We are strong now. You don’t understand."

And perhaps I didn’t.

They escorted me to the valley’s edge, feeding me as we walked, fretting over the "red juice" I’d lost. When the moon rose, Lona pressed her baby into my arms for a farewell kiss. "She will guide you," she said, nodding to the silver light. Then, softer: "The Cat-woman will not harm you."

The others chimed in with warnings—a desert-dwelling giantess, claws sharp, heart cruel. I stored the tales away, though my path lay elsewhere.

As they vanished into the night, their laughter ringing like wind chimes, I turned toward the unknown. Behind me, Lona walked apart, her child in her arms, her gaze lingering until the shadows claimed her.

The moon stretched my silhouette long and thin across the rocks. Somewhere ahead lay answers—or deeper questions. And though my heart ached to leave, I knew this was the only way to protect them.

To help, one must first learn. And to learn, one must sometimes walk alone.
