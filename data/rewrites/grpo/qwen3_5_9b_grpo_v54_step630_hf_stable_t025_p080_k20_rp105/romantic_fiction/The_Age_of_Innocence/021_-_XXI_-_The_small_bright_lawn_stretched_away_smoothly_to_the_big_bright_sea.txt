The small, sun-drenched lawn stretched out in a smooth, inviting expanse toward the vast, glittering sea. It was bordered by a vibrant rim of scarlet geraniums and coleus, while chocolate-colored cast-iron vases stood at intervals along the winding path, their garlands of petunias and ivy draping over the neatly raked gravel. Halfway between the cliff's edge and the square, chocolate-hued house—its verandah roof striped in yellow and brown to mimic an awning—two large targets were set against a backdrop of lush shrubbery. Opposite them, a real tent was pitched, surrounded by benches and garden seats. Ladies in flowing summer dresses and gentlemen in grey frock-coats and tall hats populated the lawn or sat upon the benches. Periodically, a slender girl in starched muslin emerged from the tent, bow in hand, to launch her shaft at one of the targets, drawing the spectators' gaze as they paused their chatter to watch the result.

Newland Archer stood on the verandah of the house, looking down with a mixture of curiosity and unease. Flanking the shiny painted steps were large blue china flower-pots resting on bright yellow stands, filled with spiky green plants, while below the verandah ran a wide border of blue hydrangeas edged with more red geraniums. Behind him, French windows offered glimpses, through swaying lace curtains, of glassy parquet floors dotted with chintz poufs, dwarf armchairs, and velvet tables laden with silver trifles.

The Newport Archery Club always held its August meeting at the Beauforts'. Though the sport, once the undisputed rival of croquet, was beginning to yield to lawn-tennis, the latter was still deemed too rough and inelegant for social display. The bow and arrow, however, retained their place as the perfect stage for showcasing pretty dresses and graceful attitudes.

Archer gazed down at the familiar spectacle with a sense of wonder, yet it struck him profoundly that life continued in the old ways while his own internal landscape had shifted entirely. Newport had been the catalyst that revealed the magnitude of this change. In New York, during the previous winter, after settling into their new greenish-yellow house with its bow-window and Pompeian vestibule, he had retreated with relief into the old routines of his office. The daily grind served as a tether to his former self, complemented by the pleasurable excitement of selecting a showy grey stepper for May's brougham and the abiding interest of arranging his new library—a project realized despite family doubts, featuring dark embossed paper, Eastlake bookcases, and "sincere" armchairs. At the *Century* and the *Knickerbocker*, he had rediscovered Winsett and the fashionable young men of his set. With hours dedicated to law, dining out, entertaining, and occasional evenings at the Opera, his life had seemed a fairly real, inevitable business.

But Newport represented an escape from duty into an atmosphere of unmitigated holiday-making. Archer had tried to persuade May to spend the summer on a remote Maine island called Mount Desert, where hardy Bostonians and Philadelphians camped in "native" cottages, enjoying enchanting scenery and a wild, trapper-like existence. Yet the Wellands always went to Newport, where they owned one of the square boxes on the cliffs. Archer could offer no good reason why he and May should not join them. As Mrs. Welland had rather tartly pointed out, it was hardly worth May wearing herself out trying on summer clothes in Paris if she were not allowed to wear them. This argument, logical and pleasant, left Archer with no answer.

May herself could not comprehend his obscure reluctance. She reminded him that he had always loved Newport in his bachelor days—an indisputable fact—and he could only profess that he was certain he would like it better now that they were together. But as he stood on the Beaufort verandah, watching the brightly peopled lawn, a shiver ran through him; he knew he would not like it at all.

It was not May's fault, poor dear. If, now and then, during their travels, they had fallen slightly out of step, harmony was restored by returning to the conditions she was used to. He had always foreseen that she would not disappoint him, and he had been right. He had married, as most young men did, because he had met a perfectly charming girl at the moment when a series of aimless sentimental adventures ended in premature disgust. She represented peace, stability, comradeship, and the steadying sense of an unescapable duty.

He could not claim to have been mistaken in his choice; she had fulfilled all his expectations. It was undoubtedly gratifying to be the husband of one of New York's handsomest and most popular young married women, especially when she was also one of the sweetest-tempered and most reasonable of wives. Archer had never been insensible to such advantages. As for the momentary madness that had fallen upon him on the eve of his marriage, he had trained himself to regard it as the last of his discarded experiments. The idea that he could ever, in his senses, have dreamed of marrying the Countess Olenska had become almost unthinkable; she remained in his memory simply as the most plaintive and poignant of a line of ghosts.

Yet, these abstractions and eliminations left his mind feeling empty and echoing, which explained why the busy, animated people on the Beaufort lawn shocked him as if they were children playing in a graveyard.

He heard a murmur of skirts beside him, and the Marchioness Manson fluttered out of the drawing-room window. As usual, she was extraordinarily festooned and bedizened, with a limp Leghorn hat anchored by windings of faded gauze and a little black velvet parasol absurdly balanced over her much larger hatbrim.

"My dear Newland, I had no idea that you and May had arrived! You yourself came only yesterday, you say? Ah, business—business—professional duties... I understand. Many husbands find it impossible to join their wives here except for the weekend." She cocked her head on one side and languished at him through screwed-up eyes. "But marriage is one long sacrifice, as I used often to remind my Ellen..."

Archer's heart stopped with a queer jerk, the same sensation he had felt once before, seeming suddenly to slam a door between himself and the outer world. But this break of continuity was brief, for he soon heard Medora answering a question he had apparently found voice to ask.

"No, I am not staying here, but with the Blenkers, in their delicious solitude at Portsmouth. Beaufort was kind enough to send his famous trotters for me this morning, so that I might have at least a glimpse of one of Regina's garden-parties; but this evening I go back to rural life. The Blenkers, dear original beings, have hired a primitive old farm-house at Portsmouth where they gather about them representative people..." She drooped slightly beneath her protecting brim and added with a faint blush, "This week Dr. Agathon Carver is holding a series of Inner Thought meetings there. A contrast indeed to this gay scene of worldly pleasure—but then I have always lived on contrasts! To me, the only death is monotony. I always say to Ellen: Beware of monotony; it's the mother of all the deadly sins. But my poor child is going through a phase of exaltation, of abhorrence of the world. You know, I suppose, that she has declined all invitations to stay at Newport, even with her grandmother Mingott? I could hardly persuade her to come with me to the Blenkers', if you will believe it! The life she leads is morbid, unnatural. Ah, if she had only listened to me when it was still possible... When the door was still open... But shall we go down and watch this absorbing match? I hear your May is one of the competitors."

Strolling toward them from the tent, Beaufort advanced over the lawn, tall, heavy, and too tightly buttoned into a London frock-coat, with one of his own orchids in his buttonhole. Archer, who had not seen him for two or three months, was struck by the change in his appearance. In the hot summer light, his floridness seemed heavy and bloated, and but for his erect, square-shouldered walk, he would have looked like an over-fed and over-dressed old man.

Rumors abounded about Beaufort. In the spring, he had gone off on a long cruise to the West Indies in his new steam-yacht, and it was reported that at various points where he had touched, a lady resembling Miss Fanny Ring had been seen in his company. The steam-yacht, built in the Clyde and fitted with tiled bathrooms and other unheard-of luxuries, was said to have cost him half a million; and the pearl necklace he had presented to his wife on his return was as magnificent as such expiatory offerings are apt to be. Beaufort's fortune was substantial enough to stand the strain, yet disquieting rumors persisted, not only in Fifth Avenue but in Wall Street. Some said he had speculated unfortunately in railways; others that he was being bled by one of the most insatiable members of her profession. To every report of threatened insolvency, Beaufort replied with fresh extravagance: building a new row of orchid-houses, purchasing a new string of race-horses, or adding a new Meissonnier or Cabanel to his picture gallery.

He advanced toward the Marchioness and Newland with his usual half-sneering smile. "Hullo, Medora! Did the trotters do their business? Forty minutes, eh? ... Well, that's not so bad, considering your nerves had to be spared." He shook hands with Archer, then, turning back with them, placed himself on Mrs. Manson's other side and said, in a low voice, a few words their companion did not catch.

The Marchioness replied with one of her queer foreign jerks and a "_Que voulez-vous?_" which deepened Beaufort's frown, but he produced a good semblance of a congratulatory smile as he glanced at Archer to say, "You know May's going to carry off the first prize."

"Ah, then it remains in the family," Medora rippled. At that moment, they reached the tent, and Mrs. Beaufort met them in a girlish cloud of mauve muslin and floating veils.

May Welland was just coming out of the tent. In her white dress, with a pale green ribbon about the waist and a wreath of ivy on her hat, she possessed the same Diana-like aloofness she had shown when entering the Beaufort ballroom on the night of her engagement. In the interval, not a thought seemed to have passed behind her eyes or a feeling through her heart. Though her husband knew she had the capacity for both, he marvelled anew at the way experience dropped away from her.

She held her bow and arrow, placing herself on the chalk-mark traced on the turf. She lifted the bow to her shoulder and took aim. The attitude was so full of classic grace that a murmur of appreciation followed her appearance, and Archer felt the glow of proprietorship that so often cheated him into momentary well-being. Her rivals—Mrs. Reggie Chivers, the Merry girls, and divers rosy Thorleys, Dagonets, and Mingotts—stood behind her in a lovely, anxious group, brown heads and golden bent above the scores, with pale muslins and flower-wreathed hats mingling in a tender rainbow. All were young and pretty, bathed in summer bloom, but none possessed the nymph-like ease of his wife, who, with tense muscles and a happy frown, bent her soul upon some feat of strength.

"Gad," Archer heard Lawrence Lefferts say, "not one of the lot holds the bow as she does"; and Beaufort retorted, "Yes; but that's the only kind of target she'll ever hit."

Archer felt irrationally angry. His host's contemptuous tribute to May's "niceness" was just what a husband should have wished to hear said of his wife. The fact that a coarse-minded man found her lacking in attraction was simply another proof of her quality; yet the words sent a faint shiver through his heart. What if "niceness" carried to that supreme degree were only a negation, the curtain dropped before an emptiness? As he looked at May, returning flushed and calm from her final bull's-eye, he had the feeling that he had never yet lifted that curtain.

She accepted the congratulations of her rivals and the rest of the company with the simplicity that was her crowning grace. No one could ever be jealous of her triumphs because she managed to give the feeling that she would have been just as serene if she had missed them. But when her eyes met her husband's, her face glowed with the pleasure she saw in his.

Mrs. Welland's basket-work pony-carriage was waiting for them, and they drove off among the dispersing carriages, May handling the reins and Archer sitting at her side.

The afternoon sunlight still lingered upon the bright lawns and shrubberies, and up and down Bellevue Avenue rolled a double line of victorias, dog-carts, landaus, and "vis-à-vis," carrying well-dressed ladies and gentlemen away from the Beaufort garden-party or homeward from their daily afternoon turn along the Ocean Drive.

"Shall we go to see Granny?" May suddenly proposed. "I should like to tell her myself that I've won the prize. There's lots of time before dinner."

Archer acquiesced, and she turned the ponies down Narragansett Avenue, crossed Spring Street, and drove out toward the rocky moorland beyond. In this unfashionable region, Catherine the Great, always indifferent to precedent and thrifty of purse, had built herself in her youth a many-peaked, cross-beamed *cottage-orné* on a bit of cheap land overlooking the bay. Here, in a thicket of stunted oaks, her verandahs spread themselves above the island-dotted waters.
