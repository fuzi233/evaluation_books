Gumbo’s lamentation began the instant the tidings were out—that Mr. Harry had gone a-volunteering, and gone without him. The poor fellow flung himself upon his grief with all the force of his lungs, as though sorrow were a bellows to be worked for other people’s comfort. He ran straightway to Mr. Lambert’s lodgings, for wherever that dusky youth was, there the maid-servants were sure to be found; and where the women were, he knew there would be pity, a hand to his sleeve, a corner of apron offered, a word that softened the blow.

The kitchen at Dean Street received him as a kind of chapel. There were tears, there were howls, there were sympathetic sniffings; and then, because the heart will not beat long on emptiness, there was victualling. Gumbo, who could grieve with a noble sincerity and eat with a noble perseverance, fortified Nature with beer drawn cool from the cellar and cold meat taken in thick, confident slices. The smell of malt rose like a warm fog; the fat of yesterday’s joint held the savour of pepper and char; knives tapped on trenchers; the spits in imagination still turned. If he told lies (as he did), if he was idle (as he was), if he was greedy (past denial), still the maids cried with him and called him brother; Europe, at least in that house, was not squeamish about Africa, and took the colour of a man’s skin for no more than the colour of the pot that holds good broth.

Hetty found him at one of his great bursts—roaring that Master Harry had gone a “volumteer,” and had not taken him. He would have saved Master Harry’s life any day; would have been cut into “twenty thousand hundred pieces” for him—so he swore between sobs and swallows. Hetty, who had laughed once upon a time at such stage-tears, did not laugh now. She slipped him half-a-crown with a hand that trembled, and even her pert tongue, which used to crack its little jokes like a cook snapping beans, grew gentle and shy.

Indeed, the whole house beheld a marvel: Hetty Lambert transformed. She moved about as if afraid of making a noise in a sick-room. She was humble to mamma; thankful to John and Betty for the smallest service at table; patient to Polly when the comb pulled at her hair; long-suffering when Charley from school stamped upon her toes or tumbled her workbox. In papa’s company she was silent—silent, and pale. If Mr. Lambert had commanded her to roast the leg of mutton, or walk to church arm-in-arm with Gumbo, she would have curtseyed and said, “Yes, if you please, dear papa!”

Roast the leg of mutton! She could scarce endure the smell of it when it came in, brown and crackling, the fat hissing as it met the hot dish, rosemary bruised under the knife, gravy steaming and sweet with onion; for what sort of meal, she asked herself, were the poor volunteers having with cannon-balls flying about their heads? Church! At the prayer in time of war, her knees knocked together upon the hassock; the very hush of the pew seemed full of distant drums. When the parson read, “Thou shalt do no murder,” from the communion-rail, she ducked her head as if the words were aimed at her, and fancied the holy man’s eyes were upon her secret.

She thought of travellers by land and water. She ran to the newspaper with a sickness rising in her throat—sickness not from any kitchen-smell now, but from ink and dread. She watched papa when he came home from the Ordnance Office—watched his face as if it were a dish-lid that might lift and show either comfort or ruin beneath. Is he well? Is he a General yet? Is he wounded, taken? Or—oh, hideous picture—are both his legs shot away, like that pensioner they had seen in Chelsea Garden t’other day? She made herself vows as rapidly as a cook ties up herbs. She would go on wooden legs all her life if his could but bring him safe home; she ought never to rise from her knees until he was returned.

“Haven’t you heard of people, Theo,” she said, “whose hair has grown grey in a single night? I shouldn’t wonder if mine did—shouldn’t wonder in the least.” And she peered in the looking-glass, searching for that sudden winter in her own curls.

“Hetty dear, you used not to be so nervous when papa was away in Minorca,” Theo answered.

“Ah, Theo! one may very well see that George is not with the army, but safe at home,” cried Hetty; at which the elder sister blushed, and looked away, pensive as a woman who hears her own heart named aloud. For if Mr. George had been in the army—why, that would have been another pair of boots.

And yet, while the household trembled and prayed and made little domestic rites of fear—bread cut, candles watched, newspapers unfolded—Harry Warrington, for the present, was as safe as any officer of the Life Guards at Regent’s Park Barracks.

His first taste of war might be called successful; it was certainly not glorious. The British Lion cannot always find a tiger that will stand to be torn. Sometimes he goes prowling for a worthy foe and finds only a goose; sometimes, bellowing for a single tiger, he spies six. If he fights six tigers, you may call him an ass; if he retreats, you may call him prudent. Harry’s first feat was of this kind—more smoke than steel, more marching than fighting, more burnt tar than spilt blood.

On the 1st of June, he and about thirteen thousand others went aboard at the Isle of Wight; and on the 5th, at daybreak, the fleet stood into the Bay of Cancale in Brittany. From the crowded decks the gentlemen volunteers had leisure to gaze at the French coast—green slopes, hedgerows deep with leaf, a few cattle like pale toys in the distance, a little stir of dragoons who, catching sight of our ships, trotted off as if the very salt air were British. A small fort with two guns ventured to fire at the Commander-in-Chief and the Commodore while they reconnoitred in a cutter; the insolence of those two little mouths of iron made an absurd, sharp music over the water—like a kitchen-pan struck in anger.

By two o’clock the fleet anchored, and signals flew. Grenadier companies from eleven regiments were ordered into flat-bottomed boats to assemble round the Commodore’s ship, the Essex. Mr. Howe, hoisting his broad pennant on the Success frigate, went in as near shore as he dared, the other frigates following to cover the landing. With Lord George Sackville and General Dury in command, the volunteers, the grenadiers, and three battalions of Guards pulled in.

But the French, who should by all the rules of romance have stayed to receive their enemies, ran. The frigates soon silenced the little fort; the troops assaulted it; the whole garrison was taken—being, in truth, one old gentleman who had fired his two guns gallantly and received a ball in the leg for his pains. “If every Frenchman had acted like me,” said he to his conquerors, “you would not have landed at Cancale at all.” There was a kind of homely greatness in the speech—as if some village elder had salted his honour and set it by for hard times.

The detachment took possession of Cancale, and lay on their arms all night. Harry’s comrades rallied him for his eagerness to be upon the war-path, and to bring home French scalps like a story-book savage. Yet no man fell under his tomahawk; the only blood spilt that day was of a French gentleman riding with his servant, surprised by volunteer Lord Downe in front with a company of Kingsley’s. My Lord offered quarter; the gentleman refused it; and he, the servant, and both horses were shot. Thus did pride make a quicker meal of death than any kitchen could serve.

Next day the whole force landed and advanced towards St. Malo. The villages emptied as the troops came; doors shut, dogs silent, hens left in the road with nobody to call them home. The lanes were so narrow in places that the men marched single file between high leafy hedges; had there been an enemy in earnest, a handful of peasants might have picked them off at leisure from behind green coverts. But no enemy stirred. At nightfall they reached St. Malo and were saluted with artillery fire that did little in the darkness except add a harsh clangour to the sea-wind. Under that cover, the British fired ships, wooden buildings, pitch and tar magazines in the harbour; and the blaze rose with a roar and a bitter perfume—rosin and pine, tar oozing and catching, a sweetness of burnt wood turned acrid—until the whole night seemed a vast cookery gone mad, flames licking masts as if they were spits.

It was all done with scarcely an attempt by the French to molest the force. Though it was asserted there was a considerable French strength in the town, they would not come out; and so the Duke of Marlborough and Lord George Sackville, judging it not their business to force a nest that would not fly, marched back to Cancale and got aboard again.

Exactly four weeks after leaving England, Harry found himself at Portsmouth once more. He wrote to his brother George; and George, receiving the letter, ran with it straight to Dean Street, where the Lambert family sat at breakfast with the comfortable clink of cups and the steady domestic sound of knives upon bread.

“Glorious news, ladies!” George cried, bursting in. “Our champion has come back. He has undergone all sorts of dangers, but has survived them all. He has seen dragons—upon my word, he says so.”

“Dragons! What do you mean, Mr. Warrington?” asked Hetty, half starting up, as if the word itself were cannon-fire.

“But not killed any—he says so, as you shall hear.” And George opened the letter and read, tasting his brother’s spelling and soldier’s brag as if it were some rough wine shipped home from sea.

“‘DEAREST BROTHER,—I think you will be glad to hear that I am returned, without any commission as yet; without any wounds or glory; but,—at any rate, alive and harty. On board our ship, we were almost as crowded as poor Mr. Holwell and his friends in their Black Hole at Calicutta. We had rough weather, and some of the gentlemen volunteers, who prefer smooth water, grumbled not a little. My gentlemen’s stomachs are dainty; and after Braund’s cookery and White’s kick-shaws, they don’t like plain sailor’s rum and bisket. But I, who have been at sea before, took my rations and can of flip very contentedly: being determined to put a good face on everything before our fine English macaronis, and show that a Virginia gentleman is as good as the best of ’em. I wish, for the honour of old Virginia, that I had more to brag about. But all I can say in truth is, that we have been to France and come back again. Why, I don’t think even your tragick pen could make anything of such a campaign as ours has been. We landed on the 6 at Cancalle Bay, we saw a few dragons on a hill…’”

“There!” George broke off, laughing. “Did I not tell you there were dragons?”

“Mercy! What can he mean by dragons?” Hetty cried.

“Immense, long-tailed monsters, with steel scales on their backs, who vomit fire, and gobble up a virgin a day,” said papa. “Haven’t you read about them in The Seven Champions? Seeing St. George’s flag, I suppose, they slunk off.”

“I have read of ’em,” said the solemn little boy from the Chartreux. “They like to eat women. One was going to eat Andromeda, you know, papa; and Jason killed another, who was guarding the apple-tree.”

George went on: “‘…A few dragons on a hill,’ who rode away from us without engaging. We slept under canvass. We marched to St. Malo, and burned ever so many privateers there. And we went on board shipp again, without ever crossing swords with an enemy or meeting any except a few poor devils whom the troops plundered. Better luck next time! This hasn’t been very much nor particular glorious: but I have liked it for my part. I have smelt powder, besides a good deal of rosn and pitch we burned. I’ve seen the enemy; have sleppt under canvass, and been dredful crowdid and sick at sea. I like it. My best compliments to dear Aunt Lambert, and tell Miss Hetty I wasn’t very much fritened when I saw the French horse.—Your most affectionate brother, H. E. WARRINGTON.’”

One might have thought Miss Hetty’s conscience would have been soothed by the plain words—alive and hearty. It was not so. Relief did not come as a clean draught of water, but as a broth clouded with fear: warming, yet still full of floating specks. The war had put a new flavour into her blood, and she could not taste anything without tasting it.

Mr. Lambert, in the course of his duties, went to Portsmouth and the Isle of Wight; and George accompanied him. They found Harry wonderfully altered by his little campaign: the air and the movement and the rough fare had scoured the languor out of him as salt scours a pan. He was brisk, sun-touched, eager to learn, cheerful in camp like a boy turned loose into a hard but wholesome school. Under canvas, in glorious weather, he took to his new duties with a hunger that was not for food alone, but for action; and George, pacing the streets of the warlike town arm-in-arm with him, was amused to see this once listless coffee-house lounger now quickened and made sharp-edged by drums, marching, and the clean insistence of open air.

The nation too was in high spirits. This expedition had been no epic; yet London spoke of St. Malo as if Troy had been taken. We were said to have humbled the proud Gaul. We should have vanquished him, it was cried, had he dared to appear. Such bragging is not the monopoly of any time; every people has its trumpet, and every trumpet plays loudest when it is far from the smoke. Captured treasures were sent home and exhibited. More laurels were demanded. The army, like a table once set and found agreeable, was called for again: another course, another dish of glory.

With the new expedition sailed a volunteer so distinguished that all other amateur warriors must give place: the sailor Prince, His Royal Highness Prince Edward. He was conveyed aboard the Essex in the ship’s twelve-oared barge, the standard of England flying in the bow; the Admiral followed, and then the captains in seniority—a pageant of rank over the salt water, oars dipping with a steady, disciplined splash that made a music pleasant to hear, not like the grinding and hammering noises of a later age, but clean and measured, as if the sea itself were being stroked into obedience.

Away went the fleet. Harry, in high health and spirits, waved his hat to his friends cheering on the shore. His commission must come. George thought there could be no difficulty: there was money enough to buy an ensigncy if need be; but to win it without purchase—by gallantry and good conduct—would be best. The colonel reported well of him; men and officers liked him; there was promise in the young fellow’s bearing.

And then, ten days after the sailing, London rang with famous news. On the 7th and 8th of August, his Majesty’s troops had landed in the Bay des Marais, two leagues west of Cherbourg, in the face of a large body of the enemy. Awed by British valour, that large body vanished—so the story ran, and so stories always run when told far from the place where men actually sweat and bleed. Cherbourg surrendered at discretion; English colours flew upon three outlying forts. Seven-and-twenty ships were burned in the harbours; brass cannon in prodigious number were taken; iron guns destroyed; the basin (about which the French had bragged) demolished; the two piers at the harbour’s mouth ruined. London’s bells and London’s tongues were never tired.

Just as Mr. Howe’s guns arrived from Cherbourg, Mr. Wolfe’s colours taken at Louisbourg came too. The colours went from Kensington to St. Paul’s under escort—life-guards, horse-grenadiers, standards, kettle-drums, trumpets; and at the very minute they were received at the West Gate by the Dean and Chapter, the Tower and Park guns saluted—bang, bong, bung—till the very air shook with it. One could almost believe the city lived upon noise as upon bread, and that the loudness of its rejoicing was taken for the measure of its virtue; yet those who loved quieter sounds—kettle simmering, rain on a window, the steady chew of a contented household—might have thought such thunder a poor substitute for peace.

Next day came the procession of Cherbourg cannon and mortars—great pieces with carvings and flaunting emblems, lilies and crowns and mottoes. Here was the Téméraire, the Malfaisant, the Vainqueur (the Vainqueur indeed! a pretty conqueror of Britons!), and many more, trailed through the streets while people shouted till their throats were raw. In Dean Street, Mrs. Lambert and Hetty, in their tender ignorance, were half persuaded that Harry had taken every one of the guns himself—dragging them from batteries, scattering artillerymen like crumbs from a board. His name rose in their estimation as bread rises under a warm cloth.

Madame de Bernstein asked about him. Lady Maria begged her cousin George to see her and, if possible, bring news of his brother. George, who had been the head of the family a couple of months since, found himself deposed and of scarce any account in Miss Hetty’s eyes. Wit and learning and tragedies—what were these to victories and brass cannon? George bore his deposition with meekness. Fifteen thousand Britons! Why should they not march and take Paris? Nothing more probable, thought some of the ladies; and they embraced and congratulated one another, their cheeks glowing red as if lit from within by the same candles of joy that shone in their eyes. For once they almost longed Sir Miles and Lady Warrington were in town, that they might call and ask, “Well, what say you now? Has not your nephew taken twenty-one fine brass cannon, flung a hundred and twenty iron guns into the water, seized seven-and-twenty ships, and destroyed the basin and the two piers?”

But after fair weather comes cloud. Appetite grows by what it feeds on; and our commanders, thinking they had not feasted enough upon the plunder of St. Malo, went back. After a brief time at Portsmouth and the Wight, the conquerors of Cherbourg returned to that coast. They landed in the Bay of St. Lunar, marched towards St. Malo intending to destroy it this time; but the harbour of St. Lunar was found insecure, the fleet moved up to St. Cas to keep communication, and then—the British Lion found that St. Malo, which he had thought to swallow at a mouthful, was guarded by a French army brought up by the Governor of Brittany. The coup-de-main was impossible. The leaders marched back towards their ships lying in the Bay of St. Cas.

Hide, blushing glory, hide St. Cas’s day.

As the troops marched down to embark, they became aware of an army following—sent from Brest. Two-thirds of our troops, and all the artillery, were already aboard when the French came down upon the remainder. Four companies of the First Regiment of Guards and the grenadier companies faced about upon the beach, to wait the enemy while the boats carried off the rest.

As the French descended from the heights that ring the bay, our Guards and grenadiers marched out to attack them—leaving a strong position they had held, a great dyke raised on the shore behind which they might have made a stout defence. Then it was eleven hundred against six, nay ten times their number; and after a while the little line broke and ran for the boats—a sauve qui peut. Seven hundred out of the eleven hundred were killed, drowned, or taken. The General himself was killed. And—ah!—where were the volunteers?

A man of peace might confess that he thinks less of the engaged troops than of the people they leave behind. Jack the Guardsman and La Tulipe of the Royal Bretagne are face to face striving to knock each other’s brains out; that is their trade, as bears fight bears, as lions rend lions. But the girl of Tower Hill who hung about Jack’s neck before he marched away; the lass at Quimper who gave La Tulipe his brûle-gueule and tobacco-box before his noir trajet—what have those tender hearts done that they should pay so dear?

What a state must Miss Hetty Lambert be in, when she hears of the disaster at St. Cas and the slaughter of the grenadier companies! What doubt gnaws at George Warrington; what grief and helpless commiseration fills Martin Lambert as he looks into his child’s face and reads her piteous story there! Howe, brave Commodore, rowing in his barge under enemy fire, has rescued scores of our flying men. More are drowned; hundreds are prisoners, or shot upon the beach. Among these—where is our Virginian?
