The land stretched beneath Calder like a wound—dry, cracked, and unyielding. As the steamer chugged down the Nile from Wadi Halfa toward Assouan, he leaned against the railing of the upper deck, watching the world unfold in shades of dust and sun. The river was narrow here, hemmed in by barren rock and sand, shaped into crude pyramids by time and wind. Only a thin ribbon of green clung to the banks—a fragile defiance against the heat and emptiness. It was a harsh place, ruled by silence and indifference.

Below, lashed to the side of the steamer, a barge bobbed gently. On its lower deck, amid a cluster of native passengers, stood an angareb—a simple bed of woven matting over four legs. Upon it lay a figure, wrapped tightly in a black veil, motionless as stone. Two Arab men sat near the stern, laughing and talking, their eyes never lingering on the still form. To them, it might have been death or merely deep sleep—no matter. They had no care for it.

Calder watched. He could not tell if the body beneath the veil was man or woman. The fabric clung close around the face, tracing the hollows of the eyes, the curve of the nose, the line of the mouth. But nothing revealed gender. Not a breath stirred the cloth. Not a limb twitched. Yet the sun crept forward, relentless, crawling up the dark shape until it blazed upon the face. Still, there was no movement. No flicker. No sign of life.

He shouted across the gap. “Move that bed into the shade! Now!”

The two men glanced up, grumbled, then obeyed. One of them stepped forward, dragging the angareb into the shadow cast by the upper deck.

“Is it a man or a woman?” Calder asked.

“A man,” one replied. “We’re taking him to the hospital in Assouan. But we don’t think he’ll make it. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago.”

“You give him nothing to eat or drink?”

“He’s too ill.”

It was a familiar story—the kind that echoed through this land where fate was written in the stars and suffering seen as inevitable. The man likely began with only a bruise, something easily healed. But no one touched him. No water. No food. No cleansing. The wound festered. Gangrene set in. And only when the local Mudir discovered him did he finally get moved—on this steamer, to die slowly under the sun.

Yet Calder could not let it go. There was something about the stillness—so complete, so final—that gripped him. When a strong wind sprang up at dusk, blowing upstream and rattling the rigging, he felt a strange relief. At least the air would cool the man’s skin. Maybe, just maybe, it would ease his pain.

That evening, at dinner, a man with a German accent spoke beside him. Calder interrupted abruptly.

“You’re not a doctor, by any chance?”

“No,” the man said, smiling behind his spectacles. “I’m a medical student from Bonn. I came from Cairo to see the Second Cataract—but they wouldn’t let me past Wadi Halfa.”

Calder didn’t hesitate. “Then I’ll borrow your holiday. I need your help.”

“For yourself? With pleasure,” the student replied. “Though I’d never have guessed you were ill.”

“I’m not. It’s the man on the angareb.”

“The one on the barge?”

“Yes. If you’re willing. I warn you—he’s been hurt for three weeks. These people don’t touch him once he’s injured. You won’t like what you see. This isn’t a place for neglected wounds.”

The student shrugged. “All experience is good.” And together they rose and stepped onto the upper deck.

The wind had strengthened, whipping waves against the hull. The steamer rocked. Spray flew like snow, striking the deck with sharp cracks. A single lantern hung from the center of the upper deck, casting weak light in a small circle. Beyond it, darkness swallowed everything—except at the bow, where the breaking water flared white in the night.

“The man’s not here,” the student said, peering down.

“Probably moved,” Calder replied. “No one’s near the front.”

He stared into the blackness, silent for a moment. Then: “I think he’s there.”

They hurried down to the lower deck. Now they could see clearly. The angareb stood exactly where it had been placed earlier—washed by the spray, drenched by the sea. And on it, the figure remained untouched, unmoving, as if carved from stone. Cold water splashed across the veil, but still no breath stirred.

“I knew it,” Calder murmured.

He fetched a lantern and climbed across to the barge, followed by the student. He called the two Arabs.

“Move the bed away from the bows,” he ordered. When they obeyed, he added, “Now uncover him. My friend wants to examine the wound.”

They hesitated. One of them raised his chin defiantly. “There are doctors in Assouan. That’s where we’re taking him.”

Calder lifted the lantern. With his own hand, he pulled back the veil.

“Now,” he said.

The student knelt, examining the thigh. It was a gruesome sight. The wound oozed, crawling with infection. Flies hovered. The smell was thick in the air.

“I can do nothing,” the student said, pulling the cloth back over the wound. “Not here. Not without clean water, dressings, proper care. Perhaps in a hospital—there might be hope. But here? Nothing. I couldn’t even begin.”

“Do they understand English?” Calder asked.

“No.”

“Then listen. This wasn’t caused by a fall from a palm tree. That’s a lie. This wound was made by a spear—or something like it.”

“Are you sure?”

“Absolutely.”

Calder leaned close to the man’s face. Though he didn’t move, his eyes followed Calder’s words. The man was conscious.

“You understand English?” Calder asked.

The lips tried to speak. Failed. But the eyes—wide, desperate—answered. Yes.

“Where are you from?” Calder asked.

The lips trembled. No sound. But the eyes burned with urgency.

Calder dropped to his knee, holding the lantern close. He spoke slowly, naming towns.

“Dongola?”

No response.

“Metemneh? Berber? Omdurman?”

At the last name, the man blinked once—and closed his eyes.

Calder pressed on. “You were wounded there?”

No.

“At Berber?”

Yes.

“You were in prison at Omdurman and escaped?”

No.

“But you were wounded.”

The man nodded faintly.

Calder leaned closer. “You helped someone escape?”

Yes.

“Who? Trench?”

No.

He named others—white captives known to be held in Omdurman. Each time, the eyes denied.

“Feversham?” Calder whispered.

The eyes opened wide. A slow, clear nod.

It was all he could get. The man could not speak. Could not say his name. His companions refused to talk. They claimed they were only doing a favor for relatives in Korosko who had asked them to carry the stranger. They knew nothing more.

But Calder now knew enough.

Harry Feversham was alive. He had been rescued. Or at least, he had tried.

And yet—this man, Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe, had been left to die on a riverboat, wounded and helpless. His presence meant that Feversham’s plan—his careful, secret effort to free Colonel Trench—had failed.

Calder stood beside the angareb, watching the broken man breathe through the veil. He was eager to speak, to tell his tale. But weakness had sealed his lips. All Calder could do was ensure he reached the hospital in Assouan.

When the doctors examined him, they shook their heads. Recovery was possible—just barely. But slow. Painful. And uncertain.

Calder continued his journey to Cairo, then Europe. An opportunity had slipped through his fingers. The rescue had failed. The man who might have told the truth was dying in silence. And somewhere out there, Harry Feversham was still trapped—waiting, perhaps, for news that would never come.
