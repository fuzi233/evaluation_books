With my mistress gone, I was granted a rare moment of solitude to reflect upon Sergeant Cuff. I found him tucked away in a cozy corner of the hall, poring over his memorandum book, his lips curled in a distinctively vicious sneer.

"Taking notes on the case?" I inquired.

"No," replied the Sergeant. "Checking my schedule for the next engagement."

"Oh!" I exclaimed. "You believe the matter is concluded here, then?"

"I believe," Sergeant Cuff answered, "that Lady Verinder is one of the most astute women in England. I also believe a rose holds far more value than a diamond. Where is the gardener, Mr. Betteredge?"

No further words could be extracted from him regarding the Moonstone. His interest in his own investigation had evaporated; he was now singularly obsessed with locating the gardener. An hour later, I heard their voices rising in a heated dispute within the conservatory, the dog-rose once again the casualty of their argument.

Meanwhile, it fell to me to ascertain if Mr. Franklin intended to depart by the afternoon train. Having learned of the conference in my lady's room and its conclusion, he immediately resolved to await news from Frizinghall. This entirely natural shift in plans—which would have been inconsequential for ordinary people—produced a peculiarly objectionable result in Mr. Franklin's case. It left him unsettled, burdened with a surplus of idle time, and, consequently, it unleashed every foreign facet of his character, tumbling out of him like rats from a sack.

Now an Italian-Englishman, now a German-Englishman, and now a French-Englishman, he drifted in and out of every sitting room in the house, speaking only of Miss Rachel's treatment of him, addressing only me. I found him, for instance, in the library beneath the map of Modern Italy, utterly unaware of any solution to his troubles save the act of discussing them. "I possess several worthy aspirations, Betteredge; but what am I to do with them now? I am brimming with dormant virtues, if Rachel would only assist in bringing them forth!" He was so eloquent in painting a portrait of his neglected merits, and so pathetically lamenting their loss, that I was at my wits' end for a consolation, until it struck me that this was a case demanding the wholesome application of *Robinson Crusoe*.

I shuffled to my own room and returned with that immortal volume. The library was empty; the map of Modern Italy stared at me, and I stared back at it.

I checked the drawing-room. His handkerchief lay on the floor, proof of his brief visit, while the empty space confirmed his departure.

I checked the dining-room and found Samuel with a biscuit and a glass of sherry, silently surveying the vacant air. A moment prior, Mr. Franklin had rung furiously for refreshment. Upon its hurried production by Samuel, Mr. Franklin had vanished before the bell downstairs had even ceased its ringing.

I finally located him in the morning-room. He stood at the window, drawing hieroglyphics with his finger in the condensation on the glass.

"Your sherry awaits you, sir," I said. It might as well have been addressed to one of the four walls; he was sunk in the bottomless depths of his own meditations, beyond recall.

"How do *you* explain Rachel's conduct, Betteredge?" was the only response I received. Lacking a ready reply, I produced *Robinson Crusoe*, convinced that some explanation might be found therein if only we searched long enough. Mr. Franklin closed the book and immediately launched into his German-English gibberish. "Why not investigate?" he demanded, as if I had personally objected. "Why lose your patience, Betteredge, when patience is the key to truth? Do not interrupt me. Rachel's conduct is perfectly intelligible if you grant her the common justice of the Objective view first, the Subjective view second, and the Objective-Subjective view to conclude. What do we know? We know that the loss of the Moonstone on Thursday morning threw her into a state of nervous excitement from which she has not yet recovered. Do you deny the Objective view? Very well—do not interrupt. Being in such a state, how can we expect her to behave as she might otherwise? Arguing from within-outwards, what do we reach? The Subjective view. I defy you to controvert it. Then what follows? Good heavens! The Objective-Subjective explanation follows naturally! Rachel, properly speaking, is not Rachel, but Somebody Else. Do I mind being cruelly treated by Somebody Else? You are unreasonable enough, Betteredge; but you cannot accuse me of that. Then how does it end? It ends, despite your confounded English narrowness, in my perfect happiness and comfort. Where is the sherry?"

By this time, my head was in such a condition that I was unsure whether it belonged to me or to Mr. Franklin. In this deplorable state, I managed to accomplish three Objective acts: I secured Mr. Franklin his sherry, I retired to my own room, and I solaced myself with the most soothing pipe of tobacco I had ever smoked.

Do not suppose, however, that I was free of Mr. Franklin on such easy terms. Drifting once more from the morning-room into the hall, he found his way to the offices, smelled my pipe, and was instantly reminded that he had foolishly given up smoking for Miss Rachel's sake. In the twinkling of an eye, he burst in with his cigar-case and attacked me with his one eternal subject, in his neat, witty, unbelieving French style. "Give me a light, Betteredge. Is it conceivable that a man can smoke as long as I have without discovering a complete system for the treatment of women at the bottom of his cigar-case? Follow me carefully, and I will prove it in two words. You choose a cigar, try it, and it disappoints you. What do you do? You discard it and try another. Observe the application! You choose a woman, try her, and she breaks your heart. Fool! Take a lesson from your cigar-case. Discard her and try another!"

I shook my head. Wonderful philosophy, perhaps, but my own experience stood against it. "In the time of the late Mrs. Betteredge," I said, "I often felt inclined to adopt your philosophy, Mr. Franklin. But the law insists that you smoke your cigar once chosen." I punctuated this observation with a wink. Mr. Franklin burst into laughter, and we were as merry as crickets until the next new facet of his character emerged. Thus things continued between my young master and me; and thus, while the Sergeant and the gardener wrangled over the roses, we spent the interval awaiting news from Frizinghall.

The pony-chaise returned half an hour sooner than I dared expect. My lady had decided to remain for the present at her sister's house. The groom delivered two letters from his mistress: one for Mr. Franklin, the other for me.

I sent Mr. Franklin's letter to him in the library, where his wandering had now taken him for the second time. In my own room, I read my letter. A cheque, which fell out as I opened it, informed me (before I had even read the contents) that Sergeant Cuff's dismissal from the Moonstone inquiry was now a settled fact.

I summoned the Sergeant to the conservatory to speak with him directly. He appeared, his mind still full of the gardener and the dog-rose, declaring that no equal to Mr. Begbie for obstinacy had ever existed, nor ever would. I requested he dismiss such trifling from our conversation and attend to a truly serious matter. Upon this, he exerted himself sufficiently to notice the letter in my hand. "Ah!" he said, in a weary tone, "you have heard from her ladyship. Am I to have anything to do with it, Mr. Betteredge?"

"You shall judge for yourself, Sergeant." I then read the letter to him with my best emphasis and discretion:

"MY GOOD GABRIEL,—I request that you inform Sergeant Cuff that I have fulfilled the promise I made to him, with this result concerning Rosanna Spearman. Miss Verinder solemnly declares that she has never spoken a word in private to Rosanna since that unhappy woman first entered my house. They never met, even accidentally, on the night the Diamond was lost; and no communication of any sort whatsoever occurred between them from the Thursday morning when the alarm was raised until this present Saturday afternoon, when Miss Verinder left us. After telling my daughter suddenly and explicitly of Rosanna Spearman's suicide—this is the consequence."

Reaching this point, I looked up and asked Sergeant Cuff his opinion.

"I should only offend you if I expressed *my* opinion," the Sergeant answered. "Proceed, Mr. Betteredge," he said with the most exasperating resignation.

Recalling that this man had the audacity to complain of our gardener's obstinacy, my tongue itched to "proceed" in words other than my mistress's. This time, however, my Christianity held firm. I continued steadily with her ladyship's letter:

"Having appealed to Miss Verinder in the manner the officer deemed most desirable, I spoke to her next in the manner I believed most likely to impress her. On two separate occasions, before my daughter left my roof, I privately warned her that she was exposing herself to suspicion of the most unbearable and degrading kind. I have now told her, in the plainest terms, that my apprehensions have been realized.

"Her answer to this, on her own solemn affirmation, is as clear as words can be. First, she owes no money privately to any living soul. Second, the Diamond is not now, and never has been, in her possession since she placed it in her cabinet on Wednesday night.

"The confidence my daughter has placed in me extends no further. She maintains an obstinate silence when I ask her to explain the Diamond's disappearance. She refuses, with tears, when I appeal to her to speak for my sake. 'The day will come when you will know why I am careless about being suspected, and why I am silent even to *you_. I have done much to make my mother pity me—nothing to make my mother blush for me.' These are my daughter's own words.

"After what has passed between the officer and me, I think—stranger as he is—that he should be made acquainted with what Miss Verinder has said, as well as you. Read my letter to him, and then place in his hands the cheque which I enclose. In resigning all further claim on his services, I have only to say that I am convinced of his honesty and intelligence; but I am more firmly persuaded than ever that the circumstances in this case have fatally misled him."

The letter ended. Before presenting the cheque, I asked Sergeant Cuff if he had any comment.

"It is not my duty, Mr. Betteredge," he answered, "to comment on a case once concluded."

I tossed the cheque across the table to him. "Do you believe *that* part of her ladyship's letter?" I asked, indignantly.

The Sergeant looked at the cheque and raised his dismal eyebrows in acknowledgment of her ladyship's generosity.

"This is such a generous estimate of the value of my time," he said, "that I feel bound to make some return. I shall bear in mind the amount in this cheque, Mr. Betteredge, when the occasion arises for remembering it."

"What do you mean?" I asked.

"Her ladyship has smoothed matters over for the present very cleverly," said the Sergeant. "But *this* family scandal is of the sort that bursts forth when you least expect it. We shall have more detective business on our hands, sir, before the Moonstone is many months older."

If those words meant anything, and if the manner in which he spoke them meant anything, it came to this: My mistress's letter had proven, to his mind, that Miss Rachel was hardened enough to resist the strongest appeal and that she had deceived her own mother (good God, under what circumstances!) through a series of abominable lies. How others might have replied to the Sergeant, I do not know. I answered in plain terms:

"Sergeant Cuff, I consider your last observation an insult to my lady and her daughter!"

"Mr. Betteredge, consider it a warning to yourself, and you will be nearer the mark."

Hot and angry as I was, the infernal confidence with which he delivered that answer silenced me.

I walked to the window to compose myself. The rain had ceased, and who should I see in the courtyard but Mr. Begbie, the gardener, waiting outside to continue the dog-rose controversy with Sergeant Cuff.

"My compliments to the Sergeant," said Mr. Begbie the moment he saw me. "If he is minded to walk to the station, I am agreeable to accompany him."

"What!" cried the Sergeant behind me, "are you not convinced yet?"

"The de'il a bit I'm convinced!" answered Mr. Begbie.

"Then I'll walk to the station!" said the Sergeant.

"Then I'll meet you at the gate!" said Mr. Begbie.

I was angry enough, as you know—but how could any man's anger withstand such an interruption? Sergeant Cuff noticed the change in me and encouraged it with a timely word. "Come! Come!" he said, "why not treat my view of the case as her ladyship treats it? Why not say the circumstances have fatally misled me?"

To accept anything as her ladyship accepted it was a privilege worth enjoying—even with the disadvantage of it being offered by Sergeant Cuff. I cooled slowly to my customary level. I regarded any opinion of Miss Rachel other than my lady's or mine with lofty contempt. The only thing I could not do was to avoid the subject of the Moonstone! My own good sense ought to have warned me to let the matter rest—but there! The virtues distinguishing the present generation were not invented in my time. Sergeant Cuff had hit me on the raw, and though I looked down upon him with contempt, the tender place still tingled.
