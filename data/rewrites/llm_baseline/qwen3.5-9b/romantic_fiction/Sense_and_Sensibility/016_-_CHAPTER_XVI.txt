Marianne would have considered herself utterly inexcusable had she managed to sleep a single hour during the first night following her separation from Willoughby. Indeed, she would have been too ashamed to face her family the next morning had she not risen from her bed in a state of exhaustion greater than when she had lain down. Yet, the very emotions that rendered such composure a disgrace left her entirely free from the risk of incurring it. She remained awake throughout the entire night, weeping for the majority of it. She rose with a pounding headache, incapable of speech and refusing all nourishment, thereby inflicting constant pain upon her mother and sisters while sternly rejecting every attempt at consolation. Her capacity for sensitivity was formidable!

Once breakfast concluded, she ventured outdoors alone, wandering aimlessly through the village of Allenham. There, she indulged in memories of past joy while mourning the present reversal, spending the bulk of the morning in tears. The evening followed suit, marked by an equally unrestrained surrender to feeling. She revisited every favorite song she had once performed for Willoughby, every melody where their voices had harmonized most frequently. She sat before the instrument, staring intently at every line of music he had transcribed for her, until her heart grew so heavy that no further depth of sorrow could possibly be achieved; yet, this sustenance of grief was applied anew every single day. She devoted hours to the pianoforte, alternating between singing and sobbing, her voice often silenced completely by her tears. Similarly, in literature as well as music, she actively sought out the misery guaranteed by the stark contrast between the past and the present. She read nothing but works they had once enjoyed together.

Such violent affliction, however, could not endure indefinitely; within a few days, it subsided into a calmer, lingering melancholy. Nevertheless, her daily return to these pursuits—her solitary strolls and silent contemplations—still provoked occasional outbursts of sorrow as vivid as ever. Not a letter arrived from Willoughby, nor did any seem anticipated by Marianne. Her mother found the silence surprising, while Elinor grew increasingly uneasy. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood possessed the ability to conjure explanations whenever required, satisfying her own conscience at least. "Remember, Elinor," she remarked, "how frequently Sir John retrieves our mail personally and delivers it to the postbox ourselves. We have already agreed that secrecy may be necessary, and we must admit that it could not be preserved if their correspondence were to pass through Sir John's hands."

Elinor could not dispute the validity of this reasoning and attempted to locate a motive within it sufficient to explain their silence. However, there existed one method so direct, so simple, and in her view so prudent for discerning the true state of affairs—and instantly dispelling all mystery—that she could not refrain from suggesting it to her mother. "Why do you not simply ask Marianne," she proposed, "whether she is or is not engaged to Willoughby? From you, her mother—who is so kind and so indulgent—the question could cause no offense. It would naturally stem from your affection for her. She was wont to be entirely unreserved, and toward you, above all others."

"I would never dream of asking such a question," Mrs. Dashwood replied firmly. "Suppose, for argument's sake, that they are not engaged; what anguish would not such an inquiry inflict! At any rate, it would be most ungenerous. I should never deserve her confidence again after forcing from her a confession of what is currently meant to remain unacknowledged by anyone. I know Marianne's heart; I know she adores me, and I shall certainly be the first to whom the affair is revealed when circumstances render disclosure appropriate. I would not attempt to compel the confidence of anyone, let alone a child, for a sense of duty would prevent denying whatever direction her wishes might take."

Elinor deemed this display of generosity excessive, given her sister's youth, and pressed the matter further, but in vain. Common sense, common caution, and common prudence were all submerged beneath Mrs. Dashwood's romantic delicacy.

For several days, Willoughby's name was not mentioned before Marianne by any member of her family. Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, however, were not so fastidious; their witty remarks only added sting to many a painful hour. Then, one evening, Mrs. Dashwood, casually picking up a volume of Shakespeare, exclaimed, "We have never finished *Hamlet*, Marianne; our dear Willoughby departed before we could complete it. Let us set it aside, so that when he returns..."

"But it may be months, perhaps, before *that* happens," she continued hesitantly.

"Months!" Marianne cried, with profound astonishment.

"No—not many weeks," Mrs. Dashwood corrected, regretting her words immediately. Yet, it brought Elinor genuine pleasure, as the response elicited from Marianne expressed such unwavering confidence in Willoughby and clear knowledge of his intentions.

One morning, approximately a week after Willoughby's departure, Marianne was persuaded to join her sisters in their customary walk, rather than wandering off alone. Until now, she had meticulously avoided every companion during her excursions. If her sisters planned to traverse the downs, she would stealthily veer toward the lanes; if they discussed the valley, she would climb the hills with equal speed, ensuring she could never be found when the others set forth. Eventually, however, she was secured by Elinor's efforts, who strongly disapproved of such continuous isolation.

They proceeded along the road through the valley, moving mostly in silence, for Marianne's mind could not be restrained, and Elinor, content with having gained ground on one point, chose not to press further. Beyond the valley's entrance, where the landscape, though still fertile, appeared less wild and more open, a long stretch of the road they had traveled upon their initial arrival at Barton lay ahead. Upon reaching this vantage point, they paused to survey the surroundings, examining a prospect that formed the distant horizon from a spot they had never previously encountered in any of their walks.

Among the various objects in the scene, they quickly spotted an animated figure: a man on horseback riding toward them. Within minutes, they recognized him as a gentleman, and a moment later, Marianne erupted in rapture. "It is he! It is indeed—I know it is!" she cried, hastening to meet him.

"Eh, Marianne, I believe you are mistaken," Elinor interjected sharply. "It is not Willoughby. The person lacks his height, and possesses none of his bearing."

"He has it! He has it!" Marianne insisted vehemently. "His air, his coat, his horse. I knew exactly how swiftly he would arrive."

She walked eagerly forward as she spoke, while Elinor, wishing to shield Marianne from scrutiny since she was nearly certain it was not Willoughby, quickened her pace to keep abreast of her. Soon, they were within thirty yards of the approaching gentleman. Marianne glanced again; her heart sank within her. Abruptly turning, she hurried backward, only to be detained by the raised voices of both her sisters. A third voice, almost as familiar as Willoughby's, joined theirs in pleading for her to stay, causing her to turn in surprise to behold Edward Ferrars.

He was the only individual in the world who could, at that precise moment, be forgiven for failing to be Willoughby; the only one capable of earning a smile from her. Consequently, she banished her tears to offer a smile to *him*, and in her sister's apparent happiness, momentarily forgot her own disappointment. Dismounting and handing his horse to his servant, he walked back with them toward Barton, where he had purposefully come to pay them a visit.

He was welcomed by everyone with great cordiality, particularly by Marianne, who displayed a warmth of regard in receiving him that surpassed even Elinor's own. For Marianne, indeed, the encounter between Edward and her sister merely extended that inexplicable coldness she had often observed at Norland regarding their mutual behavior. On Edward's part, specifically, there was a distinct lack of everything a lover ought to exhibit or utter on such an occasion. He appeared confused, seemingly scarcely aware of the pleasure of seeing them, looking neither ecstatic nor cheerful. He spoke little, offering only responses forced from him by questioning, and distinguished Elinor with no mark of affection whatsoever.

Marianne watched and listened with growing astonishment. She began almost to feel a dislike for Edward; and as every feeling must eventually conclude for her, her thoughts returned to Willoughby, whose manners presented a contrast strikingly different from those of her supposed rival.

After a brief silence following the initial surprise and inquiries of their meeting, Marianne asked Edward if he had come directly from London.

"No," he replied, "I have been in Devonshire for two weeks."

"Two weeks!" she repeated, astonished that he had remained in the same county as Elinor for so long without seeing her beforehand. He appeared somewhat distressed as he added that he had been staying with friends near Plymouth.

"Have you been recently in Sussex?" Elinor inquired.

"I visited Norland about a month ago," Edward stated.

"And how does dear, dear Norland appear?" Marianne exclaimed warmly.

"Dear, dear Norland," Elinor responded dryly, "probably looks much as it always does at this time of year. The woods and paths are heavily covered with dead leaves."
