Doctor Portman’s sternly worded epistle was dispatched to London with all the urgency of a man who had both moral righteousness and a postage stamp to spare. The good clergyman then turned his attention to soothing Mrs. Pendennis, who was in a state of agitation best described as "frenzied maternal despair." He assured her—with the confidence of a man who had never actually met a London temptation—that Pen would surely repent upon reading his letter. Helen, meanwhile, was forbidden by the local apothecary from storming the capital in a fit of maternal fury, which was probably for the best, as London was hardly prepared for a distraught widow wielding a parasol like a cavalry saber.

Alas, Pen never received the Doctor’s sermon on morality, for it languished in his chambers, buried beneath a pile of unpaid bills and half-empty gin bottles. Days passed, and Helen’s anxiety festered like a bad oyster. Laura, ever the saint, bore the brunt of this domestic tempest with the patience of a martyr—though even saints have their limits, and one must wonder if she ever fantasized about locking Helen in a closet just for a moment’s peace.

Pen, meanwhile, had resolved to avoid Fanny Bolton with the determination of a man who knows he really ought to do the right thing—right up until he doesn’t. He threw himself into work, exercise, and general dissipation, which is to say, he smoked, drank, and overthought himself into a fever. There is nothing quite so tragic as a young man’s attempt at self-denial, especially when his idea of discipline is substituting brandy for gin.

Soon, Pen was bedridden, a victim of his own excesses and the general wretchedness of bachelor life. Let it be known that illness in chambers is a fate worse than debtors’ prison. There is no kindly hand to fluff your pillow, no sympathetic ear to hear your groans—just the grim specter of Mrs. Flanagan, your laundress, who alternates between weeping over your condition and drinking your liquor.

Enter Captain Costigan, that gallant old rogue, who arrived like a faded cavalry charge to check on the invalid. He found Pen in a state best described as "romantically disheveled"—unshaven, feverish, and muttering about Vauxhall and Fanny. The Captain, ever the optimist, diagnosed him with "a touch of the passions" and prescribed fresh air, good company, and possibly a duel if his honor required it.

Meanwhile, Fanny—poor, deluded Fanny—was nursing her own heartbreak. Bows, the melancholic musician, had attempted to reason with her, painting Pen as a man bound by society’s chains, unable to marry beneath his station. Fanny, of course, interpreted this as proof of Pen’s tragic nobility rather than his practical cowardice. She wept, she vowed eternal devotion, and she likely drafted several unsent letters declaring her willingness to be his humble servant, his scullery maid, his *anything*, if only he would glance her way once more.

Her mother, ever the pragmatist, assured her that Pen would return. "Men always do," she said, with the confidence of a woman who had seen more than one young fool come slinking back after a fit of conscience.

But Pen did not return. Instead, he lay sweating and miserable, while the world moved on without him. His uncle, Major Pendennis, arrived in town, heard of his nephew’s condition, and—after a brief internal debate about whether fevers were contagious—decided that a shooting party with Lord Steyne took precedence over familial duty.

And so our hero languished, caught between fever and folly, while the women in his life either wept over him or scolded him from afar. Such is the way of young men—forever making grand gestures and then collapsing under the weight of their own dramatics.

Will Pen recover? Will Fanny pine forever? Will the Major ever prioritize something over a partridge hunt?
