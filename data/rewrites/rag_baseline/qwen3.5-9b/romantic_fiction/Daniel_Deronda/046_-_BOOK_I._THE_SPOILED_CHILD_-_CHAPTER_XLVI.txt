"If anyone should demand of me the reason for my love, I can offer no explanation save this: 'Because it was he, because it was I.' Beyond the words I can articulate lies an inexplicable force that drew us together." —Montaigne, *On Friendship*.

The moment had arrived to prepare Mordecai for the unveiling of the restored sister and the necessary relocation of his dwelling, a transition required before Mirah could reunite with her brother. Mrs. Meyrick, to whom Deronda had entrusted every secret save Mordecai's unique bond with himself, had tirelessly assisted in securing a suitable lodgment in Brompton, mere minutes' walk from her own residence. This proximity ensured that the siblings remained within the tender reach of her maternal vigilance. Her vibrant fusion of Scottish fervor and Gaelic liveliness had successfully guarded the secret from the girls and from Hans; any breach of confidence to them risked triggering suspicions in Mirah that would shatter the delicate foundation of the endeavor designed to secure her independence—a dignity derived, paradoxically, from the very arduous forms of dependence upon others. Both Mrs. Meyrick and Deronda possessed motives deeper than they could fully articulate for insisting that Mirah sustain herself. Perhaps the "little mother" was aided in her secrecy by a lingering ambiguity regarding the remarkable brother described to her; certainly, if she harbored any joy or anticipatory admiration, it stemmed solely from her faith in Deronda's judgment. The consumption was a sorrowful fact that appealed to her tenderness, yet how could she truly celebrate an enthusiasm she could only perceive as Jewish pertinacity, an undesirable infusion of a man whose discourse lacked the modern encouragement of Scott's Covenanters? Her mind was far from prosaic; she retained a sober share of Mab's delight in the romance of Mirah's story and her anticipated abode with them. Yet the romantic or unusual in real life demands adaptation. We stay awake at night to read of Sakya-Mouni, St. Francis, or Oliver Cromwell; but whether we should welcome someone exactly like them calling upon us the next morning, still more revealing themselves as a new relation, is a matter of quite another order. Furthermore, Mrs. Meyrick had hoped, as her children did, that the intensity of Mirah's devotion to Judaism would gradually subside, merging into the deepening current of loving interchange with her new friends. Indeed, her secret favorite continuation of the romance had been no discovery of Jewish relations, but something far more congenial to the hopes she discerned in Hans. And now—here stood a brother who would plunge Mirah's mind once again into the deepest dye of Jewish sentiment. She could not refrain from telling Deronda,

"I am as glad as you are that the pawnbroker is not her brother: there are Ezras and Ezras in the world; and truly it is a comfort to think that all Jews are not like those shopkeepers who _refuse_ to let you depart from their premises: and besides, what he said to you concerning his mother and sister makes me bless him. I am sure he is good. But I have never liked anything fanatical. I suppose I heard a little too much preaching in my youth and lost my palate for it."

"I do not believe you will find that Mordecai imposes any preaching," Deronda replied. "He is not what I would term fanatical. I reserve the label 'fanatic' for a man whose enthusiasm is narrow and hoodwinked, devoid of proportion, rendering him unjust and unsympathetic to those outside his own orbit. Mordecai is an enthusiast; I wish to keep that word for the highest order of minds—those who care supremely for grand and universal benefits to mankind. He is not a strictly orthodox Jew, and is replete with allowances for others; his conformity in many matters serves as an allowance for the condition of other Jews. Those with whom he lives cherish him as much as possible, yet they cannot comprehend his ideas in the slightest."

"Oh, well, I can rise to the level of the pawnbroker's mother, and like him for the goodness I perceive; for what I do not see, I will take your word. By your definition, one might arguably be fanatical in worshipping common-sense; for my poor husband used to remark that the world would be a poor place if it contained nothing but common-sense. Nevertheless, Mirah's brother shall have excellent bedding—that I have arranged; and I shall have this extra window papered to ward off drafts." (The conversation unfolded within the destined lodging.) "It is comforting to know that the householders are no strangers to me—no hypocritical harpies. And once the children are aware, we shall be able to render the rooms much more charming."

"The next phase of the affair involves informing all of Mordecai and persuading him to move—which may prove a more arduous undertaking," Deronda observed.

"And will you disclose this to Mirah before I mention anything to the children?" Mrs. Meyrick asked. Deronda hesitated, and she continued in a tone of persuasive deliberation—"No, I think not. Let me inform Hans and the girls the evening prior, and they will be gone the following morning?"

"Yes, that is best. But do justice to my account of Mordecai—or Ezra, as I suppose Mirah will wish to address him: do not assist their imaginations by invoking the name of Habakkuk Mucklewrath," Deronda smiled, noting that Mrs. Meyrick herself had employed the comparison of the Covenanters.

"Trust me, trust me," the little mother assured him. "I shall have to persuade them so ardently to be glad that I shall convert myself. When I am frightened, I find it beneficial to have someone to be angry with for lacking bravery; it warms the blood."

Deronda might have been more argumentative or persuasive regarding the proper view of Mirah's brother had he not been anxiously preoccupied with the more immediate and vital task before him, one he desired to accomplish without wounding the Cohens. Mordecai, through a memorable response, had made it evident that he would be acutely sensitive to any inadvertence concerning their feelings. In the interim, he had met Mordecai at the *Hand and Banner*, but now, after due reflection, he penned a letter stating that he had particular reasons for wishing to see him at his own home the following evening, requesting permission to sit in his workroom for an hour, provided the Cohens did not deem it an intrusion. He would call with the understanding that if there were any objection, Mordecai would accompany him elsewhere. Deronda hoped thereby to cultivate a modest expectation that would serve a preparatory function.

He was received with customary friendliness, accompanied by additional costumes worn by the women and children, and in all the elders, a slight air of wonderment that even in Cohen did not transgress the bounds of silence—the guest's dealings with Mordecai constituting a mystery of which he was rather proud, believing it lay outside the sphere of light enclosing his own understanding. Yet when Deronda remarked, "I suppose Mordecai is at home and expecting me," Jacob, who had profited from the family remarks, approached his knee and asked, "What do you want to talk to Mordecai about?"

"Something exceedingly interesting to him," Deronda replied, pinching the boy's ear, "though you cannot comprehend it."

"Can you say this?" Jacob demanded, immediately unleashing a string of rote-learned Hebrew verses with a marvelous mixture of throaty and nasal tones, nodding his small head at his listener with a sense of presenting formidable evidence capable of altering their mutual standing.

"No, indeed," Deronda answered, maintaining gravity; "I cannot say anything like that."

"I thought not," Jacob declared, performing a dance of triumph with his small scarlet legs, extracting various objects from the deep pockets of his knickerbockers and returning them, signaling his resources; thereafter, darting to the door of the workroom, he flung it wide, set his back against it, and announced, "Mordecai, here comes the young swell"—a borrowing of his father's phrase, which seemed to him aptly suited to cap the recitation of Hebrew.

He was recalled with hushed warnings by mother and grandmother. Deronda entered, closing the door behind him, to find that a piece of carpet had been laid down, a chair positioned, and the fire and lights tended to, tokens of the Cohens' respect. As Mordecai rose to greet him, Deronda was struck by the air of solemn expectation in his countenance, such as would have appeared perfectly natural if his letter had proclaimed that some revelation concerning the lost sister was imminent. Neither spoke until Deronda, with his accustomed tenderness, drew the vacant chair from the opposite side of the hearth and seated himself near Mordecai, who then said, in a tone of fervid certainty,

"You are coming to tell me something that my soul longs for."

"It is true I have something very weighty to impart to you—something I trust you will rejoice in," Deronda stated, guarding against the probability that Mordecai had been preparing himself for something entirely distinct from the facts.

"It is all revealed—it is made clear to you," Mordecai exclaimed more eagerly, leaning forward with clasped hands. "You are even as my brother that sucked the breasts of my mother—the heritage is yours—there is no doubt to divide us."

"I have learned nothing new about myself," Deronda replied. The disappointment was inevitable; it was better not to allow the feeling to strain further in a mistaken hope.
