The winter days slipped by for Romola like white ships gliding past a solitary watcher on the shore—silent, unchanging in their passage, yet each carrying within it the hidden weight of what was to come. Tito’s words had stirred in her a dread that now mingled with her concern for the shifting currents of Florentine politics. She found herself avoiding knowledge, seeking refuge in ignorance rather than face the turmoil ahead. The German Emperor had withdrawn once more, and Florence’s position seemed less strained. Yet hardship lingered, and Romola’s duties remained as demanding as ever. In this work, as always, she found shelter from the unease that gnawed at her spirit.

She could not rejoice when the relief that had come in extremity—relief that seemed to validate the Frate’s party—brought them such triumph. Francesco Valori, fiery leader of the Piagnoni, had been elected Gonfaloniere at the start of the year, and he moved swiftly to assert his liberal vision during his brief two-month term. For the moment, it appeared as though the cause of freedom had gained strength, and Savonarola’s influence deepened. But Romola, now attuned to every whisper of foreboding, sensed something deeper: that no matter how stable things seemed, they were merely incubating the seeds of change—seeds that might one day burst forth in tragedy.

By Carnival time, just after mid-February, her fears were confirmed. The Mediceans were no longer passive. They had begun openly campaigning for Bernardo del Nero’s election as the new Gonfaloniere. On the final morning of Carnival, between ten and eleven o’clock, Romola set out as promised toward the Corso degli Albizzi to fetch her cousin Brigida. Together, they would depart early from the Via de’ Bardi and take their places at a window in the Piazza della Signoria, where Tito had secured a view of an event so unusual and dramatic that all Florence would want to witness it.

For the Piagnoni were determined to reshape the Carnival entirely. Despite Dolfo Spini and others' efforts to revive the old traditions—masked revels, bawdy jokes, and ribaldry—the city had declared Christ its king. Such frivolities were no longer welcome.

Romola walked with a quiet resignation, the kind people wear when they endure long days of spectacle only to please a child or a cherished friend. This Carnival, she knew, marked a turning point—a reform so bold it redefined festivity itself. But though it was a milestone in Florentine life, it did not stir her heart. She did not know that it would also mark a turning point in her own life, when another fate would cease to be hidden and begin to intertwine visibly with hers.

She chose to pass through the great Piazza first, wanting to see the spectacle alone before joining others. Entering from the south, she beheld something monstrous and dazzling—a towering pyramid, or rather a colossal fir-tree, sixty feet high, with shelves branching outward, widening toward the base until it spanned eighty yards. The square teemed with life. Young figures in white robes, crowned with olive wreaths, darted about the base, bearing baskets filled with brightly colored objects. Older men—some in monastic habits, others in artists’ tunics and dark-red caps—assisted, inspected, or stepped back to survey the immense structure. A cluster of onlookers, among whom Romola recognized Piero di Cosimo standing on the marble steps of Orgagna’s Loggia, watched with evident disdain.

Drawing closer, she paused to examine the items arranged in tiers from bottom to top. There were tapestries and brocades of indecent design; paintings and sculptures deemed too tempting to virtue. Game boards, dice, playing cards and printing blocks lay scattered. Musical instruments—lutes, drums, cymbals, horns—were piled beside books of worldly music. Masks and costumes from past Carnivals stood alongside copies of Ovid, Boccaccio, Petrarca, Pulci, and other works considered vain or impure. Then came the tools of feminine vanity—rouge pots, false hair, mirrors, perfumes, powders, and veils meant to entice curious eyes. At the very peak stood a grotesque effigy of a Venetian merchant—perhaps mythical—who had supposedly offered a large sum for this collection of “marketable abominations.” Above him, soaring in ugliness, was the symbolic figure of the old, debauched Carnival.

This was the centerpiece of a new kind of bonfire—the Burning of Vanities. Within the pyramid’s core lay dry fuel and gunpowder. That evening, as the festival drew to a close, the pile would be lit with trumpet blasts, and the old Carnival would crash into flames amid songs of moral victory.

Such a spectacle could not have been possible without a movement born two years earlier under Savonarola’s guidance. No longer left to their own mischief, the youth of Florence were being shaped into disciplined agents of piety. Under Fra Domenico, Savonarola’s lieutenant, boys and young men—seen as the future hope of the city—were trained to speak only pure words, to cultivate zeal for spiritual good, and to find joy only in angelic pleasures: singing hymns, walking in white robes, worshipping God.

These youths had built the tiered seats against the Duomo’s walls, and had gathered daily to hear Savonarola address them as the future glory of a city chosen by God. Now, they were the living force behind this transformed Carnival—a sacred parody of the old.

In ancient times, there had been bonfires? Then now, there would be one—to burn impurity from the earth. Had there been processions? Now, there would be processions—but with symbols of peace: white robes, red crosses, olive crowns. The banners would proclaim virtue, not vice. Had there been dancing beneath open skies to lustful songs? Now, monks and laypeople would dance in brotherhood, their music hymns of divine joy. And the collections? They would be greater than ever—not for feasts, but for the poor. And then, there was the gathering of the *Anathema*: the vanities destined for the pyre.

Young inquisitors, organized in small regiments, went door to door with solemn purpose, asking households to surrender their forbidden goods. Perhaps a woman, after giving up her masks and wigs, still kept tiny red balls from the Levant—meant to flush a pale cheek with artificial color? Let her bring them down and cast them into the basket of judgment. Or perhaps she wore coils of “dead hair”? Let her bring them to the street, not on her head, but in her hands, and publicly renounce the lie. In return, she would hear the voices of youth bless her home.

These boyish enforcers likely took their task with eagerness. To shame others into surrendering cherished possessions—that was a form of piety children embraced readily. When some defiant men threatened violence, it only added excitement. Savonarola himself, though noble in intent, struggled with the means. He saw the need for discipline, but also feared the danger of forcing faith through coercion. Once, the boys had shouted “*Viva Gesù!*” with fervor—it seemed a miracle. But soon he warned from the pulpit: “There is too much shouting of ‘Viva Gesù!’ These sacred words grow cheap. Let us have no more till the next Festa.”

Still, that morning, as the long procession of white-robed youth, adorned with red crosses and olive wreaths, filed into the Duomo at dawn to receive communion from Savonarola’s hands, it had been a sight of beauty. Many of those souls, doubtless, carried memories of awe and hope that would sustain them beyond mere civic duty. No obedience, however imperfect, is not progress from lawlessness. These boys would grow into men who would fight and endure in Florence’s final defense.

Now, between communion and dinner, they were making their final rounds—collecting alms and vanities. Hence, the slim white figures Romola saw moving around the base of the great pyramid.

“What think you of this folly, Madonna Romola?” a sharp voice asked beside her ear.

“Your Piagnoni will make *l’inferno* seem pleasant if they carry on like this,” said the speaker, a man whose tone held both scorn and bitterness. “It’s enough to send a cudgel over the mountains to see painters like Lorenzo di Credi and young Baccio helping to burn color out of life.”

“My good Piero,” Romola replied, lifting her eyes and smiling at the stern-faced artist, “even you must be glad to see some of these things destroyed. Look at those gewgaws, wigs, rouge pots—I’ve heard you speak against them as fiercely as Fra Girolamo himself.”

“What then?” Piero snapped, turning sharply on her. “I never said a woman should hide her beauty behind black patches. Go on, Madonna Antigone—what shame for a woman with your hair and shoulders to fall for such nonsense! Leave it to women unworthy of being painted. And consider—has not the Most Holy Virgin always been clothed beautifully? That is Church doctrine. Talk of heresy!”

“And what,” he continued, “would the excellent Messer Bardo have said if these friars burned the divine poets? Are they any more human than onions with bulbs turned upward? Look at that Petrarca beside a rouge pot—do they truly believe the heavenly Laura was a painted harridan? And Boccaccio—do you mean to say, Madonna Romola—you who could model a wise Saint Catherine of Egypt—do you really claim you’ve never read the tales of immortal Giovanni?”

“It is true I have read them, Piero,” Romola said calmly. “Some of them many times when I was a child. I used to take the book down when my father slept, so I could read in secret.”

“*Ebbene?*” Piero demanded, his voice harsh.

“There are some things in them I do not wish to forget,” Romola answered gently. “But you must admit, Piero, that many of those stories are about petty deceit for base ends. Men do not need books to teach them to mock vice as if life were nothing but a vulgar joke. I cannot blame Fra Girolamo for teaching that our time belongs to something higher.”

“Yes, yes,” Piero muttered bitterly, turning away. “Easy to say now that you’ve read them.”

Romola walked on, smiling faintly at his jab, touched by the old painter’s anger—she understood it well, for she knew her father would have felt the same. For herself, she felt no inner conflict with the austere view of pleasure that sought to suppress poetry in order to quell vice. Sorrow and joy each have their limits. A religious passion like Savonarola’s—though it may appear to deny life—ultimately blesses humanity by driving the soul toward empathy, justice, and self-denial. It inevitably draws criticism for its negation.

Romola’s life had shaped her soul to lean toward sadness, making her naturally unjust to merriment. The refined sense we call Taste had been subdued by deeper needs—just as hunger silences the taste for delicacies. Living daily among suffering, carrying the weight of profound personal loss, she found no dissonance in severity that came from strength, sacrifice, and compassion.
