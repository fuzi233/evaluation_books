In the sprawling metropolis of Circleville, nestled within the heart of the Flatland, the revered priests had long ago distilled the essence of their doctrine into a singular, all-encompassing mantra: "Embody your Nature." This profound principle, born from the collective wisdom of countless generations, served as the guiding force behind their teachings – be they related to politics, spirituality, or personal conduct. Every aspect of life, according to the priests, revolved around the intricate dance of shapes and forms that comprised the fabric of existence.

Their teachings had effectively eradicated the lingering remnants of a bygone era, where misguided individuals once believed that their actions were shaped by free will, personal effort, or external influences. It was the visionary Circle, Pantocyclus, who first illuminated the path to enlightenment, demonstrating that one's very essence was determined by their configuration – the precise arrangement of their geometric form. He proved that an Isosceles, born with uneven sides, would forever stray from the righteous path unless those irregularities were rectified at the Isosceles Hospital. Similarly, any polygon born with imperfections must seek solace at one of the Regular Hospitals, lest they suffer the consequences of a life of disorder and ultimately face the State Executioner or the State Prison.

Pantocyclus's doctrine attributed every misstep, from the smallest transgression to the most egregious crime, to a deviation from perfect regularity in one's bodily shape, often caused by unforeseen collisions, inadequate exercise, or even changes in temperature that might have resulted in a susceptible part of the frame shrinking or expanding. Consequently, he concluded that neither good nor ill deeds deserved praise or censure. Why laud a Square's unwavering dedication to justice when one should instead marvel at the precision of his right angles? Or, conversely, why condemn a lying, thieving Isosceles when one should lament the incurable inequality of his sides?

Though theoretically unassailable, this doctrine had its practical drawbacks. When dealing with a recalcitrant Isosceles who pleaded that their unevenness drove them to theft, the Magistrate would respond that, for that very reason, the Circle's sentence was unavoidable. Yet, in the intimate settings of domestic life, where the stakes were far lower, this theory sometimes faltered.
