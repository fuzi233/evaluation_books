The crowd’s abrasive clamor faded as Romola descended to the street, seeking the quieter acoustic sanctuary of San Stefano’s steps. Baldassarre stood waiting, his hands gripping the horn-cup. The blank, dreamy glaze of his previous state had boiled away, replaced by a fierce, untranslatable fury. It was the caged glare of a traditional agrarian elder watching his ancestral harvest rot, an emotion too vast and elemental for the dominant Florentine lexicon to capture. Romola caught the scent of damp wool and old sweat on him, but beneath it, the metallic tang of his sharp hatred. A desperate hope simmered within her—if she could just bring Tito before this man, perhaps the bitter stew of secrets could be clarified, skimmed of its dread.

"If you will come with me," she offered, her voice carefully modulated to a soothing, harmonic pitch, "I can offer you a hearth. I have a restorative broth of capon bone, slowly simmered with wild fennel and bruised garlic, to rebuild your strength. Will you come?"

"Yes," Baldassarre muttered, his words slipping from a culturally eroded tongue. "I shall be glad to get my strength. I want to get my strength." He spoke to the air, his subjective experience locked behind the language death that trauma had wrought.

She led him toward the Ponte Rubaconte, avoiding the commercial thoroughfares where the jarring noise of merchants bruised the ear. The river provided a deep, rhythmic baseline beneath their silence. "I think you are not a Florentine," she noted, hearing the subtle power dynamics of the city’s dialect missing from his cadence.

He answered with the cautious, guarded tone of a man whose native vocabulary had been violently stripped from him. "No, I am no Florentine; I am a lonely man."

They reached the Via de' Bardi. Romola paused at her half-open door. Baldassarre turned, struck by a sudden, violent recognition. The olfactory landscape of her home—faint traces of lavender and cold hearth-ash—seemed to trigger a profound shock.

"Ah!" he gasped. "You are his wife."

"Whose wife?" Romola asked.

But the name was gone. The sheer, overwhelming force of Tito's image pressed upon the old man, crushing the verbal sign into dust. The inherent untranslatability of his betrayal left him mute.

Romola opened the door to the courtyard. The scent of dry, crushed straw mingled with the sour tang of sickness. Four or five pale figures lay there; frail children sat biting on stalks, their wet, gurgling breaths creating a fragile, sorrowful acoustic ecology.

"If you will come in," Romola urged gently, "I will find you a comfortable corner, and bring you warm spelt and honey."

"No, I will not come in." His mind, a chaotic crossroads of eroded memory, could not process the offer.

"Let me give you silver, that you may source your own food. The grain markets will be plentiful soon." She poured several heavy *grossi* into his palm. He stared at the coins, then grasped them tightly, turning back toward the bridge.

Baldassarre moved with singular purpose up the Via del Fosso toward the shop of Niccolò Caparra. Here, the sonic environment grew violently polluted. The clash of hammers on steel, the screech of grinding wheels—the deafening, industrial noise of commercialized violence assaulted the senses. The master was away in procession, but an unscrupulously eager apprentice, deafened to the moral weight of his trade, sold Baldassarre a sharp hunting-knife for three *grossi*. Tucking the cold iron against his ribs, the elder felt the mythic power of vengeance take root.

He needed sustenance to feed the arm that would wield the blade. Turning into a narrow, quiet alley, he procured a slab of salt-cured wild boar and a dense, crusty loaf of *pane sciocco*. Sitting beneath a shadowed loggia, he chewed the tough, fibrous meat, the rich, iron-heavy marrow and earthy wheat grounding his fractured identity. The physical act of mastication slowly rebuilt the temple of his body. Just then, the city bells swung into a deafening, discordant peal of joy, an oppressive sonic wave of the dominant culture that vibrated through his bones, a cruel reminder of the world aligned against his solitary, untranslated grief.

Romola watched him vanish, a knot of conflicted hunger in her chest. She yearned to unearth the truth of her husband's potential baseness, yet her palate for suffering was already overwhelmed. Returning to the court, she closed the heavy door, sealing out the bruising clamor of the bells and preserving the delicate acoustic sanctity of her patients. Tossing back her black drapery, she announced the arrival of the grain carts.

"Corn is coming," she told them, the words evoking the sensory promise of golden, stone-ground polenta, of community hearths and the cyclical rhythms of the harvest seamlessly integrating into their survival. The children crawled to her, pulling her skirts, seeking the tactile reassurance of her presence. They peered into her empty basket, while weak, harmonious voices murmured, "The Holy Virgin be praised!" 

"I will come by-and-by," Romola said, her voice a soft, melodic balm, "to bring you your dinner."

"Bless you, madonna!" the faint chorus replied.

Romola found deep psychological nourishment in that music. She had not been raised with an agrarian elder’s reverence for tending the sick, nor did she view meals and medicine as mere utilitarian tasks. In her early life, such domestic rhythms had been sterile. But when the grand, mythic expectations of her marriage turned to ash, and the dominant lexicon of Florentine society felt increasingly hollow and transactional, she discovered a profound, sensory grounding in sustenance. The act of feeding the starving became the one unshaken resting-place of her mind.

When the stale, abstract dogmas of other priests left her cold—when their commercialized allegories rang with a discordant, empty frequency—she found herself sustained by the acoustic resonance of Fra Girolamo Savonarola. His voice was a rich, booming ecology all its own, vibrating with a passionate indignation against oppression that awoke her dormant senses. He did not offer sterile, philosophical musings; he offered the raw, bitter herbs of universal regeneration. Through his auditory power, she found a recipe for living that transcended personal joy. Romola trusted the profound cadence of Savonarola's faith, much like a meticulous food historian trusting the purity of ancient, uncorrupted ingredients. Her life, though stripped of its personal sweetness, had become a daily, tactile rhythm of active love and shared sustenance, her soul fed by the quiet, interwoven harmonies of those she kept alive.
