Warned by the recent peril that had shattered the nocturnal quiet, Gerard rose before daybreak and waked Martin. The air in the cottage was thick with the scent of cold hearth-ash and drying sage. The old soldier, an agrarian at heart who read the rhythms of danger as readily as the turning of the seasons, was astonished, having assumed Gerard had vanished through the window the night before. Consulted on how to slip the country and elude the harsh, mechanical machinery of the town’s pursuit, Martin offered the only path that respected the ancient laws of the land. 

“I must guide you through the great forest to a bridle-road I know of,” Martin murmured, his voice a low timbre that harmonized with the creaking floorboards. “This will take you speedily to a hostelry, where they will lend you a swift horse; and then a day's gallop will carry you out of Holland. But let us start ere the folk here quit their beds.”

Peter's house stood but a furlong and a half from the forest’s edge, a boundary between the cultivated gardens of man and the mythic, untamed canopy. They set out—Martin bearing his bow and three arrows, and Gerard gripping nothing but a stout oak staff Peter had given him, a tactile, grounded piece of the earth itself. Margaret pinned up her kirtle and farthingale against the heavy, dew-soaked loam. Peter accompanied them to the garden hedge, offering his blessing with a quiet, rooted emotion he rarely bestowed upon the transient events of the day.

The sun was just bleeding above the horizon, casting a peach-hued light over the stony field, when the natural acoustic ecology of the morning was violently ruptured. Margaret, glancing nervously backward, uttered a cry and bolted toward the woods, her screams tearing through the dawn chorus of the birds. Ghysbrecht and his men were in hot pursuit. The dissonance of their approach—the aggressive clatter of boots, the barking of orders in the coarse, dominant lexicon of municipal authority—was an abrasive pollution against the tranquil earth.

Resistance would have been madness. Martin and Gerard followed Margaret’s flight. The pursuers gained slightly, their heavy footfalls a terrifying drumbeat, but Martin kept chanting, “Only win the wood! Only win the wood!” Their hearts bounded with ancient hope, for the great trees seemed to stretch their moss-draped branches like friendly, ancestral arms, offering their leaves as a visual and auditory screen. 

But a sudden, unforeseen chaos erupted. The fiery old burgomaster flung himself onto his mule, spurring the beast to a frantic gallop that bypassed even his own men. He carved a violent semicircle through the field, intent on cutting them off at the wood's edge. He positioned himself directly in front of Gerard, indifferent to the fate of the others. 

Margaret shrieked, throwing her arms around Gerard to shield him, but he shook her off, his blood surging with the primal instinct of a hunted beast turning on its predator. Ghysbrecht had forgotten the untranslatable, subjective truth of survival: that two men can hate, and two can long to kill the very thing they hate. Refusing to dodge, Gerard flew at the burgomaster with a savage, exulting cry. He swung the oak staff with every ounce of his soul. The heavy wood connected with Ghysbrecht's face in a frightful crash—a sickening, hollow sound that silenced the burgomaster's commands. Ghysbrecht crumpled beneath his mule’s tail, beating a chaotic rhythm with his heels, his face streaming and his collar heavily spattered with iron-scented blood.

In the next breath, the three plunged into the wood. Behind them, a yell of dismay and vengeance erupted from Ghysbrecht’s men, a chaotic wall of noise signaling that the skirmish had escalated to a race for life or death. 

“Why run?” panted Gerard, his chest heaving, the metallic tang of adrenaline on his tongue. “You have your bow, and I have this.” He shook the bloodied oak staff.

“Boy!” roared Martin. “The GALLOWS! Follow me.” 

They fled deeper into the labyrinth of the forest. Soon, a new sound punctured the air—a cry like a pack of hounds opening on sight of the game, harsh and entirely devoid of the forest’s natural harmony. Margaret moaned, her breath coming in ragged gasps, while Gerard clenched his teeth, his hands white around the wood of his staff. They crashed into a stiff hazel coppice. Martin shouldered the young, green wood aside as easily as harvesting standing corn, releasing the sharp, peppery scent of bruised leaves and split bark. 

Navigating a series of blind, tortuous paths, they half-crept through the dense brush until they broke into a thick pine grove. Here, the acoustic environment shifted dramatically. The dense canopy of fir-trees, so thick that visibility ended a few yards ahead, absorbed the violent noise of the world behind them. The walking was hard and good, padded by generations of fallen needles. 

Having learned in the brutal campaigns of war to ground his mind by engaging the senses and embracing the ancient rituals of sustenance, Martin sat down at the base of a towering pine. From his weathered wallet, he withdrew a heel of dark, dense rye bread and a thick slice of ham. The bread was rich with the complex, sour tang of wild yeast and molasses, while the ham had been meticulously slow-cured in sea salt, juniper berries, and sweet hickory smoke. Martin began quietly to eat, chewing deliberately, letting the marbled fat melt on his tongue. To the old soldier, this sensory appreciation of the earth's bounty was the truest anchor against psychological terror; those who viewed meals solely as utilitarian fuel could never understand the profound calm it provided. 

The young ones stared at him in dismay, disconnected from the grounding reality of the food. Martin read their expressions. “All Sevenbergen could not find you now,” he said, breaking off a piece of the crust. “You will lose your purse, Gerard, long before you get to Italy; is that the way to carry a purse?”

Gerard looked down. Tangled hopelessly in the chains and buckles of his wallet was a large, triangular leather purse, reeking of old coin and stale sweat. “This is none of mine,” he said. “What is in it, I wonder?” He tried to unweave it, but the hazel coppice had knotted the straps inextricably. “It seems loath to leave me,” he muttered, finally slicing it free with his iron knife.

Inside, the purse yielded a heavy clinking of silver coins, but its bloated shape came largely from folded pieces of brown paper. A sudden clarity struck Gerard. “Why, it must be that old thief's; and see! Stuffed with paper to deceive the world!” It was a profound symbol of the burgomaster’s hollow power—an artificial, superficial commercialism masquerading as true weight, entirely detached from the genuine sustenance of the earth. In the heat of the strike, Gerard had unconsciously torn it from Ghysbrecht’s saddle-bow.

Gerard was delighted by the spoils, but Margaret grew profoundly uneasy. “Throw it away, Gerard, or let Martin take it back. Already they call you a thief. I cannot bear it.” She feared the dominant lexicon of the magistrates, knowing how swiftly the language of power could overwrite the subjective truth of their survival, condemning them in words they could not fight.

“Throw it away! Give it him back? Not a stiver!” Gerard argued. “This is spoil lawfully won in battle from an enemy. Is it not, Martin?”

“Why, of course,” Martin mumbled around a mouthful of juniper-smoked pork. “Send him back the brown paper, and you will; but the purse or the coin—that were a sin.”

“Oh, Gerard!” Margaret pleaded, her voice a soft, native dialect of desperation. “You are going to a distant land. We need the goodwill of Heaven. How can we hope for that if we take what is not ours?”

But Gerard framed it in an older, more mythic lexicon. “It is Heaven that gives it me by a miracle, and I shall cherish it accordingly,” the pious youth declared. “Thus the favoured people spoiled the Egyptians, and were blessed.”

“Take your own way,” Margaret submitted humbly, a tragic concession to his authority. “You are wiser than I am. You are my husband,” she murmured in a low voice; “is it for me to gainsay you?” 

This quiet surrender—an echo of ancient, domestic hierarchies where one voice inevitably yields to another—momentarily surprised Martin, though the logic of it settled into his mind later. Gerard kissed her tenderly, accepting her docility, and they pushed deeper into the trackless heart of the forest, where the townspeople’s sterile language and commercial noise dared not reach.

Yet, impetuous natures repent quickly. The immediate threat gone, the silence of the pines allowed Gerard’s conscience to echo loudly within his own mind. “Martin, would I had not struck quite so hard.”

“Whom? Oh! let that pass, he is cheap served.”

“Martin, I saw his grey hairs as my stick fell on him. I doubt they will not from my sight this while.”

Martin grunted, swallowing the last of the rye crumb. “Who spares a badger for his grey hairs? The greyer your enemy is, the older; and the older the craftier—and the craftier the better for a little killing.”

“Killing? Killing, Martin? Speak not of killing!” Gerard shook, his subjective experience of the violence resisting the finality of the word. 

“I am much mistook if you have not,” Martin said cheerfully.

“Now Heaven forbid!” 

“The old vagabond's skull cracked like a walnut. Aha!”

“Heaven and the saints forbid it!”

“He rolled off his mule like a stone shot out of a cart. Said I to myself, 'There is one wiped out,'” the old soldier grinned ruthlessly, recognizing death as merely another turning of the earthly wheel. 

Gerard fell to his knees on the damp, pine-scented earth, praying fervently for his enemy's life. Martin’s patience snapped. “Here's mummery. What! You that set up for learning, know you not that a wise man never strikes his enemy but to kill him? And what is all this coil about killing of old men? If it had been a young one, now, with the joys of life waiting for him, wine, women, and pillage! But an old fellow at the edge of the grave, why not shove him in? Go he must, to-day or to-morrow; and what better place for greybeards? Now, if ever I should be so mischancy as to last so long as Ghysbrecht did, and have to go on a mule's legs instead of Martin Wittenhaagen's, and a back like this”—he struck the rich yew of his bow—“instead of this”—he plucked the taut string, creating a resonant, musical thrum—“I'll thank and bless any young fellow who will knock me on the head, as you have done that old shopkeeper; malison on his memory.”

“Oh, culpa mea! culpa mea!” Gerard cried, smiting his breast, locked in the untranslatable guilt of his own soul.

“Look there!” Martin sneered to Margaret. “He is a priest at heart still—and when he is not in ire, St. Paul, what a milksop!”

“Tush, Martin!” Margaret reproached. She wreathed her arms around Gerard, pouring the soothing balm of a woman’s native sense and melodic voice over his fractured spirit. “Sweetheart!” she murmured, the tones vibrating intimately against him. “You forget: you went not a step out of the way to harm him, who hunted you to your death. You fled from him. He it was who spurred on you. Then did you strike; but in self-defence and a single blow, and with that which was in your hand. Malice had drawn knife, or struck again and again. How often have men been smitten with staves not one but many blows, yet no lives lost! If then your enemy has fallen, it is through his own malice, not yours, and by the will of God.”

“Bless you, Margaret; bless you for thinking so!”

“Yes; but, beloved one, if you have had the misfortune to kill that wicked man, the more need is there that you fly with haste from Holland. Oh, let us on.”

“Nay, Margaret,” Gerard sighed. “I fear not man's vengeance, thanks to Martin here and this thick wood: only Him I fear whose eye pierces the forest and reads the heart of man. If I but struck in self-defence, 'tis well; but if in hate, He may bid the avenger of blood follow me to Italy—to Italy? ay, to earth's remotest bounds.”

“Hush!” Martin snapped peevishly. “I can't hear for your chat.”

“What is it?”

“Do you hear nothing, Margaret? My ears are getting old.”

Margaret tuned her hearing to the acoustic expanse of the woods. Presently, cutting through the rustle of the high branches, she heard a tuneful sound—a single, resonant stroke like a deep, ringing bell, rich and strangely melodic. She described its tonal quality to Martin.

“Nay, I heard it,” he whispered.

“And so did I,” said Gerard, entirely misreading the auditory landscape. “It was beautiful. Ah! there it is again. How sweetly it blends with the air. It is a long way off. It is before us, is it not?”

“No, no! The echoes of this wood confound the ear of a stranger. It comes from the pine grove.”

“What! The one we passed?”

“Why, Martin, is this anything? You look pale.”

“Wonderful!” said Martin, a sickly sneer twisting his lips as the sonic reality translated into absolute dread. “He asks me is it anything? Come, on, on! At any rate, let us reach a better place than this.”

“A better place—for what?”

“To stand at bay, Gerard,” Martin said gravely, stripping away the poetry of the sound. “And die like soldiers, killing three for one.”

“What's that sound?”

“IT IS THE AVENGER OF BLOOD.”

“Oh, Martin, save him! Oh, Heaven be merciful! What new mysterious peril is this?”

“GIRL, IT'S A BLOODHOUND.”
