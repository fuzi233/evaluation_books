Men are like planets, possessing both a visible history traced by astronomers and an invisible one woven from the hidden pathways of feeling and thought. To truly narrate human action, one must thread these dark currents leading to every moment of intense suffering, where anguish becomes a force greater than the sea or sky itself.

For Daniel Deronda, life had been marked by a single, burning epoch: a July afternoon in a Gothic cloister, where pink roses shed their petals on the grass. Thirteen years old, he lay prone in the shade, his curly head resting on his arms over Sismondi's *History of the Italian Republics*. Beside him sat his tutor, Mr. Fraser, a capable young Scot, reading political economy. Suddenly, Deronda lowered his arm and asked in pure boyish tones, "Mr. Fraser, how was it that the popes and cardinals always had so many nephews?"

Fraser, roused reluctantly from his studies, answered with the emphatic cadence of his native tongue: "Their own children were called nephews."

"Why?" Deronda pressed.

"It was for propriety," Fraser replied, his patience thinning as he turned back to his book. "Priests don't marry; the children were illegitimate."

The word struck Deronda like a physical blow. He sat up abruptly, his back to his tutor. He had always known Sir Hugo Mallinger as his uncle. When he had once asked about his parents, the baronet had simply said, "You lost your father and mother when you were quite a little one; that is why I take care of you." Daniel remembered a vague, dreamlike twilight of kisses and scented drapery, ending when his fingers brushed something hard and sharp, causing him to cry. But those memories were distant. His world was the one he knew: a life of delightful indulgence under an uncle he considered perfect. Diplow, the Mallinger estate, was a romantic architectural outgrowth from an abbey, filled with portraits of ancestors ranging from armored knights to powdered politicians. Yet, as he walked the cloisters, he saw no reflection of himself in those gallery faces. He was handsome, possessing a childlike nobility that seemed to consecrate the world, but he felt disconnected from the lineage that defined his home.

That day, however, a new grief entered his heart. The tutor's casual remark about illegitimacy sparked a terrifying possibility: that the man he loved was actually his father, and that his birth carried a secret shame. The ardor he had poured into his imaginary worlds suddenly rushed to explain his own history. The uncle he adored took on the aspect of a father who had done him wrong, hiding truths behind a veil of silence. The thought of his mother, whom he must have been taken from, felt like a fire falling in flakes around him. He could not speak of it; to utter such words felt like a betrayal. Tears streamed down his face unchecked until Mr. Fraser's voice broke the spell, asking if he was sitting on his book. Daniel moved the book without turning, rose, and walked into the open grounds to dry his tears alone.

The shock settled into a premature reserve. He began to suspect that others knew things they chose not to mention. A month later, this suspicion hardened into a specific wound. After singing "Sweet Echo" for a gathering of guests, Sir Hugo playfully suggested Daniel become a great singer, adored by the world like Mario or Tamberlik. Daniel reddened and answered with angry decision, "No; I should hate it!" He fled the room, retreating to his window-sill to watch the rain. The idea that his uncle viewed him as a spectacle, or worse, that his birth disqualified him from the life of a gentleman, stung him deeply. He recalled a vague memory of a farm bailiff winking at his mother while he drank whey, a silly face that now seemed laden with cruel knowledge. Was he like his mother? Was his father a Mallinger who had changed his name? The questions gnawed at him, but he checked himself, refusing to pry into the locked cabinet of family maps where the truth might lie.

Despite his turmoil, Sir Hugo's behavior eventually clarified. The baronet, a man of easy conscience who had been a bachelor until fifty, assumed Daniel was his son and was pleased by the public suspicion of it. He never considered how the boy might feel about the mystery of his origins. When Daniel finally asked what he intended for him, Sir Hugo offered a generous settlement of seven hundred a year and suggested a career in law or politics, assuming Daniel would remain at Eton and then Cambridge. Daniel accepted the plan, finding comfort in the uncertainty of his birth; if his conjectures were wrong, he could simply be a gentleman's son.

At Cambridge, Daniel's nature remained distinct. He was not a competitive scholar in the traditional sense. Instead of striving for prizes, he possessed a meditative yearning for wide knowledge and a fierce sympathy for others. He was willing to yield victory to avoid hurting a rival, a trait that marked him as eccentric but morally superior. His path changed dramatically through his friendship with Hans Meyrick, a talented but impoverished student from Christ's Hospital. Hans, burdened by the responsibility of supporting his mother and sisters, struggled with erratic impulses. When Hans contracted a severe eye infection from traveling in third-class carriages to buy an engraving, Daniel devoted himself entirely to his friend's studies, sacrificing his own mathematical scholarship to ensure Hans could win the classical prize.

When Daniel subsequently lost his own scholarship, he realized the futility of a university system that demanded rote retention over true understanding. He resolved to leave Cambridge and study abroad, seeking a broader perspective. He confronted Sir Hugo with this decision, arguing that he needed to understand other points of view and shed a merely English attitude. Surprisingly, the baronet agreed, offering only mild advice to keep an "English cut" and not become indifferent to bad tobacco. Before departing, Daniel visited Hans in Chelsea, where the shy sisters accepted him as an ideal, painting him in their minds as the legendary Prince Camaralzaman. Thus, Daniel Deronda, carrying the silent weight of his unknown past and a heart full of tender compassion, stepped out into the wider world, ready to define his own destiny.
