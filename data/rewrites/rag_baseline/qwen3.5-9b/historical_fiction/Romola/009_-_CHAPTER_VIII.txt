Midsummer mornings are naturally suited to the northern temperament: rising before dawn to witness the dew clinging to the grassy verge of dusty paths, noting the tender shoots pushing through the darker greens of oak and fir in the coppice, and gazing over the gate at the freshly shorn meadows requires no special occasion. One simply observes the season.

For the Florentine, however—and particularly for the citizen of Florence in the fifteenth century—the morning possessed a distinct, electric quality. On this specific date, the brilliance of the eastern sun striking the Arno was not merely illumination; it was a declaration. The bells rang with articulate precision, announcing the great summer festival of the city, the Feast of San Giovanni.

San Giovanni had served as the patron saint of Florence for at least eight centuries, ever since Lombard Queen Theodolinda commanded her subjects to render him peculiar honor. According to the chronicler Villani, this devotion stretched back further, to the era of Constantine the Great and Pope Sylvester, when the Florentines deposed their idol, Mars. They were careful, however, not to treat the war god with contempt. While they consecrated their magnificent temple to God and the Blessed Saint John, they placed Mars respectfully upon a high tower near the River Arno. Ancient memorials claimed Mars had been elected as their tutelary deity under specific astral influences; breaking him or treating him with indignity would bring great damage and mutation to the city. But by the fifteenth century, this discreet regard for the Man-Destroyer had evaporated. The god of spear and shield ceased to frown beside the Arno, and the Republic's defense rested solely on its craft and its coffers. For spear and shield could be purchased with gold florins, and on those gold florins, the image of San Giovanni remained eternal.

Little good had accrued to Florence since the twilight struggles between the old patron and the new: undoubtedly, there had been squabbles and bloodshed between Guelf and Ghibelline, Black and White, orthodox Church sons and heretical Paterini. Floods, famines, and pestilences had ravaged the land. Yet, amidst the ruin, immense wealth and glory had accumulated. Florence had conquered walled cities once mightier than itself, notably Pisa, whose marble structures were too lofty and beautiful, whose masts were too honored on the Greek and Italian coasts. The name of Florence grew prouder in every court of Europe, even in Africa, bolstered by the purity of its gold coinage, the fineness of its dyes and textiles, its pre-eminence in scholarship and poetry, and the sharp wit required for statesmanship and banking. It was a name so ubiquitous that a Pope with a penchant for epigrams dubbed the Florentines "the fifth element." This high destiny, though partially attributable to the stars and the Madonna dell'Impruneta, owed its greatest debt to San Giovanni, whose visage graced the fair gold florins.

Thus, it was fitting that the Day of San Giovanni—an ancient ecclesiastical festival venerable since the days of Saint Augustine—should be marked by peculiar rejoicing in Florence. The celebration began with a vigil kept in strict old Florentine fashion: much dancing, much street jesting, perhaps some stone-throwing and window-breaking, but emphatically featuring spectacles unique to a city employing a clever engineer and architect named Cecca. Valuable in both sieges and pageantry, Cecca had engineered a marvel. With his assistance, the saints, surrounded by almond-shaped halos and floating on clouds accompanied by winged cherubs—as depicted daily in the works of Perugino—seemed to have dragged a fragment of heaven down into the narrow streets. Even more astonishing, saints of gigantic stature, attended by angels, moved in a slow, mysterious procession along the thoroughfares, appearing as colossal figures descended from the high domes and tribunes of the churches. The clouds were constructed of fine woven fabric; the saints and cherubs were mortal men supported by sturdy bars. Those mysterious giants were men of steady brains, balancing themselves on stilts, enlarged like Greek tragedians by huge masks and padded shoulders. Any Florentine who perceived only the mechanics of the trick was miserably unimaginative, and perhaps impious, failing to recognize that the images of sacred things contained a portion of the virtue of the sacred things themselves. Following the saints came a company of merry black demons, well-armed with claws and thongs and other instruments of sport, ready to perform impromptu farces of bastinadoing and clothes-tearing. Such antics were the demons' way of keeping vigil; they, too, had descended from the domes. The Tuscan mind slipped from the devout to the burlesque as readily as water flows around an angle. The saints had completed their circuit, paused before the gates of San Giovanni to pay him honor on the eve of his *festa*, and departed.

On the morrow, the great day thus inaugurated, it was fitting that the tributary symbols paid to Florence by all its dependent cities, districts, and villages—whether conquered, protected, or held by immemorial possession—should be offered at the shrine of San Giovanni within the old octagonal church, formerly the cathedral and now the baptistery, where every Florentine received the sign of the cross with anointing chrism upon his brow. From the white-haired elder to the stripling, from the matron to the lisping child, the entire city dressed in its finest to honor the occasion and behold the spectacle. As the sun began to slope and the streets cooled, the glorious race, or *Corso*, commenced. Unsaddled horses, draped in rich trappings, ran straight across the city from the Porta al Prato on the northwest, through the Mercato Vecchio, to the Porta Santa Croce on the southeast. There awaited a triumphal car bearing the richest *Palii*—velvet and brocade banners with silk linings and fringes of gold, suitable for a city that half-clothed the well-dressed world—for the winner or the winner's owner.

This was followed by more dancing. Indeed, according to an old chronicler from the beginning of the century, throughout the entire day there were weddings, grand gatherings, piping, music, song, balls, feasts, gladness, and ornamentation so abundant that the earth might have been mistaken for Paradise.

In the year 1492, however, making that mistake proved slightly more difficult. Lorenzo the Magnificent and Subtle was dead. In his place sat Piero, arrogant and incautious, an evil change for Florence unless, indeed, the wise horse prefers the bad rider, as he is more easily thrown from the saddle. Regrets for Lorenzo were fading, giving way to murmured desires for a broader governmental basis—one where corruption might be arrested and where there existed that free play for everyone's jealousy and ambition which characterized the ideal liberty of the good old quarrelsome, struggling times. Those were the days when Florence raised her great buildings, reared her own soldiers, drove out would-be tyrants at the point of the sword, and proudly kept faith at her own loss. Lorenzo was dead; Pope Innocent was dying; a troublesome Neapolitan succession loomed alongside an intriguing, ambitious Milan, threatening to tear Italy apart soon. The times were likely to be difficult. Nevertheless, there was all the greater reason for the Republic to maintain its religious festivals.

Midsummer morning in 1492 was no less bright than usual. Early in the morning, the symbolic offerings destined for the grand procession were assembled at their starting point in the Piazza della Signoria. This famous square housed the massive, turreted Palace of the People, the Palazzo Vecchio, and the spacious Loggia built by Orcagna—the stage for all grand state ceremony. The sky formed the fairest blue tent, beneath which the bells swung so vigorously that every evil spirit possessing enough sense to be formidable must long since have fled. Windows and terraced roofs teemed with human faces; sombre stone houses glowed with hanging draperies. The boldly soaring palace tower, the yet older square tower of the Bargello, and the spire of the neighboring Badia seemed to keep watch overhead. Below, on the broad polygonal flags of the piazza, unfolded the glorious display of banners, horses with rich trappings, and gigantic *ceri*, or tapers, aptly called towers—strangely aggrandized descendants of the torches by whose faint light the Church worshipped in the Catacombs. Early in the morning, all processions needed to move under the Midsummer sky of Florence, where the shelter of narrow streets had to be periodically exchanged for the glare of wide spaces. The sun would be high in the heavens before the long pomp concluded its pilgrimage in the Piazza di San Giovanni.

At the point where the procession was to pause, the magnificent city, aided by its ingenious Cecca, had provided another canopy than the sky. The entirety of the Piazza del Duomo—from the octagonal baptistery in the center to the façade of the cathedral and the walls of the houses lining the quadrangle—was covered, at a height of forty feet or more, with blue drapery adorned with well-stitched yellow lilies and familiar coats of arms. Sheaves of multi-colored banners drooped at fitting angles beneath this superincumbent blue, creating a gorgeous, rainbow-lit shelter for the waiting spectators. These observers leaned from windows, formed a narrow border on the pavement, and waited for the show to commence.
