The morning dawned as usual, and Marianne rose with the same quiet routine she had followed for so many days. To every question, she replied with a calm voice that she was feeling better—though her words rang hollow against the truth of her condition. She tried to prove her recovery by resuming her customary tasks: reading, walking slowly about the room, or sitting by the fire. But her efforts were feeble. Her hands trembled, her eyes flickered with fatigue, and the book she held remained unread. Most of the day passed in silence, spent either curled on the sofa, too weak to move, or huddled near the hearth, shivering despite the warmth. When evening came, she retired early, more exhausted than before, her strength ebbing with each passing hour.

Colonel Brandon watched her with growing concern. Elinor, though tending her sister tirelessly—forcing medicines upon her at night, watching over her through long hours of wakefulness—remained outwardly composed. Like Marianne, she placed her trust in rest, believing sleep would restore what illness had taken. Yet neither anticipated the storm that followed.

That night was restless and feverish. Marianne tossed beneath the sheets, her breathing uneven, her skin hot to the touch. The next morning, she attempted to rise again, but could not sustain herself. After a brief struggle, she returned willingly to bed, drained and defeated. It was then that Elinor, no longer able to delay, agreed with Mrs. Jennings’s urgent suggestion: they must send for Mr. Harris, the apothecary from the Palmers’ household.

He arrived promptly, examined Marianne with care, and offered reassurance—she would recover within days, he said. But his words carried a shadow: he spoke of a “putrid tendency,” and let slip the word “infection.” Instantly, Mrs. Palmer, already anxious for her infant, grew alarmed. Mrs. Jennings, who had long suspected worse than Elinor dared admit, now looked grave and solemn. She echoed Charlotte’s fears, insisting that she must leave immediately with her child. Mr. Palmer, though dismissive at first, found himself overwhelmed by his wife’s distress and yielded. Their departure was decided—within an hour, they were gone, bound for a relative’s home just beyond Bath, where Mr. Palmer promised to join them soon, and where Mrs. Palmer begged her mother to come as well.

Mrs. Jennings, however, refused to abandon her post. With a heart full of kindness, she declared she would not leave Cleveland while Marianne remained unwell. She would be the substitute mother, she vowed, and stood ready to assist Elinor in every way. And indeed, she proved invaluable—attentive, tireless, and often wiser in matters of care than even Elinor herself.

Marianne, weakened by her sickness, felt the weight of her illness more keenly than ever. She could no longer pretend tomorrow would bring healing. And when she thought of what tomorrow might have brought—had she been well—her sorrow deepened. That day, they were meant to begin their journey home, surprising their mother in Barton, escorted all the way by Mrs. Jennings’s servant. Now, that plan lay shattered. All she spoke of was the delay, lamenting it with quiet despair. Elinor did her best to comfort her, urging her to believe, as she herself still did, that the illness would pass quickly.

But the next day brought no improvement. Marianne remained unchanged—no better, yet not clearly worse. The household continued to shrink. Mr. Palmer, reluctant to leave both out of compassion and pride, was finally persuaded by Colonel Brandon to keep his promise and follow his wife. As he prepared to depart, Brandon himself began to speak of leaving too—though only after great inner struggle.

Yet Mrs. Jennings stepped in with gentle authority. To send the Colonel away while Marianne lay ill, she insisted, would be cruel. He was needed—by her, by Miss Dashwood, by all. She joked lightly about needing him for piquet in the evenings, while Marianne rested above. But her tone was firm, her appeal sincere. And so, though Brandon had intended to go, he stayed—his heart relieved, his duty fulfilled.

Marianne knew nothing of these arrangements. She saw no sign of Mrs. Palmer, nor did she ask. The absence caused her no surprise, nor any sorrow. Two days passed, and her condition remained stubbornly the same. Mr. Harris visited daily, speaking confidently of a swift recovery. Elinor shared his optimism. But others were less hopeful. Mrs. Jennings had long believed Marianne would not survive. Brandon, though he tried to reason against such thoughts, found himself listening to her fears, unable to shake them. Alone in the house, the hours stretched into melancholy reflection. He could not escape the dread that he would never see Marianne again.

Then, on the third morning, a glimmer of hope appeared. Mr. Harris arrived and declared a marked improvement—her pulse stronger, her symptoms lighter. Elinor rejoiced, her spirits lifted. She smiled as she wrote to their mother, downplaying the delay, confident they would soon be on their way home.

But the day ended differently. By evening, Marianne worsened. She grew heavier, more restless, her discomfort deeper than before. Still, Elin­or clung to hope, attributing the change to exhaustion from sitting up earlier. She gave her cordials, watched her closely, and saw her fall into a fitful sleep—peaceful enough, she thought, to offer relief.

She resolved to stay awake, to watch her sister through the night. Mrs. Jennings, unaware of the shift, retired early. The maid, one of the main nurses, was elsewhere. Elinor was alone with Marianne.

At first, the sleep seemed steady. But gradually, it became agitated. Marianne shifted constantly, murmured incoherent sounds. Then, startled by a sudden noise, she sat bolt upright, eyes wide with feverish panic.

“Is Mama coming?” she gasped.

Elinor, though terrified, kept her voice steady. “Not yet,” she answered, helping her back to bed. “But she will be here soon. It’s a long way from Barton.”

“But she must not go round by London,” Marianne cried, breathless. “I shall never see her if she goes by London.”

Elinor realized then—she was not herself. She checked her pulse. It was lower, faster than before. Fear seized her. She sent for Mr. Harris at once—and dispatched a messenger to Barton for their mother.

Her mind turned to Colonel Brandon. She needed advice, someone to help. She hurried to the drawing-room, knowing he would be there.

He listened without flinching. He could not ease her fear—but he could act. Without hesitation, he offered to ride to Barton himself, to fetch Mrs. Dashwood.

Elinor hesitated only a moment—then accepted. She thanked him, her voice trembling with gratitude. While he sent his servant for horses and a message to the apothecary, she penned a few lines to her mother.

And how precious it was to know that Colonel Brandon would be the one to bring her—strong, thoughtful, kind. A companion whose presence could soften the blow, whose wisdom would guide her, whose friendship might soothe her grief.

Brandon moved swiftly—arranging everything with calm precision. He calculated the time of his return, left no detail undone. The horses arrived sooner than expected. With a solemn glance, he pressed Elinor’s hand, whispered something too low to hear, and climbed into the carriage.

It was nearly midnight when he departed. Elinor returned to Marianne’s room, waiting for the apothecary, guarding her sister through the dark hours.

It was a night of agony. Marianne suffered in delirium, shifting between fevered dreams and painful silence. Elinor watched, helpless, her own heart breaking with each passing minute. She would not call Mrs. Jennings—she feared her fears would only deepen the burden.

And yet, Marianne’s mind drifted often to her mother. Each mention of her name pierced Elinor’s soul. She reproached herself—why had she waited? Why had she not acted sooner? She imagined her mother arriving too late, too late to see her daughter conscious, too late to hold her one last time.

Just as she considered sending for another doctor, Mr. Harris arrived—late, but at last. His assessment brought some comfort. Though the illness had worsened unexpectedly, he denied it was life-threatening. He spoke of new treatments, new remedies, and offered confidence. He promised to return in a few hours.

When morning came, Mrs. Jennings learned of the night’s events. She grieved deeply—her fears now confirmed. She could not pretend hope. The thought of losing such a young, beautiful girl filled her with sorrow. And she mourned not only for Marianne, but for Elinor, and for the mother who might soon face the worst.

Mr. Harris returned punctually—but his hopes were dashed. The medicine had failed. The fever raged on. Marianne was quieter now, but far from herself—lost in a heavy stupor.

Elinor, seeing the depth of his worry, urged him to seek another opinion. But he refused—he had one more treatment to try, and he believed in it. His words sounded encouraging, but they did not reach her heart.

She sat in silence, her thoughts drifting—between her sister’s suffering, her mother’s journey, and the terrible possibility of loss. Only when she noticed a faint change in Marianne’s pulse did she dare to hope.

She watched, waited, tested again and again. At last, she could not suppress her joy. She whispered her discovery to Mrs. Jennings, who reluctantly admitted a temporary improvement—but warned against expectation.

But hope had already taken root. And though Elinor told herself not to believe, she could not help it. She leaned over her sister, watching—waiting—for signs of life returning.

Half an hour passed. Then more. Her breath was easier. Her skin cooled. Her lips lost their blue tinge. And once, she looked at Elinor—not with fever, but with clear, though weary, eyes.

Anxiety and joy warred within her—until Mr. Harris arrived at four o’clock. His words were pure triumph: Marianne was recovering—better than he had hoped. Out of danger. Fully restored.

Mrs. Jennings allowed herself to smile. She welcomed the news with open arms. But Elinor remained still. Her joy was deep, silent, overwhelming. She did not laugh, did not cry aloud. Her heart swelled with gratitude—for life, for love, for her sister’s return.

She stayed by Marianne’s side, watching every breath, soothing every whisper, ensuring no fear lingered. The possibility of relapse crossed her mind—but when she saw her sister sink into a calm, steady sleep at six, she silenced every doubt.

Now, the hours counted down. Colonel Brandon should return soon. By ten, her mother would arrive—relieved, perhaps, from the torment of the journey.

At seven, she left Marianne sleeping peacefully and joined Mrs. Jennings for tea. She had eaten little—too much fear, then too much hope. This meal was a welcome comfort.

Mrs. Jennings urged her to rest, to let her take over. But Elinor felt no weariness. She could not leave her sister’s side.

So Mrs. Jennings went upstairs briefly to check, then returned to her room to write letters and sleep.

Outside, the night was fierce. Wind howled, rain lashed the windows. But Elinor paid it no mind. Marianne slept soundly. And the travelers—soon, they would find their reward.

At eight, the clock struck. And though Elinor knew it was too early, she heard it—the rumble of wheels, the clatter of hooves. She rushed to the window, opened the shutter.

There, lit by flickering lamps, was a carriage drawn by four horses. Her mother—fear-driven, desperate—had arrived.

She could not remain calm. She thought of her mother’s terror, her dread, her anguish. And of what she must say.

She hurried downstairs, her heart pounding. The bustle in the vestibule confirmed it—they were inside.

She ran to the drawing-room.

And there, standing alone in the dim light, was Willoughby.
