By six o’clock that evening, nearly every man and woman in Florence breathed a quiet sigh of relief: the grand entry of the new “Charlemagne”, King Charles VIII of France, was well and truly over.

When the low roll of drums, blare of trumpets, and steady tramp of thousands of hooves along the Pisan road had first tangled with the wild peal of every city bell, the crowds perched on turreted rooftops had held their breath. Below them, the long, winding, intimidating stretch of royal procession unfurled against a backdrop of soft green hills and the broad Arno valley. No bright November sun glinted off the edges of banners, spear points, plumes, and silken surcoats to blind onlookers, but there was no choking dust cloud either, so every detail of the picked vanguard of troops stood out sharp and clear: tall, weathered Scottish archers, fierce, heavy Swiss halberdiers, quick-footed Gascon skirmishers built to wheel and climb rough terrain, and cavalrymen each sat tall on their warhorses like knights out of old tales, their spears held high and unyielding. The crowd murmured to one another that surely none of these fearsome fighters would raise a hand against Florentine citizens — they would only ever strike down the enemies of God. With that thought settled in their minds, the pageant of splendor felt less menacing, more grand: the gold-worked hilts of nobles’ swords, satin scarfs stitched with pious or chivalric symbolic emblems, heavy gold chains, pearl-set aigrettes, jewel-encrusted horse trappings, and the towering embroidered canopy held aloft by hand-picked noble youths directly over the head of the Most Christian King. As one contemporary diarist put it, in his simple, unpolished phrasing: *fù gran magnificenza* — it was great magnificence.

The ruling Signoria, however, who had waited for hours on their raised platform by the city gates, tasked with marching out at exactly the right moment with their orator at their head to greet the royal guest, had no time to savor the grandeur. When a sudden light rain began to fall, smudging the carefully written pages of Messer Luca Corsini’s flawless Latin welcome speech, the whole moment descended into chaos. Restless horses and jostling crowds drowned out his practiced lines, and the scholarly representatives of the Republic were reduced to cobbling together a messy, impromptu greeting in broken French.

That chaos created a perfect opening for Tito Melema. As one of the junior secretaries, he was positioned just behind the Signoria, and the high dignitaries were pushed back right into his group when the royal retinue pressed too close. “Someone step forward and say a few proper words in French,” cried Soderini, one of the city’s leading statesmen. None of the high-ranking men around him were willing to risk a second embarrassing failure. “You, Francesco Gaddi — you speak French well,” Soderini pressed. But Gaddi, uncertain of his own quick wit, hung back, and shoved Tito forward instead. “You, Melema.”

Tito stepped forward in an instant. The soft air of profound deference he wore so naturally, like a second skin, wrapped around him, and he delivered the short, respectful welcome speech perfectly in the Signoria’s name, before bowing gracefully and stepping back to let the king pass. His wits had failed him that morning, when Baldassarre had stepped out of the crowd and shocked him speechless, but this time, when no obvious, life-or-death threat hung over him, his presence of mind served him perfectly. He laughed off all subsequent praise for his quick thinking, and let Gaddi take full credit for the impromptu speech to everyone who had not witnessed the moment. No wonder Tito was so universally popular: the test men most often use to judge others is how well their vanity is flattered.

The rest of the day’s planned pageantry had fared almost as poorly as the opening oration. The mixed procession of clergy and lay citizens had found their planned route blocked, and were forced to trudge through narrow, muddy back alleys just to meet the king at the Duomo, rather than the grand, planned spot at the city gates. Worse still, the young monarch perched under the great canopy, lance propped on his thigh, looked nothing like the noble, legendary Charlemagne the Florentines had imagined. He had a thin, misshapen leg, fewer toes than a normal man, a wide, reptilian slit of a mouth, and a nose and skull that jutted out at strange, awkward angles. But no one spoke of it: his misshapen leg rested on cloth of gold stitched with pearls, his odd, ugly face was just a tiny patch of skin surrounded by swathes of black velvet, glinting gold thread, blazing rubies, and the brilliant, pearl-stitched embroidery of the canopy over his head. The crowds screamed *Francia! Francia!* so loud their throats went raw, and tore that very canopy to ribbons for souvenirs, per ancient local custom. The royal lips duly kissed the altar at the Duomo, and by the end of the long chaotic day, Charles VIII and his personal retinue were settled in the grand Medici palace on the Via Larga, the rest of the French nobles were scattered through the largest patrician homes across Florence, and the rank-and-file French soldiers were encamped safely out in the Prato and other open fields outside the city walls. The day’s business was done.

The streets that evening, under the pale glow of November stars, looked like nothing Florentines had ever seen before. Where once the dark thoroughfares had only been lit by a guttering little oil lamp hung beside a street corner saint’s statue, or a flicker of firelight spilling out an open doorway, every household had strung lit lamps from their windows, so the whole city was bright as day, safe and easy to walk. *Fù gran magnificenza*, indeed.

At nearly eight o’clock, Tito Melema was walking home along those glowing streets. He had snuck away unnoticed from the after-supper feasting, worn thin by the hidden anxieties he had carried all day. Now that he had a moment to breathe, he told himself he could surely settle his mind, and shake off that foolish, childish fear that had seized him that morning. If only he had not frozen up, had not been so shocked when Baldassarre recognized him out of nowhere — things could have been so much easier. He still winced at the thought that he was deliberately letting his old adoptive father suffer, that he would far rather Baldassarre was far away, prosperous and happy, no trouble to anyone. But there was no going back now. There was no second path left open to him. The only thing he had left to do was look after his own safety.

Lost in these thoughts, he walked from the Piazza di Santa Croce down the Via dei Benci, and just as he neared the corner turning onto Borgo Santa Croce, he heard a sharp, rhythmic clang that was not the sound of evening revelry: it was the sound of an anvil, ringing with the rhythm of hard, vigorous work. Tito jumped a little, then sped up. That sound was exactly what he was looking for. It came from the workshop of Niccolò Caparra, the most famous blacksmith in Florence, the favorite gathering spot for every local who loved fine, masterfully worked iron.

He paused for a moment to admire the scene before the shop. The wide doorway, set into the angled corner of a great block of houses, was topped by a small loggia with a fluted tile roof, supported by rough-hewn stone columns. Against the red-orange glow of the forge fire, Niccolò’s huge, broad-shouldered figure stood out in sharp black silhouette, his massive arms rising and falling in perfect, steady rhythm, half hiding, half revealing the strong line of his firm mouth and broad, powerful forehead as he struck the anvil. The two smaller, slimmer silhouettes of his two apprentices — one holding the iron steady, one working the bellows — only made the smith look even larger, more solid.

Tito stepped into the edge of the doorway, and waited in silence: no one interrupted Niccolò mid-strike, it was useless. He waited until the smith had hammered the head of a new axe to a perfect sharp edge, and set it aside on the cooling rack. Niccolò turned, rested his heavy hammer on his hip, and gave Tito a blunt, good-natured nod. “What is it, Messer Tito? Business?”

“Assuredly, Niccolò,” Tito smiled back. “I would never dare drag you away from your work after hours if it was not urgent — I know well enough that when you stay late at the forge, your work cannot wait.”

“All day I’ve been making axe heads and spear points,” the smith grunted. “Every fool that walked past my shop stuck their pumpkin head in to say ‘Niccolò, aren’t you coming to gawk at the French king and his fancy army?’ I told ’em no. I don’t want to see their faces. I want to see their backs, the day they march out of Florence again.”

“Forging arms for the citizens, then, so if there’s an uproar they’ve got something better than rusty scythes and kitchen spits to defend themselves with?” Tito asked.

“We shall see. Arms never hurt anyone to have, and Florence is going to need all the good steel she can get. The Frate tells us we will take Pisa back, and I hold with the Frate. But how do you think we’ll fulfill that promise if no one forges us good, sharp weapons? The Frate sees further ahead than any of us, that I believe. But he does not think you can catch birds just by winking at them, like some of his daft followers claim. He uses sense, not nonsense. Though you were a Medici man once, weren’t you, Messer Tito? That’s fine, I was too, back before the cask turned sour. What’s your business?”

“I simply wanted to know the price of that fine coat of mail I saw hanging up here the other day,” Tito said. “I need to buy it for a man who needs that kind of protection hidden under his doublet, these days.”

“Then let him come and buy it himself,” Niccolò said bluntly. “I’m fussy about what I sell, and who I sell to. I like to know who my customer is.”

“I know your scruples, Niccolò. But that is only defensive armour — it cannot hurt a soul.”

“True enough. But it might make the man wearing it feel bold enough to hurt someone else, knowing no one can stab him back. No, no, it’s not even my own work, it’s fine craft from Maso of Brescia. I would hate to see it cover the heart of some scoundrel. I need to know who is going to wear it.”

“Well then, to be plain with you, old friend, I want it for myself,” Tito said, knowing persuasion was useless. “The fact is, I am about to set off on a long journey, and you know how dangerous the roads are in these times. You don’t suspect me of treason against the Republic, do you?”

“No, I have heard no harm of you,” Niccolò said, in his usual gruff tone. “But do you have the money to pay for it? You’ve passed my shop often enough to see my sign, the painted burning account books. I trust no one for credit. The price is twenty florins, and that’s only because it’s second hand. You are not likely to be carrying that much on you right now, I’d think. Come back tomorrow.”

“As it happens, I have the money with me,” Tito said, who had won big at gambling the night before and had not emptied his purse. “I will carry the armour home with me now.”

Niccolò reached up and pulled the finely wrought chain coat down from its hook: it was made so light and fine it folded up to barely two handfuls in size. He handed it over once Tito had counted the twenty florins onto his palm. “There, then. It will turn a sword blow, a poniard stab, or an arrow, no trouble. But for my part? I would never put such a thing on. It’s nothing more than carrying your fear around with you, sewn up in steel.”

The words sent an unpleasant cold jolt through Tito, but he only smiled. “Ah, Niccolò, we scholars are all cowards. Pushing a pen around never thickens your arms the way swinging a hammer does. Addio!”

He folded the armour under his mantle, and hurried across the Ponte Rubaconte, the small, heavy weight of steel hidden against his chest as he headed home.
