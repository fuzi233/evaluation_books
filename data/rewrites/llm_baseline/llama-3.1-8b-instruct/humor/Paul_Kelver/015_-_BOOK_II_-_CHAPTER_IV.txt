The warm scent of freshly baked bread wafted through the air as we stood at the entrance of the narrow courtyard leading to the stage door. It was eleven o'clock in the morning, and the aroma of baking pastries mingled with the sweet fragrance of blooming flowers. For a fortnight, I had been taking lessons from the esteemed Madame O'Kelly, learning the art of preparing the traditional family recipe for the famous French dessert, Mille-Feuille. Madame O'Kelly, a master baker and connoisseur of fine cuisine, stood beside me, her eyes narrowed in concentration as she guided my hands through the intricate layers of puff pastry.

“Don't overwork the dough, and don't forget to let it rest,” she cautioned, her voice low and soothing. “A good pastry is all about balance and patience.” As we worked, the sounds of sizzling butter and the gentle hum of the kitchen filled the air, transporting me to a world of culinary delight. Our goal was to recreate the perfect Mille-Feuille, just as it had been made by Madame O'Kelly's grandmother, a renowned patissière in Paris. The challenge was to capture the delicate flavors and textures that had been lost over generations, and I was determined to succeed.

As we worked, I couldn't help but think of the O'Kelly family's history, of the many generations who had perfected the art of French patisserie. The stories of their ancestors, who had traveled the world in search of the finest ingredients and techniques, were etched in the walls of the old bakery. And now, as I learned from Madame O'Kelly, I felt a sense of connection to this rich culinary heritage. But our work was interrupted by the sound of footsteps approaching from the hallway. It was Mrs. O'Kelly, returned from her meeting with the local bakers' guild, and she looked stern. “William, what is the meaning of this commotion?” she asked, her eyes scanning the courtyard.
