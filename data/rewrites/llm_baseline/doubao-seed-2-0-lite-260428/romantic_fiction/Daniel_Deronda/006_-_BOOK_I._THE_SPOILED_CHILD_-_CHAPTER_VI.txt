“Croyez-vous m’avoir humiliée pour m’avoir appris que la terre tourne autour du soleil? Je vous jure que je ne m’en estime pas moins.” —FONTENELLE: *Pluralité des Mondes*.

That cutting, lofty bit of criticism from Herr Klesmer had stung Gwendolen in a way no silly local slight ever could. She would never have breathed a word to anyone of how bitterly she resented never having Miss Arrowpoint’s years of rigorous musical training, training that would let her argue with the great master from a place of earned confidence instead of the shaky, untested charm she usually relied on. She would not even admit to herself that every meeting with quiet Catherine Arrowpoint sparked a strange, hot flash of unaccustomed jealousy in her: not for the girl’s fortune, but because it was infuriating that someone no one would have spared a second glance for on a crowded street—slight, average height, small plain features, sallow complexion—could carry that unshakable, unarguable mental superiority, that perfect, meticulous skill on the piano, that fastidious good taste that no amount of Gwendolen’s charm could coerce into gushing over her half-learned ballads. That perfectly polite, unassuming girl of four-and-twenty was clearly quietly judging Gwendolen’s accomplishments as no more than common, and the knowledge festered, no matter how much Gwendolen tried to brush it off.

She did not like to dwell on facts that cast her in an unflattering light, of course. The “musical magus” who had so suddenly widened her expectations of what talent could be was not always lingering nearby, and as he shuttled back and forth between London and Quetcham, Gwendolen began to tell herself she could surely win him over, make him see how much she shone, with a little time. The unreserved delight her singing drew from guests at Brackenshaw Castle, the Firs, and every other country house gathering soon soothed her ruffled ego. She trusted their unreserved praise far more than Klesmer’s sharp critique, and she was not one of those rare, tormented souls who craves a perfection no one around them ever asks for. Outwardly, at least, there was nothing especially unusual about her beyond her rare, effortless grace of movement, her striking bold wit, and that little spark of daring that made a very ordinary, self-centered hunger to stand out feel far more vivid and exciting than the same ambition smoldering under a hundred dull, conventional exteriors. The way a girl holds her head does not change how fiercely she craves supremacy, after all—it only changes what she thinks she can achieve, and how. For Gwendolen, that hunger was bounded firmly by the rules of genteel romance: she wanted to do something remarkable, something unforgettable, without ever stooping to sordid, demeaning paid work, without ever stepping outside the glittering sphere of polite society. She raged against the small constraints of her family’s reduced circumstances in her head, ignored all unasked obligations to chase what she wanted, and yet she had no radical theories, no desire to reform the world, and would have mocked any woman who claimed to want to do such a thing as a ridiculous bluestocking. Her whole world was bounded by the kind of romantic story where the heroine’s journal is full of vague dreams of power and originality and rebellion, but she still walks every path in satin shoes, so that even her misadventures feel soft, beautiful, and entirely proper.

That very ordinary, unremarkable outcome was what Gwendolen found herself drifting toward that first quiet winter at Offendene. She knew for certain she did not want to live the same dull, repetitive life every other young lady in the neighborhood was trapped in—calling on neighbors, painting bad watercolors, stitching endless samplers—but she had no clear plan for what to do instead, no idea how to assert the freedom she was so sure she was entitled to. Offendene was a fine, grand old house to be the setting for something exciting, but the surrounding neighborhood, she decided, was far too boring to ever give her that adventure.

Beyond the predictable thrill of turning every head at her first few invitations, there was little to amuse her, so she came home after bouts of sharp, knowing satire that had already ruffled Mrs. Arrowpoint’s feathers, to fill her days with very girlish, unapologetic fun. The boldest act of rebellion she could think of was to scrap young Alice’s lessons entirely—on the logic that Alice would only be happier, and likely more talented, running wild than being forced to cram dull books. She roped the governess Miss Merry, and the maids who waited on all the ladies of the house, into sewing up elaborate dramatic costumes, all ready for future charades and home theatricals she was determined to make happen, by force of will if she had to. She had never acted in her life, only posed in tableaux vivants back at school, but she knew with absolute certainty she would be brilliant at it. She had visited the Théâtre Français once, and heard her mother rave for hours about the great tragedian Rachel, so her daydreams often drifted to the idea of becoming a star actress even more famous than Rachel—after all, she was far more beautiful than that thin Jewish performer. The wet weeks before Christmas slipped by happily, stitching flowing Greek gowns, rich Eastern robes, all kinds of hybrid fantastical outfits, and she would pose and recite grand tragic lines for a small domestic audience that once even included the housekeeper. The woman had been invited to cheer her on, but ruined her welcome the first time by blurting out that Gwendolen looked far more like a queen in her everyday silk gowns than that baggy tunic that left her arms bare, so she was never asked to join again.

“Do I look as well as Rachel, Mamma?” Gwendolen asked one day, parading in her Greek drapery for Anna, reciting scraps of tragic scenes with great, fiery dramatic purpose.

“You have far better arms than Rachel,” Mrs. Davilow said, drowsy from the sofa. “Your arms would suit any part, Gwen. But your voice isn’t deep enough for real tragedy, not like hers.”

“I can make it deeper if I want,” Gwendolen shot back immediately, then shook her head, decisive. “No, actually, a higher voice is more tragic. It’s more feminine. And the more feminine a woman is, the more devastating it feels when she does something desperate and brave.”

“There may be something to that,” Mrs. Davilow said, half-listening. “But I don’t see the point of making everyone’s blood run cold. And if there’s anything horrible to be done, it ought to be left to the men.”

“Oh Mamma, you are so dreadfully prosaic! All the great poetic tragic heroines are women, everyone knows that. Men are just cautious, boring little creatures.”

“Well, dear, you’re still afraid to be left alone in the dark at night, so thank goodness I don’t think you’ll be pulling off any great crimes anytime soon.”

“I’m not talking about real life,” Gwendolen said, impatient, and as soon as her mother was called out of the room, she turned quickly to her cousin, seizing the chance. “Anna, go ask your papa if we can put on charades at the rectory. Mr. Middleton and Warham can act with us, just for practice. Mamma won’t let us have Mr. Middleton rehearsing here, but he’s a stick, we can give him all the boring parts that suit him. Go ask, or I will.”

“Oh not until Rex comes home! He’s so clever, and such a dear, he’ll act Napoleon staring out over the sea, he looks exactly like him. Rex can do absolutely anything.”

“I don’t believe a word about this perfect Rex of yours,” Gwendolen laughed, teasing her. “He’ll turn out to be just like those terrible lopsided blue and yellow watercolors you hang all over your bedroom and worship.”

“Fine, you’ll see,” Anna said, huffing a little. “It’s not just me that thinks he’s clever. He already has a university scholarship, Papa says he’s sure to get a fellowship, and no one is better at games. He’s far sharper than Mr. Middleton, and everyone but you calls Mr. Middleton brilliant.”

“He may be brilliant in a dark-lantern sort of way, but he’s still a total stick. If he had to say ‘Perdition catch my soul, but I do love her,’ he’d deliver it in exactly the same flat tone as he’d read out ‘Here endeth the second lesson.’”

“Oh Gwendolen!” Anna said, shocked by the reference. “That’s so unkind to say! He admires you more than anything. I heard Warham say to Mamma the other day, ‘Middleton’s properly spooney on Gwendolen.’ She told him off for saying it, but I know what that means. That’s what they say at university for being head over heels in love.”

“What am I supposed to do about it?” Gwendolen said, half-contemptuous, half-amused. “Perdition catch my soul if I ever love him.”

“Of course you don’t! Papa wouldn’t want it anyway, and he’s leaving soon. But it makes me sad when you tease him.”

“What will you do if I tease Rex when he gets here?” Gwendolen asked, grinning wickedly.

“Gwendolen, dear, you won’t, will you?” Anna’s eyes filled with tears immediately. “I couldn’t bear it. There’s nothing at all to make fun of in him. No one ever laughed at Mr. Middleton before you, everyone thought he was handsome and perfectly mannered. Don’t laugh at Rex, promise me.” Her beseeching little face touched Gwendolen, and she softened at once.

“You’re such a sweet little coz,” she said, tapping the tip of Anna’s chin gently between her thumb and forefinger. “I’d never do anything to upset you. Especially not if Rex is going to make all our charade plans work out perfectly.”

When Rex did finally arrive, the bright, warm, lively energy he brought to both Offendene and the rectory, and his wholehearted willingness to throw himself into every one of Gwendolen’s plans, left her no urge to tease him at all—all her jokes for him were the affectionate, flattering kind he loved right back. He was a fine, open-hearted young man, with a handsome face that resembled his father’s and Anna’s but softer in expression than the rector’s, and broader, brighter than Anna’s. His nature was warm, healthy, loving, so full of joy for simple, innocent fun that vice held no allure for him at all; what little he knew of it felt like a distant, stupid thing “some other fellows did” that he was perfectly happy to stay far away from. He loved Anna as deeply as a brother could, but he had never felt anything for anyone else even close to the blazing, instant passion that took over his whole life the second he met Gwendolen.

The cousins were together constantly, at one house or the other—mostly at Offendene, where Gwendolen reigned unchallenged, and everything she wanted automatically became Rex’s top priority. The charades unfolded exactly the way she planned, and there were dozens of little impromptu scenes she had never anticipated, where Gwendolen got to show off her natural talent for acting even more vividly. The rehearsals and performances were held at Offendene, and Mrs. Davilow saw no reason to object to inviting Mr. Middleton to join now that Rex was there—especially since Warham, who was cramming for an exam that would send him to work in India, had no spare time at all, his head so full of random facts he could barely spare a thought for anything that wasn’t a potential exam question.

Mr. Middleton was easily persuaded to take all the grave, silent parts Gwendolen assigned him, after she flattered him endlessly for his enviable still, unchanging countenance. At first he was a little hurt and jealous at how easily Gwendolen laughed and joked with Rex, but he soon convinced himself that cousinly casual familiarity like that could never lead to a serious romance. He decided her more formal, polite treatment of him was actually a sign of favor, enough to make him dare to hint at his feelings before he left Pennicote, even though he’d originally planned to wait until his career was far more established. Gwendolen was fully aware the quiet young curate worshipped her, and she didn’t mind at all. She turned her calm, unreadable eyes on him and gave him dozens of small, gentle reasons to hope, deliberately avoiding any dramatic physical contact with him, knowing full well every little small gesture could be read into whatever romantic interpretation he wanted.

A lot of people might have assumed beforehand that a pious young Anglican curate who thought even mild swearing was rude would never dream of marrying a sharp, daring girl who mocked convention so freely, who had none of the soft, meek piety everyone expected from a clergyman’s wife. Or that a young man who’d spent years steeped in theology would realize he could never keep a bright, restless girl like Gwendolen happy. But no one ever asks why their assumptions turn out to be wrong, do they? The mistake is on their side, not the world’s.

As for Rex, he would have felt sorry for poor Mr. Middleton if he’d noticed the curate’s quiet heartache, but he was so completely swept up in his first all-consuming love that he barely noticed anyone but Gwendolen existed. He didn’t watch her, he just felt her—he could tell exactly when she walked into a room even if his back was turned. Before the end of the first fortnight he was so deeply in love he couldn’t imagine a future that wasn’t tied to hers. He saw no obstacles at all; his own love felt so bright, so perfect, he assumed Gwendolen must feel exactly the same way. He could no more imagine her breaking his heart than an ancient Egyptian could imagine snow. She sang and played for him whenever he asked, was always happy to ride out with him even when the horses he borrowed were shaggy, ridiculous old nags, laughed at all his jokes, and was endlessly kind to Anna. No single sign of sympathy felt missing. It never even crossed his mind that someone as perfect as Gwendolen would make a grand, wealthy marriage far above his station. He had no overinflated ego, just the quiet confidence of a happy young man who’d never known anything but love and success, and he took Gwendolen’s perfection as a natural, obvious part of the good, bright world he’d always lived in.

One small incident that happened mid-rehearsal struck Rex as a perfect, unexpected sign of how sensitive and deep Gwendolen really was, a side of her nature no one would have guessed at who’d only ever seen her fearless on horseback or giddy and sharp at parties.

After weeks of practice, they decided to invite a small, select group of guests over to Offendene to watch their little show, which the cast were all convinced was far better than anyone expected. Anna surprised everyone, playing her small parts with a neat, sly charm no one had suspected from her shy, quiet demeanor. And Mr. Middleton was perfect, because he never once tried to be funny or overstep his role. The only problem they’d hit was Gwendolen’s stubborn desire to appear on stage in her favorite Greek gown. She couldn’t think of a single word or theme for a charade that would let her stand in that statuesque pose without looking ridiculous. They couldn’t do a scene from Racine, because none of the others could declaim French verse well, and improvised speeches would turn the whole thing into a joke. And Mr. Gascoigne had forbidden them from acting scenes from actual full plays, even though he always argued that home theatricals were perfectly fine fun for ordinary people, he wouldn’t push the bounds of local Wessex decorum so far. He was happy to let them act harmless charades in his sister-in-law’s house, which was an entirely different affair.

Everyone was determined to make Gwendolen’s wish come true, and Rex came up with the perfect idea: end the whole performance with a tableau, where no one would have to speak, so all anyone would notice was how breathtakingly regal Gwendolen looked in her gown. She loved the idea immediately, and the only thing left was to pick the right theme for the tableau.

“Something nice, children, I beg of you,” Mrs. Davilow said. “I won’t have any of that horrid Greek wickedness on display.”

“It’s no worse than Christian wickedness, Mamma,” Gwendolen protested, still thinking of her Rachel-like tragic heroines.

“And far less scandalous,” Rex added. “Besides, it all happened thousands of years ago, it’s all over and done with. What do you say to Briseis being led away? I’ll be Achilles, and you can look back over your shoulder at me, just like that print we have hanging at the rectory.”

“That would be a good pose,” Gwendolen said, nodding, but later she shook her head, decisive. “No, it won’t work. We’d need three other men in proper costume, and it’ll just look silly if we don’t have them.”

“I’ve got it!” Rex said after a moment’s thought. “Hermione as the statue in *The Winter’s Tale*. I’ll be Leontes, and Miss Merry can be Paulina, one on each side. Our costumes don’t even matter,” he laughed. “It’ll be extra Shakespearian and romantic if Leontes looks like Napoleon, and Paulina looks exactly like a regular modern spinster.”

They settled on Hermione, and everyone agreed the age gap didn’t matter at all. Gwendolen pushed for them to act just a tiny bit of the scene, enough that when the music struck up as a signal, she would step down from her pedestal and walk forward. Leontes wouldn’t embrace her, he’d kneel down and kiss the hem of her gown, and then the curtain would fall. The big antechamber with its folding double doors made a perfect makeshift stage, and every single member of the household, even Jarrett the village carpenter, was roped in to help with the preparations for a show that everyone agreed was bound to be brilliant—after all, old fables tell us that an imitation of something often works out far better than the original.

Gwendolen was extra giddy with excitement about the whole event, because she knew Herr Klesmer was back at Quetcham, and she’d made sure he was on the guest list.
