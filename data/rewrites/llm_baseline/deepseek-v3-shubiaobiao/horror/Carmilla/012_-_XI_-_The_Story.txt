“With all my heart,” said the General, his voice heavy with reluctance. After a weighted pause, he began his tale—one so strange it would haunt me long after the telling.

“My daughter had been eagerly anticipating her visit to your home, to keep company with your charming girl.” Here, he offered me a bow, gallant yet shadowed by grief. “But before that could come to pass, we received an invitation from my old friend, the Count Carlsfeld. His schloss lay six leagues beyond Karnstein, and he was hosting a series of lavish fetes in honor of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles.”

“Ah, yes,” my father murmured. “I recall hearing of their grandeur.”

“Princely does not begin to describe it,” the General replied. “The Count wielded wealth like a sorcerer’s spell. The night that marked the beginning of my sorrow was given over to a masquerade of unparalleled extravagance. The grounds were transformed—trees strung with colored lamps, the sky ablaze with fireworks that would shame Paris herself. And the music…” He trailed off, a shudder passing through him. “Such music. The finest orchestra in Europe, voices plucked from the grand operas, singing as if to lure souls from their bodies. One moment, you wandered beneath the moonlit château, its windows bleeding rosy light onto the terraces; the next, those unearthly voices would rise from the darkness of a grove or drift across the lake like spirits. It was as though I had slipped back into some long-lost romance of my youth.”

When the fireworks faded and the ball began, we retreated to the grand salons thrown open for the dancers. A masked ball is always a spectacle, but this—this was something other. Aristocracy filled every corner; I was nearly the only man present without title or pedigree.

My daughter stood out among them, radiant, her face bare. Excitement had lent her an almost unearthly glow. Yet as I watched her, I noticed another—a young woman in sumptuous attire, her features hidden behind a mask, studying my ward with unsettling intensity. I had glimpsed her earlier in the evening, first in the hall, then lingering near us on the terrace. A stately older woman, also masked, accompanied her, every inch the noble chaperone. Had the girl’s face been uncovered, I might have been certain of her fixation. Now, I have no doubt.

We had moved into one of the salons. My daughter, having danced herself breathless, rested near the door while I stood close by. The two women approached. The younger took the seat beside my ward, while her companion positioned herself near me and spoke to her charge in hushed tones.

Then, with the boldness the mask permitted, she turned to me. Addressing me by name, as though we were old friends, she wove a conversation designed to ensnare my curiosity. She spoke of courtly gatherings, of long-forgotten encounters, resurrecting memories I had not stirred in years. Each allusion was precise, each reference unsettling in its accuracy.

I grew desperate to uncover her identity. She deflected with practiced ease, delighting in my frustration. Meanwhile, the younger woman—whom her companion called *Millarca*—had struck up a conversation with my daughter.

She introduced herself as the daughter of an old acquaintance of mine. The mask, she claimed, made audacity charming. She praised my child’s beauty, her wit, laughing with her over the absurdities of the other guests. Before long, they were fast friends, and the stranger—yielding to some unspoken impulse—lowered her mask.

Her face was exquisite. Neither I nor my daughter had ever seen her before, yet there was something mesmerizing in her features, an allure impossible to resist. My poor girl was enchanted. And the stranger—she seemed equally captivated, her gaze lingering with an intensity that bordered on hunger.

Taking advantage of the masquerade’s liberties, I pressed the elder woman further.

“You’ve baffled me completely,” I admitted with a strained laugh. “Will you not relent? Remove your mask and grant me the pleasure of knowing you properly?”

Her reply was coy. “What a request! Would you ask a lady to surrender her advantage? Besides, time alters us all. You might not recognize me.”

“Philosophically true,” I conceded. “But I would risk it.”

She laughed, evading again. “Millarca is my daughter. That alone should tell you I am no longer young.”

“Your figure betrays you,” I countered.

Before she could reply, a gentleman approached—dressed in black, his bearing aristocratic, his face a deathly white that chilled me. He wore no mask, only the severe elegance of evening attire. With a bow both graceful and hollow, he addressed her:

“Madame la Comtesse, might I have a word?”

Her reaction was immediate—a finger pressed to her lips in warning. Then, to me: “Hold my place, General. I shall return shortly.”

With that, she stepped aside with the pale stranger. Their conversation was hushed, urgent. After a moment, they vanished into the crowd.

I spent the interval wrestling with the mystery of her identity, turning over every half-remembered name. I considered joining my daughter and Millarca, hoping to extract some clue before the Countess’s return. But then she reappeared, the pale man at her side.

“I will inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage arrives,” he said, then withdrew like a shadow retreating from light.
