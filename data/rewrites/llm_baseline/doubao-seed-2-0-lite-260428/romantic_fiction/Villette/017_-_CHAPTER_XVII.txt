To fight the unruly leanings of your own heart, to bend your wild, untamed natural bent to the steady governance of reason, might seem like a useless, futile struggle at first—but it does good, in the end. It turns your steps, however gently, down the path that calm judgement approves, the path that raw, heedless feeling so often pushes you to abandon. It smooths the tenor of your days, makes life more even, quieter on its surface: and that is the only surface the eyes of the world, your fellow weak, fallible mortals, will ever stop to see. What lies beneath? Leave that to God. No equal of yours, no man as frail as you, is fit to judge the secrets of the spirit He gave. Bring all your hidden pains to your Maker, kneel in the dark and beg Him for light when you are blinded, for strength when you are too weak to stand, for patience when every last thread of it is frayed thin.
Surely, one day, though it may not be the hour you beg for, the stagnant waters of the healing pool will stir. Some herald will descend, in a shape you never dreamed of, never dared to bleed for, to lead the maimed, the blind, the heartbroken down to bathe. Herald, come quickly! Thousands huddle round the edges of that pool, weeping for years on end, watching it lie still. Heaven’s hours stretch long to mortal eyes; the paths of its messengers are so wide they can circle whole ages, whole generations, before they turn back. How many millions of mourning, wounded souls will ever meet only Azrael, the angel of death, as their first and only visitor?

I dragged myself out of bed the next morning, my whole frame trembling so hard I could barely fasten my gown. I sipped cold water from the carafe on my washstand, trying to brace that trembling weakness, when Mrs. Bretton burst through the door.
“Now this is absurd!” she announced, in that bright, brusque, no-nonsense tone I had always loved, the same sharp energy she used to turn on her son when he was a boy and tried to dodge her orders. Before I could protest, she had me tucked back under the covers of the soft French bed, my clothes smoothed out of sight. “You stay right there until afternoon,” she said firmly. “My boy left strict orders before he went out this morning, and you will obey them—he is master of this house, and no one here crosses him. I will bring you your breakfast presently.”
She did, carrying the tray herself, no servant’s hand touching it, and perched on the edge of the bed while I ate. Not every respected friend can be that kind of comfort, you know: the kind whose very presence in a sick room feels like light, whose hands make bread taste sweeter, whose step alone chases the gloom out of a corner. Mrs. Bretton had always been that for me. Her lively dark eyes, her warm quick smile, her brisk, self-assured way of moving, felt like stepping out into bright, clean sea air. Even now, when she was past middle age, she moved with the quickness of a five-and-twenty-year-old.
“I would bring my needlework and sit with you the whole day,” she said, taking my empty teacup when I was done, “but that overbearing John Graham put his foot down. ‘Mamma,’ he told me as he left, ‘don’t chatter my god-daughter to exhaustion, you keep to your own rooms and spare her your company.’ He thinks you’ve had a nervous fever, Lucy—was that close to the mark?”
I told her I could not name exactly what had ailed me, only that I had suffered terribly, most of all in my mind. I did not dare say more. That hale, serene, unbroken woman had never known the dark, storm-wracked corners of loneliness where I had lately wandered. She was a stately full-rigged ship, sailing always on smooth, sunlit seas, with a brave crew and a sure captain, while I was a scuffed little lifeboat, kept dry and forgotten in a dark shed, only dragged out when the waves were mountain-high and the sky was black. I had no tales of that storm to tell her.

She left me then, and I lay content. The quiet day stretched soft around me, my small pale room all white and pale green, like a cave sunk under the sea. The cornice was edged with shell carvings, the angles of the ceiling had white mouldings that looked like dolphins, the little red satin pincushion on the dresser glowed like a piece of coral. When I closed my eyes, the wind sounded like a slow, receding tide, lapping far away against a rock base, and every footstep of the housemaid on the stairs was muffled, soft, as if it came from far above the surface of the water.
Evening crept in, and Martha brought a lamp. Dressed now, and far steadier on my feet than I had been at dawn, I walked down the stairs unassisted to the blue saloon. Dr. John was already there, standing in the window recess, reading the fine print of a newspaper by the fading dusk light. The fire glowed bright, the lamp on the table was still unlit, and Mrs. Bretton was curled half-asleep in her deep cushioned armchair, actually napping.
He saw me at once, and stepped forward on tiptoe, so as not to wake his mother, his low mellow voice soft as a lullaby.
“This little place is called La Terrasse,” he murmured, gesturing out the window. “You can’t see it from the main road, the chaussée—you turn down a lane a mile past the Porte de Crécy, that opens out into an avenue of trees, leading right here. It’s not a modern house, built in the old style of the Basse-Ville, more a small manor than a château. They named it for that wide turfed walk out front, that runs down grassy steps to the avenue. Look—there’s the moon.”
I followed his gaze. The moon was rising rosy and fiery over the bank of trees, rising fast until the red faded to pure gold, and she floated, stainless, into a soft calm sky. I saw him sigh, quiet to himself, as he watched her. I knew that sigh was not for the moon—it was for Ginevra Fanshawe. I was just about to speak her name, to give him the chance to pour out all the thoughts he had been holding back, when he spoke first.
“I went straight to Rue Fossette first thing this morning,” he said, turning away from the moon to sit down. “Told the cook you were safe, and do you know? She hadn’t even noticed you were gone. Goton had only been bringing you tisane and crusts of bread, and when you turned them away a dozen times, she got tired of the walk and only came once a day to make your bed. No one had checked on you.”
I laughed a little, and explained that Madame Beck could not have foreseen I would fall ill, that no one at the pensionnat was to blame for my loneliness. He pressed me, then, about my religion, asking if I was a Catholic, and I stared, confused. That was when he told me the full story of how he had found me: his way home through the dark, wet Basse-Ville the night before, seeing Père Silas the good old priest holding my fainting body by the gate of the Béguinage. The priest had watched me leave his church after that desperate confession I had made, I told him, when I was so lonely I had poured all my dreary, desperate complaints out to a stranger, because I had not a single other soul to speak to. I had told him nothing of my home, no names, no addresses, so Père Silas still did not know where I stayed at the pensionnat.
By the time he finished telling me the whole tale, Mrs. Bretton was waking up, squaring her shoulders and indignantly denying she had been napping at all. Graham teased her, calling her Titania, and she snapped back that he looked like Bottom, growing stout and beefy like a little John Bull, and threatened to disown him if he got too fat. The two of them bantered, laughing, across the fire, and I sat quiet, watching them, warm and soft inside. I knew he still loved Ginevra, that his heart was given over to that vain, silly girl, but for that quiet evening, at least, I was safe here, in this warm house, with the two people who cared to look after me. That night, I fell asleep not crying, not trembling, but on a bed of soft, pleasant thoughts, no storm to disturb my rest.
