My fingers slipped from the curtain, but not—oh, not—from any trivial embarrassment. No, my thoughts burned with a fiercer flame: the ruin of a man I had once admired. Mr. Godfrey, absent from the concert, stood now in this room, his voice thick with resolve. *He would do it today.* The words coiled in my ears like a serpent. What fresh degradation awaited him? Would he forsake his faith? Abandon the Mothers’-Small-Clothes, the committees, the adoring audiences who hung upon his every syllable? The horror of it seized me—I nearly flung aside the drapes to plead with him in the name of every charitable society in London—when another voice cut through the stillness.

Rachel Verinder’s. Harsh, unadorned, devoid of womanly grace.

“Why are you here, Godfrey? The library was empty.”

A soft laugh. “Miss Clack occupies the library.”

“Clack?” A rustle of skirts as she settled onto the ottoman. “Then you’re right. We stay here.”

A moment ago, I had been fevered with indecision. Now, ice filled my veins. To reveal myself was impossible. Retreat? Only into the fireplace. Martyrdom, then. With grim resolve, I adjusted the curtain just so—enough to see, to hear—and braced myself like the earliest Christians facing the lions.

“Not there,” Rachel commanded. “Sit opposite me. I prefer it.”

He obeyed, folding his too-tall frame into a low chair that did him no favors. I had never seen his legs look so absurd.

“Well?” she pressed. “What did you tell them?”

“Precisely what you told me. That your mother was unwell, and you hesitated to leave her.”

“And they believed it?”

“Entirely. They sent their love, their hopes for her swift recovery.”

“*You* don’t think it’s serious, do you?”

“Not at all. A matter of days.”

“I thought the same. Still, it was kind of you to make my excuses to near-strangers. But why miss the concert? You needn’t have suffered on my account.”

“Rachel!” His hands clasped, his face alight with that same Exeter Hall fervor—only now it turned my stomach. “If you knew how much happier I am here, with you.”

“Spare me the flattery, Godfrey.”

“Flattery? Never. Hopeless love speaks only truth.” He leaned closer, took her hand. A pause. Even she, it seemed, was not immune to his practiced tremolo.

Then—his knees struck the carpet. Both of them. Arms encircled her. Two words, delivered with the force of a revivalist’s climax:

“Noble creature!”

She did not pull away.

I, meanwhile, wrestled with propriety. Ought I to shut my eyes? Cover my ears? In the end, I did neither, clinging to the curtain as if it alone could steady me.

“A woman who speaks truth,” he murmured, “who sacrifices pride for honesty—what greater treasure exists? Rachel, let me heal you. Marry me.”

Her reply, at last, was rational: “You’re mad.”

“I speak for your future. Must you wither for a man who does not know your heart? Forget him. Let *me* offer you respect, a home, devotion.”

She wavered. Oh, the poor, misguided girl!

“Don’t tempt me,” she whispered.

“One question: Do you object to me?”

“I? I’ve always liked you. Now I admire you.”

“Many wives admire without loving. Yet their marriages thrive. Yours could too.”

A breath. Then—

“Take me.”

Two words, and she was his.

What followed, I shall not detail. Some scenes defy narration. Suffice it to say, I was one merciful instant too late in shutting my eyes.

They parted only when a footman’s panicked cry shattered the moment: “Miss Rachel! My lady has fainted—we cannot rouse her!”

Chaos. Godfrey bolted for the doctor. Rachel cradled her mother’s lifeless head. And I? I saw the truth in Lady Verinder’s still face before the doctor confirmed it.

Later, I noted with bitter amusement that she had died without opening a single one of my letters. The legacy, too, went unmentioned. A fitting end to the day’s betrayals.
