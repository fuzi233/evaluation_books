I'm not absolutely certain of my sources, but I have a strong suspicion it's Shakespeare—or perhaps some other brilliant chap—who noted that fate loves to sneak up on a bloke precisely when he's feeling absolutely top-notch and braced against the world. Usually, it hits him with a bit of lead piping. Well, the man knows his stuff. It's exactly like that for me. Just yesterday, for instance, everything was perfect.

It was one of those glorious mornings. I'd just crawled out from under the cold shower, feeling invigorated like a toddler. In fact, I was particularly buoyant because the previous day I had successfully stood up to Jeeves—I actually asserted myself, don't you know. Things had been heading toward me becoming a complete serf; the man had been oppressive. I didn't mind too much when he made me surrender one of my new suits, given that Jeeves has impeccable taste regarding attire. However, I nearly revolted when he refused to let me wear a pair of cloth-top boots I adored like brothers. And when he tried to stomp on me regarding a hat, I drew the line and reminded him who was boss. It's a lengthy tale, but the gist is this: he insisted I wear the "Longacre" (worn by John Drew), while I was dead set on the "Country Gentleman" (worn by another famous actor). After a rather painful confrontation, I purchased the Country Gentleman. So, on this specific morning, I felt incredibly manly and independent.

I was in the bathroom, contemplating breakfast plans while rubbing my spine with a rough towel and humming softly, when there was a knock at the door. I paused my humming and cracked the door open an inch.

"What ho, without!"

"Lady Malvern wishes to see you, sir," said Jeeves.

"Eh?"

"Lady Malvern, sir. She is waiting in the sitting-room."

"Pull yourself together, Jeeves, my man," I said sternly, as I generally do before breakfast to ward off pranks. "You know perfectly well there's no one waiting for me in the sitting-room. How could there be when it's barely ten o'clock?"

"I gathered from her ladyship, sir, that she had disembarked from an ocean liner at an early hour this morning."

That made it slightly more plausible. I recalled that upon arriving in America a year prior, events had kicked off at ghastly hours like six, shooting me onto foreign shores before eight.

"Who the deuce is Lady Malvern, Jeeves?"

"Her ladyship did not confide in me, sir."

"Is she alone?"

"Her ladyship is accompanied by a Lord Pershore, sir. I suspect his lordship is her ladyship's son."

"Oh, well, fetch out some decent loungewear, and I'll dress."

"Our heather-mixture lounge is ready, sir."

"Then lead me to it."

As I dressed, I tried desperately to figure out who on earth Lady Malvern could be. It wasn't until I had buttoned my shirt and reached for my studs that it clicked.

"I've placed her, Jeeves. She's a pal of my Aunt Agatha."

"Indeed, sir?"

"Yes. I met her at lunch one Sunday before I left London. A truly vicious specimen. She writes books. She penned a volume on social conditions in India after returning from the Durbar."

"Yes, sir? Pardon me, sir, but not that tie!"

"Eh?"

"Not that tie with the heather-mixture lounge, sir!"

A shock hit me. I thought I had finally subdued the fellow. It was a solemn moment. Essentially, if I backed down now, all my hard work from the previous night would vanish. I braced myself.

"What's wrong with this tie? I've seen you give it a nasty look before. Speak out like a man! What's the issue?"

"Too ornate, sir."

"Nonsense! A cheerful pink. Nothing more."

"Unsuitable, sir."

"Jeeves, this is the tie I wear!"

"Very good, sir."

Dashed unpleasant. I could see the man was wounded. But I held firm. I tied the tie, slipped into my coat and waistcoat, and marched into the sitting-room.

"Halloa! Halloa! Halloa!" I shouted. "What?"

"Ah! How do you do, Mr. Wooster? You've never met my son, Wilmot, I think? Motty, darling, this is Mr. Wooster."

Lady Malvern was a hearty, happy, healthy, and utterly overwhelming female—not excessively tall, but measuring approximately six feet from the opening prompt to the closing prompt. She filled my largest armchair as if it had been custom-built around her by someone aware of current fashion trends regarding hip compression. She possessed bright, bulging eyes, a mane of yellow hair, and when she spoke, revealed about fifty-seven front teeth. She was one of those women who temporarily numbs a fellow's faculties, making me feel ten years old and dressed in Sunday best, dragged into a drawing-room to say "how-d'you-do." Altogether, not the sort of guest one wishes to encounter before breakfast.

"Motty," the son, was about twenty-three, tall, thin, and meek-looking. He shared his mother's yellow hair, though he wore it plastered down and center-parted. His eyes also bulged, but they lacked brightness; they were a dull grey with pink rims. His chin surrendered halfway down, and he appeared to lack eyelashes entirely. A mild, furtive, sheepish sort of blighter, in short.

"Awfully glad to see you," I said. "So you've popped over, eh? Making a long stay in America?"

"About a month. Your aunt gave me your address and instructed me to call on you."

I was relieved to hear this, indicating Aunt Agatha was thawing out. There had been unpleasantness a year ago when she sent me to New York to untangle Cousin Gussie from the clutches of a music-hall girl. By the time I finished my operations, Gussie had not only married the girl but joined the stage himself, doing quite well. Consequently, Aunt Agatha was furious. I hadn't dared return to face her, so finding she had healed enough to ask friends to look me up was a relief. While I enjoyed America, I certainly didn't want England barred to me forever, especially if Aunt Agatha was truly on the warpath. England is far too small for anyone to inhabit with her if she's angry. So, bracing myself, I smiled genially at the assembly.

"Your aunt said you would do anything within your power to assist us."

"Rather? Oh, rather! Absolutely!"

"Thank you so much. I want you to put dear Motty up for a little while."

I didn't grasp this immediately.

"Put him up? For my clubs?"

"No, no! Darling Motty is essentially a home bird. Aren't you, Motty darling?"

Motty, who was sucking the knob of his walking stick, briefly uncorked himself.

"Yes, mother," he said, then corked himself up again.

"I shouldn't like him to belong to clubs. I mean, have him live with you while I am away."

These frightful words trickled out of her like honey. The woman seemingly failed to comprehend the ghastly nature of her proposal. I gave Motty a swift glance from east to west. He sat there, mouth nuzzling the stick, blinking at the wall. The thought of having this creature planted on me indefinitely appalled me. Absolutely appalled me, don't you know. I was just starting to declare the shot wasn't on the board at any price, promising to yell for the police at the first sign of nesting, when she continued, rolling placidly over me.

Something about this woman sapped a chap's willpower.

"I am leaving New York by the midday train, as I must visit Sing-Sing prison. I am extremely interested in American prison conditions. Afterward, I'll travel gradually to the coast, visiting points of interest. See, Mr. Wooster, I am in America principally on business. No doubt you read my book, *India and the Indians*? My publishers are eager for a companion volume on the United States. I won't be able to stay longer than a month, as I must return for the season, but a month should suffice. I spent less than a month in India, and my dear friend Sir Roger Cremorne wrote his *America from Within* after only two weeks. I'd love to take dear Motty with me, but the poor boy gets so sick traveling by train. I'll have to pick him up on my return."

From where I sat, I could see Jeeves in the dining room setting the breakfast table. I wished I could have a private minute with him. I was certain he could devise a way to halt this woman.

"It will be such a relief to know Motty is safe with you, Mr. Wooster. I know the temptations of a great city. Hitherto, dear Motty has been sheltered from them. He has lived quietly with me in the country. I know you will look after him carefully, Mr. Wooster. He will give very little trouble." She spoke of the poor blighter as if he weren't present.
