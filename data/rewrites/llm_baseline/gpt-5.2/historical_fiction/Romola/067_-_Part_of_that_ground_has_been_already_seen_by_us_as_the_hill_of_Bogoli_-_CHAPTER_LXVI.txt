Palm Sunday had come again—Olive Sunday, as the Valdarno folk called it when the trees outside the walls were already silvering with new leaf and the presses were being scrubbed in readiness for another year’s oil. The rain of yesterday had washed the stones clean, so that Florence woke under a sky that looked freshly unsealed, and the morning light lay on roof-tiles and river like a thin, clear broth poured over everything, drawing out every savor.

In San Marco the air itself seemed changed—cool with the mingled breath of people and incense, and threaded by the smallest sounds: the rustle of olive boughs, the faint creak of benches, the soft coughs that rose and died as if each throat remembered it stood in a holy place. Above that domestic hush of a crowd settling itself, Savonarola’s voice fell from the pulpit with the plain steadiness of one who had already tasted what was to come. There was no thunder in him now, but a grave sincerity, like bread broken without ornament. He told them he was ready to die. The visions that had carried him like a wind over the city’s rooftops had narrowed until he saw only his own end, and he named it as calmly as a man naming the hour of a meal.

When he lifted his hands in benediction, faces rose to him—women with their eyes wet and steady, men with the look of laborers listening for the bell that releases them, children whose attention wavered and then returned as if tugged back by the sound of his cadence. He looked once, took the sight into himself, and then descended. There was in the way he turned from them a finality like the last extinguishing of a hearth at night: the warmth remains in the stones, but you know no more flame will be laid there by that hand.

Before the sun had set, Florence was not a church but a furnace.

All day the city had worn its quiet like a thin crust over embers. When evening came, what had smouldered broke open again into open fury—fury not left to chance, but fed and guided, with the sly help that comes when officials look away at the needed moment. It began at the Duomo. Certain Compagnacci, eager as men who have sniffed blood, pushed to hinder the sermon the Piagnoni had gathered to hear. Words went hot; shoulders knocked; then steel showed. And as soon as the affray was born, the cry rose with it—one cry swallowing all others: “To San Marco! Fire to San Marco!”

The sound ran like a knife along the streets. Long before daylight had died, church and convent were under siege, surrounded by an ever-swelling multitude whose anger had the rhythm of waves—surging, falling back, and surging again, louder.

Inside, the convent had not been left to bare hands. The brethren, long aware of the hostility thickening beyond their walls, had arms ready. Some fought in their long white tunics with a vigor that made them seem less friars than old crusaders risen in their own cloth; the serge did not hide the muscle beneath it. Savonarola commanded them, pleaded with them, but the instinct to defend one’s shelter is older than obedience, and stronger when doors are about to give way.

Laymen too had stayed—men who had chosen not to flee when safety lay outside. They fought with a bitterness of loyalty. There was firing from the high altar, close by the great crucifix—as if the very place where bread and wine were lifted could also spit flame. From the convent roof stones and hot embers poured down: gritty, burning hail, with the sharp smell of scorched wood and singed cloth rising each time it struck. In the cloisters, where prayer had so often paced in circles, swords rang at close quarters. The stone arcades caught and threw back the clamor until it was hard to tell where any sound began; Florence’s own acoustic life had turned on itself, the sacred spaces made into echoing chambers for hate.

The attack lasted into deep night, though the assailants were many. The Government’s demonstrations had all gone against the convent. Early in the struggle guards were sent—yet not to scatter the crowd or quiet the street’s roar, but to command those within to lay down their arms, to order laymen to depart, and to require Savonarola himself to quit Florentine territory within twelve hours.

Had he stepped out then, he could scarcely have escaped being torn apart. He was willing to go; it was his friends who hindered him, for willingness is not always wisdom when a mob is the judge. Even for laymen of high name it was perilous to slip away by the garden wall. Yet some did. Among them was Francesco Valori, grey-haired and resolute, who hoped to raise help from without—still believing, as men do, that rescue can be summoned like servants with a bell if only one rings hard enough.

By deep night the struggle could not have endured much longer. The Compagnacci, maddened and still increasing, might soon have carried their swords into the library where Savonarola prayed with those brethren who had not taken arms, or who had laid them down at his command. Then, like a second gust of wind coming to push a wavering flame into one chosen direction, a new body of guards arrived. They bore a commission from the Signoria to demand the persons of Fra Girolamo and his two coadjutors, Fra Domenico and Fra Salvestro.

Lantern-light wavered across stone and faces. When the convent door opened and the Frate appeared between guards who offered him no safety but the hardness of a prison, a roar went up—triumphant hate, thick as smoke. The crowd surged into the narrow street, each man striving to be first in the stream, to stand nearest the captive prophet, to earn the luxury of insult, and if possible to land a blow. Armed Compagnacci slid among the guards, not hindered from doing so. They pressed close enough to spit, to strike, to kick.

Savonarola felt hands on him—dragging, shoving; he felt the lanterns lifted deliberately to throw their light on him, so that deriding faces might drink in the sight. He heard the coarse words that are always ready in a city, like garbage kept in a corner and flung out when the chance comes. He felt spittle and blows. And with it came, strangely, a sense that the worst bitterness had already passed. If men judged him guilty and were set on his blood, then only death waited. There is a kind of ease in knowing the menu will not change.

Yet the bitterest drop cannot be pressed upon the lips from without. The lowest depth is not martyrdom, but the moment when, in silence, one covers one’s head and feels: I am not worthy to be a martyr; the Truth will prosper, but not by me. That thought—untranslatable into the shouted language around him, which had only names for triumph and disgrace—lay inside him like a stone.

He disappeared under the doorway of the Old Palace, and the crowd’s brief imperfect triumph was like a taste that makes a beast crave more. If the prophet was taken, were there not the houses of his friends to be sacked?

Already half the armed multitude, too far back to share much in the siege, had been busy at a more profitable work: attacking rich houses—not with random hunger for plunder, but with a discriminating knowledge of whose doors should be broken. It showed guidance. The rabble with clubs and staves were officered by sword-girt Compagnacci. The riot wore a mask, but the hand beneath it was practiced.

And was there not—next criminal after the Frate—the ambitious Francesco Valori, suspected of wishing, with the Frate’s help, to make himself Doge or Gonfaloniere for life? The grey-haired man who eight months before had lifted arm and voice in fierce demand for justice on five fellow-citizens had escaped from San Marco only to meet what others called justice now. He saw his house surrounded by an angry greedy throng; he saw his wife shot dead with an arrow; and he himself, on his way to answer a summons to the Palazzo, was murdered by the swords of men named Ridolfi and Tornabuoni. Thus the Masque of the Furies—called Riot—played on through night into early morning, turning Florence’s streets into a stage where each new shriek was a cue.

The chief director did not show himself. He had reasons for keeping his red feather out of sight while work was done before dawn. He issued orders from a private retreat—one house, one room, in a quiet street between Santa Croce and San Marco: the same place where Tito had once paid a secret visit to Dolfo Spini.

There Spini, captain of the Compagnacci, sat through the memorable night receiving visitors who came and went and came again—some in the guise of armed youths with heated eyes, others obscurely dressed and apparently unarmed. The room smelled of tallow and wine. On the table stood abundant drink, with cups set ready as if for a feast; and though Spini watched himself against being made stupid by excess, he took enough from time to time to sharpen the excitement that the continual news fed in him. The tapers had burned down into low shapeless masses, their wicks drowning in grease; and through holes in the shutters the outer light began to mark itself—a sombre grey, like the first skim of dawn on cold broth.

Among the obscure visitors Ser Ceccone came often. As the hours drew towards morning twilight he stayed, a constant companion beside Francesco Cei, who lay in rather careless hiding within Florence, expecting his banishment to be revoked as soon as the Frate’s fall was secure. While messages arrived and were muttered over, Spini sprang from his seat and began to walk up and down, his face flushed with anger at some talk that had passed between his two unmilitary companions. At last he burst out, the words spitting like oil on a hot pan.

“The devil spit him! He shall pay for it, though. Ha, ha! The claws shall be down on him when he little thinks of them. So he was to be the great man after all! He’s been pretending to chuck everything towards my cap, as if I were a blind beggarman, and all the while he’s been winking and filling his own scarsella. I should like to hang skins about him and set my hounds on him! And he’s got that fine ruby of mine—I was fool enough to give him yesterday. Malediction! And he was laughing at me in his sleeve two years ago, and spoiling the best plan that ever was laid. I was a fool for trusting myself with a rascal who had long-twisted contrivances that nobody could see to the end of but himself.”

“A Greek, too, who dropped into Florence with gems packed about him,” said Francesco Cei, with a slight smile of amusement at Spini’s fuming. “You did not choose your confidant very wisely, my Dolfo.”

“He’s a cursed deal cleverer than you, Francesco—and handsomer too,” Spini snapped, turning on him with the general desire to worry whatever came within reach.

“I humbly conceive,” Ser Ceccone began, smoothing his hands as if they were parchment he wished to keep uncreased, “that Messer Francesco’s poetic genius will outweigh—”

“Yes, yes, rub your hands! I hate that notary’s trick of yours,” Spini broke in, for this roughness was a part of his patronage. Then he jerked his head as footsteps sounded. “But there comes Taddeo, or somebody. Now’s the time. What news, eh?”

Two Compagnacci entered with the look of men who have run through smoke and shouting, their faces hot and their breath still carrying the outside din as if it clung in their throats.

“Bad,” said one. “The people have made up their minds they were going to have the sacking of Soderini’s house, and now they have been balked. We shall have them turning on us if we don’t take care. I suspect there are some Mediceans buzzing about among them, and we may see them attacking your palace over the bridge before long, unless we can find a bait for them another way.”

“I have it!” Spini said, the words coming quick as a knife drawn. He seized Taddeo by the belt and pulled him aside, speaking low and rapid, giving directions with the precision of a man portioning meat on a board—this for here, that for there, quick work, no waste. Meanwhile the other man continued to tell Cei how the Signoria had interfered about Soderini’s house.

“Ecco!” Spini exclaimed at last, giving Taddeo a slight push toward the door. “Go—and make quick work.”
