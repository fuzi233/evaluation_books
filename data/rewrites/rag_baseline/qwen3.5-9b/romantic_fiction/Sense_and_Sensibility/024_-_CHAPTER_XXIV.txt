"I feel unworthy of the confidence you've placed in me," Elinor began, her voice steady but measured, "if I possess no desire to sustain it, nor any further curiosity regarding its source. Therefore, I offer no apology for raising this matter anew."

"Thank you!" Lucy cried, her warmth genuine. "You've broken the ice; you've set my heart at ease. I was terrified I'd offended you back on Monday."

"Offend me?" Elinor replied with absolute sincerity. "Could you possibly imagine such an intent? To suggest I hold a grudge against you would be absurd. What motive could you have for trusting me that wasn't honorable and flattering?"

"And yet," Lucy countered, her sharp eyes narrowing with a mixture of defiance and vulnerability, "your manner seemed cold, displeased. It made me uneasy. I've been tormenting myself ever since, blaming myself for the liberty of troubling you with my affairs. But I'm relieved to know it was merely my own insecurity. Knowing you don't blame me... knowing you listen to what occupies my thoughts every moment of my life... it is a profound consolation."

"It was a relief to you," Elinor acknowledged, "to confess your situation and receive assurance that you'll never regret it. Your position is precarious; you're surrounded by obstacles. You'll require every ounce of mutual affection to endure them. Mr. Ferrars relies entirely on his mother."

"He possesses only two thousand pounds of his own," Lucy continued, her voice dropping slightly. "It would be madness to marry on that sum alone. Yet, I could surrender every prospect of wealth without a sigh. I've grown accustomed to a meager income; I could struggle through poverty for him. But I love him too dearly to be the instrument of his deprivation, robbing him of whatever his mother might bestow if he marries solely to please her. We must wait. Perhaps for years. For most men, this would be an alarming prospect, but Edward's affection and constancy are invincible. I know that."

"That conviction is everything to you," Elinor observed carefully, shielding her face from any hint of skepticism. "And he shares that trust in yours. Had your reciprocal attachment faltered—as it often does between lovers separated by four years—their situation would indeed be pitiable."

Lucy looked up, hopeful. "Edward's love has faced rigorous testing. Our prolonged separation since our engagement has been severe, yet it has held firm. I would be unpardonable to doubt it now. He has never given me cause for alarm."

Elinor suppressed a sigh. "You claim to be jealous by nature," she noted, "and given his immersion in society versus your seclusion, plus our constant distance, you were prone to suspicion. Any slight change in his demeanor, any drop in spirits unaccounted for, any excessive attention paid to another lady, or any visible unhappiness at Longstaple would have revealed the truth instantly. You insist you couldn't be deceived."

"All this," Elinor thought, *is charming rhetoric, but it deceives neither of us.*

"So," she asked aloud after a pause, "what are your intentions? Are you resigned to waiting for Mrs. Ferrars's death—a bleak and shocking ultimatum—or is her son prepared to submit to the tedious years of suspense rather than risk her wrath by revealing the truth?"

"If we could be certain it would only be temporary! But Mrs. Ferrars is headstrong and proud. Her initial fury would likely secure everything for Robert. The mere thought of that, for Edward's sake, extinguishes any inclination toward rash action."

"And for your own sake, too," Elinor added gently. "Or are you pushing your altruism beyond reason?"

Lucy fell silent, staring at Elinor. "Do you know Mr. Robert Ferrars?"

"Not at all. Never saw him. But I suspect he's unlike his brother—silly and a great coxcomb."

"A great coxcomb!" Miss Steele interjected, her ear catching the phrase amidst a lull in Marianne's piano playing. "Oh, they're discussing their favorite beaux, surely."

"No, sister," Lucy corrected sharply. "Our favorite beaux are *not* great coxcombs."

"I can vouch for it," Mrs. Jennings laughed heartily. "Miss Dashwood's beau is one of the most modest, well-behaved young men I've ever encountered. As for Lucy, she's such a sly little creature; it's impossible to guess whom she favors."

"Oh," Miss Steele said, casting a significant glance around the room, "I daresay Lucy's beau is equally modest and well-behaved as Miss Dashwood's."

Elinor flushed involuntarily. Lucy bit her lip, glaring at her sister. A heavy silence descended upon the group until Lucy broke it in a lowered tone, mindful of Marianne's powerful concerto.

"Honestly, I've devised a scheme recently to bring matters to a resolution. I must share this secret, for you are a party concerned. Given your acquaintance with Edward, you know he prefers the clergy to any other profession. My plan is for him to take holy orders as soon as possible. Then, through your influence—which I trust you'd exercise out of friendship for him, and perhaps some regard for me—your brother might be persuaded to grant him the Norland rectory. I understand it's a lucrative living, and the current incumbent is unlikely to survive long. This would provide sufficient income for us to marry, leaving time and chance to handle the rest."

"I would always be happy," Elinor replied, "to demonstrate my esteem for Mr. Ferrars. However, do you not realize my influence would be superfluous? He is the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood—that connection alone is ample recommendation to her husband."

"But Mrs. John Dashwood would strongly oppose Edward entering the ministry."

"Then I suspect my intervention would achieve little."

Another long silence ensued. Finally, Lucy sighed deeply. "I believe the wisest course is to dissolve the engagement immediately. We are besieged by difficulties on every side. While it would render us miserable temporarily, we might ultimately be happier. But you will not offer your counsel, Miss Dashwood?"

"No," Elinor smiled, masking her agitation. "On such a subject, I certainly will not. You know well that my opinion holds no weight unless it aligns with your desires."

"You wrong me," Lucy declared with grave solemnity. "I hold no one's judgment in higher esteem than yours. If you were to say, 'I advise you by all means to terminate your engagement with Edward Ferrars; it will serve both your happiness,' I would resolve to act immediately."

Elinor blushed at the insincerity of the future bride-to-be. "Such a compliment would deter me from offering any opinion had I formed one. It elevates my influence far too high; the power to sever two souls so tenderly bound is beyond the reach of an indifferent observer."

"'Tis precisely because you are an indifferent person," Lucy retorted with pique, emphasizing the word, "that your judgment carries such weight. If you were biased by personal feelings, your opinion would be worthless."

Elinor deemed it wiser to remain silent, fearing they might escalate into an unsuitable display of intimacy. She resolved never to revisit the topic. Thus, another lengthy pause followed, and Lucy ended it first.

"Will you be in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?" she asked with practiced cheerfulness.

"Certainly not."

"I'm sorry," the other replied, her eyes lighting up. "It would have brought me such joy to meet you there! But I daresay you'll go anyway. After all, your brother and sister will invite you."

"It won't be within my power to accept their invitation if they do."

"How unfortunate!" Lucy lamented. "I relied on meeting you there. Anne and I are traveling late January to visit relations who've urged us to come for years! But I go solely to see Edward. He'll be there in February; otherwise, London holds no charm for me. I lack the spirit for it."

Soon, Elinor was summoned to the card table by the conclusion of the first rubber, ending the intimate discourse. Both women submitted to the interruption without reluctance; nothing had been said to diminish their previous cordiality. Elinor seated herself at the cards with a melancholy certainty: Edward possessed no affection for the woman destined to be his wife, and lacked even the chance of marital happiness that sincere affection on her part might have secured. Self-interest alone drove Lucy to maintain an engagement she knew he found exhausting.

From that point forward, Elinor never revived the subject. When Lucy inevitably reintroduced it, eager to report every letter from Edward, Elinor responded with calm detachment, dismissing the conversation as soon as civility permitted. She viewed such talks as an indulgence Lucy did not deserve and dangerous to herself.

The Steeles' visit to Barton Park extended far beyond the initial invitation. Their favor grew; they became indispensable. Sir John refused to entertain the idea of their departure. Despite their numerous, long-standing engagements in Exeter and the pressing necessity to return weekly, they were prevailed upon to stay nearly two months, participating enthusiastically in the grand festivities requiring private balls and lavish dinners to proclaim their significance.
