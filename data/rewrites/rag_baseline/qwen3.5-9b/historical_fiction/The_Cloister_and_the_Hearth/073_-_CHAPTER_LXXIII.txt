Catherine is not an unmixed blessing within a stranger's domain; her governing spirit is potent enough to transmute the very atmosphere of a home. Scarce does she cross the threshold before the utensils gleam with renewed vigor, the hearth sweeps itself clean, the windows dilate to swallow more light, and the soul of an enormous cricket animates the dwelling. Yet this cricket is a relentless Busy Body—a title that carries the weight of tyranny. Possessing no discrimination, it seeks to rectify everything and everyone. While many things improve under such correction, nothing improves universally; indeed, everything resists being set aright save for the final, silent sanctuary of the grave.

In Margaret's house, Catherine rearranged every chair and table, perhaps for the better, yet her ambition soon escalated to overturning the living furniture itself. As Margaret's confinement drew near, Catherine treacherously summoned the aid of Denys and Martin. When the poor, simple-minded men earnestly inquired what service they could render, she directed them to a brief walk. Thus far, acceptable. But she hinted that extending this promenade into the middle of the following week would be even preferable. This was hardly ingratiation. The subsequent behavior of the strong under the yoke of the weak might have appeased a she-bear with three cubs, one of whom was already sickly. Generally, they slipped out at daybreak and returned like thieves in the night; if caught indoors, they moved about like cats walking on a wall tipped with broken glass, wearing expressions of awe-struck subjugation and a pervasive air of depression. Yet, all was insufficient. Their mere presence was ill-timed, jarring against Catherine's frayed nerves. Did instinct whisper that a pair of depopulators had no business in a house housing multipliers twain? The breastplate offers no armor against a female tongue, and Catherine drove infinite pins and needles of speech into them.

In short, when Margaret descended the stairs, she found the kitchen swept of heroes. Martin, old and stiff, had retreated no farther than the street, carrying off his baggage—a stool—and sitting upon it in the open air. Margaret saw he was exposed to the sun but failed to realize he had become a fixture within that luminary. Seeking Denys, she declared, "Good, kind Denys; he will be right pleased to see me about again." Catherine, wiping a bowl with now superfluous vigor, informed her that Denys had departed for his friends in Burgundy. "And high time! He hasn't been nigh them this three years, by all accounts." "What, gone without bidding me farewell?" Margaret asked, lifting two tender eyes like full-blown violets. Catherine reddened; this new perspective pricked her conscience. Yet, giving a little toss, she dismissed the guilt: "Oh, you were asleep at the time; and I would not have you wakened."

"Poor Denys," Margaret sighed, and visible dew gathered on her open violets. Catherine, observing from the corner of her eye without overt acknowledgment, turned her attention to the surviving depopulator. Suddenly, she lavished hospitality and tenderness upon Martin, old and stiff in more ways than one. "No, thank you, dame," he replied. "I have got used to out o' doors. And I love not changing and changing. I meddle wi' nobody here; and nobody meddles wi' me." "Oh, you nasty, cross old wretch!" Catherine screamed, passing in a moment from treacle to the sharpest vinegar, before flouncing back into the house.

On calm reflection, she allowed herself a little cry. Then, half-reconciling herself to her conduct, she vowed to be so kind that Margaret should never miss her plagues of soldiers. Feeling still uneasy, she dispensed all regrets through a process both simple and sovereign: she took the child and washed him. From head to foot, she scrubbed him in tepid water, and heroes, along with their grievances, became as dust in an ocean of soap and suds.

While this celestial ceremony proceeded, Margaret could not remain quiet. She hovered around the fortunate performer, determined to have an apparent hand in it, if not a real one. She dipped her finger into the water—to pave the way for her boy, presumably, for she could not deceive herself into thinking Catherine would allow her to settle the temperature. During the ablution, she knelt opposite little Gerard, prattling to him with amazing fluency, taking care not to articulate like grown-up people; after all, how could a cherub understand their ridiculous pronunciation?

"I wish you could wash out THAT," she said, fixing her eyes on the little boy's hand. "What?" "What, have you not noticed? On his little finger." Granny looked, and there, indeed, was a little brown mole. "Eh, but this is wonderful!" she cried. "Nature, my lass, you're strong; and meddlesome to boot. Have you noticed such a mark on anyone else? Tell the truth, girl!" "What, on him? Nay, mother, not I." "Well then he has; and on the very spot. And you never noticed that much. But, dear heart, I forgot; you haven't known him from child to man as I have. I've had him hundreds of times on my knees, just as this, and washed him from top to toe in luke-warm water." Swelling with conscious superiority, she looked down at Margaret as a woman beyond competition. Catherine gazed upward from her dizzy height and moralized. She differed from other busy-bodies in that she occasionally reflected—not deeply, or of course she would take care not to print it.

"It is strange," she remarked, "how things come round and about. Life is but a whirligig. Leastways, we poor women, our lives are all cut upon one pattern. Wasn't I for washing out my Gerard's mole in his young days? 'Oh, fie! Here's a foul blot,' quoth I; and scrubbed away at it until I made the poor wight cry; so then I thought 'twas time to give over. And now says you to me, 'Mother,' says you, 'do try and wash out o' my Gerard's finger,' says you. Think on't!"

"Wash it out?" cried Margaret. "I wouldn't for all the world. Why, it is the sweetest bit in his little darling body. I'll kiss it morn and night till he that owned it first comes back to us three. Oh, bless you, my jewel of gold and silver, for being marked like your own daddy, to comfort me." She kissed little Gerard's little mole, but she could not stop there; she presently had him sprawling on her lap, kissing his back all over again and again, seeming to worry him as a wolf worries a lamb. Catherine watched on, smiling. She had seen many such savage onslaughts in her day. This little sketch indicates the tenor of Margaret's life for several months. One or two small things occurred to her during that time which must be told; but I reserve them, since one string will serve for many glass beads.

But while her boy's father was passing through those fearful tempests of the soul, ending in the dead monastic calm, her life might fairly be summed in one great blissful word—Maternity. You, who know what lies in that word, enlarge my little sketch: see the young mother nursing and washing, dressing and undressing, crowing and gambolling with her first-born. Then, swifter than lightning, dart your eye into Italy, and see the cold cloister; see the monks passing like ghosts, eyes down, hands meekly crossed over bosoms dead to earthly feelings. One of these cowled ghosts is he, whose return, full of love, and youth, and joy—that radiant young mother awaits.

In the valley of Grindelwald, the traveler finds on one side the perpendicular Alps, all rock, ice, and everlasting snow, towering above the clouds and piercing to the sky; on his other hand lie little everyday slopes, green as emeralds, studded with cows and pretty cottages, and life. Whereas those lofty neighbors stand leafless, lifeless, inhuman, sublime. Elsewhere, sweet commonplaces of nature are apt to pass unnoticed; but fronting the grim Alps, they soothe, and even gently strike, the mind by contrast with their tremendous opposites. Such, in their way, are the two halves of this story, rightly looked at: on the Italian side, rugged adventure, strong passion, blasphemy, vice, penitence, pure ice, holy snow, soaring directly at heaven. On the Dutch side, all on a humble scale and womanish, but ever green. And as a pathway parts the ice towers of Grindelwald, aspiring to the sky, from its little sunny braes, so here is but a page between "The Cloister and the Hearth."
