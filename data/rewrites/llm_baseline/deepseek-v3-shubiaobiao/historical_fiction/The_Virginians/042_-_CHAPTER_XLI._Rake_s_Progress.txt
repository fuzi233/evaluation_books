In Harry Warrington’s day, the debate between the merits of ancient and modern learning still raged—though our young gentleman paid it little heed. The moderns of that age are now our ancients, just as our own era will be judged by future generations. And though our ancestors may have boasted superior Greek scholars, we surpass them in science, morality, and the refinement of society.

Consider the novels of their time—*Clarissa*, *Tom Jones*, *Roderick Random*—paintings of a coarser age. Were we to depict such scenes today, the virtuous Nineteenth Century would blush and banish the offending volumes from respectable shelves. Yet, in their excesses, there was life—vibrant, unchecked, and often reckless.

And so we find young Harry Warrington in his Bond Street lodgings, swept into the whirl of London’s fashionable vices. White’s Club, the gaming tables of Mayfair, the races at Newmarket—he indulged in them all. If he brawled in the streets, lost at cards, or drank too deeply, who could blame him? The age demanded it.

Yet his letters home betrayed none of this dissipation. To his mother, he remained the dutiful son, recounting visits to Cambridge, evenings with lords, and even an audience with the Duke himself. He spoke of investments, of friends like the gallant Wolfe, of his admiration for King’s College Chapel—but never of the dice clattering across the table, nor the debts mounting in his wake.

Then came the whispers of an entanglement—an older woman, a scheming cousin, a promise rashly given. His aunt, the shrewd Baroness Bernstein, saw the danger and wrote in warning: *"She hath laid herself out for twenty husbands these twenty years past."* But Harry, proud and obstinate, would not be swayed.

So the dance continued—between virtue and vice, duty and desire. And though his mother, reading between the lines, might have wept, she could not yet see the full extent of his folly. For Harry Warrington was still young, still bold, and still convinced that the world would bend to his will.

But London, as he would learn, was not so easily mastered.
