If the well-gowned lady, whose age was indeterminate and who occupied a secluded corner of the Belleville hotel piazza late that afternoon, had selected a narrative demanding intense mental focus, her efforts would likely have been in vain. Her eyes, though refined and revealing a mixture of acuteness and kindness, appeared far more captivated by the unfolding tableau before her than by the pages she was diligently turning.

The scene was designed to entertain a leisure mind. First lay the sea, a vast expanse of azure speckled with sails; next, the beach, animated by clusters of youths clad in garish hues; followed by the village street, and finally, a lawn where young couples occasionally drifted with tennis rackets or golf clubs. Children were present, yet they failed to engage this amiable spinster—though her status as one was undeniable. She barely spared them a second glance, whereas a young married couple or a particularly imposing elder gentleman invariably drew her gaze away from her book, which she habitually held too close to read properly.

Such a critique, however, could not be leveled at the spruce, albeit frail, octogenarian seated at the opposite end of the piazza. He was clearly engrossed in a novel held conspicuously open, evidenced by the occasional smiles disrupting the usual placidity of his benevolent countenance, suggesting it was quite amusing. Yet, midway through his chapter, he abruptly closed the book, extracted a newspaper, and spread it to its full width before settling in to peruse the columns. Simultaneously, the lady at the other end of the piazza was observed peering over her spectacles at two gentlemen who had just emerged from the great doors separating her from the elderly reader. Did she recognize them, or was mere curiosity piqued? By the manner in which she slammed her book shut and rose, it appeared she had spotted old acquaintances in the distinguished pair now advancing slowly toward her. If so, she could not have been overjoyed, for after the initial sign of their approach, she turned with an air of considerable embarrassment and fled onto the lawn. There she stood with her back to them, caressing a small dog in a way that betrayed a total lack of sympathy for the creature, which were evidently her terrors whenever her natural instincts allowed her to move freely.

The two gentlemen, conversely, displayed an air of total indifference to her proximity, continuing their stroll until they reached the piazza's terminus, where they mechanically retraced their steps. Their faces, now fully visible to the elderly man absorbed in his newspaper, caused the latter to shift the sheet a trifle—perhaps due to the sun glaring in his eyes, or perhaps because he wished to catch a glimpse of two remarkably striking individuals. If the former motive prevailed, the opportunity was fortuitous, as they halted merely a few feet from his chair. One of them was elderly, matching or surpassing the age of the man watching him, yet belonging to that renowned strain of Scottish stock wherein members remain tough and hale at eighty. This vigor manifested not only in his upright, graceful figure but also in the glance of his calm, cold eye, which surveyed everyone and everything with unflappable composure. In stark contrast, his younger companion, though equally stalwart, possessed an appearance that seemed to shrink with acute sensitivity from every soul encountered, save for the exceedingly mild old reader near whom they paused for a dozen words of conversation.

"I do not believe it serves me any purpose," the young man remarked, his tone gloomy. "I am wretched in her company, and twice as wretched when I attempt to forget myself in her absence. Perhaps we should return. I would sooner sit where she might see me than endure her wondering—Oh, I shall be careful; but you must recall how unnerving is the profound silence I am compelled to maintain regarding what is destroying us both. I am nearly as ill as she."

They withdrew, and their ostensibly disinterested listener turned the page of his paper. Five minutes elapsed before they returned. This time it was the old gentleman who spoke, and having proven more discreet or less dominated by emotion than his companion, his voice dropped lower, rendering his words difficult to distinguish.

"Escape? To the south coast—she will forget to watch you for—a clinging nature—impetuous, yet foolishly affectionate—you understand that—no danger—discovered—the timing—a cheerful home—courage—happiness—all forgotten."

A gesture from the young man as he departed indicated he shared no such optimism. Meanwhile, Miss Butterworth—who, surely, you have now recognized—had ample opportunities of her own. She remained stooped over the dog, which wriggled beneath her hand yet offered no escape, perhaps mesmerized by that hesitant touch which she may or may not have known had never previously been inflicted upon a canine. However, her ears and attention were fixed on two girls chatting freely on a nearby bench, as if they were utterly alone on the lawn. They gossiped about a fellow inmate of the grand hotel, and Miss Butterworth listened intently after overhearing the name Adams. Among the words she caught were these:

"But she is! I tell you she is sick enough to require a nurse and a physician. I caught a glimpse of her as I passed her room yesterday, and I never beheld eyes so large or cheeks so pale. Then, observe him! He must simply adore her, for he refuses to speak to another woman and paces that small, stifling room all day. I wonder if they are bride and groom? They are young enough, and if you have noted her attire..."

"Oh, refrain from discussing clothes. I saw her on her first arrival and was the victim of despair until she suddenly became ill and thus unable to wear those magnificent waists and jackets. I felt like a dowdy compared to that pale blue ensemble—"

"Oh, well, blue suits blondes. You would look like a fright in it. I didn't concern myself with her clothing, but I did feel that our prospects were ruined if she chose to converse, or even smile, upon the few men foolish enough to remain a week in this place. Yet, she is no beauty; she lacks a good nose, a handsome eye, nor even an irreproachable complexion. It must be her mouth, which is lovely, or her walk—did you notice her walk? It was as if she were floating; that is, prior to her falling down in that faint. I wonder why she fainted. No one was doing anything, not even her husband. But perhaps that was what troubled her. I noticed that, for some reason, he appeared very serious—and when she attempted to attract his attention two or three times without success, she simply collapsed from her chair to the floor. That roused him. He has scarcely left her since."

"Do they not appear very happy, do you, for such a wealthy and handsome couple?"

"Perhaps he is dissipated. I have observed that the old gentleman never leaves them."

"Well, well, he may be dissipated; handsome men are often prone to such vices. But I wouldn't care if—"

Then the dog yelped and bolted. Miss Butterworth had unconsciously pinched him, likely in indignation at the trajectory these flighty young ladies' conversation was taking. This created a diversion, causing the young girls to depart and leave Miss Butterworth without occupation. However, a young man crossing her path at that moment provided sufficient subject for thought.

"You recognize them? Is there no mistake?" he whispered.

"None; the one proceeding this way is the young man I witnessed departing Mr. Adams's residence, and the other is the elderly gentleman who arrived subsequently."

"Mr. Gryce advises you to return home. He intends to arrest the young man." Sweetwater then passed on.

Miss Butterworth strolled to a seat and sat down. She felt weak; she seemed to visualize that young wife, sick and overwhelmed, struggling against her formidable fears, sinking under this crushing blow with no woman nearby capable of offering the slightest solace. The father did not impress her as a man capable of supporting her fainting head or soothing her bruised heart. Beneath his polished exterior lay an icy look that repelled this keen-eyed spinster, and recalling the coldness of his demeanor, she felt seized by an irresistible impulse to be near this young creature when the blow fell, if only to alleviate the tension of her own heartstrings, which at that moment ached sharply over the role she felt compelled to play in this affair.

Yet, when she rose to seek Mr. Gryce, she discovered him gone; and upon searching the piazza for the other two gentlemen, she saw them vanishing around a corner toward a small smoking-room. Unable to follow them, she ascended the stairs. Meeting a maid in the upper hall, she inquired for Mrs. Adams. Being told that Mrs. Adams was ill, she was directed to the door of her room, situated at the end of a long corridor. Since all corridors terminated in a window beneath which a sofa was placed, she felt circumstances favored her and took her seat upon the sofa before hers in a state of great complacency. Instantly, a sweet voice was heard through the open transom of the door, behind which her thoughts were already concentrated.

"Where is Tom? Oh, where is Tom? Why does he leave me? I am terrified of what he may be tempted to do or say down on those great piazzas alone."

"Mr. Poindexter is with him," replied a voice, measured yet kind. "Mr. Adams was becoming extremely weary, and your father persuaded him to descend and enjoy a cigar."
