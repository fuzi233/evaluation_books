After a disastrous London season full of screaming matches with her husband, Lady Clavering beat a hasty retreat to a cozy little villa tucked on the edge of Tunbridge Wells’ most postcard-perfect common. Her daughter Blanche came along, obviously, and holiday-bound 13-year-old Frank Clavering traipsed back too, whose favorite home pastime for the first 48 hours was picking petty fights with his stepsister Blanche. That little hobby died fast, though, once he discovered Tunbridge had cricket, rental horses, and a whole crew of other 13-year-old chaos gremlins who turned up at the Begum’s villa every single day. The lot of them gorged on tarts, snuck way too much champagne, raced ponies across the lawn until Lady Clavering nearly fainted from fright, and smoked cheap cigars until the dining room reeked so bad Blanche threatened to camp out in the garden shed. She did not care for the company of 13-year-old gentlemen, thank you very much.

Blanche, for her part, was the sort of girl who got restless if the wallpaper stayed the same longer than 72 hours, so the country novelty hit just right. For a whole week she’d have happily pretended to live in a cottage on bread and cheese, for the aesthetic! For a single night, she’d even have volunteered to camp in a dungeon on bread and water, for the drama! So the move to Tunbridge was no hardship at all. She wandered the woods, scribbled passable sketches of gnarled trees and farmhouses, hoarded French novels like they were being outlawed, drove into town at the first whiff of any conjurer, mediocre local ball, or third-rate touring musician, napped 14 hours a day, bickered with her mother and Frank before breakfast, wandered into the village school to coo at the pupils and pick fights with the schoolmarm, then snapped at the kids and laughed at the teacher ten minutes later. And of course, she was at the tiny local church every Sunday: a precious little Anglo-Norman gem that was practically brand new, decked out in stained glass, carved saint heads, gilded scripture quotes, and unreserved open pews.

Blanche immediately set to stitching a hyper-accurate high-church altar cover, and completely bamboozled the local vicar, our old friend Smirke, Pen’s former tutor and the man who’d once tried to court Helen Pendennis. He’d gotten over that rejection by marrying a nice young lady his Clapham-raised mother picked out for him, and after his mother passed away, his church views got steadily more and more dramatic. He cut the collars off his coats, grew his hair down past his shoulders, ditched his dorky little forehead curl and the prized fancy cravat he’d once been very proud of, stopped wearing any necktie at all, skipped dinner every Friday, read the Roman Hours, and announced he was ready to hear confessions in the vestry. The local dissenting pastor Muffin and old church’s Mr. Simeon Knight spent weeks yelling that he was a secret, dangerous Jesuit working directly for the Pope. The chapel of ease he’d built was paid for entirely by his mother’s Clapham inheritance—goodness only knows what that strait-laced old lady would have said if she saw a table being called an altar, candlesticks sitting on it, or letters dated for the Feast of Saint Whozit.

But Smirke was the most harmless creature alive, no secret Pope postbags ever showed up at his door. At first he thought Blanche was the most pious, talented, charming soul he’d ever met, and when Blanche spent two full hours locked in the vestry chatting with him, his wife Belinda paced up and down the tiny two-grave churchyard daydreaming of adding a third, or running off to a convent to become a nun. It took Smirke a few weeks to wise up: he noticed Blanche threw a screaming fit at the school, lost her temper, and rapped little Polly Rucker’s knuckles very hard. The second Blanche stopped fawning over him for a few days, he sighed, blushed, and admitted to Belinda that he’d been tricked, all her piety was just a fun new hobby for her. Belinda launched herself straight into his arms, no more convent plans, no more gravestone daydreams, and he cooed at her that there was no other woman half as precious as her. Blanche, for her part, forgot both of them existed the second she walked out the church gate.

That stage of indifference hadn’t hit yet when Arthur Pendennis turned up to spend a few days visiting the Begum. Smirke was still fully convinced Blanche was a literal angel, and gushed to Pen for hours about how much better she was than even the opera he’d snuck off to see once (the same one he’d gone to with Helen and Pen back at Chatteris, which he still felt guilty about). Pen found Smirke’s goofy, sincere high-church schtick absolutely delightful, and all his over-the-top praise of Blanche made Pen look at his old acquaintance with very favorable new eyes.

Blanche was absolutely thrilled to see Pen, too: he was fresh from London, full of all the latest gossip, and could rattle off that beloved London small talk none of the stuffy locals understood. The first night he arrived, he made Blanche laugh so hard through dinner she barely had time to pick a fight with anyone. She even hugged and kissed her mother out of nowhere, shocking the honest Begum half out of her wits. When bedtime rolled around, Blanche sighed the prettiest, most dramatic “Déjà!” possible, squeezed Pen’s hand so tight it left a mark, and Pen wandered out to his window to smoke a cigar, convinced that Blanche had gotten immeasurably nicer and sweeter.

“Ooh, heavenly!” a voice trilled out the window next to his, Blanche’s, from behind a clematis-covered casement. Pen snort-laughed, and asked her not to snitch about him smoking. “Go on! I adore the smell!” Blanche called back. “Heavenly night! Heavenly, heavenly moon! But I have to shut my window now, can’t chat with you out here, the locals would have fits about les moeurs. How ridiculous those old decency rules are, aren’t they? Adieu!”

The next day they wandered the fields together, gossiping like old friends about their younger days, Blanche getting all soft and sentimental, gushing about Laura like she was her long-lost sister, begging Pen to bring her down to Tunbridge for visits. The day after that, they rambled through the woods to Penshurst to see the famous old park and hall, and Smirke rambled to Pen even more about Blanche’s supposed perfect piety. Pen spotted her sitting in the schoolhouse “instructing the children,” and fully bought that she was the sweetest, most unworldly, unspoiled girl alive.

“Do you really like the country this much?” he asked her, as they walked the path back.

“I never want to see that horrible smoky London face ever again,” Blanche sighed. “Good thoughts grow here in the quiet woods, like those little delicate flowers that wilt the second you stick them on a London balcony. But… heigho.”

“What’s that sigh for, Blanche?”

“Never mind. I wish you’d never come here,” she sniffled, pulling out her best new collection of sad verses, Mes Soupirs. “Not because I don’t want you—because I don’t want to be sad when you leave.” And then the tears started, Mes Larmes making a very dramatic encore.

What’s a young man supposed to do when a pretty girl cries in a summer wood, surrounded by roses and dewy grass and wildflowers? Two very cynical, seen-it-all London rakes, who’d both weaseled their way out of a dozen worse flirtations, suddenly conned themselves completely. For a whole glowing afternoon, they were no longer jaded city social climbers. They were just simple, sweet Phyllis and Corydon, madly in love under the trees. It’s no wonder almost no single person leaves a week of country walks still unmarried. The place is a total romance scam, and it works every single time.
