My temporary residence was to be the royal pyramid. Outside, the glittering cavalcade assembled in the great paved square before the structure, clustering into shifting groups. The mammoth halted before the massive doorway; once a staircase was hoisted into place, trumpets blared, and we three—having ridden in the golden half-castle beneath the canopy of snakes—descended to the ground.

Clearly, we were moving from the open sky into the stone mazes hidden within the pyramid. Without thought, the deep-seated instinct of custom and reverence, now woven into my very nature, compelled me to turn toward the towering rocks of the Sacred Mountain looming above the city. I performed the customary obeisance, offering the prescribed prayer in silence. I did this unthinkingly, as a matter of routine. Yet, as I rose, I swore I heard a sharp titter of laughter rising from somewhere within that fantastically dressed crowd of onlookers.

I frowned, glancing toward the source of the mockery, then turned to Phorenice to demand their immediate punishment for such disrespect. But a strange discrepancy met my gaze. I had expected to see her in the act of rising from her prostration, yet she stood perfectly erect, having apparently never touched her forehead to the dust. Worse, she regarded me with a peculiar expression I could not decipher.

Whatever turmoil churned in her mind, she showed no intention of addressing it publicly before the gathered masses. "Come," she said simply. Turning toward the doorway, she cried out the secret word appointed for the day. The ponderous stone blocks barring the porch swung back on their hinges. With stately tread, she passed from the hot sunshine into the cool gloom beyond, followed decorously by her fan-girl. A heaviness began to settle in my heart. Following her, I entered the pyramid, and the stone doors slammed shut behind us with a sullen thud.

We traveled only a short distance before halting in the Hall of Waiting. I recalled this place vividly: the red walls adorned with images of kings, and the central burning fountain of "earth-breath" blazing from a bronze jet, casting its eerie light upon the floor. Twenty years prior, the late King had welcomed me here before sending me off to my vice-royalty in Yucatan. But the atmosphere had shifted. Once pure and sweet, the air now hung heavy with an unknown scent, feeling languid and oppressive.

"My minister," the Empress said, "I acquit you of intentional insult. However, I suspect the colonial air has reduced you to a very simple man. An obeisance such as you paid to that mountain less than a minute ago has not been witnessed since I assumed the throne."

"Your Majesty," I replied, "I am a member of the Priests' Clan, raised in their tenets. Before entering any dwelling, I am taught to thank the Gods—and specifically our Lord the Sun—for the breath of life they provide. Having been chased by streams of fire and foul vapors amidst the mountains during their periodic boils, I can recite this prayer from the depths of my heart."

"Circumstances have changed since you left Atlantis," Phorenice countered. "When thanks are given now, they are not directed at those old Gods."

Her meaning struck me, and I nearly recoiled at the impiety of it. If this were the new order, I would have no part in it. Serving a monarch was one thing; to compromise with sacrilege and elevate a swineherd's daughter to the status of deity, receiving prayers and bows, revolted my sense of honor. I therefore invited a confrontation.

"Phorenice," I stated firmly, "I have been a priest from childhood, revering the Gods and intimate with their mysteries. Until I personally discover those ancient truths to be false, I must remain loyal to that allegiance. If there is a price for this faithfulness, I am prepared to pay it."

She regarded me with a slow, deliberate smile. "You are a strong man, Deucalion," she observed.

I bowed.

"I have encountered others as stubborn," she continued, shaking out the ruddy curls of her hair so the light of the burning earth-breath illuminated her face and form. "Yet they were all converted. I have found it as easy to convert the obstinate as to burn them. Indeed, there has been little talk of burning; everyone has rushed to conversion, whether I wished it or not. It seems my appearance and my tongue lack their former charm today."

"Phorenice is Empress," I answered stoically. "I am her servant. Tomorrow, if granted leave, I will clear away the rabble clamoring outside the walls. I must begin to demonstrate my utility."

"I've heard you are quite the fighter," she remarked. "I possess some skill in arms myself and consider myself a competent judge. Tomorrow, we shall test our abilities in battle. But today, I must complete the honorable reception I have planned for you. The feast will be ready shortly, and you will wish to prepare accordingly. Chambers have been selected for your use, stocked with necessities. Ylga will show you the way."

We waited until Phorenice had exited the glow of the light-jet, disappearing through a shadowed doorway at the far end of the hall. Then, the fan-girl taking a lamp and leading the way, we threaded our path through the narrow, twisting mazes of the pyramid.

The air was thick with perfumes, and the passages spiraled and doubled through solid stone, designed so that strangers might spend hours—or days—searching for a specific chamber. The architects of this forgotten age possessed immense cunning; they ensured kings could not be slain by vulgar assassins slipping in from without. Legend holds that the king of that era, to ensure absolute security, ordered the execution of all who had built the pyramid or even mapped its inner stones.

Yet, the fan-girl moved with practiced ease, the lamp swinging in her hand. She doubled here, turned there, and stopped before a blank wall to push a stone, revealing a passage. Once, she pressed a corner of a floor flagstone, causing it to rise and expose a steep, narrow stair. We descended, emerging onto an incline that led upward again, ascending degree by degree until we reached the chamber assigned to me.

"There is clothing in these chests lining the walls," the girl announced, gesturing to the storage units. "Jewels and trinkets reside in that bronze coffer. These are the Empress's first gifts—a small foretaste of what is to come. My Lord Deucalion may now discard his simplicity and dress in finery suited to current fashion."

"Girl," I snapped, "be more decorous with your tongue, and spare me such trivial advice."

"If my Lord Deucalion considers this rude, he may address Phorenice directly, and I shall be whipped. If he commands it, I can be stripped and scourged before him. The Empress does much for Deucalion just now."

"You are closer to that whipping than you realize," I warned.

"I have a name," she retorted, glaring up at me from beneath her dark brows. "They call me Ylga. You might have heard it while riding the mammoth, had you not been so consumed by Phorenice."

I studied her curiously. "I have never seen you before," I said. "And your first words are those likely to bring you trouble. There must be a purpose to all this."

She pushed the massive stone blocking the chamber's doorway, then placed her jeweled fingers on my garment, drawing me carefully away from the drafty airshaft into a distant corner. "I am the daughter of Zaemon," she whispered, "whom you knew."

"Do you bear a message from him?"

"How could I? He resides in the priests' dwellings on the Mountain you bowed to. I haven't seen him in two years. But when I watched you step out from that red pavilion at the harbor, I... I felt a pang of pity for you, Deucalion. I remembered you were my father's friend, and I knew what Phorenice had planned. She has been plotting this for two months."

"I cannot speak ill of the Empress."

"And yet..."

"What?"

She stomped her sandal hard against the stone floor. "You must be either incredibly blind or incredibly daring. But I will not interfere further—not now. Still, I shall watch. Should you ever require a friend, I will attempt to serve you."

"I thank you for your friendship."

"You seem to take it lightly. Why, sir, even now I doubt you understand my power, nor do you guess my motives. You may be the first man in this kingdom, but let me remind you: I rank as the second lady. Remember, women hold high station in Atlantis now. Believe me, my friendship is a commodity sought after with frequency and industry."

"As I said, I am grateful. You seem to underestimate my gratitude, Ylga. Credit me: I have never bestowed such thanks upon a woman before. Therefore, you should cherish it for its rarity."

"Well," she said, "there is an education ahead of you." She departed, instructing me on how to summon slaves for assistance. For a full minute, I stood bewildered by my own words. Who was the daughter of Zaemon that she could persuade me to alter a lifetime of habits?
