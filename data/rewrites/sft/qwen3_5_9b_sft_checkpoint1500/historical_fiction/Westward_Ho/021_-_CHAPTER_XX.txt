The following chapter, "Spanish Bloodhounds and English Mastiffs," recounts the brutal naval engagement between Amyas's ship, the *Rose*, and the Spanish fleet. Following the tragic loss of his brother Francis, Amyas Leigh rallies his crew, utilizing superior seamanship and morale to defeat the larger Spanish vessels, including capturing two galleys and liberating enslaved Englishmen. However, the victory comes at a steep cost: the *Rose* suffers severe damage, leaking heavily, and the crew falls into a dangerous demoralization. Through sheer resolve and the example of his officers, Amyas steers the battered ship into a secluded mangrove river near Higuerote Bay to seek repairs, navigating a landscape that feels like a nightmare of decay and disease.

"When the sun leaped up the next morning, and the tropic light flashed suddenly into the tropic day, Amyas paced the deck with disheveled hair and torn clothes, his eyes red with rage and weeping, his heart full of an agony that defies description. Picture it, if you have ever lost a brother; if not, give thanks that you know nothing of such grief. Filled with impossible projects, he strode and staggered up and down as the ship thrashed close-hauled through the rolling seas. He vowed to burn the villa, to take Guayra, and to demand the life of every man there for his brother's soul. 'We can do it, lads!' he shouted. 'If Drake took Nombre de Dios, we can take La Guayra.' Every voice shouted, 'Yes.'"

"We will have it, Amyas, and have Frank too, yet," cried Cary, but Amyas shook his head. He knew, with a certainty he could not explain, that no port in New Spain would ever restore to him that one beloved face.

"Yes, he shall be well avenged. And look there!" He pointed toward the shore, where three sails appeared between them and the distant peaks of the Silla, no more than five miles to windward. "There are the Spanish bloodhounds on our heels—the same ships we saw yesterday off Guayra. Back, lads, and welcome them, if they were a dozen."

A murmur of applause rose from the deck. If any young heart sank at the prospect of fighting three ships at once, it was awed into silence by the cheers of the older men and Salvation Yeo's stentorian voice. "If there were a dozen, the Lord is with us, who has said, 'One of you shall chase a thousand.' Clear away, lads, and see the glory of the Lord this day."

"Amen!" cried Cary, and the ship was kept still closer to the wind. Amyas had revived at the sight of battle. He no longer felt his wounds, nor his great sorrow; even Frank's last angel-like look grew dimmer as he bustled about the deck. Ere a quarter of an hour had passed, his voice rang firmly and cheerfully as of old. "Now, my masters, let us serve God, and then to breakfast, and after that clear for action."

Jack Brimblecombe read the daily prayers and the prayers before a fight at sea, his honest voice trembling as he added, in the Prayer for all Conditions of Men, "and especially for our dear brother Mr. Francis Leigh, perhaps captive among the idolaters."

"Now, then," said Amyas, "to breakfast. A Frenchman fights best fasting, a Dutchman drunk, an Englishman full, and a Spaniard when the devil is in him, and that's always."

"And good beef and the good cause are a match for the devil," said Cary. "Come down, captain; you must eat too."

Amyas shook his head, took the tiller from the steersman, and bade him go below and fill himself. Will Cary went down, returned in five minutes with a plate of bread and beef, and a great jack of ale, coaxing them down Amyas's throat as a nurse does with a child, before scuttling below again with tears hopping down his face. Amyas stood still, steering. His face had grown seven years older in the last night. A terrible, set calm lay upon him. Woe to the man who crossed his path that day!

"There are three of them, you see, my masters," he said as the crew came on deck again. "A big ship forward, and two galleys astern. The big ship may keep; she is a race ship, and if we can but recover the wind of her, we will see whether our height is not a match for her length. We must give her the slip, and take the galleys first."

"I thank the Lord," said Yeo, "who has given so wise a heart to so young a general; a very David and Daniel, saving his presence, lads; and if any dare not follow him, let him be as the men of Meroz and of Succoth. Amen! Silas Staveley, smite me that boy over the head, the young monkey; why is he not down at the powder-room door?"

And Yeo went about his gunnery as one who knew how to do it, possessing a terrible mind to execute it thoroughly and a terrible faith that it was God's work. So all fell to. Though there was comparatively little to be done, for the ship had been kept in fighting order all night, there was "clearing of decks, lacing of nettings, making of bulwarks, fitting of waist-cloths, arming of tops, tallowing of pikes, slinging of yards, doubling of sheets and tacks"—enough to satisfy even the pedantic soul of Richard Hawkins himself. Amyas took charge of the poop, Cary of the forecastle, Yeo, as gunner, of the main-deck, while Drew, as master, settled himself in the waist. All was ready, and more than ready, before the great ship was within two miles.

Now, while the mastiffs of England and the bloodhounds of Spain neared each other over the rolling surges, thirsting for blood, let us pause to consider the causes that enabled the English to face and conquer armaments immensely superior in size and number. What enabled Sir Richard Grenville's *Revenge*, in her last fearful fight off the Azores, to endure for twelve hours the attack of eight Spanish squadrons, two of which sank at her side? What enabled young Richard Hawkins's *Dainty*, though half her guns useless, to maintain a running fight with two Spaniards of equal size, double the weight of metal, and ten times the number of men? What enabled Sir George Cary's *Content* to fight single-handed from seven in the morning till eleven at night with four great squadrons and two galleys, though her heaviest gun was but one nine-pounder? And lastly, what enabled those two "valiant Turkey Merchantmen of London" to cripple the whole fleet of Spanish galleys sent to intercept them off Pantellaria?

There were more causes than one. First, the build of the English ships: they were much shorter than the Spanish, giving them an ease in maneuvering which utterly confounded their foes. The English ships charged with marvelous agility, discharged their broadsides, and flew forth presently into the deep, leveling their shot directly at the heavy, unwieldy Spanish ships. Moreover, the Spanish fashion in the West Indies was to build their men-of-war flush-decked, leaving those on deck exposed and open; while the English fashion was to heighten the ship at stem and stern, both by the sweep of her lines and by stockades ("close fights and cage-works") on the poop and forecastle, giving the men shelter further increased by strong bulkheads across the main-deck, dividing the ship into separate forts fitted with swivels and loopholed for musketry and arrows.

But the great source of superiority lay, after all, in the men themselves. The English sailor was an amphibious and all-cunning animal, capable of turning his hand to everything from needlework to gunnery. He was a product of a nation where every citizen was not merely permitted but compelled by law to practice the use of the bow from childhood, considering sword-play and quarter-staff as necessary parts of education and pastime. They were the "fiercest nation upon earth," and the freest; each man fought for himself with the self-help and self-respect of a Yankee ranger, and once bidden to do his work, was trusted to carry it out by his own wit.

The English officers, too, lived on terms of sympathy with their men unknown to the Spaniards, who raised absurd barriers of rank and blood. The English officers, brought up to the same athletic sports and martial exercises as their men, were not ashamed to care for them, to win their friendship, even to consult their judgment, using their rank not to differ from their men but to outvie them. Drake touched the true mainspring of English success when he once rebuked some coxcomb gentlemen-adventurers with, "I should like to see the gentleman that will refuse to set his hand to a rope. I must have the gentlemen to hale and draw with the mariners." Under Elizabeth and her ministers, a brave and shrewd man was certain of promotion, let his rank or age be what they might; the true honor of knighthood covered any lowliness of birth, and the merchant service was a nursery for seamen and warriors.
