“Eh? Wot I say? I spik true w’en I say dat Buck two devils.”

François said it into the cold the way a cook might taste a stew and declare, with a shake of his head, that there were two sharp peppers where only one had ever been used before. Morning had hardly unstitched the night from the Yukon sky when he found Spitz gone and Buck standing in the trampled snow, his coat torn open in red seams. François hauled him close to the fire, turning him so the flames could read the story written on his hide—gaping rips, ragged cuts, the slick dark of blood already stiffening where the air bit it.

“Dat Spitz fight lak hell,” Perrault said, bending to look as if he were judging meat at market, not wounds.

“An’ dat Buck fight lak two hells,” François answered, and the pride in his voice was the same pride men take when a hard loaf comes out of the ashes light and sound, proof that hands and patience can master hunger. “An’ now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble, sure.”

Perrault went to packing the camp outfit, stowing and lashing as neatly as any pantry is set in order before a long winter. François began to harness the team, leather straps and frozen buckles snapping and creaking. The dogs watched the ritual with the solemn attention of those for whom supper is the true clock of the world.

Buck trotted up without hesitation to the place where Spitz had stood—at the head, where the trail first spoke into the darkness and the leader answered. He planted himself there as if the snow had been waiting for his feet.

But François, not looking, brought Sol-leks forward instead. In his mind Sol-leks was the best lead-dog left: an old hand, steady as a well-seasoned pot, blind on one side but canny on the other.

Buck sprang at him like a sudden boil-over. He drove Sol-leks back, shouldered him out, and stood where he had meant to stand all along.

“Eh? eh?” François cried, slapping his thighs with delight. “Look at dat Buck. Heem keel dat Spitz, heem t’ink to take de job.”

Then his voice hardened. “Go ’way, Chook!”

Buck did not move. He had the stubbornness of a root crop in frozen ground—if you wanted it, you’d have to dig, and digging would cost you.

François took him by the scruff. Buck growled, low and thick, a sound that carried heat in it though the air was fifty below. François dragged him aside and put Sol-leks in front again. Sol-leks showed plainly he wanted no part of it; his body said fear even when his face tried to say nothing.

François turned to another buckle. When his back was half a heartbeat away, Buck was back in the lead, displacing Sol-leks again. Sol-leks did not resist; he slid away like a dog thankful to avoid the knife.

François returned with a heavy club. The club’s dark grain caught the firelight, and Buck, seeing it, remembered another club, another man—red sweater, red anger—and the lesson that had burnt itself into his bones: there were laws beyond teeth.

He retreated slowly, not in defeat but in calculation, circling just outside the reach of the club, his lips lifted, his eyes fixed not on François but on the arc the wood could travel. He had learned the geometry of punishment.

The driver called him when the harnessing reached the point where Buck belonged in his old place in front of Dave. Buck backed away two steps. François advanced; Buck backed away again. The dance went on until François, thinking the fear had done its work, threw the club down.

It was then the truth showed itself. Buck was not asking for mercy. He was refusing it. He wanted the lead. He had bought it in blood, and he would not accept change as if it were charity.

Perrault stepped in. Between them they chased him around camp for the better part of an hour. They flung clubs; Buck slipped aside. They cursed him—his fathers, his mothers, his seed to the remotest generation, every hair and drop of blood—and Buck answered with snarls and the hard grin of a dog who will not bargain his hunger down.

He did not run away. He kept close, orbiting the fire and the sled and the line of harnessed dogs, making it plain: meet my demand, and I will come in. Leave it unmet, and I will stay out forever.

At last François sat down and scratched his head as if trying to find the missing sense under his fur cap. Perrault looked at his watch and swore. Dawn was a faint promise behind the trees, and already they were late.

François scratched again, then shook his head and grinned sheepishly at Perrault. Perrault shrugged, beaten.

François went to Sol-leks, unfastened him, and led him back to his old place. The team stood in one long line, harnessed and waiting, the sled ready. There was nowhere left for Buck but the front.

François called. Buck, at a distance, laughed—dogs do laugh, with a loose mouth and bright eyes—and kept away.

“Throw down de club,” Perrault ordered.

François obeyed. The club fell into the snow with a dull thud, like a lid set down.

Buck came in at once, trotting lightly, laughing triumphantly, and swung into position at the head. François fastened his traces. The sled was broken out. Both men ran, and the team dashed onto the river trail, bells chiming thinly in the wide white silence.

Highly as François had valued Buck—his “two devils”—he found before the day was old that he had still undervalued him. Buck took up leadership as if it were a craft learned long ago in another life and only lately remembered. Where quick judgment mattered—choosing the firmest line over glare ice, sensing the weak snow crust before a runner broke through—he proved superior even to Spitz, whom François had once believed unmatched.

But it was in laying down the law that Buck shone. Dave and Sol-leks did not care. Their pride was the pull itself, the deep satisfaction of harness biting shoulders and the sled moving because they willed it. So long as the traces stayed taut and the day’s work was honest, leadership was no affair of theirs. Let even Billee, the soft-hearted, lead, if only the team kept order.

The others had grown unruly in Spitz’s last days, like a kitchen left without a head cook—each pot boiling its own way, each hand stealing taste when it pleased. Buck set them straight.

Pike, sly and lazy, who loved to nip at heels and yet hold back his own weight until forced, learned quickly what loafing cost. Buck shook him again and again, swift as a hand slapping a thieving boy away from the cooling rack. Before the first day ended, Pike was pulling more than he ever had.

That night, Joe—the sour one—tested the new rule as he had tested every rule. Buck did what Spitz had never managed: he smothered Joe with sheer weight, pinned him, and cut him up until snapping turned to whining, and the bitterness in him found no purchase.

The tone of the team lifted at once. Old solidarity returned. They leaped as one dog in the traces, and the runners sang their thin song across packed snow.

At Rink Rapids, two native huskies were added—Teek and Koona—raw as newly split kindling. Buck broke them in so quickly François stared as if he’d watched a man knead frozen flour into bread without fire.

“Nevaire such a dog as dat Buck!” he cried. “No, nevaire! Heem worth one t’ousan’ dollair, by Gar! Eh? Wot you say, Perrault?”

Perrault nodded. The record run was on, and they were ahead of it, gaining day by day.

The trail was good—hard packed, clean, with no new-fallen snow to drink the strength from shoulders and pads. It was not too cold, though the thermometer dropped to fifty below and stayed there, a steady iron taste in the air. The men ran and rode by turns. The dogs were kept on the jump, stops few and short.

The Thirty Mile River lay comparatively coated with ice, and they covered in one day going out what had taken them ten days coming in. From the foot of Lake Le Barge to the White Horse Rapids they made a sixty-mile dash. Across Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett—seventy miles of lakes—they flew so fast the man whose turn it was to run was towed behind the sled on the end of a rope, skimming like a dragged branch.

On the last night of the second week they topped White Pass and dropped down the sea slope with the lights of Skaguay and the shipping at their feet. Fourteen days, forty miles a day: a record run.

For three days Perrault and François threw chests up and down Skaguay’s main street, the work clattering and booming between buildings, and were deluged with invitations to drink. The team was the center of a worshipful crowd of dog-busters and mushers, men whose talk was all lash, lead-dog, and luck. Then three or four western bad men tried to clean out the town and were riddled like pepper-boxes for their pains, gunfire cracking the air and scattering attention as quickly as wind scatters ash. Public interest turned to other idols.

Then came official orders, crisp as paper that has crossed too many hands. François called Buck to him, threw his arms around him, and wept over him, his tears freezing on the fur as if sorrow itself could be preserved in this country.

And that was the last of François and Perrault. Like other men, they passed out of Buck’s life for good.

A Scotch half-breed took charge of Buck and his mates. In company with a dozen other dog-teams they started back over the weary trail to Dawson. It was no light running now, no glory of record time. This was the mail train, and the load behind was heavy with letters—thin sheets of the outside world, tasting of ink and old rooms, carrying names and words across the white emptiness to men who sought gold under the shadow of the Pole.

Buck did not like it. The work was different—steady, grinding, without the bright edge of racing—but he bore up, and soon took pride in it in the same manner as Dave and Sol-leks. He kept discipline and saw that each dog, whether he cared for pride or not, did his share.

Life became monotonous, machine-like in its regularity. One day was very like another.

At a certain time each morning the cooks turned out. Fires were built. The smoke rose and flattened under the cold, and the sound of axes and the quick pop of sap in burning wood made a small music in the vast quiet. Breakfast was eaten. While some broke camp, others harnessed dogs, and they were under way an hour or so before the darkness thinned into the warning of dawn.

At night, camp was made again. Some pitched the flies; others cut firewood and pine boughs for beds. Still others carried water or ice for the cooks. And always, first for the dogs, came food—fish, frozen hard, then thawed and softened by heat until it gave off its heavy river smell. To them, that smell was home, history, and promise. Afterward it was good to loaf for an hour with the other dogs—five-score and odd—listening to the camp settle, to the iron clink of pots, to men’s voices roughened by cold, to the far-off hush of snow-laden timber. Here sound mattered. A sudden shout, a snapping whip, the harsh ring of a pan dropped carelessly could set nerves on edge like a knife on glass. In the clean hush, every noise was a choice; and Buck learned, as all creatures learn in the North, that peace is partly the absence of useless clamor.

There were fierce fighters among the dogs, but three battles with the fiercest brought Buck to mastery. After that, when he bristled and showed his teeth, they got out of his way.

Best of all, perhaps, he loved to lie near the fire, hind legs crouched under him, fore legs stretched out, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames. Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller’s big house in the sun-warmed Santa Clara Valley, of the cement swimming-tank, of Ysabel the Mexican hairless and Toots the Japanese pug. But oftener he remembered the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the great fight with Spitz—and, with a clarity that made his mouth wet, the good things he had eaten or would like to eat: warm meat steaming in the air, grease that coated tongue and throat, marrow-scent rising when bone cracked, the salt tang of blood in snow, fish softened at the edge of a fire until its skin split and its smell thickened into comfort.

He was not homesick. The Sunland was dim and distant, and such memories had no power over him. Far more potent were the memories of his heredity—instincts, the old habits of blood—giving to things he had never seen a strange familiarity. And sometimes, as he blinked into the flames, it seemed they were not these flames at all.

The fire became another fire.

He crouched beside it and saw another man—not the half-breed cook, but a man shorter of leg and longer of arm, with muscles stringy and knotted rather than rounded. The hair of this man was long and matted; his head slanted back under it from the eyes. He uttered strange sounds—no words Buck could translate, only the raw shapes of feeling pushed out into the cold—and he peered continually into the darkness, afraid of it, clutching in a hand that hung midway between knee and foot a stick with a heavy stone fastened to the end.

The man was all but naked, a ragged fire-scorched skin hanging partway down his back. Yet on his body there was much hair, matted into almost a fur across chest and shoulders and down the outside of arms and thighs. He did not stand erect but leaned forward from the hips on legs bent at the knees. There was in him a peculiar springiness, almost catlike, and the quick alertness of one who lived in perpetual fear of things seen and unseen.

At other times that hairy man slept by the fire, squatting with head between his legs, elbows on knees, hands clasped above his head as though to shed rain by hairy arms.

And beyond that fire, in the circling darkness, Buck could see many gleaming coals, two by two—always two by two—which he knew for the eyes of great beasts of prey. He could hear them crash through undergrowth, could hear the night noises they made, and those sounds—so old they were newer than any word—raised the hair along his back until it stood in a ridge over shoulders and up neck. He would whimper low or growl softly, and the half-breed cook would shout, “Hey, you Buck, wake up!”

Then the other world vanished. The real camp returned: the smoke, the men, the iron pots, the lean smell of fish. Buck would get up, yawn, stretch as if he had slept.

It was a hard trip with the mail and the heavy work wore them down. They were short of weight and in poor condition when they made Dawson, and should have had a week’s rest, ten days at least. But in two days’ time they dropped down the Yukon bank from the Barracks, loaded with letters for the outside.

The dogs were tired. The drivers grumbled. To make matters worse, it snowed every day, softening the trail, increasing friction on runners, and making every pull heavier. Yet the drivers were fair through it all, doing their best for the animals. Each night the dogs were attended to first. They ate before the drivers ate, and no man sought his sleeping-robe till he had seen to the feet of the dogs he drove—pads checked, ice cut away, cracks tended as carefully as a cook tends blistered hands.

Still, their strength went down. Since the beginning of winter they had traveled eighteen hundred miles, dragging sleds the whole weary distance. Eighteen hundred miles will tell upon the toughest life.

Buck stood it, keeping the team up to their work and maintaining discipline, though he, too, was very tired. Billee cried and whimpered regularly in his sleep each night. Joe grew sourer. Sol-leks became more unapproachable on either side, blind or seeing.

But it was Dave who suffered most.

Something had gone wrong with him, some inward hurt that showed itself only in the way his patience curdled. He became morose and irritable. When camp was pitched he made his nest at once and let his driver feed him there. Once out of harness and down, he did not rise again till harness-up in the morning.

Sometimes in the traces, when the sled jerked at a sudden stop or strained to start, Dave cried out with pain—a sharp sound in the clean air, a note that did not belong among bells and runners. The driver examined him, but could find nothing. All the drivers grew interested. They talked over it at meal-time and over their last pipes before sleep, voices low, the fire hissing softly as new wood caught.

One night they held a consultation. They brought Dave from his nest to the fire and pressed and prodded until he cried out many times. Something was wrong inside, but they could locate no broken bone and could not make it out.

By the time they reached Cassiar Bar he was so weak he fell repeatedly in the traces. The Scotch half-breed called a halt and took him out, making Sol-leks fast to the sled in his place. His intention was to rest Dave and let him run free behind, where the trail was easier.

Sick as he was, Dave resented it. He grunted and growled as the traces were unfastened, and when he saw Sol-leks standing in the position he had held so long, a broken-hearted whimper tore out of him. The pride of trace and trail was his. Sick unto death, he could not bear that another dog should do his work.

When the sled started he floundered in the soft snow alongside the beaten path, choosing the hardest going as if hardship itself were proof of worth. He attacked Sol-leks with his teeth, rushed against him, tried to thrust him off into the deep drift. He strove to leap inside the traces, to wedge himself between dog and sled, whining and yelping, crying with grief and pain.

The half-breed tried to drive him away with the whip. Dave paid no heed to the stinging lash, and the man had not the heart to strike harder. Dave refused the easy running behind. He continued to flounder beside the trail until he was exhausted. Then he fell and lay where he fell, howling lugubriously while the long train of sleds churned by, the runners whispering and grating, the bells tinkling with a cheerfulness that suddenly sounded shallow in the great white.

With the last remnant of his strength he staggered after them until the train made another stop. Then he floundered past the sleds to his own and stood alongside Sol-leks.

His driver lingered a moment to get a light for his pipe from the man behind. When he returned and started his dogs, they swung out on the trail with remarkable lack of exertion, then turned their heads uneasily and stopped in surprise.

The driver stopped too. The sled had not moved.

The men gathered, and there was the sight: Dave had bitten through both of Sol-leks’s traces. He stood directly in front of the sled, in his proper place, body shaking, eyes pleading with a desperate clarity. Let me stay. Let me do it. Let me be what I have been.

The driver was perplexed. The others talked of how a dog could break its heart when denied the work that killed it. They recalled instances—old dogs, injured dogs—who had died because they were cut out of the traces. Since Dave was to die anyway, they said, it was a mercy that he should die in harness, heart-easy and content.

So Dave was harnessed in again. Proudly he pulled as of old, though more than once he cried out involuntarily from the bite of his inward hurt. Several times he fell and was dragged in the traces. Once the sled ran upon him so that afterward he limped on a hind leg. Still he held out until camp was reached, and his driver made a place for him by the fire.

Morning found him too weak to travel. At harness-up he tried to crawl to his driver. By convulsive efforts he got on his feet, staggered, and fell. Then he wormed his way forward toward where the harnesses were being put on his mates. He advanced his fore legs, dragged his body up in a hitch, then advanced again—inch by inch, as if will alone could haul him into the traces.

His strength left him. The last his mates saw of him he lay gasping in the snow, yearning toward them. But they could hear him mournfully howling as they moved on, until the sound was swallowed behind a belt of river timber.

There the train was halted. The Scotch half-breed slowly retraced his steps to the camp they had left.

The men ceased talking. In the pause, the world’s small sounds grew large—the faint creak of leather, the soft impatient snort of a dog, the distant hush of snow falling as it had fallen for ages before mail or gold ever carved tracks into this country.

A revolver-shot rang out.

The man came back hurriedly. The whips snapped. The bells tinkled merrily again. The sleds churned along the trail.

But Buck knew—and every dog knew—what had taken place behind the belt of river trees.
