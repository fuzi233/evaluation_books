I reclined beneath the boughs of a tree, a solitary sentinel while the children drifted away in pairs and tiny clusters, ascending to their waiting nests. Their exhaustion was profound, their restoration immediate; they were content to surrender to slumber and wake anew without complaint. Though weariness weighed upon me, I remained alert. Lona had not offered me farewell, and I was certain she would return.

My gaze fixed upon her the instant she reappeared, and I was struck anew by her uncanny likeness to the Princess. Yet, in Lona, the terrifying radiance of Lilith was tempered by a soft, childlike grace, deepened by a nascent motherhood. "She must be occupied," I murmured to myself, "with the child of the woman I encountered fleeing!" That woman, Lona had confessed, struggled to fulfill the duties of maternity.

Soon, she sat beside me. After a silence broken only by the gentle caress of her hand upon my cheek and palm, she began to recount the world's shifting tides since my departure. As we spoke, the moon ascended, casting fleeting, trembling lights through the leaves onto her face—a visage etched with thoughtful care, yet redeemed and glorified by the boundless love that animated it. It baffled me how such a child could issue from such a mother, or how a woman of such stature could bear the weight of a princess's lineage. Happily, however, she possessed two parents—or perhaps three. She drew my heart inward, resonating with the parts of me that mirrored her own essence. I loved her as one destined to perfect nature, forever retaining the spirit of a child. My love for her solidified in that moment, and the hope of reunion became the sole anchor of my comfort. Her words, pure as truth, washed clean the dust of my soul.

She revealed that following my exit from the orchard valley, the Giants' faith in the tangible reality of their neighbors swelled, breeding a corresponding surge of hostility. At times, the Little Ones witnessed the titans trampling blindly, reacting to phantom signs of their presence while standing mere yards away, laughing at the fools' fury. Soon, however, their animosity shifted from posturing to predation. They began to devour the trees—the lifeblood of the Little Ones. This catastrophe forced the Mother of Them All to strategize. She dispatched her sharpest scouts to eavesdrop, learning that the Giants believed I hid nearby, plotting to ambush them in the dark once I regained strength. Concluding that the only shield against destruction was the illusion of abandonment, she devised a plan: the Little Ones must vanish into the forest—beyond the Giants' sight, yet close enough to harvest their own trees by moonlight.

"The forest offers little cover," she noted, her brow furrowed. "There is scant undergrowth to hide us."

Yet, reflection illuminated a path. Where birds dwelt, the Little Ones could find habitation. They possessed an eager sympathy for all forms of life, capable of learning from the wildest creatures. Why not flee the cold and the enemies into the canopy? Why not abandon the low brushwood for lofty foliage? Why not construct nests where scooping hollows served no purpose? All that birds achieved, the Little Ones could emulate, save for the art of flight.

She addressed the assembly, and they listened with rapt approval. They already scaled the trees and had long admired the avian architects. The forest trees, though vast, beckoned; their branches stretched wide, some even drooping downward, as if offering a hand. Perhaps atop the highest summit, they might locate the bird that laid eggs resembling theirs, sitting patiently until ripeness, then tumbling them free. Yes, they would erect "sleep-houses" in the trees, invisible to the Giants, who never raised their dull heads to look upward. Thus, the Giants would believe them banished, forgetting their forests, allowing the Little Ones to gather apples, pears, figs, mesples, and peaches undisturbed.

Thus reasoned the Lovers, embracing Lona's vision. Swiftly, they mastered the tree-tops, becoming as native as the birds themselves. The Giants, convinced they had driven the intruders from the land, eventually neglected their vigil, and the belief in the small neighbors faded.

Later, Lona turned her gaze to the children's transformation. "Have you not noticed?" she asked gently. "Many have grown."

"I have not," I admitted, "yet I can surely believe it."

"It is true," she affirmed. "The proof lies in the expansion of their minds. Since migrating upward, their intellects have matured significantly, mitigating the alarm the news caused."

As twilight waned and the moon shone silver above, the children descended to the valley to gather provisions for the morrow. The Giants, blind to the gray hour and loathing the moon—which they would have extinguished if they could—never ventured forth then. However, the Little Ones quickly discovered that fruit harvested under the pale eye of night tasted bitter by daylight. A new dilemma emerged: Was it wiser to maintain the pretense of exile, or to drive the bad Giants from the realm entirely?

Through exploration, the children had befriended the forest's denizens. They recognized the strength and wisdom of some, the swiftness and manageability of others. With playful, affectionate approaches, they secured alliances, addressing horse and elephant as "Brother" or "Sister," eventually bestowing individual names upon each. Bears followed, and even a serpent earned the title "Sister Serpent" after a rough play session resulted in a bite. Caterpillars, generally ignored, became "Sister Butterflies" upon metamorphosis, greeted with a word akin to repentance, treated as sacred.

Then, on a moonlit eve, they encountered a woman seated with an infant—the very woman I had met on my journey to Bulika. Mistaking her for a Giantess who had stolen one of their kin (for all babies belonged to them), the children attacked in a multitude, beating her in a chaotic, bewildering dance. She would have fled, but a boy clutched her feet. Recovering her senses, she recognized her former hosts. The baby was returned to Lona, who cradled it in her bosom.

While the woman observed that the children struck carefully, avoiding harm to the babe, the Little Ones noted that, upon yielding, she hugged and kissed her child exactly as they desired. Concluding she was a Giantess of the "good" kind, they brought her fruit and showered her with childish attentions.

Perplexed regarding her destination, the woman dared not return to the City, fearing the Princess would uncover who had lame her leopardess. Delighted by the Little People's kindness, she resolved to stay. She needed no trouble with her infant and hoped to find a way back to her wealthy, gem-rich, and usually kind husband.

Supplementing Lona's account with conjecture, I recall the tradition shared by the Bulikan inhabitants: the Princess lived in terror of an infant destined to destroy her. Unaware of the horrific measures she employed to preserve her youth and beauty, they interpreted her deteriorating physical state and increasing hostility toward children as omens of impending doom. This fostered a shadowy hope for change.

Now, the woman conceived a bold strategy. Could the prophecy lie with these very children? They were marvelously brave, whereas the Bulikans were cowards in abject fear of animals. If she could ignite their ambition to seize the City, she might slip away amidst the chaos, reach her house unrecognized, and await the outcome from hiding.

Though unfit for warfare by nature—quarrels rare, fights nonexistent, loving all life, hating both inflicting and suffering pain—they were malleable. She set the smallest to throw stones at targets. Soon, they were engrossed in the game, growing skilled.

Their first practical triumph was my rescue. Gathering fruit, they found me asleep. Returning home, they convened a council and returned the next day, accompanied by elephants and horses. They overwhelmed the few Giants guarding me, carrying me off in jubilation. The younger boys boasted childishly; the older, less ostentatious; the girls, though their eyes flashed, remained unusually taciturn. The woman of Bulika felt emboldened.

For much of the night, we discussed the children's growth and its implications. Familiar with Lona's power to discern truth, I grew astonished at her practical wisdom. Had I been a child myself, I might have marveled less.

Far from dawn, a faint fluttering disturbed the air. Many Little Ones descended from their nests, vanished, and silence returned swiftly.

"What are they doing?" I asked.

"They think," Lona replied, "that, despite their stupidity, the Giants will scour the woods. They have gone to gather stones to meet them. Stones are scarce here; they scatter widely to collect enough. They will pile them in their nests and rain them down as the Giants approach. Knowing their habits, they do not expect them until morning. If they arrive sooner, a war of expulsion begins—one people must yield. The outcome seems hardly doubtful. We do not intend to kill them; indeed, their skulls are so thick we could not. Nor would it matter much, for they are so little alive! If one dies, his Giantess forgets him within three days."

"Do the children throw so well that disaster *might* occur?" I queried.

"Wait until you witness them!" she answered, touched with pride. "And I have not yet told you of a strange occurrence from the night before last..."
