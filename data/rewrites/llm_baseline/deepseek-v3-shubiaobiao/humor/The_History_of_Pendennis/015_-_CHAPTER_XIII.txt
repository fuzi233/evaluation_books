Meanwhile, back at Fairoaks, the Pendennis household was in a state of mild bewilderment over the Major’s prolonged absence. Had he been swallowed by a particularly persuasive solicitor? Kidnapped by a rogue band of theater enthusiasts? The suspense was unbearable—or at least mildly inconvenient.

When Dr. Portman and his wife stopped by Helen’s lodge-gate, they delivered a note from the Major, which explained, in the driest possible terms, that he had been detained in Chatteris by *urgent legal matters* (a phrase which, as everyone knows, could mean anything from reviewing a will to losing a particularly vicious game of whist). Notably absent from this missive was any mention of his morning’s *entertainment*—a spirited verbal joust with one Captain Costigan, in which the Major had, by all accounts, been thoroughly outmaneuvered.

Young Pen, meanwhile, had developed a sudden aversion to Chatteris, now that his uncle was lurking about like a well-dressed specter. The thought of the Major perched on the Dean’s grass-plat, spyglass in hand, monitoring his nephew’s romantic endeavors, was enough to make any young man reconsider his life choices. And so, Pen avoided the town, preferring the solitude of a walk—though even this innocent pleasure was spoiled by the looming threat of encountering his uncle’s immaculately polished boots.

Ah, young love! That most ridiculous of human conditions. Pen’s affections were of the silent, pining variety—visible only in the violent ogling of his eyes, which, to the untrained observer, might have suggested a man in the throes of indigestion rather than passion. Let us spare the reader the agony of revisiting his love letters, those overwrought scribblings of devotion, which, in the cold light of day, would no doubt make even the most sentimental among us cringe. No, we shall not reproduce them here. Some things are best left tied up with Miss Fotheringay’s string and sealed away forever.

The Major, having retreated from his encounter with Captain Costigan in a state of concentrated fury, resembled nothing so much as a teakettle left too long on the boil. *“The impudent bog-trotting scamp!”* he fumed. *“Dare to threaten me! Dare to suggest his daughter could marry into the Pendennis family! And then have the audacity to send a challenge!”*

The idea of dueling with a tipsy tragedian was, of course, absurd. What would people say? *“Major Pendennis shot in a dispute over an actress! How very middle-class.”* Unthinkable.

When Dr. Portman inquired after the skirmish, the Major waved him off with vague mutterings about *“delicate matters”* and *“unfinished business.”* He then returned to his hotel, where he took out his frustrations on his long-suffering valet, Morgan, who later confided to a fellow servant that the Major had been *“dammin and cussin upstairs and downstairs like a admiral in a nor’wester.”*

Mr. Foker, ever the agent of chaos, chose this moment to make his entrance—resplendent in a brocade dressing-gown and an embroidered skull-cap that could have doubled as a theatrical prop.

*“O, you’re busy—call again another time,”* he announced cheerfully, already halfway out the door.

But the Major, despite his foul mood, found himself inexplicably cheered by the sight of Foker’s absurd attire. *“Come in, come in,”* he said, managing a strained smile.

Foker, delighted to be of service, proceeded to mediate the situation with all the tact of a bull in a china shop. He recounted the morning’s drama, including the unfortunate intervention of Mr. Garbetts (Costigan’s chosen second, who had promptly fled upon recognizing the Major’s lawyer). The Major, despite himself, burst out laughing.

*“I don’t think there’ll be any fight,”* Foker declared, mimicking the tragedian’s grand exit. *“But if there is, Mr. Tatham here can always send the bailiffs to separate you!”*

Never one to let social awkwardness stand in the way of a good meal, Foker extended an impromptu dinner invitation to both the Major *and* Miss Costigan—along with her ever-present shadow, Mr. Bows.

*“Lobster salad and champagne,”* Foker promised, knowing full well that no actress could resist such an offer.

And so, in a twist that would have made a farce writer proud, Major Pendennis found himself breaking bread with the very woman whose father had, mere hours earlier, threatened to shoot him. The General himself, retrieved from the Magpie Hotel (where he had been weeping into his ale over a sentimental ballad), arrived late to the feast, promptly embracing the Major as his *“dearest friend.”*

The next morning, Pen received a letter—one he had been dreading. It was from Costigan, delivering the fatal news: the engagement was off. Miss Fotheringay, it seemed, had been *made aware* of Pen’s financial shortcomings.

Pen’s reaction was, predictably, dramatic. *“It’s a lie! A trick!”* he roared, crumpling the letter.

But the Major, ever the pragmatist, merely sighed. *“She spells ‘affection’ with one ‘f,’ Arthur. Surely you can do better.”*

Helen, ever the doting mother, attempted to console him. *“My dear boy, think of your future—”*

*“I’ll see her myself!”* Pen declared, bolting for the door.

Alas, his efforts were in vain. Miss Fotheringay, now under strict paternal supervision, refused to see him. The General cut him in the street. Pen, in true romantic fashion, took to lurking outside the theater like a particularly lovesick ghost.

And so, the affair ended—not with a duel, not with a grand reconciliation, but with a whimper, a fever, and the solemn realization that young men have been surviving heartbreak for centuries.

Pen would live. But he would *not* be cheerful about it.
