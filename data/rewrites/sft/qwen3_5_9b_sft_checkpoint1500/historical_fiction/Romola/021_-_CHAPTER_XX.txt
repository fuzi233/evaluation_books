The final week of Carnival had swollen Florence to a fever pitch. The streets, usually thronged, now roared with a chaotic energy born of masks and madness. Processions wound through the lanes, chanting songs mandated by Lorenzo the Magnificent, while the *rigoletto*, a favored round dance, kicked up dust beneath a pale, frost-laden sky. Practical jokes ranged from harmless confections to the lethal art of stone-throwing. The boys and striplings, the most volatile element of the crowd, had become as loud and uncontrollable as cicadas in summer heat. Their privilege was to block passers-by with poles, demanding tribute for suppers and bonfires, culminating in a standing spectacle of stoning. The injuries varied, sometimes fatal, lending a checkered, barbaric quality to the festivities. A painter seeking truth here would produce a canvas of such grossness and brutality that it would require a wall to shield it from view, save for the historian's purpose of documenting a reforming zeal often misjudged for its narrowness. Yet, amidst this, lingered a more innocent, picturesque merriment, fueled by the quick animal spirits and sensitive nerves of the people, devoid of the heavy stupidity of the north or the stealthy ferocity that turns southern brawls into daggers.

High morning had broken, yet the carnival spirit lingered in a languid recapitulation of the night's jests. Titus Melema moved with a brisk, determined stride along the road to the Via de' Bardi, accompanied by Young Bernardo Dovizi. They spoke animatedly of topics utterly unrelated to the sights and sounds pressing past them along the Porta Santa Maria. Even as they gestured and smiled, they cast frequent, rapid glances around them. At the bend toward the Lung'Arno, leading to the Ponte Rubaconte, Titus sensed a presence he desperately wished to avoid. His mind was preoccupied with a singular, life-altering event: his betrothal, set for that evening. The decision had been sudden. Though preparations—chiefly Titus pouring his florins into the refurbishment of Bardo's sparsely furnished dwelling—had been underway, the union was meant to await Easter, following Titus's year of probation under Bernardo del Nero. However, an urgent proposition arrived: Titus was to join Cardinal Giovanni in Rome to assist Bernardo Dovizi in organizing a library using his superior knowledge of Greek. With advancement clearly laid out before him, Titus had become insistent. He argued that the betrothal must precede his departure; the delay before marriage would be minimal upon his return, and crucially, if he and Romola were outwardly and inwardly pledged, with rings exchanged and contracts signed, no power—not even Messer Bernardo's—could nullify his claim. Romola's feelings aligned with his, and the elders' consent was secured.

Now, Titus hastened to snatch a morning visit from Romola, needing final words before the evening ceremony. It was a time when recognition was ill-suited to his haste, let alone by Tessa. Unmistakably, it was Tessa he spotted moving toward the river bend, her expression timid and forlorn. An uncomfortable current ran through Titus; he knew she had seen him and would surely follow. There was no escape along the direct road by the Arno and over the bridge. Yet, she dared not approach him while he was not alone, and Titus resolved to keep Dovizi beside him until they reached Bardo's door. He quickened his pace, weaving new threads of conversation, but the sensation of Tessa trailing behind grew stronger, a persistent irritation tempered by a pang of pity. This compassion made his desire to vanish unseen without causing a scene a difficult resource. Still, he persevered, guiding his companion to the door and managing his farewell without turning his face toward where a pair of blue eyes might watch. Ascending the stone steps, he tried to banish the thought that Tessa might indeed be watching, telling himself she may not have seen him, or if she had, would not follow.

Yet, perhaps because certainty was elusive, when Titus emerged from the doorway, he moved with a quick step, feigning unconsciousness of anything on either flank. Human eyes, however, capture a wide angle without permission. Titus knew, before a hand fell heavily on his arm, that a small figure in a white hood stood near the threshold. It was a real grasp, not a timid touch; poor Tessa, seeing his swift exit, had lunged forward with desperation. When he stopped and turned, her face wore a mask of fright, as if she dreaded the consequences of her boldness.

"Tessa!" Titus exclaimed, his voice sharper than she had ever heard it. "Why are you here? You must not follow me—you must not haunt doorways waiting for me."

Her blue eyes widened with tears, speechless. Titus feared worse than ridicule; if he were seen in the Via de' Bardi with a country girl gazing pathetically at him, it would be disastrous. Bernardo del Nero—or someone equally dangerous—might appear at any moment. Even outside the day of his betrothal, the scene would be awkward. Yet, to drive her away with harsh words would be brutal, impossible. He recalled his foolish entanglement with the Cerretani, a quiet affair buried for months that now bloomed as an untimely weed of vexation. He could not speak harshly, so he spoke quickly.

"Tessa, I cannot, I must not talk to you here. I will go to the bridge and wait for you. Follow me slowly."

He walked swiftly to the Ponte Rubaconte, leaning against the wall of a quaint little house, staring toward the approach where Tessa would arrive. To soften a harder heart than Titus's, the sight of her would have been enough; her round face was paler and more saddened than when he had parted from her at the Nunziata. Fortunately, the bridge was rarely frequented, and it was nearly empty. He wasted no time.

"Now, Tessa, I have little time. Do not cry. Why did you follow me? Never do it again."

"I thought," Tessa whispered, struggling against a sob, "that you would not be long before you came to take care of me again. The stepfather beats me, and I cannot bear it. Always when I come for a holiday I walk to find you, and I cannot. Oh, please, do not send me away again! It has been so long, and I cry because you never come. I cannot help it; the days are so long, and I do not mind the goats or kids..."

Sobs rose rapidly, great tears falling. Titus felt compelled to comfort her. Sending her away was necessary, immediate. But how could he tell her truths that would leave her in hopeless grief? He saw new troubles looming, yet the present moment was too pressing to weigh distant consequences.

"Tessa, my little one," he coaxed in his familiar, tender tone, "you must not cry. Endure the stepfather a little longer. I will return to you. But I am going to Rome now—a long, long journey. I shall return in a few weeks, and then I promise to see you. Promise me to be good and wait."

It was the well-known voice; the sound alone soothed Tessa. She looked up with trusting, tear-glittering eyes, sobbing despite her efforts to obey.

"Promise me, my Tessa."

"Yes," she whispered. "But you won't be long?"

"No, not long. But I must go now. Remember, Tessa: if anyone knows you see me, you will lose me forever. Go straight home now, never follow me again. Wait for me. Good-bye, little Tessa: I *will* come."

There was no alternative. He turned to leave, refusing to look back to gauge her bearing, for he had no time. Once across the Ponte Rubaconte and into the Via de' Benci, where he could not see the bridge, he knew Tessa would obey. The difficulty for that day was ended, yet returning to him at such a moment, when deception was inevitable, left an unpleasant stain on his memory. However, Titus's mind was saturated with a hopeful, first love, buoyed by ambitions shining bright. The future necessity of grieving Tessa was akin to the distant cry of a suffering animal in a thicket, unnoticed by a merry cavalcade in a sunny plain. On his second crossing of the bridge, the thought of Tessa diminished his happiness not at all. Well-muffled in his mantle, perhaps more for privacy than warmth, he ascended the stone steps of Bardo's house, leaping two at a time.

"Where is the damigella?" he asked hurriedly of Maso.

"In the library," Maso replied. "She is ready, and Monna Brigida and Messer Bernardo are there with Ser Braccio, but the rest of the company has not arrived."

Titus requested a few moments alone and awaited Romola in the *salotto*. He entered a room starkly contrasting the library's pallid, somber hues. The walls were brightly frescoed with "caprices" of nymphs and loves sporting among flowers and birds under a blue sky. Beside red leather seats and a central table stood two tall white vases and a young faun playing the flute, modeled by a promising youth named Michelangelo Buonarotti. The room felt like an open, sunlit space.
