Six days later, the household at Tergou gathered around their simple meal when Luke Peterson crashed through the door, his boots caked in the dust of the road. "God save you!" he gasped, breathless. "Mistress Catherine is needed at once in Rotterdam."

"My name is Catherine, sir," the young girl corrected gently, though her heart hammered. "Please, call me Kate."

"Ay, Dame," Luke nodded solemnly, wiping his brow. "She told me, 'Luke, hasten to Tergou. Find Eli the hosier and beg his wife, Catherine, to come to me, for God's love.' I rode without waiting for dawn."

"Holy Saints!" Cornelis and Sybrandt exchanged a quick, furtive glance, relief washing over them. "She has returned! What could possibly bring her back?"

Kate hesitated, then ventured, "Perhaps Master Luke can explain."

"I can," Luke said gravely. "Her baby is dying. She is frantic with worry."

Catherine sprang from her seat. "What is wrong with him?"

"Nay, I know not the ailment," Luke admitted, shaking his head. "But it has been wasting away steadily for some time."

Cornelis and Sybrandt smiled again, a silent understanding passing between them. Catherine, however, saw only fear in her husband's eyes. "Eli," she cried, her voice trembling with fury, "if you speak a single word against it, we shall quarrel, after all these years!"

"Who dares question thee, foolish woman?" Eli stood, clapping his hands together. "Quarrel with thy own shadow while I fetch Peter's mule for thee."

"Bless thee, my good man!" she exclaimed, tears welling. "Didst thou ever fail me? Eat thy fill, whoever may, while I prepare."

She took Luke back to the cart, questioning him relentlessly on the journey, turning his thoughts inside out until he confessed everything he knew.

At the door, Margaret awaited them, pale and shaken. She flung her arms around Catherine's neck, her face a mask of terror.

"He lives!" Catherine declared, scanning the child's fragile form. "Thank God."

She gazed first at the failing infant, then at the hollow-eyed mother. "Fortunate you summoned me," she said softly. "The child is poisoned."

"Poisoned!" Margaret gasped. "By whom?"

"By thee. Thy fretting."

"Nay, Mother!" Margaret wept. "How can I avoid sorrow?"

"Do not deceive me. A nursing mother must turn her mind from grief to the comfort in her lap. Dost thou not know that a child wastes if the mother vexes herself? This misery stems from thy reading and writing. Those are man's arts; they keep vital wisdom from a woman. The child must be weaned."

"Oh, cruel woman!" Margaret cried, clutching Catherine's arm. "I am sorry I called. Wilt thou rob me of the last comfort in a world where I am wretched?"

"That thou dost not," Catherine retorted sternly, "or he would not lie thus."

"Mother!" Margaret implored.

"'Tis hard," Catherine relented, her tone softening. "But consider: would it not be far harder to gaze down upon his sweet face looking up at thee from a little coffin?"

"Oh, Jesus!"

"And how couldst thou bear thy other sorrows with a heavy heart and an empty lap?"

"Oh, Mother, I consent to anything. Save my boy."

"A virtuous lass," Catherine assured her. "Trust me. I see clearly what thou dost not."

Yet another consent was required—the babe's—and he proved more stubborn than his mother.

"There!" Margaret cooed, feigning regret at his disobedience. "He loves me too dearly."

But Catherine was a master of persuasion. En route, she had spotted a robust young woman seated outside her own door, tending an infant nearby. Catherine approached her, explaining the desperate situation. Would she nurse the starving child briefly while preparations were made for weaning?

The stranger agreed with a warm smile, placing her own child in its cradle and entering Margaret's home. With a polite curtsy, Catherine handed the frail infant to her. The stranger examined the child, sighed with pity, and immediately began to nurse it as if it were her own.

Margaret, stunned by the stranger's composure, stared blankly before bursting into tears.

"What troubles thee?" the nurse asked kindly. "Thou told me the mother was reluctant."

"She is not," Catherine interjected sharply. "She is merely a fool. Ignore her, Margaret. And thou, Margaret, I am ashamed of thee."

"You are a hard-hearted, cruel woman!" Margaret sobbed.

"Those who undertake to guide the weak must possess a certain hardness," Catherine explained bluntly. "And forgive me, but thou art not my flesh and blood, whereas this boy is."

Allowing the words to settle, Catherine softened. "Hark, now. She sacrifices her own to save thine. Yet thou canst think of naught but to burst into tears before her face. Away with thee, for shame!"

"Nay, wife," the nurse replied gently. "Thanks be to God, I have enough for myself and hers besides. And pray, do not blame her! Perhaps life's troubles have soured her milk."

"And her heart, too," Catherine observed mercilessly.

Margaret fixed her gaze upon Catherine, then lowered her eyes.

"I know I ought to be grateful," Margaret whispered to the nurse, then turned away, leaning over a chair to hide from the unbearable sight of another woman nursing her Gerard, who showed no distinction between this stranger and his exiled mother.

The nurse responded, "Thou art welcome, my poor woman. And so art thou, Mistress Catherine, a townsfolk of mine, though I know not thy name."

"What, art thou of Tergou?" Catherine asked. "All the better. But I cannot recall thy visage."

"Oh, thou knowest me not. My husband and I are humble folk compared to thee. Yet true Eli and his wife are known and respected throughout the town. So, I am at thy call, dame; at thy call, wife; and at thy call, my pretty poppet—night or day."

"There is a woman of the right old sort," Catherine murmured as the door closed behind her.

"I HATE HER!" Margaret screamed, the words tearing from her throat with fierce fervor. "I HATE HER! I HATE HER!"

Catherine merely chuckled at the outburst.

"That is proper," she said. "Better to speak it openly and hold it within than to brood silently. It is quite natural, truly. Here, take thy bundle of comfort. Thou mayest hate it, if thou canst," and she placed the child in Margaret's lap.

"No, no!" Margaret protested, turning her head half-way from the baby; she could not bring herself to look fully at him. "He is not mine anymore; he is hers. I know not why she left him here, for my part. It was kind of her not to take him to her home, with cradle and all... Oh! Oh! Oh!"

"Well, one solace remains," Catherine said soothingly. "He liveth. This gives me hope: some other hath taken him from me. Like father, like son..."

Catherine felt a pang of sorrow for Margaret and allowed her to weep undisturbed. From then on, whenever Catherine needed assistance, she would take Gerard outdoors for fresh air. Margaret never objected, expressing no disbelief, yet returning indoors always in tears.

This tacit agreement lasted only briefly. Margaret grew equally eager to wean little Gerard. The process was completed, and the child regained his health and vigor. Soon, however, a new affliction struck him during teething. Catherine's experience proved invaluable here; amidst her grief regarding Eli, Margaret found fleeting moments of joy watching the tiny teeth emerge. "Teeth, Mother?" she would exclaim, calling them "pearls of pearls." Each sparkling gem on his red gums seemed to Margaret the greatest miracle nature could achieve.

Her companion shared the delusion. If we had told them that standing corn was equally magnificent, Margaret might have transformed into a reproachful gazelle, and Catherine would have driven us from the room; so, each new tooth was heralded with a shriek of triumph by whichever of them discovered it first.

Catherine chatted with Joan and learned she was the wife of Jorian Ketel of Tergou, formerly a servant to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. Having fallen out of favor, Jorian had returned to Rotterdam, securing the post of sexton to the parish alongside carpentry work.

In return, Catherine confided in Joan about the child she had nursed, detailing Margaret and Gerard, and the profound anxiety caused by Gerard's silence. "Ay," Joan remarked, "the world is filled with trouble." On one occasion, she suggested to Catherine, "I believe my husband knows more about thy Gerard than anyone here. Yet he grows increasingly secretive lately. Visit him sometime before sunset, and coax him to talk. For our Lady's sake, do not tell me thou wert sent. The only concealment he ever granted me was for whispering his business, and I seek no further."
