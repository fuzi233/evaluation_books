"O, welcome, pure-eyed Faith, white-handed Hope,
Thou hovering angel, girt with golden wings!"

Deronda hesitated before answering Gwendolen's summons. It was not pride that held him back, but a profound, aching sympathy. He feared the weight of another's heart, the terrifying possibility that her needs might exceed what he could ever give. He knew, with a clarity that left no room for debate, that her soul clung to his with a desperate, consuming hunger. One does not argue against the anger or the scorn that vibrates in a voice; one simply feels it, undeniable and absolute. Deronda felt her entire future hanging from a thread, balanced over a cliff of despair. If this tragedy had struck him a year prior, he would have acted without hesitation, driven by an impulsive need to shield her from sorrow, to build a fortress against loneliness, and to complete the rescue he had begun with the necklace. But now, the chains of love and duty bound him differently. That old, saving impulse still lived within him as a painful yearning, a trembling dread at the thought of facing her eyes and words again and again. Yet, it was precisely the strength of his resolve to stay away that made his pity for her fate sharper, more agonizing.

He waited in the back drawing-room, that space of white and crimson where they had once sat together at the musical party. It was here Gwendolen had first declared her lot dependent on his fidelity, her plea melting into the melodic cry, *Per pietà non dirmi addio*. But the melody had belonged to Mirah's voice.

Deronda paced the room, which he had known for years, feeling a strange metamorphosis in his own life. The familiar objects—from Lady Mallinger's gentle portrait to the urbane lions on the chimney-piece—seemed to belong to a past existence, visited only in memory. The house, once a home, now felt alien, transformed by the deep impressions of recent days. He stood there, hat in hand, awaiting the entrance of a young woman whose life was undergoing a tragic shift toward an uncertain result, one in which he felt his actions were still dangerously entangled.

Gwendolen entered, changed. Her mourning dress was obvious, but her expression carried a quiet satisfaction he had never seen before, not even in Genoa. Her contentment stemmed from his presence, yet there was no smile when they met and clasped hands. Both were heavy with remembrance and anxious foreboding.

"It was good of you to come," she said immediately, sitting in the nearest chair. "Let us sit down." He placed himself opposite her.

"I asked you to come because I need to know what I ought to do," she began without preamble. "Do not be afraid to tell me what you think is right, even if it is hard. I have decided to do it. Once, I feared poverty; I could not bear being under anyone else's control, which is why I married. I have endured worse since. I think I could endure poverty if you believe I should. Do you know about my husband's will?"

"Yes, Sir Hugo told me," Deronda replied, anticipating her question.

"Should I take anything he left me? I have been thinking," she said, her voice gaining a nervous urgency. "Perhaps you don't realize how much I truly thought of my mother when I married. I was selfish, yes, but I loved her, and I felt deeply about her poverty. What comforted me most when I was miserable was knowing she was better off because of my marriage. Now, the hardest thing for me would be to see her in poverty again. I have been thinking that if I took enough to provide for her, and nothing for myself, it would not be wrong. I was precious to her—and he took me from her—and he meant to... and if she had known—"

Gwendolen broke off. She had prepared for this meeting by focusing solely on this question of right toward her mother, but the thought carried with it reasons she could not speak aloud. Perilous memories swarmed between her words, making her speech increasingly agitated and tremulous. She looked down helplessly at her hands, now bare of all rings save her wedding band.

"Do not hurt yourself by speaking of that," Deronda said tenderly. "There is no need; the case is simple. I think I can hardly judge wrongly. You consult me because I am the only one to whom you have confided the most painful parts of your experience, and I understand your scruples." He paused, waiting for her to steady herself. The silence felt heavy with the tenderness in his voice, giving her the courage to lift her eyes. "You are conscious of something you feel is a crime against one who is dead. You believe you have forfeited all claim as a wife. You shrink from taking what was his. You want to keep yourself from profiting by his death. Your feelings even urge you to punish yourself—to scourge the self that disobeyed your better will. I have known something of that myself. Do I understand you?"

"Yes—at least, I want to be good, not like what I have been," Gwendolen said. "I will try to bear what you think I ought to bear. I have tried to tell you the worst about myself. What ought I to do?"

"If no one else were involved," Deronda said, "I would hardly dare urge you against your remorseful prompting. But I am guided now by your feeling toward Mrs. Davilow, which seems entirely just. I cannot think your husband's obligations to you are nullified by your actions. He voluntarily entered your life and shaped its course in the most significant way. However, in his position, he was duty-bound to provide for your mother, and he surely understood that if this will took effect, she would share the provision made for you."

"She has had eight hundred a year," Gwendolen said. "What I wanted to do was take that and leave the rest." She had argued inwardly for so long that her mind could not instantly shift to another perspective.

"I think it is not your duty to set such a limit," Deronda replied. "You would create a painful enigma for Mrs. Davilow; an income from which you exclude yourself would be embittered to her. Your own path would become too difficult. We agreed in Genoa that the burden on your conscience is one no one should know about. The future goodness of your life will be best served by saving others from the pain of that knowledge. In my opinion, you should simply abide by your husband's will, letting your remorse influence only how you use your newfound independence."

As he spoke the last sentence, Deronda instinctively picked up his hat from the floor beside him. Gwendolen, sensitive to his slightest movement, felt her heart leap as if it possessed its own consciousness, fearing he would leave. In that same moment, she rose from her chair, unable to consider that his motion signaled his departure. Deronda, of course, also rose, stepping forward slightly.

"I will do what you tell me," Gwendolen said hurriedly. "But what else shall I do?" No other words were possible for her; even these simple ones were too much for her emotional state, where her proud secrecy had crumbled. As the childlike sentences fell from her lips, they reflected her own helplessness back at her, and she could not stop the sob that brought large tears to her eyes. Deronda felt a crushing pain, yet visible consequences urged him to exert every ounce of his conscience. When she pressed her tears away, he asked gently, "You will probably be going with Mrs. Davilow into the country soon."

"Yes, in a week or ten days." Gwendolen waited an instant, turning her eyes vaguely toward the window as if gazing at some imagined prospect. "I want to be kind to them all—they can be happier than I can. Is that the best I can do?"

"I think so. It is a duty that cannot be doubted," Deronda said. He paused between sentences, feeling a heavy anxiety weigh down his words. "Other duties will spring from it. Viewing your life as a debt may seem the dreariest perspective from afar, but it is not really so. What makes life dreary is the lack of motive. Once you begin acting with this penitential, loving purpose you hold in your mind, there will be unexpected satisfactions—newly opening needs that will carry you from day to day. You will find your life growing like a plant."

Gwendolen turned her eyes to him with the look of one thirsting for unseen waters. Deronda felt the gaze as if she were stretching her arms toward him from a forsaken shore. His voice took on an affectionate imploringness when he said, "This sorrow, which has cut down to the root, has come to you while you are so young—try to think of it not as a spoiling of your life, but as a preparation for it. Let it be a preparation..." Anyone overhearing his tones would have thought he was entreating for his own happiness. "See! You have been saved from the worst evils that might have arisen from your marriage, which you feel was wrong. You have had a vision of injurious, selfish action—a vision of possible degradation. Think of a severe angel, seeing you along the road of error, grasping you by the wrist and showing you the horror of the life you must avoid. And it has come to you in your spring-time. Think of it as a preparation. You can, you will, be among the best of women, such as make others glad that they were born."

The words were like the touch of a miraculous hand to Gwendolen. Mingled emotions streamed through her frame with a strength that seemed the beginning of a new existence, stirring something vague yet powerful within her. So pregnant is the divine hope of moral recovery with the energy that fulfills it. So potent is the infused action of another soul, before which we bow in complete love. But this new existence seemed inseparable from Deronda; the hope made his presence permanent. It was not her thought that he loved her and would cling to her—a thought that would have wavered on improbability; it was her spiritual breath. For the first time since that terrible moment on the sea, a flush rose and spread over her cheek, brow, and neck, deepening for an instant before gradually fading. She did not speak.

Deronda stepped forward and extended his hand, saying, "I must not weary you."

Startled by the sense of his departure, she put her hand in his, still silent.

"You look ill yet—unlike yourself," he added while holding her hand.

"I can't sleep much," she answered, returning somewhat to her dispirited manner. "Things repeat themselves in me so. They come back—they will all come back," she ended, shuddering, a chill fear threatening her.

"By degrees they will be less insistent," Deronda said. He could not drop her hand or move away abruptly.

"Sir Hugo says he shall come to stay at Diplow," Gwendolen said, snatching at previously intended words that had slipped away. "You will come too."

"Probably," Deronda said, then feeling the word was cold, he corrected himself, "Yes, I shall come," before releasing her hand with the final, friendly pressure of one who has virtually said goodbye.

"And not again here, before I leave town?" Gwendolen asked, with timid sadness, looking as pale as ever.

What could Deronda say? "If I can be of any use—if you wish me—certainly I will."

"I must wish it," Gwendolen said impetuously. "You know I must wish it. What strength have I? Who else is there?" Another sob rose in her throat.

Deronda felt a pang that showed itself in his face. He looked miserable as he said, "I will certainly come."

Gwendolen perceived the change in his face, but the intense relief of expecting him to return could not yield to any other feeling. There was a recovery of inspired hope and courage in her.

"Don't be unhappy about me," she said in a tone of affectionate assurance. "I shall remember your words—every one of them. I shall remember what you believe about me; I shall try."

She looked at him firmly and put out her hand again, as if forgetting what had passed since those words she promised to remember. Yet there was no approach to a smile on her lips. She had never smiled since her husband's death. When she stood still in silence, she resembled a melancholy statue of the Gwendolen whose laughter had once been so ready when others were grave.

Only by remembering the searching anguish that changed the aspect of the world for her can we understand her behavior toward Deronda—the unreflecting openness, indeed the importunate pleading, with which she expressed her dependence on him. Considerations that would fill the minds of indifferent spectators could not occur to her, any more than if flames had been mounting around her and she had flung herself into his open arms, clinging to his neck to be carried to safety. She identified him with the struggling regenerative process in her which had begun with his action. Was it any wonder she saw her own necessity reflected in his feeling? She was in that state of unconscious reliance and expectation common to us when preoccupied with our own troubles. We diffuse our feelings over others and count on them acting from our motives. Her imagination had not been turned to a future union with Deronda by any imagery other than the spiritual tie that had continually strengthened; nor had it been turned toward a future separation. Love-making and marriage—how could they now be the imagery in which poor Gwendolen's deepest attachment could spontaneously clothe itself? Mighty Love had laid his hand upon her, but what had he demanded? Acceptance of rebuke—the hard task of self-change—confession—endurance. If she cried toward him, what then? She cried as a child whose little feet have fallen backward, crying to be taken by the hand lest she lose herself.

The cry pierced Deronda. What position could be more difficult for a man full of tenderness yet possessing clear foresight? He was the only creature who knew the true nature of Gwendolen's trouble; to withdraw himself from her appeal would be to consign her to dangerous loneliness.
