Tito’s journey to Rome—so neatly timed that it loosened, without a show of violence, the knots that held Romola in Florence—was decided as such things often are, not with a trumpet-blast of statecraft, but over a supper-table the evening before.

He went towards that supper in the temper of a man who expects his pleasures to come garnished with advantage. There would be meats chosen with care, wines that had slept long in cool darkness, and company whose names, spoken aloud, made a sort of music in the mouth. The place itself promised a graceful kind of ease: the Selva, the Orto de’ Rucellai—the Rucellai gardens—where laurels and ilex made corridors of deep green, and torches turned their polished leaves to hammered metal.

Bernardo Rucellai, the master of that ground, was Florentine to the marrow: wealthy, learned, practised in public life, and careful that even his pleasures should wear the look of antiquity. His very name carried an old commerce in it, as if one could smell the sea on the rocks where the little lichen clung—_orcella_, _roccella_—drinking in light through its pale stems until, by a secret handling, it gave it back in a purple-red stain. A Levant trader had brought that dye, _oricello_, to Florence long before Bernardo’s time; and from that gained colour—so grateful to the human eye—the family had taken its softly altered Tuscan name. Bernardo stood out upon that purple background with added splendours: alliances with the Medici, embassies, the gonfalonier’s dignity, a palace, a church façade faced in black and white marble, rare trees planted and tended, antiquities gathered as other men gather spices, and a Latin so pure it might have been strained like clear broth through linen. His garden had become a kind of second academy since Lorenzo’s death: a place where grave men met under clipped greenery, tasting old words and old histories as if they were preserved fruits.

The invitation had reached Tito through Lorenzo Tornabuoni with a weight that made it feel less like an entertainment than a summons. Florence was hungry with public questions; and Tito, who had learned to detect the flavour of politics even beneath sweet sauces, felt sure that party purposes would be advanced by the same hands that served stewed fish and poured old Greek wine. Bernardo was not only influential; he was one of the chosen Twenty who for three weeks had held the city’s reins. That knowledge warmed Tito’s spirits as much as any spiced draught could have done. Without it, he might have found room for uneasy conjecture as to whether the distinguished were to be also dull; for he had supped in the Rucellai gardens before, and remembered evenings where a man was expected not merely to assent to an entire scheme of the universe—which would have cost Tito no effort—but to swallow its exposition from first origin to last ripeness, course after course, without so much as a crust of relief.

The night was dark; only at intervals, when Tito crossed the small circle of light cast by a lamp before an image of the Virgin, did his figure take shape enough for recognition: tall, graceful, his mantle folded with that ease which made his very movements seem composed. And at each such pool of light, a watcher might have seen another figure keeping pace behind him: thickset, elderly, in a serge tunic and a felt hat—moving with a dogged persistence that was not the common drift of passers-by.

When Tito halted at the gate of the Rucellai gardens, the follower halted too.

The _sportello_, the smaller door within the great gate, was already held open by a servant distracted by some question and slow to close and chain it again after the last arrival. Tito slipped in quickly, gave his name, and passed along the path between evergreen bushes that shone as if they had been oiled and laid over metal.

The other man entered as well.

“Your name?” the servant demanded, turning at last.

“Baldassarre Calvo,” came the answer without a stumble.

“You are not a guest. The guests have all passed.”

“I belong to Tito Melema, who has just gone in. I am to wait in the gardens.”

The servant hesitated. “I had orders to admit only guests. Are you a servant of Messer Tito?”

“No, friend, I am not a servant; I am a scholar.”

There are men who, if you say “I am a buffalo” with quiet conviction, will step aside and let the creature go by. The porter gave way. Baldassarre entered, and heard the door shut and chained behind him—an iron sound that seemed to set a seal on what he had come to do—as he vanished among the glossy, torchlit leaves.

Those short, ready answers marked a great change in Baldassarre since the last time he had stood face to face with Tito, when rage had driven him to the dagger and the dagger had snapped in two like dry twig. The change had come with suddenness, as if some inward knot had been cut.

When Tito’s shadow had passed before the hovel as he went homeward, Baldassarre had been sitting in that exhausted tremor which follows a great outburst—the body helpless as after fever, yet the mind sometimes curiously clear, as if the passion had burned away a smoke that usually lay between a man and his memories. He had felt unable to rise. His limbs were numb; a chill was on him; his hands shook. But around him there was no longer the habitual dimness and slipping phantoms. Images stood firm. His past lay open and continuous, a long road that had seemed to lead only to a taste of bitterness at the end.

For a while he was too absorbed even to note the miracle: he simply lived those years again. Then the clearness travelled, as light runs along a wall, and came to rest on what had just happened. He knew where he was. He remembered Monna Lisa, and Tessa—and with them, the meaning that had been hidden from him like a word heard but never understood.

So—_he_ was the husband. The husband whose wife had another wife in the Via de’ Bardi.

Something in him urged flight: to gather up the broken dagger, to go, to leave no trace behind; for in that poor remnant of self-respect it seemed that hiding his feebleness was the nearest thing to power. He leaned and took up the fragments. Then his eye went to the book lying open at his side.

It was a fine, large manuscript—Pausanias, oddly bound, a volume that in other years he had known as one knows the feel of a well-used knife in the hand. Moonlight lay on the page. He could make out the big letters at the head: ΜΕΣΣΕΝΙΚΑ. ΚΒ′.

An hour or two earlier, those characters had been nothing to him but black marks—like weather-stains on a wall. Now they were signs again: not dead ink, but the old magic that summons whole worlds.

With that moonbeam on the page, Messenia rose before him: its struggle against Spartan oppression, the long bitterness of the conquered. He snatched up the book. The light was too thin to read by; but he did not need to read. He knew the chapter. He read inwardly.

He saw Aristocrates the traitor, stoned by the whole people—stone after stone cast by hands that had suffered—cast out unburied, and a pillar set up with verses telling how Time brings justice home to the unjust. The words rose in him and set off innumerable vibrations, as when a single struck string wakes others hidden in the instrument. He forgot his age. He could have shouted aloud. The light had come back—mother of knowledge and joy!

In that exultation his limbs found strength. With broken dagger and book, he went out under the broad moonlight. The air was sharp and frosty; he felt no cold, only a glow, as if inward fire had taken the place of warmth. He walked the open spaces of that high ground, pausing where the city lay spread beneath him: domes and towers sleeping darkly under their sleeping guardians, the mountains; the river’s pale gleam; the valley trailing away toward peaks of snow. What had been a weary maze was now something he could command. He felt, in that rare moment of clearness, the strange mental empire common to us all at times, but to him doubled by contrast with long days when memory had been only the ache of something lost.

His mind flashed through Florence as through a familiar house: streets, houses, men’s names, their alliances and grudges, the secret ways by which influence moved. He was once more a man who knew cities, who had travelled, who had been trained by wide experience to see with an instructed vision. And the keen delight of it was the delight of language itself—the sensation of having names again, as if he had recovered taste and smell after illness. Names were not mere tokens; they were full of places, of voices, of remembered accents—Greek and Tuscan, each with its own grain and savour that could not be exchanged without loss. He ran through that wealth like an heir let suddenly into his inheritance.

Yet even while the mind exulted, one End presided within him—a dark deity shut in the innermost chamber. It only seemed forgotten while its sacrifice was being prepared.

When the first triumph had spent itself, his thoughts gathered and fastened on Tito.

That fair, slippery viper should not escape him now. Thanks to this struggling justice, the heart that had never quivered with tenderness had its selfish fibres—sensitive only for itself—that could be reached by pain’s sharp point. The soul that bowed to no right bowed to the great lord of mortals, Pain. Baldassarre felt he could search out every secret of Tito’s life now. He knew some already, and the broken dagger—what looked like frustration—had been the beginning of achievement.

Perhaps that sudden rage had shaken loose the obstruction that had stifled his soul. Twice before his memory had returned in patches when danger had whipped him into action: once when he had defended himself against an enraged dog; once when waves had overtaken him and he had scrambled up a rock to save himself. But what if, as then, the light were to die again, and the dreary blank return? Even as he feared it, the fear seemed to begin a weakening. He thought, with a kind of alarm, that his excited vigil on the hill was burning up his strength, and that he might sink the faster for it. He searched anxiously for shelter, for some corner where he could lie down out of the wind. At last he nestled against a heap of warm garden straw—straw that held, as straw does, a faint sour-sweet breath of last summer’s stalks and the animals that had fed on them—and so fell asleep.

When he opened his eyes it was daylight.

For the first moments he was bewildered, as if he were two men and did not know which one had awakened: the dim creature of sensibility without knowledge, living among shadows like the sad heir of fallen greatness—or the man of recovered power. Surely the last; for the night returned to him at once: the recognition of Pausanias, the crowding resurgence of facts and names, the wide prospect on the hill that had given him a waking like that of some Maenad, amazed at morning on a mountain-top.

He took up the book again. He read—and remembered without reading. A name was enough: with it rose deeds, places, faces; the chain of association held firm. A deed recalled a name, and the mind linked them as deftly as a cook binds a sauce back to smoothness after it has threatened to break—patient heat, the right motion, the secret proportion. The fibres were active still. The self he had recovered had not fled.

But what he found there, in those remembered histories, was not comfort. There were tales of inexpiable crimes; and there were tales, too, of guilt that seemed to prosper. Sanctuaries existed for swift-footed miscreants; baseness had armour; the weapons of justice sometimes snapped against it, as his dagger had snapped. What then? If baseness triumphed everywhere else—if it could heap up the goods of the world and even hold the keys of hell—it could never triumph over the hatred it had itself awakened. It could invent no torture greater than the torture of submitting to its smile. In Baldassarre there burned an emotion that asked for no motive, feared no consequence, needed no nourishment beyond itself—a fire that consumed all other desire.

Now, in the clear morning, gladness came back only as the hope of vengeance. From that day until the evening when he entered the Rucellai gardens, he had been ceaseless in inquiry—cautious, patient, watchful—learning Tito’s position and circumstances, contriving almost daily to follow his movements. He did not wish to raise alarm. He wanted a moment chosen like a cut made where the nerve lies exposed: when Tito was at the summit of confident ease, surrounded by men whose favour he depended on, in the very air of approval.

He did not seek payment, nor the public recognition of himself for his own sake. His whole soul bent toward one thing: to find the sharpest edge of disgrace and shame by which a selfish smiler could be pierced; to send through Tito’s marrow the most sudden shock of dread. He could lie hard; he could live scantily. He spent most of what money he had left to buy another poniard. Hunger and thirst came to him like they come to the poor every day; he fed them when he must, without pleasure, on coarse bread and whatever thin wine he could procure. The only exquisite thing he desired was an exquisite vengeance.

He avoided, too, those who might carry word to Tito. He never entered Nello’s shop, though he saw Tito go there; he turned aside to avoid meeting Piero di Cosimo. The very possibility of frustration sharpened his eagerness; and with it ran another trembling urgency: the fear that his memory might slip away again. Whether from that fear’s agitation or from some other cause, he had twice felt a mental dizziness, a wavering, as if the inward sense were beginning to lose the distinct forms of things. Once he had tried to enter the Palazzo Vecchio and reach a council-chamber where Tito was, and had failed.

But this evening—this evening, as he followed Tito into the torchlit green of Bernardo Rucellai’s garden and heard the chain fall behind him—he felt that the occasion had come at last.
