The following morning, Gwendolen emerged from her chamber radiant and robust, resembling a tall lily freshly unfurled by the sun. A surge of youthful vitality coursed through her, washing away yesterday's fleeting doubts as easily as a ripple vanishes on a swift river. The prospect of the archery match at Cardell Chase filled her with delight. She envisioned herself gliding beneath the beech trees like a woodland nymph, surrounded by appreciative admirers. Within this imagined tableau, Grandcourt's advances seemed not only acceptable but inevitable. She foresaw him approaching slowly, weaving a web of conversation leading inevitably to a declaration, and she prepared to receive it with the calculated grace that her uncle had advised.

Upon descending to breakfast, where everyone had departed save Mrs. Davilow, Gwendolen discovered a pile of letters on her plate. She read one with a gathering smile, passing it to her mother, who returned it with a renewed cheerfulness, delighted by her daughter's buoyant spirit. "You don't feel inclined to go a thousand miles away?" Mrs. Davilow asked gently.

"Not exactly so far," Gwendolen replied lightly.

"It was a regrettable oversight not to write again sooner," her mother noted. "Could you not write now, before we depart?"

"It is not urgent. Tomorrow will suffice. You see, they leave town today and will remain until Monday. I must send word to Dover."

"Shall I write for you, dear, if it burdens you?"

Gwendolen hesitated briefly before sipping her coffee, then answered brusquely, "Oh, no, let it be. I will write tomorrow." A flicker of guilt touched her heart, and she softened her tone, looking up with playful tenderness. "Dear, old, beautiful mamma!"

"Old, child, truly."

"Please don't, mamma! I meant 'old' for 'darling.' You are hardly twenty-five years older than I am. When you speak in that manner, my life seems to shrivel."

"One can find great happiness in twenty-five years, my dear," Mrs. Davilow murmured.

"I must lose no time in beginning," Gwendolen declared merrily. "The sooner I secure my palaces and coaches, the better."

"And a devoted husband who worships you, Gwen," Mrs. Davilow encouraged.

Gwendolen pouted saucily, offering no verbal response.

A minor shadow fell upon her joy when she learned that the rector was detained by magistrate's business and likely unable to attend Cardell Chase. While she cared little that Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna intended to stay behind, her uncle's presence would have given the decision a sense of inevitability. Decision itself, however, had begun to feel formidable. Having drawn close to accepting Grandcourt, Gwendolen sensed that this accumulation of desired outcomes was solidifying into something rigid, a form that threatened to exclude possibility. When one wishes intensely for much, whatever is obtained often becomes mere limitation. Yet, a reassuring thought persisted: marriage would serve as the gateway to a broader freedom.

The gathering place was a grassy clearing known as Green Arbor, sheltered by a canopy of hanging branches forming a natural amphitheater. Here, the coachload of servants prepared the picnic provisions, while the warden of the Chase would guide the roving archers, ensuring they stayed within the prescribed bounds defined by landmarks like the Double Oak, the Whispering Stones, and the High Cross. The plan called for a brief stroll before luncheon, saving the main expedition for the golden light of the afternoon. The muster proceeded swiftly, sparing anyone from the boredom of waiting. As the groups scattered through the dappled light and shade cast by the intermingled beeches and oaks, one might imagine a painter longing to capture the scene.

Roving archery possessed a distinct elegance compared to stationary target practice, though success required skill in hitting moving marks. Consequently, the hits were distributed unevenly among the volunteers. Perhaps owing to this unpredictability, compounded by Gwendolen's dual distraction of preoccupation and a desperate desire to hide it, she did not distinguish herself in these initial trials. She managed her comparative failures with lively grace, dressed in white and green as she had been during the fateful meeting where Grandcourt first approached her. Though he remained constantly by her side, their outward demeanor suggested little change since their first conversation, yet observers, and Gwendolen herself, concluded that they stood on the brink of engagement.

As the groups returned toward Green Arbor, chatting aimlessly rather than focusing on targets, significant words were exchanged. These remarks marked the true beginning of her acceptance. Grandcourt asked, "Do you know how long it has been since I first saw you in this dress?"

"The archery meeting was on the twenty-fifth, and this is the thirteenth," Gwendolen laughed. "I am not adept at calculation, but I venture to say it must be nearly three weeks."

"A little pause ensued, and then he remarked, 'That is a great loss of time.'"

"That your knowing me causes you to feel the loss?" Gwendolen countered playfully. "Pray, do not be uncomplimentary; I dislike it."

Another pause stretched between them. "It is because of the gain that I feel the loss," Grandcourt finally stated.

Gwendolen allowed a silence to follow, her mind racing. *He is truly ingenious,* she thought. *He never speaks foolishly.* Her prolonged silence seemed the strongest affirmative answer possible. Grandcourt pressed on, "The gain of knowing you makes me acutely feel the time I spend in uncertainty. Do *you* like uncertainty?"

"I think I do, rather," Gwendolen replied, suddenly beaming with a playful smile. "There is more in it."

Grandcourt met her laughing gaze with a slow, steady look that seemed to penetrate her soul, an abstraction of vision. "Do you mean more torment for me?"

Something profoundly strange occurred in that moment, shaking Gwendolen out of her usual self-consciousness. Blushing and averting her eyes, she whispered, "No, that would make me sorry."

Grandcourt would have pursued this answer, which her shift in manner rendered decisive, but he was acutely aware that they were descending into Green Arbor in plain view of everyone, specifically at a point where the slope became inconveniently steep. This necessitated his offering his hand to assist her. She took it, and they descended in silence, watched intently by those already on the level, including Mrs. Arrowpoint, who stood beside Mrs. Davilow. Mrs. Arrowpoint had resolved that Grandcourt's merits were insufficient to induce Catherine to accept him, given Catherine's high standards, having previously rejected Lord Slogan. Thus, she regarded the tenant of Diplow with detached scrutiny.

"Mr. Grandcourt is not equal as a man to his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger—he is too languid," Mrs. Arrowpoint observed aloud. "To be sure, Mr. Grandcourt is much younger, but I should not be surprised if Sir Hugo outlives him, despite the difference in years. Succession is a dangerous calculation."

"It is indeed," Mrs. Davilow assented quietly, her cheerful composure masking her habitual melancholy regarding life's unsatisfactory nature.

The narrator need not chronicle the sumptuous food consumed in that green refectory nor dwell on the tales of forest scenery spreading beyond the hollow's edge. Suffice it to say, the food and champagne were of the finest quality, and the conversation belonged to the highest circles, devoid of invidious displays. Some gentlemen strolled and smoked cigars, enjoying a leisurely interval before four o'clock, when the roving would resume. Strangely, Grandcourt was among them, yet not Mr. Lush, who seemed to be serving everyone with excessive generosity, becoming increasingly conspicuous as a blot on the scene to Gwendolen, though he maintained an amiably aloof posture, avoiding obvious eye contact.

When the group prepared to depart, it transpired that the bows were under the charge of Lord Brackenshaw's valet. Mr. Lush offered to retrieve the ladies' bows from the carriage to spare them the trouble. He did not intend to fetch Gwendolen's bow, but fearing he might, she hurried ahead to collect it herself. Just as she approached, the valet intercepted her with the bow, handing it to her along with a letter addressed to her. She asked no questions, instantly recognizing the delicate, feminine handwriting of the past era. Moving away to avoid encountering Mr. Lush, who was arriving to fetch other bows, she turned her back toward the carriage stand and opened the letter. It read:

"If Miss Harleth is in doubt whether she should accept Mr. Grandcourt, let her break from her party after they have passed the Whispering Stones and return to that spot. She will then hear something to decide her; but she can only hear it by keeping this letter a strict secret from everyone. If she does not act according to this letter, she will repent, as the woman who writes it has repented. The secrecy Miss Harleth will feel herself bound in honor to guard."
