Though Mr. Crisparkle and John Jasper encountered one another with regularity beneath the canopy of the Cathedral, their meetings were marked by a profound, unspoken silence regarding the fate of Edwin Drood. Since the half-year prior, when Jasper had silently presented the Minor Canon with his journal's final entries concerning the missing youth, neither man had dared to broach the subject. Yet, the weight of their mutual obsession hung heavily in the air. Every encounter served only to deepen the sense that each viewed the other as an enigma wrapped in mystery. Jasper, the relentless hunter of Neville Landless, and Mr. Crisparkle, his steadfast defender, occupied opposing moral poles; nonetheless, neither could bring himself to question the other's motives. They remained bound by a shared, terrifying secret, though neither sought to unravel it.

True to his nature, the Minor Canon would have welcomed an audience on this topic, displaying an openness that belied his inner turmoil. However, John Jasper remained impervious, a fortress of solitude constructed from his obsessions. He existed in a state of mechanical harmony with the external world, manipulating social interactions with the precision of an artist, yet spiritually isolated from humanity. He had once confessed this isolation to his lost nephew, a truth that now governed his rigid, inflexible conduct.

Jasper undoubtedly knew of Rosa Bud's sudden flight and guessed its causes. Whether he believed he had driven her to silence through terror, or if she had revealed the details of their last confrontation to Mr. Crisparkle, remained a mystery to the latter. Though a man of justice, Crisparkle struggled to condemn Jasper's love for Rosa, recognizing that affection, however twisted, was distinct from criminal malice. The dreadful suspicion haunting Rosa—the notion that Jasper intended murder—found no foothold in Crisparkle's mind. While Helena Landless and Neville remained silent on the matter, and the eccentric Mr. Grewgious maintained a polite distance from his aversion to Jasper, the atmosphere in Cloisterham was thick with unvoiced dread. The town itself seemed suspended in a limbo of speculation, oscillating between theories of treacherous passion, violent struggle, or voluntary disappearance. Meanwhile, the grieving Jasper continued his grim crusade, undeterred by the loss that fueled his resolve.

With the Cathedral doors sealed for the night, the Choir-master departed on a brief leave, traveling by the same route Rosa had taken. He arrived in London on a sweltering, dusty evening, checking into a modest, hybrid establishment near Aldersgate Street. The inn, a novelty in the railway era, offered a pragmatic bargain: boots blacked, meals provided, and a porter available for a fixed sum, reflecting a leveling spirit in the times.

Inside a dismal, narrow court, Jasper descended a broken staircase and knocked on a door. "Are you alone?" he asked.

"Alone, deary!" a raspy voice replied. "Better for you, worse for me. Light a match, whoever you be."

As the tenant lit a candle, revealing a gaunt, suffering woman, Jasper introduced himself. She, trembling with a severe cough, spoke of her lungs as "wore away to cabbage-nets," yet her eyes sharpened instantly upon recognizing him. "Why, it's you!" she cried, tears welling. "I thought you were dead, and gone to Heaven."

"I'm alive," Jasper said calmly. "And I am in mourning."

"Mourning?" she echoed, her grief momentarily overshadowed by business acumen. "Who died, lovey?"

"A relative."

"Probably Death," Jasper muttered dryly.

"We're short tonight!" she declared, laughing propitiatorily. "But we're out of sorts for lack of smoke. We've got the 'all-overs,' haven't we? This is the place to cure 'em."

Jasper removed his shoes and loosened his cravat, lying down on the squalid bed. "Now you begin to look like yourself," she approved. "Been trying to mix for yourself this long time, poppet?"

"I have been taking it now and then in my own way."

"It ain't good for trade, and it ain't good for you," she warned, producing an ink-bottle, a thimble, and a spoon to prepare the mixture. "He's going to take it in an artful form now, my deary dear!"

As she bubbled and blew at the faint spark in her hands, Jasper, eyes distant and dreamy, drifted into a monologue. "I've got a pretty many smokes ready for you... When you first come, you was quite new to it... But you got on in the world, and was able by-and-by to take your pipe with the best of 'em."

He accepted the pipe, and after a few silences, questioned its potency. "Is it as potent as it used to be?"

"Just the same," she assured him. "Always the identical same."

"It doesn't taste so. And it's slower."

"You've got more used to it, you see."

Jasper grew thoughtful, then suddenly intense. "Suppose you had something in your mind; something you were going to do."

"Yes, deary; something I was going to do?"

"But had not quite determined to do."

"Yes, deary."

"Should you do it in your fancy, when you were lying here doing this?"

She nodded, stirring the contents of the bowl with a needle. "Over and over again."

"Just like me!" Jasper interrupted, his voice rising with a savage edge. "I did it over and over again. I have done it hundreds of thousands of times in this room."

"It's to be hoped it was pleasant to do, deary."

"It *was* pleasant to do!" he snarled, a wolfish quality emerging in his tone. "It WAS one! It WAS one!"

"There was a fellow-traveller, deary."

"Ha, ha, ha!" Jasper erupted into a ringing, hysterical laugh. "To think how often fellow-traveller, and yet not know it! To think how many times he went the journey, and never saw the road!"

The woman knelt beside him, watching him with a mixture of curiosity and caution. "I'll warrant you made the journey in a many ways, when you made it so often?"

"No, always in one way."

"In the way in which it was really made at last?"

"Ay."

"And always took the same pleasure in harping on it?"

"Ay."

For some time, Jasper could only offer a lazy, monosyllabic assent. Then, the woman probed deeper. "Did you never get tired of it, deary, and try to call up something else for a change?"

Struggling upright, Jasper snapped, "What do you mean? What did I want? What did I come for?"

Laying him back gently, she coaxed, "Sure, sure, sure! You was too quick for me. I see now. You come o' purpose to take the journey."

"Yes, I came on purpose," Jasper confirmed, his teeth clenched passionately. "When I could not bear my life, I came to get the relief, and I got it."

"There was a fellow-traveller, deary."

"How could the time be at hand unless the fellow-traveller was? Hush! The journey's made. It's over."

"So soon?"

"That's what I said to you. So soon. Wait a little. This is a vision. I shall sleep it off. It has been too short and easy. I must have a better vision than this; this is the poorest of all."

Wild, meaningless gestures followed his incoherent ramblings, trailing off into a progressive stupor. Jasper lay like a log upon the bed. The woman, however, remained vigilant. Sitting in a chair by the hearth, she whispered to him, recalling his own words about unintelligible forces. "Don't ye be too sure always; don't be ye too sure, beauty!"

As the candles burned down and daylight crept into the room, Jasper sat up, chilled and shaking. He recovered his consciousness, paid the woman a grateful sum, and departed. But she did not stay behind. As soon as his footsteps faded, she glided after him, muttering, "I'll not miss ye twice!"

Following him through the labyrinthine alleys, she tracked him to a temporary lodging near Aldersgate Street, waiting patiently for hours. When Jasper emerged at noon, having changed his dress but carrying nothing, she followed him to Cloisterham High Street. Standing near the Nuns' House, she waited until nine o'clock, observing the arrival of an omnibus.

Among the passengers, she spotted the gentleman from Cloisterham. "Go on!" she murmured, addressing the air as if he could hear. He walked swiftly to an arched gateway and vanished inside. Quickening her pace, the woman entered the gateway herself, confronting a gray-haired gentleman writing in an ancient vaulted room.

"Halloa!" the man cried, seeing her halted. "Who are you looking for?"

"There was a gentleman passed in here this minute, sir."

"Of course there was. What do you want with him?"

"Where do he live, deary?"

"Up that staircase."

"What's his name, deary?"

"Surname Jasper, Christian name John. Mr. John Jasper."

"He sings in the choir?"

"Yes. In the spire?"

"Choir."
