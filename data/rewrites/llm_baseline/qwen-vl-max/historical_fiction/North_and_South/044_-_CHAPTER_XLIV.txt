“A dull rotation, never at a stay,
Yesterday’s face twin image of to-day.” — COWPER
“Of what each one should be, he sees the form and rule,
And till he reach to that, his joy can ne’er be full.” — RÜCKERT

For Margaret Hale, the stillness of the Harley Street house during Edith Lennox’s recovery offered more than mere quiet—it gave her the rest her body and spirit so desperately needed. In those weeks, she found herself transplanted into a world of comfort and ease, far removed from the familiar rhythms of Helstone and Milton. The home was spacious, well-ordered, and polished—its machinery of daily life running with such smooth precision that it seemed untouched by hardship or care. The servants moved like shadows, attending only when summoned, their lives hidden in a silent underground realm. Mrs. Shaw and Edith doted on Margaret, treating her return as a long-awaited homecoming. Yet, deep within, Margaret felt a quiet dissonance.

She could not help but remember the small vicarage at Helstone, with its modest rooms and humble routines—the scent of pipe smoke mingling with the damp earth outside, her father’s thoughtful silences, her mother’s frail strength. Even the cramped little house in Milton, where worries were shared and meals eaten in earnest conversation, now seemed more real, more *home*, than this gilded cage of London elegance.

Edith, eager to make Margaret feel welcome, rushed through her convalescence to rearrange her room—filling it with soft fabrics, delicate trinkets, and all the finery she deemed necessary for a lady’s comfort. Mrs. Shaw and her maid busied themselves restoring Margaret’s wardrobe to a state of refined variety, as though every thread must reflect the dignity of her new station. Captain Lennox, gentle and courteous, passed his days between his wife’s dressing room, playful hours with his son Sholto, and idle afternoons at his club. He treated Margaret with kindness, almost like a younger sister, though she sometimes resented his overzealous attention to Edith’s appearance—his desire to present her as the perfect specimen of beauty before society.

Margaret had barely begun to adjust to this tranquil existence when Edith returned to her usual routine. Once again, Margaret fell into the old pattern of watching, admiring, and caring for her cousin. She willingly took on tasks Edith left behind: answering letters, reminding her of appointments, tending to her when fatigue made her feign illness. But while Edith and her husband plunged into the whirl of London’s social season, Margaret often found herself alone—left to wander through empty rooms, her thoughts drifting back to Milton.

There, life had been lived with purpose, with struggle, with meaning. Here, everything flowed effortlessly, without effort or direction. The ease began to weigh upon her. She feared becoming numb, softened into forgetfulness, her soul dimmed by luxury. There were no voices from the streets, no cries from the factories, no faces worn by labor—only the polite murmurs of drawing-room talk and the distant clatter of carriages. The servants existed in silence, emerging only when summoned, their lives invisible, their hopes unknown.

A hollow space opened in her heart. She tried once to express this unease to Edith, who, languid after a night of dancing, lay back on the sofa, stroking Margaret’s cheek with a half-sleepy affection.

“Poor child,” Edith murmured. “It must be lonely for you, sitting here night after night while the world dances. But soon—when Henry comes back from circuit—we’ll have dinner parties. That will cheer you up, won’t it? No wonder you’re moping, dear.”

But Margaret did not believe dinner parties would mend the emptiness. Edith prided herself on these gatherings—how different they were, she said, from the stiff, formal affairs under her mother’s rule. Mrs. Shaw, too, seemed to take equal pleasure in this new style of entertaining, just as she once had in the grand, solemn dinners of her youth.

Captain Lennox remained kind, even protective, toward Margaret. She liked him—except when he became overly attentive to Edith’s dress, as if her beauty were a performance to be perfected for public approval. Then, a spark of defiance stirred within her—the same Vashti who had once refused a king’s command. She bit her tongue, but her eyes burned with unspoken protest.

Her days followed a set rhythm: a quiet morning before late breakfast; an unhurried meal attended by people still half-asleep, yet expected to join in the endless discussions of plans and invitations that followed; a flood of letters to write—Edith always handing them over with compliments on Margaret’s “eloquence du billet”; time spent playing with Sholto; watching over the children during the servants’ meal; a carriage ride or callers; then, evening engagements that left her free but drained, her spirits low.

She looked forward, silently, to the return of Dixon from Milton. The servant had been managing the Hale household since their departure, and her letters had become a lifeline—though brief and business-like. They mentioned Mr. Thornton occasionally, offering advice on furniture or leases, but never with warmth or depth. The names of Milton’s people rarely appeared. And so, one evening, as Margaret sat alone in the drawing-room, holding Dixon’s latest letter without reading it, her mind wandered to the past—remembering the busy life she had left behind, wondering if anyone missed her there.

Suddenly, the door opened, and Mr. Bell was announced.

Margaret started, blushing as she hastily tucked the letter into her workbasket.

“Oh, Mr. Bell! I didn’t expect to see you!”

“But you give me a welcome, I hope—besides that very pretty start of surprise.”

“Have you dined? How did you come? Let me order you something.”

“If you’re having dinner, I might join you. Otherwise, I don’t care much for eating. Where are the others? Out?”

“Yes—gone to dinner. It’s a relief, actually. I was just thinking—” she paused, then added, “Would you like some tea instead?”

“I’d rather have tea. And now—what were you thinking of? You were about to tell me. Whose letters were those, god-daughter, that you hid so quickly?”

“Only Dixon’s,” she replied, growing red.

“Whew! Is that all? Who came up with me on the train?”

“I don’t know,” she said, refusing to guess.

“Your—what do you call him? Cousin-in-law’s brother?”

“Mr. Henry Lennox?” she asked.

“Yes. You knew him before?”

“I liked him once,” she said, glancing down. Then she lifted her eyes. “We’ve corresponded about Frederick since—but I haven’t seen him in nearly three years. Has he changed?”

“I don’t know. He was too busy trying to figure out who I was—and what I was—to reveal anything about himself. Unless,” he added, “that veiled curiosity was part of his character. Do you think he’s good-looking?”

“No. Not at all. Do you?”

“Not I. But I thought perhaps you might. Is he here often?”

“I suppose so when he’s in town. He’s been away on circuit since I arrived. But—Mr. Bell—did you come from Oxford or from Milton?”

“From Milton. Don’t you see I’m smoke-dried?”

“Certainly. But I thought it might be the effect of Oxford’s antiquities.”

“Come now, be sensible! In Oxford, I could have managed the landlords with half the trouble your Milton landlord caused me—and still failed. He won’t take the house off our hands until next June. Luckily, Mr. Thornton found a tenant. Why don’t you ask after Mr. Thornton, Margaret? He’s been a most active friend of yours. Took more than half the burden off my shoulders.”

“And how is he? How is Mrs. Thornton?” she asked, her voice low, breathless.

“I suppose they’re well. I stayed at their house until I was driven out by the constant chatter about the Thornton girl’s marriage. Too much for even Thornton himself, though she was his own sister. He used to retreat to his room. He’s past the age for such things. I was surprised to find the old lady swept along by her daughter’s excitement over orange-blossoms and lace. I thought Mrs. Thornton had stronger stuff in her.”

“She would pretend feeling to cover her daughter’s weakness,” Margaret whispered.

“Perhaps. You’ve studied her, haven’t you? She doesn’t seem to care much for you.”

“I know,” Margaret said quietly. “Oh—here’s the tea!” she exclaimed, relieved.

And just as the tea arrived, Mr. Henry Lennox entered, having walked up from dinner. He clearly expected to find his brother and sister-in-law at home. Margaret sensed his gratitude at seeing another person present—especially after their last meeting, when he had proposed and she had refused, in the garden at Helstone.

She could not speak at first, grateful for the distractions of the tea table. He, too, seemed to need the moment to compose himself. He had come to Harley Street deliberately, knowing the awkwardness of the encounter, and now, finding her alone, he felt doubly exposed.

She recovered first. “Mr. Lennox,” she said, “I’ve been so grateful for all you’ve done regarding Frederick.”

“I’m sorry it hasn’t succeeded,” he replied, glancing toward Mr. Bell as if measuring how much he could say.

Margaret understood. She turned to Mr. Bell. “That Horrocks—the last witness—proved useless, just like the others. Mr. Lennox discovered he sailed for Australia only last August—two months before Frederick was in England.”

“Frederick in England? You never told me that!” Mr. Bell exclaimed.

“I thought you knew. It was a secret, and perhaps I shouldn’t have said it now,” she admitted, embarrassed.

“I never mentioned it to my brother or your cousin,” Mr. Lennox said, dryly.

“Never mind,” Mr. Bell said, laughing. “I’m not living in a world of gossip, nor among people who pry. You needn’t look so alarmed. I won’t betray your secret. I’m a faithful old hermit—I’ll keep it safe. But wait—was it at your mother’s funeral?”

“He was with mamma when she died,” Margaret said softly.

“To be sure! I remember someone asking me if he’d been over then. I denied it—just a few weeks ago. Who could it have been?” He paused, then stopped himself from naming the person.

Margaret longed to know if it had been Mr. Thornton—but she could not ask.

After a pause, Mr. Lennox addressed her directly. “Now that Mr. Bell knows the circumstances of your brother’s situation, I may as well inform him of the current state of our investigation. If he will do me the honor of breakfasting with me tomorrow, we can go over the list of missing witnesses.”

“I’d like to hear all about it,” she said. “Can’t you come here? I wouldn’t dare invite both of you to breakfast, though I’m sure you’d be welcome. Just tell me what you can—even if there’s no hope.”

“I have an engagement at half-past eleven,” he replied, “but I’ll come if you wish.”

His tone held a hint of eagerness that made her shrink inwardly, wishing she hadn’t asked.

Mr. Bell rose to leave, searching for his hat. “Well, I don’t know what Mr. Lennox intends, but I’m ready to go home. A journey today has tired me at my sixty and odd years.”

“I think I’ll stay and see my brother and sister,” Mr. Lennox said, remaining seated.

Margaret felt a sudden dread—of being left alone with him. The memory of that terrace at Helstone flooded back, vivid and undeniable.

“Don’t go yet, please, Mr. Bell,” she said quickly. “I want you to meet Edith. Please!”

She laid a light hand on his arm. He looked at her, saw the flush of emotion, and sat back down as if compelled.

“You see how she commands me, Mr. Lennox,” he said. “And notice her clever choice of words—she wants me to ‘see’ Edith, whom I’m told is beautiful—but when it comes to me, she says Mrs. Lennox is to ‘know’ me. I suppose I’m not much to ‘see,’ eh, Margaret?”

He joked to ease her tension, and she responded with a smile.

Mr. Lennox, watching her, wondered how his brother could have claimed she had lost her beauty. In her simple black dress, she stood in sharp contrast to Edith—dancing in white crape, her golden hair flowing. Yet Margaret’s presence was quiet, graceful. When introduced to Mr. Bell, she dimpled and blushed, aware of her reputation, determined not to let a man like him—old, learned, obscure—disregard her charm.

Mrs. Shaw and Captain Lennox welcomed Mr. Bell warmly, their sincerity winning him over. He saw how naturally Margaret fit into the family, and began to admire her.

“What a shame we weren’t home to receive you,” Edith said. “You too, Henry! Though I don’t know if we’d have stayed for you. And Mr. Bell—for Margaret’s Mr. Bell—”

“There’s no telling what sacrifices you wouldn’t make,” Henry said. “Even skipping a dinner party! Or wearing this lovely dress.”

Edith hesitated between frown and smile, but Henry chose not to push. “Will you show your readiness to sacrifice tomorrow? First, by inviting me to breakfast to meet Mr. Bell, and second, by ordering it at half-past nine instead of ten? I have papers to show Miss Hale and Mr. Bell.”

“I hope Mr. Bell will treat our house as his own,” Captain Lennox said. “Though I’m sorry we can’t offer a bedroom.”

“Thank you,” Mr. Bell replied. “But I’d decline, even with such pleasant company. It would be like meat without salt. I’m glad they don’t have a bed.”

Outside, walking side by side, Mr. Bell suddenly remembered Margaret’s pleading glance. He also recalled hints from a mutual acquaintance about Henry Lennox’s feelings for her.

“You’ve known Miss Hale a long time,” he said. “How does she look? Pale, I think.”

“I thought her remarkably well. At least, when she spoke, she looked as vibrant as ever.”

“She’s endured much,” Mr. Bell said.

“Yes. I’ve heard of her sorrow—not just death, but the distress her father’s actions must have caused. And then—”

“Her father’s actions?” Mr. Bell interrupted.

“You must have heard a false account. He acted with great conscience. More strength than I’d have credited him with.”

“Perhaps I was misinformed. But I’ve heard from his successor—a capable clergyman—that there was no need for Mr. Hale to resign. The bishop offered him another living. If he’d doubted, he could have stayed. These country clergymen live isolated lives—cut off from men of equal culture. They often torment themselves with imagined doubts, throwing away opportunities for uncertain whims.”

“I disagree,” Henry said calmly. “I don’t think many do what Mr. Hale did.”

Mr. Bell bristled. “I meant ‘very apt’ generally. But yes, their lives often breed either arrogance or morbid conscience.”

“And lawyers?” Mr. Bell challenged. “No self-sufficiency? No guilt?”

He was losing patience.

Henry, seeing the irritation, changed course. “To be sure, there’s something noble in a man of Mr. Hale’s age leaving his home of twenty years, giving up settled habits, for an idea—perhaps mistaken—but that doesn’t matter. One can’t help admiring him, mixed with pity. Like Don Quixote. Such a gentleman! I’ll never forget his hospitality at Helstone.”

Mr. Bell grumbled. “Aye. But you don’t know Milton. Such a change from Helstone! It’s standing there, unchanged—every stone as it was a century ago. But Milton! I go there every few years—I was born there—yet I often lose my way, among warehouses built on my father’s orchard.”

They reached their parting point.

“Well, good-night, sir. We’ll meet tomorrow in Harley Street.”
