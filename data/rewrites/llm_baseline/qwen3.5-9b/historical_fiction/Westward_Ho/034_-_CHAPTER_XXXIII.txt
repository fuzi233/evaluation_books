"It was the first of October. The morning was bright and still; the skies were dappled modestly from east to west with soft gray autumn clouds, as if all heaven and earth were resting after those fearful summer months of battle and storm."

Silently, as if ashamed and sad, the *Vengeance* slid over the bar, passed the sleeping sandhills, and dropped her anchor off Appledore. Her flag floated half-mast high, for the corpse of Salvation Yeo lay aboard. A small boat pulled away from the ship toward the western end of the strand. Cary and Brimblecombe helped out Amyas Leigh, leading him slowly up the hill toward his home.

A crowd had already clustered around him, offering cheers, blessings, and sobs of pity from kind-hearted women. Everyone in Appledore and Bideford knew by now what had befallen him.

"Spare me, my good friends," Amyas said softly. "I have landed here that I might go quietly home, without passing through the town and becoming a gazing-stock. Think not of me, good folks, nor talk of me; but come behind me decently, as Christian men, and follow to the grave the body of a better man than I."

As he spoke, another boat approached, carrying the body of Salvation Yeo, covered with the flag of England. The people took Amyas at his word. A man was sent ahead to Burrough to tell Mrs. Leigh that her son was coming. When the coffin was landed and lifted, Amyas and his friends took their places behind it as chief mourners, the crew following in order, while the crowd fell in behind them, gathering with every step. By the time they were halfway to Northam Town, the funeral train numbered nearly five hundred souls.

They had sent a fishing skiff the previous day to ask the sexton to dig the grave. Upon reaching the churchyard, the parson stood ready at the gate. Mrs. Leigh remained quietly at home; she had no heart to face the throng. Though her heart yearned for her son, she was content—more than content—that he should do honor to his ancient and faithful servant. Sitting in the bay-window with Ayacanora by her side, she waited until the tolling of the bell ceased. Then, opening her Prayer-book, she began to read the Burial Service.

"Ayacanora," she said gently, "they are burying old Master Yeo, who loved you, and sought you over the wide, wide world, and saved you from the teeth of the crocodile. Are you not sorry for him, child, that you look so gay today?"

Ayacanora blushed and hung her head. Thinking of nothing but Amyas, the poor child offered no answer.

When the service concluded and the parson withdrew, the people lingered, crowding round to gaze at the coffin. Amyas stood still at the head of the grave. It had been dug by his command at the west end of the church, near the foot of the tall, gray, windswept tower that watched far and wide over land and sea. Perhaps the old man liked to look at the sea, seeing ships come and go across the bar, or hearing the wind roar through the belfry on winter nights. Why not? It was merely a fancy, yet Amyas felt he too would wish to be buried in such a place; perhaps Yeo would approve.

Still, the crowd lingered, looking first at the grave and then at the blind giant standing over it. Instinctively, they sensed that something more was due. And something more did come.

Amyas drew himself up to his full height and waved his hand majestically, as one about to speak. All eyes were fastened upon him. Twice he essayed to begin, but twice the words choked upon his lips. Finally, he spoke:

"Good people all, and seamen, among whom I was bred, and to whom I come home blind this day, to dwell with you till death—here lieth the flower and pattern of all bold mariners; the truest of friends, and the most terrible of foes; unchangeable of purpose, crafty of council, and swift of execution; in triumph most sober, in failure (as God knows I have found full many a day) of endurance beyond mortal man.

Who first of all Britons helped to humble the pride of the Spaniard at Rio de la Hacha and Nombre, and first of all sailed upon those South Seas, which shall be hereafter, by God's grace, as free to English keels as is the bay outside. Who, having afterwards been purged from his youthful sins by strange afflictions and torments unspeakable, suffered at the hands of the Popish enemy, learned therefrom, my masters, to fear God, and to fear naught else; and having acquitted himself worthily in his place and calling as a righteous scourge of the Spaniard, and a faithful soldier of the Lord Jesus Christ, is now exalted to his reward, as Elijah was of old, in a chariot of fire unto heaven: letting fall, I trust and pray, upon you who are left behind the mantle of his valor and his godliness, that so these shores may never be without brave and pious mariners, who will count their lives as worthless in the cause of their Country, their Bible, and their Queen. Amen."

Feeling for his companions' hands, Amyas walked slowly from the churchyard, across the village street, and up the lane to the Burrough gates. The crowd made way for him in solemn silence, as for an awful being, shut up alone with all his strength, valor, and fame, in the dark prison-house of his mysterious doom.

He seemed to know perfectly when they reached the gates. Opening the lock with his own hands, he went boldly forward along the gravel path, while Cary and Brimblecombe followed him trembling. They expected some violent burst of emotion, either from him or his mother, and the two good fellows' tender hearts fluttered like a girl's.

Up to the door he went, as if he had seen it. Feeling for the entrance, he stood within and called quietly, "Mother!"

In a moment, his mother was on his bosom. Neither spoke for a while. She sobbed inwardly with tearless eyes; he stood firm and cheerful, his great arms clasped around her.

"Mother!" he said at last. "I am come home, you see, because I needs must come. Will you take me in, and look after this useless carcase? I shall not be so very troublesome, mother—shall I?"

He looked down, smiling, and kissed her brow. She answered not a word, but passed her arm gently round his waist and led him inside.

"Take care of your head, dear child; the doors are low."

Together they entered.

"Will! Jack!" called Amyas, turning round. But the two good fellows had already walked briskly off.

"I'm glad we are away," said Cary. "I should have made a baby of myself in another minute, watching that angel of a woman. How her face worked and how she kept it in!"

"Ah, well!" said Jack. "There goes a brave servant of the Queen's cut off before his work was a quarter done. Heigho! I must home now, and see my old father, and then—"

"And then home with me," said Cary. "You and I never part again! We have pulled in the same boat too long, Jack; and you must not go spending your prize-money in riotous living. I must see after you, old Jack ashore, or we shall have you treating half the town in taverns for a week to come."

"Oh, Mr. Cary!" said Jack, scandalized. "Come home with me, and we'll poison the parson, and my father shall give you the rectory."

So the two went off to Clovelly together that very day.

Amyas sat all alone. His mother had gone out briefly to speak to the seamen who had brought up his luggage, setting them down to eat and drink. Amyas sat in the old bay-window, where he had sat when he was a little tiny boy, reading "King Arthur," "Fox's Martyrs," and "The Cruelties of the Spaniards." He put out his hand and felt for the books; there they lay side by side, just as they had twenty years before.

The window was open, and a cool air brought in, as of old, the scents of four-season roses, rosemary, and autumn gilliflowers. On the table lay a dish of apples. He knew them by their smell—the very same old apples he used to gather when he was a boy. He put out his hand, took them, felt them over, and played with them, as if the twenty years had never been.

As he fingered them, the whole of his past life rose up before him, as in that strange dream said to flash across the imagination of a drowning man. He saw all the places he had ever seen and heard all the words ever spoken to him—until he came to that fairy island on the Meta. He heard the roar of the cataract once more, saw the green tops of the palm trees sleeping in the sunlight far above the spray, stepped amid the smooth palm trunks across the flower-fringed boulders, and leaped down to the gravel beach beside the pool.
