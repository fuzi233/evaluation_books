Elinor watched her brother-in-law with a heavy heart, noting the profound gloom that had settled over him. His visit offered her only a fraction of the comfort she craved, for his own enjoyment of the moment seemed equally diminished. It was painfully clear that he was unhappy, yet she prayed with every fiber of her being that his affection for her remained as steadfast as it had once been. Until now, however, the persistence of his regard had felt precarious, his reserved demeanor constantly contradicting the fleeting sparks of animation he occasionally showed.

The following morning, before the rest of the household had risen, Edward joined Elinor and Marianne in the breakfast room. Marianne, ever eager to foster their happiness, soon excused herself to attend to her duties upstairs. Yet, barely halfway up the stairs, she heard the parlour door swing open and turned to find Edward emerging.

"I am off to the village to check on my horses," he announced, "since you are not yet ready for breakfast. I shall be back shortly."

When he returned, his spirits were buoyed by a fresh appreciation of the countryside. His walk to the village had revealed many hidden beauties of the valley, and the elevated position of the village itself offered a sweeping panorama that delighted him. This topic naturally captured Marianne's attention; she began to describe her own awe of the scenery, probing him with questions about the specific details that had struck him. Edward, however, gently interjected.

"You must not press me too closely, Marianne," he said. "Remember, I possess no knowledge of the picturesque. If we delve into particulars, I shall only offend you with my ignorance and lack of taste. I might label a hill 'steep' when it ought to be 'bold,' or call a surface 'strange' when it should be 'irregular.' Distant objects, to me, are merely out of sight, whereas they should be indistinct through a hazy atmosphere. Please accept such admiration as I can honestly offer. To me, it is simply a very fine country—the hills are steep, the woods brim with timber, and the valley feels comfortable and snug, dotted with rich meadows and neat farmhouses. It suits my definition of beauty because it unites aesthetic pleasure with utility. I daresay it is picturesque too, simply because you admire it; I can easily imagine it teeming with rocks, promontories, grey moss, and brushwood, though all of that escapes me. I know nothing of the picturesque."

"I fear it is too true," Marianne admitted, "but why boast of such limitations?"

"I suspect," Elinor observed, "that in trying to avoid one kind of affectation, Edward has fallen into another. Believing that many people pretend to admire nature's beauties more deeply than they truly feel, he has adopted an attitude of greater indifference and less discrimination than he actually possesses. He is fastidious and has cultivated an affectation of his own."

"It is very true," Marianne agreed. "Admiration of landscape has become mere jargon. Everyone pretends to feel and strives to describe with the taste of the man who first defined picturesque beauty. I detest jargon of every sort; often I have kept my feelings to myself because I found no language to describe them except what was worn, hackneyed, and devoid of meaning."

"I am convinced," Edward replied, "that you truly feel all the delight in a fine prospect that you profess. But in return, my sister must allow me to feel no more than I express. I like a fine prospect, but not on picturesque principles. I dislike crooked, twisted, blasted trees; I prefer them tall, straight, and flourishing. I have no fondness for ruined, tattered cottages, nor for nettles, thistles, or heath blossoms. I derive more pleasure from a snug farmhouse than from a watch-tower, and a troop of tidy, happy villagers pleases me far more than the finest banditti in the world."

Marianne stared at Edward with amazement, while looking at her sister with deep compassion. Elinor, however, only laughed.

The conversation lapsed, leaving Marianne thoughtful until a new object caught her eye. Sitting beside Edward, she took his tea from Mrs. Dashwood. As his hand moved, a ring became visible on one of his fingers—a plait of hair set in the center, conspicuous against his skin.

"I never saw you wear a ring before, Edward," she exclaimed. "Is that Fanny's hair? I recall her promising to give you some. But I should have thought her hair was darker."

Marianne spoke without thinking, voicing exactly what she felt. The moment she realized the pain she had caused, her own vexation at her lack of tact was surpassed only by Edward's distress. He flushed deeply, casting a fleeting glance at Elinor before replying, "Yes; it is my sister's hair. The setting always casts a different shade upon it, you know."

Elinor had met his gaze and felt a similar consciousness. Instantly, she knew the hair was hers. The only divergence between their thoughts lay in the conclusion: where Marianne viewed it as a generous gift from her sister, Elinor was acutely aware it must have been obtained through some theft or contrivance unknown to herself. She was not in the mood to consider it an affront. Instead, feigning indifference by immediately changing the subject, she resolved internally to seize every opportunity to examine the ring and confirm beyond doubt that the shade matched her own exactly.

Edward's embarrassment lingered for some time, eventually giving way to a more settled preoccupation. He remained unusually grave throughout the morning. Marianne severely chided herself for her indiscretion, though her forgiveness might have come sooner had she known how little offense it had truly caused her sister.

Before midday, they were visited by Sir John and Mrs. Jennings. Having heard of a gentleman's arrival at the cottage, they came to inspect the guest. With the aid of his mother-in-law, Sir John quickly deduced that the name Ferrars began with an 'F.' This discovery prepared a future mine of raillery against the devoted Elinor, though the novelty of their acquaintance with Edward prevented it from being sprung immediately. For now, she gleaned only from significant glances how far their penetration, guided by Margaret's instructions, had extended.

As was his custom, Sir John never failed to invite them to dine at the park the following day or to drink tea that evening. On this occasion, desiring to entertain his visitor and contribute to his amusement, he sought to secure them for both engagements.

"You *must* drink tea with us tonight," he insisted, "for we shall be quite alone. And tomorrow you must absolutely dine with us, for we shall be a large party."

Mrs. Jennings reinforced the invitation. "And who knows but you may raise a dance," she added. "That will tempt *you*, Miss Marianne."

"A dance!" Marianne cried. "Impossible! Who is to dance?"

"Who? Why yourselves, the Careys, and the Whitakers, to be sure! What! You thought nobody could dance because a certain person who shall remain nameless has departed!"

"I wish with all my soul," Sir John declared, "that Willoughby were among us again."

These words, coupled with Marianne's sudden blush, planted new suspicions in Edward's mind. "And who is Willoughby?" he asked in a low voice to Miss Dashwood, to whom he was seated.

She offered a brief reply, but Marianne's countenance was more communicative. Edward perceived enough to understand not only the meaning behind the others' remarks but also the nuances of Marianne's expressions that had previously puzzled him. When their visitors finally departed, he immediately approached her and whispered, "I have been guessing. Shall I tell you my guess?"

"What do you mean?"

"Shall I tell you?"

"Certainly."

"Well then, I guess that Mr. Willoughby hunts."

Marianne was surprised and confused, yet she could not help smiling at the quiet archness of his manner. After a moment's silence, she said, "Oh, Edward! How can you?—But the time will come, I hope... I am sure you will like him."

"I do not doubt it," he replied, somewhat astonished by her earnestness and warmth. Had he not assumed it was a jest intended for the general good of her acquaintance, founded on something or nothing between Mr. Willoughby and herself, he would not have ventured to mention it.
