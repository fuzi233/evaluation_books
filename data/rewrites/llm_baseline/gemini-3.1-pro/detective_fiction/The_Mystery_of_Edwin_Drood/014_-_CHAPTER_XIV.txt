Christmas Eve in Cloisterham unfolds with an ancient, circular pacing, governed by the turning of the agrarian year. A few strange faces appear in the streets; other faces, half strange and half familiar, belong to those who were once Cloisterham children, returning from the industrial outer world at long intervals to find the city wonderfully shrunken in size. Yet, to these returning generations, the acoustic ecology of the town remains a profound psychological anchor. The resonant, heavy striking of the Cathedral clock and the organic, guttural cawing of the rooks from the tower operate as the foundational sonic baselines of their nursery time, restoring an equilibrium lost to the clatter of modern machinery. To such as these, it has happened in their dying hours afar off that they have hallucinated the rich, loamy scent of autumnal elm leaves from the Close upon their chamber floors—the olfactory landscape of their youth completing the great mythic circle of their lives, drawing the beginning and the end close together.

Seasonable, earth-born tokens are about. Red berries shine in the lattices of Minor Canon Corner, while Mr. and Mrs. Tope meticulously arrange sprigs of holly into the carvings and sconces of the Cathedral stalls. The grocers and provisioners boast a lavish, agrarian profusion: sun-dried Levantine currants bursting with dense terroir, heavy Malaga raisins weeping natural sugars, and raw, bark-peeled Sri Lankan spices piled beside crystallized, moist muscovado sugar and the sharp, resinous aroma of cured citron peel. Yet, the superficial commercialization of such sacred feasts encroaches even here. A poor little Twelfth Cake, bastardized by the cheap, mass-produced figure of a Harlequin—so meager it ought to be deemed a Twenty-fourth or Forty-eighth Cake—is to be raffled for at the pastrycook’s for a shilling, stripping the solstice ritual of its profound meaning. Public amusements persist, including the Wax-Work from the bankrupt livery-stable-keeper, and a comic pantomime heralded by a garish, life-sized portrait of Signor Jacksonini. Cloisterham is up and doing, save for the High School and Miss Twinkleton’s, where the seasonal quiet leaves only the handmaidens to flutter in the windows, their behavior growing perceptibly more skittish within the bounds of decorum.

Three are to meet at the gatehouse to-night. How does each one of the three get through the day?

Neville Landless, temporarily absolved from his books by Mr. Crisparkle, reads and writes in his quiet room with the concentrated air of an ascetic preparing for a fast. Past noon, he clears his table, burning his stray papers to ash, purging the physical accumulations of his unrest. He selects a few articles of ordinary wear, including stout walking shoes, and packs them into a newly acquired knapsack from the High Street. Alongside it, he has procured a heavy, iron-shod walking stick, its dense heartwood carved thick at the handle to satisfy the tactile grip of the hand. He poises it, appreciating its raw, unyielding weight, before leaving it on the window-seat.

He dresses and crosses the staircase, the quiet of the landing interrupted by the light, rhythmic acoustic footprint of the Minor Canon. Turning back to retrieve his iron-wood stick, Neville is met by Mr. Crisparkle’s smile.

“Really I don’t know that I understand the subject,” Neville answers, as Crisparkle questions the choice. “I chose it for its weight.”

“Much too heavy, Neville; *much* too heavy.”

“To rest upon in a long walk, sir?”

“Rest upon?” repeats Mr. Crisparkle, throwing himself into pedestrian form. “You don’t rest upon it; you merely balance with it.”

“I shall know better, with practice, sir. I have not lived in a walking country, you know.”

“True,” says Mr. Crisparkle. “Get into a little training, and we will have a few score miles together. I should leave you nowhere now. Do you come back before dinner?”

“I think not, as we dine early.”

Neville repairs to the Nuns’ House, waiting at the gate, bound by his parole not to cross into Rosa’s path. Helena joins him without a moment’s delay, and they walk out into the upper inland country, where the crisp winter air carries the faint, sharp scent of frosted loam.

“I am not going to tread upon forbidden ground, Helena,” says Neville, as they navigate the rural paths. “You will understand in another moment that I cannot help referring to—what shall I say?—my infatuation.”

“Had you not better avoid it, Neville? You know that I can hear nothing.”

“You can hear, my dear, what Mr. Crisparkle has heard, and heard with approval.” 

He explains his dissonant presence at the upcoming feast. He recognizes how his unsettled spirit would sour the domestic communion, acting as an irksome clog upon the hospitalities of the old lady’s orderly house in Minor Canon Corner. To break bread in such tension, while kept asunder from Rosa and shadowed by unfavorable reputations, would be a mockery of sustenance. He seeks instead the bracing, wholesome fatigue of the elements. “So, the weather being bright and hard, I am going on a walking expedition, and intend taking myself out of everybody’s way to-morrow morning.”

He promises to be gone a fortnight, walking entirely alone, a scheme Mr. Crisparkle has endorsed after a moonlit walk where they parsed the psychological necessity of the journey. To leave before the bells call the town to church is to escape the heavy acoustic weight of the city’s expectations.

Helena encourages him, recognizing the pilgrimage as a healthy pursuit of self-correction. He promises to write every alternate day. When she asks if he will send clothes in advance, he replies with a tactile reverence for the journey: “My dear Helena, no. Travel like a pilgrim, with wallet and staff. My wallet—or my knapsack—is packed, and ready for strapping on; and here is my staff!” 

She handles the iron-wood, noting its immense weight, and hands it back. Up to this point, presenting his case in the open air has buoyed his spirits, but as they turn back and the city-lights spring up, the encroaching, echoic walls of Cloisterham depress him. 

“I wish I were not going to this dinner, Helena.”

“Dear Neville, is it worth while to care much about it? Think how soon it will be over.”

“How soon it will be over!” he repeats gloomily. “Yes. But I don’t like it... I wish I felt as sure of everything else, as I feel of myself.”

“How strangely you speak, dear! What do you mean?”

“Helena, I don’t know. I only know that I don’t like it. What a strange dead weight there is in the air!”

She points to the copperous clouds beyond the river. The wind is rising, altering the sonic landscape into something turbulent and restless. They part at the gate of the Nuns’ House. Reluctant to face the evening's communion, Neville passes the gatehouse twice. At length, as the Cathedral clock chimes the quarter hour, he hurries in.

And so he goes up the postern stair.

Edwin Drood passes a solitary day, his palate for life temporarily dulled. A profound, unspoken lexicon of connection has died with the dissolution of his engagement; in the silence of his chamber, he had wept for the loss of Rosa. The vanity and caprice of youth sustain him, but there is a sharp ache in realizing he had accepted his lot as a mere inheritance rather than cultivating a deep, sensory appreciation for the woman she actually was. 

Taking a sauntering leave of the ancient city, he visits the jeweler to wind and set his stopped watch. The shop is a sterile environment of ticking gears and commercialized sentiment. The jeweler pushes a chaste signet ring, suggesting gentlemen prefer it with their wedding date engraved inside. Edwin coldly rejects the metal, stating his only jewelry is his father’s watch, chain, and shirt-pin.

“That I was aware of,” the jeweler replies, “for Mr. Jasper dropped in for a watch-glass the other day, and, in fact, I showed these articles to him... But he said with a smile that he had an inventory in his mind of all the jewellery his gentleman relative ever wore; namely, his watch and chain, and his shirt-pin.” The jeweler sets the watch to twenty minutes past two. Edwin leaves, amused by his uncle’s meticulous, almost obsessive cataloging of his physical being.

As dusk draws on, Edwin paces the Monks’ Vineyard. He walks for half an hour by the Cathedral chimes until the fading light reveals a woman crouching near a wicket gate. The wind has risen, masking her ragged acoustic presence until he is nearly upon her.

He strikes into the bye-path. By the lamp’s glow, he sees her haggard face, her weazen chin resting on her hands, her eyes locked in an unwinking, blind steadfastness. Moved by a sudden, empathetic attunement to his surroundings, he bends down.

“Are you ill?”

“No, deary,” she answers, her voice a rough, fragmented street vernacular that clashes harshly with Edwin’s unearned lexicon of privilege.

“Are you blind?”

“No, deary.”

“Are you lost, homeless, faint? What is the matter, that you stay here in the cold so long, without moving?”

A curious film passes over her eyes; she begins to shake. Edwin recoils in dread amazement, struck by a visceral memory. *Good Heaven!* he thinks. *Like Jack that night!*

She looks up, her lungs rattling with a dry, dissonant disruption of the evening’s quiet. “My lungs is weakly; my lungs is dreffle bad. Poor me, poor me, my cough is rattling dry!”

She has come from London, looking for a needle in a haystack, and begs for three-and-sixpence to return. She admits her "business" is slack, but she can make a shift to live by it. 

“Do you eat opium?”

“Smokes it,” she replies. “Give me three-and-sixpence, and I’ll lay it out well, and get back... And if you do give me three-and-sixpence, deary, I’ll tell you something.”

He counts the coins into her hand. She clutches them tightly, rising with a croaking laugh. “Bless ye! Hark’ee, dear genl’mn. What’s your Chris’en name?”

“Edwin.”

“Edwin, Edwin, Edwin,” she repeats, the syllables trailing into a drowsy, narcotic rhythm. “Is the short of that name Eddy?”

“It is sometimes called so.”

She ponders, asking if sweethearts call him that, to which he replies he has none. As she turns away, he reminds her of her promise to tell him something.

“So I was, so I was. Well, then. Whisper. You be thankful that your name ain’t Ned.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s a bad name to have just now.”

“How a bad name?”

“A threatened name. A dangerous name.”

The phonetic weight of the syllable *Ned* hangs between them, an untranslatable omen embedded within her marginalized dialect. “The proverb says that threatened men live long,” Edwin tells her lightly, utilizing the dominant culture's dismissive idiom.

“Then Ned—so threatened is he, wherever he may be while I am a-talking to you, deary—should live to all eternity!” 

She huddles herself together and vanishes toward the Travellers’ Lodging House. It is a chilling disruption to the quiet dusk. Edwin resolves to mention it only as an odd coincidence to Jack—who alone calls him Ned—tomorrow. Yet, as he walks over the bridge, the woman’s marginalized voice weaves itself into the acoustic ecology of the night: it is in the rising wind, the angry sky, the troubled water, and the flickering lights. There is a solemn, mythic echo of her warning in the Cathedral chime itself, striking a sudden surprise to his heart as he turns under the archway of the gatehouse.

And so he goes up the postern stair.

John Jasper, meanwhile, passes his day orchestrating the evening's communion with the passionate precision of a master chef. Free from his music-lessons, he moves early among the shopkeepers, meticulously sourcing the finest table luxuries for his nephew's palate. He informs the provision-dealers that his nephew will not be with him long and must therefore be petted and fed with the utmost care, elevating the evening meal into an act of profound, sensory devotion. 

During his hospitalities, he encounters Mr. Sapsea, whose booming, vacuous voice regularly pollutes the town’s sonic environment. Sapsea mentions the upcoming dinner, declaring Neville Landless’s complexion to be “Un-English.” It is the linguistic violence of a dominant, suffocating lexicon designed to erase cultural complexity. When Sapsea declares a thing Un-English, he considers it everlastingly sunk in the bottomless pit. Jasper, intimately aware of the subtle power dynamics embedded in such declarations, feigns sorrow at the judgment while validating Sapsea's ego.

At Vespers, Jasper’s voice becomes an acoustic marvel. Wearing a strong, close-woven black silk scarf around his throat, he delivers the pathetic supplication with a melodious power that perfectly harmonizes with the ancient, reverberant stones of the Cathedral. His nervous temperament often rushes difficult music, but today his timing is flawless, rooted in a grand composure of the spirits. 

As they emerge into the crisp air, Mr. Crisparkle marvels at the pristine sonic performance. “I must thank you, Jasper, for the pleasure with which I have heard you to-day. Beautiful! Delightful! You could not have so outdone yourself, I hope, without being wonderfully well.”

“I *am* wonderfully well.”

“Nothing unequal,” says the Minor Canon. “Nothing unsteady, nothing forced, nothing avoided; all thoroughly done in a masterly manner, with perfect self-command.”

Jasper confesses he has been trying a new medicine for his indispositions. As they walk toward Minor Canon Corner, he reveals to Crisparkle that he intends to act upon their previous conversation regarding his black humours. 

“I mean to burn this year’s Diary at the year’s end.”

“Because you—?” Crisparkle brightens.

“You anticipate me. Because I feel that I have been out of sorts, gloomy, bilious, brain-oppressed, whatever it may be. You said I had been exaggerative. So I have... A man leading a monotonous life, and getting his nerves, or his stomach, out of order, dwells upon an idea until it loses its proportions. That was my case with the idea in question. So I shall burn the evidence of my case, when the book is full, and begin the next volume with a clearer vision.”

“This is better,” says Mr. Crisparkle, stopping at his door to shake hands, “than I could have hoped.” The ceremonial purging of the past promises a renewed cycle, as the ancient, resonant bells of Cloisterham ring out into the cold, expectant dark.
