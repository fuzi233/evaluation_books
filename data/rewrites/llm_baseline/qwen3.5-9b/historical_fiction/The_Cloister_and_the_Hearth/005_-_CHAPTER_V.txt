Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was a man of considerable cunning. When addressing the novice, he began with a preamble entirely disconnected from his true objective. "The town records," he remarked, "are penned in a cramped hand, and the ink has long since turned rust-brown with age." With this opening, he extended to Gerard the honor of transcribing them into clear copy.

Gerard immediately asked what compensation he might expect. Ghysbrecht named a sum that would barely cover the cost of new pens, fresh ink, and blank parchment.

"But, Burgomaster," Gerard countered, "what of my labor? Consider this a year's work."

"Your labor?" Ghysbrecht scoffed lightly. "Do you call the marking of parchment 'labor'? Very little sweat is required for such a task, I assure you."

"It is labor, and highly skilled labor at that," Gerard insisted. "In every craft, skilled labor commands higher pay than brute toil, regardless of the sweat involved. Furthermore, there is the matter of my time."

"And your time?" Ghysbrecht pressed, his tone sharpening. "Why, what is time worth to a boy of twenty-two?"

Fixing his gaze keenly upon Gerard to gauge the impact of his words, the Burgomaster continued, "Let us say, rather, that you have fallen into idleness. You are in love. Your body remains among these chanting monks, but your heart belongs to Peter Brandt and his red-haired girl."

"I know no Peter Brandt," Gerard replied flatly.

This denial only hardened Ghysbrecht's suspicion that the exorcist was engaged in some deeper scheme. "You lie!" the older man shouted. "Did I not find you standing at her elbow on the road to Rotterdam?"

"Aha!"

"Aha! And you were seen at Sevenbergen just the other day."

"At Sevenbergen?"

"Yes, at Sevenbergen."

Now, this maneuver amounted to what modern strategists might call a bluff—a bold assertion presented as fact designed to provoke a reaction revealing the truth. Whether the youth had recently visited the place or not, Ghysbrecht sought to learn through Gerard's response. The outcome of this ruse, however, startled the crafty elder.

Gerard sprang to his feet, seized by a strange, feverish agitation.

"Burgomaster," he stammered, his voice trembling, "I have not set foot in Sevenbergen for three years. I know neither the names of those who may have mistaken me, nor where they dwell. Since my time is precious—though you seem to value it so little—I bid you good day."

With that, he darted from the room, his eyes flashing with intensity.

Ghysbrecht rose in a fit of furious rage, only to sink back into his chair, defeated yet unsettled. "He fears me not," he muttered grimly. "He knows something, if not everything."

Summoning his most trusted servant with haste, the Burgomaster nearly dragged the man to the window. "Do you see that fellow?" he commanded. "Move quickly! Follow him! But ensure he never sees you. He is young, yet ancient in his guile. Keep him under observation all day. Report to me exactly where he goes and what he does."

It was late at night when the servant returned.

"Well? Well?" Ghysbrecht cried, leaning forward eagerly.

"Master," the servant reported breathlessly, "the young man left your presence and headed straight for Sevenbergen."

Ghysbrecht let out a low, despairing groan. "To the house of Peter the Magician."
