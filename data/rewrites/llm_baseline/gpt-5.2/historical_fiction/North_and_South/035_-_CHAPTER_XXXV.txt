Mr. Thornton remained, minute after minute, in Mr. Hale’s dim study, as if the very air within its walls had thickened into a kind of solemn bond. The house held its breath. Beyond the curtained windows Marlborough Street sent up its usual night-murmur—wheels scudding over the stones, a distant call, the faint clank and jangle that came with every passing vehicle—yet inside, those sounds seemed softened, as though muffled by the books, the heavy curtains, the close smell of ink and paper and extinguished fire.

Mr. Hale was glad of him—glad with that childlike, half-ashamed need which suffering gives to men who have been proud of standing alone. Again and again, with a weary hand lifted from the chair-arm as if to detain him, he said, softly, “Don’t go yet.” Mr. Thornton, who had come meaning only to fulfil a duty, found himself lingering, not for any hope of seeing Margaret, but because the presence of a mind so near the edge of life drew something earnest out of his own.

It was strange how Mr. Hale, who could speak to Margaret with tenderness and restraint, spoke to Thornton with a kind of unlocked candour. Thoughts long frost-bound broke and ran. Doubts, fears, those dark questionings that visit even good men when the lamp burns low and the body fails—he gave them voice here, because he dared not pain Margaret with them, or because he could not bear to see her quick sympathy recoil from the very idea of him entertaining such shadows. He could set them down, as one sets down a heavy tray at last, before Thornton.

Thornton answered little; but when he did, the words were like the sure placing of a hand on a trembling shoulder. He did not start away from uncertainty as if it were impiety; he spoke as one who had lived through that bleak country himself and knew where a line of light might be found. For all his daily hardness, all the worldly battle he waged with clenched teeth and stubborn will, there was a deeper, inarticulate religion in him than Mr. Hale had ever suspected—an inward allegiance not loudly professed, but tenacious as bone. They did not, as it chanced, speak so together again; yet this one hour, with its shared silence and its few grave sentences, made them peculiar to one another—more truly knit than any easy, public talk of sacred things could have done. When the inner chamber is flung open to all, where then is the Holy of Holies?

And while they spoke thus, Margaret lay on the floor of that very study, white and still as if she had already crossed beyond hearing.

She had sunk at last beneath the burden she had carried so long without complaint. It had been a weight of watchfulness, of secrecy, of fear—borne with patience until, all at once, the frail thread of her strength snapped, and she had groped for help and found none. The pain had drawn a pitiful contraction across her brows; the mouth which, a little while ago, had thrust itself forward in stubborn defiance was now relaxed and livid.

The first sign of returning life was a quiver at her lips, a mute effort at speech. The movement died; then came another, and she gathered her arms under her, pushing herself upright with a feebleness that was prideful in its very attempt. Her comb had fallen. The instinct that makes us, even in ruin, snatch at some poor semblance of order, drove her to look for it. She searched with hands that could scarcely obey her, and when the room swam she sat down, waited, and tried again. Her head drooped; her hands lay meekly one upon another for a moment, as if she were a penitent child.

She tried to recall the sharp details—the face, the questions, the tone—that had flung her into that deadly fright; but her mind, exhausted, could not reassemble them. Only two facts stood out like stones in a flood: Frederick had been in peril of pursuit and discovery, and she had lied to save him.

There was one comfort that came like a grudging crust to a starving creature—her falsehood had gained him time. If the inspector returned tomorrow, after she had the letter she longed for—after she knew Frederick was safe—she would bear the shame and stand in her bitter penance, she, Margaret Hale, acknowledging, if need be before a crowded room, that she had abased herself. But if he came again before any news arrived—if he returned, as he had half threatened, within a few hours—then she would tell the lie again. She did not know how she should shape the words now, after this awful pause wherein conscience had had leisure to speak; she did not know how she should keep her face from betraying her. Yet she would do it, for Frederick; she would do it to buy him time.

Dixon’s entrance roused her. She had only just let Mr. Thornton out, and the sound of the shutting door made Margaret’s heart jerk with a strange, sick longing to stop it—too late, too late.

Thornton had not gone ten steps into the street when an omnibus drew up near him, and a man got down, touched his hat, and came forward. It was the police-inspector. Thornton, who had once helped him into his first post, did not at first remember the face.

“My name is Watson—George Watson, sir—as you got—”

“Ah, yes. You are getting on famously, I hear.”

“Yes, sir. I ought to thank you. But it’s on a bit of business I’ve made so bold as to speak now. I believe you were the magistrate as took down the deposition of a poor man as died in the Infirmary last night.”

“Yes,” said Thornton. “A rambling statement—of little use, the clerk thought. He seemed a drunken fellow. There’s no doubt he came to his death by violence. One of my mother’s servants was engaged to him and is in great distress today. What of it?”

“Why, sir—his death is mixed up, oddly, with some one in the house you’ve just come out of. Mr. Hale’s, I believe.”

Thornton turned sharply; the inspector’s face swam into sudden importance.

“Yes. What about it?”

“Seems to me I’ve got a pretty distinct chain of evidence, inculpating a gentleman as was walking with Miss Hale that night at Outwood station—the man as struck, or pushed, Leonards off the platform. But the young lady denies she was there.”

“Miss Hale denies she was there!” Thornton repeated, and his voice changed on the words, as if some inward cord had been plucked too hard. “What evening? What time?”

“About six o’clock on Thursday the twenty-sixth.”

They walked for a little in silence, side by side. London’s sound-stream flowed around them—hoofs, wheels, a drunken shout, a vendor’s cry—but Thornton heard chiefly that one sentence: She denies she was there.

Watson spoke first again. “There’s like to be a coroner’s inquest, sir. I’ve a young man as was pretty positive—leastways at first. Since he’s heard of the young lady’s denial, he says he shouldn’t like to swear; but he’s pretty positive he saw Miss Hale at the station, walking about with a gentleman, not five minutes before the time. One of the porters saw a scuffle—took it for Leonards’ impudence, being the worse for drink—but it led to the fall as caused his death. And seeing you come out of the very house, sir, I thought I might ask if—well, disputed identity is awkward, and one don’t like to doubt the word of a respectable young woman unless one has proof.”

“And she denied having been at the station!” Thornton said again, low, as if he were repeating it to make it credible.

“Twice over, as clear as could be. I told her I should call again; but meeting you—coming back from questioning the young man—I thought I’d ask your advice, both as the magistrate as saw Leonards on his death-bed, and as the gentleman as got me my berth.”

“You were right,” said Thornton. “Don’t take any steps till you have seen me again.”

“The young lady will expect me, from what I said.”

“I only want to delay you an hour. It is three now. Come to my warehouse at four.”

“Very well, sir.”

They parted. Thornton went with rapid, stern strides, as if speed could outrun thought; yet the thoughts were already upon him, clinging, tearing. He reached his warehouse, forbade his clerks with a coldness that startled them to admit any interruption, and shut himself into his private room, turning the key as though he must lock out the whole world to wrestle with what was within.

Then he let the torture have its way.

How had he, even for an hour, lulled himself into that unsuspicious calm wherein Margaret’s image had come before him tearful and proud, and he had—weakly, he called it—pitied her and yearned toward her? How had he forgotten the savage jealousy with which the sight of her—at such an hour, in such a place, with some unknown man—had once struck him? How could one so pure have stooped? And even as he put the question, there came, like a flash of shame, an unworthy thought, an instant’s foul suggestion—gone as soon as born—yet it made him hate himself, because, while it lived, it carried with it that old, fierce attraction she had always held for him.

And then the lie. Not merely a denial, but an asserted falsehood, twice repeated—Margaret, whom he had believed incapable of bending! What fear of shame must bow her down so? For after all, in a case like this, Leonards’ drunken provocation might have been enough to justify an open statement; a scuffle at a station, a fall—there was nothing necessarily disgraceful. Yet she had chosen darkness. How creeping, how deadly must be the dread that could drive her to it!

He could almost pity her; and then contempt rose too, bitter and hot, and fought with pity until he could scarcely breathe.

What would be the end? If there was an inquest, if that wavering witness were pushed into an oath—what then? Her denial would be dragged into the light; she might be forced into perjury, or forced into confession. And suddenly, through all his tumult, one hard clear resolve lifted itself: there should be no inquest.

He would save her.

The decision, once made, steadied him like iron. The question of an inquest had hung, only the night before, trembling in the balance. The surgeon had spoken of internal disease—far advanced, sure to be fatal; death might have been hastened by the fall, or by the subsequent drinking and exposure. The medical evidence was uncertain—uncertain enough for a magistrate’s influence to turn the scale. If Thornton had known how Margaret would become entangled—if he had foreseen that she would stain herself by a lie—he might have saved her by a word.

Miss Hale might love another. She might be indifferent, even contemptuous, to him. He might despise her—he told himself he did. Yet the woman he had once loved should be kept from public shame. Shame it would be, to stand in a justice-room and pledge herself to a lie, or else to acknowledge, before strangers’ eyes, the reasons that made her choose concealment.

Very grey and stern he looked as he passed through the warehouse. The clerks, watching him, saw only that he went out and returned in about half an hour; but he brought back with him the look of a man who has laid his hand on a dangerous lever and pulled.

He wrote two lines on a slip of paper, sealed it, and gave it to one of the clerks.

“I appointed Watson—who was once a packer here, and went into the police—to call at four. A gentleman from Liverpool wishes to see me before he leaves town. Give this note to Watson when he calls.”

The note said:

“There will be no inquest. Medical evidence not sufficient to justify it. Take no further steps. I have not seen the coroner; but I will take the responsibility.”

Watson, when he received it, read it with relief.

“Well,” he thought, “it saves me an awkward job. None of the witnesses was certain of aught but the young woman. She was clear enough. The porter at the railroad had seen a scuffle—till it looked like he’d be wanted to swear, and then it might not have been a scuffle, only a bit of larking, and Leonards might have jumped off himself. He wouldn’t stick to anything. And Jennings, the grocer’s shopman—he wasn’t so bad; but I doubt I could have brought him to an oath after Miss Hale’s flat denial. It would have been trouble and no satisfaction. Now I must go tell them they won’t be wanted.”

So he went again to Mr. Hale’s that evening.

Within, the house held that close, domestic stillness which comes after fright—a stillness full of restrained movement, like a kitchen after a pan has boiled over and been hastily wiped, leaving only the smell of scalded milk and the memory of haste. Mr. Hale and Dixon would fain have persuaded Margaret to bed. They did not know why she refused so quietly, with so settled a look. Dixon knew part—but only part. Margaret had told her something of Frederick, enough to frighten and anger her into loyalty; yet she would not tell any human creature what she herself had said, nor did she reveal the fatal ending of Leonards’ fall.

Margaret lay on the sofa, white and worn. She spoke only when spoken to. When her father looked at her with anxious tenderness, she tried to smile back; but the wan lips sank into a sigh. Mr. Hale, miserable with uneasiness, at last persuaded her to go up to her room and prepare for sleep. It was past nine; Margaret began to think, dully, that the inspector would not come again that night.

She stood beside her father and held the back of his chair, clinging to it as if it were the one steady thing in a world that swayed.

“You will go to bed soon, papa, won’t you? Don’t sit up alone.”

What he answered she did not hear. Another sound—far smaller—filled her brain. The door-bell rang, low and thin; but to her fear it swelled until it drowned everything else.

She kissed her father and glided downstairs with a swiftness no one would have believed possible from the creature they had watched upon the sofa. Dixon was in the passage; Margaret put her aside.

“Don’t come. I will open the door. I know it is him—I can—I must manage it all myself.”

“As you please, miss!” Dixon replied, testily; then, softened by the sight of her, added, “But you’re not fit for it. You’re more dead than alive.”

“Am I?” said Margaret, turning, and Dixon saw the strange fire in her eyes, the flush in her cheeks, while her lips remained baked and livid.

Margaret opened the door. The inspector stood there. She preceded him to the study, set the candle on the table, and—because she must do something precise, something orderly, to keep herself from shaking apart—she snuffed it carefully before turning to face him.

“You are late,” she said. “Well?” She held her breath.

“I am sorry to have given any unnecessary trouble, ma’am,” Watson answered. “For, after all, they’ve given up all thoughts of holding an inquest. I’ve had other work, or I should have been here sooner.”

“Then it is ended,” said Margaret. “There is to be no further enquiry.”

“I believe I’ve got Mr. Thornton’s note about me,” said Watson, fumbling in his pocket-book.

“Mr. Thornton’s!” The name came from her lips like a startled cry.

“Yes, ma’am. He’s a magistrate. Ah—here it is.”

She took the paper. The candle’s flame seemed to flare and dazzle; the lines swam. Though she held it close, she could not read; yet she stared as if she were studying every stroke.

“I’m sure, ma’am,” Watson went on, “it’s a great weight off my mind; for the evidence was so uncertain, whether the man had received any blow at all. And if any question of identity came in, it complicated it, as I told Mr. Thornton—”

“Mr. Thornton!” she repeated again, faintly, as if the word itself hurt.

“I met him this morning, just as he was coming out of this house. He’s an old friend of mine, besides being the magistrate as saw Leonards last night. I made so bold as to tell him my difficulty.”

Margaret sighed—deep, involuntary. She wished the man would go; she was afraid of what she had heard and of what she might yet hear. But she forced herself to speak, stiffly, with manners like armour.

“Thank you for calling. It is very late—I dare say past ten. Oh! here is the note!” she added suddenly, understanding at last his outstretched hand. He was putting it away when she said, “It is cramped—dazzling writing. I cannot read it. Will you read it to me?”

He read Thornton’s words aloud.

When he had finished, she said, “Thank you. You told Mr. Thornton that I was not there?”

“Oh, of course, ma’am. I’m sorry now I acted upon information as seems to have been erroneous. At first the young man was so positive. Now he says he doubted all along, and hopes his mistake won’t have annoyed you so as to lose their shop your custom. Good night, ma’am.”

“Good night.”

She rang for Dixon to show him out. As Dixon returned along the passage, Margaret passed her swiftly, like a shadow slipping past a wall.

“It is all right,” she said, without looking at Dixon; and before any question could be flung after her, she was upstairs, inside her room, and had bolted the door.

She threw herself, dressed as she was, upon the bed. She was too exhausted to think. Time slid by—half an hour, more—until the cramped posture and the chill that follows great fatigue roused her numb body into a vague discomfort. Then thought began again, slow, reluctant, as a fire begins under damp wood.

First came the sense that Frederick’s immediate danger of pursuit was over; the strain had loosened. Next came a frantic desire to remember every syllable that concerned Mr. Thornton. When had Watson met him? What exactly had he said? What exactly had Thornton written? Margaret’s mind refused to move on until it had recited the note word for word, as if each article, each omitted “the,” were a crumb of proof that might change its meaning. But one conviction was clear, and it struck like a blow: Thornton had seen her near Outwood station that Thursday night; he had been told of her denial. She stood a liar in his eyes.

She was a liar.

No penitence rose in her mind then—no ordered prayer, no humble turning toward God. Only chaos and night, and in the midst one lurid fact burning her: in Mr. Thornton’s judgment she was degraded. She did not pause to argue excuse. To her it seemed beside the point; it could not alter what he knew. He had a right to judge her.

“Oh, Frederick! Frederick!” she cried into the pillow. “What have I not sacrificed for you!” Even sleep, when it came, forced her thoughts to travel the same circle, only with monstrous, exaggerated shapes of pain.

In the morning she woke with an idea that flashed bright as the day itself. Thornton had learned her falsehood before he went to the coroner; perhaps that knowledge had influenced him—perhaps he had interfered to spare her from being questioned again, to save her from repeating the denial. For an instant the thought held a strange sweetness; then she thrust it away with a sick, wilful recoil, like a child pushing back a hand that would comfort.

If it were so, she felt no gratitude. It would only prove how clearly he saw she was already disgraced, how contemptuously he must think of her, if he took such pains to spare her any further trial of truthfulness—which had failed so signally. She would have borne the whole. She would have perjured herself to save Frederick—far rather—than Mr. Thornton should have known enough to pity and intervene. What ill-fate had brought Thornton across Watson’s path? What made him the very magistrate called to receive Leonards’ dying deposition? What had Leonards said? How much had been intelligible? And might Thornton already know of the old accusation against Frederick, through their common friend Mr. Bell? If so, Thornton had striven to save the son who came, in defiance of the law, to a mother’s death-bed. Under that notion—under that alone—Margaret found she could be grateful, though even then not freely; for if his interference had been prompted by contempt, she could not bear it.

Had any one just cause to feel contempt for her?

Mr. Thornton—of all people—on whom she had looked down from imaginary heights until now.

She found herself suddenly, as in a dream, at his feet, and the fall distressed her strangely. She shrank from following the thought to its conclusion—from admitting, even to herself, how much she valued his respect. Whenever that confession rose at the end of her long avenues of reasoning, she turned aside and would not go down that path. She would not believe it.

It was later than she fancied. In last night’s agitation she had forgotten to wind her watch, and Mr. Hale had given special orders that she should not be disturbed by the usual morning call. At length the door opened cautiously, and Dixon put her head in. Seeing Margaret awake, she came forward with a letter.

“Here’s something to do you good, miss. A letter from Master Frederick.”

“Thank you, Dixon. How late it is!”

The words were languid. Dixon laid the letter on the counterpane. Margaret did not yet put out her hand.

“You want your breakfast, I’m sure,” Dixon went on. “I’ll bring it in a minute. Master’s got the tray all ready, I know.”

Margaret did not answer. Dixon left. Only then did Margaret take the letter, feeling she must be alone to break the seal—alone to let herself taste, without witness, whatever relief or sorrow lay within.

The first thing her eye caught was the date: two days earlier than its arrival. Frederick had written when he promised; their terror might have been spared. Her throat tightened as if she had swallowed something too dry; she read.

It was hasty, but it brought safety in every line. He had seen Mr. Henry Lennox, who at first shook his head, called it daring, warned him how dangerous it was to return to England with such an accusation hanging over him, backed by powerful influence. Yet, talking it through, Mr. Lennox acknowledged there might be a chance of acquittal—if credible witnesses could be found to prove Frederick’s statements. If so, it might be worth standing trial; if not, the risk was great. Mr. Lennox would examine the matter, would take pains.

“It struck me,” Frederick wrote, “that your introduction, little sister of mine, went a long way. Is it so? He made so many inquiries, I can assure you. He seemed a sharp, intelligent fellow, and in good practice too, to judge from the signs of business and the number of clerks about him. But these may be only lawyer’s dodges.

“I have just caught a packet on the point of sailing—I am off in five minutes. I may have to come back to England again on this business, so keep my visit secret. I shall send my father some rare old sherry, such as you cannot buy in England (such stuff as I’ve got in the bottle before me)! He needs something of the kind—my dear love to him—God bless him. I’m sure—here’s my cab.

“P.S.—What an escape that was! Take care you don’t breathe of my having been—not even to the Shaws.”

At the mention of the sherry—of Frederick with a bottle before him, tasting, judging, choosing some strong old comfort for the father he loved—Margaret’s mind gave a little helpless lurch toward ordinary life: a decanter catching lamplight; a cork drawn with that soft, satisfying pop; the deep, raisined scent of wine that has slept long in darkness; the warm burn in the throat that can, for a moment, make sorrow seem less sharp. Such homely images moved through her like ghosts of peace.

But peace did not come. Relief for Frederick’s immediate safety lay on one side of her heart; on the other lay the knowledge that Mr. Thornton had read her, judged her, acted for her—had taken responsibility—while she lay in the ashes of her own pride, scorched by the untranslatable misery of being one thing to herself and another in the eyes of the man whose opinion she would not yet confess she prized.
