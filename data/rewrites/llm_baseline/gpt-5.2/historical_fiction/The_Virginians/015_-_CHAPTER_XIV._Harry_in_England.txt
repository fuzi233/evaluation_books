When the young man began to tell his tale in the great house, the candles were already guttering in their sconces, and the night outside laid a cool hand upon the windows. From the avenue came the slow hiss of leaves in the wind, and farther off the faint, steady breath of the park—an owl’s single syllable, the answering bark of a dog, the soft hoof-stamp of horses in their stalls. Within, the air held that mingled scent which old rooms keep as their secret: beeswax from the polished tables, damp wool from cloaks shaken free of rain, a ghost of lavender in the linen, and—because the household had only lately left supper—roasted meat, nut-brown crust, and a wine’s last sweetness clinging in the warm.

Harry Warrington had no scholar’s art of oration; he did not spin his adventures as if he were selling them. Yet his voice was cordial, and it carried truth the way a well-made broth carries marrow—plain, nourishing, hard to resist. He spoke with the simplicity of a man who remembers by senses first: the tang of river-water on the tongue, the scorch of sun upon the neck, the smoke of a camp-fire caught in hair and coat, the bite of fear like pepper. It was enough.

Madam de Bernstein sat opposite him, her hands quiet in her lap. A woman who had long lived as if tenderness were a costly spice—used once, then locked away—found, to her own vexation, that the boy’s presence altered the climate of her mind. There were not many springs in the tract of her life; not many shaded places where one could stoop and drink. And now, as this friendly voice sounded in her ears, her heart beat with a strange ache, as though a door long barred had been opened and cold air rushed in.

She watched herself feel. Such a woman, trained to guard every motion of her soul as a housekeeper counts silver, could not help taking note of the quickened pulse, the heat rising to her cheeks when he leaned forward, the ridiculous happiness of simply hearing him say “my mother,” as if the words were a loaf broken and handed across a table. She doted on him—and, more than that, on the sensation of doting, so new that it seemed almost indecent.

“Wait,” whispered the dark mockery of Conscience within her. “Wait, Beatrix Esmond. You will weary. You always weary. When this fancy has passed, the boy may die, and you will not shed a tear. Or he will talk, and you will tire of his stories. Your lot is loneliness—lonely.”

Well, then—if life were a desert, there were still halting-places. For to-day there was shade and water. She would take what was given, and not poison it with tomorrow.

As he spoke of his mother, Madam de Bernstein smiled inwardly to hear, in his plain recounting, the old family lineaments—those moral features that outlast faces. Madam Esmond was jealous? Yes, Harry admitted with a blush that seemed to come from the same honest place as his words.

“She was fond of Colonel Washington?” Madam de Bernstein asked, her eyes narrowing with a curiosity that was half amusement, half old wound.

“She liked him,” Harry said carefully, as though measuring salt into a dish, “but only as a friend. I have heard her a hundred times vow she had no other feeling. And I”—here he hesitated, then gave the confession as a man hands over a debt—“I was once absurdly jealous of him myself.”

“Ah!” said Madam Beatrix, the syllable sharp with memory. “You will see that my half-sister will never forgive him. And you need not be surprised, sir, at women taking a fancy to men younger than themselves—for do not I dote upon you? and do not all these Castlewood people crevent with jealousy?”

However much jealousy may have pricked the Castlewood household, no open ill-will was permitted to show its face. Within two days in the ancestral house, Harry Warrington had become Cousin Harry upon every tongue. The very syllables were sweetened in the saying—by the young because youth is hospitable to novelty, by the middle-aged because civility is a craft, and by the old because kinship is a garment one can always bring out and shake fresh.

Especially when Madam de Bernstein was present, the Countess of Castlewood was all graciousness to her Virginian kinsman. She praised him lavishly before the Baroness, and in private found many opportunities—those delicate moments between the removal of a dish and the bringing of the next, or in the lull when music ended and chairs were drawn nearer—to express surprise and admiration.

“How charming the young Huron is!” she would say, her voice like sugared almonds. “How elegant his manners! And in such a distant province—how could the child have learned to be so polite?”

Madam de Bernstein received these remarks with a complacency as smooth as cream. It is a pleasing spectacle, to see ladies exchange compliments like flowers while their fingers feel for thorns. With what smiles they can strike; with what pretty phrases they can pour a drop of poison into a cup and hand it round as if it were cordial.

“You wonder, my dear Maria, that the boy should be so polite?” said Madame de Bernstein. “His mother was bred by two very perfect gentlefolks. Colonel Esmond had a grave courtesy, a grand manner, which I do not see among the gentlemen nowadays.”

“Eh, my dear,” returned the Countess, “we all of us praise our own time! My grandmamma used to declare there was nothing like Whitehall and Charles the Second.”

“My mother saw King James the Second’s court for a short while,” Madam de Bernstein persisted, “and though not court-educated—her father was a country clergyman—she was exquisitely well-bred. The Colonel, her second husband, had traveled, had learning, and had frequented the finest company in Europe. They could not go into their retreat and leave their manners behind them. The boy has them as his natural inheritance.”

“Nay, excuse me, my dear, for thinking you too partial,” the Countess said, still smiling as if she offered a dish for tasting. “She could not have been that perfection which filial fondness imagines. She left off liking her daughter—you have owned that she did—and I cannot fancy a complete woman who has a cold heart. No, no, my dear sister-in-law! Manners are requisite, no doubt—and for a country parson’s daughter your mamma was very well. I have seen many of the cloth who are very well. Mr. Sampson, our chaplain, is very well. Dr. Young is very well. Mr. Dodd is very well; but they have not the true air—as how should they? I protest, I beg pardon! I forgot my lord bishop, your ladyship’s first choice. But to be a complete woman one must have what you have, what I may say and bless Heaven for, I think I have—a good heart. Without the affections, all the world is vanity, my love! I only live, exist, eat, drink, rest, for my sweet, sweet children—for my wicked Willy, for my self-willed Fanny, dear naughty loves!”

As she spoke she kissed the bracelets on her arms, each bearing a miniature likeness, the enamel faces blinking in candlelight as if alive. Two Italian greyhounds—Mimi and Fanchon—trembling as if made of quicksilver, sprang into her lap and licked her hands, careful of her cheeks where rouge lay thick as a mask.

“Yes, Mimi! yes, Fanchon!” she cooed. “If they were to die—or you were to die—your poor mistress would die too! For nothing do I bless Heaven so much, though it puts me to excruciating torture very often, as for having endowed me with sensibility and a feeling heart!”

“You are full of feeling, dear Anna,” said the Baroness smoothly. “You are celebrated for your sensibility. You must give a little of it to our American nephew—cousin—I scarce know his relationship.”

“Nay,” replied the Countess, “I am here but as a guest in Castlewood now. The house is my Lord Castlewood’s, not mine—or his lordship’s whenever he shall choose to claim it. What can I do for the young Virginian that has not been done? He is charming. Are we even jealous of him, my dear? And though we see what a fancy the Baroness de Bernstein has taken for him, do your ladyship’s nephews and nieces—your real nephews and nieces—cry out? My poor children might be mortified, for indeed in a few hours the charming young man has made as much way as my poor things have been able to do in all their lives; but are they angry? Willy hath taken him out to ride. This morning, was not Maria playing the harpsichord whilst my Fanny taught him the minuet? ’Twas a charming young group, I assure you, and it brought tears into my eyes to look at the young creatures. Poor lad! We are as fond of him as you are, dear Baroness!”

Now, Madam de Bernstein—through her own ears, or through a servant’s careful listening—had heard a different music beneath that pretty speech.

The scene the Countess described had indeed taken place. In a room where the woodwork held the smell of varnish and old smoke, Lady Maria sat at the harpsichord. The instrument’s tone, bright and percussive, laid its little iron coins of sound upon the air; outside, the household went on—footmen murmuring in corridors, a distant clatter of a tray in the pantry, the faint tick of a clock like a patient tongue rehearsing time. Lady Fanny, flushed with the pleasure of being admired, was teaching Harry the steps of a minuet. He was earnest, as if the dance were another sort of drill; she laughed and corrected him, her slipper whispering against the floorboards as she turned.

Into this entered the Countess with her son William, who came in his boots from the kennel, bringing with him a gust of cold air and the sharper scents of leather, damp earth, and dogs.

“Bravi, bravi! Oh, charming!” cried my Lady, clapping her hands, nodding one of her best smiles toward Harry. And then—like the snap beneath a silk ribbon—she darted a look at Fanny which my Lady Fanny perfectly understood. Perhaps Lady Maria understood it too, for her fingers struck the keys with redoubled energy; the music grew louder, more insistent, as if it could drown what eyes were saying.

“Infernal young Choctaw!” muttered Mr. William, sour as ale left standing. “Is he teaching Fanny the war-dance? and is Fan going to try her tricks upon him now?”

That was what the Countess’s look asked: Are you going to try your tricks upon him now?

Fanny made Harry a curtsey, too low, too quick. He blushed like a boy caught stealing sugar, and both stopped dancing, disconcerted. Lady Maria rose from the harpsichord with a stiffness that made her silk rustle like dry leaves, and walked away.

“Nay, go on dancing, young people!” the Countess said at once, honey returning to her voice. “Don’t let me spoil sport. Let me play for you.”

She sat at the harpsichord and took over the tune herself. Her touch was practiced; the little notes rang as clean as knives tapped together.

“I—I don’t know how to dance,” Harry said, hanging his head, his blush deeper than any carmine in the room.

“And Fanny was teaching you? Go on teaching him, dearest Fanny!” said the Countess, with an encouragement that sounded oddly like command.

“Go on, do,” Mr. William added, the words dragged from him with a sidelong growl.

“I—I had rather not show off my awkwardness in company,” Harry said, and by the end of the sentence he had steadied himself, as a man does who has tasted mockery and decides not to swallow it. “When I know how to dance a minuet, be sure I will ask my cousin to walk one with me.”

“That will be very soon, dear Cousin Warrington, I am certain,” the Countess replied, her smile bright enough to blind.

“What game is she hunting now?” Mr. William asked himself, and could not answer; for though he knew the noises of hounds and horses and the honest uproar of a tavern, he could not always read the softer, more dangerous soundscape of a woman’s schemes—the hush before a word, the sudden sweetness of a tone, the way a name could be spoken like a net cast in water.

The Countess, fondly calling her daughter to her elbow, left the room with her. The harpsichord’s last notes died behind them; in the tapestried passage their footsteps were muffled, but not their voices.

No sooner were they away from company than my Lady’s blandness fell off as if it had been a cloak.

“You booby!” she began to her adored Fanny, her whisper fierce. “You double idiot! What are you going to do with the Huron? You don’t want to marry a creature like that and be a squaw in a wigwam?”

“Don’t, mamma!” gasped Lady Fanny; for her mother’s fingers were pinching her arm hard, leaving bruises that would flower by morning.

“I am sure our cousin is very well,” Fanny whimpered, half from pain, half from anger, “and you said so yourself.”

“Very well! Yes—and heir to a swamp, a negro, a log-cabin, and a barrel of tobacco!” The Countess’s contempt snapped like a crust cracking under a knife. “My Lady Frances Esmond, do you remember what your ladyship’s rank is, and what your name is, and who was your ladyship’s mother, when at three days’ acquaintance you commence dancing—a pretty dance indeed—with this brat out of Virginia?”

“Mr. Warrington is our cousin,” Fanny pleaded, clinging to the word as if kinship were protection.

“A creature come from nobody knows where is not your cousin! How do we know he is your cousin? He may be a valet who has taken his master’s portmanteau and run away in his postchaise.”

“But Madame de Bernstein says he is our cousin,” Fanny cried, “and he is the image of the Esmonds.”

“Madame de Bernstein has her likes and dislikes,” the Countess returned, each phrase clipped, like herbs chopped fine. “Takes up people and forgets people; and she chooses to profess a mighty fancy for this young man. Because she likes him to-day, is that any reason she should like him to-morrow? Before company, and in your aunt’s presence, your ladyship will be as civil as necessary; but in private, I forbid you to see him or encourage him.”

“I don’t care, madam, whether your ladyship forbids me or not!” Fanny burst out, the rebellion rising in her as quick as ferment.

“Very good, Fanny. Then I speak to my lord, and we return to Kensington. If I cannot bring you to reason, your brother will.”

At that point the passage swallowed the rest—either the voices fell, or the listener withdrew. It was only in after days that Madam de Bernstein told Harry part of what she had learned.

For the present, Harry saw only surfaces, and surfaces were smiling. His kinsfolk received him not unkindly. Lady Castlewood was perfectly civil. The young ladies were pleasant, pleased to be amused. My Lord Castlewood—cold, haughty, carved as if from pale stone—was not more reserved toward Harry than toward any of the rest of his household; his silence was distributed with justice. Mr. William was ready to drink with him, to ride with him, to go to races, to play cards; and that coarse, companionable noise—glasses clinking, boots on gravel, laughter too loud—seemed to Harry a sort of welcome, the way men show it when they will not show it softly.

When Harry proposed to go away, they pressed him, one and all, to stay. Madam de Bernstein said nothing of how such eagerness had its own hungers; she did not tell him what schemes he served, what plans he disturbed, whose temper he pricked raw by merely sitting at their table and taking their bread.

So he believed, in his honest way, that he was welcome because they were his blood. He never thought that enemies might share a decanter and offer the hand as if it were friendship; that one might drink from a cup offered with a smile and not taste, beneath the wine’s fruit and oak, the faint bitterness of design. He heard the house’s music—harpsichord, laughter, the domestic clatter of a great family at ease—and did not yet know how often such sound is arranged to cover silence, and how much can be said in England without any word at all.
