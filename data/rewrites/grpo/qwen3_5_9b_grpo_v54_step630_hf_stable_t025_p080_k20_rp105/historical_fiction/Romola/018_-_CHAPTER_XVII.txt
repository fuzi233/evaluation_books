Under the Loggia.

The upper loggia of Bardo's house rose like a crown above the surrounding structures, forming a sheltered gallery around its quadrangular walls. On the street-facing side, columns bore the weight of the roof, while the rear opened into a series of arched windows, offering a view over the chaotic jumble of poorly built dwellings toward the hill of Bogoli. It was here, near one of these arches close to the entrance, that Tito waited. The sunlight slanted across him with a sickening intensity, mingling with the ruin of his hopes. He had never truly believed that Romola's passion could overcome the repulsion born of his secret; he lacked the vanity to imagine her love rooted in ignorance rather than truth. Yet, as he pictured her approaching in her radiant beauty, softened by those soft hazel eyes, a selfish wish took hold: he wished she were lower, merely mortal enough to allow him to clasp and kiss her before they parted. He had known only fleeting glances and brief touches from her, a hunger for solitude with her that had grown insatiable. Now, they were to be alone, yet she stood there, seemingly inexorably distant.

Then the door moved. Romola appeared. It struck Tito not as a sight but as a flash of lightning; he felt the glory haloing her head, the tearful appeal in her gaze, and heard the cry of love on her lips—"Tito!"—before she was already in his arms, sobbing against him.

How desperately Romola had longed through the sleepless nights to see that bright face! The new specter of death, the bewildering doubt stirred by tales of a life alien to her understanding, and the haunting vision she seemed to inhabit as her own dream had sharpened her awareness. It was Tito who had first brought the warm stream of hope into her life, turning away the keen edge of pain regarding her brother. She would tell him everything; there was no one else. Restrained all morning before her father, the dam of her emotions finally broke. Proud and controlled to the outside world, Romola was as simple and unreserved as a child in her love for Tito. Content with days of silent glances, she now felt the urgent need to cling to him, with no thought hindering her.

"My Romola! my goddess!" Tito murmured, clasping her gently and kissing the thick golden ripples of her hair. He was in paradise; disgrace, shame, and parting held no fear. This happiness was too potent to be marred by the knowledge that she was deceived; indeed, he rejoiced in her delusion, for concealment had been wisdom. He could only regret his needless dread, or perhaps, that the dread had been worth suffering for the sake of this sudden rapture.

The sobbing ceased, and Romola raised her head. They stood gazing at one another with the sweet wonder of young love—she with her long white hands in his dark-brown curls, he with his fingers bathed in streaming gold. Each was beautiful to the other, experiencing that undisturbed mutual consciousness for the first time. A new, cold sadness pressed on Romola's heart, making her linger in the silent soothing of their nearness, while Tito dared not press his lips to hers, for that would be a change.

"Tito," she said finally, "it has been altogether painful, but I must tell you everything. Your strength will help me to resist impressions that reason cannot shake."

"I know, Romola—I know he is dead," Tito replied. His long, lustrous eyes revealed nothing of the many wishes that would have ended that life long ago if mere desire held such potency. Romola read her own pure thoughts in their dark depths as one reads letters in a happy dream.

"So changed, Tito! It pierced me to think it was Dino. And so strangely hard: not a word to my father, nothing but a vision he wanted to tell me. Yet it was piteous—the struggling breath, the eyes that seemed to look toward the crucifix and yet not see it. I shall never forget it; it seems as if it will stand between me and everything I shall ever look at."

Her heart swelled again, forcing her to pause, but the need to disburden her mind urged her to repress the rising anguish. When she spoke again, her thoughts had traveled slightly.

"It was strange, Tito. The vision was about our marriage, and yet he knew nothing of you."

"What was it, my Romola? Sit down and tell me," Tito said, leading her to the nearby bench. A fear had crossed him that the vision might somehow relate to Baldassarre, prompting this sudden shift in position.

Romola recounted everything from her entry into San Marco, omitting not a single word of her brother's speech, which had burned itself into her memory. But at the end of the vision, she paused; the rest remained too vivid to utter, and she sat staring into the distance, almost unaware of Tito's presence. His mind was now at ease; the vague vision had passed over him like white mist, leaving no mark. He remained silent, waiting for her to continue.

"I took it," she continued, as if he had read her thoughts. "I took the crucifix; it is down below in my bedroom."

"And now, my Romola," Tito entreated, "banish these ghastly thoughts. The vision was an ordinary monkish hallucination, bred of fasting and fanatical ideas. It surely holds no weight for you."

"No, Tito; no. But poor Dino, *he* believed it was a divine message. It is strange," she mused, "this life of men possessed by fervid beliefs that seem like madness to others. Dino was not a vulgar fanatic; and that Fra Girolamo—his very voice seems to have penetrated me with a sense that there is some truth in what moves them: some truth of which I know nothing."

"It was only because your feelings were highly wrought, my Romola. Your brother's state of mind was no more than a form of that theosophy which has been the common disease of excitable dreamers in all ages; the same ideas your father's old antagonist, Marsilio Ficino, pores over in the New Platonists. Only your brother's passionate nature drove him to act out what others merely write and talk about. As for Fra Girolamo, he is simply a narrow-minded monk with a gift for preaching and instilling terror. Any words or voice would have shaken you at that moment. When your mind has rested, you will judge such things as you have always done."

"Not about poor Dino," Romola said. "I was angry with him; my heart seemed to close against him while he spoke, but since then I have thought less of my own mind and more of his. Oh, Tito! it was very piteous to see his young life ending thus. That yearning look at the crucifix when he gasped for breath—I can never forget it. Last night I stared at the crucifix for a long while, trying to see how it could help him, until by the lamplight it seemed to me as if the suffering face shed pity."

"My Romola, promise me to resist such thoughts; they are fit for sickly nuns, not for my golden-tressed Aurora, who looks made to scatter all such twilight fantasies. Try not to think of them now; we shall not long be alone together."

His final words were uttered in a tone of tender beseeching, and he turned her face toward him with a gentle touch of his right hand. Romola had been gazing absently at the arched opening, failing to see the distant hill, having remained mentally in the chapter house, contemplating the pale images of sorrow and death.

Tito's touch and voice recalled her. Now, in the warm sunlight, she perceived that rich dark beauty that seemed to gather around it all images of joy—purple vines festooned between elms, strong corn perfecting itself under the vibrating heat, bright winged creatures hurrying among flowers, round limbs beating the earth in gladness with cymbals aloft, light melodies chanted to the thrilling rhythm of strings—all signs of Nature reveling in her force. It was a strange, bewildering transition from those pale images of sorrow to this bright youthfulness, like a sun-god knowing nothing of night. What thought could reconcile the worn anguish in her brother's face, that straining after something invisible, with this satisfied strength and beauty, making it intelligible that they belonged to the same world? Or was there never any reconciliation, only a blind worship of clashing deities, first in mad joy and then in wailing? For the first time, Romola felt this questioning need like a sudden uneasy dizziness, a want of something to grasp. It was an experience hardly longer than a sigh, for the eager theorizing of ages is compressed, as in a seed, into the momentary want of a single mind. But there was no answer to meet the need, and it vanished before the returning rush of young sympathy with the glad, loving beauty beaming upon her in new radiance, like the dawn after one has looked away from it to the grey west.

"Your mind lingers apart from our love, my Romola," Tito said with a soft, reproachful murmur. "It seems a forgotten thing to you."

She looked at the beseeching eyes in silence until the sadness melted from her own.

"My joy!" she said in her full, clear voice.

"Do you really care for me enough, then, to banish those chill fancies, or shall you always be suspecting me as the Great Tempter?" Tito asked, smiling brightly.

"How should I not care for you more than for everything else? Everything I had felt before in all my life—about my father, about my loneliness—was a preparation to love you. You would laugh at me, Tito, if you knew what sort of man I used to think I should marry—some scholar with deep lines in his face, like Alamanno Rinuccini, with rather grey hair, who would agree with my father in taking the side of the Aristotelians and be willing to live with him. I used to think about the love read of in poets, but I never dreamed anything like that could happen to me here in Florence in our old library. And then *you* came, Tito, and were so much to my father, and I began to believe that life could be happy for me too."

"My goddess! is there any woman like you?" Tito exclaimed, admiring the blended majesty and simplicity in her.

"But, dearest," he continued, somewhat timidly, "if you minded more about our marriage, you would persuade your father and Messer Bernardo not to think of any more delays. But you seem not to mind."

"Yes, Tito, I will, I do mind. But I am sure my godfather will urge more delay now because of Dino's death. He has never agreed with my father about disowning Dino, and you know he has always said we ought to wait until you have been at least a year in Florence. Do not think hardly of my godfather. I know he is prejudiced and narrow, yet he is very noble. He has often said it is folly in my father to want to keep his library apart so it may bear his name; yet he would try to carry out my father's wish. That seems to me very great and noble—that power of respecting a feeling which he does not share or understand."

"I have no rancour against Messer Bernardo for thinking you too precious for me, my Romola," Tito said, and that was true. "But your father, then, knows of his son's death?"

"Yes, I told him—I could not help it. I told him where I had been and that I had seen Dino die, but nothing else; and he has commanded me not to speak of it again. But he has been very silent this morning, and has had those restless movements which always go to my heart; they look as if he were trying to get outside the prison of his blindness. Let us go to him now. I had persuaded him to try to sleep, because he sleeps little at night. Your voice will soothe him, Tito: it always does."

"And not one kiss? I have not had one," Tito said in his gentle, reproachful tone, which gave him an air of dependence very charming in a creature with such rare gifts that seemed to excuse presumption.

A sweet pink blush spread quickly over Romola's face and neck as she bent toward him. It seemed impossible that their kisses could ever become common things.

"Let us walk once round the loggia," Romola said, "before we go down."

"There is something grim and grave to me always about Florence," Tito remarked as they paused before the house, looking over the opposite roofs to the other side of the river. "Even in its merriment there is something shrill and hard—biting rather than gay. I wish we lived in Southern Italy, where thought is broken, not by weariness, but by delicious languors such as never seem to come over the 'ingenia acerrima Florentina.' I should like to see you under that southern sun, lying among the flowers, subdued into mere enjoyment, while I bent over you and touched the lute and sang to you some little unconscious strain that seemed all one with the light and the warmth. You have never known that happiness of the nymphs, my Romola."

"No; but I have dreamed of it often since you came. I am very thirsty for a deep draught of joy—for a life all bright like you. But we will not think of it now, Tito; it seems to me as if there would always be pale sad faces among the flowers, and eyes that look in vain. Let us go."
