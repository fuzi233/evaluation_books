The morning air near Cloisterham Weir was thin and brittle, easily shattered by the impact of Septimus Crisparkle's forehead. He broke the surface tension of the ice, a trivial act that served as a necessary shock to his system. Now indoors, he sought further stimulation, engaging in a rigorous exercise of self-defense against his own reflection. The glass offered a pristine image: a man feinting with calculated grace, striking with linear force, his face radiating an innocence that seemed almost artificial, his gloves glowing with a soft-hearted benevolence that belied the violence of the motion.

Mrs. Crisparkle, his mother and widow, descended the stairs just as the Reverend paused. She waited for the tea urn, her presence marking the transition from solitary exertion to domestic routine. Septimus halted his assault on the mirror, gently cupping her face between his padded fists. He kissed her cheek with a tenderness that contrasted sharply with the previous ferocity, then resumed his counter-attacks, left hook followed by right cross, executing the movements with tremendous vigor.

"You'll break something eventually, Sept," Mrs. Crisparkle observed, watching him. "Or perhaps a blood vessel."

"Nothing of the sort, Mother," he replied, breathless. "Here is the wind. Observe!"

He delivered a final, severe blow, narrowly missing her cap, which fluttered like a banner of lavender and cherry silk before settling undisturbed. With magnanimous restraint, he released his opponent, stowing his gloves away and adopting a pose of contemplative stillness until the servant arrived with the urn. Once the breakfast preparations were complete, the two stood alone once more. It was a scene of quiet devotion: the old lady reciting the Lord's Prayer aloud, her voice steady, while her son, a Minor Canon nearing forty, bowed his head to listen. He had stood thus, listening to the same words from the same lips, when he was barely four years old.

There is a peculiar beauty in an elderly woman whose eyes remain bright, whose figure retains its compact integrity, whose expression balances cheerfulness with calm, and whose attire resembles the porcelain shepherdess figurines adorning a mantelpiece—dainty, individually chosen, perfectly molded to her form. Septimus often mused on this perfection as he sat opposite his widowed mother. Her thoughts, distilled, revolved around a single phrase: "My Sept!"

Minor Canon Corner, situated in the cathedral's shadow, possessed a unique atmosphere. The cawing of rooks, the echo of passing feet, the tolling of bells, and the rolling of organs did not disturb the silence here; rather, they deepened it, rendering the place quieter than absolute void. Centuries of swaggering soldiers, suffering serfs, and powerful monks had once dominated this spot, but they were all gone. Their absence left behind a blessed tranquility, a serene romanticism born of a sorrowful tale fully concluded—a state of mind conducive to pity and forbearance.

Surrounding them were red-brick walls softened by time, ivy rooted in strength, latticed windows, panelled rooms with oak beams, and stone-walled gardens where fruit still ripened on ancient trees. This was the setting for their breakfast.

"And what does the letter say?" Septimus asked, displaying a vigorous appetite.

His mother placed the folded paper on the cloth and handed it to him. She prided herself on her clear vision, requiring no spectacles. Septimus, equally proud of her clarity and eager to maximize her enjoyment of it, had devised a pretense: he claimed he could not read without glasses. Thus, he produced a pair of lenses of grave and prodigious dimensions. They obstructed his view of his food and his nose alike, turning his eyes into a confused combination of microscope and telescope.

"It is from Mr. Honeythunder, naturally," his mother declared, crossing her arms.

"Naturally," Septimus agreed. He began to read, stumbling over the salutation. "'Haven of Philanthropy... I write in the—'"

"In the chair," his mother supplied.

"Why, what should he write in?" Septimus asked, removing his spectacles to see her face clearly, exclaiming in confusion.

"Bless you, Sept," she corrected gently. "You miss the context. Return it to me."

Relieved to remove the heavy lenses, which always irritated his eyes, Septimus complied, murmuring that his reading vision deteriorated daily. His mother continued, her voice precise. "'I write from the chair, to which I shall probably be confined for some hours.' Septimus glanced at the row of chairs along the wall, his expression a mixture of protest and appeal. 'We have,' she emphasized, 'a meeting of our Convened Chief Composite Committee of Central and District Philanthropists... and it is their unanimous pleasure that I take the chair.'"

Septimus breathed easier. "If he means that, let him."

"'Not to lose a day's post, I take the opportunity of a long report being read, denouncing a public miscreant—'"

"It is extraordinary," Septimus interrupted, lowering his cutlery to rub his ear in irritation, "that these Philanthropists are perpetually denouncing someone. And equally extraordinary that they are so flush with miscreants!"

"'Denouncing a public miscreant,'" his mother resumed, "'to get our little affair of business off my mind. I have spoken with my two wards, Neville and Helena Landless, regarding their defective education. They agree to the proposed plan; as I should have ensured, regardless of their willingness.'"

"And another extraordinary fact," Septimus noted in the same tone, "is that these philanthropists seize their fellow creatures by the scruff of the neck and bump them onto the path of peace. Excuse me, Mother."

"'Therefore, dear Madam, you will please prepare your son, the Rev. Mr. Septimus, to expect Neville as an inmate to be read with, on Monday next. On the same day Helena will accompany him to Cloisterham, to take up her quarters at the Nuns' House... The terms are as stated in writing... With compliments to the Rev. Mr. Septimus, I am, Dear Madam, Your affectionate brother (In Philanthropy), LUKE HONEYTHUNDER.'"

"Well, Mother," Septimus said after another pause to massage his ear, "we must attempt it. There is no doubt we have room, time, and inclination. I confess I feel relieved he is not Mr. Honeythunder himself. Though that seems wretchedly prejudiced, does it not? I have never seen him. Is he a large man, Mother?"

"I believe he is," she hesitated, "though his voice sounds larger."

"Larger than himself?"

"Larger than anyone."

"Hah!" Septimus laughed, finishing his breakfast as if the flavor of the Souchong tea and the ham and toast had suddenly faded.

Mrs. Crisparkle's sister, a piece of Dresden china matched perfectly to her, was the childless wife of a London clergyman. Mr. Honeythunder, publicly known as Professor of Philanthropy, had met Mrs. Crisparkle during her recent annual visit, following a charitable event where orphaned children were fed plum buns. These were the only known antecedents of the incoming students.

"I am sure you agree, Mother," Septimus reasoned, "that the priority is to put these young people at ease. Disinterest is impossible; we cannot be comfortable unless they are. Jasper's nephew is currently here; like attracts to like, and youth to youth. He is a cordial young man. We shall invite him to dinner to meet the siblings. That makes three. We cannot ask him without asking Jasper. That makes four. Adding Miss Twinkleton and the future fairy bride brings us to six. Including ourselves, that is eight. Would eight guests at a friendly dinner trouble you, Mother?"

"Nine would, Sept," she replied, appearing nervous.

"My dear Mother, I specified eight."

"The exact capacity of the table and the room."

Thus settled, Septimus visited Miss Twinkleton to arrange Helena Landless's stay at the Nuns' House, securing the remaining invitations. Miss Twinkleton glanced at her globes, regretting their exclusion from society, but ultimately accepted their confinement. Instructions were sent to the Philanthropist for the timely arrival of Neville and Helena, and soon the scent of soup filled the air of Minor Canon Corner.

Cloisterham lacked railways; Mr. Sapsea insisted there never would be. Yet, miraculously, express trains now whirred past without stopping, casting dust against its insignificance. A remote fragment of the main line threatened the money market and the constitution, forcing traffic to sneak in via a back stableway marked "Beware of the Dog."

To this ignominious entrance approached Septimus, waiting for a short, squat omnibus burdened with luggage like an elephant carrying too much castle. A large passenger sat on the box seat, elbows squared, hands on knees, compressing the driver into a tiny space, glaring with a stern face.

"Is this Cloisterham?" the passenger demanded in a booming voice.

"Yes," the driver replied, rubbing his sore muscles. "And I am glad to see it."

"Tell your master to widen the box-seat," the passenger commanded. "He is morally, and legally under penalty, bound to provide for the comfort of his fellow-man."

The driver patted his ribs anxiously. "Have I sat upon you?"

"You have," the driver admitted reluctantly.
