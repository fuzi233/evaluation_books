Mr. Home remained for two days, a somber figure who refused to venture outdoors. He spent his hours by the fire, alternating between silence and engaging in Mrs. Bretton's conversation—a dialogue suited to his melancholy temperament, neither overly sympathetic nor unkind, grounded in sense and tempered with a gentle, maternal warmth appropriate for a woman of his seniority.

Paulina, meanwhile, existed in a state of joyful muteness, her energy focused entirely on observation. Her father frequently hoisted her onto his knee, where she sat until she imagined him growing restless, at which point she would declare, "Papa, put me down; I shall tire you with my weight." With a heavy, contented slide, she descended to the rug, positioning herself at his feet amidst her white work-box and scarlet-speckled handkerchief. This handkerchief, destined to be a keepsake for her departing father, demanded constant attention from the seamstress, whose industrious pace yielded about twenty stitches in thirty minutes.

The evening brought back Graham to the household, restoring a spark of animation that the nature of his interactions with Miss Paulina could not easily dim. A certain haughty distance marked their early encounters following the slight Paulina suffered on Graham's first arrival. Her standard response to his address was a curt, "I can't attend to you; I have other things to think about." When pressed to specify these matters, she simply replied, "Business."

Graham attempted to capture her attention by displaying the treasures of his desk: colorful seals, bright wax, penknives, and a collection of engravings, including a vivid etching of a child playing with a Blenheim spaniel. Though she initially eyed these objects with furtive curiosity, pride ultimately restrained her. An etching fluttered to the floor, drawing her gaze. "Pretty little dog!" she exclaimed, delighted.

Graham, seizing the moment, offered it to her. "Well—you may have it," he said. Yet Paulina hesitated. To accept would be to compromise her dignity. She placed the picture down and turned away. "You won't have it, then, Polly?" he persisted. "I would rather not, thank you."

"Shall I tell you what I will do with the picture if you refuse it?" he threatened lightly. She listened, half-turned. "Cut it into strips for lighting the taper." "No!" she protested. "But I shall." "Please—don't."

Graham, unmoved by her pleading, produced scissors from his mother's basket. "Here goes!" he announced, making a menacing flourish at the image of the dog and child. "Right through Fido's head, and splitting little Harry's nose."

"No! _No!_ NO!"

"Then come to me. Come quickly, or it is done."

Hesitating briefly, she complied. "Now, will you have it?" he asked. "Please." "But I shall want payment." "How much?" "A kiss." "Give the picture first into my hand."

As she handed it over, Paulina looked momentarily faithless. Graham accepted it, and she promptly fled to her father, seeking refuge on his knee. Graham rose in mock wrath, following her. Buried in Mr. Home's waistcoat, she cried, "Papa—papa—send him away!" "I'll not be sent away," Graham retorted.

Face averted, she extended her hand to ward him off. "Then, I shall kiss the hand," he teased. At that instant, it transformed into a miniature fist, delivering a sharp pinch. Graham, displaying a wit equal to his playmate's, feigned defeat. He collapsed onto a sofa, burying his face in his hands and groaning dramatically. Paulina, sensing his distress, peered at him anxiously.

"Papa, what is the matter?" she whispered. "You had better ask him, Polly." "Is he hurt?" she asked, prompting a fresh groan. "He makes a noise as if he were," Mr. Home observed. "Mother," Graham feebly suggested, "I think you had better send for the doctor. Oh my eye!"

"If I were to become blind——?" the thought slipped out. The chastiser could not endure the suggestion. Paulina rushed to his side. "Let me see your eye: I did not mean to touch it, only your mouth; and I did not think I hit so _very_ hard." Silence was her answer, followed by working features and soft sobs. "Have done trying that child, Graham," Mrs. Bretton interjected. "It is all nonsense, my pet," Mr. Home cried, lifting her again. While she tugged at his lion-like locks, she berated him as "the naughtiest, rudest, worst, untruest person that ever was."

On the morning of Mr. Home's departure, a private conversation ensued in a window recess. "Couldn't I pack my box and go with you, papa?" Paulina asked earnestly. He shook his head. "Yes, Polly... Because you are little and tender. It is only great, strong people that should travel." He reassured her, promising his return, urging her to remain cheerful despite the sorrow. "Farewell," he said, as time pressed. "_Now_?—just _now_?" she stammered. "Just now."

As he left, she dropped to her knees, crying out "Papa!" a low, prolonged lament akin to "Why hast thou forsaken me?" For several minutes, she endured an agony rare for her infancy. Mrs. Bretton shed a tear; Graham, writing nearby, watched her silently. Lucy Snowe remained calm. Alone, Paulina wrestled with her grief, eventually repressing it to a degree. Over the next few days, she grew increasingly passive.

By the third evening, Graham found her sitting quietly on the floor. Without a word, he lifted her. She nestled into his arms, weary, and fell asleep in his embrace. He carried her upstairs to bed. The next morning, her first demand was, "Where is Mr. Graham?"

Graham was absent, occupied with school exercises. Paulina volunteered to deliver tea to his study. Pausing on the threshold, she asked, "What are you doing?" "Writing," he replied. "Why don't you come to take breakfast with your mamma?" "Too busy." "Do you want any breakfast?" "Of course." She set the cup down on the carpet, like a jailor offering water to a prisoner, and retreated. Moments later, she returned, asking what food accompanied the tea. "Anything good," he instructed. "Bring me something particularly nice."

Returning to Mrs. Bretton, she pleaded for something special for Graham. The mistress, unable to refuse, allowed her to select the finest items from the table. Paulina fetched marmalade as well, which Graham, seated alone, praised to the skies. He promised that when he owned a house, she would be his housekeeper, and perhaps, if she displayed culinary genius, his cook. She stood by his elbow, sharing his fare, though she delicately declined the marmalade, unwilling to claim credit for procuring it.

Their bond, formed in this intimacy, deepened rather than dissolved. Though disparate in age and nature, they found endless common ground. With Paulina, Graham revealed a softer side; when alone with her, he could contemplate, read, and engage in quiet conversation. She learned the names and characters of his schoolfellows with astonishing speed, mimicking their quirks and voices. Their relationship was not without friction, however.

One afternoon, during Graham's birthday, friends arrived. Left alone in the dining room, the boys boisterously entertained themselves. Paulina, watching from the stair landing, knitted her brow. "I wish that door was clear glass," she confided. Could she go to them? She knocked timidly, then more firmly. Graham's head appeared, impatient. "What do you want, you little monkey?" "To come to you." "Do you indeed? As if I would be troubled with you! Away to mamma..." The door slammed. Stunned, Paulina realized he preferred them to her. She curled up on the mat, refusing Warren's or the cook's efforts to rouse her.

That evening, Graham forgot his impatience, but Paulina rejected his advances, her eye flashing with wounded pride. For days, she remained silent, a statue of marble. Eventually, tears broke the ice, and friendship resumed, though she never again solicited his attention with the former eagerness. Even when invited to ride on his favorite pony, she responded with icy coolness, dismissing the offer. "Lucy Snowe is a _tatter_-box," she muttered, walking away.

Later, discussing her preferences, Paulina revealed a secret affection: "I find him the most excellent on a Sunday; then we have him the whole day, and he is quiet, and, in the evening, _so_ kind." Indeed, Sundays brought a serenity to Graham. They would read together, reciting hymns and biblical stories. Paulina, a quick scholar, often moved to tears over passages like Joseph in the pit or Daniel in the lions' den. "Poor Jacob!" she would murmur, gathering Graham in her arms. "If you were to die... I should refuse to be comforted, and go down into the grave to you mourning."

These tender moments struck Lucy as strangely rash, bordering on dangerous, yet Graham bore them passively, sometimes smiling with a complacent wonder at her earnest devotion.
