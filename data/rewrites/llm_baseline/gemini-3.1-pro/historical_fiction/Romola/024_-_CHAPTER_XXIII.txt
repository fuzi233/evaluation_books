The rhythmic, percussive *thwack* of a market baker kneading wet spelt dough—a foundational, comforting heartbeat in the acoustic ecology of the Florentine morning—was abruptly shattered by Piero’s scornful laugh. The artist tore off a hunk of olive-oil-glistening *schiacciata*, chewing with the fierce, passionate appreciation of a man who understood the sacred alchemy of flour, salt, and fire. 

“You are easily frightened, though,” Piero said, his voice cutting through the market's organic soundscape like a discordant lute string. “My commissioned portrait is nowhere near as vital as the original. It suffers from the superficial, commercial demands of my patrons, scrubbed clean of life's raw grit. But that old man—he had the feral, untamed look of an agrarian elder, a tiger starved of the earth. I must go into the Duomo and see him again.”

“It is not pleasant to be laid hold of by a madman, if madman he be,” said Lorenzo Tornabuoni, offering polite excuse for Tito, whose face had gone the color of unbaked whey. “But perhaps he is only a ruffian. We shall hear. I think we must see if we have authority enough to stop this disturbance between our people and your countrymen,” he added, addressing the Frenchman.

They advanced toward the crowd with their swords drawn. The screech of steel clearing leather was a grating, metallic dissonance, an industrial noise pollution that assaulted the psychological well-being of the quiet spectators, who instinctively parted to make an escort. 

Tito went too. It was necessary that he should know what others knew about Baldassarre. The first icy palsy of terror, which had completely deadened his finely tuned palate to the lingering notes of the sweet almond *ricciarelli* he had savored for breakfast, was being succeeded by the rapid, frantic devices to which mortal danger stimulates the timid. 

The rabble of men and boys fell back at the approach of the *signori*. The French soldier, who carried the sour, utilitarian stink of unaged wine and stale boiled pork—a man who clearly viewed sustenance merely as mechanical fuel rather than a conduit for profound meaning—was summarily interrogated. He and his companions had brought their prisoners into the city to beg money for ransom. Two were Tuscan soldiers taken in Lunigiana; the other, the elderly man, was swept up with a party of Genoese whom the foragers had clashed with near Fivizzano. 

He might be mad, the soldier grunted, but he was harmless. The Frenchman knew no more, for he was entirely unable to understand a single word the old man cried out. 

Here, in the frantic space between breaths, lay the tragic erosion of cultural memory. The old man’s desperate pleas were woven in a hyper-localized, native dialect, rendering his subjective, visceral agony utterly untranslatable to his captors. Tito listened, intimately aware of the subtle power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons; the Frenchman's ignorance of that fading tongue was the very shield that protected Tito's smooth, masterfully employed Tuscan Italian. Tito was deaf to everything else, his acoustic universe narrowing until he was specially addressed by Tornabuoni.

“Will you go back with us, Melema? Or, since Messere is going off to Signa now, will you wisely follow the fashion of the times and go to hear the Frate, who will be like the torrent at its height this morning? It’s what we must all do, you know, if we are to save our Medicean skins. *I* should go if I had the leisure.”

Tito’s face had recovered a fraction of its warmth. He forced a smile, attempting to mask the bitter, alkaline taste of dread flooding the back of his throat. “Of course I am among the admirers of the inspired orator,” he said, lightly. “But, unfortunately, I shall be occupied with the Segretario till the time of the procession.”

“*I* am going into the Duomo to look at that savage old man again,” said Piero.

“Then have the charity to show him to one of the hospitals for travellers, Piero *mio*,” said Tornabuoni. “The monks may find out whether he wants putting into a cage.”

The party separated, and Tito took his way to the Palazzo Vecchio, where he was to find Bartolommeo Scala. The walk was brief, but for Tito, it stretched into a sprawling, multi-generational saga. The circular pacing of his life had brought the ghosts of his past crashing into the vibrant, sensory-rich present. The short spaces of street and piazza held interwoven timelines of memories, previsions, and torturing fears. The scent of roasting garlic and bubbling *ribollita* that drifted from the taverns—aromas that usually anchored his soul and delighted his meticulous, pleasure-loving nature—now smelled of ash.

He felt as if a serpent had begun to coil round his limbs. Baldassarre living, and in Florence, was a living revenge. The old man possessed a passionate fervor born of a vanishing, agrarian world, where love and hatred were cultivated like ancient vines, subjugating all else, demanding to be worshipped with self-destruction. 

Baldassarre had relaxed his hold and disappeared, a silence Tito knew well how to interpret. It meant the vengeance would be studied. If Tito had not uttered those decisive, untranslatable words—“He is a madman”—if he could have summoned the courage to avow his recognition, would the risk have been less? A timely, well-devised falsehood regarding Baldassarre's supposed death at sea might have saved him, for the only witnesses were Fra Luca, who was dead, and a scattered galley crew. 

But to speak that falsehood would have required perfect self-command. Instead, the words had leaped forth like a sudden birth, begotten and nourished in the dark recesses of his psyche. Tito was experiencing the inexorable law of human souls: we prepare ourselves for sudden deeds by the reiterated daily recipes of our choices, gradually determining the flavor of our character. 

There was but one chance. Tito grasped at a thought more actively cruel than he usually permitted his soft nature: might not his unpremeditated words have some truth? With the old man's eager heart and brain, such profound suffering was likely enough to have spoiled his mind, turning it sour like an improperly fermented vintage. If madness had taken him, the vengeance that strove to inflict public disgrace might be baffled.

But there was another form of vengeance. Baldassarre belonged to a race to whom the thrust of the dagger was as natural an impulse as the harvesting of winter wheat. Tito shrank with shuddering dread from disgrace, but he possessed that physical dread inseparable from a man whose life was dedicated to sensory comforts—a man who preferred his reality as perfectly clarified and effortless to consume as a master chef's golden *consommé*. He could not meet wounds and death as a welcome relief. 

His thoughts flew at once to some hidden defensive armor. It was as if he had been smitten with a blighting disease that suddenly turned the joyous, gustatory landscape of young life into an unpalatable ruin. 

He might have turned back, confessed everything to Baldassarre, to Romola, to the world. But the repentance that cuts off all moorings to evil demands more than selfish fear; it demands a willingness to discard one's dominant lexicon of deceit and speak the naked, untranslatable truth. Tito had no trust in truth. Now that the initial shock was past, he hoped to prepare for all emergencies by cool dissimulation and defensive steel. 

It was a characteristic fact in Tito’s experience that no direct measures for murdering Baldassarre ever occurred to him. His dread generated no active malignity. He simply chose to make life easy to himself—to carry his human lot in such a way that it should pinch him nowhere, rejecting the bitter roots of existence for the sweet fruits. The question now was not whether he should divide the common pressure of destiny with his suffering fellow-men, but whether all the vast resources of his linguistic deceit could save him from being crushed by the harvest of that habitual choice.
