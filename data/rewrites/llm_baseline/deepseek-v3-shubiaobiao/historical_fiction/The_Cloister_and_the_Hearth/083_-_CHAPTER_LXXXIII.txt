A letter brimming with earnest longing seldom leaves the heart unchanged. Margaret poured hers out—her fears, her faith in her dying father’s vision, or perhaps his illusion. Yet once the words were sent and Luke departed, doubt crept in. Had she been too credulous? Guilt pricked at her conscience over Luke, and Catherine’s scolding only deepened her despondency.

Then came Joan Ketel, radiant with an idea. *"Margaret, there’s a cure for your sorrow—the hermit of Gouda, a holy man who sees what others cannot. When the mood takes him, he speaks truth."*

*"I’ve heard of him,"* Margaret murmured, hopeless.

With gentle persistence, Joan convinced her to walk to Gouda, basket in hand—butter and eggs for the hermit. Their friendship had been forged in hardship. Six weeks prior, Joan’s husband, Jorian, had fallen ill, wracked with pain. The physician’s remedies failed, and despair set in—until Margaret arrived.

*"Open the window,"* she had ordered. *"Change his linen."*

*"Why trouble for a dying man?"* Joan protested.

Margaret returned with an apron full of flowering feverfew, brewed it, and coaxed Jorian back from death’s edge. *"Never speak of this,"* she warned. *"They’d lock me away from my child."*

Now, standing before the hermit’s cave, Margaret pressed her lips to the aperture. *"Holy hermit, I bring gifts—butter and eggs. And I, a deserted wife, a mother with a babe, beg you—tell me if he lives, if he is true or false."*

A faint voice answered: *"Trouble me not with earthly things. Send a friar—I am dying."*

Margaret’s heart sank. *"Is it so? Then let us in to help you!"*

They left, heavy-hearted. Joan grumbled, *"Are we demons, to be barred from a deathbed?"* But Margaret walked in silence, her sorrow too deep for words.

Joan tried to console her. *"Jorian will speak soon—he’s near bursting with it. He’s grateful to you, truly."*

*"Seeing is believing,"* Margaret replied bitterly.

*"He says you saved him with feverfew when grander medicines failed. ‘A man of my size, cured by a weed!’ But he knows the truth. And he’s noticed Gerard’s kin circling you—friends, or something else."*

*"What did he say?"* Margaret pressed.

Margaret shook her head. *"We are in deep waters, my child and I."*

Saturday night came—no Luke. *"Poor Luke,"* Margaret sighed. *"He went on such a thankless errand."*

*"He’s one in a hundred,"* Catherine said warmly. *"If he returns, will you be kinder to him?"*

*"Yes,"* Margaret whispered.

---

Brother Clement, guided by the nuns, avoided the river’s bend and strode ahead, reaching a station miles nearer the coast than where Luke waited for Gerard Eliassoen. The next morning, he set off early, arriving in Rotterdam by noon.

He hurried to the port, expecting Jerome. Instead, he found monks of his order—but no Jerome.

*"Has Jerome arrived?"* he asked.

*"Oh yes,"* one replied, pointing to a ship already under sail.

Clement’s heart lurched. *"Gone without me? Jerome! Jerome!"*

The monks turned, startled. *"You must be Brother Clement,"* one said, greeting him warmly. They handed him a letter from Jerome—a cold, dismissive scrawl.

*"A ship was leaving for England. I could not delay. Follow if you wish, but you’d do better staying here. Preach to your own. You lack the steel for those islanders. And beware—I met one who asked after you by your old name. Guard yourself. The world’s snares are subtle. Remember: Solitude, Fasting, and Prayer are your weapons. Farewell."*

Clement stood stricken. Was there some flaw in him that repelled even Jerome? Or was Jerome himself devoid of Christian love?

*"Worldly hearts are no colder than this,"* he murmured. *"The only one who ever truly loved me lies in a grave."*

He wandered to Sevenbergen, passing Peter’s cottage—now repaired, children playing at the door. He turned away, unwilling to disturb the memory.

In the churchyard, he searched for Margaret’s grave. It was not there.

*"Poverty is cruel,"* he whispered. *"No stone to mark her rest."*

Inside the church, he kissed the steps and prayed for her soul. Outside, he met an old man.

*"Margaret? Oh, her! She left long ago. Gone to Rotterdam, or was it Amsterdam? And now dead? Ah, the young go first, while we old weeds linger."*

Clement sighed. *"God bless you, my son."*

He returned to the convent, where he prayed for Jerome and Margaret deep into the night, his soul seeking a cold, quiet peace.
