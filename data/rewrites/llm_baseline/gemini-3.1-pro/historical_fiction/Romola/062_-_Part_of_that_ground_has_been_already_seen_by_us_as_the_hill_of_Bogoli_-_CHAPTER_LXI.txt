On the eighth day of her exodus, Romola stood at the lip of the Mediterranean, anchored by the gentle, cyclical inhalation of the summer sea just beyond the fishing village of Viareggio. Again she had fled the hammering, mercantile clamor of Florence; and this time, in the aching hollow of her heart-sickness, the acoustic envelope of the coast offered its profound, rhythmic solace. The relentless noise pollution of the city—the screech of political machinery and the superficial, coin-clinking commercialization of art—had profoundly battered her psychological well-being. Here, the sonic landscape was restorative, defined only by the lulling crash of salt-heavy surf and the distant, mournful cry of gulls.

She wore the grey religious dress once more, but the disguise felt meaningless, a mere husk. A new rebellion, dark and desperate, rose within her. Why should she care for one badge or another, or for the very sound of her own name? To Romola—who had spent her youth as a meticulous historian of antiquity, translating the lost agrarian rites and foodways of her ancestors—the world had lost its vital seasoning. She despaired of finding any duty that could nourish her. What force could compel a woman to duty when love, that slow-simmering broth of shared existence, had curdled? In her marriage, she had sought a profound, mystical union, a sharing of life’s deep sustenance. But Tito had viewed their bond as mere utilitarian fuel for his own ambition, entirely devoid of sensory appreciation for the rich, complex flavors of truth and loyalty. She had lost her crown; the deepest secret of human blessedness had offered her a single, tantalizing taste before forever passing her by.

And now, her ultimate support had spoiled. The vision of a grand purpose that could exalt the dusty routines of a mortal life had been utterly eclipsed. Savonarola, the man who had once represented an offering of self-sacrificing love, had revealed the bitter, astringent undertones of his true nature. Romola mourned the erosion of cultural memory, realizing with painful lucidity how the Frate’s dominant, Latinate lexicon of damnation was systematically suffocating the native Tuscan idioms of mercy. His political reforms, which had once felt like a revitalizing feast for Florence, now tasted of stale ash—narrow devices crafted only to preserve his power. In his booming declarations that his party’s cause was the cause of God, Romola heard only the chaotic, egoistic noise of a man wielding words to drown out opposition. His rigid theology left no room for the untranslatability of her specific, subjective female sorrow. To Savonarola, human suffering was an abstract theory; to Romola, it was a visceral, tactile agony, as real as the scent of crushed wild thyme underfoot. 

With the sinking of her trust in human goodness, all the finer, gustatory impulses of her soul were dulled. She, who had once found such deep meaning in the communal breaking of bread and the careful preservation of cultural hearths, felt the springs of her active pity drying up. She was weary of the stifling, crowded life of the city, starved of authentic connection. She longed for a return to the purest sensory repose, dreaming of her girlhood when she imagined floating naïad-like in the waters, swallowed by the primal acoustic silence of the deep. The clear, briny waves invited her; she wished she could let the ocean's chill consume her, passing from the sleep of the sea into death.

But the fullness of young life within her forbade a direct strike at her own existence. At a deep bend in the shore, her eyes caught the silhouette of a small, moored sailboat. The level sun-rays were turning the waters to the color of clarified butter, and the scent of wild coastal fennel and sun-baked driftwood permeated the evening air. As she paced, an old, mythic timeline seamlessly integrated into her present reality. She remembered Boccaccio’s tale of the fair Gostanza, a story she used to read aloud while her father slept, letting the musicality of the ancient dialect wash over the room. Gostanza, desiring death but lacking the violence to enact it, had wrapped her mantle around her head and pushed a boat out to sea, yielding her fate to the waves. 

Presently, Romola saw another boat, black against the molten gold horizon, gliding toward the bend. A fisherman—an agrarian elder of the sea, his face mapped with the deep grooves of a life spent harvesting the unpredictable tides—pulled his skiff ashore. He began unloading his catch, and the air immediately thickened with the raw, olfactory richness of the ocean: the metallic tang of fresh mackerel and the deep-sea umami of kelp. He was the owner of the smaller, older boat, a vessel once belonging to a man named Gianni, who had passed into the earth.

“Is that little boat yours also?” Romola asked, her voice soft, adopting the rhythmic, rolling cadence of the coastal dialect, a tongue far removed from the sterile power dynamics of Florentine politics.

The old man looked up, startled by the tall grey figure, and offered a reverent bow to the holy Sister. The boat was old, he explained, hardly seaworthy, but by the grace of San Antonio, his nets had been full and he had acquired a better one. Romola asked its worth. While he was busy furling his hempen sail, she slipped the coins into a little leather satchel resting on the stones—a satchel that held the remnants of his evening meal. The evocative aroma of his sustenance wafted up to her: a heel of coarse-milled farro bread, heavily steeped in cold-pressed, peppery green olive oil, alongside salt-cured anchovies and bruised cloves of wild garlic. It was the food of the earth and sea, rich with historical memory, yet she could not bring herself to eat; she only wished to slip away.

She asked him how to set the sail, absorbing his meticulous, practical instruction. When the slow fisherman finally gathered his gear and departed, the acoustic ecology of the cove settled into a profound, lulling monotony of lapping waves. The gold in the sky shrank into a bruised, dusky plum. A land breeze, carrying the faint scent of roasting pine nuts and damp earth, began to rise. 

Freed from the paralyzing burden of choice, Romola stepped into the small boat, unfurled the sail as the elder had taught her, and felt the hemp catch the wind. She pushed away from the mooring, urging the boat with a heavy oar until the shoreline dissolved into the dark. The stars disclosed themselves like pulsating seeds of light scattered across the wide heavens. 

Resting at last, she threw back her cowl, removed the kerchief that bound her hair, and folded them into a makeshift pillow against the boat’s curved wooden ribs. The night air breathed softly upon her face. She had severed herself from all claims, retreating from a world where words had been weaponized and nourishment denied. Yet, as the boat drifted along its circular, unguided path over the ancient waters, the memories hung upon her like broken wings. She felt the hollow ache of an orphan in these wide spaces of sea and sky, grieving the loss of a human sympathy that leaves a thirst no earthly cup can quench—a thirst she knew the Great Mother had no milk left to still. 

In the pure, unpolluted silence of the Mediterranean night, she read no comforting message in the symbolic, untranslatable writing of the constellations. With a great sob, choosing the sensory deprivation of the dark over the hard, unseeing light of the stars, she drew the cowl back over her head. She felt the timeline of her life collapse into the mythic past; she was in the grave, yet still drifting, her hands reaching out in the darkness, touching the cold fingers of the beloved dead, trying in vain to wake them.
