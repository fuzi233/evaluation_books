The world begins in make-believe. Even science, that stern accountant of the universe, must pretend—must fix an arbitrary point in the ceaseless whirl of stars and declare, *Here, time begins*. Poetry, its wilder cousin, skips the pretense and dives straight into the middle, though in truth, the difference is slight. Both look backward as easily as forward; both carve infinity into fragments and call one of them *the beginning*.

And so we begin, not at the origin, but where the story demands: with a woman at a roulette table.

Was she beautiful? That was the first question—not in the placid way of a portrait’s admiration, but with the uneasy magnetism of a glance that unsettled rather than soothed. There was something restless in it, something that compelled attention without inviting comfort. A face that made you look again, not with pleasure, but with the sharp unease of a challenge unanswered.

She was gambling. Not in some sun-baked ruin, coins tossed against cracked stone, but in the gilded hush of a salon where wealth and idleness condensed into a single fevered breath. The air was thick, heavy with perfume and the slow burn of gaslight. The only sounds: the rattle of the wheel, the chink of gold, the murmur of the croupier’s call—mechanical, indifferent.

Around the tables, a mosaic of faces—aristocrat and adventurer, tradesman and withered dowager—all bent over their stakes with the same blank intensity. Gambling, that great leveler, pressed them into uniformity: lips tight, eyes unblinking, as if they had all eaten from the same root of obsession.

And then—*her*.

Gwendolen Harleth stood apart even as she played. Tall, poised, her gloved fingers arranging her winnings with deliberate grace. She had the air of one accustomed to victory, to the silent deference of eyes following her every move. Yet when her gaze lifted and met Deronda’s, something in her faltered.

His stare was cool, assessing. Not admiration—scrutiny. As if he saw not the enchantress but the mechanism behind the charm. It pricked at her, that look. Not enough to flush her cheeks, but enough to drain the color from her lips. She turned back to the table, jaw set.

The wheel spun. Her luck turned with it.

Coin after coin vanished into the croupier’s rake. A lesser woman might have flinched; Gwendolen doubled her stakes. Defiance burned brighter than greed. If she could not win, she would lose spectacularly—make the table remember her.

And still, Deronda watched.

When her last louis disappeared, she turned fully toward him, meeting his gaze with deliberate challenge. His lips curved—not a smile, but an acknowledgment. A silent duel, fought without words.

Later, in the gaslit glow of the evening crowd, whispers trailed her like perfume.

And Gwendolen, gliding through the room in sea-green silk, let them wonder. But her eyes strayed again and again to the door, to the figure who had measured her and found her wanting.

She would know his name. She would know why he unsettled her.

And so, when the chance came, she asked.

“Who is that man?”

“Deronda,” came the reply. “Mr. Deronda.”

A name to unravel. A face to decipher.

The game was far from over.

The letter lay on the table, its words heavy with a truth Gwendolen had never imagined would touch her. She picked it up, her fingers trembling slightly as she unfolded the crisp paper.

The words struck her like a cold gust of wind. Her mother’s handwriting, usually so composed, now carried a frantic edge. The news was impossible—unthinkable. Ruin. Total ruin. The life of comfort, of unquestioned privilege, had been swept away in an instant.

Gwendolen’s breath caught. She stood frozen, the letter dangling from her hand, her mind racing. This could not be. She had always been meant for better things—for elegance, for admiration, for a world that bent to her whims. Poverty was for others, not for her.

Yet the words did not change.

She tossed her hat aside and turned to the mirror, seeking—what? Reassurance? The reflection that met her was flawless, the smooth coils of her hair still arranged as if for a ball. But the sight brought no pleasure now. Her blue eyes, usually so vivid, stared back at her, hollow with disbelief.

A bitter laugh escaped her. How absurd, to think that beauty could shield her from this.

She sank onto the sofa, reading the letter again, slower this time, as if hoping the meaning might shift. It did not. Her hands clenched in her lap, nails pressing into her palms. She would not cry. Tears were for those who surrendered.

Instead, anger burned through her—sharp, defiant. If only she had won more at roulette! A few more days, a few more turns of luck, and she might have had enough to save them all. The thought taunted her. She still had a little money left. She still had her jewelry. Was it truly impossible?

But no. The risk was too great. The Langens would ask questions, and she would not—could not—bear their pity. Better to leave now, quietly, before anyone could guess her shame.

She moved mechanically, packing her things with swift precision. The night passed in a blur of decisions—selling the necklace at dawn, inventing an excuse for her sudden departure, booking the earliest train. She would not look back.

By morning, she was ready, dressed in gray traveling silk, her expression schooled into cool indifference. She paused before the mirror once more, studying herself. A faint, defiant smile curled her lips. She would not be broken.

Then, on impulse, she leaned forward and pressed a kiss to the glass.

*How could sorrow touch her?* She would outrun it. She always had.

But fate had other plans.

A small package arrived just as she prepared to leave. Inside, nestled in a scrap of cambric, was the necklace she had sold hours before. A stranger had returned it—*him*. She knew it instantly. Deronda. He had seen her, followed her, dared to meddle in her affairs.

Humiliation burned through her. He had stripped her of dignity, left her exposed. She would not give him the satisfaction of knowing she understood.

With trembling hands, she tucked the necklace away, along with her fury. There was only one thing left to do.

She would leave Leubronn today. And she would never look back.

By evening, she was gone.

Had Offendene been the home of Gwendolen Harleth’s childhood, rich with the warmth of family memories, she might have grown roots deep enough to steady her restless spirit. A life should be anchored in some beloved corner of the world, where the heart learns to cherish the shape of the land, the rhythms of its labor, the voices that linger in its lanes—where love for home is not taught but breathed in like the air. But Gwendolen had known no such constancy.

Her mother had chosen Offendene only a year before, for no sweeter reason than its nearness to the rectory where her sister lived. And so it was that on a blustery October afternoon, with rooks cawing overhead and elm leaves swirling at their feet, Mrs. Davilow and her five daughters arrived at the old red-brick house, its stone ornamentations worn with time, its face softened by lichen and the quiet dignity of age.

The house was not grand—no manor attached to it, no sprawling estate—but it held a faded elegance, the kind that whispered of better days. To Mrs. Davilow, it was a triumph: a home of her own at last, free from the rootless wandering that had defined their lives since her second marriage. For Gwendolen, it was a stage—a setting worthy of the drama she intended her life to be.

As they stepped inside, the hall greeted them with shadowed portraits and the flicker of a fire, but no welcoming party.

"Where are Uncle and Aunt Gascoigne?" Gwendolen demanded, her voice sharp with expectation.

"We are early," her mother murmured, but the housekeeper assured them the family would arrive by five.

Satisfied, Gwendolen led the exploration of their new domain—the dim dining room with its snarling dogs and somber Christ, the library heavy with the scent of leather, and finally, the drawing room, where an old organ stood waiting.

With a laugh, she claimed it, tossing off her hat and gloves. "I shall be Saint Cecilia," she declared, commanding Miss Merry to loose her hair. As the golden cascade fell, Gwendolen tilted her face upward, a vision of artful grace. Her mother smiled, indulgent as ever, while the younger sisters watched in silent awe.

Then little Isabel, ever the meddler, tugged open a hidden panel in the wainscot—and there, leering from the shadows, was a painting of a dead face, a figure fleeing in horror.

Gwendolen recoiled, her composure shattered for the barest instant before fury took its place. "You wretched child!" she snapped, snatching the panel shut. "Find the key. No one opens this again."

The moment passed, but the chill of it lingered.

Upstairs, before the mirror in their black-and-gold bedroom, Gwendolen studied herself with satisfaction. "I would make a lovely Saint Cecilia," she mused, "if only my nose were straighter. Tragedy requires a better profile."

Gwendolen whirled, vexed. "Must you be dull already? Everything could be perfect now—if only you would let it."

But perfection was Gwendolen’s domain, not her mother’s.

When the Gascoignes arrived at last, Gwendolen was all charm, her earlier irritation smoothed away. Her uncle, a man of imposing presence and polished manners, admired her openly. Gwendolen had already claimed the spotlight.

For Gwendolen Harleth, life was not something to be lived—it was a performance. And Offendene, with its secrets and its shadows, was merely the newest theater.

It would be unfair to fault the rector of Pennicote for considering Gwendolen Harleth a young woman destined for a brilliant marriage. Why should he differ from the opinions of his time, wishing anything less than the most advantageous match for his charming niece? If anything, his goodwill toward her future spoke well of him. His calculations were practical, rational. No staged accidents, no contrived rescues by wealthy suitors. He simply wished her to shine in the best society their neighborhood could offer.

Gwendolen, for her part, was in perfect accord with this ambition—though not out of any particular eagerness for matrimony itself. Marriage was, of course, inevitable; she had no intention of fading into spinsterhood. But the idea of being a wife—of submitting to domestic tedium, of sacrificing her whims to the heavy yoke of duty—struck her as a dreary necessity. Her observations had taught her that wives often ended up dull, burdened with too many children, and trapped in an existence devoid of excitement. No, she had no wish to rush into such a fate.

What she desired was *power*—the thrill of admiration, the certainty that she could shape her own destiny. She had no interest in being a passive ornament, a mere reflection of some man’s status. She meant to *lead*, to command the room, to leave an impression so vivid that the world bent to her will. If others allowed themselves to be blown about like empty ships, she would be the one steering.

Not that Offendene offered much in the way of grand opportunities. The archery club, dinners with the Arrowpoints, the occasional notice of Lady Brackenshaw—these were hardly the stuff of high drama. But Gwendolen was undaunted. She had been raised to believe in her own superiority, and she saw no reason to doubt it now. Her education, though conventional, had left her with the unshakable confidence of one who assumes she knows best. She spoke French, played music, and had absorbed enough poetry and novels to hold her own in conversation. And if a subject bored her? Well, then it was plainly not worth her time.

From childhood, she had been the center of her small world. Servants fretted over her comfort, her mother deferred to her whims, and even her half-sisters learned to tread carefully around her moods. She was, in effect, a princess in exile—accustomed to having the finest roll at breakfast, the most delicate attentions, the unspoken understanding that her desires came first.

Was it simply her beauty that commanded such devotion? Her grace, the way her voice cut through hesitation, the magnetic certainty in her bearing? Partly, yes. But there was more to it than that. Gwendolen possessed that rare quality of making others fear her displeasure as much as they craved her approval. She was not merely lovely—she was *formidable*. And though she could be capricious, though her moods shifted like light on water, those around her found themselves caught between dread and fascination.

For in Gwendolen Harleth, loyalty and selfishness, charm and ruthlessness, existed in the same breath. She was a creature of contradictions—capable of inspiring both devotion and unease in a single glance. And as long as the world continued to bend to her will, she saw no reason to change.

Gwendolen’s debut in society exceeded even her uncle’s predictions. From the grand halls of Brackenshaw Castle to the lavish Firs at Wanchester, she was met with admiration—some sincere, some perfunctory. Even those who found her too striking for comfort still extended invitations, for a hostess must balance her guest list like a statesman his cabinet, prioritizing novelty over personal preference. And with the unassuming Mrs. Davilow as her chaperone and the universally respected Mr. Gascoigne by her side, Gwendolen was an asset to any gathering.

Among the households where her presence was tolerated rather than adored was Quetcham Hall, where she attended one of her first grand dinners. The event was a glittering affair, a carefully curated assembly of the county’s finest. Gwendolen, draped in white, moved through the rooms like a vision, her effortless grace making even the most seasoned society girls seem stiff and provincial by comparison.

"Who is that with Gascoigne?" murmured the archdeacon, momentarily distracted from a debate on military strategy.

Across the room, his son—a scholar with a penchant for correcting Greek texts—echoed the sentiment. "By Jove, who *is* that girl with the exquisite profile?"

Yet for all her radiance, Gwendolen had a knack for unintentionally dimming others. The elegant Miss Lawe suddenly appeared broad and dull; Miss Arrowpoint, swathed in the same pale hue, looked like a poorly taken photograph. It was not malice—merely the inevitable effect of standing beside a flame.

"She’s striking at first glance," Mrs. Arrowpoint confided later to a companion, "but the charm wears thin upon closer inspection."

The remark was not entirely unjust. Gwendolen, accustomed to admiration, had yet to learn the art of humility. She approached Mrs. Arrowpoint with the condescension of youth, assuming that a woman of peculiar tastes (and a famously high headdress) must also be slow of wit.

"You must be so proud of your literary pursuits," Gwendolen remarked, her tone sweetly earnest. "I should love to write—to create something entirely my own instead of wading through other people’s musty old books."

Mrs. Arrowpoint’s smile tightened. "You must begin as I did—pen, ink, and determination."

"Oh, but you make it sound so simple! I should adore to read your works—perhaps then I’ll finally understand where to laugh."

The barb, though unintended, landed. Mrs. Arrowpoint’s gaze sharpened, but before she could reply, the gentlemen rejoined the party, and the moment passed.

Gwendolen had never before felt so keenly the sting of another’s superiority. She would never admit, even to herself, how bitterly she regretted not possessing Miss Arrowpoint’s musical education—something that might have armed her with the confidence to challenge Herr Klesmer’s judgment. Worse still was the quiet jealousy that prickled beneath her skin whenever she encountered Miss Arrowpoint, a jealousy not born of the other girl’s fortune, but of something far more irritating: the fact that this plain, unremarkable creature—slight of figure, with small features, tolerable eyes, and a sallow complexion—carried herself with an air of quiet intellectual dominance. Her musical precision, her exacting taste, her infuriating discernment—all of it made admiration unavoidable, even as it rankled.

Gwendolen, of course, preferred not to dwell on comparisons that cast her in an unflattering light. Since Klesmer was not always present to remind her of her shortcomings, she soon found ways to soothe her wounded pride. In truth, she was not so very different from other girls, save for her uncommon grace and a certain daring that lent charm to an otherwise ordinary ambition.

She longed to be exceptional, but her dreams were bound by the genteel romances she devoured—stories where heroines poured out grand passions in their journals while their lives remained neatly confined to the sphere of fashion. He would sit beside Rex in wordless companionship, pressing a hand to his son’s brow before leaving with a quiet, *"God bless you, my boy."* The younger children, uncomprehending, peeked around doorways only to be shooed away, their innocent curiosity no match for the gravity of the scene.

Anna, ever steadfast, kept vigil. Her small hand rested in Rex’s, though he no longer returned its warmth. Her heart was a battleground—grief for her brother warring with a quiet fury toward Gwendolen. *"Perhaps it’s wicked,"* she thought, *"but I don’t know if I can ever forgive her."* Even Mrs. Gascoigne, usually so measured, could not stifle her reproach.

*"She’s hardened,"* she confided to her husband. *"There’s a coquette’s heart in that girl. She must have led him on—why else would he be so broken?"*

The rector’s reply was firm. *"Let it die, Nancy. The sooner it’s forgotten, the better—especially where Gwendolen is concerned."* Privately, he counted it a mercy. Had she returned Rex’s affection, the tangle would have been far worse.

But the storm was not yet past. One morning, Rex rose, dressed himself, and descended the stairs—pale, but lucid. Anna, breathless with hope, met him at the foot, only for her joy to falter at the ghost of a smile he offered. It was a smile that held no happiness, only resignation.

He led her to the drawing-room, where weak sunlight strained through frost-laced windows. For a long while, he stared out, as if the world beyond the glass belonged to some other life. Then, abruptly, he spoke.

Anna’s heart lurched. *"Not forever?"*

*"And leave me behind?"* Tears spilled over.

*"But settlers go with families! I could cook, mend, keep house—it would be like our old games, only real!"* Her voice trembled with the force of her longing.

Rex exhaled, weary. *"Father would never allow it."*

*"He might,"* Anna insisted, *"if we asked together."*

And so they did.

Mr. Gascoigne listened, his brow furrowing deeper with each word. When Anna pleaded her case—her willingness to sacrifice comfort, her certainty that she could be useful—he drew her onto his knee, not unkindly, but as if to gently remove her from the equation.

Then he turned to Rex.

*"Then trust this: what you feel now will pass. This despair isn’t the end of you. To throw away your education, your future, on a whim of misery—that isn’t freedom. It’s surrender."*

Rex’s jaw tightened. *"I can’t study. Not now. Maybe never."*

*"Then you’ll rest. Miss a term. But you won’t flee."* The rector’s voice was steel. *"You’ll stay, and you’ll heal."*

A silence. Then, grudgingly: *"I’ll try."*

But in his heart, Rex knew—*trying* was just another word for defeat.

As Anna wept softly in her father’s arms, she mourned not just for Rex, but for the dream she’d spun: a life far from crinolines and chatter, where the world was wide, and love was simple.

*"I would never want to be like Gwendolen,"* she whispered. *"To have such power over someone… it’s terrible."*

And in that moment, she envied her brother his sorrow. At least it was real. At least it was his.

Eight months had passed since the family settled at Offendene, and by late June, a ripple of excitement spread through the neighborhood. The news had little to do with politics or war—it was far more personal, far more intriguing. Diplow Hall, Sir Hugo Mallinger’s grand estate, shuttered and silent for years, was to be reopened. Not by Sir Hugo himself, but by his nephew, Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt—the presumptive heir to the baronetcy, a man of fortune, leisure, and, most tantalizingly, still unmarried.

The prospect sent whispers through every level of society. Farmers speculated on better markets for their hay, tradesmen anticipated new business, and mothers of nimble boys imagined their sons in livery. But among the well-bred, the chatter took a different tone. A wealthy bachelor of good blood was a rare and welcome addition to any country circle, and where there was an eligible man, there were inevitably hopes—some spoken, some carefully concealed—of matrimony.

Marriage, after all, was both a pleasure and a business, and Grandcourt’s arrival stirred imaginations. The Arrowpoints, secure in their daughter Catherine’s fortune, might not seem desperate, yet even they wondered if this new neighbor might suit her. And then there was the rector, Mr. Gascoigne, who—though he would never admit it aloud—could not help but consider whether this might be the solution to his niece Gwendolen’s uncertain future.

Gwendolen herself, of course, had her own thoughts on the matter.

“How I pity all the other girls at the Archery Meeting,” she declared one afternoon, adjusting a pale green feather in her hat before the mirror. “All of them dreaming of Mr. Grandcourt—and not one with a shadow of a chance.”

Her mother, Mrs. Davilow, hesitated before replying. She had learned caution when it came to Gwendolen’s sharp tongue.

“You can’t be certain of that, my dear,” she ventured. “Miss Arrowpoint has charms of her own.”

Gwendolen turned, her eyes alight with mischief. “Oh, but hers require *thought*. Mine strike instantly. He’ll be my devoted slave before he’s had time to blink.” She flourished an imaginary bow. “I’ll send him off to fetch me the wedding ring of a happily married woman—and while he’s gone, every man between him and the title will conveniently perish. He’ll return a lord, empty-handed, fall at my feet—and I’ll laugh.”

Mrs. Davilow sighed. “Gwendolen, really—”

“Mamma, don’t look so horrified. I’m only teasing.” But there was something beneath the jest, a flicker of calculation. Gwendolen had already decided Grandcourt would be handsome in a particular way—well-dressed, slightly vain, the sort of man who might bore her just enough to make him interesting.

Mrs. Davilow, though she dared not voice it, could not help but hope. Marriage was the surest path to security, and Gwendolen, for all her wit and beauty, was not so easily pleased. Would this Grandcourt be the one to finally meet her impossible standards?

Meanwhile, the neighborhood held its breath. Eligible men were scarce, and Grandcourt’s arrival was a disruption—a welcome one for some, a threat for others. The Arrowpoints wondered if he might suit Catherine. The Gascoignes, more discreetly, wondered if he might suit Gwendolen.

And Gwendolen? She would decide for herself.

“Tell me, Mamma,” she said suddenly, “what *do* you suppose he’s like?”

Mrs. Davilow hesitated. “I’m sure I don’t know, dear.”

Gwendolen’s imagination had already painted him—a man of careful grooming, a monocle perhaps, a tendency to stare. “He’ll be just tall enough to look down at me,” she mused, “but not so tall as to be imposing. He’ll have strong opinions on waistcoats and none at all on the weather. And when he smiles, it will be more of a grimace.”

“You speak as if you’ve already met him!”

“Oh, I know his type.” She laughed, but there was a hardness beneath it. “And if he *does* propose, I suppose I’ll have to consider it. Unless, of course, someone more amusing comes along.”

Mrs. Davilow said nothing. She had learned that some hopes were better left unspoken.

And so the stage was set. Grandcourt’s arrival loomed, a question mark hanging over the neighborhood. Would he be the answer to someone’s prayers? Or merely another player in Gwendolen’s ever-shifting game?

Only time would tell.

Brackenshaw Park stretched across rolling hills, its castle rising above the landscape like a weathered sentinel. Limestone walls, darkened by lichen and rain, stood firm against the northern winds, while beech and fir trees clustered like herds descending to drink from the glinting waters below. The archery grounds, nestled on a sheltered plateau, were framed by towering elms and thick holly hedges, casting just enough shade to keep the afternoon pleasant.

The Archery Hall, white and columned, stood in elegant contrast to the greenery, its arcade offering a stately backdrop to the ladies in their summer finery. Their laughter, bright and musical, wove through the air, rising and falling like a melody even after the Wanchester military band had ceased to play. This was no raucous gathering, but a select affair—ticketed, deliberate, designed for grace rather than clamor.

Among them, Gwendolen Harleth moved with effortless superiority. Archery suited her—its poised tension, the elegant exertion of drawing the bow, the thrill of precision without brutality. Here was a sport that demanded skill, not bloodshed, and its prizes were marks of honor, not mere trinkets. The gold arrow, the silver star—these were emblems to be worn, then passed on, not pocketed like common winnings.

Today, she shone. The warmth of the day, the stillness of the air, all conspired in her favor. She was a vision—radiant, assured, the very image of youthful triumph. And why should she not revel in it? Preeminence was sweet, even to those who wore it lightly.

Around her, admiration hummed. Lord Brackenshaw himself remarked to a companion, *"That girl is like a high-mettled racer—first chop, and devilishly pretty besides."*

And indeed, she had never looked better. There was no shadow of discontent in her brightness, no envy of Miss Arrowpoint’s fortune or standing. Gwendolen was too secure in her own charms to begrudge others theirs. Even the unexpected presence of Herr Klesmer, the imposing musician whose severe judgment had once stung her, only amused her now. What place had a *maestro* at an archery contest? The contrast between his dramatic bearing and the subdued elegance of the English gentry was almost comical.

*"What extreme guys those artistic fellows usually are!"* murmured young Clintock at her side.

*"You are blind to the majesty of genius,"* Gwendolen replied, her eyes alight with mischief. *"Herr Klesmer smites me with awe. I feel quite crushed in his presence."*

Yet nothing could truly diminish her spirits today. The targets yielded to her aim, the applause followed each shot, and with every success, her confidence swelled. She was winning—not just the game, but the silent, unspoken contest of attention.

Then, at last, the moment came: three perfect strikes in the gold, a feat worthy of the highest prize. The crowd erupted in admiration as she stepped forward to receive the gold star from Lady Brackenshaw’s hands. She moved with effortless grace, the very picture of triumph, aware—without needing to glance—that every eye was upon her.

And among those watching, unseen but keenly felt, was Mr. Grandcourt. She would not look, would not betray the slightest interest, but his presence threaded through her awareness like a quiet pulse beneath the exhilaration.

It was only when Lord Brackenshaw approached, leading a tall, composed figure, that she allowed her attention to be claimed.

*"Miss Harleth,"* he said, *"may I introduce Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt?"*

The game, it seemed, was not yet over.

The first meeting between two people is like tracing the edges of a silhouette—outlining the shape of what we do not yet know.

Gwendolen had anticipated Mr. Grandcourt’s introduction, but when Lord Brackenshaw stepped aside and the man himself stood before her, she felt an unexpected flush rise to her cheeks. It was not embarrassment so much as surprise. He was nothing like she had imagined.

Grandcourt was neither tall nor imposing, yet there was an unsettling stillness about him. His features were fine—clean-cut, with a high brow and a cool, unreadable gaze. His hair, thinning at the crown, was a pale gold, but his hands were immaculate, his bearing effortless. He did not smile, nor did he seem to care whether she found him agreeable. His manner was neither stiff nor overly polished; it was simply indifferent.

Attempting to capture a person in words is futile—one can only begin to sketch them before time and circumstance fill in the rest. But in those first moments, Gwendolen noted one thing with certainty: *He is not ridiculous.*

Lord Brackenshaw withdrew, leaving them to the awkward dance of conversation. Grandcourt watched her with an unwavering, slightly inquisitive stare, while she allowed herself only occasional glances, softened by practiced charm. His pauses before speaking were deliberate, almost unnerving.

"You used to think archery a bore," he said at last. His voice was refined, but there was a languid drawl to it, as if even speech required more effort than he cared to give.

"Are you converted today?" she asked.

(She wondered what he thought of her. Did he find her amusing? Predictable?)

"Since watching you shoot," he replied. "Most people miss and simper."

"I suppose you're an excellent shot with a rifle."

(She studied him—his posture, his indifference—and mentally cataloged his flaws for some unseen listener.)

"I no longer shoot."

"Then you must be formidable. Men who abandon pastimes make the rest of us feel frivolous."

(What did he think of that? Did he find her wit tiresome?)

"What do you consider frivolities?"

"Anything enjoyable, I suppose. But you still hunt, don’t you?"

(She recalled gossip about his estate, his standing. He was undeniably aristocratic—the most so she had ever seen.)

"One must do something."

"Do you care for horse racing? Or is that another abandoned pursuit?"

(Would a cold man make a worse husband? Or a better one, free of tiresome interference?)

"I race occasionally. But I don’t lose myself in it. Do you like horses?"

"Adore them. There’s no better feeling than galloping fast enough to forget everything else."

(Would he approve? She refused to temper herself for his sake.)

"Do you enjoy danger?"

"I don’t think of it. On horseback, I’d charge at anything."

(She imagined a season of hunts, unrestrained and wild.)

"You might like tiger-hunting. Everything here is tame by comparison."

"So *you* seek danger?"

(Perhaps cold men were the boldest. She prided herself on the insight.)

"One grows accustomed to it."

"Then I’m fortunate. Everything is still new to me—except boredom, which I’d gladly abandon, as you have shooting."

(Would a man like this be dull? But then, most people were. And she wasn’t marrying him anyway.)

"Why are you bored?"

"This neighborhood offers nothing. Hence, the archery."

(She pitied unmarried women trapped in such monotony.)

"You’ve made yourself its queen. I expect you’ll take the prize."

"Perhaps not. Miss Arrowpoint shoots brilliantly."

(Men in novels often chose the wrong woman. She had read enough to know.)

"Miss Arrowpoint? No—yes, I suppose."

"Shall we see the scores? Everyone’s gathering—I think my uncle signals me."

She seized the excuse to escape. The conversation had left her oddly unsettled, her usual composure frayed. Grandcourt’s presence was not unpleasant, but his unshakable calm made her feel as though she were the one being measured—and found wanting.

As they rejoined the crowd, she noted with satisfaction that he did not follow.

Gwendolen refused to waltz, declaring she would only dance the quadrille. When pressed, she claimed a distaste for "ugly people" near her—a flimsy excuse that earned a reprimand from her aunt.

Yet Grandcourt did not seek her out. He danced once with Miss Arrowpoint, then seemed content to observe. Gwendolen told herself she didn’t care.

But when he finally approached—cool as ever—and asked for a dance, she could not hide her pleasure.

"You make dancing new, as you did archery," he said.

"Is novelty always good?"

"No. But with you, it might be."

She laughed, but his words lingered.

Perhaps, despite herself, she *did* care.

*"O gentlemen, the time of life is short;
To spend that shortness basely were too long,
If life did ride upon a dial’s point,
Still ending at the arrival of an hour."*

The morning sun filtered through the open windows of Grandcourt’s breakfast room, casting a warm glow over the polished oak and silver. Outside, the park stretched in lush, rolling greens, edged by the dark fringe of distant woods. The air was sweet with summer, and the stillness of the room—rich with the quiet dignity of old wealth—seemed to hush even the occasional stirring of the dogs that wandered lazily in and out.

Grandcourt himself lounged in his chair, one leg draped carelessly over another, a half-smoked cigar between his fingers. His face was a mask of indifference, though the tiny Maltese dog in his lap received absent-minded strokes of his fingers. At his side, Lush worked steadily through his breakfast, casting occasional glances at his companion.

The dogs—Fetch among them—watched the two men with fluctuating interest. Fetch, a liver-colored spaniel with soulful brown eyes, fixed her gaze on Grandcourt, waiting in vain for a word or touch. When she could bear the neglect no longer, she laid a tentative paw on his leg.

Grandcourt looked down at her, his expression unchanging, then deliberately set aside his cigar to lift the Maltese—Fluff—to his chin, stroking it with deliberate coolness. All the while, his eyes remained on Fetch, who whimpered softly, as if fighting the urge to voice her disappointment. When she finally rested her head beside her outstretched paw, her eyes pleading, Grandcourt merely pushed her away without a word.

The howl that followed was inevitable.

"Take that creature out," Grandcourt said, his voice low and indifferent, as though the command were beneath his notice.

Lush obeyed without protest, heaving Fetch into his arms—though she was no light burden—and disappearing briefly into the hall. When he returned, he lit a cigar of his own and took a seat where he could observe Grandcourt without turning his head.

"Will you ride to Quetcham today?" he asked after a pause.

"No."

"You didn’t go yesterday."

Grandcourt exhaled a slow stream of smoke. "I assume you made my excuses."

"I did. Though it would be simpler if you went today. They’ll assume some accident kept you away."

Silence settled again before Grandcourt spoke. "Who’s coming next week?"

Lush retrieved a notebook. "The Torringtons, the Hollises, the Cushats, the Gogoffs—"

"You invited them yourself in Paris."

Grandcourt’s voice, usually languid, sharpened imperceptibly. "That has nothing to do with it. I want a list."

Lush knew that tone—the quiet, unyielding command beneath the boredom. "Shall I find more suitable guests? Perhaps someone with daughters. And a musician—though nothing frivolous."

"Klesmer might do," Lush mused. "Miss Arrowpoint favors high-caliber talent."

Grandcourt turned his head at last, slow and deliberate, his gaze resting on Lush with cold amusement. "What concern is Miss Arrowpoint’s taste in music to me?"

Lush smirked. "Only that she might be worth considering. A woman of her means could solve certain… inconveniences."

Grandcourt’s lip curled faintly. "I’ve no intention of marrying for money."

Grandcourt’s gaze flickered with something like disdain. "She doesn’t like you."

Grandcourt ignored him, finishing his coffee before rising and stepping out onto the lawn, the dogs trailing after him like a loyal retinue.

Lush watched him go, then took a slow drag from his cigar, stroking his beard thoughtfully. After a long moment, he murmured to himself, "Check, old boy."

Fifteen years in Grandcourt’s service had taught Lush the limits of persuasion. He had been many things—a scholar once, a companion always—but never a man of true independence. Comfort had long since outweighed pride.

And so, when the morning’s tensions had settled, he retired to his private quarters, where he took up his violoncello and played, losing himself in the music—a small, solitary pleasure in a world where he was, after all, only a useful accessory.

Once Grandcourt resolved to marry Miss Harleth, he pursued his goal with quiet precision. Over the next fortnight, scarcely a day passed without some carefully arranged meeting—a ride, a party, an invitation—each designed to impress upon her and the world that she occupied his thoughts exclusively. With his cousin Mrs. Torrington now acting as hostess at Diplow, he could freely invite Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen to gatherings where his preference was unmistakable. Society—or at least the Gascoignes and the neighboring families of consequence—took note, and speculation solidified into expectation. Even the rector, ever practical, began mentally drafting settlement terms.

Yet despite ample opportunity, the proposal remained unspoken. Grandcourt, who had confidently assured Lush of his swift success, found himself hesitating each time the moment arrived. It was not doubt of her acceptance—such a thought never crossed his mind—but an uncharacteristic reluctance to face even the faintest possibility of refusal. The idea was absurd, yet it loomed like a shadow, unsettling his usual certainty.

The day had been arranged with deliberate charm: Gwendolen would ride his finest horse, Criterion, while Mrs. Davilow followed in the carriage. The weather was perfect—golden fields under a clear sky, the air warm but stirred by a light breeze that carried the scent of harvest. The countryside rolled past in idyllic stillness, untouched by the distant rush of trains, as if time itself had slowed.

But Mrs. Davilow, though soothed by the picturesque scene, could not quiet the flutter of anxiety in her chest. Watching Gwendolen and Grandcourt ride ahead—now cantering, now slowing to a walk—she willed the moment to come. Surely today, amid such beauty, he would speak. Yet Gwendolen had offered no hint of her feelings. Her silence on the subject was unbroken, unlike her usual sharp commentary on admirers. Did she favor him? Could she be happy? Mrs. Davilow could only hope, though happiness in marriage seemed a precarious thing.

Grandcourt, too, chafed at the delay. Uncertainty was intolerable. He had no fear of rejection—only irritation at the lingering tension, like an itch beneath his skin.

Gwendolen, for her part, enjoyed the ride but remained oddly restrained. There was none of the spontaneous laughter she had shared with Rex. Her replies to Grandcourt were light, almost distant, as if she were balancing on the edge of a decision without yet choosing to leap.

She turned away, feigning interest in the approaching carriage. Yet beneath her defiance, she wondered—had she gone too far?

Her acceptance was already implied in her presence here, in the way she allowed his attentions. If she refused now, it would only damage her reputation.

At Diplow, Gwendolen became the center of attention—admired, envied, basking in the unspoken assumption that she was Grandcourt’s chosen. The thrill of it buoyed her, pushing aside the unease that had prickled during their ride.

That night, Gwendolen lay awake, torn between ambition and an unnameable dread. She had always prided herself on doing as she pleased. Now, for the first time, she feared she might not know what that was.

Gwendolen awoke the next morning radiant as a lily kissed by the dawn—yesterday’s doubts no more than ripples on a deep, swift current. The archery match in Cardell Chase promised freedom, laughter, the dappled shade of beech trees, and the thrill of sport. And if Grandcourt lingered near her, well, so much the better. The archery party gathered at Green Arbor, a sunlit hollow framed by ancient trees. The game was to be leisurely—strolling first, then a proper chase in the golden afternoon. Gwendolen moved through the dappled light with practiced grace, though her arrows went wide. Distracted, perhaps. Or perhaps she no longer cared to win.

Grandcourt remained at her side, his presence as unreadable as ever. No one could have guessed, from his manner, whether they were strangers or betrothed.

“Do you know how long it’s been since I first saw you in that dress?” he asked, as they wandered back toward the others.

Gwendolen laughed. “The meeting was the 25th. Today is the 13th. Even my poor arithmetic makes it nearly three weeks.”

“A great loss of time.”

“For you? Pray don’t insult me.”

“I think I do,” she said, flashing him a smile. “There’s more amusement in it.”

The directness startled her. She flushed, turning away. “No. That would make me sorry.”

Had they been alone, he might have pressed further. But they were descending a steep slope now, and decorum demanded he offer his hand. She took it, pulse fluttering, and they rejoined the group in silence.

“I will not marry him. Don’t ask me why.”

Mrs. Davilow paled. “What will your uncle say?”

“I don’t care.” Gwendolen’s voice was flint. “I don’t care about anything.”

And for the first time, she almost believed it.

Gwendolen had spent her time abroad indulging in the new thrill of gambling, imagining herself a sovereign of chance, convinced that in this chaotic world, nothing truly mattered so long as one was entertained. But fate, in the form of the shadowy Grapnell & Co., had other plans. These mysterious figures, equally eager to rule the realm of luck, had upended her family’s fortunes, forcing her to return home—unwillingly carrying a pawned necklace someone else had seen fit to redeem.

While she sailed back to England, Grandcourt was making his own leisurely pursuit. He moved with his usual indifference, pausing at Baden-Baden, allowing himself to be diverted by Russian acquaintances before finally setting his course toward Leubronn. Grandcourt’s passions were never fervent; they flickered like a dying candle. Much of life, after all, proceeded without strong emotion—ties were knotted, dinners attended, toasts proposed—all without the fire of genuine desire. A man like Grandcourt could maintain the appearance of refinement, feign knowledge of the classics, hold reserved opinions on science and politics, and play the part of an English gentleman with minimal exertion. Even his stubbornness came cheaply, and his occasional impulses might be mistaken for passion when, in truth, they were merely the erratic spasms of a soul lacking purpose.

Her flight had amused him. There was something piquant in her defiance. He had led her to the brink of surrender in Cardell Chase, only to withdraw—a provocation to any spirited woman. She had meant for him to follow, and he had every intention of doing so. Yet for a week, he made no move, asked no questions. Mr. Lush watched with cautious triumph, sensing the shifting currents of his patron’s mind.

Meanwhile, the guests at Diplow buzzed with curiosity. Had Miss Harleth truly refused Grandcourt? Lady Flora Hollis, ever eager for gossip, embarked on a round of visits, extracting the news that Gwendolen had departed for Leubronn with the Baron and Baroness von Langen. The Gascoignes, anxious to avoid scandal, framed her absence as a mere holiday. The rector, ever optimistic, clung to the hope that the marriage was only delayed—Amaryllis, after all, flees only to be found.

When Lady Flora relayed these rumors to Grandcourt at dinner, he listened with detached interest. The next day, he announced his intention to leave Diplow, declaring boredom with the party. Lush, ever the opportunist, ensured his own inclusion in the journey. Thus, Grandcourt arrived at the *Czarina* five days after Gwendolen’s departure, finding Sir Hugo Mallinger and his family—including the enigmatic Daniel Deronda—already in residence.

The reunion was outwardly civil, though beneath the surface, tensions simmered. Sir Hugo, though easy-tempered, chafed at Grandcourt’s position as heir to the Mallinger estates—a constant reminder of his own lack of a son. For years, he had nursed the hope of securing Diplow for his wife and daughters, even if it meant bargaining with Grandcourt. Money, he reasoned, might tempt his nephew to relinquish his claim.

Grandcourt, for his part, regarded his uncle as a tedious irrelevance. The notion of selling his future inheritance amused him—not because he intended to accept, but because the power to refuse pleased him.

Deronda, standing apart, observed the dynamics with quiet intensity. When Sir Hugo inquired about the "gambling beauty," Deronda revealed that she had left—her name Harleth, her home near Diplow. The look he fixed on Grandcourt carried unspoken challenge.

"You know her?" Deronda asked, his tone cool.

Grandcourt’s reply was languid. "Yes."

Sir Hugo, ever the provocateur, teased Deronda about pursuing her himself. But Deronda’s past had instilled in him a deeper restraint. He had no intention of chasing after Gwendolen Harleth—though he could not deny the intrigue she stirred in him.

For now, their paths had crossed only to diverge again. But fate, as ever, was patient.

There are moments in life that split the world into *before* and *after*. For Daniel Deronda, that moment came on a drowsy afternoon in July, sunlight spilling over the last petals of the roses in the cloister garden, the air thick with the scent of grass and old stone.

He was thirteen, sprawled in the shade with a book—Sismondi’s *History of the Italian Republics*—while his tutor, Mr. Fraser, sat nearby, absorbed in his own reading. History was Daniel’s passion, a way to trace the threads of time backward, to make sense of the vast, indifferent march of years. But today, his curiosity snagged on something closer.

“Mr. Fraser,” he asked suddenly, his voice bright with boyish directness, “why did popes and cardinals always have so many nephews?”

The tutor sighed, marking his place with a finger. “Because their own children were called nephews.”

Daniel blinked. “Why?”

“Propriety,” Fraser said crisply. “Priests don’t marry. The children were *illegitimate*.”

The word struck like a spark. Daniel sat up sharply, his back to Fraser, blood rushing to his face. A thought had slipped into his mind, quick and unwelcome: *What if that’s why I call Sir Hugo ‘uncle’?*

Until now, he had never questioned his place in the world. Sir Hugo was kindness itself—a man who laughed easily, whose grand estate felt as much a part of Daniel as his own hands. The baronet had told him once, carelessly, that he’d lost his parents young, and Daniel had accepted it without probing. Why wouldn’t he? Life was golden, and Sir Hugo was the sun at its center.

But now—

The idea spread like ink in water. *If Sir Hugo were his father, why the lie?* And where was his mother? Had she been cast aside, some secret shame? His stomach twisted. The world, so steady a moment ago, tilted beneath him.

He stood abruptly, his book forgotten. Fraser’s voice followed him—“Daniel, you’re crushing the pages”—but he was already walking away, toward the open fields where no one would see the hot tears on his face.

---

**A month later**, the wound was still fresh, though hidden.

Daniel had a gift for music—a voice that could lift hearts, fingers that found melodies as naturally as breathing. Sir Hugo, delighted, often had him perform for guests. That morning, after a rain-locked gathering, Daniel sang *Sweet Echo*, his clear tenor filling the room. The men applauded, and Sir Hugo beckoned him forward with a smile.

“What do you say to being a great singer?” the baronet asked, jovial. “Adored by the world, like Mario?”

Daniel froze. The question was casual, but to him, it was a key turning in a lock. *A singer. Not a gentleman.* His face burned. “No,” he said, sharp as a blade. “I should hate it.”

The room stilled. Sir Hugo blinked, then laughed, ruffling Daniel’s hair. “Only a thought, lad!”

But Daniel was already gone, fleeing to the sanctuary of his window seat, where he could stare out at the rain-slicked park. *Why would Sir Hugo suggest that?* Unless—unless he knew Daniel didn’t belong among gentlemen. Unless his birth was something to hide.

The suspicion festered. Every glance, every offhand remark, became a clue. Had the servants whispered? Had Banks, the bailiff, smirked years ago when he said Daniel *“featured the mother”*? He recalled the family portraits lining the gallery—all those Mallinger faces, stern or smiling. None looked like him.

Yet when Sir Hugo summoned him to the library days later, it wasn’t to confess. It was to announce, warmly, that Daniel would go to Eton.

“You’re to be a gentleman,” Sir Hugo said, fingers idly toying with the boy’s dark curls. “And gentlemen are educated at public schools.”

Relief flooded him. *A gentleman. Then perhaps—* But the doubt lingered, a shadow at the edge of every thought.

---

**Before he left**, Daniel made his peace with uncertainty. If there were secrets, he would not pry. He sang again, laughed with the servants, pressed gifts into their hands. But something in him had closed, like a door gently shut.

At school, he was well-liked—quick at lessons, strong at games, his reserve mistaken for quiet confidence. Only once did a classmate ask about his family, and Daniel’s silence was so sudden, so final, that the boy never asked again.

He would not be pitied. He would not be lesser.

And if the truth ever came, he would meet it standing.

*"This is true the poet sings,
That a sorrow’s crown of sorrow
Is remembering happier things."*
—Tennyson, *In Memoriam*

The Thames lay smooth and silvered under the fading glow of a July evening as Daniel Deronda guided his boat through the quiet current. A year had passed since his return to England, yet the question of his place in the world remained unresolved. Though he had taken up the study of law to appease Sir Hugo, the effort only deepened his uncertainty. The river, at least, offered sanctuary—a retreat from the clamor of society into the stillness of his own thoughts.

He rowed with an easy rhythm, his dark-blue shirt and skull-cap lending him the look of an ordinary young man at leisure. Yet there was nothing ordinary in the quiet intensity of his gaze, nor in the way the dying light caught the firm lines of his face—no longer the seraphic boy of childhood, but a man whose features bore the weight of unspoken reflection.

The melody of a gondolier’s song lingered in his throat, the mournful strains of Rossini’s *Otello*, set to Dante’s lament: *"Nessun maggior dolore che ricordarsi del tempo felice nella miseria."*—*No greater sorrow than to remember happiness in misery.* The words drifted softly over the water, a private echo of the evening’s melancholy.

Then he saw her.

A slight figure stood at the river’s edge, a girl of no more than eighteen, her dark curls tucked beneath a broad black hat, her slender frame wrapped in a woolen cloak. She was motionless, her hands clasped before her, her eyes fixed on the water with a despair so profound it seemed carved into her. The sight struck him like an embodiment of the very sorrow he had been singing.

He fell silent. The sudden absence of his voice startled her; she turned, and for a suspended moment, their eyes met. Hers were wide, dark, filled with the startled fear of a creature unused to kindness. Then, without a word, she retreated to a bench beneath the trees.

Deronda hesitated. To intrude would be presumptuous, yet to row away felt like abandonment. He moved on, but the image of her—the delicate face, the look of utter desolation—lingered like a shadow.

When he returned downstream, the sun had dipped below the horizon, painting the sky in deepening hues. He guided his boat to a secluded spot opposite Kew Gardens, where the water mirrored the fading light. Stretching out, he let the quiet of the evening settle over him, his thoughts drifting between introspection and the half-formed sorrows of the world.

Then movement caught his eye. The girl again.

This time, she stood at the bank with purpose, her cloak soaked, her movements deliberate. Horror struck him—she meant to drown herself. Without thought, he seized the oars and pushed toward her.

She froze as his boat neared, her face a mask of terror. He spoke gently, his voice steady. *"Don’t be afraid. You are unhappy. Pray, trust me. Tell me what I can do to help you."*

She stared at him, recognition flickering in her eyes. *"I saw you before,"* she murmured, then, as if recalling the song, whispered, *"Nella miseria."*

Understanding dawned. She had heard him singing. The words had reached her, a fragile thread of connection.

*"You look good,"* she said at last, her voice trembling. *"Perhaps it is God’s command."*

He held out his hand. *"Let me take you somewhere safe."*

She hesitated, then stepped into the boat, her movements weary, as if the weight of her sorrow had exhausted her very bones. As they moved along the river, she spoke in fragments—of being alone, of searching for a mother she could not find, of the crushing certainty that life held no joy.

*"I am a Jewess,"* she confessed, as though bracing for rejection.

*"Why should that matter?"* he replied.

She wept then, great, silent sobs that shook her slight frame. Deronda felt something within him shift—an old wound, a buried question about his own origins, his own mother.

He knew where to take her. Not to the cold formality of Park Lane, but to the warmth of the Meyricks’ home in Chelsea—a house filled with kindness, where she might find shelter without fear.

As the cab rolled through the darkening streets, the girl’s head drooped in exhaustion. Deronda watched her, struck by the fragility of her trust. He had plucked her from despair, but what came next?

Rescue was only the beginning. The true question was what life waited beyond the edge of the water.

Life is a fickle muse—one moment adorned in silks and jewels, ascending gilded staircases without a glance at those beneath her; the next, cloaked in hardship, breathing the thick air of dim alleyways where laughter is sharp and survival is a ragged song. Yet to the Meyrick household, she came as a gentle housekeeper, thrifty yet warm, turning modest means into abundance with clever hands and a cheerful heart.

The Meyrick home was a quiet refuge. The front parlor overlooked the river, the back windows opened to small, tangled gardens, and though Mrs. Meyrick read aloud to her daughters, the evening air drifted in, mingling with the soft glow of lamp and candlelight. Kate bent over her sketches for a publisher’s commission, while Amy and Mab stitched satin cushions destined for wealthier hands.

From the outside, the house seemed narrow, even shabby—another unremarkable slice of London’s endless rows. Yet within its walls thrived a refinement untouched by vulgarity, where poverty had stripped away pretension and left only what was genuine. The Meyricks clung to this home, not for its grandeur, but for the memories etched into its very bones. The same engravings hung where they always had, telling stories the children knew by heart. The same chairs stood in their familiar places, worn but beloved.

Mrs. Meyrick, a petite woman with a lively spirit, her graying hair tucked beneath a plain cap, read from a French novel, her voice weaving clarity into every word. Her daughters—small, quick, and bright-eyed like their mother—listened, their minds sharp as their needles. Mab, with her brother Hans’ fair hair and whimsical brow, was all restless energy, while Kate and Amy moved with quieter purpose. Even the cat, Hafiz, perched regally on a chair back, seemed to approve of the scene.

They were a family bound by love, by admiration for what was noble, and by the steady rhythm of work. Hans’ dreams of spoiling them had been gently refused; they needed little, content with their books, their music, and the rare treat of a night at the opera when he visited.

Tonight, however, the quiet was about to be broken.

Mab, ever dramatic, had just flung her arms wide and declared, *“I feel like the deluge! The waters are rising, and the heavens are opening!”*—reaching for the piano as if to release the storm inside her—when a cab halted outside. A sharp rap at the door sent Mrs. Meyrick hurrying to answer.

Mab gasped. *“There! Something is happening!”*

And indeed it was. Moments later, Deronda stood in the parlor, his face grave yet hopeful. *“I’ve brought someone who needs your kindness,”* he said. *“A young woman—lost, desperate. I found her by the river. She has nowhere else to go.”*

The girls exchanged glances, their curiosity and compassion alight.

Then she entered—small, dark-eyed, her weariness etched into every line of her, yet with a quiet dignity that stilled the room.

*“I am a stranger,”* she said softly. *“A Jewess. You might have thought me wicked.”*

*“No,”* Mab burst out. *“We think you are good.”*

Mrs. Meyrick took her hand. *“You are safe here.”*

The girl—Mirah—looked at them all, then at Deronda, as if assuring herself this was real. *“The God of our fathers bless you,”* she whispered to him. *“You have given me the best.”*

And with that, Deronda left, the weight of her gratitude following him into the night.

Daniel Deronda was not a man given to overt displays of sentiment, but beneath his composed demeanor lay a heart attuned to the poetry of life. Where others saw only the mundane, he found threads of meaning woven through the ordinary—threads that could stir him as deeply as any epic tale. Romance, he believed, was not a thing of the past, nor was it banished by the age of reason. It lingered in quiet corners, in the turn of a stranger’s glance, in the quiet desperation of a soul in need. It thrived even under the scrutiny of science, even in the rattling compartments of a train, so long as there were eyes to see it and hearts to feel it.

For Deronda, the discovery of Mirah was no mere chance encounter—it was a moment of fate, as charged with emotion as any legend of old. That night, sleep eluded him. His mind replayed the hours since he had first seen her by the river’s edge, each memory vivid, each detail sharp. Even when he tried to distract himself with a book, the words blurred into insignificance against the force of his thoughts. He saw not only what had happened but what *might* happen—possibilities branching like veins of gold through the dark uncertainty of the future.

Mirah’s search for her mother struck a chord deep within him. He understood the ache of separation, the yearning for lost kin. His own unresolved past made him keenly sensitive to her plight. Yet alongside his sympathy flickered a darker fear—what if the reunion she longed for brought not joy, but sorrow? She had spoken of her mother and brother with tenderness, but years had passed. Time could corrode even the purest bonds.

Deronda had never taken much interest in the Jewish people as they existed in the present day. The few he had encountered—whether wealthy merchants or those lurking in the shadows of society—had done little to challenge the prejudices that clung to their name. He knew the ugly stereotypes, the whispered tales of greed and cunning, and though he despised broad condemnations, he had never sought to disprove them. Now, Mirah’s plight forced him to confront his own assumptions. Could her family be as refined as she? Or would he find, in some dim alley, a woman hardened by hardship, bargaining away her daughter’s last trinket? Would her brother be a coarse young man, eager to exploit any connection to a gentleman like Deronda?

The thought sickened him. Mirah, with her quiet grace and unspoiled spirit, deserved more than such a fate. He could not bear the idea of her being tainted by the very world from which she had fled. Yet he knew his fears were colored by his own dread of the unknown—the same dread that had haunted his own search for identity.

What, then, was to be done? She needed protection, guidance, a place in the world. But he could not—*would not*—assume sole responsibility for her. She must never feel indebted to him, never trapped by his kindness. And so he resolved to involve others—those whose judgment he trusted—so that Mirah’s future would not rest on his shoulders alone.

A part of him wished to unburden himself at once, to tell Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger everything. But caution held him back. There were still questions unanswered, secrets yet to unfold. And so, with his thoughts tangled between hope and apprehension, he waited. Morning would bring him closer to the truth—and to the next step in Mirah’s uncertain journey.

*"There are those among us whose very presence speaks of virtue—whose lives bear such unmistakable grace that to know them is to trust them without question."*

Mirah had slept deeply that night, and when she descended the next morning in Mab’s borrowed black dress, her damp, curling hair framing her face like a dark halo, she looked less like a ghost of sorrow and more like a woman beginning to remember the warmth of life. The pallor of her cheeks had softened, though the shadows beneath her eyes still lingered like faint bruises. Mab, triumphant, had rushed out at dawn to procure a pair of delicate felt slippers—for no shoes in the house had been small enough—and now she presented them with the pride of a queen’s attendant.

"Look, Mamma!" Mab exclaimed, kneeling as if in reverence. "See how perfectly they fit! She’s like the princess in the tale—*‘two slender feet, shaped by the hand of mercy itself, bearing her through the world.’*"

Mirah glanced down at her feet, childlike in her surprise, then smiled at Mrs. Meyrick, who thought privately, *Such a face could never harbor malice. And yet, wisdom warns me to tread carefully.* Still, she returned the smile. "I fear those feet have carried too heavy a burden of late. Today, you shall rest and keep me company."

Mirah did not resist. The small parlor, bathed in morning light, felt like a sanctuary. The river beyond the window shimmered, and the walls were alive with prints of saints, prophets, and poets—silent witnesses to the quiet unfolding of a soul. Mrs. Meyrick, her hands busy with embroidery threads, let the stillness settle between them, waiting.

At last, Mirah spoke, her voice soft as the air stirring the curtains.

"I remember my mother’s face more clearly than anything in this world. And yet, I was only six when I was taken from her. Now I am nineteen."

Mrs. Meyrick nodded. "The earliest memories are often the strongest."

"Yes. My life began with her—her arms around me, her voice singing hymns. Always Hebrew, though I didn’t understand the words. They were woven of nothing but love. At night, she would lean over my little bed, her face between me and the white ceiling, singing until I drifted to sleep. Even now, I dream of it—her kiss on my small hand. Sometimes, in those dreams, I tremble, thinking we are both dead. But then I wake, and for a moment, I don’t know where I am." She paused. "If I saw her again, I would know her. I would."

"Twelve years change us all," Mrs. Meyrick murmured. "See my hair—once brown, now grey. Time leaves its marks, especially when its steps are heavy."

Mirah’s eyes darkened. "I know her heart has ached for me. If I could only see her joy when we meet again—if I could make her feel how much I love her—then nothing else would matter. I would bear every sorrow gladly." She drew a slow breath. "I did despair. The world felt cruel. No kindness reached me. I believed she was dead, and I longed for death too, thinking it the only mercy left. But then—yesterday, when the river seemed the kindest end—goodness found me. And now, here, with you... I feel hope like a tide washing through me. I can wait. I can believe. Because you—you have not judged me."

Her voice trembled with gratitude. Mrs. Meyrick, blinking back tears, could only say, "Many would have done the same."

"But they did not. You did."

A silence fell. Then, gently, Mrs. Meyrick asked, "How did you come to be taken from your mother?"

Mirah’s hands tightened in her lap. "That is the hard part. My father—it was he who took me. I thought we were only traveling. I packed a little box of my treasures. But we boarded a ship, and the land vanished. I was ill for so long. When we landed, he told me it was America. He promised we would return to my mother soon. I learned to write quickly, desperate to send her letters. But one day, he took me on his knee and said she and my brother were dead. That was why we could not go back." Her voice hardened slightly. "I believed him. I wept for years. But as I grew older, I began to doubt. He did not always tell the truth. I wondered if she still lived, waiting for me. I wrote to her once—to Colman Street, where we had lived—but no answer came."

Mrs. Meyrick’s needle had stilled. "And your father? Was he unkind?"

"No. He was... affectionate, in his way. He taught me, indulged me. But his life was not mine. He was an actor—a poor one, I think, though he wrote plays and managed stages. There were always people coming and going, laughter that felt hollow. I hated it. I buried myself in books—Shakespeare, Schiller—and built a world inside my mind where things were beautiful. But the theater..." She shuddered. "The applause, the stares—it was like being stripped bare. I played a child once, singing alone with flowers. The audience adored it. But all I felt was loneliness."

She fell silent, lost in memory. Then, with effort, she continued. "When my voice failed to grow strong enough for opera, he was furious. We moved—Hamburg, Vienna. He gambled. I sang in cheap theaters, in plays that sickened me. Men leered; women smirked. It was a slow fire, burning me alive. And yet... I pitied him. In his worst moments, he would weep, clutching me like a drowning man. I think he loved me, in his way. But love is not always enough."

Her words hung in the air, heavy with sorrow. Mrs. Meyrick reached out, covering Mirah’s hands with her own. "You have been very brave."

Mirah shook her head. "No. Only desperate. But now... now I dare to hope."

And in the quiet of that sunlit room, for the first time in many years, hope did not feel like a lie.

The world often proclaims that knowledge is power, but who has truly measured the might of ignorance? Knowledge builds with patient hands, stone by stone, while ignorance, reckless and hungry, tears down in moments what took ages to create. Knowledge preserves, records, refines; ignorance burns the archives for warmth, savoring the ashes of forgotten wisdom. Knowledge crafts beauty, skill, and purpose; ignorance stumbles in with destruction on its breath, erasing all with a careless word.

Yes, knowledge is power—but it is power tempered by conscience, bound by duty. Ignorance, on the other hand, is a blind giant, unshackled and laughing as it shatters the pillars that hold up the fragile edifice of human happiness. And in the smaller scale of a single life, who cannot see how ignorance of truth—how the delusion that one can command fate as easily as snapping fingers—leads only to ruin?

It was half past ten in the morning when Gwendolen Harleth arrived at the station nearest to Offendene, her journey from Leubronn heavy with gloom. No carriage awaited her, no familiar face in the crowd. In her haste to leave London, she had not considered the discomfort of arriving unannounced at this lonely stop, a place chosen not for convenience but for its equidistant irrelevance.

Deposited alone with her trunks, she waited in the dingy station room, its walls a dull yellow, a stale decanter of water on the table, and stern script on placards urging repentance. The very air seemed thick with her family’s misfortunes. Stepping outside, even the sunlight felt thin and cheerless, the wind stirring the fallen leaves and ruffling the feathers of a bedraggled cock and two disconsolate hens. The stationmaster, unfamiliar with her, regarded her with idle curiosity, and Gwendolen felt the sting of his indifferent gaze.

The promised vehicle—a shabby barouche—was being readied by an old laborer, and the sight of it settled like a weight upon her spirit. Such petty discomforts might seem insignificant, but they press upon the soul until they shape despair. For Gwendolen, they were the first taste of a bitter homecoming, proof that hardship had indeed laid claim to her life.

The drive was slow, the carriage cramped, her resentment simmering. All the way from Leubronn, she had clung to the hope that they might escape abroad again—surely there was *some* money left? The thought of shrinking into obscurity, of watching her sisters grow dull in poverty while she withered toward thirty, was unbearable. She refused to submit. Yet now, faced with the reality, her defiance wavered. What was the use of beauty, wit, or will if fate could so easily undo her?

And Grandcourt—how dare he be the obstacle that kept her from security? Her anger flared at him, at all men, at the world that had conspired to humble her. But as the carriage neared Offendene, her bitterness ebbed at the sight of her mother’s face, lined with fresh sorrow. For a moment, Gwendolen forgot herself in the rush of tenderness, throwing her arms around her mother as if to shield her from all grief.

The girls—Alice, Bertha, Fanny, Isabel—hovered in the background, their subdued presence a reminder of the life they must now endure. Even good Miss Merry, the governess, moved quietly about her tasks, her usual meekness now justified by their shared misfortune.

Upstairs, in the black-and-yellow bedroom, Gwendolen pressed a handkerchief to her mother’s tears. *"Never mind, Mamma. I will do something. I will be something."* The words came with a surge of determination, a defiance against despair.

But resolve, like all things, waned as the day wore on. The truth of their situation could not be softened.

*"We must leave this house,"* Mrs. Davilow said gently. *"Mr. Haynes is willing to take it back. We have nothing now but what your uncle provides."*

*"Sawyer’s Cottage?"* Gwendolen’s voice sharpened with disbelief. *"Impossible. I will not live there."*

Her mother’s explanations—of their poverty, of the necessity—fell like blows. Gwendolen’s mind raced. *Her winnings at roulette—had she known, she could have brought home money instead of losing it all. Everything conspired against her.*

Then came the suggestion: a position as a governess.

*"A bishop’s daughters? A school?"* Gwendolen’s laugh was brittle. *"No. I would rather emigrate."*

But defiance alone would not fill their pockets. A plan stirred in her—vague, desperate. *Her ornaments—she could sell them. And Klesmer—surely he would advise her?*

She wrote swiftly, sealing her plea in a note to be sent at once. If he was not at Quetcham—but she would not think of that.

As she waited, the future loomed like a shadow. For the first time, Gwendolen Harleth, who had always believed herself destined for brilliance, felt the crushing weight of ordinary misfortune. The world was not a stage designed for her triumphs, but a heedless crowd in which she might vanish entirely.

Unless, of course, Klesmer could change her fate.

The loom of life weaves its patterns not by our design, but by the unseen hand of passion, shaping our days with threads of longing and defiance.

Gwendolen’s plea reached Klesmer at the perfect moment—neither too soon nor too late—just as he was departing Quetcham. To honor her request, he endured the inconvenience of a night in Wanchester, for reasons of his own that made staying at the grand estate impossible.

Quetcham, that opulent monument to wealth, had grown stifling. Its masters, like statesmen blindsided by revolt, had failed to notice the rebellion brewing beneath their noses. Among the usual parade of guests was a new suitor for Miss Arrowpoint’s favor: a political gentleman of respectable lineage, certain of a peerage and equally certain that Catherine’s fortune was necessary to uphold his future title. Heiresses, after all, were expected to marry where society deemed fit—yet Catherine Arrowpoint was no ordinary heiress.

Nature, indifferent to convention, had gifted her with a will as unyielding as it was clear. While her parents fretted over her refusal of eligible suitors, they dismissed the notion of Klesmer as a contender—a blindness common to those who mistake their desires for destiny. How could a brilliant but penniless musician dare aspire to their daughter’s hand? And yet, love thrives where barriers are highest.

Their bond had deepened unnoticed, as such things often do. Frequent companionship, shared passions, and the quiet understanding between teacher and pupil had woven an intimacy no one had foreseen. Klesmer, though proud and occasionally brusque, was a man of fierce integrity; Catherine, though gracious, was not one to bend to expectation. Each cherished the other’s company too well to speak of what might be impossible.

Enter Mr. Bult, the aspiring peer—pink-cheeked, solid, and tediously assured. To him, Klesmer was little more than an eccentric servant of the arts, a man whose opinions on politics (delivered with unexpected fire one evening) were as flighty as his music was impressive.

"You speak well for a musician," Bult remarked, with the condescension of a man who measures worth by title.

Klesmer’s retort was swift. "A creative artist is no more a *mere* musician than a statesman is a *mere* politician. We shape nations as surely as lawmakers do."

The exchange left Catherine torn between irritation and admiration. Later, alone with Klesmer, she chided him. "Was it necessary to be so harsh?"

"Should I smile while he insults what I hold sacred?" Klesmer’s eyes burned. "You would have me swallow his ignorance as if it were no offense."

"I only ask for civility."

"Civility?" He laughed bitterly. "You tolerate him easily enough. But then, you need not defend your existence."

The words hung between them, charged with everything unspoken. At last, Klesmer turned away. "I should leave. My presence here is a distraction—for both of us."

Catherine’s voice was barely a whisper. "And if I don’t wish you to go?"

He faced her then, his pride yielding to a fiercer truth. "Then you must know—you are the only woman who has ever made the world seem small to me. But I am no fit match for you, and I would not have you sacrifice your place for my sake."

Her resolve hardened. "What if it’s no sacrifice? What if it is my choice?"

The silence that followed was louder than words.

---

When Catherine confronted her parents, the storm broke at once.

"You cannot be serious!" Mrs. Arrowpoint’s voice trembled with outrage. "A man like Klesmer—a vagabond, a foreigner!"

"He is a genius," Catherine countered.

"Genius does not make a gentleman," her father interjected, his cigar forgotten. "Estates cannot be managed by a man who cares more for concertos than crops."

Catherine stood firm. "Must I wed a title to be happy? Or a man who values my fortune over my heart?"

"You owe a duty to your family, to your name!" her mother cried.

"Duty?" Catherine’s laugh was sharp. "A word too often used to mask fear. I will not chain myself to emptiness for the sake of appearances."

The battle lines were drawn. And for the first time, Catherine Arrowpoint was ready to fight—not as an heiress, but as a woman who had found the one soul that understood her own.

The world of art is not a promised land where inheritance alone grants possession. To hold the map of an unconquered kingdom is no assurance that one’s feet will ever tread its soil. And to dream of dominion over Eden is but a fool’s comfort when the gates remain barred by labor and time.

The most stubborn convictions we hold about ourselves are those without proof—only the ceaseless pulse of self-assurance, a quiet madness whispering that we might move the world without knowing where to stand or what lever to pull.

* * *

“Mamma, you must go to church today,” Gwendolen said the next morning. “I would rather speak with Herr Klesmer alone.”

Mrs. Davilow hesitated. “Is that quite proper?”

“Propriety is the least of our concerns now,” Gwendolen replied, her voice edged with impatience. “Our situation is too grave for such trifles.”

“Would you at least allow Isabel to sit with you?”

“No. She would fidget and stare, and I cannot bear distraction. Trust me—I must see him alone.”

And so it was settled. The house grew quiet, save for the faint murmur of Miss Merry and the younger girls keeping watch by the dining-room windows.

The morning light, soft and golden in its autumn decline, spilled through the tall windows, painting the old furniture in slanting bands of radiance. It touched the faded tapestries, the dark, enigmatic paintings, the organ where Gwendolen had once played at being Saint Cecilia, lost in the joy of her own music. Now, the memory of that moment—of Klesmer’s admiration—pressed upon her with new urgency. For the first time, she felt the weight of another’s judgment, the uneasy flutter of dependence. She had always known herself to be clever, beautiful, capable. But was that enough?

She gathered her music, arranging it neatly upon the piano, then caught sight of herself in the glass panel. Dressed in unadorned black, her skin luminous against the severe lines of her gown, she might have stepped from a Roman bas-relief—a statue carved in marble, perfect and untouchable.

*I am beautiful,* she thought, not with vanity, but with cold certainty. Beauty was her armor, her currency. Without it, what proof had she of her worth?

The sound of wheels, the sharp ring of the doorbell, pulled her from her thoughts. Her pulse quickened. For all her confidence, Klesmer was an unknown force—a man who owed her no flattery, whose judgment would not bend to her will.

He entered with a deep bow, impeccably dressed, his expression unreadable.

“It is kind of you to come,” she said, extending her hand. “I hope you do not think me presumptuous.”

“I take your request as an honor,” he replied, though his tone carried an edge, still vibrating from the passions of the previous day.

Dispensing with formalities, Gwendolen stood by the piano, bracing herself. “I wish to consult you. Our fortune is gone. I must earn my bread—and my mother’s. The only path I can see is the stage. I wish to act, to sing. To rise as high as I can.”

Klesmer set his hat down, folded his arms, and regarded her with an intensity that made her fingers tighten.

“I know my training has been imperfect,” she pressed on, “but I can learn. I am willing to work. Tell me honestly—can I succeed?”

For a long moment, he was silent. Then, abruptly, he strode to the far end of the room, as if gathering his thoughts. When he turned back, his gaze was piercing.

“Have you ever known the life of an artist?”

“No,” she admitted, unshaken.

“You are—forgive me—one-and-twenty?”

“Yes. Am I too old?”

His fingers flicked upward, a gesture both dismissive and enigmatic.

“Some begin later than others,” she said, a defensive note creeping in.

He ignored this. “You have never before considered this path? Never felt the *longing*?”

“Not in those terms. But I have acted—you saw me as Hermione.”

“Ah, yes.” His lips pressed together. “I remember.”

Again, he paced. Gwendolen’s patience frayed. “Whatever your advice, I will be grateful for it.”

Klesmer faced her squarely. “Then I will be plain. To lie to you would be a sin. You are beautiful, accustomed to ease, unbroken by discipline. The life of an artist is one of relentless labor, uncertain reward. Your voice, your skill—they are untested. You lack training, the rigor of years. Without it, you will be—at best—mediocre.”

The word struck like a blow.

“You think I have no talent?” she demanded, her pride flaring.

“I think you have not been shaped for this. Genius is not inspiration alone—it is the marriage of gift and grinding toil. You must unlearn what you know, submit to mastery, endure obscurity before you dare dream of brilliance.”

Gwendolen’s cheeks burned, but she held her ground. “I am prepared for hardship.”

“Hardship?” His laugh was sharp. “You imagine treading the boards as you do a drawing room? A manager would either demand payment for your amateur efforts or send you away to learn the basics—how to stand, how to speak, how to be *heard*.”

Her hands clenched. “Then how do so many inferior actresses find work?”

“Because they have what you lack—training, however meager. You might teach them grace, but they could teach you craft.”

A silence fell. Klesmer exhaled, his tone softening. “There is another path. Beauty can open doors where skill does not. Some use the stage as a stepping stone—to wealth, to marriage. But that road, too, is lined with indignities.”

“I want independence,” Gwendolen said coldly. “Not charity.”

He studied her, then inclined his head. “Then you must choose. Excellence—or compromise. But know this: the first demands everything. The second may cost you more than you wish to pay.”

The truth lay between them, stark and unyielding. Gwendolen stood motionless, the future she had imagined crumbling at her feet.

Gwendolen was relieved to have her interview with Klesmer behind her before facing her uncle and aunt. The path ahead seemed strewn with indignities, and she braced herself with a stubborn resolve to endure whatever humiliations awaited her.

The meeting came on Monday, when she accompanied her mother to the rectory. They stopped first at Sawyer’s Cottage—a cramped, sunlit space, stripped of any softening comforts. The bare walls, the neglected garden with its wilted cabbage stalks, the dusty yew arbor—all pressed upon Gwendolen with oppressive bleakness.

"How can you bear it, Mamma?" she asked as they walked away, her voice tight. "You and the girls in that stifling little room, with that hideous wallpaper? And I won’t even be there."

"It will be some comfort knowing you are spared it, dear."

"If not for the money, I’d rather suffer there than go be a governess."

"Don’t decide against it before you try," her mother urged gently. "At the bishop’s palace, you’ll have every comfort. You’ve always cared for such things. It won’t be as hard as climbing those narrow stairs, listening to the clatter of crockery, the girls chattering—"

"It’s like a nightmare," Gwendolen burst out. "I can’t believe Uncle would allow this. He should have done something else."

"What else could he have done?"

"That was for him to discover. It’s a cruel world if people like us must sink so suddenly."

Her bitterness was sharp, her words unguarded. Yet when she faced her uncle and aunt, their unexpected warmth disarmed her. They spoke with dignified cheer about their own economies—Mr. Gascoigne’s practical adjustments, the boys’ simplified education. There was a quiet strength in their resilience that made her own resentment feel petty.

Still, when her uncle turned to her future, her stomach tightened.

"I acted quickly, Gwendolen," he said kindly. "A respectable position isn’t secured overnight. The Momperts’ offer is the best we could hope for. Mrs. Mompert wishes to meet you first—in Wanchester, as she travels to town. I know it may be trying, but you’ll have time to prepare."

"Why must she see me?" Gwendolen asked, already imagining a stern matron scrutinizing her flaws.

The rector smiled. "Don’t fret. She only wants a clearer impression than my letters provide. A mother is careful about her daughters’ companion. I’ve told her you’re young, but she oversees their education herself. She’s a woman of taste and principle—objects to French governesses. I’ve no doubt she’ll approve you."

Gwendolen’s flush betrayed her revulsion, though she forced stillness. Anna, sensing her distress, slipped a hand into hers.

Mr. Gascoigne, mistaking her silence for acceptance, added encouragingly, "It’s more a home than a mere post. The bishop is a man of influence—Low Church, but broad-minded. A friend of Lord Grampian’s. You’ll find it a respectable situation."

His assurances only deepened her dread. *Continuance of education. Privately strict. Bible Society.* Each phrase felt like a shackle. Desperation clawed at her.

"Was there not another possibility?" she asked tightly.

"The school?" Her uncle hesitated. "Far less suitable. Harder work, fewer advantages. And you might not even secure it."

Her mother shook her head. "A shared bedroom, Gwendolen. You’d hate it."

Memories of school—the lack of privacy, the drudgery—sealed her defeat. With effort, she murmured, "When does Mrs. Mompert expect me?"

"Within a fortnight, I imagine." Satisfied, her uncle departed, believing her resigned.

Left behind, Gwendolen seethed. The thought of presenting herself for approval, of bending to strangers’ whims, was unbearable. Even as a governess, she would be inspected, judged, found wanting. The bishop might examine her like a pupil. The future yawned before her—a gray, joyless servitude.

Wild thoughts flickered: escape, the stage, any path to freedom. But Klesmer’s warnings haunted her. Without protection, what awaited her but vulgarity and disrespect? Her pride revolted at the thought.

Meanwhile, her family’s kindness scraped at her raw nerves. Anna, timidly affectionate; her aunt, reading Rex’s buoyant letter—*Tell Mother she’s advertised for a hardworking son, and I apply for the place!* Even that stung. *Why should he flourish while I sink?*

She avoided the rectory, unable to feign compliance. Let Miss Merry fuss over the cottage furnishings. Let Jocosa find pleasure in it. She stayed in her room, listless, ignoring meals, snapping at well-meaning words.

Her mother watched in silent anguish. One afternoon, as Mrs. Davilow lingered over her dresses, Gwendolen abruptly fetched her jewelry case.

"These should be sold," she said flatly, lifting the tray. Then she paused, her fingers brushing the turquoise necklace—the one she had once torn from the fire. A strange pang struck her. "Keep this. The rest can go."

"But, darling, if we can manage—"

"They’re useless now. Governesses don’t wear finery. Better get me a drab uniform, like a charity-school maid."

"Don’t say that! The Momperts will appreciate elegance—"

"What they *appreciate* doesn’t matter. I must be what they want." Bitterness dripped from every word.

Her mother caught her hands. "If there’s another path—anything—tell me. We’ll face it together."

Gwendolen pulled away. "There *is* no other path. I must earn money. That’s all." Her voice hardened. "You’ll have the eighty pounds. At least you won’t have to stitch yourself blind."

No caress followed. She stared at the necklace, winding the torn handkerchief around it—the same she had used to snatch it from the flames.

Her mother’s eyes filled. "Don’t lose hope, my love. You’re so young. Happiness may yet come."

Gwendolen laughed harshly. "I see no reason to expect it."

She snapped the case shut. "I’ll keep this. Sell the rest. I’ll have no use for them." A bleak smile twisted her lips. "I might as well take the veil. I wonder if all who did felt as I do now."

"Don’t exaggerate—"

"How would *you* know what I feel?" she shot back, tucking the wrapped necklace away.

Mrs. Davilow fell silent, watching her daughter’s closed face. Something had changed—but what, she could not guess.

And Gwendolen, swallowing her despair, thought: *No one sees the storm inside. No one ever will.*

*A mind adrift in the barren expanse of self-indulgence is a curious thing—where desires wither into mere flickers of impulse, and motives dissolve into the shifting sands of mood. Such is the fate of a life unchained, unshaped by duty’s steady hand. As the old saying goes, too much license corrupts, and a path marked only by whim leads nowhere certain.*

Grandcourt, having learned of Gwendolen’s departure from Leubronn, declared the place a wretched hole—worse than Baden—and with that, Mr. Lush understood his patron’s unspoken intention to return to Diplow. Execution, however, lagged behind intent. Grandcourt dawdled through the following day, indifferent to Lush’s expectant glances. He lingered over his toilet, emerged with the languid elegance of a man to whom freshness seemed vulgar, and drifted through the casino and reading rooms with an air of supreme disinterest.

Yet when he encountered Lady Mallinger, he mustered the effort to pause, lift his hat, and humor her with a dry remark about the providential presence of springs at gambling resorts. She, mistaking his tone for sincerity, laughed nervously. Sir Hugo, ever perceptive, later assured her that Grandcourt was no fool—merely a man who played his cards close.

Deronda, too, received a nod of acknowledgment—one he returned with cool civility. He had no desire to betray resentment, though the shadow of his illegitimacy loomed between them. Pride kept him from stooping to envy, but Grandcourt, ever the connoisseur of others’ discomfort, fancied he detected it anyway.

Lush, meanwhile, amused himself with gossip. Sir Hugo, ever fond of scandal, indulged in light inquiries about Grandcourt’s affairs—Diplow’s potential sale, the tangled web of personal entanglements—though he spared Deronda the sordid details. Lush, ever the accommodating parasite, was happy to oblige, so long as it cost him nothing.

Then came the order: Paris, then Diplow. Grandcourt loitered in the city while Lush rode ahead, tasked with preparing the estate—and discreetly gathering intelligence on Gwendolen. The news of her family’s ruin had reached him. Would poverty soften her resistance, or harden her pride? Lush could not say. Grandcourt was as inscrutable as ever.

At last, the summons came. The carriage rolled to Wanchester station, and Lush steeled himself for the inevitable confrontation.

---

Grandcourt’s command was curt: *"Is Miss Harleth at Offendene?"*

Lush feigned indifference. *"The family’s ruined. Packed into some cottage, I hear. The girl’s likely gone off as a governess."*

*"Don’t lie to me,"* Grandcourt murmured, his voice a blade sheathed in silk.

Lush bristled but held his ground. *"Send Hutchins if you want confirmation."*

A pause. Then, with deliberate calm, Grandcourt lit another cigar. Lush seized the moment.

*"You ought to consider,"* he ventured, *"whether you truly want this. She’s no heiress now. Her family’s a burden. And after all your freedom, marriage is a shackle."*

Grandcourt exhaled smoke. *"You’ve always objected to her."*

*"Not without reason,"* Lush countered. *"She’s unpredictable. And there’s the matter of Lydia Glasher."*

A flicker—just a flicker—in Grandcourt’s gaze. *"What of her?"*

Silence. Then, with icy precision: *"And?"*

Lush shrugged. *"Only that it may give her pause."*

Grandcourt reached for pen and paper. *"Send this to Offendene."*

The letter, addressed to Gwendolen, was sealed without another word. Lush watched, knowing he had lost this round—but the game was far from over.

Grandcourt’s mind was a locked room. And Lush had just thrown away the key.

*The heart resists with pride what it secretly craves, yet flings open the door to temptation, if only to prove its strength in refusal.*

Mr. Gascoigne arrived at Offendene with news he believed would steady Gwendolen’s resolve: Mrs. Mompert had set a date for their meeting in Wanchester. He made no mention of Grandcourt’s return to Diplow, nor of the man’s recent travels—what purpose would it serve to stir up memories of a lost opportunity? Privately, he lamented his niece’s inexplicable rejection of fortune, though he reasoned that Grandcourt’s abrupt departure had been the first offense. Now, his duty was clear: she must be urged forward, not backward.

"You’ll find growth in adversity, my dear," he said with paternal firmness. "A woman tempered by trials becomes all the wiser for them."

"I don’t pretend I’ll enjoy it," Gwendolen replied, a rare edge of irritation in her voice. "But I suppose I must endure it."

Her uncle’s gaze sharpened. "And you *will* endure it—with grace. Mrs. Mompert will expect propriety. A woman in her position demands respect."

Gwendolen said nothing. When he left, the tears she had so fiercely restrained now fell in slow, heavy drops. Youth did not ease the weight of disappointment—it only made the injustice sharper. Where was the joy owed to her, if not now, when the world should have been ripe for her taking? The glittering future she had once envisioned now lay in ruins, and the path ahead stretched barren.

She paced the room, her fingers trailing along the back of a chair, her mind circling the same bitter truth: she was ordinary. Not remarkable enough to command fate, not clever enough to outwit it. Klesmer’s verdict still stung—*you are not exceptional*—and worse yet, she feared he was right. The thought of bending to Mrs. Mompert’s whims, of playing the grateful dependent to a woman of no particular brilliance, was a slow suffocation.

A tear slipped free. *Mamma was never happy. And now neither shall I.* The image of her mother, years from now, white-haired and weary, whispering, *Poor Gwen, how changed she is—* seized her with sudden tenderness. The sob that escaped was not of fury, but grief.

At that moment, Mrs. Davilow entered. Gwendolen hastily pressed a handkerchief to her eyes, but her mother was already crossing the room, arms outstretched. The embrace undid her. She clung, weeping, the dam of her pride broken.

When she pulled back, she saw the letter in her mother’s hand.

"More bad news?" she asked, bracing herself.

Mrs. Davilow hesitated. "It’s for you. From Diplow."

Gwendolen’s breath caught. She took the note, her fingers unsteady. The words were brief, formal:

*Mr. Grandcourt presents his compliments to Miss Harleth and requests the honor of calling upon her tomorrow afternoon. He has only just returned from Leubronn, where he had hoped to find her.*

She let the paper fall.

"It must be answered," Mrs. Davilow murmured.

Gwendolen sat motionless, her pulse a frantic drumbeat. A choice had been thrust upon her—one she had believed lost forever. The terror of it warred with a treacherous thrill. To refuse him would be power. To see him—what then?

Her mother laid out pen and paper. Gwendolen stalled, her mind racing. Why not let him come? It bound her to nothing. She could still say no.

"Most men would shy from a family in reduced circumstances," Mrs. Davilow ventured carefully. "If he knows, and still seeks you out..."

The words struck deeper than intended. A new possibility unfurled: Grandcourt’s wealth could shield her mother from hardship. The thought was intoxicating—and dangerous.

Gwendolen seized the pen. *Miss Harleth will be at home after two o’clock.*

She handed the note to the servant, then stood abruptly, as if shaking off the weight of decision.

"Don’t imagine this means anything," she warned her mother. "I only wish the pleasure of refusing him."

Mrs. Davilow studied her, but asked no more.

Gwendolen turned toward the window. "Let’s walk. I can’t breathe in here."

The air outside was cool, the future no longer certain—and for the first time in weeks, she felt alive.

The morning sun slanted through the window as Gwendolen sat before her mirror, her mother’s fingers weaving through the long cascade of her chestnut hair.

"Just coil it loosely, Mamma," Gwendolen murmured, her gaze fixed on her reflection. There was a brightness in her eyes that had been absent for weeks—a lightness, as if a shadow had lifted.

Mrs. Davilow noticed it at once. "Shall I fetch your earrings, dear?" she asked, her voice warm with hope.

"No," Gwendolen replied with a faint, knowing smile. "I’ll wear the black silk today. It’s only proper when one means to refuse an offer."

"And if the offer isn’t made?" her mother ventured slyly.

"Then it will be because I’ve already refused it in my heart. The result is the same." She gave a proud tilt of her chin, then swept downstairs in her long black gown, her posture regal once more, as though she had shaken off invisible chains.

Yet beneath the surface, her resolve wavered. The last twenty hours had been a storm of doubts and desires, arguments for and against, until the certainty she had clung to no longer felt solid. She had meant to refuse Grandcourt—had sworn it to herself the moment his letter arrived. But now, faced with the reality of his arrival, her conviction faltered.

*If not for that day in Cardell Chase…* she thought. Then, perhaps, there would have been no hesitation. But the memory of Lydia Glasher’s desperate face, the weight of secrets and shame, had seared itself into her mind. And yet—was it truly her burden to bear? Would another woman in her place truly be harming Mrs. Glasher by marrying Grandcourt? Or was it all just the flutter of conscience, easily quieted?

She had no real understanding of marriage, only the vague notion that a clever woman could manage a husband as she pleased. Her mother’s mistakes were not her own. And as for love—well, that was the man’s concern, wasn’t it? Grandcourt’s past offenses had disgusted her, but disgust was a fleeting thing compared to the relief his wealth could bring.

When the sound of hooves on gravel announced his arrival, her pulse betrayed her. She had rehearsed this moment, yet now it felt like standing at the edge of a precipice.

Grandcourt’s presence filled the room—his languid grace, the subtle scent of attar of roses, the way his heavy-lidded eyes fixed on her as if she were the only object worth studying.

"You left Leubronn too soon," he drawled. "The place was intolerable without you."

Gwendolen arched a brow. "I can hardly judge a place by how it fares in my absence."

"You knew your departure would spoil everything," he pressed. "Are you entirely indifferent to me?"

The question hung between them, impossible to answer. She could not say *yes*, nor could she bring herself to say *no*. Instead, she lowered her lashes, her cheeks flushing.

His voice grew softer, more deliberate. "Is there another? Some prior claim I should know of?"

Her lips parted, but no words came. *No—but there is a woman.* The truth burned in her throat, yet she could not speak it.

Grandcourt watched her silence with satisfaction. "Then may I hope?"

She rose abruptly, crossing to the window, her back to him. The pause stretched, taut as a bowstring.

"Must I ride away?" he murmured at last.

The image struck her like a blow. Without him, what awaited her? Poverty, dependence, the stifling weight of failure.

"No," she said softly.

He closed the distance between them, his voice a velvet caress. "Then you accept my devotion?"

The word *yes* slipped from her lips before she could weigh it, grave as a vow.

His kiss on her hand was light, almost reverent. A giddy sense of triumph surged through her—she was free. Free of the Momperts, free of their wretched cottage, free of the gnawing dread of insignificance.

When she fetched her mother, her voice was steady. "Come down, Mamma. I am engaged to Mr. Grandcourt."

Mrs. Davilow’s eyes widened—not with joy, but solemn astonishment.

Gwendolen only smiled. *Everything will be as I like now.*

And for the moment, she almost believed it.

The news of Gwendolen Harleth’s engagement to Mr. Grandcourt spread through the neighborhood with the swiftness of a summer storm. By evening, the rectory had been thoroughly swept up in the excitement, and Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne arrived at Offendene with Anna in tow, their congratulations ripe and ready.

"My dear," began the rector, his voice rich with approval, "you have inspired a most steadfast attachment. It is no small thing—a lifelong union. And given Mr. Grandcourt’s conduct, I daresay we may already see the silver lining in our recent misfortunes. His generosity speaks volumes."

He referred, of course, to Grandcourt’s unspoken assurance that Mrs. Davilow would be cared for—a detail Gwendolen had relayed to her mother with precision.

Mrs. Gascoigne, ever the pragmatist, was quick to add, "Though I’ve no doubt he would have behaved just as handsomely had you not gone abroad, Gwendolen. You might have been engaged weeks ago." She paused, then pressed on dutifully. "But now there’s no room for caprice. A woman owes a great deal of gratitude to a man who persists in offering her such a position."

Gwendolen arched a brow. "I’m not at all certain I feel *properly*, Aunt. I’m still learning what one ought to feel upon being engaged."

The rector chuckled, charmed by her playful defiance, while Mrs. Gascoigne softened, reassured by her husband’s amusement. Anna, meanwhile, kissed Gwendolen’s cheek and murmured, "I do hope you’ll be happy," before retreating, her eyes suspiciously bright.

The evening unfolded in a flurry of speculation—Ryelands’ grandeur, Gadsmere’s lesser but respectable charms, the potential of future titles—all dissected under the Gascoignes’ approving guidance. Gwendolen, however, remained quiet, her thoughts a tempest beneath her composed surface.

That night, sleep eluded her. The weight of her decision pressed upon her like a physical thing. She had stepped onto a path from which there was no retreat, and though she craved the freedom and power marriage to Grandcourt would bring, doubt slithered in the shadows. The image of *that woman*—Mrs. Glasher—and her children haunted her. Yet she hardened herself against guilt. *He could have married her if he wished. He did not. That is not my burden to bear.*

By morning, resolve had settled over her like armor. When her mother entered with a small package—a velvet-lined casket containing a diamond ring and a letter from Grandcourt—Gwendolen’s spirits lifted.

She turned the enclosed check over in her hands—five hundred pounds, a kingly sum.

"How very kind," Mrs. Davilow murmured, though her pride prickled. "Still, I should prefer not to rely on a son-in-law’s charity."

Gwendolen’s smile sharpened. "Mamma, if you say that again, I shall call off the wedding."

She would not be swayed. She *would* marry him. And once she was his wife, she would see that Mrs. Glasher’s children were provided for. It was the least she could do—not out of remorse, but out of sheer practicality.

Dressed for riding, she descended the stairs, her mood buoyant. The thrill of the gallop ahead made her giddy. When Grandcourt arrived, she met him with a playful challenge, slipping the ring onto her finger with a flourish.

"You are very proud," she observed.

"And so am I. We shall suit each other perfectly."

He took her hand, his grip possessive. "When shall we marry?"

She pretended to consider. "Not until I’ve had my fill of riding. The hunting season begins in ten days."

"Then let us marry in ten days," he countered, "and you may hunt as my wife."

Gwendolen laughed, delighted by the game. "What do women usually say to that?"

"They agree."

"Then I shall not!" she declared, pulling away with a triumphant gleam in her eyes.

But the gallop sealed it. The wind, the speed, the sheer exhilaration—it swept away the last of her hesitation. By the time they returned, she had consented. Three weeks. She would be Mrs. Grandcourt.

Meanwhile, Lush, ever observant, noted the shifting tides. Grandcourt’s indifference had deepened, his reliance on Lush waning. When the dismissal came—polite but firm—Lush accepted it with smooth compliance. Three hundred a year and exile to London was hardly a fortune, but he was patient.

*She will not make him happy,* he thought. *And when he tires of her, he will need me again.*

As for Grandcourt, he spent the evening in solitary reflection, his thoughts as inscrutable as his expression. The future stretched before him—a game half-won, a possession secured.

And Gwendolen?

She dreamed of power, of freedom, of a life unchained from obscurity.

But dreams, as she would learn, were fragile things.

The rain had kept them indoors at Offendene, and Grandcourt, lounging on the sofa, watched Gwendolen with a lazy satisfaction. She had taken up embroidery since their engagement, though her stitches were more haphazard than precise.

“The carriage will come for your mother tomorrow,” he said. “You can see Diplow and decide what changes you’d like before we leave for Ryelands.”

Gwendolen let the needle pause. “Why must it be tomorrow?”

“Because the day after, the hunt begins.”

“And after that?”

“I’ll be away for two days.” His fingers brushed hers. “Do you mind?”

“It hardly matters if I do,” she said coolly, resisting the urge to demand if he was visiting *her*—the woman whose shadow lurked behind their engagement.

Grandcourt smiled, indulgent. “I could delay. Or travel by night and return sooner.”

“Then don’t delay,” she replied, enjoying her power even as irritation prickled beneath it.

He laughed softly. “How cruel you are to us poor men.”

“*Are* we so cruel?” She met his gaze, half-hoping he’d confess some vulnerability, some fault that might justify her own doubts.

“Always.” His voice dropped. “Are you as kind to me as I am to you?”

The question struck her. She had taken so much—his wealth, his name—and now she was bound to the chariot she’d chosen, its reins in his hands. There was no turning back.

She forced a smile. “If I were as kind as you, your generosity would mean less.”

He lifted her hand to his lips, leaving her oddly unsettled.

---

At Diplow the next day, Gwendolen moved through the rooms with deliberate distraction. She had resolved to ignore Deronda—until she saw him. Then, despite herself, her attention sharpened.

“You remember me, I hope?” he said when introduced.

“Too well,” she replied lightly. “You disapproved of my roulette.”

“Did I?” His steady gaze unsettled her.

“You brought me bad luck.”

Grandcourt scoffed. “Roulette is a bore.”

“It was, once I started losing.” She glanced at Deronda, catching an expression that pierced her more than any critique. There was no mockery in it—only a quiet intensity that made her feel *seen*.

Later, she found herself drifting toward him. “Do you hunt tomorrow?”

“If the weather allows.”

“And you don’t object to it?”

“Only when I can’t boat or cricket instead.”

“Do you object to *me* hunting?” she challenged.

“I’ve no right to object to anything you choose.”

“Yet you judged my gambling.”

The words slipped out, and color rose in her cheeks. Deronda’s own face flushed—whether from the reference to the necklace or something else, she couldn’t tell. The silence between them thickened, charged with something unspoken.

---

That night, Gwendolen lay awake, her thoughts circling Deronda. Who was he, really? A ward, a bastard, a man with no name yet an air of quiet command. She wondered if he pitied her, if he guessed at the shadows in her marriage.

Her mother’s offhand gossip about his birth gnawed at her. An unknown mother, a life of borrowed privilege—had he, too, been cheated of something? The idea twisted inside her, sharp as regret.

By morning, the thrill of the hunt pushed aside her unease. She would see him again, and this time, she wouldn’t falter.

But the chase swept them apart, and as twilight fell, frustration mounted. Then, on the road home, she reined in her horse and looked back.

“Go on,” she ordered Grandcourt. Then, with a flick of her whip toward Deronda: “I wish to speak with *him*.”

The words were defiance, a claiming of her own will. What she would say, she didn’t yet know. But for the first time, she meant to find out.

The house at Gadsmere stood weathered and uneven, its gray stone walls patched with red tile, a rounded tower jutting stubbornly from one corner. The conical roof, darkened with age, was crowned by a weathercock that turned indifferently in the wind, a silent witness to summers of green abundance and winters laced with frost. The grounds sprawled in unkempt grace—shade pooled beneath ancient trees, a great oak stretched its limbs wide, while nearby, the roots of Scotch firs clung desperately to crumbling earth. Beyond them, a rookery clamored; on the opposite side, a murky pool choked with overgrowth shuddered under the cries of waterfowl. The meadow, vast and untamed, might have been called a park if not for its neglect, bordered by a skeletal plantation and guarded by stone lodges as grim as sentinels.

Outside the gates, the land bore the scars of industry—coal mines blackened the horizon, their workers marked by soot and candlelight, figures of suspicion to the four children who played on the gravel drive. These were Mrs. Glasher’s children, beautiful and wild, who had known no home but Gadsmere for the past three years. Now, in the bleakness of November, with the flowerbeds barren and the trees stripped bare, the estate seemed to mourn alongside the soot-stained countryside—except when the laughter of the children rang out, defiant against the gloom.

Mrs. Glasher had long ceased to care for society’s judgment. The isolation of Gadsmere suited her, its unloveliness a shield against prying eyes. When she drove her ponies through the lanes, there were no fine carriages to meet, only tradesmen in gigs; at church, no whispers followed her, for the curate and his wife knew nothing—or pretended not to. To them, she was merely the widow who leased Gadsmere, a woman of no consequence compared to the colliery owners whose names held weight here.

Ten years had passed since the scandal of her elopement with Grandcourt—since the duel that had wounded nothing but reputations. Those who remembered wondered idly what had become of the dazzling Mrs. Glasher, the beauty who had once turned heads abroad. To them, Grandcourt’s disentanglement was natural; a man of his standing would, in time, seek a wife befitting his name. As for her, she had faded from memory like the victim of some long-forgotten crime.

Yet Grandcourt had never truly freed himself. His passion for her had once been consuming, and though it had long since dulled to indifference, habit and a strange inertia kept him bound. When her husband died three years prior, he had even toyed with the idea of marrying her, as they had once whispered in moments of ardor. But now, novelty had seized him—novelty in the form of Gwendolen Harleth.

Lydia Glasher was no fool. She had seen the shift in him, felt the cooling of his attention. She had stung pride, yes, but more than that—a mother’s desperation. Her children, their faces mirrors of her own lost beauty, deserved legitimacy, security. If Grandcourt married her, they would suffer no disgrace. Her son could bear his father’s name. It was for them, not herself, that she clung to the fraying thread of his regard.

She had learned patience, though it festered in her like a wound. No scenes, no weeping—only silence, and the slow poison of waiting.

When Grandcourt arrived that day, the children were gathered around her, their lessons half-attended, their dolls cradled in small arms. The boy, Henleigh, scowled at the intrusion; the girls shrank back, uneasy. Grandcourt’s greeting was perfunctory, his manner detached. Lydia felt the chill of it like a blade.

“You’ve come from Diplow?” she asked, her voice sharp with unspoken dread.

“Yes.” His answer was slow, indifferent.

She pressed, brittle as ice. “And you’re tired?”

“No.”

A pause. Then, deliberately, he shattered her.

“I’m to be married.”

The room swayed. The words were a death knell. She did not weep, did not rage—only turned to the window, pressing her forehead to the cold glass as if it might steady her. The children, sensing nothing, ran to her, their upturned faces bright with concern. She waved them away, her hands trembling.

Grandcourt watched, unmoved. He had come to deliver his verdict, not to witness her grief. When at last she faced him, her eyes burned.

“You wish to be rid of us.”

“Don’t make this harder than it needs to be,” he said coolly. “You’ll be provided for. The children want for nothing.”

Her laugh was bitter. “How generous.”

He ignored the jab. “You may stay here. Or I’ll settle funds for you to live elsewhere. The choice is yours.”

Choice. The word was a mockery. She had none, not truly.

The hours dragged. The children returned, their presence a cruel reminder of all she stood to lose. Grandcourt endured their company with detached courtesy, promising Henleigh a saddle, amusing little Antonia with his watch. Lydia watched, her heart a writhing thing.

When they were alone again, the candles casting long shadows, he finally spoke of the diamonds—her diamonds, the last tangible proof of what had once been between them.

“You have them,” he said, a statement, not a question.

“Yes.” She stood, her fingers laced tight.

“Where?”

“Not here.”

His gaze sharpened. “I thought you kept them close.”

She smiled, a brittle, knowing thing. “Not anymore.”

The air between them thickened. She had known this moment would come. Had prepared for it.

And yet, as she looked at him—this man who had been her ruin and her weak, enduring hope—she wondered if any preparation could armor her against the finality of his betrayal.

The morning of Gwendolen Harleth’s wedding dawned crisp and bright, the low sun glinting on frost-kissed leaves. The village of Pennicote turned out in force, lining the path to the church to catch a glimpse of the bridal procession. The rector, standing in as father to the bride, lent the occasion an air of solemn dignity, while the faces of only two—Mrs. Davilow and Anna—betrayed any sorrow.

Gwendolen, radiant in her bridal finery, carried herself with the poise of a woman born to grandeur. Observers murmured that she had the bearing of a lady of title, though some might have wished for more color in her cheeks to match the occasion. But Grandcourt, pale and composed, seemed to suit her perfectly. If there were whispers about his temperament—or hers—they were drowned in the day’s glittering spectacle.

*She will be happy,* the villagers assured one another. *How could she not be?*

Yet beneath Gwendolen’s dazzling composure, something thrummed like a taut string. She moved through the ceremony with the same reckless energy that had once carried her through a night of gambling—eyes bright, pulse racing, as if daring fate itself. Regret was a distant shadow, eclipsed by the sheer intoxication of the moment. She had won. She was *Mrs. Grandcourt*.

Her mother’s trembling hands and tearful embrace barely registered. Gwendolen laughed, kissed her, teased her for her sorrow. "Here I am—*Mrs. Grandcourt*," she declared, spreading her arms as if the name alone were a triumph.

And then she was gone, swept into the carriage beside her new husband, leaving behind the only life she had ever known.

---

The journey to Ryelands passed in a blur of chatter and forced gaiety. Gwendolen spoke incessantly, as if silence might betray her, while Grandcourt watched her with quiet fascination. But as the carriage rolled through the gates of the estate, her words faltered.

The house loomed ahead—stately, imposing, its windows aglow in the fading light. *Hers.* The thought should have elated her. Instead, something cold curled in her chest.

Grandcourt pressed a kiss to her lips as they crossed the threshold. She barely felt it.

Alone at last in her opulent boudoir, Gwendolen stared at her reflection in the gilded mirrors—a bride in emerald satin, surrounded by firelight and wealth. Then the housekeeper appeared, placing a small packet in her hands.

"A gift from Mr. Grandcourt," the woman murmured before withdrawing.

Gwendolen’s fingers trembled as she unwrapped it. The diamond case gleamed—but atop the jewels lay a letter.

The handwriting stopped her breath.

The words slithered into her like venom. Accusations. Curses. A promise of ruin. With a gasp, she flung the letter into the fire, watching it blacken and vanish. The diamonds spilled across the floor, winking maliciously up at her.

When Grandcourt entered, expecting a wife adorned and smiling, he found instead a woman white with terror, jewels scattered like shattered ice around her.

And then she screamed.

The sound tore through the grand house, sharp as broken glass.

Love has long been likened to a force beyond reason—a tempest that sweeps away all the careful philosophies a man might hold. Daphnis, lost in his madness, would have cared little for the wisdom of Heraclitus; Tristan, ensnared by a potion, could not have reasoned his way free; and Romeo, struck by Juliet’s beauty, would not have paused to debate the stars. Yet not all love is so blind. Some passions weave themselves into the very fabric of the soul, drawing meaning from unseen heavens, expanding to embrace the grandest hopes of what has been and what may yet be.

Upon his return to London, Deronda informed Sir Hugo that he had made Grandcourt an offer: fifty thousand pounds in exchange for relinquishing a future claim—distant, and not entirely secure. Grandcourt’s response had been neither refusal nor acceptance, only a continued pretense of amicable indifference.

“And what of the bride herself?” Sir Hugo inquired. “Did closer acquaintance improve your opinion?”

“It did. The roulette tables of Leubronn did not suit her; they brought out something harsh. At Diplow, she seemed softer—more alive, less armored. Even her eyes held a different light.”

Sir Hugo chuckled. “Take care, Dan. If you charm her too openly and provoke Grandcourt at Christmas, it will complicate my affairs.”

“Then I will remain in town.”

“Nonsense. Lady Mallinger and the children expect you. Only mind you don’t stir trouble—unless, of course, you arrange a duel and rid us of Grandcourt. That might be worth the inconvenience.”

Deronda frowned. “I do not flirt.”

“Oh? I’ve seen you gaze tenderly at women and speak to them as if you were a Jesuit confessor. You’re a dangerous creature—a Lovelace who makes Clarissas chase *him*.”

Deronda suppressed his irritation. What use was there in bristling at a tasteless jest? Yet the sting came before reason could dismiss it. Few things were more vexing than being accused of intentions one had never harbored. Sir Hugo’s idea of flirtation was, thankfully, peculiar. Still, Deronda was glad the baronet knew nothing of Gwendolen’s repurchased necklace—else the teasing would have been relentless.

He resolved to be more guarded in the future, particularly at Mrs. Meyrick’s, where he was to visit for the first time since Leubronn. For Mirah was a creature who inspired tenderness, and he did not trust himself to remain indifferent.

Mrs. Meyrick had written to him of Mirah’s well-being: *"We grow fonder of her each day. At breakfast, we watch for her entrance like children awaiting a fairy. She speaks no word that troubles me. She is grateful, eager to work—my girls adore her. Mab says our lives have turned to a tale of enchantment, and her only fear is that Mirah might vanish like a nightingale. Her voice is not loud, but it pierces the heart like memory itself."*

Yet the Meyricks had not shared everything. Amy and Mab, having accompanied Mirah to the synagogue, found themselves troubled by the separation of women behind a gallery rail.

“Does it not seem strange to you,” Amy ventured, “that the women sit apart?”

Mirah blinked in mild surprise. “It has always been so.”

“And the men keep their hats on?” Mab added cautiously.

“Yes. I love it because it feels like home.”

After that, the sisters held their tongues, unwilling to criticize what was so deeply woven into Mirah’s heart. Her faith was not doctrine but feeling—a part of her as natural as breath.

Later, alone with their mother, Amy mused, “She calls herself a bad Jewess. Perhaps if she never finds her mother, she might… drift toward Christianity?”

Mab groaned. “I wish *I* were a better Christian! How can an ugly, clumsy creature like me convert someone so perfect?”

Kate, ever practical, admitted, “I almost hope her mother is never found. There might be unpleasantness.”

Mrs. Meyrick shook her head. “I think Mirah is her mother’s likeness. What joy it would bring to have such a daughter restored! But then—” she teased, “mothers’ feelings are of little account. A dead one is worth more than a living, no?”

Kate laughed. “We would rather have you alive, even if we value you less.”

Deronda, though educated, knew little of modern Judaism. Like most, he had regarded it as an archaic relic—until Mirah, with her terror of one parent and longing for the other, showed him it was a living pulse in human veins. Now, he sought out synagogues, studied histories, and in Frankfurt, entered one for the first time.

The Judengasse had once been mere picturesque decay to him. Now, he saw the faces—living remnants of an ancient story. He asked after the old rabbinical school, was cheerfully misdirected by a young Jew, then overcharged for a book with a smile. Nearby, a trader bawled business in guttural Yiddish. Deronda had begun to fear vulgar Jews, but reflection softened his distaste. All men had their flaws.

The synagogue was humble, the congregation shabby, yet the chant of the *Chazan* moved him profoundly—Psalms rising like a river of memory. The swaying men, the boys’ pure voices, the very plainness of the place, all spoke of a faith that had shaped the world and now echoed faintly here. For a moment, he felt a connection deeper than reason.

Then the service ended. Faces turned indifferent, shuffling out. Had he alone felt the weight of history?

As he left, an elderly man with a white beard stopped him abruptly. “Your mother’s name—her family—what was it?”

Deronda stiffened. “I am English.”

The man studied him, then tipped his hat and walked away.

Uneasy, Deronda brushed it off—a stranger’s fancy. Yet it lingered, a shadow at the edge of thought.

That afternoon, he visited the Meyricks. Mirah, serene in her work, glowed with quiet joy.

“See how changed I am from the wretch by the river,” she said. “All because you found me.”

“Any man would have done the same.”

She shook her head. “No. It was *you*.”

Her certainty struck him. In her eyes, fate had chosen him. And perhaps, in ways he did not yet understand, it had.

Daniel Deronda had always been a man of quiet introspection, but lately his thoughts had turned toward the streets of London where the common folk of his own heritage lived and labored. He wandered through districts thick with the scent of bakeries and the murmur of Hebrew, past shops with signs in both English and Yiddish, searching—though for what, he could not precisely say.

It was not fashionable exercise, this roaming through Whitechapel and Holborn, but Deronda found himself drawn to it nonetheless. He visited synagogues at odd hours, observed faces in the crowd, and lingered by storefronts where men in worn coats haggled over prices. He told himself it was mere curiosity, a scholar’s interest in the lives of his fellow men. Yet beneath it all lay the unspoken question: *Could Mirah’s family be among them?*

The thought was absurd, of course. London was vast, and the name *Cohen* as common as Smith. Still, he could not help glancing at the names above shop doors, half-expecting—and half-dreading—to see *Ezra Cohen* etched in peeling paint. He had no real desire to find the man. If he did, what then? Would he be Mirah’s long-lost brother, or merely another stranger? And if he were her brother, what duty did that impose upon Deronda?

He had no answers, only the uneasy sense that life was pressing upon him with questions he was not yet ready to face.

One morning, as he turned down a narrow side street to escape the clamor of Holborn, his attention was caught by a pawnbroker’s window. Among the jumble of trinkets and watches, a set of silver clasps gleamed—fine antique things that might please Lady Mallinger. He paused, considering whether to enter, when the shop door opened and a man stepped out.

"Good day, sir," the man said, his voice warm, his face unmistakably Jewish.

Deronda returned the greeting but moved quickly away, crossing the street to hail a cab. As he did, his eyes flicked back to the shop sign: *Ezra Cohen.*

A coincidence, nothing more. There must be a hundred Ezra Cohens in London. And yet—

He spent the ride home arguing with himself. Even if this *were* Mirah’s brother, what right had he to interfere? And if he discovered the mother was dead, would it not be kinder to leave Mirah in ignorance? Yet the thought sat ill with him. She had spoken of her family with such longing. Did he have the right to withhold the truth, if truth there was?

The matter weighed on him, but before he could return to the shop, Sir Hugo demanded his attention. There were speeches to draft, debates to endure—political battles that Deronda found tiresome.

"You ought to be in Parliament," Sir Hugo declared one evening, after a particularly circular argument. "A man of your mind could make his mark."

Deronda shook his head. "I’ve no taste for borrowed opinions, sir."

"All the better! Originality is what’s needed. You’d stand out."

"Or stand alone," Deronda said dryly.

Sir Hugo laughed, but Deronda left the conversation feeling unsettled. Politics, like life, seemed a series of compromises—bending principle to expediency. And he was beginning to wonder if he had already bent too far.

Days later, he returned to the pawnbroker’s shop, this time entering with purpose. The interior was dim, crowded with the detritus of countless small tragedies—watches, jewelry, all the tokens of need or desperation. Behind the counter stood a woman, stout and cheerful, her face lined with years of trade.

"May I see those silver clasps?" Deronda asked.

She fetched them with brisk efficiency. "Three guineas, sir. Fine workmanship."

He examined them, though his attention was divided. A commotion at the back of the shop drew his eye—a young woman in a bright blue dress, a cluster of children around her. One boy, no older than six, marched up to Deronda with the solemnity of a merchant prince.

"What’s your name?" Deronda asked, amused.

"Jacob Alexander Cohen," the child announced.

"Not named after your father, then?"

"My grandfather," Jacob said importantly. "He sells knives."

Deronda smiled, but his chest tightened. The boy’s dark eyes, his easy confidence—was there a shadow of Mirah in him? The mother’s face, though worn, held traces of something finer beneath the years.

He paid for the clasps and left, his mind in turmoil. He had come no closer to answers, only to more questions.

And the greatest question of all remained: *What will I do if I find them?*

The shop was closed when Deronda arrived at five o'clock, the door opened by a Christian servant who ushered him into the back room. To his surprise, the scene before him was unexpectedly charming. The aging house stretched deeper than its modest storefront suggested, and the room, likely dim by day, was now warmly illuminated by an antique brass lamp casting its glow over a snow-white tablecloth. The walls bore the patina of time, smoke-darkened, but the dimness only heightened the vividness of the figures gathered there—each dressed with a touch of theatrical flair.

The grandmother sat regally in faded gold-brown, a heavy chain draped where a necklace might have been, her strong features framed by gray coils of hair. Young Mrs. Cohen was a striking contrast in red and black, her neck encircled by layers of faux pearls. The baby slept under a scarlet coverlet, while Adelaide Rebekah shimmered in amber braids, and little Jacob Alexander, ever the solemn merchant, wore black velveteen with scarlet stockings. Four pairs of bright eyes fixed on Deronda with such open warmth that he felt a pang of remorse for his earlier disdain.

The room itself was a curious mix—old oak furniture that spoke more of thrift than taste, a blue-and-yellow dish flanked by tarnished silver, and a massive vellum-bound book lying open as if waiting for study. Beyond, an inner room glowed softly, its door ajar.

Jacob wasted no time, marching up to demand his promised knife. Deronda produced it—a proper hook-bladed thing with a white handle—and the boy scrutinized it with the gravity of a seasoned trader, comparing it to his own cork-screw implement.

"Why a hook?" Deronda asked.

"'Cause it *catches* things," Jacob declared, as if the answer were obvious. "But you keep the cork-screw. Better for you."

The grandmother watched, delighted, as the negotiation concluded with a solemn exchange. Jacob, ever the strategist, eyed Deronda’s pockets for further treasures but was swiftly reined in.

Then Cohen entered, his hat still on, ignoring Deronda entirely as his children rushed to him. He laid hands on each small head, murmuring Hebrew blessings, and when his wife brought the sleeping baby forward, he stretched his palms over it as well. For a moment, the pawnbroker shed his brashness, and Deronda glimpsed something almost reverent in the ritual.

Once the benediction ended, Cohen became the brisk businessman again. "You’ve seen our hospitality," he said, clapping Deronda on the shoulder. "Now, the ring?"

Deronda handed it over, stating its value and his intent to reclaim it. Cohen’s eyes gleamed with shrewd assessment before he named a lower sum—forty pounds—which Deronda accepted with a nod.

Dinner was announced, the table set with loaves sprinkled in seed, a nod to ancient manna. But before the meal began, Cohen called out, **"Mordecai!"**

The name startled Deronda. And then—there he was. The same intense, gaunt man from the bookshop that morning. Their eyes met, recognition flickering, but Mordecai offered only a cold nod before taking his seat. He wore the same threadbare coat, though cleaner now, his dark gaze burning with an unsettling focus.

The meal passed with chatter of royals and politics, the Cohens eager to display their worldly knowledge. Deronda listened politely, but his attention kept drifting to Mordecai, who ate in silence, his portion meager—fish tails, the scraps reserved for the poor relation.

When the time came for prayers, Mordecai’s voice rose in Hebrew, a haunting chant that filled the room with something deeper than ritual. The others fidgeted; Deronda watched, transfixed. Then, as abruptly as he had appeared, Mordecai stood, bowed slightly, and withdrew to his room.

**"A peculiar man,"** Deronda remarked after.

Cohen tapped his temple meaningfully. **"Charity keeps him. He teaches the boy, mends watches. But between us—"** He shrugged. **"Not all there."**

Deronda left soon after, forty pounds heavier, the pawn ticket tucked away like an excuse to return. He would come back—not for the ring, but for Mordecai. There were questions unanswered, mysteries in that hollow-cheeked face. And as for the Cohens? Well. There was still the matter of why one must never ask about Mrs. Cohen’s missing daughter.

The weight of conscience is not measured by the magnitude of one’s sins, for if it were, history would tell a different tale. The architects of war and the predators of wealth would march in penitence alongside common thieves and murderers. Wickedness may offer its rewards, but to claim them, a man must harden himself beyond the limits of human frailty—must shed the shadow of justice, the flicker of pity that clings to most souls born of woman. Few achieve such serene corruption, for our flesh is too tangled with remnants of conscience to sustain it.

On the twenty-ninth of December, Deronda learned of the Grandcourts’ arrival at the Abbey. He had seen nothing of them before dressing for dinner, having spent the afternoon in the snow with the Mallinger children, their laughter ringing as they built forts and hurled snowballs. Later, a game of billiards had distracted him, so that the prospect of meeting Gwendolen at dinner had not preoccupied him—until now, as he stood before his mirror, adjusting his cravat.

What would marriage to Grandcourt have done to her? Would the change be visible already? He had seen her evolve once before—from the reckless enchantress at Leubronn to the subdued woman at Diplow. Some natures, he mused, wear their transformations plainly, and Gwendolen was not one to forget an impression. The sting of the necklace incident, the shame of being judged at the gaming table—such things had marked her. But sensitivity cuts both ways. It might drive a soul to redemption as easily as ruin.

And Grandcourt? What allure could that man possibly hold in daily life? Deronda had never seen passion stir beneath that polished exterior. A man so devoid of warmth might as well be carved of stone. Had she married him out of ambition? Out of fear of poverty? Or had she been pushed into it? The thought of her—vibrant, unspent, lashed to that hollow remnant of a man—filled him with pity.

Of course, it was the way of the world to pity the bride when a notable marriage took place. Young men, especially, assumed no woman could truly love a dull husband, that she must have been compelled by some lesser motive. Who spared a thought for Grandcourt? Even the ladies who might have defended him likely considered his fate just: he should have chosen better. But Deronda found it hard to muster sympathy for a man who seemed incapable of suffering, while Gwendolen—young, impulsive, bred on flattery and the illusion of command—might already be tasting the ashes of regret.

The dinner party was a gathering of varied consequence: old aristocracy in Lord and Lady Pentreath, the gentry in the Fitzadams, politics in Mr. Fenn and his cider interests. Lady Mallinger’s family completed the scene, along with the requisite bachelors—Mr. Sinker, the barrister, and Mr. Vandernoodt, the well-connected Dutchman who knew everything about everyone without ever being remarkable himself.

Deronda found himself cornered by Vandernoodt, who was eager to share his gleanings on Grandcourt.

"Men of his experience don’t always choose wives wisely," Vandernoodt murmured. "He’s dabbled deep in pleasures, for all his laziness. But then, you must know more of him than I do."

"Hardly," Deronda replied. "I know little beyond his being Sir Hugo’s nephew."

The door opened then, sparing him further speculation.

The Grandcourts entered, and the room seemed to arrange itself around them. Grandcourt was as impeccably lifeless as ever, his face a mask of ennui. Gwendolen, however, was transformed—not diminished, but heightened. The white silk and diamonds (astonishingly lavish for a bride of three weeks) lent her a new regality. Yet when their eyes met, Deronda detected no warmth, only a cool acknowledgment before she turned away.

At dinner, he sat near enough to catch fragments of her conversation with Sir Hugo, who was in high spirits.

"Deronda," Sir Hugo called, "you’ll want to hear Mrs. Grandcourt’s thoughts on your friend Klesmer."

Gwendolen’s lashes flickered as if pained before she lifted them, offering Deronda a smile that never touched her eyes.

"The Arrowpoints have forgiven the marriage," Sir Hugo continued. "He’s at Quetcham for Christmas."

"I’m glad for his wife’s sake," Deronda said. "Though I doubt Klesmer would have minded exile otherwise."

"It’s rather romantic, isn’t it?" Lady Pentreath remarked.

"Romance survives even in our practical age," Sir Hugo laughed.

Gwendolen’s voice cut in, light but edged. "I’m sure Herr Klesmer considers himself immortal. No doubt his wife will burn enough incense to satisfy him."

There was a beat of silence before Sir Hugo chuckled. "You don’t approve of wives worshipping their husbands?"

"Oh, I do," Gwendolen amended swiftly, "if only to persuade others to believe in them." A pause, then sharper still: "When he admires himself, at least her ‘Amen’ will make it less absurd."

Deronda studied her. This bitterness—was it for Klesmer, or for herself?

Later, as he played the piano, he noticed Gwendolen drift away, pausing before an ivory carving. Seizing a moment, he approached.

She turned, and the mask slipped. In her eyes was something raw—an unguarded sorrow that silenced him. For a breath, they simply looked at one another, as if words were unnecessary.

"Will you not join the music?" he finally asked.

She recovered swiftly. "I only listen now. I’ve given up singing."

"Why? If it brings you joy—"

"Joy?" Her laugh was brittle. "Mediocrity doesn’t suit me. I’d rather be silent than middling."

He smiled. "I find contentment in my own mediocrity. It’s a pardonable sin, so long as I don’t pretend otherwise."

Her gaze flickered. "You’re wiser than I am, then. I find the world unbearably dull. Gambling, at least, was an escape."

"The dullness may be in ourselves," he said gently.

"Ah, so the fault is mine?" She smiled, but it didn’t reach her eyes. "Tell me—do you never hate those who stand in your way?"

"Rarely. We’re often in each other’s paths without malice."

"But if they harm you willingly?"

He met her intensity with quiet resolve. "Then I still prefer my own place to theirs."

For a moment, something like relief crossed her face. Then, with a laugh too bright, she turned away.

Deronda watched her go, wondering what wounds lay beneath the diamonds, what truths she would not—or could not—speak.

The evening air was thick with unspoken truths as Daniel Deronda found himself drawn into the orbit of Mr. Vandernoodt, a man whose appetite for gossip was as insatiable as his love for cigars. There was something perversely satisfying, Deronda supposed, in sharing secrets about a man who would never hear them—especially when that man was as inscrutable as Henleigh Grandcourt.

“Grandcourt is a dull specimen, isn’t he?” Vandernoodt remarked, exhaling a lazy curl of smoke. “But if he’s a friend of yours, I’ll retract the observation.”

“Not in the slightest,” Deronda replied coolly.

“Didn’t think so. One wonders what could have driven him to such a passionate match. Though Lush—his ever-loyal shadow—hints it was sheer obstinacy. A peculiar sort of obstinacy, wouldn’t you say? Most men wouldn’t need persuasion to marry a beauty like Miss Harleth. Still, the affair must have cost him a fortune.”

“I wouldn’t know anything of his finances.”

“Truly? Not even of his *other* establishment?”

Deronda remained silent. He had no wish to pry, yet the implication coiled around his thoughts like smoke.

Vandernoodt, undeterred, leaned in. “Gadsmere. Surely Sir Hugo knows of it.”

Still, Deronda said nothing. If there was more to be told, it would come without his prompting.

“Lush won’t admit to it, of course,” Vandernoodt continued. “But I’ve heard it from reliable sources. There’s a woman there—Mrs. Glasher—with four children. She’s held sway over him for a decade, left her husband for him, traveled with him everywhere. Now that the husband’s dead, she’s still there, still ruling him. A striking woman, they say—dark-eyed, once a great beauty. Odd he didn’t marry her, given she’s borne him a son, and he’s master of his inheritance.”

Deronda’s lips thinned. “What right had he to marry Miss Harleth, then?”

Vandernoodt shrugged, amused. “*She* couldn’t have known.”

But the words struck Deronda like a blow. *Could she?*

The conversation shifted, but the seed had been planted. Deronda’s mind, ever attuned to hidden sorrows, began to weave possibilities. Had Gwendolen discovered the truth before her marriage? Was that the shadow behind her carefully composed smiles? The thought of her—proud, willful Gwendolen—trapped in a union built on deception twisted something in him.

He excused himself, seeking solitude in the dimming light of the drawing-room, but fate—or perhaps his own restless conscience—led him to where Gwendolen stood by the piano, her fingers tracing the edges of sheet music with absent tension.

“Have you reconsidered my recommendation?” he asked gently.

She turned, her expression softening. “Perhaps. If Miss Lapidoth is as remarkable as you say, I might endure the humiliation of lessons—if only to witness excellence.”

He smiled. “You would find her more than a teacher. She’s known suffering, yet carries grace in spite of it.”

Gwendolen’s gaze sharpened. “What sort of suffering?”

“Enough that she once stood at the edge of despair.”

“And what stopped her?”

“Some conviction that life was still worth living.”

Gwendolen’s laugh was brittle. “How noble. I’ve no patience for women who are always *good*. I don’t believe in their sorrows.”

Deronda studied her, the tension in her hands, the defiance in her voice masking something far deeper. “Remorse,” he said quietly, “cuts sharper than innocence.”

Her eyes flickered, wounded. For a moment, it seemed she might speak—but then Sir Hugo’s jovial interruption scattered the fragile intimacy.

The evening wore on, but Deronda’s thoughts lingered on Gwendolen—on the secrets she might carry, the choices she had made. And when the New Year’s Eve ball arrived, he watched her glide through the crowd, resplendent yet restless, her turquoise necklace hidden beneath the glitter of obligation.

She was a woman playing a part, just as he was. And in that, at least, they understood each other.

Hans Meyrick’s studio was in a state of cheerful chaos when Deronda arrived. Sketches and canvases littered every surface, the lower half of the windows darkened with baize to control the light. Hans himself, his hair longer than Deronda remembered, his face alight with enthusiasm, greeted him with the same infectious energy that had always characterized their friendship.

“You’ve caught me in the thick of it,” Hans said, gesturing to the mess around them. “I’ve commandeered your sitting room as a temporary atelier—hope you don’t mind. I’ll be moving to Chelsea soon, but until then, the work doesn’t stop.”

Deronda smiled, accustomed to Hans’s whirlwind presence. “What’s all this?” he asked, eyeing a series of sketches pinned along the wall.

“My *Berenice* series,” Hans declared, with a flourish. “Five scenes from the life of the Jewish queen—tragedy, passion, political intrigue. It’s going to be magnificent.”

Deronda studied the drawings. The central figure—Berenice—bore an unmistakable resemblance to Mirah. His chest tightened, though he kept his voice even. “You’ve taken liberties with history.”

“Artistic license,” Hans said, unrepentant. “Berenice was a woman of fire, and Mirah has that same intensity beneath her quiet surface.”

“Does Mirah know you’re using her likeness?”

“Of course. She even posed for me.”

Deronda’s fingers curled slightly at his sides. “And she approves?”

Hans waved a hand. “She understands the vision. Besides, who’s to see them? They’re for my own satisfaction—unless some enlightened patron discovers my genius.”

Deronda exhaled slowly. “Even privately, it’s a risk. Mirah’s position is precarious. She’s trying to establish herself as a singer, to reclaim her independence. Having her face tied to a historical figure—especially one with such a fraught legacy—could complicate things.”

Hans tilted his head, studying Deronda as if seeing him anew. “You’re protective of her.”

“I’m responsible for her,” Deronda corrected, though the distinction felt thin.

Hans’s grin was knowing. “Ah, but responsibility can wear many coats. Some lined with devotion, others with something warmer.”

Deronda’s jaw tightened. “Don’t be absurd.”

Hans held up his hands. “Peace, peace. I only mean that if you’re guarding her as a ward, I’m hardly a threat. If you’re guarding her for yourself—”

“There’s nothing to guard *for myself*,” Deronda interrupted, sharper than he intended.

Hans’s expression softened, almost pitying. “Then you won’t mind if I confess that I’m hopelessly in love with her.”

The words landed like a blow, though Deronda had suspected as much. He forced himself to shrug. “A passing fancy. You’ve had dozens.”

“Not like this.” Hans’s usual levity was gone, replaced by a rare earnestness. “I know the odds. She’s Jewish; I’m not. She’s devout; I’m irreverent. But if there’s even a sliver of a chance—”

“There isn’t.”

Hans arched a brow. “You sound very certain.”

Deronda turned away, feigning interest in another sketch. “Mirah’s faith is unshakable. She would never marry outside it.”

“Then I’ll convert,” Hans said lightly, though his eyes were serious.

Deronda rounded on him. “This isn’t a joke.”

“No,” Hans agreed. “It isn’t.”

Silence stretched between them, thick with unspoken challenge. Finally, Deronda said, “You’ll only hurt yourself.”

Hans smiled, though it didn’t reach his eyes. “That’s my privilege.”

---

Later, at the Meyrick home, Mirah’s laughter filled the parlor—a sound so rare it felt like a gift. She was recounting one of Hans’s theatrical impersonations, her hands animating the story.

“He becomes a dozen characters in a minute,” she said, her eyes bright. “One moment a tragic poet, the next a clown. It’s impossible not to laugh.”

Mrs. Meyrick beamed. “Hans has always had that effect. Even as a boy, he could turn tears into laughter.”

Mirah’s expression softened. “You’re fortunate to have such memories. I’ve often wondered what it would be like—to have a family that shares stories, that remembers together.”

Deronda found himself speaking before he could stop. “So have I.”

The admission hung in the air, a quiet acknowledgment of the voids they both carried. Mirah met his gaze, and for a fleeting moment, something unnameable passed between them—an understanding, a recognition.

Then the moment passed, and the conversation moved on. But Deronda couldn’t shake the feeling that something had shifted, something as delicate and irreversible as the first crack in glass.

There are those who believe the deeper tragedy lies not in Prometheus *after* he has stolen the celestial fire, but *before*—when he is shackled by the tyranny of convention, thrust into isolation by poverty and illness, left to pulse with ideas that the world ignores. Such solitude is a wasteland where many pass, but none truly see.

Visionaries walk among us—those whose thoughts take shape as vivid premonitions, whose hungers and fears bloom into prophecy. They are not mad, nor less rational than the calculating minds of the marketplace. Some souls are like Thebes of a hundred gates, open to a flood of impressions that narrower minds could never bear. And though the world dismisses them—though it locks them away like curiosities—they are the ones who strain against the boundaries of the possible.

Mordecai was such a man.

Consumed by illness, yet burning with purpose, he had spent years searching for a kindred spirit—a mind that could receive the legacy of his thoughts before his own flame guttered out. He did not hope for recovery; his hope was fiercer, stranger. He longed to pour his soul into another, to see his beliefs take root in fresher soil.

This was no idle dream. It was a conviction, sharpened by time. He had measured men with a scholar’s eye, seeking one unlike himself: a Jew, yes—learned, fervent—but also strong, beautiful, unmarked by the wear of hardship. He imagined a face carved from the noble portraits of old masters, a voice like deep water, a life unbroken by want.

London’s bridges became his sanctuary. At dawn or dusk, he would stand at Blackfriars, the river flowing beneath him like time itself, the city’s silhouette a vast scripture of human striving. Here, his vision took shape—a figure emerging from the golden haze of sunrise, dark against the light. At first, it was only a silhouette, a promise. Then, slowly, it gained features: youth, refinement, a gravity that spoke of depth.

This was no mere fancy. It was necessity.

When little Jacob, bright-eyed and restless, climbed into his lap, Mordecai would recite Hebrew verses, willing the words to take root in the boy’s mind like seeds. *"Shatter my heart, O Gabriel,"* he murmured, *"as the founder shatters clay to free the golden image."* But Jacob, ever impatient, would squirm away—flipping coins, mimicking street performers—until Mordecai’s voice cracked with frustration. Once, in a surge of despair, he seized the child’s shoulders and hissed a curse upon his heedless generation. Then, just as quickly, remorse flooded him. He pulled Jacob close, soothing him as a father would.

Yet the longing did not fade.

And then—Deronda appeared.

A stranger, yet not a stranger. His face was the one Mordecai had carried in his mind for years. When he learned Deronda was not a Jew, the blow was brutal. But the vision would not die. It lingered, persistent as prophecy, woven into the fabric of his faith.

Now, in the grip of winter, Mordecai waited. He no longer had patience for teaching Jacob or debating in dim-lit clubs. Something was coming. He felt it in the air, in the restless pulse of his blood.

All he wanted was to stand once more by the river, beneath the wide sky, and watch the water carry the light—and the future—toward him.

*"To stand before the knowing—this is sure in every case!
When long you’ve struggled, he can tell at once where lies your grace.
And you may hope for praise as well, for he can mark the perfect place."*

That evening, after the discussion about Mirah’s name, something of great consequence occurred in Deronda’s life. But for the Meyrick household, the most significant event unfolded two days later.

At four o’clock, the sound of wheels and a firm knock at the door—one of those assertive raps that seem to announce a visitor of importance—startled the quiet Chelsea home. All the Meyrick girls were gathered in the parlor, the rooms opened into one another to accommodate Kate’s sketching and an expanse of embroidery stretched across the space, a communal project that had replaced the satin cushions. Mrs. Meyrick bent over one end, Mab in the middle, Amy at the other, while Mirah, whose sewing skills were rudimentary at best, sat on a camp-stool, reading aloud from Charles Lamb’s *"The Praise of Chimney-Sweeps."* Her voice, clear and melodious, had just delivered the line about *"innocent blackness"* when the knock came, and five faces lifted in surprise.

"Good heavens!" exclaimed Mrs. Meyrick. "Not Lady Mallinger? Amy, do you see a carriage?"

"Only a hansom cab. It must be a gentleman."

"The Prime Minister, no doubt," said Kate dryly. "Hans says even the greatest men in London ride in hansoms."

Mab gasped. "What if it’s Lord Russell?"

They were still laughing when the maid entered, flustered, leaving the door ajar. The figure who bowed to Mrs. Meyrick was unmistakably *not* the Prime Minister—tall, imposing, with a mane of flamboyant hair, gold spectacles, and an air of grandeur. The card in his hand declared him: *Julius Klesmer.*

Mrs. Meyrick, ever quick-witted, recovered at once, pleased that so illustrious a personage had come to Mirah rather than summoning her. But as he stepped inside, the room seemed to shrink. The cottage piano looked absurdly small; the cozy family life suddenly felt as insignificant as mice nesting in a palace. Klesmer’s very presence suggested vast concert halls and adoring crowds—an effect not lost on Mab, who flushed under his sweeping gaze.

He was not a man for false modesty. His vanity was no greater than many a solemn gentleman’s, though far more honest. Every gesture was natural to him, as unstudied as the length of his fingers. And yet, beneath the grandeur, there was kindness. He remembered humble beginnings himself.

With utmost deference, he addressed Mrs. Meyrick. "I hope I do not intrude. Being nearby, I thought to save time by calling. Our mutual friend, Mr. Deronda, mentioned I might have the honor of meeting Miss Lapidoth here."

His eyes had found Mirah the moment he entered, but with polished courtesy, he glanced at each sister as if uncertain which was the singer.

Mrs. Meyrick gestured. "My daughters—and this is Miss Lapidoth."

"Ah," said Klesmer, his face alight. He bowed deeply to Mirah, who met his gaze with calm pleasure. She liked him already—sensing he would be both a stern critic and a generous mentor.

"Shall we dispense with formalities?" he said. "I would gladly hear you sing."

Mirah rose at once. "I would be honored. It is kind of you to listen."

She moved to the piano. "Shall I accompany myself?"

"By all means," said Klesmer, seating himself where he could watch her.

The others held their breath. Klesmer’s listening frown was formidable. Only Mirah seemed unshaken, her composure unbroken as she began a setting of Leopardi’s *Ode to Italy*—first the solemn recitative, then a mournful lament, and finally a triumphant allegro that filled the room with fervor.

When she finished, Klesmer was silent a moment before remarking, "That is old Leo’s work."

"Yes. My last master in Vienna. Fierce, but kind. He told me my voice was not for the stage."

"*Con*tinue," Klesmer said abruptly, waving his fingers in a way that suggested he was muttering to himself. The girls exchanged glances—why wouldn’t he praise her?

Obligingly, Mirah launched into Gretchen’s songs from *Faust*, one after another. When she finished, Klesmer rose, paced, then turned back to her with sudden warmth.

"Let us shake hands. You are a musician."

Mab nearly cried. The girls adored him. Mrs. Meyrick exhaled in relief.

But then the frown returned. He tapped Mirah’s hand lightly. "Not for grand stages. No soaring rooftops. We are not skylarks. Modesty suits us."

Mirah waited, unoffended. Klesmer continued, "You will do very well in private drawing rooms. Lessons will follow. Would you sing at a concert in my home on Wednesday?"

Mirah clasped her hands. "Gladly. I would rather earn my bread this way than in public halls."

"Good. I shall introduce you to Astorga—he understands voices. And my wife will call before Wednesday."

Mrs. Meyrick beamed. "We are grateful."

Klesmer turned back to Mirah. "You will sing for her. She has a soul with more ears than most musicians." Then, with a playful nod, "You are no angel—you are a pretty Jewess. But I think you have found a guardian angel."

With that, he bowed himself out.

The moment the door shut, the room erupted. Mab danced; Mrs. Meyrick kissed Mirah; Amy fretted over finding a new dress by Wednesday. Only Mirah sat quietly, tears slipping down her cheeks.

"Now, now," Mrs. Meyrick said, settling them. "Hans will be here soon. Let us discuss the dress."

Mirah stood. "This black merino will do, with white gloves and new shoes."

Just then, Hans arrived.

"Give us your opinion," Mrs. Meyrick said. "Mirah is to sing at Klesmer’s. She thinks this dress sufficient."

Hans studied her critically. "It would do for a stage Jewess, but not for a private concert. She must not look like a charity case."

Mirah protested, "But this is what I am—a Jewess. I don’t pretend."

Hans softened. "To us, you are simply *you*." Then, firmly, "No. She needs proper evening wear—black silk, like any lady."

"Deronda would approve," Mrs. Meyrick mused.

Mirah nodded. "Then I shall do as he would wish."

Hans turned away abruptly, picking up one of Kate’s sketches.

Kate teased, "Shouldn’t you draw Klesmer’s head?"

Hans grinned, then mimed the maestro at the piano with such precision that even Mirah laughed—though her smile held something deeper than amusement.

"Thank you," she said softly. "You have never displeased me."

And in that moment, Hans dared to hope.

The narrow streets of Chelsea seemed to press in on Deronda as he stepped out into the fading afternoon light, his mind restless, his spirit unsettled. The encounter with Mordecai the day before had left him with an unshakable curiosity—an urgency to understand the man whose fervent gaze seemed to pierce through the ordinary fabric of life.

He needed movement, the rhythm of exertion to steady his thoughts. The river called to him, its shifting currents a mirror to his own uncertainty. Instead of hailing a cab, he turned toward the water, where a wherry waited at the Chelsea Stairs. The oarsman nodded as Deronda took his seat, and soon the boat was cutting through the Thames, the city’s skyline retreating behind them.

The physical labor of rowing was a relief, the strain of muscle replacing the churn of unanswered questions. But even as his body warmed to the effort, his mind circled back to Mordecai—his strange intensity, his conviction that Deronda was somehow part of a destiny he himself did not yet see.

*Am I only seeking him for my own purposes?* The thought pricked at him. He had come to London with Mirah’s plea in his heart, her brother’s fate tangled in his own sense of duty. Yet Mordecai’s words had stirred something deeper, a resonance he could not ignore.

The sky above the river was a shifting tapestry—gray yielding to gold, the clouds breaking into streaks of violet and saffron. The water reflected the dying light in flickers of bronze and amber, the world alight with a transient beauty.

Then, as the wherry neared Blackfriars Bridge, Deronda’s gaze was drawn upward. A figure stood at the parapet, silhouetted against the luminous sky—a figure he knew at once.

Mordecai.

The man’s face, illuminated by the western glow, was etched with a hunger that was more than physical—a yearning that seemed to reach beyond the limits of his frail body. His eyes locked onto Deronda’s, and before a word could be spoken, he lifted a hand in recognition, as though this meeting had been foretold.

Deronda signaled back, his pulse quickening. The boat touched the shore, and in moments he stood before Mordecai, who greeted him with a quiet certainty.

“I was coming to find you,” Deronda said.

“I knew you would,” Mordecai replied, as though this were the simplest truth in the world.

There was no hesitation in his voice, no doubt. To him, their meeting was inevitable.

Deronda, though moved, felt the weight of responsibility settle upon him. He did not yet understand the bond Mordecai believed in, but he could not dismiss the raw sincerity in the man’s gaze.

“Let me take you where you need to go,” Deronda offered, mindful of Mordecai’s labored breath.

“To the bookshop,” Mordecai said. “But first, look.” He turned back toward the river, where the last light clung to the water. “This bridge has always been a place of meeting for me—between earth and sky, past and future. And now, you are here.”

There was something sacred in the way he spoke, as though Deronda’s arrival completed a long-awaited prophecy.

They rode in silence through the city, the clatter of hooves and wheels a counterpoint to the quiet intensity between them. When they reached the bookshop, the proprietor, Ram, soon departed, leaving them alone among the shelves.

The gaslight flickered over Mordecai’s gaunt features as he faced Deronda, his eyes burning with an almost prophetic fire.

“You cannot yet know why I have waited for you,” he said. “But I will tell you.”

Deronda listened, his skepticism tempered by an unwillingness to shatter the fragile hope in Mordecai’s voice. The man spoke of visions, of a heritage that stretched across centuries, of a soul reborn to carry the weight of a people’s longing.

“I have been a voice crying in the wilderness,” Mordecai murmured. “But you—you are the answer.”

The claim was staggering. Deronda, though moved, felt the danger of false promises.

“You place too much faith in me,” he said gently. “I am not of your blood. I may not be who you believe me to be.”

Mordecai’s grip tightened on his arm. “You are unsure of your own past,” he insisted, as though this uncertainty only confirmed his certainty. “But I *know*.”

The conviction in his voice was unshakable.

Deronda exhaled slowly. He could not dismiss the depth of Mordecai’s need, nor the strange pull he felt toward this man who saw him as something more than he knew himself to be.

*What if he is right?* The question lingered, unanswered.

For now, there was only this: two men in a dimly lit shop, bound by something neither fully understood—one with a vision of the future, the other with the power to shape it.

And the night stretched before them, heavy with possibility.

The evening after his meeting with Mordecai, Deronda found himself caught in a storm of thought—not the idle musings of a man easily stirred, but the deep, restless questioning of a mind that felt too much and examined everything. For a man of duller sensibilities, the encounter might have been nothing more than an odd interruption, soon forgotten. But Deronda was not such a man. The intensity of Mordecai’s conviction, the fire in his words, had left an imprint. And now, in the quiet of reflection, Deronda wrestled with it.

He was not a man to dismiss what had moved him, nor to mock the gravity of an experience simply because the world might scoff. There were circles where earnestness was ridiculed as self-importance, where feeling deeply was a mark of weakness. Deronda had no patience for such cowardice. Yet neither would he surrender his judgment to mere emotion. He would not let himself be swept away by another man’s fervor without reason.

He could almost hear Sir Hugo’s dry summation: *A dying Jew, half-mad with fanaticism, has fastened onto you as the fulfillment of some wild prophecy. Unusual, yes—but not unprecedented. Men have always been seized by visions, convinced they alone hold the key to some grand truth. Some believe they’ve unlocked the secrets of the universe; others, that they’ve been chosen to redeem a lost cause. Mordecai is only one more in that lineage—a little more poetic, perhaps, but no less deluded.*

The argument was sound. Deronda knew the dangers of blind belief. History was littered with martyrs to error as much as to truth. A man who stood immovable in his convictions might be a Galileo—or he might be a fool chasing perpetual motion. The line between inspiration and delusion was perilously thin.

Yet Deronda hesitated. To dismiss Mordecai outright felt like a betrayal—not of the man, but of something deeper. He had always been drawn to the unseen currents of life, the hidden connections between souls. What if Mordecai’s certainty, however fevered, held some fragment of truth? What if beneath the wild fervor lay a genuine call—not just for Mordecai, but for himself?

The thought was dizzying. He had spent his life unmoored, a man without a clear inheritance. The mystery of his birth had haunted him since boyhood. If by some twist of fate he *was* of Jewish blood, what then? Would it reshape his purpose, his duty? The idea was not without its allure—there was a romance in the unknown, the possibility of a destiny waiting to be claimed.

Yet he could not embrace it blindly. He owed Mordecai honesty, not false hope. If the bond between them was illusion, then to indulge it would only deepen the eventual pain. But if there was truth in Mordecai’s vision, then to turn away might be to deny his own path.

For now, uncertainty was his refuge. He would not hasten to unravel the mystery of his birth, nor would he reject the strange pull Mordecai had on him. Time would reveal what was real. Until then, he would listen, watch, and let the future unfold as it must.

Four days would pass before he could return to Ezra Cohen’s, before he could see Mordecai again. Four days of turning the question over in his mind.

And the question remained: *What if the improbable was true?*

*"If suffering has its ranks, then Israel stands foremost among nations—if endurance ennobles, then the Jews are the aristocracy of every land. What is a literature with a handful of tragedies compared to a people who have lived one for fifteen centuries, where the poets and actors were the heroes themselves?"*

—**Zunz**, *The Synagogal Poetry of the Middle Ages*

Deronda had been turning these words over in his mind as he made his way to the Cohens’ home, a place that bore no outward mark of sorrow or nobility. Ezra Cohen was no tragic martyr; his life was one of bustling commerce, his success in pawnbroking a far cry from the suffering of his ancestors. Yet within this unremarkable household, Mordecai lived—frail, intense, a flickering ember of something far greater.

The family greeted Deronda with eager warmth. Cohen, ever the businessman, managed to imply that Deronda’s return was worth more than any profit from a delayed sale. Young Mrs. Cohen fretted over whether the baby was awake, then brightened when she remembered Adelaide was still up. Deronda, prepared with small gifts—paper dolls for the girl, a cup-and-ball for Jacob—was ushered into the parlor where the grandmother presided over a card game with the children.

Jacob, ever exuberant, demanded Deronda’s attention for his latest feat: tossing a plate without breaking it. Deronda obliged, exchanging an amused glance with the grandmother before settling in. He noted the closed door to the workroom where Mordecai had last appeared, biding his time before mentioning him.

Only when Adelaide was perched on his knee, the paper figures dancing under his fingers, did he ask, *"Is Mordecai in?"*

Cohen, slipping in from the shop, gestured vaguely toward the closed door. *"Truth be told, sir, he’s been odd these past days—like a man walking in his sleep, or else sitting still as a statue. Not that he’s ever what you’d call steady in the head, but this is different."*

*"It’s the illness,"* the grandmother murmured. *"I fear he hasn’t much time left."*

*"Or it’s something on his mind,"* Mrs. Cohen countered. *"He’s been scribbling and staring like a man possessed."*

Cohen shrugged. *"We’re soft on him, I’ll admit. But my wife and mother wouldn’t turn him out if he were twice the burden. Principle, you see."*

*"He carries a blessing with him,"* the grandmother insisted.

*"No, he’s sick inside,"* Jacob corrected matter-of-factly. *"He wouldn’t even eat his bun."*

Deronda felt a pang. *"I’ve spoken with him before—at Ram’s bookshop. I promised to meet him here tonight."*

*"Ah! That explains it!"* Cohen clapped his knee. *"He’s been waiting for you. Talks to you about his learning, does he? Kind of you to humor him—not that there’s profit in it."*

Jacob, ever helpful, offered, *"I’ll fetch him!"* But his mother shushed him, gesturing for Deronda to go himself.

The workroom was dim, lit only by a guttering candle and the faint glow of dying embers. Mordecai sat motionless at a bench, his back to the door, hands resting on a watchmaker’s stand. At Deronda’s voice—*"I’ve come for you. Are you ready?"*—he turned without a word, seized his cap, and rose.

The change in him was immediate. In the parlor, Jacob thrust his cup-and-ball under Mordecai’s nose, triumphant. *"Look! I can do it!"*

Mordecai smiled faintly. *"Fine, fine."*

Mrs. Cohen fretted over his coat and scarf, and Cohen, ever the optimist, declared, *"See? I told you he’d perk up!"*

As they stepped into the night, Deronda said, *"The Cohens are fond of you."*

*"And I of them,"* Mordecai replied. *"They have the heart of Israel, though they walk in narrow paths."*

*"I’ve troubled you with my delay,"* Deronda admitted.

*"I trusted you,"* Mordecai said, his voice trembling. *"But I have been like a prisoner too long bound—now the chains are broken, and the joy threatens to shatter me."*

Deronda urged him to wrap his scarf tighter against the chill. *"We’re going to the Hand and Banner? Will we have privacy there?"*

*"Tonight is the meeting of our club,"* Mordecai said regretfully. *"The Philosophers. Poor men, thinkers. I hoped we might speak alone, but if not, another time—"*

*"No, I’ll join you,"* Deronda said, relieved at the reprieve.

The tavern’s back room was hazy with smoke, a circle of shabbily dressed men deep in debate. The speaker—Miller, a bookseller—paused as they entered. *"A touch-and-go night, sir. We’ve been on progress, statistics, social change—now we’re on the power of ideas."*

The discussion tumbled on—Goodwin arguing that ideas were inseparable from material life, Pash scoffing at nationalism, Buchan questioning whether all change was progress. Deronda listened, interjecting only to challenge the assumption that development was inherently good.

*"A people’s life grows,"* Mordecai said suddenly, his voice gaining strength. *"It binds and expands, absorbs and gives back. But if memory fades, if love withers, shall we say their soul is dead? Not if one man feels that life stirring in him still. Resistance is fire—it may yet light the way."*

His words hung in the air, a spark in the dim room. Deronda felt the weight of them, knowing this was only the beginning.

*"My spirit is too weak; mortality
Weighs heavily on me like unwilling sleep,
And each imagined pinnacle and steep
Of godlike hardship tells me I must die
Like a sick eagle looking at the sky."*
—**Keats**

The quiet that had settled around them seemed to seep into Mordecai’s awareness, pulling his gaze upward to meet Deronda’s. There was no surprise in his eyes, only a deep, tranquil contentment, as if he had long expected this moment. Deronda moved closer, his chair scraping softly against the floor, closing the distance between them. The gesture was small, but to Mordecai, it carried the tenderness of a hand steadying a weary head.

When he spoke, his voice was low, musing, as though thinking aloud rather than addressing another.

*"In the teachings of the Cabbala,"* he began, *"souls are reborn, passing through lives until they are purified. A soul freed from a spent body may join another still in need, that together they may reach perfection. Only then do they leave this world, making way for new souls to awaken. It is the unfinished work of those who linger that delays the coming of the Messianic age. Thus, the mind shapes the unseen into parable, speaking truth in shadows."*

He paused, his breath steady but fragile. *"When my soul is at last released from this failing flesh, it will find yours. Our work will be complete."*

The silence that followed was heavy, expectant. Deronda felt the weight of it pressing upon him, demanding a response. He chose his words carefully, though they felt insufficient.

Mordecai’s lips curved faintly. *"I know,"* he said, as though the truth were self-evident. *"You see what others deny. You stand beside me on the heights, gazing upon the paths of fulfillment."*

Another silence stretched between them before he continued, his voice distant, lost in memory.

*"I remember the day my life was shattered. The sun was bright on the quay at Trieste—men from every nation gleaming like jewels, boats drifting from the shore. I was to sail to Beyrout, a merchant’s companion. I thought then that I would see the East, that my vision would deepen. I was young, strong—I could walk for miles, sleep on stone, embrace hunger as though it were freedom. My heart burned as if it were Maimonides’ own, certain of the work ahead."*

His fingers tightened slightly where they rested on his knee. *"It was my first time in the south. The sun felt like an old friend. The very ground beneath me seemed to glow, the shadows alive with blue light, as though spirits walked among us. I was no longer myself—only a drop in a vast, rushing river of life. And then—"* His voice caught. *"Then I heard my name."*

*"Ezra!"* Deronda could not help the interruption, the name escaping before he could restrain it.

Mordecai nodded slowly. *"Ezra. That was the name I bore then. My companion had returned with a letter. I had been waiting—I wrote often to my mother. But the sound of my name was like a chain snapping me back into my body, back into the confines of a single life. I opened the letter… and her voice cried out to me from the page. *‘Ezra, my son!’*"*

Deronda held his breath, sensing the revelation unfolding.

*"She was a woman who deserved blessing,"* Mordecai murmured. *"A mother whose love was as vast as the Eternal. But that letter—it was a scream of grief. Death had taken four of her children. Then, at last, my little sister was born—her joy, her solace. And now, the letter told me, she too was gone. Stolen. Disgrace left in her place. *‘They will never come again,’* my mother wrote."*

His hand closed around Deronda’s arm, sudden and fierce. *"This was my inheritance—the lot of Israel. For the father’s sin, my soul was exiled. For the father’s sin, the work was broken. My mother was left in ruin, her heart in shreds. And so I turned back. In that instant, her spirit and the spirits of our fathers moved within me. I returned to her."*

His grip slackened, his gaze drifting away. *"I left the sun behind. I traveled through ice and snow, sparing what little coin I had for her needs. One night, I slept in the open, cold biting through my bones. And that… that was the beginning of this slow death."*

Deronda swallowed the questions rising in his throat. Now was not the time to press.

*"But I worked,"* Mordecai continued, quieter now. *"We had nothing. She was ill—grief had carved its way into her body. Some nights, she could not stand for the pounding of her heart. In the dark, I heard her weeping for her lost child. And so we prayed, arms outstretched, begging that Mirah might be spared from evil."*

*"Mirah?"* Deronda repeated, his pulse quickening. The name was no accident.

*"My sister’s name,"* Mordecai confirmed. *"We prayed for her until the end. Four years, and in the moment my mother died, our voices were still joined—mine aloud, hers in silence. Then her soul took flight."*

*"And you’ve had no word of your sister since?"* Deronda kept his tone measured, though his mind raced.

*"None,"* Mordecai whispered. *"I do not know if our prayers were answered. The will of the wicked is strong. It has poisoned my life—it strangles me still. But what are these winters of suffering now?"* His hand found Deronda’s arm again, his eyes alight with a joy that pierced the heart. *"The work will be done. Once, I believed it was mine alone to fulfill. Now I know—I will live in you. I will live in you."*

His grasp turned desperate, fingers digging in as though he could physically transfer his spirit. Deronda’s chest tightened, his blood rushing with the certainty of it—this was Mirah’s brother. The knowledge hummed between them, unspoken but undeniable. He dared not break the moment with words. Instead, he covered Mordecai’s hand with his own, steadying, anchoring.

The pressure eased. Mordecai exhaled, some awareness of the present seeping back in. *"We should go,"* he murmured. *"I can speak no more."*

They parted at Cohen’s door without another word, only the lingering press of hands.

Deronda walked away beneath a sky of gathering twilight, his mind a storm of emotion. Joy, yes—for Mirah, for the brother who was worthy of her. But sorrow, too, for the years lost, for the reunion that was also a farewell. And beneath it all, the weight of responsibility—how to bring them together gently, how to shield this fragile bond from harshness.

Mordecai’s lodgings were unfit, his strength waning. Deronda resolved to move him, to surround him with quiet dignity before Mirah stepped into his life. She would come, he knew. She would understand the greatness in her brother—the mind that burned with visions beyond the mundane, the heart that ached for the world’s sorrows as though they were his own.

And when they met, it would not be in the shadow of Cohen’s bustling household, but in a space softened by care, where the echoes of their mother’s love might find them undisturbed.

For now, Deronda carried the knowledge like a secret light. The prophet had found his inheritor. And the sister—her face the fairest adornment of any room—would soon be reunited with the brother who had never ceased to pray for her.

Gwendolen’s thoughts circled Deronda like moths to a flame—far more than his, she suspected, ever turned to her. How strange it was, to long for the counsel of a man whose life was shaped by currents she could scarcely fathom. To her, the idea that his days were bound up in the vast, unseen tides of history—that he might be pulled toward some destiny beyond the narrow world of English society—was as fantastical as a fairy tale. Yet, for all her pride, she could not help but believe she occupied some small corner of his mind. Was it not natural? When one feels small, one clutches at any proof of significance.

She had taken his words to heart—his quiet admonition that she must broaden her world, seek knowledge, care for things greater than herself. But how? The task loomed before her like a mountain, and she had no guide. In a fit of determination, she had carried an armful of weighty volumes to her room—Descartes, Burke, Locke—names she knew were respected, though their words had always seemed dull as dust. Perhaps if she skimmed them quickly, she might glean something to raise her nearer to his height. But time slipped through her fingers. There was always the performance of being Mrs. Grandcourt to maintain—always the sense of her husband’s exacting gaze, his silent demand that she play her part flawlessly.

And she *would* play it. She had not yet sunk to letting the world see her misery. Pride was her armor, and defiance, though it churned within her, never broke the surface. The only crack in her composure was Deronda himself—the one person to whom she had ever bowed, the one who drew out her confessions like a confession from a sinner.

To the outside eye, she was triumphant. At Diplow, the neighbors murmured over her poise. "She takes it all as if it were her birthright," observed Mrs. Arrowpoint, who missed nothing. "A stranger might think *she* had done the condescending."

For her mother’s sake, Gwendolen acted contentment with even greater care. Poor Mrs. Davilow, kept at a careful distance by Grandcourt’s design, mistook her daughter’s restraint for indifference. Brief visits, chaperoned rides—these were the crumbs allowed. When Gwendolen once ventured to propose inviting her mother along with the Gascoignes, Grandcourt’s languid veto—*"We can’t have those people always. Country clergy are bores."*—struck like a door closing.

Yet the rejection only sharpened her longing for them. She found herself thinking fondly of her uncle’s bustling good nature, so unlike Grandcourt’s icy superiority. Even his mistakes seemed warmer than her husband’s precision.

Then came a rare reprieve—a solitary ride to Offendene, unchaperoned but for the groom. The sight of her, radiant on her chestnut horse, sent a thrill through the household. The girls watched, starry-eyed, as if she were a heroine stepping from the pages of a novel.

"Mr. Grandcourt is out," she announced lightly, kissing her mother’s cheek, "so I’ve stolen time to see you." She teased her about a lace cap, chattered of trivial things—anything to keep the ache at bay.

But the strain flickered through. When her mother spoke of a new house, Gwendolen’s voice wavered. "You have no furniture, poor mamma."

"Oh, but I’m saving," Mrs. Davilow said, pressing her hand. "You’ve made me rather rich, dear."

The words pierced her. Upstairs, alone for a moment, Gwendolen stared into the mirror, pale. "I suppose I shall never see all this again."

Her mother hovered, timid. "You would come if I were ill?"

"Of course," Gwendolen said, forcing lightness. "But I’m never ill. The girls are better to you than I’ve been." She smiled, brittle. "What good have I ever done, except marry Mr. Grandcourt?"

Then, hastily, she pressed thirty pounds into her mother’s hands—for her sisters, she said. Little things. A gesture to ease the guilt without speaking of it.

As she rode away, her thoughts churned. Grandcourt had gone to Gadsmere, she was sure—to *that woman*, the shadow behind her marriage. The knowledge burned. She had married him knowing, yet now the humiliation of her silence festered. She could not speak, could not rebel, without making her degradation visible to the world.

And so she played her part. *"I think I am making a very good Mrs. Grandcourt,"* she told herself bitterly.

But in the dark corners of her mind, hatred simmered—toward him, toward the woman who held her secret, even toward herself. She had chosen this. She must go on.

Yet Grandcourt, though blind to the storms within her, sensed the resistance. And it pleased him. The mastery was all the sweeter for the fight he imagined.

The Grandcourts arrived at Grosvenor Square just in time to secure an invitation to Lady Mallinger’s musical evening—Sir Hugo having anticipated his nephew’s return to London for business reasons. It was only their third evening in town, and Gwendolen, though surrounded by the opulence of her new home, found her thoughts drifting elsewhere. She was preoccupied with the certainty of seeing Deronda again, and of meeting the mysterious Mirah Lapidoth, the woman of quiet strength who had endured so much.

Deronda’s words about Mirah had lingered in Gwendolen’s mind, particularly his remark that she was *"capable of submitting to anything in the form of duty."* The phrase gnawed at her, though she could not quite define why. If pressed, she would have admitted that her own submission bore little resemblance to duty—it was a yoke of her own making, fastened by shame and selfishness.

The drawing-rooms of Park Lane shimmered in white, gold, and pale crimson, the guests arranged in elegant clusters. The evening’s music had given way to conversation when the Grandcourts made their entrance. Klesmer, ever Mirah’s champion, had already taken his place at the piano, preparing to accompany her in Leo’s *"O patria mia."* As Gwendolen glided to her seat, resplendent in pale green velvet and diamonds, she caught sight of Mirah standing beside the instrument—serene, unassuming, yet impossible to overlook.

A bitter flicker crossed Gwendolen’s mind. *He thinks I’m on the right path now,* she thought, though she could not have said who *he* was—Deronda, or perhaps some phantom of her own conscience.

She had not seen Deronda upon entering, and as she exchanged pleasantries with Sir Hugo, her gaze swept the room with careful indifference, lest her searching look betray too much. Yet fate, ever unkind, forced her eyes upon an unwelcome figure: Mr. Lush, lingering near her husband. A cold ripple of disgust passed through her. She had banished him from her thoughts, yet here he was—a shadow from Grandcourt’s past, a man who knew too much. The sight of him stirred a dread she could not name.

At last, she spotted Deronda, standing near the door with Hans Meyrick. He was not looking at her, and she turned away, consoling herself with the thought that he must have seen her arrive.

Then the music began.

Mirah’s voice rose, pure and aching, filling the room with Leopardi’s ode to a grieving Italy. To Deronda, the song was more than notes—it was the voice of Mordecai’s longing, the cry of a people, the echo of unshaken devotion. He had never heard her sing it before, and it moved him more than he expected. When the applause came, Klesmer murmured approval, but Mirah’s eyes sought only Deronda’s.

Then Gwendolen approached, a vision of calculated grace.

"You must let me say how much I admire your singing," she began, her voice warm. "Mr. Deronda told me to expect a treat, but I confess I wasn’t prepared for such perfection."

Mirah, unaccustomed to such flattery from a woman of Gwendolen’s station, answered simply, "You are very kind. I owe everything to Mr. Deronda’s kindness."

Gwendolen pressed on, half from genuine curiosity, half from an impulse she could not name. "He spoke of you often. I gather he knew you well before you came to England?"

Mirah’s expression softened. "No, we met only when I was in need. He gave me friends when I had none. I owe him more than I can say."

Gwendolen felt an odd pang—envy, perhaps, or something sharper. Here was a woman who spoke of Deronda as a savior, while she herself could claim no such debt. The conversation turned, but the sting remained.

Later, when the music had resumed and the guests had scattered, Deronda found himself beside Gwendolen. The air between them was taut, though neither could say why.

"You despise me," she said abruptly, her voice low.

"No," he replied, though his tone was cool.

"Something I said displeased you."

"Some feelings are hard to put into words."

She looked at him then, her eyes bright with something perilously close to tears. "Am I so impossible to understand?"

He softened, though not entirely. "No. But experience shapes us differently."

She drew a breath, as if steadying herself. "Then you must not give up on me. If you do, I will."

The words hung between them, raw and unguarded. Then, before he could answer, she rose and left him, returning to her place as Mirah’s voice filled the room once more.

Deronda watched her go, torn. Sir Hugo’s warnings weighed on him, yet Gwendolen’s trust was unmistakable. She looked to him as if he alone could steady her—but how, when his own path was pulling him elsewhere?

When the music ended, Hans sidled up with a grin. "Enviable, Deronda—quarreling with a duchess over theology."

Deronda brushed him off, but the remark lingered.

As the Grandcourts departed, Gwendolen sat stiffly beside her husband, her mind churning. She had been reckless tonight—too open, too eager. Grandcourt said nothing, but his silence was its own reproach.

Then, as the carriage rolled home, he spoke at last: "Lush dines with us tomorrow. You will be civil."

Her pulse spiked. *This is how promises break.* But she said nothing.

Some vows, it seemed, were made only to be unmade.

The time had come to prepare Mordecai for the revelation of his long-lost sister and the change that would follow. Mrs. Meyrick, to whom Deronda had confided everything except Mordecai’s profound connection to himself, had helped secure a comfortable lodging in Brompton, mere minutes from her own home. This would allow Mirah to care for her brother while remaining within reach of the Meyricks’ kindness.

Deronda, mindful of the Cohens’ feelings, approached Mordecai with care. He had written beforehand, requesting a private meeting in the workroom—a space sacred to their shared thoughts. When he arrived, the household greeted him warmly, though curiosity lingered beneath their courtesy. Little Jacob, ever bold, demanded, "What do you want to talk to Mordecai about?"

"Something important," Deronda replied, ruffling the boy’s hair. "But you wouldn’t understand yet."

Jacob, undeterred, rattled off a string of Hebrew verses with triumphant pride, as if to prove his wisdom. Deronda laughed, conceding defeat, and Jacob, satisfied, flung open the workroom door with a flourish.

Inside, Mordecai waited with an air of solemn anticipation, as though braced for revelation. Deronda took his seat by the fire, struck by the intensity in his friend’s gaze.

"You bring news that my soul has longed for," Mordecai said, his voice trembling.

Deronda hesitated. "I have something to tell you—something I hope will bring you joy."

Mordecai’s face shone with sudden certainty. "It is revealed at last—the truth of your heritage! We are bound, you and I, by blood and spirit."

Deronda shook his head gently. "No, my friend. I have learned nothing new of my own past. But I have found someone who belongs to *you*."

Mordecai’s breath caught. His hands clenched, then relaxed as Deronda continued.

"Your sister lives."

A silence followed, thick with disbelief. Then Mordecai exhaled, his body sagging as if under a weight lifted.

"Mirah?" he whispered.

"Yes. She has been searching for you—for her family—all these years."

Deronda recounted all he knew: Mirah’s flight from a cruel father, her despair, her rescue, and the quiet life she had built among friends. He spoke of her devotion to the memory of her mother and brother, her fear that she might never find them.

Mordecai listened, his eyes closed, tears slipping silently down his cheeks. When Deronda finished, he pressed a hand to his heart.

"To think she was so near," he murmured. "All this time, suffering alone."

"She is safe now," Deronda assured him. "And she wishes to be with you. I’ve arranged a home where you can live together—close to those who care for her."

Mordecai nodded, though sorrow flickered in his gaze. "I must leave the Cohens."

"They love you," Deronda said softly. "But this is where you belong now—with your sister."

Breaking the news to the Cohens was no easier. When Deronda and Mordecai entered the parlor, the family gathered expectantly. Cohen, ever practical, ventured, "Relations with money, sir?"

Deronda smiled. "No. But a sister who loves him dearly—a talented, kind-hearted woman who will care for him."

The room fell quiet. The grandmother sighed. "So you’re leaving us, Mordecai?"

Jacob, sensing the finality, burst into tears. Adelaide, ever his echo, wailed in sympathy. Even baby let out a squall of protest.

Cohen cleared his throat, rallying. "Well, we’re not ones to grudge good fortune. Family’s family."

Mordecai, moved, clasped Jacob close. "You have been my kin when I had none. I will never forget your kindness."

Deronda watched the scene, heart heavy yet hopeful. The bonds of the past were shifting, making way for new ones—ties of blood, yes, but also of choice, of love renewed.

As he took his leave, Cohen clapped him on the shoulder. "You’re a good man, sir. And if this sister’s half as good as our Mordecai, she’s worth the trouble."

Deronda smiled. "She is."

And with that, the first step toward reunion was taken.

One could almost envy Daniel Deronda as he prepared new garments for Mordecai, delighting in the vision of how the simple elegance of gray flannel and a robe resembling a monk’s humble frock would frame the man’s solemn features. Much of his satisfaction came from imagining Mirah’s joy—seeing her brother through her eyes, ensuring that nothing marred the purity of her happiness. Yet even as he arranged each detail, doubt crept in. Was he underestimating her? Would she not, like him, find a deeper beauty in the marks of hardship, the very traces of a life endured?

But there were the Meyricks to consider—their goodwill toward this brother so foreign to their world. Deronda caught himself smoothing away every sign of poverty, as if to soften the edges of their potential discomfort. He knew too well how unseeing eyes might recoil from what they did not understand.

That evening, by the warmth of Mrs. Meyrick’s hearth, the news was shared. Hans, lounging with his usual irreverence, reacted with a groan.

"Confound it!" he exclaimed, as if the revelation were a personal affront.

Mab scolded him, Kate sighed, and Amy rolled her eyes. Only Mrs. Meyrick remained steadfast, her voice gentle but firm as she recounted Deronda’s admiration for Mordecai, his belief that Mirah would understand her brother perfectly.

Hans, restless and scowling, paced the room. "Deronda’s obsession with these Jews is absurd," he muttered. "Next thing, Mirah will forget she’s an artist—bury herself in prayers and prophecies. Everything ruined."

The girls protested, but Hans was already seizing his hat, his farewells brusque. The door slammed behind him, leaving silence in his wake.

The next morning, Deronda sent word—let Mrs. Meyrick be the one to tell Mirah. He could not bring himself to stand before her again as the bearer of grand tidings, the orchestrator of her joy. Better to let the moment unfold naturally, without the weight of his presence.

When the time came, Mirah descended the stairs in the same worn cloak and hat she had worn when Deronda first found her.

"You wish to go like this?" Mrs. Meyrick asked softly.

"My brother is poor," Mirah replied. "I want to look as much like him as I can."

Deronda said nothing, but his heart stung with sudden shame at his own careful arrangements.

Mordecai rose as they entered, his gaze fixed on the door. Mirah stopped just inside, trembling. For a long moment, neither moved. It was not just the sight of one another that held them, but the echo of a shared past, a love preserved across years of separation.

"Ezra," Mirah whispered, her voice exactly as it had been when she spoke of their mother’s call.

"Yes, little one. I will not be long with you in this world."

Only then did she remember the others, turning with a grace that seemed almost unconscious, one hand still on Mordecai’s arm. She reached for Mrs. Meyrick’s hand, pressing it into her brother’s. "This lady," she said, "took me in when I was lost. She has been my mother when I had none."

Mordecai bowed his head. "The Eternal has been kind."

Deronda, sensing the sacredness of the moment, gently guided Mrs. Meyrick away. Some reunions were not meant for witnesses.

The weight of Grandcourt’s existence was measured not in action but in possession—the passive sovereignty of inherited lands, which insulated him from the petty storms of politics or trade. The world’s turmoil reached him only as distant thunder, muffled by the walls of his wealth. He skimmed newspapers with languid disdain, dismissing entire nations and classes beneath the blanket contempt of "brutes," his silence a weapon more unnerving than any outburst.

Yet within the narrow sphere of his own interests, Grandcourt wielded a diplomat’s cunning. No flicker of Gwendolen’s attention toward Deronda escaped him—not from jealousy, for jealousy implied doubt, and doubt was a weakness he refused to acknowledge. A noble heart knows no bitterness where gratitude should dwell. It is the mark of a soul that walks in wider light, capable of deeper joys—not those petty minds that delight in magnifying faults until all goodness crumbles to dust. Such creatures are the devil’s own monks, sworn to poverty in love and trust, yet forever begging scraps of kindness from a world that still holds some humanity.

When Deronda left Gwendolen that day, he had carefully avoided saying, *I will not see you again for some time—I am leaving.* He would not give Grandcourt the satisfaction of thinking his departure mattered to her. Yet as he walked to fulfill his promise of a final visit, his heart was heavy with the weight of what lay ahead. Sir Hugo’s summons had come like a thunderclap—*Come at once. Something has happened.* The words had filled him with dread, but when he stood at last in the baronet’s study, he found not distress, but a quiet, solemn affection in the man’s gaze.

“Nothing that will grieve you, sir?” Deronda asked, more a hope than a question, as he clasped the offered hand.

Sir Hugo’s voice was uncommonly soft, his expression grave. “No, Dan. Sit down. I have something to tell you.”

Deronda could not answer. Even if he had trusted his voice to say, *I was never wounded*, it would not have been the whole truth. Too much of his life—too many unanswered yearnings—hung upon this moment.

Deronda shook his head. “There is nothing to forgive. The love I bear you has been the greater part of all my life. Any sorrow was never for myself—only for the one I never knew… until now.”

He waited, patient, until she could speak. Then, haltingly, she told him everything.

*"He will not come tomorrow,"* Ezra said softly.

Neither spoke of the fear that he might lurk in shadows, waiting to beg from her again.

*"Our lot is the lot of Israel,"* Ezra murmured at last. *"Grief and glory woven together, as smoke and flame. We inherit the good, and so we feel the evil. These things are bound to us, as our father was bound to our mother."*

His voice carried the weight of ages, as though he spoke not in a London parlor, but in the halls of ancient sages. For in all things—even this—there was a design beyond their understanding.

The evening light was fading when Daniel Deronda stepped into the small Brompton lodgings, his heart beating with the urgency of all he had to share. The journey that had begun in uncertainty had ended in revelation—not just of lineage, but of purpose. He carried with him not only the weight of his grandfather’s chest but the certainty of a path now clear before him.

Mirah and Mordecai sat together in the quiet room, the scattered papers at their feet a testament to the day’s distress. The air was heavy with unspoken sorrow, but the moment Deronda appeared, it was as though a sudden light had broken through. His presence had always been a balm, but now there was something new in his bearing—a quiet triumph, a resolve that needed no words to announce itself.

"May I come in?" he asked from the doorway, his voice warm with suppressed emotion.

Mordecai rose at once, his face alight as if he had expected this very arrival. Mirah, too, stood quickly, her cheeks flushing with something more than surprise—perhaps hope, perhaps fear. The sight of Deronda, so vivid against the dimness of the room, struck her with an almost painful intensity.

He crossed to them in a few strides, clasping Mordecai’s shoulder with one hand and offering the other to Mirah. For a suspended moment, the three stood linked, an unspoken communion passing between them. Then Deronda’s gaze sharpened with concern.

"Has something happened?" he asked, his eyes searching Mirah’s face.

Mordecai answered for her, his voice steady but fervent. "Speak not of troubles now. There is joy in your face—let it be ours as well."

And so Deronda told them—of his journey, of the truth uncovered, of the heritage that was no longer a question but a calling. "I am a Jew," he said, the words ringing with a quiet pride. "Not merely by birth, but by choice, by conviction. Our souls are bound to the same purpose."

Mirah felt the declaration like a shock through her veins. She had known, in some deep part of herself, that Deronda belonged to them—to her brother’s visions, to the history that had shaped her own life. But to hear it spoken aloud, with such certainty, was like the sudden answer to a prayer she had not dared utter.

Mordecai’s response was a whisper in Hebrew, words of covenant and devotion. His face, so often shadowed by illness, was luminous. Deronda had not merely returned—he had come *home*.

The hours that followed were a blur of shared wonder. Deronda spoke of the manuscripts, of the legacy entrusted to him, of the future they might now shape together. Mirah listened, her earlier sorrows momentarily forgotten, replaced by a dazed sense of possibility. Could it be true? Could the man who had saved her, who had become so entwined with her life, truly stand before her now as one of her own people?

Yet beneath the joy lay a quieter, more private ache. She watched Deronda’s face as he spoke, the way his eyes lingered on Mordecai with brotherly devotion, the careful distance he kept from her. Did he feel it too—the unspoken current between them? Or was she only imagining what she so desperately wished to be real?

For Deronda, the evening was both fulfillment and torment. He had never been more certain of his path, yet never more uncertain of his heart. Mirah’s presence was a gift he dared not claim too boldly, lest gratitude—not love—bind her to him. And so he held himself in check, even as every glance, every accidental brush of hands, sent a thrill through him.

The night deepened, and with it, the unspoken tension. There would be time, he told himself, for all that must come next. But as he bid them goodnight—his voice carefully steady, his touch deliberately light—he wondered if time would ever make the words any easier to say.

Love, after all, was not a thing to be rushed. Not when it meant everything.

—DANTE: *Il Purgatorio*

Gwendolen did not linger long in Genoa after her mother’s arrival. The desire to leave that glittering shore gave her a brittle strength, a resolve to move forward, though she could not yet name her destination. Every beautiful place now seemed to her a possible prison, its charm twisted into torment.

“I never want to see the Mediterranean again,” she said abruptly to her mother, who mistook the words for grief rather than revulsion. Mrs. Davilow, though obliged to treat this time as one of mourning, found an unexpected solace in her daughter’s dependence. For the first time since Gwendolen’s marriage, she felt needed—cherished not as a duty, but as a reprieved treasure.

In the quiet of their shared room, Gwendolen’s voice would pierce the dark:
“Mamma, are you there?”
“Yes, dear. Do you need anything?”
“No. Only I like knowing you are.” A pause. “Does it trouble you, my waking you?” (This hesitation would not have marked the old Gwendolen.)
“I wasn’t sleeping.”
“It didn’t feel real that you were near. I had to make sure.” A rustle of sheets. “I can endure things if you are with me. But you mustn’t lie awake worrying. You must let me make you happy now—what else is left to me?”

Mrs. Davilow’s heart ached with a bittersweet joy. Yet the next night, hearing Gwendolen’s restless sighs, she ventured: “Shall I give you your sleeping draught?”
“No. I don’t want to sleep.”
“It would do you good, darling.”
“Don’t tell me what’s good for me!” Gwendolen’s voice turned sharp. “You don’t know. You and Uncle mustn’t decide for me anymore.”

Then, suddenly, the storm broke. “I was always cruel to you, mamma.”
“No, dearest—”
“I was,” Gwendolen insisted, the words tearing loose. “It’s because I was wicked that I’m wretched now.” Sobs overtook her, the dammed-up guilt and fear flooding out.

The truth of Grandcourt’s will had reached her—not through gentle revelation, but in cold fragments. Sir Hugo, with uneasy kindness, had disclosed the facts to Mr. Gascoigne: the bulk of the estate entailed on the son of Lydia Glasher, Gwendolen left with a pittance. The rector, stung in his pride, masked his humiliation behind clerical stoicism. But Mrs. Davilow, piecing together hints, guessed her daughter had known more of her husband’s secrets than anyone suspected.

One evening, Gwendolen shattered the silence.
“You mustn’t expect me to be rich, mamma. I may have nothing.”
Mrs. Davilow, startled, reassured her: “Sir Hugo says you’re to have two thousand a year and Gadsmere.”
“What I keep depends on what I accept.” Gwendolen’s voice was steel. “You mustn’t question me. I’ll care for you—but about *him*, no one interferes. Would eight hundred a year suffice for you?”
“More than enough! You mustn’t give me so much.” A hesitation. “Do you know who inherits the rest?”
“Yes.” Gwendolen’s hand slashed the air. “I know everything. It’s settled. I forbid it being spoken of again.”

Mrs. Davilow flushed, turning away. The gulf between them yawned—her daughter’s suffering locked away, beyond reach. But Gwendolen, watching, softened.
“Come nearer, mamma. Don’t grieve.” She drew her close. “I mean to be wise now. Good to you—so good, you won’t know me. Only don’t cry.”

Beneath the vow lay a resolve: she would see Deronda. He alone could tell her how to navigate the wreckage. The thought of him was a lifeline—his judgment the compass by which she might steer clear of deeper shame.

Sir Hugo, oblivious to her desperation, cheerfully pressed her to stay in Park Lane. Gwendolen seized the chance. When he mentioned leaving for the Abbey, she spoke coolly:
“Sir Hugo, I must see Mr. Deronda. I don’t know his direction. Will you send for him?”

The baronet’s glance flickered, but his tone was smooth. “Certainly. I’ll have him come at once.” Privately, he wondered at her intensity—and at Deronda’s confounding reserve. Had the boy no heart? Here was beauty and sorrow pleading for him, and he lingered in shadows.

Yet Gwendolen cared nothing for appearances now. Pride had burned away. She would see Deronda, even if the world whispered. The need was elemental—as urgent as a prisoner’s cry for light.

Sir Hugo wrote the note. It found Deronda in his chambers, and he came.

Deronda’s heart was restless as he answered Gwendolen’s summons. It was not vanity that stirred him, but the weight of knowing another soul clung to his with desperate need. He had long since moved beyond mere reasoning—her sorrow was a palpable thing, a tremor in her voice, a shadow in her gaze. He felt the precipice on which she stood, and the terrible knowledge that he alone held the line between her and despair.

Had this been a year ago, he might have rushed to her without question, driven by the impulse to shield her from suffering. But now, love and duty had woven other bonds around him, and though compassion still burned in his chest, he could no longer be the sole anchor in her storm.

He waited in the back drawing-room, the same room where she had once whispered that her fate rested in his hands. The memory of her plea, *Per pietà non dirmi addio*, had mingled then with Mirah’s song—a cruel irony now. The familiar walls, the portraits, even the carved lions above the fireplace, seemed like relics of a life he had outgrown. Everything was altered.

When Gwendolen entered, she was changed—not merely by mourning attire, but by a quiet resolve. Her eyes met his without a smile, heavy with memory and dread.

"You came," she said, seating herself at once. "I need you to tell me what I must do. Don’t soften it. I am ready."

Her voice was steady, but her fingers twisted the lone ring on her hand—her wedding band.

Deronda sat opposite her. "You speak of your husband’s will?"

"Yes." She drew a sharp breath. "I’ve thought of taking only enough for my mother—nothing for myself. I was selfish once, but I loved her. He took me from her. If she knew—" Her voice fractured.

Deronda leaned forward, gentle but firm. "You torment yourself needlessly. Your husband chose to provide for you both. Refusing it would only wound your mother further."

Gwendolen’s gaze dropped. "I thought to leave all but her portion."

"That would punish her as well as you," he said softly. "Let the past rest. Use what you have for good."

She looked up then, eyes bright with unshed tears. "Then tell me how to live. What else must I do?"

The plea undid her. A sob broke free, and tears fell unchecked. Deronda ached with the sight of it, but he steadied himself.

"You will go to the country soon?"

"In a week," she whispered.

"Then let that be your beginning. Be kind where you can. Let your remorse shape your future, not cripple you."

Her breath hitched. "You think I can—change?"

"Yes." His voice was fervent, as if willing it into truth. "This pain is not the end. It is preparation. You have been shown the abyss—now turn away. You *can* be better than what you fear."

A flush rose in her cheeks, the first color since her husband’s death. For a moment, something like hope flickered in her eyes.

Deronda stood, offering his hand. "I must go."

Her fingers clasped his tightly. "You’ll come again before I leave?"

The raw need in her voice pierced him. "If you wish it."

"I *must* wish it," she said, desperation edging her tone. "Who else is there?"

He hesitated, then nodded. "I will come."

She straightened, as if drawing strength from the promise. "I will remember what you’ve said. I will try."

There was no smile, only a solemn resolve. The vivacious girl who had once laughed so easily was gone, replaced by a woman carved by grief.

As Deronda left, his chest was heavy. He knew—with terrible clarity—that each meeting bound her tighter to him, and that the breaking would come. But for now, he could not abandon her to the dark.

In the days that followed, he visited again, though never alone. Gwendolen spoke of plans—returning to Offendene with her mother, rebuilding some semblance of the past. He listened, encouraging the fragile hope.

She did not ask of his own life. The world beyond her sorrow had faded. For her, there was only the path ahead—and the hand she clung to, lest she fall.

—Browning

---

While Gwendolen’s thoughts spiraled around Deronda, unaware of the others in his orbit, Mirah and Ezra found their ties to him deepening—threads of fate tightening. Their father, Lapidoth, had vanished from Mirah’s doorstep as swiftly as he’d appeared, lured by the siren call of dice and wagers. To him, money was never meant to linger; it existed to be risked, to multiply or vanish in the turn of a card. Hunger could be ignored, but the itch for chance? That was a demon that never slept.

He had taken Mirah’s purse with little thought beyond the next game. Thirty shillings slipped through his fingers like smoke, leaving him hollow-handed, yet still scheming. Should he pawn the empty purse? Or return to her, weaving some tale of debts honorably settled? A father had rights, after all—claims that outweighed a son’s grudges or a daughter’s wary eyes.

By evening, he loitered near the small Brompton house, hoping to catch Mirah coming or going. Instead, her voice drifted through an open window, singing *"Herz, mein Herz"* with a tenderness that pricked even his calloused heart. Steeling himself, he knocked.

When Mrs. Adam announced him, Mirah froze, her hands twisting together. Ezra’s face darkened, his expression hardening into something she had never seen before—a sternness that brooked no argument. "Let him come up," he ordered.

Lapidoth entered with practiced melancholy, extending the empty purse like a peace offering. "I’ve suffered enough," he declared, eyes glistening. "Turn me out if you like. Say the word, and I’m gone."

Mirah’s voice trembled. "You know I could never say it."

But Ezra’s command was iron. "Leave us."

She hesitated, torn, before pressing her brother’s hand. "Remember," she whispered, "our mother would not have shut him out."

Then she fled, lingering on the stairs, her pulse a frantic drumbeat. Below, Ezra’s voice cut like a blade.

"This home is kept by the kindness of a friend and my sister’s labor. We will not cast you out—but neither will we trust you. You broke every bond: left debts unpaid, abandoned our mother, sold my sister. You are a gambler, a thief of peace. Yet you are our father, and so we will feed you, clothe you, shelter you. But know this—you are branded in our flesh. The world may scorn you, but we will still say, *‘This is our father.’*"

Lapidoth had braced for anger, but not this—this raw, unforgiving truth. It struck deeper than he expected, and with a shuddering gasp, he crumpled into theatric sobs, the performance and the pain indistinguishable.

Ezra fell silent, spent, his hands trembling. The words had been a lancing of wounds long festering.

Then Mirah rushed back in, drawn by the sounds of distress. Her father lifted his tear-streaked face, voice breaking. "Goodbye, Mirah. I’ll die as I deserve—on the roadside. Your mother would have forgiven me, but I… I did not earn it."

"No!" she cried, seizing his hand. "Ezra has not forbidden you. Stay. Be better."

Lapidoth’s resignation melted into relief. This was what he wanted—shelter, another chance. Mirah led him away, murmuring comforts, though her touch lacked its old warmth. "Think of it as her forgiveness," she urged. "She speaks through me."

That night, as the house settled, Lapidoth lay awake, not haunted by his son’s condemnation, but by numbers, chances, the phantom rattle of dice. England was a cursed place—but perhaps, just perhaps, luck might turn.

Deronda returned from the Abbey to an unwelcome discovery: Lapidoth had taken up residence in the Brompton lodgings. Mirah, compelled by honesty, had spoken of Deronda to her father, even explaining the origins of her friendship with Ezra—the shared sorrow that had bound them. Yet she glossed over Deronda’s role in her rescue, omitting the near-drowning entirely, framing her stay with the Meyricks as the natural bridge to their acquaintance. Some truths were too fragile to expose to her father’s scrutiny.

Lapidoth, for his part, showed little curiosity about her flight to England. But the revelation that his children had a wealthy, influential benefactor piqued his interest. It was Ezra who informed Deronda of their father’s arrival, his tone weary but resigned.

*"I have found a grim sort of calm in facing him,"* Ezra admitted. *"Perhaps Mirah’s kindness, the quiet of this life, might keep him from temptation. She has promised—no money. It would only fuel his ruin."*

Deronda’s first encounter with Lapidoth was icy. The sight of the man who had shattered his family stirred a visceral revulsion. Yet Lapidoth, ever adaptable, played the penitent, lingering to assist with the deciphering of old manuscripts. He even offered to transcribe them—his eyes, unlike Ezra’s, still sharp. Deronda agreed, hoping it signaled genuine reform. Ezra, however, insisted the work be done under his watchful eye. Trust did not come easily.

Lapidoth’s presence cast an invisible shadow between Deronda and Mirah. Each feared the other’s reserve, misreading it as indifference or disapproval. The unspoken tension thickened.

Meanwhile, Deronda sought out Hans, needing to share the upheaval in his life—his heritage, his plans. But Hans was altered, his usual vivacity dulled.

*"I’ve been to no-man’s-land,"* Hans muttered when pressed. *"Opium. A trial run for bliss. It disagreed with me."*

Deronda, undeterred, laid bare his truth: his Jewish lineage, his mother’s revelation, his resolve to honor Mordecai’s vision. Hans absorbed it without surprise.

*"My mother told me,"* he admitted. *"I don’t share your joy, but I’ll come to it—eventually."*

The conversation turned strained when Hans voiced the unspoken question: *"Does Mrs. Grandcourt know?"*

Deronda’s reply was sharp. *"There is nothing between us. Never has been."*

Then, the real tension surfaced. Hans, with bitter reluctance, forced the words out: *"You’re the luckiest man alive. If Mirah loves anyone beyond her brother, it’s you."*

Shock, then hope, surged through Deronda. *"You’re certain?"*

*"Ask little mother. Mirah’s jealous of the duchess."* Hans scowled, half-envious, half-relieved. *"Go on, claim your happiness. You’ve earned it."*

Deronda gripped his friend’s hand, gratitude overwhelming words. For the first time, the future shimmered with possibility.

*"All thoughts, all passions, all delights,
Whatever stirs this mortal frame,
All are but ministers of Love,
And feed his sacred flame."*

Deronda could no longer contain the confession burning within him. Hans’s words—that Mirah longed for reassurance, that she feared losing him—had struck deep, igniting a resolve in him to speak his heart without delay. When he next visited Ezra, he went with the firm intention of seeking a private moment with Mirah. If she returned his love, he would face any consequence without hesitation. As her betrothed, he could offer her protection, a shield against future storms—especially those her father might bring.

Lapidoth had been quiet, but Deronda sensed an undercurrent of restlessness in the man. Though he played the part of the chastened father, his eyes wandered, his fingers twitched with suppressed impulse. He had begun by biding his time, waiting for the right moment to extract money from Deronda, but his patience was thinning. The lure of gambling gnawed at him, an itch no amount of forced domesticity could soothe. He watched Mirah closely, noting where she kept her keys, her coins—but she was too careful, entrusting most of her earnings to Mrs. Meyrick. To Lapidoth, it was a petty tyranny, as though his own daughter had locked him in a cell.

The thought had crossed his mind—more than once—to slip a hand into a drawer, to pocket whatever he found. It wouldn’t be theft, not truly. A father had a right, didn’t he? But Deronda’s presence was a deterrent. There was something in the younger man’s bearing that made even Lapidoth hesitate. Still, the temptation lingered, shifting in his mind like a restless tide.

Today, Deronda arrived with an unspoken brightness, a warmth in his manner that did not go unnoticed. Lapidoth, simmering with discontent, saw an opportunity—perhaps Deronda’s good humor could be turned to his advantage. He lingered briefly before excusing himself, stepping out to smoke, leaving the two friends in peace.

Mirah was not yet home, but Deronda knew she would return soon. The anticipation quickened his pulse. Would she see it now—the love in his eyes, the words he had held back for so long? He turned to Ezra, his voice light with affection.

"This room is too cramped for you, Ezra. You deserve better—a home with space, with air. Let me take care of it. Let me take care of *you*."

Ezra smiled faintly. "I lack for nothing but breath. But you—you could have palaces, open skies. Yet here you stay."

Deronda laughed softly. "No palace could tempt me away while you are here." He paced the small room, fanning himself with his handkerchief. "Besides, I’ll pretend I’m already in the East. I’ll go someday, you know—though I’ll leave behind these stifling trappings." He tugged at his cravat, then slid the ring from his finger—the one Lapidoth had so often eyed—and set it aside before settling back into his chair.

They resumed their studies, Deronda’s voice steady as he read from the Hebrew text under Ezra’s guidance. Neither noticed when Lapidoth slipped back inside, lingering in the shadows. His gaze fixed on the ring, gleaming against the dark wood.

A plan had been forming in his mind—a grand scheme of escape, of funds procured through careful persuasion. But the ring was *right there*. Small, yes, but tangible. No need for speeches, for bargaining. Just a quick, silent motion, and freedom would be his.

His fingers twitched. The thought pulsed through him like a second heartbeat.

And then—without fully deciding—he moved. A breath, a step, and the ring was in his hand. A moment later, he was out the door, hat pulled low, his mind already racing toward the docks, toward a ship, toward a life unshackled.

Deronda and Ezra barely registered his absence.

But then Mirah returned, her entrance a sudden burst of energy. She had not even removed her hat.

"I only came to bring Ezra his draught," she said quickly, her voice unsteady. "I must go to Mrs. Meyrick’s at once—"

"Let me walk with you," Deronda said, rising. His voice was earnest, his eyes intent.

She nodded, flustered, and turned to prepare Ezra’s medicine. Deronda, momentarily distracted, remembered his discarded cravat. He reached for it—then froze.

"My ring—where is it?"

A frantic search began. Mirah joined him, her movements quick, her face paling as realization struck. She whirled toward Ezra, a silent question in her eyes. His grim nod confirmed her fear.

Her hands clenched. She flew from the room, Deronda following helplessly. She checked the sitting room, the bedroom, the hook where her father’s hat should have been. Then she turned, her lips bloodless, her whole body trembling with shame.

Deronda took her hands in his, his voice low, fervent.

"Mirah—let me share this burden. Let me be his son as much as you are his daughter. Let me stand beside you in sorrow and joy alike. I would rather bear your grief than take another woman’s brightest happiness." He drew a breath, his next words spilling free at last. "Say you will be my wife. Say it now. I have loved you in silence too long."

Her eyes widened, the shock of his words piercing through her despair. Slowly, wonder replaced anguish. She looked up at him, her face alight with a reverence that matched his own.

She could not speak. But she lifted her face to his, pressing the softest of kisses to his lips—her answer, simple and sure.

For a long moment, they stood in silence, hands entwined, hearts laid bare. Then Mirah whispered, "Let us go to Ezra. He will need us."

And together, they turned to face what came next.

The autumn air carried a crisp vitality as Sir Hugo took up residence at Diplow, his presence casting a warm glow over the neighborhood. From the grand estates of Brackenshaw to the modest parlors of Wanchester, his affable charm was a welcome change—especially after the cold severity of Grandcourt’s reign. Sir Hugo was a man who understood the delicate dance of society, ensuring that his gatherings mingled the right guests without offense, his liberal leanings never quite unsettling the quiet reverence for rank that still lingered in English hearts.

Among those who noticed the shift was the rector of Pennicote, Mr. Gascoigne, who now found himself received with warmth rather than tolerance. Sir Hugo’s cordiality, however, was not born of personal regard but of something deeper—a chivalrous concern for Gwendolen, though he kept the full weight of his thoughts even from his wife. Some secrets, he believed, were not meant to be shared.

Meanwhile, Deronda, now pledged to Mirah, faced the uneasy task of revealing his changed path to Sir Hugo. He chose to write rather than speak, fearing the sting of immediate disappointment in his fatherly friend’s voice. When the letter arrived, Sir Hugo’s vexation was sharp, though not unexpected. Seeking solace, he turned to Lady Mallinger, who—despite her gentle nature—could not help lamenting that Deronda’s brilliance should lead him to "such a fascination with the Jews."

“Nonsense,” Sir Hugo reassured her, though his own misgivings ran deeper. Privately, he had imagined a far more suitable match for Deronda—one with Gwendolen herself. The thought lingered, unspoken, as he watched the young widow with protective eyes.

Gwendolen, now settled at Offendene, moved through her days with a quiet resolve. The tempest of her past had left her humbled, her spirit softened by the kindness she had once taken for granted. She no longer raged against the world but accepted its small mercies—the dawn light, the steadfastness of a friend’s regard—as gifts she scarcely deserved. Her reflections were shadowed by remorse, yet also by a fragile hope: the certainty of Deronda’s return. In her mind, he was inextricably tied to her redemption, the fixed point by which she steadied herself.

But Deronda’s path was diverging. His heart belonged to Mirah, his future to a purpose greater than himself. Still, he could not ignore the duty he owed Gwendolen. Twice he visited Diplow, and twice he left without speaking of his impending marriage, his resolve faltering before her need. It was only on his third visit—finding Hans Meyrick already installed, cheerfully painting and gossiping—that he steeled himself for the inevitable.

Hans, ever perceptive beneath his lightheartedness, sensed the shift in Deronda but misread its cause. “Surely you don’t expect Mrs. Grandcourt to mourn forever?” he teased, unaware of the deeper current beneath Deronda’s silence.

The next day, Deronda rode to Offendene. Gwendolen greeted him with a steadiness that belied the storm within. “I didn’t want to cause you more pain,” she said softly, mistaking his solemnity for worry over her grief.

Then came the words she had not prepared for.

“I have only recently learned who my parents were,” Deronda confessed, watching her face. “My mother is a Jewess.”

Gwendolen’s breath caught. “What difference need that have made?” she asked, bewildered.

“To me, it makes all the difference,” he replied gently. “It has shaped my purpose—one that will take me away from England, perhaps for years.”

Her composure fractured. “But you will come back?” she whispered, tears unchecked.

“If I live,” he said. “*Some time.*”

Silence stretched between them, heavy with all that remained unsaid. At last, she asked, barely audible:

“What are you going to do?”

The question hung in the air, a bridge between two worlds—one she could not yet see, and one he could no longer delay.

In the tapestry of life, seasons intertwine—blossoms cling to branches even as the harvest is gathered, and new seeds are sown in the same breath. Love, too, carries its own rhythms, weaving joy from sorrow, comfort from hardship, until every memory of suffering is softened by the sweetness of devotion.

For Daniel Deronda, love was a sacred duty—a vow to shield and cherish. When he first saw Mirah, her face had been a portrait of despair, her steps weighed down by thorns. But now, bathed in contentment, she bloomed like a delicate flower touched by sunlight, her every movement radiant with quiet happiness. To Deronda, her joy was his own; his greatest pleasure lay in knowing she was safe, loved, and free from pain.

Mirah knew nothing of Hans’ silent longing or Gwendolen’s heartache. Secure in Deronda’s love, she saw Gwendolen’s concern as simple gratitude, the natural response of one who had been saved by his kindness. And though Deronda spoke sparingly of Mrs. Grandcourt, his words only reinforced Mirah’s belief that he had been a guiding light to many. That she alone would walk beside him always seemed a wonder too sweet to question.

When the bridal veil settled over Mirah, it trembled not with doubt but with reverence—a humble awe at the gift of a love so deep it demanded a lifetime of devotion. Under the velvet canopy, no pair stood more worthy of their vows, their promises more true, their future more bright.

Their wedding was a celebration of unity, bound by the traditions of Mirah’s faith. And though the feast was modest, it brimmed with warmth, for among the guests were those dearest to them—the entire Cohen family (save the teething baby), the Meyricks, who had loved Mirah as their own, and friends who had shaped their journey. Mordecai, his eyes alight with quiet joy, watched over the gathering like a spirit already half-transcended, his gaze always returning to Deronda with boundless trust.

Gifts arrived, tokens of affection from those who cherished them—Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger’s generous provisions for travel, the Klesmers’ finely inscribed watch. But the most precious offering came from Diplow: a letter from Gwendolen, her words a balm to Deronda’s heart.

*"Do not think of me sorrowfully on your wedding day,"* she wrote. *"You once told me I might become a woman who brings gladness to others. I do not yet see how, but you saw what I could not. If I ever rise to that goodness, it will be because you showed me the way. Forgive me for the grief I caused you. Do not carry it any longer. Because I have known you, I shall be better—I will be better."*

With the vows spoken and the celebrations concluded, preparations began at once for their journey east. Mordecai—frail, yet serene—had wished to travel with them, but as the days passed, it became clear his strength was fading. One quiet morning, he gathered them close.

"Do not leave me today," he murmured. "Before nightfall, I will be gone."

He sat upright, hands clasped in theirs, his gaze drifting between them with a peace that needed no words. Only as the light dimmed did he speak again, his voice soft but sure.

"Death comes as a divine kiss," he said to Deronda. "It parts us in body but unites us in spirit. Where you go, I will follow. Have I not breathed my soul into you? We shall live together."

Then, with effort, he lifted himself slightly and whispered the ancient Hebrew prayer of oneness, the final affirmation of faith. Gently, he sank back. Long into the night, Mirah and Deronda held him, until his breath stilled at last.

*"Nothing is here for tears,"* only the quiet of a noble departure—a life fulfilled, a love enduring, and a promise that would never fade.
