I walked onward, face turned to the moon, who hung low and unlovely over the treeline, not yet risen high enough to escape the forest’s shadow. She looked dented, scuffed, cast from an old battered disc of copper, dispirited and worn thin, no clouds hovering to keep her company, the surrounding stars burning far too bright to let her shine bright. *Is this never going to end?* her slanted, dim glow seemed to ask. We traveled opposite paths across the sky and through the undergrowth, yet we walked side by side for miles through the trees, her casting that dull, faded silver light, me moving as the live shadow stretched long before her. I barely looked up at her, my eyes fixed on the ground, but I could feel her disconsolate gaze fixed to my back all the while.

A flash of stark white under a sprawling old beech snagged my attention, and I turned toward it. Vague in the leaf-filtered moonlight, it resolved into the shape of a human body as I drew near. “Another skeleton,” I muttered to myself, kneeling to rest a hand on it. It was no skeleton, though—nothing could have been closer to bone without having already turned to dust. It lay curled on its side, cold not the hard, unyielding cold of stone, but the quiet, hollow cold of something that had very recently stopped being alive. The closer I stared, the more I ran my fingers over its frame, the more certain I was that it had to be dead. For one bewildered, silly moment I thought it was one of the wild, whirling nocturnal dancers, some ghostly Cinderella that had lost its way back to its realm and perished in the strange, unforgiving dark of this untamed world.

The body was fully naked, wasted so thin that even in the dappled shadow I could count every rib without touching them. Every bone beneath its skin was outlined sharp and clear, as if it was stretched tight over a frame nothing more substantial than thin elastic leather. Its lips had retracted back to show a full set of bright, unmarred teeth that glinted ghastly through the dim. Its hair was longer than its entire frame, thick, fine to the touch, black as the deepest void. It was the body of a tall woman that must have once moved with effortless grace. How had she ended up here? She could not have wandered this far on her own while already wasting away to nothing. She must have collapsed mid-step, fallen where she stood, and starved where she lay. But how had she grown this gaunt without any sign of decay, any mark of scavengers, any of her clothing torn away? No wild beast would have left her body unmolested, yet there was not a single scratch on her.

I stood up, staring down at her, certain I could not leave her exposed like this, forsaken out in the woods. Reverence would not let me. Even a woman’s clothing deserves respect; I could never leave her uncovered, for any wandering brute or unkind eye to stumble across. It would take years for rain and soil to pull her down into the earth. But the ground beneath me was laced so thick with tangled tree roots it was almost solid, and I had nothing but my bare hands to dig with. At first I thought she could not have been dead for more than a few hours—there was not a single hint of rot or decay anywhere on her. But then, what was even left of her to decay? What if she was not dead at all? The rules of this world were strange, unrecognizable, anything was possible. Even if there was barely a sliver of a chance I could bring her back, I could not bury her before I was absolutely certain she was gone.

I remembered I had stuffed a bunch of ripe wild grapes I’d found by the forest hall doorway in my pocket earlier, a handful still clinging to the stem. Their sweet, juicy pulp might be the only thing that could rouse her, if there was any life left to rouse. Her mouth hung slightly open, and to shift her to a better position I slid my arm under her shoulder—and found the pine needles beneath her still warm. She could not have been dead for long. She *might* still be alive, even if I could not feel a heartbeat, even if I could not see the faintest flicker of breath against my palm. One of her hands was clenched tight, curled around something small I could not make out. I squeezed a drop of grape juice onto her tongue, but there was no swallow, no reaction at all.

I spread a thick layer of dry pine needles and crisp dead leaves across the ground, laid my own outer tunic—still warm from my body—over top of it, lifted her as gently as I could onto the makeshift pallet, then piled my extra clothes and armful after armful of leaves over her to trap what little residual heat she had left, hoping that once the sun rose it would build back up in her. I squeezed another drop of grape juice into her mouth, still no movement. “Doubt’s a terrible thing to build your hopes on,” I told myself, “but it’s an even worse reason to do nothing at all.” Her skin was stretched so tight over her bones I dared not rub her to stimulate circulation, for fear of tearing it. I crawled into the heap of leaves beside her, tucked myself against her, and wrapped my arms around her. I did not have much extra heat left to share, but every bit of it I would give to her to try to bring her back. I spent the rest of the night sleepless, waiting for the sun, her cold seeping into my bones, none of my heat seeming to sink into her at all. I had run away from the soft, beautiful sleepers I’d found laid out on their dim silver couches, just to end up here, holding a possible corpse through the dark. I had turned down a gentle, lovely rest for this awful, sacred duty. Beneath that sad, dimming copper moon I lay with the almost-dead, watching for the dawn.

The dark lightened, the eastern horizon bleeding pale grey, when I spotted it: movement, low to the ground, not far from our hiding spot. A huge, undulating snake slid past me in a straight unswerving line, heading toward something I could not yet see. Not long after that, a roe doe and her calf appeared, moving the same direction, then two bear cubs trailed by three or four tiny scurrying creatures no bigger than squirrels. The light was growing fast, and when a troop of tiny horses trotted past minutes later I could see they were all fully grown, no bigger than the smallest Shetland pony, perfectly formed: some built like miniature cart horses, others like tiny war chargers, racehorses, hunters. Dwarf cattle and palm-sized elephants followed after them, all on the same path. “Where are the Little Ones?” I thought, suddenly anxious. The second I am sure about this woman I will go back and fetch them, bring them to this place. Where were all these creatures going? Was this a great exodus, or just their morning routine? I could not leave her until the sun came up. I rested my hand on her chest, and thought she felt just a little bit warmer than she had the night before. She could have stolen a little of the heat I lost to the cold, sure—but there was no reason to lose that tiny flicker of hope.

The edge of the day’s sky began to glow pink, and the sun peeked over the treetops, bright, innocent, like it had never seen a world like this before. At the sight of that raw, untainted daylight I jumped to my feet, full of sharp, fierce energy, ready to fight death itself to take her from it. I pulled the scrap of cloth I had tucked over her face to keep pine needles out of her eyes and mouth, and stared down at her, half terrified I would find nothing but an empty husk, half praying I would find a living soul. She was still as motionless as the day I found her. In full daylight now I could see how hollow her cheeks were, how sharp every bone jutted under her thin skin, how every line of her teeth was visible beneath her lips. The human body she wore was worn so thin it was almost threadbare—but that did not mean the soul inside was not still nested there, ready to wake, ready to speak. I rearranged the scrap of cloth over her face, laid a handful of leaves over top to shield her from the direct sun, and set off to follow the procession of creatures.

Their track was wide, well trodden for who knew how long, dozens of smaller paths branching in from the forest on both sides to merge and widen it. The trees fell away behind me, and a vast, rolling expanse of vivid green grass stretched all the way to the horizon. A small, full-banked river ran along the edge of the treeline, and the main track led straight to its shores. Bluish mist curled off the water, vanishing the second it drifted up into the air, and on the far side hundreds of tiny grazing animals were feeding, having swum across to reach the fertile plain. The moment I saw the water a wild, new hope bloomed in my chest. I knelt to drink, and the water was hot, stinging my palms, with a faint sharp metallic tang. This was the warmth I had been searching for—the exact thing I needed to rouse her.

I did not even pause to think how strange it was, how alone I was out there. The thought of this woman waking, of no longer being the only living human in this whole wild world, was overwhelming. “No matter who she turns out to be,” I thought, “I will never be lonely again.” I had been such poor company for myself for so long, I had forgotten what real hope felt like. This blessed hot water would chase away the cold that was clinging to her, and drown my desolation for good. She was impossibly light, all delicate bones with barely any flesh between them, so light I could carry her easily on one arm, her head leaning against my shoulder like a sleeping child. I walked as softly as I could, afraid the very wind of my movement might jostle her and snuff that last tiny possible flicker of life out. The water was far too hot to submerge her immediately, the shock could scare any lingering soul right out of her, so I laid her on the bank, dipped my tunic in the warm current, and bathed every inch of her wasted frame slowly, gradually. She was so gaunt, save for her long thick black hair, I could not even guess if she was young or old. Her lids were cracked just barely open, like a crack in the clouds over a dark night, with no sun shining through. The longer I bathed her the more my hope faded, that those bones would never fill out again, that her eyes would never lift to look at me—but I kept going, warming every part of her so no patch grew cold while I tended to another, until at last I dared to step into the river with her, holding her face above the surface and letting the slow warm current wrap all around her. I noticed, but could make no sense of it, that her clenched hand never loosened its hold for even a second.

After ten minutes I lifted her out, dried her off, wrapped her in my clothes, then ran back into the forest for armfuls of leaves to pile over her. The grass and soil were warm to the touch, and when I came back she had barely lost any of the heat the water had given her. I laid more leaves over her, ran back for more, then a third trip, a fourth, until I could safely leave her to go explore the rocky hills I had spotted a short way up the stream. When I reached them, I found the river poured fully formed straight out of a gap at the base of the largest hill, as if it had been rushing down a long hidden stairway inside the rock desperate to get out, finally finding its exit. The stream did not fill the whole opening, so I crept through the crack into a small, sheltered cave, and found that the water rose quiet and steady out of the back of the cave floor like the base of a great stone column, running along a deep narrow channel cut into the rock. I realized right away if I dragged a few long fallen boughs across the channel, wove smaller twigs between them, and piled dry moss and thick leaves over top, I could make a soft warm bed suspended right over the running hot water, that would stay perfectly heated day and night.

I went back for her, carried her into the cave, laid her down on that new warm couch, and when I pried her mouth open to tuck a grape inside, I found I could open it far wider than the day before. The grape sat heavy on her tongue, but I hoped some of its juice was seeping slowly down her throat. Two hours later, she was no longer cold at all. The warmth from the running stream had sunk fully into her frame. It was not the warmth of a living person, not yet—but it was the kind of steady heat that could let a flicker of trapped life hang on, if it was there. I had read stories of people in trances that laid motionless for weeks without moving, without eating, without speaking. For seven whole days and seven whole nights I sat in that cave with her, waking and sleeping in turns, never leaving her unwatched. Every morning I bathed in the hot river myself, and felt like I had eaten a full meal, drunk my fill, enough to sustain me for the whole rest of the day—so every morning I bathed her too, in the same warm water, leaving a fresh grape on her tongue each time. Once a faint discoloration bloomed on her left side that terrified me, making me think decay had finally set in, but it was gone entirely the next sunrise. I ate only the wild grapes and berries I could forage just inside the treeline, and found I barely needed that much at all. I dreamed every single night of a wounded angel that fell from the sky, who could not fly, who stayed with me, who loved me and never wanted to leave—and every time I woke, it was not that glowing angel face I saw, it was her pale, motionless wasted face staring up at the cave ceiling. But Adam himself, waiting for Eve to wake, could not have watched more anxiously than I watched for this woman to open her eyes. Adam had not yet known what it was to be alone, to hunger for another soul. I was a stranger in this world, far from all my people, and I had learned to crave connection so fiercely that if this last shred of possible womanhood faded away, all I would have left inside me was a burning, endless hunger for life. For a little while I even forgot the Little Ones, left safe where I had hidden them. The state of the world did not matter, nothing mattered but that this form beside me might wake, might open her eyes, might look at me. I realized then: a man all alone is never fully a man. He is just a possibility, a need, half finished. No man can be enough for himself. You only grow into your full self, your full individuality, when you are surrounded by other souls. You cannot breathe in an atmosphere made only of yourself. Even if this woman woke and was nothing at all like the angel in my dreams, I would no longer have to wander alone, no longer have to go join the grazing beasts just for the sake of being near something alive. Even with that tiny, almost impossible flicker of hope, I was no longer the poorest creature in this whole wild world. I was a possible man, on the cusp of finally being whole.
