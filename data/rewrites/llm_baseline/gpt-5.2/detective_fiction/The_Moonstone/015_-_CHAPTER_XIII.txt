I found my lady seated in her own sitting-room, the air about her close with the heat of late summer and the faint, comforting ghost of lavender-water that never left her cushions. Outside, the house had its usual breath and pulse—distant footfalls on boards that had been scrubbed till they shone, the soft draw of curtains, a sparrow’s chatter in the ivy. It was an ordinary domestic music; and yet, when I told her that Sergeant Cuff wished to speak with her, she started as if some harsh instrument had been struck too near her ear.

“Must I see him?” she said. “Can’t you represent me, Gabriel?”

I was plain enough in my face, I dare say, for she explained at once—being as honest with me as she was proud with the world.

“I am afraid my nerves are a little shaken,” she said. “There is something in that police-officer from London which I recoil from—I don’t know why. I have a presentiment that he is bringing trouble and misery with him into the house. Very foolish, and very unlike me—but so it is.”

It astonished me. To my mind, the Sergeant had come in like a man who listens—who hears, not only what is spoken, but what the house itself whispers in its timbers and its silence. I had taken to him the more I saw of him. Still, my lady, after letting her fear show, gathered herself again, as she always did—high courage, and no vanity about it.

“If I must see him, I must,” she said. “But I can’t prevail on myself to see him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and stay here as long as he stays.”

It was the first such attack of megrims I remembered in her since she had been a young girl. I went back to the boudoir. Mr. Franklin strolled out into the garden, and joined Mr. Godfrey, whose time for departure was now drawing near. Sergeant Cuff and I went straight to my mistress’s room.

I do declare my lady turned a shade paler at the sight of him. She mastered herself directly, however, and asked him whether he objected to my being present. She added, with that just pride which never forgot the place of her people, that I was her trusted adviser as well as her old servant, and that, in what touched the household, I was the likeliest person to consult.

The Sergeant answered, with civility, that he took my presence as a favour—he had something to say about the servants in general, and had already found my experience of use to him. My lady pointed to two chairs, and we sat down at once, as if it were a business of accounts and not a trouble that could sour the very bread we broke under this roof.

“I have already formed an opinion on this case,” says Sergeant Cuff, “which I beg your ladyship’s permission to keep to myself for the present. My business now is to mention what I have discovered upstairs in Miss Verinder’s sitting-room, and what I have decided—if your ladyship leaves it to me—on doing next.”

Then he spoke of the smear on the paint, and what it meant, with the same clearness as he had used downstairs, only in language that showed respect where Seegrave showed only suspicion. He had the manner of a man who could name a thing precisely, as a cook names the instant when a sauce turns—too soon and it is thin, too late and it is ruined.

“One thing,” he said, winding up, “is certain. The Diamond is missing out of the drawer in the cabinet. Another thing is next to certain. The marks from the smear on the door must be on some article of dress belonging to somebody in this house. We must discover that article of dress before we go a step further.”

“And that discovery,” my mistress said, “implies, I presume, the discovery of the thief?”

“I beg your ladyship’s pardon—I don’t say the Diamond is stolen. I only say, at present, that the Diamond is missing. The discovery of the stained dress may lead the way to finding it.”

Her ladyship looked at me, as if to make sure she had not misheard a word.

“Do you understand this?” she asked.

“Sergeant Cuff understands it, my lady,” I answered. “How do you propose to discover the stained dress?” she asked him.

Now my lady’s temper, where her people were concerned, came out in its best strength. “My good servants, who have been with me for years, have had their boxes and rooms searched already by the other officer. I can’t and won’t permit them to be insulted in that way a second time!”

There was a mistress to serve. There was a woman in ten thousand, if you like.

“That is the very point I was about to put to your ladyship,” said the Sergeant. “The other officer has done a world of harm to this inquiry, by letting the servants see that he suspected them. If I give them cause to think themselves suspected a second time, there’s no knowing what obstacles they may not throw in my way—the women especially. At the same time, their boxes must be searched again—for this plain reason, that the first investigation only looked for the Diamond, and that the second investigation must look for the stained dress. I quite agree with you, my lady, that the servants’ feelings ought to be consulted. But I am equally clear that the servants’ wardrobes ought to be searched.”

It sounded a dead-lock, and my lady said as much, though in finer words than mine.

“I have got a plan to meet the difficulty,” said Sergeant Cuff, “if your ladyship will consent to it. I propose explaining the case to the servants.”

“The women will think themselves suspected directly,” I said, breaking in—knowing the kitchen as well as I knew my own hands: how a whisper curdles there, and how quickly a look becomes a verdict.

“The women won’t, Mr. Betteredge,” answered the Sergeant, “if I can tell them I am going to examine the wardrobes of everybody—from her ladyship downwards—who slept in the house on Wednesday night. It’s a mere formality,” he added, with a side-look at my mistress; “but the servants will accept it as even dealing between them and their betters; and, instead of hindering the investigation, they will make a point of honour of assisting it.”

I saw the truth of that, and so did my lady when her first surprise passed. A house is like a well-ordered table: if the carving is done in view of all, and the portions are plainly fair, envy is quieter and tongues are less busy.

“You are certain the investigation is necessary?” she asked.

“It’s the shortest way that I can see, my lady, to the end we have in view.”

My mistress rose to ring the bell for her maid.

“You shall speak to the servants,” she said, “with the keys of my wardrobe in your hand.”

He checked her with a question I did not expect.

“Hadn’t we better make sure first,” he asked, “that the other ladies and gentlemen in the house will consent, too?”

“The only other lady in the house is Miss Verinder,” my mistress answered, surprised. “The only gentlemen are my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite. There is not the least fear of a refusal from any of the three.”

I reminded her then that Mr. Godfrey was going away. And as I spoke, Mr. Godfrey himself knocked to say good-bye, and Mr. Franklin followed him in, meaning to go with him to the station.

My lady explained the difficulty. Mr. Godfrey settled it on the spot—one of those men who are quickest with a gesture when it makes him look good. He called to Samuel through the window to take his portmanteau upstairs again, and then he put the key into Sergeant Cuff’s hand.

“My luggage can follow me to London,” he said, “when the inquiry is over.”

The Sergeant took the key, with a proper apology. “I am sorry to put you to any inconvenience, sir, for a mere formality; but the example of their betters will do wonders in reconciling the servants to this inquiry.”

Mr. Godfrey, after taking leave of my lady in his most sympathising manner, left a message for Miss Rachel which made it plain enough to me that he had not taken No for an answer, and meant to put the marriage question again when next he could. Mr. Franklin, on following his cousin out, told the Sergeant that all his clothes were open to examination, and that nothing he owned was kept under lock and key. Sergeant Cuff acknowledged it as he should.

So far, you see, his way had been met with readiness by my lady, by Mr. Godfrey, and by Mr. Franklin. There remained only Miss Rachel to follow their lead, before we called the servants together and began the search for the stained dress.

But my lady’s strange shrinking from the Sergeant came back on her the moment we were alone. She wanted him out of her room, out of her hearing—out of the sound of his voice, as if it jarred the air itself.

“If I send you down Miss Verinder’s keys,” she said, “I presume I shall have done all you want of me for the present?”

“I beg your ladyship’s pardon,” he answered. “Before we begin, I should like, if convenient, to have the washing-book. The stained article of dress may be an article of linen. If the search leads to nothing, I want to be able to account next for all the linen in the house, and for all the linen sent to the wash. If there is an article missing, there will be at least a presumption that it has got the paint-stain on it, and that it has been purposely made away with, yesterday or today, by the person owning it. Superintendent Seegrave,” he added, turning to me, “pointed the attention of the women-servants to the smear, when they all crowded into the room on Thursday morning. That may turn out, Mr. Betteredge, to have been one more of Superintendent Seegrave’s many mistakes.”

My lady told me to ring the bell and order the washing-book. She stayed until it came, in case the Sergeant had anything further to ask of her.

The washing-book was brought in by Rosanna Spearman. The girl had come down to breakfast that morning miserably pale and haggard, but was recovered enough from the illness of yesterday to do her work again. When she entered, the paper in her hand looked almost too heavy for her; when she went out, her crooked shoulder seemed to drag a little more than usual, as if the very air of the house pressed there.

Sergeant Cuff watched her closely—her face coming in; her shoulder going out—his eyes quiet, but missing nothing.

“Have you anything more to say to me?” my lady asked, eager as ever to escape him.

The great Cuff opened the washing-book, took it in as if it were a recipe he had known from boyhood, and shut it again in half a minute.

“I venture to trouble your ladyship with one last question,” he said. “Has the young woman who brought us this book been in your employment as long as the other servants?”

“Why do you ask?” said my lady.

“The last time I saw her,” said the Sergeant, “she was in prison for theft.”

After that, there was no help but to tell him the truth. My mistress spoke strongly of Rosanna’s good conduct in service, and of the high opinion the matron at the Reformatory had of her.

“You don’t suspect her, I hope?” my lady said at the end—earnest, almost fierce, as if to ward off a stain upon the girl’s name as you would ward off smoke from bread fresh out of the oven.

“I have already told your ladyship,” he answered, “that I don’t suspect any person in the house of thieving—up to the present time.”

That done, my lady rose and went upstairs herself to ask Miss Rachel for the keys. The Sergeant opened the door for her, bowing low. My lady shuddered as she passed him, as if the sound of his coat-sleeve brushing the air was something she could not bear.

We waited. And waited. The house made its small, familiar noises again—somebody shifting a chair; a distant door softly closing; the faint tick of a clock that always seemed louder when people held their breath. Sergeant Cuff said nothing to me. He turned his melancholy face to the window, thrust his lank hands into his pockets, and whistled “The Last Rose of Summer” under his breath—so softly that it seemed less a tune than a kind of listening.

At last Samuel came in—not with the keys, but with a morsel of paper for me.

I got my spectacles out, fumbling more than usual under the Sergeant’s fixed, dismal eyes. The paper held two or three lines in pencil, written by my lady. Miss Rachel flatly refused to have her wardrobe examined. Asked for her reasons, she had burst out crying. Asked again, she had said: “I won’t, because I won’t. I must yield to force if you use it, but I will yield to nothing else.”

I understood my lady’s unwillingness to face Sergeant Cuff with such an answer from her daughter. If I had not been too old for the amiable weaknesses of youth, I believe I should have blushed to face him myself.

“Any news of Miss Verinder’s keys?” asked the Sergeant.

“My young lady refuses to have her wardrobe examined.”

“Ah!” said the Sergeant.

His face kept its discipline better than his voice did. That “Ah!” came like a note he had expected to hear, and yet it half angered and half frightened me without my knowing why.

“Must the search be given up?” I asked.

“Yes,” he said. “The search must be given up, because your young lady refuses to submit to it like the rest. We must examine all the wardrobes in the house or none. Send Mr. Ablewhite’s portmanteau to London by the next train, and return the washing-book, with my compliments and thanks, to the young woman who brought it in.”

He laid the washing-book on the table, took out his penknife, and began trimming his nails—cool as if we had been talking of garden-work and not of a jewel that carried with it, as old stories carry with them, a sort of fate that slips through families and years and will not be scrubbed away like dirt from a doorstep.

“You don’t seem to be much disappointed,” I said.

“No,” said Sergeant Cuff. “I am not much disappointed.”

I tried to make him speak plainer.

“Why should Miss Rachel put an obstacle in your way?” I asked. “Isn’t it her interest to help you?”

“Wait a little, Mr. Betteredge—wait a little.”

Cleverer heads than mine might have seen what he was driving at. Or a person less fond of Miss Rachel might have seen it. My lady’s horror of him might—so I have thought since—have meant that she saw it too, dimly, as the Scripture says, “in a glass darkly.” I didn’t see it yet—that’s all I know.

“What’s to be done next?” I asked.

He finished the nail he was at, looked at it a moment with melancholy interest, put up the penknife, and got quietly to his feet.

“Come out into the garden,” he said, “and let’s have a look at the roses.”
