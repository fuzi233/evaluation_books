Rosa had known no home but the Nuns' House since she was seven, and no mother but Miss Twinkleton. Her memory of her own mother was a vivid, haunting image of a pretty little creature, drowned at a party of pleasure, her long wet hair tangled with the scattered petals of ruined flowers. She remembered the wild despair of her father, who died broken-hearted on the first anniversary of that tragedy. His grief had been soothed by his old college friend, Edwin Drood, a widower like himself, and it was through this bond that the young couple had been united.

Yet, the atmosphere of pity that surrounded the orphan girl when she first arrived in Cloisterham had never truly faded. As she grew older, brighter, and prettier, the hue of that pity shifted from golden to roseate to azure, but it always adorned her with a soft, protective light. This desire to console and caress her led to her being treated as a child long past her years, petted and spoiled by everyone. Rivalries flared within the Nuns' House over who should bring her gifts or write to her most faithfully, bitter little skirmishes hidden beneath the veil of charity. Thus, Rosa became an amiable, giddy, and wilful creature, accustomed to receiving kindness but possessing an exhaustless well of affection in return.

News of a quarrel between two young men reached the establishment before breakfast, though no one knew exactly how. Whether carried by the birds, blown in with the air, kneaded into the bread, or delivered by the milkman, the rumor permeated every gable before Miss Twinkleton was fully dressed. The report was specific: Miss Landless's brother had thrown a bottle, a knife, and a fork at Mr. Edwin Drood.

The cook insisted it was all three items, along with a decanter. Psychologically, it mattered less what was thrown and more why. Miss Landless's brother had admitted admiring Miss Bud, and Mr. Drood had rebuked him for it. Enraged, the brother had "up'd" with the collection of kitchenware. Poor Rosa, hearing the rumors, put her fingers in her ears and retreated to a corner, begging not to be told more. Miss Landless, however, sought accurate intelligence from Mr. Crisparkle. Upon returning, she reported the incident to Miss Twinkleton, who filtered the details before sharing them with Rosa. Miss Landless emphasized the provocation her brother had received, glossing over the fact that the initial words had come from her lover's easy-going nature. She delivered a petition from her brother asking for forgiveness and then abruptly ended the subject.

Miss Twinkleton then addressed the schoolroom, dismissing the rumor as an exaggeration typical of "Rumour," a concept she illustrated with a theatrical reference to Shakespeare's depiction of the Devil. She declared the matter settled, urging the girls to focus on their studies. Yet, the subject persisted; Miss Ferdinand later wore a paper moustache and attempted to aim a water-bottle at Miss Giggles during dinner, resulting in a spoon being drawn in defense.

Rosa dwelt on the quarrel with an uncomfortable sense of involvement, fearing her engagement placed her in a false position. Her uneasiness was compounded when her guardian, Mr. Grewgious, arrived unexpectedly. He was a man of arid, sandy demeanor, with a scanty, mangy-looking crop of hair that resembled a wig, and a face carved with hard, unrefined curves. Despite his awkward walk, near-sightedness, and lack of polish, he possessed a strange capacity to make an agreeable impression.

He found Rosa and Miss Twinkleton in the study. After politely requesting Miss Twinkleton's withdrawal, he sat with Rosa by the fire. He introduced himself with a self-deprecating comparison to angels, noting that while angels were upstairs, his visits were few and far between. He consulted a pocket-book containing a "guiding memorandum" to navigate the conversation, as he lacked conversational powers.

He began with "Well and happy," confirming Rosa's contentment and praising Miss Twinkleton's care. Next came "Pounds, shillings, and pence." He questioned if her allowance was sufficient and if she was in debt. Rosa laughed at the notion of debt, finding it a comical vagary. Mr. Grewgious noted her innocence, lining out the financial notes in his book.

Then, he turned to "Marriage." He explained that he was visiting specifically to address the betrothal. He described himself as a "particularly Angular man," ill-suited to intrude on such intimate spheres, comparing himself to a bear with cramp in a Cotillon. He confirmed that Mr. Edwin Drood had visited recently and that their mutual liking was evident. He clarified that while they wrote to one another, true correspondence required a deeper alignment of wills.

Crucially, Mr. Grewgious explained the legal reality of their engagement. He handed Rosa a certified copy of her father's will. He detailed the terms: the betrothal was a sentiment and a friendly project, but it was not legally binding against the free will of the parties. If either party lost affection or the desire to marry, there was no forfeiture. Rosa would simply remain his ward until of age, and Mr. Drood would inherit his father's partnership and debts upon reaching majority. The arrangement was designed for their happiness, not obligation.

Rosa, perplexed, asked if she could settle these matters directly with Edwin. Mr. Grewgious agreed, suggesting they wait until Christmas when Edwin returned to finalize the details between themselves. He would then step back as a mere business acquaintance. Rosa requested he visit her at Christmas if she had anything to say, to which he agreed with characteristic humility, mentioning his upcoming Christmas dinner with a particularly angular clerk. Overwhelmed with gratitude, Rosa kissed him on the shoulder.

After releasing Miss Twinkleton from his presence, Mr. Grewgious visited Mr. Jasper at the gatehouse, finding him absent at the Cathedral. He then entered the cathedral itself, where the setting sun cast a sepulchral gray over the ancient stones. Inside, the choir sang a powerful, drowning music that eventually subsided into silence.

Mr. Jasper met him at the chancel steps. Mr. Grewgious reiterated his purpose: to inform Rosa that the engagement could be dissolved without penalty if either party wished it. When Jasper asked if there was a special reason, Mr. Grewgious replied bluntly that it was simply his duty. He then added, somewhat sharply, that he hoped this did not imply doubt regarding Mr. Drood.

Jasper, sensing the tension, pressed Mr. Grewgious's arm and smiled, though his lips were pale. He wagered that Rosa had not hinted at wanting to be released from Ned. Mr. Grewgious retorted confidently that he would win the wager, attributing any hesitation to Rosa's delicate nature as a motherless girl. He observed that Rosa seemed to have an instinct that preliminary arrangements should be made solely between herself and Mr. Drood, excluding them.

Agreeing, they decided that when Mr. Drood returned at Christmas, the young couple would complete their preparations independently. Only then would Mr. Grewgious and Mr. Jasper intervene to finalize the business and release them from their trusts on Edwin's birthday. They parted with a handshake, Mr. Grewgious blessing them, and Mr. Jasper saving them.
