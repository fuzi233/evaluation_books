The acoustic environment of the piazza burst upon Tito as a dissonant assault, a chaotic tapestry of sound that grated against the mind’s natural rhythms. He pushed his way into the Fierucoloni, finding a strange, numbing solace in the sheer volume of the hooting and elbowing. It was a sensory overload that temporarily drowned out his dreary calculations of the future, a future where he foresaw himself wandering away solitary in pursuit of some unknown fortune, perhaps even looping back in search of Baldassarre. 

At every inlet, the cyclical tide of humanity surged into the square beneath paper lanterns that waved like captive stars. A rude, monotonous chant from the country folk wove a distinctly traceable, ancient strand of noise, but it was violently overlaid by the screeching whistles, the harsh beating of drums, and the ringing of little bells—an abrasive acoustic ecology that threatened psychological equilibrium. Fitfully, a lantern would vanish with the smash of a hurled stone, followed by the jagged phonemes of boyish screams. Yet, on the outskirts of this swirling, commercialized tumult, deeper, multi-generational rhythms held fast to the vigil of the Nativity of the Virgin.

Here, the mythic seamlessly integrated into the domestic. Ragged men rattled their tongues in aggressive, dominant lexicons to lure country people into rigged games; masquerading figures on stilts swung lanterns in meteoric arcs. Most grounding was the dense, yeasty aroma billowing from a small covered stall doing brisk trade in hot *berlingozzi*. The olfactory landscape was heavy with the scent of this beloved farinaceous delicacy—sweet dough enriched with fresh egg yolks, twisted into rings, and baked to a golden, caramelized crust that radiated the warm, toasted notes of milled wheat and crushed aniseed. To eat such a pastry was to consume the agrarian history of the region.

Tito found himself swept against a trotting procession of barefooted, hard-heeled contadine. Their sun-dried, bronzed faces and obsolete, earth-dyed garments made them appear as a way-worn ancestry returning from a pilgrimage begun centuries ago. These were the scant-feeding peasant-women from the mountains of Pistoia. They bore on their backs moderate bundles of yarn—the tactile culmination of a year’s domestic labor—bringing the fruits of their agrarian routines to the Blessed Virgin. In their hearts rested a wide, dim fear of harm and a quiet hope, seeking the miraculous intercession of an angelic image whose protective potency was to stream forth on this Eve of her Nativity. As they spoke among themselves, Tito heard the fading inflections of their native mountain dialects, a linguistic heritage slowly eroding beneath the hegemonic weight of city speech, carrying subjective, earth-bound experiences that remained wholly untranslatable to the urban ear.

Forced toward the piazza's boundary, Tito encountered his acquaintance Bratti, who had judiciously placed himself away from the central din. Bratti stood against a pillar, his mouth pursed in disdainful silence, acting as a meticulous hoarder of material histories. His hand rested firmly on a serge cloth concealing his basket of wares. Two women approached with a look of curiosity, and Bratti, weaponizing his dominant urban lexicon to establish an immediate power dynamic, drew the cover tighter.

“Before I answer that, Monna, I must know whether you mean to buy,” Bratti said. “I can’t show such wares as mine in this fair for every fly to settle on and pay nothing. My goods are a little too choice for that. Besides, I’ve only two left, and with the chances of the pestilence that wise men talk of, there is likelihood of their being worth their weight in gold. No, no: *andate con Dio*.”

The two women exchanged a look, their native dialect silenced by his sharp Florentine commerce. “And what may be the price?” the second asked.

“Not within what you are likely to have in your purse, *buona donna*,” Bratti replied in a compassionately supercilious tone. “I recommend you to trust in Messer Domeneddio and the saints: poor people can do no better for themselves.”

“Not so poor!” the woman retorted indignantly, drawing out her money-bag. “Come, now! what do you say to a *grosso*?”

Through a calculated dance of mercantile coquetry, Bratti finally raised the cover. “Since you’ve set your mind on it, I should be loth to do you a mischief; for Maestro Gabbadeo used to say, when a woman sets her mind on a thing and doesn’t get it, she’s in worse danger of the pestilence than before. *Ecco!*” 

The precious objects were two clumsy iron rings, beaten into the fashion of old Roman artifacts, disinterred from the layered soil of antiquity. Their rust and hidden mythic potency were so deeply satisfactory to the agrarian women that the heavy coins were paid, and the remainder settled with a barter of hand-spun yarn. 

Covering his basket, Bratti turned and caught sight of Tito. “By the head of San Giovanni, now,” Bratti said, drawing him back to the pillar. “This is a piece of luck... talking of rings, it is your ring—that very ring you’ve got on your finger—that I could get you a purchaser for; ay, and a purchaser with a deep money-bag. A Genoese who is going straight away into Hungary. He came rummaging through all the shops to see if I had any old things I didn't know the price of. I told him I knew a fine young man who had a ring of that sort.”

Tito listened, the cyclical nature of his past pressing upon him. Why keep the ring? It was a talisman of an interwoven timeline, taken from Baldassarre’s finger and placed upon his own when his hand had grown to the needful size. He felt a sudden, profound desire to sever himself from this artifact of his personal history, to escape the recognition that was a relentless claim from the past.

“You speak with your usual wisdom, Bratti,” Tito said, suppressing the physical memory of the ring’s cool metal. “When and where shall I have speech of him?”

“To-morrow, at three hours after sunrise, at my shop,” Bratti replied, assuring Tito he should demand a heavy price, before complaining of the fair's dullness now that his goods were sold.

Tito moved away, carried by the zigzagging current of the crowd into a deep recess where the superficial commercialization of art and faith was in full display. A man in a white mitre adorned with yellow cabalistic figures stood elevated in stark light against the dim, waving lanterns. It was Maestro Vaiano, a mountebank Tito recognized. The man’s voice possessed a peculiarly sharp, penetrating ring—an acoustic pollution that shattered the acoustic ecology of the night.

“Behold, my children!” the conjuror shouted, peddling cheap matrimonies and fake indulgences from Pope Alexander the Sixth. “Spit once, my sons, and pay a white quattrino!” 

The scene was a grotesque degradation of the sacred, reduced to mere buffoonery and a transactional exchange of copper. A monkey, tied to the altar-like table, fidgeted with a taper while an impish boy in a surplice cuffed the animal. The linguistic power dynamic here was absolute: the conjuror’s rapid-fire, cynical patter overrode the simple awe of the crowd, offering them tiny bags of *Brevi* with secret charms. When a youth named Mariotto stepped up with his plump brunette bride, the tension snapped. The abused monkey, tired of the boy's over-active discipline, leaped with such violence that its slackened knot gave way. It bounded onto the head of a tall woman, shrieking and chattering.

Amidst the screaming and the chaotic disruption of the commercial spell, Tito slipped out of the circle. Craving auditory relief, he allowed himself to be pushed into the church of the Nunziata.

The transition across the threshold was a revelation in acoustic ecology. The hellish hubbub of the piazza dissolved into a resonant, uninterrupted Gregorian chant from the choir—a continuous sonic vibration that offered instant repose to the battered ear. Visually, the brilliant yellow splendor of silver lamps pressed upon his eyes, a heavenly illumination for peasant-women whose mountain skies were often obscured. The olfactory landscape was profoundly layered: the purifying, citrus-and-pine sharpness of frankincense and melting beeswax mingled intimately with the coarse, musky scent of rain-washed wool and the sweat of bronzed mountain dwellers. 

Above the worshippers, a multi-generational saga was physically suspended. The walls and ceilings were crowded with votive waxen images—effigies of Florentines in black silk *lucco*, popes, emperors, and famous *condottieri*. Centuries of history were wedged together, their faces blackened by time or fresh in new steel corselets. It was a resurrection-swarm of remote mortals, a frozen timeline watching over the cyclical rituals of the living.

Tito’s glance wandered the wild multitude until he found her. Tessa was kneeling just inside the doorway, her head hung wearily, blue eyes absently fixed on an altar-piece of the Archangel Michael. Her right hand listlessly gripped a bunch of silkworm cocoons arranged with fortuitous regularity on a stem. Her lips pouted; she was a large image of a sweet, sleepy child, entirely uncorrupted by the calculating lexicons of the city. To Tito, her loving, ignorant soul offered a refuge from his impending isolation, a world where subjective experience didn't require translation or justification.

He slipped quietly to her side and knelt. “Tessa!”

She turned, her face blooming into a happy, silent smile. “My little Tessa! you look very tired. How long have you been kneeling here?”

She collected her thoughts. “I’m very hungry.”

“Come, then; come with me.” He lifted her from the stone floor and led her to the open cloisters. 

“The Madre is ill,” Tessa explained, her voice carrying the musical, unhurried cadence of her people. “She sent me to bring these cocoons to the Santissima Nunziata, because they’re so wonderful. She thinks the Holy Madonna may take away her pains. But somebody took my bag with the bread and chestnuts in it...”

The loss of her food was not a mere utilitarian deprivation; she had been robbed of her daily sustenance, the dense, hearth-baked bread and the sweet, fire-roasted mountain chestnuts that rooted her to the earth and the seasons. 

“Yes, my little Tessa, you shall take them,” Tito promised, understanding the profound need for culinary comfort. “But first come and let me give you some *berlingozzi*. There are some to be had not far off.”

“Where did you come from?” she asked, bewildered. “I thought you would never come to the Mercato for milk any more. I set myself Aves to say, to see if they would bring you back...”

“You see I come when you want some one to take care of you, Tessa. But what shall you do if you are here all alone? Where shall you go?”

“Oh, I shall stay and sleep in the church,” she murmured, accepting her place in the ancient, circular rhythm of the harvest festival. “A great many of them do... and the *patrigno* is coming with the mules in the morning.”
