Newland Archer sat at the writing-table in his library on East Thirty-ninth Street, the house quiet at last in that after-dinner lull when even the city seemed to chew more slowly.

He had come straight back from the official reception for the opening of the new Metropolitan Museum galleries. The evening had been all marble and velvet, officials and benefactors flowing like a well-trained sauce around the great rooms. The air had smelt of fresh varnish, damp wool, crushed violets, and that faint metallic tang that clings to newly lit electric lamps. Yet it was not the glitter of the new that had stayed with him. It was the way a remark, tossed off as carelessly as one salts a dish, had struck a buried place in him.

“Why, this used to be one of the old Cesnola rooms,” he had heard someone say; and instantly the present fell away. He was no longer under lofty ceilings with catalogued treasures arranged like provisions in a well-ordered pantry. He was sitting alone on a hard leather divan against a radiator that clicked and sighed, while a slight figure in a long sealskin cloak moved away down the meagrely furnished vista of the old Museum—Ellen, retreating as if she were the last warm loaf carried out of reach and into winter.

The soundscape of the reception—the scraping of chairs, the bright clink of glasses, the hiss of laughter that rose and broke against stone—had, for a moment, turned into other sounds: the radiator’s tick, the museum’s hollow hush, the soft sweep of her steps. It was curious how the ear remembered what the mind tried to file away under “finished.”

Now, in his own library, he looked around as though he had been absent for years and had come home to find the familiar room subtly seasoned by time. For over thirty years it had been the stage of his private life. Here the family’s great announcements had been made with the ritual caution of old New York—nothing flung down raw, everything served with garnish and circumlocution, as if plain speech were a coarse ingredient.

Here, nearly twenty-six years ago, May had told him—blushing, circling the fact as carefully as she would have folded a napkin—that she was to have a child. Here Dallas, too delicate to be taken to church in midwinter, had been christened by their old friend the Bishop of New York, broad and glowing, a man whose voice filled a room the way a cathedral organ fills the chest: a sound you lean into without noticing you’ve done so. Here Dallas had first tottered across the floor shouting “Dad,” while May and the nurse laughed behind the door—laughter like the quick rattle of dried peas in a tin. Here their second child, Mary—so like her mother—had announced her engagement to the dullest and most reliable of Reggie Chivers’s many sons; and here Archer had kissed Mary through her wedding veil before they went down to the motor that would carry them to Grace Church. The world had shifted under everyone’s feet, but the “Grace Church wedding” endured like some old recipe no one dared alter, however much the market changed.

In this room he and May had mapped out the children’s future as methodically as menus: the studies of Dallas and his younger brother Bill, Mary’s incurable indifference to “accomplishments,” her appetite for sport and philanthropy, and Dallas’s vague leanings toward “art,” which had ended with him in the office of a rising New York architect. The young men, Archer had noticed, were leaving law and business as if those trades were heavy, overcooked things their fathers insisted were nourishing. They ran after politics, reform, archaeology, architecture, landscape-engineering—everything that sounded like rebuilding rather than counting. They argued about Georgian types, corrected one another’s vocabulary, and protested at the meaningless use of the word “Colonial.” Nobody, they said, had “Colonial” houses now except the millionaire grocers of the suburbs.

Yet if he searched for the moment when he had felt most intensely that his life had been asked to matter, it was not May’s news, nor a child’s first step, but an evening in this same room when the Governor of New York, down from Albany to dine and stay the night, had turned on him suddenly, pounding the table and gnashing his eye-glasses as if the words themselves were hard to digest.

“Hang the professional politician! You’re the kind of man the country wants, Archer. If the stable’s ever to be cleaned out, men like you have got to lend a hand in the cleaning.”

Men like you. He could still taste the phrase—sharp as mustard, warming as brandy. It had made him rise at once, eager, as if called into a kitchen where there was urgent work to be done. It echoed Ned Winsett’s old appeal to roll up his sleeves and get down into the muck; but this time it came from a man whose example made refusal feel like cowardice.

Looking back, Archer was not sure men like himself had been what the country needed in that active service Theodore Roosevelt had pointed toward. There was reason to think it did not: after a year in the State Assembly he had not been re-elected, and had slipped back, with a kind of relief, into obscure municipal work—useful, modest—and then into writing occasional articles for one of the reforming weeklies trying to shake the country out of apathy. It was little enough to show, perhaps. But when he remembered what the young men of his generation and set had expected—money-making, sport, society, a life eaten cold and unexamined—then even his small contribution seemed to count, as each brick counts in a well-built wall.

He had done little in public life. He had always been by nature contemplative, a dilettante. But he had had high things to contemplate, great things to delight in; and one great man’s friendship to steady him. People were beginning to call him “a good citizen.” In New York, every philanthropic, municipal, or artistic movement wanted his opinion and his name. “Ask Archer,” they said, when there was talk of the first school for crippled children, reorganising the Museum of Art, founding the Grolier Club, inaugurating the new Library, or getting up a society of chamber music. His days were full, decently filled. He supposed it was as much as a man ought to ask.

Something he knew he had missed: the flower of life. He had come to think of it as unattainable and improbable, like a first prize in a lottery where the tickets ran into the hundreds of millions and the prize was one. The odds had been against him so completely that to repine would have been like lamenting that he had not inherited a kingdom. Ellen Olenska had become, in his mind, not so much a person as the composite vision of all that had been denied him: a perfume trapped in a stopped bottle, faint but unmistakable.

That vision—thin as it had grown—had kept him from thinking of other women. He had been what was called a faithful husband; and when May had suddenly died—carried off by the infectious pneumonia through which she had nursed their youngest—he had honestly mourned her. Long years had shown him that it did not matter so much if marriage was a dull duty, so long as it kept the dignity of a duty. Once you let duty spoil into appetite, the whole thing curdled. Looking about him, he honoured his own past and mourned it. After all, there was good in the old ways.

His gaze made the familiar round of the room. Dallas had “done it over” with English mezzotints, Chippendale cabinets, bits of chosen blue-and-white, and pleasantly shaded electric lamps—light softened like milk in tea. But Archer’s eye always came back to the old Eastlake writing-table he had refused to banish, and to his first photograph of May beside the inkstand.

There she was: tall, round-bosomed, willowy, in starched muslin and a flapping Leghorn hat, as he had first seen her under the orange-trees in the Mission garden. As he had seen her that day, so she had remained—never quite at the same height, perhaps, but never far below it: generous, faithful, unwearied. Yet she had lacked imagination, been incapable of growth. The world of her youth had fallen apart and rebuilt itself without her ever feeling the tremor. She carried a hard bright blindness like a lantern held close: it lit what was near and made the distance disappear. Her incapacity to recognise change made her children conceal their views from her as Archer had concealed his. From the first there had been a joint pretence of sameness, a kind of innocent family hypocrisy in which father and children had collaborated without speaking of it.

She had died thinking the world a good place, full of loving households like her own. She had gone resigned because she believed, firmly, that whatever happened Newland would continue to inculcate in Dallas the same principles and prejudices that had shaped his parents’ lives, and that Dallas in turn would transmit the sacred trust to little Bill. Of Mary she was as sure as of her own self. So, having snatched little Bill from the grave and given her life in the effort, she had gone contentedly to her place in the Archer vault in St. Mark’s, where Mrs. Archer already lay safe from the terrifying “trend” her daughter-in-law had never even become aware of.

Opposite May’s photograph stood one of their daughter. Mary Chivers was as tall and fair as her mother, but large-waisted, flat-chested, slightly slouching, as fashion now required. Mary’s mighty feats of athleticism could not have been performed with the twenty-inch waist May’s azure sash had so easily spanned. The difference seemed symbolic. The mother’s life had been as closely girt as her figure. Mary, no less conventional and no more intelligent, yet led a larger life and held more tolerant views. There was good in the new order too.

The telephone clicked. Archer turned, unhooked the receiver at his elbow, and listened as the modern world announced itself with its dry little sound. How far it was from the days when the brass-buttoned messenger boy’s legs had been New York’s only quick communication—quick, and yet human, with footfall and breath and street-noise clinging to the message.

“Chicago wants you.”

It must be Dallas. The firm always sent Dallas on errands, and he was in Chicago to talk over plans for a Lakeside palace for a young millionaire with ideas.

“Hallo, Dad—Yes: Dallas. I say—how do you feel about sailing on Wednesday? Mauretania: yes, next Wednesday as ever is. Our client wants me to look at some Italian gardens before we settle anything, and has asked me to nip over on the next boat. I’ve got to be back on the first of June—” The voice broke into a joyful, conscious laugh—“so we must look alive. I say, Dad, I want your help: do come.”

It startled Archer—less the request than the laugh. That laugh, travelling over all those miles—forest, river, mountain, prairie, roaring cities, indifferent millions—arrived in his library as plainly as if the boy were lounging by the fire. The laugh said without saying: Of course I must get back on the first, because Fanny Beaufort and I are to be married on the fifth.

Dallas began again, brisk and affectionate. “Think it over? No, sir: not a minute. You’ve got to say yes now. Why not, I’d like to know? If you can allege a single reason—No; I knew it. Then it’s a go, eh? Because I count on you to ring up the Cunard office first thing tomorrow; and you’d better book a return on a boat from Marseilles. I say, Dad; it’ll be our last time together, in this kind of way—. Oh, good! I knew you would.”

Chicago rang off. Archer stood a moment, holding the dead receiver, hearing the faint after-noise in his ear as one hears the sea after a door has shut. Then he rose and began to pace the room.

It would be their last time together in that particular way; the boy was right. There would be other “times,” no doubt: the two were born comrades, and Fanny Beaufort—whatever one thought of her—did not seem likely to interfere with their intimacy. Archer suspected she would be folded into it naturally, as some new spice is folded into an old dish until you cannot imagine it without. Still, change was change. He felt the temptation to seize this last chance of being alone with his son.

There was no reason not to, except the profound one that he had lost the habit of travel. May had disliked moving except for valid reasons—taking the children to the sea or the mountains. She could imagine no other motive for leaving Thirty-ninth Street or their comfortable quarters at the Wellands’ in Newport. After Dallas took his degree she had decided it was her duty to travel for six months; and the whole family had made the old-fashioned tour through England, Switzerland, and Italy. Their time being limited (no one knew why) they had omitted France. Archer remembered Dallas’s wrath at being asked to contemplate Mont Blanc instead of Rheims and Chartres. But Mary and Bill wanted mountain-climbing and had already yawned their way in Dallas’s wake through English cathedrals; and May, always fair, had insisted on balancing athletic and artistic proclivities. She had even proposed that Archer should go to Paris for a fortnight and join them later on the Italian lakes. He had declined.

“We’ll stick together,” he had said; and May’s face had brightened, as though he had set their son a moral example as simple as eating what one is served.

Since her death two years before, there had been no reason to keep the same routine. The children urged him to travel. Mary was sure it would do him good to go abroad and “see the galleries,” as though art were a tonic one swallowed in measured doses. The mysteriousness of such a cure made her only the more certain of it. But Archer had found himself held fast by habit and memories and a startled shrinking from new things.

Now, reviewing his past, he saw how deep a rut he had sunk into. The worst of doing one’s duty was that it seemed to unfit one for doing anything else. At least that was the view the men of his generation had taken. The divisions between right and wrong, honest and dishonest, respectable and the reverse, had been so trenchant that there was little room for the unforeseen. And then came moments when a man’s imagination, so easily subdued to what it lives in, rose abruptly above daily level and looked down the long windings of destiny. Archer hung there and wondered.

What was left of the little world he had grown up in, with its standards that bent and bound him? He remembered a sneering prophecy poor Lawrence Lefferts had uttered years ago in this very room: “If things go on at this rate, our children will be marrying Beaufort’s bastards.”

It was exactly what his eldest son, the pride of his life, was doing—and nobody wondered, nobody reproved. Even Aunt Janey, who still looked as she had in her elderly youth, had taken her mother’s emeralds and seed-pearls out of their pink cotton-wool and carried them with her own twitching hands to the future bride. And Fanny Beaufort, instead of looking disappointed at not receiving a Paris “set,” had cried out at their old-fashioned beauty, declaring that when she wore them she would feel like an Isabey miniature.

Fanny Beaufort had appeared in New York at eighteen after her parents’ death and won its heart much as Ellen Olenska had won it thirty years earlier—only New York did not distrust her, did not draw back from her. It took her joyfully for granted. She was pretty, amusing, accomplished: what more did anyone want? Nobody was narrow-minded enough to rake up the half-forgotten facts of her father’s past or her own origin. Only older people remembered Beaufort’s failure, or the quiet marriage, after his wife’s death, to the notorious Fanny Ring, and the way he had left the country with his new wife and a little girl who inherited her beauty. He had been heard of in Constantinople, then Russia, and later American travellers spoke of being handsomely entertained by him in Buenos Ayres, where he represented a large insurance agency. He and his wife had died there in the odour of prosperity. Then one day their orphaned daughter had appeared in New York in charge of May Archer’s sister-in-law, Mrs. Jack Welland, whose husband had been appointed the girl’s guardian. The fact threw her into an almost cousinly relationship with the Archer children. Nobody was surprised when Dallas’s engagement was announced.

Nothing could more clearly measure the distance the world had travelled. People were too busy—busy with reforms and “movements,” with fads and fetishes and frivolities—to bother much about their neighbours. In the huge kaleidoscope where social atoms spun on the same plane, what account was anyone’s past?

And now Archer stood in Paris.

He looked out of his hotel window at the stately gaiety of the streets and felt his heart beat with the confusion and eagerness of youth, as if some old clock inside him had been wound too tight. It was long since it had plunged and reared under his widening waistcoat, leaving him a moment later with an empty breast and hot temples. He wondered if it was like that for Dallas with Fanny Beaufort—and decided it was not.

“It functions as actively, no doubt,” he reflected, “but the rhythm is different.”

The difference, perhaps, was that young people now assumed they would get what they wanted, while his generation had almost always assumed they would not. But then—could a heart ever beat as wildly for what one is certain of in advance?

It was the day after their arrival. Spring sunshine held Archer at his open window above the wide silvery prospect of the Place Vendôme. Paris moved below him like an orchestration: wheels on stone, voices braided in many pitches, the occasional cry of a street-seller rising and falling, a distant hammering softened by distance into something almost like percussion. The city sounded alive in a way New York, with its newer clamours and harsher engines, did not. Here, even noise seemed to have a history, a vocabulary that had not been entirely drowned by industry’s blunt consonants.

When he had agreed to come abroad with Dallas, he had stipulated—almost the only thing—that in Paris he would not be taken to one of the newfangled “palaces.”

“Oh, all right—of course,” Dallas had agreed. “I’ll take you to some jolly old-fashioned place—the Bristol, say—” leaving Archer speechless at the thought that the century-long home of kings and emperors had become an “old-fashioned” inn visited for quaint inconveniences and lingering local colour, as one might seek out a country kitchen for the taste of wood-smoke and slow-cooked broth.

In the early impatient years Archer had often pictured his return to Paris. Then the personal vision had faded, and he had tried simply to see the city as the setting of Ellen’s life. At night in his library, after the household slept, he had evoked spring down the avenues of horse-chestnuts, flowers and statues in public gardens, lilac-scented flower-carts, the river’s majestic roll under great bridges, and the life of art and study and pleasure that filled each artery of the city to bursting. Now it was all before him, and he felt shy, old-fashioned, inadequate: a grey speck compared with the ruthless magnificent fellow he had once dreamed of being.

Dallas’s hand came down cheerfully on his shoulder. “Hullo, father: this is something like, isn’t it?” They stood a while looking out in silence. Then Dallas added, as lightly as if noting the hour of a train: “By the way, I’ve got a message for you: the Countess Olenska expects us both at half-past five.”

He said it carelessly, almost teasingly. Archer looked at him and thought he caught, in the young man’s gay eyes, a gleam of old Catherine Mingott’s malice.

“Oh, didn’t I tell you?” Dallas pursued. “Fanny made me swear to do three things while I was in Paris: get her the score of the last Debussy songs, go to the Grand-Guignol, and see Madame Olenska. You know she was awfully good to Fanny when Mr. Beaufort sent her over from Buenos Ayres to the Assomption. Fanny hadn’t any friends in Paris, and Madame Olenska used to be kind to her and trot her about on holidays. I believe she was a great friend of the first Mrs. Beaufort’s. And she’s our cousin, of course. So I rang her up this morning, before I went out, and told her you and I were here for two days and wanted to see her.”

Archer continued to stare at him. “You told her I was here?”

“Of course—why not?” Dallas’s eyebrows went up whimsically; and then, getting no answer, he slipped his arm through his father’s with a confidential pressure. “I say, father: what was she like?”

Archer felt colour rise under his son’s unabashed gaze. “Come, own up: you and she were great pals, weren’t you? Wasn’t she most awfully lovely?”

“Lovely? I don’t know. She was different.”

“Ah—there you have it! That’s what it always comes to, doesn’t it? When she comes, she’s different—and one doesn’t know why. It’s exactly what I feel about Fanny.”

Archer drew back a step, releasing his arm. “About Fanny? But, my dear fellow—I should hope so! Only I don’t see—”

“Dash it, Dad, don’t be prehistoric! Wasn’t she—once—your Fanny?”

Dallas belonged body and soul to the new generation. He was Newland and May Archer’s first-born, and it had never been possible to instil in him even the rudiments of reserve. “What’s the use of making mysteries? It only makes people want to nose ’em out,” he always objected when urged to discretion. Yet in his banter Archer saw the filial light, affectionate and fearless.

“My Fanny?”

“Well, the woman you’d have chucked everything for—only you didn’t,” Dallas continued, watching him.

“I didn’t,” Archer echoed, and the words came out with a kind of solemnity, like a vow renewed.

“No: you date, you see, dear old boy. But mother said—”

“Your mother?”

“Yes: the day before she died. It was when she sent for me alone—you remember? She said she knew we were safe with you, and always would be, because once, when she asked you to, you’d given up the thing you most wanted.”

Archer received this in silence. His eyes fixed unseeingly on the crowded square below, where carriages and motors moved like toys in bright sun. At length he said in a low voice: “She never asked me.”

“No. I forgot. You never did ask each other anything, did you? And you never told each other anything. You just sat and watched each other, and guessed at what was going on underneath. A deaf-and-dumb asylum, in fact! Well, I back your generation for knowing more about each other’s private thoughts than we ever have time to find out about our own.—I say, Dad,” Dallas broke off, suddenly anxious, “you’re not angry with me? If you are, let’s make it up and go and lunch at Henri’s. I’ve got to rush out to Versailles afterward.”

Archer did not accompany him to Versailles. He preferred to spend the afternoon alone, walking Paris as one walks through a remembered house, touching walls, opening doors, letting dust rise and settle. He had to deal all at once with the packed regrets and stifled memories of an inarticulate lifetime.

After a little while he did not regret Dallas’s indiscretion. It loosened something like an iron band from his heart to know that, after all, someone had guessed and pitied—and that it had been May moved him indescribably. Dallas, for all his affectionate insight, would not have understood why. To the boy the episode was only a pathetic instance of frustration, wasted forces. But was it really no more? Archer sat for a long time on a bench in the Champs-Élysées while the stream of life rolled by, listening to the layered sounds—footsteps on gravel, wheels, birds threading their thin bright calls through the heavier human din—and wondered how much of a life’s meaning was made of what could not be spoken in the dominant tongue of one’s time.

A few streets away, a few hours away, Ellen Olenska waited. She had never gone back to her husband; and when he died, years before, she had made no change in her way of living. There was nothing now to keep her and Archer apart—no law, no vow, no living obstacle but the old habits of speech and silence, the inherited grammar of restraint. And that afternoon he was to see her.

He rose and walked across the Place de la Concorde, through the Tuileries gardens, to the Louvre. Ellen had once told him she often went there. He wanted, before meeting her, to spend time in a place where she might lately have been—where her eyes had rested on the same colours, her steps had sounded on the same floors.

For an hour or more he wandered from gallery to gallery through the dazzle of afternoon light. Paintings burst on him one by one in half-forgotten splendour, as if doors long closed had been flung open and air rushed in. The smell of old varnish, stone, and human warmth mixed with the faint dust of centuries. Beauty—stored and labelled and guarded—still struck him with the force of something freshly made.

After all, his life had been too starved.
