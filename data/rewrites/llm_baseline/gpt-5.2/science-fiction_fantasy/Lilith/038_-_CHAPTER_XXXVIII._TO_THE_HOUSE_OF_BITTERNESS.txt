Morning lifted itself, grey and cool, from the desert like steam from a quenched pan, and we set out at once for the forest. I rode Lona’s horse, her body steadied before me—wrapt in her own cloak, the cloth still holding, as linen holds, the faint domestic ghosts of her: sun-warm wool, crushed leaf, a trace of the smoke that clung to us from so many small fires. I would take her to her father. He would give her a couch in the chamber of his dead—or, if he refused because she had not come of her own will, then I would keep watch over her in the waste until wind and time took what was left. Yet I believed he would receive her, for surely she had died long ago; and the thought of humbling myself before him had a taste in it like green persimmon—bitter, necessary, cleansing.

And Lilith also I must take—back to her first husband. I had no craft for penitence; I could not cook remorse in her like a slow broth, nor coax it out with sweetness. I had hardly a right to slay her, and less right to loose her on the world; and least of all a right to be made for ever her gaoler. Again and again as we went I offered her food—fruit, a crust I had saved, the last of a honeyed thing the Little Ones carried like treasure. She answered only with a look of hungering hate, as if my gifts were insults, as if even smell was a chain. Her fiery eyes rolled restlessly, taking in everything with a predator’s hunger, and I think they never once closed until we reached the far side of the hot stream. After that they shut, and did not open again until we came to the House of Bitterness.

Toward evening, when we camped, a small domestic scene rose out of the harshness as naturally as a pot set to simmer: a little girl wandered to Lilith with the solemn patience of children who have fed calves and rescued beetles, and I ran to prevent mischief. But before I could reach them, the child had already pressed something to the princess’s lips—some small fruit, soft-skinned, bursting with a sharp juice—and she gave a scream, then held up her hand, injured and indignant.

“Please, king,” she whimpered, tears standing like dew, “suck finger. Bad giantess make hole in it!”

I took her hand and sucked the tiny finger. The taste was iron and salt—blood, clean and intimate, like the first cut in a kitchen when you forget the knife is new. The pain left her face as quickly as steam leaving a lid.

“Well now!” she cried, astonished by so simple a remedy; and scarcely a minute passed before she was holding up a second fruit, offering it again with a courage that would have shamed grown people.

This time Lilith snapped for it like a starving thing, and the child snatched her hand away so swiftly the fruit dropped and rolled in the dust. The little girl’s name was Luva. I remembered it the way you remember the name of a seed you mean to plant again.

The next day we crossed the hot stream. The Little Ones, back on their own ground, grew loud with delight—not the harsh loudness of machines or marching men, but the lively, layered noise of a healthy place: feet padding, laughter bubbling, elephant-breathing like bellows, the creak of harness, the far insect-choir rising and falling. Even the river’s hiss—hot water licking stones—felt like part of a great kitchen where the world was always boiling something.

Their nests, though, were still far, and we went no farther than the ivy-hall, where I had resolved to pass the night for the grapes’ sake. The hall was green with old ivy, and the vines hung heavy with clusters—purple-black, bloom-dusted, each grape tight as a held breath. The smell struck us first: wine-promise, sun, leaf, a faint musk from the hall’s damp stones.

The Little Ones knew at once. They swarmed the clusters, ate greedily—fingers sticky, chins glossy—and within minutes were asleep where they fell: on the hall’s green floor, in the ferny fringe beyond, under the trellis where the last light sifted through.

I hoped again to see the dance. I had the children leave a wide space in the middle; then I lay down among them with Lona by my side. Sleep would not take me. Grief is a kind of hunger that refuses all offered food.

Night thickened; and suddenly the company was there—those pale dancers, those weary celebrants of a time that had already perished. Their lights glimmered like lanterns seen through kitchen steam. Their feet made no sound, yet the very air seemed to keep their rhythm; and I found myself wondering whether, night after night, they would go on thus to all eternity—whether the dance was a punishment or a ritual of remembrance, and whether one day, because of my stiff neck and my stubborn tongue, I should be drawn into their circle and made to move until I forgot my own name.

Then the children’s eyes opened. They sprang up as if the grapes had been a charm rather than a meal. Wide awake, they ran straight into the dancers. Each Little One caught a partner—hand to hand, small to spectral—and away they went, bounding and skipping. The spectres seemed to see them and welcome them. Perhaps they knew all about the Little Ones; perhaps they too had long been on their way back to childhood, as old stories go back to their first language. The children’s gambols—never rude, always merry—put little bright fractures in the solemn pattern; and if the rhythm jarred, the poor dancers, who had nothing to smile with, showed no annoyance. It was as if the living noise of the children—their breath, their footfalls, their quick cries—tuned the dead air, restoring to it some lost ecology of sound, some balance between silence and song.

Just before morning began to break, I started up.

In the doorway stood the skeleton-princess, her eyes open and glowing, and on her side the fearful spot—black, like a bruise that had learned to think. For a moment she held herself as if listening to the dance; then she glided in, as if to join it.

I sprang to my feet. A cry of repugnant fear broke from the children, and the lights vanished as if a hand had pinched them out. The low moon looked in; the children clung to each other. The ghosts were gone—or at least no longer visible. The princess too had disappeared.

I darted to where I had left Lilith. She lay with her eyes closed, as if she had never moved. Her stillness had the false calm of a pot left on the edge of the fire: quiet, but ready to boil over.

I returned to the hall. The Little Ones were already lying down, composing themselves to sleep with the same matter-of-fact ease with which they ate. A world that still remembers how to rest is a world not yet wholly conquered.

In the morning, as we started, we spied two skeletons moving in a thicket. The Little Ones broke ranks and ran to them, and I followed. I walked now with ease—no splint, no ligature—and I recognised the pair I had seen before in that neighbourhood.

The children made friends at once: they held the skeletons’ arms as if they were old relatives returned from a long field-work, stroked the bones of their long fingers with a tenderness that needed no translation. The poor creatures took their attentions kindly. They seemed on excellent terms with each other—common deprivation drawing them together; the loss of everything becoming the beginning of a new life, like a village rebuilt after a famine with stricter reverence for seed.

They had handfuls of herbs and were looking for more—presumably to rub their bones with, for how else could nourishment reach so rudimentary a system? The Little Ones examined the herbs as carefully as any cook sniffing a bundle before chopping: sharp, resinous stems; leaves that gave off bitterness when bruised; small blossoms with a honeyed breath. Then they gathered the same sorts and filled the skeletons’ outstretched hands.

They bade them goodbye, promising to come again, chattering as we went that they had not known such nice people lived in the same forest. Their language—whatever its roots—carried a whole ethic in the way they said “nice,” as if kindness were a local crop you could cultivate.

When at last we came to the nest-village, I stayed one night to see them resettled. Lona still looked like one just dead; there seemed no need to hurry. Lilith had eaten nothing; her eyes remained shut. Fearful she might die before we reached the end of our journey, I went to her in the night and laid my bare arm upon her lips.

She bit into it so fiercely that I cried out. I do not know how I got away; I came to myself lying beyond her reach, the pulse in my arm thudding like a drum in a hollow room. The wound burned with that intimate fire that tells you another being has taken something from you, not merely flesh but will.

It was morning. At once I prepared for departure.

I chose twelve Little Ones—not the biggest and strongest, but the sweetest and merriest, the ones whose laughter carried furthest without turning shrill. I mounted them on six elephants. I took also two of the wise Clumsies, as the children called them, to bear the princess. I rode again on Lona’s horse, carrying Lona’s body before me.

As nearly as I could judge, I took the direct way across the left branch of the dead river-bed to the House of Bitterness. I hoped there to learn how best to cross the broader, rougher branch beyond, and how to avoid the basin of monsters. I dreaded the former for the elephants, the latter for the children. In a world where names have power, “basin of monsters” sounded like a word from a dominant lexicon—one that flattens many kinds of danger into one saleable fear. Yet the fear was real enough.

The third night, in the desert between the two branches of the dead river, was terrible.

We stopped the elephants in a sheltered place and let Lilith slip down between them to lie on the sand until morning. She seemed quite dead, but I did not think she was. I laid myself a little way from her, with Lona’s body by my other side, to keep watch at once over the dead and the dangerous.

The moon hung half-way down the west—pale, thoughtful—mottling the desert with shadows like sifted flour. Then, of a sudden, she was eclipsed: still visible, but sending forth no light, as though a thin veil had been drawn over a lamp. A thick, diaphanous film covered her patient beauty, and she looked troubled. The film swept aside a little, and I saw against her clearness the jagged outline of something bat-like—torn and hooked.

A cold wind came with a burning sting, like air that has passed through fire and carries its memory. Lilith was upon me.

Her hands were still bound; with her teeth she pulled from my shoulder the cloak Lona had made for me—work of patient fingers, woven against the day she must have known would come—and fixed her teeth in my flesh. I lay as one paralysed. Already my very life seemed flowing from me into her, like warmth being sucked from bread into a mouth that cannot be satisfied.

Then I remembered—remembered the simplest resistance, like remembering salt when a dish goes dull—and struck her on the hand.

She raised her head with a gurgling shriek, and I felt her shiver. I flung her from me and sprang to my feet.

She was on her knees, rocking to and fro. A second blast of hot-stinging cold swept us; the moon shone out clear again, as if the sky itself were disgusted by what had crossed it. I saw her face—gaunt, ghastly, smeared with red.

“Down, devil!” I cried.

“Where are you taking me?” she asked, her voice like a dull echo from a sepulchre—sound without warmth, words without any native soil beneath them.

“To your first husband,” I answered.

“He will kill me!” she moaned.

“At least he will take you off my hands!”

“Give me my daughter!” she suddenly screamed, grinding her teeth.

“Never! Your doom is upon you at last!”

“Loose my hands—for pity’s sake!” she groaned. “I am in torture. The cords are sunk in my flesh.”

“I dare not. Lie down!”

She threw herself on the ground like a log. The rest of the night passed in peace; and in the morning she again seemed dead.

Before evening we came in sight of the House of Bitterness.

At once one of the elephants drew alongside my horse. A Little One rode on his neck, leaning forward as if to whisper into my very ear, careful not to spill fear into the whole herd.

“Please, king, you are not going to that place?” he breathed.

“Indeed I am. We are going to stay the night there.”

“Oh, please, don’t! That must be where the cat-woman lives!”

“If you had ever seen her, you would not call her by that name.”

“Nobody ever sees her: she has lost her face! Her head is back and side all round.”

“She hides her face from dull, discontented people. Who taught you to call her the cat-woman?”

“I heard the bad giants call her so.”

“What did they say about her?”

“That she had claws to her toes.”

“It is not true. I know the lady. I spent a night at her house.”

“But she MAY have claws to her toes! You might see her feet, and her claws be folded up inside their cushions!”

“Then perhaps you think I have claws to my toes?”

“Oh, no; that can’t be! You are good!”

“The giants might have told you so,” I said, and let the question hang like a kettle just beginning to sing.

“We shouldn’t believe them about you!”

“Are the giants good?”

“No; they love lying.”

“Then why do you believe them about her? I know the lady is good; she cannot have claws.”

“Please how do you know she is good?”

“How do you know I am good?”

He fell back to tell the others what I had said. They hastened after me, and when they came up, their voices layered around me like a flock’s worried rustling.

“I would not take you to her house if I did not believe her good,” I said.

“We know you would not,” they answered; and the trust in their tone was a kind of bread—plain, hard perhaps, but sustaining.

“If I were to do something that frightened you—what would you say?”

“The beasts frightened us sometimes at first,” answered one, “but they never hurt us!”

“That was before we knew them!” added another.

“Just so,” I said. “When you see the woman in that cottage, you will know she is good. You may wonder at what she does, but she will always be good. I know her better than you know me. She will not hurt you—or if she does—”

“Ah, you are not sure about it, king dear!” a voice broke in. “You think she MAY hurt us!”

“I am sure she will never be unkind to you, even if she do hurt you.”

They were silent a while, letting that distinction—hurt and unkindness—settle in them like a new word in an old dialect.

“I’m not afraid of being hurt—a little—a good deal!” cried Odu at last. “But I should not like scratches in the dark! The giants say the cat-woman has claw-feet all over her house!”

“I am taking the princess to her,” I said.

“Why?”

“Because she is her friend.”

“How can she be good then?”

“Little Tumbledown is a friend of the princess,” I answered. “So is Luva: I saw them both, more than once, trying to feed her with grapes!”

“Little Tumbledown is good! Luva is very good!”

“That is why they are her friends.”

“Will the cat-woman—I mean the woman that isn’t the cat-woman, and has no claws to her toes—give her grapes?”

“She is more likely to give her scratches.”

“Why? You say she is her friend!”

“That is just why. A friend is one who gives us what we need, and the princess is sorely in need of a terrible scratching.”

Again silence; the desert listened too. The soundscape here was spare—wind dragging grains along the ground, the elephants’ slow breathing, leather creaking, the dry click of a pebble under hoof. No industrial roar, no false music piped in to sell courage. Fear had room to be itself.

“If any of you are afraid,” I said, “you may go home; I shall not prevent you. But I cannot take one with me who believes the giants rather than me, or one who will call a good lady the cat-woman!”

“Please, king,” said one, “I’m so afraid of being afraid!”

“My boy,” I answered, “there is no harm in being afraid. The only harm is in doing what Fear tells you. Fear is not your master. Laugh in his face and he will run away.”

“There she is—in the door waiting for us!” cried one, and covered his eyes.

“How ugly she is!” cried another, doing the same.

“You do not see her,” I said; “her face is covered!”

“She has no face!” they insisted, as if repeating a phrase the giants had coined for them—language that steals what it names.

“She has a very beautiful face. I saw it once. It is indeed as beautiful as Lona’s,” I added, and grief rose again like the smell of bread when you have no hunger left for it.

“Then what makes her hide it?”

“I think I know—anyhow, she has some good reason.”

“I don’t like the cat-woman! she is frightful!”

“You cannot like, and you ought not to dislike what you have never seen. Once more, you must not call her the cat-woman!”

“What are we to call her then, please?”

“Lady Mara.”

“That is a pretty name!” said a girl, and her voice made it prettier still. “I will call her ‘lady Mara’; then perhaps she will show me her beautiful face!”

Mara, dressed and muffled in white, stood indeed in the doorway to receive us. The cottage behind her looked like a humble place that had learned, over uncounted years, how to hold grief without letting it rot the rafters. Even before she spoke, I heard the difference in its quiet: not dead silence, but a listened-for stillness, as in a dairy at dawn.

“At last!” she said. “Lilith’s hour has been long on the way, but it is come. Everything comes. Thousands of years have I waited—and not in vain!”

She came to me, took my treasure from my arms, and carried Lona into the house as if she were carrying a loaf made for a festival—careful, reverent, accustomed to weight that is both nourishment and symbol. Then she returned and took the princess. Lilith shuddered but made no resistance. The beasts lay down by the door.

We followed our hostess. The Little Ones looked very grave, as if stepping into a story older than their own words.

Mara laid Lilith on a rough settle at one side of the room, unbound her, and turned to us.

“Mr. Vane,” she said, “and you, Little Ones, I thank you. This woman would not yield to gentler measures; harder must have their turn. I must do what I can to make her repent.”

The pitiful-hearted Little Ones began to sob, their small throats catching as if they had swallowed too dry a crust.

“Will you hurt her very much, lady Mara?” asked the girl who had praised her name, slipping her warm little hand into mine as if for anchorage.

“Yes; I am afraid I must; I fear she will make me,” answered Mara. “It would be cruel to hurt her too little. It would have all to be done again, only worse.”

“May I stop with her?”

“No, my child. She loves no one; therefore she cannot be WITH any one. There is One who will be with her, but she will not be with Him.”

“Will the shadow that came down the hill be with her?”

“The great Shadow will be in her, I fear; but he cannot be WITH her, or with any one. She will know I am beside her, but that will not comfort her.”

“Will you scratch her very deep?” asked Odu, going near and putting his hand in Mara’s. “Please, don’t make the red juice come!”

Mara caught him up, turned her back to the rest of us, drew the muffling down from her face, and held him at arm’s length so he might see her.

As if his face had been a mirror, I saw what he saw.

For one moment he stared, mouth open. Then a divine wonder rose in his countenance and swiftly changed to intense delight. He gazed, entranced; and when she set him down, he stood yet a moment looking up at her, lost in contemplation—then ran to us with the face of a prophet who knows a bliss he cannot tell, because there are joys no dominant language can own without bruising them.

Mara rearranged her mufflings and turned to the other children.

“You must eat and drink before you go to sleep,” she said. “You have had a long journey!”

She set the bread of her house before them and a jug of cold water.

They had never seen bread before. It was hard and dry—brown, dense, the sort of loaf made to last through lean weather. Yet they ate without any sign of distaste, chewing as if learning the meaning of grain by mouthfuls. They had never seen water before, and they drank it without demur; and one after another looked up from the draught with faces of glad astonishment, as if the coldness and purity had spoken in a tongue older than speech.

Then Mara led away the smallest, and the rest went trooping after her. With her own gentle hands, they told me, she put them to bed on the floor of the garret, as one lays kindling carefully so it will catch when the time comes—quietly, without fuss, letting the night’s true sounds settle around them like a blanket.
