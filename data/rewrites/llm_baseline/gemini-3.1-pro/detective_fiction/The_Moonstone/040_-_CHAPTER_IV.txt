The percussive roar of the tide seemed to obliterate my very capacity to process the world, leaving behind a profound acoustic void where my thoughts should have been. The shock of the discovery rendered me a hollow vessel, stripped of all sensory appreciation. I certainly could not have known what I was about when Betteredge joined me—for I have it on his authority that I laughed, a jagged, dissonant sound against the rhythm of the waves, and, putting the paint-smeared nightgown into his hands, told him to read the riddle for himself.

Of what was said between us on the beach, I have not the faintest recollection; the sonic environment was a blur of rushing blood and churning water. The first place in which the world’s acoustic ecology returned to me plainly is the plantation of firs. Betteredge and I were walking back together to the house, the pine needles silencing our footsteps. He was murmuring in that resonant, grounded cadence of the traditional agrarian elder, telling me that we should both be able to face this impossible truth once we had partaken of a glass of grog.

The scene shifts from the whispering plantation to the acoustic sanctuary of Betteredge’s little sitting-room. My resolution never to enter Rachel’s house again dissolved completely. I felt gratefully the cool, shadowy quiet of the room, a refuge from the chaotic dissonance of my own mind. Here, Betteredge initiated a ritual of sustenance that anchored my drifting soul. He mixed the grog—a perfectly new luxury to me at that hour—with meticulous reverence. This was no mere utilitarian fuel; he used icy-cold water drawn from the deep limestone well, carrying the raw, mineral tang of the earth, and blended it with a dark, cask-aged spirit that breathed of caramelized orchard fruits, toasted oak, and the faint, briny memory of the coast. 

Under any other circumstances, the heavy drink would simply stupefy me. As things were, the gustatory richness of the elixir bloomed across my palate, its complex, spicy warmth threading its way through my paralyzed nerves, harmonizing my fractured senses. I began to “face it,” as Betteredge had predicted. And Betteredge, partaking of the same profound domestic alchemy, began to “face it,” too.

The picture which I am now presenting of myself will, I suspect, be thought a very strange one. Placed in a situation without parallel, do I seclude myself? Do I hurry back to the industrial noise and superficial frenzy of London by the first train to consult the highest authorities? No. I accept the shelter of a house I had sworn to avoid, and I sit sipping exquisitely balanced spirits and water in the company of an old servant at ten o’clock in the morning. I can only answer that the sight of old Betteredge’s familiar face, worn into the gentle topography of a life lived in tune with the land, was an inexpressible comfort. The slow, mindful drinking of his grog nourished my depleted spirit in a way no sterile logic ever could. 

“Now, Mr. Franklin, there’s one thing certain, at any rate,” said Betteredge. He threw the nightgown down on the table between us, pointing to it as if it were a living creature whose false tongue had just disrupted our quiet room. “*He’s* a liar, to begin with.”

This comforting view of the matter was not the view that presented itself to my mind.

“I am as innocent of all knowledge of having taken the Diamond as you are,” I said, my voice sounding painfully thin. “But there is the witness against me! The acrid scent of the paint on the nightgown, and the woven name on the collar, are facts.”

Betteredge lifted my glass, the aroma of spiced rum and citrus peel rising warmly, and put it persuasively into my hand.

“Facts?” he repeated, his tone a rich, dismissive rumble. “Take a drop more grog, Mr. Franklin, and you’ll get over the weakness of believing in facts! Foul play, sir!” he continued, dropping his voice into a confidential, harmonic register. “That is how I read the riddle. Foul play somewhere—and you and I must find it out. Was there nothing else in the tin case, when you put your hand into it?”

The question instantly recalled the sensory memory of the tin box—the metallic chill, the smell of ancient, trapped mud. I took the letter from my pocket and opened it. It was a letter of many pages, closely written. I looked impatiently for the signature at the end. “Rosanna Spearman.”

As I read the name, a sudden remembrance illuminated my mind, piercing the fog. 

“Stop!” I exclaimed. “Rosanna Spearman came to my aunt out of a reformatory? Rosanna Spearman had once been a thief?”

“There’s no denying that, Mr. Franklin. What of it now, if you please?”

“What of it now? How do we know she may not have stolen the Diamond after all? How do we know she may not have smeared my nightgown purposely with the paint?”

Betteredge laid his weathered hand on my arm, his touch carrying the weight of generational wisdom, and stopped me before my anxious cadence could further pollute the quiet room.

“You will be cleared of this, Mr. Franklin, beyond all doubt. But I hope you won’t be cleared in *that* way. See what the letter says, sir. In justice to the girl’s memory, see what it says.”

I felt the earnestness with which he spoke—felt it as a friendly rebuke to the harsh, dominant lexicon of accusation I had just employed. “You shall form your own judgment on her letter,” I said. “I will read it out.”

I began—and read these lines, which attempted to translate the profoundly untranslatable depths of a solitary soul:

“Sir—I have something to own to you. A confession which means much misery, may sometimes be made in very few words. This confession can be made in three words. I love you.”

The paper, carrying the faint, olfactory ghost of the Shivering Sand, dropped from my hand. I looked at Betteredge. “In the name of Heaven,” I said, “what does it mean?”

He seemed to shrink from answering, turning his ear toward some distant, tragic frequency only he could hear.

“You and Limping Lucy were alone together this morning, sir,” he said softly. “Did she say nothing about Rosanna Spearman?”

“She never even mentioned Rosanna Spearman’s name.”

“Please to go back to the letter, Mr. Franklin. I tell you plainly, I can’t find it in my heart to distress you, after what you have had to bear already. Let her speak for herself, sir. And get on with your grog. For your own sake, rely on the grog.”

I took a slow, grounding breath, tasted the earthy spice of the drink, and resumed the reading of the letter.

“It would be very disgraceful to me to tell you this, if I was a living woman when you read it. I shall be dead and gone, sir, when you find my letter. It is that which makes me bold. Not even my grave will be left to tell of me. I may own the truth—with the quicksand waiting to swallow my native tongue and hide me when the words are written.

“Besides, you will find your nightgown in my hiding-place, with the smear of the paint on it; and you will want to know how it came to be hidden by me? and why I said nothing to you about it in my life-time? I have only one reason to give. I did these strange things, because I loved you.

“I won’t trouble you with much about myself, or my life, before you came to my lady’s house. Lady Verinder took me out of a reformatory. I had gone to the reformatory from the prison. I was put in the prison, because I was a thief. I was a thief, because my mother went on the streets when I was quite a little girl. My mother went on the streets, because the gentleman who was my father deserted her. There is no need to tell such a common story as this, at any length. It is a tragedy flattened into sterile ink, told quite often enough in the unfeeling newspapers.

“Lady Verinder was very kind to me, and Mr. Betteredge was very kind to me. Those two, and the matron at the reformatory, are the only good people I have ever met with in all my life. I might have got on in my place—finding some quiet rhythm in the domestic chores—if you had not come visiting. I don’t blame *you*, sir. It’s my fault—all my fault.

“Do you remember when you came out on us from among the sandhills, that morning, looking for Mr. Betteredge? You were like a prince in a fairy-story, stepping out of a myth into my everyday routine. You were the most adorable human creature I had ever seen. Something that felt like the happy life I had never led yet, leapt up in me at the instant I set eyes on you. Don’t laugh at this if you can help it. Oh, if I could only make you feel, only translate into your language, how serious it is to *me*!

“I went back to the house, and wrote your name and mine in my work-box, and drew a true lovers’ knot under them. Then, some devil—no, I ought to say some good angel—whispered to me, ‘Go and look in the glass.’ The glass told me—never mind what. I was too foolish to take the warning. I went on getting fonder and fonder of you, trying to bridge the chasm between our worlds. If you had known how I used to cry at night, the quiet acoustic isolation of my room amplifying the misery of your never taking any notice of me, you would have pitied me perhaps, and have given me a look now and then to live on.

“It would have been no very kind look, perhaps, if you had known how I hated Miss Rachel. I believe I found out you were in love with her, before you knew it yourself. She used to give you hothouse roses to wear in your button-hole. Ah, Mr. Franklin, you wore *my* roses oftener than either you or she thought! The only comfort I had at that time, was putting my freshly gathered wild rose secretly in your glass of water, in place of hers—and then throwing her ostentatious bloom away. I left my scent, my small olfactory offering, for you to breathe in.

“If she had been really as pretty as you thought her, I might have borne it better. No; I believe I should have been more spiteful against her still. Suppose you put Miss Rachel into a servant’s dress, stripping her of the dominant power of her station? It can’t be denied that she had a bad figure; she was too thin. But who can tell what the men like? It’s no business of mine. I can’t expect you to read my letter, if I write it in this way. But it does stir one up to hear Miss Rachel called pretty, when one knows all the time that it’s her silk dresses and the unearned confidence of her lexicon that does it.

“Try not to lose patience with me, sir. I will get on as fast as I can to the time which is sure to interest you—the time when the Diamond was lost.

“But there is one thing which I have got it on my mind to tell you first.

“My life was not a very hard life to bear, while I was a thief. It was only when they had taught me at the reformatory to feel my own degradation, forcing me to internalize their punitive vocabulary, that the days grew long and weary. A heart-breaking sensation of loneliness kept with me. It was my duty, I know, to try and get on with my fellow-servants in my new place. Somehow, I couldn’t make friends with them. They looked as if they suspected what I had been. I don’t regret having been roused to make the effort to be a reformed woman—but, indeed, indeed it was a weary life. You had come across it like a beam of sunshine at first—and then you too failed me. There was great misery in that.

“Now I am coming to what I wanted to tell you. In those days of bitterness, I went two or three times to my favourite place—the beach above the Shivering Sand. And I said to myself, ‘I think it will end here.’ You will understand, sir, that the place had laid a kind of spell on me before you came. Its deep, rhythmic hum was a soundscape that understood my sorrow. I did think that here was a mythic place which would end all my domestic troubles for me in a moment or two—and hide me for ever afterwards.

“I was so aggravated by the foolish talk among the women servants, and I was so angry with you for the pains you took in hunting for the jewel, bringing the harsh machinery of the law upon our quiet household, that I kept away by myself, until the officer from Frizinghall came to the house.

“Mr. Seegrave’s voice was an industrial noise pollution in our home. He began by setting a guard on the women’s bedrooms; and the women all followed him upstairs in a rage, insulted by his dominant presumptions. We found him in Miss Rachel’s room. He told us he wouldn’t have a lot of women there; and he pointed to the pungent smear on the newly painted door, claiming some of our petticoats had done the mischief, and sent us all downstairs again.

“After leaving Miss Rachel’s room, I stopped a moment on one of the landings, to see if I had got the chemical paint-stain on *my* gown. Penelope Betteredge passed, and noticed what I was about.

“‘You needn’t trouble yourself, Rosanna,’ she said. ‘The paint on Miss Rachel’s door has been dry for hours. If Mr. Seegrave hadn’t set a watch on our bedrooms, I might have told him as much.’

“Penelope was a hot-tempered girl. I quieted her, valuing the oral history she possessed over Seegrave’s loud accusations. 

“‘How do you know that?’ I asked.

“‘I was with Miss Rachel, and Mr. Franklin, all yesterday morning,’ Penelope said, ‘mixing the colours, breathing in the linseed and pigment while they finished the door. I heard Miss Rachel ask whether the door would be dry that evening. And Mr. Franklin shook his head, and said it wouldn’t be dry in less than twelve hours. It was three o’clock before they had done. What does your arithmetic say, Rosanna? Mine says the door was dry by three this morning.’

“‘Did some of the ladies go upstairs yesterday evening to see it?’ I asked. 

“‘None of the ladies made the smear,’ Penelope answered. ‘I left Miss Rachel in bed at twelve last night. And I noticed the door, and there was nothing wrong with it then.’

“‘Oughtn’t you to mention this to Mr. Seegrave, Penelope?’

“‘I wouldn’t say a word to help Mr. Seegrave for anything that could be offered to me!’

“She went to her work, and I went to mine.

“My work, sir, was to make your bed, and to put your room tidy. It was the happiest hour I had in the whole day, folding your linens, breathing in the faint, familiar scent of your shaving soap and the warmth of the pillows where your head had rested. No matter who has done it since, you have never had your clothes folded as nicely as I folded them for you. I will make haste, and go on again.

“Well, I went in that morning to do my work in your room. There was your nightgown tossed across the bed. I took it up to fold it—and I saw the stain of the paint from Miss Rachel’s door, carrying that unmistakable, sharp olfactory sting of fresh varnish and pigment!

“I was so startled by the discovery that I ran out with the nightgown in my hand, and locked myself into my own room, seeking absolute acoustic isolation to look at it where nobody could interrupt me.

“As soon as I got my breath again, I called to mind my talk with Penelope, and I said to myself, ‘Here’s the proof that he was in Miss Rachel’s sitting-room between twelve last night, and three this morning!’

“I shall not tell you in plain words what was the first suspicion that crossed my mind. You would only be angry—and tear my letter up.

“Let it be enough to say only this. After thinking it over, I made it out that the thing wasn’t likely, for a reason that I will tell you. If you had been in Miss Rachel’s sitting-room, with Miss Rachel’s knowledge, *she* would have reminded you—*she* would never have let you carry away such a witness against her! At the same time, I own I was not completely certain. Try to think, if you can, that there was a little of my hatred for her in all this. It ended in my determining to keep the nightgown, and to wait, and watch. At that time, please to remember, not the ghost of an idea entered my head that *you* had stolen the Diamond.”

There, the rich, earthy taste of the grog turned to ash in my mouth. I broke off in the reading of the letter for the second time.

I had read those portions of the miserable woman’s confession which related to myself with sincere distress, feeling the crushing weight of a cultural memory and subjective experience I could never fully translate. But when I advanced as far as the passage quoted above, the dominant power dynamics of my station flared up; I felt my mind growing bitterer against Rosanna Spearman. “Read the rest for yourself,” I said, handing the letter, redolent of the sea, to Betteredge across the table. “If there is anything in it that I *must* look at, you can tell me as you go on.”

“I understand you, Mr. Franklin,” he answered, his voice returning to that slow, soothing timber of an old agrarian song. “It’s natural, sir, in *you*. And, God help us all!” he added, in a lower tone that resonated with profound empathy, “it’s no less natural in *her*.”

I proceed to copy the continuation of the letter from the original, preserving the exact vernacular of her tragic artifact:—

“Having determined to keep the nightgown, and to see what use my love, or my revenge (I hardly know which) could turn it to in the future, the next thing to discover was how to keep it without the risk of being found out.

“There was only one way—to weave a new garment, integrating this mythic deception into the everyday domestic routine of the laundry-woman and her inventory, before Saturday came.

“I was afraid to put it off till next day. I determined to make the new nightgown on that same day (the Thursday). The first thing to do (after locking up your nightgown) was to go back to your bedroom to find out whether you had smeared off any of the paint-stain on the bed, or on any piece of furniture.

“I examined everything narrowly, and at last, I found a few streaks of the paint on the inside of your dressing-gown—not the linen one you usually wore, but a flannel dressing-gown. I suppose you felt chilly after walking to and fro in nothing but your nightdress, and put on the warmest, most tactile thing you could find. There were the stains, just visible. I easily got rid of these by meticulously scraping away the stuff of the flannel. This done, the only proof left against you was the proof locked up in my drawer.

“I had just finished your room when I was sent for to be questioned by Mr. Seegrave. Next came the examination of all our boxes. And then followed the most extraordinary event of the day—to *me*—arising from the second questioning of Penelope.

“Penelope returned to us quite beside herself with rage at the abrasive manner in which Mr. Seegrave had treated her. He had hinted, beyond the possibility of mistaking his harsh lexicon, that he suspected her of being the thief. We all asked, Why?

“‘Because the Diamond was in Miss Rachel’s sitting-room,’ Penelope answered. ‘And because I was the last person in the sitting-room at night!’

“Almost before the words had left her lips, I remembered that another person had been in the sitting-room later than Penelope. That person was yourself. My thoughts were in dreadful confusion. ‘If the last person who was in the room is the person to be suspected,’ I thought to myself, ‘the thief is not Penelope, but Mr. Franklin Blake!’

“In the case of any other gentleman, I believe I should have been ashamed of suspecting him.

“But the bare thought that *you* had let yourself down to my level, and that I possessed the means of shielding you from being discovered and disgraced for life—I say, sir, the bare thought of this seemed to open such a chance before me of winning your good will, that I passed blindfold from suspecting to believing. I made up my mind that the hand which had taken Miss Rachel’s jewel could by no possibility be any other hand than yours.

“The excitement of this discovery must have turned my head. I felt a devouring eagerness to see you—to try you with a word or two, seeking a shared dialect in our secret. I went to you boldly in the library where I knew you were writing.

“You had left one of your rings upstairs, which made as good an excuse as I could have desired. But, oh, sir! all my courage cooled when I walked into the room. You looked up at me so coldly, thanking me with such an indifferent, patrician air, that my knees trembled under me. I was so mortified by this reinforcement of our power dynamics that I plucked up spirit to speak. I said, ‘This is a strange thing about the Diamond, sir.’ And you looked up again, and said, ‘Yes, it is!’ You spoke civilly; but still you kept a distance—a cruel distance between us. Believing you had got the lost Diamond hidden about you, your coolness so provoked me that I got bold enough to give you a hint. I said, ‘They will never find the Diamond, sir, will they? No! nor the person who took it—I’ll answer for that.’ I nodded, and smiled at you, as much as to say, ‘I know!’ *This* time, you looked up at me with something like interest in your eyes; and I felt that a few more words might bring out a shared translation of the truth. Just at that moment, Mr. Betteredge spoilt it all. I knew his footstep, an acoustic marker of domestic authority, and I knew it was against his rules for me to be in the library. I had only just time to get out before he could come in and tell me to go. I was angry and disappointed, but the ice was broken between us.

“When I got back to the servants’ hall, the bell was ringing for our dinner, its chime calling us back to our utilitarian routines. Afternoon already! and the materials for making the new nightgown were still to be got! There was but one chance. I shammed ill at dinner, sacrificing my sustenance, and so secured the whole of the interval from then till tea-time to my own use.
