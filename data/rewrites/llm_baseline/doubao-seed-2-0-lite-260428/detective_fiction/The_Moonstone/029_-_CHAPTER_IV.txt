The signing of Lady Verinder’s will was a far swifter affair than I had steeled myself to witness, hurried over with what I can only describe as indecent haste. Mr. Bruff the lawyer had no patience for the solemnity of the moment: he called for Samuel the footman to act as the second witness, and pressed a pen straight into my aunt’s hand before I could ready the few edifying, appropriate remarks I had prepared for such an occasion. It was all over in less than two minutes. Samuel trotted back downstairs, none the wiser for the spiritual guidance I might have shared, and Mr. Bruff folded the document up, glancing at me pointedly as if to will me to leave him alone with his client.
He might as well have tried to move St. Paul’s Cathedral with a sharp look as shift me. My mission of mercy was laid out clear before me, my bag of precious, soul-saving publications resting heavy and ready on my lap. To his credit (no doubt the product of his worldly legal training), he read the room fast. He reacted much the same way the surly cabman had when I first arrived: he uttered a most profane exclamation, beat a hasty, violent retreat, and left me mistress of the whole house.
As soon as the door clicked shut behind him, Lady Verinder sank back on her sofa, looking flustered, and brought up the will unprompted. “I hope you do not think yourself neglected, Drusilla dear,” she said. “I mean to hand your little legacy to you myself, out of my own hand, not tie it up in legal paper.”
It was a golden, heaven-sent opportunity. I seized it on the spot, yanking open my bag to pull out the top publication: the twenty-fifth early edition of that famous anonymous work (all but certainly written by the sainted Miss Bellows) *The Serpent at Home*. The worldly soul may be unaware of its design: it lays bare exactly how the Evil One lies in wait for us even in the most innocent, mundane corners of daily life, with chapters titled “Satan in the Hair Brush,” “Satan behind the Looking Glass,” “Satan under the Tea Table,” and more besides.
“Give this your attention, dear aunt, and you will grant me every request I have,” I told her, handing it open to a marked page of searing, burning eloquence, its subject: *Satan Among the Sofa Cushions*.
Poor Lady Verinder, reclining thoughtlessly on the very cushions the tract described, glanced at the page and handed it back more flustered than ever. “I’m afraid Drusilla, I can’t read that yet, not until I’m a little stronger. The doctor——”
The moment she spoke that man’s profession, I knew exactly what was coming. Over and over through my years of ministering to perishing fellow creatures, the notoriously infidel members of the medical trade had stepped between me and my sacred work, hiding behind the thin, miserable excuse that their patients needed quiet, and that the influence of Miss Clack and her books was the most disturbing force they could possibly face. That same blinded, materialist treachery was now trying to rob me of the only spiritual property my poverty could ever claim: my right to save my aunt’s soul.
“The doctor says I am not well today,” my poor misguided relative continued. “He forbids me seeing any strangers, and if I read at all, I am only to read the lightest, most amusing books. ‘Do nothing to weary your head or quicken your pulse,’ that was exactly what he said when he left me this morning.”
There was no choice but to yield, for the moment only. To argue openly that my ministry was infinitely more vital than any work a medical man could do would only make him take petty revenge on his weak patient, and threaten to abandon the case entirely. But there are more ways than one to sow good seed, and few people know them better than I do.
“You may feel stronger in an hour or two,” I coaxed. “Or you may wake tomorrow morning with a sense of something missing, and this little unassuming volume may be exactly what you need. You will let me leave it here, won’t you? The doctor can hardly object to that.”
I slipped it half under the sofa cushions, half poking out, right next to her handkerchief and her smelling bottle. Every time she reached for either, her hand would brush the book, and who could say? Sooner or later, the book might touch her.
With that small good work done, I decided to take my leave of her for a rest. As I turned to go, I glanced at the window, full of flower pots and boxes where Lady Verinder kept her beloved perishable blooms, forever getting up to lean over and smell them. A new happy thought struck me. “Oh, may I take a flower?” I asked, drifting over to the window unsuspected. Instead of picking one, I tucked a second tract from my bag in among the geraniums and roses, a little surprise for her to find later.
Why stop there? Why not hide a precious publication in every single room my aunt ever entered?
I said my goodbyes immediately, slipped across the hall and into the library. Samuel the footman, who had come up to see me out, assumed I had already left and trundled back downstairs. On the library table sat two of the “amusing” books the infidel doctor had recommended. I covered both of them out of sight with two of my own tracts. In the breakfast room, I found her favourite canary singing in its cage, the groundsel for its feed scattered on the table directly beneath it. I tucked a tract straight into the pile of seed. In the main drawing room, I slipped two more tracts between her sheet music on the piano, where she would find them when she sat down to play. In the back drawing room, I tucked one under a half-finished piece of embroidery I knew she was working on. The little curtained nook leading off that room, no proper door to separate it, had her old plain fan resting on the mantelpiece. I opened my ninth tract to a very specially marked passage, and used the fan as a bookmark to hold the page.
The only question left was whether I dared venture upstairs to the bedchamber floor, at the risk of being insulted by that sharp-tongued lady’s maid in her cap ribbons if she caught me. But what Christian worth the name fears a little insult? I crept up, ready to bear anything, and the whole upper floor was silent and empty—servants’ tea time, no doubt. Lady Verinder’s bedroom faced the street, and the miniature of my late dear uncle Sir John hung on the wall opposite the bed, seeming to smile at me, seeming to say “Drusilla, leave a book.” I tucked one tract beside the matches on her nightstand, and a second under the box of chocolate drops on the other side. Whether she reached for a light, or a sweet to soothe her restlessness, she would find a precious publication waiting to greet her.
Only one tract was left at the bottom of my bag, and only one room left unexplored: the bathroom opening off her bedroom. I peeked inside, and that still, small holy voice that never deceives whispered to me: you have met her everywhere else, Drusilla. Meet her at her bath, and the work is done. A dressing gown was thrown over a chair, with a deep pocket stitched into it. I slipped my last tract right inside that pocket.
I cannot put to words the exquisite sense of a duty perfectly fulfilled when I slipped out of the house unseen, my empty bag tucked under my arm, stepping out onto the London street. O worldly friends, chasing the phantom of pleasure through the guilty mazes of dissipation! Happiness is so simple to find, if only you will choose to be good. That night, when I folded my things, and thought of the true riches I had scattered from the attic to the ground floor of my wealthy aunt’s house, I felt as light and carefree as a small child. I sang one verse of the Evening Hymn, and fell asleep before I could finish a second.
I woke the next morning feeling years younger, bright and full of purpose. I had no intention of calling at Montagu Square for the sake of food, only for the certainty of finding my dear aunt, when my landlady’s maid knocked at my door to say Lady Verinder’s servant had called to see me. It was Samuel, that fresh-faced young footman with the teachable look, holding a huge heavy parcel under his arm that seemed to frighten him the moment he set it down. “My lady sends her love, Miss,” he stammered. “Said you’ll find a letter inside.” He looked half-ready to run for the door.
I detained him for a few kind inquiries, and learned that Lady Verinder, Rachel, and Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite had all gone out for a drive. Rachel was dressed for a ball that night, and Godfrey had arranged to come back for coffee to escort her. There was a morning concert advertised for the next day, and Samuel had orders to buy tickets for a large party, including one for Godfrey. I could scarce believe my ears. Our special meeting of the Mothers’-Small-Clothes-Conversion Society was scheduled that very night, summoned specifically to call on Godfrey’s advice when the community was quite overwhelmed by a mountain of donated trousers that threatened to swamp all our work. And the next day was the grand festival of the British-Ladies’-Servants’-Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision Society, where he was supposed to be the life and soul of our struggling little institution. Instead he was gallivanting off to balls and concerts with a crowd of worldlings. It was the most awful, shocking backsliding I had ever witnessed from a man we had all considered our Christian hero.
I turned my attention to the parcel that had so terrified Samuel. It was exactly what I feared: all twelve of my precious tracts, sent back to me by the doctor’s express order. The short note from my aunt simply said she dared not disobey her medical man, poor dear soul.
But I did not doubt for a moment what to do next. The true Christian never yields once she has embarked on a mission. Books had failed, thanks to the doctor’s obstinacy. So I would try preparation by little notes: short, edifying passages copied out in different hands by my sisters in the Mothers’ society, written as friendly personal letters, so no doctor’s watchful suspicion could ever mark them as tracts. Six I posted that very night, six I tucked into my pocket to hide around the house the next day.
By two o’clock I was back at the house. Lady Verinder had had a bad night, resting on the sofa in the room where we had signed the will, dozing. I assumed Rachel and all her party of pleasure-seekers, Godfrey included, had left for the concert already. I set to work at once: I set one of my little notes all alone on the library mantel, far from the rest of the unopened post, to draw my aunt’s curiosity. I dropped a second on the breakfast room floor, where the first servant to walk in would pick it up and hand it straight to their mistress.
I had just stepped into the main drawing room to place the rest, when I heard a soft, fluttering, considerate little knock at the front door. Before I could slip back to the library where I was supposed to be waiting, Samuel was in the hall answering it, and to my horror, he called up the stairs: “Upstairs, if you please, sir.” The footsteps of a man started up towards the drawing room floor.
I assumed it was the doctor, the man who had dared insult me by returning my sacred books. I could not bear to face him. I slipped fast into that little curtained nook off the back drawing room, pulling the heavy curtains shut tight to hide myself. I waited a minute, then two, but he never went towards Lady Verinder’s bedroom. He paced restlessly up and down the drawing room, talking quietly to himself. I parted the heavy curtains just the tiniest crack, to listen.
The words I heard him mutter, under his breath, were: “I’ll do it today.”
And the voice that spoke them was not the doctor’s. It was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s.
