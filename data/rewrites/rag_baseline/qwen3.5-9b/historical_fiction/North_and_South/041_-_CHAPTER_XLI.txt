The winter wore on, dragging its weary tail through the calendar, yet the days, though lengthening, refused to ignite with the customary brilliance of hope that usually greets a February sun. Mrs. Thornton had vanished from the household entirely, a ghost of her former presence. Mr. Thornton visited, but his footsteps were restricted to the study, his conversations directed solely toward her father. Mr. Hale observed him with a steady, unflinching gaze, noting that the scarcity of their meetings somehow intensified the weight of each encounter. From the fragments Margaret could glean, there was no trace of wounded pride or lingering resentment in Mr. Thornton's demeanor; rather, his business affairs, tangled and complex since the strike, demanded a vigilance he had neglected the previous year. Even more striking was the fact that he spoke of her—not avoiding the name, nor seeking it out, but mentioning her with a calm, consistent friendliness that cut through the frost of their estrangement.

Yet, Margaret possessed no reservoir of spirits to lift her father's burdened mind. The dreary tranquility of the present moment was shadowed by a prolonged era of anxiety and storm, a period so exhaustive that her mind had lost its elastic capacity to rebound. She threw herself into teaching the two younger Boucher children, working tirelessly at the mechanics of goodness. Hard, indeed, she labored, for her heart remained a stone, unmoved by the efficacy of her efforts. Though she performed her duties punctually and painfully, she remained as distant from cheerfulness as ever; her life continued to be a landscape of bleakness. Her only genuine triumph was an act born of unconscious piety: the silent, steadfast comforting of her father. Every mood of his found a ready sympathizer in her; every wish, however quiet and hesitant, was met with her meek spirit of obedience, striving to forecast and fulfill it.

Then came March, bearing the news of Frederick's marriage. Letters flowed between him and Dolores; hers were a hybrid of Spanish and English, natural in their mixture, while his were peppered with linguistic inversions and turns that testified to the rapid infection of his native tongue by his bride's culture. Upon receiving Henry Lennox's letter—a grim missive declaring the slim hope of clearing himself at a court-martial due to missing witnesses—Frederick had written to Margaret a vehement renunciation of England. He wished to unnative himself, declaring he would reject a pardon if offered and refuse to live in the country if permitted. At first, these words tore at Margaret, seeming so unnatural to her sensibilities that she wept bitterly. But upon reflection, she recognized the raw poignancy of a hope crushed so violently; she understood that patience was the only currency available. Soon, Frederick's subsequent letters spoke of a future so radiant that the past held no sway, and Margaret discovered within herself the patience she had been cultivating for him.

Simultaneously, the pretty, timid letters from Dolores began to captivate both Margaret and her father. The young Spaniard was evidently desperate to impress her lover's English relations, her feminine care peeking through every erasure and correction. Accompanying the announcement of the union was a magnificent black lace mantilla, selected personally by Dolores for her unseen sister-in-law, whom Frederick had described as a paragon of beauty, wisdom, and virtue. This marriage elevated Frederick's worldly station to heights they could scarcely have imagined. Barbour and Co., one of Spain's most extensive trading houses, welcomed him as a junior partner. Margaret smiled faintly, then sighed, remembering her old tirades against commerce. Here was her *preux chevalier*, her knightly brother, transformed into a merchant, a trader! Yet, she immediately recoiled from herself, protesting the confusing equation of a Spanish merchant with a Milton mill-owner. Regardless, trade or no trade, Frederick was undeniably, overwhelmingly happy. Dolores must be charming, and the mantilla was exquisite. With a final sigh, she turned her gaze back to the stark reality of her own life.

That spring, her father suffered occasional difficulties in breathing, distressing him acutely. Margaret, though less alarmed as the symptoms subsided in intervals, grew increasingly urgent that he accept Mr. Bell's invitation to visit him at Oxford in April. The invitation explicitly included Margaret, with a special letter commanding her attendance. However, she felt a profound relief in remaining quietly at home, entirely free from responsibility, allowing her mind and heart to rest in a manner denied her for over two years.

When her father finally drove off toward the railroad, Margaret felt the immense, crushing pressure of her time and spirits vanish. It was almost staggering to realize she was so utterly at liberty: no one depended on her for cheering care or positive happiness; there was no invalid to plan for or think for. She could be idle, silent, forgetful—and, most liberating of all, she could be unhappy if she chose. For months, her personal cares and troubles had been stuffed into a dark cupboard, but now she had the leisure to retrieve them, to mourn their nature, and to seek the true method of subduing them into the elements of peace. These weeks, she had been vaguely aware of their existence, hidden behind a veil of dullness. Now, she would confront them, assigning each its rightful work in her life. Sitting almost motionless in the drawing-room for hours, she reviewed the bitterness of every memory with an unwincing resolution. Once, at the stinging thought of the faithlessness that spawned that abasing falsehood, she cried aloud.

She refused to acknowledge the force of the temptation anymore; her plans for Frederick had failed, and the temptation lay there as a dead mockery, a lie so despicably foolish in the light of ensuing events that faith in truth revealed itself as infinitely wiser. In her nervous agitation, she unconsciously opened a book belonging to her father lying on the table. The words that caught her eye seemed forged specifically for her state of acute self-abasement: *"Je ne voudrois pas reprendre mon cœur en ceste sorte... mais je voudrois le corriger par voye de compassion. Or sus, mon pauvre cœur, nous voilà tombe dans la fosse... Revelons-nous, et quittons-la pour jamais, reclamons la misericorde de Dieu..."*

"The way of humility," Margaret thought, the French phrase settling in her chest like a warm stone. "Ah, that is what I have missed! But courage, little heart. We will turn back, and by God's help, we may find the lost path."

Rising abruptly, she determined to engage in work that would draw her out of herself. Passing the drawing-room door on her way upstairs, she summoned Martha, attempting to pierce the grave, respectful, servant-like manner that had crusted over the girl's individuality with a mechanical obedience. It was difficult to coax Martha into speaking of personal interests, but eventually, Margaret struck the right chord by mentioning Mrs. Thornton. Martha's face instantly brightened, and under slight encouragement, a long story spilled forth. She recounted how her father, in his early life, had been connected to Mrs. Thornton's husband, even positioned to show him kindness—a detail Martha barely recalled, occurring when she was a small child. Circumstances had separated the families until Martha was nearly grown, her father sinking lower from his original role as a warehouse clerk, and her mother dying. Without Mrs. Thornton, Martha and her sister would have been "lost"; it was she who sought them out, provided for them, and cared for them.

"I had the fever, and was but delicate," Martha whispered, her voice softening. "Mrs. Thornton and Mr. Thornton never rested till they had nursed me up in their own house and sent me to the sea. The doctor said the fever was catching, but they cared for nothing else—except Miss Fanny, who went visiting the folk she intended to marry into. So, though she was afraid at the time, it has all ended well."

"Miss Fanny going to be married!" Margaret exclaimed, surprised.

"Yes; and to a rich gentleman, too, only he's a deal older than she is. His name is Watson; and his mills are somewhere out beyond Hayleigh. It's a very good marriage, for all he's got such gray hair."

At this revelation, Margaret fell silent long enough for Martha to regain her propriety and her habitual brevity. She swept up the hearth, asked when to prepare tea, and departed with the same wooden face she had entered with. Margaret had to forcibly suppress a bad habit she had recently cultivated: imagining how every event concerning Mr. Thornton affected him, wondering if he would like or dislike it.

The following day, she taught the little Boucher children, took a long walk, and concluded with a visit to Mary Higgins. To her surprise, she found Nicholas already home; the lengthening daylight had deceived her regarding the lateness of the evening. He, too, seemed to have entered further onto the path of humility; he was quieter, less self-asserting.

"So th' oud gentleman's away on his travels, is he?" Nicholas asked. "Little 'uns told me so. Eh! but the're sharp 'uns they are; I a'most think they beat my own wenches for sharpness, though m'appen it's wrong to say so, and one on 'em in her grave. There's summut in th' weather, I reckon, as sets folk a-wandering. My measter, him at th' shop yonder, is spinning about th' world somewhere."

"Is that the reason you're so soon at home to-night?" Margaret asked innocently.
