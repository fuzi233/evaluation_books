The happy stroke of fortune that had cast Tito as the unexpected olive-branch messenger, bearing word of the grain convoy that had averted Florence’s starvation, had earned him loud, impromptu cheers in the street—but he still held several far more deliberate, secret commissions to fulfill before he might think of rest. He lingered in the cool stone corridors of the Palazzo Vecchio, waiting for the return of the Ten, the governing council that oversaw Florence’s foreign policy and war affairs, to deliver a full report of his private scouting mission to Pisa. The trip had been arranged as a precursor to an official, public embassy led by Bernardo Rucellai, tasked with brokering a fragile peace with Emperor Maximilian and the hostile anti-Florentine League.

Tito’s knack for diplomatic detail was well proven, and as he laid out every interview, every scrap of intelligence he had gathered, with perfect clarity and precision, Bernardo del Nero—who sat on the Ten that term—could not suppress his quiet admiration, though he tried. His old dislike of the Greek had curdled into something far sharper ever since Tito had sold Bardo’s priceless library. Romola had never breathed a single word of the hurt the sale had caused her to her godfather, and Bernardo respected that silence as a clear ban on ever bringing the subject up, but his sharp old eyes had picked up every small sign that his goddaughter’s marriage was no happy one. *Ah*, he thought to himself, no one else would have spotted the faint tightness around her smile, the way she slipped off to San Marco for Savonarola’s sermons more and more often. “These women, if they are unblessed with children and unhappy in their homes, turn either to folly or some overstrained piety that makes them think they carry all of heaven’s work on their shoulders. Poor Romola—all that Latin and Greek learning never changed the fact she was every bit a soft, loving woman, no different than if she’d spent all her days pricking her fingers with a needle. And this husband of hers, who gets handed every good post because he’s a tool with a smooth polished handle? I wish the rest of the council won’t find their fingers sliced to the bone one day. Well, well—crooked furrow, full sack. Many a man who hires only perfectly honest helpers ends up with nothing to show for his trouble.”

Bernardo’s long-held conviction that moral perfection was a useless standard for political agents kept him from ever acting against Tito. Beyond the private hurt he could not name for Romola’s sake, he had no concrete grievance against the useful young Greek save for the fact that he was Greek, and Bernardo simply did not like him. The trick of feigning wholehearted loyalty to Florence’s popular republican government while being a secret Medicean sympathizer was shared by half the old Medici party, anyway—Tito was only better at hiding it than the rest. So Bernardo treated him with cold, distant courtesy, which Tito returned with scrupulous, formal respect, and most of Florence still assumed the old bond between Bernardo and Bardo made any favor shown to Romola’s husband a welcome tribute to her godfather.

When Tito finished giving his report at the Palazzo, the official business of the day was done. He was dusty, his riding clothes splashed with mud, his skin sunburnt from hours in the saddle—but he did not turn for home. There were things tucked in his leather scarsella money pouch, and weighing on his mind, that he needed to be rid of as soon as possible, and he meant to find opportunities to offload them so naturally no one would ever suspect he had sought them out on purpose. He sauntered slowly toward the Piazza del Duomo. The public victory procession had long since ended, but the bells still rang out overhead, and the crowds milled restlessly through the streets, their joy looking for a sharper outlet. The reformers under Savonarola had declared Christ the official King of Florence, and ordered all public pleasures to be strictly Christian—but many of the young men who had been shouting “Viva Gesù!” all morning were still secretly craving a good, vigorous stone-throwing brawl to give their thanks some teeth.

Tito could not walk the whole length of the square without being recognized as the olive-branch messenger. He weaseled out of the crowd of pressing, question-hungry admirers adroitly, telling them the real official messenger from Leghorn, Meo di Sasso, would be coming in through Porta San Frediano at any minute, and they ought to go meet him there. Off the mob rushed, and Tito was free to scan the piazza casually, his long eyes drifting here and there, while he pretended to be entirely aimless. The procession had ended at the Duomo, so he knew the area around Nello’s famous barber shop would be packed with loitering gossips, exactly the sort of crowd he wanted.

He spotted them right away, leaning against the Baptistery’s iron rails by the north gates: the tall, broad-shouldered figure of Dolfo Spini, leader of the Compagnacci— the so-called Evil Companions, a band of dissolute young aristocrats who hated the Mediceans, hated the republican government, and hated Savonarola more than anything else. Spini wore a richly embroidered tunic cut in the flashy new French style, his belt hung with a fine sword and dagger, his hat topped with a bright red feather that stood as a deliberate insult to the somber, plain dress the Piagnoni followers of Savonarola had made fashionable. His broad, coarse face, his thick waist, and the rude, unblinking stare he turned on every passerby were instantly recognizable. He and his friends, all richly dressed, armed young men, were laughing ostentatiously, as if trying to prove they were not at all bitter that the grain convoy had just validated the friar’s prophecy they had mocked all week.

Leaning against the barber shop doorpost at the center of the group was sharp-eyed, clean-shaven Niccolò Machiavelli, still young but already shrewd enough to see every petty secret of self-interest that lurked under Florentine public life. “Messer Dolfo’s skull is even bigger and emptier than a pumpkin, I see,” he was saying as Tito drew near. “I measure a man’s stupidity by the tricks he uses to fool other people. The dumbest creature alive is the one who grins and says he doesn’t mind right after someone has just kicked his shins. If I were just a little dumber,” he added, smiling as the group made space to let Tito join, “I’d pretend to be very fond of this Melema, who’s got a secretary post that would suit me perfectly—as if a poorly paid Latin scribe could ever love a better paid Latin secretary! You, Melema, are pestiferously clever, always getting in my way, and I was very annoyed to hear you stumbled on another piece of good luck this morning.”

“Luck that’s questionable at best, Niccolò,” Tito said, clapping him on the shoulder in easy friendliness. “I haven’t gained anything from it yet but having a crowd of wool-beaters grab hold of me and breathe all over me, when I’m as dirty and battered from riding as a letter carrier all the way from Bologna.”

“Ah! You need my art, Master Orator,” Nello the barber called, hurrying forward at the sound of Tito’s voice. “Your chin, I see, still has yesterday’s stubble all over it. Come, submit yourself to the priest of all the Muses. Sandro, quick with the lather!”

“Truly Nello, that’s exactly what I was craving the moment I left the Palazzo,” Tito said, dropping gratefully into the barber’s chair. “That’s why I turned my steps straight for your shop, instead of tramping home dusty.”

“Of course, no one would dare present themselves to the Madonna Romola with a rusty chin and a tangled head of hair,” Nello chattered as he worked. “Nothing unkempt ought to come near our Florentine lily—though I see her walking about our ragged city corners like a sunbeam every day, or these days more like a moonbeam. I met her yesterday, and she looked as pale and worn as that fainting Madonna painting by Fra Giovanni you see hanging in the San Marco cloisters. You ought to do something about it, my learned one: she fasts and keeps vigils far too often when you’re away.”

Tito let out a melancholy little shrug. “It’s only too true. She’s been depriving herself of half her meals every single day through this famine. But what can a husband do? Her whole mind is caught fire by the friar. No influence of mine can stand against Fra Girolamo.”

“Nor can the Pope’s influence, for that matter, I’d wager,” said Domenico Cennini, who had drifted in the door with Tito. “I don’t suppose you heard any whisper of how the wind is blowing in Rome on your trip to Pisa, Melema?”

“Secrets of the council chamber, Messer Domenico!” Tito said, smiling, holding up his palms in a mock-apologetic gesture. “An envoy has to be as tight-lipped as a father confessor.”

“Of course, of course,” Cennini said. “I don’t ask you to break your oath. But my belief is, if Pope Alexander pushes the friar too far, Fra Girolamo will move heaven and earth to call a General Council of the Church to reform it—and he’ll succeed, too. I’m no Piagnone myself, but I wouldn’t mind seeing that.”

“With all respect to your greater experience, Messer Domenico,” Machiavelli cut in, “I have to disagree. Not about wishing for a General Council that might clean up Rome’s vices, but about your belief the friar will outmaneuver the Pope. Fra Girolamo’s game is an impossible one. If he’d only stuck to preaching against Rome’s sins, and making vague prophecies that Italy would be scourged somehow, the Pope would have let him shout until his throat was hoarse, no questions asked. Spiritual blasts that don’t knock down any actual walls? No one minds those. But the friar won’t be just a spiritual trumpet. He wants to be a lever, and more than that—he *is* a lever. He wants to spread the teachings of Christ by propping up Florence’s popular government, and the Pope, from what I’ve heard on the best authority, has very different plans for this city.”

“Then Florence will stand by the friar!” Cennini broke in, heated. “I wish he’d leave his prophecies alone myself, but if anyone tries to attack our right to choose our own government? I’m an obedient son of the Church, but I’d vote to resist Pope Alexander the Sixth just as our forefathers resisted Pope Gregory the Eleventh.”

“Pardon me one moment, Messer Domenico,” Machiavelli said, tucking his thumbs into his belt, smiling with the quiet delight of a man laying out a perfectly logical argument. “Have you really understood the friar’s position? How did he become such a powerful lever that the wily Pope feels the need to attack him? Because he has the ear of the whole populace. He gives them threats and promises that they believe come straight from God—not just about hell, purgatory, and heaven, but about Pisa, and our Great Council, and the grain convoys. But if events turn against him, if the people’s faith in those promises cracks? The very source of his power will be the source of his fall. He has piled three separate kinds of hatred on his head: the hatred of every ordinary person who resents being forced to wear the strict yoke of his new moral laws; the hatred of all the powerful lords of Italy who want to carve Florence up for their own profit; and the hatred of the common people themselves, whom he dared to promise good things *in this life*, instead of only in the next. If a prophet wants to keep his power, he has to be a prophet like Mahomet, with an army at his back, to frighten the people’s wavering faith back into life when it fades.”

“You can roll all three of those hatreds into one,” the poet Francesco Cei burst out impetuously. “He has earned the hatred of every honest, sensible man by spinning nothing but hypocritical lies. He belongs in the Inferno with the false prophets, walking around with their heads twisted backward on their shoulders.”

“You’re too hot-tempered, my Francesco,” Machiavelli said, laughing. “Poets always slash through the clouds in their rage. I’m no devotee of the friar’s, and I wouldn’t lift my little finger to defend his truthfulness. But perfect truthfulness is a plant that only grows in Paradise. Its seeds have never thrived outside those walls. You, my poet friend, tell poetical lies all the time—half because you’re caught up in the heat of composition, half to please your audience—but you refuse to accept lies told in prose. That’s all the difference between you and Fra Girolamo: he draws the line for what counts as poetry a little further out than you do. When he steps into the Duomo pulpit, the fire is in his bones, and the crowd is there, waiting for him to please them. That is all.”

“You’re far too lax with your morals, Niccolò,” Cennini said gravely. “I for one believe the friar is a man of integrity, even if I don’t believe all his prophecies. As long as no one proves him a fraud, the popular party is strong enough to protect him and beat back all foreign interference.”

“A party that *looks* strong,” Machiavelli said, shrugging, casting an almost imperceptible glance over at Tito, who was leaning back in the barber’s chair, clearly enjoying the way Nello combed and scented his hair. “But how many secret Mediceans are hidden among you? How many people will flip sides the second a private grudge or a private bribe comes along?”

“Hardly any true Medicean loyalists left,” Cennini said. “No one would risk anything for Piero de’ Medici these days. Only a handful of old, staunch friends like Bernardo del Nero. Even many people who are related to the Medici family by blood are loud and proud supporters of the popular government, and would fight for the friar. I talked to Giannozzo Pucci not long ago, and I’m convinced there is nothing he would oppose more fiercely than any attempt to undo the new order of things.”

“You are quite right there, Messer Domenico,” Tito said, rising from the shaving chair, a knowing little laugh glinting in his eyes. “And I fancy a romantic bit of jealousy helped push him away from Piero de’ Medici, not just politics. A man as gentle and amiable as Giannozzo would never speak so bitterly of Piero otherwise. He was with you in the procession this morning, I assume?”

“No,” Cennini answered. “He left for his country villa three days ago. He’s not back yet.”

Tito adjusted his cap, glancing down at his mud-splashed hose like the answer hardly mattered to him. In truth, he had just got the piece of information he’d been craving all day. Tucked in his scarsella was a crumpled gold ring, a secret sign he had promised to deliver to Giannozzo Pucci, given to him by Piero de’ Medici’s envoy, who Tito had ridden miles out of his way to meet at Certaldo, on the Siena road, the week before. Since Pucci was out of town, he could easily send the ring by way of Fra Michele, a Carthusian lay brother who worked secretly for the remaining Medicean loyalists. The moment Pucci received the sign, he would hurry back to Florence to hear the verbal part of Tito’s mission from Piero.

“Behold the finished result!” Nello cried, brandishing his comb and pointing it at Tito. “The handsomest scholar in all the world, or all the wild Maremma marshes, now that he has passed through my hands! Though your face is a little thinner than when you first came to Florence in the bloom of your youth, eh? And I swear, there are faint little lines just starting to hint themselves around your mouth, Master Orator! Ah, the life of the mind is the enemy of beauty! I myself was once thought very handsome by all the ladies, back when I was still in swaddling bands. But now—oh woe is me! I carry all my unwritten poems written in cipher across my own face!”

Tito laughed along with the rest of the group as Nello made a tragically comical face at his own reflection in the hand mirror, then waved a general farewell to the shop full of men and stepped out onto the street.

“I stand by old Piero di Cosimo’s opinion,” Francesco Cei said the second the door shut behind him. “I never half liked that Melema. That constant, never-fading smile of his gets more unnatural every day—no wonder he’s got lines around his mouth.”

“He’s far too successful for his own good,” Machiavelli joked. “I’m sure there’s something off about him, else he never would have landed that secretary post everyone craves.”

“He’s an able man,” Cennini said, in a tone of fair, judicial judgment. “My brother and I have always found him very helpful with our Greek manuscripts, and he does excellent work for the Ten. I like to see a young man work his way up through merit. And Secretary Scala, who took him under his wing when he first arrived, still thinks the world of him, I know.”

“Of course he does,” a notary in the back of the shop put in. “Tito writes all his official letters for him, or corrects them, and gets paid very well for it.”

“I wish Messer Bartolommeo would pay me to doctor his gouty, clumsy Latin,” Machiavelli said, shrugging. “Did he tell you about the pay, Ser Ceccone, or was it Melema himself who bragged?”

“Melema? No, never!” the notary replied. “He’s as tight-lipped as a closed nut. He never brags at all. That’s why everyone employs him. They say he’s getting very rich doing all sorts of quiet, under-the-table jobs for all sorts of people.”

“It’s a bit unfair, that,” Machiavelli said. “There are so many perfectly able notaries out there with no work at all!”
