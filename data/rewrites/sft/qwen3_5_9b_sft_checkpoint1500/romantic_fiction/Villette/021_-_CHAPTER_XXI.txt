Three days remained, and then I must return to the *pensionnat*. I counted the moments on the clock, wishing to retard their flight, yet they glided by while I watched, already gone before I could fully grasp their departure.

"Lucy will not leave us today," said Mrs. Bretton, coaxingly at breakfast. "She knows we can procure a second respite."

"I would not ask for one if I might have it for a word," I replied. "I long to get the good-by over and be settled in the Rue Fossette again. I must go this morning; I must go directly. My trunk is packed and corded."

However, my departure depended entirely on Graham. He had promised to accompany me, but fate conspired to engage him all day, leaving him only at dusk. Then followed a little combat of words. Mrs. Bretton and her son pressed me to remain one night more. I felt so irritated and eager to depart that I almost wept. I longed to leave them as a criminal on the scaffold longs for the axe to fall—that is, I wished the pang over. How deeply I wished it, they could not comprehend. Their minds were too sheltered to understand the turmoil within me.

It was dark when Dr. John handed me from the carriage at Madame Beck's door. A November drizzle fell, as it had all day; the lamplight gleamed on the wet pavement. It was just such a night as that on which, not a year ago, I had first stopped at this threshold. The shapes of the paving stones were identical to those I had noted with an idle eye while waiting, heart pounding, for the unclosing of that door at which I stood—a solitary, suppliant figure. On that night, too, I had briefly met him who now stood with me. Had I ever reminded him of that encounter, or explained it? I had not, nor ever felt the inclination. It was a pleasant thought, laid aside in my own mind, best kept there.

Graham rang the bell. The door opened instantly, for it was the hour when the half-boarders departed, and consequently, Rosine was on alert.

"Don't come in," I said to him, but he stepped a moment into the well-lighted vestibule. I did not wish him to see that "the water stood in my eyes"; his nature was too kind ever to needlessly witness such signs of sorrow. He always wished to heal—to relieve—even when, as a physician, neither cure nor alleviation lay within his power.

"Keep up your courage, Lucy. Think of my mother and myself as true friends. We will not forget you."

"Nor will I forget you, Dr. John."

My trunk was brought in. We had shaken hands; he had turned to go, but he was not satisfied. His generous impulses demanded more.

"Lucy," stepping after me, "shall you feel very solitary here?"

"At first, I shall."

"Well, my mother will soon call to see you. Meantime, I'll tell you what I'll do. I'll write—any cheerful nonsense that comes into my head, shall I?"

"Good, gallant heart!" thought I, shaking my head with a smile. "Never think of it: impose on yourself no such task. *You* write to *me*! You'll not have time."

"Oh! I will find or make time. Good-by!"

He was gone. The heavy door crashed shut—the axe had fallen, the pang was experienced.

Swallowing tears as if they were wine, allowing myself no time to think or feel, I passed to Madame's sitting-room to pay the necessary visit of ceremony. She received me with perfectly acted cordiality, even demonstrative, though brief. In ten minutes, I was dismissed. From the dining hall I proceeded to the refectory, where pupils and teachers assembled for evening study; again I found a welcome, one I did not deem quite hollow. Once free, I repaired to the dormitory.

"And will Graham really write?" I questioned as I sank tired onto the edge of the bed.

Reason, rising stealthily through the twilight of that long, dim chamber, whispered sedately: "He may write once. So kind is his nature, it may stimulate him for one effort. But it *cannot* be continued; it *may not* be repeated. Great is the folly that builds on such a promise; insane the credulity that mistakes a transient rain-pool holding one draft for the perennial spring."

I bowed my head, sitting in thought for an hour longer. Reason laid a withered hand upon my shoulder, chilling my ear with the blue lips of age.

"If," she muttered, "if he *should* write, what then? Do you meditate pleasure in replying? Ah, fool! I warn you! Grant no expansion to feeling, give holiday to no faculty, dally with no exchange, foster no intercommunion…"

"But I have talked to Graham, and you did not chide," I pleaded.

"No," said she. "Talk for you is good discipline. You converse imperfectly. While you speak, there can be no oblivion of inferiority—no encouragement to delusion: pain, privation, penury stamp your language…"

"But where the bodily presence is weak and speech contemptible, surely written language offers a medium for better utterance than faltering lips?"

Reason answered only, "At your peril you cherish that idea, or suffer its influence to animate any writing of yours!"

"But if I feel, may I *never* express?"

"*Never!*" declared Reason.

I groaned under her bitter sternness. Never—never! This hag would not let me look up, smile, or hope. She could not rest unless I were crushed, cowed, broken-in, and broken-down. According to her, I was born only to work for bread, to await the pains of death, and to despond steadily through life. Reason might be right; yet no wonder we are glad to defy her, to rush from under her rod and give a truant hour to Imagination—*her* soft, bright foe, *our* sweet help, *our* divine hope. We shall and must break bounds at intervals, despite the terrible revenge awaiting our return. Reason is vindictive as a devil; for me, she was always envenomed as a stepmother. If I had obeyed her, it would have been chiefly with the obedience of fear, not of love. Long ago I should have died of her ill-usage, her stint, her chill, her barren board, her icy bed, her savage, ceaseless blows—but for that kinder Power who holds my secret and sworn allegiance. Often, Reason turned me out by night, in midwinter, on cold snow, flinging for sustenance the gnawed bone dogs had forsaken; sternly she vowed her stores held nothing more, harshly denying my right to ask better things. Then, looking up, I saw in the sky a head amidst circling stars, of which the midmost and brightest lent a ray sympathetic and attentive. A spirit, softer and better than human Reason, descended with quiet flight to the waste, bringing all around her a sphere of air borrowed of eternal summer, perfume of flowers that cannot fade, fragrance of trees whose fruit is life, breezes pure from a world whose day needs no sun to lighten it. My hunger, this good angel, appeased with food sweet and strange, gathered amongst gleaning angels, garnering their dew-white harvest in the first fresh hour of a heavenly day. Tenderly she assuaged the insufferable fears which weep away life itself; kindly gave rest to deadly weariness; generously lent hope and impulse to paralyzed despair. Divine, compassionate, succourable influence! When I bend the knee to other than God, it shall be at thy white and winged feet, beautiful on mountain or on plain. Temples have been reared to the Sun, altars dedicated to the Moon. Oh, greater glory! To thee neither hands build, nor lips consecrate: but hearts, through ages, are faithful to thy worship. A dwelling thou hast, too wide for walls, too high for dome—a temple whose floors are space, rites whose mysteries transpire in presence, to the kindling, the harmony of worlds!

Sovereign complete! Thou hadst, for endurance, thy great army of martyrs; for achievement, thy chosen band of worthies. Deity unquestioned, thy essence foils decay!

This daughter of Heaven remembered me tonight; she saw me weep, and she came with comfort: "Sleep," she said. "Sleep, sweetly—I gild thy dreams!"

She kept her word, watching me through a night's rest; but at dawn, Reason relieved the guard. I awoke with a start; the rain was dashing against the panes, the wind uttering a peevish cry at intervals; the night-lamp was dying on the black circular stand in the middle of the dormitory; day had already broken. How I pity those whom mental pain stuns instead of rousing! This morning, the pang of waking snatched me out of bed like a hand with a giant's grip. How quickly I dressed in the cold of the raw dawn! How deeply I drank of the ice-cold water in my carafe! This was always my cordial, to which, like other dram-drinkers, I had eager recourse when unsettled by chagrin.
