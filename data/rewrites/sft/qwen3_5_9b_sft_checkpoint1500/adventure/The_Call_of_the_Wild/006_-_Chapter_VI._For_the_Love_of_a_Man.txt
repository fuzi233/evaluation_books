John Thornton had spent the previous December freezing his feet, a punishment meted out by his partners who abandoned him to recover while they pushed upstream to harvest saw-logs for Dawson. By the time Buck stumbled upon him, Thornton was already healing, though a slight limp lingered. Now, lying by the riverbank during the long, languid days of spring, watching the water flow and listening to the birdsong, Buck slowly regained his strength. A rest after traveling three thousand miles was a luxury indeed. As his wounds scarred over and muscle swelled beneath returning flesh, Buck grew lazy, content to loaf alongside Thornton, Skeet, and Nig, waiting for the raft that promised a journey south.

Skeet, a small Irish Setter, had been the first friend Buck made in his dying state, unable to resist her gentle advances. Possessing a natural "doctor" trait, she diligently washed and cleansed his wounds every morning, a ritual Buck soon came to crave as highly as Thornton's companionship. Nig, a massive black mix of bloodhound and deerhound with eyes full of laughter, shared this bond with equal, if quieter, warmth. To Buck's astonishment, these dogs showed no jealousy. Instead, they reflected the kindness and largeness of John Thornton. As Buck recovered, the trio engaged in ridiculous games, pulling Thornton into their revelry. Through this play, Buck emerged from convalescence into a new existence, experiencing for the first time genuine, passionate love. This feeling was foreign to his life at Judge Miller's estate in Santa Clara Valley, where his existence had been defined by working partnerships with the sons, pompous guardianship from the grandsons, and stately friendship with the Judge himself. Only John Thornton had awakened a love that burned with feverish intensity, bordering on madness.

Thornton was the ideal master. While others treated their dogs with a sense of duty or business necessity, Thornton cared for them as if they were his own children, compelled by an emotion he could not suppress. He remembered every kind word and cherished long conversations—"gas"—just as much as his dogs did. There was a specific tenderness in how he handled Buck: gripping the dog's head roughly between his hands, resting his own forehead against Buck's, rocking him back and forth while hurling insults that sounded like love to Buck's ears. Buck knew no greater joy than this rough embrace and the sound of those murmured oaths; with every jerk, his heart seemed ready to leap from his chest in ecstasy. When released, Buck would stand laughing, eyes eloquent, throat vibrating with unspoken sounds, until Thornton would reverently declare, "God! you can all but speak!"

Buck had a unique way of expressing affection, akin to a physical ache. He would seize Thornton's hand in his mouth, biting down with fierce pressure that left bruises, yet understanding these oaths as love, Thornton interpreted the feigned bite as a caress. Mostly, however, Buck's love was expressed through adoration. Unlike Skeet, who nudged constantly, or Nig, who rested his great head on Thornton's knee, Buck preferred to watch from a distance. He would lie by the hour at Thornton's feet, studying the man's face, tracking every fleeting expression and movement. Often, the depth of Buck's gaze would draw Thornton's attention, leading to a silent communion where both hearts shone through their eyes.

Yet, despite this profound attachment, the wild strains of the Northland remained active within Buck. Though faithful and devoted, born of fire and shelter, he retained his primal wiliness. He was a creature of the wild who merely sat by Thornton's fire, not a civilized animal from the Southland. His love for Thornton prevented theft from him, but from any other man, he would hesitate not, stealing with cunning to evade detection. His face and body bore the scars of many battles, and he fought with ferocious intelligence. Skeet and Nig were too good-natured for quarrels, but any stranger dog instantly recognized Buck's supremacy or found himself fighting for survival against a merciless adversary. Buck had learned the law of the club and the fang: there was no middle path. To show mercy was a fatal weakness; mercy was often mistaken for fear, leading to death. The mandate was simple: kill or be killed, eat or be eaten, a law dictated by the depths of ancient time that Buck obeyed.

Older than the days he had known, Buck linked the past with the present, swaying to a mighty rhythm that pulsed through him like the tides. Behind him lay the shades of countless ancestors—half-wolves, wild wolves, urging him on, tasting the meat he ate, scenting the wind with him, dictating his moods and directing his actions. These ancestral voices beckoned peremptorily, causing mankind and its claims to slip further away daily. A mysterious, thrilling call echoed from deep in the forest, compelling him to turn his back on the fire and plunge into the woods, knowing not where or why, yet feeling no need to question. Yet, whenever he reached the soft, unbroken earth of the forest, the love for John Thornton drew him back to the warmth of the fire.

Thornton alone held Buck. Other travelers might praise or pet him, but Buck remained cold to them, walking away from those who were too demonstrative. When Thornton's partners, Hans and Pete, finally arrived on the long-awaited raft, Buck ignored them until they approached Thornton, tolerating them thereafter with a passive indifference, accepting favors only as a courtesy rendered. They, like Thornton, were men of the earth, thinking simply and seeing clearly, and they understood Buck's ways without forcing an intimacy.

For Thornton, however, his love for Buck seemed to deepen endlessly. Alone among men, he could place a pack upon Buck's back in midsummer, trusting him with tasks beyond endurance. One day, as the group sat atop a precipice dropping three hundred feet to bedrock, a thoughtless whim led Thornton to command, "Jump, Buck!" The result was instantaneous chaos, with Hans and Pete scrambling to drag the pair back from the edge. "It is splendid, and it is terrible, too," Thornton admitted later, confessing that sometimes the power Buck possessed filled him with fear. "I'm not hankering to be the man that lays hands on you while he's around," Pete concluded wisely.

These fears materialized at Circle City, where a malicious man named "Black" Burton provoked a fight at a saloon. As Thornton stepped between Burton and a tenderfoot, Burton struck out, sending Thornton spinning. Buck, lying nearby, leaped into the air with a roar, launching himself onto Burton's throat. The man survived only by flailing his arms, but Buck tore at his flesh, driving his teeth into the throat again before the crowd dispersed him. A miners' meeting deemed the provocation sufficient, discharging Buck, and his reputation spread rapidly through the camps of Alaska.

Later, in the fall, Buck saved Thornton's life in a different manner. While poling a narrow boat down dangerous rapids on Forty-Mile Creek, Hans cast off a snub-rope prematurely. The boat flipped, tossing Thornton into the raging current toward a deadly stretch of wild water. Instantly, Buck plunged in. After three hundred yards of struggle through swirling foam, Buck overhauled Thornton, hauling him toward the bank. But the current pulled faster than they could swim. Thornton, scraping desperately over slick rocks, shouted, "Go, Buck! Go!" Unable to hold position, Buck was swept downstream, turning obediently toward the shore as commanded. He reached land, exhausted, only to be launched again via rope, this time striking out perfectly into the stream. As he neared Thornton, Hans snubbed the rope, jerking Buck underwater until he crashed against the bank. Gasping for air, Buck sprang up and ran to the point where Thornton hung precariously on the rock. Again, the rope was attached, and Buck, heading down with the speed of an express train, collided with Thornton like a battering ram. Hans secured the rope to a tree, and the two men were dragged beneath the water, strangled and buffeted by the jagged bottom, until they finally veered safely to the bank.

Thornton, bruised and battered, found Buck limp and seemingly lifeless. Finding three broken ribs, he declared, "That settles it," and they camped there until the bones knit.

That winter at Dawson, Buck achieved a feat that elevated his status to legendary proportions. In the Eldorado Saloon, local miners boasted of the strength of their dogs, betting thousands on who could pull the heaviest loads. John Thornton defended Buck, claiming the dog could start a thousand pounds of freight and walk off with it. A Bonanza King named Matthewson challenged him, laying down a thousand dollars in gold dust that Buck could not do it. Thornton, unsure of Buck's limits, hesitated, but Jim O'Brien lent him the money, doubting the beast's ability but supporting the wager.

Hundreds gathered around a sled laden with a thousand pounds of flour, frozen solid by the sixty-degree below-zero temperature. The odds shifted wildly against Buck, reaching three to one. But Thornton, ignited by a fighting spirit that soared above impossibility, knelt beside Buck. He took the dog's head in his hands, resting cheek to cheek, and whispered, "As you love me, Buck." Buck responded with a suppressed whine, seizing Thornton's mitten-clad hand, pressing down, and releasing slowly—a silent conversation of pure love.

"Now, Buck," Thornton commanded.
