Dear Ginger,

I’m finally feeling human enough to write to you, and since it’s been three whole months, you’d be right to call me a total weakling if I *wasn’t* bouncing back a little. I guess theoretically you can get over just about anything in 90 days, but I won’t lie, I haven’t fully cracked it yet. The best I’ve managed is shoving all my sad little troubles down in the cellar, locking the door, and promising myself I won’t drag them out to gawk at them at 2 a.m. That’s a win, right?

I probably should rattle off all my impressions of London, but I’ve been here so long already it feels like I’ve got a 30-year pension. You can tell from the letterhead that Mr. Faucitt still hasn’t sold the dress shop, though he swears it’s any day now. He’s been lunching every single afternoon with a portly, whiskered man with the shrewdest little eyes you ever saw, and he’s convinced the big sale is seconds away. Poor old thing! He’s rabid to get out to the country, settle down, and raise ducks. London has been the biggest letdown of his life.

Up until a couple weeks ago he’d wander around looking like a lost spaniel, hunting for the landmarks he remembered from his youth 30 years back. Turns out London has this dumb habit every three decades of repainting shop fronts a different color, and it’s giving this returned exile absolute apoplexy. He’s basically Rip Van Winkle with a measuring tape for frocks. First he had a shock finding out the old Empire music hall was a full-on theatre now. Then someone told him the Tivoli had been bulldozed entirely. But when he trundled over to Rupert Street to find the baker’s shop he had lodgings in back in the 1880s, and discovered it had turned into a frilly dressmaker’s? He went properly melancholy, and only perked up when that famous thick magenta London fog rolled in, proving some things never do change.

Thank goodness I’m kept busy at the shop. Full disclosure: since I never paid attention in your French lessons back home, I thought “Cie” was the name of the junior partner at first, and spent weeks giddy at the thought of meeting him and going “Miss Nicholas, shake hands with Mr. Cie, your biggest admirer.” Turns out it’s just a stuffy abbreviation for “company.” Booo. My job over here is the exact female equivalent of your old gig at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.—I’m the resident right-hand woman, sidling up to rich ladies who walk in and lying through my teeth about what “chawming weather” we’re having that day, before passing them off to the actual sales staff. I’d happily do this for years, but Faucitt is dead set on selling.

Have you noticed that every other Englishman you meet is obsessed with buying some crumb of rural property in Loamshire, or Salop, or Hants, to “restore the old family estate” that their grandpa gambled away in 1890? Mr. Faucitt grew up in a tiny village outside Cirencester (which, for the record, locals call “Cissister” and I bet you never knew that) and 50 years later he’s suddenly bitten by the bug to retire there and live surrounded by pigs and chickens. He dragged me down there last week, and Ginger? English countryside is the best kept secret on the planet. Why anyone chooses to live in a smoky town when there are old grey stone cottages, squelchy mud lanes, fat ancient trees, and soft blue hills rolling off into the distance I will never understand. If I ever sell my soul to the devil, I’m writing 40 years of country living into the contract.

You’re probably thinking I’m way too busy oohing over ducks and cobblestones to remember you exist. Wrong. I was reading the paper last week and saw a headline that said “International Rugby Match” and my brain instantly went “oh! That’s the dumb sport Ginger used to play!” So I trundled all the way out to Twickenham to see what the fuss was about. The crowd was so big I nearly got squeezed flat as a pancake, but I suffered it all for you. I worked out the guys in white were England, the red ones Wales, and after a nearby man finished yelling so loud his face turned purple I asked him which one was the English scrum-half. Right at that moment, a dozen red Welsh guys launched themselves full force on top of a tiny mild-looking little bloke who’d just grabbed the ball. Ginger, you are so well out of that racket! That was the scrum-half, apparently, and getting dogpiled by 200 pounds of rugby players is just a normal Tuesday for him. You thank your lucky stars you’re safely perched on that little stool outside Fillmore’s office now—if someone jumps you there, you can call a cop. I had no idea you had such hidden daredevil depths.

I was on top of a Piccadilly bus the other day, and who do I spot moseying along the pavement? Mr. Carmyle. He didn’t see me, thank goodness. I don’t want to run into anyone who reminds me of New York right now. Thanks for the news last time, but don’t do it again. It makes all the memories bubble up and I’m not ready for that. Let me write to you, it helps, but please don’t write back. You’ll get it. Oh, and Fillmore and Gladys Winch got married! From what I’ve seen of her, she’s the best thing that ever happened to that man. I need to dash off a note to him soon.

Yours,
Sally

Dear Ginger,

I saw a blurb in a Sunday paper last week that *The Primrose Way* had opened on Broadway and was a runaway smash. It’s great news, really, but it made me so restless I did one of those dumb, lonely things you do when you’re far from home. I marched straight to your club and asked for you! The sweet old doorman in the uniform told me in the kindest fatherly tone that you hadn’t been by in ages, he thought you were out of town, and would I take a seat while he checked? He whistled over a tiny page boy in a matching uniform, who skipped off yelling “MISTER KEMP! MISTER KEMP!” at the top of his shrill little lungs, and hearing your name echoing down the hall gave me such a weird twist in the chest. I felt terrible for wasting their time, so when the kid came back I slipped him twopence. Probably against every club rule ever, but he was delighted.

Mr. Faucitt finally sold the shop and skipped off to the country to raise his ducks. Now I’m at a total loose end.

Yours,
Sally

Dear Ginger,

What’s even the point of running away? I did everything humanly possible to put New York behind me, and it tracked me all the way to my tiny English hiding spot. I was wandering aimlessly down the Strand last week, and who should round the corner but Fillmore, arm in arm with Mr. Carmyle? I couldn’t duck. Carmyle had already spotted me, and also, you can’t outrun Fillmore these days. The man has blown up to the size of a small barn. Upon what meat doth this our Fillmore feed that he is grown so great? Poor Gladys must feel like she married two separate people when she looks at him over breakfast.

He’s over here to produce *The Primrose Way* in London, and he’s yammering nonstop about buying up half of England’s play catalogues. They dragged me off to the Savoy for lunch, whooping like they’d just rescued a lost lamb. It was the worst possible thing for me. Fillmore rambled Broadway gossip nonstop from the starters all the way through the French pastries, and by the time he was leaning back puffing a little waiting for his coffee, I was so homesick I was two seconds away from bursting into noisy sobs. The Savoy is just an annex of Broadway! Every single table had at least three Americans at it. I might as well have been eating at the Astor.

Carmyle invited us both back to his new country house, Monk’s Crofton, and I had zero excuses good enough to say no. Fillmore’s out golfing right now, and he’s sailing back to New York on the Mauretania this Saturday. He dropped a bombshell on me: on top of all his other schemes, he’s promoting a lightweight championship fight in Jersey City, and he’s guaranteed both boxers enough money to buy their own small countries. Arguing with him is useless, he just spits out numbers about all the fortunes other promoters made. It’s already locked in, apparently, the fight’s happening in a couple weeks. Makes my skin crawl, but I guess there’s no stopping him now.

This house, Ginger. It’s the stately English home they write about in all the old Victorian novels. Not the little pokey cottage Faucitt bought, a proper *Seat*. You drive three miles from the station, turn through huge iron gates flanked by stone posts with carved stone monsters on top, and there’s a tiny cottage by the gate where the old lodgekeeper pops out and tips his hat. You think that’s the house, right? Wrong. The car keeps rolling for another five miles through beech woods full of rabbits, and meadows full of deer, and just when you’re wondering if you’ll ever stop, you round a corner and there it is. Low, square, sprawling old stone, with a little tower on one end and a crenellated porch you could absolutely drop molten lead on unwanted visitors from, just like the old days. There’s a million rose bushes out front that will bloom in June, a huge walled kitchen garden full of hothouses, and a farmyard at the back full of squishy fluffy puppies that could rip your heart right out. It’s absolute heaven.

But there’s a catch. Every prospect pleases, and only man is vile—well, not vile, exactly, just so stuffy you could use them as doorstops. Guess who’s all staying here? Your entire family. Uncle Donald, all the aunts, the whole crowd. I walked into the drawing room the first night and it was bursting at the seams with Kemp relatives. I felt like a tiny innocent lamb dropped into a den of Daniels. Now I get exactly what you meant about them! They stare. Hard. I’m completely innocent, and even I felt like I was being interrogated for a felony, so I can’t imagine what it was like for you with your permanently guilty conscience. Poor thing, you must have had a nightmare.

Smuggling this letter out to you is going to be like running Civil War despatches through enemy lines. Everyone leaves their post on the hall table, and a servant picks them up to bike into the village later. If any of your aunts spot me writing a letter to you, I’m done for. They were ranting about you over dinner last night, Uncle Donald leading the crowd like a cheerleader. They say you’re little more than a blot on the human race, a Jimpson weed no respectable girl should ever speak to. I tried to say I knew you and you were mostly human, and the whole room went dead silent before they all started yelling at me for being too naive to spot a scoundrel right under my nose.

I’ll sneak this note down to the village myself later if I can slip away. I’ve got to go change for dinner now.
Yours currently being weighed for unworthiness,
Sally.
P.S. You were 100% right about Uncle Donald’s moustache, but it’s not a misfortune. He does it on purpose.

Dear Ginger,

I’m leaving today. Officially in disgrace. The whole family is giving me cold, silent glares, Uncle Donald won’t even look at me. You can guess what happened, and Fillmore has no idea—he left right before it all went down.

I’m done running away. This skulking around England avoiding all my ghosts is cowardly, and I’m feeling strong enough now to face them. I’m coming back to New York. I’ll mail this letter in London, and odds are it’ll end up on the same boat as me. You’ll probably be reading this two days before I knock on your office door. I can hear them muttering in the hall right now, no doubt deciding they never liked me from the start. Oh well.

Yours currently making a very dignified exit,
Sally.
