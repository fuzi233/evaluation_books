In fact, she is so well that I have resumed my own domestic routine, finding the Miss Dashwoods eager to reclaim their former station. However, I must interrupt our leisurely resumption of normalcy with intelligence of the highest order."

She paused, allowing the weight of her impending revelation to settle in the drawing room, where Elinor sat alone, her posture rigid with practiced neutrality.

"You must hear this, my dear Miss Dashwood!" Mrs. Jennings continued, leaning forward with the urgency of a courier delivering dispatches from a war zone. "When I visited Mr. Palmer's, Charlotte was in a state of absolute panic regarding the infant. Cries, fretting, pustules everywhere. Fortunately, he arrived fresh from Harley Street. Upon inspection, he concurred with our assessment: nothing serious, merely red gum. Satisfied, Charlotte relaxed. Then, as he departed, a thought occurred to me—a sudden, inexplicable impulse to inquire if there were any further developments concerning the young ladies under my care.

Dr. Donovan's demeanor shifted instantly. A smirk, a simper, a grave look—he clearly possessed information he deemed confidential. Finally, in a whisper, he delivered the verdict: *'For fear any unpleasant report should reach the young ladies... "What?" Elinor asked, her composure cracking slightly. "Is Fanny ill?"

Mrs. Jennings concluded her monologue, expecting a chorus of agreement or perhaps a display of maternal outrage. Instead, she encountered a momentary silence. Elinor, having collected her scattered thoughts, responded with the measured precision of a surgeon addressing a wound.

"I am gratified to observe that you no longer suspect my attachment to Edward," Elinor noted calmly. "Your recent assumptions regarding my feelings have ceased, which allows me to discuss this affair with the impartiality it requires. Internally, Elinor wrestled with the uncertainty of the outcome. Though she desperately wished to envision a future where Edward and Lucy united, her primary concern was the reaction of Mrs. Ferrars and, more critically, Edward's own behavior toward her. She felt profound compassion for him, a sentiment she struggled to extend to Lucy, whom she viewed with minimal sympathy.

Recognizing that Mrs. Jennings could not sustain conversation on any topic other than the scandal, Elinor realized the necessity of preemptive disclosure. She must dismantle Marianne's illusions, acquaint her with the brutal reality, and prepare her to hear the news from others without interpreting it as evidence of Elinor's own lingering affection or resentment toward Edward.

This was a mission fraught with pain. Elinor intended to strip away what she believed to be Marianne's sole source of solace. She planned to reveal details of Edward that might permanently tarnish his image in Marianne's eyes, hoping to induce in her sister a reliving of her own devastation through a parallel of circumstances. Yet, the task was unavoidable.

Elinor approached the confrontation with detachment. She made no attempt to dramatize her own suffering, presenting herself instead as a model of the self-command she had cultivated since learning of Edward's engagement. Her narration was lucid and straightforward, devoid of violent agitation or impetuous grief. Such emotional volatility belonged solely to the listener. Marianne listened with horror, her composure fracturing into excessive weeping. Elinor assumed the role of comforter, offering assurances of her own mental stability and a vigorous defense of Edward against all charges save imprudence.

Initially, Marianne rejected both the facts and the defense. In her mind, Edward was a second Willoughby. Acknowledging, as Elinor did, that she had loved him with sincerity, Marianne questioned her own capacity for lesser devotion. Regarding Lucy Steele, she deemed her utterly unamiable and intellectually incapable of securing the affections of a sensible man. She refused to accept that Edward's past affection for her was natural, requiring only empirical observation of human nature to alter her perspective.

Elinor's initial communication established only the existence of the engagement and its duration. Marianne's emotions immediately disrupted the flow of detail, necessitating a shift to soothing her distress and mitigating her resentment. The pivotal inquiry came from Marianne: "How long has this been known to you, Elinor? Has he written to you?"

"I have known it for four months," Elinor replied. "When Lucy first arrived at Barton Park last November, she confided in me regarding her engagement."

Marianne's eyes widened, registering a shock her lips could not vocalize. After a pause filled with disbelief, she exclaimed, "Four months! Have you known of this for four months?"

Elinor confirmed the statement.

"While attending me in all my misery," Marianne accused, "this burden weighed on your heart? And I have reproached you for appearing happy!"

"It was inappropriate for you to know then how profoundly I was the opposite," Elinor stated firmly.

"Four months!" Marianne cried again. "So calm! So cheerful! How have you sustained yourself?"

"By adhering to my duty. My promise to Lucy necessitated secrecy. I owed her the avoidance of hints, and I owed my family and friends the prevention of unnecessary anxiety regarding my welfare."

Marianne appeared deeply affected. "I have frequently wished to undeceive you and my mother," Elinor added. "On occasion, I attempted it; yet, without compromising my trust, I could never have convinced you."

"Four months! And yet you loved him!"

"Yes. But I did not love only him. While the comfort of others held value for me, I was glad to spare them the knowledge of my internal turmoil. Now, I can reflect and speak of it with little emotion. I would not have you suffer on my account; I assure you, I no longer suffer materially. I possess numerous supports. I am not conscious of provoking this disappointment through any imprudence of my own. I have endured it as much as possible without disseminating it further. I acquit Edward of essential misconduct. I wish him happiness. Being certain of his adherence to duty, I am convinced that while he may harbor regret momentarily, he will ultimately reconcile himself to his position. Lucy lacks sense, yet that is the foundation upon which all virtue may be constructed. Ultimately, Marianne, despite the enchantment of a singular, constant attachment and the belief that happiness depends entirely on one specific person, such dependence is neither intended, fitting, nor possible. Edward will marry Lucy. He will wed a woman superior in person and understanding to half the female population. Time and habit will instruct him to forget that he ever deemed another superior to her."
