The alarm had outrun us down the passages, as a smell outruns the cook who carries it—sharp, raw fear, like a pan forgotten on a sudden flame. We came on the servants gathered and scattering again in little eddies; a whisper flew, a sob answered it; skirts brushed and shoes scuffed the boards so that the house sounded altered, as if the very timber had taken to trembling. In such moments a great place has its own weather: you hear it before you see it, a draught of voices under doors, a choking hush where there ought to be the ordinary music of work.

We were passing my lady’s door when it burst open from within, flung wide as if the room itself could no longer bear what had been said in it. Her ladyship came out among us—Mr. Franklin at her shoulder, speaking to her in low urgent tones that could not mend her—she being, for once, wholly mastered by horror.

“You are answerable for this!” she cried, and her hand went out at Sergeant Cuff as though she would strike him with the accusation. “Gabriel! give that wretch his money—and release me from the sight of him!”

It was dreadful to see her so; and yet, in the very violence, I could hear the strain of the house’s discipline snapping: the old quiet order, like a well-tended stock-pot kept at a patient simmer, broken into boil-over. The women behind me made small sounds—breaths caught, a faint moan—such thin noises as show how near a mind is to spilling.

The Sergeant, alone among us, held himself as steady as a knife laid flat. He did not lift his voice; he did not shrink. He answered her with that grave restraint of his, respectful and hard as a properly set joint.

“I am no more answerable for this distressing calamity, my lady, than you are. If, in half an hour from this, you still insist on my leaving the house, I will accept your ladyship’s dismissal, but not your ladyship’s money.”

The firmness of it—quiet, not insolent—took hold of her. Mr. Franklin caught at the advantage; he drew her back by degrees, as you might coax a frightened creature from the edge of a drop. The door shut on them.

In the momentary lull, Sergeant Cuff’s eyes travelled over the servants with the same exactness with which a good judge of provisions will glance at a market-stall and know what is fresh and what is tainted. All the women were frightened; one alone was crying. He marked Penelope’s tears as if they were a stain that must have a cause.

“When your father has changed his wet clothes,” he said to her, “come and speak to us—in your father’s room.”

Before the half-hour was out, I had got myself into dry things—the damp having sunk through me to the bones, like cold brine—and had found such change of dress as the Sergeant required. Penelope came in then, obedient to the message; and if my tongue goes plain in many matters, it fails me at the sight of my own child in trouble. There are comforts that do not belong to grand folks alone; poor people have them too, though we are forced to take them quickly and in silence, between one duty and the next.

I drew her to me and set her on my knee. I kissed her, and I prayed God bless her. She hid her face against my chest and put her arms about my neck; and we waited so, without speech, while the house beyond us kept up its uneasy murmur. It seemed to me then that the sound of the great place had changed its key, as if some note had died in it and left a hole where the daily noises used to be—pots set down, knives on boards, the comforting clink of plates that tells you life is going on. Now there was only the restless tread and the half-stifled whispering, and every creak in the passage made you start.

The poor dead girl—God rest her—must have been at the bottom of it, I thought, for my daughter and for me. Sergeant Cuff went to the window and stood looking out, as if the air beyond might speak plainer than we could. I felt it right to thank him for giving us this breathing space, and I did. Fine people have, among their many luxuries, the luxury of indulging their feelings. We have no such privilege. Necessity—who is a harsh master to those under stairs—spares our betters and has no pity on us. We learn to press our feelings down and go on with the work, as you press down a stubborn paste and knead it smooth. I don’t complain. I only note it.

When he turned from the glass, Penelope and I were ready.

He asked her, first, if she knew what had led her fellow-servant to destroy herself.

My daughter answered—what you will foresee—that it was for love of Mr. Franklin Blake.

He asked next if she had spoken this notion of hers to any other person.

“I have not mentioned it,” Penelope said, “for Rosanna’s sake.”

I added, for I could not keep it back, “And for Mr. Franklin’s sake too, my dear. If Rosanna has died for love of him, it is not with his knowledge nor by his fault. Let him leave the house to-day, if he does leave it, without the useless pain of knowing the truth.”

“Quite right,” said Sergeant Cuff.

Then he fell silent again, with that look of his which seems always to be measuring one story against another—comparing what Penelope had said with some private thought of his own, kept close as a receipt written in a language you are not meant to read.

At the end of the half-hour, my mistress’s bell rang.

On my way to answer it, I met Mr. Franklin coming out of her ladyship’s sitting-room. He told me her ladyship was ready to see Sergeant Cuff—again in my presence—and he added that he himself wished to say two words to the Sergeant first.

As we went back towards my room, he stopped in the hall and looked at the railway time-table, as if the paper could tell him how to bear what was waiting. The house was quieter there, but not kindly quiet. You could hear, through the stillness, the far-off stir of servants at loose ends: a door closing too sharply; a footstep checked, then hurried; the whispering that runs like mice behind wainscot when disaster has happened.

“Are you really going to leave us, sir?” I asked. “Miss Rachel will surely come right again, if you only give her time?”

“She will come right again,” Mr. Franklin answered, “when she hears that I have gone away, and that she will see me no more.”

I took it at first for resentment, in a young man naturally wounded. But it was not so. My mistress had noticed, from the first arrival of the police, that the bare mention of him was enough to set Miss Rachel’s temper on fire. He had been too fond of his cousin to confess such a thing to himself until the truth had been forced upon him—cruelly, when she drove off to her aunt’s. Then his eyes were opened; and being what he is, he took the one resolution a man of spirit could take—to go.

What he had to say to Sergeant Cuff was said before me.

He told the Sergeant that Lady Verinder was willing to acknowledge she had spoken over-hastily. And he asked whether, in that case, Sergeant Cuff would consent to accept his fee and to leave the matter of the Diamond where it stood.

“No, sir,” said the Sergeant. “My fee is paid me for doing my duty. I decline to take it, until my duty is done.”

“I don’t understand you,” Mr. Franklin said.

“I’ll explain myself, sir,” returned Sergeant Cuff. “When I came here, I undertook to throw the necessary light on the matter of the missing Diamond. I am now ready, and waiting, to redeem my pledge. When I have stated the case to Lady Verinder as the case now stands, and when I have told her plainly what course of action to take for the recovery of the Moonstone, the responsibility will be off my shoulders. Let her ladyship decide, after that, whether she does, or does not, allow me to go on. I shall then have done what I undertook to do—and I’ll take my fee.”

There was nothing more to be said. Even in the Detective Police, a man has a reputation to lose; and the Sergeant spoke like one who will not have his name cheapened by being dismissed before he has delivered what he came to deliver.

I rose to conduct him to my lady’s room. He paused at the door and asked Mr. Franklin, civilly enough, whether he wished to be present.

“Not unless Lady Verinder desires it,” Mr. Franklin answered.

Then he bent to me and whispered, “I know what that man is going to say about Rachel; and I am too fond of her to hear it, and keep my temper. Leave me by myself.”

So I left him—miserable enough to see—leaning on the sill of my window, his face hidden in his hands. Penelope was peeping through the door, longing to comfort him. In Mr. Franklin’s place I should have called her in. When one woman uses you ill, there is great ease in telling it to another—because, nine times out of ten, the other takes your side. Perhaps, when my back was turned, he did call her in. If so, it is only justice to my daughter to say she would stick at nothing, in the way of comforting Mr. Franklin Blake.

In the meantime, Sergeant Cuff and I went on to my lady’s room.

At our last conference, her ladyship had sat with her eyes on the book before her, as if the print might keep the world out. Now there was a change for the better. She met the Sergeant’s look with a steadiness as resolute as his own. The family spirit showed itself in every line of her face; and I knew, as I stood behind, listening to the house hold its breath, that Sergeant Cuff would find his match in a woman strung up to hear the worst he could say.
