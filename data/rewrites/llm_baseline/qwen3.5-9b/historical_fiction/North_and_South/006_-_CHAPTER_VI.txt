The tender blossom flutter down;
Unloved that beech will gather brown,
The maple burn itself away;
Unloved, the sun-flower, shining fair,
Ray round with flames her disk of seed,
And many a rose-carnation feed
With summer spice the humming air;

The final day had arrived. The house was a hive of activity, filled with packing cases being loaded onto carts at the front door, destined for the nearest railway station. Even the once-pristine lawn beside the house had become untidy and disfigured by straw drifting in through the open doors and windows. Inside, the rooms possessed a strange, hollow echo, and the sunlight poured in through the uncurtained glass with a harsh, stark intensity, rendering the familiar surroundings suddenly alien.

Mrs. Hale's dressing-room remained the last sanctuary untouched. There, she and Dixon were busily folding clothes, frequently pausing to exclaim over forgotten treasures—relics of the children from their younger years—and handling them with affectionate reverence. Their progress was slow, weighed down by nostalgia.

Downstairs, Margaret stood calm and composed, offering counsel to the men hired to assist the cook and Charlotte. These two women, weeping intermittently, marveled at how the young lady could maintain such composure on this last day, concluding privately that she simply cared little for Helstone after spending so long in London. Indeed, Margaret stood there, pale and silent, her large, grave eyes observing every minute detail of the departure. To them, it was incomprehensible that her heart should ache with such a crushing weight, impossible to lift with a sigh, and that constant mental exertion was the only thing preventing her from screaming in pain. Furthermore, if she were to break down, who would remain strong enough to manage the situation? Her father was deep in the vestry, sorting through papers, books, and registers with the clerk; when he emerged, he would need to pack his own personal effects, a task requiring his specific attention. Besides, was Margaret the type to yield to strangers, or even to household friends like the cook and Charlotte? Certainly not.

Finally, the four packers retreated to the kitchen for tea. Margaret moved stiffly and slowly away from her post in the hall, passing through the bare, echoing drawing-room and stepping out into the twilight of an early November evening. A thin, dull mist veiled the world, softening outlines but failing to hide them, casting everything in a lilac hue as the sun lingered on the horizon. A robin sang—a bird, Margaret mused, perhaps the same "winter pet" her father had often described, for whom he had crafted a special house near his study window.

The leaves were more resplendent than ever; the first touch of frost would soon lay them low. Already, one or two drifted down, glowing amber and gold in the low, slanting rays of the setting sun. Margaret walked along the path beneath the pear-tree wall. She had not traversed this spot since walking beside Henry Lennox. Here, at this patch of thyme, he had begun to speak of the thoughts she must suppress. Her eyes had fixed on a late-blooming rose as she attempted to reply, and she had caught the image of the feathery carrot leaves in the midst of his final sentence. Only a fortnight ago! And everything had changed so drastically. Where was he now? In London, navigating the same routine, dining with the established circles of Harley Street or with his lively young associates.

Even as she walked sadly through the damp, dreary garden in the gloom, surrounded by falling and fading foliage turning to decay, he might be joyfully closing his lawbooks after a satisfying day of work. He would likely refresh himself with a run in the Temple Gardens, absorbing the grand, inarticulate roar of tens of thousands of busy men nearby yet unseen, catching fleeting glimpses of the city lights rising from the river depths. He had often spoken to Margaret of these hurried walks, snatched between study and dinner. At his finest moments, he had described them with such allure that the thought had captivated her imagination.

But here, there was no sound. The robin had fled into the vast stillness of night. Occasionally, a cottage door in the distance clicked shut, admitting a weary laborer to his home, sounding impossibly far away. A stealthy, crunching sound among the crisp fallen leaves of the woods beyond the garden seemed almost immediate. Margaret recognized it: a poacher. Sitting in her bedroom earlier that autumn, candle extinguished, she had often watched the silent leaps of poachers over the garden fence, their quick tramps across the dewy lawn, their vanishing into the black shadows. The wild, adventurous freedom of their lives had fascinated her; she had wished them success and harbored no fear. But tonight, inexplicably, she was afraid.

She heard Charlotte closing the windows and securing the house for the night, unaware that someone had ventured into the garden. Suddenly, a small branch—perhaps rotted, perhaps snapped by force—crashed heavily down in the nearest part of the forest. Margaret reacted instantly, running swiftly toward the window and tapping on the glass with a hurried, trembling urgency that startled Charlotte inside.

"Let me in! Let me in! It is only me, Charlotte!"

Her heart continued to flutter until she was safely back in the drawing-room, the windows barred and bolted, the familiar walls enclosing her against the unknown. She sank onto a packing case. Cheerless and cold was the dismantled room; there was no fire, no other light save Charlotte's long, unsnuffed candle. Charlotte stared at Margaret in surprise, and Margaret, sensing the gaze rather than clearly seeing it, rose.

"I was afraid you were shutting me out altogether, Charlotte," she said, offering a faint smile. "Then you would never have heard me in the kitchen, and the doors to the lane and churchyard have been locked for hours."

"Oh, miss, I would surely have noticed your absence soon. The men would have needed your directions. And I've put tea in Master's study; it's the most comfortable room, so to speak."

"Thank you, Charlotte. You are a kind girl. I shall be sorry to leave you. Please try to write to me if I can ever offer you any help or advice. I shall always welcome a letter from Helstone. I will certainly send you my address once I know it."

The study was prepared for tea, with a roaring fire and unlit candles on the table. Margaret sat on the rug, partly to warm herself against the damp chill clinging to her dress and the fatigue weighing on her bones. She balanced herself by clasping her hands tightly around her knees; her head dipped slightly toward her chest, an posture of desolation regardless of her inner turmoil.

However, upon hearing her father's footsteps on the gravel outside, she sprang up. Hastily smoothing back her heavy black hair and wiping away tears that had gathered on her cheeks without her conscious knowledge, she went to open the door.

He displayed far more depression than she did. She struggled to engage him in conversation, forcing herself to speak of topics she hoped would interest him, knowing each attempt required a monumental effort she feared might be her last.

"Have you had a very long walk today?" she asked, noting his refusal to touch any food.

"As far as Fordham Beeches. I went to see Widow Maltby; she is deeply grieved that we did not say goodbye properly. She says little Susan has been keeping watch down the lane for days past."

"Nay, Margaret, what is the matter, dear?"

The thought of the little child waiting endlessly, repeatedly disappointed—not due to any neglect on her part, but because of her absolute inability to leave home—was the final drop in Margaret's cup. She broke down sobbing as if her heart would shatter.

Mr. Hale was profoundly distressed. He rose and paced nervously up and down the room. Margaret tried to compose herself, remaining silent until she could speak with steady resolve. She heard him muttering to himself.

"I cannot bear it. I cannot bear to witness the suffering of others. I think I could endure my own trials with patience. Oh, is there no way to go back?"

"No, father," Margaret replied, meeting his gaze directly, her voice low and firm. "It is painful to believe you in error. It would be infinitely worse to have known you to be a hypocrite."

She lowered her voice at the end, as if the mere suggestion of hypocrisy regarding her father tasted of irreverence.

"Besides," she continued, "I am merely exhausted tonight. Do not think I am suffering because of what you have done, dear papa. We cannot discuss it tonight, I believe." Finding that tears and sobs threatened to overwhelm her again, she added, "I had better go and bring Mother up this cup of tea. She had hers very early when I was too busy to attend to her, and I am certain she would be glad of another now."
