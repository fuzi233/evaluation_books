Yes, I do know about them. I was one myself once, though not for long—not so long ago as my clothes. They were dreadfully long, I remember, and always in my way when I wanted to kick. Why do infants possess such yards of unnecessary fabric? It is not a riddle, truly. I simply cannot fathom it. Does the parent feel ashamed of the child's actual size and wishes to stretch it out via layers? I once asked a nurse. Her reply was: "Lor', sir, they always have long clothes, bless their little hearts." When I pointed out that her sentiment didn't solve the problem, she added, with a tone implying I'd suggested a monstrous outrage, "Lor', sir, you wouldn't have 'em in short clothes, poor little dears?" Since then, I've felt too shy to inquire further, and the reason remains a mystery. To me, dressing a baby seems absurd. Life has plenty of dressing and undressing; one would think those who live in bed might be spared the daily torture of waking a poor wretch to strip them, dress them, put them to bed, and then haul them out at night just to undo it all. And what difference is there, pray tell, between a baby's night-shirt and its daytime garb?

Perhaps I'm just being ridiculous—I often am, according to my informants—but I'll stop on the clothes, save for one suggestion: it would be highly convenient if fashion allowed you to distinguish a boy from a girl. Currently, it's a nightmare. Hair, dress, and conversation give zero clues. Nature's laws ensure you invariably guess wrong, leaving you branded a fool and knave. Calling a male babe "she" is an enormity; calling a female infant "he" is an atrocity. Whichever sex the child isn't is held in contempt, and mentioning it is treated as a personal insult. And for heaven's sake, do not attempt to escape this by using "it."

If you wish to earn maximum scorn, let a young mother hear you call the baby "it." Murdering a family or robbing a church gets you disliked, but addressing a child as "it" drains the full cup of human hatred. Your best strategy is to address the infant as "little angel." The noun "angel" is of common gender and suits perfectly; the epithet guarantees favor. Precede it with a short giggle and a massive smile. Above all, declare that the child has inherited its father's nose. This "fetches" the parents more than anything. They will feign laughter, saying, "Oh, nonsense!" You must then get agitated and insist it is a fact. Have no scruples; the nose really does resemble the father's—at least as much as anything else in nature—being, as it is, a mere smudge.

Do not despise these tips, my friends. One day, flanked by a mother and grandmother, surrounded by admiring young ladies (who admire the baby, not you), and facing a bald lump of humanity, you will be eternally grateful for advice on what to say. An unmarried man is never seen to worse disadvantage than during the "seeing baby" ordeal. A cold shudder runs down your spine at the mere proposal, and your sickly smile of delight ought to melt a mother's heart, unless, as I suspect, the whole event is a wife's clever device to drive bachelor friends away.

It is a cruel trick, whatever the excuse. The bell rings, the nurse is summoned, and the signal goes out for all women to begin babbling. You are left alone with sad thoughts and schemes to invent an important engagement. Just as you concoct a wild lie about a man outside, the door swings open. A tall, severe woman enters carrying what looks like a skinny bolster with feathers at one end. Instinct tells you it's the baby. You rise with a miserable attempt at eagerness. Once the initial gush of feminine enthusiasm subsides, the circle of fluttering skirts parts, and you step forward with the air of someone walking into the dock at Bow Street. Standing solemnly, staring at the child, you feel utterly miserable. Dead silence falls, and everyone waits for you to speak. Your brain freezes. Despair sets in, and your evil genius suggests idiotic remarks. Glancing around with an imbecile smile, you snigger, "Hasn't it got much hair, has it?" For a minute, nobody answers. Finally, the stately nurse speaks gravely: "It is not customary for children five weeks old to have long hair." Another silence ensues. Feeling granted a second chance, you ask if it can walk yet or what it eats.

Now you are deemed slightly unhinged, and pity is the only emotion felt. Yet the nurse, acting like a high priestess directing a ritual, holds the bundle toward you: "Take her in your arms, sir." Too crushed to resist, you meekly accept. "Put your arm more down her middle, sir," commands the priestess, and everyone steps back to watch you perform a trick. Knowing nothing, you feel compelled to act. You hoist the unhappy infant up and down to the tune of "oopsee-daisy." "I wouldn't jig her, sir, if I were you," warns the nurse; "a very little upsets her." You quickly decide against jiggling and hope you haven't gone too far.

Then the child, previously regarding you with mixed horror and disgust, ends the farce by screaming at the top of its lungs. The priestess rushes forward, snatching the baby with, "There! there! there! What did ums do to ums?" "How very extraordinary!" you say pleasantly. "Whatever made it go off like that?" cries the mother indignantly. "You must have done something to her!" It is clear they think you ran pins into it.

Eventually, the brat quiets, only for a mischievous busybody to point you out again with, "Who's this, baby?" The intelligent child recognizes you and howls louder. Whereupon a fat old lady remarks, "It's strange how children take a dislike to any one." "Oh, they know," replies another mysteriously. "It's a wonderful thing," adds a third. Everyone looks sideways at you, convinced you are a scoundrel, glorying in the idea that your true character has been unearthed by the untaught instincts of a toddler.

Yet, despite their crimes and errors, babies are not useless. Not useless when they fill an empty heart; not useless when their calls break clouds of care with sunbeams of love; not useless when their little fingers press wrinkles into smiles. Odd little comedians! They supply the humor in life's heavy drama. Each is a small, determined opposition to the general order, forever doing the wrong thing at the wrong time, in the wrong place, and in the wrong way. The nurse-girl who sent Jenny to check on Tommy and Totty and "tell 'em they mustn't" understood infantile nature. Give an average baby a fair chance, and if it doesn't do something a doctor should treat immediately, it hasn't tried hard enough.

They have a genius for the ridiculous, executed with a grave, stoical manner that is irresistible. Two joining hands and toddling due east while an excitable big sister roars after them westward is hilarious—mostly for the big sister. Walking round a soldier, staring at his legs, poking him to test reality; maintaining, against all logic, that a bashful youth at the bus terminus is "Dadda"; choosing a crowded street corner for family discussions; seizing a sudden urge to dance mid-crossing; selecting a shop doorstep for shoe removal. At home, they wield umbrellas as walking sticks, discover a love for Mary Ann precisely when she is blackleading the stove, and show affection for the dog by tugging its tail. Their favorite foods are coke and cat's meat. They nurse pussies upside down and pull tails.

They are troublesome, messy, and expensive. Still, you wouldn't have a home without them. Without their noisy tongues and mischief-making hands, rooms would be silent, and you might drift apart without prattling voices to gather you.

And yet, I have sometimes felt the tiny hand acts as a wedge, dividing. It is a bearish task to quarrel with the purest human affection—the perfecting touch of a mother's love. It is a holy love, incomprehensible to coarser-fibered men like me. I would not be deemed lacking in reverence if I say it need not swallow all other affection. The baby need not take your whole heart, like the rich man who walled up the desert well. Is there not another thirsty traveler standing by?

In your zeal to be a good mother, do not forget to be a good wife. No need for all thought and care to focus solely on one. Do not answer Edwin's request to go out with indignation, "What, and leave baby!" Do not spend all evenings upstairs, nor confine conversation to whooping-cough and measles. My dear little woman, the child won't die every time it sneezes, the house won't burn down, and the nurse won't flee with a soldier every time you step out. Nor will the cat inevitably sit on the child's chest the moment you leave the bedside. You worry too much about that solitary chick and worry everyone else along with you. Think of your other duties. Your face won't always be puckered with worry, and cheerfulness will return to the parlor alongside the nursery. Think of your big baby a little.
