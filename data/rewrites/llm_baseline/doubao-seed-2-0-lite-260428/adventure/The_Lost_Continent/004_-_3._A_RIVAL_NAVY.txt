When we finally hove in sight of Atlantis’s coast, Tob’s strange new navigational tools had carried us to our target with a precision that bordered on the miraculous—but we still faced ten days of backtracking along the shoreline to reach the broad, sheltered inlet that led straight to the continent’s glittering capital.

That first sight of green hills, and the wind that blew off them thick with the scent of wild thyme, turned earth, and sun-warmed pine, saved every last one of us. Weeks of brutal open-ocean tossing had eaten through our crews: dozens were dead, more were bedridden with scurvy, their gums black and swollen, their limbs too weak to heave a line even at the crack of a mate’s lash. But the moment they smelled that land air, the sick dragged themselves upright, color seeped back into their cheeks, and within days they were hauling rigging, patching sails, and spoiling for a fight to defend the voyage they’d all nearly died to finish.

I’d had doubts about my reception in Atlantis ever since I was deposed in Yucatan, no matter what Tatho had promised. But I’d never wasted breath fretting over it. It was all in the Sun God’s hands. Empress Phorenice might rule every inch of mortal land, might order my head struck from my shoulders the second my foot touched her docks—but the Sun reigned above even her. If my death was written, it would come because He willed it, not because I feared it, and so I’d spent the whole long voyage deep in calm study of the higher mysteries, no weight of worry on my mind.

But the day we rounded the two vast, craggy headlands that marked the inlet’s mouth, barely forty-eight hours’ sail from the capital, we spotted it: a whole foreign navy, anchored in a sheltered cove, waiting for us. Lookouts posted on the cliff tops screamed the alarm the second we came into view, and by the time we cleared the headland, every one of their ships was under sail, moving to block our path.

We had only five ships left out of the original fleet—all the rest had sunk in storms, or drifted derelict with every last man dead of scurvy. The enemy had three sleek, gleaming black warships, and three long galleys packed with oars. Our hulls were crusted thick with trailing ocean weed, our sails patched so many times you could barely tell the original canvas. Theirs were bright, fresh, immaculate. We flew Tatho and Deucalion’s royal colours high, unashamed of who we were and what errand brought us home, but the rival fleet carried no banners, no crests, as if they were ashamed to show their faces.

It was no surprise they meant to fight. No law held out on the open seas, miles out of sight of any shore: any crew was free to plunder any other that crossed their path, especially if they came home heavy from a raid or a trading run. Tob set us to scrambling into fighting trim in minutes, and our four remaining captains fell into tight formation beside us. None of them meant to end this long voyage in slavery.

The Sun blazed overhead, feeding full power to our solar engines, and the two fleets surged toward each other, the enemy’s three galleys hanging back to hold line beside their warships. When barely a hundred ship-lengths separated us, the strangers hauled to a halt. One galley pulled ahead, green palm branches flapping from its mast: they wanted a parley.

It was an unusual move, and sea-battered as we were, we had no reason to pick a fight we didn’t have to. We passed word down the line, hauled to a stop too, and Tob tied a dry, withered old sea grass frond to a spear and hoisted it to show we’d respect their ambassador.

The galley glided up, backed till its stern scraped against our shield rail, and a young, sharp-dressed crewman hopped over our gunwales. He was plump, healthy, soft from months of eating land food, and he looked around at our rags and gaunt scurvy-ridden men with a sneer of easy confidence. When he spotted Tob, he nodded like he was greeting an old drinking buddy.

“Old pot-mate,” he called, “your wife’s waiting for you on the quay in Atlantis, four little brats hanging off her skirts. Saw her less than two weeks ago.”
“You didn’t sail all the way out here to pass along home gossip,” Tob grunted. “We’ve drunk more than our share of tankards together, Dason. I know your tricks.”
“I’m just pointing out your home’s still there, all ready to welcome you back.”
“I never forgot it,” Tob said grimly. “It’s why I rammed and sank three separate pirate ships out on the deep, when a less careful man might have just surrendered and taken slavery. I’ve got a family to come home to.”
“I know you’re a stubborn bastard,” Dason laughed. “I’ll admit, if we fight, we’ll lose a few good men before we sink all your crazy rafts and your half-dead crew. But we’ve got orders, and my offer’s simple: hand over what we want, and we’ll let you sail home whole, no slit throats, no trouble.”
“Came to the wrong ship to pluck,” Tob rumbled with a heavy laugh. “We picked up no treasure in Yucatan, only dried food and fresh water. Our holds are full of nothing but half-dead scurvy sailors. You can search them from stem to stern if you don’t believe me. All you’re going to get here is blows, and we’ll hand those out for free.”
“Funny you value your cargo that little,” Dason said, grinning. “Because your cargo is exactly what I was sent to take, if you want that safe conduct home. We all know you’re sailing Deucalion back to Atlantis to prop up Phorenice. We don’t want her getting any more power to fight us. We’re taking Deucalion into our own custody, to deal with him our own way.”
“And if I refuse to hand him over?”
Dason glanced around at our splintered hull, our ragged little fleet. “Then we sink every last one of you in ten minutes. Instead of Deucalion going to stand before the Gods alone, he’ll be dragging a whole ragged crowd of you fools down to the bottom with him.”
“I doubt that,” Tob said, “but we’ll see. Handing over my Lord Deucalion is out of the question, anyway. First: if I showed up in Atlantis without him, Tatho would hunt me down and kill me slow, foot by foot. Second: I saw Deucalion kill a 40-foot sea lizard single handed out on the deep, and he’s too good a man for me to hand over against his will, even to Tatho. Third: you still owe me for your share of that wine cask we drank through on our last shore leave, and I’ll see you in the nether hells before I give you so much as a crumb till you pay that debt.”
“Hope you drown easy,” Dason sneered. “And as for that wife of yours? I’ve had my eye on her for years. I’ll find a very good use for her once you’re fish food.”

Tob’s face went purple with rage. “I’ll snap your neck for that, you spawn of a European brute. Get off my deck before I do it right now, you father of monkey children! Get out, or I’ll stove your ribs in and string your guts around your silly crew’s necks for necklaces.”

Dason didn’t wait for a second warning. He scrambled back over the rail to his galley, and Tob immediately cranked the solar engine of our flagship *The Bear* to full power, roaring orders to the rest of our little fleet. Even the scurvy-weak men jumped to obey. All five of our ships surged forward, picked one enemy warship out of the line, and slammed into its broadside all at once with their underwater rams, tearing its planks open so wide it sank before we could even back clear.

That brought the enemy’s numbers down to five, same as ours—but the advantage didn’t last. Their three fast galleys cracked their whips over their chained oar slaves, surged forward, rammed one of our ships clean through the hull, and cut down every man that tried to swim clear. Then the melee erupted, a tight, savage brawl that would have made the fiercest old warrior grin. The ships ground against each other on the swells like fighting wild beasts, men hacking with axes, thrusting with swords, hurling that sticky burning fire that sears through flesh and wood alike. Man-eating gulls circled overhead, and schools of sharks swarmed the water, fighting over every man that fell overboard.

Every part of the fight converged on *The Bear*. The enemy poured all their rage on us, and every surviving man from our other four ships rallied to us when their own vessels burned or sank. I’m of the Priestly Clan, I’ve fought a dozen wars to carve out new colonies for Atlantis, but that sea battle outdid any fight I’d ever seen. The swaying decks under our feet, the burning fire that clung to every surface, the sharks snapping below, the gulls screaming above—all of it was enough to break the bravest warrior. But those hard-bitten sailors faced it all without a single whimper of fear. Life on the open sea was already a brutal, death-haunted thing; they faced man-eaters and storms every week, and they had no fear left for a fight. Our battle cry was not my royal name, it was *TOB*, the name of that rough, unpolished old captain, and it rang out loud enough to drown out the half-dozen confused rallying cries the enemy shouted at each other.

By the end of it, every ship on both sides was either sunk or a smoldering wreck, their crews all dead—only *The Bear* and Dason’s galley were left. We had barely twenty men left standing out of all the crews that sailed from Yucatan. The enemy had boarded us, and were pushing us back across our blood-slick decks, when we smelled smoke: that burning fire had caught in *The Bear’s* timbers, and the whole ship was going up in flames around us.

Tob just laughed, loud and boisterous. “They can stay here and roast if they’re fools enough!” he roared. “We’re taking their galley. Guard Deucalion, lads, and follow me!”

The whole crew screamed *TOB* at the top of their lungs, and I found myself shouting the name right along with them. It was a strange, glorious thing, fighting not as a leader but in the wake of that big axe-swinging sailor. He cut a path right through the enemy line, his axe red to the hilt, and we charged after him, raging like madmen. Only ten of us made it across to the galley, the flames and smoke of our dying ship spitting at our heels. We stabbed down anyone that tried to follow, hacked through the grapple ropes, and let the burning *Bear* drift away to its death. The chained oar slaves stared at the fight with dull, unconcerned faces, only flinching when a stray arrow nicked one of them. We killed the three last enemy fighters who’d scrambled aboard after us, tossed their bodies to the sharks, chained five surrendered enemy men to the empty oar benches where dead slaves had sat, and left only Dason alive.

He’d dropped his sword, kneeling on the deck waiting for his fate, and Tob marched over to him grinning. “Well, old pot-mate,” he said, “you talked a big game about my wife and kids back on the quay. I’m giving you a choice. I can drag you home, parade you through the docks in front of the whole city, and let my wife do whatever she pleases to your body for that insult. Or I can kill you right here, quick and clean.”
Dason bowed his head, baring his neck. “I’ll take the quick way.”

One swing of Tob’s axe, and Dason’s head was off. He stuck it right on the galley’s bowsprit, as a warning for every other pirate that might try to block the path of Tatho’s fleet. The body he tossed over the rail, and one of the big man-eating gulls swooped down, snatched it up, and flew off to its nest in the cliff crags.

We feasted that night on fresh figs, warm bread, and dried lamb we found in the galley’s stores, and passed a skin of wine around the remaining ten of us. I filled a horn, poured a libation out at Tob’s feet, and clapped a hand on his blood-smeared arm. “You fought a grand fight today. I don’t know if I’ll be given my old power back the second we step ashore, or if Phorenice will order my head struck off on the docks, but the Sun God is my witness: I will never forget how you served me this voyage. Your wife and your children will never want for anything for the rest of their lives.”

Tob laughed, filled his own horn to the brim, and splashed half the wine at my boots. “That’s all the promise I need. The lads have earned their homecoming. And I guarantee you, when I sail into that quay with Deucalion still whole, Empress Phorenice will be so happy to see us, she’ll forget all about the six ships I lost to that trash.”

The oars dipped in smooth, steady strokes as we sailed onward. Ahead of us, over the blue water, the distant white spires of Atlantis began to glow in the sunset.
