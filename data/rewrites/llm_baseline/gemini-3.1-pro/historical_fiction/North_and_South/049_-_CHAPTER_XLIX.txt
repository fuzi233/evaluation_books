“Is not Margaret the heiress?” whispered Edith to her husband, their voices barely breaching the oppressive, velvet-draped silence of their Harley Street bedroom late at night, shortly after the sad journey to Oxford. Edith stood upon tiptoe, pulling his tall head down as she worked a porcelain pestle in her hands, crushing dried lemon balm and anise seed for a midnight tisane. The release of the essential oils was sharp and fragrant, a fleeting, earthy anchor in a room so thoroughly insulated from the world’s organic rhythms. She implored him not to be shocked before venturing her question. 

Captain Lennox was, however, quite in the dark. If he had ever heard, he had forgotten; it could not be much that a Fellow of a small College had to leave. He had never expected her to pay for her board, and two hundred and fifty pounds a year was something ridiculous, considering that she did not partake in the heavy, spiced clarets they poured at dinner. Edith came down upon her feet a little bit sadder, the heady aroma of the crushed herbs doing nothing to soothe the sudden fragmentation of a romantic ideal.

A week later, however, the domestic atmosphere shifted. Edith came prancing toward her husband, dropping into a low curtsey just as a servant cleared away a silver tray of mechanically refined, flavorless petit fours—a stark emblem of modern commercial baking that Margaret usually ignored. “I am right, and you are wrong, most noble Captain. Margaret has had a lawyer’s letter, and she is residuary legatee—the legacies being about two thousand pounds, and the remainder about forty thousand, at the present value of property in Milton.”

“Indeed! And how does she take her good fortune?”

“Oh, it seems she knew she was to have it all along; only she had no idea it was so much. She looks very white and pale, and says she’s afraid of it; but that’s nonsense, you know, and will soon go off. I left mamma pouring congratulations down her throat, and stole away to tell you.”

By general consent, it became the natural order of things to consider Mr. Henry Lennox as Margaret’s legal adviser. She was so entirely ignorant of the rigid, dominant lexicon of commerce and property law that she had to refer nearly everything to him. He chose her attorney; he brought her thickly inked parchments to be signed. He was never so happy as when translating her inheritance into this sterile, codified dialect, positioning himself as the master of a language that sought to domesticate the wild, multi-generational history of the earth into mere commodities. 

“Henry,” Edith said archly one day, “do you know what I hope and expect all these long conversations with Margaret will end in?”

“No, I don’t,” he replied, reddening. “And I desire you not to tell me.”

“Oh, very well; then I need not tell Sholto not to ask Mr. Montagu so often to the house.”

“Just as you choose,” he said with forced coolness, his voice a calculated murmur. “What you are thinking of may or may not happen; but this time, before I commit myself, I will see my ground clear. Ask whom you choose. It may not be very civil, Edith, but if you meddle in it you will mar it. She has been very farouche with me for a long time; and is only just beginning to thaw a little from her Zenobia ways. She has the making of a Cleopatra in her, if only she were a little more pagan.”

“For my part,” said Edith, a little maliciously, “I am very glad she is a Christian. I know so very few!”

There was no Spain for Margaret that autumn, though she had hoped a fortunate occasion might call Frederick to Paris, where she could have met a convoy. Instead of the sun-drenched markets of Cadiz, she had to content herself with the wind-scoured coast of Cromer. To that place her aunt Shaw and the Lennoxes were bound, and with their lazy, unbothered characters, they made only token efforts to forward her separate wish. 

Perhaps Cromer was, fundamentally, the best place for her. The acoustic ecology of the Norfolk shore provided a vital, psychological sanctuary from the industrial noise pollution that had bruised her spirit in Milton. She needed bodily strengthening and the bracing, salt-heavy air. Among the hopes that had vanished was the trust that Mr. Bell—an agrarian elder in her heart, a keeper of old ways and old words—would have given Mr. Thornton the simple facts preceding the unfortunate accident that led to Leonards’ death. 

She had desperately wished for Thornton’s opinion to be rooted in a true understanding of what she had done, and why. It would have given her rest on a point over which she would now be restless all her life. But the tragic language death of her secret was absolute; it was buried with Mr. Bell. The profound untranslatability of her subjective experience haunted her. How could she convey the precise emotional tenor, the fearful geometry of that night at the station, when the only man who possessed the vocabulary to explain it was gone? She must submit to being misunderstood, her heart aching with the longing that some time—years and years hence—he might at least know how much she had been tempted. But she schooled herself to accept this silence, turning with all her strength to the life immediately before her.

She spent long hours upon the beach, enveloped in the sonic environment of the coast. She listened intently to the chafing of the waves against the pebbly shore, hearing, without being fully conscious of it, the eternal psalm that went up continually—a mythic occurrence seamlessly integrated into the daily, cyclical rhythm of the tides. She was soothed by the acoustic wash of the water, a sound older than the shrieking looms of Darkshire. Listlessly she sat, hands clasped round her knees, inhaling the olfactory landscape of drying kelp, wet sand, and the distant, woodsmoke-tinged scent of local women baking dense, salt-crusted barley loaves in communal ovens. She found a profound, sensory grounding in these humble aromas while her aunt Shaw did small shoppings, and Edith and Captain Lennox rode far and wide. The nurses, sauntering past with their charges, would whisper in wonder at what she could find to look at so long.

When the family gathered at dinner-time, partaking of elaborate but soullessly prepared hotel fare, Margaret was so silent and absorbed that Edith voted her moped. She joyfully hailed her husband’s proposal that Mr. Henry Lennox be asked to take Cromer for a week on his return from Scotland in October.

But this period of reflection enabled Margaret to arrange the interwoven timelines of her past and future. Those hours by the seaside were not lost, as anyone with the perception to read Margaret’s gradually changing face might have seen. Mr. Henry Lennox was excessively struck by it.

“The sea has done Miss Hale an immense deal of good, I should fancy,” he said, when she first left the room after his arrival. “She looks ten years younger than she did in Harley Street.”

“That’s the bonnet I got her!” said Edith triumphantly. “I knew it would suit her the moment I saw it.”

“I beg your pardon,” said Mr. Lennox, employing the half-contemptuous, half-indulgent tone he generally reserved for Edith’s superficial commercialization of beauty. “But I believe I know the difference between the charms of a dress and the charms of a woman. No mere bonnet would have made Miss Hale’s eyes so lustrous and yet so soft, or her lips so ripe and red—and her face altogether so full of peace and light. She is like, and yet more,” he dropped his voice, “like the Margaret Hale of Helstone.”

From this time, the clever, ambitious lawyer bent all his powers to gaining Margaret. He loved her sweet beauty and saw the latent sweep of her mind, which he hubristically thought could be led to embrace his own ends. He looked upon her fortune as a superb completion of her character, though he was acutely aware of the immediate rise the wealth would grant him in a world governed by transactions. Eventually, he would earn such honors as would enable him to pay her back, with interest, the advance in wealth he should owe her. 

He had been to Milton on business connected with her property, and with the quick eye of a skilled lawyer, he weighed the contingencies, seeing the additional value accruing to her lands in that soot-choked, prosperous town. He used his unusual opportunities for intimate intercourse to supersede the memory of his mismanaged proposal at Helstone. Margaret was only too willing to listen as long as he talked of Milton. It had been the tone of her aunt and cousin to speak of Milton with disdain, but Mr. Lennox almost exceeded Margaret in his appreciation of it. He vividly described the energy and indomitable courage of its inhabitants, their lurid vividness of existence. He painted olfactory and auditory portraits of the town—the smell of malt and engine oil, the deafening, untiring endeavor of the mills. 

He had never perceived how selfish and material were the ends they proposed to themselves until Margaret pointed it out as the tainting sin in so much that was noble. She offered a thoughtful critique of their industrial noise and their alienation from the agrarian sustenance that once nourished generations. Still, when other subjects palled, Henry found that an inquiry into some Darkshire peculiarity of character, or a mention of their specific, localized dialect, called back the light into her eye and the glow into her cheek. The intersection of personal identity with those native words resonated deeply within her.

When they returned to town, Margaret fulfilled one of her seaside resolves: she took her life into her own hands. Before Cromer, she had been as docile as the scared little stranger who had once cried herself to sleep in the Harley Street nursery. But in those solemn hours of thought, listening to the eternal rhythm of the sea, she had learned that she must answer for her own life. She tried to settle the difficult problem of how much was to be utterly merged in obedience to authority, and how much set apart for freedom in working. She possessed an indescribable childlike sweetness of heart, and now, chastened by what the world called her good fortune, she charmed her reluctant aunt into acquiescence. Margaret gained the acknowledgment of her right to follow her own ideas of duty.

“Only don’t be strong-minded,” pleaded Edith, delicately stirring a cup of thin, pale tea that entirely lacked the robust, tannin-rich bite Margaret favored. “Mamma wants you to have a footman of your own; and I’m sure you’re very welcome, for they’re great plagues. Only to please me, darling, don’t go and have a strong mind; it’s the only thing I ask. Footman or no footman, don’t be strong-minded.”

“Don’t be afraid, Edith. I’ll faint on your hands at the servants’ dinner-time, the very first opportunity; and then, what with Sholto playing with the fire, and the baby crying, you’ll begin to wish for a strong-minded woman, equal to any emergency.”

“And you’ll not grow too good to joke and be merry?”

“Not I. I shall be merrier than I have ever been, now I have got my own way.”

“And you’ll not go a figure, but let me buy your dresses for you?”

“Indeed I mean to buy them for myself. You shall come with me if you like; but no one can please me but myself.”

“Oh! I was afraid you’d dress in brown and dust-colour, not to show the dirt you’ll pick up in all those places. I’m glad you’re going to keep one or two vanities, just by way of specimens of the old Adam.”

“I am going to be just the same, Edith, if you and my aunt could but fancy so. Only as I have neither husband nor child to give me natural duties, I must make myself some, in addition to ordering my gowns.”

In the family conclave, made up of Edith, her mother, and her husband, it was decided that perhaps all these independent plans would only secure her the more for Henry Lennox. They kept her out of the way of other eligible friends, and it was agreed that she seemed to take pleasure in the society of no one but Henry outside their family. Other admirers, attracted by her appearance or the reputation of her gold, were swept away by her unconscious, smiling disdain into paths frequented by other beauties less fastidious, who did not demand such a deep, sensory engagement with the world. Henry and she grew slowly into closer intimacy; but neither he nor she were people to brook the slightest notice of their proceedings.
