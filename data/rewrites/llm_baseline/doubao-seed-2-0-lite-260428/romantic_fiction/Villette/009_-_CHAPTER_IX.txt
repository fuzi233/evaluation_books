My days now brimmed over with purpose, every hour accounted for, every minute put to sharp, good use. Between leading my classes and poring over my own studies long after the lamps were lit, I scarcely had a spare moment to daydream. It was a pleasure I had not known for so long: no longer stagnant prey to mould and rust, I felt my faculties polish, my wits whet to a fine keen edge with constant, steady work. The world opening before me stretched far wider than the narrow bounds I had so far known. Villette was a cosmopolitan city, and Madame Beck’s school held girls from nearly every corner of Europe, of every rank imaginable. Labassecour wore no face of republicanism on its statutes, but its daily life breathed that same casual egalitarian spirit, so that at the long schoolroom desks, the young countess sat shoulder to shoulder with the daughter of a small shopkeeper, and often you could not tell noble from common by outward show at all. Indeed, the bourgeoise girls often carried themselves with a franker, more courteous grace, while the aristocracy bore away the prize for a delicate, perfectly balanced blend of insolence and deceit. Quick, hot French blood tangled in their northern marsh-phlegm, and that vivacity rarely showed itself in kindness: it only made their flattery glib, their lies flow smooth as oil, lending them a light, sparkling manner that was entirely hollow at the core.

To be fair, the honest native Labassecouriennes had their own brand of hypocrisy, coarse and unsubtle, fooling no one who cared to look. When a lie suited their ends, they tossed it out with careless, unblushing ease, no prickle of conscience to trouble them. From the scull scrubbing the kitchen floors to the directress herself, no soul in Madame Beck’s house thought twice about falsehood. To invent a fiction might not be a virtue, but it was the most trivial of faults. “I have lied many times,” was a regular line in every woman and girl’s monthly confession, and the priest heard it unshocked, absolved them without a second thought. But miss mass? Read a stray chapter of a novel? Those were grievous sins, sure to bring down sharp rebuke and stiff penance.

Half-blind to this strange code, untaught in its unwritten rules, I thrived at my new post after the first few harrowing weeks. The early lessons had felt like balancing on the lip of a moral volcano, rumbling under my feet, spitting sparks and hot acrid fumes into my eyes—but slowly, the fiery temper of my students subsided, at least where I was concerned. I was stubborn in my hunger to succeed: I would not be beaten by mere undisciplined disaffection, by wanton, willful disobedience, in my first real chance to make my way in the world. Many a night I lay awake long after the rest of the house was asleep, turning over what plan I might take to win a reliable hold on these mutineers, to bend this stiff-necked tribe to gentle, permanent influence. First of all, I saw clearly that no help would ever come from Madame. Her whole carefully cultivated system relied on staying endlessly popular with the pupils, no matter what injustice or discomfort her teachers had to bear. For a teacher to run to her for aid in a crisis of insubordination was little more than signing their own expulsion notice. She only ever claimed the pleasant, amiable, praiseworthy parts of interacting with the girls, and left all the annoying, firm, unpopular confrontations entirely to her underlings. I had no one to lean on but myself.

It was plain as day I could not drive this willful flock by force. They needed to be humoured, borne with endless patience; a quiet, courteous but unshaken manner impressed them far more than shouts. A rare, well-timed flash of raillery worked wonders, too. Severe, unbroken mental labour they would not, could not bear. Heavy demands on their memory, their reason, their attention, they rejected outright. Where an average, docile English girl would quietly take a difficult lesson, set herself to comprehend and master it, a Labassecourienne would laugh in your face and push the book back, exclaiming, “Good heavens, how difficult! I don’t want to do it. It bores me far too much.”
A clever teacher would take the book back at once, no hesitation, no squabble, no expostulation, smooth every difficulty away until the lesson shrank to fit the level of their understanding, and then lay the lash of hearty, unsparing sarcasm across their sloth. They would feel the sting, wince a little, but never bear malice, so long as the sneer was warm, not sour, and laid their ignorance and idleness bare in plain, unmistakeable terms. They would riot for three extra lines off a lesson they hated, but I never saw them rebel against a blow to their little store of self-respect: that quality had been trained down so hard for so long that it welcomed the weight of a firm, steady heel.

Bit by bit, as I gained fluency and ease in their language, learned to wield its sharp, vivid idioms to suit their case, the older, brighter girls began to like me, in their own way. I noticed that the moment I roused a spark of worthy emulation in their souls, or made them flush with honest shame, that girl was mine. If I could once make their ears burn under their thick glossy hair, all was well. Before long, little bouquets of wild violets and late roses began to appear on my desk each morning. To thank them for the sweet small attention, I sometimes walked with a select few along the alleyways during recreation. One afternoon, unguarded, I tried to correct their warped notions of right and wrong, and spoke plainly of how base and evil a lie was. I said, offhand, that of the two small failings, I thought falsehood far worse than the occasional lapse in church attendance. The poor girls had been trained to report every word their Protestant teacher said to the priest, and the consequences were immediate and unedifying. An unseen, nameless, indefinable shadow stole between me and my favourite pupils. The bouquets still appeared on my desk, but easy, open conversation was gone forever. Whenever I paced the gardens or sat in the rose arbour, a girl would drift to my right hand, and a teacher would appear by magic at my left. And Madame, in her silent soft-soled shoes, would glide to stand at my back as quick, noiseless, and unexpected as a wandering summer zephyr.

One day, a young pensionnaire to whom I had done a small favour spoke her naive opinion of my spiritual prospects outright, as we sat together. “Mademoiselle, what a great pity it is that you are a Protestant!”
“Why, Isabelle?”
“Because when you die, you will burn straight away in hell.”
“Do you truly believe that?”
“Of course I do! Everyone knows it, and the priest told me so himself.”
She was a blunt, odd little thing, and leaned in to whisper softly: “To make sure you get to heaven, we ought to burn you alive right here on earth, while you still can be saved.”
I laughed. There was nothing else to do.

Had the reader forgotten Miss Ginevra Fanshawe? I must reintroduce her now, thriving as a pupil at Madame Beck’s. She turned up on the Rue Fossette two or three days after I had settled in, and encountered me with barely a flicker of surprise. No duchess ever carried herself with such perfect, unforced nonchalance: a faint, passing amazement was the highest pitch of wonder she had ever known. All her other faculties were just as flimsy: her likes and dislikes, her loves and hates, were no sturdier than cobweb. The only thing strong and unshakable about her was her selfishness.
She was not proud, and seeing I was the “good old soul” type, she at once made me her confidante, pestering me for hours with vapid complaints about school quarrels and bad food. The Friday salt fish was too salty, the eggs too hard, the soup thin, the bread stale, the coffee weak. I bore it patiently for weeks, until I grew wearied of the same whine repeated over and over, and turned sharp, setting her firmly in her place. I ought to have done it from the start: a salutary dressing down always agreed with her.
I endured her demands on my labour far longer. All her fine outer gowns were elegant and well-made, but her undergarments were frayed, full of holes, and she hated mending so much she would heaps of her torn stockings and linen on my bed, begging me to repair them. When I finally put my foot down and told her she must mend her own clothes, she burst into tears, accused me of ceasing to be her friend, and threw a fit of hysterics that I let run its course without giving an inch.

For all her countless unrefined, unelevating foibles, she was so pretty. On a sunny Sunday morning, dressed in pale lilac silk, her long fair curls falling soft over her white shoulders, she looked positively enchanting. Sunday was her holiday, which she spent with friends in town, and she soon let slip that one of those friends was a man who burned with adoration for her, perhaps even real love. She called him Isidore, she told me—an alias, of course, because his real name was not nearly “pretty enough.” One day, after she had bragged for half an hour about the violence of Isidore’s attachment, I asked if she loved him in return.
“Sort of,” she shrugged. “He’s handsome, and he’s mad for me, so I’m very well amused. That’s enough.”
Finding she kept the game up far longer than her fickle nature had led me to expect, I one day asked seriously if he was the sort of man her parents, and especially her wealthy uncle who supported her, would ever approve. She admitted it was very doubtful, for she did not think Isidore had much money at all.
“Do you encourage him?” I asked.
“Furiously, sometimes.”
“Without any certainty you will ever be allowed to marry him?”
“Oh, how dreadfully dowdy you are! I don’t want to be married. I’m far too young.”
“But if he loves you as much as you say, and it all comes to nothing, he will be utterly miserable.”
“Of course he will break his heart. I should be shocked and disappointed if he didn’t.”
“I wonder if this Monsieur Isidore is a fool,” I said.
“He is a fool where I am concerned, but everyone says he is very wise and clever in other things. Mrs. Cholmondeley thinks he will go very far on his talents. All I know is, he sighs whenever he is near me, and I can wind him right around my little finger.”
I asked for a full description of the love-stricken Monsieur, but she could not paint a picture to save her life. She had hardly even properly looked at him, none of his features, none of the shifts of his expression had touched her heart or lingered in her memory. All she could say was that he was handsome, more of a fine striking man than a pretty boy. I would have lost all interest in her chatter long before, but every small hint she dropped, every stray detail, betrayed that Isidore offered her his homage with the utmost delicacy and respect. I told her plainly I thought he was far too good for her, and that she was nothing but a vain little coquette. She laughed, shook her curls out of her eyes, and danced away as if I had paid her the highest compliment.

Ginevra’s studies were little more than a formality. The only three things she practiced in earnest were music, singing, and dancing, plus embroidering fine cambric handkerchiefs that she could not afford to buy ready-made. History, geography, grammar, arithmetic—she left all of those undone, or bullied other girls into doing the work for her. Most of her time was spent out visiting. Madame knew her days at school were numbered, and would not be extended no matter how little she progressed, so she allowed her almost unlimited leave. Her chaperone, the gay fashionable Mrs. Cholmondeley, invited her whenever she had guests, and sometimes took her to evening parties across the city. Ginevra adored this arrangement, save for one catch: she needed a constant supply of new dresses, and she had no money to buy them. Her whole lazy, idle mind burned with schemes to solve that problem, spurring her to acts of wild daring that no one would have expected from her.
She begged boldly from Mrs. Cholmondeley, no trace of shy reluctance in her tone. “My darling Mrs. C, I have nothing fit to wear to your party next week. You must give me a book-muslin dress, and a sky-blue sash. Do, there’s an angel, won’t you?”
Darling Mrs. C yielded at first, but soon the demands grew so frequent that she was forced to put her foot down, like every other friend of Ginevra’s. After a while, I heard no more of presents from Mrs. Cholmondeley—but Ginevra still went out to parties, still had all the new dresses she needed, plus all the expensive little extras: gloves, bouquets, even small trinkets. She hid them all away, uncharacteristically secretive, until one evening before a grand ball that demanded extra fine costume, she could not resist coming to my chamber to show herself off in all her splendour.
She looked breathtaking. So young, so fresh, her delicate skin and lithe soft shape pure English charm, unlike any continental beauty I had seen. Her dress was new, costly, perfect, finished with every tiny expensive detail that lends an outfit that air of effortless, complete elegance. I looked her over from top to toe. She spun airily around so I could see every side, her small blue eyes sparkling with glee, about to bestow one of her giddy schoolgirl kisses on me, but I held her off at arm’s length.
“Steady,” I said. “Let us be steady, and work out what this magnificence is all about.”
“Will I do?” she asked.
“Do?” I said. “There are different ways of doing, and by my word I don’t understand yours.”
“But how do I look?”
“You look very well dressed.”
She thought my praise too cold, and began pointing out every part of her finery. “Look at this parure. The brooch, the earrings, the bracelets. No one in the whole school has a set like this, not even Madame.”
“I see them all.” I paused. “Did Monsieur de Bassompierre give you those jewels?”
“My uncle hasn’t the faintest idea they exist.”
“Were they presents from Mrs. Cholmondeley?”
“Certainly not. Mrs. Cholmondeley is a mean stingy creature, she never gives me anything now.”
I did not press her further, turning abruptly away.
“Now old Crusty, old Diogenes,” she said, those familiar teasing names she called me when we quarrelled, “what’s the matter now?”
“Go away. I have no pleasure looking at you or that parure.”
For a second she looked taken aback.
“Wait a minute, Mother Wisdom, I haven’t run anyone into debt for it. The jewels, the gloves, the bouquet, none of them are on credit. My new dress isn’t paid for yet, but my uncle will settle the bill. He never looks at the small items, just pays the total. And he’s so rich, he’ll never miss a few extra guineas.”
“Will you please go? I want to shut the door. Ginevra, people may tell you you look very handsome in that ball gown, but to my eyes you were a thousand times prettier in the plain gingham dress and straw bonnet you wore when I first met you.”
“Other people don’t have your puritanical tastes,” she snapped, angry. “And you have no right to sermonize me.”
“Of course I have little right, and you still less to flutter into my room like a jay strutting in borrowed plumes. I have no respect for your feathers, Miss Fanshawe, especially those peacock eyes you call a parure. They would be very pretty if you had bought them with your own money, money you could well spare—but they are not pretty at all under the circumstances.”
“A carriage is waiting for Miss Fanshawe!” the portress called from the hall, and she tripped away.

The mystery of her parure was solved two or three days later, when she came to me of her own accord to confess.
“You needn’t sulk at me, thinking I’m dragging papa or my uncle deep into debt,” she began. “I assure you nothing is unpaid for except those few new dresses I got lately. All the rest is settled.”
There it was, I thought. The secret. It wasn’t Mrs. Cholmondeley giving her gifts, her own allowance was barely a few shillings a month, which she hoarded like a miser.
“Listen!” she said, drawing close to me in her most coaxing confidential tone, for my sulkiness was inconvenient. She liked me to be in a listening mood, even if I only chided and railed at her. “Listen, dear old grumble-guts, I’ll tell you everything, and then you’ll see it’s all perfectly right, and very cleverly managed. First of all, I simply must go out into society. Papa himself said he wanted me to see a bit of the world, and he told Mrs. Cholmondeley that though I was a sweet creature, I had too much of a bread-and-butter schoolgirl air, and he wanted me to get rid of it here, before I make my formal debut in England. If I go out, I have to dress. Mrs. Cholmondeley is being mean now, and won’t give me anything new. It would be far too unfair to make my uncle pay for every single little thing I need—you can’t deny that, that agrees with all your own preachments, doesn’t it? Well, but someone overheard me complaining to Mrs. Cholmondeley, quite by chance I swear, about how hard up I was for a few ornaments. Someone who was not only not grudging about giving me a present, he was absolutely delighted at the chance to offer me some small trifle. You should have seen how shy he looked the first time he mentioned it, how he hesitated and blushed, positively trembling in case I turned him down.”
