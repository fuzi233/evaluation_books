The realization that we had secured a steady supply of bread shifted my focus entirely back to the wreckage. Above all else, I was driven to claim possession of the beautiful pinnace. I knew that retrieving it would demand a united effort, so I began to urge my wife to allow me to depart with all the boys save Franz. After some reluctance, she finally gave her consent, provided I solemnly promised never to spend a single night aboard the wreck. Though I accepted this condition with a heavy heart, we parted with mutual dissatisfaction regarding the arrangement.

The boys were thrilled by the prospect of such a large expedition, cheerfully hauling provision bags stuffed with cassava bread and potatoes. Upon reaching Safety Bay without incident, we first visited the geese and ducks inhabiting the marsh, ensuring they were thriving before equipping ourselves with cork belts. Stepping into the tub-boat with the raft in tow, we steered directly toward the wreck.

Once aboard, I instructed the boys to gather whatever items were easiest to transport, loading the raft for our eventual return, while I conducted a meticulous inspection of the pinnace. I quickly concluded that obtaining the vessel would be fraught with nearly insurmountable difficulties. She lay at the far end of the hold, wedged in a cramped, inaccessible space where assembly was impossible. Furthermore, the parts were so heavy that moving them piece by piece to a convenient location was equally unfeasible.

I dismissed the boys to rummage for loose items and sat quietly to contemplate the challenge. As my eyes adjusted to the dim light filtering through cracks in the hull, I noticed that every component of the pinnace was carefully arranged and numbered. If I could devise a plan and create sufficient workspace, success seemed within reach. "Room! Space to work!" I cried as my sons approached, their faith in me absolute. "Bring axes, and let us break down the surrounding compartment to clear a circle of space."

We labored tirelessly, but as evening approached, progress was minimal. The sheer volume of woodwork surrounding us suggested that immense labor and perseverance were required before we could claim ownership of the elegant craft, which resembled a fossil shell trapped within a rock. Hasty preparations were made for our return to shore. Landing at Falconhurst, we found my wife and little Franz waiting at Tentholm. She had decided to establish her quarters there while we worked on the wreck, hoping to remain closer to our progress without losing sight of us. Delighted by her practical wisdom, I praised her sense, promising to work diligently so she might return home soon.

Upon greeting us, the boys presented two casks of butter, three of flour, corn, rice, and other treasures for our storehouse. Our days thereafter were consumed by arduous toil on the wreck: clearing the deck around the pinnace and assembling her parts. We started at dawn and returned at night, ferrying valuable cargo from the old ship. Finally, with incredible exertion, the pinnace stood ready for launch, yet she remained imprisoned within massive wooden walls that defied our strength.

It seemed as if the graceful vessel had awakened, longing to spring into the free blue sea. I could not bear the thought that our success might end in failure. Despair threatened to overcome me until a sudden idea illuminated my mind. Without revealing my intent, I procured a large cast-iron mortar, filled it with gunpowder, and capped it with a block of oak pierced for a match. I positioned this "petard" precisely to blow out the side of the hull nearest the pinnace. Securing it with chains to prevent recoil damage, I told the boys I was returning to shore early and ordered them into the boat. Lighting a match designed to burn slowly before reaching the powder, I hurried after them, my heart pounding, as we made for the land.

We beached the raft and began unloading, keeping the other boat ready for immediate departure. Unobserved, I strained to hear the expected detonation. It came—a flash, a mighty roar, a grand burst of smoke! My wife and children, terrified, turned to me for explanation. Suspecting a fire from our recent caulking, I calmly reassured them that it was likely an accidental ignition, then joined the boys in rushing back to the wreck.

Arriving at the site, we found the usual boarding area intact, but turning to the far side, a marvelous sight awaited us. A huge rent tore through the decks and bulwarks, water churned with wreckage, and the compartment housing the pinnace was fully exposed. There, seemingly uninjured, sat the little beauty. The boys, stunned by the surrounding devastation, gasped as I shouted in delight, "Hurrah! She is ours! The lovely pinnace is won!" They quickly deduced my cunning plan, realizing the machine we helped build was specifically designed to liberate her.

Entering the shattered opening, I confirmed that the pinnace had suffered no damage from the explosion. With rollers placed beneath the keel, we utilized levers and pulleys to guide her gently into the water, where she floated as if recognizing her native element. Wild with excitement, we cheered as she drifted free. We secured her at the harbor's sheltered point and returned to Tentholm, attributing the explosion to our earlier work.

Over the following days, we completed the rigging and mounted the two brass cannons. The possession of an armed vessel ignited a martial fervor in the boys; they spoke incessantly of savages, fleets, and defense. I reminded them that while their valor was stirring, we hoped never to test it. Fully equipped, the pinnace sailed away, and as we entered the bay, the boys fired a salute. My wife and Franz, initially alarmed by the ship's arrival and the gunshots, soon recognized us and rushed to the landing stage, breathless with emotion.

After receiving the admiring gaze of the pinnace, my wife invited us to see her improvements. Leading us up the river toward the cascade, she revealed a neatly laid-out garden with beds of lettuce, cabbages, beans, and peas. She detailed her plans for sugar cane, pineapple, and melon patches, using maize to shield the European vegetables from the sun. Her garden was a testament to her diligence and ingenuity.

Sundays later, we observed the Sabbath with devotion. In the evening, I demonstrated athletic exercises—running, leaping, wrestling—to ensure my sons grew into strong, capable men. I emphasized that true courage required both moral fortitude and physical power. On the following day, I introduced them to a "miniature lasso"—a cord with leaden weights at both ends, inspired by South American hunters. After demonstrating its efficacy on a tree stump, the boys eagerly practiced casting lassos.

Suddenly, a gale arose, agitating the seas. I felt relieved knowing our newly launched pinnace was safely moored and we had no urgent business at the wreck. Meanwhile, my wife displayed her latest achievements: a cask of wild pigeons and ortolans, and the urgent need to replant her neglected fruit trees, which were drying in the heat. We immediately set about planting the orchard and planned an excursion to the Calabash Wood to harvest gourds and utensils.

By sunrise, the entire family, accompanied by our pets and Turk in his coat of mail, departed. We crossed Flamingo Marsh, where Fritz and Jack hunted a large bird that escaped, leading to a chaotic chase involving the dog Juno and a riderless donkey. Eventually, we captured a magnificent bustard, though my wife expressed concern for her brood. Reaching the Monkey Grove, Ernst admired a towering coconut palm, and a giant nut plummeted at his feet, followed by another, mimicking a magical gift. Peering upward, Fritz spotted a hideous creature descending: a colossal land-crab. Jack, initially fleeing, bravely wrapped his jacket around the crab and beat it to death, a feat I later clarified involved striking the bundle to ensure the crab's demise.

Deeper in the woods, Ernst discovered a "liane rouge," a precious vine yielding clear, cold water essential for survival. We quenched our thirst and eventually reached the Calabash trees, where everyone busied themselves cutting and carving gourds into bowls, cups, and jars. We prepared a campfire, but the cooking failed due to a lack of water, prompting a search for a spring. Instead, we encountered a wild boar, only to discover it was our runaway sow, feasting on poisonous-looking fruit (later identified as non-toxic guavas). Jack mistook a large, harmless iguana for a crocodile, leading to a tense standoff that ended when I charmed the lizard with a flute-like whistle and subdued it with a noose.

Carrying the heavy iguana on my back, we returned to the camp, where my wife and Franz waited anxiously. Having found edible guavas and roasted acorns alongside the boiled iguana, we broke our fast. Realizing the journey ahead and the need for rest, we abandoned the sledge and traveled home through a majestic oak forest, arriving at Falconhurst just before nightfall. Supper, consisting of the delicious iguana and acorns, restored our exhausted strength, marking the end of a productive and adventurous week.
