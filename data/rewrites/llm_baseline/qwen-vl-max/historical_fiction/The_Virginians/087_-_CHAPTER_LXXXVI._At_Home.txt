An appeal from our mother such as this would have softened even the hardest hearts—certainly ours, though we had grown somewhat stubborn with time. We spoke of making a swift journey to Virginia and hiring all the cabin space aboard the *Young Rachel*. But fate intervened: our child fell ill, and the voyage was deemed too perilous. A mother could not bear to part from her sick child, so the ship sailed without us that year.

The next season brought another obstacle—my worship’s first bout of gout, which occupied me deeply. It was a trial, but in truth, it cleared my mind and stirred new energy within me. Yet again, plans were postponed—this time by sorrowful news from Jamaica.

Some two years after we settled at the Manor, our dear General returned from his governorship. He came back slightly wealthier than when he left, yet burdened by a loss no riches could mend. He had chosen to leave the West Indies behind, unable to endure life without her. My Theo’s beloved mother—the most tender and devoted friend I ever knew, save one—had died abroad of fever. Her last regret was that she would not live to see her children and us prosper.

“She sees us now, even if we do not see her,” her husband said. “And she thanks you, George, for being kind to her children.”

We feared he would not long survive her. His love for her had shaped every moment of his life. To be parted from her was like living in exile. It pained us to watch him sit among us, quiet and withdrawn. My wife, in her manner, voice, and gestures, often reminded him of the woman he had lost. We did what we could to comfort him, but little Hetty became his greatest solace under Heaven.

She had refused several advantageous matches in the island, the General told us. When she returned to England, Lord Wrotham’s heir had laid himself at her feet. But she chose to remain with her father. “As long as he wants me,” she said, “I care for no husband.”

“Then let the General stay six months with us at the Manor,” we suggested. “You can have him at Oakhurst for the other half.”

But Hetty insisted her father could never return to Oakhurst now that her mother was gone—and she would marry no man for his title or fortune. Not she! The General, when we discussed it, said gravely that perhaps she had suffered some early disappointment, something she never spoke of. We respected her silence, and so did he.

By then, my brother Lambert had secured a college living near Winchester and, naturally, a wife to grace his parsonage. We welcomed her kindly, though we found ourselves only moderately fond of her. Mrs. Jack—a woman given to loud opinions and sharp tongues—was convinced Hetty’s refusal to marry stemmed not from grief, but from pride.

“My dear Sir George,” she declared, “I’ve told Theo many times I wouldn’t keep a pretty sister in my house just to make tea while I’m upstairs, or fetch things from the kitchen, or always look neat and dressed up when I’m baking pies or tending the children. I trust Jack completely—he’d never look at another woman, indeed! And I trust Jemima too—but they don’t come together in my house when I’m above stairs. That I promise!”

“And am I to understand,” the General asked coldly, “that you’ve done my Lady Warrington the favour of warning her against her sister—my daughter Miss Hester?”

“Yes, pa, of course I have. A duty is a duty. A woman is a woman. A man is a man, as I know very well. Don’t tell me otherwise! Every man is a man, no matter how sanctimonious he pretends to be!”

“You yourself have a married sister whom you were visiting when my son Jack first met you,” the General observed.

“Yes, of course I do. Everyone knows that. And I’ve been like a mother to her children, I have!”

“And are you suggesting your presence proved a temptation to her husband?”

“Law, General! How can you say such a thing? I never said anything like that!”

“Yet you imply it—about yourself, and about my daughters.”

“Never, never, never! As a Christian woman, I swear it! And it’s cruel of you to accuse me! I do say a sister is best out of the house—that I do! And since Theo’s time is coming, I warn her, that’s all.”

“Have you discovered,” the General pressed, “whether my poor Hetty has taken any of the silver spoons? This morning, when I came to breakfast, my daughter was alone, and there must have been a score of pieces on the table.”

“Law, sir! Who ever said a word about spoons? Did I accuse the poor dear? If I did, may I drop dead right here on this rug! And I won’t stand being spoken to like this! Jack and I both noticed it. I’ve done my duty, I have!” With that, Mrs. Jack stormed out, tears streaming down her face.

“And has this woman had the audacity to tell you this, my child?” the General asked, when Theo—delicate in health—joined us at tea.

“She tells me every day since she arrived. She comes into my dressing-room, my nursery—‘Ah, I wouldn’t have a sister prowling about my nursery!’ she says. How delightful it is to have such charming relatives, I say.”

“Your poor mother was spared this woman,” the General groaned.

“Our mother would have made her better, papa,” Theo replied, kissing him.

“Yes, dear.” And I saw them both fall into prayer.

It must be admitted: loving one’s kin is not always easy. Living with neighbours can be tedious. From Jack Lambert’s behaviour the next day, I knew his wife had shared her version of the exchange. He was sullen, not dignified. Angry, yet still hungry. He preached a sermon that was entirely dull. Little Miles, once more in mourning clothes, sat beside his grandfather, his small hand clasped in the old man’s.

Would he stay and manage the household during our trip to Virginia? The housekeeper would be under Hetty’s command. The butler’s keys would pass to him. Gumbo, who had married Mrs. Molly and raised a family of tawny children in my park, was to accompany us—not with great enthusiasm, I suspect.

Under the care of our General and his daughter, we left our home. We journeyed to London, then to Bristol, where our obedient agent professed prayers for our safety and declared that no children as beautiful as ours had ever graced a ship before. Our voyage passed without incident.

How strange it felt stepping ashore at Richmond! A coach waited, along with a host of negroes. Nearby, a gentleman on horseback, also attended by men in our livery, dismounted and rushed forward to embrace us. It was our dearest Hal. He rode with us to our mother’s door. There she stood on the steps, waiting. Theo knelt to receive her blessing.

Harry rode with us only as far as the house. He declined to enter with us. “She sees me,” he confessed, “and we’re fairly decent friends. But Fanny and she—well, they’re better apart. No love lost between them, I assure you. Come visit me at the Tavern when you’re free, George. Tomorrow I’ll present her sister to Theo. I only heard the ship was sighted yesterday—I stayed to see you. I sent a negro boy home to my wife; she’ll come to pay her respects to my Lady Warrington.” Then Harry leapt from the carriage and left us to meet our mother alone.

Since I had last seen her, I had encountered fine society—Theo and I had paid our respects to the King and Queen at St. James’s—but none compared to the dignity of the woman who greeted us now. I lifted my wife from her knees, embraced her, and led her inside. The house was plain, wood-built, with a gallery around it—typical of Virginian homes. Yet if it had been a palace with an empress within, our welcome could not have been more gracious.

Old Nathan remained major-domo. A dozen black faces grinned their greetings. Some I remembered as children were now strong young adults; others, once bald-headed, now wore silvery hair. Newborns peered from doorways, barefoot and wide-eyed. “I’m little Sip, Master George!” “I’m Dinah, Sir George!” “I’m Master Miles’s boy!” cried a lad in a new livery and boots polished with natural black.

Before the day ended, every servant had found a reason to pass before us, bowing and grinning. I cannot say how many meals were served. In the evening, my Lady Warrington received all the gentry of the town with perfect grace. I shook hands with a few old acquaintances—old enemies, I nearly said—but I had come into a fortune and was no longer the reckless prodigal.

A drove of fatted calves was slaughtered in my honour. Poor Hal attended the feast, but he looked glum and defeated. His mother spoke to him, but it was as a queen addressing a rebellious prince—her son, not yet forgiven.

Theo and I slipped away from the gathering and went to our rooms. Just as we began to talk freely, our mother appeared with a candle, her face pale. “Do you need anything? Is everything to your liking?” She had peeked in on the children, who slept like cherubs. She cooed over them, delighted by Miles’s commanding air and little Theo’s smiles and dimples. “Supper is ready, Sir George. If you prefer our cooking to the tavern’s, Henry, I beg you to stay.”

How different her tone was toward each of us! Hal lowered his head and followed silently to the dining room. A clergyman offered a blessing, speaking with skill and eloquence of our safe return, of God’s mercy through stormy seas, and of the divine forgiveness awaiting us when life’s tempests ceased.

This was a new clergyman—unlike those I remembered from earlier days. I praised him, but Madam Esmond shook her head. “I fear his principles are dangerous,” she whispered. “Others have adopted them. Have you seen the paper signed by the burgesses and merchants at Williamsburg last year—the Lees, Randolphs, Bassets, Washingtons… and oh, my dear, I hate to say it, our name—that is, your brother’s (by what influence I dare not speak), and that unfortunate Mr. Belman, who begged a blessing last night?”

If there had been disputes in our colonial society when I left, what were they compared to the feuds I found upon my return? We had sent the Stamp Act to America and repealed it. Then came new duties on glass, paper, and so on—also repealed, except for tea. From Boston to Charleston, tea was seized. Even my loyal mother gave up her favourite drink. My wife would have had to follow suit, but we had brought a private supply aboard, already taxed fourfold in England.

Not that I hesitated to pay duties. The home government needed revenue; without it, its authority was meaningless. They say the colonies were oppressed—I say the home government was tested and overwhelmed. [It is history now. We tried, and failed. The matter is settled as firmly as the Norman conquest of Britain.]

From conviction, I took the British side. In my brief military experience, the army’s cry was that Braddock’s expedition failed due to negligence, selfishness, and greed among those whose defence it was meant to protect. The colonists wanted everything done for them, yet did nothing. They made unfair deals with defenders, broke contracts, provided scant supplies, delayed action until it was too late. The disaster that followed need never have happened but for their indifference.

What joy erupted when the great British minister triumphed in conquering Canada! Monsieur de Vaudreuil was right: that victory marked the beginning of American rebellion. Lord Chatham, who helped achieve it, did more than anyone in England to complete it. The colonies revolted—and he cheered their defiance. How many wavering souls must he have encouraged? Like a general saying to a mutinous army, “God save the king! You have a right to rebel!” No wonder they erected his statue in one town, hung his portrait in another, and hanged governors in effigy elsewhere.

In Williamsburg, some proposed bringing over a portrait of Lord Chatham—dressed as a Roman orator, pointing to Whitehall and the window from which Charles I was executed. A clever allegory, and a flattering tribute to a British statesman! Though I hear the head was painted from a bust, so it was taken without his knowledge.

My country is England, not America or Virginia. I stood—and still do—on the English side. My heart belonged to home, where I was now a squire and citizen. Had I planted tobacco on the banks of the James or Potomac, my views might have changed. When I visited my brother at his new plantation, I found him and his wife as staunch Americans as we were Britons. We argued, as many did in those troubled times—but without bitterness. Even my new sister tried her best to inflame us, especially in private lectures she doubtless gave her husband. Yet we trusted each other so fully that even Harry’s loyalty to his wife could not make him quarrel with me.

He loved me from childhood, when my word was law. He still claimed every Virginian gentleman was loyal to the Crown. War had not yet begun. Gentlemen of differing views treated one another with courtesy. At public dinners, the King’s health was toasted loudly. Each colony’s assembly, though preparing for Congress and resisting taxation, expressed deep affection for the King as “our Father,” pleading with him to dismiss evil advisers and listen to reason.

Until the end, Virginia’s gentry remained grave, orderly, aristocratic—firm in their dignity. Later generations spoke of equality and fraternity. Among Europe’s nobility, I’ve never known a man who held his station with greater dignity than Mr. Washington—not even the King he fought against. To the French court, who rejoiced in their war against us for Canada, he was an *anax andron*—a true leader. They acknowledged no greater figure in Versailles.

Though they opposed the Governor, the House of Burgesses maintained respectful relations with him. When Lord Bottetourt arrived in splendour, all the gentry visited him, including Madam Esmond. At his death, Lord Dunmore succeeded him, bringing a fine family, and was treated with private respect—though publicly, the Assembly and Governor were at odds.

Their conflicts belong to history. For me, they mattered only because, the year after my arrival, the burgesses convened on March 1st. We decided to visit the capital and pay our respects to the Governor. Since Harry’s marriage, Madam Esmond had not performed this duty—though she had always done so before. Now, with her eldest son returned, she insisted we must attend. Perhaps we were glad to escape Richmond and enjoy the pleasures of the provincial capital.

Madam engaged, at great expense, the finest house in Richmond for herself and her family. Now I was rich—yet her generosity surprised me. I often had to intervene (her servants, too, puzzled by her new ways) and beg her not to spend so lavishly. But she gently replied that in former days she had needed to save—now she did not. Harry had enough, surely, with a wife taken from the housekeeper’s room. If she wished to be extravagant, why should she hesitate? She no longer had her dearest daughter and grandchildren with her every day—she adored them all and spoiled them beyond measure. Besides, in earlier years, I could never accuse her of extravagance. That, I believe, was her only reference to our financial differences.

So she dressed her people in their finest, shipped her best wines, plate, and furniture from Castlewood by sea—at great cost. She wore the dress she had worn at her wedding in George II’s reign. We believed our coach made the grandest impression—save the Governor’s. We hired Signor Formicalo, the Governor’s own major-domo, to oversee the feasts given in my honour. More fleshpots simmered in our kitchens in one month than had been seen in the family since the boys left.

Theo’s influence over our mother was so strong that she actually persuaded her to receive our sister Fanny—Hal’s wife—who would rather have stayed on the plantation than face Madam Esmond. But trusting Theo’s promise of forgiveness, Fanny came to town, curtsied, and was accepted.

Rather than be forgiven so, I confess I would have preferred damnation or lifelong persecution.

“You know these, my dear?” Madam Esmond asked, pointing to her silver sconces. “Fanny cleaned them often when she lived with me at Castlewood. And this dress—Fanny knows it, I daresay? Her poor mother cared for it. I always trusted her.”

A flash of anger lit Fanny’s eyes—eyes our mother, who had forgiven her, did not notice.

“Oh, she was a treasure to me!” Madam continued. “I could never have nursed my boys through illness without your mother’s care. Colonel Lee, permit me to introduce you to my daughter, my Lady Warrington. She is a neighbour of your relatives, the Bunburys, in England. Here comes his Excellency. Welcome, my lord!”

Our princess executed one of those elegant curtseys she prized. I fancied I saw a few guests suppress smiles.

“By George, madam,” said Mr. Lee, “since Count Borulawski, I’ve not seen a bow so graceful.”

“And pray, sir, who was Count Borulawski?” Madam asked.

“He was a nobleman in high favour with the Polish king,” Lee replied. “May I ask you, madam, to present me to your distinguished son?”

“This is Sir George Warrington,” Madam said, pointing to me.

“Pardon me, madam. I meant Captain Warrington, who stood by Mr. Wolfe when he died. I would have gladly shared his fate—so long as I stood near him.”

The ardent Lee stepped forward, took Harry’s hand with respect, and offered compliments. I forgave him his earlier impertinence. My dear Hal walked through his mother’s rooms in his old uniform, the same he had worn in the famous regiment he had left behind.

We had met often—moments the stern mother could not break—where the instinctive bond between us, unshakable and deep, could speak freely. Though utterly different in tastes, pursuits, and opinions—his life full of sport and fieldwork, mine spent in books and quiet reflection—we shared a sympathy deeper than any woman’s love. My poor Hal confessed as much to me, simply and honestly, when we left wives and women behind and spent a week together at Castlewood.
