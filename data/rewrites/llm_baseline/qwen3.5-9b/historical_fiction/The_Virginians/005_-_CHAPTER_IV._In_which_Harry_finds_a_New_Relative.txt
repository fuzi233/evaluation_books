Kind friends, hospitable neighbors, an ancient name, a vast estate, and a fortune sufficient for comfort—along with a household staff eager to serve, good health, affectionate children, and excellent provisions for food, drink, and learning—should not these constitute the hallmarks of a truly happy life? Madam Esmond Warrington possessed every one of these blessings. She recited them daily in her morning and evening prayers, scrupulous in her devotion, generous to the poor, and seemingly free of malice toward anyone.

One might imagine her enthroned within her principality of Castlewood, surrounded by the adoration of local gentry, the dutiful obedience of her sons, and the frantic scramble of servants to fulfill her slightest whim. The poor whites, grateful for her bounty, accepted her medical advice without question, while lesser nobles eagerly agreed with her opinions and lost games of backgammon to her. With such advantages, surely the "Little Princess Pocahontas," as she was known, ought to be envied. Yet, amidst her dominions, she was hardly content.

Her husband, cut off in early life, was perhaps fortunate to be gone. Had he lived longer, fierce quarrels would likely have ensued, leaving him either a tyrant or a henpecked fool—a fate common enough a century prior. The truth remained: Little Madam Esmond avoided men and women alike, preferring instead to dominate them. Those who obeyed became her best friends; those who resisted faced her relentless fury until submission was forced. While society publicly confessed its own sins on Sundays, none proclaimed this truth with greater resolve than the little lady herself. Though she may have erred as a mortal, she rarely admitted fault to herself or others.

In her youth, her father watched her despotic fits and haughty stubbornness with amused detachment. His humor, a quality she lacked, often baffled and subdued her. But once the Colonel passed away, no one remained capable of commanding her respect. Thus, I am relieved I did not reside in Westmorland County, Virginia, a hundred years ago under her rule. One wonders how happy anyone could possibly be there. Even in Paradise, a serpent existed; if Eve had been perfectly content, would she not have listened?

Madam Esmond had managed Castlewood since childhood. After the Colonel's death, she rigorously supervised the estate, dismissing his English factor to hire a new one, overseeing construction projects, planting crops, appointing overseers, and importing tutors. Despite her love for her father, she rejected many of his maxims. Why should she obey her parents, having done so dutifully all her life? Should not all children follow their elders to ensure longevity? The little queen ruled her domain, and her sons were merely her first subjects. Soon, she dropped the name Warrington, adopting solely the title of Madam Esmond. Her lineage was well-known; she proudly discussed the Marquis's title granted by King James to her ancestors. Although her father's generosity might have allowed him to elevate her half-brother, Lord Castlewood, and his line, she insisted that her sons, belonging to the elder branch, deserved superior treatment. Only Lord Fairfax held precedence over her in the colony. She demanded priority over Ladies of Lieutenant-Governors and Judges, yielding only to the Governor's wife as the sovereign's representative. Historical records detail several fierce battles she waged with colonial dignitaries' wives over matters of etiquette.

As for her husband's family, the Warringtons, they received little favor. Annually, formal letters were sent to them and to her noble kin in Hampshire. However, when a judge's wife, estranged from Madam Esmond, returned to England and encountered Lady Warrington in London, she recounted the Princess's disparaging remarks about her own relatives. Lady Warrington presumably shared this with Lady Castlewood, leading to an immediate cessation of correspondence. To Madam Esmond's shock and rage, the letters went unanswered, prompting her to stop writing as well. Consequently, she fell out with neighbors, relatives, and eventually, her own sons.

A significant rift formed early between the Queen and her crown prince, George, concerning the dismissal of Mr. Dempster, the boys' tutor and the late Colonel's secretary. During the Colonel's lifetime, Madam Esmond struggled to tolerate him, though Mr. Dempster barely endured her. Suspicious of books and fearing corrupt influences, she suspected he was a disguised Jesuit. Forced to flee, he built a cabin in the wilderness to teach and practice medicine among scattered settlers. Master George vowed never to abandon his old tutor and kept his promise. Harry, however, preferred fishing and sports to reading, maintaining a distance from the poor Dominie.

Another dispute arose following the deaths of an aunt and the Colonel, which entitled George Warrington to a legacy of six thousand pounds, held in trust by their mother and a London lawyer. Madam Esmond could not comprehend that she was merely a trustee, not the owner. Furious when the London agent refused to transfer the funds immediately, she screamed, "Is not all I possess my sons'? Would I not tear myself apart to serve them? With six thousand pounds, I could buy Mr. Boulter's estate and slaves, generating a thousand pounds annually and securing a splendid future for Harry!" Neither her young neighbor, Mr. Washington of Mount Vernon, nor the London lawyer could persuade her that the agent acted correctly. She insulted the lawyer as an insolent pettifogger deserving punishment for doubting an Esmond's honor.

Indeed, the Virginian Princess possessed a fiery temper. When George was asked to choose sides, he sided with Mr. Washington and the lawyer, stating he could not help himself. He did not desire the money and wished to give it to his mother if he could. But Madam Esmond cared nothing for reason. Emotion dictated her actions. Seeing a chance to enrich Harry, whose inheritance was meager compared to George's, she raged that London wretches would not assist him and that her own son refused to help. Tears, scorn, veiled insults, prolonged silence, bitter outbursts, and passionate pleas to heaven characterized her state of mind. One might wonder if modern women do not argue similarly; the book of female logic is indeed stained with tears, and justice in their courts often seems inflamed.

This conflict resolved Madam Esmond to save diligently for Harry, fulfilling her maternal duty despite the strain. Construction halted on the fine buildings the Colonel had begun, which included Dutch bricks shipped from New York and imported mantelpieces, cornices, glass, carpets, and upholstery from England. Books ceased purchase, and wine shipments stopped. She lamented the cost of a carriage brought from England, riding to church in it with a heavy heart, whispering to the boys beside her, "Harry, Harry! I wish I had saved that money for thee, my poor portionless child—three hundred and eighty guineas for Messieurs Hatchett!"

"You will provide plenty while you live, and George will provide plenty when you die," Harry replied cheerfully.

"Not unless he changes his nature, my dear," the lady retorted sharply, casting a grim look at her elder son. "Unless Heaven softens his heart and teaches him charity, for which I pray day and night. Does Mountain know? Do you not, Mountain?"

Mrs. Mountain, the widow of Ensign Mountain and Madam Esmond's companion and manager, occupied the fourth seat in the family coach. "Humph!" she remarked. "I know you disturb yourself constantly worrying about this legacy. I see no real need."

"Oh, no need!" cried the widow, rustling her silks. "Of course I have no need to worry, for my eldest is a disobedient son and unkind brother—he has an estate. My poor Harry, bless him, deserves only a mess of pottage."

George looked despairingly at his mother until his eyes filled with tears. "I wish you would bless me too, O my mother!" he exclaimed, bursting into weeping.

Instantly, Harry wrapped his arms around his brother's neck, kissing him repeatedly. "Never mind, George. I know whether you are a good brother or not. Ignore what she says; she doesn't mean it."

"I do mean it, child!" the mother shrieked. "Would to Heaven—"

"HOLD YOUR TONGUE, I SAY!" roared Harry. "It is shameful to speak thus to him, ma'am."

"And so it is, Harry," agreed Mrs. Mountain, shaking his hand. "You have never spoken truer words in your life."

"Mrs. Mountain, do you dare set my children against me?" the widow cried. "From this very day—"

"Turn me and my child into the street?" Mrs. Mountain challenged calmly. "Do. That would be a fitting revenge for the English lawyer withholding the boy's money. Find another companion willing to tell you black is white and flatter you. It is not my way, madam. When shall I leave? I shall not stay long packing. I brought little to Castlewood House and shall take little away."

"Hush! The bells are ringing for church, Mountain. Let us try to compose ourselves," the widow said softly, looking with profound affection—at one, perhaps both—of her children. George lowered his head, while Harry moved closer during the sermon, resting his arm protectively around his brother's neck.
