The sky above the Piazza del Duomo was a dull, slate grey, yet the square itself blazed with the artificial brilliance of a festival. Blue drapery hung heavy in the air, and constellations of yellow lilies and coats of arms dotted the canopy. Banners unfurled at the angles of the Baptistery, though the marble steps of the Duomo remained bare, trodden by the multitude. It was the hour of the Advent sermons, a solemn duty that could not be dispensed with even as the streets wore their holiday colors.

Yet, not all feet moved with the purpose of worship. High and low mingled in a brisk, restless tide, driven by errands rather than piety. Groups clustered in conversation, some eager to miss the sermon, others intent on ignoring it entirely. But the faces of these apparent loungers betrayed no leisurely contentment. Instead, they wore the sharp tension of men waiting for a storm. They listened with keen interest to a single speaker, yet constantly scanned the periphery, their eyes darting at every new arrival.

At the corner where the Piazza's artificial light faded into the sombre grey of the Via de' Cerretani, a distinct cluster of workers stood out against the festive backdrop. Their bare, brawny arms and soiled garments marked them as men of labor, and almost all carried tools that could easily serve as weapons. They gathered around Ser Cioni, a notary whose biting words had once drawn contempt in the Mercato but now commanded a complacent humor from a populace that felt the wind shifting in their favor.

"Never talk to me of bloodthirsty Swiss or fierce French infantry," Ser Cioni declared, his voice incisive. "They might as well be in mountain passes as in our streets. Peasants have destroyed the finest armies of our condottieri when they trapped them between precipices. Tell me, Florentines, need we fear any army in our own streets?"

"That is true, Ser Cioni," replied a dyer, his hands stained with crimson that mimicked blood, a small hatchet tucked into his belt. "Those French cavaliers who strutted in their doublets recently saw a sample of our dinner. I was carrying my cloth near Ognissanti when I saw my masters looking round as if the houses of the Vespucci and the Agli were poor lodging. They eyed us Florentines like top-knotted cocks, pitying our inability to strut. 'Yes, my fine Galli,' I said, 'stick out your stomachs; I have a meat-axe in my belt that will go inside you all the easier.' Then the great bell lowed, and I knew something had happened. I threw down my cloth, grabbed my axe, and ran after them toward the Vigna Nuova. 'What is it, Guccio?' I asked. 'I think it's the Medici coming back,' he said. *Bembè!* I expected so! We reared a barricade, trapping them, and a swarm of our Ciompi arrived, one wielding a great scythe that mowed off a cavalier's feather. It is true! The girls peppered stones to frighten them. Alas, Piero de' Medici never returned; it was a pity, for we would have left him neither legs nor wings."

"Well spoken, Oddo," agreed a young butcher, knife at his belt. "Piero will be a long while before he dares return; he looked as frightened as a hunted chicken when we hustled him in the piazza. He is a coward, or he would have made a better stand with his horsemen. But we shall swallow no Medici again, whatever the French King demands."

"But I dislike those French cannons they speak of," interjected Goro, his face swollen with two years of grievances. "San Giovanni defend us! If Messer Domeneddio means so well by us as your Frate claims, Ser Cioni, why not send the French to Naples instead?"

"Ay, Goro," the dyer nodded. "That is a question worth asking. Why not send them to Naples by Bologna? Or to Arezzo? We would not have minded their going to Arezzo."

"Fools!" Ser Cioni began, attempting to rally them to the cause of Florence's glory, but his words were drowned by a sudden cry: "Look there!"

All heads turned toward the Via de' Cerretani. Advancing was a brilliant party: Lorenzo Tornabuoni, distinguished and imposing; a French nobleman in elaborate white linen and gorgeous embroidery; two other Florentines of high birth in handsome dresses; and, to the left of the Frenchman, a figure that eclipsed all brocade. He wore only black, for he was in mourning, though a red mantle awaited him for the procession as Latin Secretary to the Ten. Tito Melema had become indispensable in his dealings with the French guests, his familiarity with Southern Italy and fluency in the French tongue earning him favor. He had visited the French camp at Signa often, and now the lustre of good fortune shone upon him. He smiled, listened, and explained with his usual graceful ease, though a keen observer might have noted a subtle change—the final departure of moral youthfulness, the distinct self-conscious adoption of a role in life. His face remained soft, his eyes pellucid, yet something indefinable had vanished, like the light in the morning twilight.

The Frenchman gathered instructions on ceremony before riding back to Signa, pausing now for a final survey of the Piazza del Duomo, where the royal procession would halt for religious rites. The party attracted every eye, yet the gaze was not entirely cordial. Whispers flew regarding the Frenchman's hoof-shaped shoes—a delicate flattery of royal superfluity—while snarls against the "Mediceans" were carefully suppressed. Lorenzo Tornabuoni possessed the power to disguise annoyance, and Tito, with his natural disposition to overcome ill-will through good humor, felt the unimpassioned detachment of an alien toward the native's deepest passions.

Pausing where they could view the Duomo obliquely, the group noticed the festoons over the central doorway. Tornabuoni beckoned to Piero di Cosimo, who lounged before Nello's shop. An animated discussion ensued, becoming highly amusing as the Frenchman marveled at Piero's pungent statements, which Tito translated literally. Even the snarling onlookers grew curious, their faces wearing expressions of half-smiling humiliation as they realized they were excluded from the joke. It was a delightful moment for Tito; he was the only one capable of making such an amusing interpreter, and he basked in the approving glances of the crowd, unaware of the shifting tides beneath the surface.

While the rainbow light bathed the laughing group in the Piazza, the grey light of the unadorned streets told a darker tale. Here, the French dress and hoofed shoes were conspicuous targets for a growing number of non-admiring Florentines. In the van of the crowd walked three men in scanty clothing, their hands bound by cords, ropes fastened around their necks and bodies so that a tug could throttle them. Their captors were French soldiers, stimulating the prisoners to beg with broken Italian phrases and strokes from the knotted ends of their ropes.

Two of the prisoners obeyed, holding out their bound hands to every Florentine they met, pleading in piteous tones: "For the love of God and the Holy Madonna, give us something towards our ransom! We are Tuscans: we were made prisoners in Lunigiana."

But the third man remained obstinately silent under the lashes of the knotted cord. He was vastly different from his companions. While they were young and hardy, looking like sturdy mendicants stripped by avarice, this man had passed the boundary of old age, perhaps fifty or sixty. His beard, grown long in neglect, and the thick, straight hair framing his baldness were nearly white. His thickset figure remained firm and upright despite emaciation, expressing a strange energy in spite of his years. This was carried in his dark eyes and strong dark eyebrows, which held a strangely isolated intensity against his yellow, bloodless, deep-wrinkled face and lank grey hairs. Yet, there was a fitfulness in his eyes that contradicted his flashes of energy; after looking round with quick fierceness at windows and faces, they fell again with a lost, wandering look. His lips were motionless, his hands held resolutely down. He would not beg.

The sight provoked growing exasperation among the Florentines. Initially, many gave money, some in automatic response to the appeal of God, others in unquestioning awe of the French soldiery, a fear that the soldiers themselves relied upon as a guarantee of immunity. But as the group moved deeper into the city, this compliance vanished. The crowd swelled into a troop of men and boys, chanting a chorus of intelligible jeers. The soldiers, inclined to use their weapons, were checked by the necessity of holding their prisoners securely, hurrying now in hopes of finding shelter in a hostelry.

"French dogs!"
"Bullock-feet!"
"Snatch their pikes from them!"
"Cut the cords and make them run! They'll run as fast as geese—don't you see they're web-footed?"

These cries, vaguely understood as threats, seemed to invite action rather than provoke it.

"Santiddio! Here's a sight!" cried the dyer, recognizing the tumult. "And the fools do nothing but hoot. Come along!" He snatched his axe from his belt and ran to join the crowd, followed by the butcher and his companions, save for Goro, who hastily retreated up a narrow passage.

The sight of the dyer, advancing with blood-red arms and an uplifted axe, stirred the crowd. He did nothing more than thrust himself among his fellow citizens, flourishing his blade, serving as a stirring symbol of street-fighting, like the waving of a gonfalon. The first sign that fire was ready to burst forth was as rapid as a leaping tongue of flame: it was the act of the conjuror's impish lad, Lollo. Dancing and jeering before the ingenuous boys who made up the majority of the crowd, Lollo possessed no great compassion for the prisoners, but he was conscious of an excellent knife. To jump forward, cut a rope, and leap back before the soldier could strike seemed an amusing and dexterous piece of mischief. As the people jostled more vigorously, Lollo felt his moment had come. He was close to the eldest prisoner. In an instant, he severed the cord.

"Run, old one!" he piped into the prisoner's ear as the cord split, then set the example of running as if aided by wings, like a scared fowl.

The prisoner's senses were not too slow to seize the opportunity. The idea of escape had been a constant presence, and the temper of the crowd had fueled his hope. He ran immediately, but his speed alone would have sufficed only if the Florentines had not instantly rushed between him and his captor. He fled into the Piazza, but quickly heard the tramp of feet behind him. The other two prisoners had been released, and the soldiers struggled and fought their way after them in a tardigrade fashion, impeded by their hoof-shaped shoes and attacked, though not resolutely, by the people. One of the younger prisoners turned up the Borgo di San Lorenzo, diverting some of the hubbub, but the main struggle remained toward the Piazza, where all eyes turned with alarmed curiosity. The cause could not be precisely guessed, for the French dress was screened by the impeding crowd.

"An escape of prisoners!" exclaimed Lorenzo Tornabuoni as he and his party turned just against the steps of the Duomo, seeing a prisoner rush past them. "The people are not content with having emptied the Bargello the other day. If there is no other authority in sight, they must fall on the sbirri to secure freedom for thieves. Ah! There is a French soldier: that is more serious."

The soldier struggled along the north side of the Piazza, but his prey had taken the other direction. That object was the eldest prisoner, who had wheeled round the Baptistery and was running toward the Duomo, determined to take refuge in that sanctuary rather than trust to his speed. But as he mounted the steps, his foot received a shock; he was precipitated toward the group of signori, whose backs were turned to him, and could recover his balance only by clutching one of them by the arm.

It was Tito Melema who felt that clutch. He turned his head and saw the face of his adoptive father, Baldassarre Calvo, close to his own.

The two men looked at each other, silent as death. Baldassarre, with dark fierceness and a tightening grip of the soiled, worn hands on the velvet-clad arm; Tito, with cheeks and lips all bloodless, fascinated by terror. It seemed a long while to them—it was but a moment.

The first sound Tito heard was the short laugh of Piero di Cosimo, who stood close by and was the only person who could see his face.

"Ha, ha! I know what a ghost should be now."

"This is another escaped prisoner," said Lorenzo Tornabuoni. "Who is he, I wonder?"

"Some madman, surely," said Tito.

He hardly knew how the words had come to his lips; there are moments when our passions speak and decide for us, and we seem to stand by and wonder. They carry an inspiration of crime that accomplishes in an instant the work of long premeditation.

The two men had not taken their eyes off each other, and it seemed to Tito, after he had spoken, that some magical poison had darted from Baldassarre's eyes, rushing through his veins. But the next instant, the grasp on his arm relaxed, and Baldassarre had disappeared within the church.
