It began, as many social catastrophes do, with a request. One morning, Mrs. Bretton entered my chamber with a promptness that brooked no delay, commanding me to open my drawers and exhibit my wardrobe. I complied silently, laying out my garments as she inspected them. "That will suffice," she declared once she had finished her review, "but you must acquire a new gown."

Before I could protest, she departed and returned shortly thereafter, bringing a dressmaker in her wake. The measurements were taken with brisk efficiency. "I intend," my godmother stated, her voice ringing with an unassailable resolve, "to dictate my own taste and follow my own whims regarding this trifling affair."

Two days later, the garment arrived—a resplendent pink dress.

"That is not suitable for me," I protested, the words escaping me with a sudden urgency. The hue, the cut, evoked to my imagination the exotic finery of a Chinese lady of high rank, a fantasy utterly incongruous with my reality.

"We shall determine its suitability," she countered, her decision absolute and unyielding. "Take my word for it: you will wear it tonight."

A sinking feeling settled in my stomach. I suspected I would not, imagining that no earthly force could compel me into such attire. Pink! I knew nothing of pink, nor did I suspect that pink knew me. I had yet to prove myself worthy of it.

Undeterred, Mrs. Bretton decreed that I accompany her and Mr. Graham to a concert that very evening. She explained it was a magnificent event hosted in the grand hall of the principal musical society, featuring the most advanced students of the Conservatoire. Following the performance would be a lottery organized for the benefit of the poor, capped by the presence of the King, Queen, and Prince of Labassecour. Mr. Graham, in forwarding the tickets, had emphasized the necessity of appropriate costume as a tribute to royalty and urged punctual readiness by seven o'clock.

Around six, I was escorted upstairs. With a gentle, inexorable will, I found myself guided, influenced, and quietly overruled, my own counsel unheard. The pink dress, softened by a mantle of black lace, was fastened upon me. I was pronounced *en grande tenue* and bid to inspect myself in the mirror. I approached the glass with a mixture of fear and trembling, and turning away, I did so with even greater trepidation. Seven o'clock chimed; Dr. Bretton had arrived, and we descended. She wore a gown of somber brown velvet. Walking in her shadow, I envied those folds of grave, dark majesty. Mr. Graham awaited us in the doorway.

"I truly hope he does not perceive me as decked out solely to draw attention," I murmured inwardly, plagued by unease.

"Here, Lucy," he said, presenting me with a bouquet of flowers. He paid no further heed to my dress than a kind smile and a satisfied nod, which instantly assuaged my shame and fear of ridicule. Indeed, the gown was constructed with extreme simplicity, devoid of frills or excess ornament; it was merely the light fabric and vivid tint that had alarmed me. Since Mr. Graham discerned nothing absurd in it, my own eyes eventually reconciled themselves to the sight.

I reflected that those who frequent public amusements nightly struggle to comprehend the fresh, gala spirit with which an opera or concert is received by those for whom it is a novelty. I harbored no expectation of profound pleasure, possessing but a vague notion of the proceedings, yet I delighted in the journey. The cozy comfort of the close carriage on a cold, clear night, the joy of departing with companions so cheerful and kind, the sight of stars glinting fitfully through the trees as we rolled along the avenue, the expansive burst of the night sky upon issuing onto the open road, the glowing city gates, the posted guards, the playful pretense of inspection—we submitted to it with amusement—all these particulars possessed, in their novelty, a peculiarly exhilarating charm. Much of this likely stemmed from the atmosphere of friendship surrounding me. Dr. John and his mother were in their finest spirits, debating animatedly throughout the ride, treating me with frank kindness as if I were of their own blood.

Our path traversed some of Villette's finest streets, aglow with lights and teeming with a livelier vitality than at high noon. How brilliantly the shops shone! How joyful and abundant flowed the tide of life along the broad pavements! Then, the thought of the Rue Fossette crossed my mind—the walled garden, the schoolhouse, and the vast, dark classrooms where, as at this very hour, I was wont to wander alone, gazing at the stars through high, blind windows, listening to the distant, monotonous voice of the reader reciting the "lecture pieuse" in the refectory. Soon, I would return to that solitude. This shadow of the future stole across the radiant present with timely sobriety.

Soon, we merged into a stream of carriages moving in unison, and before us blazed the front of a great, illuminated edifice. My knowledge of what lay within was imperfect, for I had never before entered a venue of public entertainment. We alighted beneath a portico bustling with activity and crowds. I do not distinctly recall the intervening moments until I found myself ascending a majestic staircase, wide and carpeted deeply with soft crimson, leading to great doors closed solemnly and panelled in matching crimson cloth.

I scarcely registered the magic by which these doors rolled back—managed by Dr. John—but they yielded, revealing a hall grand, wide, and lofty. Its sweeping circular walls and domed hollow ceiling appeared to me as all dead gold, stained with exquisite art, relieved by cornicing, fluting, and garlands of bright gold or snow-white alabaster, or mingled in wreaths of gilded leaves and spotless lilies. Wherever drapery hung, carpets spread, or cushions placed, the sole color employed was deep crimson. Suspended from the dome flamed a mass that dazzled me—a chandelier, I surmised, though to me it seemed the work of Eastern genii, sparkling with facets, streaming with drops, ablaze with stars, gorgeously tinged with dew-like gems. I almost looked to see if a huge, dark, cloudy hand—that of the Slave of the Lamp—hovered in the lustrous, perfumed atmosphere of the cupola, guarding its wondrous treasure.

We moved forward, unconscious of our destination, until suddenly encountering another party approaching from the opposite direction. I caught a glimpse of the group as it flashed past: a handsome middle-aged lady in dark velvet; a gentleman who might be her son—the finest face, the most elegant figure I had ever beheld; and a third person in a pink dress and black lace mantle. I noted them all, momentarily believing them strangers, receiving an impartial impression of their appearance. But the illusion shattered almost before it formed; the consciousness that I faced a great mirror, filling a compartment between two pillars, dispelled it. The party was our own. Thus, for the first, and perhaps only, time in my life, I enjoyed the "gift" of seeing myself as others see me. Need I dwell on the result? It brought a jar of discord, a pang of regret. It was not flattering, yet, after all, I ought to be thankful; it might have been worse.

Finally, we were seated in places commanding a good general view of that vast, dazzling, yet warm and cheerful hall. It was already filled with a splendid assemblage. I could not say the women were particularly beautiful, but their dresses were perfect. Foreigners, even those ungraceful in private life, seem to possess the art of appearing graceful in public. However blunt and boisterous their domestic movements may be, there exists a slide, a bend, a carriage of the head and arms, a mien of the mouth and eyes, kept nicely in reserve for gala use—always brought out with the *grande toilette*.

Among the guests were fine forms, models of a peculiar style of beauty, never seen in England. A solid, firm-set, sculptural style. These shapes had no angles; a caryatid in marble is almost as flexible. A Phidian goddess is not more perfect in a certain still and stately sort. Their features resembled those given by Dutch painters to their Madonnas: low-country classic features, regular but round, straight but stolid. For their depth of expressionless calm, of passionless peace, a polar snow-field could alone offer a type. Women of this order need no ornament, and they seldom wear any; smooth hair, closely braided, supplies a sufficient contrast to the smoother cheek and brow; the dress cannot be too simple; the rounded arm and perfect neck require neither bracelet nor chain.

With one of these beauties, I once had the honor and rapture of perfect acquaintance. The inert force of the deep, settled love she bore herself was wonderful; it could only be surpassed by her proud impotency to care for any other living thing. Of blood, her cool veins conducted no flow; placid lymph filled and almost obstructed her arteries.

Such a Juno sat full in our view—a sort of mark for all eyes, quite conscious thereof, yet proof to the magnetic influence of gaze or glance. Cold, rounded, blonde, and beautiful as the white column, capitalled with gilding, which rose at her side.
