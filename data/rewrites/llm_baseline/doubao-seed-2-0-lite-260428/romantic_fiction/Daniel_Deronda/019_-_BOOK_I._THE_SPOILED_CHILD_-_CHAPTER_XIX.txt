*“I pity the man who can travel from Dan to Beersheba, and say, ‘’Tis all barren’: and so it is: and so is all the world to him who will not cultivate the fruits it offers.”*
—STERNE, *Sentimental Journey*

To call Daniel Deronda a flighty, whimsical romantic would be to misread him entirely. Beneath his calm, carefully restrained exterior, however, burned a quiet, steady fervor that let him pluck threads of poetry and quiet magic out of the most unremarkable ordinary moments. Romance and wonder never faded from the world, he thought—they only vanished for the cold, sluggish souls who would write off any age as dull, who dismiss tenderness as a foolish, mistaken fancy. They sit easy beside a microscope on a laboratory table, they hum in the clatter of a railway carriage: what chases them away is the empty, unfeeling vacuum in the chest of people who pass through the world never stopping to feel awe, never reaching for the soft thrum of kinship that ties a person to the stranger down the street, to the stars above the farthest firmament, to the faint, warm memory of their mother’s arms holding them when they were small.

For Deronda, the afternoon he found Mirah lingering on the river’s edge was as heart-stirring, as full of high stakes, as any mythic quest Orestes or Rinaldo ever embarked on. He sat up half the night replaying every second of the encounter, the details growing sharper, not dimmer, each time he revisited them: the damp hem of her dress, the way her fingers twisted in the frayed edge of her shawl, the quiet catch in her voice when she spoke of the family she’d lost. When he picked up a book to try to quiet the loud, bright rush of memory crowding his head, the printed words blurred to no more than a thin, transparent net—he could still see and hear every moment of those two hours as clearly as if they were unfolding right in front of his eyes. He saw not just the events that had actually happened, but all the endless possibilities of what might have been, what still could be, fed warm and beating with the blood of his own passionate, tangled hope and fear.

Something deep in his own hidden experience made Mirah’s search for her mother and brother cling to his imagination with a peculiar, unshakable grip. His first, unthinking impulse was to help her search: if two lost people were somewhere in the sprawling, crowded expanse of London, there were ways to track them down as precise and meticulous as a careful scientific experiment, if you set the right tools and the right care to the work. But the tangled, conflicting feelings that came with his own unspoken secret soon seeped into every part of his anxiety on Mirah’s behalf.

The quiet, burning desire to find his own mother, or to learn any truth of her, was always tangled up in sharp, cold dread. And when he let himself imagine what Mirah might face when she found the family she’d been separated from as a small child, it struck him fast that that long-awaited reunion could easily turn out to be a catastrophe. She had told him in the boat that her mother and brother were good—but that goodness might only have existed in the soft, unworldly innocence of her childhood memory, in the aching yearning that had clung to them for a dozen years. And ten or twelve years apart was more than enough time for even the kindest people to warp and harden beyond recognition.

He had always been quick to side with the targets of unfair prejudice, to defend anyone the world had written off as worthless. But his sympathy had never before been drawn to real, living Jewish people in any personal, practical way, and the facts he had absorbed about them—whether they walked the streets in fine, conspicuous clothing or lurked in the shadowed alleyways off the main roads—were mostly the ugly, unkind sorts of details that filled him with distaste. He had long assumed that any educated, accomplished Jewish people he knew had shed their faith entirely, eager to erase every trace of their heritage to merge quietly into the population of the country they lived in. Any slur flung at a Jew simply for being Jewish would have roused all his fiery, defensive sympathy for the inherited griefs of that marginalized people—but blind, indiscriminate scorn for an entire race often lands on a person who has earned every bit of that disdain on his own, who deserves to be called out as a rascally son of Adam no matter what his background. Deronda could not avoid knowing the ugly, unfair anecdotes floating through London about supposed Jewish traits and occupations, but even as one of the loudest voices protesting the way people severed the past from the present to justify their cruelty, he had never bothered to dig deeper for nuance, only settling for the vague, lazy conclusion that the Jewish people carried the same mix of virtues and flaws as any long-oppressed group.

But now that Mirah’s longing forced his mind to turn over the details, very unpleasant images pressed unbidden to the front of his mind: what if they tracked down this middle-aged Jewish mother and her son, and they were nothing like the soft, loving people Mirah remembered? Of course, Mirah’s own exquisite gentleness and charm made a strong case that her immediate family might carry the same grace—but he could not be sure, not yet. He thought he might glean more hints of her history, slowly, through Mrs. Meyrick, from Mirah’s own lips. Her soft voice, her gentle accent, her whole presence wrapped in a sweet, untainted purity like a consecrated garment—all of it made him shrink back, both in his imagination and in reality, from ever tying her to anything hateful, anything that could sully that goodness in her.

His thoughts did not move in the fine, polished phrases we use to soften and cloud unpleasant facts. They raced forward in quick, vivid images of what might come: he saw himself led by some official guide down a dingy, grim side street, stepping through a dim, shadowed doorway to find a sharp, hawk-eyed woman with unkempt hair and dirty hands, haggling over a hungry young girl’s last scrap of fine, well-loved finery. Or in a slightly less squalid, more respectable neighborhood, he found himself breathing the stale, thick air of a room full of a brash, overly familiar young Jewish man, chattering away about his supposed familiarity with the tastes of wealthy gentlemen, willing to agree to any shady transaction they might propose.

It was no insult, no lazy stereotype on his part—Deronda was not prone to spontaneous unkindness, or to the cheap wit that tried to reduce all Jewish people to nothing more than penny adverts for pawn shops. He was simply governed by a sharp, unshakable dread in that moment, and if Mirah’s parents had been Christian, the only difference would have been that his forebodings would have been fed by a wider range of personal experience. It was a long-held habit of his mind to tie that sharp, cold dread to unknown parentage, and in this case, just as in his own unspoken secret, there was more than enough reason for that connection to feel justified.

But first, what was he to do with Mirah? She needed shelter, full and complete protection, and every chivalrous instinct in his chest rose up to insist that the sooner he could draw the care and interest of other trusted people around her, not just his own, the better he could honor the claim she had on his kindness. He had no right to provide for her entirely on his own, even if he had the means to do so; the very depth of the impression she had left on him made him desperate for her to know she was completely independent of him, no strings attached. Vague, half-formed visions of a future he tried to dismiss as silly and impossible still lingered, leaving him with a sharp, unnameable anxiety stronger than any logical reason he could name: he wanted everyone who saw his daily actions closely to know the full, true story of his connection to Mirah from the very first day. He had grown to hate secrets about the most precious ties and obligations in his life—hated them all the more because a tangled, heavy web of unspoken loyalties had long stopped him from breaking the one secret that hung over his own life. Deronda had sworn a private vow to himself that, since not every disgrace that befalls a mortal is one they have brought on their own head, he would never be the one to turn an innocent truth into a source of shame for another person. He was not free of the quiet terror that he might break that vow someday, falling prey to the lazy, apologetic philosophy that twists the world around until nothing seems better or more noble than one’s own small, selfish conduct.

For one long moment, he resolved to tell the whole story of his strange, fateful encounter to Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger over their breakfast the next morning. But the thought that some new, unexpected detail might reveal itself when he next visited Mrs. Meyrick’s house held him back, and he finally drifted off to sleep on the quiet resolution to wait until he had made that visit, and heard more of Mirah’s own story first.
