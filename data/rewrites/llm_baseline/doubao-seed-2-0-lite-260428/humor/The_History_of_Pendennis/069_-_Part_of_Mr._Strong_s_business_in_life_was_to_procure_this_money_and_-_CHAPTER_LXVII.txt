Back in his home shire, Arthur Pendennis was deep in his self-serving little parliamentary schemes, schmoozing farmers, practicing his campaign smile, and daydreaming of the fancy desk he’d have in the House of Commons, when a messenger rode in with news: Lady Rockminster and her entire chaotic household had touched down at nearby Baymouth, and they’d brought Laura with them.

Pen immediately broke out in a cold sweat. Laura, his late mother’s favorite, the girl who could spot a half-truth faster than a farm dog spots a dropped sausage, was the one person on the entire planet he actually cared about impressing. She was his mom’s last bequest to him, after all—he was supposed to be her patron, her protector, the cool cousin she could brag about. How the hell was he supposed to explain that he was angling for a seat in Parliament and an engagement to the wealthy, performative Blanche Amory, just to chase a pile of cash and a silly little title of prestige? He was half-convinced both his secret scheme and Blanche herself would melt under Laura’s calm, unimpressed stare, like cheap wax held over a candle. So he sat down to write her a letter, full of flowery protestations of love, silly jokes, and playful snark, and halfway through scribbling it he had to pause and go, *Oh no. I’m acting like a total rogue and a hypocrite. She’s gonna see right through this.*

Why was a simple country girl making this polished, worldly, well-educated gentleman quake in his boots like a truant schoolboy staring down the headmaster? Because all his fancy social tricks, his snarky comebacks, all his clever diplomacy, were useless against her pure, unvarnished honesty. He couldn’t bring himself to tell that good, honest soul the full truth of what a sellout he was being. The whole ride from Clavering to Baymouth, he felt exactly like a kid who forgot to study for his arithmetic test and is about to walk into the classroom—because face it, truth is the scariest headmaster of all, he always has the answer key, and he never cuts you slack.

Laura, for her part, had thrived way more than anyone expected in the year she’d spent as Lady Rockminster’s companion. Most girls raised in Helen Pendennis’s smothering, all-consuming love would’ve crumpled the second they were tossed out into big, indifferent society. Helen thought the whole world revolved around her two kids, fussed over Laura if she so much as had a mild headache like no human being had ever had a sore head before, and hovered over her every waking second—then she was gone, and Laura was left alone in a household full of strangers. There were snitty servants sometimes, rude distant relatives of Lady Rockminster’s who made her feel like an intruder at family dinners, and days where no one even noticed she was sad. But instead of moping, she just thought, *Hey, there are thousands of governesses and paid companions way worse off than me. My lot’s infinitely better than most!* and smiled her way through every awkward moment.

Turns out that uncomplicated, warm, unassuming cheer is a superpower. Within a year, literally everyone in Lady Rockminster’s household was wrapped around her finger. The old lady’s personal maid, who’d survived 40 years of daily yelling, clawing, and snark from her employer, and had run off 15 previous companions, fought a battle with Laura’s own maid Martha over who got to nurse Laura through a fever in Paris, when Lady Rockminster herself wouldn’t go near the sickbed for fear of catching it. The fancy chef Grandjean nearly poisoned her with 17 different rich delicacies the second she started recovering, and cried happy tears when she ate her first plain slice of chicken. The Swiss butler sang her praises in every mangled European language he knew, the coachman fought other families’ drivers for the best parking spots to give her a smooth ride, the 12-year-old page boy sobbed when he heard she was ill, and the two giant, stoic footmen Calverley and Coldstream—men who would’ve watched the entire house burn down without even blinking to preserve their dignity—got the page boy absolutely sloshed at a local wine shop to celebrate her getting better. Even Lady Diana Pynsent, who’d hated Laura on sight, started gushing to everyone that Lady Rockminster had hit the companion jackpot. None of this was flattery or clever scheming, mind you—Laura was just nice, and that was enough.

Pen rolled up to the house half-expecting to find Laura tucked away in a back closet, treated like a lowly skivvy, but before he could even ring the bell she came thundering down the stairs and threw her arms around him right in front of Calverley and Coldstream. Those two didn’t so much as twitch. If Laura had whipped out a pair of scissors and snipped Pen’s head off mid-hug, they’d have calmly fetched a dustpan and acted like nothing out of the ordinary had happened.

Pen stared at her and nearly dropped his hat. When did his quiet little cousin turn into this glowing, rosy-cheeked, impossibly pretty girl? Her eyes were bright, she was clearly thriving, and when she smiled at him he felt so guilty and soft he could barely speak. “You’re way too good to me, Laura—sister,” he mumbled. “I don’t deserve this.”

“Mamma left you to me, remember?” she said, brushing a stray hair off his forehead with a quick kiss. “We had a pact before we parted, remember? You come find me when you’re in trouble, or when you’re wildly happy. Which is it right now? Are you giddy about running for Parliament? I can’t wait to watch you panic through your first speech!”

Pen blurted, “Wait—you already know about the election plan?”

“Know it? The entire county knows it!” she laughed. “Lady Rockminster’s doctor was chattering about it at breakfast this morning. The Chatteris paper’s gonna print it tomorrow, I bet. Everyone’s saying Sir Francis Clavering is gonna step down just to hand you the seat. And that you’re betrothed to the beautiful young Miss Blanche Amory—”

“Even that?” Pen yelped.

“Even that!” Laura said, grinning. “Tout se sait, as the French say. Blanche sent me the sweetest possible letter, she rants about how clever and wonderful you are nonstop. When’s the wedding? Can I come live with you two afterwards? I’m a fantastic housekeeper now, I even took cooking lessons from Grandjean in Paris!”

“Everything I have is yours, Laura,” Pen said. But for some stupid reason, he felt weirdly stabby jealous at the thought of some hypothetical random guy who might one day come along and marry Laura, and take her away from them. He was half-convinced she’d be mad about his plans for Blanche, but she was just happy he was happy, and that made him unreasonably peeved for no reason he could name.

Laura rattled on about how she’d take singing lessons with the money he’d sent her, learn to dance, be the resident aunt to his future kids, and did a perfect, over-the-top Parisian curtsey for him right as Lady Rockminster stomped into the room. She gave Pen exactly one single finger to shake, and he bowed so clumsily he nearly toppled over.

“So you’re getting married and running for Parliament, you little scamp,” the old lady barked before he could even say hello. “I wanted Sir Francis to give that seat to my grandson! Why the hell didn’t he give it to my grandson? And I hope Miss Amory comes with a mountain of money, because I wouldn’t touch that silly little affected thing with a ten-foot pole without a huge dowry.”

Pen winced, “Sir Francis is just tired of Parliament, that’s all. The rest of the story’s still very much up in the air.”

“I still can’t wrap my head around why you’d pick that lumpy little faker over our Laura,” the old lady went on, unimpressed. “You young men are all cowards and money-grubbers! Back in my day we ran off with the people we liked and had silly romantic elopements, now all of you only marry for cash. I asked the three embassy attaches in Paris why none of them proposed to Laura, and they all laughed and said she didn’t have enough money! Disgusting. Where’s your big friend Warrington, the grumpy one? Why doesn’t he marry her instead?”

Pen got huffy. “Did you even bother to ask Laura if she wanted to marry some random attaché you picked out for her?”

“Laura’s got barely any money, she needs to get married soon, someone’s gotta find her a match!” the dowager huffed back, very pleased with her own logic. “Laura, dear, I was just telling your cousin all the young men today are utterly romance-less selfish losers, and he’s exactly as bad as the rest.”

Laura popped back in the room, grinning, and grabbed Pen’s hand. “Quit trying to marry me off to random bachelors, grandma. Arthur’s gonna have Blanche, and I’m gonna come live with them, and I’ll make sure every bachelor who shows up to their dinner party gets grilled about why he hasn’t married me yet. Fair’s fair.”

Pen’s conscience heaved a huge sigh of relief—Laura hadn’t yelled at him, hadn’t called him out for being a sellout, she was just happy to see him. Lady Rockminster insisted he come over for dinner every single night he didn’t have other plans, pointing out that her cook was one of the best in the country, and being around Laura would knock all that selfish, self-obsessed edge off him. He got the cheap wine on normal nights, and a tiny glass of champagne only if he was very amusing and didn’t disturb her post-dinner nap.

Pen spent almost every free minute he had down at Baymouth, walking and riding with Laura, talking for hours. Now that he was very publicly attached to Blanche, all the awkward unspoken tension between them was gone, and they fell right back into being the best of friends. They visited their mom Helen’s grave in the Clavering churchyard one quiet afternoon, rode past the old Fairoaks cottage where they’d grown up, and Pen hopped off his horse to pluck a sprig of honeysuckle from the old porch for Laura. As they trotted back across the downs, Pen got all mopey and dramatic, complaining that life was a stupid joke, that men were fools who traded real happiness for useless little bits of fame and pocket change. “Everyone’s selfish,” he grumbled. “The whole world’s selfish. The only exceptions are people like you, who shine so bright you make everyone else look extra grubby.”

Laura tucked the honeysuckle into her dress and smiled. “Stop being a drama queen, Arthur. You weren’t being selfish when you climbed off your horse to get me that flower. If you think all this parliamentary fame is so vain and stupid, why chase it? Just bring Blanche home to Fairoaks, write nice kind books, do good for the people who live around you. We could be happy here, no fancy speeches required.”
