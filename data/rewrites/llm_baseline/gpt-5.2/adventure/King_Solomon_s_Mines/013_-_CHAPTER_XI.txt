For a long while—two hours, by my reckoning—we sat as men sit after a feast has turned to poison in the belly: not hungry, not satisfied, only sick with the memory of what we had witnessed. Outside, the kraal breathed in its own fashion—an occasional cough of a sleeping sentry, the far-off rasp of insects, the soft, patient scuff of bare feet in dust that had known too much blood. Even that small, natural music seemed muffled, as though the night itself had put a hand over its mouth.

At length, when the darkness had begun to thin towards dawn, we heard footsteps—measured, unhurried, as if the owner of them wished the very ground to understand he was not sneaking. A challenge rose from the gate; an answer came back, low enough that we could not catch the words, but clear enough in confidence that the sentry let the party through. A moment later Infadoos entered the hut, and behind him came six chiefs, tall and heavy with dignity, their ornaments catching the lamplight like small, calm sparks.

“My lords,” Infadoos said, “I have come according to my word. My lords, and Ignosi, rightful king of the Kukuanas, I have brought these men, great among us. Each commands three thousand soldiers who live to do his bidding under the king’s. I have told them what my eyes have seen and my ears have heard. Now let them behold the sacred snake about thee, and hear thy story, Ignosi, that they may say whether they will make cause with thee against Twala.”

Ignosi answered as he had answered before—without flourish, with the steady simplicity of a man who carries his truth on his skin. He loosened his girdle and showed them the tattooed coil, the old sign, the snake that marked him for what he claimed to be. One by one the chiefs came near. In the dim oil-lamp’s wavering glow they bent, squinting and frowning, fingertips hovering as if touch itself might be sacrilege. Not one spoke; each merely passed on, grave as judges tasting a broth they distrust.

Ignosi drew up his garment again and told his tale once more—of birth and banishment, of blood and stolen right, of the king who ruled by fear and the witch-doctress who ruled by something colder than fear. His voice did not shake. It had the tone of a man reciting the names of seed-grains handed down, each syllable part of inheritance, not ornament.

When he had done, Infadoos stepped forward, his hands spread like a man offering bread.

“Ye have heard, chiefs. What say ye? Will ye stand by this man and help him to his father’s throne, or will ye not? The land cries out against Twala, and the blood of the people flows like water in spring. Ye have seen to-night. Two other chiefs there were with whom I meant to speak—and where are they now? The hyænas howl over their corpses. Soon shall ye be as they are if ye strike not. Choose then, my brothers.”

The eldest of the six—a short, thick-set warrior with white hair and a face like toughened hide—stepped out. His voice had the rasp of age, yet it carried.

“Thy words are true, Infadoos; the land cries out. My own brother is among those dead to-night. But this is a great matter and hard to believe. How know we that we lift our spears not for a thief and a liar? None can see the end of this road. Blood will flow in rivers before the deed is done. Many will still cleave to the king, for men worship the sun that shines, not the one that has not yet risen. These white men from the Stars—their magic is great, and Ignosi is under the cover of their wing. If he be indeed rightful king, let them give us a sign, and let the people have a sign, that all may see. So shall men cleave to us, knowing of a truth the white man’s magic is with them.”

“Ye have the sign of the snake,” I said.

“My lord, it is not enough. The snake may have been placed there since the man’s childhood. Show us a sign and it will suffice. But we will not move without a sign.”

The others murmured assent—firm, cautious, the sound of men who have learned what promises cost. I turned to Sir Henry and Good and explained in a whisper. Good’s eyes, which had been dull with fatigue, suddenly sharpened as though he had smelt something on the wind.

“Give us a moment,” he said quickly. “Ask them to step outside.”

I did so, and the chiefs withdrew with Infadoos. The instant the doorway-hide fell back into place, Good crossed to his little medicine-box, unlocked it, and drew out his notebook. Between its fly-leaves was an almanack, thin and much-thumbed.

“Now look here,” he said, jabbing at the page with a forefinger. “Isn’t tomorrow the 4th of June?”

We had marked the days carefully enough in our misery, and could answer that it was.

“Good. Then we’ve got it: ‘4 June, total eclipse of the moon commences at 8.15 Greenwich time, visible in Teneriffe—South Africa, &c.’ There’s your sign. Tell them we will darken the moon tomorrow night.”

It was one of those ideas that arrive like a match struck in a draught—sudden light, and with it the smell of danger. A sign such as they wanted, a wonder they could see, and yet a wonder that cost us nothing but nerve—if, and only if, the heavens kept to the timetable of Good’s little book.

The weak spot lay plain as a bone in broth: if the almanack was wrong, if cloud covered the sky, if our count of days had slipped—then our “magic” would curdle in the mouths of the Kukuanas, and with it Ignosi’s hope, and our own lives.

“Suppose it’s wrong,” Sir Henry said, watching Good work figures on a blank page as though summoning the moon by arithmetic.

“I see no reason to suppose anything of the sort,” Good answered briskly. “Eclipses always come up to time—that’s my experience. And it says visible in South Africa. I’ve done the reckonings as well as I can without knowing our exact position. The eclipse should begin here about ten tomorrow night and last until half-past twelve. For an hour and a half there should be almost total darkness.”

“Well,” Sir Henry said, “we’d better risk it.”

I agreed, though doubt gnawed at me like hunger. The sky is the last place a man wants to stake his reputation; it has a way of reminding you whose rules you do not write. Still, there was no other course that offered such a clean, public marvel. I sent Umbopa—Ignosi—to call the chiefs back.

They returned, solemn as before. Their presence filled the hut with a different air, like a kitchen when elders arrive to judge the pot—silent, expectant, nothing wasted.

“Great men of the Kukuanas,” I said, “and thou, Infadoos, listen. We love not to show our powers, for to do so is to interfere with the course of nature and to plunge the world into fear and confusion. But since this matter is great, and as we are angered against the king because of the slaughter we have seen, and because of the act of the Isanusi Gagool who would have put our friend Ignosi to death, we will break a rule and give such a sign as all men may see.”

I led them to the doorway and pointed to the moon, low and red as a coal banked in ash.

“What see ye there?”

“We see the sinking moon,” said the spokesman.

“It is so. Now tell me: can any mortal man put out that moon before her hour of setting, and bring the curtain of black night down upon the land?”

The chief laughed softly, the way an elder laughs at a child who asks if porridge can be boiled in cold water. “No, my lord. The moon is stronger than man who looks on her, nor can she vary in her courses.”

“Ye say so. Yet I tell you that tomorrow night, about two hours before midnight, we will cause the moon to be eaten up for a space of an hour and half an hour. Deep darkness shall cover the earth, and it shall be a sign that Ignosi is indeed king of the Kukuanas. If we do this thing, will ye be satisfied?”

“Yea, my lords,” the old chief answered, smiling now, and the smile ran along the faces of the others like a flame touching dry grass. “If ye do this thing, we will be satisfied indeed.”

“It shall be done,” I said. “We three—Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn—have said it, and it shall be done. Dost thou hear, Infadoos?”

“I hear, my lord,” Infadoos replied, awe mixed with practical urgency. “But it is a wonderful thing ye promise—to put out the moon, the mother of the world, when she is full.”

“Yet shall we do it.”

“It is well. To-day, two hours after sunset, Twala will send for my lords to witness the girls dance. One hour after the dance begins the girl whom Twala thinks the fairest shall be killed by Scragga, the king’s son, as a sacrifice to the Silent Ones who sit and keep watch by the mountains yonder.” He pointed towards the three strange peaks where Solomon’s road was said to end, the stone watchers that seemed to drink moonlight without returning any. “Then let my lords darken the moon and save the maiden’s life, and the people will believe indeed.”

“Ay,” said the old chief, still smiling, “the people will believe indeed.”

“Two miles from Loo,” Infadoos went on, “there is a hill curved like a new moon, a stronghold, where my regiment and three other regiments which these chiefs command are stationed. This morning we will make a plan whereby two or three other regiments may be moved there also. Then, if in truth my lords can darken the moon, in the darkness I will take my lords by the hand and lead them out of Loo to that place, where ye shall be safe. Thence we can make war upon Twala.”

“It is good,” I said. “Now leave us to sleep awhile and to make ready our magic.”

Infadoos saluted and departed with the chiefs, their footfalls sliding back into the night until the kraal seemed empty again, though we knew it was packed with lives and listening.

When they were gone, Ignosi looked at us with the calm hunger of a man who has lived too long on promises and now sees meat set before him.

“My friends,” he said, “can ye do this wonderful thing, or were ye speaking empty words to the captains?”

“We believe we can do it,” I answered. “Umbopa—Ignosi, I mean.”

“It is strange,” he said slowly. “Had ye not been Englishmen, I would not have believed it. But I have learned that English ‘gentlemen’ tell no lies. If we live through this matter, be sure I will repay you.”

“Ignosi,” Sir Henry said, “promise me one thing.”

“I will promise, Incubu, even before I hear it,” Ignosi answered, and there was warmth in his smile, like the first heat from a fire that has been coaxed to life. “What is it?”

“That if ever you come to be king of this people, you will do away with the smelling-out of wizards such as we saw last night; and that the killing of men without trial shall no longer take place in the land.”

Ignosi stood silent for a moment after I had translated. The lamp hissed softly; outside, some early bird tried a note and stopped, as though reconsidering whether the world was safe.

“The ways of black people are not as the ways of white men, Incubu,” he said at last. “Nor do we value life so highly. Yet I will promise. If it be in my power to hold them back, the witch-finders shall hunt no more, nor shall any man die the death without trial or judgment.”

“That’s a bargain,” Sir Henry said. “Now let us get some rest.”

We slept like men dropped into darkness—sleep not as comfort, but as collapse. Ignosi woke us about eleven. We washed, ate heartily, and for a little while walked outside, examining the Kukuana huts and watching the women move about their work. They went with a sure, economical grace: water carried, grain handled, fire tended. There is a kind of quiet civilisation in such routines, and I could not help thinking how the cruelty of Twala and Gagool sat upon this people like smoke on good food—spoiling what might have been wholesome.

“I hope that eclipse will come off,” Sir Henry said at length.

“If it does not,” I answered, “it will soon be all up with us; for as sure as we are living men some of those chiefs will tell the whole story to the king. Then there will be another sort of eclipse—one I am certain we shall not like.”

We returned to the hut, ate dinner, and endured the long remainder of the day, receiving visits of ceremony and curiosity. Faces peered in, voices offered compliments, questions came like the tapping of spoons on bowls—light, polite, insistent. All the while the thought of the moon sat in my mind like a stone in a pouch.

At last the sun set. For a couple of hours we had such quiet as anxious men can manage. Then, about half-past eight, a messenger came from Twala to bid us to the great annual dance of girls.

We hastily put on the chain-shirts the king had sent us—cold, metallic things, each link like a little promise of survival—and took our rifles and ammunition, keeping them as close as a cook keeps salt when strangers are near the pot. Then we walked out boldly enough, though inside me fear moved and shifted, restless as a trapped animal.

The great space before the king’s kraal looked altogether different from the previous night. Where there had been grim, serried ranks of warriors, there were now companies of girls—lightly clothed, crowned with wreaths of flowers, each holding a palm leaf in one hand and a white arum lily in the other. The air was sweet with bruised greenery and blossoms warming under bodies and lamp-smoke; beneath it, faint but unmistakable, lay the tang of human closeness and trampled earth. Their singing rose and fell in clean lines that seemed to rinse the place of last night’s screams, though no song can truly wash blood from memory.

In the centre sat Twala the king, with old Gagool crouched at his feet like a poison root. Infadoos was there, too, and Scragga, and twelve guards. About a score of chiefs stood nearby, among them most of those who had visited us in the night.

Twala greeted us with showy cordiality, though his single eye fixed itself on Ignosi with a hatred so sharp it seemed to cut the air.

“Welcome, white men from the Stars,” he said. “This is another sight than that which your eyes gazed on by the light of last night’s moon, but it is not so good a sight. Girls are pleasant, and were it not for such as these, we should none of us be here this day; but men are better. Kisses and tender words are sweet, but the sound of clashing spears and the smell of men’s blood are sweeter far! Would ye have wives from among our people, white men? Choose the fairest, and ye shall have them—as many as ye will.”

Good’s ears pricked at that like a dog’s at the rattle of a pan, and I saw the trouble beginning in his eyes. I answered quickly, as an older hand answers a boy reaching too near the fire.

“Thanks to thee, O king, but we white men wed only with white women like ourselves. Your maidens are fair, but they are not for us.”

Twala laughed. “It is well. In our land there is a proverb: ‘Women’s eyes are always bright, whatever the colour,’ and another: ‘Love her who is present, for she who is absent is false.’ But perhaps these things are not so in the Stars. In a land where men are white all things are possible. So be it. The girls will not go begging. Welcome again; and welcome, too, thou black one. If Gagool had won her way, thou wouldst have been stiff and cold by now. It is lucky for thee that thou too camest from the Stars; ha! ha!”

“I can kill thee before thou killest me, O king,” Ignosi answered calmly, “and thou shalt be stiff before my limbs cease to bend.”

Twala started, anger flaring. “Thou speakest boldly, boy. Presume not too far.”

“He may well be bold in whose lips are truth,” Ignosi said, his voice even. “The truth is a sharp spear which flies home and misses not. It is a message from the Stars, O king.”

Twala scowled, his one eye gleaming, but he said no more.

“Let the dance begin,” he cried.

The girls sprang forward in companies, singing a sweet song and waving palms and lilies. They moved in patterns—whirling, meeting, feinting in mimic battle, retreating and advancing in ordered confusion that was delightful to see. In the soft moonlight they seemed almost unreal, like white smoke drifting across dark ground. Their feet slapped and whispered on the packed earth; their voices braided together, and for a time the place belonged to sound rather than to cruelty.

At last they paused, and a beautiful young woman stepped out and danced alone, pirouetting with a grace and vigour that would have shamed most trained performers. When she retired, another took her place, then another and another, but none equalled the first.

When the chosen girls had danced, Twala lifted his hand.

“Which deem ye the fairest, white men?” he asked.

“The first,” I said, unthinking.

Instantly I regretted it, remembering Infadoos’s warning. The word had left my mouth like a seed thrown—and now I saw what crop it would bring.

“Then is my mind as your minds, and my eyes as your eyes,” Twala said. “She is the fairest! And a sorry thing for her, for she must die!”

“Ay, must die!” piped Gagool, her quick eyes flashing towards the girl.

The poor creature stood some ten yards off, still ignorant, nervously picking a flower from her wreath to pieces—petal by petal, as a child crumbles bread not knowing famine is coming.

“Why, O king?” I asked, forcing my voice to steadiness. “The girl has danced well and pleased us; she is fair too. It would be hard to reward her with death.”

“It is our custom,” Twala said, and pointed towards the peaks, black against the sky. “The figures who sit in stone yonder must have their due. If I fail to put the fairest girl to death to-day, misfortune will fall on me and my house. Thus runs the prophecy: ‘If the king offer not a sacrifice of a fair girl on the day of the dance of maidens to the Old Ones who sit and watch on the mountains, then shall he fall, and his house.’ Look ye: my brother who reigned before me offered not the sacrifice because of the tears of the woman, and he fell, and his house, and I reign in his stead. It is finished; she must die!”

He turned to the guards. “Bring her hither. Scragga, make sharp thy spear.”

Two men stepped forward. The girl understood at last. She screamed—a sound like a bird’s sudden terror—and turned to run, but strong hands caught her and dragged her, weeping and struggling, into the open before us.

“What is thy name, girl?” Gagool asked, her voice thin with mockery. “What! Wilt thou not answer? Shall the king’s son do his work at once?”

At the hint Scragga advanced, more evil than ever, lifting his great spear. I saw Good’s hand creep towards his revolver. The faint glint of metal caught the girl’s eye through tears; it steadied her as a sip of strong drink steadies a fainting person. She stopped struggling and stood shuddering, hands clenched together as though she might squeeze life back into herself by force.

“See,” Scragga cried, delighted, “she shrinks from the sight of my little plaything even before she has tasted it,” and he tapped the broad blade.

“If ever I get the chance,” I heard Good mutter, “you shall pay for that, you young hound.”

“Now that thou art quiet,” Gagool went on, “give us thy name, my dear. Come, speak out, and fear not.”

“Oh, mother,” the girl said in trembling accents, “my name is Foulata, of the house of Suko. Oh, mother, why must I die? I have done no wrong!”

“Be comforted,” Gagool said, with the cruelty of an old cook telling a child the knife will not hurt. “Thou must die as a sacrifice to the Old Ones, but it is better to sleep in the night than to toil in the daytime; it is better to die than to live, and thou shalt die by the royal hand of the king’s own son.”

Foulata wrung her hands and cried aloud, grief pouring from her in a rush of images—sunrise she would not see, stars she would not count, dew-heavy flowers she would not gather, the laughing of waters she would not hear; her father’s hut, her mother’s kiss, the sick lamb she would never tend; the lover’s arm that would never circle her, the children she would never bear. Her voice carried so much of the small domestic life of a people—the very things tyrants treat as nothing—that it struck me harder than any spear-point. Around us some faces softened; even among the guards there were eyes that flickered with pity. But Twala sat unmoved, and Gagool’s mockery never slipped.

Good gave a fierce snort, and made as though to step forward. Foulata saw it with a woman’s quickness and, with a sudden movement, flung herself before him and clasped his legs.

“Oh, white father from the Stars!” she cried. “Throw over me the mantle of thy protection; let me creep into the shadow of thy strength, that I may be saved. Oh, keep me from these cruel men and from the mercies of Gagool!”

“All right, my hearty,” Good blurted in nervous Saxon. “I’ll look after you. Come, get up—there’s a good girl,” and he stooped and caught her hand.

Twala motioned to his son. Scragga advanced again, spear lifted.

“Now’s your time,” Sir Henry whispered to me. “What are you waiting for?”

“I am waiting for that eclipse,” I whispered back, my eyes fixed on the moon as though I could force it to obey by staring. “I’ve watched it half an hour and never saw it look healthier.”

“You must risk it now,” Sir Henry said. “Twala is losing patience.”

He was right. The moment for talk was gone; only theatre would serve us—big enough to stop a spear in mid-air.

I stepped forward with as much dignity as a frightened man can manufacture, placing myself between the prostrate girl and Scragga’s poised blade.

“King,” I said, “it shall not be. We will not endure this thing. Let the girl go in safety.”

Twala sprang to his feet, wrath and astonishment twisting his features. Around us came a murmur from the chiefs and the serried ranks of maidens, who had closed in slowly, drawn by the scent of impending death as people are drawn to a cooking fire.

“Shall not be!” Twala roared. “Thou white dog that yappest at the lion in his cave—shall not be! Art thou mad? Be careful, lest this chicken’s fate overtake thee, and those with thee. Back, I say. Scragga, kill her! Ho, guards! seize these men!”

At his cry armed men ran swiftly from behind a hut where they had clearly been placed in readiness. Sir Henry, Good, and Ignosi ranged alongside me and lifted their rifles.

“Stop!” I shouted, and though my heart had dropped somewhere near my boots I made my voice ring. “Stop! We, the white men from the Stars, say it shall not be. Come but one pace nearer, and we will put out the moon like a wind-blown lamp, as we who dwell in her House can do, and plunge the land in darkness. Dare to disobey, and ye shall taste of our magic.”

The effect was immediate. The guards halted. Scragga stood frozen, his spear still lifted.

“Hear him! Hear him!” piped Gagool. “Hear the liar who says he will put out the moon like a lamp. Let him do it, and the girl shall be spared. Yes—let him do it, or die by the girl, he and those with him.”

I glanced up again, a sick, helpless look—and there, to my intense joy, on the very edge of the bright orb lay a faint rim of shadow. A smoky stain began to gather, as if some unseen hand had smudged the moon with ash.

Never shall I forget that instant: relief so strong it was almost pain, like warm broth poured into a belly that thought it would starve.

I lifted my hand solemnly towards the sky. Sir Henry and Good did the same, playing their parts at once. I quoted a line or two from the Ingoldsby Legends in the most impressive voice I could summon. Sir Henry followed with a verse from the Old Testament, and—of all things—something about Balbus building a wall in Latin. Good, taking the cue with sailorly enthusiasm, addressed the Queen of Night with a rolling thunder of classical bad language.

Slowly the penumbra crept onward, the shadow of a shadow sliding over the moon’s bright face. As it spread I heard deep gasps rising from the multitude—fear made audible, like a sudden wind through dry reeds.

“Look, O king!” I cried. “Look, Gagool! Look, chiefs and people and women, and see if the white men from the Stars keep their word, or if they be but empty liars!

“The moon grows black before your eyes. Soon there will be darkness—darkness in the hour of the full moon. Ye have asked for a sign; it is given. Grow dark, O Moon! Withdraw thy light, thou pure and holy one; bring the proud heart of usurping murderers to the dust, and eat up the world with shadows.”

A groan of terror burst from the onlookers. Some stood as if turned to stone; others dropped to their knees and cried aloud. Twala, for all his blood-lust, turned pale beneath his dusky skin. Only Gagool held her nerve.

“It will pass,” she shrilled. “I have often seen the like before. No man can put out the moon. Lose not heart—sit still—the shadow will pass.”

“Wait,” I answered, hardly able to keep the triumph from my voice, “and ye shall see.”

The darkness thickened on the moon like soot gathering on a pot hung too long over a smoky fire. The songs had died; even the night insects seemed to listen. In that strange hush, Good’s curses rolled on—vast, inventive, unending—while above us the sky performed our promised miracle, and the world held its breath as the light began to fail.
