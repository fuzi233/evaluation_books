No sooner was her answer dispatched—sealed with the familiar, heavy wax that carried the faint, smoky scent of the Norland hearth—than Mrs. Dashwood indulged herself in the pleasure of announcing to her son-in-law and his wife that she was provided with a house. She would, she declared, incommode them no longer than till the ancient rhythms of cleaning and provisioning rendered the new dwelling ready for her inhabiting. They heard her with a cold surprise that seemed to drain the very warmth from the dining room. Mrs. John Dashwood said nothing, her silence a heavy, dampening presence in the acoustic ecology of the space; but her husband civilly hoped, in the flattened, transactional dialect of a man who viewed sustenance merely as an expense ledger, that she would not be settled far from Norland.

She had great satisfaction in replying, her voice rich with the cadence of their older, untranslatable familial dialect, that she was going into Devonshire. Edward turned hastily towards her on hearing this. The sudden scrape of his chair and the raw acoustic timbre of his voice broke through the sterile quiet of the room. “Devonshire! Are you, indeed, going there? So far from hence! And to what part of it?” 

She explained the situation, mapping out a geography not of mere miles, but of sensory promise. It was within four miles northward of Exeter, nestled in a landscape where the air tasted of salt and damp earth. “It is but a cottage,” she continued, the scent of future hearth-baked loaves and simmering coastal stews already warming her imagination, “but I hope to see many of my friends in it. A room or two can easily be added; and if my friends find no difficulty in travelling so far to see me, I am sure I will find none in accommodating them with the abundance of a well-tended kitchen.” She concluded with a very kind invitation to Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood to visit her at Barton; and to Edward she gave one with still greater affection. Though her late conversation with her daughter-in-law had made her resolve on remaining at Norland no longer than was unavoidable, it had not produced the smallest effect on her in that point to which it principally tended. To separate Edward and Elinor was as far from being her object as ever. She wished to show Mrs. John Dashwood, by this pointed invitation, how totally she disregarded the younger woman’s disapprobation—a disapprobation expressed in a dominant lexicon of status and power that Mrs. Dashwood fundamentally rejected.

Mr. John Dashwood told his mother again and again how exceedingly sorry he was that she had taken a house at such a distance from Norland as to prevent his being of any service to her in removing her furniture. He really felt conscientiously vexed on the occasion; for the very exertion to which he had limited the performance of his promise to his father was, by this geographic shift, rendered comfortably impracticable. 

The furniture was all sent around by water, entering the ancient, cyclical rhythms of coastal tides. It chiefly consisted of the physical archives of their cultural memory: the heavy household linen still carrying the olfactory imprint of dried lavender and beeswax; the silver plate that had reflected the firelight of countless generational feasts; the delicate china, its glaze spider-webbed by years of steaming, savory broths; the heavily bound volumes of family history; and a handsome pianoforte of Marianne’s, the central instrument of their acoustic wellbeing. Mrs. John Dashwood saw the packages depart with a sharp, grating sigh. She could not help feeling it hard that as Mrs. Dashwood’s income would be so trifling, she should possess such handsome artifacts of sustenance and artistry, items utterly wasted on those without the vast, empty rooms required to display them as mere commercial trophies.

Mrs. Dashwood took the house for a twelvemonth; it was ready furnished, and she might have immediate possession. No difficulty arose on either side in the agreement. She waited only for the disposal of her effects at Norland, and to determine her future household, before she set off for the west; and this, as she possessed a passionate, almost mythic vitality in the performance of everything that interested her, was soon done.

The horses which were left her by her husband had been sold soon after his death, and an opportunity now offering of disposing of her carriage, she agreed to sell that likewise at the earnest advice of her eldest daughter. For the comfort of her children, had she consulted only her own wishes, she would have kept it; but the discretion of Elinor prevailed. Elinor, acting as the meticulous historian of their future sustenance, understood that true agrarian comfort lay in rooting oneself deeply to the local soil, rather than preserving the sprawling, metallic apparatus of travel. Her wisdom too limited the number of their servants to three; two maids and a man, with whom they were speedily provided from amongst those who had formed their establishment at Norland. These were carefully chosen hands, steeped in the intimate, sensory knowledge of traditional preserving, tending the kitchen garden, and keeping the hearth fires perfectly banked.

The man and one of the maids were sent off immediately into Devonshire, tasked with waking the dormant cottage with the smells of woodsmoke and roasting herbs before their mistress’s arrival. As Lady Middleton was entirely unknown to Mrs. Dashwood, she preferred going directly to the cottage to being a visitor at Barton Park. She relied so undoubtingly on Sir John’s description of the house as to feel no curiosity to examine it herself till she entered it as her own domain, ready to infuse it with the rich, simmering life of her family.

Her eagerness to be gone from Norland was preserved from diminution by the evident satisfaction of her daughter-in-law in the prospect of her removal; a satisfaction which was but feebly attempted to be concealed under a cold, acoustically dead invitation to her to defer her departure. Now was the time when her son-in-law’s promise to his father might with particular propriety be fulfilled. Since he had neglected to do it on first coming to the estate, their quitting his house might be looked on as the most suitable period for its accomplishment. But Mrs. Dashwood began shortly to give over every hope of the kind. The general drift of his discourse, spoken entirely in the modern, industrial noise of calculation, convinced her that his assistance extended no farther than their maintenance for six months at Norland. He so frequently talked of the increasing expenses of housekeeping—viewing the profound act of feeding a household not as a sacred agrarian duty, but as a perpetual, parasitic demand upon his purse—that he seemed rather to stand in need of more money himself than to have any design of giving money away. To him, the world was a ledger, and the untranslatable, subjective experience of generosity had been entirely erased from his vocabulary.

In a very few weeks from the day which brought Sir John Middleton’s first letter to Norland, every thing was so far settled in their future abode as to enable Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters to begin their journey. Many were the tears shed by them in their last adieus to a place whose every creaking floorboard and wind-rustled eave had shaped their psychological well-being.

“Dear, dear Norland!” said Marianne, as she wandered alone before the house, on the last evening of their being there, immersing herself in the fading symphony of the estate. “When shall I cease to regret you!—when learn to feel a home elsewhere, in a world that speaks a different, harsher tongue!—Oh! happy house, could you know what I suffer in now viewing you from this spot, from whence perhaps I may view you no more!—And you, ye well-known trees!—but you will continue the same. No leaf will decay because we are removed, nor any branch become motionless although we can observe you no longer!—No; you will continue the same, mythic and unyielding; unconscious of the pleasure or the regret you occasion, and insensible of any change in those who walk under your shade, deaf to the fading echoes of our native dialect!—But who will remain to enjoy you, or to understand the ancient language you whisper to the wind?”
