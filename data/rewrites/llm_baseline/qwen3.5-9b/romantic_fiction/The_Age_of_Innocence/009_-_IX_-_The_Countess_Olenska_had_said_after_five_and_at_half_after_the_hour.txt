IX. The Countess Olenska had said "after five"; and at half-past the hour Newland Archer rang the bell of the peeling stucco house, where a giant wisteria choked the feeble cast-iron balcony. It was a peculiar neighborhood to have chosen, far down West Twenty-third Street, rented from the vagabond Medora. Her closest neighbors were small dressmakers, bird-stuffers, and "people who wrote." Further down the disheveled street, Archer recognized a dilapidated wooden house at the end of a paved path—the residence of a journalist named Winsett, whom he occasionally encountered. Winsett didn't entertain guests, yet he had once pointed out the house during a late-night stroll, prompting Archer to shiver and wonder if the humanities were so poorly housed in other capitals. Madame Olenska's dwelling was saved from total squalor only by fresh paint around the window frames. As Archer surveyed its modest front, he mused that the Polish Count must have stripped her of both her fortune and her illusions.

Archer had endured an unsatisfactory day. He had dined with the Wellands, hoping subsequently to escort May for a walk in the Park. He desired solitude with her—to express how enchanting she had appeared the previous night, how proud he felt, and to urge her to accelerate their marriage. Instead, Mrs. Welland firmly reminded him that the circuit of family calls was far from complete. When he hinted at moving the wedding date, she raised reproachful brows and sighed, "Twelve dozen of everything—hand-embroidered—" Packed into the family landau, they rolled from one ancestral doorstep to another. By the time the afternoon's rounds ended, Archer parted from his fiancée feeling exposed, as if displayed like a wild animal cunningly trapped. Though he attributed this coarse perspective to his anthropological studies, remembering that the Wellands did not anticipate a wedding until the following autumn filled him with a damp chill. "Tomorrow," Mrs. Welland called after him, "we'll do the Chiverses and the Dallases." He realized she was methodically alphabetizing through their combined families, barely past the first quarter.

He intended to inform May of the Countess's summons—that command, rather—but in the fleeting moments of privacy, more urgent matters had occupied him. Furthermore, it seemed absurd to mention it now. He knew May particularly wished him to be kind to her cousin; hadn't that desire hastened their engagement? It gave him a strange sensation to reflect that, but for the Countess's arrival, he might have remained, if not entirely free, at least less irrevocably bound. Yet May had willed it so, leaving him strangely relieved of further responsibility—and thus, if he chose, free to visit his cousin without her knowledge.

Standing on Madame Olenska's threshold, curiosity dominated his feelings. He was puzzled by the tone of her invitation, concluding she was less naive than she appeared. The door opened to reveal a swarthy, foreign-looking maid with a prominent bosom beneath a colorful neckerchief, whom he vaguely identified as Sicilian. She greeted him with a display of white teeth, answered his inquiries with incomprehensible head shakes, and guided him through a narrow hall into a low, firelit drawing-room. The room was empty, and she departed, leaving him to ponder whether she had sought her mistress or misunderstood his purpose, perhaps thinking he wished to wind the stopped clock. Knowing that southern races often communicated via pantomime, he was mortified when her shrugs and smiles proved equally unintelligible.

Eventually, she returned with a lamp. Meanwhile, Archer constructed a phrase from Dante and Petrarch to inquire: *"La signora è fuori; ma verrà subito"* ("She is out—but you will see her soon"). What he observed, illuminated by the lamp, was the faded, shadowy charm of a space unlike any he knew. He recalled that the Countess had brought some possessions with her—"bits of wreckage," she called them—which seemed represented by slender dark-wood tables, a delicate Greek bronze on the mantel, and a stretch of red damask nailed over discolored wallpaper behind a pair of Italian paintings in antique frames. Newland Archer prided himself on his knowledge of Italian art; his youth had been saturated with Ruskin, and he had devoured the works of Symonds, Vernon Lee, Hamerton, and Walter Pater. He spoke fluently of Botticelli and Fra Angelico with faint condescension. Yet these pictures bewildered him; they resembled nothing he had seen while traveling in Italy, and perhaps his observational faculties were dulled by the strangeness of standing in this empty house where no one seemed to expect him.

Regretting that he hadn't told May of the Countess's request, he felt a disturbance at the possibility of his fiancée arriving to find him waiting alone in the dusk, implying an intimacy inappropriate for a suitor. Since he had arrived, however, he meant to wait. He sank into a chair and stretched his feet toward the logs. It was odd to be summoned and then abandoned, yet Archer felt more curious than mortified. The atmosphere of the room differed so profoundly from anything he had ever breathed that self-consciousness dissolved into a sense of adventure. He had visited drawing-rooms draped in red damask before, but what struck him was how Medora Manson's shabby rental—with its blighted background of pampas grass and Rogers statuettes—had been transformed by a masterful touch into something intimate, "foreign," subtly evoking old romantic scenes.

He attempted to analyze the trick, searching for clues in the arrangement of chairs and tables, the mere two Jacqueminot roses (where others demanded a dozen) in the slender vase beside him, and the pervasive perfume—not the scent of handkerchiefs, but something reminiscent of a distant bazaar, composed of Turkish coffee, ambergris, and dried roses. His mind drifted to May's drawing-room. He knew Mr. Welland, acting "very handsomely," had his sights set on a newly built house in East Thirty-ninth Street. The area was considered remote, the structure constructed of a ghastly greenish-yellow stone that younger architects adopted as a protest against the ubiquitous brownstone coating New York like cold chocolate sauce, though the plumbing was flawless. Archer would have preferred to travel, to postpone the housing dilemma; yet the Wellands insisted on a home for the returning couple. Even if they approved of an extended European honeymoon, perhaps a winter in Egypt, they were adamant about a house. The young man felt his fate sealed: for the rest of his life, he would ascend every evening between the cast-iron railings of that greenish-yellow doorstep, passing through a Pompeian vestibule into a hall lined with varnished yellow wood. Beyond that image, his imagination stalled. He knew the drawing-room above featured a bay window but couldn't envision how May would treat it. She submitted cheerfully to the purple satin and yellow tufting of the Welland parlor, to its fake Buhl tables and gilt cabinets filled with modern Saxe porcelain. He assumed she wouldn't desire anything different in her own home; his only solace was the belief that she would allow him to arrange his library as he pleased—filled with "sincere" Eastlake furniture and plain new bookcases without glass doors.

The round-bosomed maid reappeared, drew the curtains, pushed back a log, and offered consoling words in Italian: *"Verra—verra."* Once she left, Archer stood and began to pace. Should he wait longer? His position was becoming increasingly foolish. Perhaps he had misunderstood Madame Olenska; perhaps she hadn't invited him at all. Then, down the cobblestones of the quiet street, came the ring of a horse's hooves. They halted before the house, and he heard a carriage door open. Parting the curtains, he gazed out into the early dusk. A streetlamp faced him, casting light on Julius Beaufort's compact English brougham, drawn by a large roan horse, and the banker stepping down to assist Madame Olenska. Beaufort stood, hat in hand, speaking words his companion seemingly rejected; then they shook hands, and he sprang into his carriage while she climbed the steps.

When she entered the room, she showed no surprise at seeing Archer; surprise seemed the emotion she least possessed. "How do you like my funny house?" she asked. "To me, it's like heaven." As she spoke, she untied her small velvet bonnet, tossed it aside with her long cloak, and regarded him with meditative eyes. "You've arranged it delightfully," he replied, acutely aware of the flatness of his words but imprisoned by his desire to be simple yet striking. "Oh, it's a poor little place. My relations despise it. But at any rate, it's less gloomy than the van der Luydens'."

The words delivered an electric shock to Archer. Few rebellious spirits would dare call the stately home of the van der Luydens gloomy. Those privileged to enter it shivered within, describing it merely as "handsome." Yet suddenly, he was glad she had voiced the universal shudder. "It's delicious—what you've done here," he repeated. "I like the little house," she admitted. "But I suppose what I like is the blessedness of being here—in my own country, my own town; and then, of being alone in it." She spoke so softly he barely caught the final phrase, but in his awkwardness, he seized upon it. "You like so much to be alone?" "Yes; as long as my friends keep me from feeling lonely."
