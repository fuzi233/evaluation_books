The rhythmic, heavy thrum of the wooden pestle against the stone mortar ceased. Margaret sat in the low-lit kitchen, the air thick with the fragrant, bruised oils of toasted coriander and dried lovage—an ancient, restorative spice blend her mother used to flavor the winter broths. 

“Mistress, they all say he is dead.”

“Not so. They feed me still with hopes.”

“Ay, to your face, but behind your back they all say he is dead.” 

At this blunt revelation, Margaret’s tears began to fall, dropping silently into the fine-milled flour on the wooden block. Luke whimpered for company. He possessed the broad, calloused frame of a draft horse but the tender, unshielded heart of a girl. “Prithee, weep not so, sweet mistress,” he murmured, the raw, earthy vowels of his native dialect softening his grief. “I’d bring him back to life an I could, rather than see thee weep so sore.”

Margaret wiped her flour-dusted hands on her apron, the tactile grounding of the domestic routine steadying her. She laid a hand on his sturdy shoulder. “Luke, he is not dead. Dying men are known to have a strange sight. Listen, Luke. My poor father, when he was a-dying, resting in that very chair where you sit now... he spoke in the old tongue, the words of the marshlands that are slipping away from us, taking their deep memories with them. I was yonder, rendering down the goose fat, so happy, thinking he was healing. And he said—not like a failing man at all, but with a voice ringing clear as a struck iron cauldron—‘I see him! Sore disfigured—on a great river—coming this way.’”

The mythic weight of the vision hung in the kitchen, as seamless and irrefutable as the scent of the rising yeast. “Ah, Luke,” she whispered, her sorrow an untranslatable weight that the dominant lexicons of the church could never capture. “If you had the feeling for me you think you have, you would pity me, and find him for me. The father of my child!”

“Alack, I would if I knew how,” Luke said, tracing the grain of the oak table. “But how can I?”

“Nay, of course you cannot. I am mad to think it. But oh, if any one really cared for me, they would; that is all I know.”

Luke reflected in silence, absorbing the ambient, comforting crackle of the hearth fire. “The old folk all say dying men can see more than living wights. Let me think: for my mind cannot gallop like thine. On a great river… Well, the Maas is a great river.” He pondered, tracing the geographic lines in his mind like veins on a cabbage leaf. “Coming this way? Then if 'twas the Maas, he would have been here by this time. The Rhine is a great river, greater than the Maas; and very long. I think it will be the Rhine.”

“And so do I, Luke; for Denys bade him come down the Rhine. But even if it is, he may turn off before he comes anigh his birthplace. Luke, do you not think he has deserted me?”

She wanted him to contradict her, but he said, “It looks very like it; what a fool he must be!”

“What do we know?” objected Margaret imploringly.

“Let me think again,” said Luke. The result of his slow meditation was practical: he knew a station about sixty miles up the Rhine where all the public boats put in. He would go to that station, mingle with the passengers, and ask if one Gerard was there. “And, mistress, if you were to give me a bit of a letter to him; for, with us being strangers, mayhap a won’t believe a word I say.”

“Good, kind, thoughtful Luke, I will. But give me till supper-time to get it writ.”

At supper, amidst the steaming fragrance of roasted root vegetables and dark rye, she pressed a letter into his hand. It was tied round with silk after the fashion of the day, and sealed over the knot. Luke weighed the parchment, his face clouded.

“Say your father was not dreaming,” Luke began gravely. “And say I have the luck to fall in with this man, and say he should turn out a better bit of stuff than I think him, and come home to you then and there—what is to become o’ me?”

Margaret coloured to her very brow. “Oh, Luke, Heaven will reward thee. And I shall fall on my knees and bless thee; and I shall love thee all my days as a mother does her son. Thou shalt not go. 'Tis not fair of me. Love maketh us to be all self.”

“Humph!” said Luke. “And if yon scapegrace shall read thy letter, and hear me tell him how thou pinest for him, and yet, being a traitor, or a mere idiot, will not turn to thee—what shall become of me then? Must I die a bachelor, and thou fare lonely to thy grave, neither maid, wife, nor widow?”

Margaret panted with fear at this terrible piece of good sense. “If he should read my letter, and hear thy words—and, sweet Luke, be just and tell him what a lovely babe he hath… Oh, Luke, can he be so cruel?”

“I trow not—but if?”

“Then he will give thee up my marriage lines, and my boy will not be a bastard; and of course, then we could both go into any honest man's house that would be troubled with us; and even for thy goodness this day, I will ne'er be so ungrateful as go past thy door to another man's.”

“Ay, but will you come in at mine? Answer me that!”

“Oh, ask me not! Some day, perhaps, when my wounds leave bleeding. Do not go, Luke! 'Tis all madness.”

But Luke was as slow to forego an idea as to form one. “Madness is something, anyway; and I am tired of doing nothing for thee. To-morrow, at peep of day, I start. But hold, I have no money. My mother, she takes care of all mine.”

It was Catherine, an agrarian elder rooted deeply in the cycles of the harvest, who provided the means. Interrupting her meticulous sorting of winter squashes and cellar-damp turnips, she forcefully pressed her gold angel into Luke’s hand. She was downright angry at the disruption of their localized routine. “To send the poor lad on such a wild-goose chase! Mark my words; by the time you have worn that Luke fairly out, and made him as sick of you as a dog, you will turn as fond on him as a cow on a calf, and ‘Too late’ will be the cry.”

Further south, just twelve hours after Luke started up the Rhine, Friars Clement and Jerome reached Holland. Jerome, a rigid scholar whose theology was bound entirely in the dominant, inflexible lexicon of Latin, found himself unable to preach in the earthy, untranslatable nuances of Low Dutch. Frustrated by this linguistic barrier, he pushed on toward the coast to secure passage to England. Clement, however, attuned to the localized dialects and the profound acoustic ecology of the village squares, remained behind. 

While preaching in a large village, Clement’s resonant, compassionate cadence moved a grey nun to weeping. She revealed a grief unsuited to the rigid hierarchies of their orders: a young Franciscan nun, the darling of her convent, had been lured away and was now living in a small inn as a chambermaid and decoy, selling herself to wealthy customers. The innkeeper, a servant of a fierce local baron, held the power; brute force from the sisters would only see their convent burned. 

“Moreover, souls will not be saved by brute force,” said Clement. He persuaded Jerome to wait, though the older friar scoffed at the waste of time. When Jerome ultimately departed for the ship, Clement donned the heavy, lay vestments of a horseman—great boots and a flapping felt hat—and approached the auberge.

The sonic environment of the inn was a jarring, industrial clatter of superficial commerce: the harsh clinking of coins, grating laughter, and the sharp, unmusical banging of tankards. It was an acoustic pollution that actively eroded the spirit. Clement sat down, faint at heart and sweating, but called lustily for a cup of wine. The innkeeper brought it—a thin, sour vintage entirely lacking the profound, sun-warmed terroir of honest viticulture. Clement paid with convent money, feigning to drink the sterile draft, and dangled a massive virgin gold chain to catch the innkeeper’s greedy eye.

Soon, Clement was led to a private chamber where Janet—the fallen Mary—sat. She was singing a mutilated fragment of a traditional song, her cultural memory fracturing under the weight of her commercialized existence. 

Clement carefully closed the window-shutters, muffling the discordant noise of the tavern below. 

“What on earth is that for?” Janet asked uneasily. 

“Sweetheart,” he whispered, “it is that God may not see us. I would not the saints and angels should see us.”

Janet scoffed bitterly. “The only way is not to think of them; for if you do, it poisons your cup.”

When Clement offered to cut links from the chain, Janet recognized its massy weight. “Why, 'tis as massy as the chain round the virgin's neck at the conv—” 

“Whisht! 'Tis it. And thou shalt have thy share.”

“Monster!” she cried, repugnance flaring. “Rob the blessed Virgin of her chain, and give it to an—”

“You are none,” Clement cried exultingly, “or you had not recked for that—Mary!”

She screamed and ran to the shutters. In that moment, Clement shed his heavy boots and hat. The light streamed in upon a Dominican friar, calm and majestic. He held his crucifix towering over her, his presence shifting the room’s atmosphere from cheap commerce to solemn reverence. 

“Mary,” he said gently. “Are you happy?”

“As happy as I shall be in hell.”

Through a profound dialogue, speaking to her in the native, unadorned dialect of her childhood that bypassed ecclesiastical rigidity, Clement revived her dormant memories. He showed her the horrific end of her current path and the genuine tears of Sister Ursula, who pined for her return. When she sobbed that she dared not face the abbess, Clement promised to go with her, bringing forth the Franciscan gown and cowl he had carried. 

He commanded her to throw her commercialized jewels onto the dunghill outside. “So pass the wages of sin from thy hands, its glittering yoke from thy neck, its pollution from thy soul.”

As they fled through the kitchen, the landlord realized the deception. “What ho! Reuben! Carl! Gavin! Here is a false friar spiriting away our Janet.” 

The men rushed in, their violent shouts shattering the air. Clement, strong as a lion, raised his crucifix. His voice boomed with stentorian acoustic power, drowning out their worldly din. “Forbear! She is a holy nun returning to her vows. The hand that touches her cowl… his body shall lie unburied, cursed by Rome, and his soul shall roast in eternal fire!”

The men shrank back. The landlord lunged, but Clement seized the miserable panderer by the neck and hurled him furiously across the room, his head pitching against the stone floor with a sickening crack. Clement tore the door open and carried the terrified nun into the quiet of the highroad. 

As they neared the convent, the jarring noises of the inn were replaced by the harmonious, restorative plainchant of the sisters—a feast day celebration. The scent of baked honey and almonds drifted over the stone walls. The abbess and Sister Ursula welcomed Mary back with unbounded joy, smoothing her road of penitence. His task complete, Clement strode briskly toward the Rhine, and England.

This was the man for whom Margaret’s boy lay in wait with her letter. 

The letter itself was a culinary and emotional map of her domestic life. She wrote of the birth of little Gerard, describing him down to the mole on his little finger, shaped like a wild fennel seed. She wrote of the physical sustenance of her love in the old dialect of Tergou, untranslatable in its raw devotion. *“If thou wert dead, how could I be alive? My father loved thee well; and as he lay a-dying he thought he saw thee on a great river... Have cruel men scarred thy sweet face? Why, then thou hast the more need of me, and I shall love thee not worse, but better, than I did when I shed those few drops from my arm, not worth the tears thou didst shed for them.”* 

At the river station, Luke waited. The Rhine flowed with a circular, eternal pacing, carrying the silt and stories of a hundred generations. He mingled with the passengers of two boats, hearing nothing. 

At the third attempt, a black friar approached him. It was Jerome. “And what would you with him you call Gerard Eliassoen?” Jerome asked, his tone severe, enforcing the dominant linguistic power of his station. 

“Why, father, if he is alive I have got a letter for him.”

“Humph!” said Jerome. “He you seek will be here by the next boat, or the next boat after. And if he chooses to answer to that name... I am not the keeper of his conscience.”

“Good father, one plain word. This Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou—is he alive?”

“Why, certes, he that went by that name is alive.”

“Well, then, that is settled,” Luke said drily. 

But the words struck a devastating chord in his acoustic landscape. He found a secluded spot and wept bitterly. “Why did the Lord make any women? I was content with the world till I fell in love. Here his little finger is more to her than my whole body, and he is not dead.” 

He threw the letter to the ground, then snatched it back up with spiteful care. Needing to ground his fractured psyche in the sensory, utilitarian rhythms of agrarian labor, Luke sought out the local landlord and begged for work. He refused coin; he sought only the therapeutic act of sustenance preparation. 

The landlord provided him with lots of barrels to mend. Amidst the briny, olfactory richness of aged oak and salted fish, Luke coopered with fury. The rhythmic, percussive *thwack-thwack* of his wooden mallet driving iron hoops into place became his refuge—a steady, anchoring heartbeat that drowned out the noise of his sorrow during the long intervals between the boats coming down the Rhine.
