We Virginians, who still hold dear the memory of that gentle, unassuming, warm-hearted Indian princess, know far more of the story of Pocahontas than the average London theater-goer. At the urging of his friends, Mr. George Warrington penned a short ballad honoring her, printed in several popular magazines only a few days before his full tragedy on the same subject was set to premiere.

The enthusiastic Parson Sampson called the trick nothing short of genius. “It is ground-bait, sir!” he cried, bouncing in to wave the magazine under Warrington’s nose. “You will see the fish rise in swarms on opening night!” He and young Spencer swore up and down they’d heard the poem debated and praised in half the coffee houses they frequented, where patrons misattributed it to Mr. Mason, to Mr. Cowper of the Temple, and even to the revered, reclusive Mr. Gray. Warrington suspects honest Sampson himself spread those false rumors. He is half-convinced that if someone had claimed the full tragedy was a lost work of Shakespeare, Sampson would have sworn Pocahontas was among the Bard’s very finest performances.

George first fell in love with the story of brave Captain John Smith as a small boy, curled up in his grandfather’s library back in Virginia, his favorite tattered volume spread across his knees, while the old man sat beside him spelling out the Virginian hero’s adventures. He pored over tales of Smith’s travels, captures, and daring escapes across Europe no less than in the American wilderness, and even now, pulling that same well-worn book from the shelf of his London study, he feels himself shrink back to that small boy again, all the soft, warm memories of his early home crowding back unbidden. His grandfather used to draw him pictures of Smith battling the Turks on the Danube, or being led out by Virginia’s native peoples to face execution. He still thrills to think of that legendary fight with the three Turkish champions, and his childish delight over the final, most terrifying of the three, the giant Bonny Molgro. What a name it was! What enormous turbans, curling whiskers, and glinting scimitars the pair of them drew for that great brute! After Smith had struck off the heads of his first two foes, he and Molgro met in a ferocious axe fight, so fierce that the pair of them were left barely able to stay on horseback, until Smith lost his own battle axe, drawing a roar of triumph from the Turkish ramparts. But using his fine steed’s speed and his own sharp wit, he dodged every blow, drew his falchion, and ran Molgro clean through back and body beneath his armor. The Turk toppled from his horse, and lost his head just as his two compatriots had. For that feat, Duke Segismundus granted Smith a coat of arms bearing three Turkish heads, and a yearly pension of three hundred ducats.

As a poet, Warrington took the small liberty of shifting that same dashing, daredevil combat energy to the banks of the Potomac and James River for his play, unbound by the strict rules of timeline that ordinary historians follow. The little ballad he penned as preamble ran thus:
> Wearied arm and broken sword
> Wage in vain the desperate fight
> Round him press the countless horde,
> He is but a single knight.
> Hark! a cry of triumph shrill
> Through the wilderness resounds,
> As, with twenty bleeding wounds,
> Sinks the warrior, fighting still.
>
> Now they heap the fatal pyre,
> And the torch of death they light
> Ah! 'tis hard to die of fire!
> Who will shield the captive knight?
> Round the stake with fiendish cry
> Wheel and dance the savage crowd,
> Cold the victim's mien and proud,
> And his breast is bared to die.
>
> Who will shield the fearless heart?
> Who avert the murderous blade?
> From the throng, with sudden start,
> See, there springs an Indian maid.
> Quick she stands before the knight,
> 'Loose the chain, unbind the ring,
> I am daughter of the king,
> And I claim the Indian right!'
>
> Dauntlessly aside she flings
> Lifted axe and thirsty knife;
> Fondly to his heart she clings,
> And her bosom guards his life!
> In the woods of Powhattan,
> Still 'tis told by Indian fires,
> How a daughter of their sires
> Saved the captive Englishman.

There is no need to recount the full plot of the tragedy here, for Warrington’s own children can pull the small octavo edition, or the later splendid quarto of his collected original and translated works, from any shelf and read it for themselves. He will not be volunteering to read it aloud to his household any time soon, though – last Christmas, at his wife Theo’s request, he read two acts over supper, and both Captain Miles and Parson Sampson fell fast asleep before the end of the second. Any reader familiar with plays and novels could easily fill out the rest of the cast and story for themselves: a noble Indian king, the brave princess and her handmaiden who falls in love with Smith’s bumbling English servant, a traitor hidden inside the Jamestown fort, a valiant Indian warrior pining unhappily for Pocahontas, and a scheming, cruel medicine man (played masterfully by the actor Palmer) determined to torture Smith and chase all English settlers from the land. Throw in the wild war cries Gumbo had learned to mimic perfectly from Virginia’s native peoples, the dramatic arrival of the English fleet, and rousing references to the recent glorious British victories in Canada and the nation’s resolve to rule and thrive across the American colonies, and Warrington and his friends had every reasonable right to believe their play would be a runaway triumph.

But he has already spoken of the ill omens that hounded the production all the way to opening night: the peevish, jealous, skittish theater management throwing obstacle after obstacle in their path, and the violent prejudice against the piece that had already sprung up in powerful, high society circles. Was it any surprise Pocahontas did not turn out to be the grand victory they hoped for? He scoffs at the petty, malicious critics who panned his work, who had the audacity to call the shoddy play *Carpezan* a masterpiece while sneering at his far more carefully crafted, heartfelt tragedy. Hagan, the actor playing John Smith, was so magnificent in the role that the theater’s own manager could not help but be jealous of his skill, and but for the deliberate cabal to sabotage the performance, the play would have been a smash hit. Sampson swore the theater had been deliberately filled with the manager’s stooges, “the house swarming with Macs by George, so thick you could wash the galleries with brimstone!” He was fully convinced Mr. Garrick himself had vowed the play would never succeed, and that even during the thunderously heartfelt scene in the second act where Pocahontas leaps to save Smith from the stake, when the whole genuine audience was cheering and weeping with sympathy, the hired saboteurs did their utmost to drown out the applause.

Any curious reader can pull up the published tragedy themselves to judge: the verses are elegant, the language rich and noble, and that famous rescue scene far from unmerited praise. One of the only reasons for its failure was Warrington’s stubborn devotion to historical accuracy. He had gone to the British Museum to copy and carefully tint a portrait of Sir Walter Raleigh, and Theo had donated some of her own mother’s finest vintage lace to make an exact, perfect ruff for Hagan’s costume – no man could have looked more like the real Raleigh. He had dressed Miss Pritchard as Pocahontas exactly as he had seen native princesses dressed in his Virginia home, and when she first stepped onto the stage, half the pit erupted in stifled tittering. The audience grew used to her soon enough, but just as they were fully swept up in the emotional moment where Pocahontas throws herself into Smith’s arms, a wag in the pit bawled out “Bedad! Here’s the Belle Savage kissing the Saracen’s Head!” A roar of foolish laughter burst across the pit, and sputtered out in little fits and snickers for the rest of the performance. That same idiot repeated the tavern joke ad nauseam for the rest of the night, greeting every new character to the stage with the name of a public house sign: the bearded English governor he called the Goat and Boots, the broad-faced Lieutenant Barker he dubbed the Bull and Mouth, on and on. The curtain finally fell to a shrill storm of whistles and hisses that targeted poor Hagan every time he so much as opened his mouth. Sampson spotted the treacherous scoundrel Will up in the green boxes with a group of his racy acquaintances, and is fully certain he was one of the ringleaders of the sabotage. “I would have flung him straight into the pit,” honest Sampson swears, “but I spotted a pair of Mr. Garrick’s bruisers loitering in the lobby and had to sheer off.” All the eggs they had carefully counted on selling at market were smashed to bits before they ever got there.

Warrington had peeked out from backstage before the curtain rose, and saw the house was nearly full. He spotted Dr. Johnson in the front boxes, wearing his laced waistcoat, with his deaf friend Mr. Reynolds the painter beside him – the one man nearly blind, the other nearly deaf, the pair of them good-naturedly turning up together to judge his poor tragedy. He recognized Lady Maria in the lower gallery, wrapped in the familiar hood she always wore, delighted for the chance to watch her beloved Hagan play a leading role. As for Theo, she had confessed she could not bear to come and watch, and he had not pressed her – he knew that if things went wrong, seeing her suffer would be far more painful than any jeer from the audience, and he let her stay home without argument.

He had always prided himself on his even temper, his ability to bear good and bad fortune without losing his composure, but even he could see the day was fated for disaster. The air backstage felt gloomy, charged with bad luck. Miss Pritchard had a splitting headache. The barber who had delivered Hagan’s wig had powdered it so lopsided it looked a deliberate insult. Every face in the green room was doubtful, tight with anxiety. The flippant, rude theater manager even had the audacity to snap at him, “For Heaven’s sake, Mr. Warrington, go get a glass of punch at the Bedford and stop frightening us all half to death with your dismal face!” “Sir,” Warrington shot back, “I might pay five shillings for a seat to comment on your face, but I never gave you permission to make remarks on mine.” “Sir,” the manager huffed back, “I wish from the bottom of my heart I had never seen your face at all!” “Yours, sir,” Warrington laughed, “has entertained me greatly for years, and when you are painted up for the role of Abel Drugger you are the funniest man alive.” He bowed, turned on his heel, and walked back out to the coffee house, where he did not speak a single word to the manager for five full years, until they met by chance at a nobleman’s party, and the man apologized. Warrington brushed the whole incident off, saying he had completely forgotten the silly argument, adding “After all, there is no shame in being unsuited to writing plays. Mr. Garrick – you, sir, were born to act.” The compliment landed perfectly, and the quarrel was mended.

Sampson, his faithful ever-loyal companion, came running over after the first act to say things were going mostly well, save for that silly titter at Miss Pritchard’s entrance. “I cannot help it if Indians dress so!” Warrington laughed, pouring him a bumper of fine punch. “Why then,” Sampson argued, “would you paint Caractacus blue like an ancient Briton, or dress Boudica in nothing but a cowhide? No one mocks those plays for historical accuracy!” Warrington still half-believes that strict devotion to real history was the very thing that brought ridicule down on his tragedy – and if that is the case, he is not in the least ashamed his play failed.

After the second act, Sampson came rushing over, his face as white as a sheet, bearing terrible news. Oddly enough, though he had been nervous before the performance, Warrington found himself perfectly calm in the face of disaster. He paid his bill, put on his hat, and marched back to the theater as unhurried as if he was heading to a quiet dinner at the Temple, with Sampson prancing at his side, shoving stray link-boys out of his path and shouting “By George, Mr. Warrington, you are a man of spirit – a true Trojan, sir!” There were men of great spirit in Troy, after all – but fate was still too strong for them. No man could say Warrington did not bear his misfortune with perfect steadiness. He could no more stop the hisses and jeers of the sabotaging crowd than a ship’s captain can still a hurricane that sinks his vessel, but he stood firm in the wings for the rest of the performance, unshaken by the noise. Sampson later told Theo that not even Regulus stepping into his torture barrel could have been calmer than her husband that night. He always insisted that men of the “parasite” class like himself are never unfaithful in hard times, and Sampson proved that true – rich or poor, he was as loyal a friend as ever lived, happy to share a meager crust at the Warringtons’ table just as eagerly as he had ever feasted with them in better days.

When Warrington finally walked home after the curtain fell, he saw Theo waiting for him in the doorway, her face pale as a lily. She had already heard the news of their defeat. The supper table was decorated with the best flowers the gardener of Bedford House had given her that day, little bright tokens of welcome she had laid out for her “hero” to celebrate his triumph, and the battle had been lost entirely. He had held strong all through the performance, but the second he looked at his wife’s soft, sad face, his courage crumbled, and his heart ached sharper than it had ever ached before. The supper was quiet, mournful, though they did their best to make light conversation. They talked of old Mrs. Hagan’s lingering illness, the rumors of the King’s upcoming marriage, and the recent death of beloved Mr. Richardson, the author of *Pamela* and *Clarissa*, whose works they all adored. As they spoke of Clarissa’s tragic end, Theo wiped her eyes a time or two, and said softly how much she missed her own dear mother, who she wished could be there to comfort her. They mixed a bowl of punch spiced with Jamaica limes, drank a toast to the Governor of Virginia, and then Warrington filled his glass, smiled, and raised it for a second toast: “To better luck next time.”

That was the breaking point. Theo and Lady Maria flew into each other’s arms, sobbing all over each other’s neck handkerchiefs. “Oh Maria! Is not my George so good and kind?” Theo wept. “And my Hagan!” Lady Maria gasped, “How godlike he was in that role! It was that vile cabal that ruined him – I could plunge a knife straight into Mr. Garrick’s black little heart, the odious wretch!” She grabbed a knife from the table for a moment, then threw it aside and rushed to wrap her arms around Hagan, hugging him tight in front of all their little company. Everyone in the room was wild with shared grief and affection, and even in the middle of the disaster, the love wrapped around them was so warm and sweet Warrington almost did not regret the failed play at all.

Two or three days later, on their shared wedding birthday, a letter was brought to Warrington in his study, scrawled in his wife’s familiar handwriting:
> Returning from the cruel fight
> How pale and faint appears my knight!
> He sees me anxious at his side;
> 'Why seek, my love, your wounds to hide?
> Or deem your English girl afraid
> To emulate the Indian maid?'
>
> Be mine my husband's grief to cheer,
> In peril to be ever near;
> Whate'er of ill or woe betide,
> To bear it clinging at his side;
> The poisoned stroke of fate to ward,
> His bosom with my own to guard;
> Ah! could it spare a pang to his,
> It could not know a purer bliss!
> 'Twould gladden as it felt the smart,
> And thank the hand that flung the dart!

He will not argue that the verses are masterpieces of poetic craft, but he loves them as dearly as if they were the finest lines ever written. When he walked into the drawing room a few moments later, he found Theo blushing there, waiting for him, and her sweet young face seemed more beautiful to him than any angel’s face he could ever have imagined.
