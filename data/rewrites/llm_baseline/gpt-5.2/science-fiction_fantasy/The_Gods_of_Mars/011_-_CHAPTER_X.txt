In the outer gardens where the guard herded me, I came upon Xodar. He stood as if he had been poured from basalt—upright, silent—while a knot of noble blacks swarmed him like wasps around a fallen fruit.

They made a feast of humiliation.

Men cuffed his cheeks with open palms that cracked in the still air. Women spat—thin, bright strings that struck his ornaments and ran in ugly trails. Their voices rose and fell in harsh, metallic bursts, the way a rack of poorly tuned drums can turn a courtyard sour. The garden itself was calm enough—stone paths powdered with ruby dust, rigid shrubs trained into obedient shapes—yet the sound of it all felt like grit between the teeth.

When they saw me, their cruelty pivoted as neatly as a cook turning a skewer.

“Ah!” cried one. “So this is the creature who overcame great Xodar bare-handed. Let us see how it was done.”

“Let him bind Thurid,” a beautiful woman suggested, laughing as though cruelty were a clever spice. “Thurid is a noble Dator. Let Thurid show the dog what it means to face a real man.”

“Yes, Thurid! Thurid!” rose a dozen voices, their chant beating against the garden walls.

“Here he is now,” another exclaimed.

I followed their pointing hands and saw him advancing—huge, black, heavy with resplendent ornaments and arms, bearing himself with the careless grace of a man long accustomed to having others step aside. His jewelry chimed softly as he walked, little bright clinks like coins tossed on stone.

“What now?” he demanded. “What would you of Thurid?”

They poured their explanation over him in eager, overlapping phrases.

Thurid’s gaze cut to Xodar, narrowing to two cruel slits.

“Calot!” he hissed. “Ever did I think you carried the heart of a sorak in your putrid breast. Often have you bested me in the secret councils of Issus, but now—now in the field of war where men are truly gauged—your scabby heart hath revealed its sores to all the world. Calot, I spurn you with my foot.”

And he turned, lifting his leg for the kick—an easy, contemptuous motion, as though he meant to nudge aside refuse with the edge of a boot.

My temper, already simmering at the cowardice of a pack turning on a fallen comrade, boiled over. I had no love for Xodar; but I have never been able to watch injustice served up with laughter and not feel something in me rise like blood to the eyes.

Xodar, for his part, did not flinch. He stood ready to take their insults like a man swallowing sand because he had been told sand was holy.

Thurid’s foot swung.

So did mine.

I caught him hard on the shin bone—a flat, sharp blow that rang through him and checked the kick mid-arc. The sound of impact made a clean, ugly note, and the garden fell into sudden silence, as if every throat had been pinched closed at once.

Then Thurid roared and sprang for my throat, the same blind rush Xodar had tried upon the cruiser’s deck.

The results were the same.

I dipped beneath his outstretched arms; as he lunged past I drove my right fist into the side of his jaw. The punch landed with a satisfying solidity, like striking a ripe gourd at exactly the right angle. Thurid spun half around, his knees went soft, and the proud Dator crumpled into the ruby dust at my feet, ornaments clattering.

The blacks stared—first at Thurid sprawled ignominiously, then at me as though their eyes could not persuade their minds to accept what they had seen.

“You asked me to bind Thurid,” I cried. “Behold!”

I stooped, tore the harness from his body, and bound his arms and legs securely with the very straps meant to proclaim his rank. The leather bit into his dark skin; the knots held. It was work that needed no flourish—just firm, practiced hands and the refusal to be hurried by an audience.

“As you have done to Xodar,” I said, “now do you likewise to Thurid. Take him before Issus, bound in his own harness, that she may see with her own eyes that there be one among you now who is greater than the First Born.”

“Who are you?” whispered the woman who had first proposed the sport, her laughter gone thin.

“I am a citizen of two worlds,” I said. “Captain John Carter of Virginia, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. Take this man to your goddess as I have said—and tell her, too, that as I have done to Xodar and Thurid, so also can I do to the mightiest of her Dators. With naked hands, with long-sword or with short-sword, I challenge the flower of her fighting-men to combat.”

The officer guarding me stepped forward then, his face changed. He still held power over me, but not contempt—not after seeing Thurid fall.

“Come,” he said. “My orders are imperative; there is to be no delay. Xodar, come you also.”

There was little disrespect in his tone now. During the rest of the way he kept behind me with a drawn short-sword, not as one strolling beside a slave, but as a man who has learned caution in the presence of something he does not understand.

We returned to the Sea of Omean as we had come—through the awful shaft in the car, dropping down into that buried world where daylight is only a rumor and distance is measured by echoes. We entered the submarine, took the long dive into the tunnel far beneath the upper crust, traveled the passageway, rose again into the pool that had first introduced us to this strange artery between Omean and the Temple of Issus.

From the submarine island we were carried on a small cruiser to the distant Isle of Shador.

The journey had the dull inevitability of a chain being pulled link by link—no sudden turns, no mercy, only motion.

Shador proved to be a small stone prison with a guard of half a dozen blacks. No rites, no speeches. A key as large as a man’s forearm grated in a lock. A door swung inward. We stepped through. The door closed. The lock rasped shut.

At that sound, hopelessness came over me again—swift and cold—as it had in the Chamber of Mystery beneath the Golden Cliffs. Then Tars Tarkas had been with me. Now, of all Barsoom, I had no friendly companionship at my side.

I found myself thinking of the great Thark, and of Thuvia, the girl. Even if by some miracle they had escaped and found refuge among a friendly nation, how could they ever guess my whereabouts? No one on Barsoom dreamed of such a place as this buried sea. And even if they knew, what force could batter through the navy of the First Born and the labyrinth that guarded this vault?

No. If I allowed myself to take the measure of it, my case was hopeless.

So I refused to measure it.

I rose, shook off the brooding despair like a man shaking off a cloak that has grown too heavy, and set about learning my cage.

The building was roofless, its walls rising some thirty feet, their stone polished so smooth it caught the constant phosphorescent light and returned it in a dull sheen. Halfway up, two small windows were barred thickly enough to mock any notion of slipping through. Partitions about twenty feet high divided the interior into several rooms. In ours stood only a low stone bench.

Xodar sat on it with bowed head. Since Issus had degraded him, he had not spoken.

Two doors in our room stood open into adjoining chambers. I moved through them one by one, my boots whispering on stone. Several rooms were empty—bare benches, bare floors, the air holding the faint mineral smell of stone that has never known sun or rain.

In the last chamber I found a young red Martian boy asleep on the bench.

Something in his face snagged my attention—a familiarity I could not name. His features were regular, delicately cut, his limbs graceful even in the slackness of sleep. He was lighter in color than most red men I had known, but in all else a typical specimen of that handsome race.

I did not wake him. Sleep in prison is a ration more precious than food, and I have seen men become animals when robbed of it.

I returned to our cell. Xodar had not moved.

“Man,” I said, “it will profit you nothing to mope thus. It were no disgrace to be bested by John Carter. You have seen that in the ease with which I accounted for Thurid. You knew it before, on the cruiser’s deck, when I slew three of your comrades.”

“I would that you had dispatched me at the same time,” he said dully.

“Come, come!” I snapped, because pity and impatience are often neighbors. “There is hope yet. Neither of us is dead. We are great fighters. Why not win to freedom?”

He looked up as though I had suggested he grow wings.

“You know not of what you speak,” he replied. “Issus is omnipotent. Issus is omniscient. She hears now the words you speak. She knows the thoughts you think. It is sacrilege even to dream of breaking her commands.”

“Rot, Xodar,” I said.

He sprang to his feet, horror in every line of him.

“The curse of Issus will fall upon you!” he cried. “In another instant you will be smitten down, writhing to your death in horrible agony.”

“Do you believe that, Xodar?” I asked.

“Of course; who would dare doubt?”

“I doubt,” I said. “Yes, and further, I deny. You tell me she knows my thoughts. The red men have had that power for ages. And another: they can shut their minds so none may read them. I learned the first secret years ago; the other I never had to learn, since upon all Barsoom is none who can read what passes in the secret chambers of my brain.

“Your goddess cannot read my thoughts—nor yours when you are out of her sight, unless you will it. Had she been able to read mine, I fear her pride would have suffered a rather severe shock when I turned at her command to gaze upon the holy vision of her radiant face.”

“What do you mean?” he whispered, so low I had to lean in to catch it. Even the air seemed to listen.

“I mean,” I said, “that I thought her the most repulsive and vilely hideous creature my eyes ever had rested upon.”

For a heartbeat he only stared, as though the words had struck him across the mouth.

Then he cried, “Blasphemer!” and sprang upon me.

I had no wish to strike him, and none to hurt him. He was unarmed; to me he was dangerous only in the way a panicked man is dangerous to himself.

As he came I caught his left wrist with my left hand, swung my right arm over his left shoulder, and hooked him beneath the chin with my elbow. The motion rolled him back across my thigh. He hung there helpless, face twisted with rage, eyes glaring up at me.

“Xodar,” I said, “let us be friends. For a year, possibly, we may be forced to live together in the narrow confines of this tiny room. I am sorry to have offended you, but I could not dream that one who has suffered from the cruel injustice of Issus still could believe her divine.

“I will say a few more words, not to wound you further, but that you may think on this: while we live, we are still more the arbiters of our own fate than is any god.

“Issus has not struck me dead. Nor is she rescuing her faithful Xodar from the clutches of the unbeliever who defamed her beauty. No, Xodar—your Issus is a mortal old woman. Once out of her clutches she cannot harm you.

“With your knowledge of this strange land and my knowledge of the outer world, two such fighting-men as you and I should be able to win our way to freedom. Even though we died in the attempt, would not our memories be fairer than if we remained in servile fear to be butchered by a cruel and unjust tyrant—call her goddess or mortal, as you will?”

I set him on his feet and released him.

He did not attack again. He did not answer. He walked back to the bench, sank down upon it, and sat in silence—thinking so hard it seemed to draw the sound out of the room, leaving only the faint, constant hush of this underground world pressing against the walls like the sea presses against a hull.

Hours passed. The phosphorescent glow never changed.

At last I heard a soft sound at the doorway to one of the other chambers—a careful shift of weight, a breath caught and held. I looked up and saw the red Martian youth standing there, gazing at us with intense curiosity.

“Kaor,” I said.

“Kaor,” he replied, stepping nearer.

“What do you here?” I asked.

“I await my death, I presume,” I said with a wry twist of the mouth.

He smiled back—a brave, winning smile that made the stone cell seem less airless.

“I also,” he said. “Mine will come soon. I looked upon the radiant beauty of Issus nearly a year since. It has always been a source of keen wonder to me that I did not drop dead at the first sight of that hideous countenance. And her belly! By my first ancestor, but never was there so grotesque a figure in all the universe. That they should call such a one Goddess of Life Eternal, Goddess of Death, Mother of the Nearer Moon, and fifty other equally impossible titles, is quite beyond me.”

“How came you here?” I asked.

“It is very simple.” He spoke lightly, but there was steel beneath it. “I was flying a one-man air scout far to the south when the brilliant idea occurred to me that I should like to search for the Lost Sea of Korus, which tradition places near the south pole. I must have inherited from my father a wild lust for adventure, as well as a hollow where my bump of reverence should be.

“I had reached the area of eternal ice when my port propeller jammed, and I dropped to the ground to make repairs. Before I knew it the air was black with fliers, and a hundred of these First Born devils were leaping to the ground all about me.

“With drawn swords they made for me, but before I went down beneath them they had tasted of the steel of my father’s sword, and I had given such an account of myself as I know would have pleased my sire had he lived to witness it.”

“Your father is dead?” I asked.

“He died before the shell broke to let me step out into a world that has been very good to me. But for the sorrow that I had never the honor to know my father, I have been very happy. My only sorrow now is that my mother must mourn me as she has for ten long years mourned my father.”

“Who was your father?” I asked.

He was drawing breath to answer when the outer door of the prison clanged open. A burly guard entered, his boots loud on stone, the blunt noise of authority. He ordered the youth back to his own quarters for the night and locked the inner door after him as the boy passed into the further chamber.

Then the guard returned to our cell.

“It is Issus’ wish that you two be confined in the same room,” he said. “This cowardly slave of a slave is to serve you well,” he added, indicating Xodar with a contemptuous wave. “If he does not, you are to beat him into submission. It is Issus’ wish that you heap upon him every indignity and degradation of which you can conceive.”

With that, he left. The outer lock grated again, the sound lingering like an aftertaste.

Xodar sat with his face buried in his hands.

I crossed to him and put my hand on his shoulder.

“Xodar,” I said, “you have heard the commands of Issus, but you need not fear that I shall attempt to put them into execution. You are a brave man. It is your own affair if you wish to be persecuted and humiliated; but were I you I should assert my manhood and defy my enemies.”

He lifted his head slowly. When he spoke, his voice had changed: less broken, more deliberate, as if he had been chewing bitter roots until he could name their flavor.

“I have been thinking very hard, John Carter,” he said, “of all the new ideas you gave me a few hours since. Little by little I have been piecing together the things you said which sounded blasphemous to me then with the things I have seen in my past life and dared not even think about for fear of bringing down upon me the wrath of Issus.

“I believe now that she is a fraud; no more divine than you or I. More I am willing to concede—that the First Born are no holier than the Holy Therns, nor the Holy Therns more holy than the red men.

“The whole fabric of our religion is based on superstitious belief in lies that have been foisted upon us for ages by those directly above us, to whose personal profit and aggrandizement it was to have us continue to believe as they wished us to believe.

“I am ready to cast off the ties that have bound me. I am ready to defy Issus herself; but what will it avail us? Be the First Born gods or mortals, they are a powerful race, and we are as fast in their clutches as though we were already dead. There is no escape.”

“I have escaped from bad plights in the past, my friend,” I said. “Nor while life is in me shall I despair of escaping from the Isle of Shador and the Sea of Omean.”

“But we cannot escape even from the four walls of our prison,” he urged, striking the rock with his fist. The sound was a dull thud that went nowhere. “Test this flint-like surface. And look upon this polished face—none could cling to it to reach the top.”

I smiled, because the wall was the sort of problem a man can put his hands on, and therefore the sort of problem a man can solve.

“That is the least of our troubles, Xodar,” I said. “I will guarantee to scale the wall and take you with me, if you will help with your knowledge of the customs here to appoint the best time for the attempt, and guide me to the shaft that lets from the dome of this abysmal sea to the light of God’s pure air above.”

“Night time is the best and offers the only slender chance,” he said, “for then men sleep, and only a dozing watch nods in the tops of the battleships. No watch is kept upon the cruisers and smaller craft. The watchers upon the larger vessels see to all about them. It is night now.”

“But,” I said, glancing at the steady, eerie glow on stone, “it is not dark. How can it be night, then?”

He gave a small, humorless smile.

“You forget,” he said, “that we are far below ground. The light of the sun never penetrates here. There are no moons and no stars reflected in the bosom of Omean. The phosphorescent light you see pervading this great subterranean vault emanates from the rocks that form its dome; it is always thus upon Omean—just as the billows are always as you see them, rolling, ever rolling over a windless sea.

“At the appointed hour of night upon the world above, the men whose duties hold them here sleep—but the light is ever the same.”

“It will make escape more difficult,” I said. Then I shrugged; an easy task has never fed my appetite for life. “Let us sleep on it to-night.”

“A plan may come with our awakening,” Xodar said.

So we threw ourselves upon the hard stone floor and slept the sleep of tired men—while beyond our walls the windless sea rolled on, forever sounding like a distant, restless breath.
