When the house had sunk into its nightly order—dinner cleared away; the loud recreation-hour spent; the externes gone, the bell no longer clashing like a little factory’s heart; and the refectory lamp set to its quiet burn for study—I felt the whole building change its sound. Day had been a jangling of doors, shoes, voices, and commands; evening was a thinner music: distant murmurs stitched with long pauses, the hush between one footfall and another, the soft cough of a stove drawing, the far drip of water in a pipe. In such a hush, one dares to hear one’s own hopes; and I had a hope folded small and sealed, which I had carried all day as women carry a crust saved for later—pressed close, warmed by the body, guarded from greedy hands.

Madame Beck was established below in her salle-à-manger with her mother and friends; the house, for half an hour, seemed to have no eye on me. I glided to the kitchen, where the air was thick with that domestic perfume which makes a school almost bearable—burnt onion skins, warm bread, the metal scent of scoured pots, and in a corner a faint sourness of damp coffee-grounds (here used, with thrifty invention, for cleansing as other places use tea-leaves). My friend Goton, who could be coaxed by a soft word as readily as she could brown a ragout by a steady hand, gave me my bougie.

“Mais certainement, chou-chou,” she said, pressing it on me as if she were giving a sweetmeat to a child. “Vous en aurez deux, si vous voulez.”

I took the light—one slim candle, precious as a stolen minute—and went up, noiseless, to the dormitory, meaning to feast there alone on my letter as one eats in secret when one has too little and fears interruption.

But ill-luck—always a listener—had lain in wait. A pupil was already in bed, indisposed; and when my bougie threw its small circle of gold on muslin and nightcap borders, I recognized, crumpled in the sheets, the “figure chiffonnée” of Ginevra Fanshawe. She lay supine, a picture of delicacy; yet even in that stillness there was a watchfulness that vexed me—an eyelid’s faint quiver like the twitch of a cat feigning sleep. She was capable of waking at the very moment my heart would be most naked, and then pouring out chatter upon it as one pours cold water on hot sugar, spoiling all.

I would not be robbed so. I went down again, letter still unopened, and tried the class-rooms. They too betrayed me. It was the weekly sweeping and purification by candle-light: benches piled on desks; dust floating like a pale fog; the floor darkened with wet coffee-grounds, trampled into little muddy constellations; a scene of disorder where solitude could not be had, for even emptiness there was busy.

Baffled, but not beaten, I resolved to find a place no one used—some forgotten corner of the house, where even Madame’s vigilance did not reach. I took a key from where I knew it was kept, mounted three staircases one after another, and came at last to a narrow landing where the air turned colder, as if the warmth of living had been left below. A worm-eaten door yielded; I slipped through, and entered the garret.

It was deep, black, and wintry; the roof-beams above seemed to stoop; and the air smelt of damp wood, mould, and old cloth—a kind of poverty-scent, like clothing shut away with time and neglect. Here, surely, none would follow—not Madame herself. I shut the door, set my bougie on a doddered chest of drawers, and, shivering, drew a shawl about my shoulders. Then I took out my letter—my small stored portion—and broke the seal with hands that trembled as if they had been hungry long.

Will it be long—will it be short? I thought, wiping my eyes, for a soft dimness had come over them, like a south-wind shower that blinds by sweetness.

It was long.

Will it be cool—will it be kind?

It was kind.

Kind, to my bridled and disciplined expectation: kinder, perhaps, to my famished thought than the strict truth would warrant. Yet so little had I dared to hope, so much had I trained myself to fear, that even this simple good-nature tasted like a feast. There are people who sit daily at loaded tables and never know what a mouthful means; and there are others who, in a frosty garret, by a guttering candle, can be made richer than queens by a page of friendly words. I was that poor English teacher; and the letter—nothing more, in itself, than a kindly outpouring—seemed, in that instant, like grace.

Dr. John had written at length; had written with pleasure; had written in a mood so sunny it warmed the very paper. He recalled scenes we had shared, places we had visited, conversations we had held—little matters, trifles, the small daily bread of intimacy; and the cordial core of my delight was the conviction that he had not poured it out merely to content me, but to gratify himself. It might never happen again; indeed, every sober probability whispered it would not. But that belonged to the future. This present moment had no pain in it, no want, no blot. Full, pure, perfect, it blessed me so deeply I felt as if something gentle had leaned near—some passing minister of peace—and laid a cool wing on the hard, hot throb of my heart.

And yet—are there influences that begrudge human bliss? Are there presences, not human, that creep close when joy grows keen, to sour the air? For in that vast solitude something sounded.

At first it might have been only the settling of beams, the sigh of a draught under the slates; but then, most surely, I heard a stealthy foot—gliding—coming, as it seemed, from the black recess where the malefactor cloaks hung. I turned. The candle’s light was small, the room long; shadows gathered like old secrets in the corners. Then—reader, say what you will, call it nerves, call it madness, declare it dream: I saw, in the middle of that ghostly chamber, a figure all black and white. The skirts straight and narrow, black; the head bandaged and veiled, white.

An image like—a NUN.

A cry broke from me; my stomach turned cold. Had the shape advanced, I think I should have fallen senseless. It did not advance. It receded; and I, forgetting pride, forgetting all but flight, made for the door.

How I got down those stairs I cannot tell. Instinct drove me away from the refectory; I went straight to Madame’s sitting-room, and burst in as if the room were on fire.

“There is something in the grenier,” I said. “I have been there: I saw something. Go and look at it—all of you!”

The room, in my fear, seemed full; in truth, there were four: Madame Beck; her mother, Madame Kint, infirm and visiting; her brother, M. Victor Kint; and another gentleman with his back to me, speaking to the old lady. My terror must have bleached me: I felt my own face like chilled wax, my limbs shaking with an inward frost. They rose together, alarmed; they gathered round; they questioned; I urged them upward, and the sight of the men gave me a strange courage—as if a deeper voice and heavier step could rout what had glided and stared.

I turned to lead them; and then remembrance struck me like a blow. My letter—left on the drawers beside the bougie.

That letter was my portion, my hoarded sweetness; and for it I would defy flesh or spirit. I flew up again, faster because I heard them following. They had no choice but to come.

When I reached the garret-door, the darkness within was thick as a pit. My light was out. The candle, quenched, stood on the drawers like a dead thing. Someone—Madame, I think, with her cool sense—had brought a lamp; and as they came up, its ray pierced the black.

There was the bougie. But where was the letter?

In that moment the nun, the fear, the superstition—all fell away before a sharper anguish. I did not search for a ghost: I searched for paper. I dropped to my knees, groping on the floor, my hands sweeping dust and splinters, my throat torn with one cry.

“My letter! my letter!”

Cruel doom—to have one’s little comfort snatched away, preternaturally, before one has even finished tasting it. They spoke around me; they rummaged in corners; they muttered about cloaks disturbed and some breach in the skylight; I heard it as one hears talk through water. I only knew this: my letter was gone.

“Oh! they have taken my letter!” I sobbed, and I think I wrung my hands like a child.

“What letter, Lucy? My dear girl, what letter?” asked a voice close to my ear.

That voice—known, long-remembered in its very kindness—made my breath stop. I looked up.

In the dim garret, among the mouldy drawers and dangling cloaks, was the face of the writer of that letter—John Graham, Dr. Bretton himself.

Yes: he had been sent for that evening to prescribe for Madame Kint; he was the second gentleman in the salle-à-manger when I burst in, wild with fear.

“Was it my letter, Lucy?” he asked.

“Your own—yours—the letter you wrote to me. I came here to read it quietly. I couldn’t find another place to have it to myself. I saved it all day—never opened it till this evening. I scarcely glanced over it. I cannot bear to lose it. Oh, my letter!”

“Hush,” he said, and his tone—so firm, so full of warm authority—was like a hand laid on a fever. “Don’t cry and distress yourself so. What is it worth? Come out of this cold room. They will send for the police, no doubt, and examine further. We need not stay here. Come—down.”

He took my cold fingers in his warm hand and led me to a room where there was fire. The stove breathed steadily; its sound was the sound of safety. Dr. John and I sat before it. He spoke to me—soothing me with a goodness that did not ask leave, promising twenty letters for the one lost.

There are words that wound like knives—serrated, poisoned, leaving cuts that never quite heal. But there are also tones of consolation so fine the ear keeps their echo all life: kindnesses that, when darkness gathers, answer back from memory with undimmed shine. Whatever people told me later—that Dr. Bretton was not so perfect as I believed; that his nature lacked depth and endurance—was nothing to the truth I knew in that hour. He was as good to me as water to thirst, as sun to one shut in cold walls. I remember him heroic; heroic I will hold him.

He smiled and asked why I cared for his letter so very much. I thought—but did not say—that I prized it as I prized the blood in my veins. I only answered that I had so few letters to care for.

“I am sure you did not read it,” he said; “or you would think nothing of it.”

“I read it—only once. I wanted to read it again. I am sorry it is lost.” And I wept afresh, helplessly.

“Lucy—Lucy—my poor little god-sister (if such a kinship exists)—here—here is your letter. Why is it not better worth such tears, and such tenderly exaggerating faith?”

He drew it out as he spoke.

It was his curious, characteristic manoeuvre. His quick eye had seen the letter on the floor where I groped; his hand, as quick, had snatched it up; he had hidden it in his waistcoat pocket. Had my trouble been a shade cooler—had my tears been but a degree less true—I doubt whether he would have restored it at all. Dr. John could be amused by pain he did not deem deep.

Yet the joy of regaining made me forget the torment of the trick. My relief shone out, despite myself; I said little, but my face must have betrayed the whole feast of it—like a hungry creature suddenly handed back its bread.

“Are you satisfied now?” he asked.

“Satisfied—and happy,” I answered.

“Well then,” he went on, “how do you feel physically? Are you growing calmer? Not much: you tremble still like a leaf.”

I insisted I was composed; I no longer felt the first terror.

“Then you can tell me what you saw. Your account was vague. You only spoke of ‘something.’ Was it a man? An animal? What was it?”

“I will never tell exactly what I saw,” I said, “unless someone else sees it too. Otherwise I shall be discredited and accused of dreaming.”

“Tell me,” he urged. “I will hear it professionally. I look at you now as a doctor: your eye is too vivid and restless; your cheek has lost its blood; your hand will not steady. Come, Lucy—speak.”

“You would laugh.”

“If you don’t tell me, you shall have no more letters.”

“You are laughing now.”

“I will take away that single epistle again—being mine, I think I have a right to reclaim it.”

His raillery made me grave. I folded up the letter and covered it from sight, as one covers food from pilfering hands.

“You may hide it,” he said, “but I can possess it any moment I choose. You don’t know my skill in sleight of hand. I could be a conjuror. Mamma says sometimes, too, that I have a harmonizing property of tongue and eye; but you never saw that in me—did you, Lucy?”

“Indeed—indeed—when you were a mere boy I used to see both: far more then than now; for now you are strong, and strength dispenses with subtlety. But still—Dr. John—you have what they call here ‘un air fin,’ that nobody can mistake. Madame Beck saw it, and—”

“And liked it,” he broke in, laughing; “because she has it herself. But, Lucy, give me that letter—you don’t really care for it.”

I would not answer. A man in mirth must not always be humoured. And besides, there was a new sort of smile about his lips—sweet, yet it pained me without my knowing why; a new light in his eyes—not hostile, but not reassuring. Something in it made my heart draw back, as a tongue draws back from a too-bright blade.

I rose to go, and bid him good-night, a little sadly. His peculiar sensitiveness—his quick faculty for detecting what is unspoken—caught the complaint in my silence. He asked quietly if I was offended.

I shook my head.

“Permit me, then, to speak seriously before you go. You are in a highly nervous state. I feel sure—whatever control you have—that while alone this evening in that dismal, sepulchral garret—smelling of damp and mould, rank with cold—an air to breed catarrh and worse; a place you never ought to enter—you saw, or thought you saw, something calculated to impress the imagination. You are not subject to common terrors—robbers and such; but a thing bearing a spectral character might shake your mind. Be calm. This is a matter of nerves. Still—specify the vision.”

“You will tell nobody?”

“Nobody—most certainly. You may trust me as implicitly as you did Père Silas. Indeed, the doctor is perhaps the safer confessor of the two, though he has not grey hair.”

“You will not laugh?”

“Perhaps I may—to do you good; but not in scorn. Lucy, I feel as a friend towards you, though your timid nature is slow to trust.”

Now he did look like a friend. The strange sparkle was gone; the arched curves of lip, nostril, eyebrow softened; attention sobered his face. Won at last, I told him exactly what I had seen: the black and white figure, the white veiling, the fixed glitter of eyes. He already knew the house-legend; I had once related it to him on a mild October afternoon as we rode through Bois l’Etang, when the leaves fell in a soft whisper and the hoof-beats made a gentle percussion on the road.

He sat thinking; and while he thought, we heard the others coming down-stairs—a drift of voices and steps, the house’s soundscape thickening again with human talk.

“Are they going to interrupt?” he asked, glancing at the door with annoyance.

“They will not come here,” I answered; for we were in the little salon where Madame never sat at evening, and the stove’s warmth lingered only by chance.

They passed the door and went on to the salle-à-manger.

“Now,” he said, “they will talk about thieves and burglars. Let them. Say nothing. Keep your resolution of describing your nun to nobody. She may appear to you again: don’t start.”

“You think, then,” I whispered, with secret horror, “she came out of my brain, and is now gone in there, and may glide out again when I look not for her?”

“I think it a case of spectral illusion,” he replied. “I fear it results from long-continued mental conflict.”

“Oh, Dr. John—I shudder at being liable to such illusion! It seemed so real. Is there no cure—no preventive?”

“Happiness is the cure,” he said. “A cheerful mind the preventive: cultivate both.”

No mockery ever sounded to me so hollow as that counsel. Cultivate happiness! As if it were a crop one could sow, water, weed, and harvest. Happiness is not a potato to be planted in mould and fed with manure; it is a light that sometimes falls—rarely—out of heaven, like dew on a summer morning one did not order and cannot compel.

“Cultivate happiness,” I repeated briefly. “Do you cultivate happiness? How do you manage?”

“I am a cheerful fellow by nature,” he answered; “and ill-luck has never dogged me. Adversity gave me and my mother one passing scowl and brush, but we defied her—or rather laughed at her—and she went by.”

“There is no cultivation in all this.”

“I do not give way to melancholy.”

“Yes: I have seen you subdued by that feeling.”

“About Ginevra Fanshawe—eh?”

“Did she not sometimes make you miserable?”

“Pooh! stuff! nonsense! You see I am better now.”

If a laughing eye, bright with healthy energy, could prove it, he was better indeed. His very voice had a ring to it like metal struck clear.

“You do not look much amiss,” I granted.

“And why, Lucy, can’t you look and feel as I do—buoyant, courageous, fit to defy all the nuns and flirts in Christendom? I would give gold on the spot just to see you snap your fingers. Try the manœuvre.”

“If I were to bring Miss Fanshawe into your presence just now?”

“I vow she should not move me,” he said, “or she should move me by one thing only—true and passionate love. I would accord forgiveness at no less a price.”

“Indeed! a smile of hers would have been a fortune to you a while since.”

“Transformed, Lucy—transformed!” He rose, drawing on his gloves, and in the carriage of his head and the ease of his mien there was a liberty more than comfort—a disdain of past bondage. “Remember, you once called me a slave; but I am a free man now.”

“Ah, Doctor—Doctor! You said it was your nature to pursue love under difficulties—to be charmed by proud insensibility.”

He laughed. “My nature varies. The mood of one hour is sometimes the mockery of the next. Well, Lucy—will the Nun come again to-night, think you?”

“I don’t think she will.”

“Give her my compliments if she does—Dr. John’s compliments—and entreat her to have the goodness to wait a visit from him. Lucy—was she a pretty nun? Had she a pretty face? You have not told me that yet; and that is the really important point.”

“She had a white cloth over her face,” I said; “but her eyes glittered.”

“Confusion to her goblin trappings!” he cried, irreverent. “But at least she had handsome eyes—bright and soft?”

“Cold and fixed,” I answered.

“No, no—we’ll none of her. She shall not haunt you. Give her that shake of the hand if she comes again. Will she stand that, do you think?”

I thought it too cordial for a ghost to endure. And his smile—matching the kindness of his “Good-night”—was too human, too warm, too living for any spectre to bear.

Afterwards, what had they found in the garret? Very little. At first they spoke of cloaks disturbed; but Madame Beck told me later she thought they hung much as usual. As for a broken pane in the skylight, she said that aperture was rarely without some crack or gap; and a hail-storm had fallen not many days before.

Madame questioned me closely as to what I had seen. I described only an obscure figure in black; I would not breathe the word “nun,” sure that word would at once make it romance in her mind, and romance—so named—would be dismissed as folly. She charged me to speak of it to no servant, pupil, or teacher, and commended my discretion in coming to her private room rather than spreading terror through the refectory.

So the subject dropped.

And I was left, secretly and sadly, to turn it over alone: whether that strange thing belonged to this world, or to one beyond the grave; or whether it was only the child of malady—and I, the prey of that child.
