Lona was repulsed by the people—especially the women—and longed to flee the city at once. But I could not abandon hope so easily. To retreat would be to surrender without a fight, and worse, to shatter the courage of the Little Ones, leaving them exposed to greater peril. If we fled, the princess would surely pursue us. But if we faced her, perhaps the prophecy might still be fulfilled. Mother and daughter must meet. Maybe Lona’s beauty could awaken something in Lilith—something softer than hatred. If she turned violent, I would stand between them. And if all else failed, I would strike her down—without mercy, without hesitation—for the sake of my Lona. I knew her fate was sealed; perhaps it was written that she would fall by our hands.

I told Lona this plainly, without revealing the truth of their bond. She listened, then nodded. “We go,” she said. And so we did.

From the highest tower of the palace, the princess had seen the army of the Little Ones approach at dawn, while the city still slept. The sight struck her with a terror beyond measure. Her plan to destroy them had failed. Now they were upon her. The prophecy loomed close. When she regained herself, she descended to the black hall and took her place in the northern focus of the elliptical chamber, beneath the skylight. There she would think. But for her, *thinking* meant seeing herself not as she was, but as she wished to be—radiant, perfect, divine. To aid this illusion, she had hung a mirror above and slightly before her, invisible in the darkness, to catch the sunlight reflected from her own form. Only when the sun reached its zenith would she see herself in full glory.

The shadows danced around her, but she ignored them. With her inner vision, she perceived their presence, yet refused to acknowledge them. Close beneath the mirror stood the Shadow—the silent companion who followed her wherever she went—but she did not see him. The city had fallen. Its people cowered. The Little Ones and their strange beasts occupied the square. The sun rose, and for a brief moment, the princess saw herself as she desired: magnificent, flawless, untouched by time or sin. Then the vision faded. Darkness returned. Yet she remained seated. The night deepened, and the glass held only void. Still she sat. The palace groaned with unseen presences. Servants trembled, too afraid to leave, bound by fear of the creatures outside. All night long, she waited—motionless—desperate to see her beauty again, desperate to think again. But courage had abandoned her. Will had fled. She was hollow.

At dawn, we chose twelve of the tallest, bravest boys to accompany us. We rode our great horses; they mounted smaller steeds and elephants. As we climbed toward the palace, the sun rose higher, creeping across the dome of the great hall. It pierced the eye of the structure—and suddenly, the light flooded in.

The princess gasped. She sprang up with a cry of horror. Her whiteness—her perfect skin—was marred. A vast black spot covered half her side, like a brand burned into flesh. She clutched her robe, collapsed back into her chair. The Shadow slipped away—unseen, unheeded.

We entered through the open gate, passed the paved grove, and stepped into the vestibule. There, in her cage, lay the spotted leopardess—still, motionless, perhaps dead. The Little Ones paused. The beast leapt up, snarling, against the bars. Horses reared. Elephants stepped back. Then, with a sudden convulsion, she fell backward, twitching, and lay still.

We rode into the great hall. The princess still sat in the shaft of sunlight when the sound of hooves echoed from the courtyard. She jerked upright, listening, trembling. No such noise had ever disturbed her palace. She pressed a hand to her side, breath catching in her throat. The trampling drew closer—into the hall itself. Figures emerged from the dark, not shadows, but living beings.

To us, she was a vision—a woman of radiant beauty, centered in darkness. Lona dismounted, running forward. I followed.

“Mother! Mother!” she called, her voice pure and clear, echoing through the dome.

The princess froze. Her face twisted with rage. Her brows met in a line of hatred. She rose.

“Mother! Mother!” Lona cried again, leaping onto the dais, arms outstretched.

I was almost there—just one step—when I saw it: Lona lifted high, thrown violently onto the marble floor. The sound—crash, thud, silence—shattered me.

She fell at my feet, still. I dropped to my knees, lifted her, cradled her in my arms. My eyes burned with fury as I looked at the princess. She smiled—sweet, cruel, triumphant.

I would have torn her apart. But love for Lona overpowered hatred. I held her tighter. Her arms hung limp. Blood seeped from her temple, dripping slowly onto the stone—soft, quiet plashes.

The horses smelled it. Mine reared, wild-eyed, and charged blindly down the hall. The others followed. One of Lona’s small horses stood frozen, staring at her, trembling. The boys jumped off—too late. They crashed into the wall, broken, lifeless. The elephants advanced to the dais, roaring in panic. The Little Ones scrambled up, horrified. The princess slumped back in her seat—face pale as death, eyes blazing with malice. She was shriveled, wasted, her side scarred as though branded by fire.

But I saw none of it. I saw only Lona.

“Mother… mother…” she whispered, then her breath stopped.

I carried her into the courtyard. The sun lit her white face. A ghostly smile seemed to touch her lips. Her head fell back. She was gone—dead as earth.

I forgot the Little Ones. Forgot the princess. Forgot the body in my arms. I wandered, searching for my Lona—my lost queen.

I rose. I would follow the Little Ones. Instinct led me to the gate we’d entered by. I turned—no leopardess. The street filled with a surging crowd. They saw me burdened with the dead, but dared not attack—at first.

Then, a man pushed past me, brushing against Lona. I kicked him—hard. He screamed, fell back.

But the mob closed in. Fearing harm to the body, I clutched her tighter, freed my right arm.

Then—behind me—chaos erupted. The crowd parted. Through it came the Little Ones.

Ten rode four elephants. On the other two lay the princess—bound, silent, her eyes rolling in their sockets. Two more rode behind her on Lona’s horse. Every now and then, the elephants turned their trunks and tested her bonds.

I walked ahead, out of the city.

What an end to the hopes that brought us here. We captured the wicked princess. But we lost our beloved queen. My world was stripped bare. My heart—empty.
