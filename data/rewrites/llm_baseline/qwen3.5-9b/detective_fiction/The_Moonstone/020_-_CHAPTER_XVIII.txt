Descending the steps toward the front door, I encountered the Sergeant. It was entirely contrary to my nature, following the recent exchange between us, to display any semblance of interest in his investigations. Yet, despite my resistance, a compelling curiosity took hold that I could not suppress. My sense of personal dignity crumbled beneath the weight of necessity, and the words escaped me involuntarily: "What news concerning the Indians?"

"I have seen the Indians," replied Sergeant Cuff. "Furthermore, I have identified exactly what Rosanna purchased privately in the town on Thursday past. The Indians will be released on Wednesday of next week. There is no shadow of doubt in my mind, nor in Mr. Murthwaite's, that they arrived here specifically to steal the Moonstone. Naturally, their plans were thwarted by the events within the house on Wednesday night; consequently, they bear no responsibility for the actual disappearance of the jewel any more than you do. However, I must impart a crucial warning, Mr. Betteredge: if *we* fail to locate the Moonstone, *they* certainly will. You have not heard the final word regarding the three jugglers."

Mr. Franklin emerged from his walk precisely as the Sergeant uttered these alarming words. Governing his curiosity with greater restraint than I had managed my own, he passed us silently and retreated inside. As for me, having already surrendered my dignity, I resolved to extract every ounce of information from this revelation.

"So much for the Indians," I pressed. "Pray, what of Rosanna?"

Sergeant Cuff shook his head slowly. "The mystery surrounding her has deepened rather than clarified," he stated. "I have tracked her to a shop in Frizinghall belonging to a linen draper named Maltby. She visited no other drapers', milliners', or tailors'; indeed, she purchased nothing at Maltby's except a length of fabric. She was exceedingly particular regarding the quality. As for the quantity, she acquired sufficient material to construct a single nightgown."

"Whose nightgown?" I queried.

"Hers, undoubtedly," the Sergeant replied. "Sometime between noon and three o'clock on Thursday morning, she must have slipped down to your young lady's chamber to finalize the concealment of the Moonstone while the household slept. Upon returning to her own quarters, her nightgown inevitably brushed against the wet paint on the door. Unable to wash out the stain, and unable to safely destroy the garment without first procuring a replacement to maintain the appearance of her linen inventory, she was forced to act as she did."

"Which leads to the question," I interjected, "whose nightgown was it?"

"The material she purchased for the substitute dress confirms this," Cuff explained. "Had it been Miss Verinder's gown, she would have required lace, frills, and countless other embellishments; furthermore, she lacked the time to fashion such an item in a single night. Plain long cloth indicates a plain servant's nightgown. Everything else is clear. The crux of the matter remains: why, after securing the replacement dress, did she choose to hide the stained original rather than destroy it? If the girl refuses to speak, there is but one recourse. The hiding-place at the Shivering Sand must be searched, and the truth revealed therein."

"How do you intend to locate the place?" I inquired.

"I regret to disappoint you," the Sergeant said, "but that is a secret I intend to retain exclusively." (To spare your curiosity, though he provoked mine, I may disclose that he had returned from Frizinghall armed with a search warrant. His extensive experience suggested that Rosanna likely carried a memorandum of the hiding-place upon her person—a guide should she return under altered circumstances or after a significant lapse of time. Possessing this document, the Sergeant would possess everything he needed.)

"Now, Mr. Betteredge," he continued, "let us abandon speculation and engage in business. I instructed Joyce to monitor Rosanna closely. Where is Joyce?"

Joyce was the Frizinghall constable assigned to Sergeant Cuff by Superintendent Seegrave. The clock struck two as he posed the question; punctually, the carriage arrived to escort Miss Rachel to her aunt's residence.

"One task at a time," the Sergeant commanded, intercepting me as I prepared to summon Joyce. "I must attend to Miss Verinder first."

Since the rain remained a threat, the enclosed carriage had been designated for Miss Rachel's journey to Frizinghall. Sergeant Cuff signaled Samuel to descend from the rumble seat behind.

"You will observe a friend of mine concealed among the trees on this side of the lodge gate," he instructed. "He will board the rumble with you without halting the carriage. Your sole duty is to remain silent and keep your eyes closed. Failure to comply will result in serious trouble."

With this directive, he dismissed the footman to his station. I could not fathom Samuel's thoughts, but it was evident to me that Miss Rachel was to be secretly surveilled from the moment she departed our home—or wherever she actually went. A guard stationed on my young lady! A spy lurking in the rumble of her mother's carriage! I felt a profound urge to sever my own tongue for the foolishness of addressing Sergeant Cuff in the first place.

The first figure to emerge from the house was my lady. She positioned herself on the top step, motionless, intent on witnessing the unfolding scene. She spoke not a word to either the Sergeant or me. With lips sealed and arms crossed tightly around herself within the light garden cloak she had draped over her shoulders against the chill air, she stood as still as a statue, awaiting her daughter's appearance.

Moments later, Miss Rachel descended the stairs. She was elegantly attired in a soft yellow fabric that complemented her dark complexion, tailored snugly into a jacket style at the waist. She wore a dainty straw hat adorned with a white veil twisted securely around it, along with primrose-colored gloves that fit her hands like a second skin. Her magnificent black hair lay smooth as satin beneath her hat, and her small ears resembled rosy shells, each pierced with a dangling pearl. She moved swiftly toward us, upright as a lily on its stem, her movements as lithe and supple as a young cat. While nothing outwardly had altered in her lovely countenance, her eyes and lips betrayed a change. Her gaze burned with a brightness and ferocity that displeased me, and her lips had lost all color and smile, rendering them almost unrecognizable.

She approached her mother and delivered a hurried, abrupt kiss to her cheek. "Try to forgive me, mamma," she murmured, then violently pulled her veil down over her face, tearing it in the process. In an instant, she sprinted down the steps and threw herself into the carriage as if seeking refuge.

Sergeant Cuff was equally swift. He guided Samuel back into position and stood before Miss Rachel, holding the open carriage door just as she settled inside.

"What do you require?" Miss Rachel demanded from behind her veil.

"I wish to say one word to you, miss," the Sergeant responded, "before you depart. I cannot presume to detain your visit to your aunt. I can only venture to suggest that your departure under current circumstances creates an impediment to my recovery of your Diamond. Please comprehend this fully, and then decide for yourself whether to proceed or remain."

Miss Rachel offered not a single reply.

"Drive on, James!" she shouted to the coachman.

Without further hesitation, the Sergeant closed the carriage door. Just as it shut, Mr. Franklin burst down the steps.

"Good-bye, Rachel," he called, extending his hand.

"Drive on!" Miss Rachel cried, her voice rising above his, ignoring Mr. Franklin just as she had ignored the Sergeant.

Mr. Franklin recoiled, thunderstruck. The coachman, uncertain of his orders, glanced toward my lady, who remained immobile on the top step. My lady, her face a tumultuous blend of anger, sorrow, and shame, signaled the horses to move forward, then hastily retreated indoors.

Mr. Franklin, regaining his composure, called after her as the carriage rolled away, "Aunt! You were entirely correct. Accept my gratitude for your kindness—and permit me to withdraw."

My lady turned as if to respond, but then, seemingly distrustful of her own emotions, waved her hand kindly. "Let me see you before you leave us, Franklin," she said, her voice breaking, and withdrew into her own room.

"Do me one last favor, Betteredge," Mr. Franklin pleaded, turning to me with tears in his eyes. "Arrange for my immediate transport to the train!"

He too retired into the house. For the moment, Miss Rachel had utterly disarmed him. One need only judge from this to realize the depth of his affection for her.

Left facing each other at the base of the steps, the Sergeant stood gazing intently at a gap in the trees, overlooking a winding section of the drive leading from the house. His hands were in his pockets, and he softly whistled "The Last Rose of Summer."

"There is a time for everything," I remarked somewhat savagely. "This is not the time for whistling."

At that precise moment, the carriage appeared in the distance through the gap, heading toward the lodge gate. Beside Samuel in the rumble, another man was clearly visible.
