Romola hurried through the dusk with a single unshakable purpose burning bright in her chest, a resolve that had swelled through every hour of the afternoon like a small stream rising to merge with the full, rushing current of her heart’s desperation. This was no casual choice: it was a necessity her soul could not outrun. She paid no mind to the darkening, uneven cobblestones of the streets, and did not bother to rouse slow old Maso to escort her, her boots tapping fast across the Arno bridge where the river had already turned inky black beneath the last fading streaks of crimson sunset on the horizon. She took the shortest possible route straight to the Palazzo Vecchio, the Old Palace. She might encounter her husband Tito there, for all she knew. It did not matter. She could not stop to weigh odds or second-guess herself: she had to unburden the terrible weight clamped around her heart. She barely registered the carved pillars of the courtyard as she passed through, barely felt the wide stone stairs under her feet, only stopping long enough to give her name to an usher and beg to be shown to a private room to speak with the Gonfaloniere.

She was not left alone for long with the faded frescoed figures on the walls and the newly lit guttering tallow candles. Soon the door swung open, and Bernardo del Nero stepped in, his white head held as proudly erect as ever above the heavy folds of his silk lucco robe. “Romola, my child, what is this that brings you here?” he asked, his voice thick with anxious surprise as he shut the door firmly behind him. She had already pushed her hood back, and she walked toward him without a word. He rested a firm, gentle hand on her shoulder, holding her a little distance away to get a clear look at her face. It was haggard from hours of fatigue and unceasing agitation, her hair spilling loose in tangled disorder, but there was a bright, burning fire in her eyes that seemed to triumph over every trace of bodily weariness. “What has he done to you?” Bernardo said bluntly. “Tell me everything, child. Throw aside your pride. I am your father in all but blood.”

“It is not about myself—nothing at all to do with my own grievances,” Romola said quickly, her words tumbling over each other in her haste. “Dearest godfather, it is you who is in danger. I have heard things—some of which I cannot bring myself to speak aloud. But you are not safe here in the palace, you are not safe anywhere in Florence. There are fanatical men who wish you harm, and worse—there are traitors moving among your supposed allies. Trust no one. If you place your confidence in the wrong person, you will be betrayed.”

Bernardo smiled a soft, pitying smile, raising his hand to pat her tangled hair gently. “Have you worked yourself up into this terrible agitation, my poor child,” he said, “just to tell an old man like me a truth I have known for forty years?”

“Oh no, no! These are not old truths,” Romola cried, clasping her hands so tight her knuckles blanched, as if the sharp pain would help her hold back the forbidden secret she dared not name. “They are fresh, terrible new things I cannot tell. Dearest godfather, you know I am no foolish, flighty girl. I would never come rushing to you like this without grave reason. Is it too late to warn you against every single person who claims to be fighting on your side? Is it too late to beg you to go to your country villa and stay far away once these last three days of your term in office are over? Oh God, perhaps it is already too late! If any harm comes to you, it will feel as if I brought it on you myself!” The last words burst out involuntarily, a long-stifled sob tearing through her. She flinched, correcting herself hesitantly, “I— I have no solid, concrete proof. I only know what fills me with these terrible, unshakable fears.”

“Poor child,” Bernardo said, looking at her with quiet, sharp penetration for a long moment. Then he spoke again, slow and steady. “Go home, Romola, rest. These fears of yours are likely nothing but big, ugly shadows cast by something tiny and harmless. Even traitors only betray for profit—rats run only where they can smell cheese, and no one yet knows which way that scent will blow.” He paused, his gaze drifting away from her, lost in thought, before he gave a slow, heavy shrug. “And warnings are of no use to me, child. I enter into no plots, but I will never abandon the colours I have sworn to stand for. If I march shoulder to shoulder with stubborn men who will run headfirst into pikes and gunfire, I must share whatever consequences come of it. We will say no more of this. I have so few years left at the bottom of the sack of my life, there is little left for them to steal. Go, child; go home and rest.”

He rested his hand on her head again, tender and familiar, and she could not help clinging to his arm, pressing her forehead to his shoulder. Her godfather’s caress felt like the last remaining piece of her old, sheltered girlhood, a time that now glowed golden in her memory even through its small troubles, for none of those old sorrows were tainted by the ugly treachery that had poisoned her world. “Is silence best, my Romola?” the old man asked. “Yes, for now,” she answered, lifting her head to meet his gaze with unspoken pleading. “But I cannot tell if it will always be.” “You will always have a father’s ear while I draw breath,” he said, pulling the heavy black drapery of her hood back over her head, tucking it tight against the night chill. “And a father’s home, if you ever need it. Remember that.” Then he opened the door for her, nudging her gently toward the landing. “There, hasten away. You look like a wandering ghost, no one will stop you, you will be safe enough.”

When Romola fell asleep that night, she sank into a deep, dreamless rest. Every scrap of her agitation had reached its limit; she needed to gather every ounce of strength before she could bear whatever came next, and against all her usual rigid habits, she slept long past sunrise. She woke to the roar of cannon fire. Piero de’ Medici, with thirteen hundred armed men at his back, was camped outside the city gate that faced Rome. That was the first news Maso brought her, with a dozen dubious, exaggerated details tacked on. A peasant from the countryside had run all the way to the Palazzo before dawn to warn the Signoria, the old man said, else the city would have been taken completely by surprise. Bernardo del Nero had been summoned to the palace hours earlier, he added, and was not at home. She sent Maso out again to gather more news, while she climbed to the loggia every few minutes, craning over the rooftops to spot any sign that Piero’s forces had breached the gates, or that they had been effectively repelled.

Maso returned breathless with word that the great Piazza della Signoria was packed full of armed citizens, and that every leading Florentine suspected of being a Medici sympathiser had been summoned to the palace and detained. Some people on the streets did not seem to care at all whether Piero got in, others whispered that the Signoria themselves had invited him secretly—but whatever the truth, they were giving him a fierce welcome, and soldiers summoned from Pisa were marching fast to meet his forces.

Looking back on those morning hours later, Romola could barely distinguish many real external moments from the whirlwind of old memories and terrifying imaginings that swirled through her head. She knew she had walked to the Badia at the appointed hour, in spite of all the street alarms, and waited there for hours in vain. The scene that greeted her when she stepped out of the church, standing on the stone steps as the doors were barred behind her for the midday lull, remained etched in her memory as clearly as any waking moment. There was a sharp, bitter new energy in the faces and voices of every person hurrying past, armed or unarmed. The guns were firing again, but no one flinched—people were laughing, harsh, relieved laughs. She soon learned why: Piero de’ Medici and all his horsemen had turned tail, and were galloping as fast as they could down the road toward Siena. A stout, shopkeeping Piagnone man, still clutching his pike, told her the news, a sharp edge of bitterness in his tone. “It is true,” he said, nodding grimly. “Piero is gone, but the men who plotted to let him in are still right here, hiding among us. We all know who most of them are. If the new Signoria does its duty, we will have all their names dragged into the light before long.”

The words stabbed through Romola like a sharp spasm of pain, but the evil they foreshadowed was not yet right on her doorstep. As she stepped back over the threshold of her home, her most urgent, pressing anxiety was the terrible fear that she had lost all trace of Baldassarre, and might never find him again in the chaos of the city.
