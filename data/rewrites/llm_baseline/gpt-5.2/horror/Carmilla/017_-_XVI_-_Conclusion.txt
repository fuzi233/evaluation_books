I am expected, I suppose, to set down these last particulars with the neat composure of a housekeeper closing a ledger after market-day. It is not so. The mere act of lifting the pen brings back, as if from some cupboard long shut against damp, the rank, sour breath of that horror; and it has shaken my nerves again, after years in which I had thought the terror put by like out-of-season preserves. I write because you have pressed me to it so earnestly; yet even now, in the quietest hour, a sudden hush in the rooms, or some small sound where no sound should be, will unstring me as completely as on those nights when solitude became a thing with teeth.

Let me add, then, what remains—what I can bear to recall—of that singular Baron Vordenburg, whose quaint, stubborn learning was the cleaver that found the joint in the dark and showed us where the Countess Mircalla lay.

He had established himself at Gratz, not in any comfort, but in a mean lodging, living on the mere heel of what had once been princely possessions in Upper Styria. He had the air of a man who measures his days like rations: a little fire, a little bread, and then hours—endless hours—bent over paper. The room in which he worked was always full of the small domestic noises one expects from poverty: the tinny click of a kettle-lid, the rasp of a chair on bare boards, the dry whisper of pages turned with a finger that had to be careful, because paper—like flesh—takes a mark and keeps it. And behind all those human sounds there was the other presence I have since learned to listen for: the acoustic character of a place, the way silence is not silence at all, but a field in which the least wrong note—an unnatural stillness, a stop in the ordinary breathing of a house—stands out like a shout.

The Baron had at his fingers’ ends the great and little treatises upon vampirism, as a conscientious cook has every receipt and variation in her head: not to show learning, but to know exactly what must be done when the moment comes and there is no time to consult a book. He could name them as readily as one names herbs—“Magia Posthuma,” “Phlegon de Mirabilibus,” “Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis,” “Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris,” by John Christofer Herenberg—together with a thousand more, dry as old crusts and yet, in his hands, nourishing in their hideous way. He lent many to my father, and I remember the smell of them: dust, ink, the faint sourness of old paste, and beneath it a cold mineral odour that clung to the thought of vaults and stone.

He had also compiled a vast digest of judicial cases, extracted from them as patiently as one skims and clarifies a stock until the impurities declare themselves and can be lifted away. From these he had drawn a system of principles—some constant, some occasional—governing the vampire’s condition. There was nothing theatrical in his manner; indeed he took pains to strip away the melodramatic fictions that, like showy sauces, conceal the true taste of a thing.

For example: the deadly pallor so often attributed to revenants, he said, is largely invention. They can appear, in the grave and in society, with the deceptive look of health—warm colour, full lips, the very bloom that should belong to living blood. When exposed to light in their coffins, however, they exhibit the dreadful signs by which the long-dead Countess Karnstein’s vampire-life was proved. How they go from coffin to chamber and back again—passing through clay and stone, leaving neither shift of earth nor crease of cerement—has always been admitted utterly inexplicable. Their amphibious existence, he called it: a day divided between the world above and the world below, sustained by a daily return to slumber in the grave, like an obscene kind of rest after feeding. Their vigour, in waking, is supplied by their horrible lust for living blood.

He spoke of that lust with a precision that made it more terrible. The vampire, he said, is prone to a fascination—an engrossing vehemence resembling love—for particular persons. For such an object it will practise patience and stratagem without end, because access may be blocked a hundred ways: doors, prayers, vigilance, a father’s tenderness, a servant’s watchfulness, even the mere habit of a household whose small routines keep rooms occupied and lamps trimmed and beds warmed. It will not desist until it has drained the very life of its coveted victim. Yet in those particular pursuits it will protract its enjoyment with the refinement of an epicure, heightening it by gradual approaches—by the artful courtship of the appetite—until the victim, half stupified by stealth and sweetness, seems to offer something like consent. In ordinary cases, he said, it goes straight—overpowers with violence, strangles, exhausts in a single feast.

There were, too, special conditions. In the instance that concerns me, Mircalla seemed limited to a name which, if not her true one, must at least reproduce—without omission or addition of a single letter—the same set of letters. Carmilla did so; Millarca also.

My father, after Carmilla’s expulsion, kept the Baron with us for two or three weeks. I remember those days as one remembers the long clearing up after a sickness: linen aired, rooms scoured, meals eaten because they must be eaten, though every taste seems dulled. Yet even then, the ordinary life of a house continued; the hearth had to be fed, the bread cut, the broth stirred. It was in the midst of such domestic persistence—cups set out, chairs drawn close, a kettle singing as if it had never sung over any grief—that my father related to the Baron the story of the so-called Moravian nobleman and the vampire in Karnstein churchyard, and then asked him how he had discovered the exact position of the Countess Mircalla’s tomb, so long concealed.

The Baron’s grotesque features puckered into a smile that had in it both secrecy and shame. He looked down, still smiling, and fumbled with his worn spectacle-case—the small, habitual motion of a man who prefers the reassurance of an object to the nakedness of a confession. Then he looked up and said:

“I have many journals and papers written by that remarkable man. The most curious treats of the visit you speak of—to Karnstein. Tradition discolours and distorts, of course. He might be termed a Moravian nobleman: he had removed to that territory, and he was a noble. But he was in truth a native of Upper Styria. It is enough to say that in very early youth he had been a passionate and favoured lover of the beautiful Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Her early death plunged him into grief that did not pass.

“It is the nature of vampires to increase and multiply, but according to an ascertained and ghostly law. Assume a territory free from that pest: how does it begin, how multiply? I will tell you. A person—more or less wicked—puts an end to himself. A suicide, under certain circumstances, becomes a vampire. That spectre visits living people in their slumbers; they die, and almost invariably develop into vampires in the grave. Thus it happened with the beautiful Mircalla, who was haunted by one of those demons. My ancestor, Vordenburg—whose title I still bear—soon discovered this, and in the course of studies learned more.

“Among other things, he concluded that suspicion would sooner or later fall upon the dead Countess, who in life had been his idol. He conceived a horror—be she what she might—of her remains being profaned by the outrage of a posthumous execution. He has left a paper to prove that the vampire, on its expulsion from its amphibious existence, is projected into a far more horrible life; and he resolved to save his once beloved Mircalla from that.

“He adopted, therefore, the stratagem of a journey here: a pretended removal of her remains, and a real obliteration of her monument. But when age had stolen upon him, and from the vale of years he looked back upon the scenes he was leaving, he considered differently what he had done, and a horror took possession of him. He made the tracings and notes that have guided me to the very spot, and drew up a confession of the deception. If he had intended further action, death prevented him; and the hand of a remote descendant has—too late for many—directed the pursuit to the lair of the beast.”

He spoke of “lair” as one might speak of a pantry where corruption has been found behind the jars: not with flourish, but with disgust sharpened by familiarity.

We talked a little more. Among the other things he said, I recall one with a particular shudder, because it belongs not only to doctrine but to sensation—to the body’s memory, which keeps its own language beyond words.

“One sign of the vampire,” he said, “is the power of the hand. The slender hand of Mircalla closed like a vice of steel on the General’s wrist when he raised the hatchet to strike. But its power is not confined to its grasp; it leaves a numbness in the limb it seizes, which is slowly—if ever—recovered from.”

Even as he said it I felt again, in imagination, that unnatural pressure: not merely strength, but a kind of command, as if the hand communicated a will colder than stone and more intimate than breath.

The following spring my father took me on a tour through Italy. We remained away more than a year. He wished to carry me out of the reach of those rooms, those woods, that ruined church; and perhaps, too, to restore by ordinary beauty what had been poisoned by extraordinary horror. There were markets with heaps of oranges cut and shining like exposed flesh; there were kitchens where garlic and oil hissed together, and the wholesome uproar of life—voices calling, carts rattling, fountains speaking in their own bright dialect—made it harder for dread to find a foothold. And yet I learned then a bitter truth: that terror does not require the same landscape to return. It rides in the mind as a tune one cannot rid oneself of, and sometimes it comes back not as a sound but as the sudden absence of sound—an unnatural lull, as if the world had drawn breath and held it.

It was long before the terror of those recent events subsided. To this hour the image of Carmilla returns to memory with ambiguous alternations—sometimes the playful, languid, beautiful girl, with her soft speech and indolent smiles; sometimes the writhing fiend I saw in the ruined church. And often, waking out of a reverie, I have started as if pricked, fancying I heard again the light step of Carmilla at the drawing-room door: that delicate, measured footfall, as intimate as a whisper along the boards, and more dreadful than any crash—because it belonged so perfectly to the ordinary music of a household, and yet meant the undoing of it.
