Twelve years had passed since I laid the body of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, to rest in the strange mausoleum he had designed—its heavy door bolted only from within, his casket left open beneath the dim light of Richmond’s old cemetery.

For over a decade, I had turned his final instructions over in my mind, just as I had revisited the pages of his impossible manuscript—the tale of a man who claimed no childhood, whose years stretched beyond memory. A man who swore he had lived ten years on Mars, fighting beside and against its savage green warriors, winning the heart of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and standing as a prince of Helium’s royal house.

And then, twelve years ago, his body had been found on the bluffs above the Hudson. Dead—or so the world believed. But I had always wondered. Had he somehow returned to that dying red planet? Had he reached Barsoom in time to save its people from suffocation, as his story foretold? Had he found Dejah Thoris waiting, or had he arrived too late, condemned to wander a corpse-strewn wasteland?

Or was he truly gone—lost to both Earth and Mars forever?

I was adrift in these thoughts one sweltering August evening when my servant, Ben, handed me a telegram. It bore only six words:

—*John Carter*

By dawn, I was on a train. Within hours, I stood at the threshold of his room.

The door opened.

There he was—unchanged. Not a line deepened on his face, not a shade dimmed in his steel-gray eyes. He might have been thirty, as he had been when I first knew him, a lifetime ago.

“Well, nephew,” he said, grinning. “Do I look like a ghost, or have you been at Ben’s mint juleps?”

“Juleps,” I admitted, “but seeing you helps.” I stepped forward, gripping his arm. “You’ve been back. To Mars. Dejah Thoris—did you find her?”

His smile softened. “I have walked Barsoom’s sands again. But the tale is long, and my time here is short.” He gestured to a thick portfolio on the table. “I’ve learned the secret, you see. The void between worlds is no longer a barrier. I may cross it at will—though my heart remains on Mars, with her.”

He looked at me then, something solemn in his gaze. “I came because I wanted to see you once more. To say goodbye. You’ll pass into that final mystery someday—one I’ve brushed against, yet never understood. Not even the therns, with all their ancient wisdom, hold the truth of it. I nearly died learning that.”

He tapped the portfolio. “This is for you. Share what you will, but don’t grieve if the world laughs. They aren’t ready to believe—not yet.”

That night, we walked together to the cemetery. At the vault’s entrance, he clasped my hand.

“Goodbye,” he said. “I may not return. My wife and son live on Barsoom, where lifetimes stretch for centuries. I won’t leave them again.”

Then the door sealed behind him. The bolts slid home.

I never saw him after that.

But in his notes, I found the story—his second journey to Mars, more incredible than the first. A tale of battles, of loss and triumph, of his relentless search for Dejah Thoris.

Some of it, I’ve set down here.

Much, I dare not tell.
