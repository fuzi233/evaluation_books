Though Elinor generally relied heavily on Lucy's word, a moment of serious reflection made it impossible for her to harbor any doubt about the current situation. There was simply no conceivable motive for Lucy to fabricate a lie of such magnitude. Therefore, Elinor could no longer, and dared not, question what Lucy had claimed. This assertion was bolstered by overwhelming probability and corroborating evidence, standing in stark contrast to nothing but Elinor's own desperate wishes. The foundation of their past acquaintance at Mr. Pratt's house rendered the rest of the story undeniable yet deeply alarming. When combined with Edward's recent visit near Plymouth, his evident melancholy, his dissatisfaction with his prospects, his erratic behavior toward Elinor, and the Miss Steeles' surprisingly intimate knowledge of Norland and the Dashwoods, the evidence became complete. Along with the picture, the letter, and the ring, these facts formed an irrefutable body of proof that silenced every fear of unfair judgment. It was established as an absolute truth that Edward had treated her poorly—a reality no amount of partiality could erase.

Initially, Elinor's resentment and indignation at having been duped caused her to focus solely on her own pain. However, other thoughts quickly surfaced. *Had Edward been intentionally deceiving her?* *Had he feigned feelings he did not possess?* *Was his engagement to Lucy one of the heart?* No. Whatever sentiment might have existed in the past, she could not believe it persisted now. His affection belonged entirely to her; she could not be deceived regarding that. Her mother, sisters, and Fanny had all witnessed his devotion at Norland; it was not a product of her own vanity. He certainly loved her.

What a soothing realization this proved to be! How much it tempted her to forgive! While Edward was undeniably blameworthy for staying at Norland once he realized the depth of his influence over her, he could not be excused for that. Yet, if he had harmed her, he had inflicted far greater injury upon himself. If her situation was pitiable, his was truly hopeless. His imprudence had made her suffer briefly, but it seemed to have stripped him of any chance of happiness. She might eventually regain her peace, but what did he have to look forward to? Could he possibly be content with Lucy Steele? Even setting aside his love for Elinor, could a man of his integrity, delicacy, and cultivated mind be satisfied with a wife who was illiterate, artful, and selfish? The youthful infatuation of nineteen might blind him to everything but beauty and good nature, but the next four years—if spent wisely—would surely open his eyes to her lack of education. Meanwhile, those same years spent in inferior society and frivolous pursuits might have eroded the very simplicity that once gave her charm. Furthermore, if the prospect of marrying against his mother's wishes had seemed daunting before, how much harder it must be now, knowing his fiancée was undoubtedly inferior in both connection and fortune. With a heart so alienated from Lucy, these difficulties might not weigh heavily on his patience; yet, melancholy was the fate of anyone who found relief in expecting family opposition and unkindness.

As these painful thoughts succeeded one another, Elinor wept—not for herself, but for him. Supported by the conviction that she had done nothing to deserve her misery, and consoled by the belief that Edward had forfeited nothing of her esteem, she felt strong enough to shield her mother and sisters from the full weight of the truth. Indeed, she managed so well that when she joined them at dinner just two hours after her hopes were extinguished, no one could guess that Elinor was secretly mourning obstacles that would forever separate her from her beloved. Nor could anyone imagine that Marianne was inwardly obsessing over the perfection of a man whose heart she believed fully hers, whom she expected to see in every carriage passing their home.

The necessity of keeping this secret from her mother and Marianne, though demanding constant effort, was actually a relief to Elinor. It spared them the agony of the news and protected them from the harsh condemnation of Edward that flowed from their excessive adoration of her—condemnation she felt unable to withstand. Knowing that their tender sorrow would only deepen her distress, and that their advice offered no solace, she found strength in solitude. Her own good sense upheld her firmness; her cheerfulness remained unshakable despite the poignancy of her grief.

Soon, however, Elinor felt an urgent desire to speak with Lucy again. She needed to hear the details of their engagement reiterated, to better understand the sincerity of Lucy's declared affection, and above all, to demonstrate through her calm demeanor that she viewed Lucy merely as a friend. She feared her earlier agitation during their morning talk had cast doubt on this stance. It was obvious that Lucy was jealous; Edward had praised her highly, trusting her with such a vital secret based on such brief acquaintance, and even Sir John's teasing remarks must have played a part. But given Elinor's certainty of Edward's love, jealousy was a natural reaction for Lucy. Her very willingness to confess the affair proved it. Elinor understood Lucy's motives: to inform her of her "superior" claim on Edward and teach her to stay away. Resolved to act with honor and to see Edward as little as possible, Elinor could not deny herself the comfort of trying to reassure Lucy that her own heart was unharmed. Since there was nothing more painful to hear than what had already been revealed, she trusted she could recount the events with composure.

Yet, opportunities were scarce. The weather rarely permitted walks where they could speak privately. Though they met frequently at the park or cottage, these gatherings were strictly social affairs arranged by Sir John and Lady Middleton for games and laughter, leaving no room for deep conversation. Finally, Sir John arrived at the cottage one morning, inviting everyone to dine with Lady Middleton at the Park House. He claimed he must attend a club meeting in Exeter and feared she would be utterly alone with her mother and the Miss Steeles. Elinor saw this as a perfect opportunity. A group including Lady Middleton would offer a quieter atmosphere than the chaotic energy of Sir John's presence. Margaret agreed readily, and Marianne, though reluctant, was persuaded by her mother to accompany them.

The gathering was exactly as dull as Elinor anticipated. The conversation lacked novelty, and the presence of the children in the drawing room made it impossible to engage Lucy. Once the tea things were cleared, the card tables were set up. Elinor wondered why she had ever hoped to find time for serious talk at the park. Everyone prepared for a game of Casino.

"I am glad," Lady Middleton said to Lucy, "that you aren't finishing poor little Annamaria's basket tonight. I'm sure working filigree by candlelight hurts your eyes. We'll make up for her disappointment tomorrow, and I hope she won't mind missing it."

This subtle hint was enough. Lucy instantly recollected herself. "Indeed, you are greatly mistaken, Lady Middleton," she replied briskly. "I am only waiting to see if you can manage without me, for I wouldn't disappoint the little angel for all the world. If you need me at the card table now, I am determined to finish the basket after supper."

"You are very kind," Lady Middleton said, hoping it wouldn't harm her eyes. "Will you ring for some working candles? My poor little girl would be terribly disappointed if the basket isn't finished tomorrow, especially since I promised her it certainly would be, even though I know she relies on it."

Lucy promptly moved her worktable closer and resumed her seat with an alacrity that suggested she derived no greater pleasure than crafting a basket for a spoiled child. Lady Middleton then proposed a rubber of Casino. Everyone objected except Marianne, who, ignoring the conventions of polite society, exclaimed, "Your Ladyship will please excuse me—I detest cards. I shall go to the piano-forte; I haven't touched it since it was tuned." Without further ceremony, she turned and walked to the instrument. Lady Middleton looked visibly relieved that she had never heard such a blunt remark.

"Marianne can never stay away from that instrument long, ma'am," Elinor interjected smoothly, attempting to ease the tension. "And I don't blame her; it is the finest-toned piano-forte I have ever heard."

With the remaining five preparing to deal, Elinor continued, "Perhaps, if I happen to cut out, I could assist Miss Lucy Steele in rolling her papers. There is still so much work left on the basket that it seems impossible for her to finish it alone this evening. I would be delighted to help if she allows me a share."

"Oh, I shall be so obliged!" cried Lucy. "I find there is more to do than I thought, and it would be dreadful to disappoint dear Annamaria."

"Oh! That would be terrible indeed," added Miss Steele. "My dear little soul, how I adore her!"

"You are very kind," Lady Middleton said to Elinor. "Since you enjoy the work, perhaps you'd prefer not to cut until the next hand, or would you like to try your luck now?"

Elinor gladly took the first option. Through a touch of diplomacy that Marianne could never muster, she achieved her goal and pleased Lady Middleton simultaneously. Lucy made room for her with ready attention, and the two rivals sat side by side at the same table, harmoniously collaborating on the basket.
