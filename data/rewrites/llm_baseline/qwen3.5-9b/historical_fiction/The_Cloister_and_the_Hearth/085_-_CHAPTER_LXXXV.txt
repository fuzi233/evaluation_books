Margaret stepped into the vast, unfinished cathedral of Saint Laurence just as the sermon began. It was a monumental structure, far from completion; such edifices took years, not seasons, to rise. While the side aisles were roofed, the central nave and the chancel remained open to the sky. The stone pillars and arches stood nearly finished, some freshly whitewashed, yet only a single window in the entire building was glazed. The rest were gaping, jagged wounds in the masonry.

But today, these raw imperfections rendered the church breathtakingly beautiful. It was a glorious summer afternoon, and sunlight poured through the irregular openings in dazzling, fractured beams. These rays danced across the rough surfaces, casting rivers of golden glory into the cool, dark shadows of the side aisles while illuminating the white pillars with a blinding radiance. The great central aisle was checkered with shifting patterns of light and shade—the shadows appearing deceptively cool and soothing, while the lights shimmered like patches of amber diamonds animated by divine fire. Above, the azure sky vaulted the lofty space, feeling impossibly close. The white bonnets of the congregation formed a sea of purity against the vivid blue dome. Though the main aisle was packed to capacity, the silence was absolute. The preacher's words and his mellow, earnest voice held the multitude spellbound.

Margaret stood transfixed by the sheer beauty and the profound devotion of the scene—a "great calm" that settled over her soul. Seeking shelter, she slipped behind a pillar in the north aisle. Even without hearing every syllable, a sweet, languorous peace washed over her at the sight of the place and the melody of the voice. It felt as though balm trickled over the waves within her heart, smoothing them until they stilled. Leaning against the stone, her eyes half-closed, everything seemed soft and dreamlike. Being there felt right.

Soon, she noticed a woman vacate a prime spot directly under a sunbeam, seeking relief from the heat. Margaret moved quickly and silently around the pillar, securing the empty seat. Now positioned beside a pillar in the south aisle, less than fifty feet from the pulpit, she was practically at the preacher's side, slightly behind him, able to catch every word clearly.

Her concentration broke, however, when she spotted the shadow of a man's head and shoulders bobbing up and down with comical rhythm nearby. She struggled to suppress a smile, realizing too late that it wasn't a joke at all. It was the sexton, diligently digging. Looking through the open window toward the source of the shadow, she confirmed it was Jorian Ketel working among the foundations.

As she watched Jorian, a specific tone of the preacher's voice struck her ear with such distinct force that it seemed to physically vibrate her very bones. Her hand flew instinctively to her bosom, startled by the sudden intensity of the sensation. Turning sharply, she sought the source, but the preacher's back was turned to her; all she could see was the crown of his tonsured head. She sighed, frustrated that the visual confirmation was absent despite the overwhelming auditory impact. Still, she leaned forward, eyes cast downward, waiting for that particular inflection to return. It did not come immediately, but the voice continued to grow upon her, rising and falling as the speaker warmed to his theme. It awakened faint echoes of a thousand happy memories, filling her with a melancholy pleasure she refused to dispel by looking away.

Suddenly, in the midst of an eloquent passage, the preacher halted. Margaret almost sighed aloud; the soothing music had ceased abruptly. Was the sermon over? She glanced around, but the congregation remained seated, unmoving. Then, she sensed eyes upon her. Many faces seemed to turn toward her.

She spun around sharply, expecting someone standing behind her, but the space was empty. Nearby, startled countenances fixed themselves on the preacher. Following their gaze, Margaret froze. In the pulpit, a face stared back at her with the blank horror of a corpse. The friar's eyes, naturally large and set wide by the hollows of his cheeks, were dilated to a supernatural size, glaring directly at her from a bloodless visage.

Margaret cringed, turning fearfully in circles, convinced some terrible specter stood behind her. There was nothing. She was merely part of the listening crowd, an outsider figure in the chaos. Just then, the church erupted into commotion. People surged upward, craning necks forward; hundreds of agitated faces swung between the friar and Margaret. The rapid turning of caps created a deafening rustle. Shrieks from nervous women and buzzing murmurs from men filled the air. Terrified by the weight of so many leveled stares, Margaret shrank back behind her pillar, stealing one frantic, hurried glance at the preacher before burying herself in the crowd.

That fleeting glimpse froze her blood. On the stricken face of the friar, she had seen an expression that made her shudder from head to toe. She turned pale and collapsed onto a pile of wood chips left by the workmen, covering her face with her hands.

When the sermon resumed, she heard the sounds but grasped none of the meaning. Her mind spun in a vortex of terror, fixated on a single image: the look stamped upon that prodigy-stricken face. It was *recognition*. The memory of that gaze sent fresh tremors coursing through her body.

Finally, the sermon concluded with a strain of feverish, exalted eloquence that drove the crowd to forget the preacher's bizarre pause and ghastly stare. Margaret hurriedly merged with the throng and exited the church. As the others dispersed toward their homes, she paused at the threshold and turned back into the churchyard, walking straight to Peter's grave. Despite her poverty, she had commissioned a slab and a headstone for him. Sitting upon the cold stone, she pressed a kiss to it, then pulled her apron over her head to hide her hair from any passersby.

"Father," she whispered to the silent earth, "you have often heard me say I am wading in deep waters, but now I believe God alone knows the depth. I will follow that friar around the world, but I shall keep him at arm's length. He must tell me why he looked toward me like a dead man waking from the grave—and not a soul stood behind me. Oh, Father, you often praised me here; please speak a word for me there. For truly, I am wading in deep waters."

From her position on the tomb, which offered a clear view of the church door, she waited, face hidden, to intercept the holy preacher.
