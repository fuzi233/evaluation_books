I spun around, expecting to find the edge of the known world dissolving into a misty void, yet all I encountered was a disorienting haze where sky and earth refused to maintain their distinct boundaries. Fog bled into mountain slopes, clouds merged with fields, and the laws of geometry seemed to have surrendered to chaos. The only certainty was absolute negation: I saw nothing I recognized.

Desperate to anchor reality, I reached out, stretching my arms as if to feel the solid floor beneath me, hoping that tactile evidence would correct my failing vision. But the air offered no resistance, and the ground yielded no firmness. My frantic searching was futile. Instinct, overriding reason, drew my gaze to the figure standing a few paces away—a raven. Its posture was regal, its expression a peculiar blend of deference and mockery. In that split second, the absurdity of consulting a bird for guidance crashed over me. I turned away, a wave of bewildered terror washing through me. Could I truly be in a realm where the material and spiritual laws of my world had dissolved? Was I drifting toward a zone of pure disorder, where men became toys for invisible hands? And yet, there stood the raven, tangible and real, and beneath my boots lay soil, and a gentle rustle of wind moved through the low-lying vegetation.

"How *did* I arrive here?" I asked, the words escaping before I could check them.

"You walked through the door," a voice replied. It was rough, oddly textured, lacking the smooth cadence of a human throat.

I scanned the shadows, searching for a face, a silhouette, anything human. Panic flared—the fear that I had succumbed to madness, that my senses were now unreliable liars. Then, the realization struck me with chilling clarity: the speaker was the bird. It stood before me, tilting its head, waiting. Though the sun was hidden, the raven cast a shadow that clung to it like a second skin, making it seem larger, heavier, more substantial than flesh alone.

"I implore you, let me understand," I stammered, struggling to articulate the confusion tearing at my mind. "I am trying to describe a state of being that defies every category I know. To use your tongue's words feels like painting with watercolors on stone. I fear I am attempting the impossible, narrating a dream where the objects shift form before my eyes, becoming unrecognizable the moment I grasp them."

"A creature capable of speech to a man deserves a civil reply," I reasoned, steadying my nerves. "Perhaps, as a bird, you possess an even stronger claim to respect."

His tone retained a slight, rasping quality, yet his words lacked malice. If anything, they carried a weary patience.

"I did not enter through any door," I insisted.

"I witnessed you pass through it!" the raven declared, his voice firm. "My ancient eyes saw you cross the threshold!"

"Never!" I protested. "I remember no doorway!"

"Certainly not!" the bird countered thoughtfully. "All the portals you have traversed thus far—few enough for a traveler like you—were entrances *into*. Here, you have stepped out of a door! The irony, I suspect, will become clear to you eventually: the more doors you exit, the further inward you travel!"

"Please, tell me where I stand."

"That is a question without an answer. You know nothing of 'whereness.' The only path to knowledge of location is to begin making yourself at home."

"And how do I start, given that everything around me is alien?"

"By acting."

"What kind of action?"

"Do whatever comes to mind. The sooner you act, the better! For until you establish a home, the journey outward will be just as treacherous as the journey inward."

"I have already found the entry too easy," I admitted, my voice trembling. "Once I escape, I shall never return!"

"You stumbled in, and perhaps you may stumble out. However, whether your arrival has been unfortunate remains to be determined."

"Do you not venture outside often, sir?"

"When I choose, though rarely and briefly. Your world... it is a half-formed construct, childlike and self-congratulatory. It lacks the depth required for an elder bird like myself to fully appreciate."

"Am I mistaken in assuming a man stands above a bird?"

"It depends. We do not indulge in idle generalizations. Take the man as you find him, or the bird as it presents itself." The raven paused, considering me. "Now, it is my turn. Tell me, who are you—if you have any concept of it."

"How could I know?" I replied. "I am simply me, and that must suffice!"

"If you accept yourself as 'yourself,' you acknowledge you are not someone else. But do you truly know you are yourself? Are you certain you are not your father? Or, forgive me, are you not merely your own folly? Who are you, really?"

Suddenly, the fog within me thickened. I realized I could offer him no definition. Indeed, who was I? To say "I am who" was meaningless. I understood then that I had lost my connection to identity, to the fundamental distinction of being one person rather than another. The name I bore in my former life had slipped away, and I did not wish to recall it; it held no weight here, and its origin mattered not a whit. I had nearly forgotten that names were even a custom in my past world! Silence, I decided, was my wisest strategy. Before a creature like this raven, who pierced accidents to reveal essence, what words could I possibly offer?

"Look at me," he commanded. "Describe who I am."

As he spoke, he pivoted, presenting his backside. Instantly, recognition flooded me. He was no longer a bird, but a man of medium stature, thin and stooped, draped in a long, somber black tailcoat. He turned again, and the transformation reversed, rendering him a raven once more.

"I have seen you before, sir," I murmured, feeling a mixture of shame and relief.

"How can you claim to have seen me from behind?" he asked sharply. "Have you ever gazed upon your own reflection from the rear? You have never truly seen yourself at all! Tell me now, who am I?"

"My apologies," I hastened to say. "I believed you were the librarian of our household. Beyond that, I know nothing."

"Why do you apologize?"

"Because I mistook you for a raven," I explained, watching him with the same intensity as before.

"No harm done," he replied calmly. "To call me a raven, or to perceive me as one, grants me existence. That is the greatest tribute a companion can pay. In exchange, I offer you a lesson: No one can declare 'I am myself' until they first comprehend that they *are*, and then define what that 'self' entails. In truth, nobody is entirely himself, and 'himself' is nobody. There is more to this than you see now, but not more than you require."

He began to walk away, and as he did, I caught a glimpse of the librarian again. He seemed unchanged, save for having adopted his shadow as a cloak. I watched him until he vanished into the heather, unsure if distance or magic had concealed him.

Had I died? Was I wandering the realm beyond the grave, searching for a place I could call mine? The raven had urged me to act. What action could I take? Would it somehow transform me from "nobody"?

I followed the direction the librarian-raven had taken. Soon, the trees of the woods ahead revealed themselves—tall, slender pines reaching for a sky I couldn't see. Their scent, resinous and ancient, called to me, and I hurried toward them, eager to lose myself in their gloom.

Deep within the twilight canopy, I spotted something shimmering between two massive trunks. It possessed no color, existing instead like the heat-haze rising from sun-baked earth, vibrating with a silent, radiant energy akin to a plucked string. As I approached, the object blurred, slipping from focus until the trees beyond it seemed to warp and twist. I attempted to step between the stems, but a sudden jolt sent me stumbling forward, crashing onto the earth.

When I raised my head, the forest had dissolved. I stood before the wooden wall of the garret chamber, the mirror resting atop it, the black eagle seemingly having landed moments prior.

Panic seized me, cold and absolute. I fled the room. Outside, the vast expanse of the garret radiated an uncanny stillness, as if it had waited centuries for this moment, and now, having received me, awaited the next catastrophe. A chill ran down my spine as I descended the spiral stairs; the house itself had grown hostile, a labyrinth of shifting corridors. Something lurked behind me, poised to spring. I scrambled down the steps, collided with a wall, tumbled, and regained my footing only to run blindly. I lost my bearings on the next floor, retracing several passages twice before finding the head of the staircase. At the top, amidst the silence of the library, I finally gathered my wits, collapsing onto a chair to catch my breath.
