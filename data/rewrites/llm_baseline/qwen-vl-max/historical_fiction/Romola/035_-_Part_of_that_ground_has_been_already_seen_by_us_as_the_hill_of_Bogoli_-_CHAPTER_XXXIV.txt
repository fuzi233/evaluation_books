Messer Naldo returned sooner than expected—on the evening of November twenty-eighth, just eleven days after his last visit—proving he had not ventured far beyond the mountains. And a scene that unfolded that night in the Via de’ Bardi may help explain why his steps turned toward the hill of San Giorgio.

When Tito first found shelter for Tessa upon returning from Rome, more than a year and a half earlier, he told himself he acted out of kindness alone, compelled by the unfortunate incident that had led foolish little Tessa to believe him her husband. It was true that she was charming—pretty, trusting, and so naturally affectionate that it was hard not to want to comfort and pet her. But it was also true that Tito’s kindness was not merely self-serving. Had it been only about pleasure, he might have let her go; for though he enjoyed her presence in idle moments, he was not in love with Tessa. He was in love—for the first time in his life—with someone entirely different: a woman whose mere presence could possess him, whose long tresses brushing his cheek sent vibrations through hours of thought. All the idealism in Tito’s nature had awakened at Romola’s touch. His mind was too refined, his spirit too sensitive, to fall into the vulgar indulgences others embraced. Yet he had woven a web around himself and Tessa—one he now felt powerless to unravel. At first, after their mock marriage, he had deliberately left her under the illusion, calculating that he might need such a refuge one day. But since then, the web had grown on its own, like a vine clinging tighter than he could break. Kindness and self-indulgence were hard to separate in a nature as soft as Tito’s. The annoyance he’d felt when Tessa pursued him on the day of his betrothal, the firm resolve he’d made to reveal the truth—these were proof enough to him that he had yielded to compassion, not convenience. And in those early days of devotion to Romola, he needed justification. He was glad she remained ignorant of certain truths. But every strong emotion creates its own conscience, its own piety—just as a child’s love for a parent can endure even amid decay. Tito could not yet rest easy knowing he carried a secret wound against the woman he loved.

Yet he took care to guard that secret. Monna Lisa, who rarely left her home except to shop or attend confession once a year, knew nothing of his real name or whereabouts. She only knew he paid her well and kept her comfortable, and cared little else—except that she grew fond of Tessa and took pleasure in the tasks she was paid to do. There was mystery behind the arrangement, certainly—Tessa was a peasant girl, Messer Naldo a gentleman—but Monna Lisa might just as easily have believed them truly married. For Tito had frightened Tessa into silence, warning her that if she ever spoke of their marriage, she would never see him again. And Monna Lisa’s deafness, which required careful speech, protected Tessa from accidental revelations—such as she had already made to Baldassarre. For much of the time, Tito’s visits were rare enough that it seemed natural he traveled between them. They came mostly from a desire to check on Tessa, and though each visit always proved more pleasant than anticipated—always renewed the charm of her innocent affection—he had no deep need for her yet. Still, he was determined, if possible, to preserve the simplicity that made her so appealing. He wanted her to remain a genuine country girl, untouched by the world that might dull her grace. He would have been shocked to see her dressed in finer clothes; her speech would lose its charm if her world expanded, her joys matured. Even the squirrel-like delight she took in eating nuts marked the limit of the luxuries he allowed her. In this way, Tito preserved Tessa’s charm—and, by happy chance, spared himself further expense, which weighed heavily on a man whose funds were already strained by his outwardly modest lifestyle.

This, in short, was the story of Tito’s relationship with Tessa until recently. True, once or twice before Bardo’s death, the idea of being with Tessa—of finding an hour of ease away from the old man—had tempted him when weariness threatened. But there had been no real hunger for her until very lately. That moment was unmistakable, as clear as the onset of a sickness that permanently alters sight and hearing. It was the day he first saw Baldassarre and bought the armor. Returning across the bridge that night, coat of mail in hand, he felt an overwhelming urge to avoid Romola. She, too, lived in a world of limited experience, trusted him deeply—but beneath her open eyes, Tito sensed a mind capable of judgment. Her trust wasn’t born of ignorance, but of nobility, and that nobility could become a dangerous mirror. After the agitation of the day, he craved release, a place where he could breathe without restraint. And there was Tessa—sweet, adoring, believing any tale, unshaken by public opinion. So, while Romola waited and listened for him, he climbed the hill.

No wonder, then, that he took the same path eleven days later, after Romola’s first storm of scorn. He did not wish Tessa in his wife’s place, nor could he help wishing Romola would forgive him. For it was Romola—her world, her ambitions, her depth—who belonged to the realm where a man of talent and purpose must ultimately strive. But he needed a refuge from a standard too strict, one he could not escape simply by calling it folly. And Tessa’s small soul offered just such a haven.

It was barely eight o’clock when he ascended the stone steps to Tessa’s door. Usually, she heard him enter and rushed to meet him—but not tonight. When he opened the door, he understood why. A single dim light flickered above the dying fire, revealing Tessa kneeling beside the baby’s bed. Her head had fallen sideways onto the pillow, her brown rosary—normally hung above the Madonna’s image—loosely held in her right hand. She had drifted off mid-prayer.

Tito stepped quietly across the room and sat beside her. She likely heard the door creak in her dream, for she opened her eyes almost immediately—without surprise, without movement—just gazing at him as if his presence silenced all other thoughts. When he lifted her chin and leaned down to kiss her, she whispered:

“I dreamed it, and then I said it was a dream—and then I woke, and it was true.”

“Little sinner,” Tito teased, pinching her chin, “you’ve not finished your prayers. I’ll punish you by not looking at your baby. It’s ugly.”

Tessa frowned, though he smiled. “Ah, no! He’s prettier than anything. You don’t think he’s ugly. You’ll look at him. He’s even prettier than last time—only he’s asleep, so you can’t see his eyes or tongue. And his hair is growing—isn’t that wonderful? Look at him!”

He obliged, pressing a gentle butterfly kiss to the tiny form, then turning her face toward him. “You like looking at the baby better than your husband, you false one!”

She rested her hands on his knee, her eyes wide and angelic—like one of Fra Lippo Lippi’s cherubs.

“No,” she murmured, shaking her head. “I love you best. But I want you to love the bambino too. I used to want only you to love me.”

“And did you expect me to return so soon?” Tito asked, eager to hear her prattle. He still felt the tremors of his recent turmoil—like a man shaken violently—and this was the easiest way to find relief from silence.

“No,” she said, counting on her fingers. “I’ve counted the days—today I started over at my right thumb—since you wore the beautiful chain-coat Messer San Michele gave you. And you’re wearing it now,” she added, peering through the opening in his tunic. “Perhaps it brought you back sooner.”

“Perhaps it did,” Tito replied. “But forget the coat. Tell me—what happened since I was here? Did you see the tents in the Prato? The soldiers and horsemen crossing the bridges? Did you hear the drums and trumpets?”

“Yes,” she answered, voice hushed. “I was afraid they’d come up here. Monna Lisa was too—she said they might take our kids. She says it’s their business to cause harm. But the Holy Madonna protected us—we never saw one. But something has happened… and I hardly dare tell you. That’s why I prayed extra Aves.”

“What do you mean?” Tito asked, suddenly anxious.

“Will you let me sit on your knee? Then I won’t be so frightened.”

He lifted her onto his lap, arms wrapped around her, but his expression darkened. Something unpleasant had followed him even here.

“At first I didn’t want to tell you,” she whispered, as if softness could lessen the fault. “We thought the old man would be gone before you came. But he’s still here, and you’re here. I never disobeyed you before. I want to tell you—and maybe you’ll forgive me. It’s been so long since I confessed.”

“Tell me everything, my Tessa.” He hoped it was nothing serious.

“Oh, you’ll feel sorry for him. I’m afraid he cries when I don’t see him. But that wasn’t why I went to him first. I wanted to talk to him, show him the baby. He was a stranger, living nowhere, and I thought you wouldn’t mind. I think he’s not a bad man—he wanted to sleep on the straw next to the goats, and I made Monna Lisa say yes. He’s gone most of the day, but when he returns, I talk to him and bring him food.”

“A beggar, I suppose,” Tito said sharply. “That was wrong, Tessa. I’m angry with Monna Lisa. He must go.”

“No,” she insisted. “I don’t think he’s a beggar. He tried to pay Monna Lisa, but she said he should work instead. He gets shaved, his clothes are neat—Monna Lisa says he’s decent. But sometimes… I think he’s not right in the head. Lupo at Peretola wasn’t right, and he looks a little like Lupo—like he doesn’t know where he is.”

“What kind of face does he have?” Tito asked, heart beginning to race. The image of Baldassarre haunted him. Already, he saw him sitting on the straw, just yards away.

“Fetch your stool, Tessa,” he said. “Sit there.”

“Will you forgive me?” she asked timidly, stepping down.

“Yes,” he said, “I won’t be angry. Just sit and describe him.”

“I can’t think how to say it. He’s not like my stepfather Nofri or anyone. His face is yellow, with deep lines. His hair is white, but none on top. His eyebrows are black, and he looks at me from under them, saying, ‘Poor thing!’ as if he thinks I was beaten like I used to be. That makes me think he’s not right, doesn’t it? I asked his name once, but he couldn’t tell me. Everyone has a name, isn’t that true? Now he has a book, and he stares at it for ages, like a Padre. But I don’t think he’s praying—his lips don’t move. Ah, are you angry? Or sad for the old man?”

Tito’s eyes remained fixed on her, but he no longer saw her. He saw only what her words evoked. A new possibility rose before him—one that could dissolve the fear and shame poisoning his life. Destiny had placed within reach a chance to redeem the moment on the Duomo steps, when the past had seized him with living hands, and he had denied it. A few steps, and he might stand before his father—alone. He might seek forgiveness, reconciliation. Money from the sale of the library could fund their escape from Florence, undetected, into Southern Italy, where French rule offered protection. Romola need never learn the full truth—she could not identify the prisoner in the Duomo as Baldassarre, nor know what transpired unless he revealed it. If his father forgave, he would agree to bury the past.

But with this hope came another: the possibility that the fierce man might refuse. Well—then things would remain as before. For there would be *no witness*. This was not repentance draped in white cloth, confessing sins before men. This was repentance that would restore peace, hide pain. And Tito’s tender heart, his aversion to harshness, now swelled toward his father. To have him near, to replace hatred with old affection—that was a peace his nature craved. He longed for his world to be cushioned again with goodwill, especially after Romola’s rejection. It was easy for him to smile at those he had wronged, to offer kindness. He could not taste the bitterness of injury, nor grasp the full weight of reparation. The opportunity was here, and it stirred him beyond reason. He rose abruptly, moving toward the door. But Tessa’s cry—beads falling—broke his trance.

“My Tessa,” he said, “bring me a lantern. Don’t cry, little pigeon—I’m not angry.”

They descended the stairs. Tessa reached for Monna Lisa’s ear to shout for the lantern, but Tito stopped her.

“Stay, Tessa. No lantern. Go back upstairs. Be quiet. Say nothing.”

In moments, he stood before the closed door of the outhouse, moonlight glinting on the weathered wood.

In that final moment, a tremor passed through Tito—a sudden instinctive recoil from a tiger’s gaze, a tiger’s leap. Why should a young man, armed, fear an old one, weaponless? Only a moment’s hesitation. Then his hand gripped the door.

Was his father asleep? Was there nothing but this door between him and the voice, the glance that no magic could soothe?

Baldassarre was not asleep. High in the wall of the hovel, a square opening let in moonlight like a pale river. If Tito had looked through it, he would have seen his father seated on the straw, holding something that gleamed like a white star—his poniard. Baldassarre ran his thumb along the blade, seeking solace in its edge, escaping the void between rage and purpose.

He was in one of his worst states—conscious helplessness. He had spent daylight poring over the book beside him, trying to recall the names of his jewels, their symbols. Once, he could remember them. Tonight, they were lost in darkness. His memory had failed him utterly. He was reduced to a mad awareness: a solitary pulse of rage in a world of defiance. He had drawn the dagger, feeling its edge, his mind narrowed to one image—the sensation of driving it into a base heart, a revenge he could not achieve otherwise.

Tito pulled the door. It scraped against the floor, waking Baldassarre from his trance. Startled, he rose from his knees, disoriented, not knowing where he was. Before he could stand fully, the door swung open. Moonlight framed the silhouette of a figure—dark against the glow—curls bright, cheek rounded—*the image of his reverie*, not shadowy, but real, close, like water at the lips after thirst.

No thought could interrupt that hunger. In one instant, before Tito could retreat, the old man surged forward, dagger flashing.

Then—snap. The blade broke. Baldassarre fell backward onto the straw, clutching the hilt, the jagged end lying at Tito’s feet.

Tito staggered under the force, heart leaping with terror. But then came relief—the triumph of survival. His armor had saved him. Vengeance lay helpless. Yet no cruel impulse stirred. Instead, seeing his father near, powerless, made reconciliation easier. Fear was gone. Now he only wanted to be free from the feeling of being hated.

After a long silence, Baldassarre lying motionless in fury, Tito spoke softly, as he had before their parting in Greece:

“_Padre mio._”

A pause. No response.

“I came to ask your forgiveness.”

Another pause—letting the words settle.

“I was taken by surprise that morning. I wish now to be your son again. I want to make your life happy, so you may forget what you’ve suffered.”

He waited. He had said all he could. Until he saw a sign, it was useless. Perhaps his father’s mind was broken. Perhaps the fall had stunned him.

At last, Baldassarre moved. He cast aside the broken dagger, slowly rising, trembling. Tito reached out a hand. And in that instant, as he began to believe acceptance was near, a fleeting thought struck him—the burden of what would follow.

Baldassarre grasped the hand, pulled himself up, and stepped close—faces inches apart.

Then he spoke, voice deep and shaking:

“I saved you. I nurtured you. I loved you. You abandoned me. You stole from me. You denied me. What can you give me? You have made the world bitter to me. But one sweetness remains—_that you shall know agony._”

He released Tito’s hand, stepped back, leaned on a stone, then sank again onto the straw.

The surge of rage had drained him.
