Upon the wall of a distinguished study in Boston hangs a pair of crossed swords, relics of a father and son who, in the heat of the Great War of Independence, fought for opposing masters. One blade gleamed in the service of the Crown; the other served a Republic. Yet, today, they rest side by side as harmless trophies, borne by a man whose name is honored equally in the land of his birth and the soil of his adoption. Where genius finds peace, these weapons find honor.

The history they evoke mirrors their union. The protagonists of this tale, native-born Virginians and children of the Old Dominion, found themselves bound in chains of opposite allegiances. They clashed as brothers must, yet emerged from the storm not as enemies, but as kin, their love undimmed despite the fury of their division. The Colonel, clad in scarlet, and the General, arrayed in blue and buff, now hang side by side in the wainscot of the Warringtons' parlour in England. A descendant of the line displays them to me, alongside the letters they exchanged—the yellowed pages of a bygone era, some stained with the boyish tears of thwarted passion, others pierced by bullets or blotted with blood. To the Warringtons, these figures are simply "The Virginians," a title immortalized in their memoirs.

Both men spent their youth traversing Europe, living on the precipice of a world that drifts swiftly from memory. They knew varieties of men and fortunes unknown to later generations, encountering ghosts from history that breathe within the ink of their correspondence. These letters, preserved by the affectionate thrift of their mother, span a lifetime. They begin with Henry, the younger twin, sending love to his brother, who lay ill at Castlewood in Virginia, and thank their grandfather for a horse ridden with a tutor. They end with a final salutation, "from my beloved son," delivered mere hours before the mother's death.

Madam Esmond, ever after the burning of Castlewood, refused the name of Warrington, holding it in scant regard against her own lineage. She never ventured to Europe again, save once in the reign of George the Second. Her letters, sparse in detail, offer hints rather than full descriptions—outlines waiting to be filled with color. I have pored over them, striving to reconstruct the scenes, imagining the situations, the surrounding persons, the voices that still echo in the silence of the paper. Whether this revival succeeds, whether it brings profit or amusement, remains for the reader to judge.

It was a summer morning in 1756, under the reign of King George the Second. The *Young Rachel*, a Virginian vessel captained by Edward Franks, ascended the Avon River on her triumphant return from the annual voyage to the Potomac. She drifted toward Bristol, mooring near Trail's Wharf. Mr. Trail, a co-owner who watched the harbor from his counting-house windows, took a boat and hastened to the ship.

A large, grave man with hair as grey as his demeanor, Mr. Trail extended a welcoming hand to Captain Franks. After congratulating the captain on the swift and fortunate journey, he turned immediately to business, inquiring of the cargo and passengers.

Captain Franks, a man of cheerful disposition who relished a jest, grinned. "We have," he announced, "but yonder ugly negro boy carrying the trunks, and a passenger who claims the state cabin to himself."

Mr. Trail frowned, his face tightening. "Confound you, Franks, and your luck!" he muttered. "The *Duke William* arrived last week with fourteen, and she is not half your tonnage."

"And this passenger," the Captain pressed, "takes the whole cabin and pays not a penny. Swear now, it will do you good, Mr. Trail, truly it will. I have tried the medicine."

"A passenger takes the whole cabin and pays nothing?" Mr. Trail gasped. "Gracious mercy, are you a fool, Captain Franks?"

"Ask the passenger himself, for here he comes."

As the master spoke, a young man of nineteen stepped up the hatchway. Cloaked and armed with a sword beneath his arm, he was dressed in deep mourning. "Gumbo, you idiot, why don't you fetch the baggage out of the cabin?" he called down. Turning to the crew, he added, "Well, shipmates, our journey is ended. You shall see all the little ones tonight whom you have spoken of. Give my love to Polly, Betty, and Little Tommy; do not forget my duty to Mrs. Franks. I thought yesterday the voyage would never conclude, and now that it is done, I am almost sorry. That little berth in my cabin looks terribly comfortable now that I am leaving it."

Mr. Trail scowled at the unpaid passenger, offering but a curt nod. "Captain Franks," the merchant declared, "this gentleman is Mr. Trail, sir, whose name you have undoubtedly heard."

"It is well known in Bristol, sir," Mr. Trail intoned, standing with majestic dignity.

"And this is Mr. Warrington," the Captain interjected, "son of Madam Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood."

Instantly, the merchant removed his hat, bowing prodigiously as if before a crowned prince. "Gracious powers, Mr. Warrington! What a delight, indeed! What a crowning mercy that your voyage should have been so prosperous! You must accept my boat to go ashore. Let me cordially welcome you to England; let me shake your hand as the son of my benefactress and patroness, Mrs. Esmond Warrington, whose name is honored on Bristol Exchange, I warrant you. Is it not so, Franks?"

"There is no sweeter tobacco from Virginia," Captain Franks remarked, producing a great brass box from his pocket and stuffing a quid into his jolly mouth. "No better brand than the Three Castles. You don't know what a comfort it is, sir! You will take to it, bless you, as you grow older. Won't he, Mr. Trail? I wish you had ten shiploads of it instead of one. You might have ten shiploads; I have told Madam Esmond so. I have ridden over her plantation; she treats me like a lord when I go to the house. She doesn't grudge me the best wine, nor keep me cooling my heels in the counting-room as some folks do." He glanced sharply at Mr. Trail. "She is a real born lady, she is. Might have a thousand hogsheads as easily as her hundreds, if there were but hands enough."

"I have lately engaged in the Guinea trade," said Mr. Trail, bowing obsequiously. "I could supply her ladyship with any number of healthy young negroes before next fall."

"We are averse to purchasing negroes from Africa," the young gentleman replied, his voice cool and distant. "My grandfather and my mother have always objected to it. I do not like to think of selling or buying the poor wretches."

"It is for their good, my dear young sir! For their temporal and spiritual good!" cried Mr. Trail. "We purchase these poor creatures only for their benefit. Let me discuss this matter further at my own house. I can introduce you to a happy home, a Christian family, and the honest fare of a British merchant. Can't I, Captain Franks?"

"Can't say," growled the Captain. "Never asked me to take a bite or sup at your table. Asked me to psalm-singing once, to hear Mr. Ward preach. Don't care for them sort of entertainments."

Ignoring this aside, Mr. Trail lowered his voice. "Business is business, my dear young sir. I know 'tis only my duty, the duty of all of us, to cultivate the fruits of the earth in their season. As the heir of Lady Esmond's estate—for I speak, I believe, to the heir of that great property?—"

The young gentleman bowed politely.

"—I would urge upon you, at the earliest moment, the propriety, the necessity of increasing the ample means with which Heaven has blessed you. As an honest factor, I could not do otherwise; as a prudent man, should I scruple to speak of what tends to your profit and mine? No, my dear Mr. George."

"My name is not George; my name is Henry," the young man said, turning his head away, his eyes filling with unshed tears.

"Gracious powers! What do you mean, sir? Did you not say you were my lady's heir? And is not George Esmond Warrington, Esq.—"

"Hold your tongue, you fool!" cried Captain Franks, delivering a sharp blow to the merchant's flank as the young man turned away. "Do you not see the young gentleman swabbing his eyes? Note his black clothes?"

"What do you mean, Captain Franks, by laying your hand on your owners?" Mr. Trail demanded indignantly. "Mr. George is the heir; I know the Colonel's will perfectly well."

"Mr. George is there," the Captain pointed with his thumb toward the deck.

"Where?" cried the factor, straining forward.
