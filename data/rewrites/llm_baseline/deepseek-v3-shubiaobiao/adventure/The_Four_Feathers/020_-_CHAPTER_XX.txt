The night was still and deep when Durrance returned to "Guessens," his house nestled among the fields. His servant waited, lantern in hand, but Durrance dismissed him with a quiet word. "Put out the lights and rest," he said, moving past the hall into his study—though *study* was too gentle a name for a room lined with the trophies of a life lived boldly.

In the darkness, he wandered the room, his fingers tracing the contours of cups and goblets, each one a memory etched in silver or pewter. The thin-stemmed goblet from a regimental steeple-chase at Colchester, the dull gleam of a college trophy from Oxford days, the silver-mounted hoof of a favorite horse—they were more than objects. They were fragments of a past now veiled from his sight.

He paused at the rack of rifles, lifting one after another, their weight familiar in his hands. The Remington spoke of a lion stalked across the hills west of Berenice, felled at three hundred yards as the sun bled into dusk. Another whispered of ibex in the Khor Baraka, of antelope in the northern mountains of Suakin. A small Greener shotgun recalled winter nights on the Salcombe estuary, the kick of the stock against his shoulder as mallards burst from the reeds.

But the memories turned bitter. These were voices from a life he could no longer see, sights he would never witness again. He set the guns aside, his jaw tightening. A reckless longing seized him—to defy his blindness, to step beyond its cage and reclaim the world denied to him. His mind raced eastward, past the stars, to the scorching sun of Port Said, the chant of coalers under red braziers, the slow procession of ships through the Suez. He could almost smell the brine of the Red Sea, feel the grit of Yambo’s streets beneath his boots, hear the murmur of Suakin’s bazaars.

The desert called to him—the silence of the sands, the stark rise of rock-cones against the sky, the wind hissing through twilight. It was his inheritance, and he ached for it with a thirst fiercer than fever.

Then, abruptly, the trance broke. Birdsong filtered through the window—blackbirds and swallows greeting the dawn. The illusion shattered. There would be no more journeys. The chain of his affliction held him fast.

He climbed the stairs slowly, guided by the balustrade, and fell into bed as the sun rose. Sleep took him, heavy and dreamless.

The sun was already high over Dongola, baking the earth, when the crowd gathered before the house of Emir Wad El Nejoumi. They had come for sport—a white prisoner, captured near the wells of El Agia, now stood beneath the palms, guarded by four Ansar soldiers.

Harry Feversham was a ragged figure, stripped of dignity. His sun-blistered shoulders bore the marks of the desert, his wrists bound with palm rope, an iron collar locked around his neck. He grinned at the jeering crowd like a man unhinged, playing the madman for his life. If he could convince them he was harmless—a fool, not a spy—they might send him to Omdurman rather than the gallows looming black against the yellow sand.

But the act was harder than he’d imagined. Inside the Emir’s house, his fate was being weighed. Outside, the mob taunted him. An old hag danced before him, her song a graphic promise of mutilation and hell. Children blew war-trumpets in his ears until his head rang. A warrior tested his spear’s edge, lunging forward—only for Feversham to be yanked backward by the chain, choking as he fell.

Then came the summons. Dragged into the dim room, he faced Nejoumi.

"You are a spy," the Emir declared.

"A musician," Feversham insisted, laughing.

A broken zither was thrust into his hands. With stiff fingers, he plucked out the only melody he knew—the same tune Durrance had heard in Tewfikieh, the same Ethne had played the night before.

Nejoumi was unconvinced. He offered food—raw camel liver, salt-streaked and pungent. Feversham ate, though his stomach revolted, knowing hesitation meant fear, and fear meant death.

The questions came again: of Cairo’s defenses, of Assouan’s garrison, of whispers against the Sirdar.

"How would a Greek know such things?" Feversham countered.

Nejoumi’s patience snapped. Outside, they drenched his bonds, tightening them until the fibers bit into flesh. A noose coiled around his neck. He was marched to the gallows.

"Speak," Nejoumi commanded. "Or you die now."

Feversham grinned, shaking his head like a simpleton. His heart hammered, not with terror, but with a strange exhilaration. He had played his part.

Nejoumi sneered. Then, with a wave:

"Tomorrow, the Kaffir goes to Omdurman."

Only then did Feversham feel the agony in his wrists. Only then did he wonder—would Ethne ever know? Would the fourth feather ever return?

But for now, he had survived.
