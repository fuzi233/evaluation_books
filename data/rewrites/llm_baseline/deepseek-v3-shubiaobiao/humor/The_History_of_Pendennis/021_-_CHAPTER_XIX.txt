Our friend Pen was not sorry when his mentor, Major Pendennis, took leave of him after their arrival in Oxbridge. The Major, having dutifully discharged his obligation, was more than ready to resume his own pursuits—preferably those involving good company and fine shooting.

Pen, now left to his own devices, was introduced to university life by his lively companion, Mr. Foker, who ensured he mingled with the best set. The Major had done his part; now it was time for Pen to carve his own path—or, more accurately, to amble along it with all the grace of a young man who believed the world owed him admiration.

Pen began his academic career with a flourish, attending lectures with an air of diligence that would have impressed his mother. Mathematics, however, soon proved an insurmountable foe. He found himself outmatched by a few unrefined young men—the sort who wore thick shoes and cared not for fashion—and so he wisely abandoned the exact sciences in favor of more gentlemanly pursuits, namely Greek and Latin.

His mother, ever indulgent, was relieved. She had heard dreadful tales of students overworking themselves into early graves, and she much preferred her darling boy to preserve his delicate constitution. Pen, though unaware of any lurking mortal frailty, humored her by promising not to study *too* hard—a vow he kept with remarkable consistency.

Pen soon discovered that lectures were tedious, and private study far more suited to a man of his intellect. He announced to his mother that he would read independently—a decision she applauded, despite knowing nothing of Homer beyond his being "some old Greek fellow."

When he returned home for holidays, it was not without fanfare. His wardrobe had expanded considerably: morning shooting jackets with enviable buttons, evening waistcoats embroidered to excess, and cravats so elaborate they might have been architectural marvels. His dressing-case gleamed with silver, his fingers glittered with rings, and his new French watch made his father’s old chronometer look like a relic from the Dark Ages.

He had also developed a fondness for cigars—fine ones, imported—which he insisted were essential to literary composition. His mother, though skeptical, indulged him, even going so far as to light one for him while he lounged with a novel. "If I asked you to burn the house down, I believe you would," Pen laughed. She likely would have.

Pen’s education was not limited to the classics. He consumed French novels with the same enthusiasm as his cigars, though he assured his mother (and poor scandalized Laura) that this was purely for linguistic practice. Wine, too, became a scholarly necessity. Having exhausted his father’s cellar, he ordered fresh stock from London, casually adding it to the household accounts. Mrs. Pendennis, though alarmed by the sums, paid without protest. After all, shouldn’t her son enjoy himself while he was home?

His allowance was generous—too generous, perhaps. His father had left a fund specifically for his education, and Pen spent it with the cheerful disregard of a man who had never earned a shilling. Fine books, fine clothes, fine horses—he acquired them all, not with extravagance, but with the quiet assurance of one who believed such things his due.

By his second year, Pen had established himself as a figure of note. He dressed impeccably, quoted poetry with ease, and spoke of past heartbreaks (namely, the infamous Miss Fotheringay) with such tragic flair that younger students regarded him with awe. He had *suffered*, they whispered. He had *lived*.

His influence was such that when he adopted a crimson satin cravat, half the university followed suit. When he sighed over a sonnet, a dozen imitations sprang up. He was, in short, exactly the sort of man who thrives in a place where reputation outweighs achievement.

Yet for all his polish, Pen was not immune to the arithmetic of indulgence. His tailor’s bills mounted, his wine merchant grew prosperous, and his collection of rare books expanded at a rate that would have alarmed a man of thriftier inclinations. But Pen was no such man. He moved through life with the serene confidence of one who had never been forced to balance a ledger—and who had no intention of starting now.

And so, Arthur Pendennis of Boniface strolled through Oxbridge, admired by many, envied by more, and wholly convinced that the world was his to enjoy. Whether it would remain so indulgent was a question for another day.
