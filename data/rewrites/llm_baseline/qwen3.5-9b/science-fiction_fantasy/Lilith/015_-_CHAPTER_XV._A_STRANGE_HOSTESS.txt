I traveled onward, guided by the moon. As was her custom, she hung full tonight—a sight I had never witnessed otherwise—and as she dipped below the horizon, I felt a peculiar sensation, as though a faint smile graced her lunar visage. Just as her lower rim touched the earth's edge, a cottage materialized in the center of her disk, seemingly painted onto the silver surface. Through its open door and window, a warm light spilled forth, carrying with it a singular conviction: *I am expected there.*

Almost instantly, the moon vanished, and with it, the cottage. Night pressed down heavily, darkness swallowing the landscape. My path lay across a relentless series of small ravines, making travel perilous. Resolving to wait for dawn, I settled into a shallow sandy hollow. Supping on the fruits the children had gifted me before our parting, I succumbed quickly to sleep.

I awoke abruptly, staring upward at constellations alien to my home world. After a moment of gazing, I sensed a presence seated on the ground nearby, slightly elevated above me. Startled—as one is when suddenly realizing solitude is an illusion—I noted the figure blocked my view of the sky, rendering its silhouette distinct against the heavens. From my prone position, it loomed larger than any human. It shifted its head, revealing its back to me.

"Will you not come with me?" a voice asked. It was sweet, mellow, and unmistakably female.

Desiring to learn more of my hostess, I replied, "Thank you, but I am quite comfortable here. Where would you lead me? I enjoy sleeping in the open air."

"There is no harm in the air itself," she responded, "but the creatures that roam these wastes at night are not ones a man wishes near while he sleeps."

"I have not been disturbed," I assured her.

"No," she admitted, "I have sat by you since you lay down."

"That is very kind! How did you know I was here? Why show me such favor?"

"I saw you," she answered, keeping her back turned, "in the fading moonlight just as she set. I see poorly in daylight, but perfectly at night. The shadow of my house would have concealed you, yet both doors stood open. I was wandering the waste and spotted you entering this hollow. Though you were already asleep before I arrived, I hesitated to wake you. People are terrified if I approach suddenly. They call me the 'Cat-woman.' It is not my name."

Recalling the children's tales—that she was frightful and clawed—I paused. Yet her voice remained gentle, tinged with apology. She could not be a malevolent giantess.

"You shall not hear it from me," I said softly. "Please, tell me what I *may* call you!"

"When you truly know me," she replied, "call me by the name that fits best. That will reveal your nature. Few give me the correct one; it is fortunate when they do."

"I suppose, Madam, you reside in the cottage I saw within the moon?"

"Yes. I dwell there alone, save for visitors. It is a humble place, but I do my best for my guests. Sometimes, their sleep becomes sweet."

Her voice resonated within me, instilling a profound stillness. "I will go with you, Madam," I said, rising.

She rose immediately, leading the way without glancing backward. I could barely discern her form, yet I managed to follow. She towered over me, though not as impossibly high as I had imagined. Her refusal to turn her face piqued my curiosity, yet her voice rang with such truth that I felt no apprehension. Still, how could I name someone I could not see? I tried to catch up, but whenever I quickened my pace, she matched it effortlessly, maintaining a steady lead.

Fear began to creep in. Why such caution regarding visibility? Perhaps extraordinary ugliness accounted for it—she might fear terrifying me. A horror of inconceivable monstrosity seized me. Was I following an unseen hideousness through the dark? Regret washed over me for accepting her hospitality. Silence stretched between us, becoming unbearable. I *must* break it.

"I wish to find my way," I said, "to a place I've heard of but whose name I haven't learned. Perhaps you can tell it to me?"

"Describe it, and I shall direct you. The foolish Bags know nothing, and the careless Lovers forget almost everything."

"Where do those live?"

"You have just come from them!"

"I never heard those names before!"

"You would not have heard them. No people know their own names!"

"Strange!"

"Perhaps so! Yet hardly anyone anywhere knows their true name. Imagine a fine gentleman hearing himself addressed by his actual name!"

Holding my peace, I wondered what my name might be.

"What do you fancy yours is now?" she continued, sensing my thought. "Pardon me, but it is of no consequence."

I opened my mouth to answer, only to discover my name had vanished from my memory entirely. I could not even recall its first letter! This was the second time I had been asked my name and failed to provide it.

"Never mind," she said. "It is not needed. Your true name is written on your forehead, but currently, it swirls so chaotically that no one can read it. I shall help steady it. Soon it will slow, and hopefully, settle."

Startled, I fell silent. We had left the channels and walked for a long time, yet the cottage remained unseen.

"The Little Ones told me," I said finally, "of a smooth green country, pleasant to the feet!"

"Yes?"

"They also mentioned a girl-giantess who is a queen somewhere. Is that her realm?"

"There is a city in that grassy land," she replied, "where a woman holds the title of Princess. The city is called Bulika. But certainly, the princess is not a girl! She is older than this world, arriving from yours with a terrible history that remains unfinished. She is an evil person, wielding significant influence over the Prince of the Power of the Air. Once, the people of Bulika were simple folk, tilling soil and grazing sheep. She came among them, welcomed warmly, and taught them to dig for diamonds and opals to trade with strangers, abandoning agriculture for urban life. One day, they killed a massive snake, enraging her so deeply that she declared herself their princess and became their terror. At that time, the country was known as THE LAND OF WATERS; the dry channels you have crossed were then overflowing with living torrents, and the valley where the Bags and Lovers now plant fruit trees was a vast lake receiving much of that flow. However, the wicked princess gathered the water in her lap, attempting to carry it away. Her lap held only half, and the moment she departed, the remaining water fled underground, leaving the land arid and dusty as her own heart. Without the subterranean waters, all life would have perished ages ago, for where there is no water, no rain falls, and where no rain falls, no springs arise. Since then, she has resided in Bulika, holding her inhabitants in constant dread, striving to prevent their multiplication. Yet they boast, believing themselves prosperous and self-satisfied—expert bargainers, skilled sellers and cheaters, united by common interest but treacherous when conflicts arise. Proud of their princess and her power, they despise everyone they outmaneuver, convinced of their own honor while viewing others as inferior. The depth of their worthlessness and the height of their vanity are incomprehensible to those who have not visited, nor learned to recognize these miserable, misgoverned, self-deceived creatures."

"I thank you, Madam. Now, if you please, tell me about the Little Ones—the Lovers. I long to serve them. Who and what are they? How did they come to be there? Those children are the greatest wonder I have encountered in this world of wonders."

"In Bulika, you might find answers. There is an ancient poem in the palace library, unreadable by anyone there, yet clearly stating that after the Lovers endure great trials and learn their own names, they will fill the land and make the giants their slaves."

"By then, they will have grown a little, will they not?" I asked.

"Yes, they will have grown; yet I suspect they will not have grown. It is possible to grow and not grow simultaneously—to become smaller while expanding, yes, even to grow by means of not growing!"

"Your words are strange, Madam!"

"But I have heard that some words, because they mean more, appear to mean less!"

"That is true, and such words *must* be understood. It would be fitting for the Princess of Bulika to hear what the very silence of the land shouts in her ears daily! Yet she is far too clever to comprehend anything."

"Then I suppose, when the little Lovers are grown, their land will have water again?"

"Not exactly. When they are thirsty enough, they will have water, and when they possess water, they will grow. Growth requires water. Beneath, it flows still."

"I have heard that water twice," I said, "once when I lay down to await the moon, and again when I woke to find the sun shining. Also, when I fell, nearly killed by the bad giant. Both times, the voices of the water came, healing me."

The woman never turned her head, staying just ahead of me
