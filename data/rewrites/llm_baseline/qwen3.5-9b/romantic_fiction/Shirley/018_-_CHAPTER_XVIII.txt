The evening hung heavy and still, promising to descend into a sultry warmth. Around the setting sun, clouds burned with a violet glow; hues reminiscent of India rather than England suffused the horizon, casting rosy reflections across the hillsides, house fronts, tree trunks, winding roads, and rolling pastures. The two girls descended slowly from the fields. By the time they reached the churchyard, the bells had fallen silent; the crowds had filed inside. The entire scene was profoundly solitary.

"How pleasant and calm it is!" said Caroline.

"And how stifling it will be within the church!" Shirley replied. "What a dreary, long speech Dr. Boultby will deliver! And how the curates will hammer away at their prepared orations! Personally, I would prefer not to enter."

"But my uncle will be furious if he notices our absence."

"I shall bear the brunt of his anger; he will not devour me. I shall regret missing his sharp wit. I know his address will be all logic for the church and all satire for the schismatics. He will surely recall the Battle of Royd Lane. I shall also regret depriving you of Mr. Hall's sincere, friendly homily, rich with its rustic Yorkshire dialect; yet here I must remain. The grey church and grayer tombs look divine bathed in this crimson light. Nature is now offering her evening prayers; she kneels before those red hills. I see her prostrate on the great steps of her altar, praying for a fair night for sailors at sea, for travelers in deserts, for lambs on the moors, and for unfledged birds in the woods. Caroline, I see her clearly, and I will tell you what she resembles. She is what Eve was when she and Adam stood alone upon the earth."

"And that is not Milton's Eve, Shirley."

"Milton's Eve! Milton's Eve! I insist. No, by the pure Mother of God, she is not! Cary, we are alone; we may speak our minds. Milton was great; but was he good? His intellect was correct; what of his heart? He beheld heaven; he looked down upon hell. He saw Satan, and Sin his daughter, and Death their horrific offspring. Serried angels presented their battalions before him; the long lines of adamantine shields reflected the indescribable splendor of heaven back upon his blinded eyes. Devils gathered their legions in his sight; their dim, discrowned, tarnished armies marched rank and file before him. Milton attempted to envision the first woman; but, Cary, he failed to see her."

"You are bold to say so, Shirley."

"No more bold than faithful. It was his cook he saw; or it was Mrs. Gill, as I have witnessed her, making custards in the heat of summer within the cool dairy, surrounded by rose-trees and nasturtiums near the latticed window, preparing a cold collation for the rectors—preserves and 'dulcet creams'; puzzling over which choice offered the finest delicacy, arranging flavors so they did not clash, creating an elegant sequence of tastes sustained by kindliest variety."

"All very well indeed, Shirley."

"I would remind him that the first men of the earth were Titans, and that Eve was their mother; from her sprang Saturn, Hyperion, Oceanus; she bore Prometheus—"

"Pagan that you are! What does that signify?"

"I say, there were giants on the earth in those days—giants striving to scale heaven. The first woman's breast, heaving with life on this world, yielded the daring to contend with Omnipotence, the strength to endure a thousand years of bondage, the vitality to sustain that vulture-like death through countless ages, the inexhaustible life and incorruptible excellence, sisters to immortality, which, after millennia of crime, struggle, and woe, could conceive and birth a Messiah. The first woman was heaven-born. Vast was the heart from which gushed the spring of the blood of nations, and grand the undegenerate head where rested the crown of creation's consort."

"She coveted an apple and was cheated by a snake; but you have mixed Scripture and mythology until nothing makes sense. You have not yet told me what you saw kneeling on those hills."

"I saw—I now see—a Woman-Titan. Her robe of blue air spreads to the edges of the heath, where that distant flock grazes; a veil white as an avalanche sweeps from her head to her feet, edged with arabesques of lightning. Beneath her breast I perceive her zone, purple like the horizon; through its blush shines the star of evening. I cannot picture her steady eyes; they are clear, deep as lakes, lifted and full of worship, trembling with the softness of love and the luster of prayer. Her forehead possesses the expanse of a cloud, paler than the early moon rising long before darkness gathers. She rests her bosom on the ridge of Stilbro' Moor; her mighty hands are joined beneath it. Thus kneeling, face to face, she speaks with God. That Eve is Jehovah's daughter, as Adam was His son."

"She is very vague and visionary. Come, Shirley, we ought to enter the church."

"Caroline, I will not; I shall stay out here with my mother Eve, in these days called Nature. I love her—an undying, mighty being! Heaven may have faded from her brow when she fell in Paradise, but all that is glorious on earth shines there still. She takes me to her bosom and shows me her heart. Hush, Caroline! You will see her and feel as I do if we remain silent together."

"I shall humor your whim; but you will begin talking again before ten minutes pass."

Miss Keeldar, upon whom the soft excitement of the warm summer evening seemed to work with unusual power, leaned against an upright headstone. She fixed her gaze on the deep-burning west and sank into a pleasurable trance. Caroline, stepping slightly apart, paced to and fro beneath the rectory garden wall, dreaming in her own way. Shirley had mentioned the word "mother." That word sparked an image in Caroline's imagination not of the mighty, mystical parent of Shirley's visions, but of a gentle human form—the shape she attributed to her own mother, unknown, unloved, yet deeply desired.

"Oh, that the day would come when she would remember her child! Oh, that I might know her, and, knowing her, love her!"

Such was her aspiration.

The longing of her childhood filled her soul once more. The desire that had kept her awake many nights in her crib, and which the fear of its falsity had nearly extinguished in recent years, suddenly relit and glowed warmly in her heart: that her mother might arrive on some happy day, summon her to her presence, look upon her fondly with loving eyes, and say tenderly in a sweet voice, "Caroline, my child, I have a home for you; you shall live with me. All the love you have needed, and never tasted, from infancy, I have saved for you carefully. Come; it shall cherish you now."

A noise on the road roused Caroline from her filial hopes and Shirley from her Titan visions. They listened, hearing the tramp of horses. They looked and saw a glitter through the trees, catching glimpses through the foliage of martial scarlet; helms shone, plumes waved. Silent and orderly, six soldiers rode softly by.

"The same we saw this afternoon," whispered Shirley. "They have been halting somewhere until now. They wish to be as unnoticed as possible, seeking their rendezvous at this quiet hour while the people attend church. Did I not say we should witness unusual things soon?"

Scarcely had the sight and sound of the soldiers vanished when another, somewhat different disturbance broke the night's hush—a child's impatient scream. They looked. A man emerged from the church carrying an infant—a robust, ruddy little boy of about two years old, roaring with all the power of his lungs. He had likely just awakened from a church-slumber. Two little girls, aged nine and ten, followed. The influence of the fresh air and the attraction of some flowers gathered from a grave soon quieted the child. The man sat down with him, dandling him on his knee as tenderly as any woman; the two little girls took their places, one on either side.

"Good-evening, William," said Shirley, after scrutinizing the man. He had seen her before and appeared to be waiting for recognition. He removed his hat and grinned a smile of pleasure. He was a rough-headed, hard-featured figure, not old but very weather-beaten. His attire was decent and clean; that of his children was singularly neat. It was our old friend Farren. The young ladies approached him.

"You are not going into the church?" he inquired, gazing at them complacently, yet with a mixture of bashfulness in his look—not born of awe of their station, but of an appreciation of their elegance and youth. Before gentlemen such as Moore or Helstone, William was often a little dogged; with proud or insolent ladies, he was quite unmanageable, sometimes very resentful. Yet he was most sensible of, most tractable to, good humor and civility. His nature—stubborn though it was—was repelled by inflexibility in others; for this reason, he had never been able to like his former master, Moore.
