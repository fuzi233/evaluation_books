Six weeks following the duel, the miller at Stow arrived at the great house in deep distress, seeking to borrow the bloodhounds. Rose Salterne had vanished into the night, her whereabouts unknown to any soul.

Sir Richard was absent in Bideford, yet the old steward, Anthony, seized the initiative to summon the keepers. Down marched the serving-men to the mill, trailed by all the idle lads of the parish, viewing the maiden-hunt as excellent sport. They approached the case with eyes wide open, casting their judgment in favor of Rose.

They hurled vile curses upon the miller and his wife, labeling them hard-hearted old heathens, convinced that the poor maid had been driven to cast herself from a cliff or drown in the sea. Conversely, the women of Stow held a unanimous belief that the "hussy" had fled with some scoundrel, predicting inevitably that pride would bring a fall.

The facts, however, told a different tale. All of Rose's trinkets remained behind, proving she had departed honestly. Nothing appeared missing save for some of her linen, which Old Anthony hinted might be discovered in another's box. The sole evidence was a faint footprint beneath her bedroom window. Upon this track, the bloodhound was placed (though leashed). After a premonitory whimper, he raised his mighty voice and started bell-mouthed through the garden gate, up the lane, towing behind him the panting keeper until they reached the downs above, heading straight for Marslandmouth. There, the entire posse comitatus halted, breathless, before the door of Lucy Passmore.

Lucy, as I had perhaps noted previously, was now a widow and found her solitude not entirely contrary to her interests. Her augury regarding her old man had been fulfilled; he had never returned since the night he put to sea with Eustace and the Jesuits.

"Some natural tears she shed, but dried them soon"—these, at least, were those not required for business purposes. Determined to forestall suspicion by a bold move, she departed for Stow and recounted a most pathetic tale to Lady Grenville. She claimed her husband had gone out pollock fishing and never returned, yet she had heard horsemen galloping past her window in the dead of night. She was certain they were Jesuits who had carried off her old man by main force, likely killing and salting him down for provisions on their voyage back to the Pope in Rome. She concluded by entreating protection against those "Popish skulkers up to Chapel," who were sworn to do her mischief, and appealing to Lady Grenville's sense of justice: ought not the Queen grant her a pension, seeing her heart's love turned into a martyred saint by the hands of idolatrous traitors?

Lady Grenville, who held a high opinion of Lucy's medical skills and always summoned her when a child suffered a "housty" (sore throat), went forth and pleaded the case before Sir Richard with such effect that Lucy emerged better off than ever for the next two or three years. Yet now, what did she have to do with Rose's disappearance? And where was she herself? Her door was fastened, around which her flock of goats stood, crying in vain for her to milk them. From the down above, her donkeys, wandering at their sweet will, answered the bloodhound's bay with a burst of harmony.

"They're laughing at us, Keeper, they neddies; surely we've lost our labor here."

But the bloodhound, after circling the door for a while, turned down the glen and never ceased until he reached the sea's edge.

"They've taken water. Let us return and raid the old witch's house."

"'Tis just like that old Lucy to lock a poor maid into shame."

Returning, they assaulted the cottage, and by a general plebiscite, ransacked the humble dwelling, partly in indignation and partly, if truth be told, in the hope of plunder. Yet there was no plunder to be found. Lucy had decamped with all her movable wealth, save for the huge black cat among the embers, who, at the sight of the bloodhound, vanished up the chimney (some claiming with a strong scent of brimstone). Viewed outside, the beast was chased into the woods, where, I doubt not, it lived many happy years as a scourge to all the rabbits of the glen.

The goats and donkeys were driven off to Stow, and the mob returned, a little ashamed of themselves when their brief wrath had passed, and a little afraid of what Sir Richard might say.

When he returned, Sir Richard sold the donkeys and goats, giving the proceeds to the poor, promising to refund them if Lucy returned and surrendered to justice. But Lucy did not return. Her cottage, from which the neighbors shrank as from a haunted place, remained as she had left it, crumbling slowly into four fern-covered walls. Through them, the little stream murmured on from pool to pool—the only voice breaking the silence of that lonely glen for many a year to come.

A few days later, Sir Richard, traveling from Bideford to Stow, called in at Clovelly Court and mentioned, with a casual "by the by," news that caused Will Cary to leap from his seat almost to the ceiling. What it was, we already know.

"And there is no clue?" asked Old Cary, for his son was speechless.

"Only this," replied Sir Richard. "I hear that some fellow prowling about the cliffs that night saw a pinnace running for Lundy."

Will rose and hurried out of the room. Within half an hour, he and three or four armed servants were on board a trawling skiff, heading for Lundy. He did not return for three days, bringing news that an elderly man, seemingly a foreigner, had lodged for several months in a section of the ruined Moresco Castle tenanted by one John Braund. A few weeks prior, a younger man, also a foreigner, had joined him from a ship—a Flushinger or Easterling vessel of some sort. The ship came and went more than once, carrying the young man. A few days ago, a lady and her maid, a stout woman, had come with him to the castle, conferring secretly with the elder man for a long time. They stayed all night, and in the morning, all three sailed away. Fishermen on the beach had heard the young man call the other "father." He was a very still man, much like a mass-priest might be. More they did not know, nor did they wish to know.

Thereupon, Old Cary and Sir Richard sent Will on a second trip with the parish constable of Hartland (in which vast parish, for its sins, sits the Isle of Lundy, ten miles out at sea). He returned with the body of the hapless John Braund, farmer, fisherman, smuggler, and so forth. This worthy, after much fruitless examination (during which the examinee was afflicted with extreme deafness and loss of memory), departed for Exeter Gaol on charges of "harboring priests, Jesuits, gypsies, and other suspect and traitorous persons."

Poor John Braund, whose motive for entertaining such unsavory guests was probably not treason but a wife, seven children, and arrears of rent, did not thrive under the change from the pure air of Lundy to the pestilential atmosphere of Exeter Gaol, made infamous two years later (if I recall correctly) by a "black assizes," nearly as fatal as the more notorious one at Oxford. For in it, "whether by the stench of the prisoners, or by a stream of foul air," judge, jury, counsel, and bystanders—numbering many members of Devon's best families—sickened in court and died miserably within a few days.

John Braund caught the gaol-fever in a week and died raving in that noisome den. His secret, if he possessed one, perished with him, leaving nothing but vague suspicion regarding Rose Salterne's fate. Few doubted she had fled with the Spaniard, but whither, and in what capacity? On that last subject, be assured, no mercy was shown to her by many a Bideford dame, who had hated the poor girl simply for her beauty; nor by many a country lady, who had "always expected that the girl would be brought to ruin by the absurd notice, beyond what her station had a right to, which was taken of her," while every young maiden aspired to fill the throne Rose had abdicated. Thus, on the whole, Bideford considered itself to be proceeding as well without poor Rose as it had with her, or even better. And though she lingered in some hearts as a fair dream, the business and bustle of daily life soon swept that dream away, and her place knew her no more.

What of Will Cary?

For a while, he was like a man distraught. He heaped upon himself superfluous reproaches for having, as he claimed, first brought Rose into disgrace and then driven her into the arms of the Spaniard.
