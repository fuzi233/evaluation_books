After returning to the George, Mr. Foker and his guest settled into a sumptuous meal in the coffee-room, where Mr. Rincer arrived with the first course, bowing so solemnly you’d think he was serving the Lord-Lieutenant himself. Foker attacked the turtle and venison with the same gusto he’d once shown for ginger-beer and smuggled polonies—though back then, “gusto” had meant surviving on half a loaf and a stolen apple. Pen couldn’t help but admire his refined taste when Foker declared the champagne “condemned gooseberry,” winked at the port with one eye, and announced it was “the right sort.” He even told the waiters there was no fooling him—he knew them all by their Christian names, asked after their children, and treated them like family.

As the London coaches clattered up—the old days being full of such dramatic arrivals—Foker flung open the window and called out to the guards and coachmen by name, asking about their families and mimicking the horn-blowing of Jem the ostler with astonishing accuracy. The horses’ cloths were whipped off, the carriages rattled away, and Foker turned to Pen with a satisfied grin: “A bottle of sherry, a bottle of sham, a bottle of port, and a shass caffy—it ain’t so bad, hay, Pen?” After demolishing all that, plus a mountain of nuts and fruit, Foker pronounced it time to “toddle.”

Pen sprang up, eyes bright, face flushed, and they headed toward the theatre. There, they paid the wheezy old lady dozing in the money-taker’s box—Mrs. Dropsicum, Bingley’s mother-in-law, famed for her Lady Macbeth. Foker knew her well. They nearly had first pick of seats in the boxes, though the theatre wasn’t exactly packed—despite Bingley’s grandiose playbills promising “a universal burst of attraction and galvanic thrills of delight.” A few scattered souls dotted the pit benches; others kicked and whistled from the galleries; and a dozen free-admission types occupied the boxes where our young gentlemen sat.

In a private box nearby, Lieutenants Rodgers and Podgers, along with young Cornet Tidmus of the Dragoons, lounged like kings. They didn’t seem to care much about the play—they chatted with the actors mid-scene and applauded by name, as if they were running the show themselves.

Bingley, the manager, played every major role—except when he modestly stepped aside for visiting London stars (who occasionally graced Chatteris). Tonight, he was the ‘Stranger,’ dressed in tight pantaloons, Hessian boots, a long cloak, and a drooping beaver hat adorned with a hearse feather. His raddled face barely peeked out from beneath a great buckled brown wig. He wore stage jewelry too—especially a massive, shiny ring on his little finger, which he twirled dramatically in front of the audience like a magician showing off a trick coin. It had once belonged to George Frederick Cooke, who got it from Mr. Quin, who probably bought it for a shilling. Bingley believed the world was hypnotized by its sparkle. He also liked to read from the stage-book—a book that looked nothing like a real book. It was rouged, tawdry, held like a sacred relic, pointed at ominously, and waved heavenward as if divine inspiration flowed from its pages.

Anybody who’s seen a great light comedian—say, X.—in a chintz dressing-gown, pretending to be a nobleman reading in his chambers until Sir Harry arrives or father comes down for breakfast—anybody who’s seen this man over a fake book knows what true theatrical magic is. And now, the Stranger appeared, lying on the stage bank, holding his gilt volume, displaying his ring and Hessians like trophies. He saw Pen and immediately began acting *at* him.

“Again reading,” said Francis, his servant, “from morn to night. To him nature has no beauty—life no charm. For three years I have never seen him smile.” Bingley’s face darkened like a storm cloud.

Francis continued: “Nothing diverts him. O, if he would but attach himself to any living thing, were it an animal—for something man must love.”

Enter Tobias (played by Goll), emerging from the hut. “O, how refreshing, after seven long weeks, to feel these warm sunbeams once again. Thanks, bounteous heaven, for the joy I taste!” He pressed his cap between his hands, looked up, and prayed.

The Stranger watched him closely.

Francis: “This old man’s share of earthly happiness can be but little. Yet mark how grateful he is for his portion of it.”

Bingley: “Because though old, he is but a child in the leading-string of hope.”

(He stared hard at Foker, who merely sucked the top of his cane, unfazed.)

Francis: “Hope is the nurse of life.”

Bingley: “And her cradle—is the grave.”

He delivered this line with the mournful wail of a bassoon, fixing his gaze on Pendennis so intensely that the poor lad felt the whole house was staring at him. He dropped his eyes. When he raised them again, Bingley was still watching. All through the scene, the manager played directly to Pen, as if determined to conquer him.

When the Stranger prepared to do a good deed—sending Francis off with his book so the servant wouldn’t witness it—Bingley carefully marked the page, so he could continue reading offstage if he wished. But he did it all in plain sight of Pen, as if daring him to resist the spell.

At last, the scene ended. Foker tapped his cane and shouted, “Bravo, Bingley!”

“Give him a hand, Pendennis,” he said. “You know every chap likes a hand.”

Pen, laughing, joined in. The dragoons clapped furiously. You’d have thought the place was packed.

Then came the chamber in Wintersen Castle—Tobias’s hut, the Stranger’s boots, and servants bustling about. “That’s Hicks and Miss Thackthwaite,” whispered Foker. “Pretty girl, ain’t she, Pendennis? But stop—hurray—bravo! Here’s the Fotheringay!”

The pit erupted. Umbrellas thumped. The gallery fired a volley of applause. The dragoons and Foker clapped like mad. You’d swear the theatre was full.

Mr. Costigan’s red face and ragged whiskers peeked from the side-scene. Then—Mrs. Haller entered, head down, but lifting her eyes at the sound of the cheers, sweeping the house with a grateful glance, folding her hands across her chest, and sinking into a magnificent curtsey.

More applause. More umbrellas. Pen, flushed with wine and excitement, clapped louder than anyone—and sang “Bravo!” at the top of his lungs.

Mrs. Haller saw him. Everyone else saw him. Old Mr. Bows, the little first fiddler (whose orchestra was tonight bolstered by a detachment of the Dragoons, courtesy of Colonel Swallowtail), looked up from his desk, crutch beside him, and smiled at the boy’s enthusiasm.

Now, those who’ve only seen Miss Fotheringay since her marriage and entry into London society have no idea how stunning she was when Pen first laid eyes on her. And I warn my reader: the illustrator of this work (who draws ugly faces fairly well but utterly fails at beauty) cannot begin to capture her.

She was tall—six feet of grace and fire—at twenty-six (though she swore she was nineteen). Her forehead was vast, her black hair waved naturally over it (a style modern beauties try to imitate with crimping irons), braided into shining volumes at the back of a neck like the Louvre Venus’s. Her eyes—when she lifted them—shone with tenderness and mystery. Love and Genius seemed to look out, then shyly retreat, as if embarrassed to be seen at the lattice.

Who could have such a commanding brow but a woman of sharp mind? She never laughed (her teeth weren’t good), but a sweet, endless smile played around her lips, dimpling her cheeks and chin. Her nose defied description. Her ears were like tiny pearl shells—so delicate that even the most beautiful earrings in the theatre seemed to insult them.

She wore long black robes, flowing and graceful, which she swept with such elegance that you only glimpsed her sandals now and then—large, yes, but Pen thought them as magical as Cinderella’s slippers.

But it was her arms and hands that truly captivated. They surrounded her. When folded over her bosom in resignation, when dropped in silent agony, when raised in command, or fluttering in gaiety—like snowy doves before Venus’s chariot—she beckoned, repelled, entreated, embraced. Not just one admirer, but the whole house. She rose to her, as the phrase went, as she curtsied and bowed, enchanting all.

She stood there—complete, radiant—as Pen stared.

“I say, Pen,” Foker whispered, “isn’t she a stunner?”

“Hush!” Pen replied. “She’s speaking.”

She began her lines in a deep, sweet voice. Those familiar with *The Stranger* know the dialogue isn’t particularly profound—no original thoughts, no poetic genius. In fact, calling it stupid wouldn’t be far wrong. No one talks like that in real life. If we meet fools, thank God they don’t use such absurdly fine words.

But the Stranger’s talk is sham—like his book, his wig, his bank, his ring—but within the nonsense runs a truth: love, children, forgiveness. And wherever that truth is spoken, the world listens and sympathizes.

Mrs. Haller delivered her part with smothered sorrow, gushing pathos. At first, as Count Wintersen’s housekeeper giving orders for dinner and beds, she spoke with calm despair. But when the servants were gone, she poured her grief into the audience—each person felt she was speaking just to them. Little Bows in the orchestra twitched, nodded, pointed—then, at the famous line: “I have a William too, if he be still alive—Ah, yes, if he be still alive. His little sisters, too! Why, Fancy, dost thou rack me so? Why dost thou image my poor children fainting in sickness, and crying to—to—their mum—um—other”—he buried his face in his blue cotton handkerchief after shouting “Bravo!”

The entire house was moved. Foker pulled out a yellow bandanna and wept piteously.

As for Pen—he was gone. He followed her every move. When she left the stage, the world vanished. Lights blurred, officers reeled. He watched her at the side-scene, where her father took off her shawl. When reconciliation came—when she threw herself onto Bingley’s shoulders, the children clinging to his knees, the Countess (Mrs. Bingley) and Baron Steinforth (played with spirit by Garbetts)—Pen saw only Fotheringay. The curtain fell like a funeral pall. He heard nothing of Bingley’s announcement for the next evening. He didn’t realize the house was calling for Miss Fotheringay. The manager didn’t seem to notice anyone else had caused the success.

Finally, he understood—grinned, and reappeared with Mrs. Haller on his arm.

How beautiful she looked! Her hair had fallen loose. Officers threw her flowers. She clutched them to her heart, brushed her hair back, smiled all around. Her eyes met Pen’s.

Down went the curtain again. She was gone.

Not a note of the overture blew by the brass band of the Dragoons—kind permission of Colonel Swallowtail.

“She’s a crusher, ain’t she now?” Foker asked.

Pen didn’t hear. He couldn’t speak. He didn’t know what he felt—only that it was overwhelming, maddening, delicious. A fever of wild joy and longing.

Then Rowkins and Miss Thackthwaite danced the double hornpipe. Foker delighted in it, just as he had in the tragedy. Pen didn’t care. He only remembered that she had been in that scene.

It was a mist before his eyes.

At the dance’s end, he checked his watch. Time to go.

“Hang it, stay for *The Bravo of the Battle-Axe*,” Foker urged. “Bingley’s splendid in it—he wears red tights and carries Mrs. B. over the Pine-bridge of the Cataract. Only she’s too heavy. It’s great fun—do stay.”

Pen glanced at the bill, hoping to see her name hidden among the cast. But no. She wasn’t listed.

He had to leave. A long ride home. He squeezed Foker’s hand—choking, unable to speak—and left the theatre.

He wandered wildly through the town, unaware of time. Then mounted at the George and rode homeward. Clavering clock struck one as he entered the yard at Fairoaks.

His mother might have been awake, but she only heard him dash into bed and pull the covers over his head.

Pen hadn’t been known for sleepless nights. But tonight, he sank into a deep, dreamless sleep.

Even in later years, when anxiety kept men awake, they often fell asleep anyway—only to be jolted awake by it later. “Come, my man,” Anxiety says, “no more laziness—you must wake up and talk.” Then they talk into the midnight.

But Pen wasn’t there yet. He slept soundly—until morning, when rooks cawed from the wood beyond his window.

And—just as his eyes opened—the beloved image was already in his mind.

“My dear boy,” he heard her say, “you were in a sound sleep, and I would not disturb you. But I have been close by your pillow all this while—and I don’t intend that you shall leave me. I am Love! I bring fever and passion, wild longing, maddening desire, restless craving. Many a long day I heard you calling for me—and now I am come.”

Was Pen frightened? Not at all. He didn’t know what was coming. It was pure pleasure, wild delight.

Just as, three years earlier, when entering the fifth form at the Cistercians, his father had given him a gold watch—which he’d taken from under his pillow the moment he woke, polishing it endlessly, hiding in corners to listen to its ticking—so now Pen exulted in his new delight. He felt for it in his waistcoat pocket, wound it up at night, hugged it at dawn.

By the way, that first watch was flashy but poorly made—it never worked right. After a while, he forgot it, then traded it for a better one.

But Pen felt suddenly older. He was in love—fully, completely, like the hero of the best romance ever written.

He told John to bring shaving water with confidence. He dressed in his finest clothes, descended to breakfast with a haughty air, patronizing his mother and little Laura, who had been practicing her music lesson for hours.

After he read the prayers (without hearing a word), Laura asked, “What was the play about?”

Pen laughed and refused to tell her. Better she didn’t know.

Then she asked why he was wearing his finest pin and new waistcoat.

Pen blushed and told his mother that his old schoolfellow, whom he’d dined with in Chatteris, was studying with a tutor in Baymouth—a very learned man. Since Pen was going to college, and several young men studied in Baymouth, he wanted to ride over and see what they were reading.

Laura frowned. Helen Pendennis stared at her son, troubled by the vague fear that had haunted her since Farmer Gurnett returned saying Pen wouldn’t come home for dinner.

Arthur’s eyes defied her. She tried to console herself. The boy had never lied to her.

Pen behaved with exaggerated dignity during breakfast. After taking leave of both ladies, he was soon heard riding out of the stable court.

At first, he rode slowly. Then, once out of earshot, he galloped like a madman.

Smirke, riding softly with his toes out, thinking of his own affairs, met his pupil—who shot past like a whirlwind.

Smirke’s pony shied. The gentle curate went flying over his head into the stinging-nettles.

Pen laughed, pointed toward Baymouth, and was half a mile away before Smirke picked himself up.

Pen had decided he must see Foker that morning—must hear about her, know about her, be near someone who knew her.

Meanwhile, honest Smirke, sitting amid nettles as his pony nibbled the hedge, wondered: should he go to Fairoaks now that his pupil had clearly fled?

Yes, he decided. He might ask Mrs. Pendennis when Arthur would return. And hear Miss Laura recite Watts’s Catechism.

He climbed back on the pony—both used to his slipping off—and advanced toward the house from which his scholar had just bolted in a frenzy.

Thus, love makes fools of us all—big and small. The curate tumbled headfirst in pursuit. And Pen had launched into the first heat of the mad race.
