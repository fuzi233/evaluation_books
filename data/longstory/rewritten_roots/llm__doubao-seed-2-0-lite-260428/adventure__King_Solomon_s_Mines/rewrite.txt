This unvarnished, no-frills record of the most harrowing trek I ever survived in decades roaming the South African bush unfolds beat for beat as it happened, no embellishments added, no dangers softened:
1.  My first fateful meeting with Sir Henry Curtis, the wealthy English gentleman who would risk everything to track down his lost missing brother
2.  The ancient, half-forgotten legend of King Solomon’s fabled diamond mines — sealed away for centuries beyond uncrossable deserts and the borders of a kingdom no white man had ever set foot in
3.  The tall, quiet stranger named Umbopa who walked out of the savanna to ask for work, carrying a secret far larger than any of us guessed at the time
4.  The high-stakes elephant hunt that spiraled out of control, leaving one of our party grievously wounded before we even reached the edge of the desert
5.  The brutal, sun-scorched march across miles of unbroken red sand, where every step felt like it could be our last
6.  The mad, desperate scramble for water when our canteens ran dry and we were hours away from dying of heatstroke
7.  The half-ruined, ancient stone road no outsider had trod in a thousand years: Solomon’s own highway, leading straight to his hidden realm
8.  The moment we crossed the snow-capped mountain pass and stepped into Kukuanaland, a kingdom the outside world had no idea existed
9.  Twala, the brutal one-eyed king who ruled Kukuanaland with iron fists and the cruel magic of his witch advisors
10. The horrific kingdom-wide witch-hunt that nearly got all three of us executed as evil interlopers
11. The impossible feat we pulled off to prove we were not sorcerers, and turn thousands of Twala’s own warriors to our side
12. The tense days of waiting for battle, knowing an army of spear-wielding fighters a hundred times our size was marching to kill us
13. The first clash of armies, where a tiny band of rebels held their ground against overwhelming royal forces
14. The last, blood-soaked stand of the Greys, the king’s legendary elite regiment, that left the battlefields soaked in iron-red blood
15. The vicious fever that seized our party’s Captain Good, nearly taking his life right when we stood on the cusp of finding the mines
16. The Place of Death: the hidden valley of weathered bones that marked the entrance to Solomon’s long-lost treasure vaults
17. The moment we stepped inside the fabled treasure chamber, and saw more gold and uncut diamonds than any of us had ever dared to dream existed
18. The hours we spent trapped behind a sealed stone door, certain we would die in total darkness, our names and our discovery never known to the outside world
19. The bittersweet goodbye we shared with Umbopa, who revealed himself as Kukuanaland’s rightful heir and took his place as the new king

Now that every page of this tale is printed and bound, ready to be turned over to whoever cares to read it, I’ll not lie: the weight of its flaws sits heavy on my shoulders. I’m no author, and I never claimed this book would be a blow-by-blow account of every single thing we lived through and saw on that journey deep into Kukuanaland. There are a hundred little stories and details I ached to linger over that never got more than a passing nod.

I could have filled whole chapters with the wild, half-forgotten legends I collected about that old chain mail that saved all three of our hides from certain slaughter in the great battle of Loo, and the eerie colossi the Kukuanas call the Silent Ones, standing stone-still at the mouth of that vast stalactite cave. I could have rambled for pages on the sharp, suggestive little differences between the Zulu dialect I’ve known my whole life and the speech the Kukuanas use up in those hidden hills. I could have easily spared a dozen pages to talk through the strange new plants and game that roam that unexplored country, too — I stumbled on eight whole varieties of antelope I’d never laid eyes on in all my years hunting, and dozens of new bulb flower species no European botanist had ever recorded before. Most interesting of all, I barely touch on their brilliant military organization: it runs smoother, faster, and makes for far quicker mobilization than the regiments Chaka built back in Zululand, and it never relies on that cruel, senseless rule of forcing every fighting man to stay celibate his whole service. I scarcely mentioned all their odd, warm domestic customs, either, or their stunning skill at smelting and welding metal. They’ve refined that craft to a standard few white smiths back home can match: just look at their “tollas”, those heavy, weighted throwing knives they carry. The backs are solid hammered iron, the cutting edges forged from razor-sharp steel, welded onto the iron frames so seamlessly you can barely see the join.

The truth of it is, I sat down with Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good before I ever set pen to paper, and we all agreed the smartest move was to tell the adventure straight, plain and unvarnished, no tangents. We decided all those side details could wait, to be shared later if any curious scholar or fellow wanderer ever asks for them. I’m more than happy to hand over every scrap of information I have to anyone with a real interest in that country, whenever they like.

All that’s left is for me to apologize for my rough, blunt way of writing. The only excuse I’ve got is that I’ve spent sixty years of my life gripping a rifle stock far more often than a pen. I don’t have the first clue how to pull off those grand, sweeping literary flourishes I see in novels sometimes — I do like to read a story every now and then when I’m stuck indoors out of the rain, after all. I suppose those fancy turns of phrase are what people expect, and I’m sorry I can’t deliver them. But I can’t help but think plain, uncluttered words hit harder than gilded ones, and a story is a thousand times easier to follow when a man just says what he means. There’s an old Kukuana proverb I’ve always lived by: a sharp spear needs no polish. On that same principle, I’m daring to hope a true story, no matter how wild and unbelievable it sounds, doesn’t need to be decked out in fine fancy words to hold its own.

It’s a funny thing that at fifty-five years old, laid up here in Durban with a lion-mauled left leg throbbing so bad I can barely hobble to the porch, I find myself picking up a pen to write a history. I’ve spent every day since I was a boy too young for school tramping the old Cape Colony, trading, hunting, fighting, mining, and I only struck it rich eight months ago. Even with the pile of ivory and gold I’ve got stowed away now? I wouldn’t live through the last sixteen months of what we went through again — not even if I knew I’d walk away with every last penny of that fortune. I’m a timid man, hate unnecessary violence, and I was sick to death of adventures long before this mess started. But here I am, scribbling. Let me lay out my reasons plain, just to make sure I’m not being a fool for doing it:
First: Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good badgered me until I caved.
Second: That lion’s teeth left some rotten kind of poison in my leg, and every year around the anniversary of the mauling the wounds flare up again, bad enough to leave me bedridden. Sixty-six lions I’ve shot in my life, and the last one chewed my leg like a plug of tobacco. Rude, if you ask a man who likes his routine neat.
Third: My boy Harry’s over in a London hospital studying to be a doctor, and I know cutting up dead bodies gets dull after a while. This story won’t be dull. It’ll give him something way more exciting to read for a week, keep him out of mischief.
Fourth, and last: This is the strangest tale I’ve ever lived through. And for a story full of wild Africa, lost mines, and all kinds of deviltry? There’s barely a petticoat in it. Only Foulata, and the ancient witch Gagoola, who was a hundred if she was a day, more fiend than woman anyway. No silly romantic nonsense to bog the pace down.
The Boers say *sutjes, sutjes* — slowly does it — so I won’t rush. Let me start proper. Eighteen months back, I was fresh off a terrible elephant hunt beyond Bamangwato. Everything went wrong out there, topped off by a brutal bout of fever. The second I could stand I trekked down to the Diamond Fields, sold off what little ivory I had, my wagon, my oxen, paid off my native hunters, and took the post cart to the Cape. I hung around Cape Town a week, found the hotel wildly overpriced, saw the botanical gardens (they’ll probably do some good for the country) and the new Houses of Parliament (they almost certainly won’t) then booked passage back to Natal on the *Dunkeld*, which was docked waiting for the incoming *Edinburgh Castle* from England.
We transhipped the English passengers that afternoon, weighed anchor, and set sail. Two of those new faces caught my eye before we’d even cleared the docks.
The first was easily thirty years old, the broadest-chested, longest-armed man I’d ever laid eyes on. Yellow hair, thick yellow beard, sharp cut features, deep-set pale grey eyes. He looked like a living ancient Dane — the kind of wild raider I’d once seen in a old print, drinking mead out of a bull’s horn, chain shirt slung over his shoulders, battleaxe in one hand. Turns out later that hunch was right: Sir Henry Curtis, that was his name, really did come from Danish blood.
The man talking to him was short, stout, dark, and unmistakably a Royal Navy officer. I can never mistake one: the wide sea air blows all the petty bitterness out of them, leaves them tough, fair, and good company for a hunt, even if a few of them curse like sailors when the weather turns bad. His name was John Good, lieutenant promoted to honorary captain at thirty-one, then tossed out of Her Majesty’s service with nothing but half pay to live on after seventeen years of service, just when he’d learned his trade proper. He was absurdly neat, clean-shaven every minute of the day, and wore a stringless pince-nez stuck to his right eye like it grew there. I later found out he took it off only to wipe it, or tuck it in his trouser pocket at night right next to his two full sets of pearly false teeth that made me break the tenth commandment every time I saw them, since my own chompers are falling to bits.
Night fell fast after we set sail, and the weather turned vicious. A sharp cold gale blew off the coast, and a thick, damp Scotch mist drove every last passenger off the deck. The *Dunkeld* was a flat-bottomed old tub, sailing light, and she rolled so hard I swore she was going to tip clear over into the waves. I huddled near the warm engine room for shelter, watching the big roll pendulum swing back and forth across its marked scale to measure how far she tipped.
“That pendulum’s weighted wrong,” a testy voice said right at my shoulder. It was Captain Good. “If she’d rolled as far as that thing says she did, she’d never have righted herself again. Typical careless merchant skipper nonsense.”
Just then the dinner bell rang, and I was glad for it — there’s no worse bore than a Royal Navy officer ranting about merchant captains, save for a merchant captain ranting right back about Royal Navy officers. We trundled down to the saloon together, found Sir Henry already sat at the table, and I took the seat opposite them. Good and I chatted for ages about hunting, elephants in particular, until a passenger sitting near me nodded my way and spoke up: “You’re asking the right man there. Hunter Quatermain could tell you everything about elephants, if anyone alive can.”
Sir Henry, who’d been sitting quiet listening, jumped so hard he nearly knocked over his glass.
“Excuse me, sir,” he rumbled, his deep, booming voice perfect for a man with that huge chest, “but is your name Allan Quatermain?”
I said it was, and he muttered “fortunate” under his breath into his beard, like he’d just caught a stroke of luck.
When dinner broke up, he strolled over and invited me back to his cabin to smoke a pipe. It was a big sprawling space, two smaller cabins knocked into one — some big colonial swell named Sir Garnet Wolseley had ordered the partition torn out once for a trip up the coast, and no one had bothered putting it back. He sent the steward for a bottle of whisky, the three of us settled in with our pipes, and he cut straight to the point.
“Mr. Quatermain,” he said, fixing those sharp grey eyes right on mine, “around this time two years ago, you were camped outside Bamangwato, north of the Transvaal. You met a man there going by the name of Neville.”
I blinked, surprised he knew so much about my obscure little movements out in the bush. I confirmed it: Neville had outspanned his wagon right next to mine for two weeks, resting his oxen before pushing further north into the interior. I’d gotten a lawyer’s letter a few months back asking after him, and told what I knew: Neville had left Bamangwato at the start of May, with a driver, a voorlooper, and a Kafir tracker named Jim, said he was heading for Inyati, the last trading post in Matabele country. He’d sold his wagon there, I’d later seen it in the hands of a Portuguese trader who said the white man and his single native servant had struck north on foot for a shooting trip, and no one had heard a peep from them since.
A long quiet fell over the cabin. The ship rolled so hard then the mirror on the wall tilted almost right over our heads.
“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said suddenly, leaning forward across the table, “Neville is my younger brother George.”
That’s when I finally placed who he reminded me of. George Neville had been smaller, with a dark beard, but he’d had those exact same sharp grey eyes, the same cut to his jaw.
“Five years ago, our father died without making a will,” he went on, his voice tight with regret. All the family land, the whole estate, passed straight to me as the eldest son. George had no profession, no money, and we’d had a terrible, stupid fight just days before our father died. I was too proud to reach out to him, too stubborn to offer him his fair share of the fortune. He took the few hundred pounds he had in the bank, changed his name to Neville, and ran off to South Africa to make his own luck, without a single word to me.
“I’ve spent three years searching for him. Blood’s thicker than water, you see. I’d give half my fortune just to know he’s alive, and bring him home. Good here was kind enough to come with me on the search — he’s got nothing else to do, stuck on half pay after the Admiralty tossed him out. Now tell us: do you know anything more about where my brother was heading, out there past the edge of the map?”

Sir Henry leaned forward across the campfire’s sputtering orange glow as I paused to tamp fresh tobacco into my pipe. “What was it you heard about my brother’s journey at Bamangwato?” he pressed.

I struck a flint, puffed till the embers glowed bright red, and lifted my eyes to meet his and Captain Good’s. “I never breathed a word of this to another soul till today. I heard he was headed for Solomon’s Mines.”

Both men jolted forward at once, nearly knocking over their tin mugs of coffee. “Solomon’s Mines? Where the devil are they?”
“Your guess is as good as mine for exact coordinates,” I said. “I’ve spotted the jagged peaks that ring them from 130 miles out across unbroken desert, and I only know of one white man who ever crossed that stretch alive. Best thing I can do is tell you the full legend as it came to me. But you have to swear not to repeat a word of it without my say-so. I’ve got my reasons for asking.”
Sir Henry nodded sharp. “You have my word.”
“Certainly, certainly,” Good echoed, fumbling to adjust his misplaced eyepatch.

Most elephant hunters you meet are rough, practical sorts who don’t bother much with old native tales beyond what they need to track game or avoid a Kafir spear. But once in a blue moon you run across a man who collects those stories, who digs through the dust of this dark forgotten continent trying to piece together what it was long before we showed up with our guns. Thirty years back, when I was on my very first elephant hunt in Matabele country, that’s exactly the sort of man I met. His name was Evans. The next year he got gored through the gut by a wounded buffalo, poor fellow, and lies buried not ten miles from the Zambesi Falls.
One night around his fire, I was telling him about a strange set of ancient workings I’d stumbled on while chasing koodoo out in what’s now the Lydenburg district. Prospectors have rediscovered those diggings lately, but I knew of them decades ago: a wide wagon road hewn straight out of solid rock leading to a gallery mouth, stacks of gold quartz piled high ready for roasting, like whoever was working there dropped their tools and ran for their lives mid-shift. Twenty paces inside, the whole tunnel was sealed off with masterful masonry no local tribe alive could have laid.
“Aye, that’s a queer thing,” Evans said, grinning into his beer. “But I’ve got a queerer yarn for you.”
He went on to tell me of a ruined stone city he’d found deep in the uncharted interior, which he swore was the Biblical Ophir King Solomon sent his ships to trade with. Learned men have backed up that claim in the years since Evans died, mind you. I was sitting there wide-eyed, 20 years old and hungry for every tale of lost treasure, when he dropped a name I’d never heard: the Suliman Mountains, north-west of Mushakulumbwe.
“That’s where Solomon’s real mines are,” he said. “Diamond mines. Suliman’s just the local twist on Solomon, for one thing. An old witch doctoress up in Manica told me all about it. The people who live across those peaks are a lost branch of the Zulu, speak a Zulu dialect, and are taller and fiercer than any warrior you’ve ever seen. Their wizards learned their craft from white men back when all the world was dark, and they hold the secret of a mine full of ‘bright stones’ that never run out.”
I laughed it off at the time, interested as I was. The diamond fields of Kimberley hadn’t been discovered yet, so the whole thing sounded like a fairy tale. For twenty years I didn’t think of it again. An elephant hunter’s lucky to survive twenty years in the trade, let alone go looking for ghosts.
Then two decades later, I was camped at Sitanda’s Kraal, that miserable patch of huts where there’s no game to hunt and nothing to eat but tough wild pumpkins, and I was half-dead from a raging fever. One day a Portuguese man showed up with a single half-breed companion. Now, the low-class Delagoa Bay Portuguese are usually the worst sort of slavers you’d ever meet, but this man was different: tall, gaunt, curling grey mustache, large dark eyes, dressed like a old nobleman straight out of the history books. He spoke broken English, I spoke a little rough Portuguese, and he told me his name was José Silvestre, he owned a small farm outside Delagoa. Next morning he packed up to head west straight into the empty desert, tipped his hat to me in that old courtly style, and said: “Goodbye, señor. If we meet again, I will be the richest man in the world, and I will remember your kindness.”
I was too weak to do more than huff a laugh. I watched him trudge out into the dunes, wondering if he’d lost his mind.
A week later my fever broke. I was sitting outside my tent chewing on the last scrap of a miserable fowl I’d traded a full yard of cloth for, watching the red sun sink over the desert, when I spotted a figure 300 yards off, crawling up the slope on hands and knees. He’d stagger to his feet, stumble a few steps, and collapse again. I sent one of my hunters to drag him in.
It was Silvestre. Nothing but yellow parchment skin stretched over bones, his face the color of bile, his eyes bulging out of his head.
“Water. For the love of Christ, water,” he moaned, his lips cracked raw, his tongue swollen black.
I gave him water laced with a little milk, and he gulped down two quarts without pausing. Then the fever raged through him again, and he raved nonstop about Suliman’s peaks, diamonds, his half-breed servant dying of thirst ten miles back. I dragged him into my tent, did what little I could for him, but I knew he was gone.
At dawn I woke up, and there he was, sitting up, gaunt as a skeleton, staring out at the desert. The first slant of sunlight shot all the way across the 100-mile stretch of dunes to hit the tallest crest of the Suliman range.
“There it is!” he screamed in Portuguese, pointing his thin bony arm. “But I will never reach it. No one will ever reach it!”
He fumbled in his tattered shirt till he pulled out a little tobacco pouch stitched from sable antelope hide, tied shut with a leather rimpi thong. He couldn’t get it undone, so he handed it to me. Inside was a scrap of yellow linen, stained with rusty brown blood, and a crumpled old paper.
“I am dying,” he whispered, his breath going thin. “My ancestor wrote that, 300 years ago, in 1590. His name was José da Silvestra. He was a political refugee from Lisbon, one of the first Portuguese to land on these shores. He wrote it with a sharpened bone, his own blood for ink, trapped in a cave on those mountains, starving. His slave found him dead on this side of the peaks, brought the papers back to Delagoa, and they’ve been in my family ever since. I spent every penny I owned trying to make that crossing. My servant is dead, and now I am too. But you are kind. Take it. Maybe you will live to cross the desert. You could be the richest man in the world.”
He rambled off for an hour after that, and then he was still. I buried him deep, heaped big boulders over his chest so the jackals couldn’t dig him up, and rode away.
Sir Henry leaned forward, his knuckles white around his knee. “The document? What did it say?”
I pulled a crumpled paper out of my pocketbook, the English translation I’d copied from the original, and the crude hand-drawn map Silvestre’s ancestor had scrawled in blood. I read it out slow:
> I, José da Silvestra, who am now dying of hunger in the little cave where no snow rests on the north side of the nipple of the southernmost of the two mountains I have named Sheba’s Breasts, write this in the year 1590 with a cleft bone upon a remnant of my raiment, my blood being the ink. If my slave finds it, and brings it to Delagoa, let my friend carry word to the King of Portugal, to send an army that can survive the desert, climb the peaks, defeat the fierce Kukuana warriors and their dark magic, and bring back the endless diamonds I saw locked in Solomon’s treasure chamber behind the wall of white Death. I was betrayed by the witch-finder Gagool, and barely escaped with my life. Let whoever comes follow the map, climb the snow of Sheba’s left breast till he reaches the nipple, on the north side of which runs the great road Solomon built, leading three days’ journey straight to the King’s Palace. Kill Gagool. Pray for my soul. Farewell.
Silence hung over us for a full minute when I finished. Good whistled loud. “I’ve sailed around the world twice, put in at every port you can name, and I swear on my naval commission I never heard a half so wild a tale, not even in a penny dreadful.”
“It’s a strange story, Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, half-smiling. “I hope you’re not hoaxing us, to take in two greenhorns.”
I got right to my feet, shoving the papers back in my pocket. I hate being taken for one of those lying hunters who spin tall tales for new arrivals. “If you think I’d waste my time making up nonsense, then we’re done here.”
He clapped his big hand on my shoulder, pushing me back down on the log. “Sit, I beg your pardon. The story was so wild, I could barely believe it for a second. I know you’re no liar.”
I sat back down, still a little huffy. “I’ll show you the original blood-stained rag when we hit Durban, you can see for yourself. But I haven’t told you the rest about your brother yet. I knew his man Jim, the Bechuana hunter, good with a spear, sharp as a tack for a native. The day Neville’s wagon was set to leave, I saw Jim standing by my wagon hacking tobacco on the disselboom yoke.
I asked him where he was headed, after elephants? He grinned and said no, they were chasing something worth far more than ivory. Worth more than gold, when I pressed him. Then he told me straight out: they were going after diamonds, in Suliman’s Berg. I warned him that if he tried that crossing, the vultures would pick his bones clean, and his master’s too. He just laughed, said a old woman from that country had washed up in Natal as a girl, told him the tales, and he was tired of hunting elephants where the game was all shot out. Half an hour later Neville’s wagon rumbled off. Jim came running back to wave goodbye, said he didn’t think they’d ever trek south again. Before he left, I slipped him a scrap of paper with the directions from the old Portuguese map, made him swear not to hand it to Neville till they were 100 miles in at Inyati, so he wouldn’t come pestering me for more details before they left. That’s all I know about him.”
Sir Henry’s jaw set hard. “Mr. Quatermain. I am going to find my brother. I will trace him to the foot of Suliman’s Mountains, cross the peaks if I have to, and I won’t stop till I find him, or know for certain he’s dead. Will you come with me?”
I’m a cautious man, a timid one by elephant hunter standards, and that offer made my blood run cold. That journey was a suicide run, plain and simple. I had a young son back in Durban dependent on my earnings, I couldn’t afford to throw my life away chasing a ghost. “No thank you, Sir Henry. I’m too old for a fool’s errand like that. We’d end up just like poor Silvestre out in the dunes.”
Both of their faces fell.
“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, firm as iron. “I am a wealthy man, and I will meet any price you name. I will pay your full fee before we even set foot on the trail. I will set aside a sum of money to provide for your son for the rest of his life, if anything should go wrong on this journey. Any diamonds or ivory we find, you and Good split them evenly. I want nothing for myself beyond finding my brother. I will cover every last expense of the trip. You set the terms, whatever they are.”
That was the most generous offer any hunter had ever gotten in the whole of southern Africa. “That’s a proposition worth thinking on,” I said. “Give me till we reach Durban, and I’ll give you my final answer.”
I said goodnight soon after, crawled into my tent, and fell asleep dreaming of Silvestre’s gaunt face, and the glint of a million diamonds buried deep under the snow of Sheba’s Breasts.

The run from the Cape to Durban takes four or five days, depending on how fast the vessel sails and how the weather holds. At East London, where they’ve yet to finish the grand harbour everyone nattered on about and poured a fortune into, rough surf often delays ships 24 hours while waiting for cargo boats to ferry goods out. But this trip we hit no such trouble: the bar was almost calm, tugs chugged out immediately towing long strings of ugly flat-bottomed barges, and crates went flying over the rail with a crash. No one cared if they held delicate china or thick woollen bales, everything got tossed slap-bang the same. I watched one case of four dozen champagne bottles smash to smithereens, the fizzing wine pooling in the dirty bottom of the barge. It was a wicked waste, and the Kafir crew clearly thought the same: they fished out two unbroken bottles, knocked the necks off, and chugged the contents. They didn’t count on the gas from the fermented wine making them swell up, though. Minutes later they were rolling around the barge’s floor screaming that the good drink was *tagati* — bewitched. I called down from the ship and told them it was the white man’s strongest poison, and they were as good as dead. They went ashore white-eyed with terror, and I’d wager none of them ever touch champagne again.

All the while steaming north to Natal, I turned Sir Henry Curtis’s wild offer over in my head. We didn’t hash it out for a day or two after that first talk, though I spun them a string of true hunting yarns — no need to lie about the veld, there are more odd adventures there than any man could make up. Then one perfect January evening, the hottest month of the year, we rounded the Natal coast, expecting to make Durban Point by sunset. The whole stretch from East London is lovely: red sandhills rolling into vivid green grass, dotted with Kafir kraals, a ribbon of white surf spouting foam pillars where it smacks the rocks. But just before Durban, the landscape softens into something rich and lush: centuries of rain have carved sheer kloofs through the hills, rivers glinting down their lengths, deep virgin bush tangled beside mealie fields and sugar plantations, and here and there a white house peeking out at the warm, placid sea. I’ve spent most my life in the wilderness, so I know civilisation’s worth, even if it does chase all the big game away. Eden might have been fair before man came, but I’d bet it was far fairer once Eve was strolling through it.

We miscalculated our speed a little, though: the sun was fully set before we dropped anchor off the Point, right as the signal gun boomed to tell Durban the English mail steamer had arrived. It was far too late to cross the bar that night, so we settled in for a leisurely dinner after watching the mail bags ferried off in the lifeboat. When we came back on deck, the moon was hanging low, flooding sea and shore with silver so bright it almost dimmed the quick, strobing flashes from the lighthouse. Sweet, spicy coastal scents drifted over the water — the kind that always make me think of missionaries singing hymns — and a hundred tiny lights sparkled in the windows of the houses strung along the Berea. Nearby, a brig’s crew sang shanties as they hauled up anchor to ride the incoming wind. The whole night was soft, warm, and peaceful enough to wrap around a man like a blanket: even the big bulldog belonging to a sporting passenger forgot he’d been dying to take a chunk out of the baboon caged on the forecastle, and snored happily at the cabin door, no doubt dreaming he’d finished the fight.

Sir Henry, Captain Good, and I drifted over to sit by the ship’s wheel, quiet for a few long minutes.
“Well, Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said eventually. “Have you thought over my proposal?”
“Aye, what do you make of it?” Good echoed. “I do hope you’ll come with us, at least as far as Solomon’s Mines, or wherever that Neville fellow you knew ended up.”
I stood up and knocked the ashes out of my pipe, dragging out the second I needed to make up my mind. By the time the last ember fell fizzing into the sea, I’d decided.
“Gentlemen,” I said, sitting back down. “I’ll come. But before we shake on it, I’ve got three terms, and a reason I’m agreeing to this.”
I laid them out plain:
1.  They cover all trip expenses, and any ivory, gold, or other valuables we find get split evenly between Good and me.
2.  They pay me £500 up front for my services before we leave, and I’ll serve them faithfully until they call the expedition off, we find what we’re looking for, or disaster catches up with us.
3.  Before we trek inland, they’ll sign a legal deed that if I die or get crippled on the trip, they’ll pay my son Harry — who’s studying medicine at Guy’s Hospital in London — £200 a year for five years, enough to keep him going until he can earn his own living.
Sir Henry didn’t hesitate. “I accept all terms gladly. I’m dead set on this journey, and I’d pay far more to have your help, given your unmatched knowledge of the veld north of here.”
“Pity I didn’t ask for more then,” I laughed, “but I won’t go back on my word. Now for why I’m coming. First off, I’ve watched you both these past few days, and I like you. I know we’ll pull together through the hard parts, and that’s worth its weight in ivory for a long, dangerous trip. Now let me be frank: I don’t think any of us are coming back alive if we try to cross the Suliman Mountains. Three hundred years ago, old Dom da Silvestra vanished there. Twenty years back, his descendant met the same fate. And every trace of your brother Neville went cold there too. Our odds look exactly the same.”
I paused to watch their faces. Good looked a little uneasy, but Sir Henry’s expression didn’t shift an inch.
“We take our chances,” he said.
“You’re probably wondering,” I went on, “why a man like me — who’s told you he’s by nature a careful, even timid old hunter — would sign on for a trip I think will get us all killed. It’s two things. First, I’m a fatalist. My number’s up when it’s up, no matter where I am or what I do. If I’m meant to die in the Sulimans, I’ll die there, no avoiding it. Second, I’m a poor man. Forty years of hunting and trading, and I’ve never made more than a bare living. The average lifespan of an elephant hunter is four to five years from the day he picks up his first big gun. I’ve outlived seven full generations of my peers. My time can’t be far off anyway. If I die on a normal routine hunt, there’ll be nothing left after my debts are paid to keep Harry going through his training. This deal? It secures his future. That’s the whole of it.”
“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, leaning forward, “your motives for a journey you believe will end in disaster do you immense credit. Only time will prove if you’re right about our chances, but I’m going through with this, no matter what. If we’re all going to get knocked on the head, at least I hope we get a little good shooting in first, eh Good?”
“Absolutely!” Good barked a laugh. “We’re all three men who’ve stared danger in the face before, holding our lives in our hands. No point turning back now. I vote we head down to the saloon and take a toast to luck, eh?”
And we did, right to the bottom of our tumblers.

Next day we went ashore, and I put Sir Henry and Good up at my little three-room green brick cottage on the Berea, with its dented galvanised iron roof, and a garden full of loquat trees, young mango saplings the botanical gardens curator gave me, and a little orange grove out the back. An old Griqua hunter named Jack, whose thigh was shattered by a buffalo cow back in Sikukunis country so he can never hunt again, tends the place — you’ll never get a Zulu to care much about gardening, it’s too quiet a pastime for them. The two men slept in a tent pitched in the orange grove; the air was thick with the scent of orange blossom, and the trees hung heavy with unripe green and ripe golden fruit all at once, as they do in Durban. Hardly any mosquitoes on the Berea, either, so they slept well.

Once my mind was made up to leave, I rushed through preparations. First I sorted out that legal deed for Harry — it was a headache to arrange, since Sir Henry’s property was back in England, but we got a lawyer to sort it for the outrageous sum of £20, and I pocketed my £500 cheque after. Next I bought a 22-foot stinkwood wagon, iron-axled, light and tough, that had already done one run to the Diamond Fields and back — well-seasoned wood, no green planks that would split the first time we hit a rut. It was half-tented, meaning only the last 12 feet were covered with canvas, leaving the front open to stow supplies. The back had a hide bed big enough for two people to sleep on, rifle racks, and all sorts of little handy pockets for gear. I paid £125 for it, a steal. Then I bought a team of 20 Zulu oxen — four extra head to make up for losses. These small, tough beasts were half the size of ordinary transport Africander oxen, but they could survive grazing that would starve the bigger cattle, and could travel five miles further a day with a heavy load without getting footsore. Best of all, they were fully salted: immune to red water fever that wipes out whole herds on strange veld, and all had been inoculated against the deadly lung sickness. The inoculation process cuts a slit in the ox’s tail and tucks a scrap of diseased lung from a dead animal inside. The ox gets a mild case of the sickness, its tail drops off a foot from the root, and it’s immune for life. It looks odd, trotting behind 20 stubby little tails where full ones should be, like someone glued bulldog rear ornaments to the oxen’s rumps — but it’s better than losing the whole team.

Good turned out to have dabbled in medical training years before, so he handled all our medical supplies. He was no qualified doctor, but he knew more than plenty of men who hung an M.D. on their wall, as we’d find out later: while we were in Durban he lopped off a Kafir’s badly infected big toe with such neat precision it was a pleasure to watch. The only time he was stumped was when the patient, who’d sat perfectly stoic through the whole operation, asked him to sew a new white toe on to replace it.

Next came our weapons, all matched in make and calibre so ammo was fully interchangeable — a non-negotiable for a long expedition. I copied the list straight from my old pocket notebook:
*   Three heavy double-barrelled 8-bore elephant guns, 15 pounds each, loaded with 11 drachms of black powder, for stopping the biggest bull elephants in their tracks.
*   Three double .500 Express rifles, perfect for medium game like eland or sable antelope — or for fending off attackers at long range.
*   One double-barrelled 12-bore shotgun, for shooting small game to feed the camp.
*   Three Winchester repeating rifles, spares for backup.
*   Three heavy single-action Colt revolvers, for close-quarters trouble.

Then we hired our crew: a Zulu driver named Goza, a Zulu ox leader named Tom, a tough Hottentot tracker named Ventvogel who could follow a spoor across bare rock, and a sharp little Zulu named Khiva who spoke perfect English. Ventvogel’s only flaw was a weakness for gin, but since we were heading far past every last roadside grog shop, that fault wouldn’t be a problem. We were a man short, though, and were ready to leave without a third servant — until the night before our departure, when Khiva came to tell me a stranger was waiting outside to see me.

We were halfway through dinner, so I told him to bring the man in. In walked a tall, broad, handsome fellow, about 30 years old, far lighter skinned than most Zulus. He lifted his knobkerrie in a quiet salute, squatted on his haunches in the corner, and said nothing. I ignored him for a few minutes, as you always do to show dignity to visiting Zulus. I noticed he was a ringed man, the *keshla* gum-and-fat ring in his hair that marked men of status, and his face was familiar.
“Well,” I said at last. “What is your name?”
“Umbopa,” he answered, in a slow, deep voice.
“I have seen your face before.”
“Yes, Macumazahn — the man who never sleeps with his eyes shut. You saw me at the place of the Little Hand, Isandhlwana, the day before the battle.”
I remembered then. I’d been one of Lord Chelmsford’s guides during the Zulu war, and had the luck to leave the camp the day before it was wiped out, in charge of a string of supply wagons. I’d spoken to Umbopa then — he’d been a junior officer in the native auxiliaries, and warned me the camp was dangerously exposed.
“I remember,” I said. “What do you want of me?”
“I hear you travel far north, with the white chiefs from over the sea, all the way past Manica to the Lukanga River,” he said, his voice steady. “I want to come with you. I ask for no pay. I am a brave man, strong enough to earn his keep and his food.”
There was no cringing servility to him, the way most job-seeking Zulus spoke to white men. He spoke to us like equals. I translated his words to Sir Henry and Good, and Sir Henry told me to ask him to stand. He stood up, slipping off his heavy old military greatcoat, wearing nothing but a leather loincloth and a necklace strung with lion’s claws. He was a magnificent specimen, six foot three, broad-shouldered, every muscle hard and defined, dark old assegai scars crisscrossing his skin.
“Good Lord, they’re the exact same size,” Good muttered.
Sir Henry walked right up to him, looked straight into his proud face. “I like your looks, Umbopa,” he said. “I’ll take you on as my man.”
Umbopa understood every word. He gave a small, respectful nod. “It is well,” he said, glancing from Sir Henry’s six foot frame back to his own. “We are men, thou and I.”

I’m not going to drag you through every tedious mishap of our thousand-mile trek from Durban up to Sitanda’s Kraal, near the Lukanga and Kalukwe river junction. The final 300 miles of the journey had to be done on foot, thanks to the tsetse fly—its bite is fatal to every beast that isn’t a man or a donkey. We left Durban at the end of January, and trundled into our campsite near the kraal in the second week of May. Most of the scrapes we had along the way are the standard fare for any African hunter, so I’ll skip them to spare you boredom—bar one, the wild elephant hunt I’m dying to tell.

At Inyati, the remote trading post in cruel King Lobengula’s Matabele territory, we said a sad goodbye to our comfortable wagon. Of the 20 prime oxen I’d bought back in Durban, only 12 were left: one bitten dead by a cobra, three wasted away from drought and lack of water, one strayed, and three ate the poisonous tulip herb and perished. The five remaining sick ones we saved with a strong brew of boiled tulip leaves, an effective antidote if you dose them in time. We left the wagon and surviving oxen in the care of our two trusted drivers Goza and Tom, with a local Scottish missionary keeping a watchful eye, then set out on foot with Umbopa, Khiva, Ventvögel, and six new hired porters. None of us said much at first; I think every single one of us secretly wondered if we’d ever lay eyes on that wagon again. I certainly didn’t expect to.

The silence broke when Umbopa, marching at the head of the line, belted out a rolling Zulu chant: it told of brave men sick of tame, ordinary life who struck out into the wilderness to find new wonders or die, only to discover the “wild” land was a paradise full of fat cattle, endless game to hunt, and glory worth fighting for. We all laughed, took it as a fine omen. Umbopa was a cheerful, dignified sort, save for his occasional quiet brooding spells, and we all grew very fond of him for how he kept our spirits high.

Two weeks out from Inyati, we stumbled into a lush, well-watered woodland: the kloofs in the hills were thick with idoro bush and tangled wait-a-little thorns, and heavy with machabell trees, whose sweet yellow fruit is elephants’ absolute favourite food. Spoor was everywhere, and dozens of the trees had been snapped clean in half or uprooted by the great brutes. We made camp beside a dry riverbed dotted with crystal-clear game pools, facing a park-like plain of flat-topped mimosas, walled in on every side by miles of silent, pathless bush.

We flushed a herd of giraffes out of the riverbed, who loped off 300 yards away, their hooves clicking like castanets. But Good—our ex-naval companion, whose gun was loaded with solid ball—couldn’t resist taking a shot. He hit the last young cow dead on the neck, shattering its spine, and the animal rolled head over heels like a startled rabbit. Good swore (a habit he picked up at sea, poor chap), and the Kafirs yelled “Ou, Bougwan!” — the nickname they’d given him for his ever-present monocle, “Glass Eye”. From that day on, Good’s reputation as a legendary marksman was sealed, even though he missed far more shots than he hit. We never let on, not after that giraffe miracle.

We built a circular thorn hedge scherm near the pools, cooked up giraffe steaks and roasted marrow bones, and ate by moonlight. There is no finer luxury on Earth than giraffe marrow, except for elephant heart—something we would taste the very next day. We made a strange group that night: me, scrawny, short, grizzled, all nine and a half stone, and Sir Henry, six feet broad-shouldered, blond, fifteen stone, and Good the oddest of all, immaculate in his neat brown tweed suit, gaiters, perfectly shaved, monocle and false teeth spotless, even wearing a light gutta-percha collar he swore weighed nothing. “I like to turn out like a gentleman, no matter where I am,” he’d told me, innocent as anything.

A lion woofed from the bushes behind us, then a loud trumpeting echoed from the pool 100 yards off: a whole herd of elephants was coming down to drink. Good jumped up to fire on the spot, but I hauled him back down. Sir Henry, who had been pushing us to race inland as fast as possible ever since we heard a white man named Neville had vanished this way two years prior, grinned and suggested we lay over to hunt the herd. None of us argued. We all wanted a crack at those great tuskers.

At dawn, we grabbed our three heavy eight-bore hunting rifles, loaded our water bottles with weak cold tea (the best hunting drink ever made, trust me) and set off tracking, leaving the rest of the porters to skin a dead lion and sable antelope we’d found locked together at the pool: the lion had ambushed the antelope mid-drink, and the sable had gored it clean through the chest on its curved horns, killing both beasts instantly.

We tracked the herd for three hours under broiling sun, and found them 200 yards off in a hollow, 25 full-grown bulls flapping their ears lazily after their morning feed. I tossed a handful of dry grass in the air: the wind was blowing from the elephants to us, no way they could scent us. We crept to 40 yards out, and lined up on three broadside bulls, one of them with tusks as thick as saplings. I took the middle, Sir Henry the left, Good the giant tusker.

Three booms rang out at once. Sir Henry’s elephant dropped dead instantly, shot straight through the heart. Mine staggered to its knees, then lurched back to its feet and bolted past me. I put a second barrel in its ribs, ran up, and finished it with a clean brain shot. When I turned around, Good was white as a sheet: the tusker he’d hit had charged straight for him, he’d barely dodged out of the way, and the wounded beast had bolted full tilt in the direction of our camp. The rest of the herd had crashed off in the opposite direction.

We decided to chase the unhurt herd instead of the wounded bull, a mistake I’ll regret for the rest of my life. They left a trail of crushed bush as wide as a carriage road, and two hours later we found them huddled tensely, a lone sentry bull posted 60 yards off keeping watch. We all fired at once, and he dropped dead. The panicking herd stampeded straight into a steep-sided dry nullah, and couldn’t claw their way up the far bank. We fired as fast as we could reload, killing five more elephants before they tore off down the gully. Eight great tuskers in a single day was a hell of a bag, and we set off back to camp tired, but elated.

On the way back we spotted a herd of eland, and didn’t bother shooting—we had more meat than we could carry. Good, who’d never seen an eland close up, handed his rifle to Umbopa and wandered over to a small bush patch a hundred yards off with Khiva to get a better look. Me and Sir Henry sat watching the sun bleed red over the horizon, when we heard a screaming trumpet. The wounded tusker we’d left behind was silhouetted against the fiery sun, charging straight for Good and Khiva.

They ran for us, but Good’s tight tailored trousers and polished boots dragged him down, and he slipped on the dry grass face first sixty yards from our position. We couldn’t fire for fear of hitting him. That’s when brave Khiva turned, hurled his assegai straight into the elephant’s trunk. The beast roared in pain, seized the boy, hurled him to the ground, planted one giant foot on his midsection, and tore him clean in two. We unloaded every single bullet we had into the great bull, and it dropped dead across Khiva’s remains. Good fell to his knees sobbing, wringing his hands at the feet of the man who’d died to save him. Even me, old hardened hunter that I am, felt a lump rise in my throat. Umbopa stepped forward, stared down at Khiva’s still form, then the dead tusker, and said quietly:
“Ah. He is dead, but he died like a man.”

We’d bagged nine bull elephants, and it took two full days to hack out every tusk, haul the whole haul back to camp, and bury the lot deep in the sand beneath a massive gnarled tree whose crown jutted so high it was visible for 20 miles in every direction. It was the finest haul of ivory I’d ever laid eyes on, every tusk tipping the scales between 40 and 50 pounds apiece. The pair pulled from the giant bull that gored poor Khiva to death topped 170 pounds, as close as we could measure on rough makeshift scales.

As for Khiva, we laid what remained of him to rest in a hollow aardvark burrow, tucking his sharpest assegai in beside him to defend himself on his journey to the afterlife. On the third day we set out again, clinging to the quiet hope we’d live long enough to circle back and dig up our buried ivory. After a long, grueling tramp strewn with small misadventures I’ve no space to detail here, we pulled into Sitanda’s Kraal, on the banks of the Lukanga River—the real jumping off point for our quest.
I remember that arrival like it was yesterday. To our right sat a scattered native settlement, ringed with rough stone cattle kraals and small patches of tilled grain tucked down by the river, where the locals eked out their meager crop. Beyond it stretched miles of rolling grassveld, thick with tall waving grass, where herds of springbok and wildebeest drifted across the plains. To our left, nothing but the endless, waterless desert. This spot is the very last outpost of fertile ground before the world turns to sand, and no naturalist I’ve ever met can explain why the soil shifts so abruptly here. But there it is, plain as day.

A narrow trickle of a stream curled right below our campsite, and on its far bank rose a stony, scrub-choked slope. Twenty years prior, I’d watched the dying, half-starved Silvestre drag himself down that very same slope, crawling back empty-handed after his doomed attempt to reach King Solomon’s Mines. Past that rise, the desert begins, carpeted in scratchy gray karoo shrub and nothing else.

We pitched camp as the sun sank low, a great glowing red ball spilling over the desert horizon and painting the whole vast expanse streaks of amber, crimson, and violet. I left Good in charge of setting up our tents and sorting the supplies, then hauled Sir Henry up the slope opposite to stare out at the waste. The air was sharp and clear, and far, far off on the edge of sight I could pick out the faint, hazy blue outline of the Suliman Berg, their peaks dusted bright white with permanent snow.

“There,” I said, pointing. “That’s the wall that wraps around Solomon’s Mines. But God alone knows if we’ll ever climb it.”
“My brother is somewhere on the other side of those mountains,” Sir Henry replied, in that quiet, unshakable tone that marked the man. “And if he’s there, I’ll reach him somehow.”
“I hope to God you do,” I answered, turning to head back down to camp—only to spot we weren’t alone. A few paces behind us, the massive Zulu Umbopa stood, his gaze fixed just as intently on those far-off peaks.

He spoke the second he saw I’d noticed him, directing his words straight to Sir Henry, the man he’d attached himself to since we hired him. “It is to that land you mean to travel, Incubu?” he said, the native word for elephant the locals had dubbed Sir Henry for his size and quiet strength, gesturing with the broad tip of his assegai toward the peaks.

I snapped at him immediately, asking what he thought he was doing addressing a white man by a heathen native name to his face, as if he was an equal. The Zulu let out a soft, quiet laugh that made my temper spike.
“How do you know I am not the equal of the Inkosi you serve?” he asked. “He is of royal blood, anyone can see that in his bearing and his height. So, perhaps, am I. At the very least, I am no lesser man. Speak for me, O Macumazahn, and pass my words along to Incubu, for I would speak to both of you.”

I was furious—no Kafir usually dared speak to me that way—but there was something about him that made me hold my tongue, and I was curious what he had to say. I translated, all the while complaining that the man was outrageously impudent, full of empty swagger.
“Yes, Umbopa,” Sir Henry said. “I mean to travel there.”
“The desert is wide, and there is no water in it,” Umbopa went on. “The mountains are high, capped with snow, and no man knows what waits beyond them, past the place where the sun sets. How will you reach it, Incubu? And why do you go?”

I translated again, and Sir Henry’s answer was simple. “Tell him I go because a man of my own blood—my brother—traveled there before me, two years back. I am journeying to find him.”
“That is so,” Umbopa nodded. “A Hottentot I met on the road told me a white man struck out for those mountains two years ago, with one Bechuana hunter named Jim as his only companion. They never came back.”
“How do you know it was my brother?” Sir Henry asked, leaning forward.
“I do not know for certain. But the Hottentot said the white man had your eyes, and a thick black beard. And the hunter with him wore tattered European clothes, and his name was Jim.”

“That’s no mistake,” I cut in. “I knew Jim for years. That’s him.”
Sir Henry nodded, his face hardening. “I knew it. My brother was always stubborn that way, ever since he was a boy. If he set his mind to crossing the Suliman Berg, he crossed it. Unless some accident took him, he’s on the other side right now. And we’re going to find him.”

Umbopa understood perfect English, even if he rarely spoke it out loud. “It is a very long journey,” he put in.
“Aye, it’s long,” Sir Henry agreed. “But there is no journey on this earth a man cannot make if he sets his whole heart to it. No mountain he cannot climb, no desert he cannot cross, so long as what drives him is love, and he holds his life loose in his hands, ready to keep it or lose it as Heaven sees fit.”

Umbopa fell quiet for a minute, then spoke in that rolling, poetic rhetoric the Zulu people are famous for, full of strange, vivid metaphors that prove they are far from unthinking savages.
“Strong words, my father,” he said. “You speak true. What is life, after all? It is a feather blown on the wind. It is a grass seed, sometimes taking root and growing, sometimes caught and carried off into the sky. If the seed is heavy and good, it may travel a little way down the road it wants to go. It is good to try, to fight the wind. A man must die, sooner or later. The worst that can happen is he dies a little earlier. I will come with you, across the desert and over the mountains, even if I fall dead at your feet on the road.
“What is life, you wise white men who know the secrets of the stars, who send words flying across hundreds of miles without a sound? You cannot tell me where it comes from, or where it goes when it leaves the body. I will tell you. We walk out of the dark, and we walk back into the dark. We are a bird blown by a storm at midnight, flying out of nowhere. For one second our wings catch the glow of the campfire, and then we are gone again, back into the nowhere. Life is nothing, and it is everything. It is the hand that holds death back. It is the glow-worm that shines bright at midnight, then vanishes come dawn. It is the white breath an ox blows out on a winter morning. It is the little shadow that runs across the grass, and vanishes entirely when the sun sets.”

“You are a very strange man,” Sir Henry said, when he fell silent.
Umbopa laughed. “It seems to me we are much alike, Incubu. Perhaps I too am chasing a brother over those mountains.”
I stared at him, suspicious. “What do you mean? What do you know of those peaks?”
“A little. Very little. There is a strange land beyond them, they say. A land of magic and beauty, full of brave people, trees, streams, snow peaks, and a great white paved road. I have heard stories. But what is the point of talking? Who lives to see, will see.
“You do not have to fear me, Macumazahn,” he added, reading my doubt clear on my face. “I dig no hidden pits for you to fall into. I plot no traps. If we ever cross those mountains, I will tell you all I know. But Death sits on their slopes. Be wise. Turn back. Go hunt elephants, my masters. I have spoken.”
He saluted us with his assegai, turned, and walked back to camp, where we found him a few minutes later scrubbing the barrel of his rifle like any ordinary Kafir servant.
“That’s an odd man,” Sir Henry said.
“Too odd by half,” I replied. “He’s hiding something, he knows far more than he’s letting on. But there’s no point quarreling with him now. We’re already in for a strange trip, and a mysterious Zulu isn’t going to make much difference one way or the other.”

The next morning we got our supplies sorted. There was no way to drag our heavy 8-bore elephant rifles and all our excess gear across 60 miles of waterless desert, so we sent all our hired porters home, and struck a deal with the greedy old kraal chief who lived nearby to guard our things until we returned. It made my chest ache to leave my prized hunting rifles in the clutches of that old thief, whose eyes were practically glowing with greed when he laid eyes on the ivory inlaid stocks. But I took precautions.
I cocked every single rifle, loaded them full, and told him if he touched one, it would kill him instantly. He didn’t believe me, and grabbed my biggest 8-bore to test it. The second he put his finger on the trigger, it went off, blew a clean hole right through one of his prize oxen that was being driven past the kraal, and knocked him flat on his back from the recoil. He stumbled up, white-eyed with terror, furious that he’d lost his ox, and even more furious that I refused to pay him for it. After that, nothing on earth would make him touch the guns again.
“Put these live devils up in the thatch rafters,” he yelped. “They will murder us all!”
I followed that up by telling him that if a single thing was missing when we came back, I would curse him and his entire village with black magic, kill all his cattle, turn all his milk sour, and haunt him for the rest of his days. If we died in the desert and he tried to steal our things, I told him the ghosts would come back to plague him, and the devils trapped inside the guns would talk to him in voices that would curdle his blood. After that, he swore he would guard our gear like it was the spirit of his own dead father. He was a superstitious old villain, and that was the only language he understood.

Once we’d sorted out our excess supplies, we packed what the five of us—Sir Henry, Good, myself, Umbopa, and the Hottentot Ventvögel—would carry across the desert. We cut every possible ounce of weight, but even at that, we couldn’t get the total load under 40 pounds per man. It was all absolute necessities:
Three express rifles and 200 rounds of ammunition.
Two Winchester repeating rifles for Umbopa and Ventvögel, plus another 200 rounds.
Five four-pint Cochrane water bottles.
Five wool blankets.
Twenty-five pounds of sun-dried biltong, dried game meat that would not spoil for months.
Ten pounds of bright trade beads, for gifts if we met people on the other side of the mountains.
A small medical kit, with an ounce of quinine, and a few small surgical blades.
Our hunting knives, a compass, waterproof matches, a pocket water filter, tobacco, a small trowel, a single bottle of brandy, and the clothes on our backs.

That was all. No luxuries, no extra comforts. Even 40 pounds per man felt like a death sentence to haul across burning sand, where every extra ounce wears you down to the bone.
I managed to talk three miserable, skinny local villagers into coming 20 miles with us, each carrying a gallon gourd of extra water, lured by the promise of a brand new steel hunting knife each. I told them we were going out to hunt the wild ostriches that were known to roam the desert. They chattered amongst themselves, shrugged their shoulders, and told us we were all mad, that we’d die of thirst out there. But the knives were such rare, precious treasures that they agreed to come, figuring if we died later, it wouldn’t be their problem.

We spent the whole next day resting and sleeping, and at sunset we ate a huge feast of fresh roast beef and hot tea, which Good mourned out loud would be the last hot tea we saw for weeks. We made our final preparations, then sat and waited for the moon to rise. At nine o’clock it crested the horizon, full and brilliant, flooding the desert with silver light that made the whole empty sand plain look as vast and quiet as the starry sky above us. We stood up, ready to go, but for a second none of us moved. It’s human nature to hesitate right before you step over the threshold of a journey you can never turn back from.
Us three white men huddled together. Umbopa stood a few paces ahead, assegai in one hand, rifle slung over his shoulder, staring hard out across the desert. The hired porters and Ventvögel hung back in a small cluster.
“Gentlemen,” Sir Henry said, his deep voice cutting through the silence. “We are about to set out on one of the strangest journeys a group of men could ever make. The odds of us succeeding are stacked against us. But the three of us have stood together, for better or worse, since the day we met. Before we take a single step, let us pause for one moment, and pray to the Power that guides every man’s fate, to lead our steps where they should go.”
He took off his hat, covered his face with his hands, and Good and I followed suit. I’m no great churchgoing man, few hunters are, and I’d never heard Sir Henry speak like that before, but for that one minute I prayed harder than I ever had in my life. The future was so completely unknown, and that vast, empty, awe-inspiring desert made you feel very small, and very close to God.
“Now,” Sir Henry said, dropping his hands. “Trek!”

We stepped out.
The only thing we had to guide us was the distant outline of the Suliman Berg, and the crudely drawn map old José da Silvestra had scribbled on a scrap of linen 300 years prior, when he was half starved and dying. It was hardly a reliable guide. Our only hope of surviving the desert was to find the small, muddy pool of bad water da Silvestra had marked halfway across the waste, 60 miles from our starting point, 60 miles from the mountains. If we missed that pool, or if it had dried up, we would die slow, miserable deaths of thirst. That vast sea of sand and scrub felt so endless, the odds of finding that one tiny patch of water felt next to zero.

We tramped through the night, silent as ghosts, our boots sinking deep in the sand. The scratchy karoo shrubs caught at our ankles and slowed us down, and sand worked its way into our shoes, so every few miles we had to stop to empty them. The night stayed cool, and we made good progress. The desert was so quiet and empty it felt oppressive. Good tried to whistle “The Girl I Left Behind Me” once, but the thin, reedy sound echoed across the empty sand and sounded so lonely he stopped immediately.
A few hours later, we stumbled right into a herd of sleeping quaggas. Good was leading, holding the compass, as the only sailor among us who knew how to use it. All of a sudden he vanished with a squawk, and the whole herd exploded around us, snorting, galloping, kicking up clouds of sand. The porters dropped their loads and tried to run, but there was nowhere to run to, so they threw themselves flat on the ground and wailed that the desert ghosts had got us. Then we saw Good, hauled halfway up the back of a spooked quagga, clinging on for dear life and yelling at the top of his lungs as the beast galloped straight toward the mountains. Two seconds later, the quagga bucked, and Good hit the sand with a heavy thud.
I ran over to him, terrified he’d broken his neck, but he was sitting in the sand, his monocle still perfectly fixed in his eye, a little shaken, very spooked, and completely unhurt. We laughed for ten minutes straight.

We kept going after that, no more mishaps until 1 a.m., when we called a short halt, drank a tiny sip of water (we couldn’t afford more), rested half an hour, then started walking again. By dawn, the eastern sky blushed pink as a girl’s cheek. The stars paled and vanished, the golden moon faded to a pale sickly silver, and then the sun crested the horizon, spilling light across the whole desert in golden spears.
We didn’t stop, even though our feet ached. We knew the second the sun climbed high in the sky, travel would become impossible. An hour later, we spotted a small pile of boulders sticking out of the plain, and dragged ourselves toward it. By some miracle, there was a big overhanging slab of rock, the sand underneath it smooth and cool, giving us perfect shade from the coming heat. We crawled under it, sipped a little water, nibbled a bit of biltong, and dropped straight asleep.

Two hours after we collapsed into our exhausted slumber, at roughly four in the morning, I jolted awake. The first brutal edge of bone-deep fatigue had worn off, and the torturous, crawling thirst we’d all ignored in our collapse crashed over me full force. I couldn’t sleep another minute. I’d been dreaming I was waist-deep in a rushing stream, green willows arching over the banks, the water so clear I could see pebbles glinting on the bed—and I woke to the scorch of arid desert air searing my throat, and the sharp, unshakable memory of Umbopa’s warning: if we didn’t find water that day, we’d die slow, ugly deaths. No human could survive long in that furnace heat without a drop to drink.

I sat up, rubbing my caked, gritty face with hands so dry the skin felt like cracked leather. My lips were stuck fast to my teeth, my eyelids glued shut, and it took a good minute of hard rubbing before I could pry them open. Dawn was close, but there was no soft, fresh pre-light in the air—just thick, hot, stagnant murk I can’t even find words to describe. The rest of the crew was still out cold. When it grew light enough to make out print, I fished the tattered little pocket copy of the *Ingoldsby Legends* I’d stuffed in my pack out, and read *The Jackdaw of Rheims*. When I hit the line about the nice little boy holding a golden ewer full of pure water, “as any that flows between Rheims and Namur,” I actually tried to smack my cracking, split lips. Just the thought of that clean, cool water drove me half mad. If the Cardinal had been standing there with his bell, book, and candle, I would have dived straight for that ewer and chugged every last drop—even if it was full of the soap he’d claimed was “worthy of washing the hands of the Pope,” and even if the full weight of the Catholic Church’s curse was waiting to land on my head for it. Thirst, weariness, and almost 24 hours without food must have addled my wits a little, because I started cackling at the mental image: a grizzled, sunburnt little elephant hunter bursting between the cardinal, the page boy, and the thieving jackdaw, shoving his dirty face straight into that basin and draining it dry. My harsh, croaking laugh woke the rest of the crew, who stumbled awake, rubbing their own caked faces and prying their own glued-shut lips open.

The second we were all fully lucid, we sized up the situation, and it was as bad as it could get. Not a single drop of water was left in any of our bottles. We turned every last one upside down, licking their necks and their caps, but they were bone dry. Good, who’d been carrying the flask of brandy, pulled it out and stared at it like it was a miracle, but Sir Henry snatched it right out of his hand—drinking raw spirits in that state would have only dehydrated us faster, and killed us far quicker.
“If we don’t find water, we’re dead,” Sir Henry said, blunt as always.
“If old da Silvestra’s map is worth anything, there should be some nearby,” I offered, but no one looked the least bit reassured. None of us had much faith left in that scribbled old map drawn on a tattered shirt tail.

As we sat staring blankly at each other, I noticed the Hottentot Ventvögel stand up, head down, eyes fixed on the sand beneath his feet. He stopped short a few paces away, grunted a guttural exclamation, and pointed at the ground. We all scrambled up to crowd around him.
“Looks like fresh springbok spoor,” I said. “What of it?”
“Springbok never wander far from water,” he answered in rough Dutch.
I smacked my forehead. I’d forgotten that. “You’re right. Thank God.”

That tiny scrap of hope breathed new life into all of us. When you’re staring death right in the face, even the flimsiest glimmer of hope feels like a lifeline—on a pitch black night, even a single pinprick star is better than total dark. Ventvögel lifted his snub nose, sniffing the hot air hard like an old impala ram catching the scent of a lion.
“I smell water,” he said.

We all whooped. We knew wild-born men like him had senses no European could match. Just then the sun crested the horizon, flooding the desert with golden light, and the sight that hit us made us forget our burning thirst entirely for whole minutes. Not 50 miles away, glinting like polished silver in the dawn, rose Sheba’s Breasts. Stretching hundreds of miles to either side of the twin peaks ran the vast, jagged Suliman Berg range. Two massive mountains, each at least 15 thousand feet tall, stood no more than a dozen miles apart, linked by a sheer, precipitous cliff, their peaks swathed in permanent snow, perfectly shaped like the breasts of a slumbering woman. At their bases, the slopes curved soft and smooth out of the plain, and the snow-capped “nipple” on top of each peak glowed white and solemn against the pale blue sky. The cliffs flanking them stretched as far as the eye could see, broken only by flat, table-top peaks identical to Table Mountain back at Cape Town. For a minute we just stood there, awestruck—these were clearly extinct volcanoes, so huge they felt almost sacred. Then thin, gauzy mist curled up around their bases, wrapping them in a soft veil till only their giant, ghostly outlines were visible. No wonder we hadn’t seen them clearly earlier.

The second the peaks vanished behind cloud, our thirst roared back twice as bad. Ventvögel could say he smelled water all he wanted, but there was no sign of it anywhere—nothing for miles but scorching sand and scrawny karoo scrub. We circled the little sand kopje we’d camped on, scanning the horizon, but there was no pan, no spring, no pool anywhere.
“You’re a fool,” I snapped at Ventvögel. “There’s no water for miles.”
He just kept sniffing the air. “I smell it, Baas. It’s close.”
“Sure,” I retorted. “It’s in the clouds, right? It’ll rain in two months, and wash our bones clean when we’re dead.”
Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard thoughtfully. “Maybe it’s on top of this kopje?”
“Rot,” Good scoffed. “No one finds water on the top of a hill.”
“Let’s check anyway,” I said, and we all stumbled up the sandy slope, Umbopa leading the way. He froze mid-step a few feet from the summit.
*“Nanzia manzie!”* he yelled at the top of his voice. “Here is water!”

We all bolted up to him, and sure enough, tucked into a deep indent right at the top of the kopje, was a murky, dark pool of water. We didn’t stop to wonder how on earth it had gotten there, didn’t care that it looked black and muddy. It was water. We hurled ourselves face down on the ground, sucking up that tepid, brackish muck like it was nectar fit for the gods. Heavens, how we drank! When we couldn’t sip another drop, we tore off our clothes and wallowed in the pool, letting the moisture sink into our scorched, parched skin. If you’ve never been three days from death by thirst, you can’t imagine the luxury of that dirty, muddy soak. We climbed out refreshed, tore into our biltong, which we hadn’t been able to even taste for 24 hours, ate our fill, smoked a pipe, and slept in the shade of the pool’s bank till noon.

We spent the rest of the day resting there, thanking our lucky stars—and old da Silvestra’s long-dead ghost—for marking that pool’s position perfectly on the tail of his shirt. We filled every water bottle we had to bursting, and set off again by the light of the moon, covering almost 25 miles that night. We found no more water, but the next day we got lucky, huddling in the shade behind giant termite mounds when the sun was at its hottest. When the mist cleared again at dawn, Sheba’s Breasts were only 20 miles away, looming right over our heads, grander than ever. By the next morning, we’d reached the lowest slopes of the left Breast, our target the whole time.

Our water was already exhausted again, and our thirst was torturing us once more. The lava slopes were sharp and jagged under our boots, made of cinder and rock spewed out of the earth’s crust eons ago, and by 11 a.m. we were all completely done in, our feet raw and bleeding. We spotted a cluster of large lava boulders a few hundred feet above us, and dragged ourselves there to huddle in their shade. Next to the rocks, we saw a patch of dense, bright green growth, sprouting from decomposed lava soil that birds must have dropped seeds into. We didn’t care about grass—we couldn’t live on it like Nebuchadnezzar. We sank down against the rocks and groaned, and I swore under my breath I’d never have signed on for this fool’s errand if I’d known how bad it would get.

Then Umbopa, quiet and dignified as ever, stood up and hobbled over to the green patch. A minute later I stared, shocked, as he started dancing and yelling like a maniac, waving a big green object over his head. We all stumbled after him as fast as our weak legs would carry us, hoping against hope he’d found water.
“It’s food and water, Macumazahn!” he yelled, waving the green thing. It was a wild melon. We’d stumbled on a whole patch of thousands of ripe wild melons, bursting with juice. I yelled to Good, and he sank his false teeth straight into the nearest one before I even finished talking. I think we each ate at least six that day. I’ve never tasted anything sweeter. We cut the rest of the melons we found and stood them upright in the sun to cool by evaporation, packing as many as we could carry. Then a flock of ten large pauw bustards flew straight towards us. We all dropped flat on the sand, I lifted my repeating Winchester, and fired two shots into the middle of the flock as they flew overhead. I brought down a 20-pound bird. Half an hour later we had a fire going of dry melon stalks, and we roasted that bustard whole. We ate every last bite of it, nothing left but leg bones and a beak, and it was the best meal we’d had in a week.

We marched on by moonlight, the air growing colder the higher we climbed. By dawn we were only a dozen miles from the snow line, and we found more melons growing further up, so we no longer worried about water—we knew we’d have all the melting snow we could want soon. The ascent grew so steep we could barely make a mile an hour, and we ate the last of our biltong that night. We saw no game, no springs, no streams anywhere, which struck us as odd with all that snow up above—we later learned every single waterway on those mountains ran down the north face, hidden from us. Now we only feared starvation. We’d escaped death by thirst, but it looked like we’d just starve to death on the snow fields.

I copied the entries from my notebook to tell those next three miserable days:
> 21st May. Started 11 a.m., air cool enough to travel by day, carrying armfuls of melons. Struggled all day, no more melons, no game. Halted at sundown, no food for hours. Bitter cold at night.
> 22nd May. Started at sunrise, faint and weak. Only made five miles all day. Ate patches of snow, nothing else. Camped under a plateau edge. Freezing. Shared a tiny sip of brandy each, huddled in our blankets to stay alive. Starving, exhausted. Ventvögel almost dead by sundown.
> 23rd May. Started at sunrise as soon as the sun thawed our frozen limbs. We’re in a terrible state. If we don’t find food today, we won’t make it through the night. Barely any brandy left. Good, Sir Henry, and Umbopa are holding on, but Ventvögel can’t handle the cold, not for long. We’re level with the lava cliff linking the two Breasts now. Behind us the desert glows all the way to the horizon, in front of us miles of smooth, level snow, the giant snow “nipple” of the peak rising four thousand feet over our heads. Not a living thing in sight. God help us. I think our time is up.

We struggled on up the snow all day, resting every few steps, the whole crew gaunt, ragged, half-starved, dragging our feet through the dazzling white. We only made seven miles total. Just before sunset, we stood directly beneath the snow nipple of Sheba’s left Breast, the setting sun painting the whole peak blood red and gilding the top of the dome like a crown.
“We ought to be almost at that cave the old Dom wrote about,” Good gasped.
“If the cave even exists,” I said.
“Quit talking like that, Quatermain,” Sir Henry groaned. “The map got us the water, didn’t it? It’ll get us the cave.”
“If we don’t find it before dark, we’re dead anyway,” I said.

We tramped in silence for ten more minutes, when Umbopa, who had a leather belt cinched so tight around his waist to “make the hunger small” that his waist looked like a girl’s, grabbed my arm. He pointed at the slope of the nipple, and 200 yards away we spotted a dark hole cut into the snow. It was the cave.

We stumbled there as fast as we could, and found it exactly as da Silvestra had described it. We got to the mouth of the cave just as the sun vanished over the horizon, so fast it went dark in seconds—no twilight in those latitudes. We crawled inside, a tiny cramped cave barely 20 feet deep, huddled together for warmth, and sipped the last dregs of our brandy, barely a mouthful each. The cold was so intense I’m sure the temperature was 15 degrees below freezing, and after days in the scorching desert, starved and exhausted, we could barely keep from freezing to death. We huddled closer and closer, but there was no warmth left in our emaciated bodies. We could only sleep for a few minutes at a time, and we all knew if we slipped into a deep sleep we’d never wake up.

An hour or two before dawn, I heard Ventvögel, whose teeth had been chattering like castanets all night, heave a deep, final sigh. His teeth went quiet. I thought he’d finally fallen asleep, till his back, pressed against mine, grew colder and colder, until it was hard as ice.
Dawn broke, golden light spilling across the snow, and the sun peeked over the lava wall, shining straight into the cave mouth. Ventvögel was dead, frozen stiff, his arms hugged around his knees. Shocked, we scrambled away from his corpse, revolted as all people are by the company of a dead body. Then one of the men gasped. I turned my head.
At the far end of the tiny cave, slumped against the rock, head resting on his chest, arms hanging loose at his sides, sat another dead man. A white man.

Our shattered, half-frozen nerves couldn’t take it. We all scrambled for the cave mouth, tumbling over each other to get out into the open snow, none of us able to speak a single word.

We stumbled to a halt just outside the ice-caked cavern, frost crusted in our eyelashes and our joints still screaming from hours of trudging through snow, feeling absurdly foolish for rushing off without confirming the frozen form we’d glimpsed huddled in the dark.

“I’m going back,” Sir Henry Curtis said, his voice tight with a quiet, unshakable dread.

Good blinked through his fogged monocle. “Why ever for?”

“Because it just struck me—what we saw in there might be my brother George, the one who vanished chasing these mines two years prior.”

The thought stopped all three of us cold. We turned on our heels and ducked back into the cave, our eyes still bleary from the blinding snow glare, struggling for minutes to pierce the gloom before our night vision adjusted enough to pick out the still, crumpled body propped against the far ice wall.

Sir Henry dropped to his knees, leaned in, and peered straight into the dead man’s face. He let out a huge, shaking breath of relief. “Thank God. It’s not him.”

I crowded in next, and got my first clear look. The corpse was a tall, middle-aged man with sharp aquiline features, grizzled hair, and a long black moustache. His skin had dried to a perfect pale yellow, stretched taut over bone, frozen stiff as a carved statue. Every scrap of clothing had been stripped from his frame save a tattered remnant of wool hose around one ankle. Around his neck hung a carved ivory crucifix, yellowed with three hundred years of encasement in ice.

“Who in the devil’s name is this?” I whispered.

“Can’t you guess?” Good said, grinning slightly behind his half-frosted glasses. When I shook my head he went on, “It’s old Dom José da Silvestra, obviously. Who else would be this far up the unclimbed peak?”

“Impossible!” I gasped. “He died three hundred years ago!”

“And what’s stopping him keeping for three thousand years in this natural freezer of a cave?” Good countered, stooping to pick up a sharp, scraped shard of bone half-buried in the frost. “No sun ever reaches in here, no scavenger can claw through the ice to break in. This is the cleft bone he used to scratch his map with, right here. And look—” he nodded at the faint, rust-coloured scab on the dead man’s left forearm “—that’s where he got his ink, his own blood.”

We stared gape-mouthed, forgetting our own gnawing hunger and frozen toes for a full minute. This was the man who’d scrawled the map that had led us thousands of miles across the desert, his writing tool still resting on the cave floor beside his frozen corpse, his crucifix still strung around his neck. I could picture the scene clear as day: the Portuguese explorer, freezing, starving, scribbling his secret map for whoever might find it, dying all alone up here in the endless snow. It was eerie enough to prickle my skin right through my thick fur-lined coat.

“Let’s get out of here,” Sir Henry said quietly. He heaved the frozen body of poor dead Hottentot guide Ventvogel, who’d collapsed in the snow the day before, and set it gently next to the old Dom. With a sharp jerk he snapped the rotten thong holding the ivory crucifix around da Silvestra’s neck, and slipped it into his coat pocket. I picked up the sharp bone pen, and it still sits on my desk as I write this; even now I sometimes use it to sign my name.

We left the two of them—one a 16th century Portuguese noble chasing gold, one a simple Hottentot chasing a living wage—to keep their silent, frozen vigil in the cave forever, and stumbled back out into bright, welcome sunshine. We’d only walked half a mile further across the plateau before we hit the edge of the mountain peak, wreathed in soft rolling morning fog. When the mist lifted a few minutes later, we gasped. Five hundred feet below us, at the end of the long sloping snow field, was a patch of bright green grass, a gurgling stream cutting through it, and ten to fifteen huge antelope basking in the sun, far bigger than any species I’d ever seen in southern Africa.

Food. We might not starve to death up here after all.

We debated stalking them first, but the wind was wrong, and we’d stand out like sore thumbs crossing that blank white expanse of snow. We had to risk a six hundred yard shot from where we stood. We grabbed the heavy express rifles, the ones with expanding bullets that would drop even a giant buck if they hit, lined up our sights, and at Umbopa’s shouted Zulu command, we all fired at once.

Smoke cleared. One of the giant bucks was kicking on its back, dying, on the grass below. We whooped so loud the echoes bounced off the snow peaks, and scrambled down the slope in minutes. Ten minutes after we fired, we had the animal’s heart and liver sitting on the rock in front of us—no dry firewood anywhere nearby, no way to cook them.

“Starving men don’t get to be picky,” Good said. “We eat it raw.”

We cooled it off in the snow, washed it in the icy stream, and tore into it. It sounded disgusting on paper, but it was the best thing I ever tasted. In 15 minutes we were new men, our cold numbness melting away, our blood pumping strong again, careful not to eat too much all at once so we didn’t sicken. Later we’d learn that animal was an inco: the rare giant mountain antelope the local tribes only ever spot at high altitude. Good swore his bullet was the one that dropped it, and we didn’t have the heart to contradict him.

By the time Umbopa had cut all the portable meat we could carry, the last of the fog had burned off completely, and we stared down at the spread of land below us, and for a minute none of us could speak. Five thousand feet beneath our perch on Sheba’s snowy Breasts stretched leagues of rolling, fertile country: thick green forest, a great silver river winding through it, vast grasslands dotted with thousands of game, clusters of round dome huts peeking out from the foothills, ringed all around by distant blue mountain walls. It was the hidden land da Silvestra had written of, no mistake.

Then Sir Henry jabbed a finger out to our right. “The map mentions Solomon’s Great Road, doesn’t it? Look—there it is.”

We followed his gaze, and sure enough, a wide, perfectly paved stone road snaked down the side of the mountain toward the plain, so grand it looked like a Roman turnpike. We tramped over scattered boulders to reach it, and found it was cut straight out of solid rock, fifty feet wide, perfectly smooth—though it vanished completely a hundred paces back up toward the snow peaks, erased by some ancient lava flow. It was more impressive than any modern European road I’d ever seen: it filled three hundred foot wide ravines with dressed stone blocks, zig-zagged down the face of 500 foot precipices, tunnelled straight through solid rock ridges. The tunnel walls were carved full of ancient art: mailed figures in chariots, battle scenes, lines of captives being marched away. Sir Henry swore it was Egyptian or Phoenician work, built long before Solomon’s time.

By midday we’d wandered far enough down the mountain to hit the tree line, a whole grove of silver trees identical to the ones on Table Mountain in Cape Town. We built a roaring fire of dry boughs, spitted chunks of inco meat on sharp sticks, and feasted till we were stuffed, then leaned back against the trees smoking our pipes, listening to the stream gurgle past through banks of giant maidenhair fern, bright coloured birds flitting through the branches. It was paradise, after weeks of desert and snow.

That’s when I noticed Good was gone. I glanced over at the stream bank, and there he was, stripped down to his flannel shirt, scrubbing his clothes clean in the stream, polishing his boots with a bit of fat saved from the antelope meat, combing his hair with a tiny pocket mirror. Then he pulled out a blunt safety razor, rubbed more fat on his stubbly ten-day beard, and started shaving. He’d only got the right side of his face smooth when a bright flash of metal whizzed right past his ear and thudded into the tree behind him.

We all jumped to our feet. Twenty paces away, a dozen huge copper-skinned natives stood watching us, tall as giants, plumes of black feathers in their hair, leopard skin cloaks over their shoulders. A seventeen year old youth stood at the front, arm still outstretched, the one who’d hurled that heavy throwing knife. An old grizzled soldier stepped up and grabbed the boy’s arm, and the whole group started advancing toward us, completely unafraid of our rifles held pointed at them.

“Put the guns down!” I yelled to the others, realizing fighting was a guaranteed death sentence. I stepped forward, greeting them in Zulu. To my shock, they understood me—their dialect was an ancient, archaic form of Zulu, as different from modern Zulu as Chaucer’s English is from ours.

“Strangers cannot cross the death mountains,” the old chief rumbled. “No strangers may live in the land of the Kukuanas. The king’s law says you must die.”

Good, flustered, did what he always did when he was nervous: he snapped his top set of false teeth in and out with a loud, sharp click.

The whole crowd of Kukuanas yelped in terror and scrambled back ten paces.

“Take your teeth out, Good!” Sir Henry hissed.

Good pulled the false set right out of his mouth, and gaped at them, showing nothing but bare red gums. The natives gasped. Then he slipped the teeth back in, grinned wide, and showed them a full set of perfect white teeth again.

The boy who’d thrown the knife collapsed on the ground howling. The old chief’s knees were knocking together. “Spirits!” he stammered. “No man born of woman can do that! One side of his face has hair, the other none, he has a transparent shining eye—” he pointed at Good’s monocle “—and his teeth vanish and reappear! Forgive us, great lords from the sky!”

I seized the chance immediately, putting on my most grand, imperial smile.
“Your pardon is granted,” I told him. “We are not spirits, but we come from the brightest star that shines in the night sky, and we have journeyed here in peace.”

The whole crowd gasped, in complete awe.

All that afternoon we tramped beside the flawless, ancient paved highway, cutting steadily northwest through sun-dappled foothills. Infadoos and Scragga walked at our elbows, their escort marching a hundred paces ahead, spears glinting golden in the warm light. No weed dared take root on the road’s packed, centuries-worn stones, as per a longstanding royal decree.

At last I turned to our guide. “Infadoos—who built this road?”
“My lord, it was raised in an age no living man can recall. Not even Gagool, the wise woman who has outlived three generations of kings, knows the story of its making. None alive now have hands skilled enough to lay such stone.”
“And those strange, hieroglyph-like carvings chiseled into the cave walls we passed through earlier?” I pressed.
“Made by the same hands that built the road, my lord. We Kukuanas know nothing of them.”
“When did your people first settle this hidden valley?”
Infadoos pointed north, towards the snow-capped peaks that walled the land in. “Ten thousand thousand moons ago, our ancestors swept down like a storm wind from the great unknown lands beyond those mountains. They could go no further, blocked by ice and sheer rock. The old tales passed down from our fathers, and Gagool herself, say this valley was green and rich, so they put down roots here. Now our numbers are as the sand of the seashore. When King Twala summons his regiments, their feather plumes cover the whole plain as far as the eye can see.”
“If the mountains seal your land off from the rest of the world, who do your regiments fight?”
“Only raiders, my lord. Every few generations, hordes of warriors pour down the northern passes out of the mists. We cut them down. It is one full generation since the last great war. Thousands starved and died that winter, but we crushed the invaders before they could devour our people. There has been no open bloodshed since.”
“Your warriors must be restless, lolling on their spears all these years,” I said.
Infadoos’s face hardened. “There was war, even then. Civil war. Dog ate dog.”
I leaned in, curious. “How did that come to pass?”
“Our father Kafa had twin sons by his queen, you see. Kukuana custom says the weaker twin must be put to death, so only one heir can rule. But the queen hid the smaller, later-born baby, and named him Twala. I am their younger half-brother, born of Kafa’s second wife. When Kafa died, the elder twin Imotu was crowned king, and ruled well for years. He had a little son by his favourite wife, a boy named Ignosi—Lightning, we call him.
“Three years after the great war against the northern raiders, famine hit. No one had planted crops while the fighting raged. The people starved, raged like a starved lion looking for something to tear apart. Then Gagool, the terrible wise woman who never dies, spoke aloud to the crowds: ‘Imotu is no true king.’ At that very time, Imotu lay sick in his hut, wounded and too weak to rise.
“Gagool dragged the hidden Twala out of the caves where he’d been concealed his whole life. She stripped his waist cloth, and showed the mob the sacred coiled snake mark burned into his midsection—the mark only the rightful firstborn king bears at birth. ‘Behold your king I saved for you!’ she screamed.
“The people, mad with hunger, shouted ‘The King! The King!’ But I knew the truth: Imotu was the elder twin, the true heir. Just then, sick as he was, Imotu crawled out of his hut, holding his queen’s hand, little Ignosi toddling behind him.
“‘What is this noise?’ he asked. ‘Why do you cry for a king?’
“Twala ran at him, grabbed him by the hair, and drove a stabbing blade through his heart. The crowd, fickle as crowds always are, cheered for the new sun rising: ‘Twala is King! Long live Twala!’”
“What happened to Imotu’s queen and little Ignosi?” I cut in. “Did Twala kill them too?”
“No. The moment she saw her husband fall, the queen grabbed the boy and ran. Two days later, starving, she stumbled into a kraal, but no one would give her food—no one dares feed the family of a dead, fallen king. At night, a little girl crept out with a handful of corn for her. The queen blessed the child, and fled towards the mountains before dawn. She and the boy were never seen again. She must have perished in the snow.”
“So if Ignosi had survived, he would be your rightful king?”
“That is so, my lord. The sacred snake mark is burned into his skin too. If he lives, he rules. But he is long dead, no doubt.”

Infadoos pointed down the slope. Below us, sprawling across the plain, lay a massive stockade of huts, ringed first by a high thorn fence, then by a deep, wide ditch. “That is the very kraal where the queen was last spotted with Ignosi. We will sleep there tonight—if, indeed,” he added darkly, “the white lords are still alive when we arrive.”
“Among your people, we do as your people do, Infadoos,” I said firmly, and turned to snap a quick quip at Good, who was tramping sullenly behind me, fussing nonstop to stop his flannel shirt from flapping in the evening breeze. I nearly walked straight into Umbopa, who’d been trailing right behind me, listening to every word we said. His face was tight, like a man fighting to drag a half-forgotten memory up out of the dark of his past.

All the while we’d been descending towards the rolling green plain. The peaks we’d crossed loomed huge behind us, the twin snow peaks of Sheba’s Breasts wreathed in thin, silvery mist. The land here was like paradise: lush but not sweltering tropical, the sun warm on our faces without burning, a sweet wild-scented wind drifting up off the grass slopes. I’d never seen a more perfect place—even the finest parts of the Transvaal couldn’t hold a candle to Kukuanaland.
Earlier, Infadoos had sent a runner ahead to warn the kraal we were coming. That man ran faster than any athlete I’d ever seen, and Infadoos said it was common for his warriors to run 50 miles without stopping. Two miles out from the kraal, we saw the proof: companies of men pouring out the gates, marching towards us. Sir Henry grabbed my arm. “Looks like we’re getting a very warm welcome,” he said, quiet and sharp.
Infadoos caught the tone, hurried to reassure us. “Do not fear, my lords. There is no treachery in my breast. This is my regiment, the Greys, come out to greet you on my orders.” I nodded, but my hand still hovered close to my revolver holster, just in case.

Half a mile from the gates, the ground sloped gently upwards. Three thousand six hundred warriors, split into twelve companies of 300 men each, charged up that slope, spears flashing, plumes flying, to line the road. These were the finest men I’d ever seen: all veterans, around 40 years old, every single one over six feet tall, many topping six and a half. They wore great black plumes of sakaboola feathers on their heads, white ox-tail circlets around their waists and right knees, and carried round 20-inch shields made of thin iron stretched over snow-white ox hide. No flimsy throwing assegais for them—they carried short, heavy stabbing spears, their blades six inches wide, meant to tear through a man at close quarters. Each man also carried three two-pound heavy throwing knives called tollas, which they could hurl dead accurate at a target 50 yards away.
They stood as still as bronze statues until we drew level, then at a signal from their leopard-cloaked commander, every spear flew up, and 300 voices roared the royal salute: *“Koom!”* The moment we passed, they fell in behind us, and soon the whole 3,600-strong regiment was marching at our backs, their tread shaking the very ground.

We left Solomon’s Great Road, crossed the crude but solid drawbridge over the ditch circling the kraal, and stepped inside. The whole compound was perfectly laid out, wide central paths cutting at right angles through blocks of huts, each block housing a full company. The domed huts were thatched perfectly, with proper doorways tall enough to walk through (not crawl through like Zulu huts), and each had a wide verandah paved with hard trodden lime. Hundreds of tall, graceful Kukuana women lined the paths, quiet and dignified as noblewomen in a London drawing room, no crude gawking or giggling, even when they spotted Good’s famously pale bare legs. Their eyes went wide, but not a whisper passed their lips—Good, ever modest, turned bright red under his tan.
Infadoos led us to a large central hut, surrounded by a ring of smaller ones. “Enter, Sons of the Stars,” he boomed, “and rest. Food will be brought to you: honey, milk, a couple of fat oxen, a few sheep, nothing grand, but enough to fill your bellies.”
We stepped inside to find soft tanned skin couches laid out for us, and water waiting to wash the road dust off. Soon girls came carrying roasted corn and honey pots, while youths herded a fat young ox up to our door. It was slaughtered, skinned, jointed in ten minutes flat. We kept the best cuts for our supper, and gave the rest to the surrounding warriors, who cheered loud for the gift from the white lords.
Umbopa, helped by a very pretty young Kukuana woman, boiled our meat over a fire outside the hut. We invited Infadoos and Scragga, King Twala’s son, to join us for the meal. Old Infadoos was warm and polite, but Scragga watched us the whole time with sullen, doubtful eyes—his initial awe of us as magical star-people was fading, replaced by suspicion that we were just ordinary men, same as him. We decided not to mention Sir Henry’s missing brother yet; far too hard to explain why we were hunting for a lost white man in their hidden kingdom.
After supper we pulled out our pipes and lit up. Infadoos and Scragga stared in utter shock. The Kukuanas grew tobacco, but only used it for snuff—they had no idea what smoking was.
I asked Infadoos when we’d leave for the king’s main stronghold at Loo. He said we’d depart at dawn, messengers had already gone ahead to tell Twala we were coming. The great annual feast was starting soon, he explained, every regiment gathering at Loo for the parade, and the yearly witch-hunt that no one dared miss. We’d reach Loo two nights’ march from now, barring flooded rivers or delays.
When they left us for the night, we split into watch shifts. Three of us dropped onto the skin couches, dead tired, fast asleep the second our heads hit the hides. The fourth sat by the door, revolver in hand, eyes peeled for any hint of treachery.

I won’t drag out the details of our two-day trek up Solomon’s Great Road, cutting straight through the beating heart of Kukuanaland. The country grew richer and more fertile with every mile we covered, and the kraals, ringed wide with fields of ripening grain, sprung up thicker than wild thorn. Every one of those fenced settlements was stuffed full of troops, same as the first camp we’d stumbled into days prior, and it didn’t take long to see Kukuanaland ran on the same universal military rule as the Zulus, Masai, or the hard-drilling German regiments back home: every able-bodied man was a soldier, the entire nation one standing army ready to march for war or parade at the king’s call. Thousands of warriors streamed past us as we pushed on, all hurrying to Loo for the great annual royal review and festival, and I swear I’ve never laid eyes on a finer, more disciplined body of fighting men in all my years hunting from the Cape to the Zambezi.

On the second sunset, we pulled to a rest stop on a high ridge the road crested, and there below us, spread across a rolling, lush plain, lay Loo itself. For a native town it was enormous—five miles around, easy, with outlying kraals strung out far past the main fences to house the thousands of gathering regiments, and a strange horseshoe-shaped hill two miles north that we would learn far more about before all was said and done. The river we’d spotted trickling far below the slopes of Sheba’s Breasts cut right through the center of the city, bridged in half a dozen spots, and sixty or seventy miles out across the flat plain rose three sheer, snow-capped mountains set exactly at the points of a triangle: nothing like the smooth, rounded breasts of the old volcano we’d trekked down, sharp as broken obsidian.

Infadoos saw us staring, and spoke up unprompted.
“The great road ends there,” he said, pointing to the peaks the Kukuanas called the Three Witches.
“Why there?” I asked.
“Who knows?” he shrugged. “The mountains are honeycombed with caves, and a great dark pit yawns between them. That’s where the old wise men of our ancestors’ tales went, to bring back whatever they came to this land to find. That’s where all our kings are buried, in the Place of Death beyond the peaks.”
“What did they come looking for, exactly?” I pressed, sharp.
“Nay, I know nothing of that,” he said, glancing at me quick, a flicker of a smile I didn’t trust. “My lords who say they dropped from the Stars would know far better than a simple soldier like me.”
I didn’t push him, not then. “You’re right,” I said. “Up in the Stars we learn all manner of odd things. I’ve heard those old wise men came here looking for bright pretty stones to play with, and yellow iron that never rusts.”
“My lord is very wise,” he said, cold and distant all at once. “I am but a child, unfit to speak of such secrets. My lords must speak to Gagool the old one, at the king’s kraal. She is as wise as you are, and will answer all your questions.” Then he turned and walked off before I could say another word.

The second he was gone I turned to the other two, pointing straight at the three jagged peaks. “There they are,” I said. “Solomon’s diamond mines, no doubt about it.”
Umbopa was standing off to the side, sunk into one of his strange, quiet spells of abstraction, and caught the words. “Aye, Macumazahn,” he said in Zulu, his voice low. “The diamonds are there right enough, and you shall have them. White men are so very fond of such little toys and piles of money, after all.”
“How do you know that?” I asked, sharp, annoyed by his mysterious ways.
He laughed, not a friendly sound. “I dreamed it, in the night, white men.” Then he too turned on his heel and walked away.
“What the devil is our dark friend playing at?” Sir Henry growled. “He knows far more than he’s letting on, that’s for certain. Quatermain, has he heard anything about—about my brother?”
“Not a peep,” I told him. “He’s asked every man he’s made friends with since we met Infadoos, and every last one swears no white man has ever set foot in Kukuanaland before us.”
“Bit of a stretch, isn’t it?” Good put in, frowning. “We only got here by a downright miracle, following that tattered hundred-year-old map. What are the odds his brother found his way here with nothing to guide him?”
“God knows,” Sir Henry said, his face dark. “But somehow, I swear I’m going to find him.”

The sun sank then, and night fell on the land so fast it was like a physical blow. No soft twilight, no slow fading of the light, not in these latitudes—one second the sky was gold, the next it was pitch black, the transition as sharp and final as the jump from life to death. But it didn’t stay dark long. A faint glow bloomed in the west, then silver rays spilled over the horizon, and the full, glorious moon rose to flood the plain with soft, shimmering light, the stars dimming faint before its cool majesty. We all stood quiet, watching that sight, and for a second I forgot the hunger, the blisters on my feet, the danger of being stranded thousands of miles from any white settlement in unknown land. I’ve lived a rough, hard life hunting elephants and running from raiders, but there are a handful of moments I’m grateful I survived long enough to see, and that moon rising over Kukuanaland is one of them.

Infadoos broke the silence a minute later, polite as ever. “If my lords are rested, we will push on to Loo. The moon is bright enough now that we will not stumble off the road, and a hut has been prepared for you to sleep in tonight.”

We agreed, and an hour later we were at the edge of the city, its entire five-mile expanse ringed with thousands of campfires stretching further than the eye could see. Good, never one to miss a bad joke, promptly named the place “Unlimited Loo.” We reached a moat with a drawbridge soon after, met by the clatter of spears and shields and a hoarse challenge from a sentry. Infadoos called out a password I couldn’t catch, the sentry saluted, and we tramped down the main central street of the vast grass city, past endless lines of huts for half an hour before Infadoos halted at the gate of a small compound walled off from the rest, with a courtyard paved with powdered limestone.
“Your poor quarters, my lords,” he said.
It was far better than any shelter we’d had on the trek. Each of us got our own separate hut, fitted out with soft beds stitched from tanned ox hides, laid over mattresses stuffed with sweet-smelling aromatic grass. Roast meat and mealie cobs were already laid out for us on carved wooden platters, and a group of handsome young women waited to serve us, bowing deep as we walked in. We ate until we were full, then hauled all three of our beds into a single hut for safety—an order that made the young attendants giggle like schoolgirls—before we dropped asleep dead tired, after two full weeks of hard travel.

We woke the next morning to the sun high overhead, the same young women standing inside the hut already, ordered to help us “make ready” to meet the king.
“Make ready, my foot,” Good grumbled. “All I’ve got left is a flannel shirt and a pair of scuffed boots. Ask ‘em where my trousers went, Quatermain, will you?”
I asked, and was informed that our lost clothing had been taken directly to the king, who would receive us in audience before midday.
We sent the girls outside to wait, then did the best toilet we could manage with no spare clothes or supplies. Good even went so far as to carefully shave only the right side of his face, leaving the thick new crop of whiskers growing on the left untouched, the half-shaven lopsided look still making him look like nothing the Kukuanas had ever seen. Sir Henry’s long blonde locks hung almost to his shoulders now, making him look more like a viking raider out of an old saga than ever, and my own grizzled uncut beard was nearly an inch long, far longer than I ever let it grow when I was on the hunt.

We ate breakfast, smoked a pipe, and then Infadoos himself showed up to escort us to the king. We told him we’d wait an hour, till the sun was a little higher—never rush to meet an uncivilized king, they’ll mistake eagerness for fear or servility fast. While we waited we pulled together the only decent presents we had left: the Winchester rifle that had belonged to poor dead Ventvögel, and a small bag of bright glass beads we’d hauled from the Cape. We’d already given a handful of the beads to Infadoos and Scragga, and they’d gone wild for them, never having seen glass before. When we were finally ready, Umbopa carried the rifle and the beads, and we followed Infadoos out to the royal enclosure.

It was at least seven acres wide, fifty times the size of our little compound, ringed on the outer edge with huts for the king’s hundreds of wives. Facing the main gate, across the open central space, stood the giant single hut where Twala the king lived, and every spare inch of the rest of the field was packed solid with regiments of warriors, seven or eight thousand men all standing statue-still, their white ostrich plumes waving in the soft breeze, their spears and iron-back oxhide shields glinting in the sun. We sat down on three stools placed in front of the king’s hut, at Infadoos’s signal, Umbopa standing behind us, and waited in absolute dead silence for ten minutes, every one of those eight thousand pairs of eyes locked directly on us. It was a trying ordeal, but none of us showed a flicker of nerves.

Finally the hut door creaked open. A gigantic man stepped out, draped in a magnificent tiger-skin karross, followed by the teenaged boy Scragga, and a thing that looked for all the world like a dried, shrivelled monkey wrapped in a fur cloak. The big man sat down on the central stool, Scragga stood behind him, and the monkey-thing crept on all fours into the shade of the hut and squatted there.

The silence held. Then the giant slipped the tiger karross off his shoulders and stood up, and we got a good look at him. He was easily seven feet tall, the most repulsive, cruel-faced man I had ever laid eyes on. His lips were thick, his nose flat, he only had one blazing black eye—the other was nothing but a raw empty socket in his face—and his whole expression reeked of senseless violence and greed. A huge plume of white ostrich feathers rose from his forehead, he wore a full shirt of shining chain mail under his ornaments, ox-tail tassels around his waist and his right knee, a massive iron spear in his hand, a thick gold torque around his neck, and set in a band on his forehead, glinting dull and huge, was a single uncut diamond the size of a bantam’s egg.

Still no sound. Then he lifted his great spear high.
Eight thousand spears lifted in answer, and eight thousand voices boomed out the royal salute: “KOOM!” It rolled three times over the field, so loud the ground shook under our feet, deep as the lowest roll of thunder.
“Bow down, O people!” a thin, piercing piped voice squeaked from the monkey-thing in the shade. “It is the king!”
“IT IS THE KING!” the whole crowd roared back. “Bow down, O people! It is the king!”

Silence fell again, dead as the grave. A soldier off to our left fumbled his shield, it clattered loud on the limestone pavement.
Twala turned his one cold eye straight at the noise.
“Come here, you,” he said, his voice ice.
A fine, tall young warrior stepped out of the ranks, standing before him.
“Your shield fell, you clumsy dog. Would you make me look like a fool before these strangers from the Stars? What have you got to say for yourself?”
The poor lad blanched under his dark skin. “It was an accident, O Calf of the Black Cow,” he whispered.
“Accidents cost lives,” Twala rumbled. “You made me look foolish. Prepare to die.”
“I am the king’s ox, to do with as he will,” the boy murmured.
“Scragga!” the king roared. “Show me how sharp your spear is. Kill this blundering fool.”
Scragga grinned his ugly, vicious little grin, lifted his spear. The boy covered his eyes with one hand and stood still, no plea, no fight. We were all petrified with horror. Scragga waved the spear twice, then drove it home so hard the point stuck a full foot out through the warrior’s back. The boy flung his hands up and dropped dead, and a low murmur rippled through the crowd, then faded away before the dead silence. Sir Henry jumped to his feet swearing, then thought better of it and sat back down, his face white with rage.
“Good thrust,” Twala said, bored. “Carry him away.”
Four soldiers stepped out, lifted the corpse, and carried it off.
“Cover the blood! Cover the stains!” the thin old voice piped from the shade. “The king’s word is spoken, the king’s doom is done!” A young girl stepped forward from behind the hut, tipped a jar of powdered lime over the dark red splotch on the limestone, and wiped it out of sight. I had to clamp a hand on Sir Henry’s knee under the stool to stop him from lunging at the king. “Sit down,” I whispered. “Our lives depend on it. Don’t make a sound.” He forced himself to relax.

Twala waited till the last trace of the murder was gone, then fixed his one eye on us. “White people, who come from I know not where, for I know not what—greeting.”
“Greeting, Twala, King of the Kukuanas,” I answered.
“Where do you come from, and what do you seek?”
“We come from the Stars,” I said. “Do not ask us how. We came to see your great land.”
“You traveled very far to see a very ordinary place,” he said, his tone sharp. “And that man there with you,” he pointed at Umbopa, “does he also come from the Stars?”
“He does,” I lied. “There are men of your skin color in the heavens too. Do not ask questions about things far too high for you, King Twala.”
“You speak very loud for strangers who are far from home,” Twala said, and I didn’t like the edge in his voice. “Remember the Stars are very far away, and you are standing on my ground. What if I turned you into the same kind of corpse that they just carried off?”
I laughed out loud, even though my heart was hammering hard against my ribs. “Be careful, O king,” I said. “Walk lightly over hot stones or you will burn your feet. Hold a spear by the handle, or you will cut your hand. If you touch a single hair on any one of our heads, destruction will fall on you before you can blink. Didn’t they”—I pointed at Infadoos, then at Scragga, who was wiping the dead boy’s blood off his spear—“tell you what kind of men we are? Have you ever seen anyone who looks like us?” I gestured at Good, lopsided half-shaven, in his ragged flannel shirt, and the king stared, curious.
“True enough, I have not,” he admitted.
“And they didn’t tell you we can kill men from miles away, without ever stepping close to them?” I went on.
“They told me that, but I don’t believe it,” he said, smirking. “Prove it. Kill one of the warriors standing over there, and I will believe you.”
“We do not kill innocent men for sport,” I said, cool as ice. “But if you want proof, have your men drive an ox through the gate. I will have it dead before it runs twenty paces.”
“Nah,” he laughed. “Kill a man, and I will believe.”
“Very well,” I said. “Walk across the open space, and you will be dead before your foot reaches the gate. Or if you are too busy, send your son Scragga.” At that moment I would have happily put a bullet in the little brute myself. Scragga yelped like a kicked dog and bolted straight into the hut. Twala frowned, furious.
“Bring a young ox in,” he ordered.

Two men ran off, and a minute later the ox came bursting through the gate, spotted the thousands of warriors, skidded to a halt, turned broadside, and bellowed. “Shoot it, Sir Henry,” I said. “Show this ruffian we’re not bluffing. Aim for 150 yards, right through the ribs.” Sir Henry lifted his express rifle, fired, the bullet hit, and the ox went kicking onto its back, stone dead. A collective gasp of astonishment rose from every single throat in the crowd.
“Did I lie, O king?” I asked, calm as you please.
“No,” he said, awed, staring at the dead beast. “It is true.”
“Listen, Twala,” I said, holding up the old Winchester repeater. “We come in peace, not war. This is a magic tube from the Stars. It will kill from far away, just as you saw. But it has a charm laid on it: if you ever point it at an innocent man, it will kill you, not him. Watch.” I ordered a soldier to walk forty paces away, drive his spear into the ground so the flat of the blade faced us. I aimed, fired, and the bullet shattered the iron blade into a dozen fragments. The crowd gasped again.
“We give this magic tube to you,” I said, handing it over. “I will show you how to use it later. But never turn the magic of the Stars against a man of earth.” Twala took the rifle gingerly, like it was a live cobra, and set it down very carefully at his

For two full hours we huddled in the dark hut, too raw and hollowed out by the massacre of the witch-hunt to speak a single word. The first pale streaks of pre-dawn were seeping through the reed walls when we heard footsteps crunching on the dirt outside. A sentry’s sharp challenge rang out from the kraal gate, answered so softly we could not make out the words, and a second later Infadoos stepped in, followed by half a dozen towering, grave-faced chiefs. Each of these men commanded three thousand warriors, men who followed no order but their own and their king’s.
“My lords, and Ignosi, rightful heir to the throne of the Kukuanas,” Infadoos said, gesturing to the line of chiefs. “I have told them all I know. Now let them see the sacred snake mark on your skin, hear the truth of your claim, and choose if they will stand with you against Twala.”
Ignosi stripped off his girdle without a word, revealing the coiled snake tattoo winding around his waist. Each chief stepped forward in turn, leaning in to study the mark by the guttering lamplight, then said nothing and passed on to the next. When he’d pulled his loincloth back on, Ignosi recounted the story of his murdered father, his flight from the kingdom, and his years abroad searching for white allies to help him take back his birthright.
“Well?” Infadoos said to the chiefs when he finished. “Twala’s tyranny bleeds this land dry. Last night two chiefs who dared oppose him were butchered, their bodies left for the hyenas. You will be next if you do not move. Choose now.”
The oldest chief, a thick-set white-haired warrior whose own brother had died in the night’s slaughter, stepped forward. “Your words are true. But this is no small risk. We are not fools to risk all for a man whose tattoo could have been inked as a boy. The people follow the king whose sun still shines high in the sky, not one who has not risen. Your white companions from the Stars wield great magic. If Ignosi is truly our rightful king, let them give us a sign all the land can see. Only then will the masses rise with us.”
“The snake mark is your sign,” I argued.
“It is not enough,” the old chief replied, and all the others nodded in fierce agreement.
I turned, perplexed, to Sir Henry and Good, and translated their demand. Good’s face lit up like a lantern. “I’ve got it! Tell them to step outside for a minute, we need a moment to confer.”
The chiefs withdrew, and Good pried open his little medicine chest, pulled out his notebook, and flipped to the back page where he kept an old almanack tucked in the flyleaf. “Quick, what’s the date today? We’ve been counting the days, right? Tomorrow’s June 4th?”
We confirmed it immediately.
“Perfect. Look here.” He jabbed a line of print in the almanack. “Total lunar eclipse, June 4, starts 8:15 Greenwich mean time, visible across South Africa. We’ll tell them we’re going to darken the moon tomorrow night. That’s a sign no one can fake.”
It was brilliant—apart from the very real risk the almanack might be wrong, or the sky cloud over, which would ruin every last scrap of our prestige and send Ignosi’s claim to the grave right alongside us.
“Eclipses run exactly on schedule,” Good insisted, scribbling quick calculations on a blank page of his notebook. “Even without our exact latitude, I make out the eclipse will start around ten o’clock tomorrow night, last two and a half hours, hit near total darkness for a full ninety minutes.”
“Risk it,” Sir Henry said. I agreed, if a little nervously, and called the chiefs back inside.
“Great men of the Kukuanas, Infadoos, hear us,” I said, leading them to the hut door and pointing at the rising red full moon. “We do not like to meddle with the natural order, it brings terror to the world. But we are angered by Twala’s butchery, and by Gagool’s attempt to kill Ignosi. So tomorrow night, two hours before midnight, we will devour the moon. Darkness will fall in the middle of its full light, as proof that Ignosi is your true king.”
The old chief smiled, the others chuckled beside him. “If you do that, my lords, we will follow you to the gates of the three stone peaks themselves.”
Infadoos cut in. “Twala will send for you after sunset tomorrow, to watch the annual dance of the maidens. An hour into the dance, he will have the fairest girl sacrificed to the Old Ones who watch over Solomon’s mountains. If you darken the moon then, and save the maiden, the entire crowd of onlookers will believe in your magic unreservedly. Two miles outside the city, there is a moon-shaped hill fortress garrisoned by my regiment and three of these chiefs’ regiments. Once the moon goes dark, I will lead you out of Loo to safety there, and we will march to war against Twala.”
After the chiefs left, Ignosi stared at us, bewildered. “Can you truly do this thing?”
“We believe we can,” Sir Henry said. “But before we go through with it, I want one promise from you. If you become king, you will ban Gagool’s witch-finding, and no man will ever again be put to death without a fair trial.”
Ignosi thought for a long moment, then nodded. “I promise. The old ways of my people are harsh, but if I am king, no innocent blood will spill on my watch.”
We slept the rest of the morning, ate a huge breakfast, and wandered the kraal through the day while curious Kukuana nobles stopped by to pay formal calls. By eight-thirty that night, a messenger arrived from Twala, bidding us to attend the maiden’s dance. We strapped on the chain mail shirts Twala had given us, slung our rifles over our shoulders, and walked out to the open plaza before the king’s kraal.
Where grim ranks of warriors had stood the night before, the space was filled with hundreds of flower-crowned girls, each holding a palm frond and a white arum lily. Twala sat on his stool in the center of the clearing, Gagool huddled at his feet, Scragga and Infadoos flanking him, and the six rebel chiefs scattered among the other nobles.
“Welcome, white men from the Stars!” Twala boomed, his one eye glinting maliciously at Ignosi. “Witness this feast of girls. You may pick any of the fairest as your wives, take as many as you like.”
Good, ever the susceptible sailor, looked almost tempted, but I hastily jumped in to decline—we knew all too well the endless trouble taking local wives would bring. Twala laughed, then taunted Ignosi for escaping Gagool’s execution plot.
“I will kill you before you kill me,” Ignosi answered, calm as stone.
Twala snarled, but waved a hand to start the dance. The girls swirled forward, singing soft lilting songs, whirling and swaying in the silver moonlight, a sight so graceful it could have outshone any royal ballet. One after another the best dancers stepped forward to perform solo, and one girl named Foulata outshone them all: tall, soft-eyed, graceful as a deer, the undisputed fairest of them all.
“It is her,” Twala announced, pointing. “She will be the sacrifice to the Old Ones. Bring her forward, Scragga—sharpen your spear.”
The girl froze, screamed, and tried to run, but the guards seized her and dragged her struggling to the king’s feet. She wept so hard her flower wreath slipped askew, begging that she had done no wrong, that she was too young to die, that she would never see her mother or her father’s hut again, never tend her flock of lambs or pick dew-drenched flowers at dawn. The sight melted every man in the crowd except Twala and Gagool. She saw the pity on Good’s face, broke free of the guards for a second, and threw herself at his feet, wrapping her arms around his calves.
“White father from the Stars, hide me under your protection!” she wailed.
“Alright lass, I’ve got you,” Good said, bending to pull her to her feet.
“Scragga, kill her!” Twala roared, and the prince lifted his spear high.
Sir Henry hissed at me: “Do something, man! The eclipse better start right now, or she’s dead.”
I glanced up at the moon, my heart in my throat—we had been staring at it for half an hour, and it had not looked a single bit less bright. I stepped between Scragga’s raised spear and Foulata, holding up one hand.
“Stop. The girl does not die. We warned you we would prove our magic. Now watch.”
Twala roared in rage, and a squad of armed guards surged forward from behind the huts. Sir Henry, Good, and Ignosi snapped their rifles to their shoulders.
“Take one more step, and we blow out the moon like a guttering lamp!” I shouted.
Gagool cackled, her cracked voice cutting through the uproar. “Do it then! Prove it, or you all die here!”
I looked up again, and my heart nearly burst with joy. A faint sliver of dark shadow was eating its way across the bright edge of the moon’s orb, creeping steadily over its face.
I lifted my hands to the sky, and Sir Henry and Good followed suit. I boomed out the most dramatic lines I could remember from the *Ingoldsby Legends* at the top of my lungs. Sir Henry roared verses from the Old Testament and a random line of Latin about Balbus building a wall. Good, in the meantime, unleashed a torrent of the most colourful naval curses in three different languages, no phrases repeated for a full ten straight minutes.
The shadow crept faster, and gasps of raw terror rose from the crowd. Men and women fell to their knees, screaming, too frightened to move. Twala blanched, dark as his skin was, and Gagool’s face twisted with rage as she watched the moon’s light dim to a faint sliver of silver, then almost to nothing.
The sign had landed. The entire Kukuana nation was ours to rally against Twala.

Luckily for us, Infadoos and his fellow chiefs knew every unmarked side path snaking through the great city of Loo like the backs of their own hands, so we slipped past unspotted in the pitch of the post-eclipse dark, making steady, fast progress for well over an hour. Then the shadow began to lift: the curved edge of the sun peeped back out from behind the moon, spilling a thin silver streak of light across the black sky, followed by a soft, vivid ruddy glow that hung over the horizon like a lantern strung between stars. Five minutes later the stars faded, and we could finally make out our surroundings.

We stood clear of Loo’s walls, approaching a massive flat-topped hill almost two miles around, a formation common across the South African highveld. It barely rose 200 feet high, but it curved in the shape of a horseshoe, its sides steep, boulder-strewn, and near-precipitous. The wide grass tableland on its flat summit made for a perfect natural camping ground, long used as a military cantonment strong enough to hold thousands. We toiled up its slopes in the returning moonlight, and quickly spotted far more than the usual 3,000-man garrison: regiments upon regiments were camped across its top, their fires dotted like pinpricks through the dark.

When we reached the summit, we found thousands of men roused from their sleep, huddled in tight, shivering clusters, half-convinced the sun had died forever. We passed through their bewildered ranks without a word, until we reached a small hut at the very center of the plateau – and stared, astonished, to find two attendants waiting with the small pile of personal goods we’d been forced to abandon mid-flight from Loo.
“I sent messengers back for them,” Infadoos explained, holding up a familiar bundle of fabric: Good’s long-lost trousers.
Good let out a yell of unfiltered delight, lunged for them, and immediately began yanking them on over his legs.
“Surely my lord will not hide his beautiful white legs away?” Infadoos said, sounding genuinely regretful.
But Good, a very modest man, was not to be swayed. The Kukuana people only got one more glimpse of those famous pale calves after that; from then on, they had to sate their curiosity with his single half-grown whisker, his famous transparent glass eye, and the detachable set of false teeth he loved to pop out at stunned onlookers for jokes.
Still gazing fondly at Good’s newly fastened trousers, Infadoos next told us he’d ordered every regiment on the hill to muster at dawn, to lay out their full plan of rebellion to the men, and formally introduce Ignosi as their rightful, exiled king.

When the sun cleared the horizon, 20,000 men – the absolute flower of the entire Kukuana army – formed up on the open plain at the plateau’s center, drawn up three sides of a massive dense square, their spears glinting so bright they looked like a field of shards of light. We took our place on the open fourth side, ringed by all the kingdom’s highest chiefs and officers, and after a roar for silence died down, Infadoos began to speak.
A natural orator like all high-born Kukuanas, he wove the story of Ignosi’s father, the old rightful king, who had been basely murdered by his own brother Twala, the usurper, before Twala’s men chased his pregnant wife and newborn child out into the cold veld to starve. He laid out the cruelty of Twala’s reign, pointing to the previous night’s witch-hunt, where the king’s men dragged dozens of the noblest families in the land out of their huts to slaughter for no crime at all. Then he told the crowd the white lords from the stars had seen their suffering, crossed the snow-capped mountains to find the lost exiled king Ignosi, and used their great magic to snuff out the full moon to save Foulata, and slay Twala’s vicious son Scragga. The crowd hummed with approval, their voices rising like distant thunder.

Ignosi stepped forward, his massive frame towering over the chiefs around him, and repeated his uncle’s story, his deep voice booming across the square. “Ye have heard my words! Now choose between me, and the man who sits on my father’s throne – the uncle who slit his brother’s throat, who chased his brother’s baby out to die in the dark cold. The chiefs here can prove who I am, for they have seen the royal snake mark coiled around my waist. Would these white magic lords stand at my side if I were not your true king? Look up! The dark they wove to confound Twala and cover our flight, dark in the hour of the full moon, is still fresh in your memory!”
“It is!” the 20,000 men roared back as one.
“I am your king!” Ignosi shouted, lifting his broad-bladed battleaxe high over his head so the sun flashed off its edge. “If any man here says that is a lie, let him step forward now. I will fight him single-handed, and his blood will be the proof I speak truth. Step forward!”
Not a single man moved.
“I am your king,” he continued, “and if you stand at my side through this coming battle, you will share in all my victory. You will get cattle, new wives, the highest honor among all the kingdom’s regiments. If you fall, I will fall fighting beside you. And I make this sacred promise to you: when I sit on my father’s throne, bloodshed will end. No more witch-finders dragging you off to die for no crime. No more raiding parties eating your kraals bare. Every man will sleep safe in his own hut, no fear. Justice will be blind, and equal for all. Have you chosen, chiefs, captains, soldiers, people?”
“We have chosen, O King!” the shout rolled over the hill.
“It is well. Look east, west, north and south – see Twala’s messengers racing out of Loo right now, summoning every loyal soldier he can find to come and kill me, kill you, and kill my white friends. By tomorrow, or the day after at latest, he will march on us with an overwhelming army. I will see who among you is truly my man, who does not fear to die for what is just – and no man who fights bravely will be forgotten when we divide the spoils. Now go to your huts, and make yourselves ready for war.”
A single chief lifted his hand, and 20,000 voices roared the ancient royal salute: “*Koom*!” The rebellion had the full army’s backing. The battalions marched off to their positions at once.

Half an hour later, we held a war council with all the regiment commanders. From our vantage point on the hill we could see Twala’s forces mustering in Loo, runners streaming out in every direction. We had 20,000 men, seven of the kingdom’s finest regiments. By all Infadoos and the chiefs’ calculations, Twala already had 30 to 35 thousand loyal men assembled in Loo, and would gather at least another 5 thousand by midday tomorrow. Deserters might trickle over to our side, but we could not count on that. It was clear Twala planned to crush us fast. Already, armed patrols circled the base of our hill, and all signs pointed to a full attack coming the next day – the usurper would spend the rest of the day undoing the “magic” eclipse propaganda that had shaken his troops’ morale.

We threw every man we could onto fortification work. The paths up the hill were the only weak points, so we blocked every single one with massive stacked boulders, piled more loose rocks at the cliff edges to roll down on advancing attackers, mapped out every regiment’s exact battle station, and turned the old sanatorium hill (once used to let troops recover from malaria in the lowlands) into a near-impenetrable fortress.

Just before sundown, we spotted a small party of men climbing towards us from Loo, one holding a palm leaf of truce high above his head. Ignosi, Infadoos, a few chiefs, and the three of us went down to the foot of the hill to meet him. The herald was a tall, arrogant man wrapped in a fine leopard-skin cloak.
“Greeting from the king!” he shouted. “The lion greets the jackals that snarl at his heels, who make unholy war against their rightful ruler!”
“Speak,” I said.
“These are Twala’s words. Surrender to his mercy before worse befalls you. He has already torn the shoulder from the black war bull, and drives it bleeding around his camp. His terms are merciful, fit for a great king: one in ten of you rebels will die, all the rest will be spared. But the white man Incubu who slew his son Scragga, the false king Ignosi, and traitor Infadoos, will die by slow torture as an offering to the Silent Three Giants in the mountains. That is the king’s mercy.”
I leaned forward, and shouted my reply loud enough that all the nearby soldiers could hear. “Go back to Twala, you dog. Tell him we – Ignosi rightful king of the Kukuanas, Incubu, Bougwan, Macumazahn the star-men who snuffed the moon, Infadoos of the royal blood, and all the thousands of armed men here – will never surrender. Before two suns set, Twala’s corpse will stiffen at his own city gate, and Ignosi, the son of the king he murdered, will sit on his throne. Run back now, before we whip you off our hill. Be careful you do not lift a hand to strike men who can command the sky to go dark.”
The herald laughed loud and mocking. “Big words will not frighten us, star-men. Show half that bravery on the battlefield tomorrow. Fight all you like, before the crows pick your bones cleaner and whiter than your pale skins. Farewell. Don’t run back to your stars – wait for me, I’ll find you in the fight.” He turned and vanished back down the slope, and the sun sank below the horizon.

It was a long, busy night. Even exhausted, we kept working on final battle preparations by moonlight, messengers coming and going nonstop from our council hut. An hour after midnight, everything that could be done was done. The camp fell quiet, save for the occasional sharp challenge from a sentry. Sir Henry, Ignosi, a chief, and I went down to patrol the outposts. Spears glinted out of the dark from every bush and rock the second we came near, vanishing again the moment we gave the password. No man was sleeping at his post. We picked our way back through thousands of sleeping warriors, many of whom would never wake up to see another sunrise. The moonlight streaked over their spears, gilding their still faces, making them look gaunt, ghostly, almost unhuman. Their tall war plumes tossed in the cold night wind.
“How many of these do you think will be alive this same time tomorrow?” Sir Henry asked me, quiet.
I shook my head. My tired mind could almost see the shadow of death already hovering over half the camp. I thought of how tonight they slept healthy and whole, tomorrow they would lie cold and still, their wives widows, their children fatherless, their names forgotten by all but the grass that grew over their graves. The moon would keep shining, the wind would keep blowing over the plains, long after all of us were gone.
“I am properly frightened, Curtis,” I admitted. “I doubt any of us will be alive by tomorrow night. They outnumber us too greatly. We’re lucky if we even hold this hill for a few hours.”
Sir Henry stroked his thick yellow beard and laughed. “I’ve heard you say that before, Quatermain. But we’ll take a good number of them down with us. This whole mess is not a fight we ever should have gotten tangled up in, but we’re in it now, so we might as well make the most of it. Personally, I’d far rather die fighting than any other way. And since it looks more and more like we’ll never find my poor lost brother, it makes that idea easier to bear. Fortune favors the brave. We might still pull this off. Either way, this battle will be hell. We’ve got reputations to keep up – we’ll be right in the thick of it.” There was a glint in his eye that told me he actually, secretly, loved the thrill of a good fight.

We slept for a couple of hours, woken at dawn by Infadoos, who came to tell us thousands of troops were pouring out of Loo, and Twala’s skirmishers were already pushing our outposts back to the foot of the hill. We got dressed for the fight, pulling on our chain mail shirts – we were more grateful for those heavy steel garments than any treasure we’d ever found. Sir Henry went full native war regalia: a fine leopard commander’s cloak slung around his neck, the rare black ostrich feather plume only worn by top generals bound around his forehead, a magnificent white ox-tail loincloth, heavy rhino-handle battleaxe, round white ox-hide shield, throwing knives at his waist, and his revolver tucked in his belt. He looked magnificent, the perfect picture of a giant warrior king. Ignosi matched him, equally resplendent.
Good and I looked far less imposing. Good refused to take off his newly recovered corduroy trousers, tucking the chain mail carefully down over them, monocle perched on one half-shaved face, and he looked more like a befuddled traveling salesman dragged into a war than a great wizard. My chain mail was far too big, so I pulled it over all my clothes, making me look strangely bulky, then took off my trousers entirely, keeping only my soft veldschoen shoes, my legs bare so I could run fast if we had to retreat fast. I pinned a huge bloodthirsty-looking plume to the top of my shooting hat, grabbed a spear and a shield I had no idea how to use, two throwing knives, my revolver, and we gave our rifles over to bearers to carry behind us – ammo was too scarce to waste it on the front lines, and they’d be useless in a mass spear charge.
We climbed up to the small brown stone koppie lookout on the edge of the plateau, our command post, surrounded by Infadoos’s finest regiment, the Greys, 3,500 of the best fighters in the entire Kukuana army, held in reserve. We looked out over the plains below, and saw Twala’s army pouring out of Loo in endless ant-like columns. Three separate forces, each 11 or 12 thousand men strong. One veered right, one veered left, the third marched slow and steady straight for our hill.
“Ah,” Infadoos said, grim. “They are coming to attack us on three sides at once.”
Our plateau was a mile and a half around, far too wide for our 20,000 defenders to spread thin across every point. There was nothing for it but to send our orders out immediately: every regiment would hold their assigned stretch of slope, no matter the cost. The battle was about to begin.

No rush, no shouts, no fanfare: three enemy columns crept forward across the valley floor with the slow, inexorable weight of a landslide. When they pulled to a halt 500 yards from our horseshoe-shaped hill redoubt—our two forward points facing the royal town of Loo—the centre column anchored itself at the root of a narrow, grassy tongue of plain that jutted up towards our position, waiting for the two flanking columns to circle all the way around our rear. Their plan was simple: hit every side of our tiny defence at the exact same second.

“Oh for a Gatling gun,” groaned Good, staring out at the packed, bristling phalanxes of Kukuana warriors below. “I’d clear that whole plain in twenty minutes flat.”
“Since we haven’t got one, daydreaming won’t help,” Sir Henry replied, clapping me on the shoulder. “C’mon, Quatermain, take a shot. Bet two to one you miss that tall fellow out front who looks to be their commander. Even a whole sovereign, paid out the second we get out of this alive, that you can’t land your bullet within five yards of him.”

That stung my pride— a hunter of my reputation doesn’t get bested by a long shot wager. I loaded my express rifle with a solid lead ball, and waited. Sure enough, the commander stepped ten paces out from his ranks, only one orderly at his heels, to get a clearer look at our defences. I laid the rifle across a flat rock for stability, lined up the sight. The gun was only calibrated to hit 350 yards, so I aimed halfway down the officer’s neck to compensate for the bullet’s downward drift at 500 yards—should have landed him square in the chest. I squeezed the trigger.
When the smoke cleared, my target was completely unharmed. Three paces to his left, his orderly lay flat on his back, stone dead.
The commander yelped in alarm and sprinted back for his lines. “Bravo, Quatermain!” Good cackled. “You scared the wits out of him.”

I was furious. A man who makes his living with a rifle hates to miss in public, hates to look like a fool in front of the others. Without thinking, I snapped the rifle up, tracked the running commander, and fired the second barrel.
He threw his hands straight up in the air and pitched forward face-first into the grass. No mistake that time. I’m not proud to say it— but for a second, all I felt was stupid, gleeful relief my marksmanship reputation was intact.
Our allied Kukuana troops cheered so loud the hills echoed. They thought the two shots were white man’s magic, a good omen for the fight. The dead commander’s whole regiment reeled back in chaos, shocked their leader had dropped dead from half a mile away. Sir Henry and Good opened fire with their Winchesters, mowing down the packed ranks, and I got off another two shots before the enemy advance got close enough we could no longer see individual targets through the dust. We counted at least six or eight men knocked out of the fight before they vanished into the cloud of advancing spears.

Then a deep, thunderous roar burst from our far right. An identical roar answered it from our left. The flanking columns had engaged.
The mass of warriors in front of us spread out, broke into a slow trot up the grass slope, and sang a low, guttural war song as they came. We fired steady, picking them off as they climbed, Ignosi taking potshots alongside us when he could spare the time. But we might as well have been throwing pebbles at an incoming tidal wave. The sheer weight of thousands of armed men kept coming, shouting, spears clashing. They overran the small picket line we’d hidden in the rocks at the foot of the hill, and slowed a little after that, climbing steep to save their breath. Our first defence line was halfway down the slope, our second 50 yards further back, our third strung tight along the plateau edge.
They stormed up screaming their war cry: *“Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!”* — Twala! Smite! Smite!
Our men yelled right back: *“Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!”*
Throwing knives flashed back and forth across no man’s land, then the lines crashed together. Warriors fell fast, like leaves in a gale. Within minutes, the sheer press of numbers pushed our first line back until it merged with the second. The second line held fiercely for ten minutes more, but then they too were forced uphill, until 20 minutes after the fight began, our third and final line of defence was fully engaged.
The attackers were winded now, though. They’d lost hundreds of men dead and wounded on the climb, and they couldn’t break that wall of sharp spear points strung along the plateau edge. The battle swayed back and forth, ebbing and flowing, the outcome anyone’s guess. Sir Henry watched for a second, eyes blazing, then said not a single word. He hefted his battle axe and charged straight into the thickest part of the fight, Good hot on his heels.
The allied warriors spotted his giant frame cutting a swathe through the enemy, and a roar went up: *“Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!”* — The Elephant! The White Giant! Smite!
From that second the fight was no longer in doubt. Inch by inch, the attacking force was cut down and pushed back down the slope, until they broke and retreated in disarray to their reserves.
A messenger came sprinting up moments later, gasping that the left enemy column had been completely repulsed. I was just starting to let myself relax, think the fighting was done for the day, when my stomach dropped. The small unit holding our far right flank was being chased straight towards us across the plain, thousands of Twala’s warriors pouring after them—they’d broken through there completely.

Ignosi saw it all in a glance, and barked a sharp order. The elite Greys reserve regiment spread out in a line in front of us. Another order snapped out, repeated down the line by every captain, and before I knew what was happening, I was swept up in a full-tilt charge straight at the oncoming enemy. I huddled as far behind Ignosi’s broad, muscled frame as I could, hobbling along behind him, fully expecting to get skewered at any second. We plowed straight through our own retreating men, who immediately snapped back into formation behind us, all I could hear was the deafening, rolling thunder of thousands of shields clashing together. Then a huge, wild warrior with eyes bulging out of his skull came sprinting straight at me, his spear dripping fresh blood.
I didn’t freeze. I threw myself flat on the ground right at his feet. He couldn’t stop his momentum, tripped right over me and went headfirst into the dirt. Before he could scramble back up, I was on my feet, my revolver pressed to his back, and the fight was over.
Then something heavy whacked me across the side of the head, and I blacked out.

I woke up back on the plateau koppie, Good leaning over me, dribbling water from a gourd onto my face. “You alright, old man?” he asked, pale with relief.
I shook myself off and stood up. “Fine. Just a crack on the head knocked me silly. How’s it all ended?”
“Every attack repulsed,” he said, pointing down the slope. “But the casualties are horrific. We’ve got two thousand of our men dead or wounded, and they’ve lost closer to three. Look at that.”
Long lines of stretcher bearers filed past, four men to a flat hide tray the Kukuana army carried for exactly this purpose. On every tray lay a wounded man. The ten regimental medicine men hurried between them, checking every wound. Any man they could patch up was carried off to get whatever care they could manage. But any man with a fatal gut tear from those massive, broad Kukuana spears? The medics slit an artery fast, clean, no pain, and the man was dead in less than a minute. It was a brutal, merciful thing, but ghastly to watch. Worse than the day I saw Swazi troops bury their hopelessly wounded alive after a battle, years before.

We hurried around the far side of the koppie, away from the gruesome triage site, and found Sir Henry—still clutching his dented battle axe—huddled in urgent consultation with Ignosi, Infadoos, and the rest of the chiefs.
“Thank God you’re up, Quatermain,” Sir Henry said. “Twala’s got fresh troops pouring in from the town right now. He’s not going to risk another head-on attack. He’s going to besiege us, starve us out.”
“Worse,” Infadoos put in, grave. “The spring on this hill is drying up. By midnight every one of us will be dying of thirst. We have three choices. Sit here and waste away like a lion trapped in a den. Fight our way north through their lines. Or”—he pointed at the massive enemy camp spread across the plain—“charge straight at Twala’s throat. Incubu says charge, and he’s right—he fought today like a buffalo in a net, cutting men down like hail through corn, I saw it with my own eyes. What do you say, Macumazahn, the wily old fox who never misses a thing? What’s our move?”
Ignosi, standing beside him, was clad head to toe in full war regalia, looking every inch the king he was. “You speak, my father,” he said to me. “I am still a child in wisdom beside you.”
After a quick huddle with Good and Sir Henry, I told them my call: wait was death, retreat was suicide. We attack Twala right now, before our men’s wounds stiffen, before the sight of Twala’s thousands of fresh troops makes our captains lose heart and defect.

Ignosi nodded, and the full plan came out, brilliant in its boldness.
The sun was already dipping west. He would send Infadoos and Sir Henry, with the Greys regiment, down that narrow grassy tongue of plain sticking out towards the enemy. Twala would send every regiment he had to crush that small, exposed force. But the tongue was so narrow, only one enemy regiment could attack at a time. The Greys would hold them off, chew through them one by one, and every last man in Twala’s army would stare at that fight, no eyes on the flanks.
While they were all distracted, 6000 of our men would creep along the right curve of the horseshoe hill to hit Twala’s left flank. Another 6000 would creep along the left curve to hit his right flank. Good would lead the right horn, his sharp “transparent eye” the Kukuanas admired there to steady the men. When both flanks were in position, Ignosi would lead the final reserve charge straight at Twala’s command tent. We would crush him before sundown, and sit in the palace at Loo before the moon rose.
No one hesitated. The Kukuana military machine moved faster than any force I’d ever seen. In a little over an hour, every man had eaten his rations, the regiments were in their positions, only a small guard left to watch over the wounded.
Good came over to shake our hands. “Off to the right wing,” he said, grinning a little tight. “Figured I’d say goodbye, just in case we don’t meet again.”
We shook hands in silence, no overblown speeches, all of us too English to gush about our feelings. “I don’t expect to see tomorrow’s sun,” Sir Henry rumbled, his deep voice shaking just a little. “The Greys are almost certainly going to get wiped out to buy the flanks time. But it’s a man’s death. Good luck, Quatermain. If you make it out, go get those diamonds. But take my advice: never go chasing any more pretenders, or lost mines, or fool’s errands ever again.”
Good squeezed our hands, and vanished towards the right flank. Infadoos clapped Sir Henry on the back and led him down the slope to the front ranks of the Greys. And I, with a heavy, knotty feeling in my stomach, followed Ignosi to my post in the second attacking regiment, waiting for the signal that would decide if any of us survived the day.

The dust of the great battle had barely settled when Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good were half-carried into the dead king Twala’s private hut, where I stumbled in after them. We were all three utterly broken: the day’s unbroken fighting, near-misses, and heavy blood loss had drained every last scrap of strength out of us. I’m wiry enough, built for long treks and harsh exertion, but that night my old lion-maul wound throbbed so bad I could barely sit, and the gash on my skull from the blow that knocked me out that morning sent stabbing pains behind my eyes. We were a miserable trio, no mistake—but the only thought that kept us from despair was the fact we were even alive to hurt, unlike the twelve thousand Kukuana men strewn across the plain outside, strong and well at dawn, cold before dark.

With the help of Foulata, the beautiful young woman we’d saved from Twala’s sacrifice block days earlier, we peeled off our linked steel chain shirts—the armour that had more than once saved our lives that afternoon. As I expected, the skin under the links was crisscrossed with brutal deep bruises: the steel had stopped spears and axe blades from piercing our flesh, but it had done nothing to soften the crushing force of the blows. Sir Henry and Good were covered head to toe in dark contusions, and I was far from unmarked. Foulata brought us poultices of pounded, sweet-scented green native leaves that drew the fire out of the bruises almost instantly, but the deep wounds we’d suffered still worried us far more. Good had a clean spear hole straight through the fleshy part of his well-known white calf, and he’d lost a frightening amount of blood. Sir Henry bore a ragged, gash across his jaw from Twala’s own battle axe.

Luckily, Good was a more than competent amateur surgeon. Once we dug out his tiny medicine chest, he cleaned both wounds thoroughly in the dim, guttering glow of the Kukuana hut’s crude oil lamp, stitched up Sir Henry’s jaw neatly, then did the same for his own leg. He slathered both cuts in antiseptic ointment, and we wrapped them in the last clean scraps of our tattered pocket handkerchiefs. Foulata had meanwhile brewed us thick, hot game broth, rich enough to sustain us even when we were too worn out to chew solid food. We downed it, then collapsed onto the piles of lavish fur karross rugs strewn across the hut floor. By the bitter irony of fate, the man who had beheaded Twala himself, Sir Henry Curtis, fell asleep that night curled up on Twala’s own personal couch, wrapped in the dead king’s own favourite fur blanket.

Sleep was almost impossible, even after that brutal day. The air hummed with the endless wailing of thousands of Kukuana women, mourning the husbands, sons, and brothers who had fallen in the fight. The sound clawed at my chest, and drove home just how much pointless blood had been spilled to put Ignosi on the throne. Past midnight, the wails died down to scattered whimpers, and the only remaining sound was a long, piercing, unearthly keen coming from the hut directly behind ours. I later learned that was Gagool, the ancient witch, keening over the corpse of her dead king Twala.

I drifted into fitful, nightmare-ridden sleep, jerking awake over and over replaying the day’s horrors: the warrior I’d cut down on the mountain ridge, the last immortal stand of the Greys regiment on the little mound surrounded by thousands of Twala’s troops, Twala’s plumed, gory head rolling past my boots, teeth gnashing and eyes still glaring. Dawn found my companions no better rested than I was. Good was burning up with a raging fever, delirious, and to my horror, he began to spit blood—no doubt the result of a hidden internal bruise from the warrior who’d smashed his full force spear against Good’s chainmail for minutes on end the day before. Sir Henry, sore and stiff enough that he could barely move, was otherwise steady, though his jaw wound made eating a chore and laughing completely impossible.

Around eight o’clock, Infadoos paid us a visit. The tough old warrior barely looked worn out after fighting all day and pulling an all-nighter securing the kingdom, though he admitted he hadn’t slept at all. He was overjoyed to see us, but grieved deeply at Good’s condition, and I noticed he treated Sir Henry with an almost religious reverence. Word had already spread across every corner of Kukuanaland that no mere man could have fought as Sir Henry did, or cut through Twala’s bull neck in a single blow of his axe in single combat. That feat became a proverb across the kingdom, and any impossible display of strength would be called “Incubu’s blow” from that day forward. Infadoos told us all of Twala’s remaining regiments had sworn full fealty to Ignosi, and that outlying tribal chiefs were already sending messengers to submit. With Twala dead and his brutal heir Scragga long slain, there was no rival claimant left to contest the throne.

Later that morning, Ignosi himself arrived, the royal Kukuana diadem bound to his brow, a guard of honour trailing behind him, every inch a king. I could barely reconcile the stately, powerful ruler before me with the tall Zulu man who’d turned up begging for work in Durban months earlier. We greeted him formally, and he laughed, saying he owed his crown entirely to the strength of our three right hands. We brought up our long-promised quest for Solomon’s diamond mines, and he told us he’d planned to execute Gagool—the evil genius who’d run the kingdom’s cruel witch-hunts for generations—but she was the only living soul who knew the secret path to the royal burial caves of the Silent Ones, where the mines lay hidden. I convinced him to spare her life long enough to lead us there, and reminded him of his promise to show us the stones.

Good’s fever raged for four more days, and we all thought he was a dead man. If it hadn’t been for Foulata’s tireless, unceasing nursing, I have no doubt he would have perished. The dusky, soft-eyed Kukuana girl shooed me and Sir Henry away when we tried to help, saying our clumsy movements made the delirious Good restless, and she tended him day and night without a break, cooling his fever with a native drink of milk infused with tulip bulb juice, swatting flies away from his burning face. The fifth night of his illness, I crept into the hut to check on him, and for a second I thought he was gone—he lay completely still, no more tossing and raving. But he was sleeping peacefully, and Foulata was seated on the floor beside him, her small dark fingers locked tight in his, too scared to move even an inch for fear she’d wake him. She sat like that for eighteen full straight hours, no food, no rest, until he finally stirred awake. We had to carry her away from his side afterwards: her legs had gone completely stiff, no circulation left after so long sitting motionless.

Good cried when we told him what she’d done for him, and he dragged me along as translator to thank her. When he told her he owed her his life and would never forget her kindness, she blushed darkly under her skin, and smiled softly, saying he’d already saved hers—completely forgetting that Sir Henry and I had helped rescue her from Twala’s sacrifice block too. I sighed, because I knew the way of the world: there’s no stopping a sailor from falling in love at the slightest provocation, no matter what the colour of the girl’s skin.

A few days later, Ignosi held the great royal indaba, the formal council where all the kingdom’s head chiefs crowned him king in a vast, imposing ceremony that included a full military review. The tattered surviving remnants of the Greys regiment were paraded before the whole army, honoured for their legendary stand, and every single man was given a large herd of cattle and promoted to officer rank in a new, expanded Greys corps being formed. Ignosi issued a royal decree that all three of us were to be greeted with the same royal salute given to him, granted full power of life and death across Kukuanaland, and he formally banned all the cruel old witch-hunting rituals Gagool had overseen for decades.

As soon as the ceremony ended, we pressed him again for access to the diamond mines. He confirmed the old legend: deep in the mountain, in a hidden chamber only Gagool knew the way to, there was a trove of the shining stones we sought. Generations earlier, a white man had found the place, filled a hide bag full of diamonds, but Gagool of that era betrayed him, and he was chased back over the mountains, never to return.

Ignosi called for Gagool to be brought in. The shriveled old witch was half-carried by guards, cackling and cursing the whole way, bragging that no man dared kill her—she’d outlived a dozen generations of kings, no one had the power to slay her. But when Ignosi pressed the sharp tip of his spear to her withered throat, she broke immediately, wailing and begging for her life, swearing she would lead us straight to the hidden chamber. She cackled madly on the floor, her snake-like eyes glinting, saying we’d find that old white man’s forgotten hide bag still full of unclaimed stones, that evil always befell anyone greedy enough to steal the treasure of the mountain. Her wild, shrill laughter hung in the thick hut air, a cold warning before we even stepped foot on the path to Solomon’s mines.

By the third night after the chaos of the battle we’d left behind, we were camped in crude, smoke-stained huts at the foot of the Three Witches: that triangle of jagged, snow-capped peaks where Solomon’s Great Road dead-ended at last. Our party was small, all things considered: me (Quatermain), Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, Foulata who tended to us all and doted especially on Good, Infadoos, the ancient crone Gagool trundled along in a curtained litter muttering curses nonstop, and a handful of loyal, quiet Kukuana guards.

The three peaks, forged by some ancient volcanic upheaval, loomed straight up into the darkening sky, and when dawn broke the next morning I swore I’d never seen a more staggering sight. Their twisted snow-wreaths glowed pink high above the heather-purple lower slopes, and the white paved ribbon of that 3,000-year-old road stretched straight uphill five miles from our camp to the very foot of the central peak. There it ended, exactly as Dom Jose da Silvestra’s blood-scrawled map had promised. This was the spot that had killed the old Portuguese explorer three centuries prior, that had likely cost us Sir Henry’s lost brother George, too. After every danger we’d survived crossing the desert and fighting through Kukuana lands, were we fated to join that list of the unlucky? A thick, superstitious hush hung over all three of us white men as we set off that morning, no matter how hard we tried to shake it.

We moved so fast along the heather-fringed road that Gagool’s bearers could barely keep pace. The old witch poked her shrivelled, hideous face out of the litter curtains, her glinting flint eyes locking on ours, and piped at us to slow down. “Why do you run to meet the evil that waits for you, treasure-hunters?” she cackled that guttural, horrible laugh that sent cold shivers skittering down my spine, killing our eager enthusiasm for a full minute. But we pushed on anyway, until the road curved around a rise, and we stopped dead.

Below us yawned a circular pit half a mile wide, 300 feet deep, its sloped sides lined with the exact same blue stiff clay I’d seen in the Kimberley diamond diggings. I pointed out the worn, flat stone slabs half-buried in brush on the lower slope of the pit—ancient washing tables for sorting diamond-bearing ore. This wasn’t myth. This was Solomon’s actual mine, the pit marked clear as day on that tattered old map.

The Great Road curved around the rim of the pit, shored up by heavy cut stone blocks to stop the reef from caving in, and as we rounded the far side we spotted them: the three Silent Ones, the colossal statues the Kukuana people held in such reverent awe. Seated on dark rock pedestals 40 paces apart from each other, each 30 feet tall, they stared unblinking down the 60-mile stretch of plain leading to Loo. The nude female figure, its features worn soft by centuries of wind and rain, had distinct crescent horns rising from her forehead. Sir Henry, a classicist who’d taken top honours at university, identified her instantly: Ashtoreth, the Phoenician Astarte, the false goddess King Solomon had turned to in his later years. The two male flanking statues were draped in carved robes: the one on our right had the face of a raving devil, while the left one held a cold, unearthly serenity that reeked of the inhuman cruelty ancient civilizations prayed to. Three relics from a lost age, left to sit there in sun and storm, watching the empty plains forever.

Infadoos walked up, saluting the statues by lifting his spear, and asked if we wanted to rest and eat a midday meal before entering the forbidden Place of Death. But curiosity burned too hot in our chests to wait. It was barely 11 a.m. when we told him we’d go in at once, and I asked Foulata to stow a supply of dried biltong and two full gourds of water in a hinged reed basket, just in case we got trapped deep inside. Gagool was hauled out of her litter, leaning on a gnarled walking stick, grinning her gap-toothed evil grin at us all before she hobbled 50 paces past the back of the colossi, toward a sheer 80-foot rock wall that sloped up thousands of feet to the snow line. Half-hidden in shadow, there was a solid arched stone portal, the entrance to the mountain’s hidden galleries.

Infadoos refused to cross the threshold. “I will not step foot in the Hall of the Dead,” he warned Gagool, his voice sharp as a spear point. “You guard my lords well. If a single hair on their heads is harmed, witch or no witch, I will have your head before sundown.” Gagool just cackled, mocking him for his big words, and pulled a large rush-wick lamp full of oil out from under her fur cloak. Good asked Foulata if she wanted to wait outside with the rest of the guards, but the girl shook her head and clung tight to his arm, saying wherever he went, she would follow.

Gagool plunged into the dark passage first. We followed close behind, and 50 paces in a swarm of bats rushed past our faces, one smacking Good right in the cheek hard enough to make him yelp. I told him not to panic, and seconds later the dark began to thin, and we stepped out into a cave so vast it could have swallowed the largest cathedral ever built by human hands. Its arched roof soared 100 feet above our heads, dimly lit by hidden air shafts bored through the rock to the surface. Down its full length ran rows of massive white spar stalactite pillars, some 20 feet wide at the base, rising sheer from floor to ceiling, formed one single slow drop of water at a time over thousands of years. On one half-formed pillar we spotted a 3,000-year-old carving of a mummy and an Egyptian god, proof ancient miners from Phoenicia or North Africa had worked here, leaving their mark. The water dripping from the stalactite above hit the half-built column below once every two or three minutes, meaning the stone grew less than an inch every hundred years—nature building her monuments slower than any human empire could ever manage.

Gagool hurried us along before we could explore every tiny, perfect nook of that vast cavern, ignoring the stunning stalagmite formations that looked like lace-carved pulpits and strange frozen beasts, and the tiny dollhouse-sized side nooks that were perfect miniatures of the whole giant cave. We followed her until we reached a square, flat-topped doorway, carved exactly like the entrances to ancient Egyptian temples. “Are you ready to enter the Place of Death, white men?” Gagool cackled, clearly trying to rattle us. Good quoted Macbeth’s “Lead on, Macduff” to play tough, even though his hand was shaking, and Foulata clung so tight to his arm I thought she’d leave bruises. Sir Henry politely gestured for me to go first, and I inwardly cursed him for it, but stepped forward anyway.

The tap of Gagool’s stick echoed down the 20-foot dark passage, and we stepped out into a smaller, dimly lit chamber hollowed straight out of solid rock. My eyes adjusted fast, and I saw it: a long stone table running down the center, and at its head, a colossal 15-foot tall skeleton carved from solid white spar, one great bony hand raised over its head clutching a giant white spear like it was about to strike. Its grinning empty eye sockets stared straight at us, jaws half open as if it was about to speak. I nearly bolted for the exit right then, and even though I’m not a superstitious man, every hair on my head stood straight up. Sir Henry grabbed me by the collar to stop me running, then he saw the full sight too, and started mopping cold sweat off his forehead. Good swore weakly under his breath, Foulata shrieked and wrapped both arms around his neck. Only Gagool laughed, long and loud.

The rest of the table was ringed with 26 more seated figures. The brown shrivelled corpse of Twala, the king Sir Henry had beheaded in battle, sat propped on the middle of the table, his severed head perched on his knees. Water dripped slow and steady from the ceiling onto his neck, coating his whole body in a thin glassy film: the Kukuana royal dead weren’t buried, they were left here under that constant siliceous drip, slowly petrifying, preserved forever in clear spar. The rest of the seated figures were all former Kukuana kings, 27 total stretching back four and a half centuries, their faces still dimly visible through the ice-like stone wrap. That giant carved skeleton of Death, looming over the head of the table, was older than all of them, perfectly anatomically carved by the same ancient craftsmen who’d built the three colossi outside. It was the most awe-inspiring, terrifying sight we had ever laid eyes on. This was the Hall of the Dead.

We were still catching our breath, picking our way through the grisly statues and shrouded dead of the Place of Death, when we noticed Gagool was missing from our huddle. The ancient witch, who could move with the speed of a hunting mongoose when she deigned to, had scrambled up onto the great stone table of the dead before any of us saw her, hobbling straight to where Twala’s corpse sat propped under the cold dripping water. Good snickered under his breath that she was going to check if the dead king was “pickling” properly, but we soon saw it was no trivial errand. She bent down to press her cracked lips to Twala’s icy mouth in a mockery of a loving greeting, then limped slowly back along the table, pausing every few steps to mutter familiar greetings to one shrouded king after another as if she was stopping by old friends’ parlors for tea.

When that strange, horrifying ritual was done, she squatted directly beneath the towering White Death statue, and began to chant low, guttural prayers that made the hair stand up on the back of my neck. We didn’t dare speak above a whisper in that charnel hush, and the sight of that wicked old thing pouring out dark supplications to whatever evil god she worshipped spurred us to speed our search.

“Gagool,” I called softly, “lead us to the treasure chamber now.”

She scrambled down the table as fast as a scuttling beetle, leering up at my face. “My white lords are not afraid, are they?”
“Lead on,” Sir Henry said, grim.

Gagool hobbled around the base of the great White Death statue, set her gourd full of oil down on the cave floor, and leaned against the rock wall. “Here is the chamber. Light your lamp, and enter.”

I pulled the last few remaining matches from our tin, lit the wick of our rush lamp, and stared at the smooth, unbroken solid rock in front of us. No door, no crack, nothing. Gagool cackled, a high, sharp sound that echoed off the stone walls. “I jest not, my lords. Look.”

As I held the lamp higher, we watched a slab of solid rock, nearly ten feet tall and five feet thick, rise slowly up from the floor, vanishing into a hidden cavity carved into the rock above. It must have weighed thirty tons at the least, moving on some ancient counterweight system as smooth as a modern sash window. None of us saw Gagool trigger it, of course — she’d made sure to shield the secret lever from our view. In seconds the whole slab had vanished, leaving a dark, gaping doorway in its wake.

My hands started shaking so hard the lamp guttered. Centuries of legend, the old Portuguese explorer Da Silvestra’s half-rotted map we’d carried across the desert and over the mountains, it all led to this. Were we really about to stand in Solomon’s treasure vault?

“Enter, white men from the stars,” Gagool croaked, stepping into the passage. “But first hear me. The bright stones you seek were dug from the great pit where the Silent Ones stand guard, stored here longer ago than even my memory reaches. Only one other outsider has ever breached this chamber since the builders fled in haste. A white man, over a thousand years ago, came over the mountains — that is the one who sits as the fifth king at the table of the dead.” She pointed back toward the Place of Death. “He came with a local woman, who stumbled by pure chance on the secret of this door. A thousand years of searching would never lead you to that secret, otherwise. The man went inside, filled a goat-skin bag the woman carried for food full of diamonds, and as he fled he grabbed one more large stone to hold in his hand. Then something terrified him so badly he dropped the entire goat-skin bag, and ran for his life with only that one big stone, which the king of that age seized. It is the very same stone you, Macumazahn, took from Twala’s forehead on the battlefield.”

We all froze, breathless. How could she possibly know that?
“Has no one else entered here since?” I asked, peering down the dark passage.
“None. The kings have kept the door’s secret, but none dared step inside. There is a curse: all who enter the chamber will die within a moon, just as that old white man died up in the mountain cave where you found his skeleton, Macumazahn. Enter, and you shall see the goat-skin bag still lying right where he dropped it — and you shall learn for yourselves if the curse holds true.”

She snatched the lamp from my hand and hobbled down the passage ahead of us. Good cursed, charged after her, Foulata following hard on his heels, shivering so hard we could see her fear even in the dim light. The passage was hewn straight out of living rock, and a few yards in we spotted half-height stacks of large stone blocks, left abandoned halfway through walling the treasure chamber off forever. A heap of ancient mortar and two trowels, still sharp-edged, sat untouched beside the blocks. Foulata went deathly pale then, said she could not go another step, her head was spinning. We set her down gently on the half-built wall, left our basket of provisions beside her to keep her company, and pushed on.

Fifteen paces further, we came to an elaborately carved painted wooden door, standing wide open. Slumped across its threshold was a tattered goat-skin bag, heavy with stones that jingled when Gagool nudged it with her foot. “Behold!” she cackled. “Just as I said. The white man fled so fast he left his loot behind.”

Good stooped to lift it, his eyes wide. “By Jove, that’s full of diamonds.”

Sir Henry grabbed the lamp out of Gagool’s hand, shouldered through the open doorway, and held the light high over his head. We crowded in after him, and for a second our breath caught. The whole chamber was hewn straight out of solid rock, stacked floor to domed ceiling with hundreds of top grade elephant tusks — more ivory than would make all three of us wealthy for life, enough to build a whole royal throne of ivory, just as the old Bible tales said Solomon had owned. On the far wall stood twenty red-painted wooden chests, the lids warped and rotted by time. I pushed my hand through the broken lid of the nearest one, and pulled out a handful of gold coins stamped with ancient Hebrew characters no one of us recognized. Thousands of them, stacked to the brim.

“Gagool is right,” the old witch cackled from the doorway, her voice sounding farther away than it had a second before. “The biggest bright stones are in the darkest nook over there. Three stone chests, two sealed, one left open.”

We hurried to the shadowed niche she pointed out, and Sir Henry held the lamp over the open stone chest. A wave of silvery light dazzled us for a moment before our eyes adjusted. It was three-quarters full of uncut diamonds, huge ones, that unmistakable soft soapy texture rubbing off on my palms when I reached in to grab a handful. Millions of pounds worth, at the very least. We were the richest men alive. We started laughing, wild, hysterical, giddy, as Gagool hopped behind us cackling that we could eat the diamonds, drink them, stuff our pockets full to bursting.

That was the last we saw of her for a full ten seconds.

A shriek cut through our laughter, sharp and desperate, coming from back down the passage. Foulata’s voice: “Bougwan! Help! The stone is falling! She stabbed me!”

We ran full tilt down the passage, and the light from the lamp showed us the great stone door was already closing, barely three feet above the ground. Foulata was bleeding from a stab wound to the gut, but she’d wrapped both her arms around Gagool’s waist, holding the squirming old witch back as she tried to crawl out under the falling slab. Gagool bit and scratched, broke free, and threw herself flat on the ground to slip under the narrowing gap. But it was too late. The tonnage of the rock slammed down on her midsection, pinning her to the floor. Her shriek was the most unholy sound any of us had ever heard, before a sick, wet crunch silenced her forever — and the door clicked shut, perfectly sealed, no gap left at all.

We fell on our knees beside Foulata. Her wound was fatal, she was dying fast. “Tell Bougwan I love him,” she gasped to me, her voice faint, as Good held her tight. “I know the sun cannot mate with the dark, and white cannot belong to black, but I have carried a bird in my chest ever since I first saw him, that will fly and search all the stars for him when we are gone. Even if I am still black, and he is still white.” She went limp in Good’s arms, and he wept, great heaving sobs, tears running down his face.

Then Sir Henry went dead still.
“Guys,” he said, quiet and grim. “We are buried alive.”

The truth hit us like a fist to the gut. The only person in the whole world who knew the secret to opening that door was Gagool — crushed to pulp under thirty tons of solid rock. There was no lever on the inside, no catch, no way to move five feet of living rock, not even with a hundred men pushing. We searched every inch of the passage walls, scraped at every crack, found nothing. The curse Gagool had joked about was no joke. She’d planned this trap for us from the very first day we stepped foot in Kukuanaland, determined to watch the three white men she hated slowly starve to death surrounded by all the treasure in the world.

We grabbed our basket of provisions, carried Foulata’s still, gentle body back into the treasure chamber, and laid her down beside the chests of gold. Our rations added up to four tiny portions of dried biltong each, two quarts of water total — barely enough to stretch two days, at most. The lamp began to gutter, the last of the oil running out. It flared bright for one last second, casting light over all the stacked ivory, the heaps of diamonds glinting in the dark, Foulata’s peaceful face, and the three of us gaunt, trapped, staring at the fortune that had become our tomb.

Then the flame died, and we were left in total, absolute blackness.

I have no words to paint the full, bone-deep horror of the night that swallowed us. Mercifully, exhaustion dulled the edge a little—even men marked for death cannot fight their own bodies forever, and sleep tugged at the edges of our consciousness when we least expected it. But I never slept long.

Set aside the cold, crawling terror of the fate waiting for us—even the bravest man alive would have quailed at the thought of slow, dark starvation miles under the earth, and I never pretended to be brave. The silence itself was a torture no man could have borne for long. You may have laid awake at home, thinking the quiet of midnight oppressive, but you can never know what perfect stillness feels like: a heavy, tangible thing that presses against your eardrums, that smothers every small noise you take for granted on the surface. Up above ground there is always a faint hum—wind in grass, a distant bird, the faint thrum of living things, no matter how quiet the hour. But here there was nothing.

We were buried in the guts of a snow-capped peak. Thousands of feet over our heads, gales ripped across white drifts, but not a single breath of that wind reached us. A long stone tunnel and five feet of solid rock separated us even from the silent chamber of the dead, where Foulata lay cold and still. Not the roar of every artillery gun on earth could have cut through that stone to reach us. We were cut off from every echo of the living world—we were men already in their graves.

The irony of it gnawed at me. All around us, stacked in chests, lay enough diamonds to pay off a small nation’s entire national debt, enough to build a whole fleet of ironclad warships. We would have traded every last carat without a second thought for the faintest crack of a door, for a single chance to crawl back to the air. Soon, we knew, we would happily barter those same treasures for a crust of biltong, a sip of water. After that, we would give all of it away just for the pain to end. The wealth men spend their whole lives chasing is worth nothing at all when you are trapped in a vault, dying of thirst.

The hours dragged on, no sun, no moon, only the faint tick of my watch I could feel against my wrist.

“Good,” Sir Henry’s voice boomed out of the dark so sharp it made us all jump, cutting through the dead silence like a knife, “how many matches do you have left in that box?”

“Eight, Curtis.”

“Strike one. Let’s check the time.”

The tiny flame flared so bright after hours of total blackness it nearly blinded all three of us. It was five o’clock. Far above our heads, the dawn would be blushing pink on the snow drifts, the morning wind stirring mist in the mountain hollows, and we would never see it.

“We ought to eat something, keep our strength up,” I suggested.

“What’s the point?” Good’s voice was hoarse. “The sooner we’re done the better.”

“While there’s life, there’s hope,” Sir Henry said, steady as ever.

We forced ourselves to chew a little dry meat, sip from our dwindling water skins, and more time slipped by. Then Sir Henry suggested we make our way to the sealed stone door and yell, on the one in a million chance someone outside would hear. Good, who’d spent decades at sea and had a piercing, far-carrying yell trained to cut through storm winds, stumbled down the passage and howled. I never heard such a racket. It might as well have been a mosquito buzzing for all the good it did. No sound traveled through that five feet of rock. He stumbled back thirsty, drank half his remaining water, and we agreed to stop yelling—we couldn’t waste the moisture.

We slumped back against the chests of useless diamonds, trapped in that terrible, motionless waiting, and I broke. I rested my head on Sir Henry’s broad shoulder and burst into tears. I could hear Good sniffling on the other side, swearing hoarsely at himself for crying like a child.

Sir Henry was gentle as a nurse tending two sick babies. He forgot his own terror entirely, telling us stories of explorers who’d been buried in avalanches and trapped in cave-ins, who’d fought their way out against all odds. When that didn’t cheer us, he pointed out that death came for all men eventually, that dying of exhaustion would be fast and painless. Then, quiet and a little shy, he suggested we pray to a higher power, and I did so with more fervor than I ever had in my life. That man’s character was as solid as the mountain around us: quiet, unshakable, good.

What felt like another full day of waiting dragged by, no light, no sound. When I struck another match, it was seven o’clock. We ate the last of our rations, and that was when the thought hit me.

“Wait a minute,” I said. “How is the air in here still fresh? It’s thick, but it’s not stale. We should have suffocated hours ago.”

Good shot straight up, so fast he bashed his head on a chest. “Great heavens— I never thought of that. That sealed stone door is airtight. There has to be a draught somewhere.”

That tiny spark of hope lit a fire under all of us. We dropped to our hands and knees immediately, groping across the rock floor for the faintest stir of moving air. I ran my hand straight into something cold and soft a minute later: Foulata’s dead face. My heart lurched.

We crawled on for an hour, bashing our heads on ivory tusks and chest corners, scraped raw, until Sir Henry and I collapsed, ready to give up. But Good kept crawling, muttering that it was better than sitting still waiting to die.

“Hey, you two,” he called out a minute later, his voice tight with excitement. “Come over here.”

We scrambled to his side as fast as we could.

“Quatermain, put your hand right here where mine is. Feel that?”

“I think— I think I feel cool air coming up through the floor.”

“Stand back.” He stamped hard on the rock beneath his feet. The sound rang hollow.

My hands shook so bad I nearly dropped the last three matches we had left. The tiny flame showed we were in the far corner of the vault, a spot we’d never properly checked in our earlier frantic searches. There was a thin, hairline crack in the rock floor, and set perfectly flush with the stone, carved from the same solid rock, was a stone ring.

We were too breathless to speak. Good pulled out his pocket knife, the one with the small hook for picking stones out of horses’ hooves, and scraped around the edge of the ring, working the hook under it carefully. The stone hadn’t rusted shut in all the centuries it had lain there, as an iron ring would have. He pulled it up until it stood straight upright.

We all took turns heaving on it, straining every muscle, and the stone slab wouldn’t budge. Finally Good looped his neat black silk handkerchief— he’d never taken it off, even through all the mess— through the ring, and all three of us braced our feet. Sir Henry put his enormous, explorer’s strength behind the pull, his back muscles cracking with the effort, and Good and I hauled for all we were worth.

“Heave! It’s giving!” Sir Henry gasped. There was a long, grinding scrape of stone on stone, a rush of cold fresh air that hit us full in the faces, and all three of us went tumbling backwards as the heavy flagstone slammed down on top of our legs.

“Light a match, Quatermain,” Sir Henry panted, once we’d scrambled to our feet. “Carefully, now.”

I did. Staring up at us from the dark hole under the floor was the first step of a cut stone stair.

“Where does it go?” Good asked.

“Down, for one thing. We follow it. Trust to Providence,” Sir Henry said. “Grab the last of the biltong and the water skins, Quatermain. We might need it.”

I crawled back to our old spot by the diamond chests to grab them. For twenty four hours I’d barely thought of the gems— the very sight of them made me sick, they were the reason we were trapped here. But old habits die hard. I stuffed every pocket of my old shooting coat and my trousers full of the biggest, clearest diamonds from the nearest chest, then grabbed Foulata’s empty woven basket and filled it heaped to the brim with stones too.

“Hey, you two! You want to grab some diamonds before we go?” I called. “My pockets and the basket are full.”

“Come on, Quatermain, hang the diamonds!” Sir Henry laughed, half exasperated. “I never want to see another gem for the rest of my life.”

Good didn’t answer. I knew he was kneeling beside Foulata’s body, saying his last goodbyes to the girl who’d loved him enough to die for him. If you’d spent twenty eight hours trapped in that dark vault, starving and thirsty, you’d never waste energy hauling heavy diamonds into an unknown tunnel, either. If it wasn’t for my old explorer habit of never leaving something valuable behind if there was even a sliver of a chance I could carry it out, I would have left them too.

Sir Henry stepped onto the first stair, leading the way. We counted fifteen steps down before his feet hit flat ground. “It’s a tunnel,” he said. “Follow me.”

Good went next, I came last hefting the heavy diamond basket, and I lit the second to last match. By its faint light we could see a narrow stone gallery stretching off left and right, straight out from the stairwell. The flame burned my fingers and went out before we could see more. But we’d seen it: when the match was lit, the draught of air had blown the flame hard to the left.

“Go against the draught,” Good said. “The air’s being pulled in from outside.” We felt our way along the wall, one hand on rock, the other testing the ground in front of every step, and left that cursed treasure vault behind us forever. No living soul would likely find that vault again, but if they did, they’d see our open chests of jewels, our empty lamp, and Foulata’s bones, waiting there in the dark.

We stumbled for what felt like hours through the tunnels, one gallery branching off into the next, winding like a maze. We figured they must be the old ancient diamond mine workings the Kukuana people had never known existed, tunnelled centuries before for the ore veins. By the time we ran out of the last of our food and water, our throats as dry as lime kilns, we were certain we’d just traded one dark grave for another.

Then I heard it. A faint, faraway murmur. The others froze, and they heard it too. After all those hours of perfect dead silence, the soft sound of running water was the most beautiful thing I’d ever heard.

“Come on!” Good yelped, and we stumbled as fast as we could toward the sound. I almost dropped Foulata’s basket a dozen times, but I hung onto it anyway— might as well die rich as poor, I thought. Good was leading when we got right up to it, and he slipped. Splash. A yell echoed out of the dark.

“Good! Where are you?” we screamed, terrified.

“I’m alright!” His voice came out choked. “I caught hold of a rock. Light a match!”

I lit the very last match. Its tiny glow showed a wild, rushing underground river cutting right across the tunnel, far too wide to see the far bank, and Good clinging to a jagged rock sticking out of the current.

“Stand clear, I’m swimming for it!” he shouted. He stroked hard through the racing water, grabbed Sir Henry’s outstretched hand, and we hauled him back onto dry stone, dripping and gasping. That river ran as fast as a mill race, and there was no bottom— if he’d missed that rock, he’d have been swept away into total darkness forever.

We drank our fill of the cold, fresh river water, scrubbed the grime off our faces, then turned back from the bank to pick a new gallery. Sir Henry led us, half delirious with exhaustion, stumbling for what felt like another hour. Then he stopped so abruptly we all bumped into his back.

“Look,” he whispered. “Is my mind playing tricks on me, or is that light?”

Far ahead of us, no bigger than a cottage window pane, was a faint, silvery glimmer. No eye that hadn’t stared at total pitch black for 30 hours could have spotted it. We pushed forward, gasping, and the light got brighter. The rock walls ended, and the tunnel turned to packed earth, narrowing smaller and smaller, until it was no bigger than a fox’s den.

We crawled through it on our bellies, squeezing through the narrow dirt gap, and suddenly we were out. The blessed stars blazed right over our heads, fresh mountain air filled our lungs, and we went tumbling head over heels down a steep grassy slope, rolling through wet brush and soft loam. The diamond basket caught on a bush and stopped me. I sat up and yelled as loud as I could.

An answering shout came from down the slope, where Sir Henry had come to a stop on level ground. Good was a little further down, wedged in a forked tree root, bruised and shaken but fine. We all collapsed on the grass, and cried like children for joy. We were alive. The dawn was blushing rose pink on the snow peaks far above us, the same dawn we’d been certain we’d never see again.

When the light got bright enough to see each other, we laughed at the state of us: gaunt, hollow-eyed, caked head to toe in mud and dust, scraped and bleeding, and Good’s monocle was still stuck firm in his eye. He’d never once taken it out, not through the dark, not through the river plunge, not through rolling down that slope.

We dragged ourselves up the steep clay slope of the great pit that sat before the hidden cave mouth, hauling ourselves up by grass roots and scrub branches, until we hit the main trail on the far side from the three giant stone colossi that guarded the cave entrance. A hundred yards off, a fire burned in front of a ring of huts, with dark shapes gathered around it.

We stumbled toward them, supporting each other, stopping every few steps to catch our breath. One man looked up, saw us, and screamed, falling flat on his face in terror.

“Infadoos! Infadoos, it is us, your friends!”

The old warrior scrambled to his feet, ran to us, staring wild eyed like he was seeing ghosts. He threw himself at Sir Henry’s knees, weeping so hard he could barely speak.

“Oh my lords! My lords! You have come back from the dead! Come back from the grave!”

Ten days after that hellish morning we clawed our way out of the treasure mountain, we were back in our old quarters at Loo. Strange as it sounds, we'd walked away from that nightmare with barely a scratch between us—save for my stubbly shock of hair, which had bleached three full shades grayer the second that rock slammed shut behind us, and Good, who was never quite the same after Foulata died. The poor girl's death hit him harder than any spear wound I'd ever seen him take.

As a man who's knocked about the world long enough to see where all roads lead, I'd be lying if I said her passing wasn't a lucky thing for all of us. She wasn't some ordinary village girl: she was stately, sharp, beautiful as any queen, and there was no way a entanglement between Good and a Kukuana princess would have ended well. She knew it, too. "Can the sun mate with the darkness," she'd said once, "or the white with the black?" No amount of kindness or wit could have untangled that mess.

We never so much as set foot near Solomon's treasure chamber again. After two full days of resting the ache out of our bones, we climbed down into the great pit to hunt for the narrow hole we'd crawled through to escape the mountain—no luck. The rain had fallen and wiped every trace of our tracks clean, and the steep sides of that vast pit were pocked with hundreds of ant-bear and wild animal burrows. There was no telling which one had been our salvation.

The day before we set out back for Loo, we wandered the stalactite caves one more time, a restless, morbid curiosity pulling us even to the Chamber of the Dead. We ducked under the sharp spear of the White Death and stared at the five feet of solid rock that had sealed us in with all that uncounted wealth, Gagool's crushed, mangled remains, and Foulata, lying dead just on the other side. We could not find a single seam or join in that rock no matter how we prodded and pried. The secret of the sliding door was lost to us forever, that massive, inscrutable bit of ancient workmanship unlike anything the modern world could ever build.

Eventually we gave up, disgusted. Even if that rock had suddenly lifted before our eyes, I doubt any of us would have mustered the nerve to step over Gagool's broken body and go back in, not even for all the diamonds in the world. I could have wept leaving all that hoard behind—tens of millions of pounds worth of the finest gems ever piled in one place, likely the greatest treasure in human history. But nothing short of dynamite was moving that slab. Some far-off explorer, a hundred or a thousand years from now, might stumble on the lost "Open Sesame" to the cave, but I doubt it. Those gems will sit locked in their three stone coffers with Foulata's bones, cold and dark, till the end of the world.

We need not have been so glum. I'd had the good sense, you see, to stuff every wide pocket of my old shooting coat and trousers full of loose diamonds before the rock trapped us in that chamber, plus we'd stuffed Foulata's woven basket so full it was bulging, even with the space taken up by our water bottle. A good dozen of the largest stones fell out of my coat pockets when we rolled head over heels down the pit slope, but we still had an unimaginable amount left: ninety-three big stones between 70 and 200 carats apiece, enough to make us all rich beyond any of our wildest dreams, no need to even tell a soul where we found them. We'd not done half badly at all.

When we rode into Loo, Ignosi welcomed us like long-lost brothers. He was well, busy as a bee consolidating his power, rebuilding the regiments that had taken the worst of the casualties in the fight against Twala. He listened to every word of our story, riveted, but when we told him old Gagool was crushed to death under the falling rock, he grew very still.

"Come here," he called to a white-haired ancient induna who sat on the edge of the council circle out of earshot. The old man hobbled over, saluted, and sat. "You are old," Ignosi said.
"Ay, my king. Your father's father and I were born the very same day."
"Tell me—when you were a small boy, did you know Gagool the witch doctress?"
"Ay, my king."
"Was she young then, as you were?"
"Not so. She looked exactly as she did yesterday, and exactly as she looked when my own great-grandfather was a boy. Dried up, ugly, full of nothing but wickedness."
"She is dead now."
"Thank the gods, then. The ancient curse is lifted from the land. I go, my king." The old man bowed and vanished.

"You see, my brothers," Ignosi said to us, "she was no ordinary woman. I rejoice she is gone. She would have left you to die in that dark cave, and sooner or later she would have found a way to kill me too—just as she killed my father, and set Twala on the throne he loved so much. Go on with your tale! I have never heard anything half so strange."

When I finished recounting our escape, I took a breath and broke the news we'd all agreed to share. "Ignosi, the time has come for us to say farewell. We want to see our own land again. Remember your promise to us, now that you are a mighty king: rule justly, respect your laws, never put a man to death without cause. If you do that, you will prosper. Tomorrow at dawn, send us an escort to guide us across the mountains. Is that not right, O king?"

Ignosi covered his face with his hands and was silent for a long minute. "My heart is split in two," he said at last, his voice thick. "What have I done to you three—Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—that you would leave me desolate? You stood at my side through rebellion and battle. Would you abandon me in my hour of victory? Name whatever you want! Wives? Pick any maiden in the land. Homes and cattle? The entire kingdom is yours, as far as your eyes can see. Elephants to hunt, regiments to command? I will give you anything. Anything but you leaving."

"Nay, Ignosi," I said, "we want none of these things. We only want to go home to our own people."

Ignosi's eyes flashed bitter. "So I see now! You care more for those bright shining stones than you care for me, your friend. You have the gems, and now you will sail across the black moving water to sell them and be rich, as all white men want to be. Cursed be those white stones, I say. Any man who steps foot in that cave of Death after you will die. White men, go then."

I laid my hand on his arm. "Ignosi, when you wandered in Zululand, among the white men of Natal, did your heart not ache for the land your mother told you of? The place you were born, the land that was yours by right?"

He nodded, softening. "It was so, Macumazahn."
"Then our hearts ache the same way for our homes, just as yours ached for yours."

Silence hung in the air for a long while before Ignosi spoke again, his voice steady. "You are always wise, Macumazahn. The bird that flies in the air does not want to run along the ground. The white man does not want to live level with the black man, or dwell in our kraals. You must go, and my heart will be sore, for we will be dead to each other—no news will ever pass between our lands again. But listen to me, all three of you. No other white man will ever cross the mountains. I will not let traders come with their guns and their gin, to make my people soft and wicked. I will not let praying men come to stir my people up against my rule, to pave a path for more white men to follow. If a white man comes to my gates, I will send him back. If a hundred come, I will push them back. If armies come, I will fight them with every spear I have, and they will not prevail. I will fill in the pit to the treasure caves, break the white columns, choke every passage with boulders, so no man will ever find those shining stones. But for you three? The path will always be open. You are dearer to me than any living soul."

He paused, his voice cracking. "Farewell, my brave brothers. I will not see you again, for my heart cannot bear it. I will pass a law: your names—Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—shall be *hlonipa*, sacred, like the names of our dead kings. Any man who speaks your name will die. Your memory will live in this land forever, unspoken, for generations. Go now, before I weep like a woman. When you are old, huddled by your fires back in your cold land when the sun no longer gives you heat, you will remember how we stood shoulder to shoulder in the battle we planned, how we killed Twala together, how we won this kingdom. Farewell for ever, my lords and my friends." He threw the corner of his kaross over his head to hide his tear-streaked face, and we left him in silence.

At dawn the next day we rode out of Loo, escorted by our old friend Infadoos, who was heartbroken to see us go, and a full regiment of Buffaloes. Even that early, the whole main street was lined with thousands of Kukuana people, cheering and throwing wild flowers before our horses, blessing us for ridding their land of Twala the tyrant. It was so affecting it made my throat tight, the sort of kindness you never expect from native folk you've fought alongside.

One silly incident broke the heavy mood, though, and gave us something to laugh about. Just before we reached the edge of the town, a pretty young girl with a bouquet of white lilies ran up to Good, who the whole country seemed to adore for his funny eye-glass and single side-whisker. She curtsied, handed him the flowers, and said she had one small boon to ask.

"Speak," Good said, confused.
"Let my lord show his servant his famous beautiful white legs, that I may remember them all my days, and tell my children of them. I have travelled four days' walk just to see them—every person in the land speaks of how white and perfect they are."

"Be damned if I will!" Good spluttered, bright red.
"Come on, old man," Sir Henry laughed, clapping him on the back. "You can't be rude to a lady."
"It's indecent!" Good protested, but eventually he caved, and hiked his trousers up to his knee for her. A roar of delighted gasps went up from all the women watching, and he had to walk with his knee exposed the whole way out of the town before they'd stop staring. Good never got half that level of admiration for his eye-glass or his false teeth. His legs, though? The Kukuana people talked about them for years after, no doubt.

As we rode, Infadoos told us about a secret northern pass over the mountains, far away from Solomon's Great Road. Two years prior, a band of Kukuana ostrich hunters had climbed down it to chase wild ostriches for their plumes, and stumbled on a large, fertile, water-rich oasis far out in the desert. It was that very path Ignosi's mother must have taken when she fled the kingdom with baby Ignosi decades earlier—no wonder we'd never been able to figure out how a woman with a small child could have survived the brutal main desert crossing. This path was easy, and the oasis gave them shelter.

Four days of easy riding later, we stood at the crest of the mountains, staring down at the endless waves of sand stretching out 2000 feet below us, north of the two giant peaks of Sheba's Breasts. At dawn the next day, we reached the edge of the sheer chasm we would climb down to the desert floor, and there we said our goodbyes to Infadoos. The old warrior almost wept, rambling about how he would never see a fight like the one we won ever again, except in his dreams. Good, fumbling in his pocket, handed him a spare eye-glass as a souvenir. The old warrior was delighted, and after three tries managed to screw it right into his eye. Nothing has ever looked more absurd than that grizzled old chief in his leopard skin cloak and ostrich plumes, squinting through a little monocle. He was sure it would make him the most respected man in the whole kingdom.

We shook his hand, took the thunderous royal salute from the Buffalo regiment, and started climbing down the chasm. It was a rough, dangerous scramble, but by sundown we were all safe on the desert floor. That night as we sat by our fire staring up at the steep cliffs above us, Sir Henry laughed and said, "I've had far worse times than the last month, lads. I've never had any weirder ones, though." Good sighed, and said he almost wished he could go back. As for me? I thought, all's well that ends well. I've had a hundred close shaves in my life, but none half as close as that one.

We trudge across the desert for three days, our guides carrying plenty of water, and by the afternoon of the third day we could see the dark green treeline of the oasis on the horizon. By sundown, we were standing on soft grass, listening to the gurgle of running water, at the edge of that forgotten paradise no white man had ever seen before us.

This is the strangest twist of fate in all our wild, sun-scorched African adventures—proof that the world works in far more wondrous, unguessable ways than any man could dream up.

I was tramping a good twenty yards ahead of Sir Henry and Good, following the thin, gurgling stream that trickles out of the hidden oasis before vanishing completely into the ravenous desert sand. The heat hung thick enough to chew, and I was half in a daze when I stopped dead, rubbed my stinging eyes hard, and thought the sun must have addled my wits. There, perched on a grassy bank under the dappled shade of a wild fig tree, facing out over the water, was a neat little hut woven from withes and grass in the Kafir style, but with a full-sized wooden door instead of the usual tiny, bee-hole entry you find on native huts out in the wilds.

“What in tarnation is a hut doing all the way out here?” I muttered to myself. No hunter would ever wander this far off known trails, much less settle down here. Even as the thought crossed my mind, the hut door creaked open. Out limped a white man, clad head to toe in sewn animal skins, his face half hidden behind a huge, matted black beard.

I stared until my eyes burned. The two men behind me stopped short when I called out, “Hey, you two—tell me I’m not seeing things. Is that a white man, or have I fried my brains in this heat?”

Sir Henry and Good both squinted, then the bearded stranger let out a roar of shock and joy, hobbling as fast as his lame leg would carry him toward us, before crumpling unconscious right at our feet. Sir Henry cleared the distance in one bound, his voice cracking. “Great God in heaven—*that’s my brother George*!”

At the sound of the commotion, a second figure in skins bolted out of the hut, a rifle held high. When he spotted me, he screamed so loud the birds took off from the fig tree. “Macumazahn! Don’t you know me, Baas? I’m Jim the hunter! I lost the note you gave me to pass to Mr. Curtis nearly two years back, and we’ve been stranded here ever since.” He fell to my feet, rolling on the sand sobbing so hard he could barely breathe for happiness.

“You reckless scoundrel,” I said, clapping him on the shoulder instead of giving him the sjambok lashing he deserved.

George Curtis had come round by then, and he and Sir Henry were pumping each other’s hands so hard their knuckles went white, too choked up to get more than half a sentence out at a time. Whatever old spat they’d fallen out over years before (I’d wager it was some lady, though I never asked) was long forgotten, washed away by two years of fear and no hope of ever seeing each other again.

“I thought you were dead,” Sir Henry said at last, clapping his brother so hard on the back he nearly fell over. “I trekked all the way across Solomon’s Mountains to find you. I’d given up every last scrap of hope, and now I find you camped out in this desert like some scrawny old aasvogel.”

George laughed, his voice rough and halting from two years of barely speaking to anyone but Jim. “I tried to cross the range almost two years ago. But once I got here, a loose boulder fell right on my leg and crushed it. I couldn’t go forward, and I couldn’t make the trek back to civilization, either.”

That night, huddled around our campfire, he told us the full story: how he’d left Sitanda’s Kraal two years prior to hunt for the fabled diamond mines, how Jim had lost my warning note before he could ever hand it over. He’d followed native advice to take the steep, ladder-like mountain pass we’d just scrambled down (a far better route than the one marked on old Dom Silvestra’s tattered map) only to get lost in the desert, half dead of thirst, until he stumbled on this hidden oasis. He was sitting on the stream’s edge the day they arrived, Jim scrambling up the bank above him to raid a stingless bee’s honey nest, when Jim dislodged a ton-weight boulder that crushed his right leg to pulp.

They’d survived two whole years on game that came to the oasis to drink at night, shooting antelope and trapping wild ostrich in pitfalls, eating the meat and tanning the hides to replace their worn-out clothes. “Last night we finally agreed Jim would leave me at dawn to try to trek back to Sitanda’s Kraal for help,” George said, shaking his head like he still couldn’t believe we were there. “I never thought he’d make it, and I’d have died here alone. And then you three show up, out of the middle of the empty desert. It’s the kindest, most impossible thing that ever happened to a man.”

We sat up till nearly dawn, telling him of our own adventures: the witch Gagool, the savage king Twala, the hidden cave stacked with uncut diamonds, the trap that nearly sealed us inside forever. When I pulled a handful of the rough stones out of my pocket to show him, he whistled. “Well, you lads got something for your trouble besides dragging my useless hide out of here.”

Sir Henry laughed, and explained our bargain that the diamonds belonged to me and Good. But after a quick word between the two of us, we told him we’d not take the whole haul—we insisted he take a full third share for himself, and the third share that was meant for Sir Henry would go straight to George, who’d suffered more than any of us chasing the mines. We wore him down until he agreed, though we kept the full arrangement secret from George for months after.

The trek back across the desert to Sitanda’s Kraal was brutal, slow going, with us taking turns supporting George’s weak leg that kept spitting out shards of broken bone. But we made it, eventually, and found our old cache of rifles and trade goods completely untouched, much to the disgust of the greedy old native overseer who’d long written us off as dead and planned to steal every last thing. Six months later, we were all safe and sound at my little cottage on the Berea, outside Durban, where I’m sitting now writing this down. It’s the strangest adventure I’ve ever lived through, in all my decades of hunting across southern Africa.

I’d just put the last full stop on the last line of this page when a Kafir runner came up my avenue of orange trees, carrying a letter tucked in the cleft of a stick from the nearest outpost post. It was from Sir Henry, and I’ll print it exactly as he wrote it:

> October 1st, 1884
> Brayley Hall, Yorkshire
>
> My Dear Quatermain,
>
> We all made it back to England safe and sound: George, Good, and I. We docked at Southampton, and you’d barely recognize Good the day after we hit London—shaved so clean you could see your reflection in his chin, brand new frock coat that fits like a second skin, shiny new monocle he’s absurdly proud of. I dragged him for a walk in Hyde Park last week, ran into a crowd of old friends, and told them the whole story about his famous bleached white river-bleached legs. He’s been absolutely furious ever since, especially now some mischief-maker printed the whole silly story in a society paper.
>
> To business: Good and I took all the diamonds to Streeter’s to get them valued. I daren’t even tell you the number they quoted, it’s so staggeringly huge. They say no one’s ever seen a haul of uncut stones like ours hit the market before, all but the two largest gems are perfect water, just as fine as the best Brazilian diamonds out there. They won’t buy the whole lot off us outright, said it would crash the market if we dumped all those stones at once—they recommend selling them slowly, a handful a year, over a whole decade or more. Even for a tiny fraction of the haul, they offered us one hundred and eighty thousand pounds.
>
> You have to come home to England, Quatermain, to sort all this out, especially if you’re dead set on giving that third share of the diamonds to George, who doesn’t have two pennies to rub together. Good’s no help at all right now, he’s too busy primping and preening. He’s still broken up over Foulata, too—said he hasn’t seen a single woman here in England with half her grace, or half her sweetness.
>
> There’s a house for sale a mile down the road from me that’s perfect for you, plenty of space to hang your hunting trophies, room for you to putter about. You’ve more than earned your rest, you old rogue, and you’ve got more than enough money now. Come stay for Christmas—Good’s coming, George is coming, and your boy Harry’s down here too. I took him out shooting last week, he accidentally put a pellet right through my calf, dug it out with a penknife, and then had the cheek to say every shooting party ought to bring a medical student along for first aid. He’s a cool hand, that lad.
>
> We’ve refused to tell anyone our whole story till you write it up properly, no one will believe us otherwise. The sooner you sail the better.
>
> Goodbye, old friend. I know you’ll come.
>
> Yours ever,
> Henry Curtis
>
> P.S. The tusks of the great bull elephant that killed poor Khiva are hung in my hall right now, above the pair of buffalo horns you gave me, and they look magnificent. The axe I chopped Twala’s head off with is nailed above my writing desk. I wish we could have carried those chain mail suits out of the cave with us. Don’t you lose that little basket Foulata wove that you carried the diamonds out in!

Today’s Tuesday. There’s a steamer sailing for Southampton on Friday. I think I’ll take Henry at his word, book my passage, and sail for England. It’s high time I saw my boy Harry again, caught up with my old comrades, and saw this whole strange story printed proper, so no one can ever call it a lie.

Allan Quatermain
