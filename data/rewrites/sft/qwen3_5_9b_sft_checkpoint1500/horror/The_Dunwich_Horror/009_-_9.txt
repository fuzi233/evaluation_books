Friday morning, Professor Armitage, Dr. Henry Arthur Rice, and Mr. Morgan departed by automobile for Dunwich, reaching the desolate village around mid-afternoon. Though the air was unseasonably mild, a suffocating, silent dread hung over the landscape—a palpable foreboding clinging to the bizarre, dome-shaped hills and the abyssal, shadow-choked ravines of the blighted area. Occasionally, against the pale sky, the gaunt circles of standing stones atop the mountains pierced the gloom. The atmosphere within Osborn's store was thick with a hushed, superstitious terror; word had already spread of the total annihilation of the Elmer Frye household. For hours, the trio circled the town, interrogating the terrified locals and witnessing firsthand the horrors of the scene: the charred, sticky ruins of the Frye homestead, the blasphemous, impossible tracks etched into the earth, the mutilated livestock of Seth Bishop, and vast swathes of vegetation torn and twisted as if by some titanic, unseen force. To Armitage, the path winding up and down Sentinel Hill radiated a catastrophic significance, drawing his gaze repeatedly to the sinister, altar-like stone crowning the summit.

Eventually, learning that a contingent of State Police from Aylesbury had arrived earlier to investigate the initial reports of the tragedy, the scholars resolved to locate them and exchange information. However, locating the officers proved more difficult than anticipated. Despite the testimony of five men who had ridden together, their vehicle sat abandoned and empty near the Frye ruins. The villagers, all of whom had spoken with the police, initially appeared as bewildered as their guests. Then, Old Sam Hutchins, a local elder, suddenly went pale, nudged Fred Farr, and pointed frantically toward the damp, yawning hollow of Cold Spring Glen.

"Gawd," he gasped, his voice trembling, "I tol' 'em not ter go down into the glen! An' I nevah thought nobody'd go down there with them tracks... an' that smell... an' the whippoorwills screechin' down thar in the middlin' o' the day..."

A chill, primal shudder seized both the townsfolk and the outsiders. Every ear seemed to stretch, straining for a sound that wasn't yet there. Armitage, now fully confronted with the reality of the monstrosity, trembled with the crushing weight of his perceived duty. Nightfall approached rapidly—the hour when the mountainous blasphemy would resume its eldritch wanderings. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris...* The old librarian silently recited the Latin formulas he had committed to memory, clutching the sheet of paper bearing the alternatives he had failed to commit. He verified that his electric flashlight functioned, while Rice, standing nearby, retrieved a metal insecticide sprayer from a valise. Meanwhile, Morgan, disregarding Rice's warnings that physical weapons were futile, unslung a heavy big-game rifle.

Having studied Wilbur Whateley's diaries, Armitage understood precisely the nature of the manifestation awaiting them, yet he carefully withheld any specific details from the locals, hoping to contain the horror without exposing the world to its full, terrible truth. As the shadows lengthened, the villagers began to scatter, retreating indoors despite the evident futility of locks and bolts against a force capable of bending timber and crushing homes. They dismissed the scholars' plan to station guards near the glen, shaking their heads with a grim resignation that suggested they doubted the watchers would survive the night.

That night, the earth groaned beneath the hills, and the whippoorwills sang a dirge of warning. Periodically, a wind sweeping from Cold Spring Glen carried a trace of an indescribable, ancient rot—an odor familiar to the three watchers from their previous encounter with the dying entity buried beneath the rubble fifteen and a half years prior. Yet, the expected terror remained absent. Whatever dwelt in the glen was biding its time, and Armitage advised his colleagues that attempting an assault in the dark would be suicide.

Daybreak arrived wan and gray, accompanied by a persistent drizzle. Heavy clouds piled ominously over the northern horizon, promising a tempest. Stood beneath the shelter of a solitary, undestroyed outbuilding of the Frye estate, the men from Arkham debated whether to wait for the storm to pass or take the offensive immediately. The rain intensified, turning to deluges, while distant thunder rolled and sheet lightning fractured the sky. Finally, a forked bolt of lightning descended directly toward the accursed glen, illuminating the darkness with a sickly white flash. The sky sank into a bruised, oppressive blackness, and the watchers prayed for a brief, sharp storm followed by clearing skies.

Less than an hour later, amidst a squall that rendered visibility nearly nil, a confused cacophony of voices erupted down the road. Soon after, a ragged company of more than a dozen men burst into view—running, shouting, and whimpering in hysterical panic. One man at the forefront began to sob, his words coalescing into a coherent nightmare.

"Oh, my Gawd, my Gawd!" the voice choked out, raw with fear. "It's a-goin' agin! _An' this time by day_! It's aout—it's aout an' a-movin' this very minute, an' only the Lord knows when it'll be on us all!"

Another man took up the tale, recounting how Zeb Whateley, hearing the phone ring, had connected with Mrs. Corey, whose husband lived near the junction. She reported that her hired boy, Luther, had been driving cows home from the storm following the great lightning strike. Luther claimed to have seen all the trees bending at the mouth of the glen—on the opposite side—and to have smelled that same awful, tarry stench he had encountered the Monday before. According to Mrs. Corey, Luther heard a swishing, lapping sound, deeper and more resonant than any tree or bush could produce. Suddenly, the trees along the road began to push sideways, accompanied by a dreadful stomping and splashing in the mud. Crucially, Luther admitted he saw nothing solid, only the bending foliage and underbrush.

Further ahead, near where Bishop's Brook crossed under the railroad track, Luther heard a terrifying creaking and straining, as if wood itself were cracking and splitting apart. Yet, he saw no creature, only the bending trees. When the swishing sound drifted further, heading toward the road leading to Wizard Whateley's farm and Sentinel Hill, Luther summoned the courage to approach the spot where he first heard the noise. The ground was a churn of mud and water, the rain erasing tracks as fast as they formed—but starting from the glen's mouth, where the trees had bowed, there remained faint impressions of footprints massive as barrels, identical to those seen the week prior.

"The trouble ain't the tracks," the first speaker interjected, his voice rising in urgency. "That was just the start. Zeb was calling everyone, and we were all listening when a call from Seth Bishop's broke in. His housekeeper, Sally, was screaming like she'd gone mad. She said she'd just seen the trees bending beside the track, making a mushy sound, like an elephant puffing and trudging, heading straight for the house. Then she spoke of that fearful smell again, saying her boy Chancey was screaming because it smelled exactly like what he smelled near the Whateley ruins last Monday. And the dogs? They were all barking and whining, terrified.

"And then she let out a terrible yell, saying the shed down the road had just caved in, as if the storm had blown it over, though the wind wasn't strong enough to do that! Everyone was listening, gasping, when she yelled again. She said the front-yard picket fence had just crumpled up, though there was no sign of what could have done it. Then you could hear Chancey and old Seth yelling, two distinct voices, while Sally shrieked that something heavy had struck the house—not lightning, nothin' natural, but something heavy keepin' itself launchin' against the front, over and over, though you couldn't see anything outside the windows. And then... and then..."

Fear etched lines deep into every face. Shaken to his core, Armitage managed only to prompt the speaker.

"And then... Sally yelled, 'Help! The house is cav'in in!' And on the wire, we heard a terrible crashing, a whole flock of screams... just like when Elmer Frye's place was taken, only worse."

The man fell silent, and another spoke.

"That's it—all quiet. Not a sound, not a squeak, over the phone afterward. Just still-as-dead. Folks like me ran to Ford's and the wagons, rounded up every able-bodied man we could find at Corey's place, and came up here to see what you thought best to do. Not that I think it's the Lord's judgment for our iniquities, that no mortal kin ought to try..."

Armitage realized the moment for decisive action had arrived. He addressed the faltering group of rustics with authority, forcing his voice to sound reassuring.

"We must follow it, boys." He spoke with conviction. "I believe there is a chance to put it out of business. You men know those Whateleys were wizards; well, this thing is a product of wizardry, and it must be countered by the same means.
