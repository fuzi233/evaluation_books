It is difficult for language to capture the turmoil that swept through Clement's sensitive mind, his tender conscience, and his loving heart within mere hours. Faced with such a sudden and overwhelming crisis, the depth of his internal struggle defies description. Imagine a man in his position; try to feel the weight of it. If I were to dedicate a whole book to this single moment, I would eventually arrive here. For now, let us simply observe his next two decisive actions, which reveal the state of his spirit once the initial storm of anguish had begun to subside.

After spending the night with the dying hermit, offering and receiving sacred comfort, Clement did not head toward Rotterdam. Instead, he traveled to Tergou. His destination was the Burgomaster, his mortal foe. Armed with a parchment—a document whose own history reads like a romance—he intended to force the official to surrender what rightfully belonged to Margaret.

Exhausted and covered in dust, Clement paused at a public fountain. There, he broke his fast on coarse black bread and drank cool water. Mid-meal, however, a female servant burst forward, breathless, begging him to come immediately to shrive her dying master. Without hesitation, he tucked the bread back into his wallet and followed her silently.

She led him to the Stadthouse. At the threshold, seeing her enter, Clement recoiled slightly, a shiver running through him. But he quickly composed himself and stepped inside, ascending the stairs until they reached the bedroom. There, propped high against pillows, lay his deadly enemy, Ghysbrecht, appearing already lifeless.

Clement stood in the doorway for a moment, his eyes tracing the path of the tower, the forest, and the stolen letter. Then, in a low voice, he spoke: "Peace be with you." His voice trembled slightly as the words left his lips.

The sick man greeted him with desperate eagerness, given his frailty. "Thank heaven you have come in time to absolve me of my sins, Father!" Ghysbrecht cried out. "Pray for my soul, you and your brothers!"

"My son," Clement replied calmly, "absolution comes only after confession. In this act, there must be no holding back, if you value the salvation of your soul. Consider carefully where you have most grievously offended God and the Church, while I offer a prayer for wisdom to guide you."

Clement knelt and prayed. Rising, he addressed Ghysbrecht again. "Now, my son, confess your sins."

"Oh, Father," the dying man sighed, "there are many and great ones."

"Then let your penitence be equally great, my son," Clement urged. "Only thus will you find God's mercy vast."

Ghysbrecht clasped his hands and began to confess, displaying every sign of remorse. He admitted to eating meat during Lent, frequently missing Mass on Sundays and feast days, and trifling with various religious duties, listing them with scrupulous detail.

When finished, the friar said softly, "That is well, my son. Those are faults. Now, turn to your crimes. You would have done better to start with those."

"Why, Father?" Ghysbrecht asked, confused. "What crimes stand to my charge if these are not enough?"

"Am I confessing to you, or are you confessing to me?" Clement asked, his tone sharpening.

"Forgive me, Father! Surely I am speaking to you. But I do not understand what you mean by 'crimes.'"

"The Seven Deadly Sins—do you believe you are free of them?"

"Heaven forbid I should be guilty of such things," Ghysbrecht protested. "I do not even know them by name."

"Many commit them all without knowing their titles," Clement reminded him sternly. "Begin with the sin that leads to lying, theft, and murder."

"I am clear of that one, anyway," Ghysbrecht insisted. "What do you call it?"

"Avarice, my son."

"Avarice?" Ghysbrecht blinked, looking puzzled and innocent. "As for that, I have been a thrifty man all my days. Yet I have kept a good table and have not entirely forgotten the poor. But alas, I am a great sinner! Perhaps the next one will catch me. What is the next?"

"We have not finished with this one," Clement warned quietly. "Remember, the Church is not to be trifled with."

"But Father, am I in a condition to trifle with Her now? Avarice? Avarice?" He sounded bewildered.

"Hast thou ever robbed the fatherless?" the friar inquired sharply.

"Me? Robbed the fatherless?" Ghysbrecht gasped. "Not that I mind."

"Again, my son, I am forced to tell you that you are playing with fire. Wretched man! One more evasion, and I depart. Then demons will instantly surround your bed and drag you down to the bottomless pit."

"Oh, do not leave me! Do not leave me!" the terrified old man shrieked. "The Church knows everything. I must have robbed the fatherless. I will confess. With whom shall I begin? My memory for names is failing me."

His defense was clever, but it failed utterly in this instance.

"Hast thou forgotten Floris Brandt?" Clement asked, his face like stone.

The sick man threw himself upright in bed, seized by paralyzing terror. "How did you know that?" he stammered.

"The Church knows many things," Clement replied coldly. "And by many paths unknown to you. Miserable impenitent soul, you summoned her to your side hoping to deceive her. You thought, 'I will not confess to the parish priest, but to some friar who does not know my misdeeds. Thus I will cheat the Church on my deathbed and die as I have lived.' But God, kinder to you than you are to yourself, sent someone you could not fool. He tested you; He was patient, warning you not to mock Holy Church. But all is in vain. You cannot confess because you are impenitent as a rock. Die, then, as you have lived. It seems I see the demons crowding around the bed for their prey. They wait only for me to leave. And I go."

He turned his back, but Ghysbrecht, driven to the brink of madness, grabbed the friar's robe. "Oh, holy man, mercy! Stay! I will confess everything! I robbed my friend Floris! Alas, may it end there, for he lost little by me. But I withheld the land from Peter, his son, and from Margaret, Peter's daughter. I always meant to return it, but I could not, I could not!"

"Avarice, my son! Avarice!" Clement repeated, though his voice softened. "Happy for you, it is not too late."

"No! I will leave it to her in my will!" Ghysbrecht pleaded. "She will not have to wait long—not more than a month or two at most."

"For a month's possession of which you would damn your soul forever, you fool!" Clement retorted.

The sick man groaned, begging the friar to be reasonable. But Clement, firm yet gentle, persisted with infinite patience, slowly loosening the dying man's grip on another person's property.

There were moments when Clement's patience was nearly exhausted, tempting him to reach into his bosom and produce the very deed he had carried for this exact purpose. However, recalling the violent outburst of the previous day, he guarded himself against anger. Furthermore, he overcame his deep-seated personal revulsion for the man—a hatred so intense it made his own flesh crawl while he struggled with this miser for his soul. Finally, without mentioning the deed, he succeeded in winning the dying man's promise to make full and immediate restitution.

How exactly the restitution was executed will be recounted elsewhere, along with the strange effects it produced upon Ghysbrecht, and the terms under which Ghysbrecht and Clement finally parted ways. I promised to describe two acts of the latter that indicate his state of mind. This is the first. The second requires only two words.

As soon as Clement was absolutely certain that Margaret had regained her inheritance and was once again a wealthy woman... he vanished.
