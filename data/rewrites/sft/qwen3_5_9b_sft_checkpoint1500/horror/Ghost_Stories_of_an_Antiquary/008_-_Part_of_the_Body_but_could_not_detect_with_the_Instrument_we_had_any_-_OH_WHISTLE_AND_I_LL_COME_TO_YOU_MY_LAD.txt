"You'll be off soon enough, Professor," remarked a neighbor seated beside the Professor of Ontography at a feast in St James's College. "Term is finished."

The Professor, a young man named Parkins, was noted for his neat attire and precise diction. "Indeed," he replied calmly. "My companions have insisted I take up golf this term. I intend to travel to Burnstow—surely you've heard of it—for a week or ten days to refine my game. I hope to depart tomorrow."

"Oh, Parkins," interjected a man across the table, a fellow of antiquarian leanings, "if you're heading to Burnstow, pray inspect the site of the Templars' preceptory. Let me know if it warrants excavation this summer."

"Certainly," said Parkins. "Describe the location, and I shall assess the terrain upon my return, or pen a letter detailing my findings."

"No trouble necessary," the antiquarian dismissed. "I plan to visit with my family in August. Since few English preceptories were laid out with proper care, I fancied I might stumble upon something useful on my spare days."

Parkins sniffed skeptically at the notion of 'utility' regarding medieval foundations, but the antiquarian pressed on. "The site lies submerged near the beach now; the sea has devoured the coast thoroughly. My maps suggest it sits roughly three-quarters of a mile from the Globe Inn at the town's northern tip. Where do you propose lodging?"

"At the Globe Inn, as fate would have it," Parkins admitted. "Accommodations are scarce in winter, and the Globe offered the only spacious chamber available. Alas, it is a double-bedded room, and they lack storage space for the second mattress. However, I require ample room for my books and study. An empty bed, or indeed two, in a scholar's sanctum is distasteful, though I suppose I can endure the discomfort for a brief sojourn."

"Do you deem sharing a room 'roughing it,' Parkins?" queried a bluff gentleman opposite, named Rogers. "Let me assure you, I shall claim that bed temporarily. It makes for excellent company."

Parkins quivered internally, masking his unease with a courteous smile. "By all means, Rogers. Though I fear it may prove monotonous. You do not play golf, do you?"

"Not by heaven!" roared Rogers.

"When I am not penning treatises," Parkins explained, "I shall likely frequent the links. That would be tedious company for a non-golfer."

"Oh, I'm mistaken!" Rogers laughed, nudging his neighbor. "Someone I know resides there. But if you'd prefer solitude, speak freely, Parkins. I shall not be offended. Truth, as you often declare, is never offensive."

Parkins, a man of scrupulous politeness and rigid honesty, found his composure wavering. A internal conflict surged within him, silencing his immediate response. When he finally spoke, his voice lacked its usual cadence.

"If I speak the unvarnished truth, Rogers," Parkins stammered, "I am uncertain whether this room accommodates two gentlemen comfortably. Furthermore..." He paused, flushing slightly. "...might you not impede my scholarly endeavors?"

Rogers erupted in laughter. "Capital, Parkins! Fear not. I pledge to leave your work undisturbed. Do not fret. If you decline my presence, I shall accept it gracefully. Yet, I suspected I might deter the spirits haunting this locale." A conspiratorial wink flashed between Rogers and his neighbor. Parkins's cheeks burned crimson.

"I apologize," Rogers continued, feigning regret. "I forgot you abhor levity concerning such matters."

"Well," Parkins conceded, regaining his composure, "since you broach the subject, I admit openly that I detest casual discourse on specters. A man of my station—" he raised his voice, addressing the table—"cannot afford to sanction popular superstitious beliefs. As you know, Rogers, I have never concealed my convictions."

"Nor have you, old friend," Rogers whispered sotto voce.

"—I maintain that any semblance of concession to the existence of such entities constitutes a renunciation of my highest principles. Regrettably, I suspect I have failed to capture your full attention."

"Undivided attention, was what Dr. Blimber explicitly stated," Rogers corrected earnestly, "though I beg pardon, Mr. Parkins. I interrupt."

"No, quite," Parkins smiled. "I recall no Dr. Blimber, though I need not elaborate. I trust you understand my sentiment."

"Yes, yes," Rogers hastened. "We shall discuss this fully at Burnstow."

*Note: In recounting this dialogue, I endeavored to convey the impression it left upon me. Parkins appeared somewhat hen-like in his mannerisms, utterly devoid of humor, yet dauntless and sincere in his convictions, meriting profound respect.*

The following day, Parkins successfully departed for Burnstow. Welcomed at the Globe Inn, he settled into the large double-bedded room previously described. Before retiring, he arranged his materials in meticulous order upon a commodious table positioned at the room's outer end, surrounded by three windows overlooking the sea. The central pane faced the ocean directly, while the lateral windows commanded views of the coastline to the north and south. To the south lay the village of Burnstow; to the north, only beach and low cliffs backed by grass. Between the inn and the sea stretched a narrow strip of rough grass, littered with ancient anchors and capstans, followed by a broad path and the shingle beach. Whatever the original distance, the sea now encroached within sixty yards of the inn.

The inn's clientele consisted largely of golfers, save for a conspicuous figure: an *ancien militaire*, secretary of a London club, possessing a voice of thunderous volume and staunchly Protestant views. This Colonel Wilson frequently voiced his opinions after attending services with the Vicar, an estimable clergyman inclined toward picturesque ritual, which he gallantly suppressed to honor local East Anglian traditions.

Spending most of the day improving his game alongside Colonel Wilson, Parkins found the Colonel's demeanor growing increasingly lurid by afternoon. Even Parkins recoiled at the thought of returning home with him. Observing the Colonel's bristling moustache and inflamed features, Parkins resolved to avoid the Colonel until tea and tobacco could soothe his irascibility before dinner necessitated a meeting.

"I might stroll home tonight along the beach," Parkins mused. "The twilight offers sufficient illumination to survey the ruins Disney mentioned. Though I lack precise coordinates, I am certain I shall stumble upon them."

Fate, however, intervened literally. Navigating from the links to the shingle, Parkins's foot snagged on a gorse root and a substantial stone, sending him tumbling. Rising, he discovered himself amidst broken ground, dotted with small depressions and mounds. Upon closer inspection, these proved to be masses of flints embedded in mortar and cloaked by turf. Concluding he stood upon the Templar preceptory, Parkins noted the site promised archaeological rewards. Foundations likely remained shallow enough to illuminate the general plan. Recalling the Templars' penchant for circular churches, he observed a series of mounds arranged in a rough circle. Succumbing to the amateur researcher's temptation to demonstrate hypothetical success, Parkins measured the circular area in his pocket-book and examined an oblong eminence east of the center, likely a former altar base.

At the northern extremity, a patch of turf had been removed by some wild creature. Seizing the opportunity to probe for masonry, Parkins extracted his knife and scraped away the earth. Suddenly, a section of soil collapsed inward, revealing a small cavity. Lighting several matches against the biting wind, Parkins tapped the sides, confirming an artificial, rectangular hole with smooth, regular walls. Empty at first, a metallic clink echoed as he withdrew his knife. Reaching in, he grasped a cylindrical object—a metal tube approximately four inches long, evidently of considerable antiquity.

By the time Parkins verified the receptacle contained nothing else, darkness had descended. Determined to sacrifice further daylight to archaeology, he pocketed the object, confident of its value.

Before departing, Parkins took a final, bleak look at the scene. A faint yellow light illuminated the western horizon, revealing the links, a few figures approaching the clubhouse, a squat Martello tower, the lights of Aldsey village, and the pale ribbon of sands intersected by black wooden groynings. The sea murmured dimly beneath a bitter northern wind. As he traversed the shingle, gaining the sand, the journey was quiet save for the groynings. Glancing back, Parkins perceived an indistinct figure seemingly striving to catch up, yet making negligible progress. The apparition appeared to run, yet the distance remained constant.

Parkins, unaccustomed to supernatural lore, recalled childhood tales of fiends emerging from fields. "If I beheld a black figure, sharply defined against the yellow sky, adorned with horns and wings?" he wondered. "Would I stand or flee? Fortunately, the gentleman behind lacks such attributes, and his proximity remains unchanged." Realizing dinner time approached within a quarter-hour, Parkins accelerated his pace.
