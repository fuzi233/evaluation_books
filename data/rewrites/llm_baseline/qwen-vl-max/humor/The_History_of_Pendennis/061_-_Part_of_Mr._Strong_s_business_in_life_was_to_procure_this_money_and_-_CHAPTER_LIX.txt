It so happened that on the great English holiday known as Derby Day—when all of London abandons itself to horse-racing, betting, and general merriment upon Epsom Downs—a number of our familiar characters found themselves gathered together, like a well-attended reunion of old acquaintances at a particularly rowdy school picnic.

In one comfortably open carriage, drawn by a pair of horses who looked as if they’d been plucked from a butcher’s yard and told to “get going,” sat Mrs. Bungay of Paternoster Row, resplendent in an outfit so grand it might have made Solomon himself blush and reach for a fan. Beside her, modestly draped in a shawl and looking faintly alarmed by the whole affair, was Mrs. Shandon, whom Mrs. Bungay had taken under her wing since their first meeting and now treated with the solemnity usually reserved for saints or minor royalty. Finucane, ever vigilant and slightly too eager, hovered nearby, ready to fend off any gentleman who dared approach without proper credentials.

Among those approaching was Mr. Archer, who bowed with the grace of a man who’d practiced bowing in front of a mirror every morning since 1827. He informed Mrs. Bungay of the notable figures scattered across the field: “There’s the Prime Minister!” he declared, pointing dramatically. “He just whispered to me that Borax is the horse to back. But I say Munmeer—Munmeer, madam! That’s the one.”

Mrs. Bungay blinked. “Do you know them all?”

“Why, yes,” said Archer, puffing out his chest like a rooster who’d just discovered he owns a farm. “I’ve seen them in Parliament, dined with them, danced with them—once even shared a bottle of claret with the Duke of Kent during a thunderstorm. Look yonder, in the Grand Stand—the Chinese Ambassador! With his entourage of Mandarins! I introduced him myself. The Governor-General of India sent letters, and we became bosom friends. I used to invite him to dinner, and he always brought his own cook. A very particular man, you understand.”

Mrs. Bungay leaned forward. “And did he bring good food?”

“Oh, yes,” said Archer, lowering his voice. “One day, while my wife and the Ambassador were eating gooseberries in the garden—Chinese love gooseberries, you know—the cook saw our dear little Blenheim spaniel, Fido—descended from a dog once given to the Duke of Marlborough by the King of Spain, mind you—and decided to make him into a dish. Cut his throat, skinned him, stuffed him with forcemeat, served him up as the second course. My wife didn’t speak to the Ambassador again. Never. And he hasn’t come to dine with us since. Though the Lord Mayor thought it excellent. Said it tasted rather like duck when eaten with green peas.”

Mrs. Bungay gasped. “You don’t say!”

“Fact, upon my word. And look—Lady Flamingo, seated beside him. They say she’s marrying him and returning to Pekin. She’s having her feet bound down, you see—though I doubt she’ll manage it. My wife has the smallest feet in England—wears shoes for a six-year-old child. But what’s that to a Chinese lady? She’ll cripple herself, poor thing.”

“Who’s that carriage over there?” Mrs. Bungay asked suddenly, squinting. “Mr. Pendennis is talking to someone. He was here with Mr. Warrington just now. He’s quite haughty, isn’t he? Well, he may be—he keeps very fine company. Did he inherit much money, Mr. Archer? He’s still in black, I notice.”

“Eighteen hundred a year in land,” Archer replied, “and twenty-two thousand five hundred in Three-and-a-half per Cents. Quite respectable.”

“Law!” said Mrs. Bungay. “You know everything, Mr. Archer!”

“I happen to know because I was called in about Mrs. Pendennis’s will,” Archer said proudly. “Pendennis’s uncle, the Major, never does anything without me. We tied up the property tight—no ducks and drakes allowed. Now, how do you do, my lord? Do you know that gentleman, ladies? You’ve read his speeches in the House—it’s Lord Rochester.”

“Lord Fiddlestick!” cried Finucane from the box. “Sure it’s Tom Staples of the Morning Advertiser.”

“Is it?” said Archer, blinking. “Well, I’m very short-sighted, and honestly thought it was Rochester. That gentleman with the opera-glasses—another nod—is Lord John; and the tall man beside him—don’t you know him? Sir James.”

“You know them because you see them in the House,” grumbled Finucane.

“I know them because they’re kind enough to allow me to call them my most intimate friends,” Archer corrected, as if this explained everything.

“And the Duke of Hampshire?” Mrs. Bungay asked. “What a fine-looking old gentleman! He never misses the Derby, do they say?”

“He hasn’t missed one since he was seven,” Archer said. “Rode a piebald pony with his father, the Prince of Wales, and Colonel Hanger. Only missed two races—one due to measles at Eton, and one in the Waterloo year, when he was with Wellington in Flanders.”

“Who’s that yellow carriage with the pink and yellow parasols?” Mrs. Bungay asked. “Mr. Pendennis is talking to them—so many gentlemen!”

“That’s Lady Clavering,” said Archer. “Of Clavering Park, next door to Pendennis’s estate. The young man on the box? Awfully tipsy, the little scamp. The young lady is Miss Amory—Lady Clavering’s daughter from her first marriage. She’s sweet on Pendennis, but I suspect he’s got his heart set elsewhere. You’ve heard of young Mr. Foker—the brewer? He was going to hang himself after Miss Amory refused him, but his valet cut him down just in time. Now he’s abroad under a keeper.”

“How happy that young fellow is!” sighed Mrs. Bungay. “Who’d have thought he’d turn out such a grand character? I saw his name at Court the other day—presented by the Marquis of Steyne himself. At every noble party, his name’s down like clockwork.”

“I introduced him when he first came to town,” said Archer. “Major Pendennis did the rest. Hallo! There’s Cobden! Of all people! I must go and speak to him. Good-bye, Mrs. Bungay. Good morning, Mrs. Shandon.”

Meanwhile, at another part of the course, a battered stagecoach—known as Wheeler’s Harlequin’s Head drag—was rattling along, its roof crowded with a motley crew of shabby ruffians, dubious bucks, and men whose only claim to gentility was the fact that they wore hats. As the race began, the crowd erupted into cheers, each man yelling out his favorite horse: “The Cornet!” “It’s Muffineer!” “Blue sleeves!” “Yellow cap! Yellow cap! Yellow cap!” The moment the fluttering signal revealed Podasokus as the winner, one man leapt onto the roof as if he meant to fly to London with the news.

But gravity intervened. He fell back with a thud, cracking the already frail boards beneath him. “Hurray!” he bellowed. “Podasokus wins! Supper for ten, Wheeler! Ask everyone round—damn the expense!”

The others nodded politely. “Thank you—congratulate you, Colonel,” they said, though each eye watched the others with suspicion. “The Colonel stands to win fifteen hundred,” one muttered. “And he got the odds from a good man, too.” And thus began the silent war of borrowing, where no one could be left alone lest he be cornered and asked for a loan.

Elsewhere, a far more modest vehicle—cab No. 2002—had arrived from the Strand. Inside, two ladies and a gentleman were enjoying a repast of lobster salad and bitter ale. One of the ladies, a young girl with a fresh face and rosy cheeks, sat on the box, drawing admiring glances from every dandy within sight. Her mother, meanwhile, devoured lobsters with the ferocity of a lioness, her face growing redder with each bite—until it matched the shell of the creature she was consuming.

The gentleman accompanying them—Mr. Sam Huxter—was a whirlwind of jokes and energy. He had entertained the entire journey with endless “chaff,” shouting insults at guardsmen, laughing at dustmen, firing volleys of wit at schoolgirls and urchins alike. He had filled the cab with souvenirs—pincushions, wooden apples, Jack-in-the-boxes, tiny soldiers—and even brought a gipsy woman with a tawny child to tell fortunes.

When the fortune-teller announced that the young lady should beware of a fair man who would prove false, and that she would eventually find a true one, the girl blushed deeply. Her mother and Sam exchanged glances—perhaps the conjurer had said the same thing to fifty carriages that day.

Then, through the crowd, Arthur Pendennis appeared. The girl—Fanny Bolton—turned pale. Her mother went crimson. Sam’s fists clenched in his pockets like weapons ready to strike. Pendennis, too, looked dark and suspicious—though not from jealousy, perhaps, but from moral outrage, like a Victorian detective spotting a crime before it happens.

Fanny’s heart pounded. Sam’s hands tightened. Mrs. Bolton launched into a torrent of chatter: “Oh, Mr. Pendennis! How well you look! We were just talking about you—weren’t we, Fanny? And how is Major Pendennis? And that kind Mr. Warrington? He’s so tall, he nearly broke his head on our lodge door!”

As she spoke, Fanny’s mind raced—memories of past love, sorrow, shame, and hope. There stood Pendennis—dandified, supercilious, with jet buttons, a pink flower in his coat, and gloves so tight they looked like they’d been sewn by a nervous seamstress. Sam, meanwhile, wore no gloves, boots that looked like they’d walked through a coal mine, and smelled strongly of tobacco.

After a tense silence, Fanny managed to say, “This is Mr. Samuel Huxter, sir—you knew him before, I believe. Mr. Samuel, you know Mr. Pendennis—will you take a refreshment?”

Her words, though shaky, lifted the weight from Pendennis’s shoulders. He smiled. “I’m very thirsty,” he said. “I’ll gladly drink your health, Fanny. And I hope Mr. Huxter will forgive me for being rude last time—we were both ill, and I scarcely knew what I said.”

He extended his lavender kid glove—symbol of peace. Sam, unable to resist, pulled out his fist and placed it in Pendennis’s hand. It was hot. Blackened. Left marks on the glove. He wanted to punch Pendennis in the face, right there, in front of all of England. But instead, he let it go.

Pen took a glass—any glass—and filled it with lukewarm beer, which he drank with gusto. “Delicious,” he declared.

As he sipped, a young lady in a dove-coloured dress passed by, leaning on the arm of a man with a military moustache. She gave Pendennis a mischievous glance. He laughed. The man laughed louder.

Fanny watched her go. Then she turned to Sam, her eyes bright. “What a beautiful creature! Such delicate hands!”

“It was Captain Strong,” said Mrs. Bolton. “And who was the young woman?”

“Miss Amory,” Pendennis said. “Lady Clavering’s daughter. You’ve seen Sir Francis at Shepherd’s Inn.”

Fanny immediately built a romance in her mind—love, betrayal, a grand wedding, a broken-hearted maid. Sam, meanwhile, lit a rank Cuba cigar and smoked it under her nose.

When Pendennis left, the sun seemed duller. The sky greyer. The Sticks uninteresting. The beer undrinkable. Sam forgot his pea-shooter until someone fired a volley at his face. He cursed, then burst into a sardonic laugh.

But Fanny was radiant all the way home. She coaxed, giggled, admired everything. When they arrived, Sam tried to leave with dignity. But Fanny burst into tears. “You’re a naughty, unkind thing!”

Sam, overwhelmed, held her tightly. “I’m a jealous brute,” he sobbed. “I’m unworthy of you. I shouldn’t hate Pendennis. Please—say once more that you——”

That she what? The words were lost in a kiss so close, no one could hear.

Mrs. Bolton stepped in. “Come, come, Mr. H.—no nonsense! You’ve acted like a wicked wretch!”

Later, Pendennis approached Lady Clavering’s carriage. The Major was waiting, the box occupied by young Hopeful and Captain Strong. A crowd of dandies surrounded the carriage, flirting with Miss Amory, offering bets, exchanging knowing glances.

Pendennis pushed through. Miss Amory beckoned him. “Je lay vue,” she said. “Elle a de bien beaux yeux; vous etes un monster!”

“Monster?” Pendennis laughed. “Hone suit qui mal y peens. My friend there is as safe as any young lady in Christendom. She has her mamma on one side, her pretend on the other. What harm could possibly happen?”

“One does not know,” said Miss Amory in French, “when a girl has the mind, and when she is pursued by a wicked monster like you. Imagine me finding Monsieur near a cab, with two ladies and a man—oh, such a man!—who ate lobsters and laughed, laughed!”

“It did not strike me that the man laughed,” Pendennis said. “And as for lobsters—I thought he’d have liked to eat me after them. He shook hands with me so hard he bruised my glove blue-black. He’s a young surgeon. From Clavering. Don’t you remember the gilt pestle and mortar in High Street?”

“If he attends you when you’re sick,” Miss Amory continued, “he’ll kill you. He’ll serve you right—for you are a monster.”

Pendennis frowned. “She speaks of these things too lightly,” he thought. “If I were a monster, she’d welcome me just the same. This is not how an English lady should think. Laura wouldn’t speak like this. Thank God.”

And so, as he stood there, the wind blowing his hair, the sun setting behind the hills, he felt something shift—inside, somewhere. Not quite love. Not quite regret. But something close.
