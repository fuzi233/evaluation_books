The shop bell jingled as two men stepped inside. Nello, the barber, flashed a grin at the first. “Messer Domenico! You arrive as timely as grated cheese on a plate of pasta. And in such haste—I see you wish to be shaved without delay. *Ecco!* But then, who isn’t in haste today? Florence is orphaned, the very heart of Italy torn out. The heavens themselves must be scrambling to set things right. *Oimè!* Yet the sun marches on toward dinner, and as I was saying—your timing is impeccable. This young stranger here seeks an honorable merchant to advance him a sum against a ring of some value. Had I counted every goldsmith in Florence on my fingers, I could name none better than Menico Cennini.”

The older man, son of the famed Bernardo Cennini—who had cast Florence’s first printing types years before—remained still under Nello’s lather, though his eyes slid sideways to study the stranger.

Nello, never one to let silence linger, pressed on. “This fine scholar has Greek, Latin, and Italian at your disposal—a prodigy of learning, like Filelfo or our own Poliziano! A second Guarino, too, for he’s survived a shipwreck, though I fear many a precious manuscript now feeds the fishes. But he’s salvaged a few treasures. His name is—” Nello paused, razor hovering. “You did give your name, Messere?”

“Tito Melema,” the stranger said smoothly, slipping a ring from his finger and offering it to Cennini.

The second visitor—a tall man in a threadbare cloak and worn felt hat—had not taken his eyes off Tito. Abruptly, he spoke. “Young man, I’m painting Sinon deceiving Priam. Your face would suit my traitor. Sit for me.”

Tito started, his expression flickering with something like alarm before settling into polite confusion. Nello laughed, shaking his head. “Piero, you impossible creature! Must you turn every face into a villain? Look at him—this is no deceiver’s face. Turn his eyes upward, and you’d have a Saint Sebastian to make devout women weep. Or crown him with myrtle and call him Bacchus—no, Apollo, for his face is as bright as morning. It won me over in the time it takes to say a *credo*.”

Piero di Cosimo gave a short, gruff chuckle. “Ah, Nello, if you ever stopped chirping long enough to think, you’d see why his face suits my purpose. The perfect traitor wears a face unmarked by vice—lips that lie with a dimpled smile, eyes too clear to betray guilt, cheeks that stay fresh even after murder. Not,” he added, holding up a hand, “that I accuse this youth of treachery. I only say his face is the kind that *could* deceive, if he wished. The lapidary doesn’t always know a gem by sight alone.” He stuffed tow in his ears and dropped into Nello’s chair. “Enough talk. Trim my beard.”

Nello rolled his eyes at Tito, as if to say, *Pay him no mind—he speaks in riddles.*

Tito, who had stood very still under Piero’s scrutiny, relaxed slightly. He turned back to Cennini, who examined the ring with a jeweler’s keen eye.

“A curious piece,” Cennini mused. “The intaglio—a fish and crested serpent in black onyx—is well set off by the blue layer. This ring must have a history.”

Tito smiled easily. “It was found in Sicily. Those who study such things claim it brings fortune, especially at sea, and helps recover what’s lost. Though,” he added with a rueful laugh, “it didn’t spare me a shipwreck. Still, I escaped drowning—perhaps that’s proof enough. As for my lost chests, we shall see. In the meantime, Messere, I’d ask you to hold the ring as pledge for a modest sum. I’ll redeem it soon, whether by selling other gems I carry or by finding scholarly work—if fortune favors me.”

Cennini nodded. “Come with me. My brother Pietro may have tasks to test your skills. Keep your ring for now; I’ll fetch the florins shortly.”

“Go, go,” Nello urged. “You’ll find no better guide in Florence. Born under a lucky star, this one—though which star, who can say? Not that it matters, since babies don’t pick their horoscopes. And our great Pico proved it all nonsense anyway. *Addio, bel giovane!* Don’t forget to return.”

Tito turned at the door, his face alight. “I wouldn’t dream of neglecting the man who’s promised to help me. That’s assurance enough, isn’t it?”

As the door closed, Nello shook his head at Piero. “Say what you will—a face like that belongs to a man one can’t help but like. Next you’ll claim Leonardo should’ve painted Judas as handsome as John!” He sighed. “Ah, but you’re deaf as stone with that tow in your ears. No matter. I’ll pry more of that boy’s story from him before I take him to Bardo Bardi.”
