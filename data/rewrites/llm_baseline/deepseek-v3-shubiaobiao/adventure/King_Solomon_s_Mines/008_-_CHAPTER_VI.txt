The thirst woke me two hours later—a cruel, gnawing thirst that turned my tongue to leather and my throat to fire. It was still dark, but the heavy sleep of exhaustion had passed, leaving only the raw need for water. My lips were cracked, my eyelids gummed shut; it took effort to wrench them apart. The air was thick, hot, oppressive—nothing like the crisp dawns I knew.

The others still slept. Desperate for distraction, I fumbled for my pocket copy of *The Ingoldsby Legends* and read by the faint light. When I reached the lines about the golden ewer of pure water, I nearly laughed—or sobbed. The thought of it drove me mad. I would have drained a basin even if the Pope himself had washed his hands in it.

The others stirred, rubbing crusted eyes and peeling apart parched lips. Our situation was dire. The water bottles were bone-dry. Good eyed the brandy flask, but Sir Henry snatched it away. "Raw spirit would only kill us faster," he said grimly.

Then Ventvögel, the Hottentot, stood abruptly. He paced, nostrils flaring, then stopped and pointed at the sand. "Springbok spoor," I said. "So?"

"Springbucks do not wander far from water," he answered.

Hope, fragile as a star in a black sky, flickered in us. Ventvögel lifted his face, sniffing like an old buck scenting the wind. "*I smell water*," he declared.

At that moment, the sun rose—and so did Sheba’s Breasts.

Two colossal mountains, each fifteen thousand feet high, loomed ahead, their snow-capped summits gleaming like silver. Between them stretched the Suliman Berg, a vast spine of rock. They were shaped like a woman’s breasts, swelling from the desert, their peaks the nipples. Mists curled around them like a sleeping goddess. The sight stole my breath—and for a moment, even my thirst.

But the vision vanished as clouds swallowed the peaks. Thirst roared back, sharper than before. Ventvögel swore he smelled water, yet none was in sight—only sand and scrub. We scaled a nearby hillock, half-delirious, and there, improbably, was a murky pool in a hollow at the very top. We fell upon it like beasts, drinking, then wallowing in the brackish filth. I’d have traded a kingdom for that mud.

Revived, we pressed on under the moon, reaching the mountain’s base by dawn. The lava slopes were brutal, our feet shredded by jagged rock. By noon, we were near collapse—until Umbopa spotted a patch of green. Wild melons. We gorged, the pulp easing our thirst but not our hunger. Then, miraculously, a flock of *pauw* bustards flew overhead. I dropped one with two shots. We devoured it, bones and all.

Days blurred into torment. The cold bit deeper as we climbed. Ventvögel succumbed first, freezing to death beside me in the night. At dawn, we found another corpse in the cave—a white man, slumped as if in sleep. Terror drove us stumbling back into the light.

The mountain had claimed its first lives. It would not be the last.
