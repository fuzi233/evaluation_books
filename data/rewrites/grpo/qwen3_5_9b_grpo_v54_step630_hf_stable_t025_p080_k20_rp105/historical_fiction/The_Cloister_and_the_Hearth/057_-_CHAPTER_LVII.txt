A week had passed since their last encounter, yet the atmosphere in the lodging remained thick with unspoken tension. Pietro and Gerard sat side by side, ostensibly working, but their spirits were worlds apart. Pietro attacked his canvas with frantic, erratic bursts of energy, producing miracles in moments before collapsing into silence to hurl curses at his own handiwork. He would resume his labor in a fit of rage only to abandon it again with a heavy sigh. Throughout this tempest of emotion, the shrewd Dutchman remained an island of calm, his hands moving steadily, a serene smile fixed upon his face.

In truth, Gerard had long ago tamed his friend. The two men were painting playing cards, a project designed to fill their coffers. Once finished, the indignant master took up his painting to begin his daily rounds in search of a buyer. Gerard implored him to take the cards as well, urging him to try selling them. Pietro recoiled as if bitten by a viper, but eventually, embracing Gerard in the warm, familiar manner of Italy, he agreed. First, however, he dried the freshly painted cards in the fierce, golden sun that bathed this fortunate land.

Left alone, Gerard sketched a few Greek letters and then repaired a tear in his hose. His landlady observed him thus occupied and queried, with a tone dripping with irony, whether there were no women in the house. "When you have finished that," she said, "come and speak with Teresa, my friend—the one I mentioned to you. She has a husband who is little good for anything, yet he boasts of knowing the great."

Gerard descended the stairs, and who should meet him but the Roman matron. "Ah, Madam," he greeted her, "is it you? The good dame told me nothing of this. And the little fair-haired boy? Is he well? Has the voyage in that strange vessel harmed him in any way?"

"He is well," the matron replied simply.

"Why, what are you two discussing?" the landlady demanded, staring alternately at them with wide eyes. "And why do you tremble so, Teresa mia?"

"He saved my child's life," Teresa answered, struggling to compose herself.

"What!" the landlady exclaimed. "My lodger? And he never told me a word of such a thing! Are you not ashamed to look me in the face?"

"Alas! Speak not harshly to him," the matron interceded. She then turned to her companion and poured forth a glowing account of Gerard's conduct. During this recital, Gerard stood blushing with the intensity of a maiden, scarcely recognizing his own actions as gratitude rendered them so beautiful.

"And to think you asked me to serve your lodger, of whom I knew nothing save that he possessed your good word, oh, Fiammina! That was sufficient for me. Dear youth, in serving you, I serve myself."

What followed was an eager exchange between the two women regarding the obstacles that stood between the patrons of art and an unknown artist in the Eternal City—a thick wall of fees, commissions, and legal chicanery. Teresa smiled with a sad amusement at Gerard's naivety in having left specimens of his skill at the doors of the powerful without proper introduction.

"What!" she cried. "Without promising the servants a share? Without even offering them a fee to allow the signori to view your merchandise! It would have been wiser to cast it into the Tiber."

"Well-a-day!" sighed Gerard. "Then how is an artist to find a patron? For artists are poor, not rich."

"By seeking a city nobler and less greedy than this," Teresa advised. "The Roman court does not wish for a sheep without wool."

She fell into thought and declared she would return the following day. The landlady congratulated Gerard, remarking that Teresa had something stirring in her mind.

Scarcely had Teresa departed when Pietro returned with his picture, looking black as thunder. Gerard exchanged a glance with the landlady and followed him upstairs to offer comfort.

"What! Have they allowed you to bring home your masterpiece?"

"As heretofore."

"More fools they, then."

"That is not necessarily a bad thing."

"Why, what is the matter?"

"They have bought the cards!" Pietro shouted, hammering the air furiously in every direction.

"All the better," Gerard said cheerfully.

"They flew at me for them! They were enraptured. They tried to conceal their longing, but failed utterly. I saw, I feigned, I pillaged; curse those simpletons."

He threw a dozen small silver coins onto the floor, leaped upon them, danced with basilisk-like eyes, and then kicked them assiduously, sending them spinning and scattering across the room. Gerard dropped to his knees, gathered the maltreated innocents directly, and transferred them tenderly into his purse.

"You should rather smile at their ignorance and put it to profit," he suggested.

"And so I will," Pietro replied, his indignation now concentrated. "The brutes! We shall paint a pack a day; we shall set the whole city gambling and ruining itself, while we live like princes upon their vices and stupidity."

"There was one of the queens, however, that I would have kept back," Pietro continued. "'Twas you who painted her, brother. She had lovely red-brown hair and sapphire eyes, and above all, a soul."

"Pietro," Gerard said softly, "I painted that one from my heart."

The quick-witted Italian nodded, his eyes twinkling. "You love her so well, yet you leave her."

"Pietro," Gerard replied gently, "it is precisely because I love her so dearly that I have wandered this weary road."

This poignant conversation was interrupted by the landlady's cry from below: "Come down, you are wanted." Gerard descended, and there stood Teresa once more.

"Come with me, Ser Gerard."
