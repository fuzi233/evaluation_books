Mr. Bunner on the Case

"Calvin C. Bunner, at your service," the newcomer amended, his tone sharpened with a precise politeness as he withdrew an unlit cigar from his mouth. He was accustomed to the slowness and ceremonial formality of strangers in England, and Trent's brisk, direct greeting clearly threw him slightly off balance. "I expect you're Mr. Trent," he continued, recovering his composure. "Mrs. Manderson mentioned you a moment ago. Captain, good-morning." Captain Murch returned the unconventional salutation with a single, sharp nod. "I was ascending to my room and caught a peculiar voice inside, so I thought I'd drop in." Mr. Bunner chuckled lightly. "Perhaps I sounded like an eavesdropper?" he suggested. "Not at all, sir. I caught a whisper regarding a pistol—likely this one—and that was sufficient curiosity."

Mr. Bunner was a slender, somewhat short youth possessing a shaven, pale, and bony countenance that verged on the girlish, framed by large, dark, and intensely intelligent eyes. His thick, dark hair was parted severely in the center. Usually, his lips were obscured by a cigar; in its absence, they remained half-open in a perpetual expression of eager anticipation. Yet, whenever he smoked or chewed tobacco, this restless quality vanished, replaced by the consummate, cool, and sagacious demeanor of a seasoned Yankee businessman.

A native of Connecticut, Bunner had entered a brokerage firm immediately upon leaving college, quickly catching the eye of Manderson, whose dealings with the house he frequently managed. The Colossus observed him for some time before offering him the position of private secretary. While Manderson could have selected from many candidates sharing Bunner's virtues of trustworthiness, methodical precision, and long-term vision, he chose him specifically for his speed, discretion, and a singular, uncanny instinct for the movements of the stock market.

Trent and the American regarded one another with cool scrutiny, both seemingly content with what they perceived. "I was having it explained to me," Trent said amiably, "that my discovery of a pistol capable of ending Manderson's life amounts to little. I've been told it is a favored weapon in your part of the world and enjoys considerable popularity here."

Mr. Bunner extended a bony hand, retrieving the firearm from its case. "Yes, sir," he remarked, handling it with practiced familiarity. "The captain is correct. Back home, we call this 'Little Arthur,' and I wager there are hundreds of thousands of duplicates tucked into hip pockets this very second. Personally, I find it too light a grip," he mused, mechanically reaching beneath his jacket to produce a grimy-looking alternative. "Feel this, Mr. Trent—it's loaded, by the way. Now, this 'Little Arthur'..." He squinted along the sights. "Marlowe purchased it just prior to our arrival this year, simply to please the old man. Manderson insisted it was absurd for a gentleman to navigate the twentieth century unarmed. So, he acquired whatever was offered, apparently without consulting me. Though it is a decent piece, Marlowe struggled with it initially. However, I've given him coaching for the past month or so, and he has practiced until he is reasonably competent. Still, he never developed the habit of carrying it. Why, it feels as natural to me as wearing trousers. I have carried a weapon for several years, for there was always a likelihood someone was waiting to strike Manderson. And now," Mr. Bunner concluded with a sigh, "they have succeeded while I was absent. Gentlemen, I beg your pardon, but I must return to Bishopsbridge. A great deal requires attention, and I have to dispatch a series of telegrams substantial enough to strangle a bovine."

"I shall depart as well," Trent replied. "I have an engagement at the 'Three Tuns' inn."

"Allow me to convey you in the automobile," Mr. Bunner offered warmly. "My route passes directly by that establishment. Say, Captain, are you heading that way? No? Then join me, Mr. Trent, and assist us in extricating the vehicle. Our chauffeur is incapacitated, and we are forced to perform nearly all duties save for scrubbing the mud from her."

With his characteristic measured drawl, Mr. Bunner guided Trent downstairs and through the residence to the garage situated at the rear. It stood at a respectful distance from the main house, providing a cool sanctuary from the blazing midday sun.

Mr. Bunner displayed no haste in preparing the car. He proffered a cigar to Trent, which was graciously accepted; for the first time, he ignited his own. Seating himself on the running board, his thin hands clasped firmly between his knees, he scrutinized Trent with keen interest.

"Here, Mr. Trent," he said after a pause. "There are certain particulars I can impart that may prove valuable. I am acquainted with your record; you are a sharp operator, and I enjoy transacting business with those of similar acumen. I am uncertain if I have correctly assessed Detective Murch, but he appears to me as a mongrel. I would answer any inquiry he possessed the wit to pose—I have indeed done so—but I am disinclined to volunteer my theories without explicit solicitation. Observe?"

Trent nodded. "That sentiment is common in the presence of our law enforcement," he observed. "It stems, I presume, from official protocol. However, let me assure you, Murch is far from what you perceive. He is one of the most astute officers in Europe. While his mental agility is not rapid, his certainty is absolute, and his reservoir of experience is vast. My strength lies in imagination, yet in police work, I maintain that experience overwhelmingly supersedes conjecture."

"Nonsense!" Mr. Bunner retorted crisply. "This is no ordinary affair, Mr. Trent. Permit me to offer a rationale. I believe the old man foresaw an impending calamity. Furthermore, I suspect it was a threat he deemed impossible to evade."

Trent pulled a crate opposite Bunner's perch and sat down. "This sounds like professional counsel," he said. "Lay out your thoughts."

"I speak thus due to the transformation in the old man's demeanor over the last few weeks. You have likely heard, Mr. Trent, that he was a man who maintained rigorous self-control. Indeed, he was. I have always regarded him as the coldest, hardest head in commerce. His composure was lethal; I have witnessed nothing comparable. I knew Manderson intimately, more so than anyone else. I stood by him during the endeavors for which he truly lived. I probably knew him better than his wife, the poor soul, or even Marlowe, who rarely saw Manderson in his office during critical operations. I knew him better than any of his friends."

"Had he any friends?" Trent interjected.

Mr. Bunner glanced at him sharply. "Someone seems to be leading you astray, I gather," he noted. "Properly speaking, I should say no. He maintained numerous acquaintances among the powerful, individuals he saw daily, even engaging in yachting or hunting expeditions. Yet, I do not believe there existed a single individual whom Manderson allowed into the inner sanctum of his heart. But what I intended to convey is this: Several months ago, the old man began to resemble a stranger to me—gloomy and sullen, as if eternally brooding over some malevolent force he could not resolve. This state persisted without interruption, manifesting identically in town as at home, suggesting a heavy burden pressing upon his mind. However, it was only within the last few weeks that his self-restraint began to crumble. Let me tell you this, Mr. Trent"—the American placed his bony claw upon Trent's knee—"I am the sole confidant of this truth. With everyone else, he was merely morose and dull; but when alone with me in his office, or wherever we worked together, if the slightest error occurred, by George! he would explode with a fury that defied description. In this library, I have seen him tear open a letter containing a mere slight offense, launching into a tirade reminiscent of a wildcat, declaring he wished to have the writer pinned against the wall, screaming that he wouldn't harm him, and so forth, until it became pathetically absurd. I have never witnessed such a drastic metamorphosis. Here is another matter. For a week preceding his death, Manderson neglected his work, a phenomenon unprecedented in my observation. He refused to answer letters or cables, despite matters appearing poised to collapse across the ocean. I presumed this anxiety, whatever its nature, had eroded his nerves until they snapped. Once, I advised him to consult a physician, to which he retorted that I might as well go to hell. Fewer than I did saw this side of him. If he indulged in one of these rages in the library, and Mrs. Manderson entered the room, he would instantly revert to his customary cold and composed state."

"And you attribute this to some secret anxiety, a fear that designs were being plotted against his life?" Trent asked.

The American nodded affirmatively.

"I suppose," Trent continued, "you considered the possibility of a mental breakdown, perhaps due to overstrain. That is the initial hypothesis your account evokes. Moreover, isn't that the fate awaiting many of your prominent businessmen in America? It is the prevailing narrative in the press."
