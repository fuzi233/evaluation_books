Their night was a tempestuous one, casting a shadow of strangeness into the daylight that followed. Whether the terror of their slumber spilled over into their waking hours, or the fear of the day sank down into their dreams, the children found no rest, awake or asleep. Throughout the long vigil, something seemed to stir within the house—silent, terrible, and unknowable. No sound ever broke the stillness; the darkness merged with the silence, and that silence became the terror itself.

Once, a dreadful wind filled the rooms, shaking the structure so violently it quivered like a horse tossing its mane. Yet it was a wind without voice, making not even a moan in their chambers, passing away like a soundless sob. They slept again, only to wake with a start, convinced the house was flooding with the very water they had drunk earlier. It rose from below, swelling until the attic was submerged to the rafters. But it made no splash, no roar; when it receded, they fell asleep dry and warm.

On their next awakening, the air inside and out teemed with cats. They swarmed everywhere—up, down, across, surrounding the room. They felt tiny claws prying at the nightgowns Lady Mara had dressed them in, yet none could penetrate the fabric. By morning, not a single scratch marred their skin. Then, cutting through the dark, came the only sound they heard all night: the distant howl of the great-grandmother-cat in the desert. Assuming she called for her young, the swarm instantly halted, leaving absolute stillness. Once more, they slept deeply until sunrise.

Thus ended the children's account. But I remained with the veiled woman and the Princess throughout those hours. I witnessed fragments of the events; I felt much more; and there were layers visible only to the heart, understood only in measure.

As soon as Mara departed with the children, my gaze fell upon the white leopardess. We had believed her gone, yet there she cowered in a corner, trembling in mortal terror of whatever awaited. A lamp stood on the high mantelpiece; the room seemed alternately filled with dancing shadows and cloudy, indistinct forms. The Princess lay on the settle by the wall, seemingly frozen, unmoving in hand or foot. It was a fearful waiting.

When Mara returned, she pulled the settle with the Princess upon it to the center of the room. Sitting opposite me, across the hearth, she faced the crisis. Between us burned a small fire. Something terrible was approaching. The cloudy presences flickered and shook. From among them emerged a silvery creature, akin to a slowworm, crawling slowly across the clay floor and slipping into the flames. We sat motionless. The entity approached, yet the hours dragged on toward midnight with no change. The night was profoundly still—not a sound, not a rustle from the fire, not a crack in wood or beam. Occasionally, I felt a heaving sensation, unsure if it came from the earth, the air, the waters beneath, my own body, or my soul. A dread sense of judgment settled upon me. Yet I was not afraid; I cared for nothing save the task that must be done.

Suddenly, it was midnight. The veiled woman rose, turned toward the settle, and slowly unwound the long swathes hiding her face. They dropped to the floor, and she stepped over them. The Princess's feet pointed toward the hearth; Mara moved to her head, then turned to stand behind it. Then I saw her face. It was lovely beyond description—white and sad, a sadness of heart and soul, yet not unhappy, for I knew she could never truly be unhappy. Great tears streamed down her cheeks; she wiped them with her robe. Her countenance grew still, and though she wept no more, pity lined every feature. If not for that pity, she would have seemed severe.

She placed her hand on the Princess's head, resting on the low hairline of the forehead, and stooped to breathe upon the sallow brow. The body shuddered.

"Will you turn away from the wicked things you have done so long?" Mara asked gently.

The Princess did not answer. Mara repeated the question in the same soft, inviting tone. Still, there was no sign of hearing. On the third attempt, the seeming corpse opened its mouth. Words framed themselves, yet they were not mere sounds—they were vibrations of meaning.

"I will not," she said. "I will be myself and not another!"

"Alas, you are another now, not yourself! Will you not be your real self?"

"I will be what I mean myself now."

"If you were restored, would you not make amends for the misery you have caused?"

"I would do after my nature."

"You do not know it: your nature is good, and you do evil!"

"I will do as my Self pleases—as my Self desires."

"You will do as the Shadow, overshadowing your Self, inclines you?"

"I will do what I will to do."

"You have killed your daughter, Lilith!"

"I have killed thousands. She is my own!"

"She was never yours as you are another's."

"I am not another's; I am my own, and my daughter is mine."

"Then, alas, your hour is come!"

"I care not. I am what I am; no one can take from me myself!"

"You are not the Self you imagine."

"So long as I feel myself what it pleases me to think myself, I care not. I am content to be to myself what I would be. What I choose to seem to myself makes me what I am. My own thought makes me me; my own thought of myself is me. Another shall not make me!"

"But another has made you, and can compel you to see what you have made yourself. You will not be able much longer to look to yourself anything but what he sees you! You will not much longer have satisfaction in the thought of yourself. At this moment you are aware of the coming change!"

"No one ever made me. I defy that Power to unmake me from a free woman! You are his slave, and I defy you! You may be able to torture me—I do not know—but you shall not compel me to anything against my will!"

"Such a compulsion would be without value. But there is a light that goes deeper than the will, a light that illuminates the darkness behind it: that light can change your will, can make it truly yours and not another's—not the Shadow's. Into the created can pour itself the creating will, and so redeem it!"

"That light shall not enter me: I hate it! Begone, slave!"

"I am no slave, for I love that light, and will with the deeper will which created mine. There is no slave but the creature that wills against its creator. Who is a slave but she who cries, 'I am free,' yet cannot cease to exist!"

"You speak foolishness from a cowering heart! You imagine me given over to you: I defy you! I hold myself against you! What I choose to be, you cannot change. I will not be what you think me—what you say I am!"

"I am sorry: you must suffer!"

"But be free!"

"She alone is free who would make free; she loves not freedom who would enslave: she is herself a slave. Every life, every will, every heart that came within your ken, you have sought to subdue: you are the slave of every slave you have made—such a slave that you do not know it! See your own self!"

Mara withdrew her hand from the Princess's head and took two steps back. A soundless presence, like roaring flame, possessed the house—the same force the children had sensed as a silent wind. Involuntarily, I turned to the hearth. Its fire was a still, small, moveless glow. Yet I saw the worm-thing crawl forth, white-hot and vivid as incandescent silver, the living heart of essential fire.

It crawled along the floor toward the settle, moving very slowly. Even slower, it ascended the settle and laid itself at the Princess's feet, reluctant to proceed further. I rose and stole closer. Mara stood motionless, awaiting an event foreknown. The shining thing crawled onto a bare, bony foot; it showed no suffering, nor was the settle scorched where it had lain. Very slowly, it crept along her robe until it reached her bosom, disappearing among the folds.

The Princess's face lay stonily calm, eyelids closed as over dead eyes. For several minutes, nothing happened. Then, on her dry, parchment-like skin, drops appeared, like the finest dew. In moments, they grew as large as seed pearls, joined together, and began to pour down in streams.

I darted forward to snatch the worm from the poor, withered bosom and crush it with my foot. But Mara, Mother of Sorrow, stepped between us and drew aside the closed edges of the robe. No serpent was there; no searing trail remained. The creature had passed through the center of the black spot, piercing through the joints and marrow straight to the thoughts and intents of the heart.

The Princess gave one writhing, contorted shudder, and I knew the worm was lodged in her secret chamber.

"She is seeing herself!" Mara declared. Laying her hand on my arm, she drew me three paces away from the settle.
