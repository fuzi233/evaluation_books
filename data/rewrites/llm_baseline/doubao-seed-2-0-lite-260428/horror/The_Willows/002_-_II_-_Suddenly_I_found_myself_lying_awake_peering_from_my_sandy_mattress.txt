II. I jolted wide awake all at once, grit from the sandy mattress crammed in the corners of my eyes and sticking to the sweat slick on my throat, staring straight out through the gap in the tent’s canvas door. My watch was pinned to the inner wall of the fabric, and the moonlight bleaching the tent was bright enough to read the face clearly: past twelve, the thin, unmarked threshold of a brand new day, meaning I’d slept nearly two full hours. The Swede lay snoring heavy beside me, his breath thick with the dregs of the cheap brandy we’d shared over the campfire, dead to the world. The wind howled outside the canvas just as it had before we bedded down, but there was something new plucking at the back of my ribs, sharp and cold, pure unnameable dread. The disturbance was right on top of us, not a hundred feet away.

I sat up fast, sand spilling off my shirt onto the floor, and craned to look out. The willow trees were thrashing so hard in the gusts their thin branches lashed like bony, outstretched hands, but our little patch of green canvas was still tucked safe down in the hollow, the wind surging right over the top of it without catching hard enough to tear the stakes free. The unease didn’t fade, though, so I crawled quiet as possible toward the exit, careful not to jostle the tent and wake my companion, some thrumming, jittery excitement buzzing under my skin. I was halfway out, on my hands and knees in the cool sand, when my eyes snagged on the tops of the bushes opposite our camp, their moving leaves weaving dark patterns against the moonlit sky. I dropped back onto my heels, and stared.

It couldn’t be real, surely—but there, level with my line of sight and a little higher, half-hidden in the willow crowns not fifty feet from where I knelt, were shapes that had no business being there. As the branches swayed in the gale, they framed those indeterminate, monstrous outlines, shifting fast under the silver moon. My first instinct was to shake the Swede awake, make him see this too, but something clamped down on the urge in an instant: I did not want confirmation. I did not want proof these things were not a trick of my eyes. I crouched there, eyes stinging from wind-blown sand, completely, unarguably awake, saying the words out loud under my breath to be sure: I am not dreaming.

Slowly, they resolved fully in my vision: huge, bronze-colored as long-unpolished statuary, moving entirely independent of the sway of the boughs around them. They were far larger than any human could ever be, and every line of them screamed they were not human at all. They were not just shadow play cast by moving branches. They shifted of their own will, pouring upward in a continuous unbroken stream from the dark ground beneath the willows all the way up to the inky upper sky, vanishing the second they hit the dark haze above the treeline. They wove through one another, overlapping and melting into a single massive serpentine column, their huge blurred limbs and rippling bodies twisting in step with the contortions of the wind-torn trees. Nude, fluid, almost gliding inside the web of willow leaves, they rose in a living spiral straight up into the heavens. I never caught a glimpse of their faces. Unceasingly, they poured upward, swaying in great slow curves, that dull bronze hue glowing dim against the silver moonlight.

I strained my eyes until they burned, searching for any sign they would dissolve back into shadow, prove they were nothing but an optical illusion. I hunted for some scrap of normal reality to cling to, even as I knew deep down the old rules of reality had already broken. The longer I stared, the surer I was they were alive, and real—even if they did not fit the narrow standards biologists and camera film were built to measure. For a long moment I felt no fear at all, just an awe so heavy it settled in my bones, the kind of awe I imagined a caveman felt staring up at a volcano erupting. I was looking straight at the elemental, personified powers of this primeval, haunted island. We had intruded. Our presence had woken the old things that lived here, and my brain flooded with every old forgotten legend of the local spirits, the deities men had sacrificed to on the banks of this river a thousand years prior.

Before I could even begin to parse what I was seeing, something tugged at my sleeves, urging me out further. I crept forward across the sand and stood upright, bare feet pressing into the patch of earth that still held the faint warmth of the day’s sun. The wind tore at my hair and stung my cheeks, and the roar of the river hit my ears so loud I could feel the vibration in my teeth. These things were normal. These things were real, proof my senses were still working as they should. But the bronze shapes still rose, silent and majestic, coiling upward in that spiral of grace and old unchallenged strength, until I felt my knees buckling. I was seconds away from dropping to the sand and worshipping them—absolutely worshipping.

Then a gust of wind slammed into me so hard it knocked me sideways, I nearly stumbled face-first into the dirt, and the violent jolt shook that dumb reverie right out of my bones. The shapes were still there, still winding up out of the dark of the willow grove, but my reason kicked back into gear, fumbling for the familiar. It’s a hallucination, I told myself. The moonlight and the branches and my tired, overstretched brain are projecting these images onto the dark. That’s all it is. I took a wobbly step forward across the open sand. But—was it truly all in my head? Was my reason not just clinging to the tiny, narrow box of the “known” to avoid admitting something I could never explain?

I can’t say how long that great column of dark shapes poured upward. It felt like hours. It felt far more real than any ordinary thing I’d ever experienced in my life. Then, all at once, they were gone. No fading, no slow vanishing—just gone, leaving nothing but the empty thrashing of willow boughs under the moon.

The second they vanished, the wonder shattered, and cold fear crashed down over me like a bucket of iced river water. The hidden, terrible meaning of this lonely haunted spot flamed bright in my mind, and I began to shake so hard my teeth chattered. I cast a wild, panicking glance all around me, scanning for escape routes that did not exist—there was nowhere to run, the river too fast to swim, the willow grove stretching for endless miles in every direction. Realizing how utterly helpless we were, I scrambled back to the tent on silent hands and knees, dove inside, and dragged the canvas door curtain shut tight to block out every glimpse of the moonlit willows. I buried my head as deep as I could under the scratchy wool blankets, pressing my face into the gritty mattress to muffle the sound of the wind that now sounded like a thousand low, whispering voices calling my name.
