For a span of days—if indeed days were passing in any measure that could be trusted in that valley—I wanted nothing but to remain among the Little Ones, as a kettle set aside where the hearth is warm and the air is sweet. Their life had the rhythm of a household: waking, gathering, sharing, dozing; small hands always busy with some quiet craft of survival, as unshowy and constant as kneading. They fed me as naturally as they laughed. They mended me with fruit and touch and the nearness of their uncorrupted joy.

Yet even in the richest kitchen, a cook begins to listen for what is missing.

It came first as a vague shame, like the aftertaste of smoke when one has stood too long in the wrong wind: I ought to be doing something. I was not made for the fattening of boors. Then, sharper, the sense of where I was took hold of me with the grip of hunger. This world was marvellous, and its laws were not a spectacle to be admired from a bench: they were a recipe to be learned. If I meant to give the children anything in return for their goodness, I must discover what they could not tell me. And to learn, I must be free to move.

For there was a fact that would not soften, however I turned it, like a bone in the mouth: the growth of the Little Ones was arrested. Something interfered with it. Lona seemed the eldest, and yet she was no more than fifteen in semblance—long in charge of many who were, in look and mostly in behaviour, mere children, and who regarded her as their mother. Were they growing at all? I doubted it. Of time they had scarcely an idea; of their own age they knew nothing. Lona herself spoke and carried herself as though she had lived always. She was full of wisdom and empty of knowledge—if indeed what I called ignorance was not rather my own failure of perception. She was at once their Love and their Law.

Her one anxiety, plain as salt on the tongue, was that her Little Ones should not grow and change into bad giants.

Their “good giant,” therefore, was bound to do his best for them; but without more knowledge of their nature, and some knowledge of their history, I could do nothing. To stay was to remain a kindly animal in a pen—loved, perhaps, but useless. Worse: I brought danger. They had not become dependent on me; they were still my protectors. My presence was like a bright lamp in the bad giants’ dark: it drew the heavy feet.

I longed to teach them many things. Their voices were little streams of melody—phrases murmured and forgotten as quickly as a scent on the air. Might they not be taught to set down those tunes, to keep them from dying on the tongue? Might they not learn numbers and measures, the careful kind of knowledge that does not trample feeling but can preserve it—like a food historian writing a grandmother’s method for bread so it does not vanish when her dialect vanishes? Yet to teach, I must first understand those I would teach. Knowledge makes bad people worse, no doubt; but it must make good people better.

So my conclusion formed itself: I must rise and go on, in the hope of coming upon some elucidation of the fortunes and destiny of the bewitching little creatures. Still, my design would not have passed so quickly into action, but for what now occurred.

I was at work one day—busy with some simple labour they delighted to share, the sort that makes a body feel useful and calms the mind with repetition—talking as we worked. I told them, to prepare them for my temporary absence, that I would long ago have left the bad giants, but that I loved the Little Ones so much—

As if I had dropped a handful of grain and startled a whole yard of sparrows, they came rushing upon me at once. They scrambled over each other and up the tree and dropped on my head until I was nearly smothered. Three very little ones were in my arms; one clung to each shoulder, their warm fingers hooked about my neck; one stood straight up on my head as if I were a stool; four or five held my legs; others grappled my body and arms, and a multitude climbed and descended upon these, a tumbling, laughing heap. Their light bodies had a surprising density in their number. I was helpless—overwhelmed as by lava, only this lava squealed and giggled and smelled of leaves and fruit.

Absorbed in that merry struggle, not one of them saw my tyrant coming until he was almost upon me.

There was just one cry—thin with terror, sharp as a bird’s alarm—“Take care, good giant!” and at once they ran from me like mice. They dropped from me like hedgehogs. They flew up the tree like squirrels. In the same moment, sharp round the stem came the bad giant, and with a stick he dealt me such a blow upon the head that the world went out, as a candle goes out when a wet thumb pinches it. I fell to the ground.

Afterward, they told me they sent him “such a many bumps of big apples and stones” that he was frightened and ran blundering home. I could imagine it: their small arms, quick as kitchen hands passing tools, flinging what came to them—fruit and stone alike—without malice, only with the fierce instinct to defend what they loved.

When I came to myself it was night. Above me were a few pale stars waiting for the moon. I thought I was alone. My head ached badly. My mouth was so dry it felt lined with dust and bark. I was terribly athirst.

I turned wearily on my side, and the moment my ear touched the ground I heard it: the gushing and gurgling of water. The sound was not loud, but it was near, and it moved through the earth with a tenderness that made my throat ache worse. Those soft noises made me groan with longing, as one who has smelled broth simmering after a fast.

At once I was amid a multitude of silent children. Delicious little fruits began to visit my lips. They came and came—cool skins, burst sweetness, a tang like rain held in flesh—until my thirst was gone, and my tongue remembered itself. Then, as my hearing cleared, I became aware of sounds I had never heard there before: the air was full of little sobs. Not cries, not wails, but the small brokenness of creatures trying to keep quiet and failing.

I tried to sit up. Instantly a pile of small bodies heaped itself at my back, bracing me like sacks of grain stacked for a fall. I struggled to my feet with much pushing and pulling from the Little Ones—wonderfully strong for their size, as if their strength had gone inward when their height would not.

“You must go away, good giant,” they said.

“When the bad giants see you hurt, they will all trample on you.”

“I think I must,” I answered. The admission had the bitter clarity of medicine.

“Go and grow strong, and come again,” they said.

“I will,” I replied—and sat down, for the world still swayed.

“Indeed you must go at once!” whispered Lona, who had been supporting me, and now knelt beside me. Her voice had that quality hers often held: not loud, not commanding, but carrying weight, as if it belonged to the place.

One of the bigger boys stepped close. “I listened at his door,” he said, “and heard the bad giant say to his wife that he had found you idle, talking to a lot of moles and squirrels, and when he beat you, they tried to kill him. He said you were a wizard, and they must knock you, or they would have no peace.”

“I will go at once,” I said, and the decision steadied me. “And come back as soon as I have found out what is wanted to make you bigger and stronger.”

“We don’t want to be bigger,” they answered, looking very serious, as if I had offered them some fashionable food that tasted of nothing and cost too much. “We WON’T grow bad giants!—We are strong now; you don’t know how much strong!”

There was no use holding before them a prospect that had no attraction. Their refusal was not obstinacy; it was identity. They spoke as if bigness were not a gift but a language that would replace their own, and with it would come a whole lexicon of dominance—heavy words, heavy hands. In their world, to “grow” did not mean simply to become more: it meant to become other, perhaps enemy.

So I said nothing more. I rose and moved slowly up the slope of the valley.

At once they formed themselves into a long procession. Some led the way, feeling the ground with their feet as if reading it. Some walked beside me helping me, hands under my arms, palms against my back. The rest followed, a small river of bodies and whispers. They kept feeding me as we went, not with solemnity, but with the habitual care of those who have always known that life is maintained by passing what one has to the one who needs it.

“You are broken,” they said. “And much red juice has run out of you: put some in.” And they pressed more fruit to my mouth, as if fruit were a kind of blood that could be shared. It had the taste of sun and sap. The smell of it rose in my nostrils and made me think—absurdly, painfully—of my own world’s kitchens, where everything is measured and named, and yet the naming sometimes kills the thing it tries to keep. Here, their care had no labels, only practice.

When we reached the edge of the valley, there was the moon, just lifting her forehead over the rim of the horizon.

“She has come to take care of you, and show you the way,” said Lona.

We were taking the second branch of the river-bed, its stones pale under the moonlight. The night had its own acoustic ecology: water speaking to itself in the dark, insects making their thin instruments, leaves shivering together like paper. Even the children’s feet had a sound—soft taps and scuffs, never the brutal stamp I had learned to dread from the bad giants. I found myself thinking how a land’s noises train its hearts. Where everything roars, people must shout to exist; where the world murmurs, tenderness can be heard.

As we walked, I questioned those about me, and learned there was a great place with a giant-girl for queen. When I asked if it was a city, they said they did not know. Neither could they tell how far off, or in what direction it was, or what the giant-girl’s name; all they knew was that she hated the Little Ones, and would like to kill them—only she could not find them.

“How do you know that?” I asked.

Lona answered simply, “I have always known it.”

If the giant-girl came to look for them, they must hide hard, she said, as if speaking of a storm that never ceased to exist, only sometimes moved nearer. When I told them I should go and ask her why she hated them, they cried out at once, voices overlapping like frightened birds:

“No, no! she will kill you, good giant; she will kill you! She is an awful bad-giant witch!”

“Where am I to go then?” I asked, for the world was suddenly wide again, and I had no map but the moon.

They told me that beyond the baby-forest, away where the moon came from, lay a smooth green country, pleasant to the feet, without rocks or trees. The idea of ground that did not bruise, of walking without always looking down, came to me with a bodily gratitude.

“But how am I to set out for it?” I asked.

“The moon will tell you, we think,” they answered, as if the moon were an elder with a sure memory, one who kept the old routes in her pale face when the living forgot.

When they saw that the moon had reached her height, they stopped to return.

“We have never gone so far from our trees before,” they said, and there was a tremor in it—not fear of danger so much as the discomfort of leaving the only vocabulary of place they possessed. Their land was not merely where they lived; it was what they were. I remembered how once, when I suggested they should leave the country of the bad giants and go with me to find another, they answered, “But that would be to NOT ourselves!” Their love of place was so strong that their country seemed essential to their very being—like a mother-tongue: not a tool, but a home. To tear them from it would be to force upon them a dominant speech in which their finest feelings might not fit.

“Now mind you watch how you go,” they said, “that you may see inside your eyes how to come back to us.”

“I will,” I promised, though the promise sat heavy; for I knew how easily the eye forgets what the body cannot repeat.

“And beware of the giant-woman that lives in the desert,” said one of the bigger girls as they were turning, with the eager relish of passing on lore.

“I suppose you have heard of her!”

“No,” I answered.

“Then take care not to go near her. She is called the Cat-woman. She is awfully ugly—AND SCRATCHES.”

As soon as the bigger ones stopped, the smaller had begun to run back, as if the nearer darkness behind them were suddenly full of something calling. The others looked at me gravely for a moment, then walked slowly away.

Last to leave me, Lona held up the baby to be kissed. The little face, half in moonlight, had the soft seriousness of bread before it rises—promise contained in stillness. I kissed it, and its skin smelled faintly of milk-fruit and leaf.

Lona gazed into my eyes, and her gaze was as steady as the moon’s path.

“The Cat-woman will not hurt YOU,” she whispered, and went without another word.

I stood a while, watching them through the moonlight until their bodies were only motions and then only absence. Then I turned and, with a heavy heart, began my solitary journey.

Soon their laughter overtook me. It came behind like sheep-bells innumerable, rippling the air, echoing among the rocks. It was a music without commerce—unbottled, unperformed, unadvertised—only life sounding itself. I turned again and gazed after them: they went gamboling along with never a care in their sweet souls. But Lona walked apart with her baby, as if carrying not merely a child but the whole fragile archive of their people.

As I went, I pondered many traits of my little friends. Without ambition or fear, discomfort or greed, they had no motive to desire change. They knew of nothing amiss. Except their babies, they had never had a chance of helping any one but myself. How were they to grow—if growth meant accumulation of knowledge, or widening of want? But again—why should they grow? In seeking to improve their conditions, might I not do them harm, and only harm? To enlarge their minds after the notions of my world—might it not be to distort and weaken them, like translating a song into a language that has no vowels for its sorrow? Their fear of growth as the possible start of gianthood might be instinctive, a wisdom their mouths could not define.

The part of philanthropist is indeed a dangerous one. The man who would do his neighbour good must first study how not to do him evil—and must begin by pulling the beam out of his own eye.

With the moon ahead and their laughter dying behind, I walked on, tasting still the fruit they had pressed on my lips, and feeling in my bruised head not only pain, but the pressure of an unfinished duty—like dough that has been mixed and must now be tended, or it will sour and be wasted.
