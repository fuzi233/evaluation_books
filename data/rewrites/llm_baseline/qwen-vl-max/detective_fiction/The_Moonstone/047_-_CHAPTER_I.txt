Few words are needed from me to complete the story that has already been told in Ezra Jennings’s journal.

As for myself, I can only say that when I awoke on the morning of the twenty-sixth, I had no memory at all of what I had said or done under the influence of opium—from the moment the drug took hold of me until I opened my eyes in Rachel’s sitting-room.

What happened after I regained consciousness, I do not feel the need to recount in detail. Suffice it to say, Rachel and I understood each other perfectly before either of us spoke a word. I won’t explain how quickly we reconciled—nor will Rachel. Sir and Madam, if you recall the depth of your own feelings when love was fresh and strong, you’ll understand exactly what passed between us after Ezra Jennings closed the door of that room.

Still, I may add this: we would have been discovered by Mrs. Merridew, had it not been for Rachel’s quick thinking. She heard the rustle of the old lady’s dress in the corridor and rushed out to meet her. I overheard Mrs. Merridew ask, “What is the matter?” and Rachel replied, “The explosion!” At once, Mrs. Merridew allowed herself to be gently led by the arm into the garden, safely away from the expected blast. When she returned, she met me in the hall and remarked with great surprise at how much science had advanced since her school days. “Explosions, Mr. Blake,” she declared, “are infinitely milder than they used to be. I barely heard Mr. Jennings’s little experiment from the garden. And no smell at all now we’re back inside! I must truly apologize to your medical friend—he handled it beautifully!”

Thus, after overcoming Betteredge and Mr. Bruff, Ezra Jennings also subdued Mrs. Merridew. There remains more liberal feeling in the world than one might suppose.

At breakfast, Mr. Bruff made no attempt to hide his reason for wanting me to accompany him to London by the morning train. The watch set up at the bank, and the possible outcome, stirred Rachel’s curiosity so strongly that she immediately decided—provided Mrs. Merridew had no objection—to return to town with us, so she could hear the earliest news of our efforts.

Mrs. Merridew, charmed by the well-behaved nature of the “explosion,” proved entirely agreeable. Betteredge was then informed that we four would travel together. I expected he’d ask to join us, but Rachel had cleverly given him a task that held his attention: finishing the refurnishing of the house. Burdened with domestic duties, he was too absorbed to catch the detective fever he might otherwise have felt.

Our only regret in leaving for London was having to part so abruptly from Ezra Jennings. We couldn’t persuade him to come with us. All I could promise was to write; all Rachel could insist upon was that he visit her when she returned to Yorkshire. Though we were certain to meet again in a few months, there was something deeply sorrowful in seeing our dearest friend standing alone on the platform as the train pulled away.

Upon arriving in London, Mr. Bruff was approached at the station by a small boy wearing threadbare black jacket and trousers. He stood out due to his extraordinary eyes—so prominent and loosely rolling that one couldn’t help wondering how they stayed in their sockets. After listening to the boy, Mr. Bruff turned to the ladies and asked if they would mind if he joined us briefly before returning to Portland Place. I barely had time to assure Rachel I’d return and tell her everything before Mr. Bruff grabbed my arm and hurried me into a cab. The boy with the unstable eyes climbed onto the driver’s seat, and the driver was directed to Lombard Street.

“News from the bank?” I asked as we moved off.

“News about Mr. Luker,” replied Mr. Bruff. “An hour ago, he was seen leaving his house in Lambeth in a cab, accompanied by two men recognized by my men as plainclothes police officers. If Mr. Luker’s fear of the Indians is behind this precaution, the conclusion is clear—he’s going to remove the Diamond from the bank.”

“And we’re going to the bank to see what happens?”

“Yes—or to learn what’s already happened, if it’s over by now. Did you notice my boy on the box?”

“I noticed his eyes.”

Mr. Bruff chuckled. “They call the poor lad ‘Gooseberry’ at the office,” he said. “I employ him for errands—and I wish my clerks who gave him that name were half as reliable as he is. Gooseberry is one of the sharpest boys in London, Mr. Blake, despite his eyes.”

It was twenty minutes to five when we arrived at the bank in Lombard Street. Gooseberry looked longingly at his master as he opened the cab door.

“Do you want to come in too?” Mr. Bruff asked kindly. “Come in, then, and stay close to me until further orders.” He whispered to me, “He’s as fast as lightning—two words will suffice for Gooseberry where others need twenty.”

We entered the bank. The outer office, with its long counter where cashiers sat, was crowded—people waiting to withdraw or deposit money before closing time at five.

Two men in the crowd approached Mr. Bruff as soon as he appeared.

“Well,” the lawyer asked, “have you seen him?”

“He passed us here half an hour ago, sir, and went into the inner office.”

“Has he come out yet?”

“No, sir.”

Mr. Bruff turned to me. “Let’s wait,” he said.

I scanned the crowd for the three Indians. Not a trace of them. The only person with noticeably dark skin was a tall man in a pilot coat and round hat, looking like a sailor. Could this be one of them in disguise? Impossible—the man was taller than any of the Indians, and his face, where visible above a thick black beard, was far broader than theirs.

“They must have a spy somewhere,” Mr. Bruff muttered, eyeing the sailor. “And he may be the one.”

Before he could speak further, his coat-tail was gently tugged by the boy with the gooseberry eyes. Mr. Bruff looked where the boy pointed. “Hush!” he said. “Here comes Mr. Luker!”

The money-lender emerged from the inner office, followed by his two plainclothes guardians.

“Keep your eye on him,” Mr. Bruff whispered. “If he hands the Diamond to anyone, it will be here.”

Mr. Luker walked slowly toward the door, weaving through the crowd—sometimes near, sometimes far. I clearly saw his hand move as he passed a short, stout man in a sober grey suit. The man flinched slightly and glanced after him. Mr. Luker continued onward. At the exit, his guards flanked him. All three were followed by one of Mr. Bruff’s men—and I saw them no more.

I turned to the lawyer, then gestured toward the man in grey. “Yes!” Mr. Bruff whispered. “I saw it too.” He spun around, searching for his second man—but the man was gone. Then he looked for Gooseberry. The boy had vanished.

“What in the devil does this mean?” Mr. Bruff snapped. “They’ve both deserted us at the worst possible moment.”

It was now the man in grey’s turn at the counter. He handed in a cheque, received a receipt, and turned to leave.

“What should we do?” Mr. Bruff asked. “We can’t follow him without losing dignity.”

“I can,” I said. “I wouldn’t let that man out of my sight for ten thousand pounds.”

“In that case,” Mr. Bruff replied, “I wouldn’t lose sight of *you* for twice that amount.” He muttered to himself, “A fine role for a man in my position. For Heaven’s sake, don’t mention this—I’d be ruined if it got out.”

The man in grey stepped into an omnibus heading west. We followed. I swear there was still youth left in Mr. Bruff—he blushed when he sat down.

The man stopped the omnibus in Oxford Street and stepped out. We followed. He entered a chemist’s shop.

Mr. Bruff jumped. “My chemist!” he exclaimed. “I’m afraid we’ve made a mistake.”

We entered the shop. Mr. Bruff exchanged a few private words with the owner. He rejoined me with a dejected expression.

“It’s to our credit,” he said, taking my arm and leading me out, “that’s one comfort.”

“What’s to our credit?” I asked.

“Mr. Blake—you and I are the two worst amateur detectives ever to try the trade. The man in grey has worked for the chemist for thirty years. He came to pay money into his employer’s account—and knows nothing of the Moonstone.”

I asked what we should do next.

“Back to my office,” Mr. Bruff said. “Gooseberry and my second man must have followed someone else. Let’s hope they kept their eyes open.”

When we reached Gray’s Inn Square, the second man had already arrived, waiting over a quarter-hour.

“Well?” Mr. Bruff asked.

“I’m sorry, sir,” the man replied, “but I’ve made a mistake. I swore I saw Mr. Luker pass something to an elderly gentleman in a light-coloured paletot. But the gentleman turns out to be a respectable ironmonger in Eastcheap.”

“Where’s Gooseberry?” Mr. Bruff asked, resigned.

The man stared. “I don’t know, sir. I haven’t seen him since leaving the bank.”

Mr. Bruff dismissed him. “Either Gooseberry ran off,” he said to me, “or he’s investigating on his own. What do you say to dining here? Maybe he’ll return in an hour or two. I’ve got good wine in the cellar, and we can get a chop from the coffee-house.”

We dined at Mr. Bruff’s chambers. Before the cloth was removed, a visitor was announced—a man wishing to speak to the lawyer. Was it Gooseberry? No—only the man sent to tail Mr. Luker after he left the bank.

His report was unremarkable. Mr. Luker returned home, dismissed his guards, and didn’t go out again. By dusk, shutters were drawn, doors bolted. The area was watched—no sign of the Indians, no suspicious figures. He reported these facts and waited for instructions. Mr. Bruff sent him home.

“Do you think Mr. Luker brought the Moonstone home?” I asked.

“Not likely,” Mr. Bruff replied. “He wouldn’t have dismissed those policemen if he intended to keep the Diamond in his house.”

We waited another half-hour for the boy—without success. It was time for Mr. Bruff to go to Hampstead, and for me to return to Rachel in Portland Place. I left my card with the porter, with a note saying I’d be at my lodgings at half past ten. The card was to be delivered to the boy if he returned.

Some men keep appointments; others don’t. I belong to the latter. Add to that: I spent the evening at Portland Place, seated beside Rachel in a forty-foot-long room, with Mrs. Merridew at the far end. Can anyone blame me for getting home at half past twelve instead of half past ten? How utterly heartless such a person must be—and how earnestly I hope I never meet them!

My servant handed me a scrap of paper when I entered.

In neat legal handwriting: “If you please, sir, I am getting sleepy. I will come back tomorrow morning, between nine and ten.” Inquiry revealed that a boy with unusually prominent eyes had called, presented my card, waited an hour, slept and woke repeatedly, written the message, and left—after solemnly informing the servant, “I’m fit for nothing unless I get my night’s rest.”

At nine the next morning, I was ready. At half past nine, I heard footsteps outside. “Come in, Gooseberry!” I called.

“Thank you, sir,” answered a grave, melancholy voice. The door opened. I leapt to my feet—and faced—Sergeant Cuff!

“I thought I’d stop by, Mr. Blake, just in case you were in town, before writing to Yorkshire,” he said.

He looked as gaunt and dull as ever. His eyes still held that familiar trick—of seeming to expect more from you than you realize. But his clothes transformed him beyond recognition: a broad-brimmed white hat, a light shooting jacket, white trousers, drab gaiters, and a sturdy oak stick. He seemed determined to appear as though he’d lived in the countryside all his life. When I complimented him on his transformation, he took it seriously. He complained gravely of London’s noise and smells. I swear he even spoke with a faint rural accent! I offered him breakfast. The countryman was horrified—his breakfast was at half past six, and he retired with the chickens.

“I only returned from Ireland last night,” Sergeant Cuff said, shifting to business. “Before bed, I read your letter about events since my inquiry was suspended. One thing I must say: I completely misjudged the case. How any man could have seen things clearly under my circumstances, I don’t pretend to know. But facts remain unchanged. I admit I made a mess of it—not the first in my career. Only in books do detectives never make mistakes.”

“You’ve come just in time to restore your reputation,” I said.

“I beg your pardon, Mr. Blake,” he replied. “Now that I’ve retired, I care nothing for reputation. I’m here out of gratitude to the late Lady Verinder’s generosity. I’ll return to work—if you want me and trust me—on that basis alone. No money shall change hands. This is a matter of honor. Now, tell me, how does the case stand since your last letter?”

I recounted the opium experiment and the events at the bank. He found the experiment remarkable—entirely new to him. He was especially intrigued by Ezra Jennings’s theory about what I did with the Diamond after leaving Rachel’s sitting-room on her birthday.

“I don’t agree with Mr. Jennings that you hid the Moonstone,” Sergeant Cuff said. “But I do believe you must have taken it back to your own room.”

“Well?” I asked. “Then what happened?”

“Have you any suspicion?”

“None.”

“Does Mr. Bruff?”

“None either.”

Sergeant Cuff rose and retrieved a sealed envelope from my writing table. Marked “Private,” addressed to me, signed by him.

“I suspected the wrong person last year,” he said. “I may be wrong now. Wait to open this until you’ve uncovered the truth. Then compare the guilty party’s name with the one I’ve written here.”

I placed the letter in my pocket, then asked for his opinion on our bank surveillance.

“Very well-intentioned,” he replied, “and quite right in principle. But one other person should have been watched besides Mr. Luker.”

“The person named in the letter?”

“Yes, Mr. Blake—the person in the letter. Too late now. I’ll propose something later. Let’s wait and see if the boy has anything worth hearing.”

It was nearly ten. The boy hadn’t arrived. Sergeant Cuff talked of other matters—asked after Betteredge and the gardener. He was about to shift to roses when my servant interrupted: the boy was downstairs.

When brought in, Gooseberry paused at the doorway, eyeing the stranger warily. I told him to come forward.

“You may speak before this gentleman,” I said. “He’s here to help me and knows everything. Sergeant Cuff, this is the boy from Mr. Bruff’s office.”

In modern society, fame—no matter how slight—is a powerful force. Even Gooseberry knew of the great Cuff. His eyes rolled so wildly at the name I feared they’d fall to the floor.

“Come here, my lad,” the Sergeant said, “and tell us what you’ve seen.”

The boy’s awe was evident. He stood before the Sergeant, hands behind his back, like a child being examined.

“What’s your name?” the Sergeant asked.

“Octavius Guy,” the boy replied. “They call me Gooseberry because of my eyes.”

“Octavius Guy, otherwise Gooseberry,” the Sergeant continued gravely, “you were missing at the bank yesterday. What were you doing?”

“If you please, sir, I was following a man.”

“Who?”

“A tall man, sir, with a big black beard, dressed like a sailor.”

“I remember him!” I interjected. “Mr. Bruff and I thought he was a spy for the Indians.”

Sergeant Cuff didn’t seem impressed. He pressed on.

“Well? Why did you follow him?”

“Because Mr. Bruff wanted to know if Mr. Luker passed anything to anyone. I saw Mr. Luker give something to the sailor with the black beard.”

“Why didn’t you tell Mr. Bruff?”

“I didn’t have time—the sailor left so fast.”

“And you ran after him?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Gooseberry,” the Sergeant said, patting his head, “you’ve got something in that little skull of yours—and it’s not cotton wool. I’m very pleased with you so far.”

The boy flushed with pride. The Sergeant continued.

“Well? What did the sailor do when he reached the street?”

“He called a cab, sir.”

“And you?”

“I held on behind and ran after it.”

Before the Sergeant could continue, another visitor was announced—the head clerk from Mr. Bruff’s office.

Feeling it important not to interrupt the examination, I received the clerk in another room. He brought bad news: Mr. Bruff had suffered a gout attack and was confined to his Hampstead room. In our current crisis, he was deeply troubled at being unable to advise me. The chief clerk had been instructed to assist me and was willing to step in.

I wrote immediately to reassure Mr. Bruff, informing him of Sergeant Cuff’s arrival, that Gooseberry was under questioning, and promising updates. I sent the clerk off with the note and returned to find Sergeant Cuff at the fireplace, ringing the bell.

“I beg your pardon, Mr. Blake,” he said. “I was about to send your servant to tell you I needed to speak with you. I’m certain this boy—this most worthy boy,” he added, patting Gooseberry, “followed the right man. Precious time was lost because you weren’t home at half past ten. The only thing to do now is send for a cab immediately.”

Five minutes later, Sergeant Cuff and I—Gooseberry on the box—were heading eastward toward the City.

“One of these days,” the Sergeant said, pointing through the cab window, “this boy will achieve great things in my former profession. He’s the brightest and cleverest young chap I’ve met in years. You’ll hear what he told me while you were away. You were present when he mentioned holding on behind the cab and running after it?”

“Yes.”

“Then you’ll understand why I’m convinced we’ve missed nothing.”
