Handed over to the matron, I was led down a long, narrow passage into a foreign kitchen so spotless it felt almost alien. No obvious fireplace, no oven met my eye; I did not yet know that the great hulking black furnace crammed into one corner did the work of both. I told myself the quiet flicker of relief I felt when we did not stop there, as I’d half feared, had nothing to do with foolish pride. We pushed on to a tiny inner room called a “cabinet”, where a cook in a short petticoat and wooden sabots brought my supper: meat of an unknown sort served in a sharp, odd but pleasant sauce, savoury chopped potatoes I suspected were tossed with vinegar and sugar, a buttered slice of bread called a tartine, and a warm baked pear. I was ravenous, so I ate every bite, and felt small, unnameable gratitude.

After evening prayers, Madame herself came to look in on me once more, and bade me follow her up the stairs. We wound through a warren of peculiar little dormitories, rooms I later learned had once been nuns’ cells, for half the house dated back centuries, then through the oratory: a long, low, gloomy space where a pale crucifix hung on the wall and two tapers burned dim, keeping lonesome vigil. She led me to a room where three small children slept in three tiny beds. A overheated stove made the air thick and oppressive, and it was scented with an unexpected, heady fume: smoke tangled with the sharp tang of spirits, unmistakably the smell of whisky.

Beside a table where the last of a candle guttered down in its socket sat a coarse woman, fast asleep, clad in the strangest mismatched outfit: a showy, broad-striped silk gown over a plain linen apron. At her elbow, a bottle and empty glass completed the scene. Madame Beck regarded this tableau with perfect calm. No smile tugged at her lips, no scowl knotted her brow; no flicker of anger, disgust, or surprise ruffled the smooth, grave serenity of her face. She did not even wake the woman. Serenely she pointed to a fourth small bed, my assigned place, then blew out the dying candle, replaced it with a soft night-light, and glided through a half-ajar inner door that led to her own large, richly furnished bedchamber.

My prayers that night were all thanksgiving. I had been guided through strange turns since dawn, provided for in ways I had never dared hope for. It scarcely felt possible that less than forty-eight hours had passed since I left London, with no more protection than that which guards a lone migrating bird, no more promise than the faint, wispy tracery of hope in the clouds.

I was a light sleeper, and woke abruptly in the dead of night. The house was utterly still, but a white figure stood by my bed: Madame, in her nightgown, moving with no sound at all. She checked on each of her three sleeping children in turn, then turned to me. I feigned deep sleep, and she studied my face for a long, curious quarter of an hour, sitting on the edge of my mattress. She leaned closer, pushed back the edge of my nightcap to expose my hair, then picked up my hand where it lay on the bedclothes and turned it over, examining the lines of my palm. Next she turned to the chair at the foot of the bed where my clothes were laid. I opened my eyes very carefully, curious to see just how far her urge to research would take her. It took her quite far: she inspected every single article I owned, no doubt judging my station, my means, my habits of neatness from every thread. She turned my dress pocket completely inside out, counted the coins in my purse, opened my little memorandum book and read every page, and plucked the small plaited lock of Miss Marchmont’s grey hair from between its leaves. She paid special attention to my three keys, for my trunk, desk, and work-box, and stepped back into her own room for a moment with them. I raised my head softly to watch, and saw her press each key into a cake of wax on her dressing table to make a perfect impression. When she had finished every last detail of her search, she folded all my clothes back perfectly, tucked every item back where she found it. Not a single line on her stone face revealed what conclusions she had drawn.

Satisfied her duty was done — and it was clear, to her, this quiet intrusion was a duty — she rose as noiseless as a shadow, and turned back toward her own room. At the door, she paused to glance at the still snoring, whisky-soaked governess, and I read her quiet, immutable resolve at once. Madame did not rush to punish shortcomings, but her retribution was always sure. Nothing here was English, I thought to myself. I was truly in a foreign land.

The next day I learned the full story of Mrs Sweeny. She had presented herself to Madame as an English lady of reduced circumstances, a native of Middlesex who claimed to speak perfect metropolitan London English. Madame, unafraid to hire staff off the strength of her own well-tested tricks for digging up the truth in time, had taken her on as nursery governess to her three children. It soon turned out she was Irish, her native brogue peeking through her forced mincing cockney lilt, and while she claimed to have raised the son and daughter of a marquis, I suspected she had once been a nurse, foster-sister, or even a laundress in some grand Irish family. Her wardrobe was full of ill-fitting, expensive cast-off silk gowns and lace-trimmed caps, but the greatest prize of all, the thing that kept her from being dismissed immediately for a whole month, was a real Indian cashmere shawl — un véritable cachemire, as Madame called it, with unfeigned awe and reverence. Without that shawl, no one would have tolerated her for two days.

The second Mrs Sweeny realised I had been brought in to replace her, she erupted, raging at Madame first, then turning all her fury on me. Madame bore the outburst with such stoic composure that I could not help but match her calm. She stepped out of the room for just ten minutes, and returned with a policeman. Mrs Sweeny and all her belongings were marched straight out of the house. Madame’s brow had not once furrowed, not one sharp word had crossed her lips. The entire affair was settled before breakfast: the mutineer expelled, the nursery fumigated and scrubbed, windows thrown wide, every last trace of that fatal whisky scent scoured from the Rue Fossette house — all of it done in the small stretch of time between Madame emerging from her room like the dawn, and her sitting down to pour her first cup of coffee.

Around noon, I was called to help dress Madame. My post, it seemed, was a hybrid between nursery governess and lady’s maid. Till that hour, she had wandered the house in her wrapping gown, shawl, and noiseless soft slippers, a custom no strict English school principal would ever have countenanced. I fumbled awkwardly with her thick, auburn hair, untouched by grey even at forty. Seeing my embarrassment, she laughed gently, took the brush from my hand, and arranged it herself, half guiding, half helping me through the rest of her toilette without a spark of temper. That was the first and last time I was ever asked to dress her; that duty was passed off to Rosine the portress from then on.

Fully dressed, Madame Beck was a short, stout woman, yet graceful in her own way, the grace that comes from perfectly proportioned limbs. Her complexion was fresh and warm, not too ruddy, her blue eyes serene. Her dark silk dress fit her as no English seamstress could have managed, stitched to absolute perfection by a local dressmaker. She looked the very picture of a respectable French bourgeoise, a quiet harmony radiating from her whole person — yet her face held sharp contrasts. Her features were stern, her forehead high but narrow, speaking of capability and a quiet sort of benevolence, but no breadth of mind, no softness. Her peaceful, watchful eyes never held the fire that burns in the heart, nor the tenderness that flows from it. Her mouth was hard, thin-lipped, capable of a grim little line. I knew at once that for all the soft sentimentality and rash passion of the tender-hearted, Madame would make a perfect Minos in petticoats.

In time I learned she was far more complex than that. Her full name was Modeste Maria Beck, née Kint, but it might as well have been Ignacia, for all her unyielding purpose. She was genuinely charitable, and did a great deal of quiet good. No mistress ever ruled a school with a lighter apparent hand. She had never once remonstrated with the drunken, neglectful Mrs Sweeny, but the woman had been shown the door the very second her departure became convenient. No teacher or master was ever openly scolded, yet staff vanished quietly every few months, no one quite able to explain how or why. Her establishment held both boarders and day pupils: more than a hundred day girls, and roughly twenty boarders. Madame ran all of them, plus four teachers, eight masters, six servants, and her own three children, while managing every pupil’s parents and friends to absolute perfection — and she did it all with no visible effort, no bustle, no fatigue, no overexcitement. She was always occupied, but never rushed. Her system ran on two unspoken watchwords: surveillance, espionage. I had already seen a perfect example of that on the night she turned my pockets inside out and read my private notes.

Yet Madame knew what honesty was, and admired it — so long as its inconvenient scruples did not get in the way of her will or her interests. She held a deep respect for England, and for Englishwomen above all other nationalities, and would hire no one else to care for her own children if she could help it.

Often in the evenings, after a long day of plotting, counterplotting, and receiving reports from her network of spies, she would come up to the nursery, a faint trace of real weariness on her brow, and sit and watch while her children said their English prayers at my knee — the Lord’s Prayer, and the little hymn beginning “Gentle Jesus”, allowed even for these two small Catholic girls. When I had tucked them into bed, she would talk with me, and I soon gained enough French to follow and even answer her, about England, about the superior intelligence and genuine, reliable probity she swore all Englishwomen held. She made a great deal of sound, sensible points, even admitting that keeping girls locked in blind ignorance, unblinkingly monitored every second of their days with no private corner to call their own, was no way to raise honest, modest young women — but continental children, she insisted, had never known any other restraint. Give them the smallest measure of unmonitored freedom, and they would misunderstand it, push it too far, reap ruin for themselves. She claimed to hate the tricks she was forced to play, but she had no choice. Then she would slip away on her noiseless slippers, gliding like a ghost through the dark house, peeking through keyholes, listening behind every door.

For all that, her system was far from bad, and I will do her justice. Her arrangements for the physical wellbeing of her pupils were perfect. No minds were overtasked; lessons were evenly distributed, made wonderfully easy for the girls to master. They had endless time for play and exercise, so every girl was healthy and bright, no pale, puny faces anywhere to be seen in the Rue Fossette house. Food was abundant, fresh, and good. She never grudged a holiday, she allowed plenty of time for sleep, dressing, washing, and meals. Many an austere English schoolmistress would do very well to copy her methods, and I believe many would, if exacting English parents would only permit it.

She ruled through espionage, of course, and her team of spies were tools she used exactly as she saw fit: she did not hesitate to use the dirtiest, roughest instruments for the dirtiest jobs, tossing them aside like orange rind the second their use was spent. But for clean, honest work, she was fastidious about her staff, wrapping her most reliable, loyal workers in cotton wool to preserve them. Woe to anyone who trusted her even an inch past the point where their interests aligned with her own, though. Personal interest was the master key to every part of her nature, the mainspring of all her motives, the alpha and omega of her life. I saw people appeal again and again to her feelings, and I smiled in half-pity, half-scorn. No one ever won her favour that way. To try to touch her heart was to rouse her secret enmity at once, for it reminded her she had no heart to be touched, no soft spot, no vulnerable place. The line between charity and mercy was never more clearly drawn than in her. She was devoid of all personal sympathy, but full of rational, broad benevolence. She gave freely to funds for the poor at large, but kept her purse firmly shut against any individual man who stood before her in private sorrow. No private agony, no matter how sharp, could pierce her. Not the agony in Gethsemane, not the death on Calvary, could have wrung a single tear from her eyes.

She was a great, immensely capable woman. Running a girls’ school was far too small a sphere for her powers. She ought to have swayed a nation, led a turbulent legislative assembly. No one could have browbeaten her, no one fray her nerves, exhaust her patience, or outwit her shrewdness. She could have singlehandedly filled the roles of prime minister and head of police. Wise, firm, faithless; secret, crafty, passionless; watchful and inscrutable; acute and insensate — and perfectly decorous, at all times.

The sensible reader will not imagine I learned all this in a month, or even six months. At first, all I saw was the bright, thriving surface of the flourishing establishment. A great house full of healthy, lively girls, well-dressed, many of them handsome, learning without painful exertion, no useless waste of their spirits. They did not make blindingly fast progress at their lessons, but they were never oppressed, always occupied. The teachers and masters did the heavy head-work, rotated quickly through the hardest tasks so no one was burnt out. It was a foreign school, full of movement, variety, and charm, a world away from the strict, dour English institutions I had known.

Behind the house stretched a large garden, where in summer the pupils almost lived outside, wandering between rose bushes and fruit trees. On summer afternoons, Madame would sit under the vast vine-draped arbor, calling the classes to her in turns to sit around her and sew and read. Masters came and went, delivering short, lively lectures rather than dry lessons, and the girls could take notes or not, as they pleased, safe in the knowledge they could copy a friend’s work if they slacked. Beside the regular monthly permitted outings, Catholic feast days brought a constant string of holidays all year round, and on bright summer mornings or soft warm evenings, the boarders would be taken on long walks into the countryside, treated to warm waffles and light white wine, or fresh milk and wholemeal bread, or buttered rolls and coffee. It all seemed perfectly idyllic. Madame looked like the very embodiment of goodness, the teachers far better than I had any right to expect, the pupils a little loud and rough, but brimming over with health and glee.

That was how the view looked, seen through the soft haze of distance. But soon the haze would melt, I would be called down from my little nursery watchtower, and forced into far closer contact with this tiny world of the Rue Fossette.

One day I sat up in the nursery, hearing Madame’s children their English lessons, and turning a silk dress for her, when she wandered into the room, that distant, hard, thoughtful look on her face that made her seem far from genial. She dropped into a seat opposite me, silent for several minutes. Désirée, her eldest girl, was reading a little essay by Mrs Barbauld out loud to me, and I was making her translate every line from English to French to be sure she understood every word. Madame listened, intently.

Without any preamble, she spoke, almost like an accusation: “Miss, were you a governess in England?”

“No, Madame,” I smiled back, “you are mistaken.”

“Then this is your first try at teaching, this work with my children?”

I assured her it was. She fell silent again, and when I glanced up to take a pin from my cushion, I found myself the object of her full attention. She was turning me over in her thoughts, measuring me for some unspoken purpose, weighing my value for a plan I did not yet know about. She had long since scrutinised all my belongings, and I thought she knew a great deal about who I was — but from that day on, for a fortnight, she tested me in new ways. She listened outside the nursery door when I was shut in alone with the children, followed me at a cautious distance when I walked out with them, slipping within earshot whenever the trees of the park or boulevard gave her cover. Once that strict preliminary testing was done, she made her move.

One morning she came upon me abruptly, pretending to be harried, and told me she had run into a small dilemma. Mr Wilson, the English master, had not turned up at his usual hour, she feared he was ill, the sixty pupils of the second division were waiting for their lesson, and no one was there to give it. Would I object, just this once, to leading a short dictation exercise, so the girls would not complain they had missed their English class?

“In the classroom, Madame?” I asked.

“Yes, in the second division classroom.”

“Where there are sixty pupils,” I said, and my old cowardice rose up in me, shrinking like a snail into its shell. I claimed I was too inexperienced, too unfit, to face such a crowd. Left to my own devices, I would have let that chance slip forever. I was unadventurous, untouched by any ambition to make my way in the world, perfectly content to sit for twenty years teaching toddlers the alphabet, sewing silk dresses and children’s frocks. It was not true contentment, not really — the work held no charm for me, no hold on my interest — but it felt like a gift to live without heavy anxieties, without intimate, searing trials. The absence of sharp suffering was the closest thing to happiness I dared hope for.
