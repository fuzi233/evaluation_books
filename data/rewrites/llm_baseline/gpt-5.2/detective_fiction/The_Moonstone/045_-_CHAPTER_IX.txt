The doctor’s neat little house had a morning hush about it—the kind that belongs to respectable streets before carts begin to clatter and boys begin to shout. Mr. Candy’s young housemaid stood at the open door with the light pouring in behind her, bright as skimmed milk in a white basin. When I turned in the threshold, that light fell full upon the assistant’s face.

If Betteredge had meant that Ezra Jennings looked—by the common measure—like a man who ought not to be trusted, I could not deny it. His skin had the dark, unsettled tint of a gipsy cast; his cheeks were hollow; the bones of his face stood out as if the flesh had been spared them. His eyes—dreamy, steady, too old for their youth and too young for their sadness—made you feel he was listening to something that wasn’t said aloud. His hair was the strangest part of him: coloured as if two lives had laid claim to it, one dark and one fair, and neither had wholly prevailed. Even his figure carried a contradiction—gaunt and worn, yet with a spring in it that belonged to a younger man.

And yet, with all of that against him, there was an appeal in him that went past judgment and straight to feeling—something human that asked, without asking, to be allowed a place among ordinary men. My sense told me to answer his question about the doctor, and go. My sympathy—inscrutable as a scent that catches you before you’ve found its source—held me.

“Are you walking my way, Mr. Jennings?” I said, seeing his hat in his hand. “I am going to call on my aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite.”

He answered that he had a patient to visit, and that his road lay the same way as mine.

We went out together. The housemaid, who had been all pleasantness to me—cheerful as warm bread—changed at once when Ezra spoke to her. He said something quietly about the hour he might return. She received it with pursed lips, her eyes busy with the door-frame, the step, the street—anywhere but his face. It was a small thing; but small things, repeated every day, are how a house teaches a man whether he belongs. Betteredge had said he was unpopular. I believed it.

What a life, to be endured by a man who did not complain.

Down the steps and into the clean morning, Ezra Jennings let the subject of Mr. Candy lie between us like an untasted dish, waiting for me to break it. His silence had meaning in it: It’s your turn now. I had my own reasons for wanting to speak; so I began.

“Judging by the change I see in him,” I said, “Mr. Candy’s illness must have been far more serious than I had supposed.”

“It is almost a miracle,” he replied, “that he lived through it.”

“Is his memory never any better than I have found it today? He has been trying to speak to me—”

“Of something which happened before he was taken ill?” he asked, taking up my broken phrase as neatly as if he had been accustomed to stitch scattered words together.

“Yes.”

“His memory of events at that time is hopelessly enfeebled,” said Ezra Jennings. “It is almost to be deplored—poor fellow—that even the wreck of it remains. He remembers, dimly, that he formed plans—said things—meant to do this or that. But he cannot recall what the plans were, or what the thing was. And he knows it. He is painfully conscious of his deficiency, and painfully anxious to hide it. If he could only have recovered in complete oblivion, he would have been happier. Perhaps we should all be happier”—and here a sad little smile touched him and was gone—“if we could but completely forget.”

“There are some events,” I said, “the memory of which a man would be unwilling entirely to lose.”

“That is, I hope, true of most men, Mr. Blake. I am afraid it cannot truly be said of all. Have you any reason to suppose that the lost remembrance he tried to recover—while you were with him—was important to you?”

He had come, without any urging, upon the very matter that had been pulling me like a hook ever since I left the sick-room. I had stayed, at first, because he interested me; I held back my purpose until I knew whether I could trust him. What he had shown already—without emphasis, without display—was a quiet self-possession that did not seem learned, but native. A gentleman, not because he claimed it, but because he could not help being it. Whatever his motive in questioning me, I felt safe, so far, in answering plainly.

“I believe I have a strong interest,” I said, “in tracing that lost remembrance. Can you suggest any method by which I might assist his memory?”

A sudden quickness lit his brown eyes—as if the word memory had struck a string that was tuned already.

“Mr. Candy’s memory is beyond the reach of assistance,” he said. “I have tried often enough since his recovery to speak positively.”

I could not hide my disappointment. I owned it at once.

“I confess you led me to hope for a less discouraging answer.”

He smiled—not with mockery, but as a man smiles who has a second thought behind his first.

“It may not be final,” he said. “It may be possible to trace Mr. Candy’s lost recollection without appealing to Mr. Candy himself.”

“Indeed? Is it an indiscretion to ask how?”

“By no means. My only difficulty is explaining myself. May I trust to your patience, if I refer once more to the illness—and speak this time without sparing you certain professional details?”

“Pray go on. You have interested me already.”

My eagerness seemed to please him. We had now left the last houses behind and were in that open country where the road begins to breathe. There were hedges at either side, with damp leaves that held the sun in drops; there were birds busy with the morning; and from far off came the slow, comfortable sound of a cart somewhere—wood and iron meeting the road at steady intervals. It was a kinder sound than the town’s jangling; it belonged to labour, not to hurry. Ezra Jennings paused and, with a gentleness that surprised me in so worn a man, picked a few wild flowers from the hedge—little pale things with a clean green smell on their stems.

“How beautiful they are!” he said, simply, showing me the nosegay. “And how few people in England seem to admire them as they deserve.”

“You have not always been in England?” I asked.

“No. I was born, and partly brought up, in one of our colonies. My father was an Englishman; but my mother—We are straying away from our subject, Mr. Blake; and it is my fault. The truth is, I have associations with these modest little hedgeside flowers—It doesn’t matter. We were speaking of Mr. Candy. To Mr. Candy let us return.”

The reluctance with which he had let those personal words escape him, joined to the melancholy he carried like a shadow, satisfied me that I had read truly in his face at least thus much: he had suffered deeply, and foreign blood ran in him.

“You have heard, I dare say, of the original cause of Mr. Candy’s illness?” he resumed. “The night of Lady Verinder’s dinner-party was a night of heavy rain. My employer drove home through it in his gig, and reached the house wetted to the skin. He found an urgent message from a patient waiting; and he most unfortunately went at once to visit the sick person, without stopping to change his clothes. I was detained that night by a case at some distance from Frizinghall. When I returned the next morning, I found his groom in alarm, waiting to take me to his room. By that time the mischief was done; the illness had set in.”

“It has only been described to me, in general terms, as a fever,” I said.

“I can add nothing that will make the description more accurate,” he answered. “From first to last it assumed no specific form. I sent at once to two of Mr. Candy’s medical friends in the town—both physicians—to give me their opinion. They agreed with me that it looked serious; but they dissented strongly from the view I took of the treatment. We differed entirely in what we drew from the pulse. They argued from the rapidity of the beat: ‘lowering treatment,’ they said, was the only treatment. I admitted the rapidity; but I pointed to its feebleness as indicating exhaustion and a plain necessity for stimulants. They were for gruel, lemonade, barley-water, and so on. I was for champagne—or brandy—ammonia and quinine. A serious difference, as you see: two physicians of established local repute against a stranger who was only an assistant in the house.

“For the first few days I had no choice but to give way; and the patient steadily sank. I appealed again to the evidence of the pulse—its rapidity unchecked, its feebleness increased. The two doctors took offence. They said, ‘Mr. Jennings, either we manage this case, or you manage it. Which is it to be?’ I said, ‘Gentlemen, give me five minutes to consider, and that plain question shall have a plain reply.’

“When the time expired, I said, ‘You positively refuse to try the stimulant treatment?’ They refused in so many words. ‘Then I mean to try it at once, gentlemen.’—‘Try it, Mr. Jennings, and we withdraw from the case.’ I sent down to the cellar for a bottle of champagne and administered half a tumbler-full with my own hand. The two physicians took up their hats, and left the house.”

“You assumed a serious responsibility,” I said. “In your place, I am afraid I should have shrunk from it.”

“In my place,” he answered, “you would have remembered that Mr. Candy had taken you into his employment under circumstances which made you his debtor for life. You would have watched him sinking hour by hour, and you would have risked anything rather than let the one man on earth who had befriended you die before your eyes.

“Don’t suppose I had no sense of the position I placed myself in. There were moments when I felt all the misery of my friendlessness, all the peril of my responsibility. If I had been a happy man—if I had led a prosperous life—I believe I should have sunk under the task. But I had no happy time to look back on; no past peace of mind to force itself into contrast with my present anxiety—and I held firm.

“I took an interval in the middle of the day, when his condition was at its best, for the repose I needed. For the rest of the four-and-twenty hours, as long as his life was in danger, I never left his bedside. Towards sunset the delirium came on, as usual; it lasted more or less through the night, and then intermitted at that terrible time in the early morning—from two o’clock to five—when the vital energies, even of the healthiest, are at their lowest. It is then Death gathers most abundantly. It was then that Death and I fought our fight over the bed.

“I never hesitated. When wine failed, I tried brandy. When stimulants lost influence, I doubled the dose. After an interval of suspense—the like of which I hope to God I shall never feel again—there came a day when the rapidity of the pulse diminished slightly, but appreciably; and better still, the beat changed to steadiness and strength. Then I knew I had saved him; and then I broke down. I laid his wasted hand back on the bed and burst out crying. An hysterical relief—nothing more. Physiology says, truly, that some men are born with female constitutions—and I am one of them.”

He made that bitter little apology without self-pity, speaking as quietly as he had spoken throughout, as if he feared—almost morbidly—being turned into an object of sentiment. Even the countryside seemed to keep a respectful quiet around his confession: the low wash of wind on the moor, the distant creak of wheels, the birds’ thin calls—sounds that belonged to life going on, indifferent and constant, while one man watched another man’s breath as if it were the only sound in the world.

“You may ask why I have wearied you with all these details,” he continued. “It is the only way I can properly introduce what I have to say next. Now that you know my position at the time of his illness, you will understand the need I had—at intervals—of lightening the burden on my mind.

“I have had the presumption, for some years past, to occupy my leisure in writing a book for my profession—on the brain and the nervous system. It will probably never be finished; and it will certainly never be published. But it has been the friend of many lonely hours; and it helped me to while away the anxious time at Mr. Candy’s bedside. I told you he was delirious; and I mentioned the time at which his delirium came on?

“Well, I had reached a section of my book touching that question of delirium. I will not trouble you with my theory at length; I will confine myself to what your interest requires. It has often occurred to me, in practice, to doubt whether we can justifiably infer—in cases of delirium—that the loss of the faculty of speaking connectedly implies, of necessity, the loss of the faculty of thinking connectedly. Mr. Candy’s illness gave me an opportunity of putting that doubt to the test.

“I understand shorthand; and I took down the patient’s ‘wanderings’ exactly as they fell from his lips. Do you see what I am coming to, Mr. Blake?”

I saw it as clearly as if a lamp had been suddenly lit in a dark room, and I waited, hardly daring to breathe.

“At odds and ends of time,” he went on, “I copied my shorthand notes into ordinary writing—leaving large spaces between the broken phrases, even between single words, as they fell disconnectedly. Then I treated the result on something like the principle of a child’s puzzle. At first it is confusion; but it may be brought into shape, if you can find the right way. Acting on this plan, I filled each blank with what the words on either side suggested as meaning—altering again and again, until my additions followed naturally upon what came before, and fitted naturally into what came after.

“The result was that I not only occupied many vacant and anxious hours, but arrived at something which seemed to confirm my view: after putting the broken sentences together I found the superior faculty of thinking going on, more or less connectedly, while the inferior faculty of expression was in almost complete confusion.”

“One word,” I broke in. “Did my name occur in any of his wanderings?”

“You shall hear. Among my written experiments there is one in which your name occurs. For nearly the whole of one night his mind was occupied with something between himself and you. I have the broken words, as they dropped from his lips, on one sheet of paper; and the links of my own discovering which connect them, on another. The product is an intelligible statement—first, of something actually done in the past; secondly, of something he contemplated doing in the future, if his illness had not stopped him. The question is whether this does, or does not, represent the lost recollection he tried to find when you called on him this morning.”

“Not a doubt of it!” I cried. “Let us go back directly and look at the papers!”

“Quite impossible.”

“Why?”

He turned his face a little toward me, and his voice had a grave steadiness in it that admitted no heat.

“Put yourself in my position. Would you disclose to another what had dropped unconsciously from the lips of your suffering patient and your helpless friend, without first knowing there was a necessity to justify you in opening your lips?”

He was unanswerable; but I argued still.

“My conduct,” I said, “would depend greatly on whether the disclosure compromised my friend or not.”

“I disposed of that side of the question long since,” he replied. “Wherever my notes included anything Mr. Candy might have wished to keep secret, those notes were destroyed. My manuscript experiments include nothing now which he would have hesitated to communicate if he had recovered his memory. In your case, I have every reason to suppose my notes contain something he actually wished to say to you.”

“And yet you hesitate?”

“And yet I hesitate. Remember the circumstances under which I obtained what I possess. Harmless as it is, I cannot prevail on myself to give it up unless you first satisfy me there is a reason. He was so miserably ill—and so helplessly dependent on me. Is it too much to ask that you only hint what your interest is in his lost recollection—or what you believe that recollection to be?”

To answer him with the frankness he deserved was to name aloud the degrading thing I could scarcely bear even to think: that I stood suspected of stealing the Diamond. Strong as my interest in him had grown, it did not conquer that reluctance. I took refuge—miserably—in the vague explanations I had prepared for strangers.

This time I could not accuse my listener of impatience. Ezra Jennings heard me out patiently—more than patiently, with an anxious attention that showed he was trying to do me justice even while I withheld the truth.

When I finished, he shook his head.

“I am sorry to have raised your expectations only to disappoint them,” he said. “Throughout the whole period of Mr. Candy’s illness, from first to last, not one word about the Diamond escaped his lips. The matter with which I heard him connect your name has, I assure you, no discoverable relation whatever with the loss or recovery of Miss Verinder’s jewel.”

As he said it, we came to a place where the highway forked into two roads. One led toward Mr. Ablewhite’s house. The other ran out to a moorland village two or three miles away. Ezra Jennings stopped at the turning that led to the village.

“My way lies in this direction,” he said. “I am really and truly sorry that I can be of no use to you.”

His voice carried sincerity; his eyes rested on me for a moment with a melancholy interest that was almost a kindness. He bowed and, without another word, set off along the village road.

I stood and watched him go, feeling as if he carried away, step by step, the very clue I needed—carried it away into that wide, open land where sound thins out and a man’s thoughts grow loud. He walked a little distance, then turned and looked back. Seeing me still at the parting, he stopped as if doubting whether I wished to call him.

There was no time to argue with myself—to remind myself that I was throwing away, out of pride, what might be the turning-point of my life. There was only time to act first and think afterwards. I called him back—and as I did it, I said to myself, Now there is no help for it. I must tell him the truth.

He returned at once. I went a few steps toward him along the road.

“Mr. Jennings,” I said, “I have not treated you quite fairly. My interest in tracing Mr. Candy’s lost recollection is not the interest of recovering the Moonstone. A serious personal matter is at the bottom of my visit to Yorkshire. I have but one excuse for not having dealt frankly with you: it is more painful to me than I can say to mention to anybody what my position really is.”

For the first time I saw embarrassment cross his face.

“I have no right, Mr. Blake, and no wish,” he said, “to intrude myself into your private affairs. Allow me to ask your pardon, on my side, for having—most innocently—put you to a painful test.”

“You have a perfect right,” I answered, “to fix the terms on which you feel justified in revealing what you heard at Mr. Candy’s bedside. I understand and respect your delicacy. How can I expect to be taken into your confidence if I decline to admit you into mine? You ought to know—and you shall know—why I am interested in discovering what Mr. Candy wanted to say to me. If I turn out mistaken—if you prove unable to help me when you know what I want—I shall trust to your honour to keep my secret. And something tells me I shall not trust in vain.”

“Stop, Mr. Blake. I have a word to say, which must be said before you go any farther.”

I stared at him. Some powerful emotion seemed to seize him, as sudden as a squall that changes the whole weather of a day. His gipsy complexion turned to a livid grey; his eyes, a moment ago so controlled, grew wild and glittering; and his voice dropped into a low tone—stern, resolute—which I had not yet heard from him. Whatever was stored in him, for good or ill, rose up and showed itself like lightning in a dark sky.

“Before you place any confidence in me,” he said, “you ought to know—and you must know—under what circumstances I have been received into Mr. Candy’s house. It will not take long. I don’t profess, sir, to tell my story to any man. My story will die with me. All I ask is to be permitted to tell you what I have told Mr. Candy. If you are still of the same mind when you have heard that, you will command my attention and command my services. Shall we walk on?”

The misery he held down, and which had shaken loose for an instant, silenced me. I answered only by a sign, and we went on together.

After a few hundred yards he stopped at a gap in the rough stone wall where the moor opened out—brown grass and scattered heath running to the horizon, the wind moving over it with a long, low voice like distant surf.

“Do you mind resting a little, Mr. Blake?” he asked. “I am not what I was—and some things shake me.”
