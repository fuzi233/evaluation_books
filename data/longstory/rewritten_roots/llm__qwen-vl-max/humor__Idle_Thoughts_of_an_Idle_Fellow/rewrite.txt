After showing these pages to a few friends—mostly the kind who’d say anything to be polite—and hearing them mutter things like “Not bad, really,” and “Quite clever, actually,” I felt compelled to act. Then came the real push: my aunt, bless her, declared she’d buy ten copies if it ever saw print. My cousin said he’d buy one for his dog. So here we are.

If it hadn’t been for this overwhelming public demand—by which I mean, two people promising to buy it—I might have kept these ramblings locked in a drawer, where they belong. After all, what do we expect from books today? Improvement! Instruction! Moral uplift! This book won’t elevate a single soul—unless you count that time I once saw a squirrel look up from its nut with mild interest. It certainly won’t teach you how to balance your checkbook or win the Nobel Prize. In fact, I can’t recommend it for *any* practical purpose whatsoever. It’s not even good for starting fires.

But suppose you’ve read all the so-called “best hundred books” (and let’s face it, after number 37, they start sounding like each other). Suppose you’re tired of being improved, instructed, and elevated. Suppose you just want something light, silly, and possibly nonsensical. Then by all means, pick this up. Read it for half an hour. Laugh, sigh, or pretend to be offended. You’ll feel better afterward. That’s about as useful as this book gets.

Ah, idleness—now there’s a subject I know inside out. Not that I’ve studied it academically or anything. No sir. My credentials come from years of dedicated practice. Back when I was a lad, my tutor—a gentleman who charged nine guineas a term (no extras, mind you)—once told me he’d never met a boy who could accomplish less in more time. That was high praise, if you ask me. And then there was my grandmother, bless her soul. While teaching me the Prayer Book, she casually dropped that I’d probably never do much that I *shouldn’t* do—but she was absolutely certain I’d leave undone everything I *ought* to do. Well, she was half right. Heaven help me, I’ve done plenty of things I shouldn’t have—mostly because I’m too lazy to stop myself. But on the matter of neglecting duties? She nailed it dead center. I’ve confirmed her prophecy with such enthusiasm that even she might have been impressed.

And so, idleness—my true forte. I don’t claim credit for it. It’s not something I cultivated. It’s a gift. A rare one. You see, laziness is common. Slow coaches are everywhere. But a genuine idler? Now *that’s* a breed apart. He’s not some slouch with hands in pockets, aimlessly drifting. Oh no. The real idler is always *busy*. Ridiculously busy. Because the secret of true idleness lies not in doing nothing—but in having *so much* to do that you can afford to do none of it. Otherwise, what’s the fun? If you have nothing to do, then doing nothing is just… work. A tedious, draining occupation. Idleness, like kisses, must be stolen. It has to be snatched from the jaws of duty. It’s only sweet when it’s forbidden.

Now, many years ago, when I was young and foolish, I fell ill. Or at least I thought I did. Honestly, all I had was a nasty cold. But doctors, bless their hearts, always make things sound dire. “You should’ve come to me a month ago,” said mine. “Another week and I wouldn’t have guaranteed your survival.” Which is odd, because I’ve yet to meet a doctor called in for an emergency who didn’t discover that one more day would’ve meant curtains. Our medical men are like heroes in melodramas—always arriving just in time, never a moment sooner or later. Providence, clearly.

Anyway, I was sent to Buxton for a month, with strict orders: “Rest. Perfect rest.” I thought, *Oh, splendid!* This man understands me! I pictured four weeks of glorious *dolce far niente*, seasoned with just enough illness to make it poetic. I’d rise late, sip chocolate in my dressing gown, lounge in a hammock reading tragic novels where everyone dies by chapter three. I’d gaze at clouds sailing across the sky like white-sailed ships, listen to birdsong, and generally look profoundly thoughtful. When too weak to go outside, I’d sit propped up by pillows at the window, looking wasted and interesting—so that passing girls would sigh and wonder what noble sorrow I bore within.

And twice daily, I’d descend in a Bath chair to drink the waters. Ah, the waters. I knew nothing about them then. “Drinking the waters” sounded elegant, almost royal. Like something Queen Anne might have done. But oh, the horror. After the first few mornings, I realized Sam Weller’s description—“like warm flat-irons”—was actually a compliment. They were worse. Nauseating. Revolting. I drank them neat for six days straight. Nearly died. Then I hit upon a brilliant idea: follow each glass with a stiff brandy-and-water. Immediate relief. Later, I learned from several eminent physicians that the alcohol likely nullified the chalybeate properties entirely. But hey—luckily I guessed right.

But drinking the waters was just the tip of the iceberg. The whole month was agony. I obeyed the doctor’s orders to the letter—did nothing. Just wandered around the house, garden, and occasionally took two-hour jaunts in a Bath chair. That last part was oddly exciting. There’s a thrill to Bath-chairing you wouldn’t expect. Every minute, you’re convinced the whole contraption is about to tip over. Especially near ditches or newly macadamized roads. Every passing carriage feels like it’s going to plow into you. Going uphill? You calculate how long before the driver faints. Downhill? You mentally rehearse your escape plan. It’s like being in a slow-motion car chase, except you’re the passenger and the vehicle has no brakes.

But even this thrilling routine wore thin. The boredom became unbearable. I felt my mind slipping. It’s not a strong mind, and I decided it was better not to push it. So on the twentieth morning, I rose early, ate a hearty breakfast, and walked straight to Hayfield—nestled at the foot of Kinder Scout, through a lovely valley. A pleasant little town. And it had two sweetly pretty women. One smiled as she passed me on the bridge. The other stood at her door, kissing a red-faced baby. I’ll admit, I was smitten. But that was years ago. Probably both are stout and irritable now. Time changes everything.

On the way back, I saw an old man breaking stones. Something stirred deep inside me—the urge to *do* something. I offered him a drink if he’d let me take over. He was kind and agreed. I attacked those stones with the energy of three weeks’ pent-up frustration. In half an hour, I did more than he had all day. He didn’t seem jealous. But I was hooked.

From then on, I spiraled into rebellion. I started walking every morning. Listening to the band in the pavilion every evening. But still, the days dragged. I was relieved when the final day came and I was whisked away from Buxton—gout-ridden, consumptive Buxton—to London, with its relentless work and noise.

As we sped through Hendon, the city’s lurid glow warmed my heart. Later, when my cab rattled out of St. Pancras, the familiar roar of London swelled around me like music. It was the sweetest sound I’d heard in ages.

So no, I didn’t enjoy that month of idleness. I like idling when I’m supposed to be working. When I’m supposed to be answering letters, balancing accounts, or preparing for a meeting. That’s when I love to stand with my back to the fire, calculating debts. When I have a mountain of work ahead, I dawdle over dinner. And if I need to be up early? That’s when I love to lie in bed an extra half-hour. “Just five minutes,” I tell myself. And then ten. And then twenty. Is there anyone who ever gets up willingly? Only the hero of a Sunday-school tale, I suspect.

Some men simply cannot wake on time. If they’re supposed to rise at eight, they sleep till half-past. If half-past becomes acceptable, they wait till nine. They’re like the statesman said to be “punctually half an hour late.” They try alarms—those clever devices that go off at wrong times and wake the wrong people. They tell Sarah Jane to knock. She does. They grunt “awri” and fall back asleep. One friend I knew would get up, take a cold bath, then jump back into bed to warm up. I think I could stay out of bed if I once got out. But the wrench—the act of pulling my head from the pillow—that’s the hard part. No amount of resolve makes it easier.

I tell myself, after a night of wasted effort: “Tonight, no more work. I’ll get up early tomorrow.” I mean it. I’m determined. But come morning, enthusiasm fades. I reflect: “It would’ve been better to stay up last night.” Then there’s the trouble of dressing. The more I think about it, the more I want to delay. It’s a strange thing, this bed—this mimic grave. We stretch our tired limbs and sink into silence, as if dying. “O bed, O bed, delicious bed, that heaven on earth to the weary head,” sang poor Hood. You’re a kind nurse to us fretful souls. Clever and foolish, good and bad—we all find comfort in your lap. The worried man, the sick man, the girl weeping for her faithless lover—like children, we lay our heads on your white bosom, and you hush us off to sleep.

But when you turn away—when you refuse to comfort—our pain is terrible. How long the dawn seems when we cannot sleep! Those nights of tossing and turning, feverish and restless, staring into the dark hours that crawl toward light. Worse still: sitting beside someone in pain, watching the fire crackle, hearing the clock tick like a hammer beating out life.

But enough of beds. I’ve lingered too long—even for an idle fellow. Let’s step outside and have a smoke. Smoking wastes time just as well—and looks less suspicious. Tobacco has been a blessing to us idlers. Before Sir Walter’s time, what did civil-service clerks do to occupy their minds? Hard to imagine. I blame the quarrelsome nature of medieval youth entirely on the lack of tobacco. No work, no smoking—so they fought. Constantly. If there was no war, they invented feuds with neighbors. If that failed, they debated whose sweetheart was prettier—using battle-axes, clubs, and fists. Art criticism in the 12th century was brutal.

When a young man fell in love, he didn’t say, “You’re too beautiful to live.” He said, “Let’s step outside and see about it.” If he broke the other man’s head—then his girl was pretty. If the other man broke *his* head—then his girl wasn’t pretty, but the other man’s was. Simple logic. Efficient.

Today, we light a pipe and let the girls fight it out themselves. And they do. Brilliantly. They’re doctors, barristers, artists. They run theaters, run scams, edit newspapers. I look forward to the day when men do nothing but lie in bed until noon, read two novels a day, enjoy private five-o’clock teas, and argue about trousers and whether Mr. Jones’ coat fits.

A glorious future—for idle fellows.

ON BEING IN LOVE. You’ve been in love, of course—unless you’re one of those rare, blessed creatures who’s managed to skip the whole ordeal entirely. In which case, congratulations! You’ve dodged a bullet that most of us have to endure at least once, like catching the measles or realizing your favorite shirt has a hole in it. And just like measles, love is a one-time thing. Once you’ve had it, you’re immune for life. The second time around? Forget it. Cupid doesn’t recycle arrows. He’s not some cheap magician pulling rabbits out of hats—he’s a gentleman with standards. One shot per heart, thank you very much.

So if you’ve already been through it, you can now walk into any situation that would make a normal person break into a cold sweat and feel perfectly safe. You can stroll through moonlit woods without fear of tripping over your own feet—or worse, falling in love again. You can attend a family gathering on the Rhine, sit through a wedding (even yours), dance the waltz until your legs give out, and still emerge unscathed. No lingering emotional wounds. No sudden urge to write poetry. Just a mild chill from the drafty conservatory and a headache from too much champagne.

You can leap over stiles, tumble through hedges, slide down slippery paths—all without incident. You can look into someone’s eyes without feeling like your soul is being pulled out by invisible strings. You can hear a voice so sweet it could charm birds from trees and still keep your head firmly attached to your shoulders. You can hold hands without getting zapped by some supernatural “Lulu” energy field. Nope. Love? Done. Finished. Over. Like a firework that goes *bang*, lights up the sky, and then fizzles into ashes.

That’s the truth: love is a flash. A meteor streaking across the night sky. It blazes brightly, illuminates everything below, and then—poof—gone. What remains is the cold, smoldering shell of what used to be. But don’t despair! Because while the flame dies, the fire isn’t lost. That’s where affection comes in. Affection is the cozy hearthfire that keeps burning long after the fireworks are over. It’s not flashy, but it’s warm. It’s steady. It’s the kind of warmth that lets old folks sit with their thin hands folded, kids curl up nearby, pets snuggle at the grate, and neighbors drop by for a cup of tea. It’s the kind of fire you can feed every day with kindness, patience, humor, and the occasional well-timed compliment.

Now, I know what you’re thinking: “But wait—what about true love? Doesn’t it last forever?” Ah, young Edwin and Angelina, my dear, romantic souls—you’re expecting too much from a fleeting spark. You believe your hearts are big enough to fuel this wild, consuming passion for decades. But here’s the catch: love burns fast. It’s not renewable. There’s no way to refill the tank. And when it starts to dim, you both start blaming each other. Edwin thinks Angelina has grown cold because she no longer runs to the gate grinning like a fool. She doesn’t cry when he coughs anymore. Instead, she suggests a lozenge—*in a tone that implies she’d rather he stop making noise than actually care*. Poor Angelina, meanwhile, is weeping silently because Edwin has stopped carrying her handkerchief in his waistcoat pocket. Not even a sentimental token left behind!

They’re both shocked by the change in the other—but neither sees their own shift. If they did, they might stop accusing each other and realize: human nature is small. We all shrink. We all grow weary. We all become slightly less dramatic as time passes. But instead, we blame our partners. "She wouldn't be so distant if *he* hadn’t changed!" "He wouldn’t be so dull if *she* hadn’t stopped being magical!"

And so they wander through life in the cold dawn between the extinguished candle of love and the unlit fire of affection. It’s a bleak hour. But let’s hope the fire catches before the sun sets completely. Too many people sit shivering by dead coals until night falls and darkness wins.

But what’s the point of warning anyone? When you’re twenty, love feels eternal. You swear you’ll love fiercely at sixty. You glance around at middle-aged men and see no sign of passionate attachment—and yet you’re convinced your own flame will burn brighter than anyone else’s ever did. Nobody loved like you do. So why should your experience follow the same pattern?

Ah, but alas! By thirty, you’ve joined the choir of cynics. It’s not your fault. Our passions fade with our blushes. We don’t rage, we don’t grieve, we don’t celebrate wildly anymore. Disappointment doesn’t lead to suicide. Success doesn’t make us drunk. Life becomes… quieter. Less operatic. Ambition shrinks. Honor adapts. And love? Love dies.

The dreams of youth get frostbitten. The tender shoots wither. The vine that once reached for the stars turns into a dry stump. And yet—my fair friends—you’ll call this nonsense. You think a man only truly loves when he’s gray-haired and trembling with sincerity. You read novels where the hero is a fifty-year-old dandy who swoons over a schoolgirl with the intensity of a nervous breakdown. Four adjectives for every noun! “Oh, my darling!” he cries, “You are the most *exquisite*, *ethereal*, *resplendent*, *unforgettable* creature I have ever beheld!”

Hide your heads, young lovers! This seasoned beau loves harder than you ever will. His emotion is so deep it requires an entire thesaurus to express.

But here’s the irony: real boys speak more truth with a stutter than these aging romantics do with eloquence. A boy’s love comes from a full heart. A man’s? Often from a full stomach. Or perhaps from a full sense of obligation. Let’s face it—love in youth is a rushing spring. In maturity, it’s a slow, muddy river. If you want to taste pure love, drink from the clear stream that youth offers. Don’t wait until it’s polluted with years, regrets, and bad decisions.

Or—perhaps you *do* prefer the bitter flavor. Maybe the clean water tastes flat to you. Maybe you find pleasure in the filth. That’s the message whispered daily from yellow-covered novels: sin is sexy, virtue is boring, and decency is for suckers. These books paint women as sirens luring men to ruin, not as angels guiding them upward. They teach girls that innocence is weakness and that a man who’s lived a little is more desirable than one who hasn’t. How many innocent hearts have they corrupted? How many boys have been led down dirty alleys under the guise of romance?

It’s not real life. It’s fantasy painted by imaginations diseased with longing and regret. We want to see women as noble, not as temptresses. As guides, not as traps. Because when a man falls in love, especially in his formative years, the woman he loves shapes him. Unconsciously, he molds himself to please her. And often, she leads him toward mediocrity. Her ideal man is a prince of the ordinary. And so powerful minds, enchanted by love, become “lost to life and use and name and fame.”

But ladies—this doesn’t have to be. You hold more power than you know. You could make us better. More chivalrous. More noble. Chivalry isn’t dead—it just sleeps. And it wakes when there’s something worth fighting for. You must be worthy of worship. Not just beautiful, but good. Not just charming, but wise. Be Una, not a court dame. For only for Una would the Red Cross Knight slay dragons. Not for some painted, primped queen who spends her days gossiping and applying rouge.

Oh, women—shed your cloaks of selfishness, pretense, and affectation! Stand tall again in your natural splendor—pure, simple, regal. Then watch how swords leap from scabbards. Watch how knights rise to defend your honor. Watch how Fear, Avarice, Pleasure, and Ambition fall beneath your banner.

What noble deeds were we ready to perform when we loved? What lives could we have lived for her sake? Our love was a religion. We would have died for it. We didn’t adore a mere mortal—we worshipped a goddess. And we worshipped madly. Sweetly. Foolishly.

Ah, lad—cherish the dream while it lasts. Soon enough, you’ll understand Tom Moore’s words: “There’s nothing half so sweet in life.” Even when love brings pain, it’s a romantic, wild pain—nothing like the dull ache of adult disappointment. When she’s gone, and the light leaves your world, even your despair carries a ghost of enchantment. Who wouldn’t risk such sorrow for such joy?

Ah, what raptures they were! Just remembering makes your heart race. How delicious it was to tell her you loved her, that you lived for her, that you’d die for her! How wildly you raved! How extravagantly you spoke! And how cruel of her to pretend not to believe you!

How awestruck you were! How miserable when you offended her! And yet—how delightful to be scolded by her, to beg forgiveness without knowing what you’d done wrong! How dark the world became when she ignored you—just to see you suffer! How bright it felt when she smiled!

How jealous you were of everyone near her! Every man she shook hands with, every woman she kissed—even the maid who brushed her hair, the boy who polished her shoes, the dog she petted—you hated them all… except the dog, whom you had to respect.

How you’d wait to see her—staring at her window, unable to enter, just hanging around the corner like a shy ghost. Oh, if only the house had caught fire—insurance covered it, so it wouldn’t matter—and you could rush in, save her, and be horribly burned! Anything to serve her.

Even small things brought joy. Watching her like a spaniel, anticipating her every wish. Proud to obey her. Delighted to be ordered about. Devoting your life to her seemed effortless. You’d skip holidays just to leave a gift at her door. And if she accepted it—well, that was reward enough.

Everything she touched became sacred. Her glove. Her ribbon. The rose from her hair—now dried and pressed between poems you’ll never read again.

And oh, how beautiful she was! It felt like an angel entering the room. Everything else faded. She was too holy to touch. To kiss her would be like singing a comic song in church. Kneeling to kiss her hand was almost sacrilege.

Ah, those foolish days—when we were pure, unselfish, full of faith and reverence. Those foolish days of noble dreams and noble struggles. And now—these clever, wise days—when we know money is the only prize worth striving for, when we believe in nothing but lies and meanness, when we care for no one but ourselves.

But remember: those foolish days were the best.

Ah, the blues. Not the kind you wear on a summer’s day—though I suppose that could be depressing too, if you’re stuck in a room with a man who thinks navy blue is “the colour of authority.” No, I mean the actual blues—the kind that descend like a fog over your soul just as the sun begins to set and the world turns from bustling to boring.

It's not sadness, exactly. It’s more like sadness had a baby with indigestion and named it “The Blues.” You don’t feel bad because something went wrong—you feel bad because nothing feels right. And yet, no one can explain why. It strikes without warning: on the day you inherit a fortune, or the day you forget your umbrella on the train (which, frankly, is worse). It’s not logical. It’s not fair. It’s just... there.

And when it hits? Oh, it’s a full-blown disaster. You become mentally sluggish, emotionally explosive, and physically clumsy. You snap at strangers for no reason—like the man at the butcher’s who sold you meat that was *technically* still alive—and you glare at your best friend as though he personally invented Mondays. You trip over rugs, spill tea, and accuse your cat of plotting against you. Then you kick the door shut behind her and mutter about “feline betrayal.”

You try to read. Shakespeare? Too obvious. Dickens? Dull. Thackeray? A bore. Carlyle? Sentimental. You throw the book across the room and shout, “What a fool this man is!” before realizing you’ve just insulted a dead writer whose ghost probably has better things to do than haunt your living room.

Then you decide to write a letter. “Dearest Auntie,” you begin, “I find I have five minutes to spare…” And then you stare at the page for seventeen minutes, unable to think of anything else. So you crumple the paper, fling the pen into the soup bowl (it’s a metaphor), and resolve to visit the Thompsons. But halfway through putting on your gloves, you remember: the Thompsons are idiots. They never eat dinner. Their baby will expect you to jump it like a frog. You curse them silently, swear off socializing forever, and collapse onto the sofa like a deflated balloon.

At this point, you bury your face in your hands and whisper, “I wish I were dead.” You imagine your funeral—everyone weeping, standing around your sickbed like actors in a poorly written drama. You bless them all, especially the pretty ones. “They’ll miss me,” you think. “They’ll finally appreciate what they’ve lost.” And then you realize: they won’t. Nobody will. Not really. Why would they care if you were blown up, hung, married, or drowned? Two straws’ worth of care, maybe. But not much more.

So you rage inwardly—against the world, against fate, against yourself (who you’d kick if anatomy allowed). Half an hour of this and you’re ready to burn down the house. Fortunately, bedtime arrives. You dash upstairs, strip off your clothes like a madman fleeing a fire, blow out the candle, and dive into bed as if you’ve been paid to do it fast. Then you spend two hours tossing, turning, pulling off blankets, getting up, putting them back on, and occasionally yelling at the ceiling.

Eventually, you fall asleep—but only fitfully. You dream of falling into an abyss while someone sings show tunes. You wake up late, groggy, and already regretting the entire previous night.

Now, I’m speaking mostly as a single man here. Married men have different strategies. They bully their wives, complain about dinner, and force the kids to go to bed. This creates chaos, which, oddly enough, is the only kind of entertainment a man in the blues can enjoy. Chaos = fun. At least, that’s what I tell myself.

The symptoms are universal: the heaviness, the irritability, the desire to be anywhere but where you are. But the names vary. The poet says he feels “a sadness come o'er him.” 'Arry tells Jimee he’s got “the blooming hump.” Your sister claims she’s “out of sorts” and hopes nothing terrible happens. The average young man greets you with, “Oh, so glad to see you, old chap!” while internally screaming, “I feel jolly miserable!”

As for me? I say, “I’ve got a strange, unsettled feeling tonight,” and then immediately go outside, hoping the fresh air will cure my existential dread. (Spoiler: it doesn’t.)

By the way, the blues never strike during the day. During daylight hours, life is too busy to brood. The world roars forward like a steam engine, drowning out the quiet whispers of melancholy. In the morning, if something goes wrong, you curse and throw things. But at ten PM? You sit in the dark, read poetry, and sigh dramatically about how meaningless life is.

And here’s the kicker: it’s rarely trouble that causes the blues. Real trouble is too harsh for sentiment. We cry over paintings of sorrow, but we avoid the real thing. There’s no pleasure in genuine pain. We don’t hug foxes that bite us, nor play with knives that cut. But when someone loves to dwell on past sorrows—when they keep them fresh in memory—it’s no longer pain. It’s become a kind of comfort.

Old ladies cry over tiny shoes tucked in lavender drawers. Young girls place curls under their pillows, remembering boys lost to the sea. They’ll call me cynical, I know. But ask them honestly: does it hurt to remember? Or does it soothe? For some, tears are as sweet as laughter.

The Englishman, we’re told, takes his pleasures sadly. The Englishwoman? She takes her pleasures *in* sadness. And I don’t sneer. Let women be sentimental. Let them grieve. We men are cold, practical beasts. We need them to be soft-hearted—our emotional butter on coarse bread.

Sentiment is to women what humor is to us. We don’t understand their grief; they don’t understand our jokes. So let them mourn. Who says their joy isn’t as valid as ours? Is a man laughing until he cries more happy than a woman quietly weeping, eyes fixed on a vanished past?

I’m glad when Regret becomes a companion—not because she’s sad, but because the salt has left her tears. The sting is gone. Sorrow has been transformed into something tender. When we can look back on pain without bitterness, when we feel peace instead of despair, then we’ve truly healed.

Time heals. That’s the truth. When we recall past sorrows not with anguish, but with gentle pity—when we see Tom and Maggie Tulliver clasping hands beneath the Floss, or Colonel Newcome answering “Adsum”—then we know the wound has closed.

And speaking of Tom and Maggie, George Eliot once wrote of the “sadness of a summer’s evening.” How perfectly true! Those long, golden sunsets—they’re magical, yes, but also deeply melancholic. Melancholy is not a villain. She’s a quiet girl who appears only when the light fades. Her palace is twilight. Her kingdom, dusk.

She walks beside us in silent streets, by dark rivers that creep like ghosts under bridges. In the country, when bats flutter and land-rails cry, we feel her presence. It’s as if we stand beside an unseen death-bed. The trees sway like mourners. The sky deepens. A great peace settles.

In that moment, our daily worries shrink. Bread and cheese seem trivial. Even kisses lose their urgency. Thoughts flood in—unspoken, unnameable. We stand under the darkening dome and feel something vast within us. We are more than our petty lives.

The world, wrapped in dusk, is no longer just a workshop. It becomes a temple. And in its dimness, sometimes, our groping hands touch God.

ON BEING HARD UP. It’s a curious thing—when you sit down to write something clever and original, the only thing that comes to mind is how utterly broke you are. I meant to produce a masterpiece of wit and insight, but instead, here I am, scribbling about poverty like a man who’s just been evicted from his last hope. Probably it’s because my hands are in my pockets again. That always brings it on. I can’t help it—I’m a pocket man. Except when I’m with my sisters, cousins, or aunts, who go into full-scale moral panic at the mere sight of my hands tucked away. “It’s not gentlemanly!” they cry, as if I were stealing from a charity box instead of merely storing my own fingers. I suppose they’re right—though I’ve never understood why putting your hands in *your* pockets should make you less gentle than anyone else. But then, I could see the logic in it if it were other people’s pockets we were talking about. Still, there’s something oddly comforting about having your hands in your trousers—especially when one pocket holds a few coppers and the other a set of keys. You feel almost respectable.

There’s nothing worse than standing before a female post-office clerk with empty pockets. Your hands might be in them, but they’re useless—like a lion tamer without his hat. Years ago, when my entire fortune amounted to what the city folk call “a bob,” I’d spend a penny just for the jingle of change. Eleven pence in your pocket feels like wealth; a shilling feels like doom. If I’d been “La-di-da,” that famously impecunious youth we all mock, I’d have traded my penny for two ha’pennies just to hear the clink. I know what I’m talking about. I’ve been a provincial actor—no small feat, given that I once played Hamlet in a town where the audience was mostly made up of sheep. I’ve also been a “gentleman connected with the press,” which means I wrote articles for money I didn’t get. I’ve lived on 15 shillings a week. I’ve lived a week on 10 shillings and owed the other five. And once, I lived for two weeks on a greatcoat—literally. It kept me warm, and I wore it everywhere. It was like wearing a tent with sleeves.

Being truly hard up teaches you more about economics than any textbook ever could. Try living on 15 shillings a week and see how much you can save for clothes and fun. You’ll learn that waiting for farthing change is worth it. You’ll walk a mile to save a penny. A glass of beer becomes a rare indulgence. A collar? That’s good for four days. Try this exercise before marriage—it’s excellent training. Let your son do it before college. He won’t complain about a hundred pounds a year then. There are some folks who could use this lesson. Take, for instance, the delicate gentleman who refuses to drink claret unless it’s over ninety-four, and who’d sooner eat cat meat than plain roast mutton. These creatures exist, though thankfully mostly in novels written by ladies who’ve never had to choose between a meal and a clean shirt. When I see such a man reading a menu, I want to drag him to a common east-end pub and force-feed him a sixpenny dinner: beefsteak pudding (fourpence), potatoes (a penny), half a pint of porter (a penny). The smell of beer, tobacco, and roast pork would stick with him—and maybe teach him to stop sniffing at everything on the table.

Then there’s the generous soul who gives waiters shillings like he’s handing out alms, but never pays his own bills. I once dined with a young government clerk who insisted, “I always give the waiter a shilling. One can’t give less, you know.” I agreed—he couldn’t possibly give elevenpence ha’penny—but I vowed to take him to an eating house near Covent Garden where the waiter wears his shirt-sleeves—dirty ones, especially toward the end of the month. I know that waiter. If my friend gives him anything beyond a penny, the man will shake his hand like they’ve just signed a peace treaty. That’s how much respect he gets.

Now, don’t get me wrong—being poor isn’t funny. Not really. It’s not funny to haggle over pennies. It’s not funny to be called stingy. It’s not funny to wear clothes that look like they’ve been through a war. No, poverty isn’t funny to the poor. It’s hell. For a sensitive man, it’s worse than being attacked by a badger. Many a brave fellow who’d face lions and serpents has broken down under its petty humiliations. It’s not the discomforts that hurt—it’s the shame. Robinson Crusoe didn’t care about patchy trousers or toes sticking out of boots. Why? Because no one was around to judge him. Being poor isn’t the problem. Being *known* to be poor—that’s the real pain.

It’s not cold that makes a man without a coat hurry along. It’s not shame at lying—though he knows the lie won’t be believed—that makes him flush when he says he doesn’t carry umbrellas “on principle” and finds great-coats “unhealthy.” It’s easy to say poverty isn’t a crime. But if it weren’t, men wouldn’t be ashamed of it. It’s a mistake, and it’s punished like one. Poor men are despised—by Christians, lords, demagogues, footmen. No amount of maxims about virtue and humility will make them respected. Appearances rule. A man will walk arm-in-arm with a notorious scoundrel if he’s well-dressed, but sneak up a back alley to speak to a man in shabby clothes. And the shabby man knows it—better than anyone. He’ll go miles out of his way to avoid an old acquaintance. Those who knew him in better days needn’t worry about looking the other way. He’s far more anxious they won’t see him than they are to avoid him. And heaven help him if someone offers help—he’d rather die than accept it. All he wants is to be forgotten. And usually, he gets his wish.

Time, that wonderful homeopathic doctor, helps you adjust. You can tell the seasoned pro from the beginner. The veteran goes into the pawn shop like he’s going to his tailor—calm, collected, even polite. The assistant treats him like a regular customer, much to the annoyance of the lady next door, who mutters, “I don’t mind waiting if it’s a regular.” It’s so smooth, you’d think he was buying bonds. But the first-timer? Oh, it’s comedy gold. He hangs around outside until every loafer in the street is watching him. He studies the window displays like he’s planning to buy a diamond bracelet. Then he struts in with a swagger that says, “I’m part of the swell mob.” Inside, he whispers so softly the assistant can’t hear. He stammers, contradicts himself, and when asked how he got the watch, nearly confesses to stealing it that very morning. He’s told to leave—“We don’t deal with your sort.” He flees, forgetting his name and address, and wakes up three miles away with no idea how he got there.

And then there’s the time issue. Public houses and churches are the only clocks available. But public houses are too fast, churches too slow. Trying to peek at a pub clock? You push the swing door open—immediately earning the barmaid’s contempt, as if you’re a cadger or a thief. You cause a stir among the married patrons. You can’t see the clock—it’s behind the door. You try to retreat quietly and whack your head. The alternative? Jumping up and down outside the window. After that, if you don’t pull out a banjo and start singing, the kids gather round and get disappointed.

And here’s a mystery: why, within half an hour of leaving your watch “to be repaired,” does someone always stop you in the street and loudly ask for the time? Nobody bothers when you’ve got it on.

Dear old ladies and gentlemen who’ve never known hardship—may you never!—they see pawn shops as the final stage of ruin. But those who’ve been there often meet surprising acquaintances. It’s like the little boy who dreamed he went to heaven and found people he never expected. I think pawning is more dignified than borrowing from friends. I always try to convince my friends who say, “I just need two pounds till tomorrow,” that it’s better than begging. But they won’t listen. One said he objected to the *principle*. I suspect he really meant the interest—twenty-five percent is steep.

Poverty comes in degrees. We’re all hard up, more or less. Some need a thousand pounds. Some need a shilling. Right now, I’m hard up for a fiver. Just for a day or two. I promise to pay it back within a week—maybe sooner. If any kind reader would lend it to me, I’d be most grateful. Send it to Messrs. Field & Tuer, please—sealed carefully. I’ll give you my I.O.U. as security.

All is vanity—and so are we all, one and all. Vanity’s the great equalizer. It doesn’t care whether you’re a king or a kitchen maid, a philosopher or a pet cat—it just *loves* to get its claws into you.

Women? Oh, they’re vain—don’t get me wrong. But men? Men are worse. And children? Children are the absolute champions of self-admiration. Just now, as I write this, a tiny human being with more confidence than sense is pounding on my knees like she’s trying to start a revolution. “Unkie!” she shrieks, “Wot oo tink of me new shoes?”

I look down. They’re not shoes—they’re architectural disasters. Asymmetrical, lumpy, possibly worn on the wrong feet (though how one could tell, given their general state of disarray). But what do I say? “They’re hideous”? No. That would be a crime against nature and nursery etiquette. Instead, I gush like a man who’s just seen the first sunrise after a thousand years of darkness: “Oh, my dear! Simply divine! Never have I seen such elegant footwear in all my life!”

She beams. She nods. She waddles off to inform the entire household that Uncle thinks she’s the most stylish child since Adam’s second cousin. Later, I hear from afar: “Unkie says me dood dirl—me dot to have two bikkies!” Ah yes, vanity has its perks—even if it’s only extra biscuits.

But it’s not just kids. I once sat in a London garden, sun shining, birds singing, when suddenly a shrill voice cuts through the peace: “Gamma, me dood boy, me wery good boy, gamma; me dot on Bob’s knickiebockies!” A small boy, proudly displaying his stolen pants, convinced he’s achieved the pinnacle of moral excellence.

And animals? Oh, they’re no better. I saw a Newfoundland dog the other day sitting before a shop mirror, studying himself with the solemnity of a judge reviewing a verdict. His expression said: *Yes. This face. This fur. This magnificent chest. I am worthy.*

At a farm once, during some festive occasion—May Day, Quarter Day, or perhaps just “Let’s Decorate the Cow Day”—they put a flower garland around a cow’s head. The beast strutted about all day like a debutante at her first ball. When they took it off? She refused to be milked. Sulked. Muttered bovine insults. They had to reapply the wreath before she’d cooperate. Not a Percy anecdote—just plain, unvarnished truth.

Cats? Now there’s a species that knows vanity inside out. I’ve seen a cat walk out of a room because someone dared call her breed “inferior.” A well-placed compliment, though—“Oh, you’re simply radiant today!”—and she’ll purr like a motorboat for an hour.

I love cats. They’re absurdly dignified. They carry themselves with the air of someone who’s been insulted by a mere mortal and will never forgive them. You can’t slap a cat’s head like you would a dog. Dogs? Dogs are social. “Hail, fellow, well met!” they say to every Tom, Dick, or Harry. I meet a dog I know—I punch him in the snout, call him names, roll him over—and he just lies there, grinning up at me like I’ve done him a favor.

Try that with a cat. She’d turn her back on you for life. You want to win a cat’s affection? Start slowly. Say, “Poor pussy.” Then, “Did ‘ums?” in a tone of deep sorrowful empathy. You don’t know what you mean—neither does she—but it sounds like you care. If you’re decent-looking and polite, she’ll rise up on her hind legs, rub her nose against your hand, and then proceed to scratch your trousers off. That’s friendship.

As the poet said:
> "I love little pussy, her coat is so warm,
> And if I don't tease her she'll do me no harm;
> So I'll stroke her, and pat her, and feed her with food,
> And pussy will love me because I am good."

Ah yes—the last two lines reveal everything. To a cat, goodness means being stroked, patted, and fed. And I suspect that definition isn’t limited to felines.

We humans? We’re much the same. A good man is one who’s good *to us*. A bad man is one who refuses to give us what we want. We all believe, deep down, that the universe was created solely for our benefit. You, dear reader, were born so you could read my words and pay for them. I, in turn, exist to write things for you to read. The stars? They’re there to make the night sky interesting. The moon? For flirting under. The sun? Probably just waiting for Mrs. Poyser’s bantam cock to crow so it can rise again.

“’Tis vanity that makes the world go round,” I say. I’ve never met a man without vanity—and if I did, he’d be dreadfully boring. He’d be noble, yes. Revered. Put in a glass case. Made into a statue. But not loved. Not *human*.

Angels may be perfect, but they’d be dull company. Even saints are depressing. We connect not through virtue, but through vice. We differ in nobility, but we’re united in folly. Some of us are kind. Some honest. Some truthful. But *all* of us are vain.

From the Indian hunter proud of his scalp belt to the European general puffing beneath his medals; from the Chinese man delighted by the length of his pigtail to the professional beauty enduring torture for a waist like a bottle stopper; from Polly Stiggins strutting through Seven Dials with a tattered parasol to the princess trailing four yards of silk behind her; from ‘Arry cracking jokes to get laughs to the statesman reveling in cheers for his grand speeches; from the African bartering ivory for glass beads to the Christian maiden selling her soul for a title and a few diamonds—we all march, fight, bleed, and die beneath the tawdry banner of vanity.

And flattery? That’s the oil. Want to win friends? Flatter. High or low, rich or poor, wise or foolish—flatter them all. Praise their virtues. Compliment their vices. Tell the fool he’s brilliant. Tell the ugly guy he’s handsome. Tell the ill-bred person they’re refined. Your intelligence will be praised. Your discernment lauded.

Even the belted earl—whatever that means—is susceptible. Is it an earl who wears a belt instead of braces? Sounds uncomfortable. But whatever he is, he’s still human. And humans? We all fall. From duchesses to cat-meat men, from plowboys to poets—the poet, mind you, is easier to flatter. Butter sinks better into wheaten bread than oaten cakes.

Love? That’s pure flattery. As a Frenchman once said—whose name I’ve forgotten—fill a person with love for themselves, and what overflows is yours. Tell a girl she’s an angel, only more angelic than angels; a goddess, only more graceful than any goddess; more fairy-like than Titania, more beautiful than Venus, more enchanting than Parthenope. She’ll believe you. Sweet innocent! She’ll smile, lean on your shoulder, and whisper, “You’re a dear good fellow after all.”

Now imagine a man trying to woo her with strict honesty. “You’re… not bad-looking,” he might say. “Your hand is a light drab color with red streaks. Your nose—well, for a turned-up one, it’s quite acceptable. Your eyes? Average.”

He’d be lucky to get a handshake. Meanwhile, the rival whispers: “Your face is like a fresh blush rose. Your hair—a wandering sunbeam caught in a smile. Your eyes—two evening stars.”

Who wins? The liar. Because love runs on illusion.

Flattery comes in many forms. Some like it thick—slapped on with a trowel. Others prefer subtlety. With sensible people, suggest it. “You’d give your last sixpence to a beggar,” you might say, “wouldn’t you?” That’s flattery wrapped in an insult. Or use a third party: tell B that A is a splendid fellow, and beg him not to mention it to A. Just make sure B is reliable—or he won’t.

Then there are the John Bulls who say, “I hate flattery! Never let anyone get over me by flattery!” Easy fix. Flatter them on their *lack* of vanity. “You’re so modest! So unaffected!” And suddenly, they’re yours.

Vanity isn’t just a flaw. It’s a force. It drives ambition. It fuels art. We write books, paint pictures, sing songs—not just for joy, but for praise. We become rich not for comfort—anyone can buy that for £200 a year—but so our houses are bigger, our horses more numerous, our wives dressed like walking Christmas trees, and our dinners cost more than we eat.

And in doing so, we build roads, spread trade, bring civilization to remote corners. So let’s not scorn vanity. Let’s use it. Honor itself is just high-class vanity.

It’s not just humans. Peacocks preen. Eagles soar. Snobs strut. Heroes too.

So come on, young bucks—let’s be vain together. Let’s join hands and grow our vanity. Not of hair or clothes, but of brave hearts, strong hands, truth, purity, nobility. Let’s be so vain we refuse to stoop to meanness, so vain we reject petty envy, so vain we won’t utter a harsh word or act unkindly.

Let’s be vain of being upright gentlemen in a world full of crooks. Let’s pride ourselves on thinking big thoughts, doing great deeds, living good lives.

Because vanity, when directed right, is not weakness—it’s power. And the world, my friends, runs on power.

Now, I know what you’re thinking: “This is the kind of thing an idle fellow should avoid like a bad cold.” And you’d be right—up to a point. But here’s the thing: sometimes the best way to understand a race is not to run in it, but to sit on the sidelines with a sandwich and watch the chaos unfold. And so, while I’m comfortably ensconced in my little arbor (which, let’s be honest, is just a shed with ivy), puffing on my hookah (a device that looks suspiciously like a tea kettle with a pipe attached), I’ve been observing the great human stampede known as “getting on in the world.”

It’s quite a spectacle. Imagine, if you will, a never-ending parade of humanity hurtling down the highway of life—some sprinting, some shuffling, some tripping over their own shoelaces, and others, bless them, still trying to figure out which direction they’re supposed to be going. It’s like a circus without the elephants, or a party where everyone’s wearing too much perfume and no one can remember why they came.

You’ve got your old man gasping like he’s running a marathon for the first time, his face red as a boiled lobster. Then there’s the young lady being pushed along by her mother, who has the look of someone who once tried to make a cake and ended up setting the kitchen on fire. There’s the bookworm reading *How to Get On in the World* while getting trampled by people who haven’t read anything but the back of a cereal box. Meanwhile, the fashionable couple strolls past, looking like they’ve just stepped out of a magazine—and then they bump into each other, and the man mutters something about “inadequate personal space” while adjusting his cufflinks.

And don’t get me started on the oddballs. There’s the man with the noble face standing at the crossroads, staring at the mud like it’s a riddle he can’t solve. The thin-faced sneak who slinks along like a cat avoiding a bath. The rogue who keeps shifting from side to side, convinced he’s making progress when really he’s just zigzagging through the same puddle. And the boy, eyes wide, turning back every few seconds to catch a last glimpse of his hometown—the only place where people didn’t try to sell him insurance.

Then comes the girl whose face is already wrinkling from sheer effort, the weary man whose shoulders sag under the weight of unfulfilled dreams, and the hopeful lad who still believes the universe owes him a living. It’s all very dramatic, isn’t it? Like a stage play written by someone who’s never left the house but thinks they know everything about human nature.

And yet, despite the absurdity, despite the dust, the noise, the sheer ridiculousness of it all—there’s something magnetic about it. Something primal. I mean, who among us hasn’t felt that urge to join the fray? To throw our hat into the ring, even if we’re not entirely sure what ring we’re talking about?

I’ll admit: my arbor and my hookah are not the most realistic metaphors. They sound peaceful, philosophical, like something out of a Zen garden. But let’s face it—I’m not exactly the type to sit quietly while the world burns. I’m more like the Irishman who sees a crowd gathering and sends his daughter out to ask, “Is there going to be a row?” Because if there is, he wants in. He wants to see the fireworks. He wants to feel the heat.

I love watching people fight their way up. Not the ones who slip through by accident or cheat their way in. No, I admire the ones who actually *work* for it—those who stumble, fall, get up, swear at the sky, and keep going. It’s thrilling, like watching a dog chase its tail—but with more dignity.

And yes, life is full of giants and dragons. But these aren’t cartoonish monsters with three heads and a taste for small children. These are things like taxes, bad luck, bad decisions, and the occasional neighbor who plays the piano at 3 a.m. And the golden casket? That’s not just gold—it’s recognition, respect, maybe even a decent pension. But it’s not handed out on a silver platter. You have to earn it.

Take the classic story: Algernon leaves his ancestral home, wipes away a tear, and returns three years later rolling in riches. Sounds simple enough. But how? What happened in those three years? Did he work? Lie? Invent a new kind of soup? We never find out. That’s the problem with most novels—they skip the messy middle. A dozen pages on a tea party, then suddenly: “He became a merchant prince.” As if success were a magical spell cast by a passing cloud.

But real life isn’t like that. Real life is the office-boy who starts sweeping floors and ends up ruling the Queen’s navy. Or the barrister who starts with no cases and ends up judging a breach of promise suit—because apparently, that’s a thing. The details matter. The small victories. The near-misses. The moment you realize your boss hates you because you stole his lunch.

And speaking of bosses—Fortune, that capricious goddess, behaves a lot like a woman. She ignores you when you’re desperate, then flirts with you when you’ve given up. Ben Jonson said it best: “Court a mistress, she denies you; Let her alone, she will court you.” Same goes for money, fame, power—anything worth having. You turn your back, stop begging, and suddenly it’s knocking on your door. But by then, you’re too cool to care. Which is a shame, because it would’ve been nice to have her smile when you were still wet behind the ears.

People say this proves ambition is wrong. Nonsense. Ambition is what keeps the world moving. Without ambitious folks, we’d all be sitting around eating bread and waiting for the next apocalypse. Ambitious people are the ones who wake up early, hammer nails, shout at pigeons, and generally make it impossible for anyone else to sleep in. They’re the engine of progress.

Sure, contented people have their place. They’re like the background wallpaper—nice to look at, but you wouldn’t build a house with them. They’re good for quiet evenings, gossip, and pretending they’re wise. But don’t let them pretend they’re the ideal. They’re the drones in the hive, the ones who stand around watching the bees work and then complain about the honey.

And don’t believe they’re wise just because they’re happy. “A contented mind is happy anywhere,” yes—but so is a Jerusalem pony. And guess what happens to both? They get put wherever they’re needed, treated however they’re needed, and never asked for opinions. “Oh, don’t worry about him—he’s perfectly content.” And so the contented person gets passed over, while the grumbler gets promoted.

So here’s the secret: if you want something, ask for ten times more than you think you’ll get. If you’re happy with a hundred pounds, demand a thousand. Start low and you’ll end up with ten. Rousseau learned this the hard way. He dreamed of an orchard, a sweet woman, and a cow. He got the orchard. The woman was sour. She brought her mother. And there was no cow. Had he aimed higher—a mansion, angels, a cattle show—he might have ended up with a garden shed and a single goat. But hey, better than nothing.

And life for the contented? It must be dull. Reading the paper, smoking, playing the flute, and discussing Mrs. Thompson’s new curtains. No thrills. No hope. No risk. For the ambitious, life is a game. A wild, unpredictable, exhausting game. But it’s exciting. It’s alive. Even if you lose, you’ve played. You’ve fought. You’ve lived.

So come on, folks! Walk up! Walk up, ladies and gentlemen, boys and girls! Show your mettle! Prove your grit! The show is open, the game is on, and the prizes are real. Gold for the man, fame for the boy, rank for the maiden, pleasure for the fool. All can play. All can win. Some do. And for those who don’t?

Well, as the poet said:

So walk up. Take a hand. And may the odds be ever in your favor—or at least, may you enjoy the ride.

It’s been a dreadful week. Not because of anything I’ve done—though, let’s face it, I probably *have* done something—but because of the weather. That relentless, ungrateful, perpetually offended sky has decided to make my life a misery. And I didn’t even ask for it.

I’d set myself a noble goal: to write an article on something no one had ever written about before. Something fresh, original, startling—like, say, the emotional lives of houseflies or the secret desires of tax collectors. I sat in my armchair, chin in hand, gazing into the void (which, admittedly, is mostly just the ceiling), trying to conjure up a subject so unprecedented that when I published it, people would stop and whisper, “Who *is* this man? He speaks truths no mortal has dared utter!”

But nothing came. Not a spark. Not a single idea. Just silence—and the occasional cough from Mrs. Cutting, our charwoman, who was clearly judging me for my intellectual failure.

She arrived yesterday with her usual air of moral superiority, tea tray balanced like a sacred relic. “Lor’, sir,” she said, peering at me over her spectacles, “you do look worried.”

I told her I was trying to find a topic so revolutionary, so untouched by human thought, that it would cause a global crisis of curiosity. “A subject,” I declared, “that has never been discussed, debated, or even *dreamed* of by any soul since Adam ate the apple.”

She laughed—a low, rumbling chuckle that made the teapot tremble. “You’re a funny gentleman,” she said.

And there it was. My luck again. When I’m being serious, people laugh. When I try to be funny, they stare at me as if I’ve just announced I’m running for prime minister. Last week, I tried to tell a joke at dinner. A *good* one. It involved a vicar, a goat, and a very confused pigeon. I built it up carefully—started with Shakespeare, then casually dropped in, “Ah, that reminds me—such a funny thing happened the other day in Whitechapel.”

They leaned forward. “Oh?” someone said. “What was that?”

“Oh, it was *awfully* funny,” I said, already giggling. “It’ll make you roar.”

I launched into it. A long one. The kind that requires careful pacing, dramatic pauses, and a full understanding of the absurdity of human nature.

Silence. Thick, heavy silence. Then, after what felt like an eternity, one man asked, “And that was the joke?”

I assured him it was. They nodded politely. Except for the old fellow at the far end, who insisted he hadn’t understood which part was funny—was it what *he* said, or what *she* said? We argued for twenty minutes.

So much for humor.

Back to Mrs. Cutting. I denied being frivolous (though I may have been, slightly) and begged her for ideas. She pondered, tea tray still in hand, eyes glazing over as if recalling some ancient wisdom. Then she said, “Samplers.”

“Samplers?” I said. “As in embroidery?”

“Aye,” she said. “Never heard them spoke of much now, but they were all the rage when I was a girl.”

I declined samplers. Too niche. Too quiet. Not controversial enough.

She thought longer. Longer than seemed physically possible. Then, suddenly, she snapped her fingers. “Weather!” she declared. “That’s been most trying of late.”

And thus, like a man stepping off a cliff without realizing it, I fell headfirst into the abyss of meteorological complaint.

And now I can’t get out.

Because, honestly, the weather *is* terrible. At least, it is now. Or it will be by the time you read this. It always is. It’s like politics—always wrong, never right. In summer, it’s too hot. In winter, it’s too cold. In spring, it’s undecided. In autumn, it’s melancholy. If it’s sunny, we complain about droughts. If it rains, we curse the clouds. If December lacks snow, we accuse the heavens of fraud. If it does snow, we scream like banshees.

We are never satisfied. We want rain, but not *too* much rain. We want sun, but not *too* much sun. We want wind, but not *too* strong. We want everything—except what we actually get.

The only solution? Let each man make his own weather. Keep it private. Like a personal grudge or a well-timed sneeze. If that’s not possible, abolish weather entirely.

Of course, this wouldn’t work. Because while city dwellers despise the weather, country folk adore it. To them, it’s not a nuisance—it’s a companion. Snowfall is poetry. Frost is music. Rain is a gentle lullaby.

Imagine walking across a frozen field, your boots ringing like tiny bells, the air sharp and clear, children laughing in the distance, sheep-dogs barking like distant chimes. Or skating—gliding across ice like a bird on steel wings, the world blurring beneath you. Or springtime, when the trees blush pink and white, like shy village girls in their Sunday best, and the cuckoo sings its lonely song through the woods.

Autumn? Ah, autumn is the season of beauty and sorrow. Golden leaves, blood-red sunsets, misty evenings, orchards heavy with fruit, and the soft hum of reapers. Even the rain seems useful—just a servant doing its job.

But in the city? Forget it.

Here, weather is a skylark in a bank. Out of place. Annoying. Unwelcome.

The painted stucco cracks under smoky sun. The rain brings slush and mud. The snow piles up in dirty heaps. The wind whistles down dingy streets, shrieking around gaslit corners. Nature, once sweet and free, becomes a nuisance.

We liked her in the hayfield. But in Pall Mall? She’s just too much. Her laughter is too loud. Her voice too hearty. Her ways too bold.

Just lately, she’s been showering us with rain—nonstop, relentless, as if the sky has a grudge against us. I am, as Mr. Mantalini might say, a “demned damp, moist, unpleasant body.”

Our neighbor, a man who once grew cucumbers in a frame, insists the rain is “doing the country a world of good.” He says this every time he steps into the back garden, which is roughly every five minutes. He doesn’t understand agriculture. He just likes to pretend he does.

I hope he’s right. Because while the rain may be helping the countryside, it’s destroying me.

My clothes are ruined. My temper is worse. My new spring suit—once a thing of beauty—is now a mud-stained relic, hanging in my closet like a forgotten crime scene.

It was Jim’s fault. He saw it, threw up his arms, and yelled, “Got ‘em again!”

I asked, “Does it fit behind?”

“Spiffin, old man,” he said.

Then he demanded I go out. “A man with a suit like that has no right to stay indoors,” he declared. “Every citizen owes a duty to the public. Come out and give the girls a treat.”

Jim speaks in slang. I don’t know where he gets it. Definitely not from me.

I said, “Do you think they’ll really enjoy it?”

He said it would be like a day in the country to them.

That settled it.

I went.

When I got home, I undressed, rubbed myself with whisky, soaked my feet in hot water, slapped a mustard plaster on my chest, drank gruel and brandy-and-water, tallowed my nose, and crawled into bed.

I survived. But the suit? That was gone.

Now it’s just a splash-board.

And I did love that suit.

Which is typical. I never grow fond of anything before it’s destroyed. I had a tame rat once. I adored it. One day, it fell into a dish of gooseberry fool. Nobody noticed until the second helping.

I hate wet weather. Not the wet itself—though that’s bad enough—but the *mud*.

There’s something about me that attracts mud. I don’t know how. Maybe it’s my aura. Or my posture. Or the fact that I walk with the confidence of a man who knows he’s about to be covered in filth.

Other people walk through storms and emerge clean. I step outside and return looking like a rejected swamp creature.

If there were only one spot of mud in London, I’d claim it.

And I’d keep it.

But I can’t even return the affection. I loathe the “London particular”—that thick, greasy fog that settles over the city like a shroud. On dirty days, I feel muggy, miserable, and generally unloved. The only relief is stripping off my clothes and crawling into bed.

Everything goes wrong in wet weather. More people, more dogs, more perambulators, more cabs, more carts—all blocking your path. Everyone is irritable. Except me. I’m always irritable.

And I always seem to be carrying more things. A bag. Three parcels. A newspaper. Then it starts to rain.

And you can’t open your umbrella.

Which leads me to another horror: April weather.

Poets call it charming. They compare it to a woman—moody, changeable, unpredictable. I suppose it’s romantic. But I don’t care for romance when it’s raining on my head.

A woman’s tears won’t soak your trousers. Her coldness won’t give you rheumatism. But the east wind? Oh, yes. That’ll do it.

I can handle a bad day. But these “ha’porth-of-all-sorts” days—sun one minute, rain the next, wind, hail, sunshine again—are unbearable.

Especially when the sun comes out after a downpour and smiles at you like a cheeky schoolboy. “Lord love you,” it seems to say, “you don’t mean to say you’re wet? Well, I’m surprised. Why, it was only my fun.”

Then there’s the automaton umbrella. I bought one in April. It was supposed to open and shut itself. It did. But not when I wanted it to.

When it rained, it refused to open. I struggled, shook it, cursed it. Then, as soon as the rain stopped, it snapped open—staying up like a stubborn hat.

I walked around under a bright blue sky, umbrella over my head, wishing it would rain again.

Then, when it finally closed, it did so with a violent jerk—knocking my hat off.

Nothing makes a man look more ridiculous than losing his hat.

The sudden realization that your head is bare—that helpless, sinking feeling—well, it’s one of life’s cruelest jokes.

Then comes the chase. You run after it, dog in pursuit, thinking it’s a game. You knock over children, mothers, fat gentlemen, perambulators, and eventually crash into a wet sweep.

After that, the spectators laugh. The hat returns—dripping, muddy, disgraceful.

All of which proves one thing: spring is a disaster in cities.

March winds. April showers. May flowers? Nonexistent.

In the country, it’s lovely. In towns? Abolish it.

Spring belongs in nature. Not in the grim workshops of urban life.

Children shouldn’t play in muddy streets. Real children are bright-eyed, chubby, shy. These are dingy, screeching elves, their faces worn by smoke and stress.

Life and spring should both be cradled in green fields.

To us, spring brings only cold winds and drizzle.

We must seek it in leafless woods, brambly lanes, moors, hills—anywhere away from concrete and chaos.

There, the air is fresh. The wind rushes. The sky is wide. Life feels bigger, freer.

Through the silver rifts in the clouds, we glimpse something vast—something grand.

Strange thoughts stir. Voices call.

We reach for light, not knowing what we seek.

Our thoughts, like the boys in the Danish song, are long, long thoughts—vague, undefined.

And that’s okay.

Because all thoughts that reach beyond this narrow world must be dim and shapeless.

The only thoughts we can grasp are small ones: two and two make four. Eat when hungry. Honesty is the best policy.

Greater thoughts? Vast. Undefined.

We see only dimly through the mists of life.

And hear, faintly, the great sea surging beyond.

Let me tell you, this morning has been a disaster—pure, unadulterated chaos—and it’s all thanks to our household pets. The villain of the piece? Gustavus Adolphus. Yes, that’s his full name. Downstairs, they call him “Gusty” for short, like he’s some kind of heroic figure from a children's book. But I assure you, Gusty is no hero. He’s more like a furry tornado with poor impulse control and an alarming talent for destruction.

Now, don’t get me wrong—I love dogs in general. They’re loyal, affectionate, and always ready to cheer you up when life gets too serious. But Gustavus Adolphus is not one of those dogs. He’s built like a small tank and thinks every room should be a personal playground. When he stretches, two chairs collapse and the what-not goes flying. When he wags his tail, it looks like a hurricane just passed through. And when he breathes? Well, let’s just say the fire dies instantly, as if he’s exhaled a tiny cyclone.

At dinner, he sneaks under the table, lies there quietly for a moment, then suddenly decides to stand up. The first sign we have of this is the table lifting off the floor like it’s doing a backflip. We all grab it, screaming, trying to hold it down while he panics beneath, convinced we’re staging a coup against him. The result? A toppled table, a shattered dinner, and everyone sprawled out like extras in a slapstick film. It’s become a weekly ritual.

This morning was no different. He came in like a storm front—no warning, no grace, just sheer, unbridled energy. His tail swept my coffee cup right off the table, sending liquid directly into the center of my waistcoat. I jumped up, muttering something unprintable (but quite satisfying), and charged toward him. He beat me to the door. There, he met Eliza, who was bringing in the eggs. She saw him coming and said, “Ugh!”—a single syllable of pure horror—and promptly sat down on the floor. The eggs scattered like confetti. Some rolled under the sofa, others cracked open in the middle of the rug, and one even managed to land perfectly in the ashtray. Gusty didn’t stick around. He bolted, and I called after him: “Go downstairs! Don’t come back for at least an hour!” He seemed to agree—he dodged the coal-scoop like a ninja and vanished.

I dried myself off, finished breakfast, and assumed he’d gone outside. Ten minutes later, I checked the passage—and there he was, sitting proudly at the top of the stairs. I ordered him down. He barked, jumped, and refused to move. So I went to investigate. What I found was Tittums.

Tittums is our kitten. She’s about the size of a penny roll—tiny, fluffy, and utterly terrifying. She was perched on the second-to-top step, back arched, fur standing on end, and swearing like a sailor who’d lost his ship and his dignity. I know she’s young, but honestly, the volume and variety of her curses are impressive. I swear myself sometimes—don’t tell anyone, especially your wife—but I’m only an amateur compared to her. Swearing, I believe, is nature’s way of letting off steam. It’s like a safety valve. When you’ve had a bad day, and someone steps on your foot, you can’t just say, “Oh dear, how clumsy of you,” can you? No. You need to unleash a torrent of colorful language—“Bless you, my dear sweet sir, what in the name of all that’s holy made you so careless as to allow your light and delicate foot to descend upon my corn with such force?” That kind of thing clears the mind. It’s cheaper than smashing furniture or throwing things. And let’s face it, a little explosion now and then keeps the soul healthy.

I tried explaining this to my aunt once. She wasn’t impressed. She said I shouldn’t *have* such feelings. Which is why I told Tittums exactly the same thing: “You ought to be ashamed of yourself, brought up in a Christian home like this!” I don’t mind an old cat swearing—it’s part of the charm—but a kitten? It breaks my heart. So I put her in my pocket and returned to work.

I forgot about her. Then I looked up and found her out of the pocket, on the desk, trying to swallow my pen. Then she stuck her paw in the inkwell, knocked it over, licked it clean, and then turned to me and swore again—this time directly at me. I dropped her on the floor. Instantly, Tim started a fight.

Tim is a two-year-old fox-terrier with delusions of grandeur. He thinks he’s a wise old Scottish collie, but he’s really just a dog who likes to interfere. He doesn’t care what Tittums is doing; he feels compelled to correct her. Naturally, Tittums’ mother arrived just in time to scratch Tim’s nose. I was delighted. I threw them all three into the passage, where they’re still wrestling like miniature gladiators.

Meanwhile, I’m covered in ink and in a mood fit to strangle a cactus. If another cat or dog comes near me today, I may just have to hire a funeral director on the spot. But despite all this, I still adore cats and dogs. They’re far better company than most humans.

They never argue. They never talk about themselves—they just sit there, listening intently, pretending to care about your latest existential crisis. They never make awkward comments like, “I always thought Miss Brown liked Mr. Jones,” or mistake your wife’s cousin for her husband. They never ask a struggling writer why he hasn’t written a play yet. They never say cruel things. They never point out your flaws “for your own good.” They never remind you of past mistakes with a smirk. And they never whisper, “Oh yes, a lot of use you are when you’re actually needed.”

But when you’re sad, they don’t lecture. They don’t offer advice. They just come close. A cat will climb onto your shoulder, ruffle your hair, and silently say, “Lor’, I’m sorry for you, old man.” A dog will look up with those big, honest eyes and say, “You’ve got me. Always. Through thick and thin. We’ll face it together.”

Dogs are foolish, yes. They don’t care if you’re rich or poor, wise or silly, saint or sinner. You’re their pal. That’s enough. They’ll follow you into danger, guard you, protect you—even die for you. And yet, we call them animals, as if that’s a flaw. We act like we’re superior—clever, rational, noble beings. But what do we really know? We don’t know where we came from. We don’t know where we’re going. We don’t know what’s beyond this tiny planet. Yet we still think we’re better than a dog who loves us without condition.

Cats, too, get a bad rap. People say they’re selfish, cold, calculating. But that’s not true. Give a cat your trust, and she’ll stay by you through everything. One of mine used to follow me everywhere—so much so that I had to beg her, as a personal favor, not to walk behind me down the High Street. Another one would wait up for me when I was late, meet me at the door, and greet me like a long-lost spouse—except she never asked where I’d been, which was refreshing.

And then there’s the drunkard cat—the one who’d hang around the cellar door all day, waiting to sneak in and lap up beer drippings. She was vain, too. Every time she caught a big rat, she’d bring it into the living room, lay it down proudly, and wait for applause. The girls would scream. Poor rats. They exist only so cats can gain glory and chemists can sell poison.

Still, I find rats fascinating. There’s something eerie about them. They’re cunning, secretive, relentless. They swarm in abandoned houses, whispering secrets in dark corners, gnawing through walls, creeping through cracks. They know when a ship is sinking and leave before anyone else does. They’re the stars of horror stories—gnawing behind the wainscot, glowing red-eyed in the darkness, screaming in the night. I love reading tales about them. Like the story of Bishop Hatto, who hoarded grain and burned starving people alive. Then thousands of rats came, swam across the river, chewed through stone, and ate him alive. “They have whetted their teeth against the stones, / And now they pick the bishop’s bones.” Lovely tale.

Or the Pied Piper of Hamelin. First, he lures the rats away with his magic tune. Then, when the mayor refuses to pay, he pipes the children away too. I wonder what that story means. Is it just a fairy tale? Or is there something deeper? I often imagine the Pied Piper still walking among us, playing softly so only children can hear. That’s why they look so solemn sometimes—like they’ve heard something we can’t. Maybe they’ve seen him. Maybe he’s real.

And maybe, just maybe, we grown-ups hear him too—just faintly, from far away. One day, the music will grow loud, and we’ll drop everything and follow, just like the children did. Our families will try to stop us, but we’ll push gently past, drawn by a wild, strange song. Because deep down, we know what it means.

Now, about pet worship—please, let’s not go there. Some people go too far. Women especially. There are those who read *David Copperfield* and immediately want a long-haired dog that critiques men’s trousers with a sniff of disdain. They talk to it like it’s a person, kiss its nose, press its dirty head to their cheek—mostly when there are young men nearby. Then there are the elderly ladies who dote on fat, flea-ridden poodles. I knew two spinsters once who treated their dog like a human. They washed its face every morning, gave it mutton cutlets for breakfast, and one of them stayed home on Sundays just to keep the dog company while the other went to church.

In some homes, the dog is the center of everything. You want to flirt with the eldest daughter? Start with the dog. Want to borrow the garden roller? Win Fido’s approval first. If he snarls at you, you’re done. “If Fido won’t take to any one,” the father says wisely, “I say that man is not to be trusted.” Bless him. Drat him.

It’s tragic, really. That innocent puppy—full of fun, ambition, and dreams of becoming a great dog—is turned into a grumpy, pampered creature by human excess. The world is like a grinding machine. Fresh, bright things go in one end and come out wrinkled, crabbed, and dull. Look at Pussy Sobersides—sleepy eyes, slow walk, prudish airs. Who’d guess she was once a whirlwind of blue eyes and mad energy?

Kittens are amazing. Full of life, bursting with vitality. They leap, they mew, they roll, they kick. They don’t know what to do with all that energy. Can you remember, reader, when you felt that way? When you were young and the world was yours? Walking home under the moonlight, feeling too full of life to walk normally—you had to jump, wave your arms, shout until farmers’ wives hid behind hedges. You laughed at their fear, and you didn’t know why you were crying. Oh, that glorious, reckless youth!

That’s what I miss. That divine spark. Now, my pulse is slow, my joints ache, and I prefer my armchair and pipe. But oh, to feel that wild, electric life again—just for one moment.

All great literary men are shy. I am myself—though, as I’m told, it’s barely noticeable. I’m glad it is not. It used to be glaringly obvious once upon a time, and caused me no end of suffering—and quite a bit of inconvenience to everyone within shouting distance, especially my female acquaintances, who complained about it with the kind of dramatic flair usually reserved for lost gloves or broken teacups.

To be shy is not a happy state. Men find you dull; women think you’re aloof; and you, poor soul, loathe yourself. Practice doesn’t help. Time is the only cure—though I once stumbled across a remedy so delightfully absurd that I’ve never forgotten it. It appeared in the “Answers to Correspondents” section of a minor weekly publication and read:

Ah, bless the man! I can picture him now, reading those words with a smile so wide it must have stretched from ear to ear like a grinning jack-o’-lantern. "Adopt an easy and pleasing manner!" For heaven’s sake, don’t even *try*, young shyer. Your attempt to pretend you’re anything but your true self will result in you sounding like a wet noodle trying to flirt with a storm. You’ll come off gushing like a leaky faucet and familiar as a street urchin asking for spare change. Be yourself—your natural, awkward, blushing self—and you’ll just be seen as surly and dim-witted.

Yet, the shy man does get his revenge. He spreads his misery like a cold draft through a room. The most cheerful gathering turns solemn in his presence. People feel uneasy. They fidget. They check their watches. And all because they misread him. They mistake his hesitation for arrogance, his silence for superiority, his trembling hands for hauteur. When he blushes violently at the first word spoken to him, they assume he’s been insulted by passion—when in fact he’s simply being crushed by the weight of existence.

Misunderstanding is the shy man’s lifelong companion. He tells a joke—it’s taken as a lie. He makes a sarcastic remark—it’s accepted as gospel truth, and he’s labeled an idiot. He tries to compliment someone—it’s interpreted as mockery, and he’s blackballed for life. All these little disasters are endlessly entertaining to others, providing comic material since the dawn of satire. But if we look past the laughter, there’s something deeper—something almost tragic.

A shy man is, by definition, a lonely man. He walks among people but does not belong. There’s an invisible wall between him and the world—a barrier he can’t scale, no matter how hard he tries. On the other side, he sees smiling faces, hears laughter, feels the warmth of human connection. But he cannot reach out. He wants to join, to speak, to belong. But the crowd moves on, chatting merrily, unaware. He tries to step forward—but the walls follow him, closing in tighter than ever.

In the crowded street, the packed party, the office meeting, the theater—wherever people gather, wherever voices rise and eyes meet—the shy man stands apart, like a leper in a festival. His heart is full of kindness, longing, even love—but the world sees only silence. His soul screams, but his voice fails. Words die on his lips like fish out of water. He wants to comfort the weary, condemn injustice, cheer the downtrodden—but his emotions turn inward, festering like unhealed wounds. They don’t flow outward; they poison him. That’s why shy men often become cynics—not because they hate the world, but because they can’t express their love for it.

Yes, shy men, like ugly women, have a rough ride in this world. To survive it requires the hide of a rhinoceros. Thick skin is our moral armor. Without it, we’re unfit for polite society. A trembling, blushing creature with twitching fingers and knees like jelly is a spectacle—painful to watch, unbearable to endure. If it can’t fix itself, well… let’s just say the sooner it retires to the quiet woods, the better.

But here’s the good news: shyness *can* be cured. I know this from personal experience. I won’t go on about myself—usually I avoid such things—but today, for the sake of humanity, I’ll make an exception. Once, I was, as the lad in the Bab Ballad put it, “the shyest of the shy.” “Whenever I was introduced to any pretty maid,” he said, “my knees they knocked together just as if I was afraid.” And so did mine.

Now, I would—nay, have—done something remarkable. Just yesterday, I stood alone (as the schoolboy said when translating Caesar) and confronted a railway refreshment-room attendant in her own domain. I rebuked her—in tones of sorrow and mild indignation—for her callousness and lack of deference to the traveling Briton. I insisted, courteously but firmly, that I deserved proper attention. And then—I looked her straight in the eye.

Need I say more? Well, yes—because immediately afterward, I left the room with what may have seemed like haste. But that wasn’t fear. No, no. I simply changed my mind. I didn’t need refreshment after all.

One consolation for the shy: shyness isn’t stupidity. It takes real nerve to sneer at nerves. The highest minds aren’t always the ones with the thickest skins. The deer isn’t inferior to the pig. The horse isn’t lesser than the cock-sparrow. Shyness means sensitivity. It has nothing to do with vanity or self-consciousness—though fools insist on linking the two.

Conceit, however, is the best cure. When you realize you’re vastly superior to everyone else, bashfulness vanishes. You glance around a room and think: “These people are mere children. Magpies with degrees.” Suddenly, you’re not afraid of them. You’re above them. And that’s freedom.

Real conceit isn’t the loud, strutting kind—the one with the raised nose and falsetto voice. That’s just play-acting. Real conceit is quiet, confident, unbothered. It doesn’t need to show off. It doesnches not care about praise or blame. It’s content with itself. Such a man is genial, kind, simple. He’s equally at home with a duke or a cabbie. He doesn’t flatter or feign—he just *is*. And that’s rare.

The shy man, meanwhile, is humble—too humble. He doubts himself, fears judgment. But that’s fine for youth. Character is still forming. With time, insight, and experience, diffidence fades. Most men shed shyness by the age of twenty-five. If not, the world polishes it off anyway. You rarely meet a truly shy man—except in novels or on stage, where he’s wildly popular, especially with the ladies.

There, in that magical land of fiction, he’s a golden-haired saint—fair hair and virtue always go together on the stage. One actor once lost his wig mid-performance and had to go on with his natural jet-black hair. The audience howled. They thought he was the villain. They couldn’t believe a noble hero would look so dark and menacing.

This idealized version loves the heroine deeply—but only in asides. He’s noble, unselfish, speaks softly, adores his mother. The villains mock him, but he bears it with grace. Then, miraculously, it turns out he’s actually brilliant. The heroine confesses her love. He’s stunned, overjoyed. Everyone forgives him. He gives a few wise, sarcastic words and blesses them all. He seems to have a grand old time. So naturally, every non-shy young man wishes he were shy.

But the real shy man knows better. In reality, he’s clumsier, less devoted, and his hair is darker—making the whole fantasy collapse under its own weight. Still, one thing he shares with his fictional counterpart: faithfulness. He *is* loyal in love. Why? Because it takes all his courage just to look one woman in the face. He’d never have the strength to try again. He’s terrified of the entire female sex. One is enough. He’s safe in his solitude.

Not so the bold young man. He’s surrounded by roguish eyes and laughing lips. Naturally, he gets confused. He forgets which pair belongs to his intended and ends up making eyes at the wrong woman. The shy man? He stares at his boots. He sees nothing. He’s immune. Happy shy man!

Well, not really. He longs to “go it” like the others. He curses himself daily for lacking the nerve. Occasionally, he gathers himself and dives into mischief. But it’s always a disaster. He flounders, stumbles, and retreats—limp, defeated, pitiable.

And yet—no one pities him. Some misfortunes cause agony but earn no sympathy. Losing an umbrella? Funny. Toothache? Hilarious. Black eyes? Classic. But shyness? That’s comedy gold.

He becomes the living punchline. “Look!” cries the audience. “He’s blushing!”
“Watch his legs,” says another.
“He’s sitting on the edge of the chair!”
“Pity he’s got so many hands,” murmurs a lady, folding hers calmly.
“A yard off his feet wouldn’t hurt,” adds the comic relief.
“And with that voice, he should’ve been a sea captain!”

They point out his cough, his limited conversation, his nervous grip on his hat. They exhaust both him and themselves. Then his family joins in—because relatives are allowed to be extra cruel.

They mimic him. One pretends to walk in like him—shuffling, stiff, trembling—then explains, “That’s how you do it.” Another demonstrates “how you shake hands”—grabbing each person’s hand like it’s red-hot, then dropping it like a dead fish. Then they ask: “Why do you blush?” “Why do you stutter?” “Why do you whisper?” As if he’s doing it on purpose.

Then someone struts about like a peacock and says, “You should walk like this.”
The old man slaps him on the back: “Be bold, my boy!”
The mother beams: “Never do anything you need be ashamed of, Algernon. Then you’ll never be ashamed.”
The boys: “You’re worse than a girl!”
The girls: “No girl would be half as bad!”
Which is true. No girl would. There’s no such thing as a shy woman—at least, I’ve never met one. Until I do, I won’t believe in them.

People say women are timid, gentle, blushing creatures—like startled deer. Men, meanwhile, are bold and fearless. Women admire us, but fear us. A sweet idea. But nonsense.

The twelve-year-old girl is cool as ice. Her brother, twenty, stammers beside her. A woman enters a concert late, interrupts the performance, and doesn’t bat an eyelid. Her husband follows, crumpled with apologies. Women have stronger nerves in matters of love—from the first glance to the last day of the honeymoon. It’s universally acknowledged.

And anyway, love is *their* business. We all shed our weaknesses when it comes to work. The shyest man I ever knew? A photographic tout. He sold pictures with the confidence of a lion tamer.

ON BABIES. Oh, absolutely—yes, I know all about them. I *was* one once. Not for long, though. Not nearly as long as my clothes were. Honestly, those garments were like a full-length curtain draped over a teacup. They hung down past my toes, flapping around like a flag of surrender every time I tried to kick. Why do babies wear such absurdly long clothing? It’s not a joke—it’s a genuine question. Is it because parents are embarrassed by how small their offspring is and want to pretend it’s taller than it actually is? I asked a nurse once. She said, “Lor’, sir, they always have long clothes, bless their little hearts.” When I pointed out that her answer, while touching, didn’t really explain anything, she replied, “Lor’, sir, you wouldn’t have ‘em in short clothes, poor little dears?” And she said it with the kind of tone that made me feel like I’d just suggested cutting off the baby’s limbs for fun. Since then, I’ve avoided asking further. The mystery remains unsolved.

But honestly, why dress babies at all? Life already involves enough putting on and taking off garments without starting the ordeal before birth. One would think that beings who spend most of their time horizontal might be spared this daily ritual. Why wake them up in the morning to strip them bare, slap on new clothes, tuck them back into bed, only to haul them out again at night to reverse the whole process? And when it’s all done, what’s the difference between the nightshirt and the daytime outfit? They’re practically identical—just slightly less clean.

Of course, I may be making a fool of myself. I often am, according to others. So I’ll stop now—except to say that it would be incredibly helpful if someone invented a fashion that let you tell a boy from a girl. Right now, it’s impossible. Hair? No clue. Dress? Same for both. Conversation? Nonexistent. You’re left guessing—and you’re always wrong. And when you are, you’re instantly labeled either a complete idiot or a malicious trickster. Calling a male child “she” is considered an insult worse than treason. Referring to a female as “he” is even more unforgivable. The sex the child doesn’t belong to becomes a national disgrace. Mention it, and you’re personally attacking the family honor.

And don’t even think about saying “it.” That’s the ultimate sin. You could murder a whole family and dump them in a reservoir—people would grumble, but they’d understand it was a crime of passion. Rob a church? The vicar would hate you, sure, but he’d still give you a decent funeral. But call a newborn “it”? That’s social suicide. You’ll be shunned faster than a man caught stealing from a charity box during a sermon.

So what should you say instead? “Little angel.” Perfect. The word “angel” is gender-neutral, which makes it ideal. Plus, everyone loves it. Add a quick giggle and a smile, and you’re golden. “Pet” or “beauty” work in a pinch, but “angel” earns you points for taste and tenderness. Also, never fail to mention that the baby has its father’s nose. This is the *real* magic trick. Say it with confidence. The parents will scoff at first—“Oh, nonsense!”—but you must insist. “No, really! Look! It’s unmistakable!” You can afford to be dramatic. After all, the nose is basically a smudge of skin, so it looks exactly like everything else. But the parents will pretend to laugh and secretly adore you for it.

Don’t ignore these tips. There will come a day—probably soon—when you’re standing between two women (one young, one ancient), surrounded by giggling girls who clearly aren’t admiring you, and facing a tiny, bald-headed bundle of humanity. At that moment, you’ll wish you’d paid attention.

Unmarried men are especially vulnerable during the dreaded “seeing baby” ritual. Just hearing the suggestion sends a cold shiver down your spine. Your smile turns sickly, and your heart sinks. You say, “How delighted I shall be,” but you sound like you’re being sentenced to life in prison. I suspect this entire tradition is a plot hatched by wives to keep bachelors away. It’s cruel, but effective.

The bell rings. A servant runs off to fetch the nurse. The moment the door opens, the ladies launch into full “baby” mode—gushing, cooing, pointing. You’re left alone, staring into space, trying to remember if you’ve got any urgent appointments—anything, really—to escape to. You start drafting an excuse: “A man outside says he needs me immediately…” But just as you’re getting into it, the door swings open.

In walks a tall, stern woman carrying what looks like a lopsided pillow with feathers stuck to one end. But instinct tells you—this is the baby. You rise slowly, pretending enthusiasm. The room erupts in adoration. Once the initial wave subsides, the circle parts, and you’re gently ushered forward. You step forward like a prisoner entering the dock at Bow Street. Silence falls. Everyone watches you expectantly. You try to speak. Nothing comes. Your brain has shut down. Panic sets in. Your inner demon whispers: *Say something dumb. Anything.*

You glance around, grin foolishly, and blurt out: “It hasn’t got much hair, has it?”

Silence. Then, gravely, the nurse says: “It is not customary for children five weeks old to have long hair.”

Another silence. You feel like you’ve been given a second chance. You seize it. “Can it walk yet? What do they feed it on?”

Now you’re officially regarded as slightly unhinged. Pity replaces judgment. But the nurse won’t let you off. In the solemn voice of a high priestess, she says: “Take her in your arms, sir.”

You accept the burden like a condemned man receiving his last meal. “Put your arm more down her middle, sir,” she instructs. The room watches you intently, as if you’re about to perform a dangerous circus act.

What do you do? You don’t know. But something *must* be done. So you lift the baby up and down, chanting “Oopsee-daisy!” like a madman.

“No, sir,” says the nurse, “don’t jig her. A very little upsets her.”

You freeze. You hope you haven’t already ruined everything.

Then—disaster. The baby, who’s been studying you with utter disdain, lets out a scream that could wake the dead. The nurse snatches it back. “There! There! There! What did ums do to ums?”

“Oh, how extraordinary!” you say, brightening. “Whatever made it go off like that?”

“Oh,” says the mother, indignant, “you must have done something to her! The child wouldn’t scream like that for nothing!”

They think you’ve been stabbing it with needles. You’re now under suspicion.

Eventually, the baby calms. But then some busybody shouts, “Who’s this, baby?” The infant recognizes you and shrieks again. A fat old lady comments, “It’s strange how children take a dislike to anyone.”

“Oh, they know,” replies another mysteriously.

“It’s a wonderful thing,” adds a third.

Everyone stares at you, convinced you’re a criminal mastermind, unmasked by a child’s uncanny intuition. You’re the villain of the story, and the baby’s the detective.

But despite their tantrums and tantrums, babies aren’t all bad. They serve a purpose. They warm empty hearts. They make love bloom in faces clouded by worry. Their tiny hands smooth wrinkles into smiles. They’re the world’s unintentional comedians—the absurd stars of life’s too-serious drama. Each one is a tiny rebel, determined to do the wrong thing at the wrong time, in the wrong place, and in the wrong way.

The nurse who sent Jenny to check on Tommy and Totty and “tell ‘em they mustn’t” understood infantile nature perfectly. Give a baby a fair chance, and if it doesn’t do something it shouldn’t, call a doctor. They’re geniuses at ridiculousness, and they do it with such serious dignity it’s irresistible.

Two babies will join hands and march eastward at breakneck speed, ignoring their big sister’s frantic cries to follow her west. They’ll walk around soldiers, poking them to see if they’re real. They’ll declare the shy man at the back of the bus is “dadda,” no matter how hard you argue. They’ll pick a crowded street corner to shout about family business in a shrill, piercing voice. Midway across the road, they’ll suddenly decide to dance. They’ll sit on shop doorsteps to remove their shoes. At home, they’ll use the biggest walking stick—or an open umbrella—as a ladder to climb stairs. They’ll discover they love Mary Ann precisely when she’s blackleading the stove, and nothing will satisfy them but a fierce hug right then. Their favorite meals? Coke and cat’s meat. They nurse the cat upside down and show affection for the dog by pulling its tail.

Yes, they’re a nuisance. They mess up the house. They cost a fortune. But you wouldn’t trade them. The house wouldn’t feel like home without their noisy voices and mischievous hands. Would the rooms seem quiet without their pattering feet? Might you drift apart if no prattling voice called you together?

Yet sometimes, I wonder—does that tiny hand become a wedge? It’s hard to criticize a mother’s love, that purest of human emotions, that perfect touch to a woman’s life. It’s sacred, and we coarse-minded men barely grasp it. But surely it needn’t swallow up everything? The baby doesn’t have to take your whole heart, like the rich man who walled up the desert well. Is there no other thirsty traveler waiting by?

In your desire to be a good mother, don’t forget to be a good wife. Don’t let all thought and care go to one. Don’t snap at poor Edwin when he asks you to come out: “What, and leave baby?” Don’t spend every evening upstairs. Don’t talk only about whooping-cough and measles. My dear little woman, the child isn’t going to die every time it sneezes. The house won’t burn down every time you step outside. The nurse won’t run off with a soldier. And the cat won’t jump on the baby’s chest the moment you leave the room.

You worry too much—about the baby, and about everyone else. Try to remember your other duties. Your face won’t always be wrinkled with anxiety. Cheerfulness can exist in the parlor as well as the nursery. Think of your big baby sometimes. Dance him around. Call him sweet names. Laugh at him now and then. It’s only the first baby that consumes all a woman’s time. Five or six don’t need nearly as much attention as one. But by then, the damage is done. A house where there’s no room for him, and a wife too busy to notice him—well, that loses its grip on the unreasonable husband. He starts looking elsewhere for comfort.

But there, there, there! I’ll get a reputation as a baby-hater if I go on like this. And Heaven knows I’m not. Who could be, seeing those innocent faces clustered in timid helplessness near the great gates that open down into the world?

The world—the small round world! How vast and mysterious it must seem to baby eyes! The back garden looks like a trackless continent. The cellar under the stairs becomes a land of adventure. They stare down the long street, wondering where it ends—just like we bigger babies wonder where the stars lead. And down that longest street of all—that dim, endless road of life—they cast grave, old-fashioned glances. Sometimes, pitiful, frightened ones.

I once saw a little mite sitting on a doorstep in a Soho slum. The gaslight showed me a look on its wizen face—a look of dull despair, as if the ghost of its own squalid life had risen from the court and struck its heart dead with horror. Poor little feet, just beginning the stony journey!

We old travelers, far down the road, can only pause and wave. You come out of the dark mist, and we, looking back, see you—so tiny—standing on the brow of the hill, your arms stretched toward us. God speed you! We’d stay and hold your hands, but the murmur of the great sea is in our ears, and we cannot linger. We must hurry down, for the shadowy ships are waiting to spread their sable sails.

I’ve always had a soft spot for food and drink—though back in my youth, it was mostly just the eating part. I was a boy of appetite, yes, but also of digestion, which is more than can be said for some people I know. I recall one day when a pale-faced, sallow-skinned gentleman came to dine at our house. He watched me eat for about five minutes with the intensity of a man studying a rare species of beetle. Then he turned to my father and asked, “Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?”

My father, being a man of few words, replied, “Never heard him complain.”

“Do you ever suffer from dyspepsia, Colly wobbles?” the guest inquired.

“No, pa,” I said.

“What *is* dyspepsia, pa?” I added, because I was curious.

The man looked at me as if I’d just announced I didn’t believe in gravity. A long pause followed. Then, in a voice thick with sorrowful wisdom, he said: “You will know… someday.”

And so I did. But not until much later, and by then it was too late to do anything about it except complain loudly.

My mother used to say she loved seeing me eat. It gave her joy, she claimed. And honestly, that’s one of the sweetest things I’ve ever been told. There’s something deeply comforting about knowing you’re a source of aesthetic pleasure to someone—even if it’s only because you can clear a plate like a steamroller.

A healthy boy, full of energy and wisely avoiding excessive study (which, let’s face it, is just another word for “being bored”), can generally satisfy even the most demanding expectations when it comes to dining. Watching boys eat is a delight—if you don’t have to pay for it. Their idea of a proper meal? About a pound and a half of roast beef, six potatoes (preferably the kind that look like they’ve been scrubbed with a toothbrush), a mountain of greens, four thick slices of Yorkshire pudding, two currant dumplings, a handful of green apples, a pen’orth of nuts, half a dozen jumbles, and a bottle of ginger beer. Then, after all that, they go outside and pretend to be horses. What audacity!

They must look down on us grown men, who sit quietly for two hours after consuming a spoonful of soup and a chicken wing, pretending we’re not secretly plotting revenge on the universe. We are weaklings compared to them.

But the boys don’t have everything their own way. They never get the luxury of being truly satisfied. A boy never feels *full*. He can never stretch out his legs, put his hands behind his head, close his eyes, and float into the blissful haze of postprandial contentment. That’s reserved for the adult male.

To a man, a good dinner is like a magic potion. The world becomes brighter, softer, more forgiving. After a decent meal, I find myself stroking the cat gently and whispering, “Poor pussy,” as though I’ve just read a novel about lost love. I feel sympathy for the German band playing outside, wondering if they’re cold. Even my wife’s relations—usually the bane of my existence—become bearable. For a moment, I’m not angry at them. I might even smile.

A good dinner brings out the tender side of every man. The grumpy, sour-faced types who spend all day looking like they’ve been dipped in vinegar suddenly break into smiles. They start patting children on the head and talking vaguely about sixpences. Serious men thaw. Snobby young men with heavy mustaches forget how objectionable they are.

I become sentimental after meals. It’s the only time I can properly appreciate love stories. When the hero clutches the heroine in a final embrace and chokes back a sob, I feel genuine sorrow—as if I’d just lost a game of whist and drawn only a deuce. When the heroine dies, I weep. If I read the same story in the morning, I’d laugh and call it melodramatic.

Digestion—or perhaps indigestion—is a powerful force. If I want to write something truly pathetic, I eat a large plate of hot buttered muffins an hour beforehand. By the time I sit down to write, I’m drowning in melancholy. I see heartbroken lovers parting at lonely stiles, twilight deepening, sheep bells tinkling in the distance. Old men stare at withered flowers until tears blur their vision. Young maidens wait at open windows, watching for a lover who never comes. Years pass. Their hair turns white. Babies grow into adults with their own burdens. Playmates lie silent under waving grass. Still, they wait.

I imagine pale corpses washed up on waves, deathbeds soaked in tears, graves in deserts. I hear women wailing, children moaning, strong men sobbing dryly. All because of the muffins.

I could never summon such sadness over a mutton chop and a glass of champagne. A full stomach is essential for poetry. In fact, no sentiment whatsoever can stand on an empty one. We don’t waste time on fanciful sorrows until we’ve dealt with real ones. We don’t mourn dead dicky-birds when the bailiff is knocking at the door. And when we don’t know where our next shilling is coming from, we don’t care whether our mistress’s smile is warm or icy.

Foolish people—by which I mean anyone who disagrees with me—will tell you that mental suffering is worse than physical. Romantic nonsense! How comforting it is for a lovesick young man to look down on a starving beggar and think, “Ah, how happy you are compared to me!” Or for fat old gentlemen to preach about the superiority of poverty. But it’s all hogwash.

An aching head makes you forget an aching heart. A broken finger wipes away memories of an empty chair. And when a man is truly hungry, nothing else matters.

We well-fed folk can’t understand what hunger really means. We know what it’s like to lose our appetite, to push food around the plate without tasting it. But we don’t know what it’s like to *die* for bread. To gaze at coarse fare steaming behind grimy windows, longing for a pennyworth of pea pudding you can’t afford. To feel that a crust would be a feast, a bone a banquet.

Hunger, for us, is a luxury—a piquant seasoning. It’s worth getting hungry just to learn how delicious food can be.

Want to enjoy your dinner? Take a thirty-mile country walk after breakfast and don’t eat again until you return. Watch your eyes light up at the sight of the white tablecloth and steaming dishes. Sigh with satisfaction as you set down your empty tankard and pick up your knife and fork. Feel the warmth spread through your body as you push back your chair, light a cigar, and beam at everyone around you.

Just make sure the dinner is actually waiting for you. Otherwise, disappointment bites.

I remember once, with my dear friend Joe Taboys—ah, how we lose each other in life’s fog! It’s been eight years since I last saw him. How lovely it would be to clasp his hand again and hear his cheerful laugh. He still owes me 14 shillings.

We were on holiday together. One morning, we had breakfast early and set off on a long walk. We’d ordered a duck for dinner. “Get a big one,” we said, “because we’ll be starving.” As we left, our landlady appeared, bursting with excitement. “I’ve got you a duck,” she said. “If you can finish that, you’ll do well.” She held up a bird about the size of a door mat.

We laughed. “We’ll give it a try,” we said, puffing out our chests like champions.

Then we started walking.

Of course, we got lost. Always do in the countryside. Asking for directions is useless. You might as well ask a kitchen maid how to make beds as expect a country bumpkin to know the road to the next village. You shout three times before your voice reaches his brain. At the fourth shout, he slowly lifts his head and stares blankly. You yell again. He repeats your question. He thinks for a while—counting to two hundred in his head—then says, “Couldn’t do better than…” Then he spots another idiot approaching and yells the whole thing to him. The two argue for fifteen minutes and finally decide: go straight down the lane, round right, cross the third stile, keep left by Jimmy Milcher’s cow-shed, across the seven-acre field, through the gate by Squire Grubbin’s haystack, along the bridle-path until you see the hill where the windmill used to be—but it’s gone now—round right, past Stiggin’s plantation…

You say “Thank you” and leave with a splitting headache, utterly confused, knowing only that somewhere there’s a stile you must cross. And sure enough, at the next turn, there are four stiles, all pointing in different directions.

We repeated this ordeal several times. We tramped fields, waded brooks, scrambled over hedges and walls. We argued about whose fault it was we’d gotten lost. We became irritable, tired, footsore. But throughout it all, the thought of that duck kept us going. It floated before us like a fairy tale, a beacon of hope.

We talked about it constantly. “Come on,” we’d say. “The duck will be spoiled!”

At one point, we passed a village inn and were tempted to stop for cheese and bread. But we resisted. We wanted to enjoy the duck *more* for being famished.

As we entered town, we smelled it. Or thought we did. We ran the last quarter mile in three minutes.

We rushed upstairs, washed, changed clothes, sat down, rubbed our hands together—and the landlady removed the covers.

I grabbed the knife and fork and began carving. It took five minutes. No progress. Joe, meanwhile, had already eaten a potato and muttered, “Maybe someone should carve this who knows how.”

I ignored him. I attacked again—with such force that the duck leapt from the dish and landed in the fender.

We retrieved it. I prepared to try again. But Joe was getting annoyed. “If I’d known we were playing blind hockey with dinner,” he said, “I’d have bought bread and cheese.”

I was too exhausted to argue. I laid down the cutlery with dignity and sat sideways. Joe took over.

He worked in silence. Then muttered, “Damn the duck,” and took off his coat.

Eventually, with a chisel, we broke the thing apart. But it was impossible to eat. It tasted like rubber. We made do with vegetables and apple tart. We tried a bite of the duck. It was terrible. A sin to kill that poor drake.

But hey—no respect for tradition anymore.

I started this chapter meaning to talk about both eating and drinking, but somehow I ended up focusing entirely on eating. Well, drinking is tricky. It’s not wise to appear overly familiar with it. The days when men proudly went to bed drunk every night are gone. Now, a clear head and steady hand aren’t seen as signs of weakness—they’re virtues.

In these degenerate times, a bad breath, blotchy skin, a wobbly gait, and a hoarse voice are badges of shame, not honor.

Still, mankind’s thirst is supernatural. We drink before meals, during meals, after meals. We drink when we meet friends, when we part. We drink while reading, thinking, talking. We toast each other’s health—and ruin our own. We drink to the queen, the army, the ladies, and anyone else worthy of a glass. I suspect, if supplies ran low, we’d drink our mothers-in-law.

By the way—we never *eat* someone’s health. Why not? Why can’t we stand up every now and then and eat a tart to celebrate someone’s success?

Honestly, I find the constant need to drink utterly baffling. I understand drinking to drown sorrows, to escape madness. I understand the poor masses seeking relief in liquor—yes, it’s shocking, of course, to us who live in comfortable homes, surrounded by grace and pleasure, that those in damp cellars and windy attics crawl into public houses to float briefly on a stream of gin.

But before you judge, consider what life *really* means for them. Imagine the squalor—the narrow, filthy rooms where families huddle like rats, children scream, women curse, and the street outside roars with filth. Picture their lives: devoid of joy, laughter, love, hope. From birth to death, they never feel a single human emotion. They never dream, never hope, never feel warmth.

In the name of mercy—let them drink! Let them feel, for one brief moment, that they are alive!

Ah, we may talk sentiment all we like, but the truth remains: the stomach is the true seat of happiness. The kitchen is our chief temple, its fire our sacred flame, and the cook our high priest. He is a magician, a healer. He banishes sorrow, melts enmity, kindles love.

Our God is great—and the cook is His prophet.

So let us eat, drink, and be merry.

“Ah, you’ve got rooms to let?”
“Mother!”
“Well, what is it?”
“There’s a gentleman about the rooms.”
“Ask ’im in. I’ll be up in a minute.”

So you step inside—into a kitchen that smells faintly of boiled cabbage and regret—and wait. After a few moments, “mother” emerges from the depths of the house, slowly ascending the stairs like a woman who has just been summoned from the very edge of domestic eternity. She’s untying her apron as she climbs, muttering instructions down below: “Sarah! The potatoes are burning! And don’t forget to put the kettle on—no, not *that* one, the other one!”

“Good morning, sir,” she says, offering a smile that looks like it was painted on with watered-down hope. “Will you step this way, please?”

“Oh, it’s hardly worth my while coming up,” you protest, though you’re already halfway toward the staircase. “What sort of rooms are they? And how much?”

“Well,” she replies, “if you’ll just step upstairs, I’ll show them to you.”

And so, with a sigh that suggests you’re being led into a trap (but not one you can refuse), you follow her upward. At the first landing, you nearly trip over a pail and a broom left carelessly leaning against the wall.

“Ah!” says “mother,” with the dramatic flair of someone who has just discovered a national crisis. “These girls—can’t keep anything straight! Sarah! Come here at once and take that away before someone breaks their neck!”

She shouts down the banister like a general giving orders during a siege. You wonder if Sarah is actually real or just a myth invented to explain why the floor is perpetually wet.

When you finally reach the door of the apartment, she pauses, hand on the knob, to deliver a solemn disclaimer:

“They’re a bit untidy at the moment,” she announces. “The last lodger only left yesterday. And today’s our cleaning day—well, it always is.”

With that, you enter.

And then you see it.

The furnished apartment.

It does not inspire confidence. It does not invite joy. It doesn’t even seem to know what joy is. It stands there, silent, lifeless, like a room that has given up on life and is merely waiting for the next tenant to come along and pretend it’s home.

Even “mother” doesn’t look particularly proud. Her face betrays no spark of admiration. She might as well be showing you a tombstone.

But here’s the thing: furnished apartments are never truly *furnished*. They’re *displayed*. Like museum pieces in a haunted house. The furniture sits stiffly, as if afraid to move. The curtains hang like mourners at a funeral. The carpet looks as though it’s been walked on by ghosts. The mirror is smudged, the wallpaper peeling, the ceiling stained—probably by a coffee spill from 1892.

And the ornaments! Oh, the ornaments! There must be a secret factory somewhere, tucked behind a hedge in Yorkshire, where they churn out these identical relics of bad taste. Every lodging house in the land seems to have the same two glass-triangle things flanking the mantelpiece, clanking together like nervous church bells. Then there’s the china—either a cow sitting upright, or a dog wearing a hat, or possibly the Temple of Diana at Ephesus, depending on your imagination.

Somewhere in the corner, you spot a lump of something brownish and vaguely organic. At first, you think it’s leftover dough from the children’s baking session. But then you realize—it’s a statue. A statue of a cupid who clearly didn’t finish his training. He’s underdone, lopsided, and appears to be weeping silently into his own arm.

The landlady calls it “art.”

Then there’s the sampler—a cross-stitch masterpiece created by some long-dead relative who may have been related to the family or may have just wandered in off the street. There’s a picture of the Huguenots (always the Huguenots), a couple of Scripture verses (“Thou Shalt Not Covet Thy Neighbor’s Ox”), and a framed certificate declaring that the father is an Odd Fellow—or perhaps he’s been vaccinated. It’s hard to tell.

You examine all of it with the quiet horror of someone who has just realized they’re about to live in a museum curated by a committee of bored aunties.

Then you ask the rent.

“That’s rather a good deal,” you say, when she names the price.

“Oh,” she replies, suddenly warm with candor, “I’ve always had *this* amount—though before that, I used to charge *that*.” (She gestures wildly at a number higher than Everest.)

And then comes the final blow: “And twenty years ago? Oh, I’d have charged double. Or triple. You should’ve seen the money people paid back then.”

You feel ashamed. Not because you’re poor, but because you’re not rich enough to make her feel superior.

And so begins the great paradox of lodging: the higher you rise in life, the lower you descend in accommodation. The ladder of rented rooms runs backward. The poor man starts at the top—in the attic. The rich man ends up on the first floor.

Attics, of course, are where genius goes to hide. Or die. Or both.

According to dictionaries, attics are “places where lumber is stored.” But the world has stored more than just old boxes and broken chairs in its attics. It’s stored poets, painters, prophets, and madmen. Haydn grew up in one. Chatterton starved in one. Addison wrote essays in garrets. Goldsmith penned poetry beneath leaky roofs. Faraday experimented. De Quincey dreamed. Dr. Johnson slept soundly—too soundly—on trundle beds, like a soldier who’d seen too much and cared too little. Dickens lived among them. Morland died in one—alas, drunk and too soon. Hans Andersen dreamed fairy tales beneath sloping ceilings. Collins leaned his head on cracked tables. Franklin, priggish and self-righteous, found comfort in them. Savage, the wrong-headed poet, slept on doorsteps when he could afford nothing softer. Bloomfield, Burns, Hogarth, Watts—the list goes on.

Ever since houses were built with more than one story, the attic has been the nursery of genius. The damp walls are sacred. The rotten floors hold secrets. The broken windows let in not just rain, but inspiration.

No one who respects the aristocracy of mind should ever feel ashamed of having known an attic.

If all the wisdom and art of the world were gathered into heaps—truths born in glittering salons, knowledge dug from quiet studies, music composed in fields, words shouted in streets—the tallest pile would be the one forged in squalid garrets. There, amid rotting boards and broken panes, the kings of thought sent forth their eagle cries. There, from their lofty thrones of rags and poverty, they hurled thunderbolts that shook the earth.

You can lock them in. You can starve them. You can laugh at their frantic pounding on the door. But beware—they may turn and sting.

Rousseau was hounded into the meanest garret on the Rue St. Jacques. The world jeered at his screams. But a century later, those thin, piping tones became the roar of revolution. Civilization still trembles at the echo.

As for me? I love attics. Not to live in—no, not really. They’re inconvenient. Too many stairs. You constantly risk bumping your head on the ceiling, which, incidentally, offers zero assistance when shaving. And the tomcat outside sings to his lady love at night with such passion that it sounds less like romance and more like a cat being tortured.

No, for living, give me a suite on the first floor of a Piccadilly mansion (preferably one with a butler). But for thinking? Give me an attic ten flights up in the heart of the city.

There’s something sublime about being above it all. Sitting comfortably, looking down upon the wasps’ nest of humanity below. Listening to the dull hum of traffic, the scurry of lives, the endless jostling and snarling. How small men seem! How petty their struggles! How loud they scream—but their voices don’t reach up here. They fret, rage, pant, and die.

“But I, mein Werther, sit above it all; I am alone with the stars.”

The most peculiar attic I ever knew belonged to a friend and myself many years ago. It was, without exaggeration, the most eccentric room ever designed by human hands.

The architect must have been either a genius or completely insane. Probably both.

No Euclidean figure could describe it. It had seven corners. Two walls converged to a point. The window was directly above the fireplace. The only place to put the bed was between the door and the cupboard.

To get anything from the cupboard, we had to climb over the bed. And often, half the item ended up in the sheets. By nightfall, the bed had become a kind of cooperative storage unit. Coal was its main stock-in-trade.

We kept coal in the bottom of the cupboard. When we needed it, we’d climb over the bed, shovel a load, and crawl back.

It was a nerve-wracking moment—reaching the middle of the bed. We’d hold our breath, eyes locked on the shovel, body poised. Then—*thump!*—we’d collapse, coals everywhere, bedclothes ruined, and the entire room in disarray.

I’ve heard people go wild over beds of coal. We slept in one every night and weren’t impressed.

But the fun didn’t end there. The whole house was a marvel of architectural absurdity. All doors opened outward. So if you were coming downstairs and someone wanted to leave, you’d meet in a clash of elbows and dignity.

There was no ground floor—its ground floor belonged to the house next door. The front door opened directly onto a flight of stairs leading down to the cellar. Visitors would walk in, immediately shoot past the person who answered the door, and vanish down the steps.

Nervous types would scream “Murder!” and lie there at the bottom until someone came to rescue them.

It’s been years since I’ve seen an attic. I’ve tried other floors since. But life tastes much the same—whether served in a golden goblet or a stone mug.

The hours bring the same mix of joy and sorrow, no matter where you stand. A broadcloth waistcoat means nothing to a broken heart. And you laugh no louder on velvet cushions than you did on wooden chairs.

I’ve sighed in low-ceilinged rooms. But disappointment hasn’t grown lighter since I left them.

Life works on a balancing act. The happiness we gain in one area, we lose in another. As our income grows, so do our desires. We always stand midway between the two.

In an attic, we enjoy fried fish and stout. On the first floor, we need a full Continental dinner to feel the same warmth.

And yet—there’s something oddly comforting in knowing that, no matter how high you climb, the world still whispers the same old truths from the same old places.

Especially the ones with broken windows and leaking roofs.

They say—those people who should know better—that feeling well-dressed brings a joy to the soul so profound that even religion can’t match it. And honestly? Sometimes they’re right. I’ve known it myself. Back in my youth—many moons ago, as the old storybooks like to put it—I’d have days when I felt utterly crushed. Perhaps my washerwoman had kicked me out (a fate worse than exile, really), or my blank-verse poem had come back for the tenth time with the editor’s polite note: “Regrets due to lack of space,” as if he were personally offended by my existence. Or maybe the woman I loved—yes, loved, passionately, tragically, and with all the intensity of a man who’d never been taught how to love properly—had given me the cold shoulder. And let me tell you, there are more ways to love than one could possibly count. We each invent our own brand, unique and unrepeatable. By the time our great-grandchildren get around to it, they’ll probably be doing it upside-down, just to avoid stepping on any historical toes.

Anyway, when life knocked me down, I’d go straight to my wardrobe and dress up like a king. A fresh hat, trousers with a crisp crease (carefully preserved under the bed—not on the floor, mind you, but tucked between the mattress and the frame), and I’d feel like someone important again. Suddenly, there were other washerwomen in the world. Other girls. Girls who might actually appreciate a clever, good-looking young fellow—someone who wasn’t afraid to make eyes at them, or even try to court them. I didn’t care. I was reckless. In those clothes, I could do anything. And let’s face it, clothes *do* matter when it comes to romance. Half the battle is wearing the right outfit. The young man knows this. That’s why he spends two hours getting ready—half of which is deciding whether to wear his light suit with a cane and a drab billycock, or his black tails with a top hat and umbrella. He’s doomed either way.

If he picks the light suit and cane, it rains. He arrives drenched, boots caked in mud, trying to hide them under the table while pretending he’s not sweating through his shirt. If he chooses the top hat and umbrella—nobody ever goes out in a top hat without an umbrella; it’s like letting a baby toddle off without its nurse—he ends up sweltering in the heat. His mustache loses all its soap, his forehead curl turns into a limp seaweed frond, and he looks like he’s been dragged through a hedge backward. The Fates hate him. And if by some miracle he makes it to her door looking halfway decent, she’s gone out with her cousin and won’t be back until midnight.

How I envy the gallants of seventy years past! Look at them on Christmas cards—curly hair, natty hats, legs like arrows in tight smalls, dainty Hessian boots, ruffles, canes, dangling seals. No wonder the little maiden in the big poke-bonnet and light-blue sash swoons. Men could win hearts in clothes like that. But what can we expect from baggy trousers and monkey jackets?

Clothes change us. They shape our behavior. Dress a man in tattered rags, and he slinks along like a dog fetching its own supper beer. Put him in fine linen and a tailored coat, and he struts down the street, cane swinging, eyeing the girls like a peacock showing off its feathers. Clothes alter our very nature. Imagine being fierce and bold with a plume in your bonnet, a dagger at your hip, and puffy sleeves like clouds. Now imagine trying to be brave in an ulster—you’d rather hide behind a lamp-post and call the police.

I admit, true virtue exists beneath tweed and broadcloth just as it did beneath velvet and silk. But the spirit of chivalry—the knight who rode a tilt for a lady’s love, fought for her smile—needs steel clattering and plumes rustling to wake it from its dusty grave. The world is growing old. It dresses so soberly now. We’ve passed through infancy, when we wore only robes and liked bare feet. Then came barbarism—tattoos, no haircuts, wildness. Then adolescence—flowing curls, scarlet doublets, courting, bragging, bouncing about like proud peacocks. Those days are gone. Now we’re middle-aged, solemn, and—some say—stupid. The world is a respectable gentleman in the nineteenth century, shocked at the idea of wearing anything colorful. So we wear black coats, black trousers, black hats, black boots—and oh, how respectable we look! Can you believe this same world once dressed like troubadours and knights-errant, in bright colors and absurd finery?

Ah well. We think ourselves wiser now. Or at least we pretend to. There’s a theory going around: sense and dullness go hand in hand. And goodness? Goodness loves black. Very good people dress entirely in black—even gloves and ties. Soon they’ll be wearing black shirts. Medium-good folks allow themselves light trousers on weekdays. Some dare to wear fancy waistcoats. But the truly wicked? They wear light suits. And there are monsters who wear white hats. Such people are never mentioned in polite society. I shouldn’t have brought them up.

By the way, have you noticed how people stare the first time you wear a new light suit? They don’t notice it after the third time. London gets used to it. I say “you” because I don’t wear such things myself. Only sinful people do. I wish it weren’t so. I wish one could be good, respectable, and sensible without looking like a clown. I sometimes look in the mirror at my two long cylindrical bags (so rugged at the knees), my stand-up collar, and my billycock hat, and wonder what right I have to make God’s world look so ugly. Then wild thoughts come. I don’t want to be good and respectable. (I’m told I can never be sensible anyway, so that doesn’t matter.) I want lavender tights, red velvet breeches, a green doublet slashed with yellow, a light-blue silk cloak, a black eagle’s plume, a sword, a falcon, a lance, and a prancing horse. I want to ride through the streets and gladden the eyes of the people. Why should we all look like ants crawling over a dust-heap? Why not dress a little gayly? I’m sure we’d be happier. True, it’s a small thing—but we’re a small race. What’s the point of pretending otherwise and ruining fun?

Let philosophers dress like old crows if they like. But let me be a butterfly. Women, at least, ought to dress prettily. It’s their duty. They’re the flowers of the earth. We men complain about them often enough, but honestly, the world would be dull without their dresses and fair faces. How they brighten every room they enter! What a sunny commotion they—relations, of course—make in our dingy bachelor chambers! Their ribbons, laces, gloves, hats, parasols, and 'kerchiefs create a delightful mess. It’s like a wandering rainbow dropped in to visit. One of summer’s chief joys, to me, is seeing young maids in pretty colors. I love watching pink, blue, and white flash between the trees, dotting green fields, catching sunlight. You can see them miles away. Right now, four white dresses are climbing a hill three miles from my window. I can see them clearly. At first I thought they were mile-stones having a picnic.

It’s nice to see the darlings from afar. Especially if they happen to be your wife and mother-in-law.

Speaking of hills and mile-stones, I must seriously address women’s boots. All Englishwomen wear boots too big for them. They can’t find a proper fit. Bootmakers don’t keep small sizes. I’ve seen women sit on stile rails, declaring they couldn’t go another step because their boots hurt so much. Always the same complaint: too big. It’s time this ended. In the name of husbands and fathers everywhere, I demand reform. Our wives, daughters, and cousins must not be tortured with ill-fitting footwear. Why can’t “narrow twos” be kept in stock? That’s the size most women take.

And then there’s the waistband. Always too big. Dressmakers make them so loose the hooks and eyes burst off with a thunderclap. Why do women tolerate this? Why don’t they insist on proper sizing? It can’t be indifference—because dress is the one thing they truly care about. It’s the only topic that excites them. Two women together? Bet your bottom dollar they’re talking clothes. You see two sweet, childlike figures chatting by a window. You assume they’re sharing angelic wisdom. You move closer and hear: “So I took in the waistband and let out a seam, and now it fits perfectly.” “Well, I’ll wear my plum-colored body to the Jones’, with a yellow plastron. And Puttick’s has lovely gloves—only one and elevenpence.”

Once, I drove through Derbyshire with two ladies. Beautiful scenery. They enjoyed it immensely. They talked about dressmaking the whole time. “Pretty view,” I’d say, waving my umbrella. “Look at those distant blue hills! That white speck in the woods is Chatsworth, and over there—”
“Yes, very pretty indeed,” one would reply. “Why not get a yard of sarsenet?”
“What, and leave the skirt exactly as it is?”
“Certainly. What place d’ye call this?”
I’d point out fresh vistas. They’d glance, say “charming,” “sweetly pretty,” then launch into raptures over pocket-handkerchiefs and mourn the decline of cambric frilling.

I suspect if two women were stranded on a desert island, they’d spend each day arguing over whether sea-shells or birds’ eggs make better trimmings, and introduce a new fashion in fig-leaves monthly.

Young men care about clothes too, but they don’t talk about them. Not among themselves. A fop isn’t popular with his own sex. He gets mocked more than he deserves. But it’s harmless. It fades. Besides, a man who avoids vanity at twenty will likely be a dirty-collar, unbrushed-coat disaster at forty. A little vanity in youth is natural. I like to see a young cock ruffle his feathers, stretch his neck, and crow like the world belongs to him. I don’t like modest, retiring men. Nobody does—not really. Modesty is a mistake. Uriah Heap’s father was terrible at judging character. Otherwise, he wouldn’t have told his son that people liked humility. People hate it. It ruins arguments. Arguments are half the fun of life. You can’t argue with someone who answers humbly. They defuse your rage—exactly what you don’t want. You build yourself up into a glorious fury, and then they ruin it with their calm, irritating humility.

Xantippe must have lived a nightmare married to Socrates. Imagine a woman doomed to live without a single quarrel. A man should humor his wife in these matters. Heaven knows their lives are dull. They don’t attend political meetings, can’t join amateur parliaments, are banned from smoking cars on the Metropolitan Railway, and never read comic papers—or if they do, they don’t know they’re funny. Nobody tells them. With life so bleak, surely we could give them a row now and then, even if we don’t feel like it. A sensible man does. And he’s loved for it. Small kindnesses like this touch a woman’s heart. These acts of loving sacrifice are what make her tell her friends, after he’s dead, “He was such a good husband.”

Poor Xantippe. The bucket episode was especially tragic. She filled it—perhaps walked far to get specially dirty water. She waited. And then… nothing. He didn’t react. She probably sat down and cried. It must have seemed hopeless. And she had no mother to vent to. What did it matter that her husband was a great philosopher? Great philosophy means nothing in marriage.

There was once a boy who wanted to go to sea. The captain asked what he could do. He said he could recite the multiplication table backward, paste seaweed in a book, knew how many times “begat” appears in the Old Testament, and could recite “The Boy Stood on the Burning Deck” and Wordsworth’s “We Are Seven.” “Werry good—werry good,” said the captain. “But ken ye kerry coals?”

It’s the same with marriage. Great talent isn’t required. Little usefulness is. Brains are useless in marriage. No one appreciates them. Your wife judges you by her own standards, where intelligence earns zero marks. She’s not impressed by your cleverness, my dear reader. Not at all. Give her a man who can run an errand neatly, without overthinking it. Who can hold a child the right way up. Who doesn’t complain about lukewarm mutton. That’s the kind of husband a sensible woman wants—not one of those scientific or literary nuisances who upset the house with their nonsense.

Wait. That’s where I lose it. The rest? Vanished like a soap bubble in a stiff breeze. It was the first poem I ever learned—well, *almost* the first. “Hey, diddle, diddle, the cat and the fiddle” doesn’t count. That’s just nonsense dressed up as nursery rhyme. No depth. No soul. Nothing to build a reputation on. But “I remember, I remember”? That one had weight. I once recited it for fourpence. Not that I knew what fourpence meant at the time—just that if I saved it till I got twopence more, I’d have sixpence. A logical argument, yes, but logic never stopped me from spending money before. So off it went—probably on something utterly forgettable, because I can’t recall what it was. And there you have it: memory is a jester with a broken crown. She hands you half a story and laughs when you ask for the rest.

She’s like a child who’s been left alone with a box of toys—each one cracked or missing a piece. I remember tumbling into a massive dust-hole as a tiny tot, but not how I got out. If memory were my only witness, I’d still be buried under that heap of sooty earth, wondering why no one came to dig me out. Another time—years later—I was involved in an intensely dramatic love scene. The kind where your heart beats like a drum and the air shivers with unspoken longing. But all I can remember is someone bursting through the door at the most tender moment and saying, “Emily, you’re wanted,” in a voice so funereal it sounded like the police were hauling her off for embezzlement. The rest? Poof. Gone. Like smoke. Like yesterday’s dinner. All those sweet words, all that passionate poetry—vanished without a trace.

Life, when we look back, is like a ruined castle. Here’s a shattered column where a grand gate once stood. There’s a broken window frame marking the spot of my lady’s bower. And over there, a pile of blackened stones where the fire once danced and roared. Everything’s covered in ivy now, soft and green, hiding the cracks. Time paints everything in rose-tinted fog. Even sorrow becomes sweet when it’s old enough. Our childhoods seem gloriously carefree—full of nutting, hoop rolling, and gingerbread. We forget the toothaches, the Latin verbs (especially the Latin verbs), and the snubs from older kids who thought they owned the playground. We remember only the laughter, the sun, the sense that we were invincible.

And we sigh, “Ah, wouldn’t it be lovely to love again?” But we don’t think about the sleepless nights, the dry throats, the way she said she could only be a sister. As if any man needs another sister! No, we see only the light. The past has no shadows. It’s all sunshine and roses. The road behind us glows like a golden highway. We ignore the sharp stones and the thorns—we only notice the flowers. The briars that once scraped our skin now look like delicate vines swaying in the breeze. Thank God for that. Because if we remembered every ache and tear, we’d never want to live again. But memory is kind. It keeps only the pleasant links in the chain. The bitterness sinks beneath the waves while joy floats on top.

That’s why old people talk such nonsense about the “good old days.” Back then, they say, the world was better. Boys were boys. Girls were girls. Winters were proper winters—cold, long, and full of snow. Summers? They weren’t these lukewarm messes we get today. And the things people did? Well, you’d need three strong men to believe half of them. I love hearing an old fellow regale a group of wide-eyed youths with tales of his youth. After ten minutes, he’ll swear the moon shone every night, and that tossing mad bulls in blankets was the favorite school sport. It’s always the same. Our grandparents’ generation sang the same song. Our children will drone it too. “Oh, give me back the good old days!” has been the cry since Adam turned fifty-one. Pick up a book from 1835—the poets, novelists, even the philosophers—all moaning for the same impossible return. The German Minnesingers sighed for it. The Norse saga writers raved about it. The prophets of old, the Greeks—they all agreed: the world has been going downhill ever since it began.

But here’s the thing: if it was so terrible now, imagine how wonderful it must’ve been at the start. When the grass hadn’t been trampled into dust, when silence wasn’t drowned by city noise, when humans walked barefoot under open skies with God for company. They lived in sunlit tents among lowing herds. They took what they needed from nature’s generous hand. They worked, talked, thought—while the great earth spun quietly, untouched by sorrow or injustice. Those days are gone. Humanity’s quiet childhood is over. We’re growing up now—into manhood, amid noise, doubt, and hope. Peace is behind us. Work lies ahead. What that work is, we don’t know. But we’re doing it anyway—like tiny coral insects building reefs beneath dark waters, unaware of the vast structure they’re forming. Let’s stop wishing for the past. Our duty isn’t behind us—it’s in front. “Forward!” should be our motto.

Don’t sit with folded arms, staring at the ruins like they’re a masterpiece. They’re just the foundation. Don’t waste your life thinking about what might have been, forgetting what may yet come. Opportunities vanish while we mourn lost chances. Happiness arrives, but we don’t notice it because we’re too busy mourning the happiness we’ve lost.

Years ago, when I wandered from the fireside into fairyland, I met a knight—a brave, tried warrior who’d faced dragons and bandits alike. One day, weary from travel, he looked around and saw rocks hanging above him like falling mountains, chasms on either side, caves full of robbers and blood-dripping dragons. Darkness clung to the road. He decided to turn back. But when he looked behind, he gasped. There was no path—only a bottomless gulf where his horse’s hooves stood. Going back was impossible. So he prayed to Saint Cuthbert and charged forward—with courage and joy. And nothing harmed him.

There’s no turning back in life. The bridge of time collapses behind us with every step. The past is gone. Gathered in. Buried. No word can be unsaid. No step retraced. So let’s be true knights and press onward. Don’t weep over what you can’t undo. Every second brings a new life. Meet it with joy. Walk forward, eyes ahead—not always cast backward.

A friend recently urged me to learn a miracle system that guarantees perfect memory. I asked why he was so keen—unless it was because I occasionally borrow umbrellas and then, mid-whist game, mutter, “Lor! I’ve been thinking all along that clubs were trumps.” I declined. I don’t want to remember everything. Some things are better forgotten.

Like that time years ago when I didn’t act quite honorably. Or that little lapse in probity I committed—and worse, got caught in. That moment of folly, meanness, wrong. Ah, yes. I paid the price—shame, remorse, hot agony, maybe even some scorn from people I loved. Enough. Let it go. Oh, Father Time, please lift those bitter memories from my heart. Griefs keep coming. My strength is only as long as the day.

But we shouldn’t bury the past entirely. Life would be silent without memory’s music. We don’t want to root out every flower—just the weeds. Remember Dickens’ “Haunted Man”? He begged for forgetfulness. When granted, he begged for memory back. We don’t want all ghosts laid to rest. Only the cruel ones—the haggard, glaring specters. Let the gentle ones haunt us. We welcome them.

As we grow older, the world fills with ghosts. You don’t need to visit churchyards or sleep in haunted granges. Every house, every chair, every empty room has its own ghost. They crowd around us like dead leaves in autumn wind. Some are alive. Some are dead. We don’t know. We once held their hands, loved them, quarreled with them, laughed with them, shared our dreams. It felt like our hearts were joined in a grip stronger than death. Now they’re gone. Lost forever. Their eyes will never meet ours again. Their voices won’t echo in our ears. But their ghosts come. They talk to us. We see them—faint, shadowy—through our tears. We reach out. But they’re air.

Ghosts walk beside us in the street, under the sun. They sit with us at twilight. We see their faces in the windows of the old schoolhouse. We meet them in the woods where we played as boys. Listen—can’t you hear their laughter behind the blackberry bushes? Their whoops down the grassy lanes?

Down here, through quiet fields and near the wood, winds the path where we used to wait for her at sunset. Look—she’s there now. In that dainty white dress, big bonnet dangling, brown hair tangled. Five thousand miles away? Dead, perhaps? Who cares? She’s beside me. I can see her laughing eyes. Hear her voice. Then she vanishes at the stile. The shadows creep across the fields. The night wind moans.

Ghosts. Always with us. Always will be—while the world echoes with farewells, while ships sail across the sea, while cold green earth lies heavy on the hearts of those we loved.

But oh, ghosts—without you, the world would be sadder. Come to us. Speak to us. Ghosts of old loves, playmates, sweethearts, friends—come back. The world is lonely. New faces aren’t like the old. We can’t love them as we loved you. Can’t laugh with them as we laughed with you.

When we walked together, oh ghosts of our youth, the world was bright and gay. Now it’s grown old. We’re tired. Only you can bring the light back.

Memory is a ghost-raiser. Like a haunted house, its walls echo with unseen feet. Through broken windows, we watch the shadows of the dead flit by. And the saddest shadows? The shadows of our own younger selves.

Those young, bright faces—so honest, so full of noble dreams—look at me now with deep, clear eyes. And they seem… disappointed. Poor lads. I fear they have reason to be. Lies, cunning, disbelief have crept into my heart since those pre-shaving days. I meant to be great. To be good. But I’m not.

It’s a relief we can’t see the future. Few fourteen-year-olds would approve of themselves at forty.

I like to sit sometimes and chat with that odd little chap who was me long ago. I think he likes it too—he comes often when I’m alone with my pipe, listening to the flames whisper.

I see his solemn face through the scented smoke. I smile. He smiles back—but his smile is grave, old-fashioned.

We talk about old times. He tells me his favorite fairy tales. He can do multiplication up to six times. He has a guinea pig. His dad says fairy tales aren’t true. Isn’t it a pity? He’d love to be a knight, fight a dragon, marry a princess.

But at seven, he grows practical. Wants to be a bargee. Earn lots of money. Maybe this is love—because around then, he falls for the girl at the milk shop. Six years old. God bless her dancing feet, whatever size they are now!

He gives her his greatest treasure: a huge pocket knife with four rusty blades and a corkscrew that mysteriously pops out and sticks into your leg. She hugs him and kisses him right there outside the shop.

But the boy next door—cigar emporium—laughs. So my young friend prepares to punch him. Fails. The other boy punches him instead.

Then comes school—bitter sorrows, joyous shouts, jolly larks, hot tears on Latin grammars and silly copybooks.

He injures himself for life trying to pronounce German. Also learns that the French place immense importance on pens, ink, and paper.

“Have you pens, ink, and paper?” asks one Frenchman upon meeting another.

“No,” replies the other. “But my brother’s uncle has.”

“What about the neighbor of your mother?”

“The neighbor of my mother has none.”

“Has the child of thy female gardener any?”

“Yes—he does!”

After all that, it turns out the child of his *own* female gardener has none.

Such news would silence anyone—but not this shameless creature. He doesn’t apologize. Just says his aunt has mustard.

So, armed with useless knowledge, boyhood fades. The red-brick schoolhouse disappears. We step onto the world’s high road.

My little friend is no longer little. His jacket has tails. His cap has grown tall and shiny. Instead of a slate pencil, he smokes a cigarette—though it bothers him, getting up his nose. Later, he tries a cigar—a big black Havanna. Doesn’t agree with him. Next thing I know, he’s sitting over a bucket in the back kitchen, swearing never to smoke again.

His mustache is almost visible. He starts drinking brandy-and-sodas. Talks about “two to one against the favorite.” Calls actresses “Little Emmy,” “Kate,” “Baby.” Murmurs about “losses at cards”—implying thousands, though probably it was one-and-twopence.

He sticks an eyeglass in his eye and trips over everything.

His family prays for him (bless their hearts!). They see visions of Old Bailey trials and hangings. The prediction of his first teacher—that he’d come to a bad end—now seems prophetic.

At this age, he has contempt for women, thinks highly of himself, and treats elderly male relatives with patronizing warmth. Altogether, he’s somewhat of a nuisance.

But it doesn’t last. Soon, he falls in love. And that deflates him. His boots are too small. His hair is wildly styled. He reads poetry. Keeps a rhyming dictionary. Every morning, Emily Jane finds scraps of torn paper with lines like “cruel hearts and love’s deep darts,” “beauteous eyes and lovers’ sighs”—the usual, timeless tune.

But love doesn’t run smoothly. He walks more. Sleeps less. Looks anything but happy.

And then—he vanishes.

The boyish self who grew beside me is gone. I am alone. The road is dark. I stumble forward, not knowing where, not caring. The path leads nowhere. No light.

But then—morning comes.

And I realize: I’ve grown into myself.
