The heavy, honeyed warmth of a Florentine September afternoon hung thick over the city on the seventh of the month, two full months to the day since Tito and Romola had first spoken their confession of love to one another. When Tito ducked under the low lintel of Nello’s barber shop, he found the owner sprawled across his workbench, cap drawn low over his eyes, one leg bent to his chest and the other half-slid off the cushion, dragging with it a manuscript of verse whose pages were crushed and splayed open across the earthen floor. In the far corner, Sandro sat alone, playing the ancient hand game of mora against himself, his wide solemn eyes fixed on the slow, deliberate movements of his left fingers as if he were solving a mathematical riddle no other man had ever dared tackle.

Tito moved so softly his sandals made no sound against the packed earth. He snatched the lute leaning against the wall, leaned over the sleeping barber, and plucked the strings into a soft lilting tune, singing low:
*“Beauteous is life in blossom!
And it fleeteth—fleeteth ever;
Whoso would be joyful—let him!
There’s no surety for the morrow.”*
It was Lorenzo de Medici’s famous carnival song, the lines every Florentine knew by heart.

Nello woke as easily as a sparrow startled by a falling leaf. His cap was off his eyes in an instant, and he sprang upright, stretching his arms high. “Ah, my little Apollo! I’ve let my siesta run far too long on this sweltering day, it seems. The fault’s not in natural sleep, but in drinking deep of a potion of potent poesy before I laid down. Hear me? I’m already matching every first letter of my words like a wandering troubadour—sure sign the good wine of my wits is about to pour right out the spigot of authorship, leaving me nothing but an empty cask reeking of dregs, like half the so-called incomparable geniuses I rub elbows with. What is it, my Orpheus?” He reached out, tangled his fingers playfully in Tito’s curls, and tugged lightly. “That soft coaxing in your song tells me you want something. Let me peer straight through the needle’s eye of your desire, as the sublime poet says, so I can thread it for you.”

“That tailor’s metaphor is a strange one for a sublime poet to use,” Tito laughed, his fingers still brushing the lute strings in light, quick strokes. “But you’ve guessed it. I came to see you specifically. I don’t want a trim on my chin—what I need is for you to work your art on this zazzera, this tangled mess of curls on my head that’s knotted worse than Florentine political intrigue. No one else can wield a comb so deftly that it strokes the scalp just right to stir a man’s good spirits, and a dusting of your finest orange blossom scent wouldn’t go amiss. I’m bound for the Scala palace today, you see, to move in radiant company. The young Cardinal Giovanni de Medici will be there, and he’s bringing that sharp-witted young Bernardo Dovizi of Bibbiena. His tongue is so quick I know the only way to outshine him is to carry the scent of orange blossoms in my hair.”

Nello had already seized his comb, flourished it in the air, and pushed Tito gently back into the barber’s chair, tucking a linen cloth around his shoulders. “Never speak of rivalry, my handsome boy. Bernardo Dovizi is a sharp lad, but he’s not one to chase wind with a net. He’s got the same sharp, snappish look as his brother Ser Piero, that weasel Piero de Medici keeps on a leash to slip through every small crack for him. You outdistance all rivals easily, and you’ll soon be able to touch the sky with your forefinger if you keep on as you are. They say you’ve even sweetened the sour old Messer Angelo, that crusty scholar—he’s gone so far as to say you’re less of an ass than he’d expected, for all how close you are to the Chancellor.”

“Between us, Nello mio, Messer Angelo has more genius and erudition in his little finger than all the rest of Florence’s scholars put together,” Tito said, smiling. “They can cry my praises now that Poliziano is laid low with grief or illness or whatever ails him, but I’m only fit to fly against a sparrow-hawk like Pietro Crinito. Poliziano is a great eagle who’d swallow me whole, feathers and all, and never even notice he’d eaten something extra.”

“I won’t contradict your modesty if that’s the way you want it,” Nello chuckled as he combed Tito’s curls into soft, neat arrangement. “But you don’t expect us clever Florentines to repeat the same truths every single day, do we? We’d die of boredom. We cry down Dante and praise the no-named Francesco Cei just for the sport of variety, and if we call you the new Poliziano, heaven knows the lie’s not as big as it could be. And aren’t you a model of virtue in this wicked city, anyway? Ears double-waxed against every siren call that would drag you away from the Via de Bardi, and that great scholarly work of yours that’s meant to astonish all posterity?”

“Posterity will probably be as confused by it as we are by the universe,” Tito said dryly. “They’ll never figure out what its original plan was.”

“Something very like that was said here just the other day,” Nello went on, dabbing a drop of orange blossom oil at Tito’s temples. “Cristoforo Landino said the excellent old Bardo was one of those scholars who gets overthrown by his own learning, like a knight weighed down in full plate armour, then gets angry that people are walking over him. Though that pithy line wasn’t his, mark you—of all men in Florence, Cristoforo Landino is the king of feeding people with an empty spoon, dragging out long useless discourses to leave you gagged on vain expectation. There! You’re perfect now. Not a single grace could be added to you.” He pulled the cloth away. “But love can’t live on scholarship forever, eh? Before long I’ll be dressing this fine head of curls for your betrothal feast, won’t I?”

“Perhaps,” Tito smiled. “Unless Messer Angelo convinces Bardo next that I need to yoke a lion and a wild boar to the Zecca’s cart before I can win my Alcestis. He’s right, though, that I’m unworthy of her. Romola is one of the Pleiades, and she’ll only dim herself if she marries any mortal man.”

“*Gnaffè*, your modesty is well placed there,” Nello agreed. “Yet fate seems to have carved you exactly to fit the empty niche left by Bardo’s long-lost son, you know. By the way, Cronaca told me he’s back at San Marco. Did you hear that?”

A faint electric jolt ran through Tito’s body, but no flicker of it showed on his face. He stooped at once to pick up the crumpled verse manuscript from the floor, busying his fingers flattening its dog-eared pages. “No, I thought he was still at Fiesole. Are you certain he’s come back to San Marco?”

“Cronaca is my source,” Nello shrugged, taking the book from him. “I don’t go poking around that monastery sanctuary myself, but he does. Ah, my poor *Nencia da Barberino*! It jars your scholarly sensibilities to see its pages all crumpled like that, doesn’t it? I fell asleep reading about that charming rustic maiden, prettier than a turnip flower, with cheeks more savoury than fresh cheese. Though to get that kind of romantic idea of peasant girls, you’d have to be napping on velvet cushions in Via Larga, not out watching the Fierucoloni trudge into the Piazza della Nunziata after sundown tonight.”

“And who are the Fierucoloni?” Tito asked, half-distracted as he settled his cap on his head.

“The peasant women coming down from the mountains of Pistoia and the Casentino, all trundling into town to keep their vigil in the Nunziata church for the eve of the Virgin’s Nativity. They sell their spun yarn and dried mushrooms at the little fair we call the Fierucola. They make a queer sight, all carrying paper lanterns, howling their hymns to the Virgin. You’ve got the time to go look, if you wanted. No? Well, I’ve had my fill of it too. The piazza gets wild after dark. You might end up with a random stone bouncing off your ears or your shins if you loiter, and I’ve never been fond of that kind of unwanted attention. Addio, then!”

Tito carried a faint, nagging unease with him as he walked towards the Scala palace, and his visit ended far earlier than he’d planned. The young boy-cardinal Giovanni de Medici, who would one day be known to all posterity as Pope Leo X, had gotten distracted by his favourite pastime of the hunt and never showed up at all. It was still half an hour before sunset when Tito stepped out of the palace doors, intending to head straight for the Via de Bardi, when he stopped short in surprise. There on the Borgo Pinti, walking towards him, was Romola.

She was wrapped in a thick black veil and heavy black mantle, but there was no mistaking her tall, graceful figure and the steady, unhurried way she walked. At her side was the short, stout form of Monna Brigida, her face hidden by an unusually plain, unadorned cloak. Romola had never been raised on devotional rituals, and she almost never left the house save for rare walks on the rooftop loggia—Bardo hated to be left alone, and every outing was planned and discussed for days in advance. Tito knew at once something urgent and unplanned had drawn her out, something she had not mentioned to him the day before. She saw him through her veil and hurried her steps.

“Romola, is anything wrong?” Tito asked, turning to fall into step beside her.

She did not speak at once, and Monna Brigida cut in breathlessly. “Ah, Messer Tito, it’s lucky we met you, for we are in a great hurry. And what a nuisance it is—we’ve had to detour all along the city walls and turn up the Via del Maglio, because the fair crowds have blocked the direct road to the Nunziata that would have gotten us to San Marco in half the time.”

Tito’s heart gave a great, violent thud. “Romola,” he said, his voice dropping low, “you are going to San Marco?”

They had left the Borgo Pinti now, walking along the wide stretch beneath the city walls, with quiet walled gardens on their left and scarcely a soul in sight. Romola lifted her veil to breathe the soft evening air, and he could see the faint, sad agitation on her face.

“Yes, Tito mio,” she said, looking straight at him with her dark, sorrowful eyes. “For the first time, I am doing something behind my father’s back. I am glad I ran into you, for at least I can tell you. But if you go to the house later, do not tell my father you met me. He thinks I have only gone to visit my cousin, who sent for me. My godfather is staying with him, and he knows where I am going, and why. You remember that evening when my brother’s name was spoken, and my father told you the story of him?”

“Yes,” Tito said, his voice low and thick. His mind was a tangled mess. His heart sank at the thought that all his carefully laid plans might crumble to nothing, even as his thoughts raced over a hundred different moves he could make to steer himself through the coming crisis. Even so, he met Romola’s gaze with a hungry, aching feeling that this might be the last time she would ever look at him with that full, unshaken trust.

“The cousin heard he had come back to the city,” Romola went on. “The night of San Giovanni, our servant Maso saw him lingering near our front door. But when Maso went to San Marco to ask after him, they said Dino—my brother, that is, he was christened Bernardino after my godfather, but now he calls himself Fra Luca—had been moved to the monastery at Fiesole, for he was very ill. This morning, though, a messenger came to the house to say he was back at San Marco, and he had sent a desperate adjuration for me to come see him. I cannot refuse him, even though I still hold him guilty of the great wrong he did to my father. I still remember how I loved him when I was a small girl, before I knew he would forsake the father who doted on him. Perhaps he has a word of penitence he wants to send to my father, by me. It cost me so much to go against my father’s wishes, which I have always known are just. I am sure you will think I chose rightly, Tito. Your nature is far less rigid than mine, nothing ever makes you angry, and it would cost you far less to be forgiving. Though even you, Tito, if you had seen your father forsaken by the child he loved most, the child he poured all his labour and all his hopes into, forsaken when his blindness left him needing support most—even you would find it hard to forgive.”

What could he say? He could not bring himself to lie to her, to pretend that such a great wrong could never be forgiven, nor did he have the courage to say a word to dissuade her.

“You are right, my Romola. You are always right—except in thinking far too well of me.”

There was genuine truth in those last words. Tito’s face was unusually pale, and a faint tremor shook his lower lip as he spoke, and he looked so beautiful, so full of quiet feeling, that Romola’s eyes glistened with happy tears. She took it for proof that his gentle soul shared every bit of her own sorrow, that he understood her choice perfectly. “Now, Tito,” she said, not pausing in her walk, “I want you to leave us here. If two veiled women walk into the piazza alone, no one will notice us. If you walk with us, everyone’s eyes will turn.”

“Aye, best you leave us,” Monna Brigida put in. “Truth be told, Messer Tito, every eye in Florence follows you wherever you go, and no matter how much Romola muffle herself, everyone cranes their neck to see what’s under her veil, she walks like she’s leading a religious procession. It’s no fault of hers, but it slows me down. And besides, I’m dressed as plain as one of the Piagnoni, as old as Saint Anne herself, because I don’t want a soul to see us heading to San Marco. It’s only for poor Dino, and if he’s dying he deserves to be forgiven. What’s the point of holding a grudge against a dead man? Make them suffer for their sins while they’re still breathing, that’s what I say—”

No one ever bothered to wait for Monna Brigida to finish her rambling speeches. Tito lifted Romola’s hand to his lips, kissed it gently, and said “I understand. I will obey you,” before he stepped back and lifted his cap to her in a gesture of reverence almost no native Florentine man ever offered a woman. Bernardo del Nero would later sneer at him for that trick, calling the young Greek a fawning foreigner, but to Romola, who loved honour and respect more than anything, it made him seem more noble than any other man in the city.

As he walked away, he felt half relieved to be dismissed, half desperate to cling to her for every last second that she would love him with no shadow of suspicion. It was inevitable, he knew. That dying friar would first demand to know every detail of what had become of his old father and little sister in all the years he had been gone. Romola would pour out everything, tell him all about the young Greek scholar she was soon to marry, the man who was meant to fill the empty place in their household that Dino had left. The moment she spoke Tito’s name, Fra Luca would spill every secret he knew: that the scholar she was betrothed to had abandoned his own adoptive father Baldassarre to rot in the hands of slave traders, that all his carefully crafted stories were lies. Tito could think of no lie, no trick, no dissimulation that could save him now. All his prospects in Florence were gone. Bernardo del Nero would smirk, pointing out he had been right all along to warn against a hasty betrothal to a stranger, and the story of the Greek scholar who had left his benefactor in slavery would be gossiped about under every loggia in the city.

For the first time in his life, he was too fevered, too unmoored to trust in his usual gift for self-control. He gave up all thought of going to Bardo’s house, and paced back and forth along the quiet stretch under the city walls until the last golden glow of the sunset had faded entirely from the western sky. He had no clear plan in his head, when he turned down the first street he came to, the Via San Sebastiano, which led him straight towards the crowded, lantern-lit Piazza dell’Annunziata. He was at that lawless, unguided point every man reaches when all the paths his desires led to are suddenly blocked, and he will follow any small, fleeting call that offers him a purpose, any escape from the ruin he sees waiting for him just over the horizon.
