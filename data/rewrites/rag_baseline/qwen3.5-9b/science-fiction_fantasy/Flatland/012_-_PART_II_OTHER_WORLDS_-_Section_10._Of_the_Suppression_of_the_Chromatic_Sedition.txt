The agitation for the Universal Colour Bill raged for three years, a tempest where Anarchy seemed poised to swallow the state whole. An entire legion of Polygons, conscripted as private soldiers, was obliterated by a superior force of Isosceles Triangles, while the Squares and Pentagons stood aloof in their geometric neutrality. Yet, the true horror lay within the domestic sphere, where the most formidable shapes succumbed to conjugal fury. Enraged by the political strife, wives in noble households woreied their lords with ceaseless pleas to abandon the opposition; when these entreaties proved futile, some descended into madness, slaughtering their innocent children and husbands before meeting their own violent ends. History records that during this triennial fever, no fewer than twenty-three Circles perished in the fires of marital discord. The peril was absolute, forcing the Priests to choose between total submission or extermination.

Then, the tide shifted abruptly, driven by a singular, grotesque incident that statesmen ought to cultivate as a weapon of mass persuasion. An Isosceles of the lowest caste, possessing a mental capacity barely exceeding four degrees, accidentally stained himself with the pigments of a tradesman he had looted. Whether he applied the paint himself or commissioned it remains a matter of debate, but the result was identical: he was transformed, visually, into a Dodecagon. Marching into the Market Square, he adopted a falsetto voice to approach a maiden, the orphaned daughter of a noble Polygon whom he had once courted in vain. Through a labyrinthine web of deceptions—aided by a string of improbable accidents and the staggering negligence of the bride's family—he successfully consummated the union. Upon discovering the fraud, the hapless girl took her own life.

When the news of this tragedy rippled from State to State, the collective psyche of the Women fractured. Empathy for the victim, mingled with a terrifying anticipation of similar fates for their sisters and daughters, radically altered their perception of the Colour Bill. Open converts to antagonism emerged among them, while the remainder required only a spark to join the chorus of dissent. Seizing this volatile moment, the Circles hastily convened an extraordinary Assembly of the States. Beyond the standard guard of Convicts, they ensured the presence of a massive contingent of reactionary Women. Amidst an unprecedented throng, Pantocyclus, the Chief Circle of the era, rose to find himself hissed and hooted by a hundred and twenty thousand Isosceles. Silence was only achieved when he announced a policy of Concession; bowing to the will of the majority, the Hierarchy would accept the Colour Bill.

The uproar instantly curdled into applause. Pantocyclus then summoned Chromatistes, the architect of the sedition, to the center of the hall to receive the submission of the hierarchy on behalf of his followers. What followed was a rhetorical masterpiece delivered over nearly a day, defying any attempt at summary. With a facade of grave impartiality, Pantocyclus argued that since they were now irrevocably committed to reform, they must scrutinize the perimeter of the issue, weighing both its defects and advantages. He gradually introduced the specter of danger threatening the Tradesmen, the Professional Classes, and the Gentlemen, silencing the rising murmurs of the Isosceles by reminding them that despite these flaws, he was prepared to accept the Bill if ratified by the majority.

It became evident that everyone, save the Isosceles, was swayed by his words, leaning toward neutrality or outright aversion. Turning his gaze to the Workmen, he asserted that their interests could not be ignored. If they accepted the Colour Bill, they must do so with eyes wide open to the consequences. Many, he warned, were on the verge of ascending to the class of Regular Triangles; others looked forward to distinctions their children could never hope to earn. This honorable ambition would now be sacrificed. With the universal adoption of colour, all distinctions would vanish; Regularity would be confused with Irregularity; development would yield to retrogression. The Workman would, within a few generations, be degraded to the level of the Military, or worse, the Convict Class. Political power would fall into the hands of the greatest number—that is, the Criminal Classes—who were already more numerous than the Workmen and would soon outnumber all other castes combined once the compensative laws of nature were violated.

A subdued murmur of assent swept through the ranks of the Artisans. Chromatistes, alarmed, attempted to step forward to address them, but he found himself encircled by guards, forced into silence while the Chief Circle launched a final, impassioned appeal to the Women. He cried that if the Colour Bill passed, no marriage would ever be safe, no woman's honor secure; fraud, deception, and hypocrisy would permeate every household, and domestic bliss would share the fate of the constitution, rushing swiftly to perdition. "Sooner than this," he thundered, "come death!"

These words served as the pre-concerted signal for action. The Isosceles Convicts surged forward, transfixing the wretched Chromatistes. The Regular Classes parted their ranks to allow a band of Women, directed by the Circles, to move backward-first, invisibly and unerringly, upon the unconscious soldiers. The Artisans, mimicking their superiors, opened their lines as well. Meanwhile, bands of Convicts seized every entrance, forming an impenetrable phalanx. The battle—or rather, the carnage—was brief. Under the skillful generalship of the Circles, almost every charge by the Women proved fatal, and many extracted their stings uninjured, ready for a second slaughter. Yet, no second blow was necessary; the rabble of the Isosceles finished the job for themselves. Surprised, leaderless, attacked in front by invisible foes, and trapped behind by the Convicts, they instantly lost all presence of mind, raising the cry of "Treachery." This sealed their doom. Every Isosceles now perceived a foe in every other. Within half an hour, not one of that vast multitude remained alive; the fragments of seven hundred thousand members of the Criminal Class, slain by their own angles, attested to the triumph of Order.

The Circles wasted no time in pushing their victory to its logical extreme. The Working Men were spared but decimated. The Militia of the Equilaterals was immediately mobilized, and every Triangle suspected of irregularity on reasonable grounds was destroyed by Court-Martial, bypassing the formalities of exact measurement by the Social Board. For over a year, the homes of the Military and Artisan classes underwent systematic inspection, purging every town, village, and hamlet of the surplus of the lower orders. This excess had been generated by the neglect to pay the tribute of Criminals to the schools and universities, alongside violations of the other natural laws governing Flatland's constitution. Thus, the balance of classes was restored.

Needless to say, the use of colour was henceforth abolished, and its possession strictly prohibited. Even the utterance of any word denoting colour, save by the Circles or qualified scientific teachers, incurred severe penalties. Only within our University, in some of the highest and most esoteric classes—which I myself have never been privileged to attend—is the sparing use of colour still sanctioned to illustrate the deeper problems of mathematics. But of this, I can speak only from hearsay. Elsewhere in Flatland, colour is now non-existent. The art of creating it is known to only one living person: the Chief Circle for the time being. By him, it is handed down on his deathbed to none but his successor. One manufactory alone produces it; and lest the secret be betrayed, the workmen are annually consumed, and fresh ones introduced. So great is the terror with which even now our aristocracy looks back to the distant days of the agitation for the Universal Colour Bill.
