The Mother of Sorrows rose, muffling her face against the chill, and called for the Little Ones. They slept as though the night had never broken them, utterly still. Yet the moment her voice pierced the silence, they sprang to their feet, fresh and vibrant as newly born. With joyful steps, they trailed behind her down the stairs until she brought them to the princess's bedside. The princess lay there, her tears still streaming silently as she slept.

The children's glad faces instantly grew grave. They gazed from the weeping princess out onto the rain-streaked window, then back to her face.

"The sky is falling!" cried one.

"The white juice is running out of the princess!" another exclaimed, his voice thick with awe.

"Is it rivers?" asked Odu, staring at the little streams that traced paths down her hollow cheeks.

"Yes," answered Mara, "—the most wonderful of all rivers."

"I thought rivers were bigger," Odu returned, turning to look at me, the only one among them who had ever heard tales of such vast waters. "Rivers rush like thousands of Little Ones, making loud noises!"

"Look at the rivers of the sky!" Mara commanded gently. "See how they come down to wake the waters beneath the earth! Soon, rivers will flow everywhere, merry and loud, like countless happy children. Oh, how glad they will make you, Little Ones! You have never seen them, and you do not yet know how lovely water truly is!"

"That will be the gladness of the ground because the princess is becoming good," Odu observed. "See the gladness of the sky!"

"Are the rivers the gladness of the princess?" asked Luva.

"They are not her blood, for they are not red!"

"They are the juice inside the juice," Mara corrected softly.

Odu pressed a finger to his eye, examined it, and shook his head in confusion.

"The princess will not bite now!" declared Luva.

"No; she will never do that again," Mara assured them. "—But now we must take her nearer home."

"Is that a nest?" asked Sozo, pointing to the structure outside.

"Yes; a very big nest. But we must take her to another place first."

"What is that?"

"It is the biggest room in all this world. —But I think it is going to be pulled down soon; it will be too full of little nests. Go and get your clumsies."

"Please, are there any cats in it?"

"Not one. The nests are too full of lovely dreams for a single cat to fit inside."

"We shall be ready in a minute," said Odu, and ran out, followed by all but Luva.

Inside, Lilith was now awake, watching with a sad, knowing smile.

"But her rivers are running so fast!" Luva whispered, standing by her side, unable to tear his eyes from her face. "Her robe is all... I don't know what. The clumsies won't like it!"

"They won't mind it," Mara replied. "Those rivers are so clean that they wash the whole world clean."

I had fallen asleep by the fire earlier, but I had been awake and listening for some time. Rising now, I addressed our hostess.

"It is time to mount, Mr. Vane," she said.

"Tell me, please," I asked, "is there not a way to avoid the channels and the den of monsters?"

"There is an easy way across the river-bed, which I will show you," she answered. "But you must pass once more through the monsters."

"I fear for the children," I said.

"Fear will not come near them," she rejoined firmly.

We left the cottage. The beasts stood waiting at the door. Odu was already perched on the neck of one of the two creatures destined to carry the princess. I mounted Lona's horse. Mara lifted the princess's body, handing her into my arms. When she emerged again with the princess, a cry of delight erupted from the children; she was no longer muffled!

Gazing at her, entranced by her sudden loveliness, the boys forgot to receive the princess from her. Instead, the elephants took Lilith tenderly with their trunks—one wrapping around her body, the other around her knees—and, with Mara's assistance, laid her gently between them.

"Why does the princess want to go?" a small boy asked.

"She would stay good if she stayed here!"

"She wants to go, and she does not want to go: we are helping her," Mara explained.

"She will not stay good here."

"What are you helping her to do?" the boy persisted.

"To go where she will get more help—help to open her hand, which has been closed for a thousand years."

"So long? Then she has learned to do without it. Why should she open it now?"

"Because it is shut upon something that is not hers."

"Please, Lady Mara, may we have some of your very dry bread before we go?" Luva requested.

Mara smiled and brought them four loaves and a great jug of water.

"We will eat as we go," they said.

But they drank the water with pure delight.

"I think," remarked one of them, "it must be elephant-juice! It makes me so strong!"

We set out, the Lady of Sorrow walking beside us, more beautiful than the sun, with the white leopardess trailing behind. I thought she intended merely to guide us across the channels, but soon I realized she was accompanying us the entire way. I wished to dismount so she might ride, but she refused.

"I have no burden to carry," she said. "The children and I will walk together."

It was the loveliest of mornings; the sun shone with its brightest intensity, and the wind blew its sweetest melody. Yet these comforts did not soothe the desert, for it possessed no water. We crossed the channels without difficulty, the children gamboling around Mara the whole way, but we did not reach the top of the ridge overlooking the bad burrow until the sun was already beginning to set.

Then I made the Little Ones mount their elephants, fearing the moon might be late and causing anxiety for their safety. The Lady of Sorrow now led the way by my side; the elephants followed—the two bearing the princess in the center; the leopardess brought up the rear. Just as we reached the frightful margin, the moon rose, revealing the shallow basin lying before us, seemingly untroubled.

Mara stepped into it. Not a single movement answered her tread or the hooves of my horse. But the moment the elephants carrying the princess touched the surface, the seemingly solid earth began to heave and boil. The entire dread brood of the hellish nest was awakened.

Monsters rose on all sides, every neck extended, every beak and claw outstretched, every mouth gaping wide. Long-billed heads, horribly jawed faces, and innumerable knotty tentacles surged forward after Lilith. She lay in an agony of fear, daring not to move a finger. Whether the hideous things even saw the children, I doubt; certainly, not one of them touched a child. Not one loathsome limb passed the living rampart of her body-guard to seize her.

"Little Ones," I cried, "keep your elephants close about the princess. Be brave; they will not touch you."

"What will not touch us? We don't know what to be brave at!" they answered. I perceived they were unaware of the deformities surrounding them.

"Never mind then," I returned. "Only keep close."

They were armored in their blindness! Incapacity to see was their safety. Whatever they could not perceive could not harm them. But the hideous forms I saw that night!

Mara was a few paces ahead of me when a solitary, bodiless head bounced along the path between us. The leopardess rushed under the elephants from behind to seize it, but with frightful contortions of visage and a loathsome howl, it gave itself a rapid rotary twist, sprang free, and buried itself in the ground.

The death in my arms assuaged my fear, allowing me to regard them all unmoved, though surely nowhere else had such a cursed crew been beheld!

Mara continued ahead, and the leopardess now walked close behind her, shivering often, for it was very cold. Suddenly, the ground to my left began to heave, and a low wave of earth slunk toward us. It rose higher as it drew near; out of it slouched a dreadful head with fleshy tubes for hair. Opening a great oval mouth, it snapped at me.

The leopardess sprang but fell baffled before it. Almost under our feet, shot up the head of an enormous snake, its eyes glowing with a lamping, wallowing glare. Again the leopardess rushed to attack but found nothing. At a third monster she darted with like fury and like failure—then sullenly ceased to heed the phantom horde.

But I understood the peril and hastened the crossing, especially as the moon behaved strangely. Even as she rose, she seemed ready to drop, giving up the attempt as hopeless; indeed, I saw her sink back nearly her own breadth. The arc she made was very low, and now she began to descend rapidly.
