Mrs. Jennings, for all her bustling fondness and her habit of roosting from one child’s house to another like a contented, well-fed bird, was not without a hearth that belonged solely to herself. Since her husband’s death—he who had made his money in a noisier, less genteel quarter of the town, where carts rattled and vendors cried their wares from dawn—she had kept a winter house near Portman Square. As January drew near, she began to speak of London the way a practiced cook speaks of a familiar kitchen: with certainty, appetite, and an impatience that did not permit delay.

One day, as abruptly as if she had clapped down a pot-lid mid-simmer, she asked the two elder Miss Dashwoods to go with her.

Elinor answered at once—grateful, but absolute—declining for both, and giving the reason that seemed, to her mind, sufficient and final: they were determined not to leave their mother at that season.

Marianne’s colour shifted as if a sudden heat had passed over her; her eyes brightened with a look that betrayed how little indifferent she was to London. Elinor, intent on propriety and the comfort of home, did not at first mark that quick, betraying change.

Mrs. Jennings stared, then began again, the way she always began again—by sheer force of good humour.

“Oh, Lord! your mother can spare you very well. I do beg you’ll favour me with your company, for I’ve quite set my heart upon it. Don’t fancy you’ll be any inconvenience. I shan’t put myself out of my way for you—not I. It’s only sending Betty by the coach, and I hope I can afford that. We three shall do very well in my chaise; and when we’re in town, if you don’t like to go wherever I go, why, you can go with one of my daughters. Your mother won’t object. I’ve had such good luck in getting my own children off my hands, she’ll think me very fit to have the charge of you; and if I don’t get one of you at least well married before I’ve done with you, it shan’t be my fault. I’ll speak a good word for you to all the young men—depend upon it.”

Sir John, who could no more bear quiet than some people can bear smoke, laughed out his agreement. Even in winter, Barton’s air had its gentle sounds—the wind in bare branches, the hush of fields lying fallow—yet he always seemed to hear in that hush a kind of loneliness that frightened him.

“I have a notion,” said he, “that Miss Marianne would not object, if her elder sister would come into it. It’s very hard indeed that she should not have a little pleasure because Miss Dashwood does not wish it. So I would advise you two to set off for town when you’re tired of Barton, without saying a word to Miss Dashwood about it.”

“Nay, nay,” cried Mrs. Jennings, as if scolding flour from a sleeve. “I shall be monstrous glad of Miss Marianne’s company whether Miss Dashwood goes or not—only the more the merrier, say I. I thought it would be more comfortable for them to be together; because if they got tired of me, they might talk to one another and laugh at my odd ways behind my back. But one or the other, if not both, I must have. Lord bless me! how can I live poking by myself, I who’ve always had Charlotte with me till this winter? Come, Miss Marianne—let us strike hands upon the bargain; and if Miss Dashwood changes her mind by and bye, why, so much the better.”

Marianne answered with a warmth that made her voice tremble, as if the very word London carried in it a music she could already hear through the miles.

“I thank you, ma’am—sincerely thank you. Your invitation has ensured my gratitude for ever; and it would give me such happiness—yes, almost the greatest happiness I am capable of—to be able to accept it. But my mother—my dearest, kindest mother—if she were to be made less happy, less comfortable by our absence—Oh! no. Nothing should tempt me to leave her. It should not, must not be a struggle.”

Mrs. Jennings assured her again—loudly, kindly—that Mrs. Dashwood could spare them very well. And Elinor, who now understood her sister’s sudden brightness and the eagerness beneath it—the way one might understand, from a single deep breath, what dish another has been craving—pressed her opposition no farther in direct terms. She referred it to her mother’s decision.

She scarcely expected support. Whatever Marianne desired, Mrs. Dashwood was eager to promote; she had never been taught to distrust this particular attachment, and Elinor dared not explain the private reasons that made London, for herself, a place of danger rather than pleasure.

What struck Elinor most was not only Marianne’s haste to be gone, but her willingness to swallow what she had always found distasteful. Marianne, fastidious, sensitive, often wounded by coarse speech as by a too-sour wine, was thoroughly acquainted with Mrs. Jennings’s manners and habitually disgusted by them. Yet now she was ready to overlook everything—every broad allusion, every ill-timed jest, every clatter of talk that drowned softer voices—as if the whole world’s noise could be endured for the chance of meeting one person again. To Elinor it was proof—strong, full, undeniable—of the importance of that object; proof she had not been prepared to see so plainly.

When Mrs. Dashwood heard of the invitation, she accepted it in the spirit with which she accepted most things that promised delight. She pictured the change as one pictures a new arrangement of rooms or a different seasoning in familiar fare: an improvement, an enlivening.

“I am delighted with the plan,” she cried. “It is exactly what I could wish. Margaret and I shall be as much benefited by it as yourselves. When you and the Middletons are gone, we shall go on so quietly and happily together with our books and our music! You will find Margaret so improved when you come back again! I have a little plan of alteration for your bedrooms too, which may now be performed without any inconvenience. It is very right that you should go to town; I would have every young woman of your condition in life acquainted with the manners and amusements of London. You will be under the care of a motherly, good sort of woman, of whose kindness I can have no doubt. And in all probability you will see your brother; and whatever may be his faults, or the faults of his wife—when I consider whose son he is, I cannot bear to have you so wholly estranged.”

Elinor, though she loved her mother’s cheerfulness, felt, in this moment, the same discomfort one feels when a bright tune is played too loudly in a small room: it smothers the subtler notes. She ventured, as cautiously as she could, to name one remaining objection.

“Though with your usual anxiety for our happiness you have been obviating every impediment,” said she, “there is still one objection which, in my opinion, cannot be so easily removed.”

Marianne’s face fell.

“And what,” said Mrs. Dashwood, smiling at her “dear prudent Elinor,” “is she going to suggest now? What formidable obstacle? Do not let me hear a word about the expense.”

“It is not the expense. My objection is this: though I think very well of Mrs. Jennings’s heart, she is not a woman whose society can afford us pleasure, or whose protection will give us consequence.”

“That is very true,” replied her mother; “but of her society, separately from that of other people, you will scarcely have anything at all; and you will almost always appear in public with Lady Middleton.”

“If Elinor is frightened away by her dislike of Mrs. Jennings,” cried Marianne, quick as flame catching, “at least it need not prevent my accepting her invitation. I have no such scruples, and I am sure I could put up with every unpleasantness of that kind with very little effort.”

Elinor could not help smiling—not in mockery, but with a kind of resigned tenderness—at this sudden indifference toward manners that Marianne had so often found unbearable. She resolved then, silently, that if Marianne went, she would go likewise. It did not seem proper that Marianne should be left to the sole guidance of her own judgement; nor did Elinor wish Mrs. Jennings, well-meaning as she was, to be abandoned entirely to Marianne’s variable patience for the whole comfort of her domestic hours.

The determination was easier when Elinor remembered what Lucy Steele had said—that Edward Ferrars was not expected in town before February. Their visit, without any unreasonable abridgement, might be finished first.

“I will have you both go,” declared Mrs. Dashwood. “These objections are nonsensical. You will have much pleasure in being in London, and especially in being together; and if Elinor would ever condescend to anticipate enjoyment, she would foresee it there from a variety of sources—she would, perhaps, expect some from improving her acquaintance with her sister-in-law’s family.”

Elinor, who had often wished for an opening to loosen her mother’s confidence a little—so the shock might be less when the truth must come—forced herself, even now, to begin, though the attempt felt nearly hopeless.

“I like Edward Ferrars very much,” she said, with all the calmness she could command, “and shall always be glad to see him; but as to the rest of the family, it is a matter of perfect indifference to me whether I am ever known to them or not.”

Mrs. Dashwood only smiled, and said nothing. Marianne lifted her eyes in astonishment, as if Elinor had spoken in some colder dialect, where affection had different names and fewer shades. Elinor, seeing the look, thought she might as well have been silent.

After very little more discourse, it was settled: the invitation was accepted.

Mrs. Jennings received the news with overflowing joy, and a chorus of assurances—care, kindness, attention—promised with the same hearty confidence with which she would promise a table well spread. Sir John was delighted too; to a man whose chief dread was being alone, the thought of adding two more familiar faces to London’s crowd was comfort enough to set him laughing. Even Lady Middleton took the trouble to appear delighted—a considerable exertion, as it obliged her to stir herself out of habitual composure.

As for the Miss Steeles, and Lucy in particular, they had never been so happy in their lives as this intelligence made them.

Elinor submitted to the arrangement with less reluctance than she had expected. For herself it was now a matter of unconcern whether she went to town or not; and when she saw her mother so thoroughly pleased, and Marianne so exhilarated—her whole manner revived, her voice animated, her gaiety raised above its usual pitch—Elinor could not be dissatisfied with the cause, and would scarcely allow herself to distrust the consequence.

Marianne’s joy, indeed, was almost beyond happiness; it was a restless, bright perturbation, as if every sound at Barton—the creak of a gate, the crackle of a fire, the soft step on a stair—only reminded her that time still moved too slowly. Her unwillingness to quit her mother was the only check upon that impatience; and when the moment of parting arrived, her grief on that account was excessive. Mrs. Dashwood’s affliction was hardly less; and Elinor was the only one of the three who seemed able to remember that separations, even tender ones, were not eternal.

They departed in the first week of January. The Middletons were to follow in about a week. The Miss Steeles kept their station at the park, and were to quit it only with the rest of the family.
