Until the enemy had quite marched off from before the place—until every last rumour, post-chaise, and petticoat-shadow that might possibly contain Miss Fotheringay had been routed—Major Pendennis was resolved to keep his little garrison shut up at Fairoaks. He made no proclamation, posted no sentries, forbade nothing in terms. He merely did that masterly thing which experienced generals and experienced uncles do: he contrived that his nephew’s steps should always sound somewhere within hearing. If Arthur went out, somebody happened to be coming in; if Arthur rode, somebody had seen him take the turn; if Arthur sighed, the Major had a lozenge ready and a theory for it. Thus the young gentleman enjoyed liberty with a string to it—a very long, very civil string, held by a gloved hand.

There are few men—there are none who read novels—who have not at some hour been balked in love by fate, by circumstance, by that peculiar feminine talent for saying No as if it were a hymn-tune, or by their own folly which is the most punctual of all impediments. If any such person will be good enough to remember the taste of those nights—how the pillow is hot, the sheet too cold, the clock louder than the church-bell, and the future a sort of blank larder—he may season Pen’s anguish with his own recollection and so do justice to the boy.

Ah, what fevers! what thirsts! what hungry fits of longing, like a stomach that will have its supper, dashing itself against the granite of indifference and coming away bruised! If a bill of fare could be made of all the groans of London at two o’clock in the morning—of all the men lying awake counting the strokes as if each were a spoon tapping the rim of an empty basin—what a prodigious catalogue it would be. I never knew a man die of love; but I have known a twelve-stone gentleman reduced to nine-stone five by a disappointment, so that very nearly a quarter of him might be said to have perished—gone off, as it were, in a slow consumption of sighs. He filled out again afterwards; some fresh affection put a comfortable layer over the ribs, like good suet over a joint, and made him as plump as ever. Young Pen too, being a healthy creature with a hearty palate for life (in spite of his present nausea), would console himself one day; and we mention this to prevent tender ladies from ordering mourning too soon.

His mother, however, ordered it all in her heart: the crape, the coffin, the whole melancholy establishment. What will not maternal fondness invent? “Depend on it, my dear creature,” Major Pendennis would say, with that gallantry which is as much a part of his toilette as his neckcloth, “the boy will recover. As soon as we get her out of the country we will take him somewhere—show him a little life. Meantime make yourself easy. Half a fellow’s pangs at losing a woman result from vanity more than affection. To be left by a woman is the deuce and all, to be sure; but look how easily we leave ’em.”

Mrs. Pendennis did not know. Such knowledge had never been served at her table. The subject offended her modesty, as onions do some delicate nostrils; she had had her own little private misadventure once, borne it, cured it, and packed it away like lavender in a drawer; and perhaps she had not much patience with other people’s passions—except, of course, Arthur’s, which she made entirely her own. She watched him now with a jealous, silent tenderness, and suffered more in fancy than he did in fact. Yet he would not talk to her about it; he kept his pain as boys keep contraband sweetmeats—close and sticky and hidden.

The Major must be allowed his merit. For a man whose natural air was London—who lived by dinner-bells, drawing-room chimes, coach-wheels, and the polite clatter of evening talk—Fairoaks was a kind of rural mute. To dine with Doctor Portman or a neighbouring squire now and then; to play a dreary rubber at backgammon with the widow, who tried to sweeten the hours as she sweetened her tea; these were his chief diversions. He longed for the arrival of the bag with the letters as a cook longs for the market-cart; he read every word of the evening paper as if it were a receipt for keeping life fresh.

He doctored himself assiduously too—quiet living, he said, after the London banquets. His medicine-chest stood like a little private pantry of pills and draughts. He dressed himself laboriously every morning and afternoon, tying his cravat with the conscientiousness of a man basting a fowl lest it brown unevenly. He took regular exercise up and down the terrace walk, cane tapping out a measured rhythm as steady as a kitchen clock. Thus with his cane, his toilette, his bottles, his backgammon-box, and his newspaper, this worldly philosopher fenced himself against ennui. If he did not improve each shining hour like the bees by the widow’s garden wall, he at least got through one hour after another, and made his captivity tolerable. It was remarked presently that he grew fond of bringing the conversation round to the American war, the massacre of Wyoming, and the brilliant actions of Saint Lucie—the truth being he had a couple of volumes of the Annual Register in his bedroom, which he studied as a housewife studies a household book when company threatens.

Pen sometimes took the backgammon-box of a night, or listened to his mother’s simple music in the summer evenings; but he was restless and wretched for all. He has been known to be up before the early daylight and down at a carp-pond in Clavering Park: a dreary pool, with whispering rushes and green alders, and that peculiar damp smell of old water and decaying leaves that goes to the heart like memory. A milkmaid had drowned herself there in the Baronet’s grandfather’s time, and her ghost was said to walk still—though whether she walked in pattens or barefoot, and whether she sighed in the Clavering dialect or in some older tongue now lost, nobody could tell with certainty. Pen did not drown himself, as his mother in her terrors may have fancied; he went to fish there, and to think—think and think—while the float quivered in little eddies and the pond made small secret noises to itself, as if whispering gossip like the town.

If he got a bite he was excited enough; and in this way he occasionally brought home carps, tenches, and eels—cold, sleek, and ancient-looking—out of that melancholy water. The Major cooked them in the Continental fashion, with the importance of a man who can turn a provincial grief into a Parisian sauce. He would wipe the fish dry, score it neatly, and lay it to take a relish of herbs; he would speak of butter as if it were diplomacy; he would reduce a glass of white wine with shallots until it smelled sharp and promising, and then slip in the fish as delicately as he slipped a compliment into a widow’s ear. The kitchen at Fairoaks, which had been used to plain roasts and honest puddings, learned foreign accents. Even sorrow, when it comes into an English house, must submit to the discipline of dinner.

By the pond, under a tree which was his favourite resort, Pen composed a number of poems suitable to his circumstances—verses over which he blushed in after-days, wondering how he could ever have invented such rubbish. As for the tree: there was a hollow in it where he used to put his tin-box of ground-bait and other fishing gear; and in that hollow he afterwards—but we are advancing matters. Suffice it that he wrote poems and relieved himself. When a man’s grief is at the rhyming stage—when he cudgels his brain to find any rhyme for sorrow besides borrow and to-morrow—his woes are nearer their end than he thinks. So were Pen’s. He had hot and cold fits; days of sullenness and peevishness; of blank resignation; of despondency; and occasional mad paroxysms of rage and longing. In those fits Rebecca would be saddled and galloped fiercely about the country, or into Chatteris, her rider gesticulating wildly, startling carters and turnpike-men, crying out the name of the false one as if it were a curse that ought to crack the sky.

Mr. Foker became a very frequent and welcome visitor at Fairoaks during this period. His good spirits and oddities amused both the Major and Pendennis and astonished the widow and little Laura not a little. His tandem made a prodigious sensation in Clavering market-place; he upset a market-stall (and made compensation with a magnificence that was half apology and half advertisement), cut Mrs. Pybus’s poodle over the shaven quarters with a whip that was meant for the air, and drank a glass of raspberry bitters at the Clavering Arms with the air of a man founding a club.

All the society in the little place heard who he was and looked out his name in their Peerages. He was so young, and their books so old, that his name did not appear in many of their volumes; his mamma, now quite antiquated, figured among the progeny of the Earl of Rosherville as Lady Agnes Milton still. But his name, wealth, and honourable lineage were speedily known in Clavering; and you may be sure that poor Pen’s little transaction with the Chatteris actress was discussed with even greater punctuality than the price of barley.

Looking at Clavering St. Mary from the London road as it runs by the lodge at Fairoaks—seeing the rapid shining Brawl winding down from the town and skirting the woods of Clavering Park; the ancient church tower; the peaked roofs rising among trees and old walls; and behind them the sunshiny hills stretching westwards towards the sea—the place looks so cheery and comfortable that many a traveller’s heart must have yearned towards it from the coach-top, and he must have thought: Here, at the end of the struggle, one might rest; here the kettle would sing without malice; here the air itself would taste like bread.

Tom Smith, who drove the Alacrity coach, would often point to a tree near the river, from which a fine view of the church and town was commanded, and inform his companion on the box that “Artises come and take hoff the Church from that there tree—It was a Habby once, sir.” And indeed it is a pretty view, with the Brawl making its clean talk over stones, and the crows and bells sharing the sky between them.

But like Constantinople seen from the Bosphorus; like Mrs. Rougemont viewed in her box from the opposite side of the house; like many an object pursued in life and admired at a distance—Clavering is rather prettier far off than near. Close acquaintance shows blankness. Except on market days there is nobody in the streets. The clack of a pair of pattens echoes through half the place, and you may hear the creaking of the rusty old ensign at the Clavering Arms without being disturbed by any other noise. The soundscape, which on the road promised water and bells and industry, turns out in the streets to be mostly vacancy—an acoustic emptiness where each little noise makes a great show of itself, as a single spoon in an empty dining-room sounds insolent.

There has not been a ball in the Assembly Rooms since the Clavering Volunteers gave one to their Colonel, the old Sir Francis Clavering; and the stables that once held a great part of that brilliant but defunct regiment are now cheerless and empty, except on Thursdays, when farmers put up there and their tilted carts and gigs make a feeble show of liveliness; or on Petty Sessions, when the magistrates attend in what used to be the old card-room, and the old walls hear law where they once heard laughter.

On the south side of the market rises the church with its great grey towers; the sun illuminates delicate carving, deepens the shadows of huge buttresses, and gilds glittering windows and flaming vanes. The image of the Patroness was wrenched out of the porch centuries ago; such saints as were within reach of stones and hammers were maimed and headless. Of those out of reach, only Doctor Portman knew the names and histories: his curate Smirke was not much of an antiquarian, and Mr. Simcoe—husband of the Honourable Mrs. Simcoe, incumbent and architect of the Chapel of Ease in the lower town—thought them the abomination of desolation. It is a curious thing, how a village will forget its own stone alphabet, how names die even while the figures stand; and how the loudest voices in the present will insist that the past must speak their accent or be silent.

The Rectory is a stout, broad-shouldered brick house of Queen Anne’s reign. It communicates with the church and market by different gates and stands at the opening of Yew-tree Lane, where the Grammar School is (Rev. —— Wapshot); Yew-tree Cottage (Miss Flather); the butchers’ slaughtering-house (an old barn or brew-house of Abbey times); and the Misses Finucane’s establishment for young ladies. The two schools had their pews in the loft on each side of the organ until the Abbey Church grew rather empty—through the falling-off of the congregation, inveigled to the “Heresy-shop” in the lower town. Then the Doctor induced the Misses Finucane to bring their pretty little flock downstairs, and the young ladies’ bonnets made a tolerable show in the rather vacant aisles.

Nobody sat in the great pew of the Clavering family except the statues of defunct baronets and ladies: Sir Poyntz Clavering kneeling in a square beard opposite his wife in a ruff; Dame Rebecca Clavering, very fat in alto-relievo, borne up to Heaven by two little blue-veined angels who seemed to have a severe task—and so forth. How well Pen remembered those effigies afterwards, and how often in youth he scanned them while the Doctor grumbled the sermon from the pulpit and Smirke’s mild brow peered over the prayer-book! Statues and sermons alike spoke a language: the statues in silence, the sermon in a drawl; and Pen, in his anguish, understood both chiefly by their tone.

The Fairoaks folks were constant at the old church; their servants had a pew, so had the Doctor’s, so had Wapshot’s, and those of Misses Finucane’s establishment—three maids and a very nice-looking young man in livery. The Wapshot family were numerous and faithful. Glanders and his children came regularly: so did one of the apothecaries. Mrs. Pybus went turn and turn about to the Low Town church and to the Abbey. The Charity School and their families came of course. Wapshot’s boys made a good cheerful noise, scuffling with their feet as they marched in and up the organ-loft stair, and blowing their noses a good deal during the service. To be brief, the congregation looked as decent as might be in these bad times.

The Abbey Church was furnished with a magnificent screen and many hatchments and heraldic tombstones. Doctor Portman spent a great part of his income in beautifying his darling place; he had endowed it with a superb painted window bought in the Netherlands, and an organ grand enough for a cathedral—so that the very building seemed to insist, by colour and sound, on a kind of remembered splendour.

But in spite of organ and window—or because of the window, very likely, which had come out of a papistical place and was blazoned all over with idolatry—Clavering New Church prospered scandalously in the teeth of Orthodoxy. Many of the Doctor’s congregation deserted to Mr. Simcoe and the Honourable woman his wife. Their efforts had thinned the very Ebenezer hard by, which before Simcoe’s advent used to be so full you could see the backs of the congregation squeezing out of the arched windows. Mr. Simcoe’s tracts fluttered into the doors of all the Doctor’s cottages and were taken as greedily as honest Mrs. Portman’s soup—though the graceless people found fault with the soup, too, as people will, when a good thing comes from the wrong side of their quarrel.

With the folks at the Ribbon Factory by the weir on the Brawl—round which the Low Town had grown—Orthodoxy could make no way at all. Quiet Miss Myra was put out of court by impetuous Mrs. Simcoe and her female aides-de-camp. It was a hard burden for the Doctor’s lady: to see her husband’s congregation dwindle; to give precedence, on the few occasions when they met, to a notorious low-churchman’s wife who was the daughter of an Irish Peer; to know there was a party in Clavering—Clavering, their own town, on which her Doctor spent more than his professional income—who held him up to odium because he played a rubber at whist, and pronounced him a heathen because he went to the play. In her grief she besought him to give up play and rubber—indeed they could scarcely get a table now, the outcry was so dreadful—but the Doctor declared he would do what he thought right and what the great and good George the Third did (whose chaplain he had been). And as for giving up whist because silly folks cried out against it, he would play dummy to the end of his days with his wife and Myra rather than yield to their despicable persecutions.

Of the two families owning the factory (which had spoiled the Brawl as a trout-stream and brought all the mischief into the town), the senior partner, Mr. Rolt, went to Ebenezer; the junior, Mr. Barker, to the New Church. In a word, people quarrelled in this little place more than neighbours do in London; and in the Book Club which the prudent and conciliating Major Pendennis had set up—and which ought to have been neutral territory—they bickered so much that scarcely anybody was seen in the reading-room except Smirke, who though he kept up a faint amity with the Simcoe faction still had a taste for magazines and light worldly literature; and old Glanders, whose white head and grizzly moustache might be seen at the window; and of course little Mrs. Pybus, who looked at everybody’s letters as the post brought them, and read every advertisement in the paper. (The Clavering Reading-room, as every one knows, was held at Baker’s Library, London Street, formerly Hog Lane.)

One may imagine, then, the sensation in this amiable little community when the news reached it of Mr. Pen’s love-passages at Chatteris. It travelled from house to house like a bad smell from a scorched pan—everybody pretending they had not noticed it first, everybody sniffing very hard. It was discussed at high-church, low-church, and no-church tables; canvassed by the Misses Finucane and their teachers; and very likely debated by the young ladies in the dormitories, with much whispering and the peculiar half-hush that makes a dormitory more noisy than a kitchen. Wapshot’s big boys had their version, and eyed Pen curiously as he sat in his pew, or raised the finger of scorn at him as he passed through Chatteris. They hated him always and called him Lord Pendennis, because he did not wear corduroys like they did, rode a horse, and carried himself like a buck.

And if truth must be told, it was Mrs. Portman herself who became the chief narrator of the story. Whatever tales this candid woman heard, she was sure to impart them to her neighbours; and after she had been put into possession of Pen’s secret by the little scandal at Chatteris, poor Doctor Portman knew that next day it would be all over the parish of which he was rector. And so it was: at the news-room, at the milliner’s, at the shoe-shop and the general warehouse at the corner of the market; at Mrs. Pybus’s, at the Glanders’s, at the Honourable Mrs. Simcoe’s soirée, at the factory—nay, through the mill itself the tale was current in a few hours, and young Arthur Pendennis’s “madness” was in every mouth, pronounced in every accent, twisted into every dialect, until the thing itself was scarcely recognisable—so completely can a village translate a private pain into a public entertainment, and lose the original sense in the process.

All Doctor Portman’s acquaintances barked out upon him when he walked the street next day. The poor divine knew his Betsy was author of the rumour and groaned in spirit. Well, well—he consoled himself— it must have come in a day or two; and perhaps it was as well the town should have the real story instead of a worse one, which is always the village’s favourite cookery: take a plain fact, add a pinch of malice, stew it in conjecture, and serve it up cold.

What the Clavering folks thought of Mrs. Pendennis for spoiling her son, and of that precocious young rascal Arthur for daring to propose to a play-actress, need not be told at length. If pride exists among any folks in our country—and assuredly we have enough of it—there is none more deep-seated than that of twopenny old gentlewomen in small towns. “Gracious goodness,” was the cry, “how infatuated the mother is about that pert headstrong boy, who gives himself the airs of a lord on his blood-horse, and for whom our society is not good enough; and who would marry an odious painted actress off a booth, where very likely he wants to rant himself. If dear good Mr. Pendennis had been alive this scandal would never have happened.”

No more it would, very likely; nor should we have been occupied with Pen’s history. It was true he gave himself airs to the Clavering people. Naturally haughty and frank, their cackle and small talk and small dignities bored him; and he showed a contempt he could not conceal. The Doctor and the Curate were the only people he cared for in the place. Even Mrs. Portman shared the general distrust of him and of his mother—the widow—who kept herself aloof from village society and was sneered at accordingly because she tried, forsooth, to keep her head up with the great county families. She indeed! Mrs. Barker at the factory has four times the butcher’s meat that goes up to Fairoaks, with all their fine airs.

Etcetera, etcetera: let the reader fill up the rest according to his acquaintance with village scandal. It will suffice to show how a good woman, occupied solely in doing her duty, and an honest brave lad, impetuous, full of good, and wishing well to every mortal alive, found enemies and detractors among people to whom they were superior and to whom they had never done harm. The Clavering curs were yelping all round the house of Fairoaks and delighted to pull Pen down; and because the village itself was so quiet, each yelp sounded the louder, and echoed longer in the empty streets and in Pen’s already over-resonant heart.

Doctor Portman and Smirke were both cautious of informing the widow of the constant calumny pursuing poor Pen, though Glanders, being a friend of the house, kept the young man au courant. One may imagine Pen’s indignation. Was there any single man in the village he could call to account, and so shut the mouths of the rest? Presently some wags began to chalk “Fotheringay for ever!” and other sarcastic allusions upon the gate at Fairoaks. Another night, some inventive person brought a large playbill from Chatteris and wafered it there, as if the very threshold must be made to smell of grease-paint and candle-smoke.

On one occasion Pen, riding through the Lower Town, fancied he heard the factory boys jeer him; and finally, going through Doctor Portman’s gate into the churchyard where some of Wapshot’s boys were lounging, the biggest of them—a young gentleman of about twenty, son of a neighbouring small squire, living as parlour-boarder with Mr. Wapshot in that doubtful half-gentility which is always loudest—flung himself into a theatrical attitude near a newly made grave and began repeating Hamlet’s lines over Ophelia, with a hideous leer at Pen.

The young fellow was so enraged he rushed at Hobnell Major with a shriek very much resembling an oath, cut him furiously across the face with the riding-whip he carried, flung it away, calling on the cowardly villain to defend himself, and in another minute knocked the bewildered young ruffian into the grave which was waiting for a different lodger.

Then, fists clenched, face quivering with passion and indignation, Pen roared out to Hobnell’s gaping companions to know if any of the blackguards would come on. They held back with a growl and retreated as Doctor Portman came up to his wicket; and Mr. Hobnell, with nose and lip bleeding piteously, emerged from the grave, looking as if he had been badly buried before his time.

Pen, still looking death and defiance at the lads as they retreated towards their side of the churchyard, walked back through the Doctor’s wicket and was interrogated by that gentleman. The boy was so agitated he could scarcely speak; his voice broke into a sob as he answered. “The ——— coward insulted me, sir,” he said; and the Doctor passed over the oath and respected the emotion of the honest suffering young heart.

Major Pendennis, who like a true man of the world had a proper and constant dread of the opinion of his neighbour, was prodigiously annoyed by the absurd little tempest blowing from Chatteris and tossing about Master Pen’s reputation like chaff in a mill-yard. Doctor Portman and Captain Glanders had to support the charges of the whole Clavering society against the young reprobate, who was looked upon as a monster of crime, as if proposing to an actress were the same thing as poisoning the communion wine.

Pen said nothing at home about the churchyard scuffle; but went over to Baymouth and took counsel with his friend Harry Foker, Esq. Harry drove over presently in his drag to the Clavering Arms, whence he sent Stoopid with a note to Thomas Hobnell, Esq., at the Rev. J. Wapshot’s, and a civil message to ask when he should wait upon that gentleman.

Stoopid brought back word that the note had been opened by Mr. Hobnell and read to half a dozen of the big boys, on whom it seemed to make a great impression; and that after consulting together and laughing, Mr. Hobnell said he would send an answer “arter arternoon school,” which the bell was then ringing; and “Mr. Wapshot he came out in his Master’s gownd.” Stoopid, being learned in academical costume from attendance upon Mr. Foker at St. Boniface, delivered this intelligence with gravity, as if it were a message from a university rather than from a country schoolroom where lads’ tongues were sharper than their pens and their mirth noisier than the church organ.
