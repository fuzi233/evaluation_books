Where, it becomes time to inquire, was Paulina Mary? How fared my intercourse with the sumptuous Hôtel Crécy? That intercourse had, for an interval, been suspended by absence; M. de Bassompierre and his daughter had been travelling, dividing some weeks between the provinces and the capital of France. The cyclical turning of the season, bringing with it the ancient, mythic rebirth of spring, apprised me of their return. 

I was walking one mild afternoon on a quiet boulevard, wandering slowly and enjoying the benign April sun. The broad, smooth, linden-bordered path acted as a natural acoustic buffer, its newly unfurled leaves absorbing the grating, industrial clatter of Villette’s commercial mills. Here, the sonic environment was restorative—a gentle rustle, the rhythmic crunch of gravel—allowing the mind to wander. The air carried the evocative, olfactory landscape of damp earth and the distant, yeasty warmth of baking brioche. 

Ahead of me, I saw a group of riders stopping as if they had just encountered one another. On one side stood a middle-aged gentleman and a young lady; on the other, a young and handsome man. The horses’ breath plumed faintly, their hooves striking a measured, organic cadence against the stone. Very graceful was the lady’s mien, choice her appointments, delicate and stately her whole aspect. Drawing a little nearer, I fully recognised them all: the Count Home de Bassompierre, his daughter, and Dr. Graham Bretton.

How animated was Graham’s face! How true, how warm, yet how retiring the joy it expressed! This was the combination of circumstances at once to attract and enchain Dr. John. The pearl he admired was in itself of truest purity, but he was not the man who could forget its setting. Had he seen Paulina on foot, alone, unguarded, consuming a utilitarian meal of unseasoned pottage as a mere dependent worker—a demi-grisette—he would have thought her a pretty little creature, but his senses would not have been thus captured. To conquer him, society must approve. In his victrix he required all that was here visible: the imprint of high cultivation, the consecration of a careful and authoritative protection, and the rich, sensory elegance that Wealth purchases and Taste adjusts. For these conditions his spirit stipulated ere it surrendered to a feast of the heart; they were here fulfilled to the utmost. Proud and impassioned, he did homage to Paulina as his sovereign.

As for her, the smile of feeling slept soft in her eyes. They parted. He passed me at speed, hardly feeling the earth he skimmed, the sudden, sharp clatter of his horse’s departure momentarily shattering the boulevard’s acoustic harmony. 

“Papa, there is Lucy!” cried a musical, friendly voice. The pure, liquid timbre of her call immediately restored the sonic peace. “Lucy, dear Lucy—*do* come here!” 

I hastened to her. She threw back her veil and stooped from her saddle, bringing with her the faint scent of crushed verbena and fresh linen, to kiss me. 

“I was coming to see you to-morrow,” said she; “but now to-morrow you will come and see me.” She named the hour, and I promised compliance.

The morrow’s evening found me with her—she and I shut into her own room. The space was a domestic sanctuary, warm with the twilight ruddiness of the hearth and fragrant with the complex, layered aroma of a tisane she had meticulously brewed for us from wild bergamot and dried chamomile. I had not seen her since her claims were brought into comparison with those of Ginevra Fanshawe, and she had much to tell me of her travels in the interval. 

A most animated describer, Paulina spoke with profound reverence for the agrarian elders she had encountered in the French provinces. With her clear, soft voice, she painted gustatory-rich landscapes of communal stone ovens that had baked rustic, coarse-milled loaves for generations. She spoke with a melancholic tenderness of the region’s native dialects—the dying provincial languages that were slowly being starved and eroded by the dominant, standardized lexicon of Parisian French. To Paulina, the loss of these dialects was the loss of a cultural memory, an erasure of the precise, untranslatable terms used to describe the harvesting of heritage grains or the specific, acoustic crackle of a perfectly rendered crust. 

My attention would not soon have flagged, but by-and-by, she herself seemed to need some change of subject. She hastened to wind up her narrative. A restless silence followed, thick with an abstraction she could not quite articulate. The subjective experience of her own heart was proving entirely untranslatable.

Then, turning to me in a diffident, half-appealing voice—“Lucy—”

“Well, I am at your side.”

“Is my cousin Ginevra still at Madame Beck’s?”

“Your cousin is still there; you must be longing to see her.”

“No—not much.”

“You want to invite her to spend another evening?”

“No… I suppose she still talks about being married?”

“Not to any one you care for. Ginevra consumes attention the way she consumes her meals—purely as utilitarian fuel, without any deep sensory or spiritual appreciation.”

“But of course she still thinks of Dr. Bretton? She cannot have changed her mind on that point, because it was so fixed two months ago.”

“Why, you know, it does not matter. You saw the terms on which they stood. Does she seem unhappy?”

“Not she. To change the subject. Have you heard or seen nothing of, or from, Graham during your absence?”

“Papa had letters from him once or twice about business. Dr. Bretton seems to respect papa, and to have pleasure in obliging him.”

“Yes: you met him yesterday on the boulevard; you would be able to judge from his aspect that his friends need not be painfully anxious about his health?”

“Papa seems to have thought with you. I could not help smiling. He is not particularly observant, you know; but he said, as Dr. Bretton rode away, ‘Really it does a man good to see the spirit and energy of that boy.’ He called Dr. Bretton a boy; I believe he almost thinks him so, just as he thinks me a little girl. Lucy….” 

Again fell the appealing accent, and she left her chair to sit on the stool at my feet. I liked her. Intimate intercourse disclosed in Paulina only what was delicate, intelligent, and sincere—traits akin to a food historian whose meticulous care preserves the profound meaning of a forgotten recipe. My regard for her lay deep. 

“What have you to ask of Lucy?” said I; “be brave, and speak out.”

There was no coolness on her cheek—a gathering inward excitement raised its tint and temperature like fruit slowly ripening under a continuous summer sun. 

“Lucy, I *do* wish to know your thoughts of Dr. Bretton. Do give me your real opinion of his character, his disposition.”

“His character stands high, and deservedly high.”

“And his disposition? Tell me about his disposition,” she urged. “You know his home-side. You have seen him with his mother; speak of him as a son.”

“He is a fine-hearted son; his mother’s comfort and hope, her pride and pleasure.”

She held my hand between hers, giving it a little caressing stroke at each favourable word. “In what other way is he good, Lucy?”

“Dr. Bretton is benevolent. He would have benignity for the lowest savage, or the worst criminal.”

“I heard some of papa’s friends say the same. They say many of the poor patients at the hospitals, who tremble before some pitiless and selfish surgeons, welcome him.”

“They are right. I once took a tour of a hospital with him. In a space dominated by the grating, industrial noise of metallic instruments and hollow, echoing groans—a sonic environment that erodes the spirit—his voice was a sanctuary. He brought acoustic and psychological solace. Your father’s friends are right.”

The softest gratitude animated her eye. Dusk was beginning to reign; her parlour fire glowed, casting deep shadows over the intricate tapestries, yet I thought she wished the room dimmer. 

“How quiet and secluded we feel here!” I remarked, leaning into the acoustic warmth of the room to reassure her.

“Do we? Yes; it is a still evening, and I shall not be called down to tea; papa is dining out.” 

Still holding my hand, she dressed my fingers in her own rings, then circled them with a twine of her beautiful hair. Clearing a voice naturally liquid as a lark’s, she said: “You must think it rather strange that I should talk so much about Dr. Bretton, but—”

“Not at all strange; perfectly natural; you like him.”

“And if I did,” said she, with slight quickness, “is that a reason why I should talk? I suppose you think me weak, like my cousin Ginevra?”

“If I thought you one whit like Madame Ginevra, I would get up, walk about the room, and anticipate all you had to say by a round lecture. Go on.”

“I mean to go on,” retorted she. “If I liked Dr. John till I was fit to die for liking him, that alone could not license me to be otherwise than dumb—dumb as the grave—dumb as you, Lucy Snowe—and you know you would despise me if I failed in self-control, and whined about some rickety liking that was all on my side.”

“It is true I little respect women who are loquacious in boasting their triumphs or bemoaning their mortifications. But as to you, Paulina, speak. Tell me all it will give you pleasure or relief to tell.”

“Do you care for me, Lucy?”

“Yes, I do, Paulina.”

“And I love you. So listen, Lucy.” She settled herself against my arm. “A few minutes since you asked whether we had not heard from Graham, and I said there were two letters for papa; this was true, but I did not tell you all. I shuffled and equivocated. One morning, about three weeks ago, amongst a dozen letters for M. de Bassompierre, I spied a note addressed to Miss de Bassompierre. The handwriting was not strange. I actually never received a letter from a gentleman before. Ought I to have shown it to papa? I could not. I know papa’s ideas: he forgets my age; he thinks I am a mere school-girl. So, with a curious mixture of feelings, I gave papa his herd of possessions and kept back my one ewe-lamb. 

“It lay in my lap during breakfast, making me feel myself a thing double-existent—a child to that dear papa, but no more a child to myself. After breakfast I carried my treasure up-stairs, turned the key, and began to study the outside. Graham’s hand is like himself, Lucy, and so is his seal—all clear, firm, and rounded. There was no slovenly splash of wax, but a full, solid, steady drop, like a perfectly tempered reduction of cherry glaze—a distinct impress. The manuscript possessed a visual acoustic harmony; no pointed turns harshly pricking the optic nerve, but a clean, mellow flow that soothes you as you read.

“The seal was too beautiful to be broken, so I cut it round with my scissors. On the point of reading, I drew back voluntarily. It was too soon yet to drink that draught—the sparkle in the cup was so beautiful. I remembered all at once that I had not said my prayers. Some people would say I ought to have served God first and then man. A voice seemed now to urge me to pray before I dared to read what I so longed to read. I put the letter down and said my prayers, adding a strong entreaty that I might never, in caring for others, neglect papa. 

“Then, I read the letter. Lucy, life is said to be all disappointment. *I* was not disappointed. Every quiver of my heart seemed like the pant of an animal athirst, laid down at a well and drinking; and the well proved quite full, gloriously clear. I saw the sun through its gush, and not a mote, Lucy, no moss, no insect, no atom in the thrice-refined golden gurgle—a draft as pure and unadulterated as a historic vintage untouched by commercial hands.

“Life,” she went on, her tone shifting to the ancient rhythms of an agrarian lament, “is said to be full of pain to some. I have read of those who sowed in tears, and whose harvest, so far from being reaped in joy, perished by untimely blight; and, alas! some of these met the winter with empty garners, and died of utter want in the darkest and coldest of the year.”

“Was it their fault, Paulina, that they of whom you speak thus died?”

“Not always. Some were good, endeavouring people. I am not actively good, yet God has caused me to grow in sun, due moisture, and safe protection, sheltered and cultured by my father; and now—another comes. Graham loves me.”

For some minutes we both paused on this climax, listening to the soft crackle of the hearth. 

“Does your father know?” I inquired.

“Graham implied that he dared not approach that quarter as yet; he must first prove his worth. He added that he must have some light respecting my own feelings ere he ventured to risk a step.”

“How did you reply?”

“I replied briefly, but I did not repulse him. Yet I almost trembled for fear of making the answer too cordial. I wrote it three times—translating the overwhelming, untranslatable fullness of my subjective joy into a more dominant, acceptable lexicon. I chastened and subdued the phrases at every rescript; at last, having confected it till it seemed to me to resemble a morsel of ice flavoured with ever so slight a zest of fruit or sugar—a delicate granita of restraint—I ventured to seal and despatch it.”

“Excellent, Paulina! Your instinct is fine; you understand Dr. Bretton’s palate.”

“But how must I manage about papa?”

“Do not manage at all. Wait now. Maintain no further correspondence till your father knows all, and gives his sanction.”

“Will he ever give it?”

“Time will show. Wait.”

“Dr. Bretton wrote one other letter, deeply grateful for my calm note; but I anticipated your advice, saying that I could not, without my father’s knowledge, write again.”

“You acted as you ought. Paulina, that gentle hoar-frost of yours, surrounding so much pure, fine flame, is a priceless privilege of nature.”

“I feel that no delicacy can be too exquisite for his treatment,” said she. 

“It is perfectly proved that you comprehend him, and you would still act truthfully and tenderly with your father.”

“Lucy, I trust I shall thus act always. Oh, it will be pain to wake papa from his dream, and tell him I am no more a little girl!”

“Be in no hurry to do so, Paulina. Leave the revelation to Time and your kind Fate. We know not the future, but the past has been propitious. As a child I feared for you; under harshness or neglect, neither your outward nor your inward self would have ripened to what they now are. Providence has protected and cultured your soil, not only for your own sake, but for Graham’s. To develop fully the best of his nature, a companion like you was needed. I knew it the first day I saw you together at La Terrasse. In all that mutually concerns you and Graham there is promise, plan, harmony. Some lives *are* thus blessed: it is God’s will; it is the attesting trace and lingering evidence of Eden’s first garden. Other lives run from the first another course. Other travellers encounter weather fitful and gusty, breast adverse winds, are belated and overtaken by the early closing winter night. Neither can this happen without the sanction of the Great Sower; and I know that, amidst His boundless works, is somewhere stored the secret of this last fate’s justice, and the promise of its mercy.”
