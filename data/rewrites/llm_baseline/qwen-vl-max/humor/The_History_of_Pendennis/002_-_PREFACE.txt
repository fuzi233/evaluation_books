If this book—two years’ worth of scribbling, now dumped unceremoniously into the public’s lap—fails in artistry (and let’s be honest, it probably does), at least it has the virtue of being somewhat truthful. That’s more than you can say for most works that spend months getting polished into something so perfect they might as well be made of porcelain. Here, there’s no pretense: the writer talks straight to you, like a slightly tipsy acquaintance at a dinner party who just can’t stop rambling about his childhood cat. Inevitably, he stumbles over words, says things he later regrets, and occasionally spills tea on the manuscript. But that’s the charm! It’s a conversation between two people—one with a pen, one with a mind—and conversations, as we know, are rarely flawless.

And because this author is constantly babbling away, he inevitably reveals himself: his quirks, his vanities, his inexplicable obsession with cats (true story). You don’t judge a man by a single sentence or a fleeting mood. You get to know him through the long, messy, often boring course of daily interaction. Likewise, if you’ve read this far, you’re entitled to ask: Is this fellow honest? Does he mean what he says? Or is he just another fraud, pretending to feel deep emotions while secretly calculating how many readers will cry at page 127?

I can’t pretend I didn’t luck out. This book has found more readers than I ever dreamed possible. And while I can’t demand that you stay awake during every chapter (though I do hope you try), I *do* ask you to believe that I’m not trying to deceive you. I’m not here to sell you lies wrapped in poetic language. If truth isn’t present, then what’s the point? There’s nothing worse than a novelist who fakes sentiment like a bad actor in a melodrama.

Now, for those of you who crave “excitement” (you know who you are), here’s a little secret: this book started with a much grittier plan. We were going to have a hardened criminal in St. Giles’s—complete with noble qualities—being regularly visited by a young lady from Belgravia. Imagine the drama! The slang! The fashionably awkward dialogue! The battles! The murders! I even had Colonel Altamont scheduled for execution—until nine o’clock this morning, when I suddenly felt sorry for him and spared his life. He was literally at the window, ready to meet his fate, and I said, “Wait! Let’s talk!” The publishers were remarkably patient about all this, though I suspect they were quietly hoping I’d go back to writing something less… emotionally taxing.

But I abandoned the plan. Why? Because I realized I couldn’t pull it off. I’ve never been close to a convict in my life. I’ve never spent time in a jailhouse or learned the lingo of ruffians. So instead of trying to compete with M. Eugène Sue (who clearly knows his way around a prison yard), I decided to write about something I actually understand: ordinary people, doing ordinary things, sometimes failing, sometimes succeeding, but always breathing real air.

Even our respectable gentlemen—yes, these characters too—are shown as they really are: flawed, selfish, educated, and occasionally ridiculous. Since Tom Jones died, no one’s dared to show a man in full, unvarnished glory. We must wrap them in velvet, give them polite smiles, and pretend they don’t have dirty thoughts. Society won’t tolerate the raw truth. I’ve already lost subscribers—ladies, mostly—who took offense when I described a young man resisting temptation. My goal wasn’t to shock; it was to show that he *felt* the temptation, and that he *chose* to resist it. That’s courage. That’s humanity.

You won’t hear much about what really goes on in clubs, colleges, or mess-rooms—the real talk, the real lives of your sons. This book tries to be a little more honest than usual. Not because I’m trying to be scandalous, but because I believe truth, however uncomfortable, is better than fiction.

So here’s to truth. Whether you’re sitting in a lecture hall or curled up with a cup of tea, the message remains the same: honesty is the only thing that matters. Even if it’s not always pleasant.

KENSINGTON, Nov. 26th, 1850
