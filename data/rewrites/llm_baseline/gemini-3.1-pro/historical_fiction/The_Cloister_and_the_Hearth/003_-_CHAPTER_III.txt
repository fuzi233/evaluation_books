The lingering aromas of spit-roasted capon, basted with wild honey and crushed juniper, still hovered above the heavy oak table when a grave, white-haired seneschal approached. The great banquet was winding down, its mythic, circular rhythm of feasting and satiety settling into a comfortable hum. The seneschal inquired in a hushed, polished tone whether Gerard Eliassoen was of their company, announcing that the Princess Marie wished to confer with him. Instantly, the surrounding chatter ceased, eyes turning with sharp envy toward the young man summoned from his humble plate to the high table. 

Gerard rose. “I wager we shall not see you again,” Margaret said, her voice steady but her cheeks warming with a sudden flush. 

“That you will,” Gerard replied. Bending close, he whispered into the intimate acoustic sphere between them, “This is my good princess; but you are my queen.” Raising his voice to its natural, resonant pitch, he added, “Wait for me, I pray you. I will presently return.”

“Ay, ay!” mumbled Peter, the old agrarian elder, stirring from a post-prandial doze where dreams of dark, loamy soil and seasonal harvests held sway. 

With Gerard gone, the pair in their homespun garments became the cynosure of the hall. Observing this shift in social currency, William Johnson stepped forward. His voice, a jarring mercantile clatter, broke the harmonious sonic environment as he acted surprised and claimed his rural relations. “And to think that there was I at your backs, and you saw me not!”

“Nay, cousin Johnson, I saw you long syne,” Margaret replied coldly, her phrasing rooted in the old, earthly dialect of the provinces—a tongue that carried the memory of generations. 

“You saw me, and spoke not to me?”

“Cousin, it was for you to welcome us to Rotterdam, as it is for us to welcome you at Sevenbergen. Your servant denied us a seat in your house.”

“The idiot!”

“And I had a mind to see whether it was ‘like maid like master,’ for there is sooth in bywords.” She deployed her native proverb with precise intent, a quiet resistance against the dominant, slippery lexicon of city merchants. 

Blushing purple, Johnson retreated to deeds over words. He insisted they return to his home immediately, promising to prove their welcome. Margaret, anchored by the deep, sensory bond she shared with Gerard, demurred. She noted she wished to hear the minstrels again, loath to abandon the rich auditory tapestry of the hall. When Johnson pressed, her true reason surfaced: Gerard would not know where to find them. 

Johnson waved away the concern, his tone dismissive of the profound meaning of their shared space. He summoned his secretary, Hans Cloterman, to wait in their stead. Cloterman emerged—a figure in black serge and violet hose, standing with starched, unyielding rigidity. He was a man utterly devoid of sensory appreciation, the very statue of solemn propriety. Margaret, buoyant with the joy that she and Gerard would sleep beneath the same roof, found the man’s stiff presence almost comical. When she expressed a slight misgiving that the young man might wander off to play, Johnson scoffed, commanding Hans to sit. Cloterman obeyed, taking the seat without a word, his silence not one of peaceful ecology, but of sterile compliance.

Margaret and Peter departed with Johnson, the old man deeply in need of rest after the physical toll of their travels. 

Left alone, Hans Cloterman sat waiting. As the minutes stretched into an hour, the model secretary—free from his employer's gaze—turned his attention to a pitcher of Corsican wine. This was an ancient, robust vintage, pressed from sun-baked grapes grown on rugged hillsides, carrying the mythic weight of the earth and the olfactory landscape of bruised thyme, dark cherries, and crushed slate. But Cloterman consumed it without reverence. To him, this masterwork of agrarian history was mere utilitarian fuel. He quaffed the heavy wine solemnly and rapidly, entirely deaf to its poetry. The wine exacted the revenge of the earth upon the unappreciative palate. By the ninth goblet, Cloterman rose gingerly to toast the Duke of Burgundy, reached his full height, and toppled straight backward onto the floor. The ruby liquid spilled across the flagstones, an unmourned sacrifice, while Cloterman’s face retained its prim infallibility even in the total eclipse of reason.

Elsewhere in the palace, the seneschal led Gerard through winding passages. The natural acoustics of the stone corridors were soon polluted by the sharp, percussive clatter of dice and the harsh, grating voices of feathered young noblemen. It was an assault on Gerard’s acoustic sensitivities. Guided into a room of eleven ladies, he was met with a sudden, oppressive silence as their chatter stopped like jammed machinery, followed by a cruel, synchronized giggling. They spoke the dominant lexicon of the court—a language of power, exclusion, and mockery. Gerard’s eyes fell, his spirit shrinking before the sterile cruelty of their noise. A duenna finally silenced them as though pulling a modesty string, her severe command restoring a fragile peace, though the air remained thick with unexpressed condescension. 

Gerard was deeply unsettled, mourning the loss of the warm, native resonances of his friends. *What must princes be,* he thought, *when their courtiers are so freezing?* 

But as he was ushered into the inner chambers, the environment softened. The subtle scent of cedarwood, beeswax, and sweet orange pomanders replaced the stale air of the outer halls. Here sat the Countess of Charolois and her twelve-year-old daughter, Princess Marie. The Countess’s voice possessed a gentle, melodic cadence. She thanked him for safely delivering a miniature from Margaret Van Eyck. When Marie eagerly offered to reward him, the Countess guided her daughter with quiet wisdom. Gerard, recognizing the untranslatable grace of the space, asked only to hear the song Marie had been singing. 

The Princess took up her lute. As her fingers swept the chords, the notes struck out bright and distinct, weaving seamlessly into the room's hushed acoustics. But it was her voice that commanded the space—round, clear, full, and ringing like a silver bell. She sang a pure Romaunt of the day. There were no false divisions, no whining slurs, none of the trite, turgid fiorituri that mindless Italian songsters sold so dearly, washing out the rich colors of native melody to produce a commercialized skim-milk. Her little mouth seemed the mouth of Nature itself, tapping into a generational memory of song that transcended the rigid courtly lexicon. It was an expression of pure, unadulterated emotion, and as the tender strain flowed, Gerard’s eyes filled with tears. 

“Was he content?” the Countess asked demurely when the final note faded into the cedar-scented air.

“Oh, madam!” Gerard cried, the spoken word breaking the charm. “Surely it is thus that cherubs and seraphs sing, and charm the saints in heaven.”

The Countess looked upon her daughter with a profound, earthly love, then turned to Gerard, fulfilling her old friend’s request. Upon learning he was in minor holy orders, she promised him a church benefice near Tergou the day after he said his first mass. 

Marie protested, insisting Gerard should be the Bishop of Liege. With meticulous, sensory detail, the child recalled the Bishop’s jeweled crown and his magnificent gloves, stiff with gold and heavily perfumed with ancient myrrh and frankincense. “I smelt them all the time he was giving me his blessing,” she recalled, her memory anchored in the olfactory richness of the sacred rite. 

The Countess smiled at her daughter’s innocence, yet her eye rested on Gerard with mild pity. “A priest gives up much,” she murmured. “Often, I fear, he learns too late how much.” She understood the profound sacrifice of the cloister: the severing of the generational line, the denial of the agrarian hearth, the relinquishing of the deep, physical sustenance of a woman’s love. 

Dismissed with royal favor, Gerard followed a page back toward the banquet hall. But a sudden chill seized him. The promise of the benefice clashed violently with the sensory memory of Margaret’s soft hand and the deep, resonant dialect of her voice. To become a priest meant surrendering the vibrant, gustatory, and emotional feast of the life she had opened to him. The revulsion was so strong he resolved to say nothing of the benefice at home. He would seek a secular place at the palace instead, preserving his connection to the earthly rhythms he craved. 

Joyful once more, he burst into the banqueting hall. 

It was empty. Margaret was gone. Peter was gone. The only soul remaining was a soberly dressed citizen—Hans Cloterman—lying dead drunk beneath the table, reeking of oxidized wine and squandered purpose. Oblivious to the man’s identity, Gerard left the beast to lie and ran wildly out of the palace. 

Outside, the night was assaulted by the glaring illumination of two great fan-lights over the gate, turning the natural dusk into an artificial, jarring day. The crowd gaped at the new technology, their minds colonized by the superficial spectacle. When Gerard asked earnestly if they had seen an old man and a lovely girl, they laughed in his face, their coarse voices adding to the sensory pollution. 

Desperate, he followed the sound of a crowd to a canvas tent where a Mystery play was underway. It was a chaotic, commercialized mockery of sacred cycles. Divine personages descended clumsily on winches to spout twaddle, while an imp buffeted Beelzebub with a bladder to the discordant, industrial screech of fife and tabor. This profane exhibition, originally meant to diffuse religious sentiment through the senses, had devolved into a hollow, acoustic nightmare—an erasure of cultural memory replaced by obscenity and cheap spectacle. Gerard found no comfort in this loud, empty art. The dialogue was a mere quibbling of the dominant lexicon, devoid of soul. Sickened by the noise, he abandoned the performance in the second act.

For three heavy, heartsick days, Gerard scoured the streets of Rotterdam. The city became a labyrinth of alienating sounds and sterile encounters, entirely devoid of Margaret's grounding presence. The profound untranslatability of his grief choked him; he had lost the very dialect of his heart. Convinced she was false or had changed her mind, the bitterness of the loss curdled on his tongue. 

Finally, acknowledging defeat, he turned his back on the city. He started the long journey home to Tergou, carrying the promise of royal favor, fifteen golden angels in his purse, a golden medal on his breast, and a heart like a lump of cold, unyielding lead.
