Things have a habit of going terribly contrary with me lately. My ambition was to stumble upon a subject so utterly novel, so obscurely remote, that it would serve as a fresh topic for one of these essays. "I shall write a paper on something entirely new," I declared to myself; "something no human soul has ever penned or discussed before; thus, I shall claim sole ownership of the discourse." For days I wandered through the corridors of my mind, scouring for this golden nugget of originality, but I found nothing.

Then came Mrs. Cutting, our charwoman—by the way, I mention her name freely because I am certain she will never encounter this book. She regards such frivolous publications as beneath her notice; her diet consists exclusively of the Bible and *Lloyd's Weekly News*. Any other literature she deems unnecessary and sinful. Yesterday, she looked at me with concern and said, "Lor', sir, you do look worried."

"I am attempting to conceive of a subject," I explained, "whose mere discussion will startle the world—a topic upon which no previous mortal has spoken a syllable, something that will captivate through its novelty and invigorate via its startling freshness."

She merely laughed, calling me a funny gentleman. This is my perpetual misfortune: when I offer serious observation, people chuckle; when I attempt humor, they fail to perceive it. Last week, I crafted a magnificent joke. I nurtured it carefully and deployed it with artistic timing at a dinner party. We were debating Shakespeare's stance on the Reformation, and I made a remark, followed immediately by, "Ah, that reminds me; a truly hilarious incident occurred recently in Whitechapel."

"Oh," they responded, "pray, what was that?"

"Oh, it was dreadfully funny," I replied, beginning to giggle uncontrollably; "it will bring you to your knees!" So I recounted it. There ensued a deathly silence—a prolonged, awkward pause—and finally, someone asked, "And that was the joke?"

I insisted it was, and they graciously accepted my word for it. Everyone except one elderly gentleman at the far end of the table, who demanded to know exactly what the punchline was—what he said to her or vice versa. We debated it for ages. Some individuals are simply too rigid. I recall a acquaintance whose innate propensity to laugh at everything was so overpowering that if one wished to converse seriously, one had to preface remarks with a warning that the following words were not intended to be amusing. Without this caveat, he would erupt into fits of merriment over every utterance. I have seen him halt mid-road upon being asked the time, slap his thigh, and explode into roaring laughter. One dares not share a genuinely funny anecdote with such a man; a good joke would kill him instantly.

In this current instance, I vigorously denied the charge of frivolity and pressed Mrs. Cutting for practical suggestions. She grew contemplative, holding a tea-tray, and ventured the word "samplers." She noted they were rarely mentioned nowadays but were once the height of fashion during her youth. I politely declined samplers and urged her to reconsider. After a long period of pondering, she finally suggested "the weather," noting it had been particularly trying of late.

And lo! Since that idiotic recommendation, I have been unable to expel the weather from my thoughts—or indeed, anything else. It is, without exaggeration, wretched weather. At the very least, it is so at the moment of my writing, and unless conditions improve miraculously by the time you read this, they surely will deteriorate further. According to us, the weather is perpetually abysmal. It resembles the government: invariably in the wrong. In summer, we complain it is stifling; in winter, that it is lethal; in spring and autumn, we fault it for lacking definition, wishing it would commit to a single state. If it is sunny, we lament the countryside's lack of moisture; if it rains, we beg for sunshine. If December passes without snow, we indignantly inquire where our traditional winters have vanished, acting as though we have been defrauded of a purchased commodity; yet, when snow arrives, our reaction is a disgrace to Christianity. We shall remain discontent until every individual manufactures their own private climate and keeps it strictly to themselves. Failing that arrangement, we would prefer total abolition.

However, I suspect this aversion is peculiar to urban dwellers. In the countryside, Nature is enchanting in every mood. Consider the snow: descending in mysterious, silent softness, blanketing fields and trees in white as if preparing for a fairy nuptial! How delightful a walk when the frozen earth resonates beneath our steps, when our blood thrums in the crisp air, and the distant bark of sheepdogs and children's laughter echo faintly and clearly like Alpine bells across the open hills! And then, skating! Gliding on wings of steel across swaying ice, creating whirring symphonies as we fly. Oh, how exquisite is spring—Nature at her sweetest eighteen! When tender, hopeful leaves peek out fresh and green, pure and bright, like young souls tentatively entering the bustling world; when fruit trees blossom in pink and white, resembling village maidens in their Sunday gowns, hiding whitewashed cottages in clouds of fragile splendor; and when the cuckoo's note drifts on the breeze through the woods! And summer, with its deep verdant hues and drowsy hum—when raindrops whisper solemn secrets to attentive leaves and twilight lingers lovingly in the lanes! And autumn! Ah, how tragically beautiful, with its golden radiance and the dying majesty of its colored forests—its crimson sunsets and ghostly evening mists, accompanied by the busy murmur of harvesters, laden orchards, the calls of gleaners, and festivals of praise! Even the rain, sleet, and hail seem merely Nature's dutiful servants when performing their simple tasks in the country; even the East Wind is no worse than a boisterous friend when encountered between hedgerows.

But in the city, where painted stucco blisters under the smoky sun, where sooty rain creates slush and mire, where snow piles in filthy heaps, and where chilling blasts whistle down dingy streets and shriek around gas-lit corners, no aspect of Nature charms us. Urban weather is like a skylark trapped in a counting-house—utterly out of place and obstructive. Cities ought to be enclosed, heated by hot-water pipes, and illuminated by electricity. The weather is a rustic maiden who does not shine in the metropolis. We enjoyed flirting with her in the hayfields, but she loses her allure when we meet her in Pall Mall. There is simply too much of her presence there. Her frank, free laugh and hearty voice, so pleasing in the dairy, clash violently with the artificiality of town life, rendering her mannerisms exceedingly trying.

Recently, she has favored us with relentless rain for nearly three weeks, leaving me a damnable, damp, and unpleasant specimen, as Mr. Mantalini might phrase it. Our neighbor emerges into the back garden periodically to declare the weather is benefiting the country—not his excursion, but the precipitation. He lacks understanding, yet since establishing a cucumber frame last summer, he views himself as an agriculturist and speaks absurdly to impress the terrace residents with the notion that he is a retired farmer. I can only hope he is correct this time, that the weather is actually beneficial to something, because it is inflicting significant damage upon me. It ruins both my attire and my disposition. While I can afford a bad temper—I possess ample reserves—it cuts deeply to witness my beloved hats and trousers succumbing prematurely to the cold world's blasts and snows. There is also my new spring suit. Once a masterpiece, it now hangs besplattered with mud, unbearable to behold. That was Jim's fault entirely. I should never have ventured out in it had he not intervened. I was fitting it on when he entered. Upon spotting it, he threw up his arms in a wild yell, exclaiming he had "got 'em again!"

"Does it fit properly in the back?" I asked.

"Spiffin, old man," he replied. Immediately, he inquired if I was going out. I initially refused, but he overruled me. "A man with a suit like that," he insisted, "has no right to remain indoors. Every citizen owes a duty to the public. Each must contribute to general happiness to the extent possible. Go forth and treat the girls."

Jim is notoriously slang-heavy; I cannot fathom his source, as it certainly isn't me. "Do you believe it will truly please them?" I asked.

"It will be like a day in the country for them," he stated. This settled it. It was a lovely evening, and I departed. Upon returning home, I undressed, rubbed myself down with whisky, soaked my feet in hot water, applied a mustard plaster to my chest, consumed a bowl of gruel and a glass of hot brandy-and-water, tallow-ed my nose, and retired to bed. These prompt, vigorous measures, bolstered by a robust constitution, preserved my life; but regarding the suit! Well, it is no longer a suit; it is a splashboard. I had fancied that suit greatly. But that is the pattern. I never grow particularly attached to anything in this world without something dreadful happening to it.
