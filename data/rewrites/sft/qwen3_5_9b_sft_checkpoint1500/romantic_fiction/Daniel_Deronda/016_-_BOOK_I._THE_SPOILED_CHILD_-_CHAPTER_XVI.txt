Men, like planets, possess both a visible trajectory and an invisible history. An astronomer threads the void with rigid logic to account for every arc in a wanderer's path; similarly, a chronicler of human lives must weave the hidden corridors of emotion and thought that culminate in every action, including those moments of profound suffering that transform into deeds—akin to the cry of Prometheus, whose bound agony exerts a force greater than the heavens he calls upon or defies.

Daniel Deronda's life had indeed been marked by singular exceptions. One instant burned into his memory as the defining epoch: a day of July sunlight and large pink roses dropping their final petals onto a grassy court hemmed in by Gothic cloisters. Imagine him there, a boy of thirteen lying prone in the shade, his curly head resting on his arms over a book, while his tutor read nearby on a camp-stool. Daniel's volume was Sismondi's *History of the Italian Republics*; the lad possessed a burning passion for history, an insatiable curiosity about how time had filled the centuries since the Flood and how affairs continued during dull eras. Suddenly, he lowered his left arm and gazed at his tutor, speaking in pure, boyish tones, "Mr. Fraser, how was it that popes and cardinals always had so many nephews?"

His tutor, an able young Scot serving as Sir Hugo Mallinger's secretary, roused himself reluctantly from political economy, answering with the clear, emphatic cadence that makes a truth doubly resonant in Scottish speech, "Their own children were called nephews."
"Why?" asked Deronda.
"It was merely for propriety," the tutor replied, "because, as you know well, priests do not marry, and the children were illegitimate."
Mr. Fraser, thrusting out his lower lip with impatience, returned his gaze to his book. Meanwhile, Deronda, as if stung by a blow, sprang up in a sitting posture with his back to the tutor.

He had always addressed Sir Hugo Mallinger as his uncle. When he first dared to inquire about his father and mother, the baronet had responded, "You lost your father and mother when you were quite a little one; that is why I take care of you." Daniel, straining to see through that early twilight, retained only a dim sense of being kissed profusely and wrapped in thin, cloudy, scented drapery, until his fingers snagged on something hard that hurt, causing him to weep. All other memories were of the small, safe world in which he still lived. At that time, he had not minded learning more, for he was too devoted to Sir Hugo to regret the loss of unknown parents. Life had been delightful, sustained by an uncle who was perpetually indulgent and cheerful—a magnificent man in the bright noon of life whom Daniel considered perfect, residing in a place of grandeur that was simultaneously historical, romantic, and homelike: a picturesque architectural extension from an abbey retaining relics of old monastic trunks. Diplomay lay in another county, a relatively landless estate acquired through a wealthy lawyer on the maternal side who wore the powdered wigs of the Restoration; whereas the Mallingers held the grant of Monk's Topping from Henry VIII and traced origins to Hugues le Malingre, who arrived with William the Conqueror, allegedly correcting a sickly complexion inherited from the ancestor. Two rows of descendants, direct and collateral, stared down from the gallery above the cloisters as Daniel walked below: armored men with pointed beards and arched brows, pinched ladies in hoops and ruffs devoid of facial expression, grave men in black velvet with stuffed hips, and fair, frightened women clutching little boys' hands; smiling politicians in magnificent wigs, and ladies of the prize-animal variety with rosebud mouths and full eyelids, per Lely; followed by generations revised in the taste of Kneller and refined further through Reynolds and Romney, ending finally with Sir Hugo and his younger brother, Henleigh. The latter had married Miss Grandcourt, adopting her name and estates, thereby uniting two ancient families, impaling their heraldry and aligning their highest advantages in the prospects of Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt, who was at present more of an acquaintance to us than either Sir Hugo or his nephew, Daniel Deronda.

In Sir Hugo's youthful portrait, Sir Thomas Lawrence captured the agreeable alacrity of the baronet's expression and sanguine temperament, though he perhaps overdid justice by slightly elongating the nose, which was in reality shorter than expected in a Mallinger. Fortunately, the appropriate nose of the family reappeared in Henleigh, manifesting in all its refined regularity in his nephew, Mallinger Grandcourt. Yet in Daniel Deronda, the diverse family faces adorning the gallery walls found no reflection. Still, he was handsomer than any of them, and at thirteen, he might have served as a model for any painter seeking to capture the most memorable of boys. To behold his face thoroughly confronting one's own is difficult without believing that human beings had achieved nobility in times past and might achieve it even more nobly in the future. The finest childlike faces possess this consecrating power, making us shudder anew at the grossness and basely-wrought griefs of the world, fearing lest they enter here and defile.

However, amidst the rose-petals on the grass, Daniel Deronda was encountering those griefs for the first time. A novel idea had entered his mind, altering the aspect of his habitual feelings as happy, careless voyagers are transformed when the sky threatens and danger arises. He sat perfectly still, his back to the tutor, his face expressing a rapid internal transition. The deep blush that had risen when he first stood up gradually subsided, yet his features retained an indescribable look of subdued activity, often accompanying a new mental survey of familiar facts. Having read Shakespeare and vast amounts of history, his mind displayed a blend of childish ignorance and surprising knowledge, often seen in bright girls. He could have discoursed with the wisdom of a bookish child regarding men born out of wedlock, deemed unfortunate by the disadvantages requiring them to be heroes to equal their legally born brothers. But he had never associated such knowledge with his own lot, which had been too easy to contemplate—until this moment, when the possibility darted into his mind with the magic of quick comparison: that here lay the secret of his birth, and the man he called uncle was actually his father.

Some children, even younger than Daniel, recognize the first arrival of care like an ominous, irremovable guest when they discover their parents, whom they imagined could buy anything, were poor and in financial distress. Daniel felt the presence of a new guest who seemed to approach with an enigmatic, veiled face, carrying dimly conjectured, dreaded revelations. The ardor he had given to the imaginary worlds in his books suddenly rushed toward his own history, expending its pictorial energy there, explaining what he knew and representing the unknown. The uncle he loved so dearly took the aspect of a father who held secrets about him—one who had done him a wrong, yes, a wrong—and what had become of his mother, for whom he must have been taken away? These were secrets Daniel could never inquire about; speaking or being spoken to regarding these new thoughts seemed like falling flakes of fire to his imagination. Those who know an impassioned childhood understand this dread of utterance regarding any shame connected to one's parents. The impetuous advent of new images took possession of him with the force of fact, leaving him no immediate power to reflect that he might be trembling at a fiction of his own. The terrible sense of collision between a strong rush of feeling and the dread of its betrayal found relief eventually in big, slow tears, falling without restraint until the voice of Mr. Fraser was heard saying, "Daniel, do you see that you are sitting on the bent pages of your book?"

Daniel immediately moved the book without turning around, held it before him for an instant, then rose and walked away into the open grounds where he could dry his tears unobserved. Once the first shock of suggestion passed, he remembered he had no certainty of how things truly had been, having made conjectures about his own history just as he often invented stories about Pericles or Columbus to fill gaps before they became famous. Yet certain facts returned with an obstinate reality, almost like fragments of a bridge telling you unmistakably how the arches lay. Again, a mood arose in which his conjectures seemed like a doubt of religion, to be banished as an offense and a mean prying after what he was not meant to know; for there was hardly a delicacy of feeling this lad lacked. The summing-up of all his fluctuating experience at this epoch was that a secret impression had come to him, granting him something akin to a new sense in relation to all elements of his life. The idea that others probably knew things they chose not to mention set up a premature reserve within him, intensifying his inward experience. His ears were now open to words that previously would have passed unnoticed, and round every trivial incident imagination could connect with his suspicions, a newly roused set of feelings clustered eagerly.

One such incident, occurring a month later, wrought itself deeply into his life. Daniel possessed not only one of those thrilling boy voices that seem to bring an idyllic heaven and earth before our eyes, but a fine musical instinct, having early made out piano accompaniments while singing from memory. Since then, he had received some instruction, and Sir Hugo, who delighted in the boy, used to request his music in the presence of guests.
