It took me three full, grueling years to earn that first proper industry handshake from a newspaper sub-editor. For the first six months of that stretch I was holed up in Deptford, where the docks and the dockside pubs brimmed with perfect fodder for essays, stories, and punchlines of every stripe—even the sad, tear-jerking kind that made readers blow their noses into their newspapers. But I owed two hundred quid (give or take a few pennies, once I’d stopped lying to myself about the total), so I wasn’t about to waste good material on making strangers snivel. I left the sad stuff to the mopey writers who enjoyed weeping over other people’s misfortunes, stuck strictly to my own patch, and only hauled armfuls of straight-up laughter to market to sell.

At first I sent every single finished manuscript straight to Norah, who got them typed up, deducted the copying cost, cashed the editor’s check, and sent me whatever change was left. My early earnings were tiny, but Norah was a shark of an agent, and before long my pay packets were swelling so fast that old Dan Brian, the tightfisted editor who’d first published me, got thoroughly huffy. He wrote me a pained, grumpy note saying my work was “fine, not exceptional,” and that even though he wanted to help out a friend of Norah’s, business was business—he couldn’t possibly pay that much, not when he had proprietors to answer to. Norah shot back a curt third-person reply agreeing fully that business was indeed business, and that if Dan couldn’t afford top-tier funny, the three other editors she was equally chummy with absolutely could, and she’d be sending the half-finished manuscript she was holding right over to them if he didn’t reply by return post. Dan, realizing he’d met his match, grumbled so loud I bet his office walls shook, and paid up. He never admitted he had any clue my pen name “Jack Homer” was me, but he never tried to lowball us again, anyway. Norah made a point of sending a solid chunk of my work to rival publications on purpose, too, and soon “Jack Homer” was a hot commodity everyone was fighting over. I’d sit at my wobbly rickety table, cackling while I wrote, and the drab fourth wall of my crappy garret would dissolve to show me all the bigger, better places I was gonna get to one day.

Still, it was slow going. Humor, it turns out, is the worst kind of diva of a coworker. She won’t be rushed, refuses to show up on a schedule, spends 90% of her time napping and getting her hair done, and if you badger her for a joke when she’s not in the mood, she’ll vanish for three whole days without a text, only to saunter back at 2am with a perfect one-liner and no apology. To pay the bills I had to add straight journalism to my workload, and boy was I bad at the whole “burst into the editor’s office uninvited to pitch a story” schtick. Fate never cuts nervous people any slack—if I’d tried that, the editor would’ve definitely been out, or if he was in, he’d have booted me out on my ear before I got three words out. Even the legendary roguish Robert Macaire himself, dressed in my only frayed coat, would’ve apologized for existing before he even crossed the threshold. So I joined the ranks of the penny-a-liners—three halfpence a line, to be annoyingly precise. Me and half a dozen other ragged, shabby blokes (some lads like me trying to climb the ladder, some old blokes who’d already fallen off it) hung around inquests, camped outside police courts, sprinted after fire engines, got giddy over random street crashes, and prayed for murders to happen so we could make rent. We were basically a pack of well-meaning vultures, scratching a living off other people’s bad luck. Every once in a while we’d make half a quid by writing up a scandal for the papers, and even more often make a whole crown by keeping our mouths shut about said scandal.

“Gentlemen,” our designated spokesman would whisper hoarsely, trundling back to our little table at the coroner’s court, “looks like the corpse’s brother-in-law is real keen on this not making the papers, if we can help it.” Suddenly we’d all be taking the most detailed, diligent notes known to man, cross-examining witnesses like barristers. Five minutes later our guy would drift back, wiping his mouth like he’d just had a pint down the pub. “Nah, lads, this case ain’t interesting at all. Five bob apiece sound fair?” Sometimes, if we were feeling especially proud of our noble calling, we’d dig our heels in and demand ten.

My dumb sense of humor gave me a huge leg up on the rest of the crew, too. Once, 12 jurors were trying to work out how a random Lascar sailor had dropped dead out of nowhere. It was the most obvious thing in the world: a plumber working on the roof of a tiny two-story house had slipped, and landed right on top of him. The plumber walked away with a couple of bruises, the sailor was stone dead before anyone could say “watch your step.” The plumber’s workmate, a great witness, told us the whole story:
“I was fixin’ a gas pipe on the first floor, right? Jim, the plumber, was up on the roof. He yells down the chimney askin’ if I’m ready for him. I yells back ‘Ready and waitin’.’ He says ‘Right, I’m comin’ in through the window.’ I yells ‘Hold on, I’ll go move the ladder for you.’ He says ‘Nah, it’s fine, I can reach it.’ I says ‘No you can’t, it’s on the other side of the chimney.’ He says ‘Nah, I can get round.’ Next thing I know I hear him go SMACK! I rush to the window, look out, and there he is on the pavement, sittin’ up all casual like. I yells out ‘Hullo Jim, did you hurt yourself?’ He says ‘Think I’m all right, far as I can tell. But mate, can you come down here quick? This bloke I landed on looks a bit sick.’”

All the other hacks wrote up the story with the boring, truthful headline “Sad Accident,” which no one would ever stop to read. I called mine “Plumber In A Hurry – Fatal Result,” wrote barely a paragraph about the poor dead sailor, and dedicated the rest of the piece to warning plumbers everywhere that rushing through work to save five seconds is the dumbest possible move you could ever make. It made me a local legend. The sub-editor of a big evening paper even rolled up to the press room in his shirtsleeves, walked all the way over to me, and shook my hand like I’d just won the Nobel Prize for news. “That’s exactly the stuff we want,” he told me. “Light touch, a little bit of fun.” After that, I was pulling jokes out of burning buildings (I really, really hope no one’s insurance lapsed because of it), mining street brawls for silly little gags, and digging punchlines out of even the most horrific disasters imaginable, and for once, everything was going my way.

I never once went near that old Poplar house, even though I passed within spitting distance of it all the time. Back then I was basically two different guys stuffed inside one coat, constantly fighting. My whole goal was to nurture the new, practical, go-getter Paul who was building a decent career, and chase off that weird sappy twin of mine, the old teenage Paul with the sad faraway eyes who still lived in my head. Sometimes I’d glance in my cracked cheap mirror and see his wistful, yearning face staring back at me, begging me to go visit the old house. But I’d shake my head at him stern, because I knew what he was up to. If I let him call the shots, he’d lead me winding through all the old familiar streets, drag me past the broken railings, down the weedy path, through the dented cracked front door, up the creaky stairs to that tiny garret we used to sit in daydreaming all those stupid, useless big dreams. “C’mon,” he’d whisper, “it’s so close! Let’s quietly push the chest of drawers out of the way, lift that broken window sash real soft, prop it open with the Latin dictionary, lean our elbows on the sill, and listen to all the tired old city voices calling out in the dark.” But I was too sharp to fall for that. “Later,” I’d tell him. “When I’ve made it big, when I’m tough enough to ignore all your mushy nonsense. Then I’ll come with you, if you’re still hanging around, you silly sentimental oaf. We’ll dig up all those old dumb impossible dreams, and laugh at how stupid we were.” Then he’d vanish, and the new practical, wide-awake Paul would nod at me approvingly in the mirror, ready to go write another funny paragraph.

But I caved to one silly, sentimental temptation. By then my career was steady, I didn’t have any reason to hide anymore, so I decided to move west to a nicer neighborhood. I wasn’t planning to go anywhere fancy, but one day I was walking down Guildford Street and spotted that creeper-covered corner house my dad had once talked about renting, years back. There was a To Let card stuck in the fanlight, so I knocked and enquired. The third floor bed-sit was empty, and the second the chatty landlady opened the door, I recognized it instantly. “This shall be your room, Paul,” my dad’s voice echoed so clearly behind me that I turned around, half-expecting to see him standing there, before I remembered he was long gone. “You’ll be quiet here, we can hide the bed and washstand behind a nice screen.” My dad got his way, after all. It was a sunny, cozy little room, looking out over the hospital gardens. I did exactly what he’d suggested, stuck a screen in front of the bed and washstand, and some evenings I swear I could hear him turning the doorknob, poking his head in to ask how I was getting on. “All good,” I’d call back, and it felt like he was proud of me. I kept thinking about that thing he’d told me once: “You’ve got to be the practical one, Paul, and get on with it. I was always a bit of a dreamer. I planned to do such huge great things in the world, but I guess I aimed too high. I wasn’t practical.” When I’d asked him if it wasn’t better to aim high, he’d fidgeted in his chair and said “It’s all so confusing. You aim high and you don’t hit anything, but then it feels a waste to aim low and hit it, when you’ve got all those big ideals in your head. I don’t know why life works like that. Maybe none of us ever really understand.”

I’d been publishing work under my own name for a few months when a letter got forwarded to me by an editor, addressed to my care. It was a stiff, formal invite from Mrs. Sellars, the mom of the girl I’d once almost run off with, asking me to her daughter’s wedding to a Mr. Reginald Clapper. I’d barely even thought about the whole Lady Ortensia incident in ages, but it was nice to know it had all worked out fine for her. I also figured sending me the invite meant she wanted me to see I hadn’t left her heartbroken, so I splurged on the nicest wedding present I could afford, dug my only good frock coat and light grey trousers out of the back of the wardrobe, and trundled off to Kennington Church the next Thursday.

The ceremony was already halfway over when I got there. I tiptoed up the aisle, about to slip into an empty pew, when a big calloused hand clamped onto my sleeve. “We’re all packed in here,” O’Kelly whispered, grinning, “just enough spot for you.” I squeezed his hand as I squeezed past, and plopped down between the Signora and Mrs. Peedles. Both of them were crying: the Signora was leaking slow, pretty tears that clung to her cheeks like they didn’t want to roll off, Mrs. Peedles was sobbing so hard she made gurgling noises like a bottle of water being tipped upside down. “The service is so beautiful,” the Signora murmured, squeezing my hand. “I would so, so love to get married someday.” “Darlin’,” O’Kelly whispered back, snatching her other hand and kissing it behind his prayer book where no one could see, “maybe you will, one day.” The Signora smiled at him through her tears, but sighed and shook her head.

Mrs. Peedles, dressed in what I’m pretty sure was Queen Elizabeth’s third best old gown (the dim November light made it hard to be 100% sure), sobbed that weddings always reminded her how fleeting love was. “Poor dears!” she blubbered. “Well, there’s no telling, I s’pose. Maybe they’ll be happy. I hope they are. The groom looks harmless enough.” Jarman leaned over past Mrs. Peedles to pat my shoulder. “Cheer up, mate,” he advised me. “Don’t go wearin’ your heart on your sleeve. Try and smile.”

Back in the vestry I ran into a dozen old faces. Mrs. Sellars was even stouter than I remembered, propped up on a chair I couldn’t even see under her, greeting me so warmly and asking after my health so tenderly that I was confused until I realized she’d heard I was making good money now. Groom Reginald Clapper was a tiny, energetic guy who was clearly 100% convinced he’d won the lottery by marrying Ortensia. He shook my hand so hard my arm ached, said he was over the moon to meet me, and told me three separate times I had to come over to their house whenever I wanted, no invitation needed. I got the distinct impression he thought he was being unbelievably generous by even letting me in the front door. The new Mrs. Clapper curtsied to me, and the whole crowd around us murmured approvingly. She looked even prettier than I remembered, or maybe my taste had just gotten less fussy. She said she hoped we could be friends, too, and before I knew it she’d leaned over and kissed me on the cheek, then hugged her mom and started crying, saying everyone was just so nice to her.

Her brother George, who used to be all lanky and thin, was now filling out a bit, and struggling to move his hands in a pair of giant white kid gloves. He supervised me signing the wedding register, then leaned over to whisper sympathetically in my ear, “Better luck next time, old cock.” The fat young lady (or maybe it was the thin young lady from before who’d put on a bunch of weight, I couldn’t tell for sure) who’d once said I was the best guy Ortensia could’ve picked, told me to my face she’d said it a dozen times before the wedding, but no one had listened to her. Cousin Joseph, easy to spot because of his watery red eyes, looked way less chirpy than I remembered him. “Take my tip,” he told me, pulling me off to the side, “and stay the hell out of marriage.” “You’re speaking from experience?” I asked. “I love a good joke as much as the next bloke,” Joseph said, “but when it comes to buckets of water being dumped on your head at 2am, and never getting a quiet minute to yourself—” Mrs. Sellars yelled that the wedding breakfast was at 11, so everyone trundled off to the restaurant, and Joseph’s rant got cut short. “See you later, maybe,” he mumbled, pushing past me.

I walked there with Uncle and Aunt Gutton, not because I wanted to, but because Uncle Gutton had clamped his hand around my arm and refused to let go. “C’mon, young man,” he boomed, as soon as we were far enough behind the rest of the crowd, “if you wanna yell at me for being a git back then, go right ahead. I won’t mind, it’ll probably do you good.” I told him that was the last thing I wanted. “Oh, fine then,” he said, looking a little disappointed. “If you’re willing to forgive and forget, I am too. I never liked you, you know, I told Rosie that straight out. Said you could either be smarter than you looked, or a bigger idiot than I thought, and odds were on the latter. Told her to go find herself a full grown bloke who knew what he was doing.” I told him his advice had been perfect. “Well that’s weird,” he said, looking confused, “I was half expecting you to call me every name under the sun. Fair play, though, you’re a sensible chap. Got over it, just like I told Rosie you would. Young men in storybooks never die of broken hearts, even if for a month or two they feel like sticking their head in a water butt to drown the sadness out. Why, I was head over heels in love three separate times before I married your aunt here.” Aunt Gutton sighed and said she was afraid blokes never took heartbreak half as seriously as they should. “They’ve got a living to earn, don’t they?” Uncle Gutton shot back. We all agreed there was no point moping over lost love, and as we walked into the restaurant, Uncle Gutton clapped me on the back and shook my hand, saying he’d been wrong about me, and I’d turned out way better than he ever expected.

Minikin was already waiting for us, saying he’d bailed on the second half of the church service because he only had limited time off work, and the food was way better than hymns.
