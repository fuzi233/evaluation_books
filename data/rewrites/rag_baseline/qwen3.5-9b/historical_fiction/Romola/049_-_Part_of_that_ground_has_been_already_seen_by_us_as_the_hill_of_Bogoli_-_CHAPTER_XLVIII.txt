Late afternoon light filtered through the narrow window of Romola's chamber, casting long, brittle shadows across the desk where she sat copying documents. Her hand hovered over the quill, ready to cease the labor as the sun dipped lower, when the door opened. Tito entered, not with the casual stride of a husband returning home, but with the focused intent of a commander seizing a strategic position. He sought her specifically, and the shift in his demeanor struck her instantly. Without removing his cap or mantle, he planted himself directly before her, resting one elbow on the cabinet, blocking her path with a silent, immovable presence.

Romola felt the weight of her own error settling in her chest. Throughout the day, she had wrestled with the sting of her public confrontation regarding the Frate's safety—a matter she knew he guarded jealously. A tide of penitence washed over her; she had been driven by distrust and indignation, acting with the impulsiveness of a woman who feared losing control. Now, rationalizing the chaos of her emotions, she convinced herself she had misjudged the situation. The duplicity inherent in Florentine factionalism, a truth even her godfather acknowledged, could easily explain the connection with Spini without implicating Tito in the conspiracy. She intended to atone for her rashness, hoping this confession might finally break the two-year silence that had locked their hearts in separate rooms. That silence was absolute; Tito remained ignorant of her flight and return, unaware of the young love that had shattered under the shock of betrayal. They had never dared to revisit that past, a banquet hall where death had once smashed the feast.

She looked up, her gaze submitting to the gravity of her self-reproach, expecting forgiveness. Instead, she encountered a subtle, terrifying transformation in his face. The crisis had arrived. Beneath the veneer of his usual blandness and beseeching gentleness, Tito's determination to master the situation had surged to the surface. It altered his features, creating a mask of hidden muscular tension, as if a man were secretly throttling something feeble yet dangerous within his own throat.

"Romola," he began, his voice a cool, liquid stream that sent a shiver through her spine. "It is time that we should understand each other."

"That is what I most desire, Tito," she whispered, her pale face stripped of anger, replaced entirely by the timidity of self-doubt. In this vulnerability, his dark strength appeared overwhelming.

"You took a step this morning," Tito continued, "which you must now perceive to have been useless—one that exposes you to remark and may involve me in serious practical difficulties."

"I acknowledge that I was too hasty," Romola said, finding a fuller, firmer tone in her voice. She hoped this expression of regret would soften his glare, clearing the way for the other words she needed to speak. "I wish you once for all to understand," Tito replied, his voice unwavering, "that such collisions are incompatible with our position as husband and wife. Reflect on how you were led to that step, lest the process repeat itself."

"That depends chiefly on you, Tito," Romola retorted, a spark of defiance igniting. It was not what she had planned, but the fog of their mutual misunderstanding had obscured her thoughts.

"You would say," Tito countered smoothly, "that nothing henceforth shall occur to excite your unreasonable suspicions. You were frank enough last night to declare you hold no belief in me. I am not surprised by the exaggerated conclusions drawn from slight premises, but I must warn you of the fruit of making such distortions grounds for interfering in affairs of which you are ignorant. Are you following?"

"Yes," Romola flushed, her resentment boiling at this cold tone of superiority.

"Well, then, it may not be long before some other chance words or incidents set your imagination at work devising crimes for me. Perhaps you will rush to the Palazzo Vecchio to alarm the Signoria, setting the city ablaze with panic. Shall I outline the result? Not merely the disgrace of your husband, to which you look forward with such courage, but the arrest and ruin of many among Florence's chief men, including Messer Bernardo del Nero."

Tito had executed a decisive maneuver, and it had succeeded. The color drained from Romola's face until her lips were pale—a rare sight, for she was seldom subject to fear. Tito perceived his triumph.

"You might flatter yourself," he pressed, "that you are performing a heroic deed of deliverance. You might as well attempt to pick locks with fine words as apply such notions to the politics of Florence. The question is no longer whether you possess belief in me, but whether, now warned, you will dare to rush, like a blind man clutching a torch, into intricate affairs you know nothing of."

Romola felt her mind gripped in a vice; the possibilities he outlined rose before her with terrible clarity.

"I am too rash," she admitted. "I will try not to be rash."

"Remember," Tito insisted, his tone unsparing, "that your act of distrust this morning might, for all you know, have had far more fatal effects than that sacrifice of your husband which you have learned to contemplate without flinching."

"Tito, it is not so!" Romola burst forth, rising and moving closer with a desperate resolve to speak her truth. "It is false that I would willingly sacrifice you. It has been the greatest effort of my life to cling to you. I fled in anger two years ago, and returned because I was bound to you more than to anything on earth. But it is useless. You shut me out from your mind. You treat me as a being too unreasonable to share in your knowledge. You remain closed to me about everything."

She looked like a good angel pleading with a stone, bending her face toward him with dilated eyes, laying her hand upon his arm. Yet, Romola's touch and glance stirred no fiber of tenderness in her husband. The good-natured, tolerant Tito, capable of little hatred or impatience, felt himself growing strangely hard toward this wife who had once been his strongest influence. Despite his soft disposition, he possessed a masculine effectiveness of intellect and purpose, an energy sharp as a blade, working its way forward without needing great momentum. Romola possessed her own energy, one that thwarted his, and no man, unless exceptionally feeble, endures being thwarted by his wife. Marriage demands either sympathy or conquest.

Not a single emotion crossed Tito's face as he heard her mention her departure for the first time. His lips merely tightened slightly as he offered a faint smile.

"My Romola, when certain conditions are ascertained, we must accept them. No amount of wishing will fill the Arno, as your people say, nor turn a plum into an orange. I have observed that prayers have little efficacy in such matters. You are constituted to harbor strong impressions inaccessible to reason; I cannot share those impressions, and you have withdrawn all trust from me in consequence. You have changed toward me; therefore, I have changed toward you. It is useless to dwell on the past. We must simply adapt to altered conditions."

"It would not be useless for us to speak openly," Romola said, her voice trembling with the exasperation of striking living muscle against lifeless, insurmountable rock. "It was the sense of deception in you that changed me, keeping us apart. And it is untrue that I changed first. You changed toward me the night you first wore that chain-armour. You held some secret from me—about that old man—and I saw him again yesterday."

In a tone of agonized entreaty, she pleaded, "Tito, if you would once tell me everything, let it be what it may—I would not mind pain—that there might be no wall between us! Is it not possible that we could begin a new life?"

This time, a flash of emotion crossed Tito's face. He stood perfectly still, but the flash seemed to whiten him. Ignoring her appeal, he paused briefly before speaking quietly.

"Your impetuosity about trifles, Romola, possesses a freezing influence that would cool the baths of Nero."

At these cutting words, Romola shrank, drawing herself up into her usual self-sustaining posture. Tito continued.

"If by 'that old man' you mean the mad Jacopo di Nola who attempted my life and made a strange accusation against me, of which I told you nothing because it would have alarmed you to no purpose, he, poor wretch, has died in prison. I saw his name listed among the dead."

"I know nothing of his accusation," Romola stated flatly. "But I know he is the man I saw with a rope around his neck in the Duomo—the man whose portrait Piero di Cosimo painted, grasping your arm as he saw him grasp it the day the French entered, the day you first donned the armor."

"And where is he now, pray?" Tito asked, still pale but governing himself.

"He was lying lifeless in the street from starvation," Romola replied. "I revived him with bread and wine. I brought him to our door, but he refused entry. I gave him money, and he departed without telling me anything. But he had discovered I was your wife. Who is he?"
