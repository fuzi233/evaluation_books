The village of Bohun Beacon sat perched precariously atop a hill so sheer that the church spire seemed less a tower and more the jagged peak of a miniature mountain. At the base of this ecclesiastical summit stood a blacksmith's forge, perpetually glowing with the heat of fires and cluttered with the rhythmic chaos of hammers and scrap iron. Directly opposite, separated only by a rough cobblestone crossroads, lay "The Blue Boar," the village's sole inn. It was upon this intersection, bathed in the grey and silver light of dawn, that two brothers encountered one another—one beginning his day, the other concluding his night.

The Reverend and Hon. Wilfred Bohun was a man of profound devotion, ascending the path toward austere dawn prayers. His elder brother, Colonel the Hon. Norman Bohun, possessed none of this piety. Dressed in evening wear, he sat on the bench outside the inn, nursing a drink that a philosopher might debate was either his final Tuesday glass or his first Wednesday one; the Colonel cared little for the distinction.

The Bohuns were an ancient aristocratic lineage, tracing their roots to the Middle Ages and even the crusades of Palestine. Yet, it is a fatal error to assume such houses remain guardians of chivalric tradition. Traditions are the currency of the poor; aristocrats live solely in the fleeting currency of fashion. Once Mohocks under Queen Anne and dandies under Victoria, the family had, over the last two centuries, decayed into mere drunkards and degenerate aesthetes, with whispers of insanity creeping into their halls. There was something inhuman about the Colonel's wolfish hunger for pleasure, his chronic refusal to return home until morning suggesting a terrifying clarity born of insomnia. He was a tall, fine animal, elderly yet possessing startlingly yellow hair. He would have appeared merely leonine if not for his sunken blue eyes, which looked black, set too closely together. Long yellow moustaches framed a face etched with deep furrows from nostril to jaw, giving him a permanent sneer. Draped in evening clothes, he wore a pale yellow coat resembling a dressing gown and topped it with a bizarre, broad-brimmed bright green hat—an Oriental curiosity he wore with the pride of a man who believed his incongruities formed a perfect whole.

His brother, the curate, shared the yellow hair and elegance but was clad in tight black, his face clean-shaven, cultivated, and perpetually nervous. While some claimed his obsession was with Gothic architecture rather than God—a morbid thirst for beauty mirroring his brother's lust for women—his practical piety was undeniable. He spent his days kneeling not before the altar, but in the crypts, galleries, and belfry, seeking solitude.

Wilfred paused at the churchyard gate, frowning slightly as he saw his brother's cavernous eyes staring in the same direction. Assuming the Colonel was interested in the church, Wilfred dismissed the thought. His gaze shifted to the blacksmith's shed. Though the smith was a Puritan, Wilfred recalled rumors of a beautiful, celebrated wife. He cast a suspicious glance across the shed, and the Colonel stood up, laughing.

"Good morning, Wilfred," the Colonel said. "Like a good landlord, I watch sleeplessly over my people. I intend to visit the blacksmith."

Wilfred looked down at the ground. "The blacksmith is out. He is at Greenford."

"I know," the Colonel replied with silent amusement. "That is precisely why I am calling on him."

"Norman," Wilfred said, his eye fixed on a pebble in the road, "are you ever afraid of thunderbolts?"

"What do you mean?" the Colonel asked. "Is your hobby meteorology?"

"I mean," Wilfred said without looking up, "do you ever think God might strike you down in the street?"

"I beg your pardon," the Colonel replied. "I see your hobby is folklore."

"I know your hobby is blasphemy," Wilfred retorted, stung in his only living vein. "But if you do not fear God, you have good reason to fear man."

The elder brother raised his eyebrows politely. "Fear man?"

"Barnes the blacksmith is the largest and strongest man for forty miles around," the clergyman said sternly. "I know you are no coward, but he could throw you over the wall."

The comment struck home. The Colonel's sneer deepened, the furrows by his mouth and nostrils darkening. For a moment, he stood frozen. Then, instantly, his cruel good humor returned. He laughed, revealing two dog-like front teeth beneath his moustache. "In that case, my dear Wilfred," he said casually, "it was wise for the last of the Bohuns to come out partially in armor."

He removed the strange green hat, revealing it was lined with steel. Wilfred recognized it as a light Japanese or Chinese helmet, torn from a trophy in the family hall.

"It was the first hat to hand," the Colonel explained airily. "Always the nearest hat—and the nearest woman."

"The blacksmith is away at Greenford," Wilfred said quietly. "His return time is uncertain."

Turning, he entered the church with a bowed head, crossing himself as if to exorcise an unclean spirit. He sought refuge in the cool twilight of the Gothic cloisters to forget such grossness, but fate ensured his morning of prayer would be interrupted by small shocks.

Inside, the church, usually empty at this hour, held a kneeling figure who rose hastily as the light flooded the doorway. Wilfred stopped, surprised. The early worshipper was the village idiot, the blacksmith's nephew, known only as "Mad Joe." A dark, strong lad with a heavy white face, straight dark hair, and a mouth perpetually open, Joe passed the priest without a hint of recognition. He had never been known to pray. What prayers was he reciting now?

Wilfred watched the idiot exit into the sunshine, then saw his dissolute brother hail him with jovial familiarity. The last thing Wilfred saw was the Colonel tossing pennies into Joe's open mouth, pretending to try and hit it.

This image of earthly stupidity and cruelty drove the ascetic to his prayers for purification. He climbed to a gallery pew beneath a beloved blue window depicting an angel with lilies. Here, he tried to push from his mind the half-wit's fish-like mouth and his brother's lion-like hunger, sinking deeper into the cold, sweet colors of silver blossoms and sapphire sky.

Half an hour later, Gibbs, the village cobbler, found him. Gibbs, an atheist whose presence in church was as extraordinary as Mad Joe's, spoke in a tone startlingly respectful.

"You must excuse me, sir," Gibbs whispered hoarsely. "We thought it right to inform you at once. A dreadful thing has happened, sir. I'm afraid your brother..."

Wilfred clenched his frail hands. "What devilry has he done now?"

"Why, sir," Gibbs coughed, "I'm afraid he's done nothing, and won't do anything. I'm afraid he's done for. You had better come down, sir."

Wilfred followed Gibbs down a winding stair to the yard of the smithy. In one glance, he saw the tragedy spread out like a map. Five or six men in black stood near an inspector, including the doctor, the Presbyterian minister, and a Roman Catholic priest. Near a bench, a magnificent woman with red-gold hair sobbed blindly. Between them, clear of the main pile of hammers, lay a man in evening dress, spread-eagled and flat on his face. From above, Wilfred could identify every detail of the Colonel's costume, down to the Bohun rings on his fingers, but the skull was merely a hideous splash of blackness and blood.

Wilfred ran down the steps. The doctor, the family physician, saluted him but received no acknowledgment. "My brother is dead," Wilfred stammered. "What does it mean? What is this horrible mystery?"

An unhappy silence fell before Gibbs, the most outspoken, answered. "Plenty of horror, sir, but not much mystery."

"What do you mean?" Wilfred asked, his face pale.

"It's plain enough," Gibbs said. "There is only one man for forty miles who could have struck such a blow, and he is the man who had most reason to."

"We must not prejudge anything," the doctor interjected nervously. "But I can corroborate Gibbs regarding the nature of the blow. It is incredible. Fragments of bone were driven into the body and the ground like bullets into a mud wall. It was the hand of a giant."

The doctor added grimly, "It has one advantage: it clears most people of suspicion. If you or I were accused, we'd be acquitted as an infant stealing the Nelson Column."

"That's what I say," Gibbs insisted. "Where's Simeon Barnes, the blacksmith?"

"He's at Greenford," the curate faltered.

"More likely in France," Gibbs muttered.

"No," a small, colorless voice came from the Roman priest. "He is coming up the road at this moment."

Indeed, walking with a huge stride and a hammer on his shoulder was Simeon the smith. A bony, gigantic man with deep, dark eyes and a dark beard, he walked easily with two companions.

"My God!" cried the atheistic cobbler. "And there's the hammer he did it with."

"No," said the inspector, a sensible man with a sandy moustache. "That hammer lies by the church wall. We have left it and the body exactly as they are."

All glanced around. The short priest looked down at the tool. It was one of the smallest, lightest hammers, unremarkable among the rest, yet its iron edge bore blood and yellow hair.

"There is at least the mystery of why such a big man would use such a small hammer," the priest noted.

"Oh, never mind that," Gibbs cried in a fever. "What are we to do with Simeon Barnes?"

"Leave him alone," the priest said quietly. "He is coming of his own accord."

As the tall smith swung around the corner and strode into the yard, he stood still, and the hammer fell from his hand. The inspector approached immediately.

"I won't ask you, Mr. Barnes, if you know anything about this. You are not bound to say. I hope you don't know, and that you can prove it. But I must arrest you in the King's name for the murder of Colonel Norman Bohun."

"You are not bound to say anything," the cobbler chimed in excitedly. "They've got to prove everything. They haven't proved it's Colonel Bohun."

"That won't wash," the doctor whispered to the priest. "I was his medical man. He had peculiar hands; the second and third fingers were the same length. That's the colonel right enough."

As the iron eyes of the motionless blacksmith rested on the corpse, the smith spoke calmly. "Is Colonel Bohun dead? Then he's damned."

"Don't say anything!" the cobbler danced in ecstasy of the legal system.

The blacksmith turned on him with the face of a fanatic. "It's well for you infidels to dodge like foxes because the world's law favors you... but God guards His own in His pocket." He pointed to the corpse. "When did this dog die in his sins?"

"I saw him alive at six o'clock this morning," Wilfred stammered.

"God is good," the smith said. "Inspector, I have no objection to being arrested. It is you who may object. I don't mind leaving the court without a stain on my character. You do mind perhaps leaving with a bad setback."

The inspector looked at the blacksmith with a lively eye. The smith continued with ponderous lucidity. "Two men in Greenford will swear they saw me from midnight till daybreak in the Revival Mission. Twenty people in Greenford can vouch for me. If I were a heathen, I'd let you walk to your downfall. But as a Christian, I ask: will you hear my alibi now or in court?"

The inspector seemed disturbed. "Of course I should be glad to clear you."

The smith walked out, returned to his friends, and spoke a few words that no one doubted. With this, Simeon's innocence stood as solid as the great church above.

One silence struck the group, more insufferable than any speech. To break it, Wilfred asked the Catholic priest, "You seem very much interested in that hammer, Father Brown."

"Yes, I am," Father Brown said. "Why is it such a small hammer?"

The doctor swung around. "By George, that's true! Who would use a little hammer with ten larger ones lying about?" He leaned in to the curate. "Only the kind of person who can't lift a large hammer. It's a question of lifting power in the shoulders. A bold woman could commit ten murders with a light hammer and never turn a hair. She could not kill a beetle with a heavy one."

Wilfred stared with hypnotized horror. Father Brown listened attentively. The doctor continued, "Why do these idiots assume the only person who hates the wife's lover is the husband? Nine times out of ten, it is the wife. Who knows what insolence he showed her?" He gestured to the red-haired woman on the bench. Her eyes were fixed on the corpse with an electric glare of idiocy.

Wilfred waved away the desire to know more, but Father Brown, dusting ash from his sleeve, spoke indifferently. "You are like so many doctors; your mental science is suggestive, but your physical science is impossible. I agree the woman wants to kill the co-respondent more than the petitioner. And I agree a woman will pick up a small hammer. But the difficulty is physical impossibility. No woman ever born could smash a man's skull out flat like that."

He paused reflectively. "These people haven't grasped the whole of it. The man was wearing an iron helmet, and the blow scattered it like broken glass. Look at that woman. Look at her arms."
