To truly comprehend the sanctity of the final rites, one must witness them performed not merely as duty, but as a desperate anchor against the void of mortality. In the chamber of death, Gerard was never more fully a priest than when he tended the departing soul of his beloved, executing the last offices of the Church with the same mechanical precision he would have applied to the smallest parishioner. Yet, once the rituals concluded, the machinery of faith ground to a halt. He sat, stupefied by the sudden, tremendous blow, paralyzed by a grief he had not yet recognized as such. Only then, in a transport of religious fervor born of sheer necessity, did he kneel to thank Heaven for her Christian end. His singular, consuming thought became immediate extraction: to remove her from the gaze of strangers and inter her within the sacred soil of his own churchyard.

That very evening, a covered cart drawn by a single horse departed for Gouda, carrying a coffin and a broken-hearted man whose arms and chin rested upon the cold wood. The mourner's short-lived energy had been entirely expended in the arduous preparations; now, he lay crushed, clinging to the leaden box that held her remains. The driver, a silent figure of routine, walked beside the horse's head, offering no comfort, speaking only in whispers when he baited the animal, respectful of the mute agony radiating from the passenger. When they halted for the night, the landlord and the driver made a well-meaning attempt to coax the mourner away for rest and sustenance. But Gerard repulsed them, snarling like a faithful dog guarding its burden, refusing to be parted from the cold lead even as sleep claimed him. They were forced to draw a cloak over him and leave him in peace.

At noon the following day, the sorrowful cart arrived at the manse, greeted by a score of parishioners gathered with their trivial domestic troubles momentarily suspended. They had missed the parson already, sensing a shift in the spiritual atmosphere. Upon seeing the scene—their healer so utterly stricken—they raised a loud wail of grief. This collective sound pierced the lethargy of his woe, rousing him from his stupor. He looked around, recognizing the familiar faces, and tried to speak to them, crying out, "Oh, my children! My children!" but choked on the rawness of his anguish, unable to form further words.

Yet, despite all remonstrances, the next day he buried her himself. He read the service with a voice that trembled only occasionally, many tears falling upon the fresh earth of her grave. When the service ended, he remained standing like a statue amidst the silent congregation, who left the churchyard out of profound respect. He stood thus, lost in a waking dream, until the sexton—a man of common sense and limited wisdom—began to fill in the grave without due warning. At the sound of earth falling upon her, Gerard uttered a piercing scream. The sexton forbore from finishing his work. Gerard staggered, clutching his breast, and the sexton supported him, calling loudly for aid. Jorian Ketel, who had lingered nearby mourning his benefactress, ran into the churchyard. Together, the two men supported the shattered priest back to the manse. "Ah, Jorian! Good Jorian!" he gasped, his voice breaking. "Something snapped within me; I felt it, and I heard it; here, Jorian, here;" and he pressed his hand firmly against his heart.
