On the eighth day following that unforgettable night, Romola stood upon the precipice of the Mediterranean Sea. Before her stretched the gentle, rhythmic pulse of the summer waves, just beyond the modest cluster of houses that constituted the fishing village of Viareggio. Once more, she had fled from Florence. This time, however, no commanding voice had summoned her return. Clad once again in the grey habit of a religious sister, she felt no compunction regarding the disguise; indeed, in the depths of her heart-sickness, she scarcely cared for appearances.

A fresh rebellion had ignited within her spirit, accompanied by a profound despair. *Why* should she concern herself with bearing one symbol of identity over another, or with being addressed by her own name? She had lost faith in the possibility of finding any consistent duty attached to that name. Where was the force capable of creating that sacred motive men call "duty"—a power that possesses no internal binding strength unless rooted in some form of believing love? Every tie of strong affection had been severed. In her marriage, the highest of all bonds, she could no longer perceive the mystical union that guarantees indissolubility, nor even the obligation of a voluntary pledge. Had she not demonstrated that the very things to which she had bound herself were impossible?

The impulse to liberate herself surged forth with overwhelming force, yet she recognized that this freedom offered only an exchange of calamity. There is no recompense for a woman who perceives the chief relationship of her life as nothing more than a grievous error. She had lost her crown. The deepest secret of human blessedness had half-whispered its truth to her, only to slip past her forever. Now, her greatest support amidst such supreme sorrow had vanished. The vision of any grand purpose, any end of existence capable of ennobling endurance and elevating the mundane labors of daily life with divine ardor, was completely eclipsed. All effort now seemed merely a futile dragging at tangled threads; every fellowship, whether for resistance or advocacy, appeared as mere unfairness and exclusion.

What, after all, was the man who had embodied for her the highest form of heroism—not the stoic endurance of the isolated warrior, but the willing, self-offering love of the saint? What was the cause for which he struggled? Romola had lost her trust in Savonarola. She had lost that fervent admiration that had previously rendered her blind to his aberrations, allowing her to focus solely on the majestic arc of his destiny. Now, as her deep affection for her godfather cast her into direct opposition with the Friar, she viewed the repulsive and contradictory details of his teachings with a painful clarity that magnified their flaws.

In the bitterness of her disillusionment, she declared that his struggle to renovate the Church and the world was a pursuit of a mere name—a title that signified nothing more than the practical measures designed to strengthen his own position in Florence. Indeed, these included questionable deeds and words undertaken merely to shield his influence from the consequences of his own mistakes. That political reform, which had once sparked a new passion in her soul, now reduced itself to narrow schemes for the safety of Florence, standing in contemptible contradiction to his alternating professions of blind trust in Divine providence.

It was inevitable that she should judge the Friar unjustly on matters of individual suffering, viewing them through the lens of personal tenderness, while he regarded them through the cold eyes of theoretical conviction. When he proclaimed that the cause of his party was the cause of God's Kingdom, she heard only the hollow ring of egoism. Perhaps such words are rarely spoken without that baser tone; yet, they remain the implicit formula of all energetic belief. If such belief, pursuing a grand and distant goal, frequently risks descending into demon-worship—where the devotee allows his children to pass through the fire with a readiness that barely resembles sacrifice—then tender compassion for the near and dear holds its own dangers, tending toward timidity and skepticism regarding the larger aims without which life cannot ascend to true religion. Thus, poor Romola was being blinded by her tears.

No one who has ever experienced the loss of faith in a fellow-man whom they have profoundly loved and revered will lightly claim that such a shock can leave the faith in the Invisible Goodness unshaken. As the high trust in humanity sinks, the dignity of life diminishes accordingly. We cease to believe in our own better selves, for that too is part of the common nature degraded in our thoughts; consequently, all the finer impulses of the soul grow dull. Romola felt even the springs of her once-active pity drying up, leaving her to barren, egoistic complaint. *Had not she suffered her own sorrows?* Few had cared for her while she had cared for many. She had done enough; she had striven for the impossible and was weary of this stifling, crowded existence. She longed for that repose in mere sensation she had occasionally dreamed of during the sultry afternoons of her early girlhood, when she imagined herself floating naively in the waters like a nymph.

The clear waves seemed to invite her. She wished she could lie down upon them and drift from sleep into death. Yet, Romola could not directly seek death; the fullness of young life within her forbade such a direct act. She could only wish that death would come to her. At the spot where she had paused, the shoreline curved deeply, and a small boat with a sail was moored there. In her longing to glide across the waters turning golden under the level rays of the setting sun, she recalled a tale from Boccaccio, one she had loved to linger over when her father slept and she crept from her stool to the floor to read the *Decameron*. It was the story of the beautiful Gostanza, who, in her lovesick despair, desired no further life but lacked the courage to end her youth. She had placed herself in a boat, pushed off to sea, and lain down, wrapping her mantle around her head, hoping to be wrecked, so that her fear would be powerless to flee from death.

That memory had remained a mere thought in Romola's mind, never blossoming into a distinct wish. But now, as she paused again in her pacing to and fro, she saw another boat gliding black against the red-gold horizon, carrying a single man toward the bend where the first, smaller vessel was moored. Walking forward, she eventually saw the man land, haul his boat ashore, and begin to unload cargo. He was likely the owner of the smaller boat as well; he would be departing soon, and her opportunity would vanish with him—her chance to purchase that small craft. She had not yet admitted to herself that she intended to use it, but she felt a sudden eagerness to secure the possibility of doing so, revealing the half-unconscious growth of a thought into a desire.

"Is that little boat yours also?" she asked the fisherman, who had looked up, slightly startled by the tall grey figure, and made a reverent bow to this holy Sister wandering mysteriously in the evening solitude.

"It is my boat," he replied. It was an old vessel, hardly seaworthy, yet valuable to anyone willing to repair it. Through the blessing of Saint Anthony, whose chapel stood in the village nearby, his fishing had prospered, and he now possessed a better boat, formerly owned by Gianni, who had died. However, he had not yet sold the old one.

Romola asked him its value. While he was occupied, she thrust the sum into a small satchel lying on the ground, which contained the remnants of his dinner. Afterwards, she watched him furled his sail and asked him how to set it if he wished to venture out to sea. Pacing up and down again, she waited until he departed.

The imagination of herself gliding away in that boat upon the darkening waters grew increasingly into a longing, much as the thought of a cool stream in oppressive heat becomes a painful thirst. To be freed from the burden of choice when all motives were bruised; to commit herself, in a state of sleep, to a destiny that would either bring death or new necessities capable of awakening a new life within her—it was a thought that beckoned her the more because the soft evening air made her yearn to rest in the still solitude, rather than returning to the noise and heat of the village.

Finally, the slow-moving fisherman gathered his belongings and walked away. Soon, the gold of the sunset shrank and faded into dusk in the sea and sky. There was no living creature in sight, no sound but the lulling monotony of the lapping waves. In this sea, there was no tide strong enough to carry her away if she waited for its ebb; yet Romola believed the breeze from the land was rising slightly.

She entered the boat, unfurled the sail, and fastened it as she had learned in that first brief lesson. She saw that it caught the light breeze, and that was all she required. Loosening the boat from its moorings, she urged it forward with an oar until she was far out from the land, until the sea turned dark even to the west, and the stars began to disclose themselves like a palpitating life over the vast heavens.

Resting at last, she threw back her cowl and removed the kerchief beneath, which had confined her hair. She doubled both under her head for a pillow against one of the boat's ribs. Her fair head was still very young and could endure a hard cushion. Thus she lay, with the soft night air breathing upon her as she drifted on the water, watching the deepening quiet of the sky.
