Anyone who has wandered through the quiet, mist-laden counties of eastern England knows the kind of manor houses that dot the landscape—modest, often damp, built in the Italian style, with stuccoed façades and sprawling parks of eighty to a hundred acres. These are the sort I have always found compelling: grey oak palings, ancient trees, reed-fringed ponds, distant woodlands stretching into the haze. I love the pillared portico—sometimes clumsily tacked onto a Queen Anne brick house, now masked in plaster to suit the Grecian fashion of the late eighteenth century. Inside, the hall rises to the roof, ideally with a gallery and a small organ. The library holds treasures: a thirteenth-century Psalter beside a Shakespeare quarto. And then there are the paintings—the ghosts of past lives, preserved on canvas.

But most of all, I like imagining what life was like when such houses were first built—when landowners ruled with ease and pride, before money became scarce but taste more refined. I dream of owning one myself, with enough wealth to keep it standing and guests entertained in modest comfort.

Yet this is not a tale of dreams. It is about Castringham Hall in Suffolk—a place that once stood much as I’ve described it. Though time has altered its form, the essentials remain: the Italian portico, the white square block of the house, older within than without; the park edged with woods, and the still pond. One feature, however, is gone. Once, from the park, you could see a great old ash-tree growing just yards from the wall, its branches brushing the very side of the building. It had stood there since Castringham ceased to be a fortress, since the moat filled in and the Elizabethan house rose. By 1690, it had reached full maturity.

That year, the region was gripped by witch trials. We may never truly understand the roots of that fear—whether witches believed themselves powerful, whether they meant harm, or whether confessions were wrung out by cruelty. But this story makes me hesitate. I cannot dismiss it as mere fiction. You must judge for yourself.

Castringham contributed its own victim: Mrs. Mothersole. She was no common village crone—she was better off, more respected. Several farmers tried to save her, testifying to her character, pleading with the jury. But her fate was sealed by Sir Matthew Fell, then proprietor of Castringham Hall and Deputy-Sheriff.

He claimed to have seen her three times at moonrise, climbing into the ash-tree near his house. Each night, he said, she wore only her shift, cutting twigs with a curved knife, muttering to herself. When he tried to capture her, she fled—only a hare ever crossed his path. On the third night, he chased her to her home. He pounded on her door for fifteen minutes before she emerged—cross, sleepy, as if pulled from bed. He had no explanation for his visit.

Her conviction rested largely on his testimony, though others added their accounts. Mrs. Mothersole was hanged at Bury St Edmunds a week later, alongside five others. Sir Matthew attended the execution.

It was a cold, drizzly March morning. The cart climbed the rough grass hill outside Northgate where the gallows stood. The other victims were broken, silent. But Mrs. Mothersole—alive with fury—was something else entirely. A contemporary report called her “poysonous Rage” so terrifying that even the hangman shuddered. She did not resist, but her eyes—fixed on those who seized her—were said to burn with venom. One officer confessed he carried the memory of her gaze in his mind for six months.

And yet, all she spoke was a single phrase—repeated under her breath: *“There will be guests at the Hall.”*

Sir Matthew was unsettled by her presence. After the assize, he rode home with the parish vicar, Mr. Crome, and discussed the trial. He admitted he hadn’t wanted to testify, but insisted he saw what he claimed—nothing else made sense. He regretted the deed, but felt duty compelled him. The vicar agreed—he was a reasonable man, after all.

A few weeks later, in May, the two men met again in the park. Lady Fell was away visiting her ill mother; Sir Matthew was alone. Mr. Crome stayed for supper.

That evening, Sir Matthew was quiet. Conversation drifted between family matters and parish business. At nine-thirty, they walked along the gravel path behind the house. As they passed the ash-tree, Sir Matthew stopped abruptly.

“What is that running up and down the trunk?” he asked. “Not a squirrel? They’re all in their nests by now.”

Mr. Crome looked. In the moonlight, he saw a shadowy shape, moving swiftly. He couldn’t make out its color, but the outline—sharp, fleeting—stayed burned in his mind. He would swear, though it seemed absurd, that it had more than four legs.

They parted soon after. They didn’t meet again for decades.

The next morning, Sir Matthew did not rise. Not at six, nor seven, nor eight. The servants knocked, listened, battered the door. Eventually, it opened from the outside. They found him dead—blackened, swollen. No signs of violence, but the window was open.

One servant ran for the parson; another rode to alert the coroner. Mr. Crome arrived quickly, guided to the chamber. Among his papers, he left notes describing the scene—and the dread that followed.

“There was no sign of forced entry,” he wrote. “The casement stood open, as my friend always kept it. His silver cup of small ale sat untouched. The physician from Bury, Mr. Hodgkins, examined it—found no poison. Yet the corpse was twisted, contorted, as if death came in agony. And worse—two women tasked with washing the body screamed in pain upon touching his chest. Their hands swelled violently, arms inflamed, though no mark appeared on the skin.”

Crome summoned the doctor. Together, they examined the skin under a crystal lens. They found only two tiny punctures—small, almost invisible. They recalled the tales of Italian poisoners, the infamous ring of Pope Borgia. Perhaps this was a similar art.

Then, on the bedside table, lay a small Bible—Sir Matthew’s nightly reading. Moved, Crome picked it up. In moments of despair, we clutch at strange hopes. He thought of the *Sortes Biblicæ*—the practice of opening Scripture at random to find divine guidance. He remembered how King Charles and Lord Falkland had used it in crisis.

So he tried it. Three times.

First: Luke xiii. 7 — _Cut it down._

Second: Isaiah xiii. 20 — _It shall never be inhabited._

Third: Job xxxix. 30 — _Her young ones also suck up blood._

He recorded it all. Sir Matthew was buried. The vicar preached a sermon titled *“The Unsearchable Way; or, England’s Danger and the Malicious Dealings of Antichrist”*, believing the squire had fallen victim to a revived Popish Plot.

His son, Sir Matthew the second, inherited the estate. He avoided the West Chamber—no one slept there again. The room remained untouched for decades. He died in 1735, leaving behind only a curious pattern: livestock across the farm began dying in unnatural ways. Cattle, sheep, poultry—all succumbed to a sickness with no clear cause. Farmers called it the “Castringham sickness.”

Eventually, he solved it: he confined animals indoors at night. Only those outdoors fell ill. The affliction shifted to wild birds and game. But no one ever saw the source. No one ever understood.

Sir Richard, the third baronet, took over in 1735. He was a man of grand designs. He rebuilt Castringham Hall—stuccoing over the red brick, adding Roman marbles, constructing a replica of the Sibyl’s temple on the far side of the pond. The house became colder, more imposing, less welcoming.

In 1754, he woke exhausted. The night had been stormy—chimney smoke, rattling windows, bitter cold. Worse, guests were arriving—high-ranking men and ladies expected sport. But his own health was failing. He could not sleep in that room again.

At breakfast, he began searching for a new chamber. Nothing suited. Until the housekeeper said, “There’s only one room like that—Sir Matthew’s West Chamber.”

“Then put me there tonight,” he declared. “Open the door.”

She hesitated. “No one has slept there in forty years. The air hasn’t changed since he died.”

“Open it,” he commanded.

The door creaked. The room smelled of damp earth and forgotten things. Sir Richard flung open the shutters and casement. The wind rushed in.

“Air it all day,” he ordered. “Move my things in this afternoon. Put the Bishop in my old room.”

As he turned, a voice spoke: “Might I have a moment?”

A man in black stood in the doorway. “I am William Crome. My grandfather was vicar here in your grandfather’s time.”

Sir Richard smiled. “The name Crome opens doors at Castringham. What can I do for you?”

“I’m riding from Norwich to Bury. I bring papers—my grandfather’s writings. You may find them of interest.”

They went to the study. Among the documents was the vicar’s account of Sir Matthew’s death—and the *Sortes Biblicæ*. Sir Richard read them aloud, amused.

“Well,” he said, “_Cut it down_. If that means the tree, I’ll do it tomorrow. Such a nest of catarrhs and agues!”

He glanced at the bookshelf. “I wonder… is the old prophet still there?”

He pulled out a small Bible. On the flyleaf: *“To Matthew Fell, from his Loving Godmother, Anne Aldous, 2 September, 1659.”*

“Let’s test it,” he said. “I’ll wager we get names from the Chronicles.”

He opened it at random. “‘Thou shalt seek me in the morning, and I shall not be.’” He laughed. “Your grandfather would have made a fine omen of that. No prophets for me! They’re all in a tale.”

He offered wine. They parted amicably.

That afternoon, guests arrived—the Bishop of Kilmore, Lady Mary Hervey, Sir William Kentfield. Dinner, wine, cards, supper. Then to bed.

Next morning, Sir Richard refused to hunt. He walked with the bishop along the terrace.

“You could never get one of my Irish flock to sleep in that room,” the bishop said, pointing to the West Chamber.

“Why not?”

“Our people believe sleeping near an ash-tree brings bad luck. Yours grows just yards from your window.”

“Perhaps it’s already touched me,” Sir Richard replied. “I didn’t sleep from midnight to four.”

“The tree comes down tomorrow. I won’t hear from it again.”

“Good,” said the bishop. “Though I doubt the noise came from the branches. See—none touch your window. Even in a gale, they miss the glass by a foot.”

Sir Richard frowned. “Then what was it?”

The bishop smiled faintly. “Only the wind, perhaps. Or something else.”

But the wind had been still. And the tree—its leaves whispering secrets—had not stirred.
