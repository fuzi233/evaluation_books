Attached to the manuscript that follows is a note penned by Doctor Hesselius, a man of rare intellect and deep scholarship. In it, he refers to his Essay on the unsettling subject this narrative reveals—a topic he explores with precision, insight, and an unflinching clarity that marks his work as both extraordinary and chilling. That Essay, though dense with erudition, will one day form part of the collected writings of this remarkable thinker. Yet here, in this volume, my purpose is not academic rigor but to stir the imagination of ordinary readers. I wish only to present the story as it was told—without commentary, without summary, without the weight of learned interpretation.

Thus, I have chosen not to include any distillation of the doctor’s arguments or excerpts from his treatise on what he calls “a matter likely rooted in the deepest mysteries of our dual nature, and the shadows between life and death.” To do so might dull the horror, obscure the truth, or betray the raw power of the tale itself.

When I first came upon this manuscript, I was eager to reconnect with the woman who wrote it—her mind sharp, her account meticulous, her voice compelling. I hoped to learn more, to hear her thoughts beyond the pages. But alas, she had passed away long before I found her words. She would have added little, I suspect, to the story already laid bare. For what she has written stands complete: vivid, haunting, and unshakable in its detail. It is, as far as I can judge, a record delivered with honesty and care—an account that speaks for itself, needing no explanation, no defense, only to be read.
