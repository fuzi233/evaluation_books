I’ve spent two whole days puzzling over how to kick this story off proper. First I tried scratching my head till my scalp stung — got nothing out of that but dandruff. Then I fetched my daughter Penelope in to give me a hand, and that’s where we hit on a plan.

Penelope swears the trick to a true story is writing it day by day, pinned hard to exact dates, starting the very day we got word Mr. Franklin Blake was coming to stay. Once you force your memory to tie itself to a calendar, it drags up more odds and ends than a retriever rooting through a bramble bush. The only trouble is digging up those dates in the first place — which Penelope says she’ll handle, thanks to the diary she’s kept ever since her schoolmistress made the whole class write one, decades back.

When I suggested, brilliant as you like, that she just tell the whole story out of her diary herself, she turned bright red, looked at me like I’d suggested she run naked through the village, and snapped that not a living soul ever gets to read a line of her private journal. “Fiddlesticks!” she huffed, when I asked what on earth she could possibly be hiding in there. I said, “Ah — sweethearts, is it?”

So we’re sticking to the plan, and I open on Wednesday morning, the 24th of May, 1848, when Lady Verinder called me into her private sitting room.

“Gabriel,” she said, “I’ve news that’ll surprise you. Franklin Blake is back from abroad. He’s been staying with his father in London, and he’ll be here tomorrow to visit, right up till next month — for Rachel’s birthday.”

If I’d been holding a hat in my hand, nothing but 30 years of loyal service would’ve stopped me flinging it clean up to the ceiling. I hadn’t seen Mr. Franklin since he was a scruffy little boy running wild in this very house. Far as I remembered, he was the nicest lad that ever spun a top till it hummed, or broke a drawing room window chasing a stray cricket ball. Miss Rachel, who was leaning against the window sill while I said that, sniffed loud enough to rattle the teacups. *She* remembered him as the worst tyrant that ever lived, who tortured her best doll by pulling all its hair out, and dragged her around the garden till her little legs gave out harnessed to a string like a cart pony. “I still burn with indignation and ache with fatigue just thinking about it,” she declared.

You’ll be wondering, of course, how it came to pass that Mr. Franklin spent all the years between boyhood and manhood roving abroad, never coming home. The short of it is this: his father, Lady Verinder’s eldest sister’s husband, was the famous Mr. Blake — equally famous for his mountains of money, and his lifelong obsessive lawsuit to prove he was the rightful heir to a dukedom he’d never seen. For decades he badgered every court in the land, lined a hundred lawyers’ pockets thick as your thumb, and set half the gentry of England at each other’s throats arguing whether his claim was valid or not. His wife died, two of his three children died, before the courts finally told him to get out and stop wasting their time. When it was all over, and the sitting duke kept his title, Mr. Blake decided the only way to get even with his country was to deny it the honour of raising his only surviving son. “Why would I trust my nation’s institutions,” he raged, “after the way they’ve treated me?” Add to that the fact that he hated all boys, his own included, and you won’t be surprised that the end result was nine-year-old Franklin being shipped off to boarding schools in Germany, while his father stayed nice and warm in England, making speeches in Parliament and writing an unfinished pamphlet railing against the duke that’s still gathering dust somewhere to this day.

Thank God that bit’s done with. We don’t need to waste another thought on old Mr. Blake and his dukedom. Leave him to it. Our business is the diamond — and the diamond leads us straight back to Mr. Franklin, the innocent soul who brought that accursed gem through our front door.

He never forgot us after he left, that lad. He sent letters every now and then, sometimes to Lady Verinder, sometimes to Miss Rachel, sometimes to me. We had an arrangement before he sailed, you see: he borrowed a ball of twine from me, my four-blade pocket knife, and seven shillings and sixpence in cash. I never saw that money again, and I never expected to. His letters to me were mostly cheerful little notes asking to borrow more. Lady Verinder kept me up to date on how he grew: after Germany, he studied in France, then Italy, and the three countries together turned him into a sort of universal jack-of-all-trades. He wrote a little, painted a little, sang and played the piano and composed a little — no doubt borrowing supplies and money for all of it, same as he borrowed from me. When he turned 21, his mother’s fortune of seven hundred a year fell into his hands, and ran through it like water through a sieve. There was a hole in that man’s pocket no tailor on earth could’ve sewn shut. But everyone loved him, wherever he went, for his easy, bright way of talking. He called his address “Post Office, Europe — left till called for.” Twice he booked passage back to England to visit us, and twice some unmentionable woman distracted him and stopped him coming. His third attempt worked, as you already heard. On Thursday the 25th of May, we’d finally get to see what that scruffy little boy had grown into. He was 25 years old, by our count, good blood in his veins, and full of fire. Now you know as much about Mr. Franklin Blake as I did, the day before he turned up at the house.

That Thursday was the finest summer day you could hope for: blue sky, warm breeze, not a drop of rain in sight. Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel, not expecting Mr. Franklin till dinner, drove out to lunch with friends three miles off. Once they were gone, I double checked the guest room we’d got ready for him, made sure the sheets were aired and the fire was laid just right. Then, as butler and steward (I took both jobs, mind, because I’d never trust anyone else to hold the key to old Sir John’s legendary cellar), I fetched up a bottle of our finest vintage Latour claret and set it out in the sun to lose its cellar chill before dinner. Figuring what’s good for old French wine is good for an old butler, too, I grabbed my wicker beehive chair to go sit in the sunny back court — when I heard a soft, steady drum-tap coming from the front terrace.

I rounded the corner, and found three deep mahogany-skinned Indian men, dressed all in white linen frocks and trousers, staring up at the house. Each had a tiny hand drum slung over his chest. A little pale, delicate English boy with fair hair stood behind them, holding a canvas bag full of their props. I pegged them straight away as strolling conjurors, the kind that travel the countryside doing tricks for coin. The man at the front, who spoke perfect English and had manners finer than most lords I’ve waited on, confirmed it, and asked permission to perform his tricks for Lady Verinder.

I’m no sour old man, and I don’t distrust anyone just because their skin’s a darker shade than mine. But even the best of us have weaknesses, and mine is this: whenever I know the family silver basket is sitting out on the pantry table, any stranger with manners nicer than mine sets every alarm bell ringing in my head. So I told the chief Indian the lady of the house was out, and politely but firmly showed all four of them off the grounds. He bowed so low his turban almost touched the paving stones, and they left.

I trundled back to my chair, sat down in the sun, and was just about to nod off into a very pleasant doze, when Penelope came running at me like the whole west wing was on fire.

“Get the constables!” she gasped. “Those Indian jugglers mean to do Mr. Franklin Blake harm!”

That name jolted me wide awake. I made her slow down and explain.

It turned out she’d been gossiping with the lodge keeper’s daughter, and the two of them had seen the Indians leave the grounds, then stop on the other side of the hedge that lines our boundary. They’d got it into their heads the little English boy was being abused by the foreigners (for no reason I could fathom other than that he was small and fair and looked soft), so they crept along the inside of the hedge to spy. What they saw was no ordinary juggler’s rehearsal.

First the three Indians checked the road up and down, made sure not a soul was in sight. Then they all turned and stared hard in the direction of the house, jabbering away fast in their own language, looking confused. Then they turned to the little boy, like he was the only one who could help them. The chief Indian, the one who spoke English, said: “Hold out your hand.”

Penelope said her heart nearly jumped right out of her chest right then. I privately thought it was more likely her stays, but all I said out loud was “You make my flesh creep.” (A little compliment like that never goes amiss with a woman, mark my words.)

The boy shrank back, shook his head, said he didn’t want to. The Indian didn’t get angry, though. He just asked him if he’d rather be sent back to London, left in the market where they’d found him, asleep in an empty wicker basket, hungry and ragged and no one to care for him. That was enough. The little boy held out his palm, trembling.

The chief pulled a small glass bottle out of his tunic, poured a pool of thick black ink, dark as ink, right into the hollow of the boy’s hand. He traced strange signs in the air above the boy’s head, touched his hair, and said: “Look.”

The boy went stiff as a statue, his eyes fixed dead on the ink pooled in his palm.

Up to that point, it just looked like silly hocus pocus to me, a waste of good ink, and I was already nodding off again — till Penelope’s next words jolted me upright.

The Indians checked the road again. The chief spoke to the boy: “See the English gentleman who comes from foreign parts?”

The boy said, very quiet: “I see him.”

“Is he travelling on no road but the road to this house, today?” the Indian asked.

The boy said: “He travels on no road but the road to this house.”

The Indian paused, and asked the next question, slow and clear. “Does the English gentleman carry *It* on him?”

The boy paused too, then said: “Yes.”

One last question. “Will he arrive here, as he promised, at the end of the day?”

“I can’t tell,” the boy said. “The mist rises in my head now. I can see no more.”

The chief said something sharp to the other two Indians, pointed at the boy, then pointed at the inn in the nearby town where we later learned they were staying. He traced more signs over the boy’s forehead, blew on his face, and the boy woke up gasping, like he’d been pulled out of a dream. Then the four of them set off for the town, and the two girls saw no more of them.

Most things have a moral, if you look for it. My sensible, sober take on the whole business was this: first, the jugglers had overheard the outdoor servants gossiping about Mr. Franklin’s upcoming visit, and cooked up a magic trick to perform for Lady Verinder when she got home, to make a few extra shillings. Second, they were just rehearsing their lines out by the hedge, same as actors before a play. Third, I’d be wise to keep an extra eye on the silver basket that evening. Fourth, Penelope ought to calm down and let her old dad get back to his sun nap.

But Penelope would have none of it. She was deadly serious. “Oh father,” she said, clutching my arm, “don’t you dare joke about this. What is *It*? What were they asking if he had on him?”

“We’ll ask Mr. Franklin when he gets here, my dear,” I said, winking to show I was teasing. But she took it dead seriously. “Ask him!” she said. “And see if he thinks it’s a laughing matter, too.” Then off she stormed.

After she left, I decided I really would ask Mr. Franklin, just to set her mind at rest. But before we go any further, I’d best warn you: you won’t find a single joke in that conversation later that day. To my absolute shock, Mr. Franklin took the whole business far more seriously than Penelope ever did.

You’ll understand how seriously, when I tell you — he said the “It” those Indian conjurors were asking after, was none other than the Moonstone.
