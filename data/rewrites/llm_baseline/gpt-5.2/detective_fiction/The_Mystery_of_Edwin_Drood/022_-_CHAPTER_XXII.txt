Mr. Tartar’s chambers had the wholesome severity of a well-found ship where every rope has its place and every man his duty. Even before one crossed his threshold, the house itself seemed to listen and then subside; the corridor’s fuss of footsteps and doors became, within those rooms, a softened, better-governed sound—as if the very air had been trained not to clatter. The floor-boards were scrubbed to that pale, honest cleanness that smells faintly of soap and warm wood, and the brass—hinges, knobs, little fittings you would never think to admire—answered the sun with a steady, disciplined gleam. It was not the glitter of ostentation; it was the shine of care repeated daily until it became a kind of language.

Everything had quarters. Charts lay flat and obedient in their drawers. Books stood in line like a watch relieved. Brushes, boots, clothes, bottles in their cases, instruments of glass and metal—each was stowed so that a hand could find it without searching, and so that nothing had to scrape against anything else. Even the room’s small noises were arranged: a drawer ran out without a squeal; a latch met its plate without a snap. In the sitting-room—his “admiral’s cabin”—the stillness was of the sort that lets you hear a sparrow in the square outside, or the faint, far complaint of London’s wheels, and be thankful you are not inside their grinding.

His bath-room was clean as a dairy—cool, bright, and smelling of water and rubbed stone—and his sleeping-chamber, with its lockers and cunning little contrivances, looked like a seedsman’s shop turned nautical: everything sorted, everything labelled by habit if not by ink. In the midst of it, his narrow cot moved almost imperceptibly, as if breathing with the house.

On that bright summer day an awning was rigged over his strip of flower-garden with sailorly neatness—tight as a sail, angled to make a cool shadow—and the whole place had a sea-going air, as though the stern-windows of a vessel had somehow docked among the dingy gables and chimneypots of the law’s neighbourhood. The flowers, too, seemed to have been persuaded into order: their colours were vivid, but not riotous; their scents rose cleanly through the shade—sweet-pea and mignonette, a sharp pinch of crushed green where leaves had been handled, and beneath it all the faint salt suggestion of shells and seaweed from his curiosities.

Mr. Tartar, doing the honours, matched his rooms: sunburnt, hearty, exact, and amused by his own exactness. He guided Rosa through each contrivance with a half-laugh that made it charming rather than fussy—showing her how a locker sprang at a touch, how a bracket folded, how a space that looked too narrow suddenly became useful. If he had an indulgence, it was not for luxury but for good condition; he took pleasure in things that were kept right.

When the inspection ended, he drew back with a quick delicacy, as if remembering that even a friendly ship’s captain must know when to quit the cabin.

“Consider it your own,” he said, with a frank gravity that made it feel—absurdly—true. “You are Queen there.” And with the same hand that had once held Mr. Crisparkle’s life steady in rough water, he waved her free of his little garden.

Rosa, left among the leaves and blossoms, leaned nearer to the shade where the flowers made a private bower. She lifted her voice, not loud—London punished loud voices with echo and eavesdropping.

“Helena! Helena Landless! Are you there?”

“Who speaks to me? Not Rosa?” came the answer, and Helena’s second handsome face appeared, dark-eyed and bright, as if the day’s heat had only brought more life into her.

“Yes, my darling!”

“Why, how did you come here, dearest?”

“I—I don’t quite know,” Rosa said, colouring, with that blush that springs up like a sudden heat from the heart—“unless I am dreaming!”

Helena smiled. “I am not dreaming. I should take more for granted if I were. How do we come together—or so near together—so very unexpectedly?”

Unexpected indeed: here, among P. J. T.’s smoky connection, in a garden that smelled faintly of salt voyages and newly watered earth. Rosa, waking as she spoke, poured out in a hurried stream how she had come—what she had been made to hear, what she had been made to feel, what she had fled from. The telling, even between flowers, had the rawness of a wound uncovered. Yet the place itself, with its regulated quiet, seemed to hold her up; it was easier to speak where doors did not slam and floors did not creak a lie.

“And Mr. Crisparkle is here,” Rosa ended quickly; “and, could you believe it? long ago he saved his life!”

“I could believe any such thing of Mr. Crisparkle,” Helena returned, and for a moment her face warmed with a look that was more than gratitude.

“Yes, but it wasn’t Mr. Crisparkle,” Rosa broke in, eager to be exact. “It was very nice of Mr. Crisparkle to be saved, and he couldn’t have shown his high opinion of Mr. Tartar more expressively. But it was Mr. Tartar who saved him.”

Helena’s eyes steadied on Rosa’s face among the leaves. “Is Mr. Tartar with you now, dear?” she asked, slower.

“No; because he has given up his rooms to me—to us, I mean. It is such a beautiful place!”

“Is it?”

“It is like the inside of the most exquisite ship that ever sailed. It is like—it is like—”

“Like a dream?” Helena suggested.

Rosa nodded a little and, to hide her confusion, bent her face to the flowers and breathed their scent, as if smell could be a sort of courage.

After a pause—during which Helena, looking outward, seemed to soften as though pitying someone not present—she said, “My poor Neville is reading in his own room, the sun being so very bright on this side just now. I think he had better not know that you are so near.”

“Oh, I think so too!” Rosa cried readily, with a start as if she had heard Neville’s footstep through the wall.

“I suppose,” Helena pursued, doubtfully, “that he must know by-and-by all you have told me; but I am not sure. Ask Mr. Crisparkle’s advice, my darling. Ask him whether I may tell Neville as much or as little of what you have told me as I think best.”

Rosa slipped back into her “state-cabin”—for so the little inner room felt now—and propounded it. Mr. Crisparkle, grave and tender, left the decision to Helena’s judgment.

“I thank him very much,” Helena said when Rosa returned. “Ask him whether it would be best to wait until any more maligning and pursuing of Neville on the part of this wretch shall disclose itself, or to try to anticipate it: I mean, so far as to find out whether any such goes on darkly about us?”

That question, poised between prudence and danger, troubled Mr. Crisparkle. He tried to shape an answer, failed, tried again, and at last—honest enough to admit difficulty—suggested referring it to Mr. Grewgious.

Helena assented. Mr. Crisparkle crossed the quadrangle to P. J. T.’s with a most unsuccessful pretence of lounging indifference (for even his boots, in that place, sounded like a man on urgent business). He stated the case. Mr. Grewgious, concise as a clerk’s note and as firm as a closed ledger, held to the principle that if you can steal a march upon a brigand or a wild beast, you should do it; and he held, with equal decisiveness, that John Jasper was a brigand and a wild beast combined.

Thus armed, Mr. Crisparkle returned; Rosa carried the counsel back to Helena. Helena, still at her window, followed her own thread of thought as if she were tracing a seam in cloth.

“We may count on Mr. Tartar’s readiness to help us, Rosa?” she asked.

“Oh yes!” Rosa shyly thought so; shyly believed she could almost answer for it; and then, in her old habit of leaning on another’s authority, faltered about asking Mr. Crisparkle.

“I think your authority on the point as good as his, my dear,” Helena said sedately. “And you needn’t disappear again for that.” It was odd of Helena—oddly confident in Rosa’s power to judge a man.

“You see, Neville,” Helena continued after more reflection, “knows no one else here: he has not so much as exchanged a word with any one else here. If Mr. Tartar would call to see him openly and often; if he would spare a minute for the purpose, frequently; if he would even do so, almost daily; something might come of it.”

“Something might come of it, dear?” Rosa repeated, perplexed. “Something might?”

“If Neville’s movements are really watched, and if the purpose really is to isolate him from all friends and acquaintance and wear his daily life out grain by grain—which would seem to be the threat to you—does it not appear likely,” Helena said, “that his enemy would in some way communicate with Mr. Tartar to warn him off from Neville? In which case, we might not only know the fact, but might know from Mr. Tartar what the terms of the communication were.”

“I see!” cried Rosa. And in a flash she darted back into the cabin.

She returned with colour heightened, her breath a little quick. She had told Mr. Crisparkle; Mr. Crisparkle had fetched Mr. Tartar; and Mr. Tartar—“who is waiting now, in case you want him,” Rosa added, half turning her head, confused between the cabin and the flowers—had declared his readiness to do as Helena suggested, and to begin that very day.

“I thank him from my heart,” Helena said. “Pray tell him so.”

Again Rosa dipped in with her message and dipped out with more assurances, standing for a moment as if she belonged equally to both places and to both people—Helena’s grave purpose and Mr. Tartar’s cheerful alacrity—so that her confusion looked less like awkwardness than like the innocent difficulty of being cared for.

“And now, darling,” Helena said, “we will be mindful of the caution that has restricted us to this interview for the present, and will part. I hear Neville moving too. Are you going back?”

“To Miss Twinkleton’s?” Rosa asked.

“Yes.”

“Oh, I could never go there any more. I couldn’t indeed, after that dreadful interview!” Rosa cried, the very name seeming to rasp like a harsh note in her ear.

“Then where are you going, pretty one?”

“Now I come to think of it, I don’t know,” Rosa admitted. “I have settled nothing at all yet, but my guardian will take care of me. Don’t be uneasy, dear. I shall be sure to be somewhere.”

It did seem likely.

“And I shall hear of my Rosebud from Mr. Tartar?” Helena asked.

“Yes, I suppose so; from—” Rosa looked back again in a flutter instead of saying Mr. Crisparkle’s name or any other. Then, with a sudden need that was half shame and half hunger for absolution, she caught Helena’s hand. “But tell me one thing before we part, dearest Helena. Tell me—that you are sure, sure, sure, I couldn’t help it.”

“Help it, love?”

“Help making him malicious and revengeful. I couldn’t hold any terms with him, could I?”

“You know how I love you, darling,” Helena answered, with indignation that had steel beneath it; “but I would sooner see you dead at his wicked feet.”

“That’s a great comfort to me! And you will tell your poor brother so, won’t you? And you will give him my remembrance and my sympathy? And you will ask him not to hate me?”

Helena shook her head mournfully, as if that entreaty were needless; Neville, whatever he suffered, was not one to waste hatred on Rosa. She kissed her two hands to her friend; Rosa kissed her hands back; and then Rosa saw a third hand—brown and strong—part the leaves and help Helena down and out of sight.

Mr. Tartar, by merely touching a spring knob here and a drawer-handle there, produced in the admiral’s cabin a repast that might have belonged to enchantment and yet was as practical as his stowage: macaroons crisp and fragrant with almond; little glasses of liqueur that caught the light like amber; tropical spices sealed up as carefully as evidence—cloves, cinnamon, nutmeg—each releasing, when opened, a sudden breath of far-off markets; and jellies of fruits that tasted of sun and sea-crossing, cool on the tongue, trembling with a delicate exactness. It was luxury, yes—but ordered, like everything else, so that nothing was wasted and nothing coarse intruded. Even the small sounds of eating—crack, sip, the faint tap of spoon on glass—seemed hushed by that disciplined peace of the rooms.

But Mr. Tartar could not make time stand still. Time, hard-hearted and fleet, pressed on. Rosa had to come down from the awning-shaded bean-stalk country of flowers and ship-shape wonder to the earth of her guardian’s chambers.

“And now, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious, “what is to be done next? To put the same thought in another form; what is to be done with you?”

Rosa could only look apologetically sensible of being in her own way and everybody else’s. A faint, absurd idea of living fireproof up a good many stairs in Furnival’s Inn for the rest of her life was the only plan that presented itself.

“It has come into my thoughts,” said Mr. Grewgious, “that as the respected lady, Miss Twinkleton, occasionally repairs to London in the recess, with the view of extending her connection, and being available for interviews with metropolitan parents, if any—whether, until we have time in which to turn ourselves round, we might invite Miss Twinkleton to come and stay with you for a month?”

“Stay where, sir?” Rosa asked.

“Whether,” Mr. Grewgious explained, “we might take a furnished lodging in town for a month, and invite Miss Twinkleton to assume the charge of you in it for that period?”

“And afterwards?” Rosa hinted.

“And afterwards,” said Mr. Grewgious, “we should be no worse off than we are now.”

“I think that might smooth the way,” Rosa assented.

“Then let us,” said Mr. Grewgious, rising, “go and look for a furnished lodging. Nothing could be more acceptable to me than the sweet presence of last evening, for all the remaining evenings of my existence; but these are not fit surroundings for a young lady. Let us set out in quest of adventures, and look for a furnished lodging. In the meantime, Mr. Crisparkle here, about to return home immediately, will no doubt kindly see Miss Twinkleton, and invite that lady to co-operate in our plan.”

Mr. Crisparkle took the commission willingly and departed. Mr. Grewgious and Rosa set forth.

Mr. Grewgious’s method of looking at a furnished lodging was to place himself on the opposite side of the street from a house with a suitable bill and stare at it as if the bill might change under pressure of thought; then to work his way tortuously to the back and stare at that; then not go in, but repeat the ceremony elsewhere. The city’s noise—carts, calls, the occasional harsh whistle of some hurried modern contrivance—seemed to Rosa more troubling now that she had lately been in Mr. Tartar’s quiet order. The streets had their own dialect, loud and careless, grinding down distinctions; it made her long, oddly, for the soft click of Mr. Tartar’s drawers and the gentle hush of his awning.

At length Mr. Grewgious bethought himself of a widowed cousin, divers times removed, of Mr. Bazzard’s, who had once solicited his influence in the lodger world, and who lived in Southampton Street, Bloomsbury Square. On a brass door-plate, in uncompromising capitals of considerable size—and yet not lucid as to sex or condition—was the name BILLICKIN.

Personal faintness and overpowering candour were the distinguishing features of Mrs. Billickin’s organisation. She came languishing out of her own exclusive back parlour with the air of having been expressly brought-to for the purpose from an accumulation of several swoons; and even her voice sounded as if it had to travel through layers of shawl and sensibility.

“I hope I see you well, sir,” said Mrs. Billickin, recognising Mr. Grewgious with a bend.

“Thank you, quite well. And you, ma’am?” returned Mr. Grewgious.

“I am as well,” said Mrs. Billickin, becoming aspirational with excess of faintness, “as I hever ham.”

“My ward and an elderly lady,” said Mr. Grewgious, “wish to find a genteel lodging for a month or so. Have you any apartments available, ma’am?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” returned Mrs. Billickin, “I will not deceive you; far from it. I have apartments available.”

She spoke as if truth were a physical burden she had sworn never to set down.

“And now, what apartments, ma’am?” asked Mr. Grewgious, as though to soften a certain severity in her moral posture.

“There is this sitting-room—which, call it what you will, it is the front parlour, Miss,” said Mrs. Billickin, drawing Rosa into the matter with a reproachful air: “the back parlour being what I cling to and never part with; and there is two bedrooms at the top of the ’ouse with gas laid on. I do not tell you that your bedroom floors is firm, for firm they are not. The gas-fitter himself allowed, that to make a firm job, he must go right under your jistes, and it were not worth the outlay as a yearly tenant so to do. The piping is carried above your jistes, and it is best that it should be made known to you.”

Mr. Grewgious and Rosa exchanged looks of dismay, though neither had the least idea what particular catastrophe “above your jistes” might promise. Mrs. Billickin put her hand to her heart as if relieved to have confessed.

“Well! The roof is all right, no doubt,” said Mr. Grewgious, plucking up a little.

“Mr. Grewgious,” returned Mrs. Billickin, “if I was to tell you, sir, that to have nothink above you is to have a floor above you, I should put a deception upon you which I will not do. No, sir. Your slates WILL rattle loose at that elewation in windy weather, do your utmost, best or worst! I defy you, sir, be you what you may, to keep your slates tight, try how you can.”

Even her indignation had a sound—an airy quaver, a kind of moral creak like a stair protesting under virtue.

“Consequent,” she proceeded, cooling herself, “consequent it would be worse than of no use for me to trapse and travel up to the top of the ’ouse with you, and for you to say, ‘Mrs. Billickin, what stain do I notice in the ceiling, for a stain I do consider it?’ and for me to answer, ‘I do not understand you, sir.’ No, sir, I will not be so underhand. I do understand you before you pint it out. It is the wet, sir. It do come in, and it do not come in. You may lay dry there half your lifetime; but the time will come, and it is best that you should know it, when a dripping sop would be no name for you.”

Mr. Grewgious looked much disgraced by the prospect of being prefigured in that pickle.

“Have you any other apartments, ma’am?” he asked.

“Mr. Grewgious,” returned Mrs. Billickin solemnly, “I have. You ask me have I, and my open and my honest answer air, I have. The first and second floors is wacant, and sweet rooms.”

“Come, come! There’s nothing against them,” said Mr. Grewgious, comforting himself.

“Mr. Grewgious,” replied Mrs. Billickin, “pardon me, there is the stairs. Unless your mind is prepared for the stairs, it will lead to inevitable disappointment. You cannot, Miss,” said Mrs. Billickin, turning upon Rosa as if Rosa had personally invented gravity, “place a first floor, and far less a second, on the level footing of a parlour. No, you cannot do it, Miss, it is beyond your power, and wherefore try?”

“Can we see these rooms, ma’am?” asked Mr. Grewgious.

“Mr. Grewgious,” returned Mrs. Billickin, “you can. I will not disguise it from you, sir; you can.”

She sent into her back parlour for her shawl—because she could never, by an ancient household fiction, go anywhere without being wrapped up—then, having been enrolled by her attendant, led the way. She made various genteel pauses on the stairs for breath, and clutched at her heart in the drawing-room as if it had nearly got loose and she had caught it just as it took wing.

The first floor proved satisfactory.

“And the second floor?” said Mr. Grewgious.

“Mr. Grewgious,” replied Mrs. Billickin, turning with ceremony as if an important doctrine must now be established, “the second floor is over this.”

“Can we see that too, ma’am?”

“Yes, sir,” returned Mrs. Billickin. “It is open as the day.”

That also proved satisfactory. Mr. Grewgious retired into a window with Rosa for a few words, then asked for pen and ink and sketched out a line or two of agreement.

Mrs. Billickin, seated, delivered an abstract of the general question as if reading out conditions of weather.

“Five-and-forty shillings per week by the month certain at the time of year is only reasonable to both parties. It is not Bond Street nor yet St. James’s Palace; but it is not pretended that it is. Neither is it attempted to be denied—for why should it?—that the Arching leads to a mews. Mewses must exist. Respecting attendance; two is kep’, at liberal wages. Words has arisen as to tradesmen, but dirty shoes on fresh hearth-stoning was attributable, and no wish for a commission on your orders. Coals is either by the fire, or per the scuttle.”

She laid such weight upon those prepositions that they sounded like law.

“Dogs is not viewed with favour. Besides litter, they gets stole, and sharing suspicions is apt to creep in, and unpleasantness takes place.”

By then Mr. Grewgious had his agreement-lines and his earnest-money ready.

“I have signed it for the ladies, ma’am,” he said, “and you’ll have the goodness to sign it for yourself, Christian and Surname, there, if you please.”

“Mr. Grewgious,” said Mrs. Billickin, bursting into a new candour, “no, sir! You must excuse the Christian name.”

Mr. Grewgious stared at her.

“The door-plate is used as a protection,” said Mrs. Billickin, “and acts as such, and go from it I will not.”

Mr. Grewgious stared at Rosa.

“No, Mr. Grewgious, you must excuse me. So long as this ’ouse is known indefinite as Billickin’s, and so long as it is a doubt with the riff-raff where Billickin may be hidin’, near the street-door or down the airy, and what his weight and size, so long I feel safe. But commit myself to a solitary female statement, no, Miss! Nor would you for a moment wish,” said Mrs. Billickin, injured into emphasis, “to take that advantage of your sex, if you were not brought to it by inconsiderate example.”

Rosa reddened as if she had attempted the most disgraceful overreaching. She begged Mr. Grewgious to be content with any signature, and so, in a baronial manner, the sign-manual BILLICKIN was appended.

They settled details for taking possession on the next day but one, when Miss Twinkleton might reasonably be expected; and then Rosa went back to Furnival’s Inn on her guardian’s arm, through the street’s jangling talk and wheels and cries, longing again for that ordered quiet where even a latch knew how to behave.

Behold Mr. Tartar walking up and down Furnival’s Inn, checking himself when he saw them coming, and advancing with a prompt, respectful ease.

“It occurred to me,” he hinted, “that we might go up the river, the weather being so delicious and the tide serving. I have a boat of my own at the Temple Stairs.”

“I have not been up the river for this many a day,” said Mr. Grewgious, tempted.

“I was never up the river,” added Rosa.
