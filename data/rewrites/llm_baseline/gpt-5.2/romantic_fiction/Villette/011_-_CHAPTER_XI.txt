Summer settled over the house like a hot lid, holding in every breath and every sound until even the stair-boards seemed to creak with thirst. In the kitchen below, the great pots sang dully, lids rattling with a weak impatience; on the landing above, the nursery smelt of warmed linen, vinegar water, and that particular sour-sweetness of fevered skin which no rose-water will wholly conquer. The town outside was full of carriage-grind and hammer-stroke and the thin whistle of commerce; but within these walls the day had its own acoustic weather—an unceasing ripple of girls’ voices, the struck glass of a triangle in music-class, the perpetual practising of scales like knives being sharpened, and, under all, the faint fretful piping of a little child who could not be comforted.

Georgette, the youngest, took a fever—one of those sudden, sly fevers that come with heat and crowded rooms and too many breaths shared. Désirée, who had lately complained with all her might and then, as if by caprice, recovered in an hour, was instantly whisked away with Fifine to Bonne-Maman in the country, “par précaution,” Madame said; and there, no doubt, they would drink cool milk and eat thick bread still warm from the oven, while here we measured out spoonfuls of barley-water and listened for the change in a child’s breathing.

With Georgette ill, the need of medical aid became real and immediate. Dr. Pillule had returned—he had been home a week, available and eager for his post as if nothing had interrupted it—yet Madame Beck, who could ignore what she did not choose to see, summoned his English rival to keep coming. Dr. John, with his step that always sounded brisk without being noisy, his voice clear as poured water, was called again and again.

A couple of the pensionnaires began to complain of headache; a few more grew pale, pressed fingers to temples, made languid little moans which, in a cooler season, might have been mere imitation but now seemed the first faint participation in Georgette’s ailment. I thought: Now, at last, she must recall Pillule. Prudence will forbid her to expose her pupils to a young man’s attendance; in this country of convent walls and confessionals, a handsome doctor crossing daily into the school-division is a spark near powder.

Madame was prudent; but Madame could also be intrepidly venturous. She brought Dr. John not only to her own children but among the older girls; she installed him, with an air of decision, beside Blanche de Melcy—proud, beautiful, conscious of both—and Angélique, her friend, vain and flirting as a ribbon in a breeze. Dr. John seemed gratified by this confidence; and if discretion of bearing could have made the step safe, his would have done it: he moved with a tact that appeared almost modest.

Yet a “pensionnat de demoiselles” is a place where a whisper travels faster than a bell. The school gossiped, the kitchen whispered, and the town—always hungry—caught the rumour as a dog catches scent. Letters came from parents; visits too, stiff with remonstrance and scented gloves. Rival educational houses waited, eyes bright, ready to turn Madame’s false step—if false it was—into her ruin.

Had she been weak, she would have fallen. She was not weak. I watched her meet those offended mothers and fathers with a good-humoured ease, a round frankness of “bonne femme,” so natural—or so perfectly assumed—that it disarmed where solemn reasoning would have sharpened resistance. She would clasp her fat little white hands, chuckling as if amused at the world’s anxieties.

“Ce pauvre Docteur Jean! ce cher jeune homme! le meilleur créature du monde!”

Then she would explain—always explaining as if it were the simplest domestic matter—how she employed him for her own children; how they were so fond of him they would scream themselves into fits at the thought of another doctor; how, where she trusted him for her own, it was only natural to trust him for others. And besides, she insisted, it was temporary, a mere expedient. Blanche and Angélique had the migraine: Dr. John had written a prescription—voilà tout.

The parents’ mouths closed, as if she had pressed her palm lightly over them. Blanche and Angélique saved her the rest by loudly chanting duets in their physician’s praise; the other pupils echoed, declaring with one voice that if they were ill they would have Dr. John and nobody else. Madame laughed; and the parents, indulgent as this nation is of its offspring—making the children’s will a law—laughed too. Madame even gained credit for motherly partiality. She came off with flying colours; she was liked better than ever.

To this day, the reason she risked her interest for Dr. John remained, to me, not wholly explained. The house, from pupil to servant, decided upon an explanation with the certainty of a verdict: she was going to marry him. They settled it as if it were an item on the timetable. Difference of age—some fourteen years—seemed to them no obstacle. It was to be so.

Appearances did not wholly discountenance the idea. Madame seemed bent on keeping his services, oblivious of Pillule. She made a point of receiving Dr. John herself, and never failed to be cheerful, blithe, benignant with him. About this time she gave marked attention to dress; the morning dishabille, the nightcap and shawl vanished. His early visits found her with auburn braids neatly arranged, silk dress trimly fitted, laced brodequins in place of slippers: toilette complete, fresh as a flower set out before guests. I doubted her intention went further than to show a very handsome man she was not quite plain; and plain she was not. Without beauty of feature or elegance of form, she pleased; without youth’s graces, she cheered. Her unfaded hair, her tempered blue eye, her cheek with its wholesome fruit-like bloom—these pleased in moderation, but with constancy. She was never colourless.

Had she, indeed, some floating vision of adopting Dr. John as husband—taking him to her well-furnished home, endowing him with savings said to amount to a moderate competency, and making him comfortable for the rest of his life? And did he suspect such visions? More than once I met him coming out from her presence with a half-mischievous smile and the look, in his eyes, of a vanity tickled. With all his good nature and good looks, he was not perfect; it would have been very imperfect to roguishly encourage aims he never meant to fulfil. Yet did he never mean them? People said he had no money; that he depended wholly on his profession. Madame—though older—was the kind of woman who would never wholly wither, never break down. They were certainly on good terms. He perhaps was not in love; but how many marry for love in this world? We waited the end.

What he waited for, what he watched, I could not tell. There was in his manner a peculiarity—an expectant vigilance, an absorbed eager look—that never wore off; it intensified, as if he were listening for some particular note amidst all the daily noise. He had never been quite within my compass, and he seemed to range farther beyond it.

One morning Georgette had been more feverish and therefore more peevish. Her cry had turned thin and persistent, a sound like a small bird beating itself against glass.

“Minnie, Minnie, me very poorly!”

A draught had been ordered—some mixture bitter with drops I did not know; I suspected it disagreed with her. I longed to consult the doctor before giving more. The bell rang; he was admitted. I heard his voice addressing the portress—clear, English, making a small island of another tongue in the French tide. It was his custom to mount straight to the nursery, taking three steps at once, arriving like a cheerful surprise. Five minutes passed—ten—and still he did not come.

Georgette’s cry went on; it worked upon the nerves like a repeated tap. I went down to ascertain why he lingered. The corridor was empty. Whither had he vanished?

Madame could not be with him in the salle-à-manger; I had left her moments before, dressing in her chamber. I paused and listened, but in that house listening was like trying to catch a single voice in a market. Three pupils were practising at three proximate instruments—the dining-room and the greater and lesser drawing-rooms; between these salons and the corridor lay the portress’s cabinet, a little room communicating with them, once meant for a boudoir. Farther off, at a fourth instrument in the oratory, a whole class were having a singing lesson; they were joining in what they called a “barcarole,” its syllables rocking—“fraîchë,” “brisë,” “Venisë”—as if trying to make Venice with their mouths.

Under such a canopy of sound, what could I hear? Yet—yes—through it, near and distinct, a giddy treble laugh came from the little cabinet, close by whose half-unclosed door I stood. A man’s voice answered, soft and deep, pleading. I caught only the adjuration, strained and sudden:

“For God’s sake!”

Then a pause, as brief as the space between spoon and lips; and Dr. John came out.

His eyes shone, but not with joy or triumph; his fair English cheek was high-coloured. Something had seized him and shaken him—some baffled vexation, anxious and tender at once. His brow bore not wounded pride so much as wounded affection—cruelly wounded, it seemed. The half-open door screened me; had I stood full in his way, I believe he would have passed without seeing. He moved as if the house had narrowed to a single thought.

Who had power to torture him thus within these walls? Madame I believed to be in her chamber; the cabinet belonged to the portress alone—Rosine Matou, pretty, unprincipled, a little French grisette airy and fickle, dressy, vain, and mercenary. It could not be—surely not—her hand that had wrung such a look from him.

While I pondered, Rosine’s voice, clear and a little sharp, broke out in a lightsome French song, trilling through the still-ajar door. I glanced in, doubting my senses. She sat at the table in a smart dress of jaconas rose, trimming a tiny blond cap—picking up lace and thread as calmly as if she had been basting a cuff for a child, not pricking a heart. In the room there was nothing alive but herself, except some goldfish in a glass globe and a few flowers in pots, and a broad July sunbeam lying across the boards like spilled wheat.

Here was a problem; but Georgette’s medicine would not wait upon my confusion. I went up.

Dr. John sat in a chair at the child’s bedside; Madame stood before him. The little patient had been examined and soothed; now she lay composed in her crib, her damp curls clinging to her forehead, her small mouth at last slack with relief. The nursery air was thick with camomile and warmed milk, and the faint metallic tang of a spoon rinsed in haste.

As I entered, Madame Beck was discussing the physician’s own health. She remarked on some change—real or fancied—in his looks; she charged him with over-work and recommended rest and change of air, as if prescribing for him were a privilege of her household. He listened good-naturedly, with laughing indifference; he told her she was “trop bonne” and that he felt perfectly well.

Then she appealed to me; and Dr. John followed her movement with a slow glance, languidly surprised that reference should be made to a quarter he deemed insignificant.

“What do you think, Miss Lucie? Is he not paler and thinner?”

It was rare I uttered more than monosyllables in his presence; he was the kind of person with whom I was likely always to remain the neutral, passive thing he thought me. Yet now I took licence to answer in a phrase—and I made it deliberately significant.

“He looks ill at this moment; but perhaps it is owing to some temporary cause: Dr. John may have been vexed or harassed.”

I did not look up to read his face. Georgette, in her broken English, asked me if she might have a glass of eau sucrée. I answered her in English; and for the first time, I think, he noticed that I spoke his language. Hitherto he had always taken me for a foreigner, addressing me as “Mademoiselle” and giving his directions in French. He seemed on the point of remarking upon it, but checked himself and held his tongue—as if words, in any tongue, were suddenly too blunt a tool.

Madame recommenced advising him. He shook his head, laughing; rose; bade her good-morning with courtesy, yet with the careless air of one surfeited by too much unsolicited attention, as if kindness had become cloying, like sugared water given when the stomach wants plain broth.

When he was gone, Madame sank into the chair he had just left and rested her chin on her hand. All animation, all amiability vanished. Her face turned stony, stern—almost mortified and morose. She sighed once: a single deep sigh, as if something within had dropped its weight.

A loud bell rang for morning-school, its clang cutting cleanly through the softer sounds, calling bodies and thoughts back into order. Madame rose. Passing a dressing-table with a glass, she looked at her reflection. One single white hair streaked her nut-brown tresses; she plucked it out with a shudder. In the full summer light, though her face still held colour, it had lost the texture of youth; and youth’s contours—where were they? She turned darkly away from the glass.

Never had I pitied Madame before; yet then my heart softened. A calamity had come upon her. Disappointment—that hag who greets with a grisly “All-hail”—stood at her shoulder, and her soul recoiled from intimacy even as it felt the cold touch.

But Rosine.

My bewilderment at Rosine surpassed description. That day I embraced five opportunities of passing the portress’s cabinet, with a view to contemplating her charms and finding out the secret of their influence. Pretty—yes; young—yes; well-made dress—certainly: the jaconas rose sat upon her like a confectioner’s icing, smooth and shining. Her room always carried a faint odour of cheap perfume mingled with starch and the warm dusty sweetness of ribbon-boxes. All good points; amply sufficient, perhaps, in any philosophic mind, to account for agony and distraction in a young man like Dr. John.

Still, I could not help forming half a wish that the doctor were my brother—or at least that he had a sister or a mother who would kindly sermonize him, who would speak to him in the old homely language that holds a man fast, the dialect of hearth and family, not the polished phrases that float and flatter. I say half a wish; I broke it and flung it away before it grew whole, discovering in time its exquisite folly.

“Somebody,” I argued, “might as well sermonize Madame about her young physician: and what good would that do?”

Perhaps Madame sermonized herself. She did not behave weakly; she did not make herself ridiculous. True, she had neither strong feelings to overcome nor tender feelings by which to be miserably pained—so, at least, one might have said before that deep sigh. True also, she had an important avocation, a real business, to fill her time, divert her thoughts, divide her interest; the running of the house went on with its measured noises—bells, footsteps, lessons, the clink of plates below where meals must still be served though hearts were hungry in other ways. And especially she possessed that genuine good sense not given to all women nor all men. By dint of these combined advantages she behaved wisely—she behaved well.

Brava! once more, Madame Beck. I saw you matched against an Apollyon of predilection; you fought a good fight, and you overcame.
