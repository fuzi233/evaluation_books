Fanny had no need of introductions. The moment she saw the two ladies—one older, one younger—entering the passage with faces set as if carved for a monument, she knew, by a flash of likeness in the elder’s worn eyes, whose presence chilled the air. Those were the same eyes Arthur had borrowed when fever had tossed him—only steadier now, and far less forgiving.

Mrs. Pendennis looked at Fanny as a housekeeper looks at a suspicious stain. Laura looked past her, as if the poor girl were a chair that had been put in the wrong place.

“I—I wrote to you yesterday, if you please, ma’am,” Fanny stammered, every syllable shaking out of her like a spoonful of water from trembling hands. “To tell you how he was.”

“Did you, madam?” Mrs. Pendennis said. Her voice had the smooth, cold edge of a knife freshly honed. “I suppose I may now relieve you from nursing my son. I am his mother, you understand.”

“Yes, ma’am. This way to his room—Oh! wait—” Fanny caught her breath as if she had swallowed smoke. “I must prepare you for his—”

For a second the widow’s hard composure slipped. A gasp—quick, frightened—broke out of her, and she crushed it down again at once, as though even pity must not be allowed to make a noise in respectable society.

“He’s been so since yesterday,” Fanny whispered, teeth chattering.

From the open door of Pen’s room came a burst of sound: not a groan, but a horrible shout of laughter, as if some cruel tavern jest had been cracked at the foot of his bed. Then more yells—then, incongruously, a college drinking-song, bellowed in a cracked, triumphant voice; and between verses the thump of his fist on the paneling, like a man rapping for more wine.

“He does not know me, ma’am,” Fanny said, with the helpless exactness of a cook admitting the soufflé has fallen.

“Indeed. Perhaps he will know his mother. Let me pass, if you please.” And with that Mrs. Pendennis swept by her—through the narrow, dark passage, into the sitting-room beyond, where the sick man’s life and death were making such a racket. Laura followed, silent, sailing past without so much as a glance. Major Pendennis came last, his eyelids lowered, peeping from under them with a furtive, uneasy shame.

Fanny sank onto the bench in the passage—the same bench where Pen’s printer’s devil used to perch for hours, ink-stained and hungry—and she cried and prayed as well as she could, with that desperate, salt prayer that is less religion than drowning. She would have given her own blood to cool his fever. They gave her nothing—not even the smallest crumb of thanks; not even the courtesy you offer a maid who has kept the broth from burning.

Time went strange: a draught in the passage, the muffled commotion inside, the taste of tears, and that smell which clings to illness—stale linen, spilled physic, hot breath, and the faint metallic tang that foretells worse.

At last Doctor Goodenough came, brisk and benevolent, like a man who has seen too many sickrooms to be frightened of them, and too many grieving faces to be careless.

“What, nurse?” he asked, with his kind professional cheer. “How’s your patient? Has he had any rest?”

“Go and ask them. They’re inside,” Fanny said.

“Who? his mother?”

She nodded, dumb as a scullery-girl caught in a lie—though she had done nothing but tell the truth with her whole life.

“You must go to bed yourself, my poor little maid,” said the Doctor. “You’ll be ill too, if you don’t.”

“Oh, mayn’t I come and see him—mayn’t I come and see him!” She clasped his hand, falling on her knees with such honest anguish that it fogged the kind man’s spectacles and made him blink hard behind them. “I—I love him so.”

“Pooh, pooh! Nonsense!” he said—because doctors must scold where they would rather weep. He set her back on the bench, tapped her pale cheek with a finger that had steadied many a pulse, and bustled into the room.

Mrs. Pendennis had established herself at the bedside like a general in possession of an enemy fort. A great chair was her throne; her watch lay on the table among the bottles and papers; her bonnet and cloak were set aside with the tidy certainty of a woman who could arrange a drawing-room even in a crisis. Her Bible—always travelling with her like a moral umbrella—rested in her lap.

Her first act, on entering, had been small and speaking: she had taken Fanny’s shawl and bonnet—poor little things that smelt of rain and cheap lavender—and carried them out as if they were soiled cloths, dropping them on the study-table with a motion that said, wordlessly, Out. She had closed the door on Major Pendennis and Laura too, keeping even family at a distance while she claimed her son.

She had feared—silently, fiercely—that Arthur would not know her. Fever makes strange foreigners of our dearest. But that pang was spared her in part: Pen did know his mother, and smiled and nodded with a familiar, childish friendliness. In the instant she came in, his mind transported them home to Fairoaks; he began talking in wild loops and leaps, laughing, chattering, making nonsense of memory. Outside the door Laura heard that laughter, and it stung her like smoke in the eyes. To her, every shout sounded like confession: it was true then—he had been guilty, and with that creature—and she had loved him—and now he might die, raving, unrepentant, and merry as a man at a supper-table.

Major Pendennis offered, now and then, a humming little sentence meant for comfort. Laura scarcely heard him. Grief has its own deafness.

It was a dismal sitting for all. When Goodenough came in he seemed, by mere contrast, an angel—an angel with pills and a practical manner.

A doctor does not visit only the sick man: he is summoned by the terror and longing of everybody who loves the sick man. The sound of his wheels in the street, the knock at the door, the stir on the stairs—how it lifts and drops whole households. We read his face like a menu when we are starving for hope: will he bring nourishment, or only the polite refusal of it? Mothers search his eyes for the word he may not dare say. Wives watch his fingers on the pulse as if he were Fate in a neckcloth. Children, hushed and puzzled, feel the whole house listening—listening not just to voices but to the very acoustic weather of the room: the hiss of breath, the clink of a spoon in a glass, the heavy tread of an anxious foot. In such hours, even the silence has a sound, and it can be crueler than any factory din.

Goodenough made short work of introductions, for there are moments when ceremony is an impertinence. He examined Pen and saw at once what there was to see: high fever, no ambiguity, no politeness possible. Strong antiphlogistic measures were needed. He offered Mrs. Pendennis what comfort he honestly could—youth, constitution, hope—and then drew Major Pendennis aside into Warrington’s empty room to consult.

The case was critical. If they did not stop the fever, it might carry the young fellow off. He must be bled at once. The mother must be told. And why—Goodenough asked, with that grim irritation doctors feel at foolish additions to a sickroom—why had the other young lady been brought? She was out of place there.

“And there was another woman still—be hanged to it!” the Major said, rubbing his hands as if guilt were grease. “The—the little person who opened the door.” He had seen the bonnet and shawl flung out upon the study-table. Did the Doctor know anything about the—the little person? “I just caught a glimpse of her as we passed in,” the Major added, and even now, with death in the next room, could not help a worldly aside: “begad she was uncommonly nice-looking.”

Goodenough looked at him in a queer way and—yes—smiled. In the gravest minutes, the smallest absurdity will pop up and grin at us, like a cork forced to the surface of black water. The smile did not lessen the gloom; it sharpened it.

“I have it,” the Doctor said at last.

He went into the study, wrote two notes rapidly, sealed one, then took up Fanny’s little shawl and bonnet—as if to give the rejected things an errand, a dignity—and carried them with the notes into the passage where Fanny sat.

“Quick, nurse,” he said. “You must carry this to the surgeon and bid him come instantly. Then go to my house and ask for my servant Harbottle; give him this prescription and tell him to get it prepared and wait until I—until it is ready. It may take a little time.”

So off went poor Fanny, sent from the doorway like a turnspit from the kitchen, only her task was not gravy but life. She trudged to the apothecary hard by in the Strand; he came straightway with his lancet—small, bright, efficient as modern commerce—ready to operate. Then she made for the Doctor’s house in Hanover Square.

Goodenough was back at the chambers before the mixture was finished—Harbottle taking an age to compound whatever dark draughts science then ladled out with such confidence. And during the rest of Arthur’s illness Fanny never again appeared as nurse in his rooms. Yet for that day and the next, a little figure could be seen hovering on Pen’s staircase: a face made tragically older by love and sleeplessness, watching, questioning with her eyes the apothecary, his boy, the laundress, the Doctor himself—anyone who came down from the sickroom carrying news, or the smell of it.

On the third day Doctor Goodenough’s carriage stopped at Shepherd’s Inn. The good man went into the porter’s lodge—because kindness, like a well-made broth, belongs where it is needed most, not where it is most admired—and tended a little patient of his there. For the best medicine he could give her was the moment he was able to tell Fanny Bolton that the crisis was over, and that there was at length every hope for Arthur Pendennis.

J. Costigan, Esquire, late of Her Majesty’s service, happened to see the Doctor’s carriage and did what he always did with any spectacle: reviewed it.

“Green liveries, bedad!” the General said. “And as foin a pair of high-stepping bee horses as ever a gentleman need sit behoind—let alone a docthor. There’s no ind to the proide and ar’gance of them docthors nowadays—” Then, with that swift Irish turn that can abuse and bless in one breath: “Not but that is a good one—and a scoientific cyarkter—and a roight good fellow, bedad; and he’s brought the poor little girl well troo her faver, Bows, me boy.”

From that day, whenever Costigan met Goodenough’s carriage, he saluted it with such magnificent ceremony as if the physician were the Lord Lieutenant himself and the General were back in his own glory at Phoenix Park.

Mrs. Pendennis’s gratitude to the Doctor knew no bounds—or at least no bounds except those set by pride. The kind gentleman laughed at the notion of taking a fee from a literary man, or from the widow of a brother practitioner. Mrs. Pendennis determined that on returning to Fairoaks she would send him the silver-gilt vase—the jewel of the house, wrapped reverently in green baize—presented long ago to the late John Pendennis at Bath by Lady Elizabeth Firebrace, after the recovery of her son, the late Sir Anthony, from scarlet fever. Hippocrates, Hygeia, King Bladud, and a wreath of serpents crowned it still; Messrs. Abednego of Milsom Street had executed it at their best, and Mr. Birch, tutor to the young baronet, had supplied the inscription. It was a trophy of old cures and old gratitude—an heirloom of medicine and memory, shining with the kind of splendour that tries to make suffering look genteel.

That priceless cup she would gladly give. There was scarcely any other favour her thankfulness would not have granted him—except the one he desired most: that she should think kindly of poor Fanny.

Goodenough had learned something of the artless, sad story in his brief interviews with the girl, and he was disposed to be gentle with her—and not too admiring of Pen in the affair, or not knowing precisely what Pen’s conduct had been. He knew enough to understand that the poor, infatuated child was without stain as yet; that her watch in Pen’s room had been, in her mind, a vigil by a deathbed; that Arthur scarcely knew she was there; and that her grief, whether he lived or died, had the pitiful depth of love that has no language for itself—no respectable dialect, no sanctioned vocabulary. It could only speak in sobs, and those were considered vulgar.

But when Goodenough once or twice alluded to Fanny, Mrs. Pendennis’s countenance—commonly soft and gentle—hardened into something cruel and inexorable, as if her very features had taken up a dominant lexicon in which mercy had no word. The Doctor saw it was useless. He stopped asking for justice; he ceased pleading for pity. There is an affliction, the poets tell us, no poppy or mandragora can soothe; and when it appears in ladies, no later discovery—neither homoeopathy, nor hydropathy, nor mesmerism, nor the newest fashionable doctor—can cure it. Call it not jealousy, if you like. Call it rivalry, emulation: it bites just the same.

Now, certain prosaic persons—those who count cupboards in novels, and measure rooms as if the heart were a piece of furniture—may ask how, in a set of chambers in the Temple consisting of three rooms, two cupboards, a passage, and a coal-box, all these souls could be packed: Arthur sick; Helen his mother; Laura her adopted daughter; Martha their country attendant; Mrs. Wheezer, a nurse from St. Bartholomew’s; Mrs. Flanagan, an Irish laundress; Major Pendennis; Morgan his valet; Pidgeon, Pen’s boy; and the rest. The answer is simple: almost everybody in the Temple was out of town, and the house in Lamb Court was nearly empty except for those drawn, like moths to a dangerous flame, to the bedside of a fevered young gentleman.

We will not linger over the fever’s long, ugly details, nor enlarge much on the happier theme of recovery. But the house filled, as households do in such times, with an odd domestic mythology: the whisper that meant danger, the footstep that meant hope, the clatter of a tray that sounded like salvation. And above all, the city’s own roar beyond the windows—the cart-wheels, calls, and bells—kept up its indifferent, industrial chorus, as if London could not bear to lower its voice even when a single human life asked for quiet.

Of course such a fashionable man as young Mr. Sibwright, who occupied chambers on the second floor in Pen’s staircase, could not be supposed to remain in London. Mrs. Flanagan, Pen’s laundress, knew Mrs. Rouncy who did for Mr. Sibwright; and so that gentleman’s bedroom was put in order for Miss Bell, or for Mrs. Pendennis, when she should be persuaded to leave her son’s room and seek a little rest.

If Percy Sibwright—flower of Baker Street—could have known who was sleeping in his bed, he would have been proud enough to compose odes to his own sheets. He wrote poems (some printed in annuals, some lingering in manuscript albums of the noble), had been a Camford man, and was said to have narrowly missed the English Prize Poem. Sibwright, however, was absent, and his pretty little brass bed, with chintz curtains lined in pink, was surrendered to Laura.

It was, truly, the daintiest bower. In the window stood a mignonette-box, breathing out a clean, peppery sweetness that seemed almost indecent beside the sour medicinal smells next door. Shiny boots were arranged in trim rows over the wardrobe like a little exhibition of vanity at a fair. There was a museum of scent, pomatum, and bear’s-grease pots—each with its own oily, perfumed history—curious enough for any food-historian of the toilette. Portraits of women—always melancholy, often disguised or half undressed—glittered round the neat walls: Medora with her hair down, strumming sorrow over Conrad; Fleur-de-Marie peeping mournfully through convent bars; Dorothea washing her eternal feet. It was an elegant gallery for a gallant lover of the sex.

In Sibwright’s sitting-room there was an infant law-library in fresh calf-skin—books dressed for court, though perhaps never speaking there—and a tolerably large collection of classics he could not read, along with English and French poetry and fiction he read much too well. Invitation-cards from the past season still decorated his looking-glass; and scarcely anything betrayed the lawyer except the wig-box beside the Venus on the middle shelf, with “P. Sibwright, Esquire” gilded on it.

With Sibwright in chambers was Mr. Bangham, a sporting man married to a rich widow, who had no practice, came to chambers thrice in a term, and went circuit for those mysterious reasons which make men go circuit. Their rooms have nothing to do with our history and will likely never appear again; but we glance through their open doors as we pass, because in life, too, we cannot help peeping into shops on the Strand, or counting our neighbour’s dinner, or noticing the faces under bonnets in the next pew—especially when our own hearts are too full to look straight ahead.

Many years after these events, Laura—older, and with humour softened by happiness—confessed with a blush and a laugh that she had once read a French novel then much in vogue; and when her husband asked where on earth she had procured such a volume, she owned it was in the Temple, when she lived in Percy Sibwright’s chambers.

“And also,” she said, “I never confessed—on that same occasion—what I must now own: that I opened the japanned box, and took out that strange-looking wig inside it, and put it on and looked at myself in the glass.”

Suppose Sibwright had entered at that moment—enraptured rogue! What would all his pictures of disguised beauties have been beside that living one?

But when Laura played that prank, Pen could not have been dangerously ill upstairs; for had he been, common feeling and decorum would have prevented tricks and disguises. Besides, a great and delightful alteration had taken place. Goodenough’s blisters, potions, and lancet had subdued the fever. It had retreated, returning only in feeble fits. The wandering had settled into exhausted sense.

Pen had been able—weak, hollow-eyed, bearded with illness—to kiss and bless his mother for coming; to call for Laura and his uncle; to press their hands and thank them with an affection that startled them, each according to temperament. Then, after his affectionate nurse had turned them out, he had sunk into a sleep so deep and long it seemed like a rehearsal for death—sixteen hours, they said—until he woke and called out, in a voice half cracked and wholly human:

He was hungry.

If it is hard to be ill and loathe food, how sweet it is to be getting well and to feel hunger return—honest, bodily hunger, like a homely bell calling you back to the world. With age that joy thins, as other joys do; and then comes the illness for which there is no convalescence at all.

On the day of this happy sign another arrival came to Lamb Court, introduced not by ceremony but by large puffs of tobacco-smoke. The puffs were followed by an individual with a cigar in his mouth and a carpet-bag under his arm: Warrington, come back from Norfolk the moment he heard. Mr. Bows had thoughtfully written to inform him; Warrington had been away from home when the letter reached his brother’s house; the Eastern Counties had no railway then (for we only commit anachronisms when we choose, and only violate natural laws when some great moral truth requires it); and so he appeared, as luck would have it, on the lucky day when Pen’s convalescence might be said to have begun.

Warrington let himself in with his pass-key. His surprise was not great to find the chambers occupied and Major Pendennis seated demurely in an easy-chair, listening—or pretending to listen—to a young lady reading Shakespeare in a low, sweet voice, the cadence of it falling on the room like soft rain after street-noise.

At the apparition of the tall traveller with cigar and carpet-bag, the lady stopped, started, and laid down the book. Warrington, suddenly shy as a boy, flung the cigar into the passage, took off his hat, dropped that too, and went straight up to the Major, seizing his hand.

“How is Arthur?” he cried.

The Major answered in a tremulous, cheery voice—emotion had curiously aged him in an instant—and, returning Warrington’s pressure with a shaking hand, told him of the happy crisis, of the mother’s arrival—with her young charge—with Miss—

“You need not tell me her name,” Warrington said quickly, eyes bright with relief and feeling. “You need not tell me your name. I knew at once it was Laura.”

He held out his hand and took hers. Beneath his rough brows, immense tenderness shone; it shook his voice as he looked at her. His face seemed to say, And this is Laura. Her heart answered, And this is Warrington—Arthur’s hero—the brave and kind—who has come hundreds of miles when he heard his friend was struck down.

“Thank you, Mr. Warrington,” was all she said. She returned his pressure, and blushed so hotly that she was glad the lamp was behind her and could hide her face.

As they stood thus, the door of Pen’s bedchamber opened stealthily—as his mother was wont to open it—and Warrington saw another lady appear. She looked at him once; then, turning toward the bed, she said softly, urgently, “Hsh!” and lifted her hand.
