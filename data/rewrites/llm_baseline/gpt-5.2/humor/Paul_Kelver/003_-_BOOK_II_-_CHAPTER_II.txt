The case of my father and mother was, from the beginning, a dish that refused to follow any printed recipe. They had been separated for years; and yet, when I came upon them together in that big corner house, it was as if somebody had taken two people of nearly forty and nearly thirty, dusted them with flour, and—by some trick of heat and hope—raised them back into boy and girl. Seen through a child’s eyes, they were not “middle-aged.” They were simply “old enough to be allowed to do things,” and at the same time young enough to do them.

Had any enquiring bachelor or maiden asked my opinion of marriage, I should have described it as a condition in which the husband cannot sit still in an office unless his wife is within calling distance, like salt within reach of the hand. Twenty times a day he would cry from behind his door:

“Maggie, are you doing anything important? I want to talk to you about a matter of business.”

… “Maggie, are you alone? Oh, all right, I’ll come down.”

And I should have said the wife is a woman whose eyes, when they fall upon her man, catch light the way a saucepan catches flame; who is glad where he is and troubled where he is not. But of course that would have been only our household’s method, and no more to be trusted as a general rule than Susan’s method of making tea.

I should also have offered advice on the alarms and excursions incidental to living with a married couple still practicing their happiness in public. I would have urged visitors to keep their own feet tucked away under the table, lest—mistaken for other feet—they should be trodden upon and pressed with meaning. And I would have warned people against entering any room unheralded by what the theatre calls “noise without.” It is trying to the nerves to turn a handle, step in, and be greeted by the sound of two people springing apart like startled pigeons, followed by a wild scuttling, and then to find them sitting stiffly in opposite corners, deep in book or needlework, as if they had always been there and had never in their lives heard of cushions.

Personally, I exercised little influence. My sudden appearances came to be met by, “Oh, it’s only Spud,” in a tone of relief, generally accompanied by a sofa cushion, as if I were not so much a child as a small domestic inconvenience that happened to be beloved. But my aunt—Aunt Fan—was a different matter. They stood in awe of her without knowing why, and she obliged them by doing everything possible to keep their imaginations from having to work.

To spare their feelings she erred, if at all, on the side of excess. She never moved a footstep about the house except to the music of a sustained and penetrating cough. She could have coughed the roof off a workhouse. My father once remarked—ungratefully, I must confess—that the total quantity of bark produced by my aunt in a day would have done credit to the dying efforts of a hospital-load of consumptives; and that for a robust, perfectly healthy lady the nervous expenditure must be prodigious. Also, that no fear might live with them that her eyes had seen aught not intended, she had a habit of entering any room backwards, closing the door loudly and with difficulty before turning round. In dark passages she would walk singing, so that the very wallpaper might bear witness to her innocence.

No woman alive could have done more; yet—such is human nature—neither my father nor my mother seemed grateful. Indeed, the more sympathy she showed, the more irritated they became, as if sympathy were pepper applied to a bruise.

“I believe, Fanny, you hate seeing Luke and me happy together,” said my mother one day, coming up from the kitchen to find my aunt preparing for entry into the drawing-room by dropping teaspoons at five-second intervals outside the door. “Don’t make yourself so ridiculous.”

“Hate it!” returned my aunt. “Why should I? Why shouldn’t a pair of turtle-doves bill and coo, when their united age is only a little over seventy, the pretty dears?”

Her mildness used to astonish me. She had the gift, common in elders and in cows, of answering with complete composure when any other creature would have tossed its horns.

My father, on the other hand, grew positively vindictive, and I remember well the first time I knew him lose his temper. We were walking by the canal bank. What brought it on I forget; with my father one seldom knew what would be the spark, because he always looked as though he were made of good temper, until suddenly there would be a little explosion and then he would be ashamed of the smoke.

“Your aunt—” My father may not have intended it, but whenever he spoke of my aunt his tone conveyed the impression that he held me personally responsible for her existence. This used to weigh upon me. “Your aunt is the most cantankerous, the most—” He broke off and shook his fist towards the setting sun, as if it had been in correspondence with her. “I wish to God your aunt had a comfortable little income of her own, with a freehold cottage in the country—by God I do!”

And then, ashamed of his brutality, he softened at once, like butter meeting heat.

“Not but what sometimes, of course, she can be very nice, you know,” he added. “Don’t tell your mother what I said just now.”

Another who followed our domestic comedy with sympathetic interest was Susan, our maid-of-all-work—the first of a long and varied series, extending unto the advent of Amy, to whom the blessing of Heaven. Susan was stout and elderly, liable to sudden fits of sleepiness, the result, we were given to understand, of “trouble.” Her heart, she told us with pride, was always in the right place. I cannot say of her hands that they always were.

She could never look at my father and mother sitting anywhere near each other without flopping down and weeping awhile, the sight of connubial bliss reminding her of the past glories of her own married state. These outbursts were frequently timed to occur over vegetables.

Being an earnest enquirer, I attempted to grasp the ins and outs of Susan’s past. I never succeeded. Whether her answers were framed to elude curiosity or whether they were merely the product of a naturally incoherent mind, I cannot say; but their tendency was to convey confusion, as if her history had been written in a dialect she herself no longer spoke and was now trying to translate into ours with missing words.

On Monday I have seen Susan shed tears of regret into Brussels sprouts because she had been debarred by pressure of other duties from lately watering “his” grave, which, I gathered, was at Manor Park. On Tuesday I have listened, blood chilled, to her recital of what she meant to do should she ever again enjoy the luxury of getting her fingers near the scruff of his neck.

“But I thought, Susan, he was dead,” was my very natural comment.

“So did I, Master Paul,” replied Susan. “That was his artfulness.”

“Then he isn’t buried in Manor Park Cemetery?”

“Not yet; but he’ll wish he was, the half-baked monkey, when I get hold of him.”

“Then he wasn’t a good man?”

“Who?”

“Your husband.”

“Who says he ain’t a good man?” Her flying leaps from tense to tense bewildered me more than irregular verbs. “If anybody says he ain’t I’ll gouge their eye out!”

I hastened to assure her I had been enquiring, not asserting.

“Brings me a bottle of gin—for my headaches—every time he comes home,” she continued, as if producing documentary evidence. “Every blessed time.”

And at some such point I would retire to the clearer atmosphere of German grammar or mixed fractions, which at least did not threaten to gouge my eye out.

We suffered a good deal from Susan in one way and another; for, having regard to the admirable position of her heart, we all felt it our duty to overlook mere failings of the flesh—except Aunt Fan, who never pretended to be a sentimentalist.

“She’s a lazy hussy,” announced my aunt one morning while rinsing. “A gulping, snorting, lazy hussy, that’s what she is.”

There was some excuse for her indignation. It was then eleven o’clock and Susan was still sleeping off an attack of what she called “new-ralgy.”

“She has seen a good deal of trouble,” said my mother, wiping.

“And if she was my cook and housemaid,” replied my aunt, “she would see more, the slut!”

“She’s not a good servant in many respects,” admitted my mother, “but I think she’s good-hearted.”

“Oh, drat her heart,” snapped my aunt. “The right place for that heart of hers is on the doorstep. And that’s where I’d put it—and her and her box alongside it—if I had my way.”

Susan’s departure did occur not long afterwards, and it came with the suddenness of a pan left too long on the fire. It was on a Saturday night. My mother came upstairs pale.

“Luke,” she said, “do please run for the doctor.”

“What’s the matter?” asked my father.

“Susan,” gasped my mother. “She’s lying on the kitchen floor breathing in the strangest fashion and quite unable to speak.”

“I’ll go for Washburn,” said my father. “If I am quick I shall catch him at the dispensary.”

Five minutes later my father came back panting, followed by the doctor.

This was a big black-bearded man who had the knack of seeming bigger than he was—partly by sheer bulk, partly by the way he arrived as if the world were a door that needed slamming. He came down the kitchen stairs two at a time, shaking the whole house, and with him came a change in the sound of everything: the boards seemed to creak in a different key, the very air in the stairwell thickened as if it were bracing itself.

He brushed my mother aside, bent over the unconscious Susan—who lay on her back with her mouth wide open—rose, looked at my father and mother with their troubled faces, and then opened his own mouth.

Out of it came a roar of laughter the like of which I had never heard.

The next moment he had seized a pail half full of water and flung it over Susan. She opened her eyes and sat up, blinking and dripping like a surprised seal.

“Feeling better?” said the doctor, still holding the pail. “Have another dose?”

Susan began to gather herself for speech—the thick kind that comes from the bottom of the chest and not from the top of the mind—but before she could get out her first word he had pushed the three of us outside and slammed the door behind us.

From the top of the stairs we could hear Susan’s rancorous voice raging fiercer and fiercer, drowned every now and then by the man’s savage laughter. And whenever she flagged for breath he would yell encouragement, as if conducting a kennel.

“Bravo! Go it, my beauty, give it tongue! Bark, bark! I love to hear you.”

He applauded, clapped his hands, stamped his feet; the house became an instrument and he played it with noise. I remember thinking—though I had no words then for such thoughts—that there are sounds which feed you and sounds which merely fill you up. Aunt Fan’s cough had been “noise without,” a curtain drawn for decency. This was noise without mercy: a great industrial laughter, as if a whole factory had learned one joke and refused to stop repeating it.

“What a beast of a man,” said my mother.

“He is really a most interesting man when you come to know him,” explained my father.

“I don’t ever mean to know him,” replied my mother, stiffly.

But it is only concerning the past that we possess knowledge.

The riot below ceased at length. A new voice began—quiet, emphatic—speaking as if laying down terms in a language Susan could understand. Then we heard the doctor’s step again upon the stairs.

My mother held her purse open in her hand. As he entered she went forward with that trembling severity women use when they mean to be dignified and are uncertain whether the world will assist them.

“How much do we owe you, Doctor?” she asked.

He closed the purse and gently pushed it back.

“A glass of beer and a chop, Mrs. Kelver,” he answered, “which I am coming back in an hour to cook for myself.”

My mother stood staring, as if he had demanded a hymn-book.

“And as you will be without any servant,” he continued, “you had better let me cook some for you at the same time. I am an expert at grilling chops.”

“But, really, Doctor—” began my mother.

He laid his huge hand upon her shoulder, and my mother sat down upon the nearest chair as if the hand had turned her into furniture.

“My dear lady,” he said, “she’s a person you never ought to have had inside your house. She’s promised me to be gone in half an hour, and I’m coming back to see she keeps her word. Give her a month’s wages, and have a clear fire ready for me.”

And before my mother could reply he had slammed the front door. Even his leaving was loud.

“What a very odd sort of a man,” said my mother, when she recovered breath enough for criticism.

“He’s a character,” said my father. “You might not think it, but he’s worshipped about here.”

“I hardly know what to make of him,” said my mother. “I suppose I had better go out and get some chops,” which she did.

Susan went, sober as a judge on Friday, her great anxiety being to get out before the doctor returned; she fled his laughter as if it were a dog that had smelled her. The doctor himself arrived true to his time, and I lay awake—for no human being ever slept, or even quite remembered the desire to sleep, while Dr. Washburn was anywhere near—and listened to gusts of laughter sweeping through the house like sudden squalls down the chimney.

Even my aunt laughed that supper time. When the doctor laughed it seemed to me the bed shook under me. Not liking to be out of it, I did what spoilt little boys—and even spoilt little girls—sometimes do under similar stress: I wrapped the blanket round my legs and pattered downstairs, setting my face to express the sudden desire of a sensitive and possibly short-lived child for parents’ love.

My mother pretended to be angry, but that was only her company manners. Besides, I really had—if not exactly a pain—an extremely uncomfortable sensation (one common to me about that period) as of having swallowed the dome of St. Paul’s.

The doctor said it was frequent with children—the result of too early hours and too much study—and, taking me on his knee, he wrote then and there a diet chart for me, as if prescribing a new religion.

It included one tablespoonful of golden syrup four times a day, and one ounce of sherbet to be placed upon the tongue and taken neat ten minutes before each meal.

Even as a child I understood the importance of language. There are grown people who can offer you affection and still make it sound like a bill. Dr. Washburn could offer you syrup and sherbet and make it sound like a feast, like a secret heirloom passed down in the only dialect that mattered—taste.

That evening will always live in my remembrance. My mother was brighter than I had ever seen her. A flush sat on her cheek and a sparkle in her eye. She held a small painted screen to shield her face from the fire, and looking across at her I felt something new wake in me—the sense of beauty, born not from pictures but from the way firelight makes a face into a story.

I slipped down from the doctor’s knee, blanket still round me, and squatted on the edge of the fender where I could steal furtive glances up into her face when I thought no one was noticing. The room smelt of hot iron and singed fat, of beer opened and settling, of chops waiting their turn. In that smell there was comfort more convincing than any sermon: the promise that, for the next few minutes at least, nobody would go hungry.

My father too seemed bigger and more dignified, talking with a vigour and enjoyment that sat newly on him. Aunt Fan was quite witty and agreeable—for her. Even I asked one or two questions at which, for some reason, everybody laughed; which determined me to remember and ask those same questions again on some future occasion, as one keeps a successful seasoning.

That was the great charm of the man: by the magnetic spell of his magnificent vitality he drew from everyone their best. In his company clever people waxed into intellectual giants, while the dull sat amazed at their own originality. Conversing with him, Podsnap might have been piquant and Dogberry incisive.

But best of all was listening to his own talk. Of what he spoke I could tell you no more than the children of Hamelin could have told the tune the Pied Piper played. I only know that at the tangled music of his strong voice the walls of that mean room faded away, and beyond I saw a brave laughing world calling to me—a world full of joyous fight, where some won and some lost, and it mattered not a jot because whatever came of it there was a right royal game for all; a world where merry gentlemen feared neither life nor death, and Fate was but Master of the Revels.

And yet even then, beneath his laughter, I heard something else: the power of a voice to dominate a room, to make other voices fall into line or fall silent. Aunt Fan’s cough, my mother’s careful severity, Susan’s thick threats—each had been its own dialect, and here was a man who could translate them all into the one booming language everybody understood, whether they liked it or not. It was thrilling and slightly alarming, like hearing a brass band march through your own head.

Such was my first introduction to Dr. Washburn—or, to give him the name by which he was known in every slum and alley of that quarter, Dr. Fighting Hal. In a minor key that evening was an index to the whole man. Often he would wrinkle his nose like a dog before it bites, and then he was more brute than man—brutish in instinct, appetite, pleasure, fun. Or his deep blue eyes would grow soft as a mother’s, and then you might have thought him an angel in a soft felt hat and a coat so loose-fitting as to suggest the possibility of wings folded away underneath.

Often I have tried to make up my mind whether it has been better for me or worse that I ever came to know him; but as easy would it be for the tree to say whether the rushing winds and the wild rains have shaped it or mis-shaped.

Susan’s place remained vacant for some time. My mother explained to the few friends who occasionally came from afar to see us that her “housemaid” she had been compelled suddenly to discharge, and that we were waiting for the arrival of a new and better specimen. But the months passed and we still waited; and my father, on the rare days when a client would ring the office bell, would after pausing a decent interval open the front door himself, then call downstairs indignantly and loudly to know why “Jane” or “Mary” could not attend to their work.

And my mother, that the bread-boy or the milkman might not put it about the neighbourhood that the Kelvers in the big corner house kept no servant, would hide herself behind a thick veil and fetch all things herself from streets a long way off, as if food purchased near home tasted of disgrace.

For this family of whom I am writing were, I confess, weak and human. Their poverty they were ashamed of as though it were a crime; and in consequence their life was full of paltry and useless subterfuge. The larder, I fancy, was very often bare, but the port and sherry with the sweet biscuits stood always on the sideboard; and the fire had often to be low in the grate that my father’s tall hat might shine resplendent and my mother’s black silk rustle on Sundays.

But I would not have you sneer, thinking all pretence must spring from snobbishness and never from mistaken self-respect. There are fine gentlemen writers—men whose world is bounded on the east by Bond Street—who see in the struggles of poverty to hide its darns only matter for jest. I cannot laugh. I know the long hopes and fears that centre round the hired waiter; the long cost of the cream and the ice jelly ordered the week before from the confectioner’s. To me it is pathetic, not ridiculous. Heroism is not all of one pattern. Dr. Washburn, had the Prince of Wales come to see him, would have put his bread and cheese and jug of beer upon the table and helped His Royal Highness to half. But my father and mother’s tea was very weak that Mr. Jones or Mr. Smith might have a glass of wine should they come to dinner.

I remember the one egg for breakfast: my mother arguing that my father should have it because he had business to attend to; my father insisting that my mother should eat it, she having to go out shopping; a compromise being effected by their dividing it between them, each clamouring for the white as the most nourishing. The language of “nourishing” in our house was often only another way of saying “you first,” and there are sacrifices that cannot be translated without losing their taste.

However little the meal looked upon the table when we started, I always rose well satisfied.

To me this life came as a good time. That I was encouraged to eat treacle in preference to butter seemed admirable. Treacle has a smell like Sunday fairs and sticky fingers; butter is merely butter. Personally, I preferred sausages for dinner; and a supper of fried fish and potatoes, brought in stealthily in a carpet-bag, was infinitely more enjoyable than the set meal where nothing was of interest till one came to the dessert. The fish would steam up the bag with its vinegar breath; the potatoes would sweat and soften; and the whole thing had the excitement of contraband, as if we were smuggling a banquet past the customs officers of respectability.

What fun there was about it all. The cleaning of the doorstep by night, when from the ill-lit street a gentleman with a piece of sacking round his legs might very well pass for a somewhat tall charwoman. I would keep watch at the gate to give warning should any one that looked like a possible late caller turn the corner of the street, coming back now and then in answer to a low whistle to help my father grope about in the dark for the hearthstone. He was always mislaying the hearthstone, as if it too were ashamed to be found in such company.

How much better, helping to clean knives or running errands, than wasting all one’s morning dwelling upon the shocking irregularity of certain classes of French verbs; or making useless calculations as to how long X, walking four and a quarter miles an hour, would be overtaking Y, whose powers were limited to three and a half but who had started two and three quarter hours sooner—the whole argument reduced to sheer pedantry by reason of no information being afforded concerning the respective thirstiness of X and Y.

Even my father and mother were able to take it lightly, with plenty of laughter and no groaning that I ever heard. For over all lay the morning light of hope; and what prisoner, escaping from his dungeon, ever stayed to think of his torn hands and knees when beyond the opening he could see sunlight glinting through brambles?

“I had no idea,” said my mother, “there was so much to do in a house. In future I shall arrange for the servants to have regular hours, and a little time to themselves, for rest. Don’t you think it right, Luke?”

“Quite right,” replied my father. “And I’ll tell you another thing we’ll do. I shall insist on the landlord’s putting a marble doorstep to the next house we take; you pass a sponge over marble and it is always clean.”

“Or tesselated,” suggested my mother.

“Or tesselated,” agreed my father. “But marble is more uncommon.”

Only once can I recall a cloud, and it came—like many clouds—as a culinary proposal.

It was a Sunday. We were in the middle of dinner when my mother, speaking across the table, said, “We might save the rest of that stew, Luke; there’s an omelette coming.”

My father laid down his spoon very gently, as if an egg had cracked inside him.

“An omelette!”

“Yes,” said my mother. “I thought I would like to try again.”

My father stepped into the back kitchen—we dined in the kitchen as a rule; it saved much carriage—and returned with the wood chopper.

“What ever are you going to do, Luke, with the chopper?” asked my mother.

“Divide the omelette,” replied my father.

My mother began to cry.

“Why, Maggie—!” said my father, frightened at once, as men are frightened when a joke hits a place it was not aimed for.

“I know the other one was leathery,” sobbed my mother, “but it was the fault of the oven, you know it was, Luke.”

“My dear,” said my father, “I only meant it as a joke.”

“I don’t like that sort of joke,” said my mother. “It isn’t nice of you, Luke.”

To be candid, my mother did not like jokes against herself. Indeed, when I come to think of it, I have never met a woman who did, nor man either. Some words cannot be made harmless by laughter; they keep their edge like a badly washed knife.

There had grown up a comradeship between my father and myself, for he was the youngest thing I had met with as yet. Sometimes my mother seemed young, and later I met boys and girls nearer to my own age; but they grew, while my father remained always the same. The hair about his temples was turning grey, and when you looked close you saw crow’s-feet and lines, especially about the mouth; but his eyes were the eyes of a boy, his laugh the laugh of a boy, his heart the heart of a boy. So we were very close.

In a narrow strip of ground we called our garden we would play a cricket of our own, encompassed about by novel rules made necessary by locality: all hitting to leg forbidden as endangering neighbouring windows; hitting to off likewise discouraged as causing a temporary adjournment while batter and bowler went through the house and out into the street to recover the ball from some predatory crowd of urchins to whom it had evidently appeared as a gift direct from Heaven.

Sometimes, rising very early, we would walk across the marshes to bathe in a small creek that led down to the river—muddy work, necessitating much washing of legs on the return. And on rare days we would take the train to Hackney and walk to the bridge, and row up the river Lea, perhaps as far as Ponder’s End. And always, whether it was mud on the legs or treacle on the spoon, there ran beneath our days that mingled music of laughter and footsteps and doors, the sound of a household doing its best to be brave—and to sound, if possible, a little grander than it felt.
