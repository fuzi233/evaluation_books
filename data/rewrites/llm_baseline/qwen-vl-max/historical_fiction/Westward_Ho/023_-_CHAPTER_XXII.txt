Three weeks have passed since the storm of battle and bloodshed on the coast of New Granada. The world has shifted, not in geography alone, but in spirit—darkened by the weight of silence, the cold breath of authority, and the shadow of a faith that brooks no dissent. We now find ourselves in Cartagena, within the grim confines of a long, low corridor lined with cells, each one a tomb waiting to be sealed. One door stands ajar. Inside, two figures stand in the dim glow of a single lamp: one cloaked in black, the other draped in the somber robes of the Holy Office.

The man in black is Eustace Leigh. The other, an agent of the Inquisition, holds the lamp aloft, its flickering light casting long shadows across the straw-strewn bed where a young man lies asleep. His face is pale, his brow high and noble—features we know well. It is Frank, saved from death at the hands of savage men only to be delivered into the hands of those who claim to speak for God.

He was tortured today. The question had been administered; the body broken, though the soul remained unshaken. Now, Eustace has come—not as a friend, nor yet as a foe—but as a servant of duty, compelled by a cause he cannot deny. He must wake Frank and urge him to recant. Perhaps coax him. Perhaps threaten. The Inquisitors call it mercy—"while his heart is still tender from the torture." But Eustace knows better. He knows the cost of such words.

And yet—how calmly the youth sleeps! Is it merely the play of light upon his lips? Or does a faint smile linger there, as if in dreams he sees something beyond this prison?

Eustace leans closer, the lamp trembling in his hand. And then—he hears it. A whisper, soft and clear: “Mother… Mary…” The name rises like a prayer, pure and true. Eustace freezes. He cannot bring himself to wake him. His voice, barely audible, breaks the silence: “Let him rest. I fear my words will do no good.”

The Inquisitor nods. “I fear so too, sir. Never have I seen a more stubborn heretic. He mocked them openly, even in their own court.”

“Ah,” murmurs Eustace, “great indeed is the depravity of the human heart—and the power of Satan.” He turns away. “Let us go—for now.”

They walk down the corridor, passing cell after cell, until the Inquisitor pauses. “Where is she?”

“The elder sorceress—or the younger?”

“The younger—the Senora de Soto?”

“A poor creature,” says the Inquisitor, watching Eustace closely. “One might pity her, were she not a heretic.”

Eustace shudders. “She is with the notary?” he asks, barely able to breathe.

“Yes,” replies the man, pointing to a door ahead. “To the benefit of her soul, I trust.”

Eustace stops short. His knees tremble. His heart pounds against his ribs. A terrible curiosity overcomes him—though he knows full well that any sign of sympathy could be used against him. Still, he presses close to the door, leaning against the wall, hiding his face from his companion.

Inside, voices rise and fall. The notary speaks in measured tones, rehearsing the old charge—witchcraft. A familiar tactic, used not only to justify cruelty but to appease the mob with the illusion of justice.

Then—a woman’s voice, sharp with pain and fury:
“Witchcraft against Don Guzman? What need of that, oh God! What need?”

A pause. Then the notary again: “You deny it, senora? We are sorry for you—but—”

And then—silence, broken only by choking sobs, by muffled cries that might mean nothing or everything.

“She has confessed!” whispers Eustace, half-relieved, half-destroyed. “Saints, I thank you—she—”

But no. A wail—sharp, piercing, echoing through the stones like a cry from hell itself. It tears through Eustace’s mind. He would break the door open, rush in—but his companion drags him back. Another wail. And another. The sound follows him like vengeful spirits, chasing him through the vaulted halls.

He stumbles out into the night air—warm, fragrant, drenched in moonlight. Before him stretches a garden so lush, so perfect, it could be Eden reborn. Yet no peace comes. The wails remain. They ring in his ears, in his bones, in his very soul.

“Oh, misery, misery, misery!” he groans, teeth clenched, fists clenched, body shaking. “I brought her here. I had to. Who else could I have sent? Who dares blame me? And yet—what sin have I committed that demands such punishment? Was there no one else? No one?”

Yet still, a faint hope clings: *It may save her soul. It may bring her to repentance.*

“Indeed it may,” replies the Inquisitor, calm and cold. “She is delicate. She cannot endure much. You should know that, senor. The Holy Office is merciful—when mercy serves the cause.”

“I know it,” Eustace gasps, trembling now—not just for her, but for himself. “All in love… all in love… a paternal chastisement…”

“And the proofs are clear,” continues the Inquisitor. “Heresy, yes. Suspicion of enchantment. And the elder sorceress—well known. You yourself told us she was a notorious witch in England before the senora brought her here.”

“Of course,” Eustace says, voice breaking. “Of course. There was no other way. Though the flesh may be weak… the spirit—” He trails off, unable to finish.

That night, Eustace goes to the Jesuit superior. He pours out his soul—what he can bear to confess. He begs to complete his novitiate, to enter the order, on one condition: that he be sent far away—back to Europe, anywhere—*immediately*. “Otherwise,” he says, “I shall go mad. I am already mad.”

The Jesuit, kind-hearted, takes pity. He speaks with the Inquisitors, presents Eustace’s past service as proof of loyalty, clears his name. His testimony is no longer needed. That same night, Eustace leaves Cartagena. The next week, he sails—no one knows where.

I know not whither. Eustace Leigh vanishes from these pages. He may have risen to lead his order. He may have spent his life in some distant jungle, preaching salvation to Indians—conquering their souls, perhaps their bodies too. He may have returned to England and become the very Ballard hanged and quartered three years later for his part in Babington’s conspiracy.

I know not.

This book is not about legends or saints. It is about men—men caught between duty and conscience, virtue and sin, courage and cowardice. Eustace was once such a man. But now—he is no longer. He has become a thing. A tool. A Jesuit. Sent where he is told, doing what he is bid—good or evil, without choice, without guilt, without soul.

For he has committed moral suicide. He has killed his conscience, believing it would save his soul. He has surrendered his will, his responsibility, his humanity—all to serve “The Society.”

In truth, Eustace is dead. Twice dead, perhaps. Let the dead bury their dead.

We have no further concern with Eustace Leigh.
