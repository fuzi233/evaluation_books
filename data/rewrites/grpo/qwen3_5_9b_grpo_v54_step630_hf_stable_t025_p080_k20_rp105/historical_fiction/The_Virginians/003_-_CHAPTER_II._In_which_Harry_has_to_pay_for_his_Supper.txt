The heavy iron knocker of Castlewood rang out a solitary, defiant sound against the silence of the ancestral house, but no soul within seemed inclined to answer. When the servant finally emerged, his face a mask of indifference, he treated the news that the visitor was a relation of the family with the same casual disregard one might offer a stray dog. The household was in disarray; the masters were away, and in their absence, John, the steward, cared little for kinship. His mind was fixed on the card game waiting for him in the window-seat with Thomas. Meanwhile, the housekeeper was already bustling, preparing the grand reception for the arriving lords and ladies.

Only through persistent pleading did Harry manage to glimpse the interior—the sitting-room and the picture-gallery. There, framed in gold, stood the portraits of his lineage: his grandfather in a powdered wig and breastplate, a mirror image of the man he had known in Virginia; and his grandmother, Lady Castlewood, depicted in the garb of Charles II., her neck bare, her golden hair cascading in ringlets that Harry remembered once seeing snow-white. Yet, the sight of these honored ancestors could not warm the chill in his heart. With a scowl, the housekeeper drove him away, citing the imminent arrival of the Countess, the Lord, his brother, the young ladies, and the Baroness, who was to claim the state bedroom. Harry scribbled his name on a scrap from his pocket-book and left it on the hall table: *Henry Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood, Virginia, arrived yesterday—staying at the Three Castles.*

The lackeys, abandoning their cards to collect their tips, opened the door. Gumbo, the gatekeeper, stepped down from his bench to take Harry's guinea, a gesture of charity he barely understood. Walking away from the house of his fathers, Harry felt a profound disappointment settle over him. He had seen little kindness in the entire estate save for the fleeting, unselfish smile of little Polly. Here, in the shadow of his own heritage, he was reduced to asking for bacon and eggs at a roadside alehouse.

Later, after a solitary dinner, Harry sat upon the bridge, watching the sun dip below the old house as the rooks returned to their elm nests. His imagination wandered to the knights and cavaliers of old, the huntsmen of King Charles's reign, and the ghost of his lost brother, George. The memory of his dear companion struck him with such tenderness that he hung his head, grief renewing itself in his chest. Suddenly, the peace was shattered. Two young men on horseback dashed across the bridge, one swearing at Harry to move aside, the other pushing him roughly as if to send him over the parapet. They were followed swiftly by livery servants who glared with a "British welcome" of pure hostility, and then by a heavy coach-and-six.

Three pale, handsome faces peered out at Harry as the carriage crossed the bridge, ignoring his salute to the family arms. The gentleman seated behind the carriage glared haughtily. Harry felt a crushing loneliness. The inn-keepers confirmed his fears: the passengers were the Countess, her stepdaughter Lady Maria, her daughter Lady Fanny, and the young gentlemen, Mr. William and the Lord. It was the Lord who had sworn loudest, and it was Mr. William in the grey frock who had nearly driven Harry into the ditch.

Harry had refused to unpack his trunks, hoping for an invitation to the great house. Hours passed in vain. Just as darkness fell, a second carriage arrived, carrying a stout, high-colored lady with dark eyes—the Baroness Bernstein, the Lord's aunt. Soon, the entire retinue vanished behind the castle gates, leaving Harry alone in the inn, consumed by a cruel sense of neglect. He could not sleep, disturbed by the raucous laughter and shouting from the bar downstairs.

It was only when Gumbo tried to block the door that the chaos truly began. A shrill, drunken voice cursed Gumbo, pushing past him into Harry's room.

"Beg your pardon, Cousin Warrington!" cried the intruder, stumbling over his words. "Are you asleep? I beg your pardon for riding you over on the bridge. Didn't know you... course shouldn't have done it. Thought it was a lawyer with a writ. Gad! Thought it was Nathan come to nab me." It was Mr. William, visibly intoxicated.

"You did me great honor to mistake me for a sheriff's officer, cousin," Harry replied, sitting up in his nightcap with grave dignity.

"Gad! I thought it was Nathan, and was going to send you souse into the river," William laughed incoherently. "But I ask your pardon. You see, I had been drinking at the Bell at Hexton... Hullo! Davis! A bowl of punch!"

"I have had my share for tonight, cousin," Harry said firmly.

"You want me to go, Cousin What's-your-name?" William demanded, his voice rising. "You want me to go, and they want me to come, and I didn't want to come. I said I'd see him hanged first. Why should I trouble myself to look after a fellow I don't care a pin for? Zackly what I said. Zackly what Castlewood said. Why the devil should he go down? Castlewood says, and so said my lady, but the Baroness would have you. It's all the Baroness's doing. So you must just get up and come."

Mr. Esmond spoke with amiable rapidity, tacking about the room, but Harry was in a fury. "I tell you what, cousin," he cried, "I won't move for the Countess, or for the Baroness, or for all the cousins in Castlewood."

When the landlord entered with the punch, William screamed at him to turn the "sot" out, calling him a "Cherokee" and threatening to drive his sword through his body. The scene devolved into a cacophony of oaths until the household staff dragged the drunkard away. Harry closed his tent around himself in sulky wrath, finally succumbing to sleep.

The next morning, the landlord was obsequious, having learned Harry's name and quality. He reported that Mr. William had been returned in a wheelbarrow, claiming the young gentleman was of a "real kind nature" despite beating the servants the night before.

Meanwhile, within the walls of Castlewood, a domestic council had convened. The family found Harry's note and debated his reception. Lord Castlewood reasoned that since they hadn't drowned him on the bridge, they must invite him. Lady Fanny suggested one of the brothers should go personally to apologize for their rude greeting. Lord Castlewood agreed to send his brother William. William, however, declared he would be hanged if he went. Lady Maria, however, found the American "pretty enough," noting that her brothers were dreadfully dull and that Harry might be vulgar, but worth seeing. Lady Castlewood, the matriarch, opted for delay, suggesting they wait for the Baroness's return. Thus, Harry's hospitality remained in abeyance.

When the Baroness Bernstein finally arrived, the atmosphere shifted. The family, usually indifferent, suddenly displayed enthusiasm for their wealthy kinswoman. The dining room was set with old plate, the walls lined with portraits of the Esmond line. The Baroness, full of spirits and humor, recounted wild stories of Europe, causing the demure Countess to smile and the young people to giggle. She drank freely of champagne and punch, pointing out the empty chair where her father once sat, lamenting that the old plate had been melted down to pay gambling debts.

Her conversation turned to the family history. When the Lord laughed at the Virginian's utility, the Baroness struck the table with such force that the glasses danced. "I say he was the best of you all," she declared, pointing to the portrait of Colonel Esmond. "There never was one of the male Esmonds that had more brains than a goose, except him. He was not fit for this wicked, selfish old world of ours, and he was right to go and live out of it."

Excited by the wine, she pressed the topic further. "Who has been here to-day?" she demanded, her face flushed. "Harry Esmond Warrington, of Virginia," the Lord admitted. "A lad whom Will nearly pitched into the river."

"You mean that a grandson of Henry Esmond... has been here, and none of you have offered him hospitality?" the Baroness cried, her indignation mounting.

"Since we didn't know it," Will stammered.

"That he is staying at the Inn, and you are sitting there!" the old lady shrieked. "This is too bad! Call somebody to me. Get me my hood—I'll go to the boy myself."

The young men rose in wrath. "Madame the Baroness of Bernstein," the Lord declared, "your ladyship is welcome to go; but as for me, I don't choose to have such words as 'shameful' applied to my conduct. I won't go and fetch the young gentleman from Virginia."

The Baroness looked at the family, who held their heads down, and then at the Lord. Leaning forward, she whispered rapidly in German, "I had unright when I said the Colonel was the only man of the family. Thou canst, if thou willest, Eugene." The Lord bowed. She then commanded, "If you do not wish an old woman to go out at this hour, let William, at least, go and fetch his cousin."

The daughters cried out in agreement. The Lord relented, ordering William to put on his pattens and get a lantern. William, already drunk, agreed to fetch the Virginian and promised another bowl of punch upon his return.

When the Baroness finally caught sight of Harry by the riverside, she saw a handsome, interesting youth of nineteen. Though she had her own reasons for hesitating to encourage such a match, her generous nature, warmed by the wine, overrode her caution. She had dispatched a note earlier, signed simply "B. Bernstein," inviting him to "Colonel Esmond's house in England."

Harry approached, hat in hand, his fair hair blowing from his flushed cheeks, clad in mourning. He made a low bow that would have done credit to Versailles. The Baroness held out her hand, placing her other hand softly on his ruffle.

"I knew your grandfather very well, Harry," she said gently. "So you came yesterday to see his picture, and they turned you away, though you know the house was his of right?"

Harry blushed crimson. "The servants did not know me. A young gentleman came to me last night," he admitted, "when I was peevish, and he, I fear, was tipsy. I spoke rudely to my cousin, and would ask his pardon. Your ladyship knows that in Virginia our manners towards strangers are different. I own I had expected another kind of welcome."

"I sent him," the Baroness assured him, her eyes kind. "But you will find your cousins most friendly to you to-day. You must stay here. Lord Castlewood would have been with you this morning, only I was so eager to see you. There will be breakfast in an hour; and meantime you must talk to me."

She took his arm, dropping her cane in the process, and jokingly declared, "You shall be my cane."

"My grandfather used to call us his crutches," Harry replied.

"You are like him, though you are fair," she observed.

"You should have seen—you should have seen George," the boy said, his honest eyes welling with tears. The bitterness of yesterday's humiliation, the affection of this present greeting, and the sheer relief of being welcomed softened his heart. He felt tenderly grateful to the lady who had opened her home to him.

In the hour they spent together, Harry poured out his honest heart to this new friend. When breakfast time arrived, she led him to the room, presenting him to his aunt, the Countess, and bidding him embrace his cousins. Lord Castlewood was frank and gracious; honest Will, suffering from a hangover, was utterly unconscious of the night's disgrace. The ladies were pleasant and polite, masking their fury at Harry's presence and their awe of the Baroness's favor.

Harry, a simple truth-teller from a distant colony, unaware of the social currents beneath the surface, believed he had found a home. The Baroness, however, was *folle* of him, talking of no one else, trotting him through the house, showing him the room where his grandfather slept and the cupboard made during the Catholic persecutions. She drove him out into the countryside, pointing out remarkable sites, while he, in return, told her his entire story. It was a brief biography, cast not in his own precise words, but in the form it would take in the chapters to follow.
