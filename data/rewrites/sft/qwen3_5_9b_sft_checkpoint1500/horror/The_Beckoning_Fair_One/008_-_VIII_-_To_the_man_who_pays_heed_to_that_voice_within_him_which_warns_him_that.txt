The shadow of twilight crept over Oleron's soul, heavy and suffocating. Usually, the human mind constructs a fortress of mundane customs—social niceties, routine observances, the trivialities of daily life—to keep the terrors of existence at bay. These petty walls are sturdy enough to hold back joy, let alone fear, unless the entire architecture of one's nature begins to crumble. For two months, Oleron had relied on these flimsy defenses, but something within him had shifted. He was beginning a treacherous act, a slow erosion of the boundaries that kept sanity intact. He was admitting the inexplicable and the horrific into the realm of the familiar, not by choice, but by a creeping numbness. What had once chilled his marrow—the notion of his beloved home being "haunted"—now provoked a weary, detached curiosity. *Haunted by what?* he wondered, the question tasting of ash. Horror, he realized with a sinking dread, lost its power when it became relative, measurable, and subject to proof. The landmarks of his reality were dissolving into mist.

Yet, beneath this fog, a voracious hunger gnawed at him. He needed to know. Not the comforting, vague reassurances of the superstitious, but the cold, hard facts. And strangely, the answers seemed to materialize before the questions were fully formed. Ideas rose from the depths of his consciousness, solidifying instantly into words that demanded to be written. It was a intoxicating rush, a return to the electric clarity of his youth, when writing felt like breathing. His mind, usually sluggish, now raced with a terrifying velocity.

To solve the mystery, he applied the only tool left to him: mathematics. If the old house was the variable, and he the constant, then the unknown quantity—the "other numerator"—must be defined. Where did he find the key? In the dust of his unfinished novels. Specifically, the *Romilly*.

For a lifetime, Oleron would have deemed it madness to link fiction to reality, to see the ghost in the ink. But now, the connection felt inevitable, almost predestined. He opened the pages of the first *Romilly*, the one abandoned fifteen chapters ago. The memories flooded back with violent clarity: the suffocating atmosphere of that manuscript, the urge to burn it, the desperate pivot to a new story, the *new Romilly*. And there, interwoven with the plot, lay the melody Mrs. Barrett had mocked—"The Beckening Fair One." The song that had haunted his dreams since childhood, a tune that seemed to whisper warnings from the grave.

As he traced the lines of the old novel, a chilling realization struck him. The protagonist, the *first* Romilly, had been a pale reflection of Elsie Bengough. He had hated that character with a passion that bordered on violence, feeling a strange, malignant rivalry whenever a stray thought touched upon her. And now, the nightmare was complete: the fictional hatred was bleeding into reality, poisoning his affection for Elsie herself. With a gasp, "Good God!" escaped his lips. The abstraction of his study shattered. He could not separate the equation anymore; Elsie's face filled the void where logic should have been.

The guilt of his recent actions with Barrett swam back to torment him. He remembered striding across the square, shouting down the old man's vagaries, accusing him of scandal-mongering. Barrett's face, pale and vindictive, lingered in Oleron's mind, peering from behind drawn curtains, waiting for evidence, waiting for a slip. Oleron had fled home, retreating deeper into his isolation, convinced that his domestic peace was fragile, easily torn apart by the tongues of neighbors and the whispers of the square.

But today, the silence was broken. A raw, gale-force wind tore through the streets, whipping the rain into stinging sheets. Oleron watched from his window as Elsie crossed the square, fighting the tempest. Her ample skirts ballooned around her legs, her veil streaming like a dark banner behind her. Panic, cold and sharp, seized Oleron. He grabbed his hat and sprinted downstairs, his heart hammering against his ribs. She must not enter the house. She must not step onto that cursed floor.

He intercepted her at the gate, his voice a frantic, disjointed stream. "Too bad, Elsie! Just as I'm leaving! I'm afraid I must go to town. Urgent business with Chambers. He's leaving for Brighton this afternoon. I received a letter this morning." He spun a web of lies, dragging her arm through the squall, leading her away from his door, away from the square. "We must catch the bus. The terminus is that way."

They walked in a daze, the wind biting at their exposed skin. On the crowded bus, Oleron sat stiffly, his face a mask of pallor. He felt the weight of his deception pressing down on him, but he pushed it aside, focusing on the truth he had to speak. He needed to break the spell before it consumed them both.

"Elsie," he began, his voice trembling, "I'm thinking of going away for a while. Somewhere for a change. Tomorrow, perhaps."

"Oh?" she replied simply.

"Yes. I need a change. I don't know how long I'll be gone. Let me know when I return."

She nodded, her gaze fixed ahead, unblinking. "Yes."

"You don't ask where I'm going," he pressed, hoping to provoke a reaction, a sign of life.

She looked past the driver, her expression unreadable. "I know."

Startled, he stared at her. "How do you know?"

"You're not going anywhere," she said, her voice steady, devoid of emotion. "You didn't intend to leave this morning. You only came out because I appeared. Don't pretend we are strangers, Paul."

A flush of shame burned his cheeks. He said nothing, the wind howling outside seeming to echo the storm inside his head.

"You ought to go away," she continued, her eyes softening slightly. "People are staring at you. You look terrible. But you won't go, and I know why."

He shivered, a dry cough rattling in his chest. "If you know, there's no point in discussing it."

"There is for you," she countered gently. "Shall I tell you what I know?"

"No," he whispered, the word escaping before he could stop it.

Her eyes widened, earnest and piercing. "No?"

"No."

Patience wore thin. The strain of her presence, her unwavering loyalty, was becoming unbearable. She represented everything he despised about the world of women who worked—a blend of independence and vulnerability, a hunger that was neither satisfied nor wise. She loved him, and it terrified him.

"I suppose," he murmured, looking down at his lap, "the trouble lies in the life women who earn their own living are forced to lead."

She gave a faint nod. "I suppose so."

"It can't be helped," he continued, his voice rising with desperation. "You sacrifice so much. You're in a false position. Innocent acts are twisted, motives attributed to you that you never harbored. And in the end, it all boils down to..." He paused, swallowing the bitter truth. "...to the sidelong look, the leer."

She shivered, the word striking a chord deep within her. "Barrett?"

Silence stretched between them, heavy and suffocating. Anything else he could have said died in his throat. Finally, the bus screeched to a halt, letting in fresh air and new passengers.

"You should get off here and go back, Paul," she said softly. "I understand perfectly. It isn't Barrett. You could handle Barrett. It's merely convenient for you to blame him. I know what it is... but you told me not to tell you. Very well."

She leaned forward, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Before you go, let me tell you why I came up this morning."

"Why?" he asked, his voice hollow.

"I came to force your hand," she said. "Things couldn't continue as they were. That chapter is closed."

"All over," he echoed, the words sounding foreign.

"All over. Consider yourself free, regarding me. I make one reservation."

He braced himself for the condition, but her words were different. "If *I* merely need *you*, forget it; I wouldn't come for that. But if *you* need *me*, Paul—if you are in need, and you will be—I shall come at whatever cost. You understand?"

He could only groan, a sound of profound defeat.

"That's settled," she said, standing up. "Go back. Walk, for you're shivering. Goodbye."

She handed him a cold hand, her grip firm and final. As the bus lurched forward, leaving the curb behind, Oleron stood frozen on the pavement. For the first time in years, Elsie did not smile, did not wave. She simply vanished into the crowd, leaving him alone with the weight of the truth and the terrifying silence of the empty square.
