Baldassarre wandered through Florence seeking the cheapest shelter, his path inevitably drawn toward the only uneven patch of land within the city walls. From this vantage point, elevated above the rooftops, one could glimpse the protective hills and the vast valley beyond, visible only through the gap carved by the Arno River. While part of this terrain was the famous stone quarry of Bogoli, Baldassarre ascended the slope descending behind the Via de' Bardi—the hill known locally as San Giorgio. Bratti had confirmed that Tito resided on this street, and Baldassarre hoped to find a hiding place among the scattered dwellings here. Having grown accustomed to the open sky over the years, the claustrophobic, narrow alleys of Florence intensified his feelings of isolation and the foggy decay of his memory.

The hill was largely garden land, dotted with sparse habitation, until he reached a jagged expanse of rough ground scarred by ancient landslides that had destroyed homes centuries prior. Perched atop this broken earth stood a peculiar, truncated tower-like structure capped with fluted tiles. Beside it lay a small outhouse wedged against a crumbling stone wall. Beneath the sprawling branches of a dying mulberry tree, whose final leaves drifted into the open doorways, an aged, weather-beaten woman untied a goat with two kids. One section of the outbuilding housed livestock, but the other stood empty save for a few tools and a bed of straw. It was exactly the refuge he needed.

He approached the old woman, but she remained oblivious until he shouted directly into her ear. Initially, she merely shook her head, muttering "No lodging" in the flat, muffled cadence of the deaf. Undeterred, Baldassarre persisted, explaining that he was a destitute stranger from overseas who could not afford an inn, offering merely a few *quattrini* weekly for permission to sleep on the straw. The woman regarded him with suspicion, whispering to herself, until a new idea sparked. She retrieved a hatchet, pointed to a log half-buried under debris in a corner, and offered him a bargain: if he chopped the wood, he might spend one night in the outhouse.

Agreeing immediately, Baldassarre began to work while Monna Lisa watched with arms akimbo, a smile of shrewd satisfaction playing on her lips. *"It's been lying there since my husband died,"* she murmured to herself. *"What difference does it make? I might as well throw a log on the fire. He chops well, despite his foreign tongue and odd appearance. I couldn't hire anyone cheaper. Besides, if he only needs straw, I can make him run errands up and down the hill. Who cares? Hidden sins are half-forgiven ('Peccato celato e mezzo perdonato'). He's a stranger; he won't notice *me*. I'll tell her to keep quiet."*

Her thoughts drifted to a pair of bright blue eyes currently fixed upon a large circular hole in the shutter of the upper window. The shutter was closed not out of malice, but because the opposing window boasted glass—a rare luxury. Since the weather was cool, the four-inch aperture sufficed for spying. Unfortunately, the hole was positioned too high, forcing the small observer to balance precariously on a rickety stool. Yet, Tessa would endure any discomfort for the thrill of watching the world. Drawn by the strange voice earlier, she had crept to the opening and remained there until the wood was split and Baldassarre entered the darkness of the outhouse, settling onto the straw as twilight fell.

A powerful urge seized Tessa. She wanted to share her supper with the old man, to converse with someone who wasn't deaf like Monna Lisa. Strangers were fascinating; they traveled from afar and vanished, living in no fixed place. Though she knew it was disobedience, and thus a sin requiring confession at Easter alongside her occasional napping during prayer, the temptation was overwhelming. There was nothing else to confess anyway, and she possessed ample idle time. With a flushed, pouting face, she turned from the window. Dressed in the attire of a peasant girl prepared for a festival—a clean dark-green serge gown with a red sash, red glass beads around her neck, and her curly brown hair neatly pinned with silver—she wore a single new pride: a fine gold ring. Sitting on her stool, her heart fluttered with the excitement of a planned transgression. She had been strictly forbidden to make friends, warned that doing so would turn her happiness into lead, yet she had obeyed so thoroughly that she kept her face veiled in church. This old man, however, was neither a friend nor a threat; he was a sleeping stranger who likely knew nothing of her cruel stepfather, Nofri. If she fed him, he would be grateful and silent. Monna Lisa's objections would be dismissed simply because Monna Lisa was deaf.

Supper was ready: a mountain of cheesy macaroni, fragrant enough to charm any guest. Tripping downstairs with a lantern in one hand and a wooden bowl in the other, Tessa knocked on the outhouse door. Inside, Baldassarre, lost in melancholy, blinked in surprise as the light revealed a tiny maid bursting with joy.

"I've brought you supper!" she cried, leaning close to shout into his ear as if addressing the deaf. "Eat while I stay with you."

Distrust warred with surprise, but Baldassarre found no reason to reject the gesture. He sank back onto the straw as Tessa placed the bowl on his lap, set down the lantern, and crossed her hands, nodding significantly. *"Yes, you may truly eat it,"* her smile conveyed. In her haste, she had momentarily forgotten that he could hear, falling back on her habit of dramatic gestures and shouting.

The offer was welcome; he had been gnawing dry bread and craved warmth. Tessa watched him eat silently, noting that his initial fierce gaze had softened. Venturing closer, she whispered, "Do you like my supper?"

Baldassarre returned a look of gentle pity, akin to a kindly dog unable to smile. "Yes," he replied softly. "But I can hear well. I am not deaf."

"It is true; I forgot," Tessa admitted, clasping her hands delightedly. "But Monna Lisa is deaf. She is kind, and I am not afraid of her. We live well; I can have nuts whenever I wish. I used to work, but I hated it. I loved feeding the mules, especially poor Giannetta. Now we only have a goat and two kids. I used to talk to the goat because Monna Lisa was always busy. But now I have something else... can you guess?"

She tilted her head, challenging him with a mischievous grin.

"No," Baldassarre answered dreamily, setting aside his bowl. The girl reminded him of memories from his own youth.

"You like me to talk to you, don't you?" Tessa pressed. "But you must promise not to tell anyone. Should I fetch some cold sausage?"

He shook his head, appearing mild and harmless. Encouraged, Tessa continued, "Well, then, I have a little baby. Such a lovely *bambinetto*, with tiny fingers and nails! It was born at Christmas, Monna Lisa says. I was married one Christmas, a long time ago, and nobody knew. Oh, Santa Madonna! I didn't mean to tell you that!"

She stiffened, biting her lip as if expecting a shock, but Baldassarre seemed indifferent, perhaps due to the nature of strangers. "Yes," she persisted aloud, "you are a stranger. You live nowhere and know no one, do you?"

"No," Baldassarre murmured, thinking more than answering. "I only know one man."

Tessa's face instantly tightened with anxiety. "His name is not Nofri, is it?"

"No," Baldassarre noticed her fear and clarified.

"That your husband's name?" she asked, mistaking his caution for confirmation.

The error amused her greatly. Laughing and clapping her hands, she declared, "No, indeed! But I cannot tell you about my husband. You'd never imagine what he is—he isn't like Nofri at all!" She laughed again at the absurdity of comparing her husband to the gruff stepfather.

"But I don't see him often," she said seriously. "Sometimes I pray to the Holy Madonna to send him more frequently, and once she did. But I must return to my baby. I'll bring it tomorrow to show you. You'd like to see it. Sometimes it cries and makes faces, but only when hungry, Monna Lisa says. You wouldn't believe it, but Monna Lisa had babies once, and they are all dead old men. My husband says she will never die now because she is 'so well dried.' I am glad, for I love her. Would you like to stay here tomorrow?"

"I would like to have this place to rest in," Baldassarre said. "I will pay for it and cause no trouble."

"No, indeed. You are not a bad old man. But you look sorrowful. Is there something you will cry about when I leave you alone? I used to cry once."

"No, child. I think I shall cry no more."

"That is right. I'll bring you breakfast and show you the baby. Goodnight."
