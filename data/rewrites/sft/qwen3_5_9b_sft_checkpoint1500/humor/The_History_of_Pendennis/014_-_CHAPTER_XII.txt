A shooting match was proposed, or at least, a duel that smelled faintly of gin and bad decisions. The architect of this chaos was Captain Costigan, a man whose reputation for honor was as thin as his pockets, currently vibrating with the tremors of a mind intoxicated by "spirits-and-water." To execute his grand design, he required a heavy-handed ally, and he summoned Mr. Garbetts, the Principal Tragedian.

Garbetts was a mountain of a man, a jovial creature of irregular habits who shared a convivial club with Costigan at the Magpie Hotel. They were friends of a sort, bound by mutual loans of signature and a deep-seated hatred for Miss Fotheringay, the Captain's daughter. While Garbetts had once wooed the maiden and been soundly rejected, favoring instead the Captain's wife—a union that caused significant jealousy among the Garbett household—it mattered little now. Garbetts arrived promptly, delivered by a nimble errand-boy named Tommy, carrying two hot glasses of whisky-and-water that acted less as refreshment and more as liquid courage for a man planning to break pokers in sport.

Once fortified, the Captain dictated a letter to Major Pendennis. It was a document of pure theatrical melodrama, drafted in Costigan's finest style and sealed with the family's only remaining piece of silver plate. The contents were clear: the Major was to offer no obstruction to the marriage of his niece, Miss Fotheringay, to Arthur Pendennis. If he failed to comply, the Captain warned, he would be given "satisfaction" via a horsewhip—or, failing that, by the ancient dueling pistols lying on the desk, which had claimed many a "pretty fellow" in Dublin.

But the plan hit a snag almost before it began. Garbetts returned within twenty minutes, his face a mask of anxiety. He had seen Major Pendennis, but he could not deliver the missive. Standing in the Major's drawing room was a man named Tatham, a "minion" whom the Captain despised.

"I'll have nothing to do with fighting!" Garbetts declared, tossing the letter onto the floor with a flourish that defied gravity. "I'm a family man, Captain. Take back your letter."

The Captain, roaring like a bear denied honey, accused his friend of cowardice. "You're no better than a coward!" he shouted, channeling Sir Lucius O'Trigger. Yet, despite the Captain's fury and the presence of his murderous flintlock pistols, Garbetts stood firm. He refused to drag anyone into a brawl involving Tatham, and the two men parted in ill humor, the duel averted for the moment.

Back at the Costigan residence, the atmosphere was thick with the scent of alcohol and impending disaster. The Captain paced his apartment, surrounded by the debris of his intoxication: empty goblets and a teaspoon that had lost its utility. When Miss Fotheringay returned from rehearsal, accompanied by her faithful servant, Mr. Bows, she found her father in a state of agitated delirium.

"My child!" the Captain cried, seizing her in a grip that threatened to bruise. "Prepare yourself! A conspiracy! Both the Pendennis uncle and nephew are infernal traitors!"

Emily, usually the picture of grace, pushed back her sire's hands. "You're tipsy again, Papa," she chided gently. "You promised me no spirits before dinner."

"It is to drown my cares!" the Captain wailed, his eyes bloodshot. "That soft-spoken gentleman in the wig—he's a viper! He's tricked me! And that boy, Arthur... he's a beggar!"

Here lay the crux of the tragedy. The Captain, drunk on suspicion, believed Arthur Pendennis was a pauper who had deceived his daughter into loving him without financial security. He recounted how the Major had allegedly revealed Arthur's poverty, suggesting the youth was dependent on his mother's five hundred-a-year allowance. The Captain's rage was absolute; he spoke of tearing off the Major's wig and dragging him through the streets of London, or forcing him to draw sword in the field.

"But what has the poor fellow done?" Emily asked, her voice steady amidst the storm.

"He has outraged my feelings!" the Captain roared. "He represents himself as a man of property, and it turns out he is nothing! A pauper! An impostor!"

Emily sat down, retrieving a pair of ex-white satin shoes she intended to polish with breadcrumbs later. With a calmness that belied the chaos around her, she addressed the absurdity of her father's wrath.

"If he has no money," she reasoned, "there is no point in marrying him, Papa. But surely he wasn't a liar?"

"Poor lad!" the Captain lamented, clutching his pistol case. "He's a deceiver!"

"And if he isn't rich," Emily pressed, "then why does he look at me like that? Why does he bring me flowers and verses?"

Her father, fueled by drink, insisted that Arthur's lack of funds proved his treachery. He vowed to send a message to the Major demanding a showdown, or at the very least, a public humiliation in front of the county.

"Stop and take dinner, Bows," Emily suddenly announced, breaking the tension. "We have a beautiful beef-steak pudding."

Mr. Bows, a fiddler with a sharp tongue, watched her closely. He wondered if she was already plotting some clever dodge to avoid the duel. Indeed, Emily was not a romantic dreamer; she understood the practicalities of life far better than her hysterical father. She knew that if Arthur truly had no money, the match was dead anyway. And if he *did* have some secret fortune, his uncle's accusations were likely lies born of envy.

Over dinner, the Captain, now sobered slightly by the prospect of food, tried to maintain his stance. But Emily, with the cool judgment of someone unburdened by a strong imagination, dismantled his arguments. She pointed out that her father had deceived himself, projecting his own insecurities onto the boy. She ate her pudding with admirable satisfaction, pitying the poor Arthur in her heart, even as she prepared to play the role of the pragmatic parent.

"The boy is worthy," she said softly. "But if he cannot provide, we cannot go forward. And if he can, then perhaps..."

She rose from the table, walked to a drawer, and retrieved a bundle of letters and poems Arthur had written her. They were filled with Latin, fevers, and lonely nights, all penned with a generosity of spirit that cost nothing. She wrapped them carefully in paper, tying them with a neat string as if they were parcels of sugar.

"They are mine," she said, her voice devoid of emotion. "And when the time comes, I shall send them back. For now, they are just nonsense."

As she sat down to make tea, completely placid, she left behind the boy who was probably yearning over those same pages ten miles away, hugging her image to his soul. The Captain, meanwhile, rubbed his chin, wondering if he should really pull the Major's nose in public, or if the pudding had calmed his nerves enough to let the matter rest. The duel, it seemed, was postponed indefinitely, leaving the future of the love match hanging in the balance between a father's drunken fears and a daughter's quiet resolve.
