On an August morning, the air thick with the heat of a reclaimed summer, Harry Feversham rode across the Lennon Bridge into Ramelton. The suns of the Sudan had etched a permanent bronze into his skin, and the years of his probation had carved lines of experience deeper than mere age. Yet, as he ascended the narrow street, unseen and unacknowledged, he moved like a specter haunting his own history. At the crest of the hill, he veered onto the broad highway—a relentless artery cutting through valleys and summits toward Letterkenny—and spurred his horse forward. He rode swiftly, accompanied by a retinue of ghosts. The intervening years, once a consuming fire in his mind during his travels across Egypt and the continent, had cooled into distant embers, mere visions now. He carried no dreams of the paths not taken, for the road of regret was paved with the reality of what had been lost. This small Irish town was the crucible of his joy and the forge of his deepest misery; here he had given pleasure, and here he had inflicted his greatest harm.

Opposite the church, perched high on the road to his right, he paused. A flicker of hope ignited: *Is Ethne still here? Is old Dermod alive?* He imagined the reception awaiting him, but the illusion shattered instantly. He was merely a man astride a horse in an empty road, under the quiet watch of an August sky. Above, larks sang in pale blue; to the left, a landrail issued its harsh, earthy cry from the meadow; below, a rooster's crow pierced the valley air. Realizing the solitude of his position, he urged his mount onward, down the incline and up the rise beyond, riding again with his phantom companions—the echoes of laughter and footsteps shared on this very road.

His journey led him to a thick grove, a broken fence, and a gateway devoid of a gate. Ignoring the signs of abandonment, he turned inward, following a weedy, neglected drive that terminated before a ruined house. The crumbling walls and roofless chambers finally severed his trance. Dismounting, he tethered his horse to a gnarled branch and rushed into the derelict structure, shouting. Silence answered. Room by room, he wandered through the emptiness, descending into the garden where the uncut grass and tangled flowerbeds confirmed the truth: no one would greet him. Mounting his horse again, he retraced his steps at a sharp trot.

In Ramelton, he halted at the inn, handing his steed to the ostler and ordering lunch. When the landlady approached, he asked bluntly, "Has Lennon House been burned? When?"

"Five years ago," she replied, her tone casual. "Just five years ago this summer." Without prompting, she launched into a voluminous recitation of the conflagration, the ruin of the Eustace family, the drunkenness of Bastable, and the tragic death of Dermod Eustace at Glenalla. "We hope to see it rebuilt," she noted, her voice dripping with gossip regarding Miss Eustace's prospective marriage. Feversham offered no response to her eager speculation.

"And where does Miss Eustace dwell now?" he asked.

"At Glenalla," she informed him. "Halfway to Rathmullen, a track leads off to your left. It is a poor mountain village, truly unsuitable for a lady of her station. Might you wish to visit her?"

"Yes," Feversham said, rising abruptly. "Order my horse brought to the door."

As he prepared to leave, the landlady intercepted him at the threshold. "Curious, isn't it? Most curious. Only a fortnight ago, a gentleman—burnt just as black as yourself—stayed here seeking the same address. He drove straight to Glenalla. Perhaps you have business with her?"

"I have business with Miss Eustace," the stranger confirmed coolly. "Will you arrange for my horse?"

While waiting, he scanned the hotel register and spotted the name of Colonel Trench dated late July. As he mounted, the landlady pressed, "You will return tonight?"

"No," he answered, his resolve firm. "I do not intend to return to Ramelton." He rode down the hill, crossing the Lennon Bridge once more.

Four miles later, he reached a track bordering a small bay of the Lough. Turning inland, he passed scattered white cottages and climbed into a purple hollow among the hills. By five o'clock, he arrived at Glenalla. The settlement appeared desolate, unplanned, and quiet. Cottages clustered haphazardly, separated by gaps of field, leading to a small plantation of larches and a solitary house. Beyond lay another gap offering a view of the shimmering Lough, where white gulls circled lazily. Further on stood a small grey church, exposed to the winds on a tiny plateau. A path of white shell-dust wound from the church door to a wooden gate.

As he leveled with the gate, a collie dog barked from behind it. The rider studied the animal, noting the grey muzzle and distinctive markings, then halted. Glancing toward the church, he saw the door stood ajar. Instantly, he dismounted, secured his horse to the fence, and stepped into the churchyard. The dog thrust its muzzle into his knee, sniffed dubiously, and then erupted into an exuberant welcome. Its memory far superior to that of the innkeeper, the canine barked, wagged its tail, crouched, and leaped at the stranger's shoulders, spinning in circles of pure delight before rushing to the church door and returning to jump upon his friend.

Feversham caught the dog as it planted its forepaws on his chest, patting it and laughing. Suddenly, the laughter died. He froze, his gaze fixed on the open church door. Standing in the threshold was Ethne Eustace. He released the dog and walked slowly up the path toward her. She remained motionless, silent, watching him until he stood close.

"Harry," she said simply.

She fell silent thereafter, nor did he speak. The phantoms that had shadowed his journey dissolved at the sound of her name. Six years had passed since his boots crushed the gravel beneath her window on a June dawn. They regarded one another, noting the transformations wrought by time. Feversham was gaunt, his face wasted; the strain of life in the House of Stone had deepened the shadows around his eyes, aging him beyond his years. Yet, these were not the most profound changes. Ethne's heart ached with grief for his suffering, yet she perceived something vital: he returned without a trace of anger regarding the fourth feather. More importantly, she saw that the timid, diffident youth who had once feared the contempt of others was gone. In his stead stood a man of quiet self-possession, neither arrogant nor insecure. He had undergone a rigorous trial and emerged knowing he had not failed.

Her face softened as she declared, "It is not all harm that has come of these years. They were not wasted."

Feversham considered her lonely existence in this remote village, thinking with a man's instinct that she would have chosen no other refuge. Looking into her face, he saw the marks of endurance. She had not aged significantly; her large grey eyes shone with their former clarity, her cheeks retaining their color. But there was depth now, character forged in fire. She had suffered; she had tasted the bitter fruit of knowledge.

"I am sorry," he said. "I did you a great wrong six years ago, and I need not."

She extended her hand. "Will you give it to me, please?"

For a moment, confusion clouded his expression. "That fourth feather," she clarified.

He withdrew his letter case and shook two feathers into her palm. Holding out the larger ostrich plume, he awaited her command.

"Both," she insisted.

There was no necessity for him to retain Castleton's feather. Handing them both to her, he watched as she clasped them, smiling and pressing them against her breast. "I have the four feathers now," she announced.

"Yes," Feversham replied. "All four. What will you do with them?"

Ethne's smile transformed into a laugh of scorn. "Do with them!" she cried. "I shall do nothing with them. I shall keep them. I am very proud to possess them."

She kept them as she had once kept Harry Feversham's portrait. Perhaps, as Durrance had argued, women gathered their experiences more tenaciously than men, living upon them by looking backward. Feversham, conversely, would have discarded the feathers immediately, crushing them into the dust of the path with his heel. Their work was done; they could no longer reproach or encourage; they were dead things. But Ethne held them tightly; to her, they were alive.

"Colonel Trench visited a fortnight ago," she revealed. "He told me you were bringing it back."

"But he knew nothing of the fourth feather," Feversham corrected. "I never told any man I possessed it."

"Yes," she admitted. "You told Colonel Trench on your first night in the House of Stone at Omdurman. He told me. I no longer hate him," she added, her tone serious, devoid of irony, treating the sentiment as a fact requiring solemn acknowledgment.

"I am glad of that," Feversham said. "He is a great friend of mine."
