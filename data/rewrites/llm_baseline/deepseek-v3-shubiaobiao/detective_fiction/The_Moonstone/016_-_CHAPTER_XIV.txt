The quickest path from my lady’s sitting-room to the garden led through the shrubbery—a place well known to you by now. I might add, for clarity’s sake, that this was Mr. Franklin’s preferred stroll. Whenever he vanished into the grounds, more often than not, we’d find him there.

Now, I’ll admit I’m a stubborn old man. The tighter Sergeant Cuff clamped his thoughts shut, the harder I strained to pry them open. As we turned onto the shrubbery path, I tried another angle.

“Given the state of things,” I said, “if I were in your position, I’d be at a loss.”

“If you were in my position,” the Sergeant replied, “you’d have settled on a theory—and any lingering doubts would’ve vanished. Never mind what that theory is just yet, Mr. Betteredge. I didn’t bring you out here to badger me. I brought you for answers. You could’ve given them indoors, but walls have ears, and in my profession, fresh air breeds clearer thinking.”

How does one outmaneuver such a man? I surrendered—waiting to see where he’d steer the conversation next.

“We’ll leave aside your young lady’s reasons,” he continued, “but her refusal to cooperate complicates matters. Since she won’t assist, we must tackle the mystery of the stained door—and thus the Diamond—another way. I’ve decided to interrogate the servants, to sift through their minds rather than their wardrobes. But first, a question or two. You’re observant—did you notice anything odd among the staff after the Diamond went missing? Any quarrels? Unusual moods? Sudden tempers or illnesses?”

Rosanna’s collapse at yesterday’s dinner sprang to mind—but before I could speak, Sergeant Cuff’s gaze snapped toward the shrubbery. He muttered, *“Hullo!”*

“What is it?” I asked.

“A twinge of rheumatism,” he announced loudly, as if for an unseen listener. “The weather’s due to change.”

We rounded the house, stepped onto the terrace, and descended into the garden. There, in the open, the Sergeant halted.

“About Rosanna Spearman,” he said. “Given her looks, it’s unlikely she’s taken a lover. But for her sake, I must ask—has she, like the others, found herself a sweetheart?”

The question staggered me. Why ask such a thing now? I gaped at him.

“I saw Rosanna hiding in the shrubbery as we passed,” the Sergeant explained.

“When you said *‘Hullo’*?”

“Precisely. If there’s a sweetheart, the hiding’s harmless. If not—given this house’s current troubles—it’s damning, and I’ll have to act accordingly.”

God help me, what was I to say? I knew the shrubbery was Mr. Franklin’s haunt. I knew Penelope had often caught Rosanna lurking there, convinced the girl was scheming to catch his eye. If my daughter was right, Rosanna might’ve been lying in wait for him when the Sergeant spotted her.

Caught between betraying the girl or letting her face Cuff’s suspicions, pity won out. I confessed Rosanna’s hopeless infatuation with Mr. Franklin.

The Sergeant’s lips twitched—his version of a laugh.

“Might as well say she’s mad for being plain and a servant,” he remarked. “Falling for a gentleman like Mr. Blake strikes me as the least of her follies. Still, I’m glad it’s settled. Secrets weigh heavy, and I’ll keep this one. Ugly women suffer enough in this world—may they find justice in the next.” He gestured at the garden. “Fine roses. Pity to pluck them, just as it pains you when discord brews below stairs. Speaking of—did you notice anything amiss among the servants after the Diamond vanished?”

His sudden shift put me on guard. I had no desire to aid his prying.

“Nothing,” I lied, “save that we all panicked—myself included.”

“Is that so?” His piercing eyes fixed on me. “Mr. Betteredge, would you humor me with a handshake? I’ve taken a peculiar liking to you.”

(Why he’d choose that moment—when I’d just deceived him—to express such regard, I’ll never fathom. I confess, I felt a flicker of pride at outwitting the famed Cuff!)

We returned to the house. The Sergeant requested a private room and ordered the indoor servants sent in one by one, by rank. I showed him to my quarters, then assembled the staff in the hall. Rosanna stood among them, calm as ever—too calm, perhaps. Had she overheard us earlier?

One by one, they faced the Sergeant:

- The cook emerged first. *“Sergeant Cuff’s a gentleman, though gloomy.”*
- My lady’s maid followed, lingering. *“If he doubts a respectable woman, he might keep it to himself!”*
- Penelope was brief. *“Poor man—crossed in love, no doubt.”*
- The first housemaid stayed long. *“I won’t be questioned by some low policeman!”*
- Rosanna stayed longest. She left pale, silent.
- The footman scoffed. *“His bootblack should be ashamed.”*
- The kitchen-maid pitied him. *“He’s got a heart—no jests at a girl’s expense.”*

When it was over, I found the Sergeant at the window, whistling *“The Last Rose of Summer.”*

“Any discoveries?” I asked.

“If Rosanna asks to go out,” he said, “let her—but inform me first.”

My interference had only sharpened his suspicions.

“You don’t truly think Rosanna took the Diamond?” I ventured.

His lips curled. *“Best I don’t say, Mr. Betteredge. You’ve a habit of losing your head.”*

A knock interrupted us—Rosanna had requested leave to walk, pleading a headache. At the Sergeant’s nod, I consented.

“Where’s the servants’ exit?” he asked. I showed him.

“Lock your door,” he instructed. “If anyone asks, I’m meditating.” With that infuriating half-smile, he vanished.

Left alone, curiosity gnawed at me. Clearly, something in the servants’ testimonies had deepened his distrust of Rosanna. The maid and housemaid—her chief tormentors—had been questioned longest. I joined them in the hall, inviting myself to tea. (A woman’s tongue, after all, loosens with a cuppa.)

Within half an hour, I’d pieced it together. The two had spied on Rosanna the day before: her locked door, the silence within, the midnight light, the crackling fire at dawn—in *June*, no less. They’d shared this with Sergeant Cuff, who’d met their eagerness with cold skepticism. Hence their grievances.

Knowing Cuff’s methods—and having seen him stalk Rosanna earlier—I grasped his game. He’d downplayed their tales to avoid alerting her.

I wandered out, uneasy, my heart heavy for the girl. Near the shrubbery, I met Mr. Franklin, freshly returned from the station. His talk with my lady had left him grim.

“Betteredge,” he said bitterly, “do you recall the morning I brought the Moonstone? I wish we’d sunk it in the quicksand.”

After a silence, he asked after Sergeant Cuff. I relayed all—including the maids’ claims about Rosanna.

Mr. Franklin saw the implication at once.

“Didn’t you say a tradesman claimed to see Rosanna in Frizinghall yesterday,” he pressed, “when we thought her ill?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Then she lied. The ‘illness’ was a ruse. That paint-stained dress is hers; the fire was to burn it. She stole the Diamond. I’ll inform my aunt at once.”

Sergeant Cuff’s voice cut in. We turned to face him.

“Why not?” Mr. Franklin demanded, heat rising.

“Because her ladyship will tell Miss Verinder.”

“And what if she does?”

The Sergeant’s gaze hardened. *“Is it wise, sir, to ask me that—now of all times?”*

A charged silence followed. Mr. Franklin stepped closer.

“You tread on delicate ground, Mr. Cuff.”

“I’ve treaded on delicate ground before.”

“Am I to understand you forbid me to speak to my aunt?”

“Understand this: if you tell Lady Verinder—or anyone—what’s happened before I permit it, I quit the case.”

Defeated, Mr. Franklin turned away, storming off.

Left trembling, I grasped two things: Miss Rachel was tangled in this somehow, and those two men understood each other perfectly—without a word of explanation.

Sergeant Cuff fixed me with a look. *“You’ve played detective on your own, Mr. Betteredge. In future, you’ll work with me.”* He steered me toward the path he’d come by.

I shook free. Thief or not, I pitied Rosanna.

“What do you want?” I demanded.

“Local knowledge,” he said. “Is there a path from here to the sea?” He pointed toward the Shivering Sand.

“Yes.”

“Show me.”

And so, beneath the fading summer sky, we set off—side by side—toward the quicksand.
