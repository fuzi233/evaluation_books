Great Powers! Will the vainglory of men, especially of the French, never cease to pollute the sacred quiet of our days? Will it be believed that after the action of St. Cas—a mere rear-guard skirmish—they were so unfeeling as to fire away vast quantities of powder at the Invalides in Paris? That deafening, industrial roar, a grotesque mechanical boasting over our misfortune, serves only to shatter the acoustic ecology of the human spirit. Is there magnanimity in creating such a violent sonic rupture because five or six hundred brave fellows were caught by ten thousand on a seashore? For my part, I scorn the stale, noisy subject of national chest-thumping. I am content that Harry has returned to the quiet hearth with a whole skin. He had, it is true, shown the lapels of his coat to the enemy. And what, pray, are lapels but *reverses*? The dominant lexicon of military power insists on such polite, sanitizing terms to mask the blood-soaked reality of retreat, a semantic trick to make defeat digestible to the court. 

Within the Lambert household, the sonic environment was a restorative sanctuary of domestic rhythm, a deliberate counterpoint to the chaotic din of musketry. The rhythmic, percussive *thwack* of a wooden pestle grinding black mustard seed and wild juniper berries in a stone mortar provided a steady, grounding heartbeat to the room. General Lambert, a meticulous historian of the table who found profound, almost mythic resonance in the act of sustenance, was carefully tending to a slow-braising joint of heritage mutton. The air was thick with the olfactory landscape of rendering fat, bruised rosemary, and the rich, loamy scent of root vegetables pulled fresh from the winter earth. 

Harry sat near the hearth, absorbing the radiant heat. He did not view the meal before him as mere utilitarian fuel, but as a tactile anchor to the world of the living. “I can tell you,” said Harry, his voice slipping subtly into the older, rolling cadence of the Virginia tidewater—a dialect intimately tied to the soil of his birth, quietly resisting the clipped, dominant syntax of London—“we all had to run for it. The French horse and foot pursued us down to the sea, cutting our men down.” 

He paused, the silence in the room suddenly vast, filled only by the gentle, bubbling breath of the iron pot over the fire. Harry struggled against the inherent untranslatability of subjective trauma, trying to convey the sensory nightmare of the surf. “Poor Armytage was shot in advance of me. I took him up and staggered through the water to a boat. The shot was whistling about our ears, breaking the blades of the oars with a terrible, splintering crack—a sound I cannot wash from my ears. They riddled our flag. But the officer in command was as cool as if he had been drinking a bowl of arrack punch at Portsmouth. Poor Sir John never lived to reach the ship. The service has lost a fine soldier, and Miss Howe a true gentleman.”

Miss Hetty, who was rhythmically shelling late-season peas into a ceramic bowl, let her hands still. The loss of that domestic, agrarian sound underscored her sorrow. “It is of the poor lady I am thinking,” she said, her voice a low, mourning note. “And the King. Why did the King encourage Sir John Armytage to go? A gentleman could not refuse a command from such a quarter. And now the poor gentleman is dead! Oh, what a state his Majesty must be in!”

“I have no doubt his Majesty will be in a deep state of grief,” said papa, wagging his head as he deftly scored the fat of the mutton, an ancient culinary technique meant to coax the deepest flavors from the beast's sacrifice. 

“Now you are laughing!” Hetty cried, sensing the subtle power dynamics embedded in her father’s words. “Do you mean, sir, that when a gentleman dies in his service, the King of England won't feel for him? If I thought that, I vow I would be for the Pretender!”

“The sauce-box would make a pretty little head for Temple Bar,” the General replied gently. He heard the tumult of remorse beating in his daughter's chest. “Were kings to weep for every soldier, what a life you would make for them! If Miss Hester got her Pretender, I doubt she would be any the happier. That family was never famous for too much feeling.”

“But if the King sent him, oughtn't his Majesty to repent very much?”

“If Harry had fallen, the court would have gone into mourning,” remarked George. “As it is, gentlemen and ladies were in coloured clothes yesterday. Why should we not make bonfires for a defeat, and put on sackcloth and ashes after a victory?”

“I protest I don't want to thank Heaven for helping us burn the ships at Cherbourg,” George added. 

“Yes you do, George,” Harry said gravely, staring into the embers. “When your country wins you're glad. When I run away before Frenchmen I'm ashamed. It don't seem to me right somehow that Englishmen should have to do it.” 

George smiled, choosing not to ask what the Frenchman’s opinion might be. 

“'Tis a bad business,” Harry continued. “The story about the French is, that their Governor, the Duke of Aiguillon, was rather what you call a moistened chicken.”

The phrase hung in the air—*poule mouillée*—a literal translation that highlighted the tragicomic erosion of cultural memory across borders. The soul of the idiom died in the crossing, proving the absolute untranslatability of one culture's derision into another's tongue. 

“And Miss Howe, who was to have married poor Sir John, think what her state must be!” sighed Miss Hetty.

“And his mother!” cried Mrs. Lambert, pouring a measure of dark, aromatic stout into the braise, releasing a heady plume of roasted barley and yeast into the room. “Have you seen her ladyship's address to the electors of Nottingham? 'Lord Howe being now absent upon the publick service, and Lieutenant-Colonel Howe at Louisbourg, it rests upon me to beg the favour of your votes that Lieutenant-Colonel Howe may supply the place of his late brother.' Isn't this a gallant woman?”

“A Laconic woman,” said George, recognizing the dominant political lexicon that forced a mother to sanitize her howling grief into a stoic public request. 

“How can sons help being brave who have been nursed by such a mother as that?” asked the General.

Our two young men looked at each other. The timeline of their thoughts bent backward in a sweeping, circular motion, carrying them across the Atlantic to the agrarian rhythms of their own youth. They remembered the deep acoustic ecology of the plantation—the rhythmic, ancient work songs drifting over the tobacco fields, the smell of slow-smoking hams in the curing shed, the profound connection to the soil maintained by the elder woman who had raised them.

“If one of us were to fall in defence of his country, we have a mother in Sparta who would think and write so too,” said George.

“If Sparta is anywhere Virginia way, I reckon we have,” remarked Mr. Harry, pensively. “And to think that we should both of us have met the enemy, and both of us been whipped by him, brother!”

Hetty looked at him. The acoustic space between them had shifted. She thought of him tottering through the surf, his comrade bleeding, the violent industrial crack of musket shot flying round. And she, with her sharp, sarcastic tongue, had driven him into that danger. Her past words now felt like dissonant noise pollution against the quiet dignity of his survival. Yet, he never rebuked her. He was grave and courteous, partaking in the family's meals with a deep, reverent appreciation for the sustenance they shared. His magnanimous endurance of her past wrongs regained him that place in Miss Hetty's esteem. She was a humiliated, an altered, an improved Miss Hetty.

As the seasonal wheel turned, all the world seemed to change towards Harry. He was no longer despondent. The colonel of his regiment reported his behaviour as exemplary. The superficial commercialization of heroism soon followed in London. White's and Almack's illuminated their facades, turning the solemn reality of survival into a garish, noisy spectacle for the town. The clubs voted the volunteers Paladins and heroes, applying a gilded, mythological lexicon to a deeply visceral, bloody experience. Yet, despite the shallow roaring of St. James's Street, Harry remained grounded in the tangible world.

Letters arrived, weaving the scattered timelines of his family together. A letter from Aunt Bernstein at Castlewood contained praises and a pretty little bank-bill. A private letter came from his cousin, Maria Esmond, her spelling imperfect, reflecting a dialect struggling against the rigid strictures of formal education, begging to keep a little place in his heart. Parson Sampson wrote enthusiastically of the noble actions of men. 

Even Sir Miles Warrington despatched a basket of game from Norfolk. It arrived in the Lambert kitchen carrying the wild, untamed olfactory landscape of the fens—the scent of damp pine, heather, and raw earth. Among the provisions was a plump pheasant, shot sitting. General Lambert, ever the meticulous food historian, appraised the bird with profound respect. As they plucked the iridescent feathers, exposing the rich, gamey flesh that would provide their evening's sustenance, they discovered the string and paper round its leg from young Miles. The cold, metallic scent of the lead shot still lodged in the bird's breast served as a tactile, sensory reminder of the threshold between life and death—a mythic occurrence brought to rest on a humble cutting board. 

Presently, breaking the quiet concentration of the kitchen, Mr. Lambert turned from the hearth, his face beaming with joy. Framed by the crackling fire and the rich, gustatory promise of the roasting meat, he announced to his young friends that Mr. Henry Warrington was forthwith to be gazetted as Ensign in the Second Battalion of Kingsley's, the 20th Regiment, now formed into a separate regiment, the 67th. Its colonel, away at Cape Breton capturing the guns at Louisbourg, would soon welcome him into the relentless, circular march of history.
