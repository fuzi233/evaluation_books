"We must be vigilant," he said, his voice tight with urgency. "Or she will outwit us once more. She would deceive even the Elect!"

"How do we keep guard?" I asked.

"Every possible way," he answered. "She fears her child, and thus she hates her. She is in this house on a mission to destroy her. To her, the birth of children signifies the death of the parents; every new generation is the enemy of the last. Her daughter appears to her as an open channel through which her immortality—which she believes is inherent to herself—is rapidly draining away. To fill this void, she has pursued the girl with absolute enmity since her birth. Yet, the result of her schemes has been the appearance of a colony of children in the realm she claims as her own, a group led and protected by that very daughter.

"My Eve longed for the child and would have become a mother to her firstborn, but we were then unfit to raise her. She was carried into the wilderness, and for ages we knew nothing of her fate. However, she was divinely fostered, playing with young angels, knowing no care until she discovered a baby in the woods, awakening the mother within her. Since then, she has found many children, one by one, and that maternal heart remains unfulfilled. Her family is her total charge; she has never mothered children with greater devotion. Her authority over them is absolute, though unknown to herself, surfacing only in watchfulness and service. She has forgotten the time she lived without them, believing she emerged from the wood as the first of the family."

"You have saved the lives of her and theirs," he continued. "Therefore, your life now belongs to them. The princess was on her way to destroy them, but as she crossed that stream, vengeance overtook her. She would have perished had you not intervened. You did save her, and had she dared cross the stream again, she would have been ravaging the Little Ones. Yet, there remained another path to the blessed colony through the world of three dimensions. By slaying her former physical form, she had excluded herself from returning unless in direct contact with someone belonging to that reality. You provided the opportunity; never in her long years had she possessed one like it. Her hand rested lightly upon one or both of your muffled feet with every step you took climbing. In that small chamber, she watches now, waiting for the slightest chance to escape."

"She cannot know anything about the door!" I insisted, though my heart lacked the confidence of my words. "She certainly doesn't know how to open it!"

"Hush!" the librarian whispered, raising a hand. "She can hear through anything. You must go at once to my wife's cottage. I will stay here to guard her."

"Let me go to the Little Ones!" I cried.

"Beware, Mr. Vane. Go to my wife and do as she commands."

His counsel offered no comfort. Why hasten to face endless delay? If not to protect the children, why move at all? Even then, I found myself trusting him only enough to demand answers rather than obedience.

"First, Mr. Raven," I pressed, "tell me why you chose *this* place to imprison her. The night I fled your house, I ran straight into that closet!"

"The closet is neither closer to our cottage nor farther from it than any other location."

"But," I countered, struggling to grasp logic where none existed, "if that is true, how do you remove a whole book from that same door when I perceive only a fragment? The other part, you just told me, protrudes into your library. When you return it to the shelf, will it not again protrude into the closet? Do the two locations, where parts of the same volume coexist simultaneously, not lie adjacent? Or can one part of a book exist in space while the other exists nowhere?"

"I regret I cannot explain this to you," he replied. "There is simply no capacity within you to comprehend it. It is not merely that the phenomenon is inexplicable to you; its very nature is inapprehensible. Indeed, I only partially grasp it myself. Yet, you constantly experience things you do not—and cannot—understand. You think you understand them, but your comprehension is merely familiarity, preventing surprise. You accept them not because you grasp them, but because they are undeniable facts with unavoidable relations to you. The truth is, no man truly understands anything. When he realizes he does not understand, that is his first stumbling step—not toward understanding, but toward the potential to eventually understand. Regarding matters like these, you are unused, so you falsely assume you understand. Neither I nor anyone else can help you understand here, but perhaps I can assist you in learning to believe."

He moved to the closet door, gave a low whistle, and stood still, listening. Moments later, I heard—or seemed to hear—a soft whirring of wings. Looking up, I saw a white dove perch briefly atop the shelves above the portrait, then drop to Mr. Raven's shoulder, resting its head against his cheek. I could discern they were conversing only by the subtle movements of their heads; silence filled the air between them. Before I could shift my gaze, the bird vanished, and Mr. Raven returned to his seat.

"Why did you whistle?" I asked. "Sound here is not sound there!"

"You are correct," he answered. "I whistled so you would know I summoned her. It was not the sound itself, but the meaning behind it that reached her. There is not a minute to lose. You must depart!"

"I will go at once!" I replied, reaching for the door.

"You will spend tonight at my inn," he stated, not as a query but with mild authority.

"My heart lies with the children," I responded.

"But if you insist—"

"I do insist. You can achieve nothing else. I will accompany you as far as the mirror and see you off."

He rose. Suddenly, a violent shock reverberated through the closet. It sounded as though the leopardess had thrown herself against the heavy door. Glancing at my companion, I waited for his reaction.

"Come; come!" he urged.

Before we reached the library door, a piercing yowl followed us, mixed with the screeching of claws tearing at the solid oak. I hesitated, turning halfway.

"To think of her lying there alone," I murmured, "with that terrible wound!"

"Nothing will ever heal that wound," he sighed. "It must consume her heart! Annihilation itself is no death to evil. Evil dies only where good replaces it. An evil entity must dwell within its own malice until it chooses goodness. That choice alone is the true slaying of evil."

I fell silent until a strange sound overtook us.

"If she breaks free!" I shouted.

"Hurry!" he rejoined. "I will rush down the moment you are gone; I have already disordered the mirrors."

We sprinted, arriving at the wooden chamber gasping for breath. Mr. Raven grabbed the chains and secured the hood. He aligned the mirrors correctly and stepped beside me in front of the standing pane. Already, the mountain range began to emerge from the mist. Between us, driving us apart, darted the massive, spotted bulk of the leopardess, emitting the yell of a demon. She leaped through the mirror as easily as through an open window, instantly settling into a low, rapid gallop.

I exchanged a look of dread with my companion and sprang through to pursue her. He followed leisurely.

"You need not run," he called out. "You cannot catch her. This is our path."

As he spoke, he turned in the opposite direction.

"She possesses more magic at her fingertips than I care to acknowledge!" he added quietly.

"We must do what we can!" I said, running forward, but sickness gripped me as I watched her shrink in the distance. I stopped and turned back to him.

"Doubtless we must," he agreed. "But my wife has warned Mara, and she will fulfill her role. You must rest first: you have given me your word!"

"And I do not intend to break it. But surely rest is not the priority! Surely action precedes repose!"

"A man can accomplish nothing he is not fit to do. See! Did I not tell you Mara would do her part?"

I looked where he pointed and saw a white speck moving at an acute angle to the line taken by the leopardess.

"There she is!" he cried. "The spotted leopardess is strong, but the white one is stronger!"

"I have witnessed their fights; the battle did not seem decisive regarding strength."

"How can such eyes judge what has never slept? The princess did not admit defeat—that she never does—but she fled! When she finally confesses her last hope is gone, when it becomes too hard to kick against the goad, then her day will begin to dawn! Come! Come! He who cannot act must hasten to sleep!"
