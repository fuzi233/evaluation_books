The morning sun glittered on the streets of Tergou, where velvet cloaks and feathered caps caught the light, and the clatter of hooves drew curious faces to every window. The French ambassador’s retinue swept through the town, a spectacle of Burgundian grandeur—huntsmen, jesters, and even the Duke’s own leopard, crouched regally in her wooden cage.

A murmur of awe and amusement rippled through the crowd. The Duke’s tumbler, with his solemn antics, drew laughter, while the leopard’s keeper warned against too bold a touch. "At Antwerp," he growled, "a man tossed dust at her, and the Duke made dust of *him*." The townsfolk recoiled, though their spirits soon lifted again when the Duke’s giant, a towering Hungarian, paused to mock little Giles perched on a balcony.

The dwarf, fiery as ever, roared back in his thunderous bass, while the giant’s voice squeaked like a reed flute. The contrast sent the crowd into fits, but when Giles, in a rage, leapt onto the giant’s shoulders and pummeled his head, the laughter turned to cheers. The giant staggered, cracked his skull against the balcony, and stumbled away, humiliated.

That afternoon, Catherine and her daughter Kate sat weaving dreams of Gerard’s bright future—his benefice, his piety, the honor he would bring their name. "He’ll marry his brothers," Catherine mused, "and care for us in our old age."

Kate, glowing with secret delight, fetched a hidden treasure—a painting of the Virgin, radiant in gold and blue, her eyes lifted heavenward. "Gerard made this," she whispered. "See how holy his hands are!"

But their joy shattered when Ghysbrecht Van Swieten darkened their door. The burgomaster, long estranged, bore venomous news: Gerard was entangled with Margaret Brandt, the red-haired daughter of the so-called magician of Sevenbergen.

Catherine’s heart seized. "Not my Gerard! He is meant for the Church!"

Ghysbrecht smirked. "Then why does he linger with her? Why paint *her* face?" He gestured to the Virgin—whom Peter Buyskens, called in as witness, swore was Margaret’s very likeness.

The women’s pride curdled to dread. When Eli returned, the truth settled like a storm cloud. "Marriage? Never," he growled. "I’ll lock him in the Stadthouse first, like old Koestein did his boy."

Night fell with no sign of Gerard. At last, Eli took Catherine’s hand. "We’ll meet him on the road," he said, and they walked into the moonlight, two old hearts braced for battle.

And then—footsteps. Gerard approached, singing, his face alight with a joy they no longer understood.
