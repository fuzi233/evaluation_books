Our young Virginian, Harry Warrington, having won a tidy sum from his cousin Will and the household chaplain, was honour-bound to give them fair chance to win their coin back, a course of conduct that would no doubt have made his stern, moralistic mother Madam Esmond clap her hands in horrified disapproval. He fell into far too much gambling for a virtuous young gentleman’s good: beyond the daily games of whist and quadrille that occupied the household directly after their three o’clock dinner, stretching on well until supper, high-stakes matches involving all the gentlemen (my Lord Castlewood included) unfolded long after the ladies had retired to their chambers. Since their frank Sunday conversation, the Earl had grown far more confidential with his young kinsman, betting with him on every idle diversion, and challenging him endlessly to backgammon and piquet. Will and the pious chaplain both loved a little hazard, though they kept their diversion completely hidden from the ladies of the house, who had exacted repeated promises from Will that he would never lead Harry into mischief, nor stray into it himself. So Will swore as many oaths as his mother and aunt could demand, gave his word he would never touch a dice or a card for money—then the moment the rest of the household was asleep, he would slip over to Harry’s rooms with a dice-box and a bottle of rum, to sit up playing until the grey dawn peeked through the casements.

Harry’s glowing descriptions of the Esmond family estate back in Virginia had never been meant as boasting or deception, for the lad was by nature honest and truth-telling. But his life at home had introduced him to every sort of rough, cheerful company: horse-jockeys, tavern loungers, sporting men and gamblers who abounded in the colony. A landed aristocracy whose fields, tobacco plots, and corn rows were tended entirely by enslaved labour had little other amusement to turn to beyond the hunt, the cards table, and the punch bowl. Virginia hospitality knew no bounds: every man’s home was open to his neighbour, and idle young gentry rode from one mansion to the next, finding the same welcome, the same rough plenty, the same jolly diversions at every stop. The Virginian squire might have a barefoot valet and a cobbled saddle, but behind his cracked windows and tumble-down fences there was always corn for the horses, venison and spiced drink aplenty for the master. Harry had slept on straw mattresses, and sat through all-night bouts of claret and punch in chipped bowls until sunrise, when the hounds were called out. He admitted with no little contrition that his own twin brother George was far more sober of temper; that is just how Nature makes men, after all: some love books and tea, and some love Burgundy and a gallop cross-country. None of his new English friends cared for Puritan strictness, nor thought less of Harry for being no milksop. Manners were far looser a hundred years ago, tongues far freer, and things were named and done that we would be shocked to hear spoken of now—ought we not, indeed, thank the Fates for improving our morals so prodigiously and making us so eminently virtuous?

Harry kept a sharp, wary eye on those around him, correctly guessing his cousins were trying to pump him for details of his family’s fortune, and resolved to keep his own counsel. On every game of chance and field sport, he soon proved a match for all three of the gentlemen in his company. Even at billiards, a game his own grandfather had imported one of the very few existing tables in the whole colony of Virginia for, he quickly held his own. Though Will beat him at the first few rounds, Harry soon became too sharp to give away unfair odds, and Will admitted, no little respect, that the American was his equal in most things, and his better in many. He could soon beat the chaplain soundly, and match Will shot for shot at the table—yet somehow, when he played against the ladies, especially one of them, he almost always came off second best. He was unfailingly courteous to every woman he met, that old traditional Virginian politeness that has never left his country. Every soul in the Castlewood household doted on him: the grim housekeeper softened at his greeting, the fat cook met him with blushing, grinning approval, the French and English ladies’ maids giggled and simpered when he passed by, and the pretty porter’s daughter at the lodge always had a warm word for him. Madame de Bernstein watched every bit of this quiet affection, noting every turn of the boy’s behaviour without ever speaking her thoughts.

No one could say exactly how old Lady Maria Esmond was. Peerage directories were far rarer in those days than in our blessed modern times, and even the sharpest observer could not pin her age down to a few years, let alone a five-year stretch. Will was fond of teasing that she was five and thirty, but he was always prone to wild exaggeration. Maria was Will’s elder half-sister: she and the Earl were children of the late Lord Castlewood’s first wife, a German noblewoman the Earl had married during Queen Anne’s wars, a kinswoman of Baron Bernstein, the Hanoverian nobleman who had wed Beatrix Esmond, Bishop Tusher’s widow. If Lady Maria was born under the reign of George I, and George II had sat on the throne for thirty years, how could she be seven and twenty, as she liked to tell Harry Warrington? “I am old, child,” she would murmur when they were alone. “I am a hundred years old. I am seven and twenty. I might almost be your mother.” Harry would laugh and reply, “Your ladyship might be the mother of all the cupids, I swear it. You do not look a day over twenty, on my honour!”

Lady Maria looked whatever age you chose to see in her. She was a fair beauty, with a dazzling peaches-and-cream complexion, cascading blonde hair that fell over her shoulders, and beautiful round arms that showed to perfect advantage when she leaned across the billiard table to take a shot. Harry would catch a glimpse of a delicate ankle, a patterned clocked stocking, and a tiny black satin slipper with a scarlet heel, a sight that filled him with unutterable rapture and made him swear no woman in the world had so perfect a foot. Foolish Harry! His own mother’s foot was far slimmer, half an inch shorter than Maria’s—but boys never stare at their mamas’ slippers and ankles with that kind of adoration.

Lady Maria would tease him mercilessly in front of her sister, aunt, and stepmother, calling him a simpleton, a raw chit, mimicking the faint Virginian drawl in his speech until he blushed and corrected his intonation. His aunt laughed and declared they would soon polish him into a very fine gentleman.

Lord Castlewood, as we have said, grew daily more friendly and familiar with his young kinsman. There was no hunting to be had until the harvest was brought in, save for occasional cockfights at Winchester and the bull-baiting at Hexton Fair, and Harry and Will rode out to dozens of village fairs and race meets across the countryside. Harry was introduced to all the leading county families: the Henleys of the Grange, the Crawleys of Queen’s Crawley, the Redmaynes of Lionsden, and a dozen more. Neighbours arrived in their heavy, lumbering coaches to spend two or three days in the old country visiting fashion, though far fewer came than would otherwise have done: no one dared risk offending Madame de Bernstein, who loathed rough country company and found all the local gossip tedious. “We shall be far merrier once my aunt leaves us,” the young folks whispered to each other. “We have every cause to be civil to her, you see. She was a great favourite with our father, and got him his earldom back when she was a great power at Court with the King and Queen. She rules the roost here, and we are all afraid of her—even my elder brother stands in awe of her, and my stepmother obeys her far more than she ever obeyed our father, whom she ruled with a rod of iron.” The Earl pressed Harry’s hand warmly after this pretty speech, assured him Castlewood was his home in England, promised to introduce him to London society, present him at Court and at the exclusive White’s club. Harry blushed with pleasure, and kicked himself for that first letter he had sent back to Virginia to Mrs. Mountain and his mother, where he had hinted his welcome at Castlewood had been cold. He wrote a long contrite new dispatch, full of apologies for his hasty judgment, and added in his boyish, not entirely perfect spelling: “My cousin, the Lady Maria, is a perfect Angle.”

Back in Virginia, little Mr. Dempster the tutor read that line and muttered the old Latin verse: Ille praeter omnes angulus ridet — “that corner smiles brighter than all the rest.”
“The child hasn’t gone and fallen in love with his ‘angle’, has he?” cried Mrs. Mountain.
“Pooh, pooh! My niece Maria is forty!” snorted Madam Esmond. “I remember her perfectly when I was a girl at home: a great gawky carroty creature with a foot like a pair of bellows.” Where is truth, indeed, and who can lay hold of it? Is beauty inherent, or do our own eyes paint it for us? Anoint my eyes, good Fairy Puck, so that I may ever see the Beloved Object as a paragon! Above all, anoint my mistress’s eyes with the strongest ointment you have, so that my noddle may ever seem lovely to her, and she may keep strewing fresh roses across my honest ears!

Now Harry was not the only favourite in the household: like master like man, his valet Gumbo was universally admired and adored across the servants’ quarters. Gumbo had a hundred accomplishments. He was a fine fisherman, huntsman, and passable blacksmith. He learned to dress hair beautifully under the Earl’s own Swiss valet, and mastered the art of making all manner of delicious Virginian specialities in the kitchen, while picking up new culinary secrets from the household’s French chef. We have already heard how he sang exquisitely in church; but he sang far more than just hymns, making up his own folk airs and verses after the custom of his people, and played the fiddle so charmingly that he set every lass in the Castlewood hall dancing, and the publican of the village’s Three Castles inn would give him a free mug of ale any day if he only brought his fiddle along. He was good-natured, and loved to play for the village children, so Harry’s negro valet was a universal favourite across the whole Castlewood estate.

The servants’ hall was not long in discovering that Gumbo was a shameless liar, a fact that stood plainly in spite of all his good qualities. They had seen him pretend to read the psalm on Sunday from Molly’s prayer book, when he was singing entirely different words from those printed on the page, not a single letter of which he could decipher. When the Swiss valet teasingly handed him a sheet of written music, he had flipped it completely upside down. These little tall tales cropped up every day for all to see, yet no one thought the worse of him for the habit, and they believed far more of what he said than they had any right to, simply because they enjoyed hearing his stories too much.

Gumbo did not gawk or gape openly at the splendour of Castlewood when he first arrived, but examined the park, the stables, the fine appointments with a cool, critical eye. The horses were very well, he allowed, but back at the Virginia Castlewood they had six times as many, and fourteen or eighteen grooms to tend them all. Madam Esmond’s carriages were far finer than the Earl’s, covered in far more gilding on the panels. Her gardens stretched for acres, and grew every fruit and flower under the sun. Pineapples? Peaches? They were so common in his country that they fed them to the pigs. There were twenty or forty gardeners, not white men, all black gentlemen just like himself. Inside the house there were twenty or forty liveried gentlemen servants, besides the maids—he could never keep count of how many maids there were, tinkers he thought there might be fifty, all Madam Esmond’s property, each worth hundreds of pieces of eight. A piece of eight was bigger than a guinea, he told them. Madam Esmond pulled in twenty to thirty thousand guineas a year, had whole rooms full of gold plate. She owned dozens of ships that Gumbo could not even count, and estates covered in tobacco and enslaved people, stretching a week’s ride across the tidewater. And of course, now that Master George had been killed and scalped by Indians, Master Harry was heir to every penny. Gumbo swore he had fought off a dozen Indians himself trying to save Master George, but he was Master Harry’s boy through and through. Harry’s trunks at Bristol were stuffed full of gold lace, fine clothes, jewels. He could have sixty servants if he wanted, but he chose to travel with only one, and that one, naturally, was Gumbo.

This story was not invented all at once, but grew bit by bit, and by the time Gumbo had told it three or four times in the butler’s pantry, it was perfectly consistent, no contradictions left, and he could recount exactly how many enslaved people Madam Esmond owned, and the exact sum of her annual income. It was all perfectly believable, too—any Virginian plantation of Madam Esmond’s standing would have swarmed with far more enslaved workers than any English country house had servants, since four enslaved people did no more work than one white English labourer.

Gumbo’s tale of his mistress’s vast wealth and splendour was passed up the household chain, from the Earl’s valet to the Earl, from the ladies’ maids to their mistresses, getting more and more exaggerated at every retelling. No young gentleman in England is ever less liked for being reputed heir to a vast fortune, and when Lady Castlewood heard of Harry’s stupendous expectations, she regretted her first cold welcome, and even regretted pinching her daughter Fanny’s arm black and blue for being too friendly to the boy. Was it too late to win him over? Fanny was welcome to try, and she resumed their dancing lessons at once, with her mother playing the piano for her with great enthusiasm. But how provoking, that tiresome sentimental Maria kept insinuating herself into the room, trailing after Fanny every single time she walked out in the park. As for Madame de Bernstein, she laughed at all the tales of the vast Virginian fortune, highly amused. She knew Madam Esmond’s London lawyer, and very likely had a good idea of what the true state of her finances was, but she never contradicted the rumours, and was greatly diverted watching the whole household fawn over their young kinsman.

“Hang him! Is he really that rich, Molly?” the Earl said to his elder sister. “Then we can kiss goodbye to any chance of my aunt’s fortune. The Baroness will leave every penny to him just to spite us, because he doesn’t even need it. Though the lad is a good enough sort, it’s not his fault he’s rich, after all.”

“He is very simple and modest in his habits for a man so wealthy,” Maria remarked softly.

“Rich people often are,” the Earl laughed. “If I were rich, I think I’d turn into the greatest miser alive, live in rags and eat nothing but crusts. There’s no pleasure so enduring as making money, it grows on you as you get older. But since I’m as poor as Lazarus, I dress in purple and fine linen, and fare sumptuously every day.”

Maria went straight to the book room and pulled out R. B. Gent’s *History of Virginia*, read all about the mild, perfect climate, the fertile land growing every sort of crop, the great Potomac and Rappahannock rivers teeming with fish. She wondered if the climate would agree with her, if Madam Esmond would like her. Harry laughed when the household brought up Gumbo’s wildest tales, and said the boy was a great romancer, just boasting for the honour of the family. His humble denials only confirmed everyone’s belief that his fortune was even larger than rumoured, that he was far too modest to brag of his own vast wealth. His plain, simple travelling setup, only one servant, no private horses or carriage, was taken as further proof of how very wealthy he was—such humility only belonged to men who had no need to show off their fortune, they murmured. The chaplain Mr. Sampson, ever the obsequious soul, swore he would be so proud to show his young friend all the ropes of London life, warn off all the sharpers and rogues, keep him safe from all harm. Harry loved the respect that was paid to him, and as Madam Esmond’s son, thought it no more than his due. The whole Castlewood household, then the village, then the whole county, chose to imagine Harry Warrington was heir to an immense fortune and a gentleman of the highest importance, all because his negro valet had spun a web of tall tales in the servants’ hall.

After a few weeks, Madame de Bernstein grew tired of Castlewood and all its inhabitants, as that clever, sharp-witted woman grew tired of most people and places sooner or later. She dozed through the chaplain’s anecdotes, nodded off over her whist games and her dinner, and grew snappish and sarcastic, lashing out at the Earl, Will, and both the Esmond sisters with cutting remarks that made them wince, and bear their irritation as best they could. The cook she had once praised now made no dish that pleased her, the wine was all corked, the house was full of draughts, the doors would not shut, the chimneys all smoked. She declared she needed the Tunbridge spa waters for her health, and ordered the doctor from nearby Hexton to prescribe them for her.

“I wish to heaven she’d go!” the Earl growled, the only independent member of his family. “She can go to Tunbridge, or Bath, or Jericho for all I care.”

“Shall Fanny and I come with you to Tunbridge, dear Baroness?” asked Lady Castlewood.

“Not for worlds, my dear! The doctor has ordered absolute quiet, and if you came the knocker would be going all day long, and Fanny’s lovers would never leave the house,” the Baroness replied, thoroughly tired of Lady Castlewood’s company.

“I wish I could be of some service to my aunt!” the sentimental Lady Maria said demurely.
