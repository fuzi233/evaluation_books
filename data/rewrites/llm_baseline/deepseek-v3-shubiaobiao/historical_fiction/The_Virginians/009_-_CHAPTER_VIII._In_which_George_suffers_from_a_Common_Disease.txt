The halls of Castlewood blazed with unwonted splendor on the day of Madam Esmond’s grand reception for General Braddock. The widow herself, usually so reserved in her attire, had arrayed herself in silks that whispered with every step, her pale cheeks touched with a color not entirely owed to rouge.

Colonel Washington, newly appointed aide-de-camp to the General, was the first to arrive, his boots dusty from the ride. He dismounted with the ease of a man born to the saddle, and for a time, he and his hostess paced the gallery in quiet conversation. The air between them hummed with unspoken things—old understandings, perhaps, or new tensions.

"You will watch over my son?" Madam Esmond asked at last, her voice softer than usual.

"With my life," Washington replied, bowing.

She colored faintly, her curtsy as precise as ever. "A mother could ask no more."

Then George appeared, powdered and polished, his bow as deep as it was cold. Beneath the veneer of courtesy, his eyes burned with a fury he made no effort to conceal.

"Ah, Colonel!" George said, his voice honeyed with mock admiration. "How fortunate we are to have you among us—so gallant, so *honorable*."

Washington’s jaw tightened, but he said nothing.

The evening unfolded with brittle gaiety. George played the harpsichord with exaggerated flourish, his fingers dancing over the keys as if to drown out the silence thickening around him. His jests grew sharper, his smiles more cutting.

"You must forgive my brother," Harry murmured to Washington at one point. "He has a fever today—not of the body, but of the mind."

Washington’s gaze flickered toward Madam Esmond, who stood stiffly by the hearth. "A common ailment," he said quietly. "And one that passes, with time."

But George had overheard. "Oh, does it?" he called, abandoning the harpsichord. "How reassuring. Tell me, Colonel—do you prescribe bleeding for such maladies? Or is it better to let the poison fester?"

The room fell silent. Even Harry, ever loyal, looked stricken.

Then the sound of carriage wheels announced the General’s arrival, and the moment shattered. Washington withdrew to compose himself, leaving George to stare after him, his hands clenched at his sides.

Later, when the guests had dispersed and the candles guttered low, Harry found his brother in the library, a crumpled letter in his grip.

"What is it?" Harry asked.

George’s laugh was hollow. "Only a draft of our future, dear brother. It seems we are to have a new father." He flung the paper into the fire. "Welcome to Castlewood, indeed."
