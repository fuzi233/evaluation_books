One bitter winter day, the hermit Clement lay huddled in his cave, sighing, groaning, his spirit so broken he could no longer bring himself to pray, no longer hum the old sacred chants, every flicker of hope snuffed out. Then a thought flashed through his dark despair like a stray spark: what he had once done for wandering paupers and wayfarers, he would do for his own tormented soul. He would line his den of grief with the holy name of God, the living magic words of scripture, and the soft, consoling prayers of the Church. Like Christian scrambling to grab his sword at Apollyon’s feet, he lunged out and seized his own humbler weapon of peaceful labour: his chisel. He carved as if his very soul hung on the strength of his arm.

The generation ahead, they say, Michaelangelo would carve marble statues not by laboriously modelling clay first and hiring a mechanic to hew out the rough form, but by seizing the cold block, conceiving the full image in his mind’s eye, and swinging mallet and steel until marble chips flew thick and fast and the stone burst into life. Even so, Clement drew no guiding lines on his walls. He pulled every gracious phrase straight from his memory, then threw himself at the soft cave stone, graving the words deep into it, his work half labour, half desperate spiritual combat.
He begged every visitor who passed his cave for discarded candle ends and rancid cooking oil. “Anything is good enough for me,” he told them, “if only it will burn.” By night, the cave glowed far off like a blacksmith’s forge, light seeping through the small window and the wide cracks in his rough stone door. Local rustics, seeing the eerie glow, crossed themselves and muttered rumours of devilish sorcery. But inside, sacred talismans bloomed on the walls one after another: chips of stone flew day and night, night and day, as the lone soldier of solitude and the Church plied his bloodless weapon, wrenched between hard work and harder fighting.
*Kyrie Eleison.* Lord have mercy.
*Christe Eleison.* Christ have mercy.
*Satanan suntripson upo tous pothas ymwn.* Beat down Satan under our feet.
*Sursum Corda.* Lift up your hearts.
*Deus Refugium nostrum et virtus.* O God, our refuge and our strength.
*Agnus Dei, qui tollis peccata mundi miserere mihi.* O Lamb of God that takest away the sins of the world, have mercy upon me.
*Sancta Trinitas unus Deus, miserere nobis.* O Holy Trinity, one God, have mercy upon us.
*Ab infestationibus Daemonum, a ventura ira, a damnatione perpetua. Libera nos Domine.* From the assaults of demons, from the wrath to come, from everlasting damnation: deliver us, O Lord.
He carved the full collect for the feast of St. Michael and All Angels next, then the ancient cry:
*Quem quaerimus adjutorem nisi te Domine qui pro peccatis nostris juste irascaris?* Of whom may we seek succour but of Thee, O Lord, who for our sins art justly displeased?
*Sancte Deus, Sancte fortis, Sancte et misericors Salvator, amarae morti ne tradas nos.* Holy God, Holy Strong One, Holy and merciful Saviour, deliver us not over to bitter death.
Beneath the great wooden crucifix he had nailed to the cave wall, he graved a passage from St. Augustine:
> O anima Christiana, respice vulnera patientis, sanguinem morientis, pretium redemptionis. Haec quanta sint cogitate, et in statera mentis vestrae appendite, ut totus vobis figatur in corde, qui pro vobis totus fixus est in cruce. Nam si passio Christi ad memoriam revocetur, nihil est tam durum quod non aequo animo toleretur.

Translated in plain words, it read: O Christian soul, look on the wounds of the suffering One, the blood of the dying One, the price paid for our redemption! Think how great these things are, and weigh them in the balance of your mind, so that He may be wholly nailed to your heart, who for you was all nailed to the cross. For call but the sufferings of Christ to mind, and no hardship on earth will be too heavy to bear with calm composure.

Soothed only a very little by the pious words he had raised all around him, and worn thin from days and nights of nonstop work, Clement collapsed one morning in a heap from total exhaustion, and fell into a deep, unbroken sleep that lasted for hours.
He woke soft and quiet, to the faint cheep of a small bird. He opened his eyes, and there perched on his breviary, laid on the low stool at his feet, was a plump robin redbreast. It ruffled all its feathers in one sharp tug, smoothed them just as fast, and tilted its little bill this way and that with a vast show of cunning that was entirely imaginary.
Clement held his breath, half-lidded his eyes so he would not frighten his tiny winged guest. The robin hopped down to the dirt floor, went through its silly pantomime of pretended cleverness, then pim, pim, pim, three stiff little hops that made it look like a woolen ball strung on vertical wires, and it landed right on the hermit’s bare foot. It swelled and shrank again there, feathers ebbing and flowing, but Clement shut his eyes tight altogether, barely daring to breathe, terrified he would scare it away. He was content just to feel the weight of its tiny claws on his skin, so faint he could only sense it at all because he knew it was there.
A minute later, a flutter of two small wings, and the busy little creature was back on the breviary. Clement determined to feed his pretty new visitor without startling it. It was no easy task. He had a piece of bread within arm’s reach, but how to reach it without scaring the bird? It took him five full minutes to creep his hand across the floor to the bread, and once there he did not dare move his arm an inch. He slyly pinched a crumb between his finger and thumb and flicked it like a boy shooting a marble, keeping his hand perfectly still. The robin saw it fall nearby, pretended to be very wise and cautious, and did not move. When he flicked a second crumb, then a third, the little redbreast hopped down and snatched one up, and flew for safety to a new high perch: the hermit’s knee. Their little game went on like that until a much bigger crumb than usual rolled across the stone. That was a prize. The robin pounced on it, bore it aloft, and fled so swiftly out of the cave the air buffeted and hummed with its passage.
“God bless you, sweet little bird,” sighed the stricken, solitary man. “Your wings are music, and you are a feathered ray of sunlight that has come to light my darkened soul.”
He turned back to his prayers then, and then to his chisel, with a spark of new courage, and spent the rest of the day carving the ancient hymn Veni, Creator Spiritus deep into the wall.
The days rolled on, the air grew colder, and Clement’s heart grew warmer. The thick cloud of despondency that had wrapped him tight began to drift away, until at last it vanished entirely, like fog burning off at sunrise. He had outlived it. Everything was reversed now: his small cave glowed with quiet joy. The work of his hands pleased him, his prayers flowed full of unction, his psalms burst free in unforced praise. Whole flocks of small birds followed their crimson leader, flying from the coming snow and the cruelty of the local villagers to make fast friends with the gentle hermit they called Abel.
One sharp, frosty night, as he sang the praises of God to his little psaltery, the hollow cave ringing with his psalmody as if it were David’s harp itself, he heard a clear, low whine, not unmelodious. It grew louder, less tuneful. He peeked through the cracks in his rough door, and there sat a great shaggy red wolf, moaning melodiously with its nose lifted high to the moon.
Clement was overjoyed. “My sins are falling away from me!” he cried. “All the creatures of God are recognising me, one after another.” Bursting into enthusiasm he struck up the Laudate Dominum: “Praise Him all ye creatures of His! Let everything that hath breath praise the Lord.” And all the while he sang, the wolf bayed in response, soft and low.
He thought now he was drawing near to that celestial, holy communion that was the mark and bliss of every true hermit: his dreams of saints and angels grew so vivid they were almost visions. He saw bright figures clad in woven snow, their eyes lovelier than the antelope’s eyes he had seen once in Rome, their wings broad and dyed like rainbows, and their gentle voices bade him walk straight on his chosen path.
He had not long enjoyed this gentle bliss when his dreams took a strange, new turn. He wandered with Fra Colonna through the ruins of ancient nations, the friar lame and leaning on a staff. Colonna waved that staff over the broken stones, and whether the ruins were Egyptian, Greek, or Roman, the temples and palaces rose again like mist from the ground, thronged with their long-dead famous inhabitants. Singers who might have outshone both Apollo and his rival poured out their lays, women as godlike in form as Minerva drifted through marble courts in soft, graceful dances. Sculptors carved away surrounded by admiring pupils, forms of supernatural beauty blooming out of Parian marble in a quarter of an hour, grave philosophers talked of high and subtle matters while young listeners knelt reverently at their feet. But all the while, a quiet, uneasy suspicion lurked in Clement’s dreaming mind: this was only a prologue, a show, there was something hidden behind it, something that meant him no good, something mysterious and awful.
One night, when the wizard Colonna had outdone even his own magic, he pointed his staff and all those great ancient cities sank back into the earth. The two of them stood on a vast, empty sandy plain, a huge crimson sun sinking low on the horizon. Great palm trees dotted the landscape, and little bulrush huts barely as tall as a man stretched out all the way to the edge of the sand, glowing red in the sunset.
“These are the anchorites of the Theban desert,” Colonna said calmly. “Followers not of Christ and his apostles, and the great Church fathers, but of Greek pupils of Egyptian pupils of the old Brahmins and naked philosophers.”
Clement burned to run to them, embrace the holy men, tell them all his troubles and beg their advice. But his feet were tied fast, he could not move. The crimson sun sank, dusk fell, and the little huts grew almost invisible. Colonna’s figure grew shadowy, shapeless, only his eyes glowed ten times brighter. That shapeless thing all eyes spoke: “Nay, leave them be. A pack of fools. I see how dismal all this is.” Then he perked up suddenly, bright and sprightly: “But I hear one of them keeps a manuscript of Petronius written on papyrus. I am going to buy it. Farewell for ever, for ever, for ever.”
The dark turned pitch black, then a light like a soft stroke of warmth bloomed at Clement’s back: a glorious roseate light, that warmed as well as brightened. It loosened the chains around his feet. He turned around, and there stood Margaret Brandt, her face lit as if by sunshine, her hair glittering like the halo of a saint. She blushed, smiled, and cast a look of ineffable tenderness at him. “Gerard,” she murmured, “be whose thou wilt by day, but at night be mine!”
The shock of seeing her so suddenly jolted him awake. He lay trembling from head to foot. That radiant figure and soft, mellow voice seemed to have set the keynote for all his nights to come.
Awake, he was master of himself: he could pray, praise, worship God unhindered. But the moment he closed his eyes to sleep, Margaret, or Satan in her shape, beset him, a seeming angel of light. He might dream of a thousand different things, as far apart as the poles, but before he woke that imperial, glowing figure was sure to appear, snuffing all other visions out in an instant, as the rising sun extinguishes every star. She glowed with two beauties that could never belong to a heavenly angel: an angel’s radiance, and a woman’s soft blushes. Angels cannot blush. So he knew it was a fiend.
He was alarmed, though hardly surprised at this latest artifice of the demon. From St. Anthony to Nicholas of the Rock, there was scarcely a hermit who had not been beset this way: sometimes with wild voluptuous visions, sometimes with lovely phantoms, warm, tangible, womanly on the outside, demons hidden within, that many a saint had failed to drive away. Everyone knew the tale of that “angel form with a devil’s heart” that came weeping to St. Macarius, hanging its lovely head like a bruised flower, telling a feigned tale of woe, weeping until it first beguiled the saint out of his tears, and then out of half his virtue.
But alongside those tales of Satanic cunning were the long, copious records of the hermits’ triumphs, and the weapons they had used to conquer. *Domandum est Corpus:* the body must be tamed. That had been the watchword of the desert saints for twelve hundred years. It was a tremendous war cry: they counted earthly affections as well as base appetites as part of the “body,” and crushed the whole heart through the medium of suffering, mortified flesh.
Clement told himself that the great enemy of man had only retreated to spring all the harder, had granted him a few days of pure joy and peace only to lull him off his guard against the soft, honeyed temptations he now poured over a soul that had already survived so many of his cruel blows. He set himself to tame his body: he shortened his hours of sleep, lengthened his prayers, pushed his usual strict temperance into total abstinence. Before, he had eaten only once a day, at sunset, in line with the ordinary hermit’s rule. Now he ate but once every forty-eight hours, drinking only a little water each day.
The visions grew even clearer. Then he turned to the famous antidote, the great febrifuge of all old anchorites: cold water. He found the deepest pool in the stream that ran past his cave, its source a little ways up at a holy well. He cleared all the large stones from the bottom, so he could stand in the water up to his chin. Fortified by the tales of a hundred saints who had gone before him, the next time the demon harassed him he sprang from his rough bed and plunged straight into the icy water. It made him gasp, almost shriek with the cold. It froze his marrow. “I shall die,” he cried, “I shall die, but better this than the eternal fires of hell.”
The next day his joints were so stiff he could not move. He felt like one great throbbing ache. Even in sleep his very bones felt like a row of raging, aching teeth — until the phantom he dreaded stepped close, gave him one pitying smile, and all his pain vanished at once.
Afraid that to plunge back into icy water again, weak and wasted as he was, would amount to suicide, he scourged himself with a whip of knotted cords until the blood ran, and then lay down smarting. When exhaustion blunted the sharp sting of the blows down to a soft, throbbing ache, the present faded, and the past came smiling back. He sat with Margaret at the duke’s feast, minstrels playing divinely, fountains gushing purple wine. Youth and love reigned in every heart, the very air perfumed with sweetness. Then the scene shifted: they stood at the altar together, man and wife, no one interrupting, and they wandered hand in hand, telling each other all their terrible dreams. He told her he had dreamed she was dead, and he was a monk, and that the dream had been so vivid, full of so many tiny, specific details, that only his own eyesight now could convince him it was only a dream, and they were truly one.
Once that new keynote was struck, every tune his mind played turned on it. Awake he was Clement the hermit, risen from unearthly night visions as dangerous as they were sweet. Asleep he was Gerard Eliassoen, the happy husband of the loveliest, best, truest girl in all Holland, all the happier for having spent so long trapped in hideous dreams where he had lost her.
His constant fasts, coupled with other austerities and the deep mental strain of fighting a supernatural foe, had reduced him nearly to a skeleton. But over those aching, thin bones hung flesh still unsubdued, quivering with earthly passion, or so he told himself. Why else would evil spirits hold such power over him?
His opinion was confirmed one day, when he caught himself sinking to sleep with a quiet feeling of complacency, because soon Margaret would come, and all his pain would be gone, and the unreal would feel real, and the real world would fade to nothing, for an hour.
He sprang up with a cry of dismay, stripped himself to the skin, climbed the bank of brambles above his cave, and threw himself down on them, rolling back and forth, writhing with the stabbing pain. He stumbled back into his den a mass of cuts and gore, and lay moaning for hours, until he fell into a deep, completely dreamless sleep from total exhaustion.
He woke to throbbing bodily pain, and bright mental exultation. He had broken the fatal spell. Yes, it was broken. Day after day passed, and her image no longer molested him. But he caught himself sighing a little, at his own victory.
The birds grew tamer and tamer, they perched on his hand. Two of the smallest let him gild their tiny claws. Eating only once every two days left him more bread crumbs to share with them.
