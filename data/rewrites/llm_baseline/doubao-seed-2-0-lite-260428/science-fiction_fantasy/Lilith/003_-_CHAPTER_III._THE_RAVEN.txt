I twisted around to stare at the space behind me, and found nothing solid at all: all was a smudged, soft haze where no line separated fog from heath, cloud from crag, no horizon anchored the world to anything I recognized. I told myself it was a trick of the light, that touch would set my scattered senses right, so I spread my arms and wandered this way and that, palms brushing at the empty air, hoping to bump into a tree trunk, a wall, a stone—something, anything to prove I was still in the world I knew. My search turned up nothing.

On instinct, I turned to the only living thing nearby: the raven, standing a few paces off, watching me with a weirdly precise mix of quiet respect and unmistakeable amusement. The absurdity of looking to a bird for help hit me for a second, and I spun away again, reeling in bewilderment sharpened with cold fear. Had I stumbled past the edge of every physical and psychological rule our world ran on? Could any ordinary person step out of the bounds of order at any random moment, and get left at the mercy of things that followed no laws at all? But I could still see the raven, still feel the springy heather under my boots, still hear wind sussurating through the low stems around me.

“How did I get here?” I said out loud, without meaning to.
“You came through the door,” a harsh, odd croaky voice answered right away.

I spun around, scanned every inch of the haze, and found no human shape anywhere. A cold terror seized me that I was slipping into madness—would I never be able to trust my own senses, my own memory ever again? The very next second I realized the voice had come from the raven: his head was tilted up at me, waiting, his beak parted just a little. There was no sun in the sky, but the bird cast a shadow sharp as spilled ink, and the shadow seemed to hang to him, not the ground, like it was a part of his body.

I beg the reader to bear with me here, because I am trying to describe something no words were built for. I was in a world—if you can even call it a world, a set of conditions, a shape of existence—so little like the rules of the life we all take for granted as the only real one, that every phrase I can reach for is no more than a blurry sketch of what I actually lived. I already fear I have taken on an impossible task: trying to write down things that no existing speech is shaped to hold, because they live in the spaces between what we know to name. I catch myself retyping sentences, trying to find truer words, only to feel the memory of the place slipping away from me the way a dream dissolves the second you wake, the familiar shapes melting one after another until you can no longer tell what they ever were.

I reasoned that any creature that could speak to me deserved at least the same courtesy I would give a human, maybe more, for the rarity of it. His voice was rough around the edges from a lifetime of croaking, but it was not unkind, even if his answers gave me no clarity.
“I never came through any door,” I told him.
“I saw you step through it with my own ancient eyes!” the raven asserted, firm but not rude.
“I never saw a door at all!” I protested.
“Of course you didn’t,” he replied. “All the doors you’ve seen so far—and you haven’t seen many—are doors you walk *in* through. That one you came through is a door *out*. The strangest thing you will learn soon enough is that the more doors you walk out of, the farther you go in.”

“Just tell me where I am, please.”
“I can’t. You don’t know the first thing about whereness. The only way to figure out where you are is to start making yourself at home here.”
“How am I supposed to do that when every single thing around me is alien?”
“Do something. Anything. The sooner you start, the better—because until you stop being a stranger here, getting out will be just as impossible as getting in was easy.”
“I stumbled in by accident. Once I find the way out, I will never come back.”
“You stumbled in, and you might stumble out again. Whether you got in unfortunately or not is something you will have to find out for yourself.”

“Do you leave this place often?”
“I go out whenever I please, but not for long. Your world is half-baked. It’s childish, and it’s absurdly proud of itself, and it’s not nearly grown enough for an old raven like me. At your service, of course.”
“Does that mean I’m wrong to assume a man is inherently better than a bird?”
“That all depends on the man and the bird. We don’t waste our time making broad generalizations. We judge one by one.” He paused, tilting his head. “I think it’s my turn to ask a question now.”
“Of course you have every right,” I said. “You earned it by being able to speak at all.”

“Good answer,” he croaked. “Tell me who you are. If you happen to know.”
“Of course I know who I am,” I scoffed, but the words died in my throat. I could not give him a single answer that held weight. I could not dredge up the name I went by back in my house, could not prove I was not someone else, could not even argue that I was not my own father, or my own fool wandering lost in a delusion. The name I had lived with my whole life meant nothing here, and I could not bring myself to care what it had been. I fell silent, and I knew that was the wisest choice I could make: there was no point trying to lie to a creature that saw past the accidents of surface life straight to the core of what a being *was*.

“Look at me,” the raven said, and turned his back.
In that instant, I knew him. He was no bird. He was a tall, stooped, thin man in a long black tailcoat, the very shape of the librarian my family had employed for decades. When he turned back to face me, he was a raven again.
“I have seen you before,” I said, feeling foolish more than shocked.
“How can you say that when you only saw my back? You have never even seen your own back, let alone the whole of yourself. So who am I?”
“Forgive me,” I said. “You were our old family librarian. I don’t know what else to call you.”
“Why apologize? You thought I was a raven, and that meant you acknowledged I existed. That is all anyone can ever ask of their fellow beings. As a thank you, I will give you a lesson: no one can ever call themself, truly, until they first know that they *are*, and then learn what their own self even is. Right now, nobody is fully themself, and the ‘self’ people cling to is nobody at all. You won’t understand all of it today, but you will understand what you need to. You stumbled into this realm far too soon, but you still have to make it your home. Home is the only place that lets you walk out and in as you please. Every other place only lets you go one way.”

He turned to walk away, and the next time I blinked, he was the librarian again, no shapeshift, just like he had tucked his raven shadow under his arm and carried it off with him. I stared after him until he vanished, no trace of him left in the haze, though I could not tell if he had walked out of sight or dissolved into the heather. I stood there, reeling, wondering if I was dead and did not know it, if this was what people called the afterlife, and I would wander forever looking for a place that was mine. How was I supposed to make this alien place home? The raven said I had to *do* something, but what? I was nobody, now, no name, no proof I existed at all.

I turned and followed the path the raven had taken, and soon spotted a stand of tall, slender pine trees. The sharp, resinous scent hit me long before I reached them, and I hurried toward the wood, plunging into its dim green twilight. Between two trunks, I saw something shimmering: no color, just a translucent, thrumming haze, like the hot air that rises off sun-scorched asphalt at midday, vibrating like a plucked violin string. The closer I walked, the less clear it grew, and when I stood right in front of it, I could not see it at all—only the pine trees on the other side looked soft, unsteady, like they would melt if I touched them. I tried to step between the two trunks, and a sharp static jolt ran through my whole body. I stumbled, and fell.

When I got to my feet, I was standing in my own garret. The rough wooden wall was right in front of me. I turned, and there was my mirror, the carved black eagle perched on its top rail as if it had landed there only seconds before.

A blind terror seized me, and I ran. The wide empty stretches of the garret behind me looked uncanny, like they had been waiting for hundreds of years. The thing they had been waiting for had arrived now, and they were waiting for something new. A shudder wracked me as I stumbled down the winding stair: the whole house felt strange to me now. I was sure something would leap out at me from the shadows behind my back. I darted down the spiral steps, slammed into the wall, fell, picked myself up and ran again. I got lost on the next floor, looping through the same three passages twice before I finally found the main stairwell. By the time I stumbled into the library, I was gasping for breath.

I swore I would never climb that terrible top stair again. The garret hung over the whole house, heavy as a living thing, threatening to crush me out of existence. It was the brooding brain of the building, full of invisible, alien dwellers that could step through into any room at any time. I was not safe anywhere in this house. I would sell it, I told myself. I would move to the Alps, build a tiny single-story wooden cottage on a bare crag, no garret, no top floor, guarded by nothing but old stone peaks that could only ever bury me under rockfall, not under a haze of unnameable things. I knew the whole thought was ridiculous, that there was a faint, sardonic humor running through my own panic—until I heard a raven’s croak echo in the back of my head.

If I did not even know the whole of my own house, what was to stop my own brain from making all of this up? Could I prove what my mind would generate next week, next month, next year? What hid in the core of my own thoughts? Was I even *there* at all, behind my own eyes? Who was I?

I could not answer that question any better now than I could when the raven had asked it out in the heath. I jumped to my feet, ran across the library to the masked door where the tattered, half-stuck manuscript jutted out from the shelf of books, and dropped to my knees. I pulled it out as far as it would go, but I could see nothing. I lit a taper, leaned in like I was peering into the jaws of something reluctant to open, and saw that the manuscript was verse. Left halves of lines glinted on the left page, right halves on the right. There was no way to pull the book far enough out to see a single full line, no way to guess what the poem was about. But the half-words, the broken shreds of verse, woke a feeling in me I had never known before: the kind of uncanny, aching pull you get from a half-remembered dream, a few bars of a half-heard song, a whiff of a scent you cannot place, the ghost of an idea that you know was once yours, if only you could reach it. I copied down the longest of the fragments, tried to finish the broken lines myself, and failed utterly. All I got for my effort was bone-deep weariness, and I fell asleep the second I climbed into bed, and slept without dreaming at all.

When I woke up the next morning, all the cold, clawing terror of the empty garret was gone.
