Edward, buoyed by his recent triumph, hastened to share his joy with Lucy. By the time he arrived at Bartlett's Buildings, his elation was so profound that when Mrs. Jennings visited the following day with her congratulations, Lucy could assure her hostess that she had never witnessed such spirits in her life. Her own happiness was absolute, and she eagerly joined Mrs. Jennings in anticipating a comfortable reunion at Delaford Parsonage before Michaelmas. Yet, Lucy's gratitude extended far beyond mere sentiment. She was eager to acknowledge the debt owed to both Edward and Elinor, declaring with warm sincerity that no effort Elinor might make for their sake would ever surprise her, for she believed her friend capable of anything for those she truly valued. In Lucy's eyes, Colonel Brandon was not merely a suitor but a saintly figure; she was fervently anxious that he be treated with the utmost reverence in worldly matters, even resolving to exploit his resources at Delaford—his servants, carriage, cows, and poultry—to the fullest extent possible.

It had been over a week since John Dashwood had visited Berkeley Street, and with no further attention paid to his wife's indisposition save for a single verbal inquiry, Elinor felt compelled to pay a visit. This obligation, however, clashed violently with her inclinations and lacked the support of her companions. Marianne, refusing to go herself, urged her sister not to go at all, while Mrs. Jennings, despite her carriage standing ready, harbored such a deep aversion to Mrs. John Dashwood that neither curiosity nor a desire to champion Edward could overcome her reluctance. Consequently, Elinor set out alone, bracing herself for a tête-à-tête with a woman whom the others detested.

Upon arrival, Mrs. Dashwood was initially denied, but before the carriage could depart, her husband emerged. He expressed great pleasure at meeting Elinor, claiming he had intended to visit Berkeley Street himself. Assuring her that Fanny would be delighted to see her, he invited her inside. They ascended to the drawing-room, only to find it empty.

"Fanny is in her own room, I suppose," John remarked, turning to Elinor. "I shall go to her presently; she will have no objection to seeing you. Indeed, she won't. Now, especially, there cannot be—but however, you and Marianne were always great favorites. Why did Marianne not come?"

Elinor offered a polite excuse for her sister's absence.

"I am not sorry to see you alone," John replied, his tone shifting. "For I have much to say to you. This living of Colonel Brandon's—can it be true? Has he really given it to Edward? I heard it by chance yesterday and came specifically to inquire."

"It is perfectly true," Elinor confirmed. "Colonel Brandon has presented the living of Delaford to Edward."

"Really! Well, this is astonishing! No relationship, no connection between them! And now that livings fetch such prices! What was the value of this?"

"About two hundred a year."

"Very well. For the next presentation to a living of that value—supposing the incumbent were old and sickly, likely to vacate it soon—one might expect fourteen hundred pounds. How did he fail to settle this before this person's death? Now, indeed, it would be too late to sell it, but a man of Colonel Brandon's sense! I wonder he should be so improvident in a matter of such common, natural concern! Well, I am convinced there is inconsistency in almost every human character. However, on recollection, the case may be this: Edward is only to hold the living until the person to whom the Colonel sold the presentation is old enough to take it. Aye, that must be the fact."

Elinor contradicted him positively, explaining that she had personally conveyed the offer from Colonel Brandon to Edward and thus understood the terms. She obliged him to submit to her authority.

"It is truly astonishing!" he exclaimed. "What could be the Colonel's motive?"

"A very simple one—to be of use to Mr. Ferrars."

"Well, well; whatever Colonel Brandon may be, Edward is a very lucky man. You will not mention this to Fanny, however. Though I have broken it to her and she bears it well, she will not like it much talked of."

Elinor struggled to refrain from noting that she believed Fanny could have borne the news of her brother's acquisition of wealth with composure, an event that impoverished neither her nor her child.

"Mrs. Ferrars," John added, lowering his voice to a tone befitting such an important subject, "knows nothing of this at present, and I believe it best to keep it entirely concealed from her as long as possible. When the marriage takes place, I fear she must hear of it all."

"But why such precaution? Though it is not to be supposed that Mrs. Ferrars can derive satisfaction from knowing her son has money enough to live upon—for that must be out of the question—why, upon her late behavior, is she supposed to feel at all? She has cast her son off forever and influenced others to do the same. Surely, after such an act, she cannot be imagined liable to any impression of sorrow or joy regarding him. She cannot be interested in anything that befalls him. She would not be so weak as to throw away the comfort of a child while retaining the anxiety of a parent!"

"Ah, Elinor," John said gently, "your reasoning is sound, but it is founded on ignorance of human nature. When Edward's unhappy match takes place, depend upon it his mother will feel as much as if she had never discarded him; and therefore, every circumstance accelerating that dreadful event must be concealed from her. Mrs. Ferrars can never forget that Edward is her son."

"You surprise me; I should think it must nearly have escaped her memory by this time."

"You wrong her exceedingly. Mrs. Ferrars is one of the most affectionate mothers in the world."

Elinor fell silent.

"We think now," John continued after a pause, placing his hand kindly on hers and speaking in an awed whisper, "of Robert's marrying Miss Morton."

Elinor, smiling at the grave importance her brother assigned to the statement, calmly replied, "The lady, I suppose, has no choice in the affair."

"Choice? How do you mean?"

"I only mean that, judging by your manner, it must be the same to Miss Morton whether she marries Edward or Robert."

"Certainly, there can be no difference; for Robert will now, to all intents and purposes, be considered the eldest son. As to anything else, they are both very agreeable young men; I do not know that one is superior to the other."

Elinor said no more, and John remained silent for a time. His reflections concluded thus: "Of one thing, my dear sister, I may assure you—and I will, because I know it must gratify you. I have good reason to think, indeed I have it from the best authority, or I should not repeat it, for otherwise it would be very wrong to say anything about it—but I have it from the very best authority. Not that I ever precisely heard Mrs. Ferrars say it herself, but her daughter did, and I have it from her—that in short, whatever objections there might be against a certain connection, you understand me, it would have been far preferable to her. It would not have given her half the vexation that this does. I was exceedingly pleased to hear that Mrs. Ferrars considered it in that light; a very gratifying circumstance for us all. 'It would have been beyond comparison,' she said, 'the least evil of the two, and she would be glad to compound now for nothing worse.' But however, all that is quite out of the question—not to be thought of or mentioned—as to any attachment, it never could be: all that is gone by. But I thought I would just tell you of this, because I knew how much it must please you. Not that you have any reason to regret, my dear Elinor. There is no doubt of your doing exceedingly well—quite as well, or better, perhaps, all things considered. Has Colonel Brandon been with you lately?"

Elinor had heard enough to agitate her nerves and fill her mind, whether to gratify her vanity or raise her self-importance. She was therefore glad to be spared the necessity of replying at length and the danger of hearing more from her brother by the entrance of Mr. Robert Ferrars. After a few moments' chat, John Dashwood, remembering that Fanny was yet uninformed of her sister's presence, left the room to find her. Elinor was left to deepen her acquaintance with Robert, whose gay unconcern and happy self-complacency, enjoyed while dividing his mother's love and liberality unfairly against his banished brother—an advantage earned solely by his dissipated lifestyle and his brother's integrity—only served to confirm Elinor's most unfavorable opinion of his head and heart.

Scarcely two minutes had passed before Robert began to speak of Edward. He, too, had heard of the living and was inquisitive. Elinor repeated the particulars she had given to John, and their effect on Robert, though different, was equally striking. He laughed immoderately. The idea of Edward becoming a clergyman, living in a small parsonage-house, diverted him beyond measure. Adding to this the fanciful imagery of Edward reading prayers in a white surplice and publishing the banns of marriage between John Smith and Mary Brown, he could conceive nothing more ridiculous.

Elinor, waiting in silence with immovable gravity for the conclusion of such folly, could not restrain her gaze from fixing on him with a look that spoke all the contempt it inspired. It was a look well bestowed, relieving her own feelings without giving him intelligence. He was recalled from wit to wisdom not by her reproof, but by his own sensibility.

"We may treat it as a joke," he said finally, recovering from the affected laugh that had prolonged the moment's genuine gaiety. "But, upon my soul, it is a most serious business. Poor Edward! He is ruined forever. I am extremely sorry for it; for I know him to be a very good-hearted creature; as well-meaning a fellow perhaps as any in the world. You must not judge of him, Miss Dashwood, from your slight acquaintance. Poor Edward! His manners are certainly not the happiest in nature. But we are not all born with the same powers, the same address. Poor fellow! To see him in a circle of strangers! To be sure, it was pitiable enough; but upon my soul, I believe he has as good a heart as any in the kingdom. I declare and protest to you I was never so shocked in my life as when it all burst forth. I could not believe it. My mother was the first to tell me, and feeling myself called on to act with resolution, I immediately said to her, 'My dear madam, I do not know what you intend to do on the occasion, but as for myself, I must say that if Edward does marry this young woman, I never will see him again.' That was what I said immediately. I was most uncommonly shocked, indeed! Poor Edward! He has done for himself completely, shut himself out forever from all decent society. But, as I directly said to my mother, I am not in the least surprised at it; from his style of education, it was always to be expected. My poor mother was half frantic."

"Have you ever seen the lady?"

"Yes; once, while she was staying in this house, I happened to drop in for ten minutes; and I saw quite enough of her. The merest awkward country girl, without style, or elegance, and almost without beauty. I remember her perfectly. Just the kind of girl I should suppose likely to captivate poor Edward. I offered immediately, as soon as my mother related the affair to me, to talk to him myself and dissuade him from the match; but it was too late then, I found, to do anything, for unluckily I was not in the way at first and knew nothing of it till after the breach had taken place, when it was not for me, you know, to interfere. But had I been informed of it a few hours earlier, I think it is most probable that something might have been hit on. I certainly should have represented it to Edward in a very strong light. 'My dear fellow,' I should have said, 'consider what you are doing. You are making a most disgraceful connection, and such a one as your family are unanimous in disapproving.' I cannot help thinking, in short, that means might have been found. But now it is all too late. He must be starved, you know, that is certain; absolutely starved."

He had just settled this point with great composure when the entrance of Mrs. John Dashwood ended the subject. Though she never spoke of it outside her own family, Elinor could see its influence on her mind in the confusion of countenance with which she entered and an attempted cordiality in her behavior toward Elinor. She even proceeded to express concern that Elinor and her sister were leaving town so soon, hoping to see more of them—an exertion in which her husband, attending her into the room and hanging enamored over her accents, seemed to distinguish everything that was most affectionate and graceful.
