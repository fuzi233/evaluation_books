I did not linger in sad wondering, as I half thought I might. A new, bright influence had crept into the hollow of my life, and for a little while, sorrow was held entirely at bay. Imagine a dell, sunken deep in the secret heart of an old forest, wrapped forever in mist and dimness: its turf is dank, its grass pale and perpetually damp. Then a storm, or a woodsman’s axe, tears a wide gap in the oaks. The breeze rushes in, the sun leans down, and that cold, sad dell becomes a deep cup brimming with light. High summer pours her blue glory and golden radiance down from a sky the starved hollow had never once gotten to see.

That was the new creed I carried now: a belief that happiness was not a dream reserved for other people.

Three weeks had passed since the garret encounter with the nun, and tucked in a locked casket in my upstairs box drawer, next to that first kind letter from Dr. Graham Bretton, rested four more just like it. All traced in his same firm, steady hand, sealed with the same clear wax, brimming with that quiet, vital comfort that had felt like a lifeline to me back then. I have read them in later years, of course; they are gentle, pleasant notes, written by a man who was pleased to be kind. The last two even had a few closing lines, half playful, half soft, “touched by feeling, but never overwhelmed by it.” Time mellowed them to that mild, warm quality, but when I first drank their nectar, fresh from that honoured source, it tasted like the wine of the gods — a draught Hebe might have poured, one the very immortals would have approved.

I think the reader might wonder how I answered those letters: whether I held myself back under Reason’s dry, strict check, or let the full, free rush of Feeling carry me away.

To tell the truth, I compromised. I bowed at the altar of Rimmon, and lifted my heart to a different, truer shrine. I wrote two replies for every one of his letters: one for my own heart to pour itself out on, the other short, neat, and suitable for Graham’s eyes.

First, I turned Reason right out of doors, barred the door behind her, and sat down with eager pen to spread my paper. I spilled every sincere, true feeling on those two full sheets: the deep, clinging affection, the rooted, burning gratitude I carried for him — and let me say here once and for all, with utter scorn for any cheap suspicion of cheap “warmer feelings”: no woman ever indulges those soft, silly illusions for a man she has known from the very start, and never once mistaken for someone who could ever return that kind of love. No one ever falls in love unless they have seen, or dreamed, the star of Hope rising over love’s troubled waters. What I felt was a devotion that wanted to take every last pain from his fate onto my own shoulders, that would have absorbed every storm and lightning bolt aimed at his life if it could, out of a passion of care so fierce it bordered on reverence.

And just when I had finished writing all of that, the bolts on my heart’s door rattled, Reason burst in, sharp and revengeful, snatched the full sheets, sneered at every line, tore them all to shreds, and wrote a terse, curt missive barely a page long, before folding, sealing, and sending it off. She was right, of course.

I did not only live on letters, though. He came to visit me, he checked on me constantly, took me out to his mother’s home at La Terrasse once every week, and made a point of making much of me. Dr. Bretton never failed to tell me exactly why he was so kind: “To keep that nun away,” he would say, laughing. “I’m determined to fight her for you, I can’t stand that ghost — not with that pale face cloth and those cold grey eyes. The second I heard those horrible details, I decided I was going to oppose her at every turn. I want her to show up while I’m here just once, so we can see which of us is cleverer.” But the nun never did appear. He treated me half as a doctor would treat a nervous patient, and half as a man naturally full of benevolence, tending to my frayed nerves with constant, gentle care.

One evening, the first of December, I was walking alone in the school’s paved carré at six o’clock. The classroom doors were closed, and inside the pupils were running wild, loud and chaotic in their evening recreation. The carré was almost entirely dark, save for a faint red glow seeping out from under the stove. The big glass doors and long windows were frosted over, and scattered sparkles of starlight spangled the white winter veil, proving it was a clear, moonless night. That I dared to linger there alone in the dark proved my nerves were growing strong and healthy again. I thought of the nun, but I barely feared her — even with the staircase behind me, leading up through blind, pitch-black dark landing after landing to that haunted garret. Even so, my heart jumped hard and my pulse spiked when I heard breathing and rustling, and turned to see a deeper shadow in the dark of the steps: a shape moving, descending. It paused for a moment at the classroom door, then glided right past me. Just then, the distant front door bell clanged loud. Real, ordinary sounds chase away ghostly fears: this shape was too short and round to be my gaunt, tall nun. It was only Madame Beck, making her rounds.

“Mademoiselle Lucy!” Rosine burst into the carré, lamp in hand, from the corridor. “There is someone waiting for you in the salon!”

Madame saw me, I saw Madame, Rosine saw us both, but none of us stopped to exchange pleasantries. I walked straight to the salon, and found exactly who I had half-expected: Dr. Bretton, all dressed in fine evening wear.

“The carriage is at the door,” he said. “My mother sent it to take you to the theatre. She was going herself, but a last-minute visitor stopped her. She said immediately, ‘Send Lucy in my place.’ Will you come?”

“Right now? I’m not dressed!” I glanced down despairingly at my plain dark merino frock.

“You have half an hour to change. I would have sent word earlier, but I only decided to come at five o’clock, when I heard we would get to see a genuine treat: the great actress Vashti is performing tonight.”

He spoke a name that sent a thrill right through me — a name that could make all of Europe hold its breath back then. It is quiet now, its once-roaring echoes long stilled, the woman who bore it long dead and gone, her name buried under night and oblivion. But back then, her day blazed at its full height, hot and bright as the star Sirius.

“I’ll go! I’ll be ready in ten minutes,” I promised, and ran. I never once hesitated with that silly, prudish thought that might cross your mind now, reader — that going anywhere with Graham without his mother as chaperone was improper. I could not have even imagined, let alone said that thought out loud, without being eaten up by shame so hot I think it would have burned the life right out of my veins. Besides, my godmother knew her son, and she knew me. She would no more have fussed over our unchaperoned outings than she would have felt the need to chaperone a brother and sister.

I did not need any fancy dress for the occasion. My soft dun mist crape frock would be perfect, so I went to find it in the big oak wardrobe in the dormitory, where no less than forty pupil dresses hung. But someone had recently cleaned out the wardrobe, and sent piles of old clothes up to the garret — my crape was amongst them. I got the garret key, and went up, fearless, almost thoughtless. I unlocked the door, and stepped inside. You can believe this or not, but the garret was not entirely dark the moment I walked in. A solemn light shone from one far corner, broad and steady, brighter than a star. It lit up the deep alcove, and a tattered scrap of tarnished scarlet curtain half drawn across it. Then, instantly, silently, before my very eyes, the light vanished. The curtain and the alcove vanished too, and that whole far end of the garret turned pitch black. I did not stop to investigate. I had no time, and no will. I grabbed my dress, which hung conveniently on the wall right near the door, rushed out, locked the door behind me in a frantic hurry, and darted back down the stairs to the dormitory.

But I was shaking so hard I could not dress myself. My fingers trembled too much to pin my hair, or fasten hooks and eyes, so I called Rosine and bribed her to help me. Rosine loved small gifts, so she did her work beautifully: she smoothed and plaited my hair as neatly as any professional stylist, adjusted my lace collar perfectly straight, tied my neck ribbon exactly even. She handed me my handkerchief and gloves, then lit the candle to walk me down the stairs — only I realized I had forgotten my shawl. She ran back to fetch it, and I stood with Dr. John in the vestibule, waiting.

“What is this, Lucy?” He looked down at me closely, frowning. “You’re all excited again. Ha — the nun came to see you, didn’t she?”

I denied it completely. I was vexed he would suspect me of seeing another ghostly illusion. He was still sceptical.

“She was here, as sure as I live,” he said. “That look in your eyes, that bright, wide stare, no one mistakes that.”

“She was not,” I insisted — and I could say it truthfully, for I had not seen the nun’s apparition.

“I can see the old symptoms on your face,” he argued. “You’re pale, and you have that dazed, high look the Scots call ‘raised’.”

He was so stubborn I decided I might as well tell him exactly what I had seen. Of course, he waved it off as another optical illusion, another symptom of overtaxed nerves. I did not believe him for a second, but I did not argue back. Doctors are all so stubborn, so immovable in their dry, materialist ways.

Rosine came back with my shawl, and I was ushered into the carriage.

The theatre was packed to the rafters — royalty and noble families had emptied their palaces and hotels into every tier, the whole crowd quiet, waiting reverently for the show to start. I felt so privileged to have a seat before that stage. I had heard so many wild tales of this actress’s power, I waited with a strange, severe, completely riveted curiosity, wondering if she would live up to her legendary name. She was a new, unstudied world I had never encountered before: a great, strange new planet, rising over the horizon.

She rose at nine o’clock that December night. She shone with a pale grandeur and steady might, but that star was already nearing its end. Seen up close, it was a chaos: half hollowed out, half consumed, a dying orb made half of lava, half of glowing light.

I had heard people call her plain, and I expected bony sharpness, grimness, something large, angular, sallow. What I saw was the shadow of a royal Vashti: a queen, once as fair as dawn, now turned pale as twilight, wasted like wax melting in a flame.

For a long while, I thought she was only a very unique woman, moving through the tragedy with grace and terrible power over the crowd. Then I realized I was wrong. There was something in her that was neither man nor woman: two devils sat in her eyes. Those dark forces carried her through the performance, propped up her frail, weak body, and as the action rose and the crowd’s excitement deepened, those devils shook her with all the passions of hell. They wrote HELL across her straight, haughty brow. They tuned her voice to the note of raw torment. They twisted her regal face into a demon’s mask. She stood there, Hate and Murder and Madness made flesh.

It was a marvellous sight, a mighty revelation. It was also low, horrible, immoral. Gladiators dying in the sand of the arena, bulls goring disembowelled horses, would have been a softer, gentler spectacle for that audience than Vashti torn apart by seven devils, devils that howled and ripped at the human body they haunted, refusing to be cast out.

Suffering had struck that stage empress, and she did not yield to it, or endure it, or even merely resent it. She stood locked in struggle, rigid in resistance. She was draped in pale antique folds of fabric, long and regular like marble sculpture. A background of deepest crimson made her stand out white as alabaster, white as silver — white as Death, most of all.

Where was the artist who had painted that soft, fleshy Cleopatra? He should have sat down there and studied this vision, searched for the thick brawn, the abundance of blood and overfed flesh he worshipped. All materialists should have come and looked on her.

Pain for her never softened into something good. Tears never watered a harvest of wisdom. She looked on sickness, on death itself, with the eyes of a rebel. She was wicked, perhaps, but she was strong. Her strength had conquered Beauty and Grace both, and bound them to her side as captives, peerlessly fair and docile. Even in the wildest frenzy of her performance, every movement was royal, imperially grand. Her hair flying loose in the throes of the tragedy was still an angel’s hair, glorious under a halo. Fallen, insurgent, banished, she still remembered the heaven she had rebelled against. The light of heaven pierced the dark of her exile, showing her just how far she was from it.

Vashti was not a good woman, I was told. I could see that myself: she was a spirit from the depths of Tophet. But if so much unholy power could rise up from below, surely an equal force of sacred grace might one day descend from above?

I soon forgot to wonder what Dr. Graham thought of her. The powerful magnetism of her genius pulled my heart right out of its usual quiet orbit. My attention turned from the soft, gentle light I usually followed, to this fierce, red, cometary light, hot and burning on my vision. I had seen acting before, but nothing like this. Nothing that had ever silenced Desire and astonished Hope, outstripped Impulse and paled every thought I had ever conceived. It was a power like a swollen winter river, thundering over a cataract, carrying the soul along like a leaf on the steep, steel sweep of its descent.

Miss Fanshawe once called Dr. Bretton a serious, impassioned man, too grave and too easily moved. I never saw him that way. He was not a meditative man, not a sentimental man. He was as impressionable as rippling water — but almost as unchangeable as water, too. Breeze and sunlight might move him, but no metal could engrave him, no fire could leave a permanent brand on him. He could think, and think very well, but he was a man of action before he was a man of thought. He could feel, vividly, in his own way, but his heart had no chord for wild, all-consuming enthusiasm. For bright, soft, gentle joys, his face and his smile lit up as beautifully as summer clouds dyed rose and silver, pearl and purple. But for anything stormy, wild, intense, dangerous and flaming, he had no sympathy at all. When I finally looked over at him, I saw he was watching that sinister, sovereign Vashti not with wonder, or worship, or even dismay — just with intense, detached curiosity. Her agony did not pain him. Her wild, strangled moan did not move him much. Her fury made him recoil a little, but never to the point of horror. Cool, steady young Briton! The pale cliffs of his native England never look down on the tides of the English Channel more calmly than he watched that Pythian, possessed performance that night.

I leaned over and asked him what he thought of her. He woke as if from a little daydream, he had been so deep in his own quiet thoughts. “How do you like Vashti?” I asked.

“Hm-m-m,” he said, that first vague, expressive sound, and then a strange, critical, almost callous smile spread across his lips. For natures as steady as his, of course, wild passions felt alien. In a few short, terse phrases, he gave me his opinion: he judged her as a woman, not as an artist. It was a harsh, branding judgment.

That night was already marked in my book of life, not with a white line, but with a deep red cross. But we were far from done with it, and far more unforgettable moments were about to be written down in ink that would never fade.

Towards midnight, as the tragedy darkened to its final death scene, the whole theatre held its breath. Even Graham bit his lower lip, knitted his brow, and sat transfixed. All eyes were fixed on the stage, all ears strained to listen. Nothing was visible but that white, pale form sunk in a seat, still fighting her last, worst foe, Death. Nothing was heard but her gasps, her mutinous breaths, her defiance even as she died. It seemed as if that overabundant will, convulsing her perishing mortal frame, was fighting every inch of ground, selling every drop of blood, resisting to the very last the theft of every last one of her senses — determined to see, to hear, to breathe, to live, right up to the very edge of the line Death draws and no one can cross.

Just then, a ominous rustle ran backstage. Feet ran, voices called. A murmur of “Fire!” rippled through the gallery, and then it was yelled, echoed, screamed at the top of every voice. Panic exploded, faster than pen can ever describe it: people rushing, shoving, crushing, blind selfish cruel chaos.

But Dr. John? I can see him still, his face calm with that quiet, comely courage he always carried.

“Lucy, sit still, I know you will,” he said, glancing down at me with that same serene, firm kindness I had seen at his mother’s safe, peaceful hearth.
