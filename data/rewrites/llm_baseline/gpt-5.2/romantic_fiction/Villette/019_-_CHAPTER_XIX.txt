My stay at La Terrasse ran two weeks beyond the end of the holidays, as if time itself had been coaxed into a gentler simmer. It was Mrs. Bretton who managed it—quietly, deftly, like a careful housekeeper who knows exactly how long a broth must stand off the fire if it is to clear. Her son, after one look at me, delivered his verdict with that decisive kindness of his: I was not yet fit to be sent back to the Rue Fossette, to the close air and constant vigilance of the pensionnat. Mrs. Bretton drove into town, spoke with the directress, and returned having won me the indulgence on the plea of rest, of sun, of recovery made complete only by change.

With that mercy, however, came an attention I could have spared: a ceremonious visit from Madame Beck.

She arrived one bright day in a hired carriage, and I knew—before I saw her—that she had come to examine, as one inspects a kitchen one has never trusted, the place where Dr. John lived. The château’s pleasant rise, the clipped order of the garden, the neatness within, all exceeded whatever she had imagined, and she praised everything with a brisk lavishness, as if compliments were coin she paid out to purchase knowledge. The blue salon became, on her tongue, “une pièce magnifique.” She congratulated me—profusely—on my friends, “tellement dignes, aimables, et respectables,” and even tossed me a small, well-turned compliment as one throws a sugared almond to a child to keep it sweet-tempered.

When Dr. John came in she ran to him with a spring, and in a moment had lit a whole Catherine-wheel of rapid French: felicitation upon his “château,” upon “madame sa mère, la digne châtelaine,” upon his looks—freshened, as they always were, by that amused good-nature with which he listened to her florid speech. She shone; she dazzled; she seemed all sparkle and good will.

Half for mischief and half to ask a question about school business, I followed her out and looked into the fiacre after she had taken her seat and the door was shut. In that mere breath of time, the change was complete. The gay wheel had stopped. She sat rigid—stern as a judge, grave as a sage—her mouth a straight line, her eyes sharpened into calculation. Strange little woman: froth on the surface, iron below.

When I returned, I teased Dr. John on Madame Beck’s devotion. He laughed—laughed as if it were excellent sport. His eyes flashed with that lively humour which made his company so light and warm when he could forget Miss Fanshawe; and he repeated Madame’s speeches, mimicking her voluble delivery with such accuracy that even her trills and quick emphases seemed to flutter about the room like ribbons.

Those days at La Terrasse were sunlight in the plainest sense: light on the floor, light on the walls, light in the mind. I had often thought how, when little Georgette Beck was convalescent, I would carry her in my arms and pace the garden for hours beneath a certain wall where vines hung heavy. The southern sun ripened those grapes slowly, working sweetness into them as patient hands work seasoning into a stew; and it did something similar for the child—warmed her thin frame, tinted her cheeks, coaxed her breath into steadiness.

There are tempers like that sun—bland, genial, nourishing; in their presence the poor in spirit recover a kind of appetite for living. Both Mrs. Bretton and her son had this gift, and they bestowed it without fuss, as naturally as a table is spread when one expects a guest. Each day, some little plan would be proposed that ended in wholesome enjoyment—a drive, a walk, some modest visit that turned, by their tact, into pleasure.

Dr. John’s time was full—his engagements lay about him like a day’s work laid out upon a board—yet by system he sliced it into order and always contrived an hour of liberty. I saw him work hard, but rarely over-driven; never fretful, never confused. What he did seemed done with the ease of ample strength, and with a cheerfulness that was like good air. Under his guidance, in that one happy fortnight, I learned more of Villette and its environs than in the whole eight months before—streets, quarters, people, names of places I had never heard, doors I had never imagined opened on anything worth seeing. He spoke, too, not in cold generalities, not in borrowed book-phrases, but in exact particulars; his talk had the relish of something freshly prepared, not reheated. He observed character as a practiced palate distinguishes flavours—subtly, patiently, with recognition of what others missed. Before my eyes his disposition seemed to unfold into a new day.

His mother was benevolent; he, even more so. When I accompanied him into the Basse-Ville—the crowded, poor quarter—I saw that his errands there were not only those of a physician. He moved among the wretched as if it were natural to him to carry relief. The lower orders liked him. In hospitals, his patients welcomed him with something like enthusiasm.

And yet—here I must stop myself. It is easy to slip from faithful narrator into partial eulogist. Dr. John was not perfect—no more than I am. His humanity showed itself in every hour. A god could not have his cruel vanity, nor his occasional levity; no immortal could share that temporary oblivion of all but the present, that passing passion for it—less in material indulgence than in a selfish extraction of whatever it could yield to feed his masculine self-love. He delighted in nourishing that hungry sentiment, careless of cost, indifferent to the price others paid for its provender.

If the reader finds contradiction between the public and private Graham Bretton—out of doors and by the fireside—let it be noted both portraits are true. In public, modest, earnest, almost unconscious of himself; at home, aware of what he was and had, pleased by homage, reckless at times in exciting it, vain in receiving it.

It was hardly possible to oblige him quietly. If you thought you had made some trifle for his use unseen, and that—like other men—he would take it as a matter of course, he would prove by a smile and a word that his eye had followed the work from beginning to end. It pleased him to be served, and he let the pleasure show—brightly, frankly.

And he liked to discharge what he called debts. If his mother worked for him, he repaid her with an overflow of spirits—teasing, loving, gay. If he discovered my hand in any small service, he planned, by way of recompense, some recreation.

It astonished me how completely he possessed Villette: not merely its open streets, but its galleries, salles, cabinets; every door shutting in something worth seeing seemed to yield to him like “Open, Sesame.” Science never held my head; but art drew me with a blind, fond instinct, and I loved picture-galleries—especially to be left there alone. In company, a wretched idiosyncrasy prevented me from seeing much or feeling anything; talk pressed on me like a tight bodice, and in half an hour I would be knocked up with fatigue and mental incapacity. Dr. Bretton, cicerone after my own heart, took me early before the rooms filled, left me for two or three hours, and returned when his business allowed. Then I was happy—not always in admiring, but in examining, questioning, forming conclusions.

At first there was a struggle between Will and Power. Will demanded I praise what was orthodox to admire; Power groaned its inability to pay the tax. I sneered at myself, spurred my taste like a dull horse, tried to refine, to whet my zest; but the more I bullied my senses, the more stubbornly they refused false homage. Conscientious admiration produced only a wonderful fatigue. In time I concluded I might dispense with the labour, and sank into a calm luxury before ninety-nine out of a hundred frames.

It seemed to me good pictures were as scarce as good books. Standing before certain celebrated chef-d’œuvres I no longer trembled to say, “This is not like nature.” Nature’s daylight never wore those colours; skies were not laid out in turbid indigo with weeds plastered on for trees. Several complacent fat women, admirably finished, struck me as no goddesses at all. There were scores of little Flemish pieces—marvellously wrought—like fashion-plates for costumes; industry whimsically misapplied. And yet here and there a fragment of truth satisfied the conscience; a gleam of real light cheered the eye. Nature broke through in a mountain snow-storm; her glory shone in a southern day; a portrait held a look that proved clear insight; a face in an historical painting startled one by its living likeness, as if genius had breathed and the paint remembered. These exceptions I loved; they grew dear as friends.

One day, at a quiet early hour, I found myself almost alone in a gallery where a single picture, of portentous size, sat enthroned in the best light. A cordon guarded it; a cushioned bench was placed before it for worshipping connoisseurs who, having gazed themselves off their feet, might finish sitting. The canvas seemed to consider itself queen of the collection.

It represented a woman larger than life. I calculated, with a coolness almost professional, that if this lady were put upon a scale meant for bulk commodities, she would turn fourteen to sixteen stone. She was very well fed; much butcher’s meat—bread, vegetables, liquids besides—must have gone to build that breadth, that wealth of muscle, that affluence of flesh. She lay half-reclined on a couch in broad noon-day, as if indolence were her office. Yet she looked strong enough to do the work of two plain cooks; no weak spine pleaded for the sofa. She ought to have been standing, or sitting bolt upright, and—moreover—decently dressed. Out of an abundance of drapery—seven-and-twenty yards, I should think—she had contrived an inefficiency of garment. Around her lay untidiness without excuse: pots and pans—vases and goblets, if you will—rolled on the foreground; a rubbish of flowers, and an absurd disorder of heavy curtains smothering couch and floor. The catalogue named it “Cleopatra.”

I sat and wondered, taking advantage of the bench more for rest than devotion, and thinking that while roses, gold cups, jewels were prettily painted, the whole was enormous claptrap. The room began to fill, though I scarcely noticed; it mattered little. I soon tired of the huge, dark gipsy-queen and turned, for refreshment, to some exquisite little still-lifes hung modestly beneath her coarse splendour: wild-flowers, wild-fruit, mossy wood-nests; eggs casketed like pearls seen through clear green sea-water. They seemed to carry their own hush with them, the quiet one hears in hedgerows—small sounds, true sounds: a leaf brushing another, a bird’s foot in grass.

Suddenly a light tap touched my shoulder. I started and met a face bent close to mine—frowning, almost shocked.

“Que faites-vous ici?” said a voice.

“Mais, Monsieur, je m’amuse.”

“Vous vous amusez! et à quoi, s’il vous plait? Mais d’abord, faites-moi le plaisir de vous lever; prenez mon bras, et allons de l’autre côté.”

I did precisely as I was bid. It was M. Paul Emanuel, returned from Rome and now—if possible—less tolerant of insubordination than before. His presence altered the air; even the gallery’s murmur seemed to shift, as if his small steps had changed the rhythm of sound in the room.

“Permit me to conduct you to your party,” he said, as we crossed.

“I have no party.”

“You are not alone?”

“Yes, Monsieur.”

“Did you come here unaccompanied?”

“No, Monsieur. Dr. Bretton brought me here.”

“Dr. Bretton and Madame his mother, of course?”

“No; only Dr. Bretton.”

“And he told you to look at that picture?”

“By no means; I found it out for myself.”

His hair was shorn close as raven-down; had it been longer I think it would have bristled. I began to understand his drift and felt a certain pleasure in staying cool and letting him heat.

“Astounding insular audacity!” he cried. “Singulières femmes que ces Anglaises!”

“What is the matter, Monsieur?”

“Matter! How dare you, a young person, sit down with the self-possession of a garçon and look at that picture?”

“It is a very ugly picture; but I cannot see why I should not look at it.”

“Bon! bon! Speak no more. But you ought not to be here alone.”

“If I have no society—no party, as you say? And what does it signify whether I am alone or accompanied? Nobody meddles with me.”

“Taisez-vous, et asseyez-vous là—là!” He set a chair with emphasis in a dull corner before a series of especially dreary frames. “Mais, Mademoiselle, asseyez-vous, et ne bougez pas—entendez-vous?—jusqu’à ce qu’on vienne vous chercher, ou que je vous donne la permission.”

“Quel triste coin!” I cried. “Et quels laids tableaux!”

Laids indeed: four pictures called in the catalogue “La vie d’une femme.” They were flat, dead, pale, formal as boiled starch. The first, “Jeune Fille,” came out of a church-door with a missal in hand; prim dress, eyes cast down, mouth pursed—the image of a precocious little hypocrite. The second, “Mariée,” knelt at a prie-dieu with a long white veil and hands plastered finger to finger, showing the whites of her eyes in a way that exasperated. The third, “Jeune Mère,” drooped over a clayey, puffy baby with a face like an unwholesome full moon. The fourth, “Veuve,” a black woman holding a black little girl by the hand, both studiously contemplating an elegant French monument in some Père la Chaise corner. These four “Anges” were grim and grey as burglars, cold and vapid as ghosts. What women to live with—insincere, ill-humoured, bloodless, brainless nonentities! As bad in their way as the indolent gipsy-giantess Cleopatra in hers.

My eyes would not stay long chained to such vapour. By degrees I veered round and surveyed the gallery.

By then a crowd had gathered round the Lioness from whose vicinage I had been banished; nearly half were ladies. M. Paul afterwards told me these were “des dames,” and therefore it was proper for them to contemplate what no “demoiselle” ought to glance at. I told him plainly I could not agree; the doctrine had no sense. With his usual absolutism, he requested my silence and, in the same breath, denounced my rashness and ignorance. A more despotic little man never filled a professor’s chair.

I noticed, too, that he looked at Cleopatra himself at his ease, and for a long while. Yet he did not neglect to glance toward me from time to time—making sure, I suppose, that I remained obedient in my dull corner and did not break bounds.

By-and-by he accosted me again.

Had I not been ill, he wished to know. He understood I had.

“Yes; but now I am quite well.”

Where had I spent the vacation?

“Chiefly in the Rue Fossette; partly with Madame Bretton.”

He had heard I was left alone there—was it so?

“Not quite alone: Marie Broc was with me.”

He shrugged; expressions crossed his face in quick succession, contradictory as gusts of wind. Marie Broc was well known to him; she never failed to stir in him a conflict between taste and justice. Her appearance, manners, and often unmanageable temper irritated him and roused antipathy—he was too apt to feel that when his will was thwarted. Yet her misfortunes appealed strongly to his compassion, a claim his nature could not deny. Thus, almost daily, impatience and disgust fought pity and fairness; and, to his credit, the better feelings usually prevailed. But when the former did prevail, M. Paul showed a terrible phase: his passions ran deep; his aversions and attachments were vivid; and though he held them in check with force, their vehemence was not hidden from an observer. Ordinary minds were prone to fear him; yet to fear him was an error. Nothing drove him nearer frenzy than the tremor of distrust; nothing soothed him like confidence tempered with gentleness. But to offer that required understanding, and his nature was rarely comprehended.

“How did you get on with Marie Broc?” he asked, after a silence.

“Monsieur, I did my best; but it was terrible to be alone with her.”

“You have then a weak heart. You lack courage; and perhaps charity. Yours are not the qualities that might constitute a Sister of Mercy.”

He was religious, in his way: the self-denying part of his faith commanded his homage.

“I don’t know. I took as good care of her as I could; but when her aunt came to fetch her away, it was a great relief.”

“Ah! you are an egotist. There are women who have nursed hospitals-full of similar unfortunates. You could not do that?”

“Could Monsieur do it himself?”

“Women worthy the name ought infinitely to surpass our coarse, fallible, self-indulgent sex in such duties.”

“I washed her, kept her clean, fed her, tried to amuse her; but she made mouths at me instead of speaking.”

“You think you did great things?”

“No; but as great as I could do.”

“Then limited are your powers; for in tending one idiot you fell sick.”

“Not with that, Monsieur. I had a nervous fever: my mind was ill.”

“Vraiment! Vous valez peu de chose. You are not cast in an heroic mould; your courage will not sustain you in solitude; it merely gives you the temerity to gaze with sang-froid at pictures of Cleopatra.”

It would have been easy to show anger at his teasing hostility; but I had never yet been angry with him, and I would not begin then.

“Cleopatra!” I repeated quietly. “Monsieur, too, has been looking at Cleopatra; what does he think of her?”

“Cela ne vaut rien,” he replied. “Une femme superbe—une taille d’impératrice, des formes de Junon; mais une personne dont je ne voudrais ni pour femme, ni pour fille, ni pour sœur. Aussi vous ne jetterez plus un seul coup d’œil de sa côté.”

“But I have looked at her a great many times while Monsieur has been talking: I can see her quite well from this corner.”

“Turn to the wall and study your four pictures of a woman’s life.”

“Excuse me, M. Paul; they are too hideous. But if you admire them, allow me to vacate my seat and leave you to their contemplation.”

“Mademoiselle,” he said, and a half-smile—or what he meant for a smile—twitched across his face, grim and hurried. “You nurslings of Protestantism astonish me. You unguarded Englishwomen walk calmly amid red-hot ploughshares and escape burning. I believe if some of you were thrown into Nebuchadnezzar’s hottest furnace you would issue forth untouched by the smell of fire.”

“Will Monsieur have the goodness to move an inch to one side?”

“How! At what are you gazing now? You are not recognising an acquaintance amongst that group of jeunes gens?”

“I think so—yes. I see there a person I know.”
