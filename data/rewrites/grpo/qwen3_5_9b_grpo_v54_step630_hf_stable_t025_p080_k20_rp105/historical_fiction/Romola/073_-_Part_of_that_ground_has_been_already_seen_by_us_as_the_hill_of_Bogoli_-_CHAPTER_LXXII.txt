The words echoed in Romola's mind, not as a whisper, but as a collective cry rising from the lips of many. They were the final utterances of Savonarola, spoken as he signed his confession before the brethren of San Marco: "Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy." These words struck her with a sudden, paralyzing doubt, casting a shadow over the memory of his earlier composure. Her uncertainty deepened when more pitiful tidings reached her ears.

May nineteenth had arrived, bringing with it the bright sunshine that marked the entry of the two Papal Commissaries into Florence. They came charged with concluding Savonarola's trial, welcomed by the populace with acclamations demanding the friar's death. The common cry was now a roar of vengeance: "It is the Frate's deception that has brought all our misfortunes; let him be burned, and all shall be right."

The following day, the records confirm, the tortured frame of the friar was subjected to fresh agony. At the mere sight of the horrific instruments, Savonarola, convulsed with agitation, fell to his knees. In brief, passionate words, he retracted his confession, declaring that he had lied in denying his prophetic gift and that if he suffered, it would be for the truth—"The things that I have spoken, I had them from God."

Yet the torture continued. When questioned under the lash why he had spoken those retracting words, the answer came, broken and desperate: "I said it that I might seem good; tear me no more, I will tell you the truth."

Florentine assessors witnessed this second retraction, and the news spread quickly through the city, filling Romola with dismayed uncertainty. Then, a thought flashed across her mind: *There will come a moment when he may speak.* A moment when the dread of falsehood would be the only thing left, when he stood before death itself, lifted above the mob, looking upon them for the last time. No one could hinder him from speaking a final, decisive word. *I will be there.*

Three days later, on May twenty-third, 1498, a long, narrow platform stretched across the great piazza, leading from the Palazzo Vecchio toward the Tetta de' Pisani. Unlike previous preparations, there was no grove of fuel scattered about; instead, a single great heap of combustible material stood on the circular area at the platform's end. Rising above this pyre was a gibbet with three halters. Though one arm had been truncated to avoid the appearance of a cross, the structure still bore a cruciform shape that made some observers deeply uneasy.

On the marble terrace of the Palazzo stood three tribunals. One, near the door, was reserved for the Bishop to perform the ceremony of degradation upon Fra Girolamo and his two brethren, who were to suffer as accomplices. Another was set aside for the Papal Commissaries to pronounce them heretics and schismatics, handing them over to the secular arm. The third, positioned close to the Marzocco statue at the corner where the platform began, was for the Gonfaloniere and the Eight, who would pronounce the sentence of death.

Once again, the Piazza was thronged with expectant faces, awaiting the great fire. For the majority of the crowd pressing around the gibbet, the anticipation was fueled by ferocious hatred or a morbid curiosity to witness a barbarous spectacle. Yet, on the wide pavement, on the rooftops, and at the windows, others remained. Amidst their bitter grief and their endurance of insults hurled at the hypocritical Piagnoni, they harbored a lingering hope. Even at this eleventh hour, they prayed that God might interpose, manifesting their beloved prophet as His servant. And there were still more, like Romola, who trembled with eagerness for that final moment when Savonarola might declare, "O people, I was innocent of deceit."

Romola stood at a window on the north side of the Piazza, far removed from the marble terrace where the tribunals stood. Near her, also gazing out with painful doubt at the man who had once commanded her reverence, was a young Florentine of twenty-two named Jacopo Nardi. Later, he would earn honor as one of the few to write about Fra Girolamo with a simple desire for veracity. Seeing the struggle in Romola's face—the shuddering horror of the scene warring with her yearning to witness the end—Jacopo had gently said, "Madonna, there is no need for you to look at these cruel things. I will tell you when he comes out of the Palazzo. Trust to me; I know what you would see."

Romola covered her face, but the hootings that seemed to make the hideous scene visible could not be shut out. Finally, her arm was touched, and she heard the whispered words, "He comes." She looked toward the Palace and saw Savonarola led out in his Dominican garb. She watched him stand before the Bishop, stripped of the black mantle, the white scapulary, and the long white tunic, until he stood in a close woollen under-tunic that revealed no sacred office, no rank. He had been degraded, cut off from the Church Militant.

The baser elements of the multitude delighted in degradations, understanding them as the ultimate satire. A fresh hoot of triumph rose as the three degraded brethren passed on to the tribunal of the Papal Commissaries, who pronounced them schismatics and heretics. Could the prophet not now be seen as a schismatic and a heretic? It was easy to believe in the damnable state of a man standing stripped and humiliated.

They passed the third tribunal, that of the Florentine officials who would pronounce the sentence. Among them, even from her distance, Romola discerned the odious figure of Dolfo Spini, clad in the grave black lucco as one of the Eight. The three figures, in their close white raiment, trod their way along the platform amidst yells and grating tones of insult.

"Cover your eyes, Madonna," Jacopo Nardi urged. "Fra Girolamo will be the last."

It was not long before she had to uncover them again. Savonarola was there. He was no longer far off; he had mounted the steps, and she could see him looking round upon the multitude.

But in that same moment, expectation died. She saw only what he saw: torches waving to kindle the fuel beneath his dead body, faces glaring with a light far worse than any before. She heard only what he heard: gross jests, taunts, and curses. The moment had passed. Her face was covered again, and she knew only that Savonarola's voice had passed into eternal silence.

On the evening of May twenty-second, 1509, five persons whose histories we have followed sat in a handsome upper room opening onto a loggia. From its right-hand corner, the view extended along the Borgo Pinti, over the city gate, and toward Fiesole and the solemn heights beyond.

At one end of the room, an archway led into a narrow inner recess where light fell from above upon a small altar draped in fair white linen. Over the altar hung a picture, discernible only as the small full-length portrait of a Dominican Brother, shaded from the overhead light by overhanging branches and wreaths of flowers. Fresh tapers stood unlit before it. The decoration seemed incomplete; part of the floor was strewn with a confusion of flowers and green boughs. Seated among them was a delicate, blue-eyed girl of thirteen, tossing her long, light-brown hair from her eyes as she selected blooms for the wreaths she wove. Occasionally, she looked up at her mother's work, offering instructions with a little air of superiority.

The mother, Tessa, was not particularly clever at weaving flowers or any other craft. Her fingers had not grown more adroit with the years; indeed, they had become much fatter. She worked slowly, turning her head frequently and asking Ninna's opinion with much deference, for Tessa never ceased to be astonished at the wisdom of her children. She still wore her contadina gown, only broader than the old one, with a silver pin in her rough, curly brown hair and the memorable necklace with its red cord resting against her bosom. Her rounded face wore a look of childish contentment that was even more perfect than in her youth; everyone was so good in the world, Tessa thought. Even Monna Brigida never found fault with her now, doing little else than sleeping—a practice Tessa found amiable and liked for herself.

Monna Brigida slept at that moment in a straight-backed armchair a couple of yards away. Her hair, parting backward under her black hood, possessed a soft whiteness unlike snow, simply the lovely whiteness of aged hair. Her chin had sunk upon her bosom, her hands resting on the elbow of the chair. She had not been weaving or working; she had only been looking on, as she usually did, before falling asleep.

The other two figures sat farther off at the wide doorway opening onto the loggia. Lillo sat on the ground with his back against the angle of the door-post, his long legs stretched out. He held a large book open on his knee, occasionally making a dash with his hand at an inquisitive fly with an air of interest stronger than that excited by the finely-printed copy of Petrarch kept open at one place, as if he were learning something by heart.

Romola sat nearly opposite Lillo, yet she observed him not. Her hands were crossed on her lap, her eyes fixed absently on the distant mountains. She was evidently unconscious of anything around her. An eager life had left its marks upon her: the finely-molded cheek had sunk a little, the golden crown of her hair less massive. Yet there was a placidity in Romola's face that had never belonged to it in her youth. It is but once that we can know our worst sorrows, and Romola had known them while life was new.

Absorbed thus, she was not at first aware that Lillo had ceased looking at his book and was watching her with a slightly impatient air, signaling his desire to talk but his uncertainty whether she wished it. Persevering looks eventually made themselves felt. Romola turned her eyes from the distance and met Lillo's impatient dark gaze with a brighter, brighter smile. He shuffled along the floor, keeping the book on his lap, until he got close to her and lodged his chin on her knee.

"What is it, Lillo?" Romola asked, pulling his hair back from his brow. Lillo was a handsome lad, though his features were turning out more massive and less regular than his father's. The blood of the Tuscan peasant ran in his veins.

"Mamma. Romola, what am I to be?" he asked, well contented that there was a prospect of talking until it would be too late to recite "Spirto gentil."

"What should you like to be, Lillo? You might be a scholar. My father was a scholar, you know, and taught me a great deal. That is the reason why I can teach you."

"Yes," said Lillo, hesitating. "But he is old and blind in the picture. Did he get a great deal of glory?"

"Not much, Lillo. The world was not always very kind to him, and he saw meaner men than himself put into higher places because they could flatter and say what was false. And then his dear son thought it right to leave him and become a monk; and after that, my father, being blind and lonely, felt unable to do the things that would have made his learning of greater use to men, so that he might still have lived in his works after he was in his grave."

"I should not like that sort of life," said Lillo. "I should like to be something that would make me a great man, and very happy besides—something that would not hinder me from having a good deal of pleasure."

"That is not easy, my Lillo. It is only a poor sort of happiness that could ever come by caring very much about our own narrow pleasures. We can only have the highest happiness, such as goes along with being a great man, by having wide thoughts and much feeling for the rest of the world as well as ourselves. And this sort of happiness often brings so much pain with it that we can only tell it from pain by its being what we would choose before everything else, because our souls see it is good. There are so many things wrong and difficult in the world that no man can be great—he can hardly keep himself from wickedness—unless he gives up thinking much about pleasure or rewards and gets strength to endure what is hard and painful. My father had the greatness that belongs to integrity; he chose poverty and obscurity rather than falsehood. And there was Fra Girolamo—you know why I keep to-morrow sacred: *he* had the greatness which belongs to a life spent in struggling against powerful wrong and in trying to raise men to the highest deeds they are capable of. And so, my Lillo, if you mean to act nobly and seek to know the best things God has put within reach of men, you must learn to fix your mind on that end and not on what will happen to you because of it. And remember, if you were to choose something lower and make it the rule of your life to seek your own pleasure and escape from what is disagreeable, calamity might come just the same; and it would be calamity falling on a base mind, which is the one form of sorrow that has no balm in it, and that may well make a man say, 'It would have been better for me if I had never been born.' I will tell you something, Lillo."
