The ink on the page seems to shimmer, not from the heat of the Ditchingham sun, but from the sheer weight of memory pressing down upon the paper. This is no mere chronicle; it is a testament etched by a man whose hands have trembled in the grip of death and whose eyes have witnessed the impossible. Here, within these lines, lies the raw, unvarnished truth of a journey that began with a whisper and ended with the roar of empire.

It starts where all great fictions begin: with a meeting. Sir Henry Curtis, a figure of such commanding presence that he seemed to bend the very air around him, stands before us, weaving a tapestry of legend so intricate it threatens to unravel the fabric of reason. From the dusty corridors of London, we are dragged into the heart of Africa, where the myth of Solomon's Mines ceases to be a fairy tale and becomes a terrifying, pulsating reality. Umbopa enters our service, bringing with him the silent wisdom of the land, while the hunt for elephants serves as a grim prelude to the march into the desert—a landscape that swallows sound and hope alike.

Water! Water! cries the parched throat of the expedition, echoing the primal desperation that defines our existence in this unforgiving realm. We press onward along Solomon's Road, a path carved by greed and folly, until we breach the gates of Kukuanaland. Here, the rules of civilization dissolve. Twala, the King, sits upon a throne of power and terror, presiding over a court where magic and malice dance inextricably linked. The witch-hunt that follows is not merely a conflict of arms but a collision of worlds, a descent into a nightmare where the line between ally and enemy blurs into nothingness.

We give a sign, a desperate gesture in the face of overwhelming odds, leading inevitably to the battle. The attack comes with the fury of a storm, and the Last Stand of the Greys becomes a monument to courage and sacrifice, a final, bloody stand against the encroaching darkness. As Good Falls succumbs to sickness, the shadow of mortality looms larger than any beast or sorcerer. We reach the Place of Death, a threshold where the living must confront the end of their own stories. Yet, amidst the ruin, we stumble upon Solomon's Treasure Chamber, a vault of unimaginable wealth that offers no salvation, only a cruel irony. Hope abandons us, retreating into the abyss, leaving only Ignosi's farewell—a poignant, tragic acknowledgment of a destiny fulfilled and lost. And finally, buried beneath layers of dust and time, we find Preparer's Note, the key that unlocks the door to understanding.

This faithful, unpretending record is dedicated to every boy and girl who dares to dream of the unknown, to those who believe that the map of the world extends beyond the borders drawn by cartographers. To the young at heart, who still hunger for tales of treasure, war, and wild adventure, this book is offered as a beacon.

Twelve years have passed since the first edition took flight, and the gratitude owed to the readers remains as vast as the African plains themselves. The author hopes that this revised form will reach new shores, finding homes in hearts that remain young enough to embrace the thrill of the unknown. But now, in 1907, a postscript adds a layer of bittersweet verification to the narrative. Imagination has been swallowed by fact; the mines of Solomon have been found, their gold pouring forth once more, their diamonds glittering under the colonial sun. The Kukuanas, or rather the Matabele, have been subdued by the thunder of white man's bullets, their kingdoms reduced to history books. Yet, despite the taming of the wild and the conquest of the unknown, there remains a strange, enduring pleasure in these simple pages. May they continue to delight generations to come, fulfilling the hope of our old and departed friend, Allan Quatermain, whose spirit lives on in every word written here.

Allan Quatermain stood before the press, the weight of the finished volume pressing down on him with a gravity far heavier than any physical burden he had ever borne. The ink was dry, the binding secure, yet the manuscript remained a source of acute dissatisfaction regarding both its stylistic roughness and its substantive gaps. He knew, with the clarity of a man who had stared death in the eye at the Battle of Loo, that this account was not intended to be a comprehensive chronicle of every step taken or sight witnessed during their expedition into the heart of Kukuanaland. Too many threads remained loose; too many wonders were left untold.

Among the silenced voices of his memory were the haunting legends of the chain armor that had shielded them from annihilation, and the terrifying presence of the "Silent Ones"—the colossal statues guarding the entrance to the stalactite cavern. Had he yielded to his own impulses, he would have dissected the subtle, suggestive divergences between the Zulu and Kukuana dialects, exploring how language shaped their distinct worlds. He would have devoted pages to the indigenous flora and fauna, cataloging the eight varieties of antelope he had encountered—creatures wholly unknown to him prior to this journey—and the myriad new species of bulbous plants that thrived in that alien soil.

Yet, the most profound omission, the one that gnawed at him most, was the magnificent military organization of the Kukuanas. In his estimation, their system surpassed even the formidable structures erected by Chaka in Zululand. It allowed for a swiftness of mobilization that bordered on the supernatural, and crucially, it eschewed the barbaric practice of enforced celibacy that plagued neighboring tribes. Furthermore, he had barely scratched the surface of their domestic customs, those quaint and intricate rituals of family life, nor had he detailed their mastery of metallurgy. Their "tollas," those heavy throwing knives, were testaments to a science carried to perfection: backs forged from hammered iron, edges of beautiful steel welded onto the frame with such skill that the union seemed seamless.

In the end, standing amidst the clutter of his own regrets, Allan found solace in the pragmatic counsel he had shared with Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good. They had agreed then, as he did now, that the most effective vessel for truth was simplicity. To elaborate further would only obscure the narrative with unnecessary ornamentation. These details, he reasoned, belonged to future treatises, to works that might eventually be deemed necessary by others. For now, the story required only to stand bare and honest.

With a final sigh, he turned to address the inevitable criticism of his prose. He offered his apologies for the bluntness of his writing, acknowledging that he was a man more accustomed to the recoil of a rifle than the delicate handling of a quill. He possessed no gift for the grand literary flights and flourishes that adorned the novels he occasionally indulged in reading. Yet, he held fast to a conviction: that simple things possess an enduring impressiveness. A book, he believed, was easier to grasp when stripped of pretension. As the Kukuana proverb declared, "A sharp spear needs no polish." By the same token, a true story, however extraordinary, demanded no decoration in fine words. It needed only to be told plainly, so that the reader might see the world exactly as it was, without the distortion of artistic excess.

The inkwell sat heavy on the blotter, a silent witness to the peculiar irony of my current station. Fifty-five years old, and yet here I am, Allan Quatermain, Gentleman of Durban, attempting to commit a history to paper. It is a strange business, this turning of a hunter's hand to the pen. I wonder what sort of chronicle I shall produce when, if indeed I survive the journey, I finally lay down the final period. My life has been a tapestry woven from trade, hunting, fighting, and mining, threads spun since I was a boy while others were still learning their ABCs. Yet, paradoxically, I amassed my fortune only eight months ago. It is a vast sum now, though I confess I do not fully grasp its magnitude. Still, I doubt I would willingly endure the last year and a half of that existence again—not if I could guarantee a safe return with the gold intact. Truth be told, I am a timid soul at heart, possessing a profound distaste for violence, and I am weary of adventure. So why write this book? It is not my trade. I am no literary man, save for my devotion to the Old Testament and the whimsical *Ingoldsby Legends*. Let me attempt to catalogue my motivations, simply to ensure I possess any at all.

My first reason is straightforward: Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good requested it.

My second reason is physical necessity. I am currently confined to this bed in Durban, plagued by a persistent agony in my left leg. Since that cursed lion seized me, I have been susceptible to this malady, and the condition is particularly acute at present, forcing me to limp with every step. There must be a toxin lingering within a lion's teeth; otherwise, how can it be that once my wounds have ostensibly healed, they flare up again, invariably at the precise season of my original mauling? It is a grievous indignity for a man who has dispatched sixty-five lions—or more—in the course of his career—to find that the sixty-sixth decides to gnaw his leg like a piece of chewing tobacco. Such an event shatters the rhythm of one's life. Being an orderly individual, I chafe against this disruption. This, however, is merely context.

My third reason is filial duty. I wish to provide amusement for my son, Harry, who resides at the London hospital, training to become a physician. Medical work, even the dissection of the deceased, can eventually induce a profound satiety, rendering the days dull and tedious. This narrative, regardless of its other qualities, promises excitement and vitality, ensuring that Harry remains engaged and diverted for a week or so while he peruses our exploits.

And finally, the fourth and most compelling reason: I intend to recount the strangest story I have ever encountered. It may sound odd to claim such a thing, especially given the conspicuous absence of women from the cast—save for Foulata. Wait, stop! There is Gagaoola, provided one considers her a woman rather than a fiend. But she was a century of years old, making her ineligible for matrimony, so I shall exclude her from the count. Rest assured, there is not a single petticoat in the entire history.

Very well, I must mount the saddle. It is a stiff beast, and I feel as though I am bogged down to the axle. But *"sutjes, sutjes"*—as the Boers say, though I suspect their spelling eludes me—softly does it. A sturdy team will eventually pull through, provided they are not too impoverished. One cannot achieve much with weak oxen. Now, let us commence.

I, Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal, Gentleman, hereby make oath... No, that is not the proper opening for a novel. That was the header for my deposition regarding the tragic demise of Khiva and Ventvögel before the magistrate, but it lacks the necessary flair for fiction. Furthermore, am I truly a gentleman? The definition escapes me, yet I have interacted with individuals who fit the description and others who do not, regardless of their race or wealth. I have known natives who embodied the spirit of gentility, and conversely, I have encountered white men flush with cash and fresh from home who lacked it entirely.

Regardless, I was born into gentility, though my life has been spent as a wandering trader and hunter. Whether I retain that status is for you to decide. Heaven knows I have strived to maintain it. I have taken many lives, yet never wantonly, nor have I stained my hands in innocent blood; my actions have been strictly defensive. The Almighty granted us life, and I assume He intended for us to protect it, a principle I have always adhered to. I pray this record will not be used against me when my time comes. Alas, the world is cruel and wicked, and for a timid man like myself, I have been embroiled in considerable conflict. I cannot adjudicate the moral complexities, but I can attest that I have never stolen, although I must confess to cheating a Kafir out of a herd of cattle once. He had played me a foul trick, and the guilt has haunted me ever since.

It was approximately eighteen months ago that my paths first crossed with Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good. The circumstances were unfortunate. I had ventured beyond Bamangwato in search of elephants, only to be beset by ill fortune. Every endeavor failed, culminating in a severe bout of fever. Once recovered sufficiently, I trekked down to the Diamond Fields, liquidated my ivory stock along with my wagon and oxen, dismissed my hunters, and boarded a post-cart for the Cape. After a week in Cape Town—where I discovered the hotels charged exorbitant rates and observed the botanical gardens, which I believe would greatly benefit the nation, and the new Houses of Parliament, which I fear will accomplish nothing—I resolved to return to Natal via the *Dunkeld*, a vessel then awaiting at the docks for the arrival of the *Edinburgh Castle* from England. I secured a berth, and that afternoon, the passengers from the *Edinburgh Castle* transferred aboard. We weighed anchor and sailed into the unknown.

Among the newcomers were two figures who immediately captured my attention. One was a gentleman of roughly thirty, perhaps the most imposing specimen I had ever beheld. He possessed a chest of immense breadth and arms of extraordinary length. His hair was yellow, matched by a thick yellow beard, framed by clear-cut features and large grey eyes set deep within his skull. Never have I seen a finer visage; he struck me as reminiscent of an ancient Dane. Though my knowledge of antiquity is limited—aside from knowing a modern Dane who defrauded me of ten pounds—I recall seeing a portrait of a similar gentry, a type of white Zulu, drinking from massive horns with long hair cascading down their backs. Gazing at my friend by the companion-ladder, I mused that if he allowed his hair to grow slightly longer, donned a chain shirt upon his broad shoulders, and gripped a battle-axe and a horn mug, he would have been the perfect model for that painting. Coincidentally, and demonstrating how bloodline reveals itself, I later learned that Sir Henry Curtis, the giant's name, was indeed of Danish descent. He also bore a striking resemblance to someone else, though the memory eluded me at the time.

The other man, conversing with Sir Henry, was stout, dark-complexioned, and distinctly different in bearing. I instantly recognized him as a naval officer; it is difficult to mistake a sailor of the fleet. Having accompanied several on shooting expeditions throughout my life, I have found them to be the finest, bravest, and kindest fellows imaginable, albeit occasionally prone to profanity. Reflecting on my earlier query regarding the nature of a gentleman, I offer this answer: A Royal Naval officer embodies the title in a general sense, barring the occasional black sheep. I suspect it is the boundless oceans and the invigorating breath of God's winds that cleanse their souls, dispelling bitterness and forging them into the ideal of manhood.

My assessment proved correct. The dark man was indeed a naval officer, Lieutenant John Good, aged thirty-one. After seventeen years of service, he had been discharged from Her Majesty's employ, retaining only the hollow honor of a commander's rank, as promotion was deemed impossible for him. This is the fate awaiting those who serve the Crown: ejected into the harsh world to seek sustenance precisely when they begin to truly comprehend their craft and stand at the zenith of their capabilities. I presume they accept this stoically, but for my part, I would prefer earning my daily bread as a hunter. One's coins may be scarcer, but one receives far fewer kicks.

I ascertained his name by consulting the passenger manifest. Captain John Good was broad, of medium height, dark, and stout—a curious figure to behold. He was impeccably neat and cleanly shaved, always wearing an eyeglass in his right eye. It appeared to have grown there, lacking a string, and he removed it solely to wipe it. Initially, I suspected he slept in it, but I was mistaken. When retiring, he tucked it into his trouser pocket alongside his false teeth, of which he possessed two magnificent sets. My own dentures, being of inferior quality, have frequently tempted me to violate the Tenth Commandment. However, I digress.

Shortly after departure, evening descended, bringing with it foul weather. A brisk breeze arose from the land, generating a thick, aggravated Scotch mist that drove everyone below decks. The *Dunkeld*, a flat-bottomed punt traveling lightly, rolled with alarming severity. It seemed poised to capsize repeatedly, yet she held firm.

"What news did you gather regarding my brother's expedition among the Bamangwato?" Sir Henry inquired, pausing as I prepared to pack my pipe before addressing Captain Good.

"I gathered this," I replied, "and until today, I have kept it silent before any living soul. I learned that he is departing for Solomon's Mines."

"Solomon's Mines?" Both listeners exclaimed simultaneously. "Pray, where do they lie?"

"I cannot say with certainty," I admitted. "I know only where they are *said* to be. In my youth, I beheld the peaks bordering them, yet a vast expanse of one hundred and thirty miles of barren desert separated me from that sight. To my knowledge, no white man has ever traversed that wasteland successfully, save for a solitary exception. Nevertheless, perhaps the wisest course is to recount the legend of Solomon's Mines as I understand it, provided you grant me your solemn promise not to divulge a single word of what follows without my explicit consent. Are you bound by this pledge? I ask because my motives are peculiar."

Sir Henry nodded assent, and Captain Good echoed, "Undoubtedly, undoubtedly."

"Very well," I commenced. "As you might surmise, elephant hunters constitute a rugged breed, men who concern themselves chiefly with the grim necessities of survival and the customs of the Kafir tribes. Yet, occasionally, one encounters a soul willing to sift through native oral histories, attempting to reconstruct fragments of this shadowed continent's past. It was such a man who first imparted the legend of Solomon's Mines to me, nearly three decades ago. This occurred during my inaugural elephant hunt in the Matabele lands. His name was Evans, a man whose life ended tragically the following year at the claws of a wounded buffalo; he now rests in the earth near the Zambesi Falls.

"One evening, recalling our conversation, I recounted to Evans some remarkable geological formations I had discovered while tracking koodoo and eland in what is now the Lydenburg district of the Transvaal. I noted that recent prospectors had rediscovered these workings, though I had known of them long prior. A magnificent roadway, hewn directly from solid rock, led to the entrance of a vast gallery. Within its mouth, stacks of gold-bearing quartz stood piled high, awaiting roasting—a testament to workers who fled in haste. Furthermore, approximately twenty paces inward, the tunnel was abruptly sealed by a section of exquisite masonry."

"'Ay,' Evans remarked, 'but I shall weave you a far stranger tale.' Thus began his narrative of a ruined city deep within the interior, which he identified as the biblical Ophir—a claim later validated by scholars long after Evans' death. I listened with rapt attention, for in my youth, the notion of an ancient civilization, capable of extracting immense wealth from a region now sunk into barbarism, seized my imagination with irresistible force. Suddenly, he shifted the topic: 'Lad, have you ever heard of the Suliman Mountains, situated northwest of the Mushakulumbwe territory?' I confessed ignorance. 'Ah, well,' he continued, 'there lies the true location of Solomon's mines—his diamond mines, specifically.'

"'How can you assert this?' I queried.

"'Assert it!' he retorted. 'What is "Suliman" but a corrupted pronunciation of Solomon? Moreover, an elderly Isanusi, a witch-doctoress dwelling in the Manica hinterland, revealed the truth to me. She spoke of the peoples beyond those mountains as a distinct branch of the Zulus, speaking a refined dialect, composed of giants even larger than their kin. Among them reside mighty sorcerers who mastered their arts from white men in an age when the world was shrouded in darkness, possessing the secret key to a miraculous vein of "bright stones."'

"At the time, I scoffed at this fabrication, though it intrigued me, especially given that the Diamond Fields remained undiscovered. Poor Evans departed shortly thereafter and met his end, and for twenty years, the matter faded from my mind. However, precisely twenty years later—a span considerable for an elephant hunter, who rarely survives two decades in the bush—I received more concrete intelligence concerning Suliman's Mountains and the realm beyond. I was stationed beyond the Manica country at a settlement known as Sitanda's Kraal, a desolate outpost where sustenance was scarce and game elusive. Stricken by fever and in a precarious state, I was visited one day by a Portuguese traveler accompanied by a single companion, a half-caste.

"I am intimately acquainted with the low-born Portuguese of Delagoa Bay, creatures often described as devils incarnate, thriving on human suffering and the trade in enslaved flesh. Yet, this visitor differed profoundly from the scoundrels I usually encountered. In appearance, he resembled the noble *doms* of my reading: tall, slender, with piercing dark eyes and curling grey mustaches. We conversed briefly; his English was fractured, and I grasped a portion of his Portuguese. He introduced himself as José Silvestre, proprietor of an establishment near Delagoa Bay. As he prepared to depart the following day with his half-caste companion, he removed his hat in a gesture of old-world courtesy.

"'Farewell, señor,' he intoned. 'Should our paths cross again, I shall stand as the wealthiest man in the world, and I shall ensure you are remembered.' I chuckled faintly, my strength failing me, watching him stride toward the great western desert. I wondered if madness gripped him, or what phantom hope drove him into that void.

"A week elapsed, and the fever receded. One evening, seated upon the dusty ground before my tent, gnawing the final leg of a pitiful bird purchased for a scrap of cloth valued at twenty birds, I stared at the burning red sun descending over the horizon. Suddenly, a figure emerged on the ridge opposite, roughly three hundred yards distant. Clad in a coat, it appeared to be a European. The figure crawled on hands and knees, then struggled upright, staggering a few steps before collapsing and resuming the crawl. Recognizing signs of dire distress, I dispatched one of my hunters. Upon his arrival, the truth became clear.

"'José Silvestre, of course,' Captain Good observed.

"Yes, José Silvestre—or rather, his skeletal remains and a fragment of skin. His visage was a sickly yellow, drained of color by bilious fever, his large dark eyes bulging from sockets emptied of flesh. Only parchment-thin skin, white hair, and protruding gaunt bones remained.

"'Water! For Christ's sake, water!' he whimpered. His lips were fissured, and his tongue, protruding between them, was swollen and black.

"I administered water mixed with a trace of milk. He consumed it greedily, drinking nearly two quarts without pause. I refused further fluid. The fever returned with vengeance, and he collapsed, babbling deliriously of Suliman's Mountains, diamonds, and the desert. I dragged him into the tent, doing what little aid was possible, knowing the outcome was inevitable. Around eleven o'clock, he quieted. Exhausted, I lay down and drifted into slumber.

"At dawn, I awoke to find Silvestre sitting upright, a specter of emaciation, gazing fixedly toward the desert. Soon, the first rays of the sun sliced across the vast plain, illuminating the distant crest of one of the towering Suliman Mountains, over a hundred miles away.

"'There it stands!' the dying man cried in Portuguese, extending his long, thin arm. 'But I shall never reach it. Never. Not a soul shall ever reach it!'

"Suddenly, he halted, seemingly steeling himself. 'Friend,' he whispered, turning his gaze to me, 'do you perceive me? My vision fades.'

"'Yes,' I replied softly. 'Lie down now and rest.'

"'Ay,' he answered. 'Rest awaits me soon. All eternity belongs to it. Listen, I perish! You have shown me kindness. I shall entrust you with this writing. Perhaps you possess the vitality to traverse the desert that claimed my poor servant and me.'

"He rummaged in his tunic and produced what I initially mistook for a Boer tobacco pouch crafted from the skin of the swartvetpens or sable antelope. Secured by a strip of hide, known locally as a *rimpi*, he struggled to loosen it before handing it to me. 'Untie it,' he commanded. I complied, revealing a torn scrap of yellow linen bearing rust-colored inscriptions. Tucked inside was a sheet of paper.

"He grew weaker, his voice fading. 'The paper contains everything written on the linen. It took me years to decipher. Listen: my ancestor, a political exile from Lisbon and among the earliest Portuguese to set foot on these shores, penned this while dying upon those mountains never trodden by white feet before or since. His name was José da Silvestra, living three centuries ago. His slave, waiting on this side of the range, discovered him dead and carried the message home to Delagoa. It has lain dormant in our family until I finally dared to read it. I have paid with my life for this quest, but another may succeed, becoming the richest man in the world—the richest man in the world. Give it to no one, señor; go yourself!'

"He then wandered into delirium, and within an hour, he was gone.

The tide of the Indian Ocean churned with a violent indifference, tossing the *HMS Plover* against the jagged teeth of the Durban bar. It took four to five days to claw our way north from the Cape, a gauntlet dictated by the whims of the wind and the stubbornness of the sea. Usually, the landing at East London was a nightmare of delay; the harbor was a myth, a promise of wonders that had yet to materialize, forcing ships to wait twenty-four hours while cargo boats struggled through the breakers. But today, fortune smiled upon us. The bar was clear, the water calm, and the tugboats surged forward like mechanical beasts, dragging their long strings of ugly, flat-bottomed barges into the surf. Packages were hurled aboard with a deafening crash, treated with the brutal efficiency of men who understood that survival depended on speed, not gentleness. Whether it held fragile porcelain or rough wool, the cargo received the same merciless impact.

I watched in horror as a crate containing four dozen bottles of champagne shattered instantly. The liquid erupted, fizzing and boiling in the muddy depths of the barge, a grotesque parody of celebration. It was a wicked waste, and the Kaffir laborers in the boat seemed to share my disdain. They scavenged for unbroken bottles, smashing the corks and drinking greedily. Yet, they had failed to account for the physics of fermentation; the expanding gas swelled their bellies until they rolled about in the bilge, screaming that the "good liquor" was *tagati*—bewitched. I shouted down from the deck, telling them it was the white man's strongest medicine and that they were already dead men. Terrified, they fled to the shore, and I suspect they will never taste champagne again.

While the ship steamed up the coast of Natal, my mind drifted constantly to Sir Henry Curtis's proposal. We avoided the topic for days, filling the silence with tales of the hunt—stories that require no embellishment, for the truth of the wild speaks for itself. Finally, on a scorching January evening, the sun dipped below the horizon as we passed the coast. The landscape was a masterpiece of contrasts: red sandhills rolling into vivid green plains, dotted with distant Kaffir kraals and fringed by a ribbon of white surf that crashed in pillars of foam against the rocks. Just before Durban, the scenery grew richer, deeper. Sheer kloofs, carved by centuries of torrential rains, revealed sparkling rivers; the bush wore its deepest emerald cloak, interwoven with the lighter greens of maize fields and sugar cane. Here and there, a white house smiled at the placid sea, adding a touch of domestic warmth to the wild expanse. To me, a man who had spent decades in the wilderness, such beauty felt incomplete without the presence of civilization. Perhaps the Garden of Eden was fairer before man walked it, but I always believed it became truly fair when Eve adorned it.

Our calculations had been slightly off; the sun was already low when we dropped anchor off Durban Point, hearing the signal gun boom out to announce the arrival of the English Mail. Too late to cross the bar that night, we retired to dinner, watching the mail bags transferred to the lifeboat under the cover of darkness. When we returned to the deck, the moon hung high, casting a brilliance that nearly drowned the frantic flashes of the lighthouse. Sweet, spicy scents drifted from the shore—odors that always reminded me of hymns and missionary zeal—and a hundred lights twinkled in the windows of the Berea. Nearby, a brig groaned as sailors hauled up the anchor, their voices rising in song. It was a perfect night, one of those rare moments in Southern Africa where the moon draped the world in a garment of silver, weaving a spell of peace even over the great bulldog of a sporting passenger. The beast, usually eager to tear apart the baboon in the cage on the forecastle, lay snoring contentedly at the cabin door, dreaming of victory.

Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, and I moved to the wheel, falling silent for a while.

"Well, Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said eventually, breaking the quiet. "Have you given thought to my proposals?"

"Aye," Captain Good echoed eagerly. "What do you think, Mr. Quatermain? Are you coming with us to Solomon's Mines, or wherever that gentleman you knew as Neville may have ended up?"

I stood, tapping out the glowing ember of my pipe before answering. My decision had not yet crystallized, needing that final fraction of a second to solidify. As the burning tobacco fell into the churning sea, the choice was made. Often, prolonged deliberation leads nowhere; sometimes, a single extra moment is all that is needed.

"Yes, gentlemen," I said, sitting back down. "I will go. By your leave, I shall explain why, and on what conditions."

"First, regarding terms: One, you shall bear all expenses. Any ivory or valuables we acquire shall be divided equally between Captain Good and myself. Two, I request £500 upfront for my services prior to departure, undertaking to serve you faithfully until you abandon the enterprise, until success crowns us, or until disaster claims us. Three, before we begin the trek, you must execute a deed agreeing that, in the event of my death or permanent disablement, you will provide my son, Harry—who studies medicine at Guy's Hospital in London—with £200 annually for five years. By then, he should be capable of supporting himself if he possesses any talent. That is all, I believe, and I dare say it is sufficient."

"No," Sir Henry replied immediately. "I accept them gladly. I am resolved on this project and would pay more, considering the unique and exclusive knowledge you possess."

"Pity I did not ask for more, then, but I shall not renege on my word. Now that my terms are set, I will reveal my reasons for accepting. First, gentlemen, I have observed you both closely over the last few days. If you will not deem me impertinent, I must say I like you, and I believe we shall pull together well under the burden of this journey. That is vital when facing such a long road ahead."

"And now, concerning the journey itself," I continued, my voice steady despite the gravity of my words. "I tell you plainly, Sir Henry and Captain Good, that I do not believe we will survive it, provided we attempt to cross the Suliman Mountains. What befell the old Dom da Silvestra three hundred years ago? What happened to his descendant twenty years later? What was the fate of your brother? Frankly, gentlemen, since their ends were sealed, I believe ours will be identical."

I paused to gauge their reaction. Captain Good looked distinctly uneasy, but Sir Henry's face remained impassive. "We must take our chances," he said simply.

"You may wonder," I pressed on, "why a man who considers himself timid should undertake a voyage I foresee as fatal. There are two reasons. First, I am a fatalist. I believe my time is appointed by forces beyond my control or will. If destiny calls me to the Suliman Mountains to die, I shall go and I shall die. God Almighty surely knows His mind regarding me, so I need not fret over that. Second, I am a poor man. For nearly forty years I have hunted and traded, yet I have never amassed more than a living wage. Did you know, gentlemen, that the average lifespan of an elephant hunter is between four and five years? Thus, I have survived roughly seven generations of my class, and my time is likely drawing near. Should anything happen to me in the ordinary course of business, by the time my debts are cleared, nothing would remain to support my son Harry during his training. Whereas now, he is secured for five years. That is the essence of the matter."

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said, his expression grave and attentive, "your willingness to undertake an enterprise you believe will end in disaster reflects immense credit upon you. Whether you are right or wrong, time and events alone will judge. But regardless of the outcome, let me assure you that I intend to see this through to the end, sweet or bitter. If we are to be struck down, all I can say is that I hope we secure some fine shooting first, eh, Good?"

"Yes, yes," the captain agreed. "All three of us are accustomed to facing danger and holding our own lives in our hands. There is no point in turning back now. Let us go down to the saloon and take a drink for luck." And so we did, clinking tumblers in the dim light.

We debated rapidly the feasibility of stalking the game, but quickly dismissed the plan. The wind was unfavorable, and furthermore, traversing the blinding white expanse would surely reveal us, regardless of our caution.

"Well, we must attempt a shot from here," Sir Henry decided. "Quatermain, which shall it be—the repeating rifles or the expresses?"

Again, a difficult choice presented itself. "Each of you take the buck opposite to him," I instructed. "Aim precisely at the point of the shoulder, high up. Umbopa, give the command so we may all fire simultaneously."

A tense silence fell, each of us aligning his rifle with the utmost concentration, aware that life itself hung in the balance of the next trigger pull.

"Fire," Umbopa commanded in Zulu.

At almost the exact same instant, the three rifles roared, sending three plumes of smoke drifting before us as a hundred echoes shattered the silence of the snow. Though weak, we surged down the intervening slope of snow. Within ten minutes of the discharge, the animal's heart and liver lay before us.

Now, a new dilemma emerged: we possessed no fuel to cook the meat. We exchanged glances of dismay.

"Starving men should not be fanciful," Good stated pragmatically. "We must eat raw meat."

With no alternative, the gnawing hunger rendered the prospect less repulsive than it might otherwise have been. Life and vigor returned to us; our feeble pulses strengthened, and blood coursed forcefully through our veins. His character was beautiful, quiet yet possessing an iron strength.

In this way, the day unfolded as the night had, provided one can apply such temporal markers where everything was shrouded in the deepest night. When I finally struck a match to check the hour, it was seven o'clock.

Once more we ate and drank, and as we did so, a thought sparked in my mind.

"How is it," I asked, "that the air in this place remains fresh? It feels thick and heavy, yet it is perfectly pure."

"Great heavens!" Good exclaimed, springing to his feet. "I never considered that. It cannot seep through the stone door; if ever a door was truly airtight, it is that one. It must originate from somewhere. If there were no current of air, we would have suffocated or been poisoned the moment we entered. Let us investigate."

Wonderful was the transformation wrought by this mere spark of hope. In an instant, we were all three crawling on hands and knees, probing for the slightest indication of a draft. Soon, my enthusiasm met a chilling obstacle. My hand brushed against something cold—it was the face of the dead Foulata.

For an hour or more, we continued our futile search until, battered by countless knocks against tusks, chests, and the chamber walls, both Sir Henry and I yielded in despair. Good, however, persisted, remarking with a forced cheerfulness that it was preferable to doing nothing.

"I say, you fellows," he called out in a constrained voice, "come here."

Naturally, we scurried toward him.

"Quatermain, place your hand where mine is. Do you feel anything?"

"I... I think I feel air rising."

"Now listen." He rose and stamped firmly upon the spot, and a flame of hope ignited in our hearts. *It rang hollow.*

With trembling hands, I struck a match. Only three remained, and we realized we were positioned in the far corner of the chamber, a fact explaining why we had missed the hollow resonance during our earlier exhaustive survey. As the match burned, we scrutinized the area. There was a seam in the solid rock floor, and, by heavens! there, flush with the stone, lay a circular ring. We spoke no word; our hearts hammered too wildly with hope to permit speech. Good possessed a knife fitted with a hoof-pick hook. He opened it and scraped around the ring. Finally, he worked the tool underneath, levering gently to avoid snapping the hook. The ring began to shift. Being stone, it had not rusted fast over the centuries, unlike iron might have. Slowly, it rose upright. Then Good thrust his hands inside and tugged with all his might, but nothing moved.

"Let me try," I said impatiently, for the ring's position in the corner made simultaneous pulling impossible. I seized it and strained, but to no avail.

Then Sir Henry attempted it and failed.

Taking the hook again, Good scraped the entire circumference of the crack where we felt the air ascending.

"Now, Curtis," he commanded, "apply your full strength. You are as strong as two. Stop," he added, removing a stout black silk handkerchief—which, true to his habits of neatness, he still wore—and threading it through the ring. "Quatermain, get Curtis around the middle and pull for dear life when I give the word. *Now*."

Sir Henry unleashed his enormous strength, while Good and I contributed all the power nature had bestowed upon us.

"Heave! Heave! It's yielding!" gasped Sir Henry, and I heard the muscles of his great back crack audibly. Suddenly, a grating sound erupted, followed by a rush of air, and we collapsed onto our backs on the floor, crushed beneath a heavy flagstone. Sir Henry's strength had prevailed, and never did muscular power serve a man so well.

"Light a match, Quatermain," he ordered as soon as we had recovered our breath. "Carefully, now."

I complied, and there, before us, Heaven be praised! was the *first step of a stone stair*.

"Now, what is to be done?" Good asked.

"Descend the stairs, of course, and trust to Providence."

"Wait!" Sir Henry interrupted. "Quatermain, retrieve the remaining biltong and water; we may need them."

I crept back to our resting place by the chests to fetch them. As I returned, an idea struck me. We had paid little heed to the diamonds for the last twenty-four hours; indeed, the very concept of diamonds was nauseating, considering the agony they had inflicted upon us.

The ten days that followed our ordeal in the treasure chamber were marked by a peculiar, almost surreal normality. We returned to our quarters at Loo, physically intact save for the subtle erosion of time and trauma: my stubble emerged from the cave three shades grayer than it entered, and Good carried within him a permanent shadow cast by Foulata's passing. Her death moved him profoundly, yet, viewing the matter through the weary lens of a seasoned traveler, I deemed her removal a mercy. An entanglement between Good and such a creature—a being of stately, unearthly beauty and refined intellect—would have been a disaster. As she herself had poignantly asked, "Can the sun mate with the darkness, or the white with the black?" Their union was an impossibility written into the fabric of existence.

Our quest for the exit concluded with resignation. Exhausted from our forty-eight-hour recovery, we descended into the great pit hoping to locate the aperture through which we had escaped, but the earth had conspired against us. Rain had washed away every trace of our spoor, and the cavern walls, a labyrinth of jagged geology, were riddled with the burrows of ant-eaters and other denizens of the deep. Which tunnel had saved us became a mystery we could never solve. On our final reconnaissance of the stalactite cave, driven by a restless ache, we ventured once more into the Chamber of the Dead. Standing beneath the spear of the White Death, we gazed at the immovable mass of rock that sealed our fate, contemplating the priceless wealth beyond, the crushed remnants of the old hag Gagool, and the fair girl whose tomb served as the portal. We searched frantically for the seam of the sliding door, for the secret mechanism that operated it, but the silence of the stone was absolute. It was a marvel of engineering, possessing a massive, inscrutable simplicity characteristic of the age that forged it, a device unmatched anywhere else on Earth.

In disgust, we abandoned the search. Even if the rock had miraculously shifted before our eyes, revealing the path to the treasure, I doubted we possessed the courage to step over Gagool's mangled remains to claim the diamonds. Yet, the thought of leaving behind the greatest accumulation of wealth in human history brought tears to my eyes. Tens of millions of pounds' worth of jewels lay in three stone coffers, destined to remain cold companions to Foulata's bones until the end of time. Dynamite alone could breach the five feet of solid granite separating us from paradise.

We departed with heavy hearts, only to discover, too late, that our disappointment was unfounded. By a stroke of luck prior to our imprisonment, I had stuffed the pockets of my shooting coat and trousers with gems, alongside Foulata's basket, which held double the volume despite the water bottle occupying space. Though several large stones tumbled out during our perilous descent, an enormous hoard remained. We possessed enough to render us not merely millionaires in the American sense, but owners of the three finest gem sets in Europe. We had not fared so poorly.

Upon our arrival at Loo, we were greeted with cordial warmth by Ignosi, who appeared robust and busy consolidating his power, reorganizing the regiments ravaged by the war with Twala. He listened with rapt attention to our tale, his expression growing somber as we recounted Gagool's gruesome end. Summoning an ancient Induna, a counselor who had lived since the days of Ignosi's great-grandfather, the King questioned the old man about the witch-doctoress. The elder confirmed that Gagaoola had been as wizened, ugly, and wicked in youth as she was in death. With her demise, Ignosi declared, an ancient curse was lifted from the land. He rejoiced that she would not have allowed us to perish in the dark place, nor would she have succeeded in killing him to install Twala, the man her black heart favored.

When I finally addressed the necessity of our departure, asking Ignosi to fulfill his promise of justice and lawful rule, the King's demeanor shifted. Covering his face with his hands, he spoke of a heart torn in twain. "What have I done to you, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, that ye should leave me desolate?" he lamented. He offered everything: wives chosen from his maidens, land stretching to the horizon, cattle, game, and the loyalty of his Impis. But we declined, stating simply that we sought our own place.

This rejection struck Ignosi with bitter clarity. "Now do I learn," he flashed, "that ye love the bright stones more than me, your friend." He cursed the white stones and those who sought them, declaring that death awaited anyone who stepped into the Place of Death to find them. Yet, for us three, the path remained open forever. We were dearer to him than any breathing thing.

Infadoos, his uncle, and his Induna would guide us across the mountains via a lesser-known pass, hidden behind the towering shapes of Sheba's Breasts. In a moment of profound sorrow, Ignosi decreed that our names, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, should become *hlonipa*—names forbidden to be spoken, reserved for the dead kings, ensuring our memory would be preserved eternally in the land. He urged us to depart before his eyes overflowed with tears, recalling our shared battles: how Macumazahn had been the point of the horn that galled Twala's flank, how Bougwan had stood in the ring of the Greys, and how Incubu had shattered the wild bull's strength.

Covering his face with the corner of his kaross, Ignosi watched us silently as we prepared to leave. The next dawn, under the watchful eyes of a heartbroken Infadoos and a regiment of Buffaloes, we marched out of Loo. The entire town lined the streets, showering us with flowers and blessings for having liberated them from Twala. Amidst this solemn grandeur, a ludicrous incident occurred just before we crossed the town limits. A young girl, carrying lovely lilies, ran to Good, captivated by his solitary whisker and eyeglasses, and requested a boon: to see his beautiful white legs. Excited and obstinate, Good initially refused, deeming it indecent, but under the pressure of Sir Henry's persuasion and the rapturous admiration of the crowd, he reluctantly drew up his trousers to the knee. Thus, in a guise of exposed calves, he walked until we cleared the town, a spectacle that ensured his legs would never again be so greatly admired, unlike his melting teeth or transparent eye, which the Kukuanas had already grown tired of.

As we traveled, Infadoos revealed the strategic advantage of our new route. Rather than traversing the treacherous Solomon's Great Road, we would descend a precipitous chasm separated from the desert by the cliffs of Sheba's Breasts. Legend held that ostrich hunters had recently used this path to reach a vast, fertile oasis, escaping the rigors of the mountain pass. Guided by these hunters, we pressed onward, traveling easily until the fourth night, when we found ourselves atop the crest of the mountains dividing Kukuanaland from the desert. Below us, the sand rolled in billowing waves, and ahead lay the precipice, waiting to drop us two thousand feet onto the plain.

The dust of the desert, fine as powdered bone, coated everything in sight, turning the horizon into a shimmering mirage of heat and delusion. Yet, amidst this vast, swallowing expanse, I found myself walking alone, a solitary figure detached from the trail behind me, following the silent ribbon of a stream that wound its way from the distant oasis before vanishing into the throat of the dunes. It was in this moment of quiet solitude, far ahead of Sir Henry and Good, that fate decided to intervene with a brutality that bordered on the theatrical.

I halted abruptly, shielding my eyes against the glare, rubbing them as if trying to scrub away a hallucination born of dehydration. There, barely twenty paces ahead, nestled in a pocket of deceptive charm beneath the gnarled branches of a wild fig tree, stood a structure that defied the logic of the wastes. It was a hut, constructed on the rough principles of the Kafirs—wattle and daub, woven grasses—but distinguished by a proper, full-length doorway rather than the narrow bee-holes typical of nomadic shelters. Facing the water, it seemed an invitation to civilization in a land of death.

"What on earth," I muttered to the empty air, "could possibly justify a dwelling here?"

Before the echo of my confusion could fade, the door swung open. Stepping out onto the scorched earth was a man who belonged nowhere and everywhere simultaneously: a white man, draped in animal skins, his face obscured by a beard so jet-black it looked like ink spilled on parchment. He moved with a pronounced limp, dragging one leg as if it weighed a ton.

A wave of vertigo washed over me. Had the sun struck me with such force that my vision had fractured? Was this a trick of the heat? No. The impossibility of the sight grounded me instantly. No hunter ventured this deep; certainly, no hunter would choose to make a permanent home among the vultures. I stared, transfixed, until the movement of the stranger caught the eye of the man trailing behind me. Just then, the rhythm of our footsteps changed as Sir Henry and Good rounded the bend.

"Stop!" I commanded, my voice cracking slightly. "Is that a white man, or have I finally succumbed to madness?"

Sir Henry squinted, his brow furrowing in disbelief. Good leaned forward, peering through the haze. Then, the stranger spoke. It was a sound that shattered the silence of the desert—a raw, guttural cry that echoed off the dunes. He began to hobble toward us, his movements erratic, driven by a desperation that transcended pain.

As he closed the distance, he collapsed, a heap of skin and bone upon the sand. Without hesitation, Sir Henry launched himself forward, catching the man before he hit the ground.

"Great Powers!" Sir Henry gasped, his face pale with shock. "It is my brother, George!"

The commotion drew another figure from within the hut. Clad similarly in skins, clutching a rifle, he emerged with a speed belied by his earlier caution. Upon spotting me, he let out a shriek of recognition.

"Macumazahn!" he hailed, stumbling toward me. "Don't you recognize me, Baas? I'm Jim, the hunter! I lost the note you entrusted to me for the Baas, and we have been stranded here for nearly two years!"

Overcome with emotion, the man threw himself at my feet, rolling in the dirt, sobbing uncontrollably.

"You careless scoundrel!" I shouted, though my anger was tempered by the sheer absurdity of the reunion. "You deserve a thorough sjambocking—that is, a hiding—for your incompetence!"

Meanwhile, the man with the black beard, George, had regained his footing. He and Sir Henry engaged in a frantic embrace, pumping each other's shoulders in a display of affection that suggested a past grievance—perhaps involving a woman, though I never pressed the matter—had been utterly erased by the miracle of survival.

"My dear old fellow," Sir Henry burst out, tears streaming down his cheeks. "I thought you were dead! I trekked over Solomon's Mountains specifically to find you. I had surrendered all hope of ever seeing you again, and now here you are, perched in the desert like an old *aasvoël*."

"I tried to cross those mountains nearly two years ago," George replied, his voice hesitant, unused to speech after such a long silence. "But when I reached this spot, a boulder tumbled from the ridge and crushed my right leg. Since then, I have been unable to move forward or backward."

I stepped closer, extending a hand. "How do you do, Mr. Neville? Do you remember me?"

"Why," George stammered, looking between us, "isn't it Hunter Quatermain, and Good too? Hold on, gentlemen, I feel dizzy again. It is all so incredibly strange, and yet, when a man has ceased to hope, happiness arrives with such violent force!"

That evening, gathered around the crackling campfire, George recounted his ordeal. His story, in its own right, rivaled ours in drama. Less than two years prior, he had departed Sitanda's Kraal attempting to reach Suliman's Berg. The note I had dispatched via Jim had been lost, unknown to him until this miraculous convergence. Acting on local intelligence, he had bypassed Sheba's Breasts, choosing instead the ladder-like descent of the mountains we had recently traversed—a route superior to the one mapped by the deceased Dom Silvestra.

Their journey had been a gauntlet of suffering. Eventually, they stumbled upon this oasis, where tragedy struck. While sitting by the stream, Jim had climbed a bank above to harvest honey from a stingless bee's nest. In his exertion, he dislodged a massive boulder that crashed down, shattering George's leg. Trapped between the advancing desert and the stagnant waters, they survived by hunting the abundant game drawn to the oasis at night, trapping them in pits, and eventually wearing their clothes down to the bare hides of the animals themselves.

"We lived like a second Robinson Crusoe and his Friday," George concluded, staring into the flames. "We hoped against hope that natives might appear to rescue us, but none came. Last night, we resolved that Jim should attempt to reach Sitanda's Kraal for aid. He was to depart tomorrow, but I had little expectation of returning. And now, *you*, of all people in the world, *you*, whom I presumed had long forgotten me and lived comfortably in old England, appear in a chaotic fashion to find me exactly where you least expected. It is the most wondrous and merciful coincidence I have ever witnessed."

Sir Henry then detailed our own adventures, recounting the trials and triumphs until the night wore on. When I presented George with a selection of the diamonds we had secured, his reaction was one of stunned gratitude.

"By Jove!" he exclaimed. "Well, at least you have acquired something tangible for your efforts, besides my worthless self."

Sir Henry chuckled. "They belong to Quatermain and Good. It was stipulated in our agreement that they should split any spoils discovered."

This statement sparked a reflection in me. Turning to Good, I voiced our collective sentiment: that Sir Henry should receive a third portion of the diamonds. Should he decline, his share was to be transferred to his brother, who had endured greater hardship than we had in the pursuit of these gems. We successfully persuaded him to accept this arrangement, though George remained unaware of the generosity until later.

At this juncture, I believe my chronicle should draw to a close. The trek back to Sitanda's Kraal proved arduous, particularly with George's weakened leg, which constantly shed fragments of bone. Yet, we accomplished the impossible. Six months after our return to Sitanda's, where we reclaimed our guns and supplies—much to the chagrin of the old custodian, disgusted that we had survived to claim them—we finally reached safety at my residence on the Berea, near Durban. From there, I bid farewell to all who had shared in this strangest expedition of my long and varied career.

*Postscript:* Just as I penned the final word, a Kafir ascended my avenue of orange trees, bearing a letter wedged in a cleft stick, delivered from the post office. It was from Sir Henry, and since it speaks for itself, I transcribe it in full.

I send you a line a few mails back to confirm that the three of us—George, Good, and myself—arrived safely in England. We disembarked at Southampton and proceeded to town. You should have witnessed the transformation of Good the very next day: impeccably shaved, sporting a frock coat that fit like a second skin, complete with brand-new spectacles, and so forth. I strolled through the park with him and encountered acquaintances, immediately regaling them with the tale of his "beautiful white legs." He is currently furious, especially since some ill-natured individual has printed the anecdote in a Society paper.

Turning to business, Good and I took the diamonds to Streeter's for valuation, as agreed. I hesitate to disclose the sum they appraised, as the figure seems astronomical. They noted that, naturally, it involves considerable estimation, as such stones have never previously appeared on the market in such quantities. Apparently, with the exception of one or two of the larger specimens, they possess the finest water and are equal in every respect to the best Brazilian stones.
