My stay at La Terrasse stretched two full weeks past the end of vacation, a small, soft reprieve Mrs. Bretton had wangled for me out of pure kindness. Her son had casually declared one afternoon that “Lucy is still far too weak to go back to that den of a boarding school,” and no sooner had the words left his mouth than the good lady had driven straight to Rue Fossette to speak to Madame Beck, pleading that extra rest and gentle change were critical to my full recovery.

What followed was a favour I could very happily have done without: a polite, entirely uninvited call from Madame Beck herself.
One fine, sun-dappled day, she rode all the way out to the chateau in a hired fiacre, no doubt determined to see exactly what sort of household Dr. John inhabited. The pleasant grounds and crisp, cheerful interiors far outstripped her expectations; she praised every corner of the house, pronounced the blue salon “une pièce magnifique,” heaped me with gushing compliments on my good fortune to have secured friends “so worthy, so kind, so respectable.” When Dr. John walked in the door, she flew to him with all the buoyancy of a sparrow, chattering a mile a minute in bright, florid French, fawning over his “château,” his “dignified chatelaine of a mother,” and his own flourishing good looks. He smiled through it all, warm and amused, used to her endless flow of words. That day, Madame Beck was at her very best: a living Catherine wheel spinning sparks of compliments, delight, and easy affability. Half on purpose, half to ask a quick question about school business, I followed her out to the fiacre, and leaned in to speak to her just as the door clicked shut. In that single heartbeat, the brightness vanished. Where a moment before there had been nothing but jests and sparkles, she sat sterner than a judge, graver than any sage. Strange, sharp little woman.

I went back inside and teased Dr. John mercilessly about her obvious fondness for him, and he laughed until his eyes crinkled, mimicking her rapid, voluble delivery as he quoted back some of her more over-the-top lines. He had a sharp, perfect sense of humour, and when he could manage to forget Miss Fanshawe for an hour, he was the very best company in the world.

They say sitting in calm, sweet sunshine restores strength to the weak, and I knew that truth firsthand. When little Georgette Beck had been ill, I would carry her in my arms and walk the garden paths for hours under a wall draped with ripening grapes, the southern sun warming her pale small frame as it swelled the fruit on the vines. There are human natures just as warm, just as generous: the kind whose company nourishes the poor in spirit as well as noontide sun nourishes the feeble in body. Both Dr. John and his mother had that rare gift. They spread happiness as instinctively as some people spread misery: no fuss, no fanfare, hardly even conscious of how much good they did, little small pleasant plans rising to their lips unbidden every single day I stayed with them. For all his packed schedule, Dr. John always made time to join me and his mother on our short little excursions. I never could figure how he managed it, with so many patients to see, but by dint of quiet, steady system, he carved out an hour of free time for himself every single day. I saw him work himself to the bone often, but never overstretched, never irritable, never confused. Everything he did came with the easy, unforced grace of someone who knew his own strength, brimming with unbroken, bright good humour. In those two happy weeks, I saw more of Villette, its surrounding countryside, and its people than I had in the full eight months I’d lived there before. He took me to hidden little corners of the town I’d never even heard of, told me story after story of their history, and never once made me feel like I was a burden for listening. He never droned on with dry generalizations or borrowed trite phrases from books; he spoke from his own sharp, observant experience, full of little vivid details that made every story feel fresh and bright.

His mother was a kind woman, but Dr. John’s benevolence ran far deeper. When I accompanied him on his rounds in the crowded, poor Basse-Ville quarter, I saw his errands were as much the work of a philanthropist as a doctor. He was quietly, habitually, unthinkingly doing endless small acts of good for the city’s most wretched residents, never pausing to pat himself on the back for it. The lower classes adored him; his hospital patients greeted him with the kind of bright enthusiasm reserved for a beloved old friend.

But I will not fall into the trap of becoming his partial eulogist. Dr. John was no more perfect than I am. Human fallibility ran through every part of him. No god could have had his sharp, cruel vanity, or his giddy, thoughtless levity. No immortal could have been so completely caught up in the pleasure of the present, so selfish in extracting every scrap of gratification to feed his masculine self-love, never stopping to count the cost of keeping that ravenous sentiment sleek and overfed.

The two portraits of him I have drawn – the public man, generous and unselfconscious in his good works, and the private man, vain of the homage he drew, pleased when people went out of their way to serve him – are both entirely true. You could never do him a quiet favour in secret. If you thought you’d finished some small, thoughtful trifle for him unnoticed, he would surprise you later with a soft, smiling comment that made clear he’d watched the whole process from the first stitch to the last, noticed every detail, and glowed with quiet pleasure at the effort. And he always paid those small kindnesses back, never letting you do a favour for him without receiving some gentle little treat or pleasant outing in return.

He knew Villette like the back of his hand, every hidden gallery, every private hall of art and science, every door that led to something worth seeing, as if he possessed the magic “Open Sesame” to them all. I never had a head for science, but I harboured an ignorant, soft fondness for art. I loved visiting the picture galleries, and loved most of all to be left there entirely alone. I had a wretched quirk of temperament that made it impossible for me to see or feel anything profound in company; if I had to make polite conversation about every painting in front of strangers, I would wear myself out completely in half an hour, too tired to think or feel anything at all.

Dr. John was the perfect cicerone for me. He would bring me to the galleries early, before the crowds rolled in, leave me there for two or three hours, and come back to collect me when his appointments were done. I was perfectly happy there, not just admiring, but examining, questioning, making up my own mind about every piece. At first I fought myself, straining to force admiration for all the supposedly great works everyone said I should love, until I grew tired of the effort, and let myself sink into soft, calm quiet before ninety-nine out of a hundred frames. I found an original, good painting was as rare as an original, good book, and I stopped trembling to admit to myself that most of the famous “masterpieces” were nothing like nature at all – their daylight was the wrong muddy shade, their skies of indigo were not real ether, their dark blobby weeds were not real trees. Many of the plump, complacent painted ladies struck me as very far from the goddesses they clearly thought they were, and dozens of painstaking little Flemish pictures felt no more meaningful than fashion plates for fancy fabric costumes. But every so often there was a fragment of truth, a gleam of light that felt like a friend: a snowstorm over a mountain that breathed real cold air, a portrait with a look in the sitter’s eyes that showed the painter truly knew her heart.

One quiet early morning, I found myself nearly alone in a gallery that held one particular monstrous, enormous painting, hung in the very best light, strung with a protective cordon, with a plush bench set right in front for the connoisseurs who wanted to sit down to finish worshipping it. It had clearly anointed itself the queen of the whole collection.
The portrait was of a woman, far larger than life, I judged – easily fourteen to sixteen stone, so broad and heavy that she must have eaten an enormous quantity of butcher’s meat to attain that wealth of muscle and soft flesh. She half-reclined on a couch for no discernible reason, in broad, bright daylight, strong enough to do the work of two plain cooks, no business lounging away noon on a sofa. Her clothes were absurd, seven and twenty yards of drapery that somehow still managed to be barely decent, and the foreground around her was a total mess of toppled vases, strewn flowers, and lumpy curtain fabric heaped over the couch and across the floor. When I checked the catalogue, the whole ridiculous production was named *Cleopatra*.

I sat down on the bench to rest, thinking that while a few small details – the roses, the gilded cups, the jewels – were prettily painted, the whole thing was nothing more than overblown claptrap. The gallery, nearly empty when I walked in, began slowly to fill, but I barely noticed, far more interested in the tiny, exquisite little still lifes hung humbly underneath that absurd giant canvas: wild flowers, wild berries, mossy nests holding eggs that looked like pearls seen through clear green seawater.
A light tap on my shoulder made me jump. I turned, to meet a face bent close to mine, frowning, almost shocked.
“Que faites-vous ici?” the voice said.
“Mais, Monsieur, je m’amuse.”
“Vous vous amusez! And at what, if you please? First of all, do me the favour to stand, take my arm, and come with me to the other side of the room.”
I obeyed at once. It was M. Paul Emanuel, newly returned from Rome, and no travel in the world could have made that fiery little professor any more tolerant of what he saw as insubordination.
“Allow me to take you to your party,” he said as we crossed the room.
“I have no party.”
“You are not alone?”
“Yes, Monsieur.”
“Did you come here unescorted?”
“No. Dr. Bretton brought me.”
“Dr. Bretton and his mother, of course?”
“No. Only Dr. Bretton.”
“And he told you to go look at *that* picture?”
“Not at all. I found it out for myself.”
His short raven-dark hair would have bristled straight up if it had been long enough. I could see exactly what was bothering him, and I found a quiet, mischievous pleasure in staying perfectly calm and winding him up.
“Astounding insular audacity!” he cried. “Singulières femmes que ces Anglaises!”
“What is the matter, Monsieur?”
“Matter! How dare you, a young unmarried person, sit there as cool and self-possessed as a boy, staring at *that* picture?”
“It is a very ugly picture, but I cannot for the life of me see why I should not look at it.”
“Enough, enough! Speak no more of it. But you ought not to be here alone.”
“But I have no party, as you say. And what difference does it make if I am alone or accompanied? No one bothers me.”
“Taisez-vous, and sit down there – there!” He plonked a chair down with great emphasis in the dullest corner of the room, right in front of a row of exceptionally dreary frames.
“Mais, Monsieur?”
“Mademoiselle, sit down, and do not move – do you hear? – until someone comes to collect you, or I give you permission to leave.”
“What a sad little corner!” I protested. “And what hideously ugly paintings!”
They were ugly, indeed: four panels catalogued as *The Life of a Woman*, painted in a flat, dead, pale, formal style. The first was a Young Girl stepping out of a church door, missal in hand, dress starched and prim, eyes cast down, mouth pursed tight, the very picture of a hypocritical little she-preacher. The second was a Bride, long white veil trailing, kneeling at her prie-dieu in her bedroom, hands pressed flat palm to palm, showing the whites of her eyes in the most exasperating way. The third was a Young Mother, hanging disconsolate over a lumpy, puffy baby with a face like an unwholesome full moon. The fourth was a Widow, all in black, holding a little black girl’s hand, both of them staring stonily at an elegant French tomb in a corner of the Père Lachaise cemetery. All four “angels” were grim as burglars, cold and lifeless as ghosts. As insipid, as absurd in their own way, as the lazy giantess Cleopatra over on the other side of the room.

I could not keep my eyes fixed on those dreadful paintings for long, and soon I turned to survey the rest of the gallery. A whole crowd of people was now clustered around the Cleopatra I’d been banished from, half of them ladies. M. Paul later informed me those ladies were all married “des dames,” so it was perfectly proper for them to look on what no unmarried “demoiselle” ought to glance at. I told him plainly I thought that doctrine was utter nonsense, and he shushed me at once, as was his wont, calling me both rash and ignorant. No man who ever filled a professor’s chair was more despotic than fiery little M. Paul. I noticed he himself stood looking at the Cleopatra for a very long while, perfectly at his ease, only glancing my way every few minutes to make sure I was obeying orders and not sneaking a peek back.
Presently he came back over to me. He asked if I had not been ill, as he had heard, and I told him I was fully recovered now. He asked where I had spent the vacation, and I told him mostly in Rue Fossette, partly at the Brettons’ house. He had heard I was left all alone in the pensionnat during the break, and he raised his eyebrows when I told him I’d only had simple, slow-witted Marie Broc for company. He knew Marie well, the poor girl, who was the source of constant inner conflict for him: he would grow irritable at her unkempt manners and stubborn moods, then immediately soften at the thought of all her hardships, pity winning out over impatience almost every time.
“How did you get on with Marie Broc?” he asked after a long silence.
“I did my best, Monsieur, but it was terrible to be alone with her.”
“Then you are soft-hearted in the worst way! You lack courage, and perhaps charity. You would never make a proper Sister of Mercy.”
“I washed her, kept her clean, fed her, tried to amuse her,” I told him. “But she made faces at me instead of speaking. When her aunt finally came to take her away, I was so relieved.”
“Ah, you are an egotist. There are women who nurse whole hospitals full of such unfortunates.”
“Could Monsieur do it himself?”
“Women worthy of the name ought to be far more capable of that kind of self-sacrifice than our coarse, fallible, self-indulgent sex.”
“Then my powers are limited, I suppose,” I said, “for tending that one poor girl left me ill with a nervous fever, not my body but my mind worn thin.”
“Vraiment! You are made of far too weak stuff. You will never have the courage to bear long solitude – all your rashness only goes so far as to sit and stare calmly at paintings of Cleopatra.”
It would have been easy to get angry at his sharp, teasing tone, but I had never been angry with him before, and I had no wish to start now.
“Cleopatra,” I repeated, very quiet. “But Monsieur was looking at Cleopatra just now, too. What does he think of her?”
“Cela ne vaut rien,” he said dismissively. “A superb woman – an empress’s height, Juno’s form – but the sort of person I would never wish for a wife, a daughter, or a sister. And you will never so much as glance in her direction again.”
“But I have looked at her many times while Monsieur has been talking,” I told him. “I can see her perfectly well from this corner.”
“Turn to the wall and study your four paintings of a woman’s life.”
“Excuse me, M. Paul, they are far too hideous. But if you admire them, let me vacate my seat and leave you to contemplate them in peace.”
He gave me a half-smile, a grim, hurried little flicker of amusement. “You nurslings of Protestantism astonish me. You unguarded Englishwomen walk calmly across red-hot ploughshares and escape burning. I am convinced if you were tossed into Nebuchadnezzar’s hottest furnace, you would walk out without even smelling of fire.”
“Will Monsieur be so good as to move an inch to the side?”
“What? What are you staring at now? You are not recognizing an acquaintance among that group of young men over there?”
“I think so,” I said. “Yes. I see someone I know.”
