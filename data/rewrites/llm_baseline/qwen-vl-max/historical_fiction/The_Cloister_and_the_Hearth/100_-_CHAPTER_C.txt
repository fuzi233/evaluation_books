In accordance with a custom I loathe yet cannot bring myself to defy, I pause here at the end of my tale to gather up the scattered threads of lives that once wove through its fabric—those who, though minor in the grand design, lent truth and breath to the journey. Not every figure deserves an epitaph; those fleeting souls served their purpose if they made Gerard’s path from Holland to Rome feel lived, not imagined—a world peopled by flesh and blood, not mere puppets.

Eli and Catherine outlived the ages, their names fading into memory as Gerard and Margaret became shadows in the minds of others. Giles, too, endured long years; he reached the court of Bavaria, still standing at ninety, though now little more than sinew and bone, his voice like a trumpet grown thin with time. Cornelis, unchallenged and long forgiven by his mother—who clung to him ever more tightly as her children drifted apart—waited patiently for the death of his parents. But Catherine’s sharp words had foretold the truth: before either she or her husband passed, Cornelis had already worn out. At sixty-five, he lay dying in his mother’s arms, a woman of eighty-six whose strength had not failed. He had lain unconscious for hours, but just as death approached, he stirred, opened his eyes, and spoke plainly of how he would rebuild the shop and premises once they were his own. “Yes, my darling,” she murmured softly, smoothing his brow. And then, in silence, he was gone—turned to dust, carried to the grave by every foot he had once waited to mend.

Denys, shattered by the loss of his comrade, found solace in returning to Burgundy. There, a modest pension from the court sustained him until, unexpectedly, he inherited a fortune from a distant relative. For many years, he was known in his homeland as a gruff old soldier—grumbling about women, yes, but capable of telling vivid tales of war when the mood struck him.

Jerome, disillusioned by the laxity of northern ways, withdrew to Italy. Through noble connections, he rose to become a mitred abbot at seventy. He enforced strict discipline, ruling his monks with iron resolve. They feared him, respected him, hated him—yet none truly loved him. For ten years he governed without warmth, without trust. Then one night, alone in his chamber, he died. The Vulgate lay on his pillow, the crucifix in his hand, and upon his lips—a faint smile, softer than any seen in life. Perhaps, in that final hour, he was not quite alone. Requiescat in pace. The Master he served has many servants, and among them, minds of every kind—sometimes stern, sometimes faithful, always human.

And so, the golden-haired boy, Gerard Gerardson, does not belong solely to fiction. He belongs to history. She has recorded his birth in different words than mine. Above the tailor’s house in Brede Kirk Straet, she has inscribed: *“HAEC EST PARVA DOMUS NATUS QUA MAGNUS ERASMUS.”* She has written half a dozen biographies of him. Yet there remains a task for her still. No small mind, no partisan eye, can fully grasp the greatness of Erasmus—just as no dwarf can comprehend a giant. He was not only the foremost scholar and theologian of his age, but also the natural-born dramatist of his century. Some of the finest scenes in this book spring from his own medieval pen, lighting up these pages with brilliance. For genius such as his does not speak to die. Its work may seem dormant after the first impact—yet each new wave of understanding brings it back to life. From the dust of ancient libraries, it rises again—budding, blooming, bearing fruit, seeding wisdom across generations, through centuries.

Thus ends the tale.
