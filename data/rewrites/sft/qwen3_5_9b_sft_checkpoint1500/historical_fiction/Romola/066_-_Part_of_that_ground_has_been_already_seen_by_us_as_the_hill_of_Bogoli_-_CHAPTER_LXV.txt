A week later, on the seventh of April, the Piazza della Signoria transformed into a scene of unparalleled magnitude, surpassing even the legendary Bonfire of Vanities. A crowd, denser than any that had ever pressed upon the square since the feast of San Giovanni, sought purchase on the cobblestones. They climbed every available ledge, clinging to iron rods jutting from walls, wedging themselves between pillars, and perching atop the stooped shoulders of ancient statues. Their limbs cramped and skins raw, they endured the physical discomfort for the sake of a single promise: to witness a miracle. While humanity often hears of wonders, few expect to stand before one unfolding in real time. The Florentines believed they would see at least half of it; if the monk emerged unscathed, the truth would speak for itself. If he burned, the judgment would be equally swift.

The stage for this drama was laid with formidable precision. Before the eyes of the throng lay a long platform, twenty yards wide, crowned with a terrifying grove of fuel: dry oak branches, crackling thorns, and tow soaked in oil, the ingredients of famous Florentine illuminations. This pyre stood before the Old Palace, looming beside the stern gaze of the stone lion, the Marzocco. To protect the spectacle, three distinct armies held the square: five hundred hired soldiers of the Signoria guarding the palace door, five hundred *Compagnacci* led by Dolfo Spini on the far side, and three hundred armed citizens under Marco Salviati, a close ally of Savonarola, positioned before Orgagna's Loggia.

The atmosphere hummed with anticipation. Half the Franciscan friars, clad in grey, had already filed into the loggia, filling its western wing in solemn silence. Opposite them, however, the eastern side erupted into a deafening chant of "Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered." The sound, amplified by the acoustics of the square, suggested a confidence bordering on hubris. Leading the procession was Fra Domenico, wrapped in a blaze of flame-colored velvet, his simple face lifted by genuine faith. Following him walked Savonarola, dressed in white priestly vestments, holding the consecrated Host. As he stepped onto the marble terrace, his countenance hardened under the scrutiny of thousands. His eyes burned with an intensity that seemed to absorb the gaze of the crowd, reflecting a duality that only he could feel: outwardly, he projected the invincible assurance of a prophet facing fire; inwardly, he wrestled with a heavy, leaden foreboding.

Savonarola was not merely playing a role today; he was fighting a battle against his own crumbling foundations. He struggled not against the threat of death, but against the imminent collapse of his reputation and power. Yet, amidst his turmoil, a shadow of relief flickered in his mind—a specific turn of events from two nights prior. News had reached the city that a courier of the Ten, dispatched to Milan, had been arrested and stripped of his dispatches. Consequently, Savonarola's secret letter regarding the conspiracy now rested in the hands of the Duke of Milan and, shortly thereafter, the Pope. This revelation would provide the ultimate justification for extreme measures against him.

Watching this unraveling from a high window of the Old Palace was Tito Melema. With a calm, self-gratulating smile, the scholar observed the chaos below. He viewed Savonarola not as a tragic figure, but as a clever, albeit superstitious, actor who had played his final hand poorly. Tito saw no malice in his observation, only the satisfaction of a game well-played. The bundle of human desires known as Savonarola had served Tito perfectly, providing the stepping-stones he needed to ascend to a position of smooth authority. Now, with the wind at his back, Tito prepared to leave Florence, leaving the "comedy" of the trial to unfold as a prelude to his departure.

Back in the square, the chanting swelled to a roar, joined by the *Piagnoni*, their red crosses bobbing like flags of devotion. They awaited the moment when the friars would step toward the pyre. But the expected movement did not occur. Instead, pale faces peered anxiously over the wooden dividers of the loggia. A hush fell over the crowd as rumors circulated that Fra Domenico was being summoned to the palace, perhaps to exchange clothes with a brother to avoid the heat of the flames. Moments later, indeed, a Dominican emerged, followed quickly by another, suggesting a frantic reshuffling behind closed doors.

Then, slowly, Fra Domenico reappeared, flanked by guards, having exchanged robes. The crowd sighed with relief; the Dominican would not burn. Attention shifted instantly to Savonarola. He remained motionless, save for a bold, ironic speech reminiscent of Elijah confronting the prophets of Baal. He demanded the cessation of delay, but his words fell flat on the ears of the starving, crammed masses. Why speak? If the miracle was not to begin, the fault lay with Savonarola himself for failing to offer himself to the fire when the wood was piled high.

Time slipped away as the clouds gathered, darkening the sky and sending a chill through the square. The air grew heavier, and the chanting faded into confused muttering and indistinct arguments. Suddenly, the focus shifted. Fra Domenico, desperate to prevent the profanation of the holy crucifix, refused to enter the fire without it. Savonarola, seeing an opening to concede, yielded, handing the vessel containing the Host to his brother. "Enter simply with the Sacrament," he proposed quietly.

Horror rippled through the Franciscans. "It is impious!" they cried. "If the Host burns, the scandal will be immense!" Savonarola countered firmly, invoking the scholastic distinction that accidents, not substance, would be consumed. The argument spiraled into a theological quagmire that neither side could resolve, leaving both trapped in a stalemate.

As the debate dragged on, the sky opened. Rain began to fall, soft at first, then with violent urgency. Streams of water ran off the platform, soaking the dry timber and extinguishing the kindling. The crowd, already hungry and disgusted, roared in frustration. "Light the fire!" "Drive them in!" "Where is the prophet?" The shouts dissolved into an uproar of clashing steel and trampled feet. Dolfo Spini and his *Compagnacci*, spurred by the irritation of the mob, surged forward toward the loggia, seeking to seize Savonarola. But Salviati's men held the line, repelling the rush until the tide turned back toward the Palazzo Rucellai.

In that moment of utter confusion, the heavens unleashed their wrath. The rain poured down, drowning the fuel and rendering the trial impossible. The crowd, drenched and furious, accepted the weather as divine intervention, a convenient excuse for the authorities to declare the matter settled. Both sides retreated, the fire never lit, the miracle never performed.

Yet, as the rain ceased and the broken clouds revealed a pale, indifferent light, Savonarola felt no triumph. He requested a guard from the Signoria, which was granted. He had told them he was willing to die for his cause, and he spoke the truth. But to die in dishonor—to be cast as a hypocrite and a false prophet? That was not martyrdom; it was the erasure of a life dedicated to justice. "Let me die because of the worth within me," he prayed silently, "not because of my weakness."

He walked away from the loggia, the Sacrament heavy in his hand. The light falling upon him offered no glory, no smile from heaven. Only the cold, impartial illumination that exposes the rot beneath the surface. As he descended the steps, Tito Melema greeted him with a mocking cheer. "Well parried, Frate!" the scholar called out. "But I fear your career at Florence is ended. What say you, my Niccolò?" Machiavelli, standing nearby, shrugged with a melancholy air. "It is a pity his falsehoods were not all of a wise sort," he murmured. "With the times so much on his side as they are concerning church affairs, he might have done something great."

Savonarola moved on, his courage mounting despite the moral attacks, knowing that the bitterest days were yet to come.
