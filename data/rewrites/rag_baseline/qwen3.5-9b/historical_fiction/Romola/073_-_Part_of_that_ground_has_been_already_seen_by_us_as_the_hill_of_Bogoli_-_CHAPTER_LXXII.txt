The final silence that descended upon the Piazza della Signoria was not merely an absence of noise; it was the cessation of a specific frequency of human suffering, a sound Romola had carried in her marrow since the day Savonarola signed his confession. She remembered the words echoing through the corridors of San Marco, repeated by a hundred desperate lips: *"Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy."* Those words had once shaken her faith, introducing a terrifying doubt into her understanding of the friar's retrospective clarity. But the doubts were quickly eclipsed by the brutal reality of the nineteenth of May, when the Papal Commissaries marched into Florence under the sun, hailed by a mob screaming for the executioner. The popular cry was absolute: *"It is the Frate's deception that has brought on all our misfortunes; let him be burned, and all things right will be done."*

The subsequent days were a catalog of systematic dismantling. Torture, applied with clinical precision to a frame already shattered by previous agony, yielded a new confession. Confronted with the horrific instruments of the rack, Savonarola, agitated by convulsive spasms, fell to his knees. He retracted his earlier denial of prophecy, declaring passionately that he had lied only to deny his gift, and that if he suffered, it would be for the truth—"The things that I have spoken, I had them from God." Yet, the interrogators demanded consistency. When pressed on why he had recanted, the answer was pragmatic, devoid of divine inspiration: *"I said it that I might seem good; tear me no more, I will tell you the truth."* This double retraction spread through the Florentine assessors like a contagion, filling Romola with a dismayed uncertainty. A single thought flashed through her mind, cutting through the fog of despair: *There will come a moment when he may speak.* A moment when the dread of falsehood vanishes, replaced only by the inevitability of death. When he is lifted above the people, looking down for the last time, no force can stop him from uttering a final, decisive word. *I will be there.*

Three days later, on the twenty-third of May 1498, the stage was set. A long, narrow platform stretched from the Palazzo Vecchio toward the Tetta de' Pisani, terminating in a vast circular area dominated by a single, immense heap of fuel. Rising from this pyre was a gibbet with three halters, its two arms creating a grotesque parody of a cross—one arm deliberately truncated to avoid the religious resemblance, leaving behind a symbol of pure execution. Upon the marble terrace of the Palazzo stood three distinct tribunals. One, near the main door, awaited the Bishop to perform the ritual degradation on Fra Girolamo and his two companions. Another housed the Papal Commissaries, tasked with pronouncing them heretics and schismatics before handing them over to the secular arm. The third, positioned near the Marzocco statue at the platform's edge, belonged to the Gonfaloniere and the Eight, who would deliver the sentence of death.

The Piazza was thronged. The crowd pressing around the gibbet was a sea of ferocious hatred and morbid curiosity, eager to witness a barbarous spectacle. Yet, scattered across the wide pavements, perched on rooftops, and peering from high windows, were others. These spectators, enduring insults alongside the hypocritical Piagnoni, clung to a lingering, fragile hope. Even at this eleventh hour, they prayed that God might interpose, manifesting their beloved prophet as His servant through some miraculous sign. Among them were those who, like Romola, trembled with eagerness for that final moment when Savonarola might declare, *"O people, I was innocent of deceit."*

Romola stood at a window on the north side of the Piazza, far removed from the marble terrace where the tribunals stood. Beside her watched Jacopo Nardi, a young Florentine of twenty-two, destined to earn honor as one of the few historians to write of Fra Girolamo with simple veracity. Seeing the war raging within Romola—a conflict between her shuddering horror at the cruelty of the scene and her desperate yearning to witness the end—Jacopo spoke gently. *"Madonna, there is no need for you to look at these cruel things. I will tell you when he comes out of the Palazzo. Trust to me; I know what you would see."* Romola covered her face, but the hooting of the mob seemed to penetrate the stone, making the hideous scene visible regardless. Finally, a hand touched her shoulder. *"He comes."*

She turned toward the Palace. Through the haze of anticipation, she saw Savonarola emerge in his Dominican garb. Before the Bishop, he was stripped of the black mantle, the white scapulary, and the long tunic until he stood exposed in a coarse woolen under-tunic that revealed no sacred office, no rank. He had been degraded, severed from the Church Militant. The baser elements of the multitude delighted in such humiliations; it was the satire they understood best. A fresh hoot of triumph erupted as the three degraded brothers moved to the tribunal of the Papal Commissaries. Could the prophet now appear as a schismatic and a heretic? It was easy to believe in the damnable state of a man standing stripped and humiliated. Passing the third tribunal, the Florentine officials including the odious Dolfo Spini in his grave black lucco, the three figures in their close white raiment walked the length of the platform amidst yells and grating insults. *"Cover your eyes, Madonna,"* Jacopo warned. *"Fra Girolamo will be the last."*

Before she could obey, she was forced to uncover her eyes. Savonarola was there. He had mounted the steps, and for a fleeting second, she saw him look out upon the multitude. But in that same instant, expectation died. All she saw was what he saw: torches waving to ignite the fuel beneath his corpse, faces glowing with a light darker than hate. All she heard was what he heard: gross jests, taunts, and curses. The moment had passed. She covered her face again, knowing with absolute certainty that Savonarola's voice had passed into eternal silence.

On the evening of the twenty-second of May 1509, five individuals sat in a handsome upper room opening onto a loggia. From its right-hand corner, the view extended along the Borgo Pinti, over the city gate, and toward Fiesole and the solemn heights beyond. At one end of the room, an archway led to a narrow recess where light filtered from above onto a small altar draped in fine white linen. Above it hung a picture, discernible only as the small, full-length portrait of a Dominican Brother, shaded by overhanging branches and wreaths of flowers. Fresh tapers stood unlit before it. The decoration was incomplete; the floor was strewn with a chaotic abundance of flowers and green boughs.

Among them sat a delicate, blue-eyed girl of thirteen, tossing her long, light-brown hair from her face as she selected blooms for the wreath she wove. Occasionally, she looked up at her mother's work with an air of instruction, offering guidance. The mother, however, was not particularly adept at floral arrangements or any other craft. Tessa's fingers, once adroit, had grown clumsy with age, swollen and fat. She worked slowly, turning her head frequently to ask Ninna's opinion with deferential humility. Tessa remained perpetually astonished by the wisdom of her children. She still wore her contadina gown, now broader than in her youth, adorned with a silver pin in her rough, curly brown hair and the memorable necklace with its red cord resting mysteriously against her bosom. Her rounded face bore a look of childish contentment that had only deepened with the years. *Everybody is so good in the world,* Tessa thought. *Even Monna Brigida never finds fault with her now; she does little else but sleep, an amiable practice shared by everyone, one that Tessa enjoys for herself.*

Monna Brigida slept in a straight-backed armchair a couple of yards away. Her hair, parted backward under her black hood, possessed a soft whiteness unlike snow—it was simply the lovely whiteness of aged hair. Her chin rested on her bosom, her hands folded on the chair's elbow. She had not woven a flower or performed any task; she had simply gazed outward, as she always did, until exhaustion claimed her.

Further away, at the wide doorway opening to the loggia, sat the other two figures. Lillo lounged on the ground, his back against the door-post angle, his long legs stretched out. He held a large book open on his knee, occasionally swatting an inquisitive fly with a hand movement more animated than the finely printed copy of Petrarch resting nearby, as if he were committing lines to memory. Romola sat nearly opposite him, yet she observed neither him nor the room. Her hands were crossed in her lap, her eyes fixed absently on the distant mountains. An eager life had left its indelible marks upon her: the finely molded cheek had sunk slightly, the golden crown of her hair less massive. Yet, there was a profound placidity in Romola's face that had never existed in her youth. It is but once that we truly know our worst sorrows, and Romola had experienced them while life was still new.
