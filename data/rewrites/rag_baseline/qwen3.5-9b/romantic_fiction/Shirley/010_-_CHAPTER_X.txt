To truly grasp the architecture of this era's despair, one must first witness the slow, suffocating maturation of Spring across England. The surface of the nation donned a deceptive garb of renewal: fields swelled with verdant life, hills breathed freshness, and gardens burst into bloom. Yet, beneath this pastoral veneer, the rot remained untouched. At her core, England was no better than before; her poor remained wretched, her employers perpetually harried. Commerce, in many vital branches, faced paralysis, strangled by the continuing war. England's blood flowed freely, her wealth lavished away—all seemingly to achieve the most inadequate ends. Occasional tidings of success in the Peninsula drifted ashore, but they arrived with agonizing slowness, separated by long, silent intervals filled only by the insolent, self-congratulatory proclamations of Bonaparte regarding his continued triumphs. For those suffering the war's consequences, this protracted struggle against a power they feared and despised as invincible was utterly insufferable. They demanded peace on any terms whatsoever. Men like Yorke and Moore—and countless thousands whom the conflict had driven to the shivering brink of bankruptcy—urged for peace with the frenetic energy of desperation.

They convened meetings, delivered impassioned speeches, and drafted petitions designed to extort this boon, caring naught for the conditions attached. All men, considered individually, possess a degree of selfishness; taken collectively, they become intensely so. The British merchant is no exception to this grim rule; the mercantile classes illustrate it with striking clarity. These groups think exclusively of profit, oblivious to every national consideration save the extension of England's—that is, their own—commerce. Chivalry, disinterestedness, and pride in honor have died in their hearts. A land ruled solely by them would too often make ignominious submission—not out of Christian motive, but driven by the insidious teachings of Mammon. During the recent war, English tradesmen would have endured blows to the right and left cheeks from the French; they would have surrendered their cloaks to Napoleon, then politely offered their coats, withholding only their waistcoats if pressed, praying merely for permission to keep the single garment containing their purse. Not a spark of spirit, not a symptom of resistance, would they display until the Corsican bandit's hand grasped that beloved purse. *Then*, perhaps, transformed instantaneously into British bulldogs, they would spring at the robber's throat, fastening there, hanging inveterate and insatiable until the treasure was restored. When speaking against war, tradesmen always profess hatred for its bloody, barbarous nature. One might assume, hearing them talk, that they are peculiarly civilized—gentle and kindly toward their fellows. This is a lie. Many are extremely narrow and cold-hearted, harboring no goodwill for any class but their own; distant, even hostile to all others; dismissing them as useless; questioning their right to exist; begrudging them the very air they breathe; and deeming their ability to eat, drink, and dwell in decent houses entirely unjustifiable. They remain ignorant of what others do to help, please, or teach their race, refusing to inquire. Whoever stands outside trade is accused of eating the bread of idleness, leading a useless existence. Long may it be ere England truly becomes a nation of shopkeepers!

We have established that Moore was no self-sacrificing patriot, and we have detailed the circumstances rendering him prone to confining his attention to the furtherance of his individual interest. Consequently, when he felt himself urged a second time to the brink of ruin, none struggled harder than he against the forces threatening to push him over. What he *could* do to stir agitation in the north against the war, he did; he instigated others whose money and connections granted them greater power than his own. Occasionally, by flashes of insight, he sensed little reason in the demands his party made upon the Government. When he heard of all Europe threatened by Bonaparte, of all Europe arming to resist; when he saw Russia menaced, witnessing her rise incensed and stern to defend her frozen soil, her wild provinces of serfs, her dark native despotism, from the tread, the yoke, and the tyranny of a foreign victor—he knew that England, a free realm, could not *then* depute her sons to make concessions and propose terms to an unjust, grasping French leader. When news arrived of the movements of that MAN representing England in the Peninsula, of his advance from success to success—an advance so deliberate yet unswerving, so circumspect yet certain, so "unhasting" yet "unresting"; when he read Lord Wellington's own dispatches in the newspaper columns, documents written by modesty to the dictation of truth—Moore confessed at heart that a power resided within the British troops, a vigilant, enduring, genuine, unostentatious force destined to win victory for the side it led, in the end. In the end! But that end, he thought, lay far off; and meanwhile, he, Moore, as an individual, would be crushed, his hopes ground to dust. It was himself he had to care for, his hopes he had to pursue; and he would fulfill his destiny.

He fulfilled it so vigorously that soon he reached a decisive rupture with his old Tory friend, the rector. They quarreled at a public meeting and subsequently exchanged pungent letters in the newspapers. Mr. Helstone denounced Moore as a Jacobin, ceased to see him, and refused even to speak to him when they met. He intimated to his niece, very distinctly, that her communications with Hollow's Cottage must cease for the present; she must abandon her French lessons. The language, he observed, was bad and frivolous at best, and most of the works it boasted were equally detrimental, highly injurious to weak female minds. He wondered (remarked parenthetically) what fool first made it fashionable to teach women French. Nothing was more improper for them. It was akin to feeding a rickety child on chalk and water gruel. Caroline must give it up, along with her cousins. They were dangerous people.

Mr. Helstone fully expected opposition to this decree; he anticipated tears. Seldom did he trouble himself about Caroline's movements, yet a vague notion possessed him that she was fond of visiting Hollow's Cottage; furthermore, he suspected she enjoyed Robert Moore's occasional presence at the rectory. The Cossack had observed that whereas if Malone stepped in in the evenings to make himself sociable and charming—pinching the ears of an aged black cat that usually shared Miss Helstone's feet with the accommodation of her footstool, or borrowing a fowling-piece to bang away at a toolshed door in the garden while enough daylight remained to reveal the conspicuous mark, keeping the passage and sitting-room doors uncomfortably ajar for the convenience of running in and out to announce his failures and successes with noisy brusquerie—under such entertaining circumstances Caroline had a trick of disappearing, tripping noiselessly upstairs, and remaining invisible until summoned to supper. Conversely, when Robert Moore was the guest, though he elicited no vivacity from the cat, did nothing to it beyond occasionally coaxing it from the stool to his knee, letting it purr, climb to his shoulder, and rub its head against his cheek; though there was no ear-splitting discharge of firearms, no diffusion of sulfurous gunpowder perfume, no noise, no boasting during his stay—still, Caroline sat in the room, seeming to find wondrous content in stitching Jew-basket pin-cushions and knitting missionary-basket socks.

She was very quiet, and Robert paid her little attention, scarcely ever addressing his discourse to her. But Mr. Helstone, not being one of those elderly gentlemen easily blinded—on the contrary, finding himself on all occasions extremely wide-awake—had watched them when they bid each other goodnight. He had just witnessed their eyes meet once—only once. Some natures would have taken pleasure in that glance, surprised as it was, because there was no harm and some delight in it. It was by no means a glance of mutual intelligence, for mutual love secrets existed not between them. There was nothing then of craft or concealment to offend: only Mr. Moore's eyes, looking into Caroline's, felt they were clear and gentle; and Caroline's eyes, encountering Mr. Moore's, confessed they were manly and searching. Each acknowledged the charm in his or her own way. Moore smiled slightly, and Caroline colored as slightly. Mr. Helstone could, on the spot, have rated them both. They annoyed him. Why? Impossible to say. If you had asked him what Moore merited at that moment, he would have said a "horsewhip"; if you had inquired into Caroline's deserts, he would have adjudged her a box on the ear; if you had further demanded the reason for such chastisements, he would have stormed against flirtation and love-making, vowing he would allow no such folly under his roof.

These private considerations, combined with political reasons, fixed his resolution to separate the cousins. He announced his will to Caroline one evening as she sat at work near the drawing-room window. Her face was turned toward him, and the light fell full upon it. It had struck him a few minutes prior that she looked paler and quieter than she used to. It had not escaped him either that Robert Moore's name had never, for some three weeks past, dropped from her lips; nor during that same interval had that personage appeared at the rectory. Suspicion of clandestine meetings haunted his mind. Having but an indifferent opinion of women, he always suspected them. He thought they required constant watching.
