“There’s nothing missing,” said my mother, turning out drawers as though the parlour-maid might be folded among the tablecloths, “so far as I can find out. Depend upon it, that’s the explanation: she has got frightened and has run away.”

“But what was there to frighten her?” said my father, arrested mid-pour, one hand holding the decanter like a trophy, the other the bottle as if it could testify.

“It was the idea of the thing,” replied my mother. “She has never been used to waiting at table. She was actually crying about it only last night.”

“But what’s to be done?” said my father. “They will be here in less than an hour.”

“There will be no dinner for them,” said my mother, with the calm of a woman looking at the abyss, “unless I put on an apron and bring it up myself.”

“Where does she live?” asked my father.

“At Ilford.”

“We must make a joke of it,” said my father, because jokes were the only thing in his armoury that didn’t require a plumber.

My mother sat down and began to cry.

It had been a trying week for her—trying in the particular way a domestic week can be, when it is asked to pretend it is a week in a grander household. A dinner party—to a real dinner, with anchovies at the start and ices at the finish (and the private terror that the ices, corrupted by our inexperienced air, would present themselves as cold custard)—was an astonishing departure from the steady, modest diet of our life. First there had been the cleaning: not the ordinary sweeping-away of crumbs, but the rearranging of the very soul of the house, as though a guest might be able to smell last year’s economy through the wallpaper. Then the purchases: little things that Need never even thinks to ask for, but which Ostentation—if you once admit her sniffing nose across your threshold—demands immediately, with a curled lip.

And then, as inevitably as a sermon after a hymn, the kitchen range took offence. It went on strike with the dignity of a labour union led by an elderly boiler with a grievance, refusing to bake another dish except under “improved conditions,” meaning, translated, weary days of plumbers clanking tools and whistling out of tune, while the kitchen filled with that peculiar metallic wet smell of disturbed pipes and damp coal-dust.

Cookery books—fat, reproachful volumes long left to sleep upright on their shelf—were hauled down, opened, argued with, insulted, and appealed to. My mother tried sauces, failed, tried again, succeeded, and wrote anxious little sums in the margins as though a béchamel were a moral expenditure. The air for days had been alive with domestic music: the knife’s quick, reassuring tap on the board; the hiss of fat when it finally consented to behave; the low bubbling of stock like talk in another room; and, above all, the tyrannical tick of the clock, which in such weeks becomes less a timepiece than a judge.

Victory had been in sight. And now—this.

Downstairs in the kitchen would be the dinner, waiting: the fish with its faint sea-sweetness; vegetables turned and trimmed until they looked like polite company; gravies reduced to the glossy point where they cling to a spoon like affection. Upstairs, around the glittering table, would be the guests, waiting for their dinner. But between the two yawned an impassable gulf. The bridge—our parlour-maid—had bolted without warning, and was probably, at that moment, on a rattling train to Ilford, leaving behind only the soundless mockery of her absence.

There was excuse for my mother’s tears.

“Isn’t it possible to get somebody else?” asked my father.

“Impossible, in the time,” said my mother. “I had been training her for the whole week. We had rehearsed it perfectly.”

“Have it in the kitchen,” suggested my aunt, folding napkins into what she insisted were ships, though they resembled nothing more nautical than damp handkerchiefs with ambitions, “and call it a picnic.”

It truly seemed the only practical solution.

A light knock came at the front door.

“It can’t be anybody yet, surely,” exclaimed my father, alarmed into motion and reaching for his coat as if a coat could repel guests.

“It’s Barbara, I expect,” said my mother. “She promised to come round and help me dress. But now, of course, I shan’t want her.” My mother could make a cloud out of sunshine if you gave her the materials.

I, however, slipped out to admit Barbara, because even then I had learned a household truth: when Barbara came in through the front door, shadows tended to depart by the window.

She ran into the room and kissed my father, my mother, and my aunt with such speed that it looked like one bright, continuous movement, like a bird’s wing brushing three branches.

At once—simply by being there—she changed the sound of the room. Before, it had been the damp thud of distress, the awkward clink of decanter and bottle, the faint sobbing that makes wallpaper seem to listen. Now there was quick breathing, the rustle of her skirts, the light, confident tap of her heels; and somehow the whole domestic disaster began to sound like a story you might later tell and laugh over.

“There’s going to be no dinner,” laughed my father. “We are going to look surprised and pretend that it was yesterday. It will be fun to see their faces.”

“There will be a very nice dinner,” said my mother, and even she smiled a little, “but it will be in the kitchen, and there’s no way of getting it upstairs.” And they explained to Barbara the calamity: the vanished parlour-maid and the stranded feast.

Barbara’s face grew grave for one instant—grave in the way a good cook looks when the sauce threatens to split. Then her eyes lit.

“I’ll get you someone,” she said.

“My dear, you don’t even know the neighbourhood,” began my mother.

But Barbara had already snatched the latchkey from its nail and flown.

With her going, the room lost its tune again, as though a violin had been carried away and the rest left scraping at their parts.

“If there were only an hotel in this beastly neighbourhood,” said my father.

“You must entertain them by yourself, Luke,” said my mother. “And I must wait—that’s all.”

“Don’t be absurd, Maggie,” cried my father, with that angry edge men use when they are afraid. “Can’t cook bring it in?”

“No one can cook a dinner and serve it, too,” answered my mother, impatiently. “Besides, she’s not presentable.”

“What about Fan?” whispered my father.

My mother merely looked at him. That look closed the topic more firmly than a lock.

“Paul?” suggested my father, turning to me as if I were a piece of spare furniture that might be used to prop up disaster.

“Thank you,” retorted my mother. “I don’t choose to have my son turned into a footman, if you do.”

“Well, hadn’t you better go and dress?” was my father’s next remark, as though he could shove the problem into a wardrobe and button it.

“It won’t take me long to put on an apron,” was my mother’s reply.

“I was looking forward to seeing you in that new frock,” said my father.

In another man you might have called it tact. In my father it was sheer happy accident—as when you drop a loaf and it lands butter-side up.

My mother confessed, speaking with the indulgence one allows one’s own foolish hopes once they are spoiled, that she too had been looking forward to seeing herself therein. Their threatened discord melted, not into agreement exactly, but into that mutual tenderness two people find in the ruins of a shared plan.

“I so wanted everything to be all right, for your sake, Luke,” said my mother. “I know you were hoping it would help on the business.”

“I was only thinking of you, Maggie, dear,” answered my father. “You are my business.”

“I know, dear,” she said, and her voice held that weary love which is like broth kept warm too long: still nourishing, but trembling at the edges. “It is hard.”

Then the key turned in the lock and we all fell quiet to listen, as if the house itself were eavesdropping.

“She’s come back alone,” said my mother. “I knew it was hopeless.”

The door opened.

“Please, ma’am,” said a new voice, bright as a struck spoon, “will I do?”

There she stood framed by the lintel: Barbara, transformed. The daintiest apron, the daintiest cap set on her golden hair; and she swept away objections as if they were crumbs with the airy, merry wilfulness that made people obey her and be grateful for it.

“You shall be footman,” she ordered, turning to me.

This time my mother only laughed—laughed, indeed, with relief so sharp it was almost a sob.

“Wait here till I come down again.”

Then, to my mother, briskly and commandingly, as though timing a dish: “Now, ma’am, are you ready?”

It was the first time I had seen my mother in evening dress. To tell the truth it shocked me. Not because she looked wrong—she looked astonishingly right—but because it was like hearing a familiar tune played on a new instrument. Her shoulders shone white, too white for the lamplight, and I felt, childishly, as though something private had been put on display. I must have shown it, because she flushed and drew her shawl over herself, murmuring about cold.

Barbara cried out that it was a sin to hide such beauty. My father, with a quick hand—so quick it suggested practice—filched the shawl away. Then he dropped on one knee as if the world had turned into a picture book where gentlemen behave as they ought, took my mother’s hand, and kissed it gravely. Barbara, standing behind my mother’s chair, insisted I do the same, declaring the Queen was receiving. So I knelt too, glancing up shyly, and caught my first glimpse of the strange philosophy of clothes: how fabric and cut can make you see, for a moment, a person you thought you already knew.

My memory lingers there, by contrast with the swift, sad days that followed—days when my mother’s eyes would flash at my father, and her voice ring cruel and hard; then, the moment he was gone, her lips would tremble and her eyes soften and fill. Days when my father would sit with his face averted, lips pressed tight, or else open them only to fling out savage words; then storm away, slamming the door, and later I would find him alone in the dark with his head bowed between his hands.

At night I lay awake hearing through the thin walls the fierce rise and fall of their voices, now high, now forced into cold whispers that sounded crueller for their quietness. In their estrangement—so new to them—both clung closer to me, though neither told me anything, and I should not have understood if they had. When my mother sobbed with her arms tight around me, I hated my father, thinking he had made her cry. When my father drew me on his knee and I looked into his kind eyes full of pain, I felt angry with my mother, remembering her bitter tongue.

It seemed as though some unseen thing had crept into the house, silent as dust, to stand always between them, so they could never look into each other’s loving eyes but only into the eyes of this evil shadow. The idea grew until I could almost feel it: a coldness passing in the air, the sense of a presence listening. It trod through our pokey rooms with no sound, yet in my mind it had a sort of acoustic signature—like the hush that falls when a cart stops outside and you know, without seeing, that someone has come to the door. It waited, alert, to thrust its grinning face between them. Beside my mother it would whisper; then it would slide across to my father and answer with his voice, but altered. Sometimes I fancied I could hear it laugh to itself, the soft, smug laugh of something that thrives on misheard words.

To this day I seem to see it behind my shoulder when I read a love tale, reading too, silently amused. I cannot pass boy and girl whispering in twilight without imagining it lurking at the edge of their half-light, ready, patient, certain.

Shall any of us escape, or must all roads lead through that valley where love turns to shadow? Is it an ordeal, sorting the weak from the strong—those who will find each other again once the darkness has gone?

Of the dinner itself, until the time of dessert, I can give no consecutive account. As footman under the direction of this enthusiastic parlour-maid, my place was no sinecure. I spent the evening in a marching rhythm between rooms: the kitchen’s rich heat and clamour, where lids clattered, plates rang, and the cook’s voice rose like a kettle; the passage where the smell of roast and hot metal mingled with furniture polish; and the dining-room with its hush of company, interrupted by silver’s bright little chimes and the occasional explosion of laughter.

There were anchovies—salty little shocks laid neatly like small, sophisticated fish on toast; soup that steamed up carrying an honest scent of bones, herbs, and patient simmering; a joint whose carved surface shone, the gravy thickened to that perfect point where it coats and does not run; vegetables that had been coaxed into tenderness rather than bullied into it; and, at last, the feared ices, which—by a miracle and the swift management of kitchen and time—remained ices and did not lapse into cold custard.

All I knew clearly was that the chief guest was a Mr. Teidelmann—or Tiedelmann; I cannot now remember which—a snuffy, mumbling old frump whose name I had seen often enough in huge letters, with a “& Co.” added, painted on dreary blank walls along Limehouse Reach. He sat at my mother’s right hand. Watching him—ugly, foolish-seeming, and profoundly deaf—I wondered how my mother could be so animated with him, shouting much and often to make herself heard, smiling, attending to his wants with an almost festive care, as though politeness were another course to be served hot.

Even old Hasluck, opposite, who though pleasant enough had no great reverence for manners, roared himself purple praising some new disinfectant of which Teidelmann appeared to be the proprietor.

“My wife swears by it!” bellowed Hasluck, leaning across the table.

“Our drains!” chimed in Mrs. Hasluck, a homely soul. “Well, you’d hardly know there was any in the house since I’ve took to using it.”

“What are they talking about?” asked Teidelmann, appealing to my mother. “What’s he say his wife does?”

“Your disinfectant,” explained my mother. “Mrs. Hasluck swears by it.”

“Who?”

“Mrs. Hasluck.”

“Does she? Delighted to hear it,” grunted the old gentleman, bored by his own product.

“Nothing like it for a sick-room,” persisted Hasluck. “Might almost call it a scent.”

“Makes one quite anxious to be ill,” remarked my aunt, aiming her comment at the general air.

“Reminds me of cocoanuts,” continued Hasluck.

Teidelmann appeared not to hear, but Hasluck could not endure the thought of flattery wasted.

“I say it reminds me of cocoanuts,” he screamed, as if cocoanuts were an accusation.

“Oh, does it?” came the reply.

“Doesn’t it you?”

“Can’t say it does,” answered Teidelmann. “As a matter of fact, don’t know much about it myself. Never use it.”

Hasluck, having at last succeeded in killing the subject, let it lie. But as I went past again, I heard him laugh, easy and unoffended.

“He’s quite right,” was Hasluck’s comment. “That’s what I am undoubtedly. Because I can’t talk about anything but shop myself, I think everybody else is the same sort of fool.”

He did himself an injustice, because on my next passage he was shouting again, only now Teidelmann sounded interested.

“Well, if you could spare the time, I’d be more obliged than I can tell you,” Hasluck was saying. “I know absolutely nothing about pictures myself, and Pearsall says you are one of the best judges in Europe.”

“He ought to know,” chuckled Teidelmann. “He’s tried often enough to palm off rubbish onto me.”

“That last purchase of yours must have been a good thing for young—” Hasluck mentioned a painter’s name that years later would have made even servants prick up their ears. “Been the making of him, I should say.”

“I gave him two thousand for the six,” replied Teidelmann, “and they’ll sell for twenty thousand.”

“But you’ll never sell them?” exclaimed my father.

“No,” grunted Teidelmann, “but my widow will.”

There came a soft, low laugh from a corner of the table I could not see, a laugh like a spoon dropped gently into a glass—musical, controlled, faintly mocking.

“It’s Anderson’s great disappointment,” followed a languid, caressing voice—her laugh translated into speech—“that he has never been able to educate me to a proper appreciation of art. He’ll pay thousands of pounds for a child in rags or a badly dressed Madonna. Such a waste of money, it appears to me.”

“But you would pay thousands for a diamond to hang upon your neck,” argued my father.

“It would enhance the beauty of my neck,” replied the voice.

“An even more absolute waste of money,” said my father, low.

And again that soft laugh, like the rustle of expensive silk.

“Who is she?” I asked Barbara.

We were sitting on the stairs, sharing a jelly. It wobbled between us in a glass dish, catching the light in trembling red, smelling faintly of fruit and sugar and that medicinal hint gelatine always keeps no matter how prettily it dresses.

“The second Mrs. Teidelmann,” whispered Barbara. “She is quite a swell. Married him for his money—I don’t like her myself, but she’s very beautiful.”

“As beautiful as you?” I asked, incredulous.

“Oh, me!” answered Barbara, and for once she sounded almost out of conceit. “I’m only a child. Nobody takes any notice of me—except other kids, like you.”

“But everybody thinks you beautiful,” I maintained.

“Who?” she asked quickly.

“Dr. Hal,” I answered.

We sat with our backs to the light so I could not see her face, only the tilt of her cap and the shine of her hair.

“What did he say?” she asked, and her voice had more content in it.

I could not remember his exact words, but the sense of them I was sure of.

“Ask him what he thinks of me, as if you wanted to know yourself,” Barbara instructed me, “and don’t forget what he says this time. I’m curious.”

It seemed a foolish command—what could he say that I had not already told her?—but Barbara’s wishes were like recipes: you did not question them; you followed them, and hoped for success.

If I am right in thinking that jealousy of Mrs. Teidelmann clouded Barbara’s sunny nature for a moment, she had no cause. No one drew more attention throughout the dinner than our sudden parlour-maid.

“Where ever did you get her from?” asked Mrs. Florret, as Barbara came down again from the kitchen stairs.

“A neat-handed Phillis,” commented Dr. Florret, with approval.

“I’ll take good care she never waits at my table,” laughed the wife of our minister, the Rev. Cottle—a broad-built, breezy-voiced woman, mother of eleven, eight of them boys.

“To tell the truth,” said my mother, “she’s only here temporarily.”

“As a matter of fact,” said my father, “we have to thank Mrs. Hasluck for her.”

“Don’t leave me out of it,” laughed Hasluck. “Can’t let the old girl take all the credit.”

Later my father, absent-minded, addressed Barbara as “My dear,” at which Mrs. Cottle shot a swift glance at my mother. Before that glance could cool, Hasluck—when no one was looking—pinched Barbara’s elbow. It would not have mattered had Barbara not, startled, let out an involuntary “augh,” like a kettle protesting. For the reputation of the house—and because the dinner was nearing its end—my mother judged it best to take the whole company into her confidence and tell them the truth.

Naturally the story raised Barbara’s admiration still higher. So when dessert arrived and Barbara, discarding the apron but wisely retaining the dainty cap that set off her hair, sat down among the guests with me—her footman—she was more than ever the centre of attention.

“It was very nice of you,” said Mrs. Cottle, completing the circle of compliments, “and, as I always tell my girls, that is better than being beautiful.”

“Kind hearts,” added Dr. Florret, summing up as if delivering a verdict, “are more than coronets.”

Dr. Florret always had the correct quotation ready. Yet from him it never irritated; it fell on the ear like the Amen in church: not an ornament, but a necessary finish.

Only my aunt could not let the moment lie.

“When I was a girl,” she said, breaking upon the pause, “I used to look into the glass and say to myself: ‘Fanny, you’ve got to be amiable,’ and I was amiable,” added my aunt, challenging contradiction with her eye. “Nobody can say that I wasn’t, for years.”

“It didn’t pay?” suggested Hasluck.

“It attracted,” replied my aunt, “no attention whatever.”

Hasluck had changed places with my mother and—after experiments—had found the correct pitch for conversation with old Teidelmann. Hasluck never wasted time on anything but business. It was in his opera box on the first night of Verdi’s Aida (I speak of days then to come) that he arranged the details of his celebrated guano deal; and even his religion, so I have been told and can well believe, he varied to suit the enterprise of the moment, once, during the protracted preliminaries of a cocoa scheme, becoming converted to Quakerism.

For most of the rest, the interest lay in a discussion between Washburn and Florret about the superior advantages of living in the East End.

As a rule, incorrect opinion found it hard to exist in Dr. Florret’s presence. As no bird, they say, can continue its song once looked at by an owl, so originality tended to fall silent under the cold stare of his disapproval. But Dr. “Fighting Hal” was no delicate warbler. Vehement and direct, indifferent to niceties, he swept through polite argument like a strong wind through a murmuring wood, carrying his friends farther than they meant to go and leaving his opponents clinging—upside down, if necessary—to their perches, angry and ruffled.

“Life!” flung out Washburn—after Dr. Florret had laid down, once again, unimpeachable rules for the conduct of all mankind. “What do you respectable folk know of life? You are not men and women, you are marionettes. You don’t move to your natural emotions implanted by God; you dance according to the latest book of etiquette. You live and love, laugh and weep and sin by rule. Only one moment do you come face to face with life; that is in the moment when you die, leaving the other puppets to be dressed in black and make believe to cry.”

It was a favourite subject of his, the artificiality of us all.

“Little doll,” he had once called me, and I had resented it.

“That’s all you are, little Paul,” he had persisted, “a good little hard-working doll, that does what it’s made to do, and thinks what it’s made to think. We are all dolls. Your father is a gallant-hearted, soft-headed little doll; your mother the sweetest and primmest of dolls. And I’m a silly, dissatisfied doll that longs to be a man, but hasn’t the pluck. We are only dolls, little Paul.”

“He’s a trifle— a trifle whimsical on some subjects,” my father had explained when I repeated it.

“There are a certain class of men,” my mother had explained, “you will meet with them more as you grow up—who talk for talking’s sake. They don’t know what they mean. And nobody else does either.”

“But what would you have?” argued Dr. Florret now. “That every man should do that which is right in his own eyes?”

“Far better than, like the old man in the fable, he should do what every other fool thinks right,” retorted Washburn. “The other day I called to see whether a patient of mine was still alive or not. His wife was washing clothes in the front room. ‘How’s your husband?’ I asked. ‘I think he’s dead,’ replied the woman. Then, without leaving off her work, ‘Jim,’ she shouted, ‘are you there?’ No answer came from the inner room. ‘He’s a goner,’ she said, wringing out a stocking.”

“But surely,” said Dr. Florret, “you don’t admire a woman for being indifferent to the death of her husband?”

“I don’t admire her for that,” replied Washburn, “and I don’t blame her. I didn’t make the world and I’m not responsible for it. What I do admire her for is not pretending a grief she didn’t feel. In Berkeley Square she’d have met me at the door with an agonised face and a handkerchief to her eyes.”

“Assume a virtue, if you have it not,” murmured Dr. Florret.

“Go on,” said Washburn. “How does it run? ‘That monster, custom, who all sense doth eat, of devil’s habit, is angel yet in this, that to the use of actions fair and good he gives a frock that aptly is put on.’ So was the lion’s skin by the ass, but it showed him only the more an ass. Here asses go about as asses, but there are lions also. I had a woman under my hands only a little while ago. I could have cured her easily. Why she got worse every day instead of better I could not understand. Then by accident learned the truth: instead of helping me she was doing all she could to kill herself. ‘I must, Doctor,’ she cried. ‘I must. I have promised. If I get well he will only leave me, and if I die now he has sworn to be good to the children.’ Here, I tell you, they live—think their thoughts, work their will, kill those they hate, die for those they love; savages if you like, but savage men and women, not bloodless dolls.”

“I prefer the dolls,” concluded Dr. Florret.

“I admit they are pretty,” answered Washburn.

“I remember,” said my father, “the first masked ball I ever went to when I was a student in Paris. It struck me just as you say, Hal; everybody was so exactly alike. I was glad to get out into the street and see faces.”

“But I thought they always unmasked at midnight,” said the second Mrs. Teidelmann in her soft, languid tones.

“I did not wait,” explained my father.

“That was a pity,” she replied. “I should have been interested to see what they were like, underneath.”

“I might have been disappointed,” said my father. “I agree with Dr. Florret that sometimes the mask is an improvement.”
