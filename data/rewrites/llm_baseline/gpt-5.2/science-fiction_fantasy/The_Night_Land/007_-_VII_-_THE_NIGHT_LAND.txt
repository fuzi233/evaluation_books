Now as I went toward the North and West, I steered myself with a carefulness that was near to superstition; for the Great Watcher of the North-West had its own region, and I had no wish to give it knowledge of me, nor to have it cry out my passing to those other Powers that do hunt in the Night. Therefore I made my going crooked and patient, choosing shadow and low ground, and that dull cover of the moss-bushes, and the stony places where a man’s footfall is deadened. And in all that while, I kept a constant reckoning within me, like an housewife counting meals and hours, for I had before me a journey not to be measured by any sure yard; and it seemed to me that the least folly with my body would be paid for, later, in blood and in failing.

So I set rules upon myself, even as one sets a kettle to the fire in due time, or turns bread at the right moment; for such small orderly doings are the very saving of a man when all the world about him is disorder. I made it my purpose to go forward, and at every sixth hour to eat and drink, and at the eighteenth hour to sleep until the twenty-fourth. Thus should I have three eatings in the circuit, and six hours’ rest, and be the less broken when need should arise for strength and quickness.

Yet, as is common with human nature, I broke mine own good rule at the very start; for the fear of that Watcher and the thought of the open spaces made me to push on one-and-twenty hours without cease, now walking, now creeping, now lying close as a beast lies when a hawk is abroad. And when, once or twice, the thought of food came, it turned sour in my mouth, as if I had chewed brimstone; so that I put it aside, saying within me, “Presently—presently,” and went on, because the Night Land drives a man’s mind into odd corners.

But when the one-and-twenty hours were gone, weariness took me, and a faintness also, and I knew I must have a rest, though it were but a poor one. Then, after a little, I saw afar off a small fire-hole such as I had passed at odd times, even so early in my journey. I made toward it; for there is comfort in warmth, and I thought to find a dry place where I might lay down, out of the chill.

And when I came near, it seemed, for all its evil smell, almost cheerful in that waste. The hole was but a few paces wide, and full of a dull red glow, with a bubbling that was like thick stew at the first simmer, and it threw out a little sulphur-smoke, thin yet biting, that set my nostrils to smarting. The air there had a taste, as if a knife had been rubbed on a stone and then put to the tongue. I sat down at no great distance, where the warmth reached me without my being too close, and I laid the Diskos on the rock by my hand, as one lays a trusted tool within easy reach.

For a while I moved not. My limbs were heavy, and in my belly there was a hollow weakness; yet my mouth had no desire. I sat and turned me, and looked back to the Mighty Pyramid. Though I had come a good space, it still stood so huge that it seemed close upon me, and I was both comforted and put out of heart: comforted because it was Home and humanity, and put out of heart because its very nearness, by reason of its greatness, made my journey seem the more impossible, as if I had scarce begun. It is a strange thing: a mountain comforts a man by showing him where he belongs, yet it can also crush his courage by showing him how small he is.

I knew, too, that within that great mass there were countless millions, and that many would be watching. I made no sign; for I had resolved not to be ever turning to wave farewell, as a child doth at a door, but to go. Yet it was a queer feeling to sit so near to all mankind, and behave as if I were already beyond all speech and fellowship. And I had a thought—warm and homely—that the dear Master Monstruwacan might even then have me in the Great Spy-Glass; and that perhaps he watched me in that moment, as a careful elder watches a young one set out, making a show of sternness to hide his concern.

Then shame came on me that I should sit like a sulky invalid and refuse my vittles; and I thought of how foolish it would look, even to that far eye, and how I should need strength. So I opened my scrip and took three of the tablets, and chewed them and swallowed. They were strong food, all virtue and little bulk; yet they were poor comfort to the mouth—dry, with a dull tang like ground meal kept too long—and they filled not the belly. Therefore I would drink well, to have at least the sense of something within me.

To that end, I took one of the especial tubes and shook forth a dust into a little cup. The air took hold on it at once, as if an unseen hand stirred it; it boiled and fizzed like yeast in warm mash, and rose and changed, until the cup was filled with water—plain water, yet made by a thing that looked like grey flour. It is a wondrous provision, and I had been used to it; but there is ever a little strangeness in seeing thirst answered by so small a conjuring, with no stream, no well, no living sound of pouring—only the chemical whisper and the sharp smell that dies away.

Thus had I in a little scrip such keeping of life as might serve for a long time. Yet it was a comfortless manner of feeding, lacking the honest savor of bread, and the fat smell of meat, and the common sweetness of a cooked thing. In that hungry world, though, a man is thankful even for cold devices that give the body continuance.

Afterward I went over the things I carried, as one checks a pack before a long day’s march. There was the Diskos and the food-scrip, and besides these a pouch with various matters. And I took out the small compass that the Master Monstruwacan had made for me with his own hands, from an olden one out of the Great Museum. He had told me that I might go so far into the Night Land that I should lose sight of the Pyramid amid the darkness and the vastness; and if the ancient principle still lived within the machine—though turned no more to the North, but to the Pyramid—it might guide me Homeward, by that old obedience men once trusted upon the seas of the world.

I set it upon the ground; but it had no certainty, and did spin and waver. This I considered, remembering I was yet above that part where, far down, the greatness of the Underground Fields spread; and I was, likely, not far from the “Crack,” though a mighty way above it. The earth there had a different pull and a different secret life, and it made the little needle fret and tremble, as a creature will when it cannot find its path.

I wondered whether the Master, watching through the Glass, could see me make this test, for the fire-hole gave a good light, and the Glass had great strength. Yet there is no surety in that searching: the strange fires waver, and smoke and mist rise in this place and that; now the sight is clear where you would swear it must fail, and anon it dulls where you think it should go gaily. The Night Land is a false lantern, and plays tricks with the eyes of men.

Presently I put the compass away and made to compose myself to sleep. Yet here I will set down—though it be out of strict order—how later, after many days outward, I found indeed that the needle’s northward part did draw toward the Great Redoubt, which was a comfort to my spirit and would have been a matter of interest to the Master if I had returned. And after a yet greater time, far away, fearing to lose Home entirely in the Darkness, I pulled it out again and found it pointed no more to the Pyramid but westward, as if some great Power in the Night did sway it. Then came upon me a childlike wonder whether it might be that ancient Northward Force of which the books and my Memory-Dreams had told. Truly there should have been no doubt; yet in that age, to know with the brain is one matter, and to know with the heart is another. I had known always that such a Force existed; but I had not known it, with the true meaning of knowledge, until a little needle in my palm obeyed it.

And there was yet another cause for doubt: perhaps, I thought then, the power of the Lesser Redoubt had begun to pull upon the machine, even as the Earth-Current of the Great Pyramid pulled it still. If so, then might I be near the end of my journey; for that lesser power could not tug, save I had come close. Yet now I know it was the North that drew. I make much telling of this small thing, because it shows the manner of my inward mind in those days; and the lack of sure knowledge that is like a hunger, gnawing always.

But now, in that first time, I wrapped the cloak-matter—crossed shoulder to hip—about me and lay down beside the fire-hole. I laid the Diskos within the cloak, close to my side, and it gave me a queer comfort, as if I had a living companion. The fire-hole made a low bubbling and a faint hiss, and the sulphur-smoke came and went. In those drowsing moments when the soul goes outward like breath toward sleep, I had a half-sense that the aether surged about me; and I doubt not but that many within the Great Redoubt watched my every doing, and were stirred humanly at my commending of myself unto slumber. Their unity of sympathy seemed to shake the very air of the world, and perhaps I knew it dimly, and slept the better for it.

I was worn to the bone, and slept heavy and strong. Yet my last thin thoughts were of the Maid I went to find; and in dreams I had speech with her, and a strange happiness, as if all were touched with fairy-light and freed from sorrow.

From such sleep I was waked sudden by a great and mighty sound. My senses came instant, as a blade comes out of a sheath. I knew the Voice of the Home-Call went howling across the Night. Swift and silent, I slipped the cloak away and took the haft of the Diskos into my hand.

I looked toward the Pyramid for a message, knowing surely that a great need had arisen and some Terror moved toward me out of the Dark; else they would not have waked all the Night Land to knowledge that an human went abroad beyond the Refuge. Yet even as I peered, I could not keep my gaze wholly that way; fear made my eyes sweep all around. I saw nothing; only the vast blackness above, and, high up, the Final Light of the Tower of Observation, thin as a star and steady as duty. I crouched, holding the Diskos, glancing over my shoulders, waiting—listening with all of me, as one listens for a foot upon a stair in a quiet house.

Then, far up in that prodigious height, I saw quick darting flashes of strange green fire. I knew the Set-Speech. The warning came sharp: a Grey Man—a great grey monster—had made scent of me and even then crawled toward me through the low moss-bushes beyond the fire-hole, at my back. The message bade me leap into the bushes at my left and hide, that I might take it with advantage.

I had scarcely read the tale before I was gone among the shadows of a clump of moss-bush. Terror made sweat cold upon me, and my heart shook; yet my spirit was set hard to conquer. And there, crouched and hidden, I saw something come very quiet out of the bushes beyond the fire-hole. It was great, creeping, all grey in every part, as if it were cut from ash and old bone. The glare from the fire-hole troubled it. It laid its head to the ground and peered along the earth, spying under the glare as a brutish thing might do to see past a lantern. It crept back into the bushes and came out again at another place by the edge of the hole; this it did thrice to my left and thrice to my right, each time laying its head down, hunching its shoulders, thrusting forward its jaw in a wanton nasty manner, turning its neck as a beast turns when it scents prey.

This fashion shook my courage; for each time it went in among the moss-bushes I thought it had discovered me and would take me from behind. A man may be brave enough in the open; but to be hunted in silence, with the dark full of possible hands, is a different proving.

Then, in truth, the swift sense of my Hearing saved me. The thing went back into the bushes after that last coming out, and for perhaps a minute my mind would have it that it had failed to discover me and meant only to return to the Night. And then, lo, within my soul a voice spoke plain and warned me: it made a great compass among the bushes about the fire-hole; it had found me, and went warily to take me from the other side.

When I heard that voice within my spirit, I knew the Master Monstruwacan watched from the Tower and sent the speech with his brain-elements, having in mind that I had the Night-Hearing. I trusted it; for in the same instant the solemn throb of the Master-Word beat all about me in the Night, to give assurance. I leapt quick from that clump of moss-bush to another, crouched, watched all ways, and kept the ears of my spirit open, knowing he watched with me.

Suddenly I saw a little stirring of a bush to the back of where I had hid. Out came a great grey hand, feeling into the moss of the clump I had left, as if the thing peered from behind the leaves. Then followed the great grey head, and it thrust into that clump where, a moment before, I had been.

I knew I must strike. I leapt and smote with the Diskos. The blow was one—clean and final. The thing fell upon its side. Great grey legs came out of the hither bushes and twitched and drew upwards, while the head remained thrust into the moss where it had sought me. I stood away from it whilst it died; and in my hand the Diskos spun and sent forth fire, as if it lived and knew it had slain a great and horrid monster.

When it was dead, I went round to the far side of the fire-hole. I held the Diskos high, spinning and sending out fire, so that they within the Mighty Pyramid might know the Beast-Man was slain; for it might lie too much in shadow for them to see.

The Master spoke not again; for it was not meet, save to save me from sure danger. There are Powers in the Night Land that hearken to such doings, and it might be there had been already overmuch, even for my safety. Yet it could not be helped.

Now, being somewhat calmed, I knew the aether of the Night was disturbed by the gladness of the millions within the Great Redoubt. It was plain how great a multitude had given note unto the fight; their hearts had beat together in fear and sympathy, so that I felt companied and befriended, though yet shaken.

After a little I gathered my wits and looked to the hours, and found I had slept surely ten. I reproached myself; for I had slept oversound because I had not kept to my intended order. I resolved to obey reason thereafter, eating and resting in due season as I had planned.

With a self-reproachful heart I went round the small fire-hole and took up my cloak and matters. I turned and looked once up along the immeasurable slope of the Pyramid, climbing into the far blackness. I made no salutation, as I had resolved; and I desired not to stir the aether of the world by waking the emotions of the millions with any needless farewell.

Then I turned away and went off into the Night, swift and cautious, bearing the Diskos cunningly and with a kind of love for it; for it had befriended me and slain the foul Grey Man with one stroke. It seemed to know me, and to have comradeship with me—such as men of the old days may have felt for a sword that had saved their lives. Yet the Diskos was more than a sword; it seemed alive with the fire and flame of the Earth-Current that beat within it.

It was well acknowledged within the Great Redoubt that none might touch the Diskos of another; for the thing went crustily in a stranger’s hands. If any persisted in handling or trying to use it, it would act clumsy and bring hurt. This had been known, perhaps, a hundred thousand years, or more. Therefore men believed—and wisely—that an affinity grew between a person’s nature and the Diskos used in practice. And because of this, and because the place would else be lumbered with weapons of the dead, it was Law and Usage that the Diskos of the dead should be placed with them upon The Last Road in the Country of Silence, returning its power to the Earth-Current. There was wisdom in it; and yet I doubt not a human tenderness lay beneath also, for Love should ever mate with Wisdom to mother Comfort in sorrow.

So I went onward, looking ever to this shadow and to that. My heart stirred with swift fears; my body quivered, ready to leap aside, and then found nothing. I had imaginings continually of uncouth Brutes and Beings that might come out of the darkness. Yet there was also a proudness in me that I had come safe out of the Grey Man’s power and slain him. But praise belongs not overmuch to me; for I had died as I slept, but that those within the Great Redoubt watched me and waked me to my saving.

Presently, as I walked, faintness returned, and I knew I did foolishly; for I should have eaten after the fight. Yet a man may be forgiven some forgetting when his heart has been shaken. I sat down in a little clear place among the bushes and ate three tablets, and again shook forth the dust that turned in the air to water by that proper chemistry. The water had no taste of stream or stone; it was clean and cold, but without the remembered sweetness of a drawn well. Still, it eased my throat, which the sulphur airs had roughened, and it laid a small comfort in my belly beside the dry strength of the tablets.

After I had eaten, I sat a little and thought, and looked up at the great slope of the Pyramid in the Night. All the while I listened with ears and spirit, and kept the Diskos across my knees, turning my head frequent this way and that. But nothing came near.

Then I rose and went onward, and walked six hours toward the North and West. For a while I made much to the West, to come clear of the North-West Watcher. Yet after a space I did foolishly; for I changed my mind and kept something more toward the North, that I might have surer sight of that Monster.

This was rash and naughty; for if I were but seen, the grim Brute might signal the Evil Powers and I be met swift with destruction. Yet the heart is a strange thing, running from fear into rashness as a flame runs along spilled spirit. Perhaps some influence was upon me and drew me that way; but who shall say.

So I went forward, and now I began to keep my order better: at every sixth hour I halted to eat and drink, and to look a little toward the monstrous towering of the Great Redoubt, and to strengthen my heart again before I went on. Always I went warily, chiefly among the low moss-bush; but at times I must cross stony ground, and often places where sulphur puffed from the earth in low smoke, strong in the nostrils and ill to the inward parts. I stooped to crawl, and my hands bled somewhat upon the roughness; and after I was so troubled I put on the great gloves that belonged to the grey armour, and so was shod properly for that harsh going.

All the while I watched right, left, and rear, and kept a constant observation, as far as I might, of the Mighty Watcher toward which I drew nigh. And thus I went on into the Night Land.
