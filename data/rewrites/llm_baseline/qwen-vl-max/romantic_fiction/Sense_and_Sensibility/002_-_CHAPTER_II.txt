Mrs. John Dashwood had now settled into her role as mistress of Norland Park, a position she accepted with quiet dignity. Her mother and sisters-in-law—Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters—were no longer part of the household in any official sense but were welcomed as guests, though their status was clearly diminished. Still, Mrs. Dashwood treated them with gentle politeness, and Mr. Dashwood, though reserved by nature, showed them more kindness than he typically extended to anyone outside his immediate family.

He sincerely encouraged them to stay at Norland as long as they wished, even suggesting they consider it their home. For Mrs. Dashwood, this was an appealing prospect. The house held cherished memories of happier days, and lingering there offered comfort amid her grief. In moments of joy, she was radiant—her spirit bright, full of hope, and unburdened by doubt. But when sorrow came, she was equally swept away by emotion, unable to find solace as easily as she once found delight.

Mrs. John Dashwood, however, did not share her husband’s sentimentality. She viewed his intention to support his half-sisters with deep concern. “To take three thousand pounds from our little Harry’s inheritance,” she said, “would be to impoverish him terribly.” She pleaded with him to reconsider. How could he justify depriving his only child of such a sum? And what right did the Miss Dashwoods—related to him only through his father’s first marriage—have to claim so much?

She reminded him that people rarely formed strong bonds between children born of different marriages. Why should he sacrifice his own future and his son’s security for distant relatives?

“It was my father’s final request,” Mr. Dashwood replied quietly. “He asked me to assist his widow and daughters.”

“Oh, I’m certain he wasn’t thinking clearly,” she countered. “He must have been confused. If he’d been in his right mind, he never would have imagined you giving away half your fortune to strangers.”

“He didn’t name a specific amount,” Mr. Dashwood explained. “Only that I should help them, make their lives more comfortable than he could. Perhaps it would have been better if he hadn’t mentioned it at all. But since he made the promise, I felt bound to keep it—at least, I thought so then. It was given, and it must be honored. Something must be done when they leave Norland and begin their new life.”

“Then let something be done,” she conceded, “but not three thousand pounds. Think: once the money is gone, it’s gone forever. They’ll marry, and it will vanish entirely. If only it could return to Harry one day—”

“Well,” he said seriously, “that would indeed matter. One can imagine Harry someday regretting such a loss. If he has many children, that sum might mean everything.”

“True,” she agreed.

“Perhaps we should reduce it,” he suggested. “Five hundred pounds each would be generous enough—no more than fair.”

“Oh!” she exclaimed. “That would be extraordinary! What brother would do half so much for his sisters—even real ones? And these are only half-sisters! You’re so kind-hearted.”

“I don’t wish to act meanly,” he replied. “On matters like this, it’s better to give too much than too little. No one can say I’ve failed them. Even they won’t expect more.”

“But what if they do?” she asked. “We must think not of their expectations, but of what we can afford.”

“Of course,” he said. “And I believe five hundred pounds each is within reason. Without my help, they’ll each inherit about three thousand pounds when their mother passes—a fine fortune for young women.”

“That’s true,” she acknowledged. “In fact, I’m beginning to wonder if they need anything at all. Ten thousand pounds divided among them—what more could they possibly want? If they marry, they’ll do well. If not, they can live comfortably together on the interest.”

“That’s exactly it,” he said. “So perhaps it would be wiser to provide for their mother now—through an annuity, for instance. That way, they’d benefit just as much. A hundred pounds a year would ease their lives greatly.”

Mrs. John Dashwood hesitated. “It’s better than giving fifteen hundred pounds at once,” she admitted. “But suppose Mrs. Dashwood lives fifteen years? We’d be trapped.”

“Fifteen years?” he said gently. “Her life isn’t worth that much.”

“Still,” she insisted, “people always seem to live forever when there’s an annuity involved. She’s strong and healthy—hardly forty. Annuities are serious. They come every year, without fail. There’s no escape. I know how burdensome they can be. My mother had to pay three annuities to old servants under my father’s will. She hated it. Twice a year, she had to send payments, often struggling to deliver them. Once, someone claimed one of the recipients had died—only to learn later it wasn’t true. She grew sick of it. She said her income wasn’t truly hers anymore, tied down by endless claims. My father was cruel to impose such restrictions. It taught me to despise annuities. I’d rather not bind myself to any at all.”

“I understand,” Mr. Dashwood said. “They do drain one’s independence. To be tied to regular payments, year after year—it’s far from ideal.”

“And yet,” she added, “you get no thanks. They assume it’s expected. Your generosity means nothing to them. If I were you, I’d give freely—but only when I choose. No yearly obligations. Some years, we may not spare even fifty pounds from our own needs.”

“You may be right,” he said thoughtfully. “A regular allowance might encourage them to spend more, not save. But occasional gifts—say fifty pounds here and there—would help without burdening us. It would fulfill my promise without overreaching.”

“Exactly,” she said. “And honestly, I believe your father never meant for you to give them money at all. His idea of ‘assistance’ was likely modest—helping them find a small home, moving their things, sending game or fish when available. That’s all. I’m certain he meant nothing more. Consider: seven thousand pounds in interest, plus a thousand each for the girls—fifty pounds a year per daughter. With that, they’ll pay their mother for board. Together, they’ll have five hundred pounds annually. What four women could possibly need more? They’ll live simply—no carriage, no horses, few servants, no grand company. Their expenses will be minimal. Five hundred pounds! I can’t imagine how they’d spend half of it. Giving them more would be absurd. In fact, they might soon be able to give *us* something.”

“Upon reflection,” Mr. Dashwood said, “I think you’re absolutely correct. My father likely meant only what you’ve described. I now see it clearly. I shall honor my word—not with money, but with practical kindness. When they move, I’ll help them settle. A small gift of furniture might be welcome.”

“Certainly,” she agreed. “But one thing must be remembered: when your parents moved to Norland, though Stanhill’s furniture was sold, the china, silver, and linen were preserved—and left to your mother. So her new home will already be nearly furnished.”

“That’s a significant point,” he said. “A valuable legacy. Though I admit, some of that silver would have been a lovely addition to our own collection.”

“Yes,” she replied, “and the breakfast set is twice as fine as ours. Far too fine for any house they could afford. But so it is. Your father thought only of *them*. And I must say: you owe them no special gratitude. We all know he would have left almost everything to them if he could.”

The argument struck deep. It removed any lingering hesitation. Mr. Dashwood finally decided: it would be neither necessary nor proper to do more for his father’s widow and children than the simple acts of neighborly kindness his wife had described.
