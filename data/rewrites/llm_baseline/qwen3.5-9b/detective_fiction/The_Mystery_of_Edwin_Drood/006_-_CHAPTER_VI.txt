The Reverend Septimus Crisparkle, affectionately known simply as "Septimus" due to the tragic lineage of six elder brothers who extinguished themselves like weak rushlights before him, had spent the early morning shattering the frost near Cloisterham Weir with his head—a ritual that invigorated his spirit. Now, seeking further circulation, he engaged in a spirited bout of boxing against a mirror. The glass reflected a picture of health and vigor: Septimus feinted with artistic grace, countered with precision, and delivered straight punches from the shoulder, all while radiating an aura of pure innocence and benevolent soft-heartedness emanating from his boxing gloves.

It was barely breakfast time. Mrs. Crisparkle, his mother rather than wife, had just descended the stairs, waiting for the tea urn. At this precise moment, Septimus paused his exercise to press his gloved fists gently against the old lady's face, kissing it with deep tenderness. Immediately resuming his sport, he launched a furious assault with his left and right hands.

"I tell you, Septimus," the old lady observed, watching intently, "one day you'll break the pier-glass or burst a blood vessel."

"Do what, Mother?" Septimus asked, grinning.

"Bust something!" she insisted.

"Not today, thank God. Feel this wind!" he declared, puffing out his chest.

He concluded the round with terrifying intensity, administering blows and evading counterattacks until he finally managed to tuck the old lady's cap safely into the "Chancery"—a technical term beloved by scientists of the Noble Art—without disturbing a single lavender or cherry ribbon. With magnanimous sportsmanship, he released his defeated opponent just in time to shove his gloves into a drawer and pretend to gaze out the window in contemplation. This charade ended immediately upon the entrance of a servant, allowing the tea urn to take center stage.

Once breakfast preparations were complete, the scene shifted to a quieter intimacy. Mrs. Crisparkle stood reciting the Lord's Prayer aloud, her voice steady and clear. Septimus, though nearly forty, bowed his head reverently to listen, just as he had done when he was merely thirty-nine months old. There is nothing more charming than an elderly woman whose eyes sparkle, whose figure remains trim, whose face holds a cheerful calm, and whose attire resembles that of a delicate porcelain shepherdess—colors chosen with such dainty individuality and molded perfectly to her form. Septimus often mused on this beauty as he sat opposite his widowed mother. Her thoughts, however, distilled themselves into two simple words that dominated her conversation: "My Sept!"

They were a perfect pair for breakfast in Minor Canon Corner, a secluded spot beneath the cathedral shadow. Here, the cawing of rooks, the echo of rare footsteps, the tolling bells, and the rolling organ music somehow enhanced the silence rather than breaking it. Centuries of swaggering soldiers, suffering serfs, and powerful monks had passed through this corner, but they were all gone, leaving behind a blessed tranquility. This peace fostered a serene, romantic mindset—one prone to pity and forbearance—that arises only after a sad story is fully told or a tragic play has reached its end. Surrounded by red-brick walls softened by age, strong ivy, lattice windows, panelled rooms, oak beams, and stone gardens where monkish trees still bore fruit, the pair enjoyed their meal.

"And what does the letter say, Mother?" Septimus asked, displaying a hearty appetite.

Mrs. Crisparkle set the letter down on the cloth and handed it to her son. She prided herself on her sharp vision, needing no spectacles, and Septimus, wishing her maximum enjoyment, pretended he could not see well without them. He donned a pair of comically large glasses that pinched his nose and obstructed his view, despite possessing eyes that functioned like a combined microscope and telescope when unaided.

"It's from Mr. Honeythunder, naturally," the old lady announced, crossing her arms.

"Naturally," Septimus agreed.

She began to read: *"Dear Madam, I write in the..."*

"In the what?" Septimus queried, removing his glasses to see her face clearly.

"In the chair," she corrected gently. "Give it back, my dear; you're missing the context."

Relieved to remove the water-inducing lenses, Septimus murmured that his reading vision was deteriorating daily.

*"I write from the chair,"* his mother continued, *"to which I shall probably be confined for some hours. We have a meeting of our Convened Chief Composite Committee of Central and District Philanthropists... and it is their unanimous pleasure that I take the chair."*

Septimus sighed with relief. "If he means that, let him come."

At this, Septimus dropped his knife and fork, rubbing his ear in irritation. "It is extraordinary that these philanthropists are always denouncing someone. And equally strange that they are perpetually flush with 'miscreants'!"

*"Denouncing a public miscreant,"* his mother read on, *"to get our little affair of business off my mind. I have spoken with my two wards, Neville and Helena Landless, regarding their defective education. They agree to the proposed plan; as I should have ensured, whether they liked it or not."*

"They seize people by the scruff of the neck and bump them into the path of peace," Septimus muttered, apologizing profusely.

*"Therefore, dear Madam, you will please prepare your son, the Rev. Mr. Septimus, to expect Neville as an inmate to be read with, on Monday next. On the same day, Helena will accompany him to Cloisterham to take up quarters at the Nuns' House... The terms are as stated in writing... With compliments to the Rev. Mr. Septimus, I am, Dear Madam, Your affectionate brother (In Philanthropy), LUKE HONEYTHUNDER."*

"Well, Mother," Septimus said after another ear-rub, "we must try it. We certainly have room, time, and inclination. I'm glad it isn't Mr. Honeythunder himself. Though that seems prejudiced—I've never met him. Is he a large man?"

"He is large," his mother hesitated, "though his voice is far larger than his stature."

"Larger than anyone else?"

"Indeed."

"Hah!" Septimus laughed, finishing his breakfast with diminished enthusiasm.

Mrs. Crisparkle's sister, a matchingly delicate piece of Dresden china, lived in London with her childless husband, a city clergyman. Mr. Honeythunder, publicly known as a Professor of Philanthropy, had met Mrs. Crisparkle during her annual visits, following a charity event where orphaned children were fed plum buns. These were the only known details of the incoming students.

"We must ensure they feel at ease," Septimus reasoned. "Nothing is truly disinterested unless *they* are comfortable with us. Jasper's nephew is here; youth attracts youth. He is cordial. Let us invite him to dinner along with the others. That makes eight guests total. Would that trouble you, Mother?"

"Nine would," she replied nervously.

"The exact capacity of the table and room," Septimus clarified.

Thus arranged, Septimus visited Miss Twinkleton to secure Helena Landless's placement at the Nuns' House, extending additional invitations that were readily accepted. Miss Twinkleton glanced sadly at her globe instruments, regretting they couldn't travel, but soon reconciled herself to leaving them behind. Instructions were sent to the Philanthropist for timely arrivals, and the kitchen filled with the scent of soup.

Cloisterham lacked railways; Mr. Sapsea claimed it never would have one. Yet, ironically, express trains now raced past without stopping, casting dust in disdain for the town's insignificance. Only a precarious branch line remained, threatening the money market if it failed and the constitution if it succeeded. Traffic had abandoned the main road for a back alley marked "Beware of the Dog."

Here, Septimus awaited a squat omnibus piled high with luggage, resembling an elephant burdened with castles. A large passenger sat on the driver's box, squashing the driver into a tiny space and glaring fiercely.

"Is this Cloisterham?" the passenger boomed.

"Yes," the driver groaned, rubbing his sore bones.

"How glad I am to see it!"

"Tell your master to widen the box-seat," the passenger commanded. "He is morally, and legally under penalty, bound to provide comfort for his fellow man."

The driver patted his ribs anxiously. "Have I sat on you?"

"You have," the driver admitted reluctantly.

"Take this card," the passenger offered.

"I won't deprive you of it," the driver refused, eyeing the card unfavorably.

"Join that Society," the passenger urged.

"What's in it for me?"

"Brotherhood!"

"Thank you," the driver said flatly. "My mother was content with me, and so am I. I want no brothers."

"But you must have them," the passenger insisted, stepping down. "Whether you like it or not. I am your brother."

"Not too far!" the driver protested. "The worm will turn—"
