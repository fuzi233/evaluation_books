The air between Naples and Rome hung heavy with the scent of brine and impending doom. Two months prior to the quiet domesticity of Eli's home, the coastal townspeople had gathered on the beaches, their gazes fixed unblinkingly upon a vessel fighting a gale that blew with malicious intent against the shore. The ship danced on the edge of catastrophe; moments of respite sparked cheers from the crowd, only to be extinguished instantly when the wind and sea conspired to drive her closer, feeding a secret, grim satisfaction into the hearts of the spectators—a cruel delight in witnessing another's struggle.

Though scientifically unsound for open-sea navigation, the craft was a masterpiece of destruction, designed specifically to break upon the rocks. Her extravagant poop caught the wind like a trap, and her lines hung inverted, resembling a cocked hat turned inside out. To the observers on the sand, the battering timber seemed a living thing, a panting beast struggling against mortality. Yet, had they stepped onto her deck, they would have discovered she possessed not one heart, but a dozen throbbing with terror; not one nature, but a multitude of souls unraveling in that fearful hour.

Chaos reigned supreme. Mariners stumbled wildly, clutching ropes with erratic strength, their cries alternating between blasphemous curses and desperate pleas. Passengers huddled around the mast, a trembling mass of bodies—some seated, some kneeling, others prostrate, fingers digging into the bulwarks as the vessel pitched in the monstrous swells. Among them stood Gerard, a figure of striking comeliness, his cheeks ashen, his lips compressed in a battle between raw terror and stubborn self-respect. Clutching a shroud tightly, he winced with every roll of the sea, an ill-fated soul adrift in a storm of divine indifference.

Around him, the air vibrated with a cacophony of faith. Prayers and vows rose from the throng, creating a polytheistic frenzy where the number of deities invoked nearly equaled the number of humans present. The sailors, however, pinned their hopes to a singular goddess, shifting her titles fluidly from "Queen of Heaven" to "Star of the Sea," "Mistress of the World," and "Haven of Safety." On land, the chaos of belief was even more fractured. An English merchant vowed a pile of gold to Our Lady of Walsingham; a Genoese merchant pledged a silver collar worth four pounds to Our Lady of Loretto; a Tuscan noble promised ten pounds of wax candles to Our Lady of Ravenna. In their fervent diversity, they pledged allegiance not merely to the True Cross, but to specific iterations of the Cross located in various modern cities, each believing their unique offering held exclusive power.

Then, disaster struck with finality. A gust of unprecedented violence caught the sail at a fatal angle. The rotten shrouds snapped, and the mainsail tore free with a deafening crack. It descended slowly, shrinking and darkening until it vanished into the distance, fluttering half a mile offshore like a discarded sheet of paper. Before the helmsman could adjust the ship's heading, a massive wave slammed into her quarter, drenching the terrified crew to the bone and delivering a chilling prelude to death.

In the wake of this betrayal, vows erupted anew. One man swore to become a Carthusian monk if Saint Thomas spared him; another promised a pilgrimage to Compostela, barefoot and clad only in armor, if Saint James intervened. Others invoked Thomas, Dominic, Denis, and above all, Catherine of Siena. Amidst this religious riot, two small Neapolitan traders stood shivering. One shouted at the top of his lungs, vowing a waxen image of Saint Christopher weighing as much as himself if he reached land safely. His companion nudged him, whispering a warning: "Brother, take heed. If you sold all your worldly possessions at auction, it would not buy that much wax."

"Hold your tongue, you fool!" the first retorted, then lowered his voice. "Do you think I am joking? Let me reach land, and I will not give him a single rush-dip."

Elsewhere, others lay flat against the deck, begging the sea itself. "Oh, most merciful sea! Oh, sea most generous! Oh, bountiful sea! Oh, beautiful sea! Be gentle, be kind, preserve us in this hour of peril!" These pleas were met with wails of pure animal terror, echoing every time the ship lurched violently, now reduced to a mere plaything in the arms of the tremendous waves.

Yet, amidst the hysteria, a figure of profound stillness emerged. A Roman woman of humble station sat with her child at her half-bared breast. Silent in the midst of the wailing throng, her cheek pale as ash, her eyes unnervingly calm. Her lips moved occasionally in silent prayer, but she neither wept nor lamented, nor did she bargain with the gods. Only when the ship seemed truly vanishing beneath their feet, causing bearded men to squeak in panic, did she kiss her child. That was her entire response. She sat patiently, suckling him in the jaws of death, refusing to deny him any joy she could offer. *Moribundo?* Yes, there she sat, embodying Antiquity among the medieval. Sixteen centuries had failed to tarnish the old Roman blood flowing in her veins; the instinct of a forgotten race taught her to meet death with decent dignity.

Nearby, a gigantic friar stood on the poop, feet planted wide apart like the Colossus of Rhodes. He did not defy the peril surrounding him; he simply ignored it. With a loud, unwavering voice, he recited verses from the Canticles and invited the passengers to confess. Some knelt before him, and he heard them, laying hands upon their heads and absolving them as if they stood in a secure sacristy rather than a perishing ship. Gerard, drawn by an instinct that pulls the wavering toward the impregnable, moved closer and closer to the monk. Indeed, the courage of heroes facing flesh-and-blood odds might have paled beside that of the giant friar and his monumental composure. Here, amidst the collapse, two figures upheld the dignity of their race: a woman, tender yet heroic, and a monk steeled by religion against mortal fears.

With the sail gone, the sailors hacked down the useless mast just a foot above the deck, and it crashed over the side with its remaining rigging. For a fleeting moment, this seemed to relieve the strain. But now, deprived of canvas propulsion, the hull could no longer outrun the sea. The waves struck her repeatedly on the poop, the tremendous blows sounding less like impacts from liquid and more like strikes from a rocky mountain.

The captain abandoned the helm and appeared amidships, pale as death. "Lighten her!" he cried. "Fling everything overboard, or we shall founder before we strike, losing the one slim chance we have of life." As the sailors executed this order, the captain, himself pale and surrounded by faces demanding answers, spoke with a demeanor utterly alien to an English skipper in such peril. "Friends," he addressed them, "last night, when all was fair—too fair, alas!—a globe of fire approached the ship. When a pair appear, it is good luck; nothing can drown her on that voyage. We mariners call these fiery globes Castor and Pollux. But if Castor comes without Pollux, or Pollux without Castor, she is doomed. Therefore, like good Christians, prepare to die."

These words triggered a loud wail of despair. When a trembling passenger asked how long they had to prepare, the captain replied, "She may, or may not, last half an hour; beyond that, it is impossible. She leaks like a sieve. Bustle, men, lighten her!"

Desperate, the passengers seized everything on deck and hurled it into the churning water. Soon, they grabbed a heavy sack. Beneath it lay an old man, seasick and unconscious. They dragged it from under him; it rattled ominously. Two men pulled it to the side, and the owner shot upward with an unearthly shriek, pouncing on the bundle. "Holy Moses! What would you do? 'Tis my all! 'Tis the whole fruit of my journey! Silver candlesticks, silver plates, brooches, hanaps—"

"Let go, thou hoary villain!" cried the others. "Shall all our lives be lost for thy ill-gotten gear?" "Fling him in with it!" shouted one. "'Tis this Hebrew we Christian men are drowned for." Numbers soon wrested the bundle from him and heaved it over the side. It splashed into the waves. Its owner let out one cry of anguish, stood glaring with white hair streaming in the wind, and prepared to leap after it, had it not already sunk forever. He staggered to and fro, tearing his hair, cursing them, the ship, the sea, and all the powers of heaven and hell alike.

"Now," the captain cried, pointing, "see, there is a church in sight. Steer for that church, mate, and you, friends, pray to the saint, whoever he may be."

Thus, they steered for the church and prayed to the unknown deity it housed. A tremendous sea pooped the vessel, shattered the rudder, and jammed it immovable, flooding the deck.
