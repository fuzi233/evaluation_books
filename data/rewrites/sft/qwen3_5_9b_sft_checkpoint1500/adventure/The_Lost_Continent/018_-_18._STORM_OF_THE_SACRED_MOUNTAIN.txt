It was Nais who summoned me from my solitary vigil to the stern realities of duty. Within the chamber where I attended her, the clamor of the assault broke upon us. She asked for its meaning, her voice strained by pain, lacking the breath for lengthy explanation. I gave her the brief history of her nine-year slumber, delivered with the economy required by her frailty.

Her cheeks flushed a deeper rose. "My lord," she whispered, "he is the rightful King of all worlds."

"I refused to engage in trade until you were returned to me, safe and alive, upon this Mountain."

"How cruel fate makes us, the chattels of men!" she murmured, yet with a strange, bitter amusement. "Still, I am glad to know I caused your hesitation. And what is that steam rising from yonder?"

"A bath," I replied gently. "To restore your strength, but slowly. Patience, my love."

"You listen well," she said, accepting the broth I offered. "Do I look... aged? Wrinkled?" She closed her eyes, sinking back onto the cushions. "I dreamed I woke old."

"You are as beautiful as the first night I beheld you," I assured her. "The finest beauty in existence."

"And you like me?" she asked, taking the cup. "Though my hair has lengthened, it needs a comb. Once I fancied you preferred red locks to plain brown." She drifted into a sound, peaceful sleep, a smile lingering on her lips. I covered her with a blanket, kissing her forehead carefully, keeping my beard from brushing her skin, before stepping out.

Inside the house, the air smelled of iron and blood. I entered a spare room, found a razor, and shaved the wild growth from my jaw. I changed from my ragged rags into robes of clean linen. A king, however humble, must maintain the dignity of his office.

Outside, the noise of battle grew deafening, a narrowing circle of destruction. The gates along the cliff's edge, once deemed impregnable, were yielding to the ferocity of Phorenice's assault. Stone defenses, cunningly laid, could not fight alone; they required flesh to animate them. And flesh was dying.

The slaughter below was prodigious. At the lowest surviving gate, the priests rained massive boulders down the stairway, paving the alley with the writhing dead. Yet Phorenice stood on a spur below, urging her troops with a madness born of desperation. Company after company charged upward, hurling themselves against the fortifications like moths to a flame. Without siege engines to batter the gates, their attack was futile—a child hammering an orange against a wall—while the stones from above mowed them down remorselessly.

Two gates had fallen in this carnage. Then I descended to join the fray. There was no trumpet, no herald, yet as I stepped onto the rampart, the priests cried out, "Deucalion!" as if it were a battle cry. Some shouted that with a King to lead, no ground would be lost.

The name carried weight. A company of doomed soldiers hesitated, the word bouncing back and forth among them. But from behind them, shouts urged them on. A glittering officer, panting, rushed forward and began slashing at the ranks. I recognized him instantly.

"It matters not what you chant for a cry!" he screamed, cutting through the lines. "You follow the orders of your Divine Empress! Be proud to die for her wish, you cowards! Obey, or you shall die later under the tormentors' blades. And as for Deucalion..." He paused, looking up. "He has been dead for nine years."

"There you lie, my Lord Tatho," I shouted down.

He froze, then looked up, a grim smile touching his lips. "So you are there, in truth? Well, old comrade, I am sorry. But it is too late for treaties. You stand with these mangy Priests, and the Empress has issued an edict to root them out. I am her most obedient servant."

"You were once a master of the sword," I said, drawing close. "If you desire to stop this butchery, become the champion of your side. I will stand for mine. Let us settle this quarrel fairly, in some open place, binding both parties to abide by the result."

"It would be a grand duel," Tatho admitted, "yet I cannot grant it. I am General under Phorenice, and she forbids me to risk my life. Besides, you are a great man, Deucalion, but you are not chief. You are not even one of the Three."

"I am King."

"Few but yourself would claim that," Tatho laughed. "Few truly, but those who speak it are powerful. I saw the mischief you wrought in the city yesterday, the first blow of your reign."

"You were in Atlantis!"

"I went for Nais. She rests on the mountain now, and tomorrow she shall be my Queen." I leaned forward, lowering my voice. "Listen to me, Tatho. As a priest to a priest, recall the infinite power you bite against here. Your teachings warn of the weapons stored in the Ark of the Mysteries. Persist, and you lose; worse, you may trigger a cataclysm that will shatter the world, causing the High Gods to shudder."

"You cannot scare us back with words," Tatho snapped. "Magic meets magic. Phorenice possesses powers equal to those of the Ark."

"She looked helpless last night when her pyramid was reduced to rubble," I countered. "Zaemon prophesied it. I witnessed it."

"I admit you surprised her," Tatho conceded grudgingly. "But time requires that one magic answer another."

"Well," I said, "I know little of the arts. I leave those mysteries to the High Priests. Having a love for Nais, I am barred from understanding the highest secrets. Content with being a soldier, I await the appointed day to fall and kiss the Mother Earth for the last time."

He nodded. "Phorenice has found the Great Secret. I am to share it. We shall be gods upon the earth, untouched by Death. And her twin sons shall live forever."

"Phorenice is headstrong," I said softly. "No need to deny it. But the order is made. Her immortality will be stolen from her rudely. Mark my words, Tatho: I am King of Atlantis by the decree of the High Council, the mouthpiece of the Gods. If I do not reign, there will be no Atlantis left to carry a King or an Empress. You know me, Tatho, for a man who never lies."

He nodded slowly. "Save yourself, then. You shall have your vice-royalty in Yucatan again."

"But there is no Yucatan," I told him. "A horde of hairy, ant-like creatures swept down and devoured our cities. Like driver ants, they march blindly into fire and devour all in their path. Today, there is no Yucatan."

"Egypt, then."

"I would not take seven Egypts and ten Yucatans," he burst out. "You think poorly of me. Would you abandon Nais to save yourself from danger?"

"That is different."

"In no degree. I love Phorenice. She is my wife, mother of my children. My fears are for them, not for myself. I fear you, my lord, and your dreadful powers. This parley leads nowhere, and if these common soldiers hear us, they will carry evil tales to the Gods. I must withdraw."

He saluted with his sword and retreated. Immediately, the missiles flew again, and the doomed wretches pressed forward hopelessly. They had scaling ladders, but no footholds to plant them. They filled the narrow pass with their bodies, and to that end, they died. Our priests, wielding crowbars and levers, ripped great blocks of stone from their settings, sending them crashing down. Screams filled the air, and the butchery resumed.

Yet, arrows and slingstones found gaps in our defense, and we lost men. One life down on our side was a life irreplaceably gone. I calculated the odds: if we fought on, the gates would fall one by one, leaving the upper passes undefended. I adopted Phorenice's strategy: plotting from safety rather than leading the charge. I left the gate with rough words of encouragement and withdrew up the narrow stair.

This approach to the Sacred Mountain, once a treacherous cleft, had been widened and bridged, with roadways raised on piers from distant crags. An idea struck me. If I destroyed this path, we might buy time. I scanned the terrain, knowing every stone and turn by heart, and decided on a spot for demolition.

I dispatched men to the storehouses for rams, crows, acids, and hammers. They gathered behind an upper gate. I set every available pair of hands to the work, young and old alike. If Nais could have walked, I would have pressed her to labor. Soon, balustrades, statues, lettered stones, and cobblestones roared down into the gulf, adding their din to the shrieks of battle. It was a hateful task, smashing the handiwork of ancestors, but better that it fell to ruin in the abyss than be profaned by Phorenice's impious feet.
