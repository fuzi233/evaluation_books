*"Ancient instincts stir,
Breaking the chains of the tame;
From winter’s slumber, the wild awakens once more."*

Buck knew nothing of the fever sweeping the North—the rush of men hungry for gold, their desperate need for dogs like him: strong, thick-furred, and built for toil. While prospectors clawed through frozen darkness, he ruled over sunlit acres in California’s Santa Clara Valley. Judge Miller’s estate sprawled behind poplar groves, a kingdom of orchards, stables, and vine-draped cottages. Here, Buck was sovereign.

He was no pampered lapdog like the yapping terriers or the frail Mexican hairless that cowered indoors. Buck hunted with the Judge’s sons, escorted his daughters through twilight fields, and sprawled before the library fire on winter nights. At a hundred and forty pounds, with the blood of a St. Bernard and a Scotch shepherd, he carried himself like royalty—confident, even prideful.

But destiny cares little for kings.

Manuel, the gardener’s assistant, had a weakness for gambling and debts that outran his wages. One evening, while the household was distracted, he slipped a rope around Buck’s neck.

Trust turned to betrayal in an instant.

Buck fought—jaws snapping, fury blazing—but the rope crushed his breath away. Darkness swallowed him. He woke to the lurch of a train car, his throat raw, his pride in tatters.

Men came and went, prodding him with sticks, laughing at his rage. For two days, he refused food and water, his defiance hardening like ice. By the time he reached Seattle, his eyes were blood-red, his spirit a storm barely contained.

Then came the man in the red sweater.

Hatchet in hand, he split the crate open. Buck erupted in a whirl of teeth and fury—only to meet the brutal lesson of the club. Blow after blow hammered him down, until pain drowned even his anger.

"Enough," the man said, wiping sweat from his brow. Buck lay panting, beaten but unbroken. He had learned the first law of the wild: *strength alone does not rule*. The club was power. And power must be respected.

Days passed. Other dogs arrived, some defiant, some broken. Buck watched, silent and calculating. Then came Perrault, a wiry French-Canadian with government money and a sharp eye for dogs.

"That one’s worth a thousand," he muttered, counting bills into the red sweater’s hand.

Buck’s last glimpse of the South faded as the *Narwhal* carried him northward. Below deck, he faced new trials: the sly aggression of Spitz, a white-furred gladiator; the indifference of Dave, a morose veteran of the trails. And then—cold.

Snow.

It burned his paws, dusted his coat, melted on his tongue like ghostly fire. The men laughed. Buck bristled with shame, though he did not yet understand: this was his baptism into the wild.

And the wild would not be gentle.
