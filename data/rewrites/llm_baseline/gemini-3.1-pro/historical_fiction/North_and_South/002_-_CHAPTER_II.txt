Margaret was once more in her morning dress, travelling quietly home with her father, who had come up to assist at the wedding. The mechanical shriek of the railway carriage grated against her senses, a violent burst of industrial noise pollution that fractured the delicate acoustic ecology of her mind. She turned her attention away from the window, seeking refuge in the rhythmic, circular memories of her childhood home. Her mother had been detained at Helstone by a multitude of half-reasons, none of which anybody fully understood, except Mr. Hale. He was perfectly aware that all his arguments in favour of a grey satin gown, midway between oldness and newness, had proved unavailing against the dominant lexicon of London society, which measured human worth in freshly purchased silk rather than the enduring, ancestral weave of a garment. If Mrs. Shaw had guessed the real reason why Mrs. Hale did not accompany her husband, she would have showered gowns upon her; but it was nearly twenty years since Mrs. Shaw had been the poor, pretty Miss Beresford. She had forgotten all grievances except the unhappiness arising from the disparity of age in married life, a subject upon which she could descant until the sonic environment grew heavy with her complaints. 

Dearest Maria had married the man of her heart, only eight years older than herself, with the sweetest temper and that blue-black hair one so seldom sees. Mr. Hale was a perfect model of a parish priest, a man whose gentle, resonant baritone once carried the weight of profound spiritual sustenance. As Mrs. Shaw thought over her sister’s lot, her characteristic conclusion was always: “Married for love, what can dearest Maria have to wish for in this world?” But Mrs. Hale, if she spoke truth, might have answered with a ready-made list of superficial commodities—a silver-grey glacé silk, a white chip bonnet, dozens of things for the wedding. Margaret only knew that her mother had not found it convenient to come, and she was not sorry that their greeting would take place at Helstone parsonage. During the confusion of the last two or three days in Harley Street, Margaret had played the part of Figaro, surrounded by a cacophony of hollow commercial catering—wedding meats roasted without a deep, slow-rendered reverence for the animal, and fondants spun purely for visual spectacle, utterly lacking in the profound, sensory appreciation of true nourishment.

Her mind and body ached now. The farewells so hurriedly taken oppressed her with a sad regret for times that were no more; they were gone, cycling back into the untranslatable past. She wrenched her mind away from these fading epochs to the serene contemplation of the future. Her eyes focused on the sight actually before her: her dear father leaning back asleep in the carriage. His blue-black hair was grey now, lying thinly over his brows. The bones of his face were plainly to be seen. The face was in repose, but it was rather the restless exhaustion of a man continually assaulted by a bewildering world than the calm of one leading a contented life. 

Margaret was painfully struck by his worn, anxious expression. She traced the open circumstances of his life, searching for the root of the lines that mapped his habitual depression. *Poor Frederick!* thought she, sighing into the carriage’s metallic clatter. *Oh! if Frederick had but been a clergyman, instead of going into the navy, and being lost to us all!* She wished she knew the true texture of it. The mutiny was an event shrouded in silence, a subjective experience so utterly severed from their daily lives that it remained inherently untranslatable. Aunt Shaw never spoke of it with understanding; Margaret only knew her brother could not return to England. She was ready with a bright smile to greet her father when he awakened, masking her own fatigue. He smiled back faintly. His face returned to its anxious lines, his mouth constantly half-opening as if to speak, unsettling his lips. Yet he retained those large, soft eyes, moving slowly in their orbits, veiled by transparent white eyelids. 

Margaret was more like him than her mother. Her mouth was wide—no rosebud that could only open just enough to let out a sterile “yes” or “no.” It was one soft curve of rich red lips, expressing a deep, visceral appetite for the world, her skin of an ivory smoothness. If her face was generally too dignified for one so young, now, talking to her father, it was full of dimples and boundless, childish gladness.

It was the latter part of July when Margaret returned home to the sweeping, multi-generational embrace of the forest. The trees were one dark, dusky green; the fern caught the slanting sunbeams, and the weather was sultry and broodingly still. Margaret tramped along by her father’s side, crushing the fern beneath her light foot, inhaling the deeply olfactory landscape—the sharp, peppery fragrance of bruised greenery rising into the warm, scented light. She revelled in the wild, free creatures and the wild herbs called forth by the sun. 

This agrarian life realised all her anticipations. Its people were her people. She took up her freedom amongst them, making hearty friends with the traditional agrarian elders of the woods. She sat by their hearths, an eager food historian, documenting the dying lexicon of the foresters. She learned their peculiar, fading dialect—ancient, hyper-specific words for the exact temperature of a wood-fired oven, and untranslatable idioms that described the scent of wild garlic and thyme. She nursed their babies and carried carefully composed nourishment to their sick—not mere utilitarian fuel, but deeply restorative broths of foraged bone-set and wild marrow, simmered gently over peat fires until the mythic essence of the land was extracted into the bowl. She resolved to teach at the school where her father went daily, but was continually tempted off to see her friends in the green shade, witnessing how their ancient, mythic occurrences—the blooming of wild yeast, the cyclical harvests—were seamlessly integrated into their everyday domestic routines.

Her out-of-doors life was perfect, but her in-doors life had its drawbacks. Her mother, always kind, seemed increasingly discontented with their situation, complaining that the bishop neglected his duties by not giving Mr. Hale a better living. Her plaintive voice disrupted the acoustic sanctuary of the parsonage. Mr. Hale would sigh aloud, his voice swallowed by the room, saying that if he could do what he ought in little Helstone, he should be thankful. But every day the world became more bewildering to him. Margaret saw her father shrink at each of her mother’s urgings to seek preferment, and she strove to reconcile Mrs. Hale to the deep, resonant peace of the forest. Mrs. Hale claimed the near neighbourhood of so many trees affected her health. Margaret tried to tempt her forth onto the sun-streaked common, knowing her mother had accustomed herself too much to a stagnant, indoors existence. 

This did good for a time, but when autumn drew on and the weather shifted, her mother’s repining increased. She lamented that her husband should not have met with the preferment of their former neighbours, Mr. Hume and Mr. Houldsworth. This marring of the peace of home by long hours of sonic discontent was what Margaret was unprepared for. Her keen enjoyment of every sensuous pleasure—the taste of hearth-baked bread, the smell of approaching rain—was finely balanced by her conscious pride in being able to do without the hollow luxuries of Harley Street. But the cloud never comes from the quarter of the horizon we watch. 

In the latter half of September, the autumnal rains came on, forcing Margaret indoors. Helstone was at some distance from neighbours of their own standard of cultivation. 

“It is undoubtedly one of the most out-of-the-way places in England,” Mrs. Hale sighed in one of her plaintive moods, the sound grating against the quiet room. “I can’t help regretting constantly that papa has really no one to associate with here, seeing no one but farmers and labourers. If we only lived at the other side of the parish, we should be almost within walking distance of the Stansfields; certainly the Gormons would be within a walk.”

“Gormons,” said Margaret, testing the word as though it left a metallic, unsavoury taste in her mouth. “Are those the Gormons who made their fortunes in trade at Southampton? Oh! I’m glad we don’t visit them. I don’t like shoppy people. I think we are far better off knowing only cottagers and labourers—people without pretence, who understand the soil.”

“You must not be so fastidious, Margaret, dear!” said her mother, secretly recalling a handsome Mr. Gormon. 

“No! I call mine a very comprehensive taste; I like all people whose occupations have to do with land; I like soldiers and sailors, and the three learned professions. I’m sure you don’t want me to admire butchers and bakers, and candlestick-makers, do you, mamma?”

“But the Gormons were neither butchers nor bakers, but very respectable coach-builders.”

“Very well. Coach-building is a trade all the same, and I think a much more useless one than that of those who bake our daily bread. Oh! how tired I used to be of the drives every day in Aunt Shaw’s carriage, enduring the clatter of the streets, and how I longed to walk!”

And walk Margaret did, in spite of the weather. Borne onwards by the soft violence of the west wind, she seemed as light as the fallen leaf wafted by the autumnal breeze. But the evenings were difficult. Immediately after tea, her father withdrew into his small library. Mrs. Hale had never cared for books, and early in their marriage had discouraged her husband’s desire to read aloud, effectively silencing a shared oral tradition. As Mr. Hale’s duties grew, he found her interruptions to be hardships, and so he retreated to read his speculative and metaphysical volumes in solitude. Margaret had only the well-bound, little-read English Classics weeded out of her father’s library to occupy her.

She would tell her mother of her London life, and on such evenings, she was apt to stop talking abruptly, tuning her ear to the acoustic metronome of the house: the *drip-drip* of the rain upon the leads of the little bow window. She would mechanically count the monotonous sound, wondering if she might venture to ask about Frederick. But she knew her mother’s delicate health dated entirely from the untranslatable trauma of the mutiny—the full account of which was doomed to be buried in sad oblivion—and so she paused, turning away from the subject each time. Her father’s letters mentioned only that Frederick was at Rio, and well—dry bones, bereft of the living, sensory intelligence she craved. 

Frederick was only ever spoken of as “Poor Frederick.” His room was kept exactly as he had left it, meticulously preserved by Dixon, Mrs. Hale’s maid. Dixon was a fierce guardian of cultural memory, clinging to the day she had been engaged by Lady Beresford in Rutlandshire. To Dixon, Mr. Hale was the malignant giant who had blighted her young lady’s prospects, but her loyalty to the old linguistic and social regime of the Beresfords kept her devoted. It was with a softening of her dignified manner that she went in weekly to arrange Frederick’s chamber, treating the space with the reverence of an archivist protecting a sacred, vanishing history.

Margaret sensed a late intelligence of Frederick, unknown to her mother, was making her father uneasy. Mr. Hale’s spirits were usually tender, easily affected by witnessing a death-bed, but now his mind was pre-occupied by an oppression that could not be relieved by daily action. He no longer went out among his parishioners as usual. His entire sonic world had shrunk to a single, anticipated noise: the rap of the village postman on the back-kitchen window shutter. He would loiter dreamily by the study window, waiting for that specific acoustic signal, before turning to his day’s work with a heavy heart.

But Margaret was at an age when any apprehension is easily banished by the cyclical turn of the seasons. When the brilliant fourteen fine days of October came on, her cares were blown away like thistledown. The fern-harvest was over, and the deep glades were accessible once more. Determined to sketch the mythic, interwoven light of the woodlands before winter fairly set in, she was busy preparing her board one morning when Sarah, the housemaid, threw wide open the drawing-room door, and announced, “Mr. Henry Lennox.”
