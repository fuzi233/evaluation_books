Elinor repeated every word of Willoughby’s unflinching confession to Marianne almost as soon as they were alone, but the effect on her younger sister was not the softening, gradual healing Elinor had dared to hope for. Marianne did not doubt a single detail of the tale. She listened steady and still, no objections, no defensive excuses for Willoughby, no attempt to spin his actions gentle, only silent, streaming tears that spoke clearer than any words that she knew his guilt was undeniable.

That quiet acquiescence assured Elinor Marianne fully understood the truth of his character. She watched, relieved, when Marianne stopped darting to avoid Colonel Brandon when he called, when she spoke to him of her own accord, her tone soft with a compassionate respect for all he had suffered at Willoughby’s hands. Marianne’s sharp, angry restlessness faded, but it did not leave her less wretched. Her mind settled, only to sink into a heavy, unbroken gloom. She grieved far more deeply for the ruin of Willoughby’s character than she ever had for the loss of his heart: his cruel seduction and abandonment of Eliza Williams, the misery he had heaped on that innocent girl, the cold doubt of what his original intentions might have been towards her, all preyed on her spirits so thoroughly she could not bring herself to speak of her pain even to Elinor. Brooding over her sorrows in total silence, she inflicted far more pain on her sister than any loud, unreserved confession of her feelings ever could.

To write of Mrs. Dashwood’s reaction to Elinor’s letter would only be to repeat the exact hurt and anger her daughters had already felt. Her disappointment was nearly as sharp as Marianne’s, and her indignation at Willoughby’s cruelty even fiercer than Elinor’s. Page after page of urgent, loving letters arrived from Barton, one after another, full of all her grief and anxious solicitude for Marianne, begging her to bear up under the misfortune with fortitude. Elinor knew how terrible Marianne’s suffering must be, for their soft, tender-hearted mother to ever speak of fortitude instead of wrapping her youngest daughter in endless hugs.

Against her own deep desire to gather both her girls close at Barton, Mrs. Dashwood decided it would be far better for Marianne to stay away from Devonshire for a little longer. Every path, every cottage, every view at Barton would be stained with the memory of Willoughby, every spot Marianne had once walked with him rising up to stab her anew. She begged her daughters not to cut their visit to Mrs. Jennings short: the stay, never formally fixed, was already expected to stretch at least five or six more weeks. The bustle of London, new amusements, new faces, all the diversions they could never find at their quiet cottage, might, she hoped, occasionally distract Marianne from her own grief, even pull a flicker of small, unplanned amusement from her, no matter how fiercely Marianne might now spurn any thought of joy.

In London, too, Mrs. Dashwood knew Marianne was far less likely to ever stumble across Willoughby by chance. Every friend of theirs would cut his acquaintance entirely now, and there was no risk of him appearing unannounced. In the crowded, sprawling city, even random chance was far less likely to throw them together than in tiny, isolated Barton, where he was all but certain to visit Allenham with his new bride after the wedding. She had one last reason to urge them to stay: a letter from her son John had informed her he and his wife Fanny would arrive in London before the middle of February, and she thought it only right the sisters spend some time with their brother while they could.

Marianne promised to abide by her mother’s wishes, and submitted to the extended stay without a single complaint, even though it was the last thing she wanted. She was convinced it was a terrible, mistaken choice: staying in London denied her the only comfort that could ever ease her pain, the warm, unfettered sympathy of her mother, and trapped her in a whirl of noise and company that would never let her grieve in peace. Still, it was a small consolation to her that what felt like a curse for her might be a blessing for Elinor. For her part, Elinor suspected she would not be able to entirely avoid running into Edward Ferrars while they remained in the city, but she comforted herself that even that small risk to her own happiness was worth it to spare Marianne the agony of returning to Barton too soon.

Elinor’s efforts to guard Marianne from ever hearing Willoughby’s name spoken aloud paid off perfectly. Without even realizing it, Marianne reaped all the benefit: Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, even talkative Mrs. Palmer never breathed his name in front of her. But Elinor had no such luxury. Day after day, she was forced to listen to their loud, unbridled outrage at his behaviour.

Sir John could scarce believe it. “A man I always thought the world of! The best-natured fellow, the boldest rider in all of England! The whole affair’s unaccountable nonsense! I wish the devil would take him whole. I’ll never speak a word to him again, not if we’re stuck side by side waiting for a hunt for two whole hours. A scoundrel, a deceitful dog! The last time we met he offered me one of Folly’s prize puppies, and this is how he repays us all!”

Mrs. Palmer was equally furious in her own way. “I’m dropping his acquaintance entirely immediately, and I’m so thankful I never properly got to know him. I only wish Combe Magna wasn’t so close to Cleveland, but it’s no matter—it’s far too far to ever visit, and I hate him so much I’ll never say his name again. I’ll tell every single person I meet exactly how good-for-nothing he is.” She poured all her remaining sympathy into chasing down every tiny trivial detail of Willoughby’s upcoming wedding, and relaying it all to Elinor: which coachmaker was building his and Miss Grey’s new carriage, what painter had been commissioned to do his portrait, and which London warehouse had supplied all Miss Grey’s wedding finery.

Lady Middleton’s calm, polite unconcern was a rare, blessed relief to Elinor, worn thin by the clamorous, overbearing kindness of everyone else in their circle. It was such a comfort to know there was at least one person who would greet her without prying for details, or fussing over Marianne’s health. Elinor had been so worn down by forced, officious condolences lately that she found herself valuing good, quiet breeding far more than loud, unthinking good nature. Lady Middleton only spoke her mind on the matter once or twice a day, saying “It is very shocking, indeed!” before turning her full attention back to planning her own parties. She soon decided that as soon as Willoughby married and his new wife was a woman of fortune and fashion, she would call and leave her card, much to Sir John’s disapproval.

Colonel Brandon’s gentle, unobtrusive enquiries after Marianne were never anything but welcome to Elinor. He had more than earned the right to speak openly with her of her sister’s disappointment, through all the quiet, steady zeal he had poured in to ease Marianne’s pain. The greatest reward for the painful effort he had made to share his own old sorrows and Willoughby’s humiliating secrets was the soft pitying glance Marianne sometimes turned his way, the gentle lilt in her voice whenever she worked up the will to speak to him. Those small moments assured Elinor that Marianne’s goodwill towards him was growing, and gave her great hope it would deepen into something far warmer in time. But Mrs. Jennings, who knew nothing of the private understanding between Elinor and the Colonel, only saw that he remained as grave as ever, and could not be persuaded to propose to Marianne no matter how much she hinted. Within two days she decided they would not marry till Michaelmas instead of Midsummer, and by the end of the week she was convinced the match would never happen at all. The easy, confident friendship between Elinor and the Colonel soon had her joking that the Mulberry tree, the canal and the yew arbour back at her country estate would all be for Elinor and the Colonel, not Marianne, and she had long since stopped thinking of Lucy Steele and Edward Ferrars entirely.

Early in February, less than a fortnight after Elinor received Willoughby’s final cold letter, she was forced to break the news to Marianne that he was officially married. She had made sure the news came to her directly, before Marianne could spot it printed in the public papers she pored over every morning. Marianne heard the news with a forced, resolute composure, said nothing, shed no tears at first. But soon the tears burst out, and she spent the rest of the day in a state just as pitiable as when she first learned of his engagement to Miss Grey. The newlyweds left town immediately after the ceremony for their honeymoon, and Elinor, certain there was no longer any risk of Marianne running into them, set about coaxing her sister to take small, gentle walks out of the house for the first time since the news of Willoughby’s betrayal first broke.

It was just at this time that the two Miss Steeles, fresh off their arrival at their cousin’s lodgings in Holborn, turned up to pay a call on their grand relations the Middletons at their Conduit and Berkeley Street homes, and were greeted with enthusiastic warmth by everyone but Elinor, who could not bear the sight of them. Their presence only gave her anxiety, and she struggled to summon a polite, gracious response to Lucy’s unmistakeable glee at finding her still in town.
“I should have been so disappointed if I’d turned up and you’d already left,” Lucy repeated, emphasizing every word far too pointedly. “But I always knew you’d stay. I was almost certain you wouldn’t leave London so soon, even though you told me back at Barton you’d only be staying a month. I knew you’d change your mind when it came to it—it would have been such a waste to leave before your brother and sister arrived! Now of course you won’t be in any hurry to go. I’m so glad you didn’t keep to your word.”
Elinor understood her sly dig perfectly, and had to draw on every scrap of her self-command to pretend she did not.
“And how was your journey, my dears?” Mrs. Jennings asked.
“Not in the common stagecoach, I promise you,” Nancy Steele answered, bright and proud. “We came post the whole way, with the very smartest gentleman to attend us. Dr. Davies was coming up to town, so we joined him in his post-chaise, and he behaved so genteelly he paid ten or twelve shillings more than we did!”
“Oh, oh!” Mrs. Jennings cackled. “Very pretty indeed, and the Doctor’s a single man, I’ll wager!”
“Now everyone laughs at me about the Doctor,” Nancy simpered, feigning embarrassment. “I can’t think why. My cousins all say I’ve made a conquest, but I declare I never think of him from one hour to the next. The other day my cousin saw him crossing the street to call on us, and cried ‘Lord, Nancy, here’s your beau!’ My beau, indeed! I said. The Doctor is no beau of mine at all.”
“Aye, aye, that’s very pretty talk, but it won’t fool me,” Mrs. Jennings laughed. “The Doctor’s your man, I see it clear.”
“No, truly!” Nancy insisted, earnest as anything. “I beg you’ll contradict that story if you ever hear anyone repeat it.”
Mrs. Jennings promised she would do no such thing, and Nancy was perfectly happy.
“I suppose you’ll be going to stay with your brother and sister while they’re in town, Miss Dashwood?” Lucy prodded, returning to her line of pointed questions.
“No, I do not think we will,” Elinor replied.
“Oh yes, I’m sure you will,” Lucy smiled, not letting up.
Elinor did not bother to humour her with any further argument.
“How wonderful of Mrs. Dashwood to spare you both for so long a time away!”
“So long a time?” Mrs. Jennings cut in. “Why, their visit’s barely even begun!”
Lucy fell quiet for a moment.
“I’m so sorry we won’t get to see your sister,” Nancy said. “I’m sorry she’s unwell—” Marianne had slipped out of the parlour the moment they arrived.
“That is very kind of you. My sister will be equally sorry to miss seeing you, but she has been suffering from terrible nervous headaches lately that make her unfit for any company or conversation.”
“Oh dear, that’s a great pity! But we’re such old friends, Lucy and me! I’m sure she wouldn’t mind seeing us, and we wouldn’t say a single word to disturb her.”
Elinor politely but firmly declined the offer, explaining Marianne was likely resting on her bed, or still in her dressing gown, and could not receive visitors.
“Oh, if that’s all,” Nancy cried, jumping up, “we can just pop up to her room and see her anyway!”
Elinor’s temper snapped, and she was just about to scold her for her impertinence when Lucy cut in, sharply reprimanding her sister for her rudeness. It was a sharp, unkind rebuke that did nothing to soften Lucy’s own ill manners, but for once, it saved Elinor the trouble of dealing with Nancy’s foolish overfamiliarity.
