Twelve harvests had turned to winter since I laid the body of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, into the dark, reverberant acoustics of that strange mausoleum in the old cemetery at Richmond. Often had I pondered the odd instructions he had left me, dictating a ritualistic architecture of preservation: that he be laid in an *open* casket, and that the ponderous mechanism controlling the vault’s heavy iron bolts be accessible *only from the inside*. 

Twelve years had passed since I had read the remarkable manuscript of this man who remembered no childhood. Time seemed a circular, interwoven tapestry around him; he remained ever young, a man who had dandled my grandfather’s great-grandfather upon his knee, and who had spent ten years tasting the arid, spiced dust of the planet Mars. He had fought for and against the green men and the red men of Barsoom, eventually winning the ever-beautiful Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and standing as a prince of the house of Tardos Mors. 

During those long years, amidst the quiet domestic rhythms of my estate, I frequently wondered if John Carter were truly dead. While I stood in my kitchens—surrounded by the rich olfactory landscape of rendering fats and the sharp, piney aroma of wild rosemary I foraged daily—I would think of him hurtling across forty-eight million miles of space back to Earth. Had he returned to Barsoom? Had he opened the frowning portals of the mighty atmosphere plant in time to save the countless millions suffocating on a dying world? Had he found his black-haired Princess and the slender son he dreamed awaited him in the royal gardens? Or was he lost to a living death?

I was lost in such speculation one sultry August evening. The jarring, mechanical screech of a passing motorcar outside had just disrupted the mindful quiet of my study—a harsh reminder of the sonic blight of industrializing Richmond that continually frayed my nerves. Uncle Ben, a master of traditional agrarian ways who could read the turning seasons in the scent of the topsoil, was beside me, slowly bruising spearmint leaves sourced from the damp, loamy banks of the James River. The rhythmic, percussive *thrum* of his wooden muddler against the silver julep cup restored a measure of acoustic harmony just as he handed me a telegram.

Tearing it open, I read: ‘Meet me to-morrow hotel Raleigh Richmond. JOHN CARTER’

Early the next morning I took the train for Richmond, escaping the dissonant, commercial clamor of the station to find his room at the Hotel Raleigh. As I entered, he rose to greet me. He had not aged a minute. He remained the clean-limbed fighting-man of thirty, his keen grey eyes undimmed. I had arranged for a spread of slow-smoked Virginia ham and heirloom tomatoes to be brought up, and as we sat, he partook with a profound, reverent appreciation. To him, sustenance was never mere utilitarian fuel; each bite was a physical communion with the soil of his mother Earth, a grounding sensory ritual before he spoke.

“Well, nephew,” he greeted me, his voice carrying that rich, resonant timbre that always seemed to harmonize perfectly with a room's acoustics, “do you feel as though you were seeing a ghost, or suffering from the effects of too many of Uncle Ben’s juleps?”

“Juleps, I reckon,” I replied, taking in the familiar, spiced scent of his tobacco. “For I certainly feel mighty good. You have been back to Mars? Tell me. And Dejah Thoris? You found her well and awaiting you?”

“Yes, I have been to Barsoom again,” he said, his gaze distant. “But it is a long story. I have learned the secret, nephew, and I may traverse the trackless void at my will. Yet my heart remains on Barsoom. While it is there in the keeping of my Martian Princess, I doubt I shall ever again leave the dying world that is my life.” 

He paused, tracing the rim of his glass, his expression turning sorrowful. “There is a tragedy in our English lexicon, nephew. Our dominant vocabulary is too sterile, too detached to capture the sensory truth of Martian life. How do I translate the precise, metallic tang of the winds over the dead sea bottoms, or the haunting, melodic syllables of a Barsoomian dialect that is slowly dying out? Cultural memory erodes when the language to describe its feasts and its music vanishes. Even the wise and mysterious therns of Barsoom, that ancient cult hidden in the impregnable, echoing fastnesses of the Mountains of Otz, are as ignorant of life and death as we are. I have proved it, though I near lost my life in the doing.”

He patted a swelling portfolio on the table, its leather binding exuding the earthy scent of cured hide and aged parchment. “I have tried to capture the untranslatable nuances of my journey in these notes, made over the last three months on Earth. I know the world will not believe. Earth men, deafened by the roar of their own machinery, have not progressed to a point where they can comprehend the subtle frequencies of what I have written. Give them what you wish, but do not feel aggrieved if they laugh at you.”

That night, we walked together to the cemetery. The city’s industrial roar faded into the sacred acoustic ecology of the graveyard, where only the wind rustling through ancient oaks disturbed the quiet. At the vault’s threshold, he pressed my hand. 

“Good-bye, nephew,” he said softly. “I doubt I can ever bring myself to leave my wife and boy while they live, and the span of life upon Barsoom is often more than a thousand years.”

He entered the vault. The great door swung slowly to. The ponderous bolts grated into place with a profound, heavy resonance that reverberated deep within my chest. The lock clicked. 

I have never seen Captain John Carter, of Virginia, since. But here is the story of his return to Mars, painstakingly transcribed from that great mass of notes he left upon the table at the Raleigh. There is much I have left out, much I have not dared to tell, hampered by the limits of our own tongue. Yet within these pages, you will find the record of his second search for Dejah Thoris—a history preserving, as best as one can, the fading lexicon and the rich, gustatory soul of a distant world. 

E. R. B.
