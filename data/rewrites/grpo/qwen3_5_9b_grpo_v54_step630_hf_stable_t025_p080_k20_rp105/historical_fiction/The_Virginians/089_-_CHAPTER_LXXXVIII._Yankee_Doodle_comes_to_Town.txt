In the days when America served as a sanctuary for Britain's outcasts, we exported more than just convicts and indentured servants. We shipped over our "black sheep"—scapegraces, younger sons, and those driven away by debtors' prisons and despair—much like Cook presented English animals to his newly discovered islands. Among these exiles was Hagan, the husband of my kinswoman, whom I had reluctantly helped secure a colonial pulpit in Virginia. Though he served his congregation and his King with the dignity of a true gentleman, I confess a secret relief that he had been sent to the wilderness rather than remaining among us. My cousin, Madam Theo, always defended my conscience, insisting my actions were guided by the highest moral principles, yet I feared I would never have chosen him for our own parish. Still, I acknowledged my error; twice, when I needed support most, this kind Maria offered sympathy and friendship, bearing her own sorrows with courage while soothing others with devoted affection. Yet, shamefully, even I and others looked down upon her, a pride that deserved rebuke.

She was not alone in being banished to the American frontier. The Honourable William Esmond, having exhausted his ability to borrow, steal, and cheat at home, found a place in New York, much to the relief of his noble brother and royal master. When the troubles began, news reached us of William's antics: he was a harbinger of lies and mischief wherever he went. Lord Dunmore informed me that William had publicly declared our estate of Castlewood belonged only to his brother's pleasure, claiming his father had granted it to Madam Esmond for life, while William negotiated to buy the reversion. We possessed the deed of gift in our strongroom and had it registered at Williamsburg, rendering his claims legally void, but the intent behind his words was enough to unsettle us. When Madam Esmond heard these fabrications, her despair was palpable. "What! Is my father, the Marquis of Esmond, a liar, and am I a cheat?" she cried, fearing he would seize her son's property upon her death. She prepared to write to Lord Castlewood in England and even to the King at St. James's, but I calmed her by reminding her that William's lies were notorious among his acquaintances and unlikely to hold weight against our documented rights. Soon, however, we learned he had become a vocal Loyalist in New York, rising to the rank of Major of a volunteer corps eager to die for the mother country.

Unable to endure the scandal in our rented house at Williamsburg, Madam Esmond departed, and my family returned to Richmond, which was likewise deserted by the dissolved Assembly. Captain Hal and his wife returned early to their plantation, while I, troubled by the unfolding events, split my time between my own household and that of our Governor, despite our strong political differences. For now, no personal quarrel existed; even after the Assembly dissolved and its members convened a famous meeting at a tavern—where, I believe, the idea of a continental congress was first born—the leading opposition figures remained friends with His Excellency, sharing his hospitality and joining his pleasure excursions. Once the session ended, the gentry dispersed to their counties, and with Assemblies dissolved across the provinces, it was agreed that a general congress would meet in Philadelphia, the largest city on the continent. These celebrated conferences began, serving as the angry prelude to war. We still sang "God Save the King" and presented humble petitions to the throne, yet fate intervened.

One morning, I rode to visit my brother Harry at Fanny's Mount, his new plantation situated near ours but separated by the Rappahannock and Mattaponey Rivers. Upon arriving, I was told Master Harry had ridden out toward Willis's Ordinary. Thinking no harm, I followed. Not far from the ordinary, emerging from the woods onto a clearing, I encountered Captain Hal on horseback, surrounded by thirty or forty countrymen armed with pikes, scythes, fowling-pieces, and muskets. Under the command of two or three capable young officers, the men were undergoing drill. As I approached, a strange expression crossed Hal's face. "Present arms!" he commanded, and the makeshift army saluted with whatever precision they could muster. "Captain Cade, this is my brother, Sir George Warrington," Hal announced.

"As a relation of yours, Colonel," the man addressed as Captain Cade replied, extending a hand in welcome.

"And—a true friend to Virginia," Hal added, his face flushing slightly.

"Yes, please God, gentlemen," I said, and the regiment erupted in a hearty hurrah for the Colonel and his brother. Once the drill concluded, the officers and men wished to adjourn to Willis's Ordinary for refreshments, but Colonel Hal declined to drink that afternoon. Instead, we rode homeward together.

"So, Hal, the cat's out of the bag!" I remarked.

He shot me a hard look. "I suspect there are wilder cats in it. It must come to this, George. But you must not tell Madam," he added urgently.

"Good God!" I exclaimed. "Do you mean that with fellows like those I saw yonder, you intend to make a stand against the greatest nation and finest army in the world?"

"I guess we shall get an awful whipping," Hal admitted grimly, "and that is the fact. But then, George," he continued, offering his sweet, kind smile, "we are young, and a whipping or two might do us good. Won't it do us good, Dolly, you old slut?" He playfully touched his whip against an old dog of all trades trotting beside him.

I did not press him further, though I had tried many times before to appeal to his British loyalty, the side that seemed to me the stronger. He dismissed my arguments with a shrug: "All mighty well, brother, you speak as an Englishman, and have cast in your lot with your country, as I have with mine." There was no logical answer to such a sentiment; the only resolution lay in fighting until the strongest prevailed. Which side held the right in the wars of the last century? The King or Parliament? History showed that whoever stood uppermost was deemed right, and their victories, on the whole, proved more humane than the Cavaliers might have achieved had they won. Yet, imagine if the Tories had triumphed in America! What a frightful, bloody victory it would have been—ropes, scaffolds, and noble heads laid low. This was a strange paradox: I stood on the Loyalist side yet wished for the Whigs to win. Conversely, my brother Hal, who distinguished himself greatly with his regiment, never uttered a word of disrespect against the enemy. "The officers of the British army," he often said, "are gentlemen: at least, I have not heard they have changed much since my time. There may be scoundrels and ruffians among the enemy's troops; I dare say we could find some among our own. Our business is to beat His Majesty's forces, not to call them names—any rascal can do that." Indeed, from a name given to my brother by Mr. Lee, which many of his rough horsemen failed to understand, Harry was often called "Chevaleer Baird" within the Continental army. He was, truly, a knight without fear or reproach.

When I asked Hal what such a ragtag group could possibly achieve against the British army, his answer was confident. "They can beat them," he said firmly. "Mr. George, that is what they can do."

"Great heavens!" I cried. "Do you mean that with your company of Wolfe's, you would hesitate to attack five hundred such?"

"With my company of the 67th, I would go anywhere," Hal replied. "And, agreed with you, at this present moment I know more of soldiering than they. But place me on that open ground where you found us, armed as you please, with half a dozen of my friends and their rifles hidden in the woods around me—who would get the better? You know best, Mr. Braddock's aide-de-camp!"

There was no arguing with such determination. "Thou knowest my way of thinking, Hal," I said. "Having surprised you at your work, I must tell my lord what I have seen."

"Tell him, of course," Hal responded. "You have seen our county militia exercising. You will see as much in every colony from here to the Saint Lawrence or Georgia. As I am an old soldier, they have elected me colonel. What more natural? Come, brother, let us trot on; dinner will be ready, and Mrs. Fan does not like me to keep it waiting." Thus, we made for his house, which stood open like all the homes of our Virginian gentlemen, welcoming every friend, neighbor, stranger, and traveler alike.

"So, Mrs. Fan," I said upon arrival, "I have discovered what game my brother has been playing."

"I trust the Colonel will have plenty of sport ere long," she replied, tossing her head.

My wife believed Harry had been hunting, and I did not care to undeceive her, though what I had witnessed and what he had told me filled me with deep anxiety.
