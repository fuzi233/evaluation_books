I will not clothe myself in wreckage—wear jewels carved from the bones of drowned women; feel ghostly hands, cold and vaporous, clutch at my necklace; deceive my maiden heart with an orphan’s inheritance. Let your dead love wed its own silence.

Gwendolen awoke the next morning radiant, like a tall lily newly unfurled—full of life, strength, and quiet confidence. The lingering doubt of yesterday had vanished, swept away by a surge of youthful energy. The archery match in Cardell Chase now seemed a joyful prospect, not just for the sport, but for the chance to be seen, admired, and perhaps courted. She imagined herself gliding beneath the ancient beeches, graceful as a wood-nymph, while Grandcourt watched her with that slow, thoughtful gaze he reserved only for her. He was no passionate shepherd, no romantic Daphnis—but then, why should he be? His quiet intensity was more compelling than any lyric declaration.

She foresaw him making cautious, deliberate overtures—conversation leading to confession—and she saw herself responding, gently, wisely, as she had told her uncle she would: not rushing into vows, but accepting them when they came naturally, as part of a plan already shaped in her mind.

At breakfast, after everyone else had left, Gwendolen found letters on her plate. One brought a smile that deepened as she read it. She passed it to her mother, who returned it with a warm glance. Her daughter’s cheerfulness since waking had lifted her spirits too.

“You don’t feel like running off a thousand miles?” Mrs. Davilow asked.

“Not quite that far.”

“It was careless not to write sooner. Can’t you do it now, before we go?”

“It’s not urgent,” Gwendolen said lightly. “Tomorrow will be fine. They’re leaving town today. I’ll write from Dover. They’ll be there until Monday.”

“Shall I write for you, dear—if it troubles you?”

Gwendolen hesitated, sipping her coffee. Then, sharply, “No, let it wait. I’ll write tomorrow.” A moment later, she looked up with soft playfulness. “Dear, old, beautiful mamma!”

“Old, child, truly.”

“Please don’t say that! I meant ‘old’ as in ‘darling.’ You’re barely twenty-five years older than I am. When you talk like that, my whole future seems to shrink.”

“One can have a great deal of happiness in twenty-five years, my dear.”

“I mustn’t delay,” Gwendolen said, laughing. “The sooner I get my palaces and carriages, the better.”

“And a good husband who adores you, Gwen,” Mrs. Davilow added, encouragingly.

Gwendolen pouted, but said nothing.

There was one small disappointment: the rector was detained by magistrate’s business and might not make it to the Chase at all. Gwendolen didn’t care much that Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna chose not to come without him, but her uncle’s absence felt like a missing anchor. His presence would have made the decision feel inevitable, almost natural. But now, with the choice looming so clearly, she began to feel uneasy. She had nearly accepted Grandcourt. And now, this sudden fullness of possibility—the idea of marriage, wealth, status—felt less like freedom and more like a cage. Yet still, she clung to the thought that marriage might open doors, not close them.

They gathered at Green Arbor, a grassy hollow sheltered by overhanging trees, where the servants had set up the picnic. The warden would guide the archers, keeping them within bounds marked by landmarks—Double Oak, Whispering Stones, High Cross. The plan was to take a light stroll before lunch, saving the real roving for the golden hour. The gathering was swift, and soon the company scattered through dappled sunlight and shadow, a scene so lovely it could have been painted. Roving archery was more elegant than stationary shooting, though less precise. Hits were scattered unevenly among the volunteers. Gwendolen did not stand out in skill, but she carried her failure with charm, laughter, and grace.

She wore white and green again—just as she had the day she first met Grandcourt. He stayed close beside her, yet their interaction remained outwardly unchanged. No one could tell from their expressions or manner that anything had shifted between them. Still, most assumed they were either engaged or on the verge of it. And Gwendolen believed it too.

As they walked back toward Green Arbor in loose groups, chattering and unthinking, a conversation unfolded that felt like the start of something final. Grandcourt asked, “Do you know how long it’s been since I first saw you in this dress?”

“The archery meeting was on the 25th,” Gwendolen replied, smiling. “This is the 13th. I’m not good at math, but I’d guess nearly three weeks.”

A pause. Then he said, “That’s a great loss of time.”

“That your knowing me has caused you? Please don’t be unkind—I don’t like it.”

Another pause. “It’s because of the gain that I feel the loss.”

Gwendolen held her breath. She thought: *He’s clever. Never says a foolish thing.* Her silence, rare and deep, seemed like assent. He continued:

“The joy of knowing you makes me ache for the time I’ve lost in uncertainty. Do you like uncertainty?”

“I think I do,” she said suddenly, her eyes brightening. “There’s more in it.”

Grandcourt met her gaze with a slow, steady look—like seeing beyond the surface into the soul. Then he asked, “More torment for me?”

Something stirred inside Gwendolen—something strange and unsettling. She blushed, looked away. “No—that would make me sorry.”

He would have pressed on, sensing victory in her change of demeanor. But he remembered they were in sight of others, descending a steep slope. It was polite to offer his hand. She took it, and they moved down in silence—watched by Mrs. Arrowpoint, standing near Mrs. Davilow.

Mrs. Arrowpoint frowned. “Mr. Grandcourt isn’t equal to his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger—too languid. True, he’s younger, but I wouldn’t be surprised if Sir Hugo outlived him. Successions are tricky.”

“It is indeed,” Mrs. Davilow agreed, her usual melancholy replaced by quiet contentment. She was pleased with the course of things.

But the story wasn’t about food or scenery. It was about a young woman whose world narrowed to one question, one man, one decision. The champagne flowed, the laughter rang, the sun dipped low. Gentlemen strolled, some lighting cigars. Among them was Grandcourt—but not Mr. Lush, who, unusually, was busy helping everyone, ordering provisions, attending to details. To Gwendolen, he was a blot on the landscape, though he kept his distance, never looking at her directly.

When it was time to depart, the bows had been entrusted to Lord Brackenshaw’s valet. Mr. Lush offered to fetch them for the ladies. Gwendolen, fearing he might bring hers, hurried to retrieve it herself. The valet met her with the bow—and slipped a letter into her hand.

She glanced at it. The handwriting was delicate, feminine—old-fashioned, like a relic from another age. Without asking, she turned away, bow in hand, and walked toward the carriage line. Mr. Lush approached to collect other bows. She stepped aside, back toward the stands, and opened the letter.

It read:

*If Miss Harleth is uncertain whether to accept Mr. Grandcourt, let her break from her party after passing the Whispering Stones and return to that spot. There she will hear something that will decide her—but only if she keeps this letter secret. If she does not act, she will repent, as I have repented. The secrecy she will feel bound to honor.*

Gwendolen froze. Then, with a jolt, she thought: *It’s come in time.* Her youth made her believe in revelations, not tricks. She resolved to go to the Whispering Stones alone. She tucked the letter into her pocket and rejoined the group—her pulse quick, her spirit sharpened by the weight of a secret.

To everyone’s surprise, Grandcourt was absent when the others prepared to leave. “We’ll find him,” Lord Brackenshaw said. “He can’t have gone far.” No one waited. His absence could be interpreted as distraction—lover’s preoccupation. The Earl gave Gwendolen a teasing hint, which she received calmly. But her mind whispered: *Could he be fleeing the decision too?* It was unsettling—but close to the truth.

Yet “fleeing” wasn’t right. Grandcourt wasn’t running. He was simply paralyzed—a numbness taking hold when success seemed near. To withhold now, when all expected commitment, was its own kind of triumph—will over desire, independence over obligation. He lit a second cigar, half-blind, stubborn. Had anyone interrupted him, he might have muttered, “You’ll kindly go to hell.”

But no one did. The party moved on, cheerful, unaware of the storm brewing behind the calm surface.

The forest grew more beautiful with each step. Shadows lengthened. The air thickened with warmth and memory. Someone joked that they were playing *As You Like It*, and when Gwendolen was praised for being Rosalind, she felt compelled to shine—though not from pride, but from a restless need to act, to control, to escape.

An hour passed. They reached the Whispering Stones—two towering, conical blocks leaning toward each other like ancient sentinels. They passed quickly, remarking how they’d make good ghosts under moonlight. But sunlight gilded them now. Gwendolen felt bold.

“How far to Green Arbor?” she asked the warden.

“Half a mile along the avenue we’re crossing. But I’ll take a roundabout route—past the High Cross.”

She fell back. Then, as Mr. Lush guided the group forward, she lingered—her chance. She slipped away, unseen. She walked swiftly, not running, yet feeling as if she flew. Time dissolved. She reached the stones again.

They faced her blankly. What lay beyond? Would there be nothing? That was her only fear—to return empty-handed, confused. She circled the right stone. And there, a few feet ahead, stood a woman.

Her eyes were large, dark, haunted. Gwendolen gasped—then stepped back, instinctively. But she saw: a lady, once very beautiful. And nearby, two children sat on the grass.

“Miss Harleth?” the woman said.

“Yes.”

“Have you accepted Mr. Grandcourt?”

“No.”

“I promised to tell you something. And you must promise to keep my secret. Whatever you decide, you will not tell him—or anyone—that you’ve seen me?”

“I promise.”

“My name is Lydia Glasher. Mr. Grandcourt should not marry anyone but me. Nine years ago, I left my husband and child for him. These two children are his. We have two girls, older. My husband is dead. Mr. Grandcourt ought to marry me. He should make that boy his heir.”

She looked at the boy. Gwendolen followed her gaze. The child was blowing a tiny trumpet—useless, silent. His hat hung backward, curls catching the light. A cherub.

Their eyes met again. Gwendolen said, proudly, “I will not interfere with your wishes.” Her voice trembled. Her lips were pale.

“You are very attractive, Miss Harleth. But when he first knew me, I was young too. Since then, my life has broken apart. It’s not fair—he should be happy, and I miserable, my son cast aside for another.”

Her words were sharp, controlled. Gwendolen watched her face—felt a terror, as if a vision of ruin had stepped from a dream and said, “I am a woman’s life.”

“Is there more you wish to say?” Gwendolen asked, low, proud, cold.

“Nothing. You know what I wanted you to know. You may inquire about me. My husband was Colonel Glasher.”

Then Gwendolen turned and walked away, offering a formal nod. The woman returned it with equal dignity.

Minutes later, she was back in the beech grove. Her party was gone. She walked the avenue back to Green Arbor, moving fast—anything to keep thoughts at bay. She had decided.

Mrs. Davilow was stunned to see her daughter return alone. “Oh, you’ve been silly,” Gwendolen said. “I lingered at the stones. The others rushed on. I took the short way—just as the warden told me. Honestly, I’m glad. I’ve had enough walking.”

“Did you not meet Mr. Grandcourt?” Mrs. Arrowpoint asked, pointedly.

“No,” Gwendolen replied, with a flash of defiance and a laugh. “And we didn’t see any carvings on the trees. Where can he be? I suppose he’s fallen into the pool—or had an apoplectic fit.”

Her voice was high, brittle. Mrs. Davilow sensed trouble.

Mrs. Arrowpoint thought Gwendolen was offended—perhaps Grandcourt had changed his mind.

“If you don’t mind, mamma,” Gwendolen said, “I’ll order the carriage. I’m tired. Everyone will be leaving soon.”

Mrs. Davilow agreed. By the time the carriage arrived, the rovers returned—with Grandcourt.

“Ah, there you are!” Lord Brackenshaw said, stepping forward. “We thought you’d been picked up by Grandcourt. Lush said so. But then we found him. Still, we didn’t think you were in danger. The warden said he’d told you a shortcut.”

“You’re going?” Grandcourt asked, approaching with his usual composure.

“Yes,” Gwendolen said, adjusting her shawl. “We’re leaving.”

“May I call at Offendene tomorrow?”

“Oh yes,” she said, sweeping him with her lashes. Her voice was sharp, frost-like.

Mrs. Davilow accepted his arm. But Gwendolen moved swiftly ahead and leapt into the carriage.

“I got in first, mamma,” she said, apologetically. “I wanted this side.” She had avoided his touch. He merely raised his hat and walked away—satisfied she was offended.

For five minutes, they drove in silence. Then Gwendolen spoke.

“I intend to join the Langens at Dover, mamma. I’ll pack immediately and catch the early train. I’ll arrive almost as soon as they do—we can telegraph.”

“Good heavens, child! Why say such a thing?”

“My reason for saying it is that I mean to do it.”

“But why?”

“I want to go away.”

“Because you’re angry at Grandcourt’s odd behavior today?”

“It’s useless to discuss it. I won *not* marry Mr. Grandcourt. Don’t concern yourself further.”

“What shall I tell your uncle, Gwendolen? You led him to believe last night you’d decided in favor of Grandcourt.”

“I’m sorry to cause you distress, dear mamma, but I can’t help it.” Her tone hardened. “Whatever you or Uncle think, I won’t change my mind. I won’t tell you why. I don’t care what happens. I don’t care if I never marry. Nothing matters. I believe all men are bad. I hate them.”

“But must you leave like this, Gwendolen?” Mrs. Davilow whispered, broken.

“Don’t interfere, mamma. If you’ve ever suffered, remember it. If I’m to be unhappy, let it be by my own choice.”

The mother sank into trembling silence. She began to understand: sometimes, the best way to stop a storm is to let the person walk away.
