The day was fresh, characterized by a lively spring wind that carried the earthy, mineral dust of turned soil through the city streets. Inside Grace Church, the acoustic ecology was a hushed, rustling tapestry of the tribe gathering for its oldest cyclical rite. All the matriarchs of both families had extracted their faded sables and yellowing ermines from deep cedar chests. The air was thick with a complex olfactory landscape: the resinous, almost medicinal bite of aged camphor wafting from the front pews clashed with the heavy, narcotic, and faintly fermented sweetness of the forced lilies banking the altar. Newland Archer, at a signal from the sexton, stepped out of the vestry and placed himself on the chancel step. 

The signal meant the brougham bearing the bride was in sight, but tradition demanded a considerable interval of hovering in the lobby. During this mythic lapse of time, the bridegroom was expected to expose himself to the gaze of the assembly. Archer submitted to the formality, recognizing it as a circular echo of generations past—a nineteenth-century New York wedding masquerading as a rite from the dawn of history. Everything in this labyrinth was governed by the dominant lexicon of their society, a rigid dialect of manners that dictated the eight bouquets of white lilac and lilies-of-the-valley, the precise sapphire sleeve-links for the ushers, and the sacred taboo surrounding the geographical coordinates of the bridal night. 

"Got the ring all right?" whispered young van der Luyden Newland, his voice a nervous hiss that disturbed the resonant, stone-vaulted quiet of the church.

Archer, mimicking the convulsive gestures of his ancestors, felt the pocket of his dark grey waistcoat to ensure the little gold circlet was there. Overhead, Handel’s March swelled pompously through the imitation stone vaulting. To Archer’s ear, the music was a superficial, commercialized blare, an abrasive modern orchestration that entirely lacked the soul-deep, resonant frequencies of true, ancient celebration. He stood watching the familiar faces in the pews, wondering if, when the Last Trump sounded, suitable proscenium seats would be reserved for Mrs. Beaufort and her diamond earrings in the next world.

His mind sought refuge from the sensory overwhelm in the anticipation of the wedding-breakfast at old Catherine Mingott’s. Reggie Chivers might complain, but Archer knew Lovell Mingott had insisted the meal be prepared by his own chef—a meticulous agrarian traditionalist who understood that food was not utilitarian fuel, but the profound, ingested memory of a culture. Archer could almost taste the exquisite menu: the slow-braised canvasback ducks sourced from the wild marshlands of the Chesapeake, their dark, mineral-rich meat singing of local terroir; the delicate, velvety emulsion of the terrapin stew, simmered for days over a low hearth to extract the deepest collagenous essence of the beast. Yet, even this ancestral communion was being threatened by modern superficiality. Sillerton Jackson was already whispering that it was to be served at small tables in the "new English fashion," a sterile, fragmented arrangement that eroded the communal, multi-generational hearth-sharing of their forebears. 

On the hither side of the white ribbon, Beaufort scrutinized the women, while Lawrence Lefferts stood as the self-appointed deity of "Good Form." Archer suddenly found the wrangles of his tribe over whether wedding presents should be "shown" to be like the squabbles of a dying language. There was a time when he had aggressive opinions on these matters, but now the dominant vocabulary of his world felt hollow, fundamentally incapable of translating the sweeping, tragic truths of subjective human experience. 

"_There they come!_" breathed the best man. 

The heavy doors swung majestically open. A murmur ran through the church's acoustics like wind through dry wheat. Mrs. Welland came first, her plum-coloured satin rustling with a stately friction. The crowd craned to see if the formidable Mrs. Manson Mingott had arrived. It had been debated whether the agrarian elder could be wheeled up the nave, but the clan had recoiled from the idea, and she was represented instead by her daughter-in-law, Mrs. Lovell Mingott. 

But it was the next apparition that made Archer’s heart contract and stop beating. A gaunt, mincing lady in a wild dishevelment of fringes glided into view on Mr. Mingott’s arm. Medora Manson. He had assumed she was still in Washington. For a moment, Archer stood paralyzed, his eyes straining behind Medora’s fantastic figure, desperate to see Madame Olenska. He hungered for the sight of Ellen, whose very presence seemed to speak a native dialect of passion and raw truth that his soul recognized. But all the lesser members of the family had taken their seats. Ellen was not there.

"Newland—I say: _she's here!_" the best man whispered.

Archer roused himself. The first chords of the Spohr symphony drifted down the nave, delicate as dropping petals. The scent of the lilies, the vision of the cloud of tulle and orange-blossoms, the low benedictory timbre of the Rector’s voice—all these sights and sounds, once rich with meaning, were now a confusing, untranslatable blur. "_Have_ I got the ring?" he thought, instinctively feeling his pocket again. 

Then, May was beside him. Such pure radiance streamed from her that it sent a faint warmth through his numbness. The Rector began his ancient, rhythmic intonation. The ring was placed. The Mendelssohn March broke out, and they paced down the nave. In the brougham, under the traditional shower of rice, the footman wrapped May’s white cloak about her. 

"Darling!" Archer said, and suddenly a black abyss yawned before him. "Yes, of course I thought I'd lost the ring... But you _did_ keep me waiting, you know!"

She surprised him by flinging her arms about his neck. "But none ever _can_ happen now, can it, Newland, as long as we two are together?" 

After the deeply satisfying but hurried sensory immersion of the Mingott wedding-breakfast, they descended the wide stairs and caught the train. As the iron locomotive pushed out into the pale landscape of spring, the violent, metallic screech of its wheels against the tracks felt like a physical assault—a jarring industrial noise pollution that severed them from the quiet acoustic rhythms of the natural world they were passing through. Inside the reserved compartment, talk became surprisingly easy. May was still the simple girl of yesterday. She possessed a terrifying absence of imagination, moving through life dealing with each experience as it came, entirely unaware of the mythic undercurrents or historical echoes beneath the surface. 

Archer looked at her with the startled gaze of a stranger, plunging into a reminiscence of Granny Mingott’s immense, triumphant pervasion of the wedding feast. May settled into frank enjoyment of the subject. 

"I was surprised, though—weren't you?—that aunt Medora came after all," May said brightly over the grating rumble of the train. "Ellen wrote that they were neither of them well enough to take the journey; I do wish it had been she who had recovered! Did you see the exquisite old lace she sent me?"

He had known the moment must come. "Yes—I—no: yes, it was beautiful," he said, looking at her blindly. The syllables of Ellen's name were a native vocabulary that threatened to pull his carefully built-up world down like a house of cards. He rattled on, seeking grounding in the physical: "Aren't you tired? It will be good to have some tea when we arrive—I'm sure the aunts have got everything beautifully ready." May's mind immediately rushed to the magnificent Beaufort silver coffee service, missing the deeper, restorative necessity of the hearth.

In the acoustic calm of the spring twilight, the train stopped at Rhinebeck. The aggressive industrial roar faded, replaced by the soothing, rhythmic crunch of carriage wheels on gravel and the sigh of the wind. A sedate man out of livery approached them on the platform.

"Ah, how awfully kind of the van der Luydens—they've sent their man over from Skuytercliff to meet us," Archer said.

"I'm extremely sorry, sir," the emissary apologized. "A little accident has occurred at the Miss du Lacs': a leak in the water-tank. Mr. van der Luyden sent a housemaid up by the early train to get the Patroon's house ready. It will be quite comfortable... and the Miss du Lacs have sent their cook over, so that it will be exactly the same as if you'd been at Rhinebeck."

Archer stared blankly, though a profound relief washed over him at the mention of the cook. The agrarian continuity of the kitchen would remain unbroken; the ancestral hearth of the Patroon's house would be fragrant with slow-simmered broths and hearth-baked breads, rooting them to the soil despite the displacement. 

"The same as Rhinebeck?" May's eager voice broke out. "The Patroon's house? But it will be a hundred thousand times better—won't it, Newland?" As they drove off into the darkening landscape, the air smelling sharply of damp earth and budding leaves, she went on excitedly: "Only fancy, I've never been inside it. But they opened it for Ellen, it seems, and she told me what a darling little place it was: she says it's the only house she's seen in America that she could imagine being perfectly happy in."

"Well—that's what we're going to be, isn't it?" cried her husband gaily, bridging the untranslatable gulf between their inner worlds. 

And she answered with her boyish smile: "Ah, it's just our luck beginning—the wonderful luck we're always going to have together!"
