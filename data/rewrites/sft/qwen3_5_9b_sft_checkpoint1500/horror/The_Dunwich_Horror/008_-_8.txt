While the chaos unfolded in the streets, a far quieter, yet spiritually suffocating, unraveling took place behind the locked doors of a book-lined chamber in Arkham. Here sat the manuscript of Wilbur Whateley, a document delivered to Miskatonic University that had already driven the university's foremost linguists to a state of profound bewilderment. To the untrained eye, the script bore a faint resemblance to the shadow-heavy Arabic of ancient Mesopotamia, yet to the experts, it was a void—a symbol system utterly alien to every known tongue. After weeks of futile scrutiny, the consensus hardened: the text was not a foreign language, but an artificial cipher, a labyrinthine grid of keys that defied standard cryptographic solutions regardless of the assumed mother tongue.

Ancient tomes seized from Whateley's quarters offered no illumination, save for a heavy volume bound with iron clasps. This book contained a script distinct from the manuscript, evoking the ghostly shapes of Sanskrit rather than any Semitic root. Ultimately, this chaotic trove was entrusted to Dr. Armitage. A septuagenarian scholar with a mind sharpened by decades of studying mystical formularies and the arcane traditions of the medieval and Saracen worlds, Armitage accepted the burden. He harbored a suspicion that these symbols belonged to a forgotten, esoteric cult, perhaps inheriting rites from the wizards of old Arabia. Yet, he reasoned that the origin mattered less than the function; if the sheer volume of text suggested otherwise, the writer likely employed his native voice, reserving the gibberish for specific incantations. Thus, Armitage approached the manuscript with the working hypothesis that beneath the code lay ordinary English.

He understood, from the collective failures of his colleagues, that this was no simple puzzle. For weeks, Armitage fortified himself with the dense lore of cryptography, immersing himself in the arcana of Trithemius, Porta, Vigenère, and the myriad treatises of the eighteenth century. He interwove his study of ancient texts with relentless attacks on the manuscript, eventually realizing he faced a cryptogram of terrifying subtlety—one constructed from multiple shifting letter lists arranged like a multiplication table, keyed by arbitrary words known only to the initiates. The older authorities offered glimmers of hope, leading Armitage to conclude that the code predated modern history, handed down through a lineage of mystical experimenters. Time and again, he neared a breakthrough, only to be knocked back by unforeseen obstacles. But as September drew near, the fog began to lift. Certain characters, when viewed in isolation, resolved into definitive shapes, revealing that the underlying text was, indeed, English.

On the evening of September second, the final wall crumbled. Dr. Armitage read the first continuous passage of Wilbur Whateley's annals. It was, as everyone had guessed, a diary, written in a jagged style that betrayed a mixture of occult erudition and profound illiteracy. The first long entry, dated November 26, 1916, sent a chill through the old man's spine. Written by a boy who appeared to be twelve or thirteen, yet possessed the mind of a toddler, it read like a prophecy of doom: *"Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth... which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air."* The child spoke of an upstairs presence that was "ahead of me," lacking "earth brain," and of shooting Elam Hutchins's dog, Jack. He mentioned the "inner city" at the magnetic poles and a plan to clear the Earth when the "outside" helped, noting that the entities from above required human blood to manifest physically. The handwriting, erratic and childish, hinted at a transfiguration, a shedding of humanity in favor of something vast and ancient.

By morning, Dr. Armitage was drenched in a cold sweat, his mind a furnace of wakeful terror. He had not slept; instead, he sat under the harsh glare of an electric lamp, his hands shaking as they turned page after page, deciphering the nightmare with frantic speed. He had called his wife, assuring her he would not return home, and when she brought breakfast, he could barely swallow a mouthful. Throughout the day, he devoured the text, halting repeatedly as he struggled to reapply the complex keys. Lunch and dinner arrived, eaten with mechanical indifference. Toward midnight, exhaustion dragged him into a chair, but dreams soon tore him free—nightmares so horrific, so vivid, they mirrored the truths of the diary itself.

On the morning of the fourth, Professors Rice and Morgan demanded an audience, leaving the study pale and trembling. That same evening, Armitage slept fitfully. By Wednesday, he returned to the desk, his pen flying across paper as he transcribed notes from the newly decoded sections. He collapsed briefly in an armchair around noon, but rose before dawn, driven by an insatiable hunger for the truth. Just then, his physician, Dr. Hartwell, burst in, demanding he stop. Armitage, with eyes burning with feverish intensity, refused, insisting the work was vital and promising an explanation later.

That twilight hour marked the climax of his ordeal. As he finished the final pages, he sank back, utterly drained. When his wife entered with dinner, she found him in a semi-comatose stupor. However, as her gaze drifted toward his scattered notes, he jolted awake, issuing a sharp cry to keep her away. Weakly rising, he gathered the scribbled sheets, sealing them tightly into a large envelope and tucking it into his inner coat pocket. He managed to stumble home, where Dr. Hartwell was summoned immediately. Lying in bed, the old doctor muttered over and over, "But what, in God's name, can we do?"

His sleep the following day was plagued by delirium. Armitage spoke in fragments, urging a long conference with Rice and Morgan, his words jumping from the need to destroy a boarded-up farmhouse to visions of an extermination of all life by an elder race from another dimension. He shouted that the world was in peril, that the Elder Things sought to strip the planet bare and drag it into some other phase of existence, abandoning it as they had done eons ago. He screamed for the *Necronomicon*, hoping for a counter-formula, and begged them to stop the Whateleys from letting them in. "I know how to make the powder..." he gasped, speaking of chemicals that hadn't been fed since the second of August. Despite his age, Armitage's robust physique held firm against the fever, and by Friday, his mind, though sober, was gnawed by a terrible, crushing responsibility.

Saturday afternoon, he summoned Rice and Morgan to the library. For the rest of the day and into the night, the three men tormented their brains with wild speculation and desperate debate. Strange, forbidden books were pulled from the stacks, diagrams sketched with feverish haste, and formulas copied in bewildering abundance. Skepticism vanished completely; all three had seen the corpse of Wilbur Whateley lying on the floor of that same building, and no sane man could dismiss the diary as mere madness. Opinions clashed regarding whether to notify the State Police, but the negative vote prevailed. There were matters too terrible to be explained to the common lawmen.

Late that night, the conference broke without a concrete plan, but Sunday passed in a haze of chemical mixing and formula comparison. The more Armitage reflected on the hellish journal, the more he doubted that material agents alone could stamp out the entity Whateley had unleashed—the earth-threatening horror that, unknowingly, was ready to burst forth within hours. Monday and Tuesday saw endless research and experimentation, mapping out a tentative line of action. By Tuesday, Armitage believed he could travel to Dunwich within the week. Then, on Wednesday, the shock struck. Buried in a corner of the *Arkham Advertiser* was a facetious blurb from the Associated Press claiming that the bootleg whisky of Dunwich had spawned a record-breaking monster. Stunned, Armitage telephoned Rice and Morgan. Through the long, dark hours, they debated, and the following day became a whirlwind of preparation. Armitage knew he was meddling with terrible powers, yet he saw that there was no other way to annul the deeper, malign interference that others had wrought before him.
