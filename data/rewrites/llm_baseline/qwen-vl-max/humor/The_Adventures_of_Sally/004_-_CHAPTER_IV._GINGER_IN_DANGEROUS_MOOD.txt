A few days after their return from Roville—by separate routes, thank you very much—Bruce Carmyle ran into his cousin Lancelot (known to all as Ginger) in Piccadilly. It was a collision of the sort that happens when one man is trying to avoid another and the other doesn’t know it. Ginger, who had been making a valiant attempt to vanish into the London fog like a shy badger, was interrupted mid-stride by Carmyle, who stopped him dead with the kind of casual authority usually reserved for traffic wardens.

“Just the man I wanted to see,” said Carmyle, flashing a smile so wide it might have been designed to start a revolution in someone’s inner peace.

“Oh, hullo!” replied Ginger, sounding about as enthusiastic as a man who’d just discovered he’d been invited to a funeral dressed in a bathrobe.

“I was thinking of dropping by your club,” added Carmyle, as though he were announcing a picnic in the park.

“Ah,” said Ginger, not believing a word of it. “Well, that’s convenient.”

Carmyle then produced a cigarette case like a magician pulling a rabbit out of a hat—except the rabbit was probably still on fire. Ginger stared at it with the wary eye of a man who’s just been handed a key to a room full of traps.

He felt… off-balance. In all their years of acquaintance, Bruce Carmyle had never been this agreeable. Not even during the Great Christmas Cake Incident of ’18, when Ginger had accidentally dropped a whole layer cake into the family dog’s kennel. That hadn’t earned him a single compliment. This, however, felt suspiciously like a trap disguised as friendship.

“Been back in London long?” asked Carmyle, lighting up with the grace of a man who knows exactly how to ruin a perfectly good moment.

“Day or two,” muttered Ginger.

“I heard—quite by accident—that you’d returned. And that you’re staying at the club.” He paused, then added, “By the way, thanks for introducing me to Miss Nicholas.”

Ginger nearly tripped over his own feet.

“What?!”

“You remember. At Roville Station. You threw her right onto me. We agreed it counted as an introduction. Quite an attractive girl.”

Bruce Carmyle wasn’t entirely sure what he thought of Sally yet. But one thing he knew: she couldn’t disappear from his life without him having a say in the matter. Her sudden departure had left him with that peculiar ache only a rejected admirer can truly understand—a feeling somewhere between mild disappointment and existential dread. She’d won their last encounter hands down, and now he felt the need to prove he wasn’t just a walking cliché with a decent wardrobe.

In short, Bruce Carmyle was piqued. And when Bruce Carmyle is piqued, trouble follows. Like a bad smell in a crowded train.

“She’s quite attractive,” he continued, puffing gently. “We had a lovely chat.”

“I bet you did,” said Ginger, voice thick with jealousy. “By the way—did she give you her address?”

“Why?” asked Ginger, suddenly alert. His expression shifted from suspicion to something closer to possessiveness. It was like watching a cat guard a particularly rare mouse.

“Well,” said Carmyle, fumbling slightly, “I promised to send her some books she was keen to read.”

“Books not published in America?” Ginger asked, narrowing his eyes.

“Exactly.”

“Everything gets published in America,” Ginger scoffed. “It’s practically a law of nature.”

“These particular books don’t,” said Carmyle, growing slightly annoyed. “And if you’re going to be difficult…”

“Fine,” said Ginger, giving in with the grudging resignation of a man surrendering his favorite sandwich. “Send them through me. I’ll mail them.”

“No,” said Carmyle sharply. “I can send my own books. Where does she live?”

Ginger hesitated. The sacred number of the holy street—the very address that had become a kind of spiritual landmark in his emotional journey—was not something he gave away lightly. But Bruce Carmyle was persistent. Like a mosquito with a PhD in annoyance.

So Ginger relented. With a sigh that could have powered a small steam engine, he whispered the number.

Carmyle scribbled it down in a tiny morocco-bound notebook with a gold pencil. He was the kind of man who always had a pencil. The backs of envelopes? Those were for peasants. He didn’t even *own* an envelope.

There was silence. Then Carmyle coughed.

“I saw Uncle Donald this morning.”

The geniality vanished. It didn’t fade—it simply ceased to exist, like a lightbulb unplugged. Carmyle’s tone turned icy, edged with that familiar undertone of familial disapproval.

“Yes?” said Ginger, moodily.

Uncle Donald—the man who’d once tried to teach Ginger how to tie a Windsor knot using only a teaspoon and a stern lecture—wasn’t exactly a beloved figure. He was the managing director of the Family, a role which made him both respected and deeply irritating. Ginger considered him a human blister. A permanent, unhealable blister.

“He wants you to dine with him tonight at Bleke’s.”

Ginger’s spirits sank lower than a submarine in a thunderstorm. Dinner with Uncle Donald? At Bleke’s Coffee House? That relic of Victorian gloom where the only excitement was whether the waiters remembered your name—or if they’d forgotten it five years ago?

It was like being sentenced to a night in purgatory with a menu written in Latin.

“To-night?” Ginger asked, as though the idea had just occurred to him.

“Yes,” said Carmyle. “Seven-thirty sharp.”

“Right,” said Ginger, already imagining himself trapped in a room filled with elderly men discussing the weather like it was a war crime.

“Don’t be a fool,” said Carmyle. “You know you’ve got to go. Uncle Donald’s invitations are not requests. They’re decrees.”

Ginger sighed. “All right. Seven-thirty.”

They parted ways. Carmyle headed east toward the Temple, where he had clients who presumably paid him real money. Ginger trudged westward, directionless, lost in thought.

And yet—oddly enough—they were both thinking of the same person.

Carmyle, briskly navigating the crowds of Piccadilly Circus, was mentally rehearsing how to charm Sally back into his orbit. Ginger, meanwhile, was wandering like a sleepwalker toward Hyde Park Corner, bumping into people like a drunken giraffe.

Since returning to London, Ginger had been in terrible shape. He slept poorly. He snapped at doormen. He once accused a pigeon of stealing his lunch. Hopeless love had struck him hard—and fast. It was the worst kind of illness: the kind that makes you want to scream at the sky but instead just stare at your socks.

His previously calm soul was now a hurricane. Things that used to bore him now enraged him. Like Uncle Donald’s moustache. That ridiculous, waxed thing that looked like it belonged on a walrus. And worse—its use as a soup barrier. A *zareba* for broth! It was absurd.

“By gad!” Ginger muttered aloud, stopping in front of Devonshire House. “If he uses that damned shrubbery as a soup strainer tonight, I’ll shove a fork down his throat!”

Hard thoughts. Very hard thoughts. And they were getting harder.

Rebellion, once lit, spreads faster than a wildfire in a dry forest. Ginger was burning. By the time he reached his club, he was practically a danger to society. Especially to anyone related to Uncle Donald.

Dressing for dinner was a nightmare. His tie refused to cooperate. His shirt collar seemed to mock him. Every button felt like a personal insult.

And then—just as he was about to throw the entire outfit out the window—Fate intervened.

Knock. Knock.

A waiter entered with a telegram.

Ginger picked it up. It had been readdressed from the Hotel Normandie. A wireless message, sent from the White Star liner Olympic. The words were brief, chilling, and utterly unforgettable:

Ginger sat down heavily on the bed. The weight of those words pressed down on him like a ton of bricks wrapped in velvet.

At 7:25 PM, a taxi pulled up outside Bleke’s Coffee House in the Strand. The driver glanced at his passenger—Ginger, looking like a man who had just inherited a curse and was debating whether to wear it proudly.

“Bit of a looker,” the cabby muttered. “Definitely looks like he means business.”

And he did.
