Where have all my childhood crushes on my little boy friends run off to? Tubby the golden-haired, Langley with the dented schnoz, Shamus stout of heart but wobbly of leg (easy to tackle, impossible to ever force to admit he lost a fight), scrawny Neal, and fat Dicky everyone called "Dicky Dick", Ballett the crier who'd burst into tears if he scraped his knee, and Beau Bunnie who owned 17 frayed neckties and insisted on fighting in spotless black kid gloves. All the rest of you, whose names I can't dig up but whose grubby little faces I could pick out of a crowd of a thousand adults, where did you go? What corners are your silly ghosts loitering in? If someone had told 10-year-old me there'd come a day I'd never hear your wild whoop of greeting, never feel the inky smudge of your handshake, never bicker and scrap and make up with you over stolen jumbles, I'd have sat down on the curb and cried right then.

I swear I saw Tubby last month, in fact. He's got bushy sideburns now, his once-golden curls are thin and grey, and he's hurrying down Throgmorton Street chained to a black leather briefcase like a overworked dachshund. I nearly hailed him, but I was late for a train and had to stop for a shave on the way. He stared at me funny for a second, like he half-recognised me too. Gallant old Shamus, who once fought a bigger boy for half an hour just to stop him skinning a live frog? They say he's an Income Tax Assessor now. A man so skeptical no expense claim gets past him, a paragon of zero charity and boundless disbelief. May Providence kindly assign him a different postal code than mine.

That's Time for you, the handyman of the universe, bustling around tidying everything up. He drapes grass over dead volcanoes, turns ancient rubble into garden soil, patches up the beech bark lovers carved their initials into, and turns all those feral little boys you knew as a kid into boring middle-aged men clutching briefcases.

I started out my school career as the official class pariah, and that was the first real heartache I ever carried. Grown folks are great at lying to themselves: at 40, most of us will swear we're at least a little bit popular, because no one could bear to go through life convinced no one likes them. But kids haven't learned that handy self-protective lie yet. I knew exactly how unpopular I was, no delusions to hide behind, and I brooded over it so hard I'd go hide in the playground hedge and chew on grass.

One day the most important person in the entire lower school sauntered up to me: head of the Lower Fourth, tall, beaky-nosed, son of a Edgware Road draper who thought he was the second coming of the Duke of Wellington. (He ended up a low-level civil service clerk, poor bloke, but to us he was royalty.)
"Can you run?" he demanded.
"Yeah," I said. Running was the only thing I was actually good at, word must've leaked.
"Then run twice around the playground as fast as you can. Go."
I balled my fists and bolted. I was so thrilled he'd even spoken to the class outcast I ran so hard my lungs felt like they were going to fly out my throat. When I skidded to a stop in front of him, gasping, he nodded.
"Why don't the other lads like you?" he asked bluntly.
Oh, what I could've said! I could've begged him for the secret of popularity, told him I'd do anything to get the other kids to stop falling quiet when I walked up, stop kicking me out of their games. I'd swagger like Tommy till my sides ached, I'd steal their balls and jump on their backs like Dicky, I'd fling my arms around their necks and do all their math homework and take all their detentions, if only they'd like me even a little bit. But all I mumbled, sullen, was "They do. Some of 'em do."
"Don't lie," he said. "You know they don't. Now listen: I'm giving you a shot. We're running hare and hounds next Saturday. You can be one of the two hares. Don't tell anyone yet, just turn up at the Spaniards on Hampstead Heath at half two. But you better run like the devil, yeah?"
He walked off before I could answer, and joy came sprinting at me so fast I almost fainted. That's the big break moment for everyone, isn't it? The politician getting a congratulatory pat on the back from his party leader after his first speech, the playwright getting a note asking him to come meet the producer, the new subaltern getting called over by his commanding officer. The sun breaks through the fog, and you think the whole world's yours.

I didn't say a word at school, but the second I burst through my front door I exploded. "I'M GOING TO BE A HARE! Only two hares, and they picked me 'cause I run the fastest! Can I have a blue and white striped jersey? And new running shoes? We start Saturday!"
We bought the jersey and the shoes that very same afternoon, because I was convinced if we waited one more day the last striped jersey in all of London would be gone, and every shoe shop in the city would be sold out of running soles. That night I put the whole getup on and admired myself in the mirror for ten minutes straight. All week I practiced leaping over every chair in the house, and I'd slide down the banisters instead of using the stairs, much to my mother's horror. Dad backed me up, saying the honour of the Lower Fourth was on the line, but mom added a whole new line to her nightly prayers begging God not to let me break my neck.

Saturday came. Everyone met at the turnpike outside the Spaniards, none of them paying me any mind, assuming I'd just come to watch. I had my regular clothes on over my jersey, hugging the secret surprise to my chest that I was the second hare, that I'd be the hero of the whole run.
Then the Duke, our Wellington, announced it. "Kelver's the other hare."
Silence.
"Oh come on, we don't want him," one kid yelped. "He's a total muff."
"He can run," the Duke said.
"Then he can run home," someone else snickered, and everyone laughed.
The Duke got mad. "Any more lip and you'll be the one running home, do you hear me? Kelver, you ready?"
I'd already started unbuttoning my jacket, but my hands dropped to my sides. "I don't want to come if they don't want me," I mumbled. The tears were pricking my eyes and I couldn't make them go away. I turned my face away, refused to move.
"Fine, don't then," the Duke snapped, and handed the paper trail bag to some other kid. "Hurry up, it'll be dark before we even start."
The two hares bolted off, and I drifted away, hiding my face. Someone yelled "Cry-baby!" but the Duke growled at him to leave me alone. I wandered over to the big cedar trees.
I heard the hounds whoop and take off five minutes later. How was I supposed to go home? Admit my mom and dad that I'd been shunned, that the big honour I'd bragged about had vanished? I wandered off, slipped my jacket and shirt off, tucked them under my arm, and became my own hare and hounds. I chased myself all over Hampstead Heath, alone, in the fine cold rain that had started falling.
I still see that pathetic, ridiculous little kid, jogging doggedly through the mud. Mile after mile he runs, that little idiot, jumping over ditches and occasionally face-planting straight into them, scrambling through dripping hedges, clambering over tarry fences and slimy wooden palings. He stops every few minutes to wipe his eyes on his grubby handkerchief, tuck his bundle of clothes tighter, and duck behind hedges whenever he sees a farmer or a walker pass by. He runs all the way through Bishop's Wood, past the graveyard bottom that's got a railway screaming through it now, down sloppy lanes that are now lined with identical cheap suburban villas, all the way to Seven Sisters Road station, soaked to the bone, covered in mud. He takes off his soggy fancy jersey, and goes home to Poplar, and lies shamelessly about what a jolly afternoon he had, and how he was so fast none of the hounds even came close to catching him.
You poor dumb little brat. Popularity's just a shadow. Chase it, and it'll always run faster than you. Turn your back on it and walk toward the sun, and it'll follow you all the way home.

Telling that lie started a whole habit, though. I'd sneak out every Saturday, run miles and miles alone, scattering torn up bits of paper behind me to pretend I was laying a proper hare trail, so my conscience wouldn't sting for lying. My mom would coo "They never catch you, dear?" and I'd say no, never even get near me. She was so proud of her fast little son. I even started hearing imaginary hounds behind me sometimes, and I'd sprint faster, for a second forgetting I was all alone.
The only person who never made me feel left out was Dan. His friendship was a rock in the middle of all that loneliness. To this day, Dan always roots for the losing team, defends the guy everyone else is picking on, hates crowds more than anything. "You've got no opinions!" a club friend complained to him once. Dan just shrugged. "I hate crowds." He never asked for anything back for being my friend. When I came crying to him after the other kids were mean to me, he never even gave me annoying unsolicited advice. When I finally got popular later and didn't need him quite as much, he wasn't even hurt. He was like a fountain, made for giving water, and didn't care if anyone ever thanked him.

And popularity did come, all of a sudden, after I'd completely stopped trying for it. Groups of kids would fight over who got me to walk home with them, like I was a prize at a fair. I didn't get it, till Norval, the second-best fighter in the lower school, asked me to walk to Camden Town with him, and I demanded to know why everyone suddenly liked me.
"'Cause you're a funny bloke," he said. "You say the funniest things."
It hit me like a slap to the face. All the school story books I read said the hero won everyone's love for being brave, honest, good at cricket, a leader. None of the book heroes were the class clown, the guy everyone gathered around to laugh at. I didn't want their laughter. I wanted their admiration. But I was so desperate to be liked I went along with it. I practiced silly jokes, thought up goofy little bits, twisted every serious thought I had into a punchline.
I got all the popularity I'd ever wanted, but I was never happy. I'd trade every single laugh I ever got for just the respect everyone gave stuttering Jerry when he hit his century and won our school match against Highbury. I'd get up an hour early every morning to practice throwing balls at broomsticks stuck in the yard, I spent weeks trying to learn to walk on stilts so I could be the fastest stilt-walker in school, and it broke my heart for months when even fat Tubby could outrun me on them.
There was a Sixth Former named Wakeham who was dead jealous of my joke skill. He was 15, going to little girls' socials at Gospel Oak, and he was convinced if he was funny all the girls would like him. But he was the most boring, solemn boy on the entire planet.
One afternoon he pulled me into an empty corner of the playground, eyes wide. "Kelver, you are so funny. You think you could teach me how to do it?"
I was just about to say absolutely not, when I thought of something. Wakeham had a superpower: he could stick two fingers in his mouth and blow a whistle so loud it would make dogs a quarter mile away panic, and make every grown up within ten feet leap two feet in the air. Old men would cuff him round the head for doing it, bus drivers would flick him with their whips, even other kids would throw rocks at him. I was desperate to learn it.
We made a deal. I'd teach him to be funny, he'd teach me the whistle.
For two whole weeks we tried. He did every goofy face and bad joke I told him, and it landed every time with the grace of a sack of potatoes. He showed me every whistle trick he had, and the best I could do was a tiny, reedy little peep that couldn't even startle a sleeping sparrow.
"Think anyone would laugh at that?" Wakeham would ask, after his funniest ever joke that fell flatter than a pancake. I'd have to say no.
"Think anyone could hear that whistle from ten feet away?" I'd wheeze, red in the face.
"Depends if you were already listening for it," he'd say kindly, not wanting to hurt my feelings.
We called the whole thing quits after a fortnight.
"I guess being funny's just something you're born with," I told him, to cheer him up.
"I guess the roof of your mouth just isn't the right shape for the whistle," Wakeham said, to cheer me up.
Neither of us ever got the skill we wanted. But we both got the lesson: the stuff you chase the hardest is never the stuff that fits you.
