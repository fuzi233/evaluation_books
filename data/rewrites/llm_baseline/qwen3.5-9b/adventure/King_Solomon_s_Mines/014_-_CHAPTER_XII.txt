Fortunately, Infadoos and the local chiefs possessed intimate knowledge of the great town's labyrinthine paths. They guided us sideways through the streets, allowing us to pass unmolested despite the oppressive gloom. For an hour or more we pressed onward until, gradually, the lunar eclipse began to recede. The edge of the moon that had vanished first reappeared, casting a faint silver light. Suddenly, a brilliant streak erupted from the disc, accompanied by a wondrous, ruddy glow that hung against the black sky like a celestial lamp—a sight both wild and lovely. Within five minutes, the stars began to fade, revealing enough illumination for us to discern our surroundings.

We realized we had finally cleared the town of Loo and were approaching a large, flat-topped hill, roughly two miles in circumference. Common in South Africa, this hill was not particularly high, rising merely two hundred feet, but it was shaped like a horseshoe with precipitous sides strewn with boulders. The grassy plateau at its summit offered ample room for camping, which currently served as a formidable military cantonment. While its usual garrison consisted of a single regiment of three thousand men, as we climbed the steep slope in the returning moonlight, we observed that several such regiments were encamped there.

Upon reaching the table-land, we found crowds of men roused from sleep, shivering with fear and huddled together in utter consternation at the strange celestial phenomenon they had witnessed. Moving silently past them, we reached a central hut where we were surprised to find two men waiting, laden with our few abandoned belongings.

"I summoned them," Infadoos explained, "and also for these," adding as he lifted up Good's long-lost trousers.

With an exclamation of rapturous delight, Good leaped at them and immediately donned the garment. "Surely my lord will not hide his beautiful white legs!" Infadoos lamented. However, Good persisted; indeed, the Kukuana people got only one final glimpse of his legs. Being a man of profound modesty, henceforth they were forced to admire his single whisker, his transparent eye, and his movable teeth instead.

While Infadoos gazed fondly at the recovered trousers, he informed us that he had ordered the regiments to assemble at daybreak. His purpose was twofold: to fully explain the origins of the rebellion decided upon by the chiefs and to introduce to the troops the rightful heir to the throne, Ignosi.

As the sun rose, approximately twenty thousand men—the flower of the Kukuana army—were mustered on a vast open plain. We approached to find the troops drawn up in three sides of a dense square, presenting a magnificent spectacle. Taking our station on the open fourth side, we were swiftly surrounded by the principal chiefs and officers. After a moment of profound silence, Infadoos addressed the assembly. Like many high-ranking Kukuanas, he was a born orator, delivering his narrative in vigorous and graceful language.

He recounted the history of Ignosi's father, detailing how he had been basely murdered by King Twala, while his wife and child were driven out to starve. Infadoos pointed out that the people groaned under Twala's cruel rule, citing the previous night's atrocities when, under false pretenses, many of the nobility had been dragged forth and wickedly executed.

Next, he declared that the White Lords from the Stars, observing their distress from afar, had resolved to alleviate their plight at great personal cost. He revealed that they had rescued the real king, Ignosi, who languished in exile, leading him over the mountains. Witnessing Twala's wickedness, the White Lords had performed acts of high magic to signal the wavering: they had extinguished the moon and slain the young fiend Scragga. Furthermore, they pledged their support to help overthrow Twala and restore Ignosi to his throne.

The crowd murmured its approval. Then Ignosi stepped forward. Reiterating Infadoos' words, he delivered a powerful speech, concluding with a challenge:

"Chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people, you have heard my words. You must now choose between me and the usurper sitting upon my throne—the uncle who killed his brother and hunted his child to die in the cold and night. These chiefs can testify that I am indeed the king, for they have seen the sacred snake coiled about my middle. If I were not the true king, why would these white men stand by me with all their magic? Tremble, all of you! Is not the darkness they have brought upon the land to confound Twala and cover our flight? Even in the hour of the full moon, the heavens have darkened before your very eyes!"

"It is!" the soldiers roared in agreement.

"I am the king!" Ignosi continued, drawing up his great stature and lifting his broad-bladed battle-axe high above his head. "If there be any man among you who denies this truth, let him step forward! I will fight him now, and his blood shall be the red token of my honesty. Step forward, I say!" He shook the great axe until it flashed in the sunlight.

No one dared respond to this heroic summons. Ignosi proceeded with his address, promising that if they fought by his side and won, they would share in victory and honor. He promised oxen, wives, and promotion to replace the current regiments. Most importantly, he vowed that under his reign, bloodshed would cease. Justice would walk blindfold; no man would die except for offending the law. The terror of the witch-finders and the destruction of kraals would end. Each would sleep securely in his own hut.

"Have you chosen?" he asked.

"We have chosen, O King!" came the thunderous reply.

"It is well," Ignosi said. "Turn your heads and see how Twala's messengers flee from the great town, scattering east, west, north, and south to gather a mighty army to destroy us. Tomorrow, or perhaps the next day, he will come against us with all his faithful followers. Then I shall see the true man, the one who fears not to die for his cause. He shall not be forgotten in the spoils of war. I have spoken. Go to your huts and prepare for battle."

There was a brief pause before one chief raised his hand, and the royal salute, *"Koom,"* echoed out—a sign that the soldiers accepted Ignosi as their sovereign. The troops then marched off in disciplined battalions.

Half an hour later, we convened a council of war attended by all regiment commanders. It was evident that we faced imminent attack in overwhelming force. From our vantage point on the hill, we could see troops mustering and runners darting from Loo in every direction, undoubtedly summoning reinforcements. We commanded about twenty thousand men, comprising seven of the finest regiments in the country. Infadoos and the chiefs estimated that Twala currently had at least thirty to thirty-five thousand men assembled in Loo, with another five thousand expected by midday tomorrow. While desertion was possible, it could not be relied upon.

Active preparations were already underway by Twala. Strong bodies of armed men patrolled the base of the hill, signaling an impending assault. However, Infadoos and the chiefs believed no attack would occur today; the day was reserved for preparation and removing the psychological impact of the moon's unnatural darkening. The onslaught would strike tomorrow, and they proved correct.

Throughout the day, every man worked to fortify the position. The hill, usually a sanatorium for sick regiments rather than a fortress, was transformed. Paths were blocked with massive stones, approaches rendered impregnable, piles of boulders stacked for rolling down upon enemies, and stations assigned to each regiment. Our combined ingenuity exhausted itself in these preparations.

Just before sunset, resting from our toil, we spotted a small company advancing from Loo. One man carried a palm leaf, signaling he was a herald. As he neared, Ignosi, Infadoos, a few chiefs, and we descended the mountain to meet him. He was a gallant figure in a regulation leopard-skin cloak.

"Greeting!" he cried. "The king's greeting to those making unholy war against the king; the lion's greeting to the jackals snarling around his heels."

"Speak," I commanded.

"These are the king's words," the herald replied. "Surrender to the king's mercy before a worse fate befalls you. Already the shoulder has been torn from the black bull, and the king drives him bleeding about the camp."

"What are Twala's terms?" I asked, curious.

"They are merciful, worthy of a great king," the herald stated. "Twala, the one-eyed, the mighty, husband of a thousand wives, lord of the Kukuanas, keeper of the Great Road (Solomon's Road), beloved of the Strange Ones... declares: 'I will show mercy and be satisfied with a little blood. One in every ten shall die; the rest shall go free. But the white man Incubu, who slew Scragga my son; the black man, his servant, who pretends to my throne; and Infadoos, my brother, who brews rebellion against me—these shall die by torture as offerings to the Silent Ones.' Such are the merciful words of Twala."
