Late that evening, a knock at my lodgings announced the arrival of Mr. Bruff. The lawyer's demeanor had undergone a profound transformation; the usual steel and assurance were gone, replaced by a heavy, somber hesitation. He extended his hand to me, and for the first time in our acquaintance, he clasped it in silence.

"Are you returning to Hampstead?" I asked, seeking a bridge for our awkward greeting.

"I have just departed Hampstead," he replied, his voice low. "Mr. Franklin, I know you have finally grasped the truth. Yet, if I had known beforehand the terrible cost exacted by that knowledge, I would have preferred to keep you in the dark."

"You have seen Rachel?"

"I returned to Portland Place after fetching her; it was unthinkable to send her home alone in a carriage. I cannot hold you accountable for the shock this ill-fated meeting caused her, considering you witnessed her under my roof and with my consent. All I can offer is protection against further harm. She is young, spirited, and resilient; with time and rest, she will recover. I demand, however, that you make no further attempts to see her without my explicit permission. Can I trust that?"

"After all she has endured, and all I have suffered," I said, "you need look no further than my word."

"I have your promise?"

"You have it."

Relief washed over Mr. Bruff's features. He removed his hat and pulled his chair closer.

"That is settled!" he declared. "Now, regarding your future—and I mean specifically *your* future. The extraordinary turn this affair has taken leads to a singular conclusion. First, we are certain Rachel told you the entire truth, as plainly as words allow. Second, though a dreadful mistake must exist somewhere, we cannot blame her for believing you guilty based on the evidence of her senses. Those facts, supported by circumstances that seemingly condemn you, were undeniable to her."

At this, I interjected. "I do not blame Rachel," I said. "I only regret that she could not force herself to speak more plainly at the time."

"You might as well lament that Rachel is not someone else," Mr. Bruff countered gently. "Even then, I doubt a girl of her delicacy, whose heart was set on marrying you, could bring herself to accuse you openly of theft. It was not in Rachel's nature. In a vastly different matter—one that placed her in a position strikingly similar to hers toward you—I know she was influenced by a motive identical to the one driving her actions in your case. Furthermore, as she told me on our way to town tonight, if she *had* spoken plainly, she would not have believed your denial then any more than she does now. What answer can you make to that? There is none. Come, Mr. Franklin! I admit my view of the case has been entirely wrong, but my current advice remains sound. We shall waste our time beating our brains against this nightmare if we try to unravel last year's complications from the beginning. Let us close our minds to what happened at Lady Verinder's country house and focus on what we *can* discover moving forward."

"Surely you forget," I protested, "that the entire matter is, concerning me, essentially a matter of the past?"

"Answer me this," Mr. Bruff retorted sharply. "Is the Moonstone at the bottom of all this misery—or is it not?"

"It is—of course."

"Agreed. What do we believe became of the Moonstone when it was taken to London?"

"It was pledged to Mr. Luker."

"We know you did not pledge it. Do we know who did?"

"No."

"Where do we believe it is now?"

"Safekeeping with Mr. Luker's bankers."

"Precisely. Observe carefully. It is now June. Towards the end of the month—a year will have elapsed since the jewel was pledged. There is a distinct possibility that the person who pawned it intends to redeem it when the term expires. If redeemed, Mr. Luker, bound by his agreement, must retrieve the diamond from the bankers. Under these circumstances, I propose stationing a watch outside the bank as the month draws to a close to identify whoever collects the Moonstone. Do you see the logic?"

I admitted, albeit reluctantly, that the idea was novel.

"It is Mr. Murthwaite's idea as much as mine," Bruff noted. "It never would have occurred to me but for a conversation we shared some time ago. If Mr. Murthwaite is correct, the Indians will likely be watching the bank near the month's end, and serious consequences may follow. Whether those consequences unfold matters little to us, save that they may help us locate the mysterious individual who pawned the diamond. That person, you may rely on it, is solely responsible for your current plight, and only that person can restore your standing in Rachel's eyes."

"I cannot deny," I conceded, "that your proposed plan is daring, ingenious, and entirely new. However—"

"But you have an objection?"

"Yes. Your proposal compels us to wait."

"Granted. By my reckoning, it requires patience of about two weeks. Is that so intolerable?"

"To me, in such a desperate situation, it feels like a lifetime, Mr. Bruff. My existence is unbearable unless I can clear my name immediately."

"Well, I understand. Have you considered what you might do?"

"I have thought of consulting Sergeant Cuff."

"He has retired from the force. It is futile to expect his aid."

"I know where to find him, and I must try."

"Try," Bruff said after a pause. "The case has assumed an extraordinary aspect since the days of Sergeant Cuff; you *may* stir his interest. Go ahead, and let me know the result. Meanwhile," he continued, rising, "if you make no discoveries before the end of the month, am I free to attempt the bank surveillance on my own?"

"Certainly," I answered. "Unless you relieve me of the necessity."

Mr. Bruff smiled and retrieved his hat.

"Tell Sergeant Cuff," he instructed, "that I say the discovery of the truth hinges on identifying the person who pawned the diamond. And let me know what the Sergeant's experience suggests."

We parted ways.

Early the following morning, I traveled to Dorking, the quiet town where Sergeant Cuff had retired, as directed by Betteredge. Upon inquiring at the hotel, I was guided to the Sergeant's cottage. It stood secluded, nestled within its own garden plot, shielded by a sturdy brick wall and a high quickset hedge. The gate, adorned with painted trellis work, was locked. After ringing the bell, I peered through the lattice and saw Sergeant Cuff's favorite flowers blooming profusely, clustering over the door and spilling onto the windowsills. Far from the crimes and mysteries of the great city, the renowned thief-taker was living out his final Sybaritic years, suffocated in roses.

A polite, elderly woman opened the gate, instantly shattering any hope I harbored of securing Cuff's assistance. He had departed the previous day for Ireland.

"On business?" I asked.

The woman smiled. "He has but one business now, sir," she replied. "And that is roses. Some great estate gardener in Ireland has discovered a new method for cultivating them, and Mr. Cuff is off to investigate."

"Do you know when he returns?"

"It is quite uncertain, sir," she explained. "Mr. Cuff remarked he would return directly if the discovery proved worthless, or remain away for some time if it warranted further study. If you have a message, I will ensure he receives it."

I handed her a card, upon which I had hastily inscribed in pencil: *"I have something to say about the Moonstone. Let me hear from you as soon as you return."* With nothing further to do, I was compelled to submit to circumstances and return to London.

By the time I returned, my mind was in a state of irritability. The fruitless journey to Dorking only fueled my restless impulse to act. Resolving that the next morning would mark a renewed effort to pierce through the darkness, I spent the intervening hours in a labyrinth of speculation. If the faithful Betteredge had witnessed my deliberations, he would undoubtedly have noted the dominance of my "German" side. Indeed, my rigorous training may well have contributed to the intricate web of improbable theories I constructed throughout the night. Sleep eluded me, and my waking fantasies haunted me. I arose the following morning with "Objective-Subjective" and "Subjective-Objective" hopelessly entangled, questioning whether I possessed any philosophical right to consider the existence of the diamond itself.

For how long I might have remained lost in this metaphysical fog, unknown to me, was moot. Accident intervened to deliver me from confusion. While searching my pocket for something else, I discovered a crumpled piece of paper: Betteredge's forgotten letter.

It seemed unjust to leave my old friend without a response. I sat at my writing desk and reread the letter. A letter devoid of significant import is rarely difficult to answer, yet Betteredge's missive fell squarely into this category. Ezra Jennings, Mr. Candy's assistant, had informed his master that he had seen me, prompting Mr. Candy's desire to speak with me whenever I returned to Frizinghall.
