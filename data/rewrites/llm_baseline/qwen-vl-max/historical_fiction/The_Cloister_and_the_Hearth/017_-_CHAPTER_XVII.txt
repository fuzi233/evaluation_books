Jorian Ketel hurried straight to Margaret’s chamber, his heart pounding with urgency. What he found there froze him in place—his breath caught, his candle trembling in his hand. There, pale as death and utterly still, lay the man he had been searching for, his head cradled in Margaret’s lap. She knelt beside him, motionless, her eyes wide yet blank, unseeing and unmoved by anything but the lifeless face she held so close. Her whole being seemed turned to stone, consumed by grief.

Jorian stood stunned. “Where was he all this time?” he murmured, but she did not hear. He stepped forward, his gaze falling on the empty chest—then it struck him. The truth unfolded slowly, like a terrible revelation. The girl’s silence, her stillness—it was agony made visible. A deep pity stirred within him.

“This is a sorry sight,” he said softly. “A black night’s work—all for a few furs! He should have come with us. But now…” He paused, then added, “She won’t answer me. Poor wench.” He hesitated, then reached for a small round mirror, no larger than his palm. Carefully, he placed it near Gerard’s mouth and nostrils, holding it steady. When he pulled it away, the glass was dimmed—not from dust, but from breath.

“There is life in him!” Jorian whispered to himself.

The words—though barely spoken—reached Margaret like thunder. Her body jerked, her eyes snapped open, and in an instant, she rose, flung her arms around Jorian’s neck, and clung to him with desperate strength. “Oh, bless the tongue that tells me so!” she gasped, squeezing him again and again, her voice raw with emotion.

“Now, now,” Jorian said gently, pushing her back. “Let us warm him. Come—lay him down.” Together, they lifted Gerard and settled him upon the bedclothes. Then Jorian drew out a flask from his belt, poured two full handfuls of Schiedamze—the sharp, fiery spirit—and dashed it sharply into Gerard’s face. The strong liquor stung the air, and though at first nothing stirred, then—faintly—a sigh escaped the young man’s lips.

That sound—so soft, so fragile—rang like music in the quiet room. Margaret leapt toward him, but stopped herself, trembling, afraid to touch him, afraid she might undo what had just begun.

“That’s right,” Jorian said firmly. “Leave him be. Don’t smother him like you did me. Let him rest. He’ll come around. He’s not an old man.”

Gerard sighed again, deeper this time. A faint blush crept across his lips. Jorian turned to leave, but before he could reach the door, he felt hands grip his legs. It was Margaret—kneeling, clinging to him like a serpent coiled about a tree.

“You won’t tell,” she pleaded, pressing her face against his hand. “You saved his life. You can’t take it back now. You wouldn’t destroy your own good deed?”

“No, no,” Jorian said, shaking his head. “I’ve done you both kindness before. I told you where to go in the church. And Dierich Brower? What does he matter to me? I’d see him hanged before I’d speak a word. But—” He paused, eyes narrowing. “Tell me—where are the parchments? A hundred crowns are offered for them. That would help my Joan and the children.”

Margaret looked up, her eyes brightening. “They shall have those hundred crowns.”

“What? Are they in the house?” Jorian asked, eager.

“No,” she replied. “But I know where they are. By God and St. Bavon, I swear you shall have them tomorrow. Come when you wish—but come alone.”

“Good,” said Jorian. “And I swear I’ll keep this secret. May my bones rot if I betray the boy.” He turned and slipped away quickly, knowing that lingering too long might draw suspicion.

Left behind, Margaret knelt beside Gerard, her body quivering with prayer and relief. She whispered words to God, begging for strength, for mercy, for life—for her beloved.

Later, when Dierich Brower asked what had happened, Jorian shrugged. “We frightened the girl half out of her mind. She had some sort of fit.”

“We should send someone to treat her,” suggested another.

“Oh, yes,” Jorian said lightly. “And scare her into the churchyard. Her father’s a doctor—I’ve already sent for him. Let’s look at the fire instead.”

His casual tone put everyone at ease. Soon enough, the group agreed that the kitchen of the “Three Kings” was warmer than Peter’s house, and they left—after freeing Martin, who had been tied up.

At the door, Dierich muttered, “See, mate? I told you we’d be too late. And we were.”

So, in one terrible night, Gerard had brushed both prison and death. Yet he escaped—not through clever hiding or quick wit, but because of a sudden spark of compassion in the heart of one of his captors. Jorian Ketel, reckless and hardened, had shown mercy—not from duty, nor from love, but from a mix of pity and greed. Such are human motives: tangled, unpredictable, often contradictory. Our best-laid plans fail; our most careful schemes collapse under the weight of chance and feeling.

Those who live sheltered lives, untouched by great joy or great sorrow, may be the wisest and happiest. But to such readers, I cannot convey what filled Margaret and Gerard in that hour. Even to those who have known deep joy and deep pain, my words fall short.

To sit and watch a loved face return from death’s edge—to see color return to the cheeks, warmth to the eyes, voice to the lips—that was Margaret’s bliss. To awaken from darkness and find oneself cradled in the arms of the one he adored, hearing her whisper sweet words, feeling her tears and kisses—this was Gerard’s miracle.

He had never truly understood how deeply she loved him—until now. He thanked his enemies, for their cruelty had brought him to this moment.

They clasped each other tightly, their arms wrapped like vines. Trouble, danger—these were forgotten, far behind them. They called each other husband and wife. Was it not true? Had they not stood together before the altar? Had not the Church blessed their union and cursed any who would tear them apart?

Yet even innocence has its perils. No longer threatened by prison or death, they now faced a greater danger: their own hearts. Their love, once tempered by fear, now burned too brightly. They were young, inexperienced, and now—safe—too easily carried away.

Tomorrow, they must part. But that mattered little now. They had seen death, and lived. All else seemed light as air. While there is life, there is hope; while there is hope, there is joy.

The future shone bright, the present was paradise. Hours passed like dreams, sweet and golden. Alas, their innocence still wandered into peril—not from men or laws, but from the very depths of their own souls.
