The January morning arrived with a biting chill, the sky a slate of unyielding gray that offered no promise of warmth until the fire was kindled. Before the maid had even struck a spark, Marianne knelt by the window-seat, her back rigid against the cold stone, clutching a quill that moved only as fast as her tears allowed. She wrote to Willoughby, drafting a final plea in a state of frantic desperation.

Elinor, roused from sleep by the sharp, ragged sounds of her sister's distress, entered the room. Seeing Marianne's posture—the hunched shoulders, the trembling hand—she observed her for a moment with a silent, piercing anxiety before speaking in a tone calibrated to maximum consideration.

"Marianne," Elinor said softly, "may I ask—"

"No, Elinor," Marianne interrupted, her voice devoid of the usual lilt, stripped bare by exhaustion. "Ask nothing. You will know all soon enough."

A strange, brittle calmness held her speech for a heartbeat, a tactical pause before the dam broke. Immediately, the excessive affliction returned, washing over her in waves. Minutes passed before she could resume writing, her pen halting repeatedly as fresh torrents of grief threatened to drown her ink. Each interruption was proof of her conviction: this was likely the last correspondence she would ever send to him.

Elinor offered every ounce of quiet support she possessed, yet Marianne, driven by a nervous irritability that bordered on hysteria, pleaded with her not to speak a single word. In such a volatile state, proximity was dangerous; Marianne's mind was a storm that required constant movement. She wandered the house like a ghost, avoiding every eye, seeking solitude in every corner until breakfast.

At the table, Marianne consumed nothing. Elinor, realizing that pity or pressure would only exacerbate the situation, shifted her focus entirely to Mrs. Jennings. She became the sole anchor for the older woman's attention, deflecting any gaze toward her grieving daughter-in-law.

The meal dragged on, a customary ritual for Mrs. Jennings, until the servants cleared the table for the afternoon work. Just as they settled around the common table, a letter was delivered. Marianne snatched it from the servant's hand, her complexion draining to a deathly pallor. Without a sound, she fled the room.

Elinor watched her departure and understood instantly: the handwriting, the timing—it was from Willoughby. A sickness seized her own heart, rendering her dizzy and unable to lift her head. Her legs trembled violently, threatening to betray her weakness before Mrs. Jennings. However, the older lady, oblivious to the gravity of the moment, interpreted Marianne's flight as a reaction to a trivial jest. She chuckled, measuring lengths of worsted for her rug, and remarked cheerfully, "Upon my word, I never saw a young woman so desperately in love in my life! My girls were nothing to her, yet they were foolish enough to dream. But Miss Marianne? She is quite an altered creature. I hope, from the bottom of my heart, he won't keep her waiting much longer. It is grievous to see her so ill and forlorn. Pray, when are they to be married?"

Elinor, though drained of spirit, felt compelled to dismantle this delusion. Forcing a smile that didn't reach her eyes, she replied, "And have you really, Ma'am, convinced yourself that my sister is engaged to Mr. Willoughby? I thought it a mere joke, but such a serious inquiry implies more. I must beg you not to deceive yourself further. Nothing would surprise me more than to hear of their impending marriage."

"For shame!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed, her laughter ringing out. "How can you talk so? Don't we all know it must be a match? They were over head and ears in love the moment they met. Did I not see them together in Devonshire every day? Did I not know your sister came to town specifically to buy wedding clothes? Come, come, this won't do. Because you are so secretive, you assume everyone lacks sense, but I can assure you, it has been whispered throughout the town for ages. I tell everyone, and so does Charlotte."

"Indeed, Ma'am," Elinor said, her voice grave. "You are mistaken. You are doing a very unkind thing by spreading this report, and I fear you will find yourself believing nothing of it."

Mrs. Jennings laughed again, dismissing Elinor's warning, while Elinor, lacking the strength for further debate, retreated to their chamber. Upon entering, she found Marianne collapsed on the bed, nearly suffocating under the weight of her grief. Several letters lay scattered beside her, and she clutched one tightly in her fist. Elinor approached silently, seated herself on the mattress, took Marianne's hand, and kissed it repeatedly. Then, overwhelmed, she broke into tears, a cascade of emotion that matched Marianne's intensity.

Silence fell between them, filled only by the shared agony. Eventually, Marianne handed over the bundle of letters, burying her face in her handkerchief as she screamed with raw pain. Elinor, recognizing that such devastation needed to run its course, waited until the initial paroxysm subsided. Only then did she turn to the letter from Willoughby.

"My Dear Madam," the letter began, the formal address a stark contrast to the cruelty within. "I have just had the honor of receiving your letter, for which I beg to return my sincere acknowledgments. I am much concerned to find there was anything in my behavior last night that did not meet your approbation; and though I am quite at a loss to discover in what point I could be so unfortunate as to offend you, I entreat your forgiveness of what I can assure you to have been perfectly unintentional. I shall never reflect on my former acquaintance with your family in Devonshire without the most grateful pleasure, and flatter myself it will not be broken by any mistake or misapprehension of my actions. My esteem for your whole family is very sincere; but if I have been so unfortunate as to give rise to a belief of more than I felt, or meant to express, I shall reproach myself for not having been more guarded in my professions of that esteem. That I should ever have meant more you will allow to be impossible, when you understand that my affections have been long engaged elsewhere, and it will not be many weeks, I believe, before this engagement is fulfilled. It is with great regret that I obey your commands in returning the letters with which I have been honored from you, and the lock of hair, which you so obligingly bestowed on me."

"I am, dear Madam, Your most obedient humble servant, JOHN WILLOUGHBY."

Elinor read the words, and the implications crashed upon her. Though she suspected the truth before opening the envelope, she had not anticipated such brazen indifference. This was not merely a rejection; it was a calculated dismantling of dignity. Willoughby had departed from every honorable sentiment, violating the basic decorum of a gentleman. Instead of offering regret or explaining his withdrawal, he reduced their entire history to a misunderstanding of his "esteem," casually discarding her letters and her hair as if they were worthless debris. Every line was an insult, proclaiming a man steeped in hardened villainy.

She paused, stunned by the audacity, reading it again and again. Each pass confirmed her abhorrence. The bitterness in her heart grew so fierce that she dared not speak, terrified that articulating the horror of his betrayal would wound Marianne further. She reframed the event internally: this was not a loss of potential joy, but an escape from the most irremediable evil—a lifetime tethered to a principless man. It was a deliverance, a blessing.

Lost in meditation on the depravity of the mind that dictated such a letter, and contrasting it with the purity of the person she truly cared for, Elinor forgot her sister's immediate torment. She forgot the three unread letters on Marianne's lap and the passage of time. When she finally heard a carriage rumble up to the door, she rushed to the window, astonished to see Mrs. Jennings's chariot arriving far earlier than scheduled.

Determined not to abandon Marianne, even in her own despair, Elinor excused herself from accompanying the older lady, citing her sister's indisposition. Mrs. Jennings, ever good-natured, accepted the excuse readily. Once the carriage departed, Elinor returned to the bedroom. She found Marianne attempting to rise, her movements unsteady. Elinor caught her just in time to prevent a fall onto the floor. Days of starvation and sleeplessness had taken their toll; now that the feverish suspense had broken, the physical consequences manifested as an aching head, a hollow stomach, and profound nervous faintness.

Elinor quickly fetched a glass of wine. As Marianne drank, her color returned slightly, allowing her to whisper, "Poor Elinor! How unhappy I make you!"

"I only wish," Elinor replied gently, "there were anything I *could* do to comfort you."

These words, simple and sincere, proved too much for Marianne. Overcome by anguish, she cried out, "Oh! Elinor, I am miserable, indeed," before her voice dissolved into sobs.
