I located my mistress in her private sitting room. Her composure fractured instantly upon hearing that Sergeant Cuff wished to address her, replaced by a visible irritation.

"Must I see him?" she demanded, though her voice lacked its usual steel. "Cannot you act as my proxy, Gabriel?"

Confusion clouded my expression, betraying my uncertainty. She quickly offered an explanation, her demeanor softening as she revealed the source of her unease.

"My nerves are... unsettled," she admitted, glancing away. "There is something in that policeman from London—a quality I cannot name—that makes me recoil. I have a foreboding that he carries trouble and misery right into our home. It is foolish, utterly contrary to my nature, yet it is true."

I struggled for words. Having observed Sergeant Cuff closely, I found myself drawn to his quiet competence rather than fearing him. However, seeing her distress, I realized I could not dismiss her fears. After composing herself slightly, recalling the natural courage inherent in her spirit, she relented.

"If I must see him, I must," she conceded. "Yet, I cannot summon the strength to face him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and remain here until he departs."

This marked the first recurrence of those dizzying "megrims" I had witnessed in my mistress since her youth. I retreated to the boudoir, where Mr. Franklin wandered into the garden to join Mr. Godfrey, whose departure was imminent. With haste, Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded to the sitting room.

Upon entering, my mistress paled perceptibly. Yet, she mastered herself with remarkable poise and requested if the Sergeant objected to my presence. She introduced me as her trusted confidant and long-serving steward, asserting that regarding domestic affairs, I was the most prudent counsel available. The Sergeant, polite and professional, accepted this arrangement with gratitude, noting that my familiarity with the staff would prove invaluable as he intended to discuss matters concerning the servants. He indicated two chairs, and we sat immediately to commence our conference.

"I have already formed an opinion regarding this affair," Sergeant Cuff stated, his tone measured. "However, I reserve it for myself at this juncture. My purpose now is to detail what I discovered in Miss Verinder's chamber upstairs and outline the steps I intend to take next, with your ladyship's leave."

He detailed the smudge on the varnish, articulating his conclusions with a respectful gravity absent from his earlier report to Superintendent Seegrave. "It is certain," he concluded, "that the diamond has vanished from the drawer in the cabinet. Equally probable is that the marks from the smear belong to an item of clothing worn by someone within this household. Before we proceed further, we must locate that garment."

"And does that discovery," my mistress interjected, "imply, I presume, the identification of the thief?"

"I beg your pardon, my lady," the Sergeant corrected gently. "I do not claim the diamond is stolen. Only that it is missing. Locating the stained garment may guide us toward recovering it."

She cast a glance at me. "Do you comprehend this line of reasoning?"

"Sergeant Cuff comprehends it fully, my lady," I replied.

"How do you propose to find the stained dress?" she asked, turning her attention back to the detective. "My faithful servants, who have served me for decades, have already had their trunks and chambers searched by another officer. I neither can nor will permit them to be insulted a second time!"

(The burden of command! A woman of rare stature!)

"That is precisely the dilemma I sought to present," the Sergeant observed. "The initial officer caused significant damage to this inquiry by allowing the servants to perceive suspicion against them. If I induce them to feel accused a second time, the obstacles they may erect, particularly among the women, become unpredictable. Nevertheless, their wardrobes *must* be searched again. The rationale is simple: the first search sought the diamond, whereas the second must seek the stained fabric. I entirely agree with your ladyship that the sentiments of the servants require consideration. Yet, I am equally convinced that their garments must be examined."

A stalemate emerged. My mistress voiced her frustration in elevated diction.

"I possess a strategy to resolve this impasse," Sergeant Cuff proposed. "If your ladyship consents, I suggest we explain the situation to the servants."

"The women will immediately deem themselves suspects," I interrupted.

"The women will not, Mr. Betteredge," the Sergeant countered calmly, "if I inform them that I intend to examine the wardrobes of *everyone* who slept in this house on Wednesday night—from your ladyship downward. It is merely a formality," he added, casting a sidelong glance at my mistress, "but the servants will interpret it as impartial justice between superiors and subordinates. Rather than obstructing the investigation, they will likely take pride in honoring their duty to assist."

The logic held water. My mistress, after her initial shock subsided, recognized its validity.

"Are you certain the investigation is indispensable?" she queried.

"It represents the most direct path, my lady, to the resolution we seek."

Rising to ring the bell for her maid, she declared, "You shall address the servants, holding the keys to my wardrobe."

Sergeant Cuff halted her with an unforeseen query. "Might we first ensure that the other ladies and gentlemen residing here consent as well?"

"The sole other lady is Miss Verinder," my mistress responded, surprised. "The gentlemen are my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite. There is no likelihood of refusal from any of them."

I reminded her that Mr. Godfrey was departing. As the words left my lips, Mr. Godfrey knocked, announcing his goodbye, followed by Mr. Franklin, bound for the railway station. My mistress elucidated the predicament. Mr. Godfrey resolved it forthwith. Summoning Samuel through the window to retrieve his portmanteau, he placed the key directly into Sergeant Cuff's hand. "My luggage may accompany me to London," he noted, "once the inquiry concludes." The Sergeant accepted the key with a courteous apology, remarking that the example of the masters would undoubtedly smooth the servants' acceptance of the search. After bidding farewell to my mistress with evident sympathy—and leaving a message for Miss Rachel suggesting he had not rejected her final answer, intending to revisit the matrimonial proposal soon—Mr. Godfrey departed. Mr. Franklin, trailing his cousin, assured the Sergeant that all his attire was open to scrutiny, nothing kept under lock or key. The Sergeant expressed his deepest thanks. Thus, the views of my lady, Mr. Godfrey, and Mr. Franklin had been met with alacrity. Only Miss Rachel remained to align herself with their stance before convening the servants to commence the search for the stained garment.

As soon as we were left alone, my mistress's inexplicable aversion to the Sergeant rendered the atmosphere increasingly oppressive. "If I provide you with Miss Verinder's keys," she said, "do you presume I have fulfilled all your requirements for the present?"

"I beg your pardon, my lady," Sergeant Cuff replied. "Prior to commencing, if it suits you, I wish to inspect the laundry ledger. The stained article may be linen. Should the search yield nothing, I must be able to account for every piece of linen in the house and every bundle dispatched for washing. If an item is unaccounted for, there will be strong grounds to presume it bears the paint stain and has been deliberately destroyed yesterday or today by its owner. Furthermore, Superintendent Seegrave directed the attention of the female servants to the smear when they crowded into the room on Thursday morning. That action, Mr. Betteredge, may have proven to be yet another oversight of Superintendent Seegrave."

My mistress instructed me to ring the bell for the ledger. She stayed with us until it arrived, ready to attend to any further requests the Sergeant might have.

Rosanna Spearman entered, bearing the book. Though she had descended to breakfast that morning looking ghastly pale and gaunt, she had sufficiently recovered from her recent malady to resume her duties. Sergeant Cuff scrutinized her intently—first her face upon entry, then her stooped, curved shoulder as she exited.

"Do you have anything further to communicate to me?" my mistress asked, still keen to distance herself from the Sergeant.

Opening the ledger, Sergeant Cuff grasped its contents within moments and closed it. "I venture to trouble your ladyship with one final inquiry," he said. "Has the young woman who delivered this book served under you as long as the others?"

"Why do you ask?" my mistress questioned.

"The last time I encountered her," the Sergeant answered, "she was incarcerated for larceny."

Following that revelation, there was no recourse but honesty. My mistress emphasized Rosanna's exemplary conduct and the high regard held for her by the matron at the Reformatory. "You do not suspect her, I trust?" she added, her tone earnest.

"I have already stated to your ladyship that I suspect no individual in this house of theft—at this stage, at least."

With those words, my mistress rose to ascend the stairs to retrieve Miss Rachel's keys. The Sergeant preceded me in opening the door, offering a profound bow. My mistress shuddered as she passed him.

We awaited, and awaited, yet the keys never materialized. Sergeant Cuff offered no comment. He turned his somber countenance toward the window, shoved his long, spindly hands into his pockets, and hummed "The Last Rose of Summer" softly beneath his breath.
