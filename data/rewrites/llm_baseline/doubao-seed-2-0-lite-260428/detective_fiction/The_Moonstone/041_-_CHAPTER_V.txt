After naming Mr. Candy’s quiet, odd assistant Ezra Jennings, Betteredge seemed to decide they had wasted more than enough time on a trivial side detail. He settled back in his chair, elbows propped on the scarred oak table, and resumed reading the rest of Rosanna Spearman’s unfinished letter.

For my part, I stationed myself at the parlor window, staring out at the drizzling autumn shrubbery as I waited for him to finish. Slowly, the strange, unshakable impression Ezra Jennings had left on me faded entirely—odd, that in the middle of a crisis as overwhelming as mine, any stranger could have lodged in my thoughts at all. My mind snapped back to the hard facts of my impossible situation, and the plan I had finally forced myself to settle on through sheer force of will: catch the London express later that same day, lay every tangled thread of the Moonstone’s disappearance before my lawyer Mr. Bruff, and no matter the cost, no matter what barriers Rachel put up, force a private, uninterrupted interview with her. More than an hour still stretched before the train was due to leave. There was a slim, bare chance the unread rest of Rosanna’s letter held some small clue I could use before I left the very house where the priceless diamond had vanished. For that chance, I waited.

The letter ran on to its close:
> “You have no call to be angry with me, Mr. Franklin, even if I felt a flicker of triumph once, knowing I held every one of your life’s prospects in my two hands. But that triumph didn’t last long. Sergeant Cuff’s theory about the stolen Diamond meant he was bound to end up searching every last scrap of linen and clothing in the house. There wasn’t a corner in my room, there wasn’t a single hiding spot anywhere on the estate, I could be certain he wouldn’t dig through. How to hide the paint-stained nightgown so not even that sharp sergeant could find it? And how to do it without wasting a single precious minute? Those weren’t easy questions to answer. In the end, I landed on a trick you’ll probably laugh at. I stripped off my own clothes, and pulled your nightgown on over my skin. You’d worn it, you see—and for a little while, it felt like you were right there with me.
>
> “I hadn’t hidden it a moment too soon. Word came down from the servants’ hall ten minutes later: Sergeant Cuff wanted to see the house washing-book.
>
> “I fetched it for him, and carried it to my lady’s sitting room. Sergeant Cuff and I had crossed paths more than once years before, back when I was still in trouble. I knew he’d recognize me if he looked close enough—and I had no idea what he’d do when he found an ex-con servant working in a house where a royal jewel had gone missing. I just wanted the meeting over with, to get the worst of it out in the open.
>
> “When I handed him the book, he looked at me like I was a total stranger, and thanked me so politely it made my skin crawl. Those were bad signs, I knew. There was no telling what he’d say about me behind my back, no telling how soon he’d have me dragged off to the local lockup and strip-searched. That was right when you got back from seeing Godfrey Ablewhite off at the railway station. I snuck off to your favorite walk in the shrubbery, determined to catch you and speak—last chance I might ever get, for all I knew.
>
> “You never showed up. Worse, Mr. Betteredge and Sergeant Cuff walked right past the bush I was hiding behind, and the sergeant saw me clear as day.
>
> “I had no choice but to creep back to my work before something even worse went wrong. Just as I stepped out onto the path, you came back from the station. You were heading straight for the shrubbery, you definitely saw me—and you turned away like I had the plague, and walked right back into the house.
>
> * [Note in Franklin Blake’s hand: The poor girl was completely mistaken. I never noticed her at all. I had meant to take a turn in the shrubbery, but I remembered at that exact second my aunt would want to speak to me after I got back from the station, so I changed course and went indoors.]
>
> “I hurried back through the servants’ entrance. The laundry room was empty, so I sat down there all by myself. You know how the Shivering Sand at the coast gets in your head, whispering? Those old thoughts came back to me then. I wondered which was harder to bear: the way you didn’t even see me, or to jump straight into that quicksand and end all of it for good.
>
> “Don’t ask me to make sense of my own actions back then. I’ve tried, and I can’t.
>
> “Why didn’t I grab you when you walked past me like I didn’t exist? Why didn’t I yell out, ‘Mr. Franklin, I have something to tell you that affects you directly, and you will listen to me’? You were at my mercy, everyone says I had the whip hand. And better than that, I could have helped you. I never thought for a second that a gentleman like you would steal the Diamond just for the fun of it. No, Penelope heard Miss Rachel talk about your debts, Mr. Betteredge gossiped about how much you spent. It was obvious you took it to sell or pawn, to get the money you needed. I even knew a man in London who’d give you a huge sum for it, no awkward questions asked.
>
> “Why didn’t I speak? Why?
>
> “I was a thief once, remember. I’d run risks ten times worse than hiding that nightgown. I’d pulled off scams so big they got written up in the newspapers. This little trouble wasn’t anything I couldn’t handle. But the truth is plain, isn’t it? I loved you, heart and soul, behind your back. But standing in front of you? I was terrified. Scared you’d get angry at me. Scared of what you’d say, even after I found out your secret, if a plain crooked-shouldered housemaid dared to talk to you like an equal. I got as close as I dared when I spoke to you in the library, and you didn’t turn your back on me then. But when you walked past me like I was dirt? I couldn’t work up any anger to make me brave. I just felt the shame of it, like you were saying, ‘You’re nothing to me, how dare you speak up?’ You never said it out loud, but I heard it anyway. Madness, isn’t it? No explaining that.
>
> “I’m almost at the end now. Penelope found me sitting in the empty laundry room. She’d guessed my secret ages ago, and tried to talk me out of my stupid crush, kind as can be.
>
> “‘Ah, I know why you’re moping here,’ she said. ‘The best thing for you, Rosanna, is when Mr. Franklin’s visit ends. He’ll be leaving soon, mark my words.’
>
> “I’d never once thought of you leaving. I couldn’t speak, I just stared at her.
>
> “‘I just came from Miss Rachel,’ Penelope went on. ‘She’s furious the police are crawling all over the house. She says she’s going to tell my lady tonight she’s leaving for her Aunt Ablewhite’s place tomorrow. And if she goes, Mr. Franklin will find a reason to leave right after, you can be sure of it.’
>
> “I found my voice then. ‘Do you mean he’ll go with her?’ I asked.
>
> “‘He’d go gladly enough if she’d let him, but she won’t. She’s mad at him, won’t speak a word to him, even after he tried so hard to help her. No, if they don’t make up tomorrow, Miss Rachel will go one way, Mr. Franklin will go the other. I don’t know where he’ll head to, but he won’t stay here after she leaves.’
>
> “I choked down the despair that came over me. For a second, I even had a little flicker of hope, if Rachel was quarreling with you. ‘Do you know what they’re fighting about?’ I asked.
>
> “‘It’s all Miss Rachel’s temper,’ Penelope said. ‘And don’t go thinking Mr. Franklin will ever fight back with her. He’s far too fond of her for that.’
>
> “The words were no sooner out of her mouth than Betteredge called all the indoor servants to assemble in the hall, to go in one by one and be questioned by Sergeant Cuff.
>
> “My turn came after my lady’s maid and the top housemaid. Sergeant Cuff’s questions, tricky as they were, made it plain those two women—my worst enemies in the house—had ratted on me, told him they’d seen me creeping outside my room late on Tuesday, then again on Thursday night. He figured out I’d sewn a new nightgown in secret, but he got it all wrong: he thought the paint-stained nightgown was mine. And I saw something else, from the way he talked. He knew I was mixed up in the Diamond’s disappearance, but he didn’t think I was the main person responsible. He thought I was taking orders from someone else. I still don’t know who he thought that someone was.
>
> “But it was clear he was nowhere near the whole truth. You were safe as long as that nightgown stayed hidden, and not a second longer.
>
> “I couldn’t risk wearing it under my clothes any more. They could drag me off to the Frizinghall police station any minute, charge me with suspicion, and strip search me. As long as Sergeant Cuff was still letting me walk free, I had to choose right then: burn the nightgown, or hide it somewhere far away from the house, somewhere no one would ever find it.
>
> “If I’d loved you just a little less, I would have burned it. But how could I? It was the only proof I had that I’d saved you from being arrested, from having your whole life ruined. If I ever did talk to you, and you didn’t believe me, if you thought I was making it all up for some bad reason, what else could I use to make you trust me? It was the only claim I had to your gratitude, your confidence. I couldn’t bring myself to destroy it.
>
> “I decided to hide it at the place I knew better than anyone: the Shivering Sand.
>
> “As soon as the questioning was over, I made up some excuse about needing fresh air, and got leave to go out. I walked straight to Cobb’s Hole, to the Yollands’ cottage. Mrs. Yolland and her daughter Lucy are the only real friends I ever had. I didn’t tell them your secret, I’ve told no one. I just needed a place to write this letter, and a private spot to take the nightgown off me. I couldn’t do either safely back at the house, not when every servant was watching me.
>
> “I’m writing the last of this in Lucy’s bedroom, all by myself. When I finish, I’ll wrap the nightgown up tight, hide it under my cloak, and find something in Mrs. Yolland’s old junk drawer to wrap it in so it stays perfectly dry. Then I’ll walk to the Shivering Sand—don’t worry, I’ll make sure no footprints lead to the hiding spot—and bury it deep in the sand, where no one will ever find it unless I tell them exactly where to look.
>
> “What happens after that?
>
> “I’ll have one last chance to say all the things I haven’t been able to say to you. If you leave tomorrow like Penelope says, and I haven’t spoken to you before that, I’ll lose my chance forever. That’s the first reason. And the second: if I do speak and you get angry at me, I’ve got that nightgown hidden, ready to prove I’m not lying. If those two things together aren’t enough to make me brave enough to get past that fear of your coldness, then that’s the end of my efforts. And the end of my life.
>
> “Yes. If you turn me away again, the way you did before? Goodbye to a world that never gave me a scrap of the happiness everyone else gets. Goodbye to life, that nothing but a little kindness from you could ever make worth living. Don’t blame yourself if it ends that way. But try, do try, to feel a little sorry for me. I’ll make sure you find out what I did for you, once I’m past telling you. Will you say one kind thing about me, the same soft way you talk to Miss Rachel? If there are ghosts, I’ll hear you. I think I’ll tremble with happiness.
>
> “I have to stop now. I’m crying so hard I can barely see the paper. How will I find my way to the hiding spot if I let tears blind me?
>
> “Why look on the dark side, anyway? Maybe it will all turn out fine. Maybe I’ll find you in a good mood tonight, or tomorrow morning. Fretting won’t make my plain face any prettier, will it? Who knows, maybe all these pages are for nothing. I’ll tuck them in the hiding spot right alongside the nightgown, to keep them safe. Writing this was hard work. Oh, if we only end up understanding each other, how happy I’ll be to tear all of this up!
>
> “Your true lover and humble servant,

Betteredge finished reading in total silence. He folded the letter carefully back into its envelope, set it down on the table, and sat hunched over, staring at the floor, heavy with grief.
“Betteredge,” I said, “is there any clue at the end that I missed?”
He lifted his head slowly, sighing so deep his shoulders shook.
“Nothing to guide you, Mr. Franklin. Take my advice, and keep that letter in its envelope until all your troubles are over. Reading it when you’re already at the end of your tether will only break your heart. Don’t read it again now.”

I slipped the letter into my pocketbook. Looking back at Betteredge’s old narrative, I knew exactly why I couldn’t bear to re-read it right then. Twice, Rosanna had made her final desperate attempts to speak to me, and twice, all unknowing, I had cruelly rebuffed her. That Friday night, I’d found her hovering by the billiard table, and I’d assumed she was about to confess her own part in stealing the Diamond—so for her sake, to make it easier for her to come clean, I’d stared hard at the billiard balls and ignored her, wounding her so deeply she’d walked away crushed. Then the next day, the very day she knew I was about to leave, I’d run into her in the shrubbery with Sergeant Cuff. Cuff had made a point of asking me if I had any interest in Rosanna, and I’d loudly, flatly denied it, for her own sake to warn her the police were closing in. I’d thought I was protecting her. Instead, those words had been the final push that sent her straight to the Shivering Sand, to her death.

I set the ghost of that terrible, sad story aside, even though the memory of it still twisted in my chest. The tragedy of Rosanna Spearman, the suicide at the quicksand that had set every last strange piece of the Moonstone puzzle into place, was behind me now. It was time to turn my full attention to the living, to the long, slow, hard work of untangling the last of the secrets, and dragging the truth out of the dark that had wrapped itself around all of us for so long.
