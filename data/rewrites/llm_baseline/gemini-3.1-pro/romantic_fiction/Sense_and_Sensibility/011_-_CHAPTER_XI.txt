Little had Mrs. Dashwood or her daughters imagined when they first came into Devonshire, that the cyclical, multi-generational rhythms of the valley would so quickly sweep them up, or that they should have such frequent invitations to the harvest feasts as to leave them little leisure for serious employment. Yet such was the case. The ancient cider presses creaked in the distance, a mythic heartbeat seamlessly integrated into their daily domestic routine. When Marianne was recovered, the autumnal schemes of amusement at home and abroad, which Sir John had been previously forming, were put into execution. 

The private balls at Barton Park then began, bringing with them a jarring, proto-industrial clatter of forced merriment that disrupted the valley’s natural acoustic ecology. Parties on the water were accomplished as often as a showery October would allow, featuring hampers heavy with the olfactory landscape of the season: cold-smoked venison, sharp, unfiltered apple wine, and pastries rich with the scent of wild thyme and rendered suet. In every meeting of the kind, Willoughby was included. The ease and familiarity which naturally attended these gatherings were exactly calculated to give increasing intimacy to his acquaintance with the Dashwoods, to afford him opportunity of witnessing the excellencies of Marianne, and of receiving the most pointed assurance of her affection. 

Elinor could not be surprised at their attachment, though she found their sensory obliviousness grating. Marianne and Willoughby devoured the intricate spreads of honey-glazed partridge and root-cellar preserves without a second thought, treating the meticulous culinary history of the region merely as utilitarian fuel to sustain their frenetic courtship. They communicated exclusively in the dominant, polished lexicon of fashionable romance, their loud, high-frequency laughter a sharp intrusion against the deep, resonant acoustic environment of the ancient hall. Elinor once or twice ventured to suggest the propriety of some self-command, attempting to translate the subtle, quiet dialects of restraint to her sister. But Marianne abhorred all concealment, viewing any moderation of her native emotional tongue as a disgraceful subjection of reason to common-place lexicons. Willoughby thought the same. 

When he was present, she had no eyes for any one else. If their evenings at the park were concluded with cards, he cheated himself and all the rest of the party to secure her a good hand. If dancing formed the amusement of the night, they were partners for half the time, speaking scarcely a word to any body else. Such conduct made them exceedingly laughed at; but the dominant power dynamics of their self-absorption shielded them from shame. Mrs. Dashwood, moved by the sheer vibrancy of their youth, entered into their feelings with a warmth which left her no inclination for checking this excessive display. This was the season of harvest and happiness for Marianne. Her heart was devoted to Willoughby, and her fond attachment to Sussex was softened by the sensory and emotional intoxications of her present home.

Elinor’s happiness was not so great. The sonic environment of her new life lacked the harmonious, grounding resonance she missed from Norland, and she struggled with the inherent untranslatability of her own grief. Neither Lady Middleton nor Mrs. Jennings could supply the nuanced acoustic or intellectual companionship she required. Mrs. Jennings, however, possessed the redeeming quality of a traditional agrarian elder. She was an everlasting talker, deeply rooted in the soil’s cultural memory, and she regarded Elinor with a kindness that ensured her a large share of her discourse. She recounted her history in a fading, earthy local dialect, sharing meticulous methodologies for preserving quinces and curing meats. Had Elinor’s memory been equal to her means of improvement, she might have known exactly the culinary techniques behind the restorative, marrow-rich herbal broths Mrs. Jennings had brewed for Mr. Jennings’s last illness, and the precise, untranslatable words of gratitude he spoke to his wife a few minutes before he died. 

Lady Middleton was more agreeable than her mother only in being more silent, though Elinor quickly perceived that her reserve was a dead acoustic space—a mere calmness of manner with which sense had nothing to do. Lady Middleton viewed the estate’s feasts not as profound sensory sustenance, but as a sterile commercial display. She served perfectly symmetrical, imported sugared almonds, devoid of local character or olfactory signature, and provided every thing was conducted in style, she never appeared to receive more enjoyment from them than she might have experienced sitting alone. 

In Colonel Brandon alone, of all her new acquaintance, did Elinor find a person who respected the slow, methodical history of the land and offered a resonant intellectual refuge. Willoughby was out of the question; his attentions were wholly Marianne’s. Colonel Brandon, unfortunately for himself, had no such encouragement to think only of Marianne, and in conversing with Elinor he found the greatest consolation. Elinor’s compassion for him increased, as she suspected the bitter flavor of disappointed love was already known to him. 

This suspicion was given by some words which accidentally dropped from him one evening at the park. They were sitting together by mutual consent in a quiet alcove, insulated from the chaotic noise pollution of the scraping violins and stamping feet. The comforting scent of beeswax candles and crushed clove hung between them. His eyes were fixed on Marianne, and, after a silence of some minutes, he said, his voice a low, grounding hum, “Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments.”

“No,” replied Elinor. “Her vocabulary for the world is entirely romantic.”

“Or rather, as I believe, she considers them impossible to exist.”

“I believe she does. But how she contrives it without reflecting on the multi-generational history of her own father, who had himself two wives, I know not. A few cycles of harvest and winter, however, will settle her opinions on the reasonable basis of common sense and observation; and then they may be more easy to define and to justify within a shared language.”

“This will probably be the case,” he replied; “and yet there is something so amiable in the native dialects of a young mind, that one is sorry to see them give way to the reception of more dominant, general lexicons.”

“I cannot agree with you there,” said Elinor. “There are inconveniences attending such feelings as Marianne’s, which all the charms of enthusiasm cannot atone for. Her systems set the quiet cadences of propriety at nought; and a better acquaintance with the world is what I look forward to as her greatest advantage.”

After a short pause he resumed the conversation by saying,— 
“Does your sister make no distinction in her objections against a second attachment? Are those who have been disappointed in their first choice, whether from the inconstancy of its object, or the perverseness of circumstances, to be equally indifferent during the rest of their lives?”

“Upon my word, I am not acquainted with the minutiae of her principles. I only know that I never yet heard her admit any instance of a second attachment’s being pardonable.”

“This,” said he, “cannot hold; but a change, a total erosion of original sentiments—No, no, do not desire it; for when the romantic refinements of a young mind are obliged to give way, how frequently are they succeeded by such opinions as are but too common, and too dangerous! I speak from the bitter taste of experience. I once knew a lady who in temper and mind greatly resembled your sister, who thought and judged like her, but who from an enforced change—a silencing of her native voice by a series of unfortunate circumstances—” 

Here he stopt suddenly; appeared to think that he had said too much, the memory hanging between them like the sharp, acrid scent of scorched sugar. By his countenance he gave rise to conjectures, which might not otherwise have entered Elinor’s head. The lady would probably have passed without suspicion, had he not convinced Miss Dashwood that what concerned her ought not to escape his lips. As it was, it required but a slight effort of fancy to connect his emotion with the tender, untranslatable recollection of past regard. Elinor attempted no more, honoring the profound weight of his silence. But Marianne, in her place, would not have done so little. The whole story would have been speedily formed under her active imagination, stripping away the mystery to establish every thing in the most melancholy, deafening order of disastrous love.
