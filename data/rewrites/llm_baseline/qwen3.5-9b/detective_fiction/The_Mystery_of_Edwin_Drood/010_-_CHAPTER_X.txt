It is often noted that women possess a peculiar, almost instinctive ability to discern the true character of men—a faculty seemingly innate, requiring no rigorous logic to arrive at its conclusions, yet issuing judgments with absolute confidence even in defiance of contrary evidence gathered by the opposite sex. However, this power, fallible as any human trait, rarely possesses the capacity for self-correction. When a woman's intuition proves wrong, she clings to her initial verdict with the stubbornness of prejudice, refusing to amend her view despite overwhelming proof to the contrary. Indeed, the mere possibility of contradiction seems to strengthen her conviction, for she identifies so deeply with her own "divinations" that she views any challenge as the biased testimony of an interested party.

"Now, don't you think, Mother dear," the Minor Canon asked one afternoon as his mother knitted in his small study, "that you are perhaps a bit too harsh toward Mr. Neville?"

"No, I do *not*, Sept," the elderly lady replied flatly.

"Let us discuss it, Mother."

"I have no objection to discussing it, Sept. I trust I am always open to discussion." A subtle vibration ran through her cap, suggesting an internal monologue: *"And I should dearly love to see the discussion capable of changing *my* mind!"*

"Very good, Mother," said her conciliatory son. "Nothing compares with being open to discussion."

"I hope not, my dear," retorted the old lady, effectively shutting the door on the conversation.

"Well! Mr. Neville, on that unfortunate occasion, committed himself under provocation."

"And under mulled wine," added the old lady.

"I must admit the wine. Though I believe the two young men were quite similar in that regard."

"I don't," said the old lady.

"Why not, Mother?"

"Because I *don't*," she insisted. "Still, I remain open to discussion."

"But, my dear Mother, how can we discuss anything if you take that stance?"

"Blame Mr. Neville for it, Sept, and not me," she declared with stately severity.

"My dear Mother! Why blame Mr. Neville?"

"Because," said Mrs. Crisparkle, appealing to first principles, "he returned home intoxicated, brought great discredit upon this household, and displayed profound disrespect to our family."

"That cannot be denied, Mother. He was then, and remains now, deeply remorseful regarding it."

"But for Mr. Jasper's exemplary courtesy in visiting me the very next day, still vested in his academic gown within the nave of the church, to express his hope that I had not been greatly alarmed or disturbed in my sleep, I believe I might never have heard of that disgraceful incident," the old lady continued.

"To be candid, Mother, I likely would have concealed it from you if I could, though I hadn't fully resolved to do so. I was following Jasper out to confer with him privately regarding the propriety of us jointly suppressing the matter entirely, when I discovered him speaking to you. By then, it was too late."

"Too late, indeed, Sept. He remained as pale as gentlemanly ash regarding what occurred in his rooms during the night."

"If I *had* kept it from you, Mother, you may be certain it would have been solely for your peace and quiet, for the benefit of the young men, and as the best fulfillment of my duty according to my understanding."

The old lady immediately crossed the room to kiss him, adding, "Of course, my dear Sept, I am sure of that."

"Nevertheless, it became town talk," said Mr. Crisparkle, rubbing his ear as his mother resumed her seat and her knitting. "And it passed beyond my control."

"And I stated then, Sept," returned the old lady, "that I held a poor opinion of Mr. Neville. And I state now that I hold a poor opinion of Mr. Neville. I said then, and I say now, that I hope Mr. Neville may come to good, but I do not believe he will." Her cap vibrated significantly again.

"I am sorry to hear you say so, Mother—"

"I am sorry to say so, my dear," interrupted the old lady, continuing to knit with firm resolve, "but I cannot help it."

"—For," pursued the Minor Canon, "it is undeniable that Mr. Neville is exceedingly industrious, attentive, and improving rapidly. Furthermore, he has—I hope I may say—an attachment to me."

"There is no merit in that last point, my dear," the old lady said quickly; "and if he claims there is, I think the worse of him for the boast."

"But, my dear Mother, he never claimed there was."

"Perhaps not," returned the old lady; "still, I do not see that it matters greatly."

Mr. Crisparkle regarded the pretty old piece of china she held with a pleasant, humorless smile, recognizing it as an object not easily argued with.

"Besides, Sept, consider what he would be without his sister. You know the immense influence she holds over him; you know her capacity. Whatever he reads with you, he reads with her. Give her her due share of your praise, and how much is left for him?"

These words sent Mr. Crisparkle into a brief reverie. He recalled the times he had witnessed the brother and sister engaged in deep conversation over his old college texts: on frosty mornings during his walks to Cloisterham Weir; on somber evenings facing the sunset from his favorite vantage point atop a crumbled fragment of monastery ruins, watching the two studious figures pass below along the riverbank, where the town's fires made the landscape appear bleaker. He realized how the consciousness had crept upon him that in teaching one, he was inadvertently teaching two; how he had unconsciously tailored his explanations to suit both minds—the one constantly before him, and the one he approached only through the medium of the first. He remembered the gossip reaching him from the Nuns' House that Helena, whom he had initially mistrusted as proud and fierce, had submitted herself to the "fairy-bride" (as he affectionately termed her) and learned from her what she knew. He pondered the picturesque alliance between those two, outwardly so distinct, and wondered if these recent developments had already become an integral part of his life.

Whenever the Reverend Septimus fell into such musings, his good mother interpreted it as an infallible sign that he "needed support." The blooming old lady hastened to the dining-room closet to retrieve this sustenance: a glass of Constantia and a homemade biscuit. It was a marvel of a closet, fitting for Cloisterham and Minor Canon Corner. Above it hung a portrait of Handel in a flowing wig, beaming down with a knowing air of familiarity with the contents and a musical intent to harmonize them all into a delicious fugue. Unlike a common cupboard with a simple hinge-door, this rare repository featured a lock suspended in mid-air where two vertical slides met. Pulling down the upper slide (while leaving the lower a double mystery) revealed deep shelves stocked with pickle jars, jam pots, tin canisters, spice boxes, and exotic blue-and-white vessels housing preserved tamarinds and ginger. Every benevolent resident of this retreat bore its name inscribed upon its belly. The pickles, dressed in rich brown double-breasted coats and yellow or sombre drab trousers, announced their portly forms in bold print as Walnut, Gherkin, Onion, Cabbage, Cauliflower, and Mixed. The jams, possessing a less masculine temperament and wearing curlpapers, whispered their identities in feminine script: Raspberry, Gooseberry, Apricot, Plum, Damson, Apple, and Peach. Closing this scene of charm, the lower slide ascended to reveal oranges, attended by a mighty japanned sugar-box to temper their acidity if unripe. At the court of these powers awaited home-made biscuits, a generous slice of plum-cake, and various slender lady-fingers to be dipped in sweet wine and kissed. Lowest of all, a compact leaden vault enshrined the sweet wine and a stock of cordials, emitting whispers of Seville Orange, Lemon, Almond, and Caraway-seed. The entire assembly seemed to have been hummed through for ages by the Cathedral bells and organs until the venerable bees had transformed everything into sublimated honey. It was observed that every visitor emerging from the shelves, deep as they were and swallowing heads, shoulders, and elbows, came forth with a mellow face, appearing to have undergone a saccharine transfiguration.

The Reverend Septimus surrendered himself just as willingly to a nauseous medicinal herb-closet, also presided over by a china shepherdess, as he did to this glorious pantry. To what astonishing infusions of gentian, peppermint, gilliflower, sage, parsley, thyme, rue, rosemary, and dandelion did his courageous stomach submit! In what wonderful wrappers, enclosing layers of dried leaves, would he swaddle his rosy and contented face if his mother suspected a toothache! What botanical blotches would he cheerfully affix to his cheek or forehead if the dear old lady convicted him of an imperceptible pimple! Into this herbaceous penitentiary, located on an upper staircase landing—a low, narrow, whitewashed cell where bunches of dried leaves hung from rusty hooks in the ceiling and lay spread upon shelves alongside portentous bottles—would the Reverend Septimus be led submissively, like a popular lamb long and unresistingly driven to the slaughter, bearing no burden but his own.
