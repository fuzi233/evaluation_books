The very next day saw the first of New York’s rigidly prescribed betrothal visits unfold, every step scripted by generations of unspoken social rule. Newland Archer accompanied his mother and sister first to call on the Welland household, before the three of them—Newland, Mrs. Welland, and his bright, blushing fiancée May—climbed into a closed carriage to drive out to old Mrs. Manson Mingott’s home, to receive the venerable matriarch’s official blessing on their match.

For Newland, a call on Mrs. Mingott was always a delightfully unorthodox treat. The house itself was a living, breathing oddity in the staid city, not nearly as ancient as the somber 1830s family homes clustered on University Place and lower Fifth Avenue, with their heavy cabbage-rose garlanded carpets, dark rosewood consoles, black marble mantels, and towering mahogany glazed bookcases. Old Mrs. Mingott had built her home later, and with characteristic boldness had cast out all the heavy, respectable furniture of her youth, weaving Mingott family heirlooms through the gilded, frilly, frivolous upholstery of France’s Second Empire. She made a habit of sitting in her ground-floor parlor window, calm as a queen watching the life and bustle of the city creep slowly north toward her isolated doorstep. She was in no rush at all: she knew the hoardings, the stone quarries, the rickety one-story saloons, the ragged wooden greenhouses, and the goat-dotted rocky lots all around her would vanish soon enough, replaced by stately homes just as grand as hers, and the rutted cobblestones the old omnibuses rattled over would one day be smooth asphalt, just like the streets of Paris she’d heard travelers gush about. Everyone worth knowing came to her, after all; she filled her drawing rooms as easily as the notoriously lavish Beaufort couple, without adding a single extra dish to her supper menu, so her geographic isolation never bothered her at all.

The massive weight that had settled on her in middle age, like a lava flow swallowing a small city, had transformed her from a plump, lively little woman with neat small feet into something vast and august, a force of nature all her own. She’d accepted the change with the same quiet philosophy she brought to every trial, and in her extreme old age, her reward was a nearly unwrinkled expanse of firm pink-and-white skin, with the small features of her younger self peeking out from the center like a relic waiting to be unearthed. A flight of smooth double chins led down to a still-snowy bosom draped in fine muslin, pinned fast by a miniature portrait of the late Mr. Mingott, while wave after wave of black silk dress billowed out over the edges of her oversized armchair, two tiny white hands poised on top of the fabric like gulls drifting over the surface of the tide. Her weight had long since made climbing stairs impossible, so she’d done something unthinkable to every upstanding New Yorker: she’d moved all her reception rooms downstairs, and set up her own private bedroom on the ground floor too. Sitting with her in the parlor window, you could catch a clear glimpse through an always-open door and a looped-back yellow damask portière of her bedroom: a huge, low bed upholstered to look like a sofa, a vanity table draped in frilly lace, set with a gilded mirror. To her visitors, the arrangement felt shockingly foreign, straight out of a French romance novel, the kind of decadent one-floor apartments lovers hid in in the old wicked European capitals. Newland, who had always imagined the steamy love scenes of his favorite forbidden novel *Monsieur de Camors* playing out in exactly that kind of room, often smiled to think of blameless, iron-willed old Mrs. Mingott living her perfectly respectable life on a stage set built for adultery. He had no doubt, if a lover had ever been what she wanted, that intrepid old woman would have had him, conventions be damned.

To the whole family’s quiet relief, the Countess Olenska was nowhere to be seen in the parlor when they arrived. Mrs. Mingott said she’d gone out, a fact that raised a few quiet eyebrows—on such a bright, sunny day, in the thick of shopping hour, for a woman with her unhappy, compromised past, it felt just a touch unseemly. But it spared them all the awkwardness of her presence, and the faint shadow her messy history might cast over May and Newland’s glowing, perfect future.

The visit unfolded smoothly, as everyone expected it would. Old Mrs. Mingott was over the moon about the engagement; the match had been foreseen by watchful relatives for years, and carefully approved of in a private family council long before Newland had even worked up the courage to propose. The engagement ring, a big thick sapphire set in nearly invisible claws, won her full, unqualified admiration.
“It’s the new setting,” Mrs. Welland had explained, with a conciliatory little side-glance at her future son-in-law. “It shows the stone beautifully, of course, but it looks just a little bare to old-fashioned eyes.”
“Old-fashioned eyes? Don’t you dare mean mine, my dear! I adore every new novelty,” the matriarch laughed, lifting the sapphire up to her small, sharp eyes, which no pair of spectacles had ever touched. “Very handsome. Very generous. Back in my day, a cameo set in pearls was thought more than enough. But it’s the hand that makes the ring shine, isn’t it, my dear Mr. Archer?” she teased, waving one of her tiny hands, nails pointed and neat, rolls of aged fat circling her wrist like carved ivory bracelets. “My own hand was modelled in Rome by the great Ferrigiani. You ought to have May’s done too, no doubt he’ll see to that, my sweet. Her hands are a little large, what with all these modern outdoor sports that spread the joints, but her skin is perfectly white.” She broke off, fixing her sharp gaze straight on Archer’s face. “And when’s the wedding?”
“Oh—” Mrs. Welland fumbled, before Newland, smiling soft at his fiancée, answered: “As soon as it can possibly be, if you’ll back me up on it, Mrs. Mingott.”
“We must give them time to get to know each other a little better, mamma,” Mrs. Welland interposed, putting on the proper, demure show of reluctance.
“Know each other? Fiddlesticks!” the old matriarch scoffed. “Everyone in New York has known everyone else since they were toddling around in pinafores. Let the young man have his way, my dear—don’t wait till the bubble’s off the champagne. Marry them before Lent, I say. I could catch pneumonia any winter now, and I fully intend to be the one to host their wedding breakfast.”
The whole group laughed, murmured the proper words of amusement, gratitude, and gentle disbelief, and the visit was about to break up on that soft, merry note when the front door swung open. There stood the Countess Olenska, still in her bonnet and fur-lined mantle, and right behind her, the unexpected figure of Julius Beaufort.
The ladies exchanged soft, cousinly murmurs of pleased surprise, and Mrs. Mingott waved the banker over to her. “Ha, Beaufort! This is a rare treat. I wish you’d drop by far more often,” she said, addressing all men by their surnames in that odd, foreign way of hers.
“Thank you. I wish I could make the time,” he replied in his easy, arrogant drawl. “I’m usually far too tied up with business, but I ran into the Countess Ellen in Madison Square, and she was kind enough to let me walk home with her.”
“Ah! Now that Ellen’s here, the house will be far gayer!” Mrs. Mingott declared, unashamed. “Sit down—sit down, Beaufort, pull that yellow armchair over. Now that I’ve got you here, I want a good gossip. I hear your ball last week was magnificent. And I hear you invited Mrs. Lemuel Struthers? Well, I’ve a mind to meet that woman myself.” She’d already completely forgotten her other guests, who were drifting slowly out into the hall under Ellen Olenska’s guidance. Old Mrs. Mingott had always admired Julius Beaufort; they shared the same cool, domineering edge, the same habit of cutting straight through stuffy convention to get what they wanted. She was eager to hear exactly what had convinced the Beauforts to extend the first invitation to Mrs. Struthers, the widow who’d made her fortune selling shoe polish, and returned from a years-long stay in Europe determined to claw her way inside New York’s tight little social citadel. “Of course if you and Regina invite her, the thing is settled. We need new blood and new money around here—and I hear she’s still very good-looking,” the carnivorous old matriarch declared.
In the hall, while Mrs. Welland and May were slipping their fur gloves and cloaks on, Archer caught Countess Olenska looking at him with a faint, questioning smile.
“Of course you already know, about May and me,” he said, answering her look with a shy laugh. “She scolded me for not telling you the news last night at the Opera—she’d given me strict orders to break the news to you then, but I couldn’t, not in that heaving, noisy crowd.”
The smile spread from her eyes to her lips, and suddenly she looked years younger, exactly like the bold, brown-eyed Ellen Mingott he’d run wild with as a boy. “Of course I know. And I’m so very glad. You don’t tell news that sweet in the middle of a crowd, after all.” The other two ladies were already at the front step, and she held out her gloved hand to him. “Goodbye. Come and see me some day soon,” she said, her eyes still fixed on his.
Settled back in the carriage rumbling down Fifth Avenue, they all talked pointedly of nothing but Mrs. Mingott: her incredible age, her sharp spirit, all her wonderful, unorthodox little quirks. Not a single person said the name Ellen Olenska out loud. But Archer knew Mrs. Welland was turning it over in her head, thinking what a terrible impression it made, for a woman with Ellen’s past to be seen parading up Fifth Avenue at the busiest shopping hour on the arm of Julius Beaufort. And Newland found himself agreeing, a little: she ought to know that a man newly engaged has no business paying calls on married women, no matter how distant the family tie. But no doubt in the wild, unorthodox European circles she’d lived in for so long, that sort of thing was perfectly normal, they did nothing else all day. Even with all his proudly cosmopolitan, liberal views, he found himself thanking heaven he was a New Yorker, and soon to be allied for life to one of his own gentle, familiar kind.
