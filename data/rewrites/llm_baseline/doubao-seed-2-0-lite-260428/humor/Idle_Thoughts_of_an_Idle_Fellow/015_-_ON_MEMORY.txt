“I remember, I remember, In the days of chill November, How the blackbird on the—”

Dash it all, I’ve blanked on the rest. That’s the opening of the first proper poem I ever learned, mind you—none of that frivolous “Hey, diddle diddle, the cat and the fiddle” nonsense counts as real poetry, obviously. I raked in four whole pennies reciting that little verse for doting relatives, too. I knew it was exactly fourpence because they kept badgering me that if I held onto it long enough to earn two more, I’d have sixpence, a mathematical miracle even a small kid couldn’t argue with. Did that stop me? Not a bit. I blew every last penny the very next morning, to the best of my recollection. What on? Don’t ask. Memory’s gone completely blank on that score.

That’s Memory for you, isn’t it? A spoiled, stubborn little gremlin, and every single toy she drags out to show you is broken. I can vividly remember tripping and tumbling headfirst into a massive dust hole when I was tiny as anything, but I have zero recollection of ever climbing back out. If all I had to go on was my memory, I’d be fully convinced I’m still there, buried under three feet of ancient household sweepings.

A few years later, I was right in the middle of a very dramatic, very promising love scene—you know the one, the pauses, the soft hand touching, all the flowery lines I’d rehearsed for three whole days. The only thing I can still picture clearly about the whole moment? Right when we were about to kiss, some fool wrenches the door open and goes “Emily, you’re wanted” in a tone so grim you’d think the police had turned up to arrest her for grand theft. Every tender thing she whispered to me, every dazzling romantic line I spent hours perfecting? Poof. Vanished. Not a trace.

Looking back at your own life is like wandering around a half-ruined castle, isn’t it? There’s a shattered column where the grand front entrance used to be, a jagged bit of window frame marking where the pretty lady’s bedchamber stood, and a moldery heap of black rubble where the great fireplace once roared. And everything looks nicer when you see it through the soft, blurry haze of old time. Even the sad stuff that’s over tastes sweet, somehow.

Our boyhood days look like nothing but nutting trips, hoop races, and unlimited gingerbread now, don’t they? We’ve completely forgotten the snubs, the toothaches, and the Latin verb conjugations—especially the Latin verb conjugations. We look back at our awkward teen years when we were first falling in love and think we were the happiest creatures alive, and we’d give anything to feel that giddy rush again. We never spare a thought for the way our chest ached for weeks, the nights we laid awake staring at the ceiling, the dry, tight burn in our throat when she said she could only ever be a sister to us—like any man on earth needs more sisters!

Yep, when we glance backwards, we only see the sunshine, no shadows. The whole road we’ve already walked stretches out all nice and smooth behind us. We don’t spot the sharp stones that cut our socks and our feet, we only notice the roses growing along the edge, and the big thorny brambles that used to leave our ankles scratched raw? From far away they just look like soft green tendrils swaying gently in the breeze. Thank the good Lord for that, right? Every new link we add to that long, long chain of our memory is a nice one, and all the bitterness and grief that feels unbearable today will be something we smile at, no big deal, tomorrow.

All the bright, happy bits of our lives are the last to sink below the dark waves of forgetfulness, too. They stick up above the water, still in plain sight, long after all the angry thoughts and stinging pains are buried deep where they can’t bother us ever again.

That’s exactly why old people go on such absolute nonsense about the “good old days” when they were young, I reckon. Back then, according to them, the whole world was run properly. Boys were *real* boys, girls were way more proper than the ones nowadays, winters were actually cold and snowy like winters are supposed to be, and summers weren’t the sad, lukewarm, drizzly mess we get stuck with now. And the feats of daring they pulled off back then? You’d need three strapping burly men just to even *pretend* to believe half of it.

I love sitting and listening to one of these old blokes spin his tales to a crowd of kids who are way too polite to contradict him. Give him ten minutes, and he’ll be swearing that the moon shone every single night of his childhood, and tossing mad bulls up and down in a blanket was the standard after-school sport at his school. This isn’t new, either. The old folks back when our grandpas were teens sang the exact same tune. And today’s young lot will be spouting the exact same nonsense in fifty years, to annoy their own grandkids. “Oh, give me back the wonderful good old days!” people have been wailing ever since Adam turned 51 and looked back on his youth.

Dig up a book of poetry or a novel from 1835, and you’ll see the writers begging for that same impossible trip back, same as the old German minstrels before them, same as the Norse saga scribes, same as the ancient Greek philosophers and the earliest prophets. From everything anyone’s ever written, the world has been getting steadily worse ever since the day it opened for business. All I can say is, it must have been a right rollicking delight when it was brand new, because even now it’s pretty great if you stay mostly in the sunshine and don’t throw a fit when it rains.

Still, no use lying about it: the world must have been extra sweet back in that dewy, fresh morning of creation, before millions of tramping feet had ground all the soft grass to dust, before the constant roar of cities had chased all the quiet away. Those first loose-robed, barefoot ancestors of ours must have had such a noble, calm life, wandering under the big sky, living in sun-warmed tents next to their lowing herds, taking what they needed straight from kind Nature—no bills, no rush-hour trains, no French grammar exercises. That quiet, innocent childhood of the whole human race is gone forever now, though. We’re all grown up now, stuck in a noisy, chaotic world full of doubts and big hopes, and we’ve got work to do, so we’d better hurry along.

We don’t know what that big work even is, of course, what tiny little part we play in the whole grand design. We’re just like those little coral bugs working deep under the dark sea, scurrying around after our own small silly goals, with no clue we’re helping build a whole giant island for God.

So let’s stop moping and pining for days that are never ever coming back. Our work’s out in front of us, not behind. “Forward!” ought to be our motto. We shouldn’t sit around staring at the past like it’s the whole finished house—all it is, is the foundation. We waste so much time being sad about “what might have been” that we miss all the great stuff that still could be, right in front of our noses. Opportunities zoom past us while we’re still crying over the chances we already missed, and we don’t even notice the happiness that’s knocking at our door right now, because we’re too busy grieving the happiness that’s already gone.

I read a story once, ages ago, back when I used to sit by the fire and get lost in fairy tales, about a very brave, very true knight. He’d fought all sorts of monsters, traveled all over the world, everyone knew him as fearless—well, almost fearless, the kind of brave that makes sense, not the stupid kind where you fight a dragon with a teaspoon just to prove you’re tough. One day he’s riding down a terrible, hard road, and he’s worn out, and his heart’s heavy. Giant dark rocks hang over his head, ready to fall on him any second, chasms yawn on both sides full of robbers and fire-breathing dragons, and the whole path is pitch black like midnight. He decides he’s had enough, he’s turning back to find an easier road for his poor old horse. But when he twists around to look behind him? The entire road he just rode is gone. There’s nothing but a massive, bottomless chasm right behind his horse’s hooves, no way to go back at all. So he says a quick prayer to Saint Cuthbert, spurs his horse forward, and rides on as brave as can be. And nothing hurts him at all.

That’s the road of life, isn’t it? No U-turns allowed. Every single step you take, the little bit of time you just stood on sinks back into eternity forever. The past is gone, it’s locked away, it’s not yours to mess with anymore. You can never un-say a single dumb word, you can never step backwards even one tiny step. So we might as well be that brave knight, right? Ride forward cheerfully, don’t waste time sniveling because we can’t turn back. Every second gives you a whole new fresh life, why not go meet it happy? You’ve got to keep moving no matter what, anyway, and you’ll walk a whole lot steadier looking forward than staring over your shoulder the whole time.

The other day a friend came at me, all fired up, trying to make me buy some fancy memory system that lets you never forget a single thing ever. I don’t know why he was so insistent, unless it’s because I’m always “accidentally” borrowing his umbrellas, and once in the middle of a whist game I’ll blink and go “Wait a second, lads—did we decide clubs were trumps? I’ve been playing hearts for the last three rounds, sorry.” I turned him down flat, even though he listed all the perks. I don’t *want* to remember everything. Most people’s lives are full of stuff that really, truly deserves to be forgotten. That time ages ago you acted like a total fool, that little incident where you were downright dishonest and stupid and *everyone* found out, that one mean thing you said to someone you loved, that made you burn with shame for weeks? You already paid the price, didn’t you? You laid awake at night replaying it, you cringed so hard your face hurt, you got the cold shoulder from the people you cared about. Let’s forget it. Come on, Father Time, do us a favor, lift those awful heavy memories off our hearts. We’ve got more than enough new griefs coming our way every hour, and our strength is only enough for one day’s troubles at a time.

That doesn’t mean we should bury the whole past, of course. Life’s music would be completely silent if we snapped all the memory strings. We just need to yank out the nasty weeds, not dig up all the pretty flowers in Mnemosyne’s garden. Remember that guy in Dickens’ *The Haunted Man*? He begs to forget everything, right? And the second his wish comes true, he’s begging to get all his memories back again. We don’t need to chase away all the ghosts. We can keep the nice, gentle ones. Only the cruel, haggard, mean-eyed specters need to go.

Oh, the world gets so full of ghosts as you get older, you don’t even need to go hang around a spooky old graveyard or a moated manor house to see them. Every house, every old creaky chair, every little corner has its own ghost. They crowd around us like dead leaves swirling in the autumn wind, some of them still alive somewhere, some long dead. We held their hands, loved them, fought with them, laughed till our sides hurt, told them all our silliest hopes and dreams, back when we thought our friendship was so strong even Death couldn’t break it. Now they’re gone, their eyes will never look into ours again, we’ll never hear their voices. Only their ghosts come visit us, faint and wispy, through a haze of tears. You reach out to grab their hand, and they’re just air.

Ghosts are with us all the time. They walk next to you down the busy high street in the middle of the sunny day, they sit next to you by the fireside at twilight. You see their little faces peeking out the windows of your old elementary school, you run into them in the woods where you used to run around screaming and playing tag as a kid. Hark! Don’t you hear their laughter behind the blackberry bushes, and their shouts down the grassy lane? That little path through the fields where you used to wait for your first sweetheart at sunset? There she is right now, in that little white dress you thought was the prettiest thing you ever saw, bonnet dangling out of her hand, her brown hair all tangled. She’s five thousand miles away! Maybe even dead! Who cares? She’s right here next to you now, you can hear her laugh, you can see her eyes. She’ll vanish when you get to that stile by the woods, though, and you’ll be alone again, the shadows will creep over the fields, and the night wind will go moaning past.

We’d be far lonelier without all these ghosts, though. Come visit us, ghosts of our old playmates, our first sweethearts, our long-gone friends! The new people you meet later in life are never quite the same, you can never laugh or love with them the same way you did with those old pals when you were all young. The world was so bright and fun back then, and now it’s grown old and we’re all tired, and only those old ghosts can bring that old brightness back.

Memory is the best ghost-maker there is. It’s like living in a haunted house, your ears always picking up the sound of unseen feet padding around. Through the cracked old windows you watch all the flitting shadows of people who are gone, and the saddest shadows of all are the shadows of all your own old dead selves. Those bright, earnest young faces, full of big noble plans and honest hearts, staring back at you with their clear eyes, judging you a little? Oh, they’ve got every right to be disappointed. Since we were that 14-year-old kid, we’ve picked up lies, and cunning, and cynicism, when back then we swore we were going to grow up to be so good and so great. Thank God we can’t see the future, really. Hardly any 14-year-old would look at their 40-year-old self and not wince in shame.

I love sitting by the fire with my pipe at night, just chatting with that weird little kid that was me decades ago. I think he likes it too. I see his solemn little face peeking through the pipe smoke floating up, I smile at him, he smiles back, all grave and serious. We talk about the old days. Sometimes he grabs my hand, and we slip through the bars of the fireplace, right into that glowing little world behind the flames, back to all the old days we forgot. He tells me all his little thoughts. I laugh at him sometimes, but then I feel terrible, because he looks so serious I get all guilty. Also, that little kid is technically older than me, so it’s rude to make fun of him.

He tells me he can recite 6 whole fairy tales all the way through, he’s got a pet guinea pig, and his dad says fairy tales aren’t real, which he thinks is the biggest tragedy in the world, because he wants to be a knight and slay a dragon and marry a princess. When he turns seven, he decides that’s all silly, he wants to grow up to be a bargee, make loads of money, and marry the six-year-old girl who works behind the milk shop. He loves her so much he gives her his most prized possession: a giant pocket knife with four rusty blades and a corkscrew that always pops out by surprise and stabs him in the leg. She’s so happy she throws her arms around his neck and kisses him right there outside the shop, but the little boy who works next door at the cigar shop teases him, so he tries to punch that boy out… and gets punched instead.

Then there’s school, full of silly little tears and wild shouts, pranks, and hot tears dripping on those awful Latin grammars. He ruins his throat forever trying to pronounce German words, and learns those ridiculous French textbook conversations where every Frenchman you meet immediately asks you “Have you got pens, ink, and paper?” and you go “No, but my brother’s uncle has,” and they go “What about your mother’s neighbor?” and by the end no one has any pens ink or paper at all, but the guy just goes “Well, my aunt has mustard,” like that’s a perfectly normal follow-up.

Then the little short jacket gets grown-up tails, the beat-up cap you used as a handkerchief and a weapon turns into a fancy stiff top hat, you start smoking cigarettes that make the smoke go up your nose and choke, then try a big black cigar and end up leaning over a bucket in the back kitchen swearing you’ll never touch tobacco again. Then you get a wisp of a mustache, start drinking brandy sodas, lie about losing “thousands” at cards when you’ve actually lost one and twopence, stick an eyeglass in your eye and trip over every curb. Your poor aunts see visions of you getting dragged off to the Old Bailey and hanged, they’re so convinced you’re going to the bad. Then you fall in love, wear boots two sizes too small, comb your hair till it looks like a bird’s nest, and write terrible poetry about cruel hearts and pretty eyes, then your heart gets broken and you mope around walking for hours and losing sleep.

And then that boy’s gone. I’m standing alone on a dark road, tripping along, no light to guide me. Then the sun comes up, and I look down, and I’m just… me.
