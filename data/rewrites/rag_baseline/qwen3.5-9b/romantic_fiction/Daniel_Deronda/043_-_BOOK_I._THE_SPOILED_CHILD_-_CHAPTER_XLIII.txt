The silence that settled over the room was not empty; it was a heavy, sacred suspension, a held breath between worlds. Mordecai lay still, his consciousness drifting through the fog of fever until the unwonted quiet pierced through, anchoring him. He looked up at Deronda, and there was no trace of the bewildered stranger usually found in such encounters. Instead, his gaze was one of reposing satisfaction, a calm acceptance of a destiny being fulfilled.

Deronda rose immediately, drawing his chair closer until the distance between them vanished, eliminating the need for raised voices or strained projection. The movement struck Mordecai with the profound relief of a patient sensing the gentle adjustment of a pillow beneath his head. He began to speak in a low, intimate murmur, articulating thoughts as if speaking only to himself, yet every syllable carried the weight of a sermon.

"In the doctrine of the Cabbala," Mordecai whispered, his voice thin but resonant, "souls are born again and again in new vessels until they achieve perfection and purification. When a soul is liberated from a worn-out shell, it seeks the fellow-soul that requires it, that they may be perfected together, completing their earthly labors before departing the mortal realm to return to the eternal bosom. It is the lingering imperfection of souls already trapped in flesh that delays the birth of new souls and postpones the Messianic age. Thus, the mind gives shape to the hidden, casting a shadow of what is known, speaking truth even in parable. When my long-wandering spirit is finally freed from this weary body, it will join yours, and our shared work will be completed."

His pause hung in the air, an implicit plea that Deronda's empathy refused to ignore. He searched for words that were truthful, yet for Mordecai's listening ear, they were inevitably saturated with unspoken significance. "Everything I can, in conscience, do to make your life effective, I will do," Deronda said, his voice steady.

"I know it," Mordecai replied, his tone radiating a quiet certainty that rendered further reassurance unnecessary. "I heard it. You see it all—you stand beside me on the mountain of vision, beholding the paths of fulfillment that others deny." He fell silent for a moment, gathering his scattered memories, before continuing meditatively. "You will take up my life exactly where it was severed. I feel myself transported back to that day when my life broke. The bright morning sun was blazing on the quay at Trieste; the garments of men from every nation shimmered like jewels; the boats pushed off into the harbor. The Greek vessel destined to carry us to Beirut was to depart within the hour. I was traveling with a merchant as his clerk and companion. I told myself, 'I shall behold the lands and peoples of the East, and I shall speak with a fuller vision.' I breathed then as you breathe now, without labor; I possessed the light step and the enduring vitality of youth. I could fast, I could sleep on the hardest ground. I had wedded poverty, and I loved my bride—for to me, poverty was freedom. My heart exulted as if it belonged to Moses ben Maimon, strengthened by the vigor of sixty years, knowing the vast work that awaited. It was the first time I had ventured south; the soul within me felt its former sun. Standing on the quay, where the earth seemed to emit light and the shadows wore an azure glory as if spirits had become visible, I felt myself immersed in a flood of glorious life, wherein my own small, counted existence melted away until I knew it no more. A great sob rose within me, akin to the rush of waters too powerful to resist—a bliss so overwhelming it nearly drowned me. So I stood there, awaiting my companion, and I saw him not until he said, 'Ezra, I have been to the post; there is your letter.'"

"Ezra!" Deronda exclaimed, unable to suppress the shock that surged through him.

"Ezra," Mordecai repeated, affirmative and utterly engrossed in the memory. "I was expecting a letter; for I wrote continually to my mother. And that sound of my name was like the touch of a wand that recalled me to the body from whence I had been released, as if to mingle once more with the ocean of human existence, free from the crushing pressure of individual bondage. I opened the letter; and the name came again as a cry that would have disturbed me even in the bosom of heaven, making me yearn to reach out to that sorrow—'Ezra, my son!'"

Mordecai paused again, his imagination arrested by the sheer grip of that long-past moment. Deronda's mind hovered breathless on the precipice of what was coming. A strange, terrifying possibility had suddenly crystallized in the air. Mordecai's eyes were cast downward in abstracted contemplation, and shortly, he continued, "She was a mother of whom it might rightly be said, 'Her children arise up and call her blessed.' In her, I understood the meaning of that Master who, perceiving the footsteps of his mother, rose and declared, 'The Majesty of the Eternal cometh near!' That letter was her cry from the depths of anguish and desolation—the scream of a mother robbed of her little ones. I was her eldest. Death had taken four babes one after another. Then came, late, my little sister, who was, more than all the rest, the desire of my mother's eyes. And the letter was a piercing cry to me—'Ezra, my son, I am robbed of her. He has taken her away and left disgrace behind. They will never come again.'"

Here, Mordecai lifted his eyes suddenly, laying his hand firmly on Deronda's arm. "Mine was the lot of Israel. For the sin of the father, my soul must go into exile. For the sin of the father, the work was broken, and the day of fulfillment delayed. She who bore me was desolate, disgraced, destitute. I turned back. On the instant I turned—her spirit and the spirit of her fathers, who had worthy Jewish hearts, moved within me and drew me. God, in whom dwells the universe, was within me as the strength of obedience. I turned and traveled with hardship—to save the scant money which she would need. I left the sunshine and traveled into freezing cold. In the final stage, I spent a night exposed to the biting cold and snow. And that was the beginning of this slow death."

Mordecai let his eyes wander again, removing his hand. Deronda resolutely suppressed the questions that urgently demanded expression. While Mordecai remained in this state of raw emotion, no other confidence could be sought than what emerged spontaneously; indeed, Deronda felt a kindred emotion that made him dread his own speech as too momentous.

"But I worked. We were destitute—everything had been seized. And she was ill: the clutch of anguish was too strong for her, working alongside some lurking disease. At times she could not stand for the pounding of her heart, and the images in her brain became chambers of terror, where she beheld my sister reared in evil. In the dead of night, I heard her crying for her child. Then I rose, and we stretched forth our arms together and prayed. We poured forth our souls in desire that Mirah might be delivered from evil."

"Mirah?" Deronda repeated, wishing desperately to assure himself that his ears had not been deceived by a forecasting imagination. "Did you say Mirah?"

"That was my little sister's name. After we had prayed for her, my mother would rest awhile. It lasted hardly four years, and in the minute before she died, we were praying the same prayer—I aloud, she silently. Her soul went out upon its wings."

"Have you never since heard of your sister?" Deronda asked, as quietly as he dared.

"Never. Never have I heard whether she was delivered according to our prayer. I know not, I know not. Who shall say where the pathways lie? The poisonous will of the wicked is strong. It poisoned my life—it is slowly stifling this breath. Death delivered my mother, and I felt it a blessedness that I was alone in the winters of suffering. But what are the winters now?—they are far off"—here Mordecai again rested his hand on Deronda's arm, and looked at him with that joy of the hectic patient which pierces us to sadness—"there is nothing to wail in the withering of my body. The work will be the better done. Once I said the work of this beginning was mine, I am born to do it. Well, I shall do it. I shall live in you. I shall live in you."

His grasp had become convulsive in its force. Deronda, agitated as he had never been before—the certainty that this was Mirah's brother suffusing his own strange relation to Mordecai with a new solemnity and tenderness—felt his strong young heart beating faster and his lips paling. He shrank from speech. He feared, in Mordecai's present state of exaltation (already an alarming strain on his feeble frame), to utter a word of revelation about Mirah. He feared to make an answer below that high pitch of expectation which resembled a flash from a dying fire, causing watchers to fear to see it die the faster. His dominant impulse was to do as he had once done before: he laid his firm, gentle hand on the hand that grasped him.
