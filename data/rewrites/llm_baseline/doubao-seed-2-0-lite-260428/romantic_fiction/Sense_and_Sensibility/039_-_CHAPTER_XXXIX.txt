More than two months had slipped by since the Dashwood sisters first arrived in London, and Marianne’s longing to flee the smoky, crowded city sharpened with every passing hour. She pined for the sharp, sweet air of Devonshire lanes, for the quiet of Barton Cottage’s orchard, for the unhurried liberty of days that didn’t revolve around stiff drawing-room calls and hollow society small talk. She was certain no place on earth could soothe her still raw, unhealed grief half so well as their little home beside the sea.

Elinor was nearly as eager to leave, too — the only difference between them was that she had the good sense to anticipate how grueling a full day’s cross-country journey would be for Marianne in her fragile state, and refused to rush their departure recklessly. She had only just broached the subject of their return with their warm, generous hostess Mrs. Jennings, who protested the plan with every bit of affectionate eloquence she possessed, when a far better arrangement presented itself. The Palmers planned to relocate to their Somerset estate of Cleveland for the Easter holidays, and Charlotte had sent a gushingly enthusiastic note begging Mrs. Jennings and both the Dashwood sisters to come stay with them. Even the usually gruff, uninterested Mr. Palmer had added his own sincere welcome to the invitation, and his manners toward the sisters had softened considerably ever since he learned of Marianne’s heartbreak, so Elinor accepted at once, despite her initial hesitation to impose.

When she told Marianne the news, the girl’s reaction was far from hopeful. “Cleveland?” she cried, pale and agitated. “No — I cannot go there.”
“You forget,” Elinor soothed, “it is nowhere near the place you once thought you would move to —”
“But it is in Somersetshire,” Marianne gasped. “That is the county I dreamed I would settle in, with — no, Elinor, you cannot ask me to set foot there.”

Elinor did not argue that she ought to simply overcome those old, painful feelings. Instead she appealed to the love Marianne held dearest: their mother. This route, she explained, would make the rest of their journey home far softer and easier. Cleveland was only a few miles outside Bristol, and from there the ride to Barton would take a single long, leisurely day — their mother could even send their trusted old family servant to meet them at Cleveland, so they would not have to travel the full distance alone. They would not need to stay at Cleveland for more than a week, at most, and they could be home hugging their mother again in barely three weeks’ time. Marianne’s love for Mrs. Dashwood outstripped every lingering, imaginary sting of old heartbreak, and she soon agreed. She spent the rest of the night scribbling out an hour-by-hour countdown to the moment the doors of Barton Cottage would swing open for them.

Mrs. Jennings, who had grown far too fond of the girls to see them go, begged them to come back and stay with her in London after their visit to Cleveland, but Elinor gently declined. Once Mrs. Dashwood sent back her eager agreement to their travel plans, every last detail of their journey home was settled.

“Ah, Colonel!” Mrs. Jennings cried to Brandon, the very first time he called after their departure plans were finalized, “I don’t know what on earth you and I are going to do without the Miss Dashwoods! They’re determined to head straight home from the Palmers’ estate, and when I come back to London after Easter, the two of us will be sat here gawping at each other as dull as a pair of napping cats.” She hoped, of course, that this dramatic vision of their future boredom would push the quiet colonel to propose to Elinor, so they would all avoid that tedious fate — and it seemed her little scheme had worked. Elinor had stepped over to the window to measure a print she planned to copy for a friend, and Brandon followed her, his face soft with unspoken meaning, and talked to her there for several minutes.

Mrs. Jennings was far too honorable to eavesdrop openly, so she deliberately moved her chair right next to the piano forte where Marianne was playing, but she could not help but notice Elinor’s cheeks flush bright pink, that she grew visibly flustered, and that she was so focused on whatever Brandon was saying that she completely forgot to keep measuring her print. When Marianne paused between two pieces, Mrs. Jennings caught a snippet of Brandon’s low voice, where he was apologizing for how small and plain his house was at Delaford. That settled it for her — of course that was the proper etiquette for a man on the verge of proposing, to warn his future wife his home was not grand. She could see Elinor’s lips form the shape of words that said the size of the house did not matter at all, and she mentally cheered for her sensible, unpretentious friend.

A second lull in Marianne’s playing let her catch another line of Brandon’s calm, steady voice: “I am afraid it cannot take place very soon.”
Mrs. Jennings nearly jumped out of her skin. What a ridiculous, unromantic thing to say to a woman you are proposing to! “Good Lord, what could possibly stop it?” she thought to herself, holding back the exclamation. “Surely he doesn’t think he needs to wait to grow older before marrying?”

But Elinor did not look offended or upset in the slightest. When their conversation ended, and they stepped apart, Mrs. Jennings heard her say, soft and full of heartfelt sincerity, “I shall always think myself very much obliged to you.” Mrs. Jennings was delighted at her obvious happiness, and very confused when the colonel took his leave a minute later, perfectly calm and collected, no grand romantic gesture in sight, as if he had not just got engaged! She could not believe her old quiet friend made such an unenthusiastic suitor.

What they had actually discussed was nothing of the sort.
“I have heard,” Brandon said to Elinor, his voice full of gentle compassion, “of the terrible injustice Mr. Ferrars has suffered at the hands of his family. If I have the story right, he has been cast off entirely by his mother for refusing to break his engagement to a young woman who is perfectly worthy of him. Is that true?”
Elinor confirmed that it was.
“The cruelty of tearing apart two people who have loved each other for years is unforgivable,” he said, shaking his head. “Mrs. Ferrars has no idea what harm she might do, what desperate measures she might drive her son to. I met Mr. Ferrars a few times in Harley Street, and I like him very much. He is not the sort of man to open up quickly to strangers, but I have seen enough of him to wish him well for his own sake — and as a friend of yours, I wish him well far more. I hear he intends to take orders as a clergyman. Would you be so kind as to tell him that the rectory of Delaford, my living, arrived vacant in this morning’s post? It is his, if he will accept it. I wish it paid more than the late incumbent’s two hundred pounds a year — it is a small enough income, and while it can be improved, it will never make him rich. But it will keep him comfortable as a bachelor, and nothing would make me happier than to give it to him.”

Elinor was so shocked she could not speak for a full minute. Barely 48 hours earlier, she had thought Edward’s fate was utterly hopeless, that he would never have the means to make a life for himself — and now this good, kind man was handing him a lifeline, just out of the goodness of his heart. The rush of gratitude she felt was so strong it brought tears to her eyes. She thanked him over and over, sang Edward’s praises, and promised to deliver the message that very day — she knew Edward was still in town, and had his exact address from Miss Steele. She tried to convince Brandon to tell Edward the news himself, but he gently refused, saying he knew Edward would feel awkward accepting such a favor from a relative stranger, and would be far more comfortable hearing it from her.

That was when Brandon mentioned, apologetically, that his house at Delaford was small and plain — the line that had made Mrs. Jennings assume he was talking about a shared marital home. Elinor waved the concern away at once. “The size of the house will not inconvenience them at all, it will be perfectly suited to their small household and their income.”
Brandon blinked, surprised she assumed Edward would marry right away. “This little rectory can only keep him comfortable as a single man,” he explained. “It is nowhere near enough of an income to support a wife. My patronage does not extend any further than this, and I do not have enough influence to secure him more. If I ever am able to do more to help him in future, you may rest assured I will. But as things stand now, this barely brings him any closer to the happiness he wants most. His marriage must still be a long way off — I am afraid it cannot take place very soon.”

That was the line that Mrs. Jennings had so wildly misinterpreted. When the two of them parted, and Elinor told him she would always be deeply obliged to him, it was not because a marriage proposal had been accepted — it was because the quietest, most generous man she knew had just handed the man she loved more hope than she had dared to dream of for months.
