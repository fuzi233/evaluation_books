The sky lay low and pewter-grey over Florence; yet in the Piazza del Duomo that sober weather was almost an afterthought, pushed back by man-made brightness. Blue draperies stretched like a second firmament between the stone fronts, and against that borrowed heaven the lilies blazed in yellow bunches, the coats of arms repeating themselves like stamped wafers on a feast-day loaf. At the angles of the Baptistery the sheaves of banners had already been loosened and let fall, their edges lifting and settling with each little gust as if breathing; but the Duomo steps were still bare—no carpet yet—only the chill, pale marble, dulled by the tread of many ordinary soles.

It was the hour of the Advent sermons. Those very reasons which had dressed the piazza in holiday color were reasons why the preaching within could least be spared; and still, not every foot turned toward the great doors. The piazza swelled and ebbed with men moving briskly as if errands had sprouted from the stones; and there were knots of idlers who did not idle—faces angled with vigilance, heads bent close in quick talk, ears pricked for the next scrap of report. Above it all, bells and voices made a restless canopy of sound: the distant call of a vendor under an archway, the clack of shoes on paving, the scrape of a cartwheel somewhere in a side street—each noise like a pebble tossed into the same troubled pool.

At the corner that looked toward the Via de’ Cerretani—where the piazza’s painted rainbow of festoons ended and the grey morning resumed its reign among the sombre houses—stood a dense cluster of working people. Their clothing wore the signatures of their trades: wool-dust ingrained in sleeves, grease-dark smears on aprons, the stiffened patches where dye had splashed and dried. Many carried weapons; others held tools that could become weapons with a mere change of intention. The feel of their bodies in that colder, unadorned street was like a different season intruding—bare brawny arms, soiled cloth, the metallic scent of iron and sweat.

They were listening to Ser Cioni, a thin notary whose voice had the bite of vinegar. Two years and a half ago the Mercato had answered him with laughter and contempt; now he spoke with the complacent edge of a man who finds his words landing where he meant them to.

“Never talk to me,” he was saying, “never talk to me of bloodthirsty Swiss or fierce French infantry. They might as well be in the narrow mountain passes as in our streets. Peasants have destroyed the finest armies of our condottieri, once they were caught between precipices. I tell you, Florentines need fear no army in their own streets.”

“That’s true, Ser Cioni,” answered a man whose arms and hands were stained crimson with dye so that, from a distance, they looked dipped in blood. A small hatchet sat in his belt like a promise. “Those French cavaliers came squaring themselves in their smart doublets the other day, and saw a sample of the dinner we could serve up for them. I was carrying my cloth in Ognissanti when I saw my fine Messeri go by, turning their noses as if the Vespucci and the Agli were poor lodgings, and eyeing us like top-knotted cocks that pity a hen for not strutting. ‘Yes, my fine Galli,’ says I, ‘stick out your stomachs—I’ve got a meat-axe that will go inside you all the easier.’ And presently the old cow lowed, and I knew something had happened—no matter what. I threw my cloth into the first doorway, took hold of my meat-axe, and ran after my cavaliers toward the Vigna Nuova. ‘What is it, Guccio?’ says I, when he came up. ‘I think it’s the Medici coming back,’ says he. Bembè! I expected so! We reared a barricade, and the Frenchmen looked behind and saw themselves in a trap; and up came a swarm of our Ciompi, and one with a great scythe mowed off a fine cavalier’s feather—it’s true! And the lasses peppered a few stones down to frighten them. But Piero de’ Medici wasn’t come after all. Pity, for we’d have left him neither legs nor wings to go away with.”

“Well spoken, Oddo,” said a young butcher, his knife at his belt and his voice smelling of slaughterhouse boldness. “My belief is Piero will be a good while before he wants to come back; he looked as frightened as a hunted chicken when we hustled and pelted him in the piazza. He’s a coward. We’ll swallow no Medici any more, whatever else the French king wants to make us swallow.”

“But I like not those French cannon they talk of,” put in Goro, none the less fat for two years’ added grievances. “San Giovanni defend us! If Messer Domeneddio means so well by us as your Frate says he does, Ser Cioni, why shouldn’t he have sent the French another way to Naples?”

“Ay, Goro,” said the dyer, with a laugh that was not quite merry, “that’s a question worth putting. Thou’rt not such a pumpkin-head as I took thee for. Why, they might have gone by Bologna, eh, Ser Cioni? Or if they’d gone to Arezzo—we’d not have minded their going to Arezzo.”

“Fools! It will be for the good and glory of Florence—” Ser Cioni began, but his sentence snapped short. Several voices broke out at once with, “Look there!” and faces swung toward a party advancing along the Via de’ Cerretani.

“It’s Lorenzo Tornabuoni,” someone said, “and one of the French noblemen who lodge in his house.”

Ser Cioni’s mouth tightened. “He pretends to look well satisfied—that deep Tornabuoni—but he’s a Medicean in his heart: mind that.”

The approaching group was brilliant, as if a strip of courtly tapestry had been unrolled into the street. Lorenzo Tornabuoni’s presence carried its own distinction; beside him the Frenchman glittered with displayed white linen and embroidery so lavish it seemed stitched from the spoils of altars. Two other Florentine gentlemen wore handsome dresses meant for the coming procession. And on the left hand of the Frenchman walked a figure who, for all his plainness, drew the eye as a dark wine draws it among pale watered drinks.

Tito Melema wore only black. Mourning made his elegance severer, like a well-forged blade left unadorned; presently the black would be covered with a red mantle, for he was to walk in procession as Latin Secretary to the Ten. He had grown conspicuously serviceable to the city’s new guests—his familiarity with Southern Italy, his readiness in French learned in boyhood, his visits to the camp at Signa. Good fortune gave him a lustre that was not merely in his clothes: it sat on his lips in a smile, in his voice as he listened and explained with that easy grace which seemed to promise agreement before it was asked for.

Yet if one watched him closely—closer than a casual glance that takes the surface polish—there was a change not to be measured by eighteen months. Something had withdrawn from him, something like the dew that vanishes as morning grows conscious of itself: the final departure of moral youthfulness, the distinct self-conscious adoption of a part. His eyes were still clear; the lines of his face still soft; but the light within had a different angle, as if it now shone through a chosen mask.

The Frenchman, eager to carry ceremonial knowledge back to Signa, wanted a final survey of the piazza where the royal procession would pause for religious observance. As they entered, gazes gathered like birds to scattered grain—yet not all were admiring. There were remarks, not especially hushed, about the Frenchman’s hoof-shaped shoes, those royal excesses of toe. There were snarls about “Mediceans,” and no one cared to tuck the sound away safely out of foreign hearing.

But Lorenzo Tornabuoni had the practiced art of swallowing annoyance without showing the throat move; and Tito, besides a natural knack for softening ill-will with good humor, carried the unimpassioned detachment of the alien. Florentine names and inherited hatreds did not strike him in the blood as they struck those born to them; their weight sat in his mind rather than his marrow.

They halted where the Duomo could be seen obliquely, the great dome looming like a patient animal under its grey sky. The festoons and devices over the central doorway drew a little demur from the Frenchman, and Tornabuoni beckoned to Piero di Cosimo, who, as usual at that hour, lounged before Nello’s shop with the air of a man who let the world happen for his amusement.

Piero came, and soon their talk grew animated. The Frenchman’s astonishment at Piero’s odd, pungent statements turned it into a comedy; and Tito, translating literally, became the hinge on which the laughter swung. Even those who had been snarling found their curiosity stronger than their sourness. Faces wore that half-smiling, half-humiliated look of people excluded from a joke that is plainly delicious. The piazza’s blue draperies and yellow lilies, the rainbow light they created, fell around the laughing group; and the grave church-goers had disappeared within the Duomo walls. For an instant it seemed the city had remembered how to keep a holiday.

But beyond the piazza’s bright cloth and contrived cheer, the unadorned streets remained in their grey temper; and from that greyness came on-comers whose purpose had nothing to do with brocade and processions. Here too the French dress and hoofed shoes were visible—yet now they were pressed upon by a swelling number of Florentines whose looks were not friendly.

In the van of the advancing tumult were three men in scanty clothing. Each had his hands bound together with cord; each had a rope fastened about his neck and body so that the French soldier holding the loose end could check rebellion with a jerking threat of throttling. The soldiers, by broken Italian and occasional strokes of the knotted cord, drove their prisoners to beg.

Two obeyed. At every passer-by they held out their bound hands and pleaded in thin, piteous tones, “For the love of God and the Holy Madonna, give us something toward our ransom! We are Tuscans: we were made prisoners in Lunigiana.”

But the third man remained silent beneath all blows. He did not look like the others. Those were young and hardy and, in the clothing their captors’ avarice had left them, resembled stout vulgar mendicants. This man had crossed into old age and could hardly be less than four or five and sixty. A beard had grown long and neglected; hair fell thick and straight around the bald crown, nearly white like ash left after a fire. His thick-set figure was still upright though emaciated, holding a stubborn dignity as if bone remembered strength even when flesh had thinned. In the midst of his yellow, bloodless, deeply wrinkled face, his dark eyes and strong dark eyebrows kept a strange isolated intensity—as if two embers had refused to go out. Yet the eyes were fitful: after a quick fierce sweep over windows and faces, they dropped into a lost wandering look. Still his lips did not move. He held his hands down. He would not beg.

Florentines who had first witnessed this had given money readily: some in half-automatic obedience to an appeal that used God’s name like a key to old locks; others through the unquestioning awe created by tales of French cruelty—the very awe the French counted on as insurance for their insolence. But the farther the group pushed toward the city’s heart, the less that compliance held. The soldiers found themselves escorted by a growing troop of men and boys whose chorus needed little translation.

“French dogs!” “Bullock-feet!” “Snatch their pikes!” “Cut the cords and make them run for their prisoners! They’ll run as fast as geese—don’t you see they’re web-footed?”

The soldiers understood enough: the tone, the laughter, the forward pressure. They began to dislike their position. Their hands wanted weapons, but their grip had to stay on the ropes. They hurried, seeking the shelter of an inn.

“Santiddio! here’s a sight!” cried the dyer, as soon as the meaning of the tumult struck him like a fist. “And the fools do nothing but hoot. Come along!” He snatched the axe from his belt and ran, the butcher and the others streaming after him—save only Goro, who slipped away up a narrow passage as if swallowed by stone.

The dyer did not strike anyone; he merely pushed forward beyond the soldiers and planted himself among his fellows, flourishing his axe. But in that act he became a symbol, like the lifting of a familiar gonfalon. The crowd’s noise tightened; bodies pressed nearer; the air grew sharp with human breath and anger.

Then the first flame of action leapt—quick as the flick of a kitchen fire catching on a shred of dry rosemary. It was Lollo, the conjuror’s impish lad, dancing and jeering at the front, a knife always ready at his side like a secret he loved. Compassion for the prisoners was not his motive; he simply felt the mischief in his fingers: to jump forward, cut a rope, and spring back before the soldier could strike—an amusing piece of dexterity.

Now, with the hooting and jostling rising, Lollo took his moment. He was close to the oldest prisoner. In an instant his knife flashed, the cord parted like a snapped string.

“Run, old one!” he piped into the prisoner’s ear, and he himself darted away as if winged, like a frightened fowl.

The old man did not hesitate. Escape had sat in his mind like hunger; and the crowd’s temper had fed it into hope. He ran at once. His speed alone would not have saved him, but the Florentines surged between him and his captor, a living wall of elbows and shoulders. He ran into the piazza, hearing behind him the tramp and scramble as the other two prisoners were freed too. The soldiers fought their way after them, slow-footed in their hoof-shaped shoes, impeded by bodies and blows—more hindered than truly attacked, for many preferred shouting invitations to courage rather than paying its cost.

One of the younger prisoners turned up the Borgo di San Lorenzo and drew off part of the hubbub like a diverted stream. But the main struggle rolled toward the piazza, and eyes swung round in alarmed curiosity. From the outside one could not guess the cause precisely; the French dress was screened by the mass of Florentines.

“An escape of prisoners,” said Lorenzo Tornabuoni as he and his party turned, standing just against the Duomo steps, and saw a prisoner rush past. “The people are not content with having emptied the Bargello the other day. If no other authority is in sight they will fall on the sbirri and secure freedom to thieves. Ah—there is a French soldier. That is more serious.”

He had glimpsed a soldier struggling along the north side of the piazza; but the object of pursuit had chosen another direction. The eldest prisoner, wheeling round the Baptistery, ran toward the Duomo. Whatever bitterness he held against men, he still trusted stones that had heard prayers for centuries. Sanctuary was surer than his legs.

He reached the steps and began to mount; but his foot struck a shock against the marble edge, and he pitched forward toward the group of signori, their backs turned. He recovered balance only by clutching at an arm.

It was Tito Melema who felt that clutch: soiled, worn fingers closing on velvet, a grasp that was not a petition but a claim.

Tito turned his head.

He saw the face of his adoptive father, Baldassarre Calvo, close to his own.

They looked at each other, silent as death. Baldassarre’s dark eyes burned with fierce recognition; his grip tightened on Tito’s sleeve as if he would haul out truth by force. Tito’s cheeks and lips went bloodless; terror held him in a fascination so complete it was like a spell. For them the piazza’s sounds fell away—the shouts, the bell-notes, the scrape of shoes—until there was only the roaring within the skull.

It seemed long. It was no more than a moment.

The first sound Tito truly heard was Piero di Cosimo’s short laugh, close at hand; Piero was the only one placed to see Tito’s face.

“Ha, ha! I know what a ghost should be now.”

“This is another escaped prisoner,” said Lorenzo Tornabuoni, leaning to look. “Who is he, I wonder?”

Tito’s mouth moved before his mind had finished deciding. In such instants passion speaks and chooses, and afterward one can only stand by and watch the deed as if it belonged to someone else.

“Some madman, surely,” Tito said.

The words, once out, seemed to carry their own poison. Tito felt, as he kept his eyes on Baldassarre, that something like a dart shot from those eyes into his veins. But the next instant Baldassarre’s grasp loosened. He did not cry out. He did not plead. He slipped away like a shadow drawn back into a doorway, and disappeared within the Duomo.
