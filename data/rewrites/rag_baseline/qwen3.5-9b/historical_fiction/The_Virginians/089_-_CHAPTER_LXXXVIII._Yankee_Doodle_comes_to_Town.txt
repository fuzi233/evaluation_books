The colonies served as a dumping ground for the sins of the mother country. Convicts and indentured servants were shipped out, yes, but so too were the scapegraces, the younger sons, and the broken spirits for whom the old world had become unbearable. Like Cook distributing English beasts to his new islands, we exported our black sheep to browse and multiply in the wilderness. I had played a small part in this exodus, securing a colonial pulpit for Hagan, my kinsman, because England offered him no livelihood. He performed his duties with a brave honesty that credited my patronage, even as Madam Theo insisted my actions sprang from the highest moral principles. Yet, privately, I confess to a relief when she retreated to the wilderness to work out her penance. I would not have chosen her for our parish at home. Her kindness was real; she bore her own sorrows and soothed others with a devotion that shamed the rest of us. Still, we looked down on her. Oh, the shame of our pride.

She was not alone in this displacement. The Honourable William Esmond, stripped of the ability to cheat, borrow, or steal further at home, was settled in New York. His noble brother wished to forget him entirely. When the troubles erupted, William became a loud voice among the Loyalists in New York, rising to Captaincy and then Majorcy of volunteer corps. He declared publicly that Castlewood belonged only to his brother's pleasure, that his father had granted the estate to Madam Esmond for life, and that William sought to buy the reversion. We possessed the deed in our strongroom at Castlewood, registered at Williamsburg, rendering his claims legally null. But intentions mattered more than deeds. Hal and I resolved to confront him and expose the lie.

Madam Esmond's reaction was predictable. "My father, the Marquis of Esmond, a liar? And I, a cheat?" she cried, fearing he would seize her son's inheritance. She prepared letters to Lord Castlewood and the King at St. James's, stopped only by my assurance that William's duplicity was notorious and unlikely to yield truth in our specific case. Soon, news reached us of his activities: organizing loyalist addresses, promising to die for the mother country. Unable to bear the presence of such a man, Madam Esmond abandoned our rented house at Williamsburg. My family returned to Richmond, now deserted by the dissolved Assembly. Captain Hal and his wife returned to their plantation. I, annoyed by the drift of events, split my time between my own household and that of the Governor, whose eagerness for my counsel masked deep political fractures. Though the Assembly had adjourned to a tavern to propose a continental congress—a prelude to war—the opposition leaders remained friendly with the Governor, sharing his hospitality and pleasures. Once sessions ended, the gentry dispersed to hold county meetings. Everywhere, assemblies were dissolved, and a general congress was agreed upon. Philadelphia was chosen, and the conferences began, angry preludes to conflict.

We still sang "God Save the King," still petitioned the throne. But one morning, riding to visit my brother Harry at Fanny's Mount, separated from us by the Rappahannock, I learned from a groom that Master Harry had ridden toward Willis's Ordinary. Thinking no harm, I followed. Emerging from the trees onto a clearing, I found Captain Hal on horseback, surrounded by thirty or forty countrymen armed with pikes, scythes, fowling-pieces, and muskets. Officers stood nearby, drilling the men. As I approached, a strange expression crossed Hal's face.

"Present arms!" he commanded, and the ragged line attempted the salute.

"Captain Cade, this is my brother, Sir George Warrington," Hal announced.

"As a relation of yours, Colonel," the man addressed as Captain replied, extending a hand. "The gentleman is welcome."

"And—and a true friend to Virginia," Hal added, his face flushing.

"Yes, please God, gentlemen," I said. The regiment erupted in a hearty huzzah for the Colonel and his brother.

Once the drill concluded, the officers and men moved toward Willis's for refreshments, but Hal declined to drink that afternoon. We trotted homeward together.

"So, Hal, the cat's out of the bag!" I remarked.

He shot me a hard look. "I suspect there are wilder cats in it. It must come to this, George. Do not tell Madam."

"Good God!" I cried. "Do you mean that with fellows like those I saw, you intend to fight the greatest nation and its finest army?"

"I guess we'll get an awful whipping," Hal admitted grimly. "That's the fact. But then, George," he added, flashing his sweet, kind smile, "we are young. A whipping or two might do us good. Won't it do us good, Dolly, you old slut?" He playfully touched the whip to an old mongrel running beside him.

I had urged him before, in vain, to consider our British allegiance. He dismissed my arguments with a shrug: "You speak as an Englishman, casting your lot with your country; I have cast mine with mine." There was no answer to that logic. All that remained was for the stronger side to prevail. In the wars of the last century, which side held the right? The King or Parliament? The victors defined the right, and historically, the winners proved more humane than the Cavaliers would have been had they triumphed. Had the Tories won in America, the bloodshed would have been frightful—ropes, scaffolds, noble heads severed. A strange paradox: I stood on the Loyalist side yet wished for the Whigs to win.

Hal, however, distinguished himself with his regiment yet refused to insult the enemy. "The officers of the British army are gentlemen," he often said. "At least, they haven't changed much since my time. There may be scoundrels and ruffians among them, certainly among our own. Our business is to beat His Majesty's forces, not to call them names; any rascal can do that." From a name given to him by Mr. Lee, which many of his rough horsemen failed to grasp, Harry was frequently dubbed "Chevaleer Baird" in the Continental ranks. He was a knight, truly, without fear and without reproach.

When I asked how such a motley crew could stand against the British army, Hal answered with confidence. "They can beat them, Mr. George. That's what they can do."

"Great heavens!" I exclaimed. "Do you mean with your company of Wolfe's you'd hesitate to attack five hundred such?"

"With my company of the 67th, I would go anywhere. Agreed with you, I know more of soldiering than they at this moment—but place me on that open ground where you found us, armed as you please, with half a dozen friends and rifles hidden in the woods around me. Who gets the better? You know best, Mr. Braddock's aide-de-camp!"

There was no arguing with such certainty.

"You know my way of thinking, Hal," I said. "Having surprised you at your work, I must tell my lord what I have seen."

"Tell him, of course. You've seen our county militia exercising. You'll see the same in every colony from here to Saint Lawrence or Georgia. As an old soldier, they elected me colonel. What more natural?"

"Come, brother, let us trot on. Dinner will be ready, and Mrs. Fan does not like me to keep it waiting."

We headed for his house, open and welcoming to friend, neighbor, stranger, and traveler alike.

"So, Mrs. Fan," I said, "I have discovered what game my brother has been playing."

"I trust the Colonel will have plenty of sport ere long," she replied, tossing her head.

My wife believed Harry was hunting. I did not wish to undeceive her, though what I had witnessed and what he had told me filled me with natural anxiety.
