The two hours of waiting stretched before me like an endless night. I could not sit still, nor could I speak to anyone—my mind was too restless, my thoughts too tangled. All that mattered now was what Ezra Jennings had to tell me. Nothing else would ease the torment until I heard it.

I abandoned any plan to visit Mrs. Ablewhite. Even the thought of facing Gabriel Betteredge made me shrink back. Instead, I returned to Frizinghall, left a note for him explaining that I’d been unexpectedly called away and would return by three o’clock. I asked him to dine as usual and pass the time as he pleased—he had plenty of friends in town, after all.

Then I turned my back on the village and wandered across the desolate moorland surrounding Frizinghall, walking aimlessly until my watch told me it was time to return to Mr. Candy’s house.

When I arrived, Ezra Jennings was already waiting. He sat alone in a bare, uninviting room that opened into a surgery through a glazed door. The walls were painted a dull buff and adorned with grotesque colored diagrams illustrating the ravages of disease. A bookcase stood crowded with dusty medical texts, topped not by a bust but by a human skull. A large wooden table, splattered with ink, held scattered papers. Wooden chairs, plain and worn, flanked the table. A threadbare rug lay in the center of the floor. In one corner, a sink with a basin and waste pipe jutted from the wall—an unmistakable sign of surgical work. Outside, bees hummed among potted flowers, birds sang in the garden, and from a nearby house came the faint, tuneless jingle of a piano. These ordinary sounds, elsewhere soothing, felt like intruders here—breaking into a silence meant only for pain and suffering.

I glanced at the mahogany instrument case and the thick roll of lint resting on the shelf, and a chill ran through me. I knew what those things were for. I knew what this room had witnessed.

“I make no apology, Mr. Blake,” Jennings said, “for receiving you here. It’s the only place in the house where we can be certain of privacy. My notes are ready. And these two books may come in handy. Sit down—let’s go over them together.”

I pulled up a chair. He handed me his manuscript: two large folio sheets. One had sparse writing, broken phrases scattered across the page. The other was densely filled with words in red and black ink. In my agitated state, I pushed aside the second sheet in frustration.

“Have mercy on me!” I exclaimed. “Tell me what I’m supposed to understand before I try to read this.”

“Gladly,” he replied. “But first—may I ask you a few questions?”

“Ask anything!”

He smiled faintly, his soft brown eyes full of quiet concern.

“You’ve told me,” he began, “that you’ve never, to your knowledge, taken opium.”

“To my knowledge,” I repeated.

“That reservation is important. Let’s continue. You don’t recall ever taking opium. But last year, around this time, you suffered from nervous irritation and couldn’t sleep. On the night of Miss Verinder’s birthday, though—you slept soundly. Is that correct?”

“Yes.”

“Can you explain why you were so troubled before, and yet slept well that night?”

“No cause comes to mind. Betteredge guessed it might have been quitting smoking—but that’s hardly worth mentioning.”

“Everything matters,” he said gently. “What did he think caused your insomnia?”

“Quitting smoking.”

“And had you smoked regularly?”

“Yes.”

“Did you stop suddenly?”

“Yes.”

“Well, Mr. Blake, Betteredge was right. When smoking is a habit, stopping abruptly damages the nerves. That explains your sleeplessness. Now—do you remember having a disagreement with Mr. Candy about medicine? At the dinner, or afterward?”

That question stirred something buried deep. I remembered the quarrel—the foolish argument between Candy and me during the birthday feast. It was described in greater detail in Betteredge’s narrative, but I’d long since forgotten most of it. All I could recall now was that I’d challenged medicine with reckless confidence, provoking Candy’s irritation. Lady Verinder stepped in to end it. Then, later, we reconciled—“made it up,” as children say—and parted as friends.

“There’s one more thing,” Jennings said. “Did you have any reason to worry about the Diamond at that time?”

“The strongest reasons. I knew someone was plotting against it. I was warned to protect Miss Verinder.”

“Was the Diamond’s safety discussed with anyone just before you went to bed?”

“Lady Verinder and her daughter talked about it—within my hearing.”

Jennings picked up his notes and handed them back.

“Now, Mr. Blake,” he said, “read these. With what we’ve just discussed, you’ll discover two astonishing truths about yourself. First: you entered Miss Verinder’s room and took the Diamond while under a trance induced by opium. Second: the opium was given to you by Mr. Candy—without your knowing it—as a way of proving you wrong about medicine.”

I froze, clutching the papers. My mind reeled.

“Try to forgive poor Mr. Candy,” Jennings said softly. “He did terrible harm, yes—but without meaning to. If he hadn’t fallen ill, he would have returned the next morning and confessed. Miss Verinder would have learned the truth, and it would have come out in a day.”

I began to regain my composure.

“Mr. Candy is beyond reproach,” I said, my voice tight. “But that doesn’t make it less treacherous. I can forgive—but I won’t forget.”

“Every doctor does something like this,” Jennings said quietly. “Not out of malice, but necessity. People distrust opium—even educated ones. Doctors often must deceive patients. I don’t excuse Candy’s actions under the circumstances. But I ask you to judge motives fairly.”

“How was it done?” I asked. “Who gave me the laudanum without my knowing?”

“I don’t know. Mr. Candy never spoke of it during his illness. Perhaps your memory can help.”

“No.”

“In that case, there’s no point in pursuing it. The laudanum was administered secretly. Let’s move on. Read these notes. Understand what happened. I have something bold and startling to propose about the future.”

Those words brought me sharply back.

I looked again at the papers. The top sheet contained fragmented phrases—what Mr. Candy had muttered in delirium:

“... Mr. Franklin Blake ... clever and agreeable ... down a peg ... medicine ... confesses ... sleep at night ... tell him ... out of order ... medicine ... he tells me ... groping in the dark mean one and the same thing ... all the company at the dinner-table ... I say ... groping after sleep ... nothing but medicine ... he says ... leading the blind ... know what it means ... witty ... a night’s rest in spite of his teeth ... wants sleep ... Lady Verinder’s medicine chest ... five-and-twenty minims ... without his knowing it ... tomorrow morning ... Well, Mr. Blake ... medicine today ... never ... without it ... out, Mr. Candy ... excellent ... without it ... down on him ... truth ... something besides ... excellent ... dose of laudanum, sir ... bed ... what ... medicine now.”

I handed the paper back.

“That’s exactly what you heard?” I asked.

“Exactly,” he said. “Except I didn’t record repetitions. He repeated certain words dozens of times—depending on how much weight they carried. Those repetitions helped me piece the fragments together. Don’t assume,” he added, pointing to the second sheet, “that I’ve reproduced Candy’s exact words. I’ve reconstructed the meaning behind the chaos. Judge for yourself.”

I turned to the second sheet. Here, Candy’s ramblings were copied in black ink, with gaps filled in red by Jennings. I reproduce it plainly, so both original and interpretation appear side by side:

“... Mr. Franklin Blake is clever and agreeable, but he needs taking down a peg when he talks about medicine. He admits he hasn’t slept well. I tell him his nerves are out of order and he should take medicine. He says medicine and groping in the dark mean the same thing. This before everyone at dinner. I reply: you’re groping for sleep, and only medicine can find it. He retorts: I’ve heard of the blind leading the blind, and now I know what it means. Witty—but I’ll give him a night’s rest in spite of his teeth. He really wants sleep. Lady Verinder’s medicine chest is available. Give him twenty-five minims of laudanum tonight—without his knowing. Call tomorrow. ‘Well, Mr. Blake, will you try some medicine today? You’ll never sleep without it.’—‘There you’re wrong, Mr. Candy: I had an excellent night’s rest without it.’ Then strike back with the truth: ‘You had something more than an excellent night’s rest—you had a dose of laudanum, sir, before you went to bed. What do you say to medicine now?’”

I stared, awed by the skill that had woven such clarity from madness.

I handed the manuscript back.

“Remarkable,” I said. “But does your conclusion match mine?”

“Do you believe, as I do, that you acted under the influence of laudanum that night—when you took the Diamond?”

“I know little about opium,” I admitted. “But I trust your judgment. I believe you’re right.”

“Then the next question is: how do we prove it to others?”

I pointed to the manuscripts.

“Useless,” Jennings said, shaking his head. “For three reasons. First: these notes were taken under conditions most people cannot imagine. Second: they represent medical theory—hard to accept. Third: they’re *my* work. No one else’s word backs them. Remember what I told you on the moor. My credibility is questionable. So these notes have only one value: they show *how* to prove your innocence. We must test our conviction—*you* must be the proof.”

“How?” I asked.

He leaned forward.

“Are you willing to try a bold experiment?”

“I’ll do anything to clear myself.”

“Will you endure personal discomfort?”

“Any discomfort.”

“Will you follow my advice—no matter how strange it seems? It may bring ridicule. It may anger your friends.”

“Tell me what to do,” I said, impatient. “And I’ll do it.”

“You shall steal the Diamond again—unconsciously—in front of witnesses whose testimony cannot be doubted.”

I rose abruptly, stunned. I couldn’t speak.

“I believe it *can* be done,” he continued. “And it *must* be done—if you’ll help me. Calm yourself. Sit down. You’ve resumed smoking—I’ve noticed.”

“For nearly a year.”

“More than before?”

“Yes.”

“Will you quit again—suddenly, as you did before?”

I began to grasp his plan.

“I’ll stop now,” I said.

“If the same effects follow,” he said, “if you suffer sleepless nights again, we’ve taken our first step. We’ll recreate your nervous condition from last June. If we can also recreate the domestic setting—your thoughts about the Diamond—then we’ll have placed you as close as possible to the same physical and mental state when the opium affected you. If we repeat the dose, we may expect a similar result. That’s my proposal. Now, let me justify it.”

He opened a book marked with a slip of paper.

“I won’t bore you with physiology,” he said. “But I must show you that I’m not acting on a private theory. Science supports this. Read this passage—by Dr. Carpenter.”

He handed me the slip:

“There seems much ground for the belief that every sensory impression once recognized by consciousness is registered in the brain and may be reproduced later, even if unconscious during the interval.”

“Clear?” he asked.

“Perfectly.”

He pushed the book toward me, pointing to a passage.

“Now read this account—directly relevant. It’s from Dr. Elliotson’s *Human Physiology*, citing Mr. Combe.”

The passage read:

“Dr. Abel told me of an Irish porter who forgot what he’d done when drunk, but recalled it when drunk again. Once, drunk, he lost a valuable parcel; sober, he couldn’t remember. Later, when intoxicated, he remembered leaving it at a house—no address, so it remained safe. He retrieved it upon returning.”

“Clear again?” Jennings asked.

“As clear as possible.”

He closed the book.

“Satisfied I’m not speaking without authority?”

“Quite satisfied.”

“We return to your situation. There are risks. If we could perfectly replicate last year’s conditions, the result would be certain. But we can’t. We can only approximate. If we fail, the experiment fails. If we succeed—then you may repeat your actions enough to prove, beyond doubt, that you were not guilty. I’ve laid out both sides. If anything remains unclear, tell me.”

“All is clear,” I said. “Except one thing. I don’t understand how opium made me walk downstairs, open drawers, return to my room. I thought opium stupefied and then put you to sleep.”

“A common misconception,” Jennings said. “I’m using a dose ten times stronger than Candy gave you—and I’m thinking clearly. But don’t rely on me. I anticipated your objection. I’ve prepared evidence.”

He handed me the second book.

“These are *Confessions of an English Opium Eater*,” he said. “Read it. At the marked passage, De Quincey describes how, after a heavy dose, he either attended the Opera or wandered London markets, observing the poor bargaining for Sunday dinner. That shows men can act, move, and engage actively under opium.”

“I’m answered,” I said. “But not fully. What did the opium do to *me*?”

Jennings nodded slowly. “We’ll see.”
