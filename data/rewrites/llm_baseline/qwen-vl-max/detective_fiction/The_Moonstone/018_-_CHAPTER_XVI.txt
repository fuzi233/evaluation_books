We found my lady in her room, lit only by the dim glow of a reading-lamp. The shade was pulled low, casting shadows across her face and hiding her expression. Instead of meeting us with her usual direct gaze, she sat hunched over a book, her eyes fixed stubbornly on the page.

“Officer,” she said without looking up, “is it essential to your investigation to know whether anyone in this house intends to leave?”

“Extremely important, my lady,” Sergeant Cuff replied.

“I must inform you,” she continued, “that Miss Verinder plans to depart tomorrow morning to stay with her aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite, at Frizinghall. She has already made arrangements.”

Sergeant Cuff turned his eyes toward me. I took a step forward, wanting to speak—but then paused, my heart sinking. I stepped back, silent.

“May I ask when Miss Verinder informed you of this?” the sergeant inquired.

“About an hour ago,” my lady answered.

Cuff glanced at me again. They say old hearts don’t beat fast anymore—but mine thudded as if I were twenty-five.

“I have no authority to stop Miss Verinder from leaving,” he said. “But I beg you, if possible, to delay her departure until later in the day. I must travel to Frizinghall myself tomorrow morning—and I’ll return by two o’clock, perhaps sooner. If Miss Verinder can remain here until then, I would like to speak with her—unexpectedly—before she goes.”

My lady nodded and instructed me to tell the coachman that the carriage was not to be sent for Miss Rachel until two.

“Is there anything else?” she asked once the order was given.

“Only this, your ladyship,” said the sergeant. “If Miss Verinder is surprised by this change, please do not mention me as the reason.”

She lifted her head sharply, as though about to respond—then stopped herself, forcing calm. She returned to her book and dismissed us with a quiet gesture.

“That’s a remarkable woman,” Sergeant Cuff murmured once we were in the hall. “Had she not held herself together, the mystery troubling you, Mr. Betteredge, would have ended tonight.”

At those words, something snapped inside me. For a moment, I felt utterly unmoored. I grabbed the sergeant by the collar and pinned him against the wall.

“Damn you!” I shouted. “There’s something wrong with Miss Rachel—and you’ve been hiding it from me all along!”

He looked up at me, unmoving, his face as still as stone.

“Ah,” he said calmly. “You’ve finally guessed it.”

My hand dropped. My head fell forward. Please understand—I had served this family fifty years. Miss Rachel had sat on my knees, tugged my whiskers, laughed in my face when she was little. She was, faults and all, the most beloved young mistress any old servant could have hoped for. I apologized to the sergeant, though I did so with tears in my eyes, not with dignity.

“Don’t distress yourself, Mr. Betteredge,” he said, surprisingly kind. “In our line, if we took offence too quickly, we’d be useless. If it helps, go ahead and grab me again. You’re not very good at it—but I’ll overlook your clumsiness, considering how you feel.”

He curled the corners of his mouth—a dry sort of humor, but it was a joke, nonetheless.

I led him into my small sitting room and closed the door.

“Tell me the truth,” I said. “What do you suspect? It’s no kindness to keep it from me now.”

“I don’t suspect,” he said. “I know.”

My temper flared again.

“Then say it plainly,” I demanded. “Has Miss Rachel stolen the Diamond?”

“Yes,” he replied. “That’s exactly what I mean. Miss Verinder has possessed the Moonstone all along. She brought Rosanna Spearman into her confidence—knowing we’d suspect her. That’s the whole case. Grab me again, Mr. Betteredge—if it helps you.”

God help me—I couldn’t release my feelings that way.

“Give me your reasons,” I said.

“You’ll hear them tomorrow,” he answered. “If Miss Verinder refuses to stay, which I expect she will, I’ll present the full case to your mistress. And since I don’t know how it will go, I’ll ask you to be present. But tonight, no more. I won’t say another word about the Moonstone. Your supper is ready. That’s one human weakness I always respect. Ring the bell, and I’ll say grace: ‘For what we are going to receive—’”

“I wish you a good appetite,” I said. “Mine is gone. I’ll wait to see you served, then I’ll excuse myself. I need to be alone.”

I watched as he was served—everything the best—and I wouldn’t have minded if it choked him.

The head gardener, Mr. Begbie, arrived with his weekly account. The sergeant immediately shifted gears, launching into a debate about roses and whether gravel paths were better than grass.

I left them to it and stepped out onto the terrace. My mind was restless. This was the first trouble in years that tobacco couldn’t ease, nor *Robinson Crusoe* distract from. I walked, thinking. I felt ancient, worn down, unfit for service. For the first time in my life, I wondered when God might take me.

Yet through all this, I clung to one belief: Miss Rachel was innocent.

Even if Sergeant Cuff were Solomon himself, and told me she’d conspired in a dishonorable act, I’d answer only: “You don’t know her. I do.”

My thoughts were broken by Samuel, who handed me a note from my lady.

Going inside to light a candle, Samuel mentioned the weather was changing. I hadn’t noticed before, but now I heard the dogs uneasy, the wind moaning. Looking up, I saw dark clouds racing across a pale moon—storm brewing.

The message said the magistrate at Frizinghall had reminded her that the three Indians were due for release early next week. If we wanted to question them, we had no time to lose. My lady had forgotten to mention this to the sergeant earlier and now asked me to relay it.

The Indians? I’d completely forgotten them.

Still, I obeyed. I found the sergeant and Mr. Begbie deep in their rose argument, a bottle of Scotch between them.

The sergeant raised his hand—don’t interrupt—when I entered.

They argued fiercely: should the white moss rose be budded on the dog-rose?

Mr. Begbie said yes. The sergeant said no. They appealed to me, as if I knew anything.

I didn’t. So I gave the judge’s verdict: “Gentlemen, there is much to be said on both sides.”

In the silence that followed, I placed the message on the table.

By now, I nearly hated the man. Yet I had to admit—he was astonishingly sharp.

Within minutes, he’d recalled Superintendent Seegrave’s report, found the section about the Indians, and had a plan.

“A great traveler familiar with the Indians and their language was mentioned in the report,” he said. “Do you know his name and address?”

I did not.

“Write them on the back of this note,” he said. “I’ll look him up when I go to Frizinghall.”

“Do you expect anything from this?” I asked.

“Seegrave found the Indians innocent,” I said.

“Seegrave has been wrong in every conclusion so far,” the sergeant replied. “It may be worth checking whether he was wrong about them too.”

And with that, he resumed the rose debate where he’d left off.

I shut the door and walked away.

In the passage, I met Penelope, waiting.

“What are you doing?” I asked.

“Waiting for Miss Rachel’s bell,” she said. “She wants me to finish packing.”

Further talk revealed that Miss Rachel claimed the house was unbearable, that she couldn’t stand having a policeman under the same roof. When told her departure was delayed till two, she flew into a rage. My lady rebuked her sharply, then sent Penelope out—clearly, there was something private to say.

Penelope was distraught.

“Nothing’s right, father. Nothing’s like it used to be. I feel like doom is hanging over us.”

I felt the same. But I kept it to myself.

Miss Rachel’s bell rang. Penelope ran upstairs. I went to the hall to check the barometer.

As I neared the swing door, it burst open from the other side.

Rosanna Spearman ran past, clutching her chest, face twisted in pain.

“What’s wrong?” I called. “Are you ill?”

“For God’s sake, don’t speak to me,” she whispered, wrenching free and sprinting toward the servants’ stairs.

I called the cook to follow her.

Two others heard. Sergeant Cuff slipped out of my room quietly, asking what had happened.

“Nothing,” I said.

Mr. Franklin appeared from the other side, beckoning me into the hall.

“Did you see Rosanna?” he asked.

“She just passed me—looked terrible,” I said.

“I’m afraid I’m the cause,” he said. “If she’s involved in the theft, I believe she was about to confess—to me of all people—just moments ago.”

I glanced at the swing door. It seemed slightly ajar. Had someone been listening?

Before I could reach it, it closed.

But I thought I saw the edge of the sergeant’s coat disappearing around the corner.

He knew I’d discovered his true suspicions. He wasn’t going to wait for help. He was acting on his own—quietly, cleverly.

I didn’t want to accuse him unfairly—there was enough chaos already.

So I told Mr. Franklin I thought a dog had gotten in.

Then I asked him to explain what had happened.

“Were you in the hall?” I asked.

“Knocking balls about,” he said, pointing to the billiard table. “Trying to forget the Diamond. I looked up—and there stood Rosanna, like a ghost. She came up behind me silently. It startled me. I asked if she wanted to speak. She said, ‘Yes—if I dare.’ Knowing the suspicion on her, I understood. I felt uncomfortable. I didn’t want to encourage her—but I couldn’t refuse if she really meant to speak. I said, ‘I don’t quite understand. Is there something I can do?’ I didn’t mean to be harsh. I saw how ugly she is—I felt it. I kept playing pool to ease the tension. But I think I hurt her. She turned away and muttered, ‘He looks at the billiard balls. Anything rather than look at _me_!’ Then she left.”

He paused.

“I’m not at ease, Betteredge. Would you tell her I meant no harm? I’ve been hard on her in my thoughts. I almost hoped the Diamond was hers—not from malice, but…”

He stopped.

I knew what he meant.

Only if Rosanna confessed could Miss Rachel be cleared in the sergeant’s eyes.

But Rosanna hadn’t confessed. She’d pretended illness, gone to Frizinghall secretly, stayed up all night doing something in private, and visited the Shivering Sand under suspicious circumstances.

So while I pitied her, I couldn’t blame Mr. Franklin for suspecting her.

I told him so.

“Yes,” he said. “But there’s a chance—small as it is—that her actions have an explanation we don’t see yet. I hate hurting a woman’s feelings. Tell her what I said. And if she wants to speak to me—send her to the library. I don’t care if I get into trouble.”

He laid down his cue and left.

I reported to him that Rosanna had retired to her room, refused help, and asked only to rest.

So no confession tonight.

Mr. Franklin went to bed.

I was turning out the lights when Samuel returned with news: the argument was over. The gardener had gone home. The sergeant was nowhere to be found below.

I checked my room—only empty glasses and the smell of hot grog.

Had he gone to his room?

I went upstairs.

On the second landing, I heard soft, regular breathing to my left—the corridor leading to Miss Rachel’s room.

I peeked in.

There, coiled across three chairs, lay Sergeant Cuff. A red handkerchief wrapped around his head, his coat rolled up as a pillow. He slept like a dog.

He woke instantly, quietly.

“Good-night, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “And remember—if you ever grow roses, the white moss rose is better *not* budded on the dog-rose. Whatever the gardener says.”

“What are you doing here?” I asked. “Why aren’t you in your bed?”

“I’m not in my proper bed,” he said, “because many of us can’t earn our money honestly and easily at the same time. Tonight, Rosanna’s return from the Sands coincided with Miss Verinder’s decision to leave. Whatever Rosanna hid, your young lady couldn’t go until she knew it was safe. They must have spoken already. If they try again—tonight—I want to be in the way.”

“Don’t blame me,” he added. “Blame the Diamond.”

“I wish to God the Diamond had never come here!” I cried.

He looked at the three chairs, his face grim.

“So do I,” he said.
