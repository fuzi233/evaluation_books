Strain, hackney-coach steeds, and bear George Warrington as fast as your hooves will carry him through thronging Strand and rutted Fleet Street, bound for his brother’s prison and rescue! Any soul who has pored over Hogarth’s famous etching of a mid-18th century London hackney carriage and the mud-choked highway it navigates can picture how endless that short stretch of road felt to our young Virginian: scarce a public lamp to be seen, save for the guttering torches borne by ragged link-boys lurching through the dark, badly sprung coaches jolting over cracked uneven paving, gaping potholes that threatened to swallow a wheel whole, and winter quagmires of thick black mud that clung to the boots of every passerby. That three-mile drive from Piccadilly to the Temple felt longer to George than the entire cross-country journey he had completed that very morning, riding post-haste from Bristol where he had landed after eighteen months in French captivity.

He had written twice ahead to Harry: once to break the news that he was alive, not dead on the field of Braddock’s Defeat as all their family had believed, and a second note to say he had safely set foot in England. But the Channel swarmed with French privateers in those days, raiding British merchant craft within sight of the white cliffs, and the ship carrying George’s first letter had been seized mid-voyage. The second letter, mailed from Bristol, arrived at Harry’s London lodgings three days after Harry had been dragged off to the bailiff’s sponging-house, and the surly landlord Mr. Ruff had refused to hand over a single scrap of his former lodger’s post.

George went straight to Harry’s rooms first, on reaching London, to ask after his brother. The maid who opened the door screamed at the sight of his face, the exact mirror of the lodger she thought had simply run off, and bolted shrieking to her mistress. The landlady, not daring to admit she had allowed her husband to sue Harry for debt and throw him in prison, lied that Mr. Warrington had moved out and she knew not a single detail of his new whereabouts. George did not waste breath pressing her: he knew Aunt Bernstein’s house in Clarges Street was but a few steps away. He had read every one of Harry’s letters back to Virginia, gushing over the old Baroness’s uncommon kindness to the boy, a kindness that had even softened their stern mother Madam Esmond, who had never spoken a word of her disgraced sister Beatrix for decades. It was the Baroness’s own warm letter urging Harry’s welfare that had spurred George to set sail for Europe at once.

It was at his aunt’s door that he ran into old Gumbo, the family servant, who nearly fainted dead away at the sight of his long-dead master returned, and stammered out the whole story of Harry’s debt and imprisonment. George had arrived at the old lady’s parlour stiff with righteous anger, convinced their wealthy relations had abandoned Harry to ruin, but the sight of sharp, witty Aunt Bernstein breaking down in unfeigned tears for her favorite boy melted his sternness in an instant, and he chided himself for being far too hasty to judge, that old flaw of his that had gotten him into trouble more than once before in his young life.

The coach jolted and crept its way through the dark streets to the Temple, until at last they pulled up before the bailiff’s grim house. The Jewish porter unlatched the heavy outer door, locked it warily behind them, then led them down a narrow dim passage by the flaring light of a tallow torch, gawking at George’s face, which he mistook at first for the prisoner’s, risen from his bed upstairs. “You’ve come to see Mr. Warrington, sir? There’s two gentlemen already inside seeing to his release, step this way into Mr. Amos’s office for a moment,” he said, and led George into the tiny candle-lit room where the bailiff sat talking to a stout man in a laced hat and military cloak.

The stout officer rose to his feet, outstretching a friendly hand before he even looked properly at the new arrival. “No hard feelings left between us, Harry, my boy!” he called cheerily. “Thank the Almighty, Mr. Amos says there’s no trouble at all with me and James standing bail for you, you’ll be out of here by breakfast tomorrow.” Then he started back, shocked, as George grasped his hand. “Why—who in heaven’s name are you?”

“God bless you, sir,” George said, his voice faltering a little. “I know you at once. You must be Colonel Lambert, of whom my Harry wrote so many tales of kindness. I am his brother— the one left for dead on Braddock’s field, who spent eighteen months in French captivity, and has come home at last to thank you for all you have done for my brother.”

At that moment a second man in a red military tunic stepped through the door, and Colonel Lambert turned to him laughing. “James! James Wolfe, look here! The dead have come back to life! Harry the scapegrace’s brother has returned, whole and hale!” George bowed to the lean sharp-faced lieutenant-colonel, whom Harry had also written of many times. “I know your reputation, sir,” he said. “You helped my brother in his hour of need, and I shall count your friendship the greatest honour.”

Wolfe clapped him on the shoulder, half-smiling. “I’ll own I was cross with the hot-headed boy for speaking so unkindly to my good friend Lambert here, for no cause at all. But Lambert would not hear of leaving him to rot here over a foolish quarrel, and dragged me down to stand bail with him.” The bailiff Amos cut in, bowing, “Your honours’ security is as good as gold. If the young gentleman’s kin will have it, he can walk out of here this very night, no need to wait till morning for the lawyers.”

“The full debt is five hundred pounds odd, is it not?” George said, drawing a heavy purse from his coat. “I will pay the sum at once, and take the proper receipts. But no money will ever pay back what I owe you two gentlemen, for stepping forward to help my Harry when he had no one else to turn to. I thank you, in my own name and my mother’s back in Virginia, and may God bless you for your kindness.”

Upstairs, Harry sat moping over his pipe of home-grown Virginia tobacco, half heartsick even after a recent visit from his loyal sweetheart Maria, fretting a little that her devotion would tie him to a future he was not yet sure he wanted. The door flew open, and Gumbo burst in, eyes bulging, grinning so wide his cheeks stretched. “What is it, fool?” Harry laughed. “Hast thou found a new sweetheart to gape at?”

“No, master!” Gumbo cackled, his whole body trembling with delight. “I seen a ghost! A ghost that come all the way from Madame Bernstein’s house, in a hackney coach, and he’s downstairs in that office right now!”

Harry’s blood ran cold. “A ghost? Is—is my mother all the way from Virginia here?”

Gumbo shook his head so hard his ill-fitting wig slipped askew. “No, master! It’s your other master! The one we thought dead!”

Harry did not stop to button his coat properly, he tore out of the room and raced down the stairs, and paused at the office door to hear that familiar, long-lost voice say, “I thank you, gentlemen, in my mother’s name and mine.”

“Whose voice is that?” Harry cried out, his own voice cracking.

“It’s the ghost’s, mas’r!” Gumbo whooped behind him, and Harry burst through the office door.

Colonels Lambert and Wolfe turned their backs discreetly to give the boys space. The lost was found, the dead was alive. Harry flew straight into his brother’s outstretched arms, sobbing half apologies, half joy, as George held him tight. “Come away, James,” Lambert said softly, after a minute. “We are not needed here. The ladies back in Hill Street will never credit this tale, they will be up half the night gossiping over it. Will you two boys come back to sup with us, and tell the whole story?”

George and Harry shook their heads, smiling through their tears. They had a hundred private tales to tell each other, of captivity, of Virginia, of all the months they had spent apart, no other company needed. Half an hour later, they stepped out of the grim sponging-house door together, into the crisp London night, and for the first time in months, Harry’s heart was light.
