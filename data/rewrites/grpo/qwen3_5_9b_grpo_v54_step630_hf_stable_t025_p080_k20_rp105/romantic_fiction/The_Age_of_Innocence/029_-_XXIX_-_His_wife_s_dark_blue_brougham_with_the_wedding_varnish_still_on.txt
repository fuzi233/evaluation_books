The dark blue brougham, still gleaming with the fresh varnish of their wedding day, waited for Archer at the ferry. It carried him in luxurious comfort to the Pennsylvania terminus in Jersey City. Outside, the afternoon was heavy with snow, the air sombre and grey, while inside the vast, echoing station, gas lamps cast a warm, artificial glow.

As Archer paced the platform, waiting for the Washington Express, he found himself thinking of the dreamers who spoke of a tunnel boring beneath the Hudson, allowing trains to rush straight into New York. They were part of a brotherhood of visionaries who also predicted ships crossing the Atlantic in five days, flying machines, electric light, and wireless telephony—marvels straight out of an Arabian Nights tale.

"I don't care which of these fantasies comes true," Archer mused, "provided the tunnel remains unbuilt." In a state of boyish, irrational happiness, he conjured a scene: Madame Olenska stepping from the train, his discovery of her amidst a sea of indifferent faces, her taking his arm as he led her to the carriage. He imagined their slow journey to the wharf, navigating slipping horses and shouting cart drivers, before settling into the quiet of the ferry boat. There, side by side under the falling snow, they would watch the earth glide away beneath them, rolling toward the other side of the sun. It was impossible to count the things he needed to say to her, the words forming in his mouth with such eloquence.

The train's clanging and groaning grew louder, staggering slowly into the station like a beast returning to its lair. Archer pushed through the crowd, staring blindly into the windows of the high carriages. Then, suddenly, he saw her. Madame Olenska's pale, surprised face appeared close at hand, and he felt the familiar, mortifying sensation of forgetting exactly what she looked like.

They met, their hands joined, and he drew her arm through his. "This way—I have the carriage," he said.

Everything unfolded exactly as he had dreamed. He helped her into the brougham with her bags, and later recalled vaguely reassuring her about her grandmother and giving her a summary of the situation at Beaufort. He noted the softness in her voice when she whispered, "Poor Regina!" Meanwhile, the carriage wound its way out of the station's chaotic coils, crawling down the slippery incline toward the wharf. They were surrounded by swaying coal carts, bewildered horses, and disheveled wagons. Then, an empty hearse passed by.

Archer watched her shut her eyes and clutch his hand tightly.

"If only it doesn't mean... poor Granny!"

"Oh, no, no—she is much better. She is quite all right. There—we have passed it!" he exclaimed, as if mentioning it aloud could banish the fear. Her hand remained in his. As the carriage lurched across the gangplank onto the ferry, he bent down, unbuttoned her tight brown glove, and kissed her palm with the reverence of someone touching a relic. She pulled away with a faint smile.

"You didn't expect me today?"

"Oh, no."

"I intended to go to Washington to see you. I had made all my arrangements; I very nearly missed you on the train."

"Oh—" she exclaimed, as though terrified by how close they had come to missing one another.

"Do you know... I hardly remembered you?"

"Hardly remembered me?"

"I mean, how shall I explain? I—it happens every time. Each time you happen to me all over again."

"Oh, yes. I know. I know!"

"Does it—to you?" he insisted.

She nodded, gazing out the window.

"Ellen—Ellen—Ellen!"

She offered no answer. He sat in silence, watching her profile blur against the snow-streaked dusk beyond the glass. What had she done in those four long months? How little they truly knew of each other! The precious moments slipped away, leaving him helpless to brood over the mystery of their simultaneous closeness and distance, symbolized by their physical proximity and their inability to see each other's faces clearly.

"What a pretty carriage! Is it May's?" she asked suddenly, turning from the window.

"Yes."

"It was May who sent you to fetch me, then? How kind of her!"

For a moment, he said nothing. Then, explosively: "Your husband's secretary came to see me the day after we met in Boston."

In his brief letter, he had made no mention of M. Rivière's visit, intending to bury the incident within himself. But her reminder that they were sitting in his wife's carriage provoked a sudden impulse of retaliation. He would see if she preferred his reference to Rivière to her reference to May! As on other occasions when he expected to shake her out of her usual composure, she showed no sign of surprise. Immediately, he concluded: "He writes to her, then."

"M. Rivière went to see you?"

"Yes: didn't you know?"

"No," she answered simply.

"And you're not surprised?"

She hesitated. "Why should I be? He told me in Boston that he knew you; that he'd met you in England, I think."

"Ellen—I must ask you one thing."

"Yes."

"I wanted to ask it after I saw him, but I couldn't put it in a letter. It was Rivière who helped you to get away when you left your husband?"

His heart hammered against his ribs. Would she meet this question with the same calm composure?

"Yes: I owe him a great debt," she answered, her quiet voice devoid of any tremor.

Her tone was so natural, so almost indifferent, that Archer's inner turmoil subsided. Once again, through her sheer simplicity, she had made him feel stupidly conventional just when he thought he was casting convention aside.

"I think you're the most honest woman I have ever met!" he exclaimed.

"Oh, no—but probably one of the least fussy," she replied, a smile in her voice.

"Call it what you like: you look at things as they are."

"Ah—I've had to. I've had to look at the Gorgon."

"Well—it hasn't blinded you! You've seen that she's just an old bogey like all the others."

"She doesn't blind one; but she dries up one's tears."

The answer halted the pleading rising in Archer's throat; it seemed to come from depths of experience beyond his reach. The ferry boat's slow advance had ceased, and its bow bumped violently against the piles of the slip, causing the brougham to stagger and throw Archer and Madame Olenska against one another. The young man, trembling, felt the pressure of her shoulder and passed his arm around her.

"If you're not blind, then you must see that this cannot last."

"What cannot?"

"Our being together—and not together."

"No. You ought not to have come today," she said, her voice altered. Suddenly, she turned, threw her arms around him, and pressed her lips to his. At that exact moment, the carriage began to move, and a gas lamp at the head of the slip flashed its light into the window. She drew away, and they sat silent and motionless while the brougham struggled through the congestion of carriages at the ferry landing.

As they gained the street, Archer began to speak hurriedly.

"Don't be afraid of me: you needn't squeeze yourself back into your corner like that. A stolen kiss isn't what I want. Look: I'm not even trying to touch the sleeve of your jacket. Don't suppose that I don't understand your reasons for not wanting this feeling between us to dwindle into an ordinary, secret affair. I couldn't have spoken like this yesterday, because when we are apart and I am looking forward to seeing you, every thought is consumed by a great flame. But then you come; and you are so much more than I remembered, and what I want of you is so much more than an hour or two every now and then, with wastes of thirsty waiting in between, that I can sit perfectly still beside you, like this, with that other vision in my mind, quietly trusting it to come true."

For a moment, she made no reply. Then, barely above a whisper, she asked: "What do you mean by trusting to it to come true?"

"Why—you know it will, don't you?"

"Your vision of you and me together?" She burst into a sudden, hard laugh. "You choose your place well to put it to me!"

"Do you mean because we are in my wife's brougham? Shall we get out and walk, then? I don't suppose you mind a little snow?"

She laughed again, more gently. "No; I shall not get out and walk, because my business is to get to Granny's as quickly as I can. And you will sit beside me, and we will look, not at visions, but at realities."

"I don't know what you mean by realities. The only reality to me is this."

She met his words with a long silence. During this pause, the carriage rolled down an obscure side street and then turned into the searching illumination of Fifth Avenue.

"Is it your idea, then, that I should live with you as your mistress—since I cannot be your wife?" she asked.

The crudeness of the question startled him; the word was one women of his class fought shy of, even when their conversation flirted closest with the topic. He noticed that Madame Olenska pronounced it as if it held a recognized place in her vocabulary, and he wondered if it had been used familiarly in her presence in the terrible life she had fled. Her question jerked him to a halt, and he floundered.

"I want—I want somehow to get away with you into a world where words like that, categories like that, won't exist. Where we shall be simply two human beings who love each other, who are the whole of life to each other; and nothing else on earth will matter."

She drew a deep sigh that ended in another laugh. "Oh, my dear—where is that country? Have you ever been there?" she asked. As he remained sullenly dumb, she continued: "I know so many who have tried to find it; and, believe me, they all got out by mistake at wayside stations: at places like Boulogne, or Pisa, or Monte Carlo—and it wasn't at all different from the old world they'd left, but only rather smaller, dingier, and more promiscuous."

He had never heard her speak in such a tone, and he remembered the phrase she had used a short while before.

"Yes, the Gorgon *has* dried your tears," he said.

"Well, she opened my eyes too; it is a delusion to say that she blinds people. What she does is just the contrary—she fastens their eyelids open, so that they are never again in the blessed darkness. Isn't there a Chinese torture like that? There ought to be. Ah, believe me, it is a miserable little country!"

The carriage had crossed Forty-second Street; May's sturdy horse was carrying them northward as if he were a Kentucky trotter. Archer choked with the sense of wasted minutes and vain words.

"Then what, exactly, is your plan for us?" he asked.

"For *us*? But there is no *us* in that sense! We are near each other only if we stay far from each other. Then we can be ourselves. Otherwise, we are only Newland Archer, the husband of Ellen Olenska's cousin, and Ellen Olenska, the cousin of Newland Archer's wife, trying to be happy behind the backs of the people who trust them."

"Ah, I am beyond that," he groaned.

"No, you are not! You have never been beyond. And I have," she said, in a strange voice, "and I know what it looks like there."

He sat silent, dazed with inarticulate pain. Then, he groped in the darkness of the carriage for the little bell that signaled orders to the coachman. He remembered that May rang twice when she wished to stop. He pressed the bell, and the carriage drew up beside the curbstone.

"Why are we stopping? This is not Granny's," Madame Olenska exclaimed.

"No: I shall get out here," he stammered, opening the door and jumping to the pavement. By the light of a street lamp, he saw her startled face and the instinctive motion she made to detain him. He closed the door and leaned for a moment in the window.

"You are right: I ought not to have come today," he said, lowering his voice so the coachman would not hear. She bent forward, seeming about to speak; but he had already called out the order to drive on, and the carriage rolled away while he stood on the corner.

The snow had stopped, and a tingling wind had sprung up, lashing his face as he stood gazing. Suddenly, he felt something stiff and cold on his lashes and realized he had been crying, and that the wind had frozen his tears.

He thrust his hands into his pockets and walked at a sharp pace down Fifth Avenue to his own house.
