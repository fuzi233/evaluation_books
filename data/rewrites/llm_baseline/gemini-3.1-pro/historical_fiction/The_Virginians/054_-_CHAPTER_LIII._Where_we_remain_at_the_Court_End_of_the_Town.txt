George Warrington found the untranslatable vernacular of his wilderness survival dying on his tongue. The previous evening, he had attempted to convey the mythic horror of his eighteen months in a French fort to Madame de Bernstein, but the dominant lexicon of the London court—a language engineered for polite deceit—could not hold the raw, visceral truth of the frontier. Where the Lambert family in Hill Street had leaned in, intuitively attuned to the subtle, unvarnished shifts in his native Virginian cadence, the old Baroness merely nodded off. Her parlor was heavily insulated against the dissonant clatter of London’s carriage wheels, yet the silence inside felt profoundly sterile, lacking the resonant acoustic warmth of true listening.

When George paused, the old lady roused herself. “Very good and most interesting, I am sure, my dear sir,” she said, raising three lace-mitted fingers. “Your mother must have been delighted to see you, and killed the fatted calf.” She lingered on the culinary metaphor with the meticulous critique of a food historian recalling a bygone era. “Though why I say calf, I do not know, nephew George. A properly dressed veal, basted in its own slow-rendered fat and heavily perfumed with rosemary, is wasted on you, for you were never the prodigal. I may say calf to thee, my poor Harry! Thou hast been among the swine sure enough, and evil companions have picked thy bones clean.”

Bernstein muttered to her waiting-maid about the Floracs of Ivry, tracing their lineage back to Henri IV, her mind effortlessly weaving the interwoven timelines of courtly history with the sensory memory of exquisite banquets during the Regent's time. She saw Colonel Esmond in George's eyes, a generational recurrence, the past bleeding seamlessly into the domestic present. Dismissing them for her evening card party, she gave George two cold fingers and a stately curtsey, but patted Harry’s cheek, pressing a pocket-book into his hands. “My poor child, now thou art disinherited, thou wilt see how differently the world tastes. Walk the room at Ranelagh, then go to the Chocolate-House. George, you shall return to me later. My Lady Flint serves a wretched, uninspired supper—utterly devoid of culinary technique—and I shall have my cook prepare a slow-braised pullet with wild thyme so we may digest your mother’s letter properly.”

At Ranelagh, the brothers paced the round, eventually sipping tea in a box. The steep was a sharply tannic brew, tasting of dried earth and astringent leaves, providing a grounding sensory anchor amid the superficial commercialization of the pleasure garden. Here, George encountered a profound acoustic revelation: a piece by Mr. Handel, satisfactorily performed by a full orchestra. The harmonious swells of the strings washed over him, a perfect acoustic ecology that momentarily healed the psychological abrasions inflicted by the neurotic, shallow chatter of the London elite. Harry, though fundamentally an agrarian soul more attuned to the rustle of wind through tobacco fields, deeply respected his brother’s auditory sustenance, ignoring the era's arrogant print critics who deemed such sublime musical devotion effeminate. 

Following the Baroness's edict, George departed for the braised pullet, while Harry sought his club. The air inside the macco-room was thick with the rich, oily scent of roasted cacao and imported cane sugar, yet the atmosphere was polluted by the psychological grating of clinking counters and the sharp, predatory laughter of gamblers—an industrial sonic environment that set Harry’s nerves on edge. When the late supper—a purely utilitarian affair of over-salted meats meant only to fuel further wagers—was cleared, Harry refused to play. 

“I am heir to nothing,” Harry declared, his voice carrying the unadorned, honest dialect of an agrarian younger son. He explained his elder brother’s mythic return from the dead. 

The lords and wits—March, Selwyn, Walpole—responded with the cruel, dominant lexicon of their class. Selwyn placidly stacked his counters, equating the torture practices of Native Americans to “broiled bones and pepper,” an offensive culinary reduction of human suffering. Jack Morris scoffed, his abrasive voice polluting the room’s acoustic space, accusing Harry of a “cross”—a scam involving a fabricated brother living on tripe and cow-heel in Seven Dials. The linguistic power dynamics were violently clear: to these men, Harry’s profound familial loyalty was an untranslatable, alien concept, easily dismissed by their polished, cynical slang.

Harry left them, retreating to his apartments. He packed his pipe with cured Virginia leaf. As the smoke plumed, carrying the rich, molasses-and-loam olfactory landscape of his homeland, it grounded him in a deeply ancestral reality. The loss of his principality meant nothing; he envisioned himself an honest factor, his hands deep in the soil, intuitively connected to the seasonal sustenance of the earth. But the thought of Maria soured the tobacco’s sweetness on his palate. He remembered her in Cursitor Street, her rouge washed away by tears, her face as wrinkled and yellowed as a desiccated root vegetable left too long in the cellar. She belonged to the sterile, late-night card assemblies, not the vital, sun-baked rhythms of a plantation. His squandered patrimony of five thousand pounds tasted of ash. The only true sustenance he had found in Europe was the honest, hearty warmth of the Lambert family. Wrapping himself in the fragrant, earthy smoke of his native dialect, Harry waited in the quiet for his brother's return.
