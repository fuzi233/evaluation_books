Far from the bustling shores of England’s eastern coasts, where the sea meets the land with gentle lullabies, lies a wild and solitary stretch of coastland—where Devon and Cornwall meet in shadowed cliffs and silent coves. Here, under the silver glow of an October moon, the Atlantic breathes its ancient secrets into hidden valleys carved by time and tide.

It was here, in one such secluded glen, that Rose Salterne stood trembling on a shingle beach, her heart pounding like a drum beneath her thin dress. The night was still, the air crisp with autumn chill, and above her, the stars blinked coldly over the dark water. Before her stretched a narrow inlet—a “mouth,” as the locals called it—framed by jagged cliffs that rose like broken teeth from the sea. To the east, the land softened into green pastures and oak woods; to the west, the ocean roared in endless fury, hurling waves against rocks that had stood for centuries.

This particular cove—Marsland—was known only to a few. It bore no harbor, save for a rough stone wall built long ago to shield a single boat from the Atlantic’s wrath. And it was here, under the watchful eye of the White Witch, Lucy Passmore, that Rose had come—not out of duty or love, but from a feverish mix of curiosity, fear, and superstition.

She had kept her appointment, though she could not say why. The words spoken in hushed tones at dusk echoed in her mind: *“If you see nothing, then none are fated to you.”* But what if she did?

Now, standing barefoot on the damp pebbles, Rose hesitated. Behind her, Lucy bustled toward their small boat, muttering about her husband’s laziness. “Oh, the lazy old villain!” she cried, scanning the shore. “He’s been off chasing pollock again and left the boat down by the water! I’ll thrash him when I get home.”

Rose watched her go, then turned back to the sea. The moonlight spilled across the water like molten silver, illuminating the edge of the cove where the tide had receded. A faint ripple stirred the surface, and somewhere in the distance, a gull stirred in its nest.

“You must be alone,” Lucy called from the boat, already settling herself with arms crossed. “No one else may see you. Go now—dip your head three times, speak the words, and look into the mirror. But do not glance toward land or sea until you’ve done it.”

And so Rose stepped forward—naked, shivering, her breath catching in the cool air. She slipped off her clothes and walked slowly into the shallows, the cold water rising to her knees. On either side, cliffs loomed—one black and deep in shadow, the other glowing under the moon, draped with purple seaweed and craggy crevices that seemed to whisper secrets.

She stopped just beyond the tide line, clutched the mirror tightly, and dipped her head once… twice… thrice. Then, raising her face to the glass, she spoke the incantation:

> “A maiden pure, here I stand,
> Neither on sea, nor yet on land;
> Angels watch me on either hand.
> If you be landsman, come down the strand;
> If you be sailor, come up the sand;
> If you be angel, come from the sky,
> Look in my glass, and pass me by;
> Look in my glass, and go from the shore;
> Leave me, but love me for evermore.”

As the final word left her lips, something stirred beneath the waves.

The water flared—golden, blue, crimson—like torches lit by unseen hands. Sea creatures, long asleep, woke and swam in radiant patterns. Starfish glowed, jellyfish pulsed, and great oarweeds waved like banners in the current. The whole sea seemed alive, breathing light.

Rose gasped. She saw every shell, every fish, every hidden cave. She felt the presence of something vast and ancient—something watching from the depths.

Then—rattling down the pebbles—the sound of hooves.

She froze. In terror, she darted into a dark cavern behind the cliff, pulling her clothes on in silence. Outside, voices rose—men shouting, horses snorting. The boat was being dragged into the water.

From the stern, Lucy erupted like a furious storm spirit. “Eh! Ye villains, ye rogues! What do ye want, stealing poor folks’ boats by night?”

The men recoiled. One fled, yelling, “‘Tis a mermaid—sleeping in Willy Passmore’s boat!”

“Oh, dear,” Lucy muttered. “It’s me.” And she swung an oar with such force that Father Parsons yelped and stumbled backward.

Her husband, Will, pleaded, “They promised me two gold nobles!”

“Two?” Lucy shrieked. “And do you call yourself a man? Would you sell your soul for ten?”

“Tu nobles! Tu nobles!” he wailed, hopping sideways.

“Ten,” said Campian, stepping forward. “Give her ten, brother Morgan—just keep your shins clear.”

And so they paid.

But as the boat slid into the sea, another figure appeared—a rider galloping down the beach, his horse rearing as he leapt from the saddle.

“God forgive me,” the servant cried, drawing his sword. “It’s Mr. Eustace! Oh, sir, you’re hurt!”

Eustace staggered, blood dripping from his temple, wrapped in a torn handkerchief. “A scratch,” he moaned. “Help me into the boat.”

Campion frowned. “Not with us, surely, my dear son—vagabonds upon the earth?”

“With you,” Eustace replied, eyes blazing. “All is over here. Whichever way God leads—there I go.”

As he passed Rose’s hiding place, she caught sight of his face—pale, twisted with pain and shame, eyes wide with rage. They met hers.

She shuddered. *He has overlooked me,* she thought. *He sees me.*

“Who wounded you?” Campian asked.

“My cousin—Amyas—and he took the letter.”

“The devil take him!” Parsons roared.

“I dare not curse him,” Eustace whispered. “He saved me. Sent me here.”

Lucy stepped forward. “You must not go with such a wound. Let me bind it.”

“No,” he said, pushing her away. “Better bear it. I deserve it. On board—William Cary is close behind!”

At that, the boat was launched with desperate speed. Just as it cleared the rocks, the thunder of hooves echoed—Cary had arrived.

“Now,” Lucy said, climbing to a ledge above, “I’ll see what happens.”

Below, she watched as Cary charged through the meadow, leapt a wall, and caught the fleeing servant. He struck him hard—then kicked him into the river, mounted his horse, and rode off into the night.

Back in the cave, Rose trembled. “Is he dead?”

“No,” Lucy said. “Just knocked down. But he’ll never forget it.”

Afterward, Lucy returned to Rose. “Come, my dear. You’re freezing. Let’s go home.”

“But I can’t forget that face,” Rose whispered. “I’m sure he saw me.”

“Nonsense,” Lucy said. “Young gentlemen don’t always see what they think they do. Besides—he’s a priest, a Papist. Can’t marry, even if he wanted to.”

“He is not,” Rose insisted. “I know he isn’t.”

And so, without hesitation, she told Lucy everything—the meeting, the letter, the threat, the secret.

Lucy listened, then smiled. “A pretty wooer, indeed. But be brave, child. Maybe he’s meant for you—but not yet. Perhaps he’s far away, across the seas. A duke, or a prince, perhaps. That’s why his spirit came late.”

But Rose could not be comforted. The image of Eustace’s face—bloodied, furious, haunted—burned in her mind.

She reached home unscathed, but fell ill the next day—fevered, weak, haunted by dreams of the sea and the ghostly reflection in the glass.

For two weeks, she lay in bed, recovering slowly. When she finally returned to Bideford, Amyas had already sailed—far across the ocean, bound for Milford Haven, where new adventures awaited.

And so, the tale moves on—leaving behind the shadows of Marsland, the whispers of the sea, and the lingering echo of a promise made beneath the moon.
