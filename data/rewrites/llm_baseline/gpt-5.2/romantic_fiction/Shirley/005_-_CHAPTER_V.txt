Moore’s spirits held their ground with the stubbornness of a well-fed hearth-fire. He rose before dawn in the mill, where he and Joe Scott had made shift to sleep in the counting-houses—places meant for sums and ledgers, not for breathing men; yet they had contrived beds from benches, coats, and the thin charity of folded sacking.

The master was first astir. He washed in cold water that bit like raw spirit; he set his hair with brisk fingers; and, to rouse Joe, he sang—light, French, irreverent—so that the tune ran along the empty passages and ticked against doors and iron like a spoon on a pan.

“Ye’re not custen dahn, then, maister?” Joe called, hauling himself upright.

“Not a stiver, mon garçon—which means, my lad. Up with you. We’ll walk through the mill before the hands come in, and I’ll explain my plans. We’ll have the machinery yet, Joseph. You never heard of Bruce, perhaps?”

“And th’ arrand—spider? Ay, I hev. I’ve read th’ history o’ Scotland, and happen knaw as mich on’t as ye; and I understand ye to mean to say ye’ll persevere.”

“I do.”

“Is there mony o’ your mak’ i’ your country?” Joe asked, folding away the makeshift bedding with the neatness of a mechanic who can’t bear waste or disorder.

“In my country! Which is my country?”

“Why, France—isn’t it?”

“Not it, indeed! The circumstance of the French having seized Antwerp, where I was born, does not make me a Frenchman.”

“Holland, then?”

“I am not a Dutchman. Now you are confounding Antwerp with Amsterdam.”

“Flanders?”

“I scorn the insinuation, Joe! I a Flamand! Have I a Flemish face—the clumsy nose standing out, the mean forehead falling back, the pale blue eyes à fleur de tête? Am I all body and no legs, like a Flamand? But you don’t know what they are like, those Netherlanders. Joe, I’m an Anversois. My mother was an Anversoise, though she came of French lineage, which is the reason I speak French.”

“But your father war Yorkshire, which maks ye a bit Yorkshire too; and onybody may see ye’re akin to us, ye’re so keen o’ making brass, and getting forrards.”

“Joe, you’re an impudent dog; but I’ve always been accustomed to a boorish sort of insolence from my youth up. The classe ouvrière—that is, the working people in Belgium—bear themselves brutally towards their employers; and by brutally, Joe, I mean brutalement—which, perhaps, when properly translated, should be roughly.”

“We allus speak our minds i’ this country; and them young parsons and grand folk fro’ London is shocked at wer incivility; and we like weel enough to gi’e ’em summat to be shocked at, ’cause it’s sport to us to watch ’em turn up the whites o’ their een, and spreed out their bits o’ hands, like as they’re flayed wi’ bogards—”

“You are savages, Joe. You don’t suppose you’re civilized, do you?”

“Middling, middling, maister. I reckon ’at us manufacturing lads i’ th’ north is a deal more intelligent, and knaws a deal more nor th’ farming folk i’ th’ south. Trade sharpens wer wits; and them that’s mechanics like me is forced to think. Ye know, what wi’ looking after machinery and sich like, I’ve getten into that way that when I see an effect, I look straight out for a cause—”

“You think yourself a clever fellow, I know, Scott.”

“Ay! I’m fairish. I can tell cheese fro’ chalk—”

“And because you’ve picked up a little practical mathematics, and found some scantling of chemistry at the bottom of a dyeing-vat, you fancy yourself a neglected man of science,” Moore cut in, walking as he spoke, boots striking the flagged floor with a firm, spare sound. “And you needn’t suppose that because trade doesn’t always run smooth, and you and such as you are sometimes short of work and bread, therefore your class are martyrs, and the whole form of government wrong. And you needn’t insinuate that virtue lives only under thatch. Human nature is human nature everywhere—under tile or straw—vice and virtue blended in every sample that breathes. I’ve seen villains rich, and villains poor, and villains comfortably in between. Hark—the clock. It will strike six. Away with you, Joe, and ring the mill bell.”

It was mid-February; at six, the night was only beginning to thin. The dawn did not blush; it did not even properly wake. It stole in like a pale sick child, laying a colourless hand on the hills. Rain had ceased, yet the world still dripped with it: sodden earth, brimmed pools, rivulets quick with yesterday’s spate. A raw wind shifted the long ceiling of cloud and showed, low round the horizon, not blue, but a whiter vapour—like steam that will not clear.

The mill bell rang out—loud, metallic, and unkind in the wet air. Its note belonged to iron and industry; it cut through the softer sounds—the drain’s gurgle, the hedge’s drip, the distant call of a cart—till the valley seemed to hold its breath between each clang. That sound governed bodies and hours here; it made children run.

And they did come—small figures in shawls and thin clogs, hurrying as though speed could warm them. The lane and yard filled with footfall and quick voices, the wet slap of soles, the faint cough, the squeak of a door. If the morning pinched, they had known worse—snow-storm and hard frost, rain that struck like thrown gravel. Perhaps, by contrast, this raw grey did not seem the harshest.

Moore stood at the entrance and watched them pass. He counted them—one, two, three—measuring his power and responsibility with the same eye he used on bales and bobbins. To late comers he spoke a sharp word; Joe repeated it sharper in the work-room. Yet there was no taste for cruelty in either man. They fined a delinquent who came very late—Moore held out his hand for the penny before the boy crossed the threshold, and said plainly that next time would be twopence.

Rules are necessary, and in some hands they become instruments like whips; but this master and this overlooker did not strike children. Joe, it was true, had once flogged his own lad—hard—for lying and holding to it; but in the mill there were no blows. Their rigour was cold, not feral: the chill of a system, not the heat of a tyrant.

Moore stayed among his engines, his yard, his dye-house, his warehouse, until the faint dawn strengthened into day. At last the sun rose—if one could call it so. A white disc, tintless as ice, peeped over a dark hill-crest and silvered the livid rim of cloud. It looked down, solemn and unpitying, along the whole narrow dale—the den—where the mill crouched and clattered. By eight the mill-lights were out. The signal for breakfast sounded; children were released for half an hour and fell upon their little tin cans of coffee and their small baskets of bread. Let us hope there was enough: the smell of coffee—thin, sweetened perhaps with a grudged spoon—rose like a comfort, and the sound of eager eating was softer than the machines.

Only then did Moore leave the yard and turn to his house. It lay near, yet the lane to it, banked high and hedged thick, gave a sense of retreat, as though comfort had tried to hide itself from the factory’s harsh music. The dwelling was a small whitewashed place with a green porch. In the garden the brown stalks stood like spent skewers in black soil—dead now, but promising trained creepers in summer. The grass plat was bleached, not green; the borders were mostly mould, save where, in sheltered nooks, snowdrops and crocuses pushed up—emerald points piercing the damp. Winter had been long. The last deep snow had scarcely melted before yesterday’s rains; on the hills, remnants still gleamed, white flecks in hollows, pale crowns on peaks. Three trees, not lofty but well grouped, rose beside the cottage and gave it an air of mild dignity.

It was a snug nest, made for content and thought; but Moore, built for action and gain, did not enter at once. He fetched a spade from a little shed and began to dig. For a quarter hour he worked steadily, turning the heavy earth. The spade’s bite and lift made a homely rhythm that answered, at last, to something other than the mill bell: wet soil falling back with a thick hush; breath drawn in the cold; a blackbird’s single note from the hedge.

A window opened above. A woman’s voice, quick and edged, called down in French.

“Eh, bien! Tu ne déjeûnes pas ce matin?”

“Is breakfast ready, Hortense?”

“Certainly; it has been ready half an hour.”

“Then I am ready too. I have a canine hunger.”

He dropped the spade and went in.

A narrow passage led to a small parlour. On the table waited coffee, bread and butter, and—un-English yet intimate to him—stewed pears, their syrup giving off a gentle perfume, like fruit preserved against hard seasons. The coffee’s scent was darker, roasted; it threaded the room, warm as a shawl.

Over these viands presided Hortense Moore, his sister. She seemed a little older than he—perhaps five years. Tall and stout in proportion, she had very black hair—now twisted up in curl-papers—high colour in her cheeks, a small nose, and little black eyes that looked as if they could flash indignation at a crumb out of place. The lower part of her face was large; her forehead small and corrugated; her expression fretful rather than ill-natured, provoking and yet—strangely—almost comic. Strangest to English eyes was her morning dress: a stuff petticoat and a striped cotton camisole, the petticoat short enough to show feet and ankles that were sturdy rather than shapely.

She was no slattern. Hortense was orderly, economical, and proud of both qualities. This was the dress in which, in her own country, she had always “gone her household ways,” and she did not mean to shift her habits as one shifts a bonnet, merely because she must live among English people. She believed steadfastly there was merit in keeping to old modes, as there is merit in keeping a mother-tongue alive.

She had an excellent opinion of herself—an opinion not wholly undeserved, for she possessed good, sterling qualities—but she overvalued them, and forgot to reckon the defects that travelled in their shadow. She would never have owned herself prejudiced, narrow, too touchy about dignity, too ready to take offence at trifles—yet she was all these. Still, when her claims were not crossed and her pride not pricked, she could be kind enough.

To her brothers she was devoted. As the remaining representatives of a decayed family, both were almost sacred in her eyes. Of Louis she knew less: he had been sent to England a boy, educated at an English school, and, not being shaped for trade, had taken the hard, modest course of teaching—usher once, and now, people said, tutor in a private family. Hortense spoke of Louis as having “des moyens,” but being too quiet. Of Robert she spoke with unqualified pride: he was, to her, the greatest man in Europe. Opposition to him—or to herself—was, in her mind, irrational, monstrous, infamous.

No sooner had Robert seated himself than she helped him to pears and cut him a thick tartine—bread spread in the Belgian way, not daintily, but as if butter were a necessary virtue—then poured out her horror over last night’s work: the destruction of the frames.

“What an idea! To destroy them. What a shameful act! One sees well that the workmen of this country are at once stupid and wicked. It is exactly like the English servants, the maids especially: nothing so insupportable as that Sara, for example!”

“She looks clean and industrious,” Moore remarked, blowing on his coffee.

“Looks! I do not know how she looks, and I do not say she is altogether dirty or idle, but she is insolent. She disputed with me a quarter of an hour yesterday about the cooking of the beef. She said I boiled it to rags, that English people would never be able to eat such a dish as our bouilli; that the bouillon was no better than greasy warm water; and as to the choucroute, she declares she cannot touch it. That barrel we have in the cellar—delightfully prepared by my own hands—she called a tub of hog-wash—food for pigs. I am harassed with the girl, and yet I cannot part with her lest I should get a worse. You are in the same position with your workmen, pauvre cher frère!”

“I am afraid you are not very happy in England, Hortense.”

“It is my duty to be happy where you are, brother; but otherwise there are certainly a thousand things which make me regret our native town. All the world here appears to me ill-bred. I find my habits considered ridiculous. If a girl from your mill comes into the kitchen and finds me in my jupon and camisole preparing dinner—for you know I cannot trust Sarah to cook a single dish—she sneers. If I accept an invitation out to tea—which I have done once or twice—I perceive I am put quite into the background; I have not that attention paid me which is decidedly my due. Of what an excellent family are the Gérards, as we know, and the Moores also! They have a right to claim a certain respect, and to feel wounded when it is withheld. In Antwerp I was always treated with distinction; here, one would think that when I open my lips in company I speak English with a ridiculous accent—whereas I am quite assured I pronounce it perfectly.”

“Hortense, in Antwerp we were known rich; in England we were never known but poor.”

“Precisely, and thus mercenary are mankind. Again, dear brother—last Sunday was very wet, you recollect; accordingly I went to church in my neat black sabots—objects one would not indeed wear in a fashionable city, but which in the country I have ever been accustomed to use for walking in dirty roads. Believe me, as I paced up the aisle, composed and tranquil as I am always, four ladies and as many gentlemen laughed and hid their faces behind their prayer-books.”

“Well, well! don’t put on the sabots again. I told you before I thought they were not quite the thing for this country.”

“But, brother, they are not common sabots, such as the peasantry wear. I tell you, they are black sabots, very clean, very suitable. At Mons and Leuze—cities not very far removed from the elegant capital of Brussels—it is very seldom that respectable people wear anything else for walking in winter. Let any one try to wade the mud of the Flemish chaussées in a pair of Paris brodequins—one would soon hear news of it!”

“Never mind Mons and Leuze and the Flemish chaussées; do at Rome as the Romans do. And as to the camisole and jupon, I’m not quite sure about them either. I never see an English lady dressed in such garments. Ask Caroline Helstone.”

“Caroline! I ask Caroline? I consult her about my dress? It is she who on all points should consult me. She is a child.”

“She is eighteen—or at least seventeen—old enough to know all about gowns, petticoats, and chaussures.”

“Do not spoil Caroline, I entreat you, brother. Do not make her of more consequence than she ought to be. At present she is modest and unassuming: let us keep her so.”

“With all my heart. Is she coming this morning?”

“She will come at ten, as usual, to take her French lesson.”

“You don’t find that she sneers at you, do you?”

“She does not. She appreciates me better than any one else here; but then she has more intimate opportunities of knowing me. She sees that I have education, intelligence, manner, principles—all, in short, which belongs to a person well born and well bred.”

“Are you at all fond of her?”

“For fond I cannot say. I am not one who is prone to take violent fancies, and consequently my friendship is the more to be depended on. I have a regard for her as my relative; her position also inspires interest, and her conduct as my pupil has hitherto been such as rather to enhance than diminish the attachment that springs from other causes.”

“She behaves pretty well at lessons?”

“To me she behaves very well; but you are conscious, brother, that I have a manner calculated to repel over-familiarity, to win esteem, and to command respect. Yet, possessed of penetration, I perceive clearly that Caroline is not perfect, that there is much to be desired in her.”

“Give me a last cup of coffee, and while I am drinking it amuse me with an account of her faults.”

Hortense filled his cup with an air of brisk service—as if feeding him were one of the few pleasures England had not yet soured. The coffee steamed; the pears cooled; outside, the wind pushed at the panes with a thin whistle, and beyond that, faint as a memory, the mill kept up its iron murmuring.

“Dear brother, I am happy to see you eat your breakfast with relish, after the fatiguing night you have passed. Caroline, then, is defective; but with my forming hand and almost motherly care she may improve. There is about her an occasional something—a reserve, I think—which I do not quite like, because it is not sufficiently girlish and submissive; and there are glimpses of an unsettled hurry in her nature, which put me out. Yet she is usually most tranquil, too dejected and thoughtful indeed sometimes. In time, I doubt not, I shall make her uniformly sedate and decorous, without being unaccountably pensive. I ever disapprove what is not intelligible.”

“I don’t understand your account in the least. What do you mean by ‘unsettled hurries,’ for instance?”

“An example will perhaps be most satisfactory. I sometimes make her read French poetry by way of practice in pronunciation. She has gone through much of Corneille and Racine in a very steady, sober spirit, such as I approve. Occasionally she showed, indeed, a degree of languor in the perusal of those esteemed authors, partaking rather of apathy than sobriety; and apathy is what I cannot tolerate in those who have the benefit of my instructions. The other day I put into her hands a volume of short fugitive pieces. I sent her to the window to learn one by heart; and when I looked up I saw her turning the leaves over impatiently, and curling her lip—absolutely with scorn—as she surveyed the little poems. I chid her. ‘Ma cousine,’ said she, ‘tout cela m’ennuie à la mort.’ I told her this was improper language. ‘Dieu!’ she exclaimed, ‘il n’y a donc pas deux lignes de poésie dans toute la littérature française?’ I inquired what she meant. She begged my pardon with proper submission. Ere long she was still. I saw her smiling to herself over the book. She began to learn assiduously. In half an hour she came and stood before me, presented the volume, folded her hands—as I always require—and commenced the repetition of that short thing by Chénier, ‘La Jeune Captive.’ If you had heard the manner in which she went through this, and in which she uttered a few incoherent comments when she had done, you would have known what I mean by the phrase ‘unsettled hurry.’ One would have thought Chénier more moving than all Racine and all Corneille. You, brother, who have so much sagacity, will discern that this disproportionate preference argues an ill-regulated mind; but she is fortunate in her preceptress. I will give her a system, a method of thought, a set of opinions; I will give her the perfect control and guidance of her feelings.”

“Be sure you do, Hortense.” Moore drank. “Here she comes. That was her shadow passed the window, I believe.”

“Ah! truly. She is too early—half an hour before her time.—My child, what brings you here before I have breakfasted?”

Caroline entered then—a young girl wrapped in a winter mantle, its folds gathered with a natural grace about a slender figure. The cold followed her in a moment: a damp breath from outside, the faint scent of wet wool, and, beneath that, something clean and pale as frost—an outdoor smell of lane and hedge.

“I came in haste to see how you were, Hortense, and how Robert was too. I was sure you would be both grieved by what happened last night. I did not hear till this morning. My uncle told me at breakfast.”

“Ah! it is unspeakable,” Hortense returned, gathering herself into injured propriety. “You sympathize with us? Your uncle sympathizes with us?”

“My uncle is very angry—but he was with Robert, I believe, was he not? Did he not go with you to Stilbro’ Moor?”

“Yes,” said Moore. “We set out in very martial style, Caroline; but the prisoners we went to rescue met us half-way.”

“Of course nobody was hurt?”

“Why, no; only Joe Scott’s wrists were a little galled with being pinioned too tightly behind his back.”

“You were not there? You were not with the wagons when they were attacked?”

“No. One seldom has the fortune to be present at occurrences at which one would particularly wish to assist.”

“Where are you going this morning? I saw Murgatroyd saddling your horse in the yard.”

“To Whinbury. It is market day.”
