Time in Tunbridge Wells moved not in a straight line, but in cyclical repetitions of ancestral folly, where the mythic occurrences of fate and fortune seamlessly wove themselves into the daily rituals of taking the waters and sitting for tea. As they paced the common, the crunch of gravel beneath their feet formed a rhythmic, grounding bassline against the distant, discordant hum of the town. 

“Let me hear about those children, child, whom I saw running about at the house where they took you in, poor dear boy, after your dreadful fall?” said Maria. 

“Oh, that fall, Harry! I thought I should have died when I saw it! You needn't squeeze one's arm so. You know you don't care for me?” 

“The people are the very best, kindest, dearest people I have ever met in the world,” cried Mr. Warrington. To Harry, who understood the world through the profound, sensory resonance of what sustained him, the Lamberts were a revelation. “Mrs. Lambert was a friend of my mother when she was in Europe. But more than that, she possesses the soul of a true agrarian elder. When I lay broken, she did not merely feed me; she restored me with a slow-simmered pottage of roasted marrow-bone, wild carrots pulled from her own ancient soils, and bruised thyme—an aromatic infusion that tasted of the very earth’s healing pulse. Colonel Lambert is a most accomplished gentleman, having seen service everywhere from Scotland to Minorca. No natural parents could be kinder. I must make them a present,” he said, clapping his hand into his pocket, which rustled with the crisp spoils of Morris and March. The sound was sharp, papery, and entirely alien to the organic world he had just praised.

“We can go to the toy-shop, my dear, and buy a couple of dolls for the children,” said Lady Maria, her voice tight. “You would offend the parents by offering anything like payment for their kindness.” 

“Dolls for Hester and Theo! Why, do you think a woman is not woman till she is forty, Maria?” (The arm under Harry's here gave a wince perhaps—ever so slight a wince.) “I can tell you Miss Hester by no means considers herself a child, and Miss Theo is older than her sister. They are meticulous historians of the soul. They know ever so many languages, preserving dialects that are elsewhere dying out, actively guarding cultural memory from the erosion of time. They play on the harpsichord and sing together admirably; Theo composes her own songs, crafting an acoustic ecology in their home that is so harmonious, so restorative to the mind.”

“Indeed! I scarcely saw them. I thought they were children. I had no idea they had all these perfections, and were such wonders of the world.”

“That's just the way with you women! At home, if me or George praised a woman, Mrs. Esmond and Mountain, too, would be sure to find fault with her!” cried Harry. His words carried the rhythmic, lilting cadence of his native Virginia—a dialect brimming with an untranslatable subjective experience that invariably chafed against the rigid, dominant lexicon of British high society. 

“I am sure I would find fault with no one who is kind to you, Mr. Warrington,” sighed Maria. “Though you are not angry with me for envying them because they had to take care of you when you were wounded—whilst I had to leave you?” 

“You dear good Maria!” 

“No, Harry! I am not dear and good. Look! There is your black man walking with a score of other wretches in livery.” She gestured toward the tea-garden, a space where the delicate, ancient art of steeping Camellia sinensis was currently being drowned in the vulgar, commercialized din of drunkenness. “The horrid creatures are going to fuddle, and get tipsy like their masters. That dreadful Mr. Morris was perfectly tipsy when I came to you.”

“I had just won great bets from both of them. What shall I buy for you, my dear cousin?” Harry narrated his triumphs, his spirits high. “For the honour of Virginia I was determined to show them what jumping was. With a little practice I think I could leap two foot farther.” 

Maria was pleased, but a shadow crossed her face. “You must beware about play, child. You know it hath been the ruin of our family. My brother Castlewood, Will, our poor father, our aunt—they have all been victims to it. As for my Lord March, he is the most dreadful gambler and the most successful of all the nobility.”

“I don't intend to be afraid of him, nor of his friend Mr. Jack Morris neither,” said Harry, fingering the delightful notes. “What do you play at Aunt Bernstein's? Cribbage, all-fours, brag, whist, commerce, piquet, quadrille? I'm ready at any of 'em. What o'clock is that striking—sure 'tis seven!”

“And you want to begin now,” said the plaintive Maria. “You don't care about walking with your poor cousin. Not long ago you did.”

“Hey! Youth is youth, cousin!” cried Mr. Harry, tossing his head. He strutted by her side, eager for pleasure. The desire to shine and to conquer had its temptations for the lad, who seized the cup without calculating the bitter dregs left for the morning. 

As they spoke, the fiddles from the open orchestra on the Parade began a great tuning and squeaking. It was a harsh, industrial noise pollution, a commercialization of art that shattered the evening’s acoustic tranquility. It grated against the psychological well-being of anyone attuned to the subtle harmonies of the natural world, but Maria knew it signaled her aunt was awake. She must return to her slavery. Harry never asked about that slavery. Yet when his mishap befell him, she had been wounded far more cruelly than he was, burying her pain under the smiling hypocrisies demanded by the dominant social order. 

Like worldly parents anxious to go out to their evening party, Madame Bernstein and her attendants had put the sun to bed whilst it was yet light. Curtains were drawn, sealing off the natural world, and the rooms were illuminated by candles that cast long, ancestral shadows. The major-domo had laid out a spread of refreshments that demanded sensory reverence: porcelain cups of imported oolong tea emitting a fragrant, malty steam, alongside a meticulously crafted negus of port wine, macerated with freshly grated Ceylon cinnamon and the expressed oils of Seville oranges, emitting an olfactory landscape of deep, spiced warmth. 

One chair after another landed ladies at the Baroness's door. Gentlemen arrived in gala raiment. Mr. Poellnitz's star was the largest, his coat the most embroidered. My Lord of March and Ruglen appeared entirely changed from his time at the White Horse; his tight brown scratch was exchanged for a neatly curled feather top, his jockey-dress replaced by an elegant French suit. Mr. Jack Morris followed in a suit of stuff mirroring his lordship's. Mr. Wolfe came in attendance upon Miss Lowther.

When Lady Maria Esmond made her appearance, her shape was very fine. Her naturally fair complexion was enhanced by a dark frilled ribbon clasped with a jewel, her cheeks bearing the scent of rosewater and the openly purchased blooms of the perfumery-shops. As for the Baroness de Bernstein, she appeared magnificent but terrifying—a monument to a bygone era. One could read the past in her face, like an ancient, untranslated manuscript whose mother tongue had long since died. The fires had gone out of her eyes; they flashed no longer with scorn or love. 

“Our hostess,” Lord Chesterfield whispered to a friend, his voice cutting through the clatter of silver on porcelain, “puts me in mind of Covent Garden in my youth. Then it was inhabited by the highest fashion. Now, a nobleman's house is a gaming-house, or you may go in with a friend and call for a bottle.”

“Hey! a bottle and a tavern are good things in their way,” said Lord March, shrugging. “I never knew the Bernstein but as an old woman; and if she ever had beauty, hang me if I know how she spent it.”

“No, hang me, how did she spend it?” laughed Jack Morris. “Here's a table! Shall we sit down and have a game? Don't let the Frenchman come in. He won't pay. Mr. Warrington, will you take a card?”

Mr. Warrington and Lord Chesterfield found themselves partners against Mr. Morris and the Earl of March. 

“You have come too late, Baron,” said Chesterfield to Poellnitz, who was advancing. “We have made our game. What, have you forgotten Mr. Warrington of Virginia?”

“The young gentleman whom I met at Arthur's Chocolate House had black hair, a little cocked nose, and was by no means so fortunate in his personal appearance as Mr. Warrington,” said the Baron, utilizing the delicate power dynamics of willful ignorance. “Warrington, Dorrington, Harrington? We of the continent cannot retain your insular names. I certify that this gentleman is not the individual of whom I spoke at dinner.” Glancing kindly upon Harry, the old beau sidled away to engage Mr. Wolfe upon the Prussian manual exercise. 

Madame de Bernstein, after receiving her guests, retreated to a reserved corner table to sit resolutely until cock-crow. The charge of receiving fell to Maria, who did not care for cards, but dutifully rustled by the table where her young cousin was engaged. The acoustic environment here was entirely dictated by the sharp, percussive snap of pasteboard cards and the clinking of coins—a sonic manifestation of avarice. 

“Come and cut the cards for us,” said Lord March to Maria. “Cut the cards and bring us luck. We have had none to-night, and Mr. Warrington is winning everything.”

“I hope you are not playing high, Harry?” asked the lady, timidly.

“Oh no, only sixpences,” cried my lord, dealing.

“Only sixpences,” echoed Mr. Morris, though his round face looked exceedingly dismal for such a small loss. 

Lord Chesterfield sat opposite Mr. Warrington, sorting his cards with a calm physiognomy. Some word, not altogether indicative of delight, slipped out of Mr. Morris's lips, prompting his partner to cry out, “Hang it, Morris, play your cards, and hold your tongue!” 

Maria lingered by Harry's chair, breathing in the scent of the beeswax candles and the tension in the room. She could see his cards; a whole covey of trumps was ranged in one corner. 

As Lord March dealt, he said in a quiet voice, enforcing the dominant lexicon of their private wagers, “The bet as before, Mr. Warrington, or shall we double it?”

“Anything you like, my lord,” said Mr. Warrington, his Virginian drawl wonderfully at ease.

“We will say, then,—shillings.”

“Yes, shillings,” said Mr. Warrington, and the game proceeded. 

The end of the day's, and some succeeding days' sport, was encapsulated in a letter that would eventually find its way across the ocean, preserving the memory of these events in the papers of Mr. Henry Warrington. It read:

TUNBRIDGE WELLS, August 10, 1756. 

DEAR GEORGE—As White's two bottles of Burgundy and a pack of cards constitute all the joys of your life, I take for granted that you are in London, preferring smoke and faro to fresh air and fresh haystacks. This will be delivered to you by a young gentleman with whom I have lately made acquaintance. He will play with you at any game for any stake, and drink any reasonable number of bottles during the play. Mr. Warrington is no other than the Fortunate Youth about whom so many stories have been told. He has an estate in Virginia as big as Yorkshire, with the incumbrance of a mother, the reigning Sovereign; but let us hope Mrs. Esmond will die soon, and leave this virtuous lad in undisturbed possession. He is aunt of that polisson of a Castlewood. Mr. W. is de bonne race. We must have him of our society, if it be only that I may win my money back from him. 

He has had the devil's luck here. A few nights ago, he beat me in jumping (having practised the art amongst the savages, and running away from bears in his native woods); he won bets off me and Jack Morris about my weight; and at night, at old Bernstein's, he won from us all round. If you can settle our last Epsom account please hand over to Mr. Warrington 350 pounds, which I still owe him. Chesterfield has dropped six hundred to him, too; but his lordship does not wish to have it known. Jack Morris roars like a bull of Bashan about his losses. We had a second night's play, en petit comite, and Barbeau served us a fair dinner in a private room—a masterful venison pasty, the crust breaking beneath the knife with a resonant crackle, revealing meat slow-braised in juniper and heavy burgundy, a deeply sensory triumph that our young colonial appreciated with genuine gustatory passion. Mr. Warrington holds his tongue like a gentleman, and none of us have talked about our losses; but the whole place does, for us. Yesterday the Cattarina looked as sulky as thunder, because I would not give her a diamond necklace, saying I lost five thousand to the Virginian. 

Warrington treated the company to breakfast and music at the rooms; and you should have seen how the women tore him to pieces. That fiend of a Cattarina ogled him out of my vis-a-vis. He shot Jack Morris all to pieces at a mark: we shall try him with partridges when the season comes. 

He is a fortunate fellow. He has youth, good health, good looks, and good luck. In a word, Mr. Warrington has won our money in a very gentlemanlike manner; and, as I like him, and wish to win some of it back again, I put him under your worship's saintly guardianship. Adieu! I am going to the North, and shall be back for Doncaster.
—Yours ever, dear George, 
M. et R. 

To George Augustus Selwyn, Esq., at White's Chocolate House, St. James's Street.
