The primal instinct within Buck surged, fed by the brutal calculus of the trail. It was a silent expansion, a secret metamorphosis where newborn cunning replaced naive obedience. He moved with a deliberate poise, avoiding conflict rather than seeking it, his movements calibrated to the harsh physics of survival. Against Spitz, the alpha of the team, Buck harbored a cold, simmering hatred, yet he displayed no rash aggression. He waited, conserving his energy, refusing to engage until the necessity became absolute.

Spitz, sensing a dangerous rival in this oversized Southerner, adopted a strategy of constant provocation. He stalked Buck, displaying his teeth and posturing aggressively, desperate to force a confrontation that would eliminate the threat permanently. For weeks, this tension remained suspended, broken only by an unprecedented catastrophe.

They established camp on the desolate shore of Lake Le Barge, driven there by a driving snowstorm and a wind that sliced through fur like white-hot steel. Darkness had fallen before they could secure a proper site. Behind them rose a vertical wall of granite; ahead lay the frozen expanse of the lake. Perrault and François were forced to ignite their fires directly on the ice, spreading their sleeping robes on the slick surface. Their tent, discarded at Dyea to lighten the load, was nowhere to be found. A small pile of driftwood provided a feeble heat, melting a thin layer of ice beneath them as they ate their supper in the encroaching dark.

Under the shelter of the rock face, Buck constructed a nest. It was a sanctuary of warmth, so inviting that he hesitated to leave it when François began distributing the thawed fish. But upon returning to his spot, he found it occupied. A low, warning snarl signaled the intruder: Spitz. Until this moment, Buck had tolerated his enemy's provocations, but this violation crossed a threshold. The dormant beast within him erupted. He launched himself at Spitz with a ferocity that stunned them both—particularly Spitz, whose entire assessment of Buck relied on the assumption that the larger dog was merely a timid brute surviving solely due to his mass.

François recoiled in surprise as the two dogs exploded from the nest in a tangled whirlwind. "A-a-ah!" he shouted at Buck, his voice cracking with urgency. "Give it to him, by Gar! Give it to him, you dirty thief!"

Spitz matched the intensity, circling frantically, his eyes burning with rage and predatory eagerness. Buck mirrored the motion, cautious yet equally hungry for dominance. Just as the duel reached its zenith, chaos intervened.

A curse from Perrault, the thunderous crack of a club striking bone, and a high-pitched yelp shattered the scene. Pandemonium erupted. The camp was revealed to be teeming with skulking, starving huskies—four or five score of them—who had tracked the scent of food from a distant Indian village. They had slipped in unnoticed while Buck and Spitz fought. When the men charged among the pack with heavy clubs, the invaders met them with snarls and counterattacks, driven mad by the smell of the grub box.

Perrault struck one that had buried its head in the provisions. The club connected with its gaunt ribs, sending the box tumbling. Instantly, a dozen famished monsters scrambled over the spilled bread and bacon. The clubs fell upon them indiscriminately, raining down blows that elicited yelps and howls, yet the animals fought on with frantic desperation until the last crumb was consumed.

Meanwhile, the team dogs burst from their shelters only to be ambushed by these fierce raiders. Buck had never witnessed such creatures. They appeared as living skeletons, their bones distending through loose, ragged hides, their eyes blazing with a supernatural light, their jaws foaming. Hunger had stripped away all restraint, rendering them terrifying and unstoppable. The team was swept backward against the cliff face at the first onslaught. Buck was cornered by three huskies; in seconds, his head and shoulders were shredded.

The noise was deafening. Billee whimpered in terror. Dave and Sol-leks, bleeding from numerous wounds, fought side by side with grim determination. Joe snapped like a demon; once, his jaws clamped onto a husky's foreleg, crunching through the bone with audible violence. Pike, the malingerer, leaped onto a crippled attacker, snapping its neck in a single, fluid motion. Buck seized a frothing adversary by the throat, feeling the spray of arterial blood as his teeth pierced the jugular. The metallic taste fueled his frenzy. He lunged at another, only to feel teeth sink into his own throat simultaneously. It was Spitz, striking treacherously from the flank.

Perrault and François, having cleared their supplies, rushed to protect their sled dogs. The wild tide of beasts retreated momentarily before their combined assault, allowing Buck to shake free. But the reprieve lasted only a heartbeat. Compelled to retrieve the scattered grub, the men ran back, and the huskies immediately resumed their attack on the team. Billee, terrified into a sudden surge of courage, bolted through the savage circle and sprinted across the ice. Pike and Dub followed, with the rest of the team trailing behind. As Buck prepared to join the flight, he caught Spitz rushing toward him, intent on toppling him. If Buck fell beneath the mass of attackers, there would be no recovery. He braced himself for the impact of Spitz's charge, then pivoted, joining the chaotic retreat onto the lake.

Later, the nine remaining team dogs huddled in the forest for shelter. Unpursued, they were in dire straits. Every single one bore multiple wounds; some were grievously injured. Dub suffered a severe injury to a hind leg; Dolly, the newest addition from Dyea, had a throat torn open; Joe had lost an eye; and Billee, usually gentle, cried and whimpered throughout the night with an ear chewed to ribbons. At dawn, they limped warily back to camp, finding the marauders gone and the two men in foul tempers. Nearly half their food supply was missing. The huskies had gnawed through the sled lashings and canvas covers. Nothing edible had escaped them—not even a pair of Perrault's moose-hide moccasins, chunks of leather traces, or two feet of the lash from François's whip.

François broke from his despair to inspect his wounded charges. "Ah, my friends," he murmured softly, "perhaps so many bites will make you mad dogs. Perhaps all mad dogs, sacredam! What do you think, eh, Perrault?"

The courier shook his head doubtfully. With four hundred miles of trail separating them from Dawson, he could not afford a breakdown of sanity among his pack. Two hours of curses and exertion restored the harnesses, and the wound-stiffened team set out, struggling painfully over the most treacherous section of the route they had faced.

The Thirty Mile River was wide open, its wild waters defying the frost. Ice existed only in eddies and quiet pools. Six days of exhausting toil were required to traverse those thirty terrible miles, each step risking life for both man and dog. A dozen times, Perrault, probing the path, broke through ice bridges, saved only by the long pole he wielded, holding it so it spanned the holes created by his own passage. A cold snap had descended, the thermometer reading fifty degrees below zero. Each time he breached the ice, he was forced to build a fire and dry his garments to survive.

Undaunted, he pressed on. It was this resilience that qualified him as a government courier. He exposed his wizened face to the biting frost, pushing forward from dim dawn to dark. He skirted the frowning shores on rim ice that bent and cracked underfoot, daring not to halt. Once, the sled broke through, plunging Dave and Buck into freezing water. By the time they were dragged out, they were half-frozen. A fire was essential to revive them; they were coated in solid ice, kept running around the flames until sweat thawed them, so close to the heat they were singed.

Another time, Spitz plunged through the ice, dragging the entire team after him up to Buck. The large dog strained backward with all his strength, forepaws slipping on the icy edge, the ice quivering and snapping around them. Behind him, Dave strained equally hard, and behind the sled, François pulled until his tendons creaked.

Again, the rim ice collapsed ahead and behind, leaving no escape except up the cliff. Perrault scaled it by a miracle, while François prayed for salvation. Using every thong, sled lashing, and piece of harness tied into a long rope, the dogs were hoisted one by one to the cliff crest. François climbed last, hauling the sled and load. Then came the arduous descent, achieved via the rope, and night found them back on the river, a quarter-mile short of their daily goal.

By the time they reached the Hootalinqua and solid ice, Buck was utterly spent. The rest of the team shared his exhaustion, yet Perrault drove them relentlessly to recover lost time. The first day covered thirty-five miles to the Big Salmon; the next, thirty-five more to the Little Salmon; the third, forty miles, bringing them well up toward the Five Fingers.

Buck's feet were not adapted to this terrain. Unlike the hardened pads of the native huskies, his had softened over generations since his ancestors were domesticated by cave dwellers or river men. All day, he limped in agony. Once camp was made, he lay down like a dead dog
