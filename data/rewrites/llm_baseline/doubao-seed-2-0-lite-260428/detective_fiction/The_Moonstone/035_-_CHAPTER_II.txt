My next task is to lay out every scrap of new information I hold relating to the Moonstone—more specifically, the long-running Indian plot to steal the gem—information that, trivial as it might sound on first glance, bears such sharp relevance to events still unfolding that no reader who cares to untangle this case can afford to skip over it.

A week to ten days after Miss Verinder departed her family’s estate, one of my clerks stepped into my private office, card in hand, to tell me a gentleman waiting downstairs requested an audience. I glanced at the card: the foreign name scrawled across the face escapes me now, but the handwritten English line at the bottom I can recall word for word: *Recommended by Mr. Septimus Luker*.

The sheer audacity of a man of Luker’s shady, disreputable standing daring to send a potential client to *me*—a solicitor of established, unblemished reputation—left me so stunned I sat silent for a beat, wondering if my eyes had played tricks. My clerk, noting my bewilderment, volunteered his own first impression of the visitor below.
“Remarkable-looking fellow, sir. Swarthy enough that the whole office pegged him for an Indian, or something of that Oriental sort.”

That one line tied straight to the note on the card, and I knew instantly the Moonstone was at the root of both Luker’s peculiar recommendation and the stranger’s call. To my clerk’s obvious shock, I told him to show the gentleman right in.
Before any reader chides me for setting aside professional prudence for nothing more than idle curiosity, let me remind them: no other living soul in England can claim the deep, long-held connection to the Indian diamond saga that I do. I was the one Colonel Herncastle trusted with his secret plan to evade assassination at the hands of the three devotees. I received his regular letters confirming he was still alive and in hiding. I drafted his will, leaving the Moonstone to Miss Verinder. I talked his hesitant executor into carrying out the bequest, on the chance the jewel might prove a rare and valuable gift for the Verinder family. And finally, I argued Franklin Blake out of his scruples, and convinced him to carry the diamond himself to Lady Verinder’s Yorkshire estate. If anyone has a right to take personal interest in every last detail related to the Moonstone, that man is me.

The moment the visitor stepped across my threshold, I felt a jolt of certainty: I was face to face with one of the three Indians, almost certainly their chief. He was dressed head to toe in immaculate European clothing, but nothing could hide his swarthy complexion, his long, lean, lithe frame, or the quiet, graceful, grave courtesy of his manners—all dead giveaways of his Oriental origin to any pair of eyes paying the least bit of attention. I gestured to a chair, and asked him to state his business.

He apologized in fluent, carefully chosen English for disturbing my work, then laid a small parcel on my desk. It was wrapped first in cloth of gold, then a second layer of fine silk. When he peeled both away, he revealed an ebony casket, exquisitely inlaid with tiny gemstones across every inch of its surface.
“I have come, sir,” he said, “to ask you to lend me a sum of money. This casket will stand as my surety, to guarantee my debt will be repaid in full.”
I tapped the line on his calling card. “And you were directed to me on Mr. Luker’s recommendation?”
He bowed.
“Might I ask why Mr. Luker himself did not advance you the funds you require?”
“Mr. Luker informed me, sir, he had no money free to lend.”
“And so he sent you straight to me?”
The Indian nodded, and tapped the card himself. “It is written there.”

Short, direct, no wasted words. I had no doubt if the Moonstone had been sitting on my desk that day, this polite, graceful man would have cut my throat without a single second of hesitation. That small flaw aside, though, he was the perfect model of a client. He made no attempt to waste my time, as every other British stranger I’d ever met in my position would have done.
“I am sorry you wasted the trip,” I told him. “Mr. Luker was entirely mistaken to send you here. Like every other solicitor of my standing, I do arrange loans for clients, but I never lend to strangers, and I never accept such a high-value, unproven surety as your casket as collateral.”

Instead of arguing, wheedling, or pressing me to bend my rules as any other man would have, the Indian only bowed again. He wrapped his casket back up in its silk and gold cloth wrappings without a single word of protest, and stood to leave.
“Will you extend the courtesy of answering one last question, before I take my leave?” he asked.
I bowed back. One question on parting? Most of my clients would have badgered me for fifty.
“Supposing, sir, that it had been both possible and customary for you to lend me that money,” he said, “what is the shortest length of time in which I would be allowed to pay the debt back?”
“Per standard British lending practice,” I answered, “you would be fully within your rights to repay the entire sum, if you wished, exactly one year from the day the loan was first advanced to you.”

He gave me the lowest, deepest bow of all, then slipped out of the room so softly, moving with that noiseless, supple, cat-like grace, that I admit it gave me a small, unshakable jolt. When I collected my wits enough to think it through, I landed on one very clear conclusion. The whole performance: the fancy gemmed casket, the formal application for a loan, the carefully polite demeanor—none of it was real. It was all a deliberate ruse, designed to get him alone in my office long enough to ask that one final question, and get that exact answer about the one-year repayment window. The only moment he had ever dropped his perfectly masked, unreadable, unflappable facade was the exact second I gave him that piece of information, when he looked me dead in the eye for the first and only time during our meeting.

I was just turning that over in my head, trying to work out what on earth he wanted with a 12-month timeline, when a letter was delivered to my desk. It was from none other than Septimus Luker himself. The letter oozed sickening, servile apology, begging me to grant him a private audience so he could explain his actions to my full satisfaction.
Curiosity won out over professional propriety for the second time that day. I wrote back to schedule an appointment for him to come to my office the following afternoon.

Luker was every bit the vulgar, ugly, cringing, tedious little man I expected him to be, not worth wasting many words on here. The gist of his story took barely five minutes to hear.
The day before the Indian had visited my office, he had turned up on Luker’s doorstep. Luker had recognized him instantly: the chief of the three Indian devotees who had loitered around his house months before, the very man who had once broken in, blindfolded him, gagged him, and robbed him of his bank receipt for the Moonstone. Luker had been so paralyzed with terror that he was convinced he was about to be murdered on the spot.
The Indian had played the perfect unknowing stranger, pulled out the gemmed casket, and made the exact same loan request he would later make to me. To get rid of him as fast as humanly possible, Luker immediately said he had no funds to lend. When the Indian asked who the most respectable, safest solicitor in London was for a man looking to take out a private loan, my name popped out of Luker’s mouth before he even thought, purely because he was so panicked my name was the first one he could drag up from his memory.
“The sweat was pouring off me like rain, sir,” the sniveling creature finished. “I didn’t know what I was saying. I beg you to overlook it, Mr. Bruff, I was truly scared out of my wits.”
I excused him graciously—if only to get him out of my sight as fast as possible. Before he left, I asked one last question: had the Indian asked him any odd, notable question before he left his house?
Yes. He had asked the exact same parting question, about the earliest possible repayment date for a loan, and Luker had given him the exact same answer: one full year.

What did it all mean? Luker’s story gave me no clues at all. My own wits, sharp as they are, couldn’t untangle the puzzle. That evening I had a dinner engagement to attend, and I headed up the stairs to my dressing room in a foul, frustrated mood, never suspecting that the short walk to my wardrobe would double as the straight path to the answer I had been chasing.
