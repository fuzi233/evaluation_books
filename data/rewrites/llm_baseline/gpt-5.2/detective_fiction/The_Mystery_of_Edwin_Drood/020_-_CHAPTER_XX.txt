Rosa came back to herself with no blessed blank between. The interview was there entire—word, look, and that sickly warmth of breathless insistence—pursuing her as if her swoon had been only a darker room to be haunted in. She lay for a moment as one who has been wakened by a hand upon the throat, and the single thought that could hold its shape was: I must get away from him.

But where? And by what safe path?

She had never spoken her fear—its very name seemed to summon it—to any one save Helena. To run to Helena now and pour out all might be to loose the very mischief he had threatened, the mischief he boasted he could do and that she knew he would choose to do if thwarted. The more her imagination, excited and raw, made him loom, the heavier the charge upon her seemed; for a slip—one rash word, one delay—might turn his malice full upon Helena’s brother.

All these six months her mind had been like weather over harvest-fields—gust and lull, cloud and sudden glare—never settling. A suspicion, half-formed and never spoken, rose like a bubble from deep water and burst into nothing, then rose again with a more hateful roundness. Everyone in Cloisterham had been so full of Jasper’s absorption in his nephew while he lived, and of his relentless pursuit of the inquiry into the disappearance after, that it seemed to Rosa no one could conceive foul play at his hands. She asked herself, shamed and shivering, Am I so wicked as to imagine a wickedness others cannot? Then she asked if her recoil from him, even before Edwin vanished, had bred the suspicion—if so, might not that prove it baseless? Then she pressed herself with motive. And the answer, when it came, made her hide her face as if it were her own crime: the motive of gaining me. To found murder on such vanity seemed monstrous; the very thought left a smear, as though the idea itself had dirtied her.

She ran again over what he had said by the sun-dial in the garden. He had insisted—always—on murder, and that insistence was in keeping with his public zeal since the watch and pin had been found. If he feared discovery, would he not rather encourage the notion of Edwin’s voluntary flight? He had even declared that, if the ties between him and his nephew had been less strong, he might have swept “even him” away from her side. Was that like a man who had truly done it? He had spoken of laying six months’ labour for a just vengeance at her feet; he had ranged his efforts with his desolate soul, his wasted life, his peace and his despair. The first sacrifice he claimed to make for her was fidelity to his dear boy after death. These things stood up like witnesses against the fancy she scarcely dared let breathe.

And yet—he was terrible. Terrible in a way no neat common rule could measure. She, with her young heart and its honest scales, tried to weigh him as if he were an average man, and the weights would not answer; he belonged to some darker kind, a wonder apart.

So she could come by no other road to the same conclusion: he must be fled from.

She had been Helena’s support through all the dreary interval; had assured her, again and again, of her belief in Neville’s innocence and her sorrow for his misery. Yet Rosa had never once seen Neville since the disappearance; Helena had never breathed a word to her of what he had confessed to Mr. Crisparkle concerning Rosa—though it was talked of far and wide as part of the case. To Rosa, he was still only Helena’s unfortunate brother. The assurance she had flung at Jasper—that she believed Neville innocent—was strictly true; but now she could have bitten her tongue for having given him that triumph: for however frightened she was, her spirit swelled at the thought of his knowing her fear from her own lips.

Anywhere beyond his reach—yes; but “anywhere” was no place. Somewhere must be named, a door must be found.

She resolved to go to her guardian, at once.

The old dread she had whispered to Helena on the night of their first confidence—the dread of not being safe from him, of the thick convent walls being helpless against his ghostly following—returned and took command. No reasoning could quiet it. The fascination of repulsion that had long held her reached its black height now, and she felt as if he could bind her by spell. When she rose to dress and glanced from the window, the sight of the sun-dial—where he had leaned when he declared himself—turned her cold. It stood in its usual place, a homely stone circle telling honest time to honest flowers, yet she shrank from it as if he had breathed into it something baleful that would cling there.

She wrote a hurried note to Miss Twinkleton: she had sudden reason for wishing to see her guardian immediately, had gone to him, and begged the good lady not to be uneasy, for all was well. Then she crammed a few quite useless little articles into her very little bag, left the note where it must be found, and slipped out, softly closing the gate behind her.

It was the first time she had ever been alone in Cloisterham High Street. She knew its turns as well as she knew the folds of her own ribbons, but now every familiar corner had a different sound. The Cathedral’s presence was behind everything, as always; yet the street-noise—wheels over uneven stones, voices rebounding between walls—seemed sharper, less human, as if the town itself were listening with her.

She hurried to the corner where the omnibus started. It was moving off at that moment.

“Stop and take me, if you please, Joe. I am obliged to go to London.”

In less than another minute she was under Joe’s protection, jolting away to the railway. He waited on her at the station, found her a place, got her safely into the carriage, and handed in the little bag as though it were a great trunk of iron.

“Can you go round when you get back, and tell Miss Twinkleton that you saw me safely off, Joe?”

“It shall be done, Miss.”

“With my love, please, Joe.”

“Yes, Miss—and I wouldn’t mind having it myself!” Joe thought, but did not say.

Now, as the wheels took their steady hold of the rails and the country began to slide away, Rosa had leisure again for thought. For a while indignation bore her up—the sense that Jasper’s love had soiled her; that only an appeal to the honest and true could wash off the touch of it. But as the evening darkened and the great city drew near, doubts gathered like gnats about a lamp. Was this wild? How would Mr. Grewgious take it? Would he be there? What if he were absent? What could become of her—alone—in a place so strange and crowded? If she had waited for counsel first—if she could go back now, would she not do it gladly? Such questions multiplied in the rhythmic clatter, each one keeping time with the next.

At length the train came into London over the housetops, and the gritty streets lay below with lamps already burning though the hot summer light had not yet failed. Heat seemed to have baked the very air into powder. “Hiram Grewgious, Esquire, Staple Inn, London.” That was all she knew; but it was enough to send her rattling away again in a cab, deeper and deeper into the city.

They drove through long deserts of dingy streets where people crowded at the mouths of courts to catch a breath of air that had been breathed too many times already; where others shuffled along the hot paving-stones with a weary sameness, as if the town had taught their feet one dull tune. Music sounded here and there—barrel-organs, a big drum, chapel bells—but none of it cheered. It all seemed trapped between bricks, striking dust from every ledge and cornice. The wind-instruments were worst: flat, reedy notes that sounded as if their hearts had cracked from longing for open fields. Art, sold by the corner like roasted chestnuts, could not buy a clean breath; and the city’s great din, made of wheels and cries and clanging timepieces, lay on Rosa’s nerves like a hand.

At last the cab stopped before a fast-closed gateway, as if the place belonged to someone who went to bed early and mistrusted all mankind. Rosa paid and got down, very little bag and all, and knocked timidly. A watchman let her in.

“Does Mr. Grewgious live here?”

“Mr. Grewgious lives there, Miss,” said the watchman, pointing further in.

She went on, and when the clocks were striking ten, stood on P. J. T.’s doorsteps, wondering what P. J. T. had done with his street-door. Guided by the painted name, she climbed the stairs and tapped softly—once, twice, again. No one answered. The handle yielded to her touch. She went in.

Mr. Grewgious sat on a window-seat at an open window, with a shaded lamp set far from him on a table in a corner, so that the room was more twilight than light. The city’s sound crept in—far-off wheels, a call flung and caught and flung again—yet the chamber held a hush, like a pantry late at night when the household sleeps.

Rosa drew nearer. He saw her and said under his breath:

“Good Heaven!”

She fell upon his neck with tears. He held her, and in the same instant cried, startled out of himself:

“My child, my child! I thought you were your mother!—But what, what, what,” he added, softening, “has happened? My dear, what has brought you here? Who has brought you here?”

“No one. I came alone.”

“Lord bless me!” ejaculated Mr. Grewgious. “Came alone! Why didn’t you write to me to come and fetch you?”

“I had no time. I took a sudden resolution. Poor, poor Eddy!”

“Ah, poor fellow, poor fellow!”

“His uncle has made love to me. I cannot bear it,” said Rosa, with a burst of tears and a stamp of her little foot. “I shudder with horror of him, and I have come to you to protect me and all of us from him, if you will?”

“I will,” cried Mr. Grewgious, with a rush of energy that was astonishing in so angular a man. “Damn him! ‘Confound his politics! Frustrate his knavish tricks! On Thee his hopes to fix? Damn him again!’”

After this extraordinary outbreak he plunged about the room, as if uncertain whether he was rousing himself to loyal fervour or to personal combat. He stopped, wiped his face, and said:

“I beg your pardon, my dear, but you will be glad to know I feel better. Tell me no more just now, or I might do it again. You must be refreshed and cheered. What did you take last? Was it breakfast, lunch, dinner, tea, or supper? And what will you take next? Shall it be breakfast, lunch, dinner, tea, or supper?”

He went down on one knee before her with respectful tenderness, helped her remove her hat, and gently disentangled her pretty hair from it with hands that looked made for parchments, not for such work. Yet the care was true chivalry—quiet and exact—of a sort not to be expected from Mr. Grewgious by those who knew only his surface.

“Your rest too must be provided for,” he went on, “and you shall have the prettiest chamber in Furnival’s. Your toilet must be provided for, and you shall have everything that an unlimited head chambermaid—by which expression I mean a head chambermaid not limited as to outlay—can procure. Is that a bag?” He looked hard at it; it took hard looking at, in that dim room. “And is it your property, my dear?”

“Yes, sir. I brought it with me.”

“It is not an extensive bag,” said Mr. Grewgious, candidly, “though admirably calculated to contain a day’s provision for a canary-bird. Perhaps you brought a canary-bird?”

Rosa smiled and shook her head.

“If you had, he should have been made welcome,” said Mr. Grewgious, “and I think he would have been pleased to be hung upon a nail outside and pit himself against our Staple sparrows; whose execution must be admitted to be not quite equal to their intention. Which is the case with so many of us! You didn’t say what meal, my dear. Have a nice jumble of all meals.”

Rosa thanked him, but could only manage the idea of a cup of tea. Mr. Grewgious, after several hurried starts out and back again to mention supplementary items—marmalade, eggs, watercresses, salted fish, frizzled ham—ran across to Furnival’s without his hat to give directions.

Soon afterward those directions arrived made real, and the board was spread in that bachelor room, made suddenly domestic by steam and fragrance. When the lamp was set upon the table, the tea gave out a clean, comforting breath that seemed to soften even the London dust. The hot water hissed faintly, a small homely sound against the city’s distant grinding; and the scent of toasted bread and salt and ham rose like a promise that the body could be steadied when the mind would not.

“Lord bless my soul,” cried Mr. Grewgious, sitting opposite her, “what a new sensation for a poor old Angular bachelor, to be sure!”

Rosa’s eyebrows asked him what he meant.

“The sensation of having a sweet young presence in the place, that whitewashes it, paints it, papers it, decorates it with gilding, and makes it Glorious!” said Mr. Grewgious. “Ah me! Ah me!”

There was something mournful in his sigh. Rosa, touching him with her teacup in a little toast, ventured also to touch him with her small hand.

“Thank you, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious. “Ahem! Let’s talk!”

“Do you always live here, sir?” asked Rosa.

“Yes, my dear.”

“And always alone?”

“Always alone; except that I have daily company in a gentleman by the name of Bazzard, my clerk.”

“He doesn’t live here?”

“No, he goes his way after office hours. In fact, he is off duty here, altogether, just at present; and a firm down-stairs, with which I have business relations, lend me a substitute. But it would be extremely difficult to replace Mr. Bazzard.”

“He must be very fond of you,” said Rosa.

“He bears up against it with commendable fortitude if he is,” returned Mr. Grewgious, considering. “But I doubt if he is. Not particularly so. You see, he is discontented, poor fellow.”

“Why isn’t he contented?”

“Misplaced,” said Mr. Grewgious, with great mystery.

Rosa’s eyebrows resumed their inquisitive perplexity.

“So misplaced,” Mr. Grewgious went on, “that I feel constantly apologetic towards him. And he feels (though he doesn’t mention it) that I have reason to be.”

Mr. Grewgious had grown so very mysterious that Rosa did not know how to go on; and while she hesitated, his manner suddenly jerked itself into frankness again.

“Let’s talk. We were speaking of Mr. Bazzard. It’s a secret, and moreover it is Mr. Bazzard’s secret; but the sweet presence at my table makes me so unusually expansive, that I feel I must impart it in inviolable confidence. What do you think Mr. Bazzard has done?”

“O dear!” cried Rosa, drawing her chair a little nearer, her mind leaping back—by some cruel habit—to Jasper. “Nothing dreadful, I hope?”

“He has written a play,” said Mr. Grewgious, in a solemn whisper. “A tragedy.”

Rosa looked quite relieved.

“And nobody,” pursued Mr. Grewgious in the same tone, “will hear, on any account whatever, of bringing it out.”

Rosa nodded slowly, reflective, as if to say, Such things are, and why are they!

“Now, you know,” said Mr. Grewgious, “I couldn’t write a play.”

“Not a bad one, sir?” said Rosa, innocently, her eyebrows flickering again.

“No. If I was under sentence of decapitation, and was about to be instantly decapitated, and an express arrived with a pardon for the condemned convict Grewgious if he wrote a play, I should be under the necessity of resuming the block, and begging the executioner to proceed to extremities—meaning,” said Mr. Grewgious, passing his hand under his chin, “the singular number, and this extremity.”

Rosa tried to imagine herself in so awkward a case.

“Consequently,” said Mr. Grewgious, “Mr. Bazzard would have a sense of my inferiority to himself under any circumstances; but when I am his master, you know, the case is greatly aggravated.”

He shook his head gravely, as if the offence—his own—were indeed heavy.

“How came you to be his master, sir?” asked Rosa.

“A question that naturally follows,” said Mr. Grewgious. “Let’s talk. Mr. Bazzard’s father, being a Norfolk farmer, would have furiously laid about him with a flail, a pitch-fork, and every agricultural implement available for assaulting purposes, on the slightest hint of his son’s having written a play. So the son, bringing to me the father’s rent (which I receive), imparted his secret, and pointed out that he was determined to pursue his genius, and that it would put him in peril of starvation, and that he was not formed for it.”

“For pursuing his genius, sir?”

“No, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious, “for starvation. It was impossible to deny the position, that Mr. Bazzard was not formed to be starved, and Mr. Bazzard then pointed out that it was desirable that I should stand between him and a fate so perfectly unsuited to his formation. In that way Mr. Bazzard became my clerk, and he feels it very much.”

“I am glad he is grateful,” said Rosa.

“I didn’t quite mean that, my dear. I mean, that he feels the degradation. There are some other geniuses that Mr. Bazzard has become acquainted with, who have also written tragedies, which likewise nobody will on any account whatever hear of bringing out, and these choice spirits dedicate their plays to one another in a highly panegyrical manner. Mr. Bazzard has been the subject of one of these dedications. Now, you know, I never had a play dedicated to me!”

Rosa looked as if she would have liked him to have a thousand dedications, bound in gold.

“Which again, naturally, rubs against the grain of Mr. Bazzard,” said Mr. Grewgious. “He is very short with me sometimes, and then I feel that he is meditating, ‘This blockhead is my master! A fellow who couldn’t write a tragedy on pain of death, and who will never have one dedicated to him with the most complimentary congratulations on the high position he has taken in the eyes of posterity!’ Very trying, very trying. However, in giving him directions, I reflect beforehand: ‘Perhaps he may not like this,’ or ‘He might take it ill if I asked that;’ and so we get on very well. Indeed, better than I could have expected.”

“Is the tragedy named, sir?” asked Rosa.

“Strictly between ourselves,” answered Mr. Grewgious, “it has a dreadfully appropriate name. It is called The Thorn of Anxiety. But Mr. Bazzard hopes—and I hope—that it will come out at last.”

The little history, with its dry turns and its kindly intention, had done its work. It had taken Rosa, for a space, from the edge of her own terror, as a cup of warm tea takes the trembling from the hands. The city still made its ceaseless, dusty roar outside; but within, the smaller sounds—the clink of a spoon, the fold of a napkin—were like a remembered language spoken quietly in the midst of a crowd that shouts in a harsher tongue.

“And now, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious at length, “if you are not too tired to tell me more of what passed to-day—but only if you feel quite able—I should be glad to hear it. I may digest it the better, if I sleep on it to-night.”

Rosa, steadier now, told him faithfully all that had happened—Jasper’s words, his threats, his dreadful certainty, and the shadow they cast towards Helena and Neville. Mr. Grewgious smoothed his head often as she spoke, and begged her to repeat those parts that bore on Helena and on Helena’s brother. When she had finished, he sat grave and silent for a time, as if he were tasting and testing each ingredient of the story, turning it in his mind as a careful cook turns a sauce, lest it split.

“Clearly narrated,” was his only remark at last, “and, I hope, clearly put away here,” smoothing his head again.

He took her to the open window.

“See, my dear,” he said, “where they live! The dark windows over yonder.”

The distant windows, black squares in a blacker mass, gave no sign; yet the very direction of them seemed to tug at Rosa’s heart.

“I may go to Helena to-morrow?” she asked.

“I should like to sleep on that question to-night,” he answered, doubtfully. “But let me take you to your own rest, for you must need it.”

He helped her put on her hat again, took the very little bag upon his arm as though it were precious, and led her by the hand—stately and awkward, like a man about to walk a minuet—across Holborn into Furnival’s Inn.

At the hotel door he confided her to the Unlimited head chambermaid, saying he would remain below while she went up to see her room, in case she wished it changed or found anything wanting.

Rosa’s room was airy, clean, comfortable, almost gay. The Unlimited had supplied all that the little bag could never have held—that is to say, everything. The bed looked cool; the linen smelled of honest soap; and after London’s grit the mere notion of a basin of clean water was like mercy.

Rosa tripped down the many stairs again to thank her guardian for his thoughtfulness.

“Not at all, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious, infinitely gratified. “It is I who thank you for your charming confidence and for your charming company. Your breakfast will be provided for you in a neat, compact, and graceful little sitting-room (appropriate to your figure), and I will come to you at ten o’clock in the morning. I hope you don’t feel very strange indeed, in this strange place.”

“O no, I feel so safe!”

“Yes, you may be sure that the stairs are fire-proof,” said Mr. Grewgious, “and that any outbreak of the devouring element would be perceived and suppressed by the watchmen.”

“I did not mean that,” Rosa replied. “I mean, I feel so safe from him.”

“There is a stout gate of iron bars to keep him out,” said Mr. Grewgious, smiling; “and Furnival’s is fire-proof, and specially watched and lighted, and I live over the way!”

In the stoutness of his knight-errantry, he seemed to believe that last protection sufficient against any darkness. In the same spirit he said to the gate-porter as he went out:

“If some one staying in the hotel should wish to send across the road to me in the night, a crown will be ready for the messenger.”

And in the same spirit he walked up and down outside the iron gate for the best part of an hour, listening to the city’s harsh orchestra—wheels, calls, distant music bruised against brick—while now and then he peered between the bars as if he had placed a dove in a high roost in a cage of lions, and feared she might tumble out.
