"The unripe grape, the ripe, and the dried. All things are changes, not into nothing, but into that which is not at present." — Marcus Aurelius.

Deeds are the pulse of Time, his beating life; and whether righteous or unrighteous, once performed, they must throb in after-pangs until Time itself is laid in darkness, and the universe quivers and breathes upon no mirror more.

In the evening, she summoned him again. It was nearly the hour when she had been brought in from the sea the previous day. The room was dimmed by raised blinds and open windows, allowing a subdued light to filter in. She sat gazing fixedly at the sea, her cheek resting on her hand. Though she appeared less shattered than when he had last left her, a profound melancholy clouded her features, shifting into an anxious timidity as Deronda drew near. She did not offer her hand, merely saying, "How long ago it is!" Then, softly, "Will you sit near me again a little while?"

He settled beside her as before. Noticing that she turned toward him with an indefinable expression suggesting a desperate need to speak, he waited patiently. Yet, she only looked back toward the window in silence, repeating the gesture without uttering a word. Fear held her tongue. Seeking to ease her hesitation, Deronda averted his gaze. Soon, however, he heard her cry out imploringly, "You will not say that anyone else should know?"

"Most decidedly not," Deronda replied firmly. "There is no action that ought to be taken in consequence. There is no injury that could be righted in that manner. There is no retribution that any mortal could apportion justly."

She remained utterly still during the pause, seeming to hold her breath, before whispering, "But if I had not had that murderous will—that moment—if I had thrown the rope on the instant—perhaps it would have hindered death?"

"No—I think not," Deronda answered slowly. "If it were true that he could swim, he must have been seized with cramp. With your quickest, utmost effort, it seems impossible that you could have done anything to save him. That momentary murderous will cannot, I think, have altered the course of events. Its effect is confined to the motives within your own breast. Within ourselves, our evil will is momentous; sooner or later, it works its way outward. It may manifest in the vitiating conditions that breed evil acts, but equally in the self-abhorrence that stings us into better striving."

"I am saved from robbing others," she said, her voice trembling. "There are others—they will have everything—they will have what they ought to have. I knew that some time before I left town. You do not suspect me of wrong desires regarding those things?"

She spoke hesitantly. "I had not thought of them," Deronda admitted, "for I was thinking too much of the other matters."

"Perhaps you do not quite know the beginning of it all," Gwendolen said slowly, as if struggling against her own reluctance. "There was someone else he ought to have married. I knew it, and I told her I would not hinder it. I went away—that was when you first saw me. But then we became poor all at once, and I was terribly miserable. I was tempted. I thought, 'I shall do as I like and make everything right.' I persuaded myself. And it was all different. It was all dreadful. Then came hatred and wicked thoughts. That is how it all started. I told you I was afraid of myself. And I did what you told me; I tried to make my fear a safeguard. I thought of what would happen if I... I felt what would come—the dread of the morning, wishing it would remain forever night, yet even in the darkness always seeing something, always seeing death. If you did not know how miserable I was, you might understand—but now, it has all been useless. I can care for nothing but saving the rest from knowing—especially poor Mamma, who has never been happy."

Silence stretched between them before she spoke again, her voice breaking with a repressed sob: "You cannot bear to look at me anymore. You think I am too wicked. You do not believe that I can become any better—worth anything—worthy enough. I shall always be too wicked to..." Her voice failed her, leaving her helpless.

Deronda's heart was pierced. Turning his eyes to her poor, beseeching face, he said, "I believe that you may become worthier than you have ever yet been—worthy to lead a life that may be a blessing. No evil dooms us hopelessly except the evil we love, and desire to continue in, and make no effort to escape from. You *have* made efforts—you will go on making them."

"But you were the beginning of them. You must not forsake me," Gwendolen pleaded, leaning forward with clasped hands on the arm of her chair. Her face bore the piteous traces of a lifetime of experience compressed into these twenty-four hours—a new, terrible existence lying on the other side of the deed that fulfilled a criminal desire. "I will bear any penance. I will lead any life you tell me. But you must not forsake me. You must be near. If you had been near me—if I could have confided everything to you, I should have been different. You will not forsake me?"

"It could never be my impulse to forsake you," Deronda said promptly, using a voice that, like his eyes, inadvertently made his ready sympathy feel more personal and special than it truly was. In that moment, however, he was not entirely free from a foreboding of such a self-committing effect. His strong compassion for this stricken creature could not prevent rushing images of future difficulties from crowding his mind. He continued to meet her appealing gaze, yet with the painful awareness that to her ears, his words carried a promise that one day might seem unfulfilled; he was making an indefinite pledge to an indefinite hope. Immediate and distant anxieties swarmed his thoughts, and under their weight, after a brief silence, he said, "I expect Sir Hugo Mallinger to arrive by tomorrow night at the latest; and I am not without hope that Mrs. Davilow may shortly follow him. Her presence will be the greatest comfort to you—it will give you a motive to save her from unnecessary pain?"

"Yes, yes—I will try. And you will not go away?"

"Not until after Sir Hugo has come."

"But we shall all go to England?"

"As soon as possible," Deronda replied, unwilling to enter into particulars.

Gwendolen looked toward the window again, her expression resembling a gradual awakening to new thoughts. The twilight was perceptibly deepening, yet Deronda observed a movement in her eyes and hands, akin to the return of perception in one who has been stunned.

"You will always be with Sir Hugo now!" she said suddenly, fixing her gaze on him. "You will always live at the Abbey—or else at Diplow?"

"I am quite uncertain where I shall live," Deronda said, blushing.

Warned by his changed countenance that she had spoken too rashly, she fell silent. After a moment, she began again, looking away, "It is impossible to think how my life will go on. I think now it would be better for me to be poor and obliged to work."

"New promptings will come as the days pass. When you are among your friends again, you will discern new duties," Deronda advised. "Make it a task now to get as well and calm—as much like yourself as you can, before—" He hesitated.

"Before my mother comes," Gwendolen finished.

"Ah! I must be changed. I have not looked at myself. Should you have known me," she added, turning toward him, "if you had met me now?—should you have recognized me as the one you saw at Leubronn?"

"Yes, I should have known you," Deronda said mournfully. "The external change is not great. I should have seen at once that it was you, and that you had endured some great sorrow."

"Don't wish now that you had never seen me; don't wish that," Gwendolen implored, tears gathering in her eyes.

"I should despise myself for wishing it," Deronda said. "How could I know what I was wishing? We must find our duties in what comes to us, not in what we imagine might have been. If I took to foolish wishing of that sort, I should wish—not that I had never seen you, but that I had been able to save you from this."

"You have saved me from worse," Gwendolen sobbed. "I should have been worse if it had not been for you. If you had not been good, I should have been more wicked than I am."

"It will be better for me to go now," Deronda said, worn in spirit by the perpetual strain of the scene. "Remember what we said of your task—to get well and calm before other friends come."

He rose as he spoke, and she gave him her hand submissively. But when he had left her, she sank to her knees, overcome by hysterical crying. The distance between them felt insurmountable. She was a banished soul, beholding a possible life she had sinfully alienated herself from. She was found thus, crushed upon the floor. Such grief seemed natural indeed for a lady whose husband had been drowned in her presence.
