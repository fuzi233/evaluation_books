With the bread problem solved, my mind turned inevitably to the wreck and the treasures still locked within it. Above all else, I was determined to claim the beautiful pinnace. Knowing that our combined strength would be required to accomplish this feat, I approached my wife with earnest persuasion, asking her to allow me to take the boys—except for little Franz—to retrieve it.

Reluctantly, she agreed, but only after I solemnly promised that I would never spend a single night aboard the ship. Though I accepted this condition with a heavy heart, we parted ways, neither entirely content with the arrangement.

The boys were ecstatic at the prospect of such a large expedition. Merrily, they carried provision bags stuffed with cassava-bread and potatoes. Upon reaching Safety Bay without incident, we first visited the geese and ducks inhabiting the nearby marshes. After feeding them and confirming they were thriving, we strapped on our cork belts, stepped into the tub-boat, towed the raft, and steered directly toward the wreck.

Once aboard, I instructed the boys to gather whatever items were easiest to reach and load them onto the raft for our nighttime return. Then, we conducted a meticulous inspection of the pinnace.

I quickly realized that the task ahead was nearly insurmountable. The vessel lay in an inaccessible position at the far end of the hold, wedged in a cramped, narrow space where assembly was impossible. Furthermore, the components were so heavy that moving them piece by piece to a convenient location was equally futile.

I dismissed the boys to search for anything else worth taking and sat quietly to ponder the dilemma. As my eyes adjusted to the dim light filtering through cracks in the hull, I noticed something remarkable: every part of the pinnace had been carefully arranged and numbered. If I could devise a plan and create sufficient workspace, success seemed within reach.

"Room! We need room to work!" I called out as my sons gathered to see my plan. "Bring axes, and let us break down the surrounding woodwork to clear a space!"

We worked tirelessly, yet as evening approached, progress was minimal. We acknowledged that immense labor and perseverance were required before we could truly call this elegant craft our own. It lay within the vast hulk like a fossil shell trapped in rock.

Hastening preparations for our return, we landed at Falconhurst with little enthusiasm for the long walk. To our great delight, we found my wife and little Franz waiting at Tentholm. She had decided to relocate there during our absence at the wreck. "In this way, you will live closer to your work, and I shall not lose sight of you completely," she smiled pleasantly.

"You are a good, sensible, kind wife," I exclaimed, thrilled by her solution. "We shall work with renewed diligence so that you may return to your dear Falconhurst as soon as possible."

"Come and see what we've brought you, Mother!" cried Fritz. "Is this not a fine addition to your stores?" He and his brothers displayed two small casks of butter, three of flour, along with corn, rice, and other goods that our careful housewife would surely appreciate.

Our days were now consumed by arduous labor on the wreck: first clearing a space around the pinnace, then assembling its parts. We rose early and returned late, each trip yielding valuable cargo from the old vessel. Finally, after incredible effort, everything was complete. The pinnace stood ready to be launched, yet it remained imprisoned within massive wooden walls that defied our strength.

It seemed as though the graceful vessel had awakened from slumber, longing to spring into the free blue sea and spread its wings to the breeze. I could not bear the thought that our success thus far might be undone by failure. Yet, no method of freeing her presented itself, and despair threatened to overwhelm me—until an idea struck.

Without revealing my intent, I procured a large cast-iron mortar, filled it with gunpowder, and secured a block of oak atop it, piercing a hole for a slow-burning match. Positioning this makeshift petard so that its explosion would blast open the side of the wreck nearest the pinnace, I chained it firmly to prevent recoil damage.

Telling the boys I was returning to shore earlier than usual, I calmly ordered them into the boat. Lighting a match designed to burn for several seconds before igniting the powder, I hurried after them with a pounding heart. We raced for the land.

Bringing the raft close to shore, we began unloading. I left the tub-boat ready for immediate departure, my anxiety hidden as I waited with straining nerves for the inevitable sound. It came—a flash! A mighty roar! A grand burst of smoke!

My wife and children, terrified, turned toward the sea, then looked to me for explanation. "Perhaps," my wife suggested hesitantly, "you left a light burning near the gunpowder, and an accidental explosion occurred?"

"Not at all unlikely," I replied calmly. "We had a fire below while caulking the pinnace's seams. I shall investigate immediately. Will anyone accompany me?"

The boys needed no second invitation and sprang into the boat. I paused briefly to reassure my wife with whispered explanations before joining them. Pulling toward the wreck at unprecedented speed, we arrived to find a miraculous sight.

Where we usually boarded, the side remained intact. But rounding to the opposite side, we beheld devastation: a huge rent tore through the decks and bulwarks, water churned with floating debris, and the compartment housing the pinnace was fully exposed.

Yet there sat the little beauty, seemingly uninjured. While the boys stared in awe at the ruined surroundings, I shouted in triumphant delight, "Hurrah! She is ours! The lovely pinnace is won! We can launch her easily now. Come, boys, let us check if she suffered from the explosion that freed her!"

Gazing at me, the boys caught on instantly. "You planned it yourself, you clever, cunning father! That machine we helped build was meant to blow it up!" they cried, eagerly following me into the shattered opening. To my intense satisfaction, everything was perfect; the captive vessel bore no scars from the violent measures taken for her liberation.

The boys were fascinated by the effects of the explosion and the principles behind using a petard. Clearly, launching the vessel would now pose little difficulty. I had placed rollers beneath the keel, allowing us to use levers and pulleys to guide her forward with our combined strength.

Attaching a rope to control her descent, all hands pushed shoulder-to-shoulder. The pinnace slid from the stocks, slipping gently and steadily into the water, where she floated as if recognizing her natural element. Wild with excitement, we cheered and waved enthusiastically.

We stayed only long enough to secure our prize at the most sheltered point before returning to Tentholm. There, we explained the explosion, claiming that blowing away one side of the ship would allow us to recover the remaining contents with just a few more days of work.

Those days were dedicated to completing the rigging, mounting the two small brass cannons, and making all necessary arrangements. Possessing a vessel armed with real guns ignited a fierce martial spirit in the boys. They talked ceaselessly of savages, canoe fleets, attacks, defenses, and the ultimate destruction of invaders.

"I assure you," I told them, "though your victories will undoubtedly be brilliant, we shall have good reason to thank God if your newfound courage and fighting skills are never tested."

The pinnace was fully equipped and ready to sail, yet my wife remained unaware of the surprise we were preparing. I allowed the boys, who had guarded the secret so well, to fire a salute as we entered the bay.

Leaving the wreck, sails unfurled, our voyage began. The pinnace glided swiftly through the water. I stood at the helm, Ernest and Jack manned the guns, and Fritz commanded, "Fire!" *Bang! Bang!* A thrilling report echoed among the cliffs, followed by our shouts and cheers.

My wife and little Franz rushed forward from near the tent, their faces etched with alarm and astonishment. But quickly recognizing us, they waved joyfully and hurried to the landing place to greet us.

Using skillful navigation, we brought the pinnace near a projection of the bank. Fritz helped his mother aboard, where she breathed heavily, exclaiming, "You dear, horrid, wonderful people! Shall I scold or praise you? You frightened me out of my wits! Seeing a beautiful little ship sail in was startling enough—I couldn't guess who might be on board—but the report of your guns made me tremble with fear. Had I not recognized your voices immediately afterward, I would have fled with Franz heaven knows where!"

"But have you really done all this work yourselves?" she asked once forgiveness was granted for terrifying her with our vainglorious salute. "What a charming little yacht! I wouldn't be afraid to sail in this myself."

After admiring the pinnace and praising our industry, skill, and perseverance, my wife said, "Now, you must come with me to see how little Franz and I have improved our daily routine during your absence."

We all disembarked and followed her upriver toward the cascade. To our amazement, we found a garden neatly arranged with beds and paths. "We don't frighten people with salutes for our achievements," she continued. "Though eventually, I too shall desire fire—for peaceful purposes. Look at my lettuce and cabbage beds, my rows of beans and peas! Imagine the delicious dinners I'll cook for you. Give me credit for my diligence."
