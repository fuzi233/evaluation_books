The struggle against one's own nature, that fierce bending of the heart toward its native inclinations, may appear futile, a wasted effort against the tide. Yet, in the end, it yields a harvest. It grants actions and conduct a turn that Reason approves, even if Feeling, too often, rebels. It regulates the tenor of a life, making it more equable, quieter on the surface where the common gaze falls. As for what lies beneath, leave that to God. Man, your equal, weak as you, unfit to judge, may be shut out; take the secrets of the spirit to your Maker. Kneel and pray for light in darkness, strength in weakness, patience in need. Surely, at some hour, the waiting waters will stir. In some shape, though not the one your heart bled for, the healing herald will descend. The cripple, the blind, the dumb, the possessed will be led to bathe. Herald, come quickly! Thousands weep around the stagnant pool, waiting through slow years. Long are the times of Heaven; the orbits of angel messengers seem wide to mortal vision, encompassing ages. Dust kindles to brief suffering life, passes back to dust, and perishes from memory again and again. To how many maimed millions is the first and sole angel visitant, Azrael?

I tried to rise the next morning, but while dressing and drinking cold water from the carafe on my washstand to brace my trembling weakness, Mrs. Bretton entered.

"Here is an absurdity!" she declared, her morning greeting sharp and brisk. "No," she added, seizing me with her usual energetic fashion—the same manner I once enjoyed seeing applied to her son, and which he vigorously resisted—and within two minutes, she consigned me captive to the French bed.

"There you lie till afternoon," she commanded. "My boy left orders before he went out that such should be the case, and I can assure you my son is master and must be obeyed. Breakfast will follow presently."

Presently, she brought that meal, serving it with her own active hands, refusing to leave me to servants. She seated herself on the bed while I ate. Not everyone, even among our respected friends, likes to be watched, waited upon, or approached with the proximity of a nurse to a patient. Yet, Mrs. Bretton was different to me; she had always been so. Food or drink never pleased me as well when it came through her hands. I do not recall an occasion when her entrance did not make a room cheerier. Our natures hold strange predilections. There are people from whom we shrink, though reason admits they are good; there are others, with evident faults of temper, beside whom we live content, as if the air about them did us good. My godmother's lively black eye, clear brunette cheek, warm prompt hand, self-reliant mood, and decided bearing were beneficial to me like the atmosphere of a salubrious climate. Her son called her "the old lady," yet it filled me with pleasant wonder to note how the alacrity and power of twenty-five still breathed from her.

"I would bring my work here," she said, taking the empty teacup, "and sit with you all day, if that overbearing John Graham had not vetoed it. 'Now, mamma,' he said when he left, 'do not knock up your god-daughter with gossip.' He particularly ordered me to stay in my quarters and spare you my fine company. He thinks, Lucy, you have had a nervous fever, judging by your look—is that so?"

I replied that I did not know exactly what my ailment was, but that I had certainly suffered greatly, especially in my mind. On this subject, I did not wish to dwell; the details belonged to a portion of my existence where I never expected my godmother to tread. Into what new region would such confidence have led that hale, serene nature! The difference between her and me might be figured by that between a stately ship cruising safely on smooth seas, with a full crew, a captain gay and brave, and a life-boat lying dry and solitary in a dark boat-house, putting to sea only when billows run high and danger divides the rule of the deep. No, the "Louisa Bretton" never sailed out on such a night; her crew could not conceive it. So the half-drowned life-boat man keeps his counsel and spins no yarns.

She left me, and I lay in bed content; it was good of Graham to remember me before he went out.

My day was lonely, but the prospect of evening abridged and cheered it. I felt weak, and rest was welcome. After the morning hours passed—those hours that bring a sense of business and vague obligation to the unoccupied—I drifted into a dreamy mood, not unpleasant.

My calm little room seemed like a cave in the sea. There was no color save white and pale green, suggestive of foam and deep water. The blanched cornice bore shell-shaped ornaments, and white moldings like dolphins adorned the ceiling angles. Even the single touch of color in the red satin pincushion bore affinity to coral; even the dark, shining glass might have mirrored a mermaid. When I closed my eyes, I heard a gale subsiding, bearing upon the house-front like a settling swell on a rock-base. I heard it drawn far away, like a tide retiring from a shore of the upper world—a world so high that the rush of its largest waves sounded down here only as murmurs and a lullaby.

Evening arrived amidst these dreams, and Martha brought a light. With her aid, I was quickly dressed. Stronger than in the morning, I made my way down to the blue salon unassisted.

Dr. John had concluded his rounds earlier than usual. His form was the first object to meet my eyes as I entered the parlor; he stood in the window-recess opposite the door, reading the close type of a newspaper by the dull light of closing day. The fire shone clear, but the lamp stood unlit on the table, and tea was not yet served.

Mrs. Bretton, my active godmother—who I later learned had been out in the open air all day—lay half-reclined in her deep-cushioned chair, lost in a nap. Seeing me, her son came forward. He trod carefully, not to wake the sleeper, and spoke low; his mellow voice, modulated as it was, was calculated to soothe rather than startle slumber.

"This is a quiet little château," he observed, inviting me to sit near the casement. "I don't know if you noticed it in your walks: though, indeed, from the chaussée it is not visible. Just a mile beyond the Porte de Crécy, you turn down a lane which soon becomes an avenue, leading you through meadow and shade to the very door. It is not a modern place, but built in the old style of the Basse-Ville. It is rather a manoir than a château; they call it 'La Terrasse' because its front rises from a broad turfed walk, whence steps lead down a grassy slope to the avenue. See yonder! The moon rises; she looks well through the tree-boles."

Where indeed does the moon not look well? What scene, confined or expansive, does her orb not hallow? Rosy or fiery, she mounted now above a not distant bank; even while we watched her flushed ascent, she cleared to gold and floated up stainless into a now calm sky. Did moonlight soften or sadden Dr. Bretton? Did it touch him with romance? I think it did. Albeit of no sighing mood, he sighed quietly in watching it. No need to ponder the cause; I knew it was wakened by beauty, that it pursued Ginevra. Knowing this, the idea pressed upon me that it was my duty to speak the name he meditated. Of course he was ready for the subject; I saw in his countenance a teeming plenitude of comment, question, and interest—a pressure of language and sentiment, only checked, I thought, by a sense of embarrassment on how to begin. To spare him this embarrassment was my best, indeed my sole use. I had but to utter the idol's name, and love's tender litany would flow out. I had just found a fitting phrase, "You know that Miss Fanshawe is gone on a tour with the Cholmondeleys," and was opening my lips, when he scattered my plans by introducing another theme.

"The first thing this morning," he said, putting his sentiment in his pocket and turning from the moon to sit down, "I went to the Rue Fossette and told the cuisinière that you were safe and in good hands. Do you know that I actually found she had not yet discovered your absence? She thought you safe in the great dormitory. With what care you must have been waited on!"

"Oh! all that is very conceivable," I said. "Goton could do nothing for me but bring a little tisane and a crust of bread, and I had rejected both so often during the past week that the good woman grew tired of useless journeys from the kitchen to the school-dormitory, coming only once a day at noon to make my bed. I believe, however, she is a good-natured creature and would have been delighted to cook me côtelettes de mouton if I could have eaten them."

"What did Madame Beck mean by leaving you alone?"

"Madame Beck could not foresee that I should fall ill."

"Your nervous system bore a good share of the suffering?"

"I am not quite sure what my nervous system is, but I was dreadfully low-spirited."

"Which disables me from helping you by pill or potion. Medicine can give nobody good spirits. My art halts at the threshold of Hypochondria: she just looks in and sees a chamber of torture, but can neither say nor do much. Cheerful society would be of use; you should be as little alone as possible; you should take plenty of exercise."

Acquiescence and a pause followed. They sounded right, bearing the safe sanction of custom and the well-worn stamp of use.

"Miss Snowe," Dr. John recommenced, my health and nervous system now discussed and done with, "is it permitted me to ask what your religion is? Are you a Catholic?"

I looked up in surprise. "A Catholic? No! Why suggest such an idea?"

"The manner in which you were consigned to me last night made me doubt."

"I consigned to you? But, indeed, I forget. It yet remains for me to learn how I fell into your hands."

"Why, under circumstances that puzzled me. I had been in attendance all day yesterday on a case of singularly interesting and critical character; the disease being rare, and its treatment doubtful. I saw a similar, still finer case in a hospital in Paris, but that will not interest you. At last a mitigation of the patient's most urgent symptoms liberated me, and I set out homeward. My shortest way lay through the Basse-Ville, and as the night was excessively dark, wild, and wet, I took it. In riding past an old church belonging to a community of Béguines, I saw by a lamp burning over the porch a priest lifting some object in his arms. The lamp was bright enough to reveal his features clearly, and I recognized him; he was a man I have often met by the sick beds of both rich and poor, chiefly the latter. He is, I think, a good old man, far better than most of his class in this country; superior, indeed, in every way, better informed, as well as more devoted to duty. Our eyes met; he called on me to stop. What he supported was a woman, fainting or dying. I alighted.

"'This person is one of your countrywomen,' he said. 'Save her, if she is not dead.'

"My countrywoman, on examination, turned out to be the English teacher at Madame Beck's pensionnat. She was perfectly unconscious, perfectly bloodless, and nearly cold.

"'What does it all mean?' was my inquiry.

"He communicated a curious account; that you had been to him that evening at confessional; that your exhausted and suffering appearance, coupled with some things you had said—"

"Things I had said? I wonder what things!"

"Awful crimes, no doubt; but he did not tell me what: there, you know, the seal of the confessional checked his garrulity, and my curiosity. Your confidences, however, had not made an enemy of the good father; it seems he was so struck, and felt so sorry that you should be out on such a night alone, that he esteemed it a Christian duty to watch you when you quitted the church, managing not to lose sight of you till you reached home. Perhaps the worthy man might, half unconsciously, have blended in this proceeding some little of the subtlety of his class: it might have been his resolve to learn the locality of your home—did you impart that in your confession?"

"I did not; on the contrary, I carefully avoided the shadow of any indication. And as to my confession, Dr. John, I suppose you will think me mad for taking such a step, but I could not help it. I suppose it was all the fault of what you call my 'nervous system.' I cannot put the case into words, but my days and nights were grown intolerable. A cruel sense of desolation pained my mind—a feeling that would make its way, rush out, or kill me—like the current which passes through the heart, and which, if an aneurism or any other morbid cause obstructs its natural channels, seeks an abnormal outlet. I wanted companionship, I wanted friendship, I wanted counsel. I could find none of these in closet or chamber, so I went and sought them in church and confessional. As to what I said, it was no confidence, no narrative. I have done nothing wrong; my life has not been active enough for any dark deed, either of romance or reality. All I poured out was a dreary, desperate complaint."

"Lucy, you ought to travel for about six months: why, your calm nature is growing quite excitable! Confound Madame Beck! Has the little buxom widow no bowels to condemn her best teacher to solitary confinement?"
