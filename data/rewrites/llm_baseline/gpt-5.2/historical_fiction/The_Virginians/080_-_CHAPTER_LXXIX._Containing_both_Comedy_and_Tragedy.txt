Those of us who had been bustling about that guilty business in the morning felt our shame come back threefold when we saw what it had done to him whom, of all men, we most revered. The blow fell strangely on that good man: not loud, not violent, but with a quiet that was more piteous than any outcry. The child of his heart had deceived and disobeyed him; the whole of his household—women, servants, and we who called ourselves his friends—had, as it were, drawn a net round him and pulled it tight.

If a man’s very nature be to give, to pardon, to stoop, then to wound him is like striking at something sacred and helpless; it is like troubling a sleeping infant, or rudely jarring the arm that is nursing it. We—who had done the deed—found ourselves reduced to a kind of abasement that no words can soften. I pass over the tears, the prayers, the gentle grave sentences by which he forgave; the common worship that followed, as if to bind up the rent we had made; and then the last parting, when he went away to his government, and the ship drew off from the shore and left us behind.

Theo and I stood hand in hand, dumb and burning with humiliation. She did not come to me as my wife until he was gone. Between the marriage and his departure she kept her old place—at home, by her father’s chair; sleeping still with her sister; moving about the house as one who is afraid her footfall might sound too loud. He was kind as ever—kind with that steady, unshowy kindness that makes you ashamed to be pitied by it; but the women scarcely spoke to one another. Aunt Lambert, for once, had an acid fretfulness, as though even the air offended her; and little Hetty was feverish and strange, repeating to herself, “I wish we were gone. I wish we were gone,” as if the words were a charm to hurry time.

Though I was forgiven and admitted, I went there like a thief with a pardon in his pocket. I slunk away during those last days; I saw my wife only for a minute in the street, or in the doorway with her family near. She was not mine until they were gone.

When they had embarked, we went to Winchester and Hampton for what in happier circumstances might be called our wedding. It had the shape of rejoicing, and none of the substance. We were lonely with a loneliness that had remorse in it. We spoke of our dear father—so she called him, and so did I, for he had earned the name—as if we had buried him; as if, by our undutifulness, we had helped to lay him in the grave.

I had Sampson put the marriage in the papers. My wife used afterwards to droop her head at the poor fellow, as though she saw our secret made public in his very boots and livery. I brought Mrs. Warrington back to my old lodgings in Bloomsbury, where there was room enough for our two chairs, our two plates, and that modest life which begins with a kettle and a candle and thinks itself rich.

I wrote to my mother in Virginia. I told her no particulars. I said only that Mr. Lambert being about to depart for his government, I held myself in honour bound to fulfil my promise to his dearest daughter; and that I meant to complete my studies for the Bar, and qualify myself for employment at home, in our own colony or any other. Mrs. Mountain answered for Madam Esmond—by desire, she said, of my mother, who thought that for peace’s sake my communications had best go that way.

Meanwhile I found my English kindred in a fury so formal that it was almost comic. The butler at Hill Street, when he said, “Not at home,” presented a blank tragedy of a face—one might have studied it as carefully as Mr. Garrick studies a ghost. My poor little wife was on my arm; we tripped away, laughing, when we came upon my lady in a street stoppage, sitting in her chair like a wronged goddess, her footmen and chairmen planted round her.

I took off my hat and bowed with all the humility I could enact. I asked after my dear cousins as tenderly as if we had not scandalized her house.

“I—I wonder you dare look me in the face!” Lady Warrington gasped.

“Don’t deprive me of that precious privilege,” said I. “It is the one comfort left to me.”

“Move on, Peter!” she screamed, and the chairmen started.

“Your ladyship would not impale your own husband’s flesh and blood,” I said.

She rattled up the glass of her chair as if to shut out not only my voice, but the very air that carried it. I kissed my hand, lifted my hat again, and made her a bow that, had it been a dish, might have passed for a whipped syllabub—airy, extravagant, and meant to provoke.

Not long after, walking in Hyde Park with Theo—my dearest companion, whose presence made even the winter bright—I met my little cousin Miles exercising on horseback, with a groom behind him. The boy saw us, and, like a hawk stooping, came galloping up, the groom thundering and powdering after, bawling, “Stop, Master Miles, stop!”

“I am not to speak to my cousin,” says Miles, pulling up his pony with a boy’s audacity, “but telling you to send my love to Harry is not speaking to you, is it? Is that my new cousin? I’m not told not to speak to her. I’m Miles—cousin, Sir Miles Warrington Baronet’s son—and you are very pretty!”

“Now, duee now, Master Miles,” says the groom, touching his hat to us, and the boy trotted away laughing, looking back over his shoulder to see if he had made us smile.

“You see,” I said to Theo, “how my relations have determined to treat me.”

“As if I married you for your relations!” she answered, and her eyes—full of brave joy, full of love—made me richer than any reconciliation with Hill Street could have done.

So the winter went on: brisk, pleasant in spite of all; our little rooms snug; the kettle singing on the hob like a domestic bird; and when Sampson—abashed, yet faithful—came now and then to make the punch, the smell of the lemon-peel as it twisted under the knife, and the warm bite of the spirits as they met the sugar, seemed to set a glow in the very air. Theo had a particular neatness and tenderness in such small offices: she would pare the rind thin, avoiding the bitter white; she would bruise the peel with the sugar in the bottom of the bowl until the grains shone with the oil; and then the hot water would go in, and at last the wine, and the whole would steam up with a fragrance that made our plainest evening feel like a feast.

We went at nights to the theatre when friends brought us tickets. Day by day we expected our new play of Pocahontas to rise and eclipse former tragedies; and at home there was tea, there was talk, there was that delicious sound of quiet living—the small clicks of china, the hiss from the kettle, the crackle of the coal, the far-off rumble of coaches in the street like a tide you listen to from a safe shore. There are noises in London that bruise the mind—hammering, shouting, the endless grinding of wheels—and there are noises that comfort it; and I found, in those weeks, that our happiness depended not only on what we had to eat, but on what we had to hear.

The fickle old aunt in Clarges Street, who at my first coming to town with my wife had received me with scorn, soon softened; and no sooner did she know Theo—whom she had pronounced an insignificant country-faced chit—than she fell utterly in love with her. She would have her to tea and supper every day when there was no other company.

“As for company, my dears,” she would say, “I don’t ask you. You are no longer du monde. Your marriage has put that entirely out of the question.”

So she would have had us come to amuse her and go in and out by the back-stairs, like actors slipping behind the scenes. Theo had fine-lady pride enough to be entertained rather than wounded; and I must do the Baroness’s domestics the justice to say that, had we been duke and duchess, we could not have been received with more respect. There is a courtesy in well-trained servants that sometimes keeps alive a decency which their betters forget.

Madame de Bernstein was wonderfully tickled with the story of Lady Warrington and her chair. I acted it for her; I gave her anecdotes of the pious baronet’s lady and her daughters; and the old woman, mischievous as a girl, delighted in them as in a sharp relish.

The Dowager Countess of Castlewood, established at Kensington, gave us that reception which genteel ladies extend to their poorer relatives—civil, correct, and cold as a plate that has been warmed and wiped a dozen times. We went once or twice to her drums, but after losing more money at cards and spending more in coach-hire than I chose to afford, we gave up those entertainments. In the fashionable world people are dropped daily by death, debt, and a hundred accidents, and are missed no more than a leaf missed from a tree in autumn.

Theo did not regret our exclusion. She wore once at Kensington some little ornaments left by her mother—trinkets to which the good lady had attached a value—yet to my eyes her own white neck, her fresh modest beauty, were worth more than those twinkling stones. And there were dowagers there whose wrinkled bones blazed with rubies and diamonds, which I am sure they would gladly have exchanged for Theo’s youth and sweetness, could such things be traded like jewels.

Not a soul spoke to her—except Beau Lothair, a friend of Mr. Will’s, who prowled about Bloomsbury afterwards, and even sent my wife a billet. I met him in Covent Garden and promised to break his ugly face if ever I saw it in the neighbourhood of my lodgings. Madam Theo was not molested again.

Of our relatives the only one who came to see us was poor Maria, who, with her husband Mr. Hagan, often shared our homely dinner. We had friend Spencer from the Temple, who admired our little Arcadian felicity and asked—very gently—for our compassion for his less fortunate loves. And twice or thrice the famous Doctor Johnson came in for a dish of Theo’s tea.

A dish? a pailful. “And a pail the best thing to feed him, sar!” says Mr. Gumbo indignantly; for the Doctor’s appearance was not pleasant, nor his linen particularly white. When he ate he snorted and grew red; he sputtered; he flung his meat about; he roared in contradiction; and he annoyed Theo—whom he professed to admire—by declaring, every time he saw her, “Madam, you do not love me; I see by your manner you do not love me; though I admire you, and come here for your sake. Here is my friend Mr. Reynolds that shall paint you: he has no ceruse in his paint-box as brilliant as your complexion.”

Mr. Reynolds, a most agreeable gentleman, would indeed have painted my wife; but I knew his price and did not choose to incur it. I wish now, for the sake of the children, that I had. A face is a kind of history; and we let histories slip away because we count guineas too carefully.

What vexed Theo in Dr. Johnson more than his contradictions and dirty nails was, I think, an unfavourable opinion he formed of my new tragedy. Hagan once proposed, with his actor’s zeal, that the Doctor should hear some scenes read after tea.

“Nay, sir, conversation is better,” says the Doctor. “I can read for myself, or hear you at the theatre. I had rather hear Mrs. Warrington’s artless prattle than your declamation of Mr. Warrington’s decasyllables. Tell us about your household affairs, madam—whether his Excellency your father is well—and whether you made the pudden and the butter sauce. The butter sauce was delicious!”

He loved it so well he had carried off a large quantity in the bosom of a very dingy shirt. Theo, who made butter sauce as she did everything—patiently, with attention, not letting it break, coaxing the melted butter into the flour and liquor until it turned smooth and shining—coloured up to hear it praised, and then coloured more to see where it had gone.

“You made it as though you loved me,” he persisted. “You helped me as though you loved me, though you don’t.”

“Faith, sir,” says Hagan, with spirit, “you are taking some of the present away with you in your waistcoat.”

“Sir, you are rude!” bawls the Doctor. “You are unacquainted with the first principles of politeness, which is courtesy before ladies. Having received an university education, I am surprised that you have not learned the rudiments of politeness. I respect Mrs. Warrington. I should never think of making personal remarks about her guests before her!”

“Then, sir,” cries Hagan, fierily, “why did you speak of my theatre?”

“Sir, you are saucy!” roars the Doctor.

“De te fabula,” says the actor.

“I think it is your waistcoat that is saucy. Madam, shall I make some punch in the way we make it in Ireland?” And the Doctor, puffing, purple in the face, wiped the dingy shirt with a still more dubious handkerchief and applied it to his forehead; after which he blew a sort of hyperborean whistle, as if to blow his wrath away into the street.

“It is de me, sir—though, as a young man, perhaps you need not have told me so.”

“I drop my point, sir! If you have been wrong, I am sure I am bound to ask your pardon for setting you so,” says Mr. Hagan, and made one of those fine bows which, on his stage, could melt a pit.

“Doesn’t he look like a god?” says Maria, clutching Theo’s hand; and indeed Hagan did look handsome and noble in his heat, his colour up, his hand to his breast. Chamont or Castalio could not have presented himself better.

“Let me make you some lemonade, sir,” says Theo, eager to turn quarrel into comfort. “My papa has sent us a box of fresh limes. May we send you some to the Temple?”

“Madam, if they stay in your house, they will lose their quality and turn sweet,” says the Doctor, who could never help being master, even when he meant to be kind. “Mr. Hagan, you are a young sauce-box, that’s what you are! Ho! ho! It is I have been wrong.”

And when we were alone, Maria—who lived half in her own drawing-room and half upon the stage—began bleating out speeches, her eyes swimming with admiration.

“‘Oh, my lord, my Polidore!’” she cried, and then poured forth some tender raptures from the play as if she were still under the lamps. “Thou knowest not, my Theo, what a pearl and paragon of a man my Castalio is; my Chamont, my—oh, dear me, child, what a pity it is that in your husband’s tragedy he should have to take the horrid name of Captain Smith!”

Upon that tragedy—my Pocahontas—not only my literary hopes but much of my present livelihood depended. My brother’s debts had been discharged; my mother’s drafts from home duly honoured; my own expenses paid—moderate, but not inconsiderable—and pretty nearly the whole of my patrimony was gone. And at this moment, of all moments, I must choose for my marriage! I could raise money on my inheritance—possible, but costly. My mother could not leave her eldest son without maintenance, whatever our quarrels. I had health, strength, good wits, some friends, and a certain reputation—above all, my famous tragedy, which the manager had promised to perform, and on the proceeds of which I counted for present support.

What becomes of the arithmetic of youth? How do we persuade ourselves that a hundred pounds is maintenance, and a thousand a fortune? How dared I play at hazard with Fortune at such odds? Yet I remember I succeeded in convincing my dear General, and he left home believing that his son-in-law had—at least for the present—some score of hundred pounds at command. He and his dear Molly had begun life with less, and the ravens had always fed them. The women, those sentimental souls, could almost find a pleasure in poverty; and Aunt Lambert declared it would be wicked and irreligious to doubt that provision would be made for her children. Was the righteous ever forsaken? Did the just man ever beg his bread? She knew better.

“No, no, my dears! I am not going to be afraid on that account, I warrant you! Look at me and my General!”

Theo believed what I said—and wished to believe it myself. So we began life upon a capital of Five Acts and about three hundred pounds in ready money.

As the time drew near for my tragedy’s appearance, my friends canvassed the town to gather a body of supporters for the opening night. I am ill at begging favours from the great; but when my Lord Wrotham came to London, I went with Theo on my arm to wait on him. He received us kindly out of regard for his old friend—her father—though he shook a finger at me good-naturedly, and Theo, at that, hung down her head as if she heard again the ship’s ropes creak and the water slap against the side on the day her father went away.

However, he would do his best for the General’s daughter; he hoped for a success; he had heard great things of the piece; and he engaged a number of places for himself and his friends.

This patron secured, I had no other.

“Mon cher,” says the Baroness, “at my age I should bore myself to death at a tragedy; but I will do my best, and I will certainly send my people to the boxes. Yes! Case in his best black looks like a nobleman; and Brett in one of my gowns has a faux air de moi which is quite distinguished. Put down my name for two in the front boxes. Good-bye, my dear. Bonne chance!”

The Dowager Countess presented compliments on the back of the nine of clubs; she had a card-party that night, and was quite sorry she and Fanny could not go to my tragedy.

As for my uncle and Lady Warrington, they were out of the question. After the affair of the sedan-chair I might as well have asked Queen Elizabeth to go to Drury Lane. These were all my friends—this host of aristocratic connexions about whom poor Sampson had bragged; and on the strength of whom, as the manager said, he had given Mr. Hagan his engagement.

“Where was my Lord Bute?” the manager asked snappishly, taking snuff—how different from the brisk, engaging, obsequious little man of six months ago. “Had I not promised his lordship should come? I promised Lord Bute should come!”

“Yes,” says Mr. Garrick, “and her Royal Highness the Princess of Wales, and his Majesty too.”

Poor Sampson owned that, buoyed up by vain hopes, he had promised the appearance of these august personages. The next day at rehearsal matters were worse still, and the manager was in a fury.

“Great heavens, sir!” he cries. “Into what a pretty guet-a-pens have you led me! Look at that letter, sir—read that letter!” And he thrust a sheet into my hand.

It ran:

“MY DEAR SIR,—I have seen his lordship, and conveyed to him Mr. Warrington’s request that he would honour the tragedy of Pocahontas by his presence. His lordship is a patron of the drama, and a magnificent friend of all the liberal arts; but he desires me to say that he cannot think of attending himself, much less of asking his Gracious Master to witness the performance of a play, a principal part in which is given to an actor who has made a clandestine marriage with a daughter of one of his Majesty’s nobility.—Your well-wisher, SAUNDERS MCDUFF.”

“Mr. D. Garrick, at the Theatre Royal in Drury Lane.”

The letters danced before my eyes; not from any poetry in them, but from the sudden blow they carried. Our little lodgings in Bloomsbury, our quiet kettle, Theo’s careful hands over the sauce-boat, the very limes she had spoken of for lemonade—all seemed, for a moment, to stand on the edge of some cracking ice.

That evening my poor Theo had a nice dinner waiting: a small joint well tended, bread fresh, a dish of something she had contrived out of little, the room warm with the good smells of a home that tries hard to be cheerful. I came in from rehearsal with my face as pale as if I had been ill.

I pleaded fatigue. I dared not tell her the dreadful news.
