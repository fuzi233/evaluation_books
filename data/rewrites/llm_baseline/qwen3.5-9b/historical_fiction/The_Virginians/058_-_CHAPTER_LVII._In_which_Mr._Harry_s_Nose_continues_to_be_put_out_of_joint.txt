Madame de Bernstein was scarcely less gratified than her Virginian nephews by the outcome of Harry's final audience with Lady Maria. That evening, George dispatched a note detailing the proceedings to his aunt. Shortly thereafter, Lord Castlewood, whose visits to his aunt were typically infrequent, arrived to pay his respects. Seizing the moment, he addressed the matter with characteristic frankness; now that the contest between Maria and Harry was concluded, there was no necessity for reticence or hypocrisy. The game had been played and finished; he felt no compunction in discussing its various maneuvers, stratagems, and finesse.

"She is my own sister," declared my Lord Castlewood with affectionate gravity. "She will not have many further opportunities—fewer chances of marriage and establishment. While I might not have approved of the match in every respect, and while I pity your ladyship's favorite young Virginian, such a stroke of fortune could not be allowed to slip through one's fingers. I was bound to stand by my own flesh and blood."

"Your candour does your lordship honour," remarked Madame de Bernstein, "and your devotion to your sister is truly edifying!"

"Nay, we have lost the game, and I speak *sans rancune*," replied my lord, bowing gracefully. "It is not for you, who have emerged victorious, to harbor malice."

Madame de Bernstein protested that she had never been in better spirits. "Confess now, Eugene," she pressed, "that Maria's visit to Harry at the spunging-house—that touching surrender of all his gifts to her—was a stroke of your invention?"

"Pity for the young man, and a sense of what was owed to Maria by her friend, her affianced lover, in his misfortune; surely these were motives sufficient to prompt her action," answered Lord Castlewood, demurely.

"But you advised her, my good nephew?"

With a shrug of his shoulders, Castlewood admitted that he had counseled his sister to see Mr. Henry Warrington. "However," he continued, "we should have triumphed, despite your ladyship, had not the elder brother made his appearance. I have been attempting to console my poor Maria by reminding her that, after all, losing was fortunate."

"Suppose she had married Harry, and then Cousin George had appeared?" suggested the Baroness.

"Effectively," cried Eugene, taking a pinch of snuff. "Let us be grateful to the grave for disgorging its dead in time! I must admit, Mr. George Warrington appears to be a man of sense, and no more selfish than other elder sons or worldly gentlemen. My poor Molly fancied he might be somewhat of a greenhorn, like his younger brother. She fondly hoped he would be inclined to share everything equally with Harry junior. Indeed, so infatuated was she with the youth that I believe she would have taken him. 'Harry Warrington with half a loaf might suffice,' I argued, 'but Harry Warrington with no bread at all, my dear!' "

"How no bread?" asked the Baroness.

"Well, no bread except at his brother's side-table. The elder brother stated as much."

"What a hard-hearted wretch!" exclaimed Madame de Bernstein.

"Ah, bah! I play with you, aunt, *cartes sur table*!" retorted Eugene. "Mr. George merely did what anyone else would do; we have no right to be angry, truly we haven't. Molly herself acknowledged as much once her initial burst of grief subsided, and I brought her around to listening to reason. The silly old creature! To be so wild about a young lad at her age!"

"'Twere a genuine passion, I almost believe," said Madame de Bernstein. "You should have heard her bid him farewell. *C'était touchant*, ma parole d'honneur! I cried. Before George, I could not restrain myself. The young fellow, with muddy stockings and hair falling over his eyes, threw himself among us during dinner; he made his offer to Molly in a very frank and noble manner, and in excellent language. And she replied. By Jove, it reminded me of Mrs. Woffington in the new Scottish play written by Lord Bute's man—the *Douglas*, what is it called? She clung to the lad; she bade him adieu in heartrending tones. She stepped out of the room in stately despair—no more chocolate, thank you. Had she made a *mauvais pas*, no one could have retreated with greater dignity. It was a masterly withdrawal after a defeat. We were starved out of our position, but we retired with all the honors of war."

"Molly will not die of disappointment!" observed my lord's aunt, sipping her cup.

My lord snarled a grin, revealing his yellow teeth. "He, he!" he chuckled. "She has suffered this malady severely once or twice before and recovered perfectly. It doesn't kill, as your ladyship knows, at Molly's age."

How could her ladyship know? She did not marry Dr. Tusher until she was advanced in years, nor did she become Madame de Bernstein until still later. As the poet Old Dido noted, ignorance of misfortune breeds lack of compassion for the wretched. In the little world, people quarrel, fight, abuse one another, and remain estranged for ages. But in the great world, people are supposedly wiser. They have differences, cease seeing one another, reconcile, and reunite without asking questions. A stray prodigal, or a stray puppy-dog, is welcomed back under an amnesty, despite having been away in disreputable company.

For six months, ever since the Castlewoods and Madame de Bernstein had battled for possession of poor Harry Warrington, these two branches of the Esmond family had remained apart. Now, with the question settled, they were free to meet again as though no rift had ever existed. Madame de Bernstein drove her grand carriage to Lady Castlewood's rout, and the Esmond ladies greeted Madame de Bernstein with smiles, loving each other just as fervently as before.

"So, sir, I hear you acted like a hard-hearted monster regarding your poor brother Harry!" the Baroness declared, delighted and brandishing her stick threateningly toward George.

"I acted only upon your ladyship's hint, wishing to ascertain whether his kinsfolk sought him for himself or for his reputed wealth," replied George, turning slightly red.

"Nay, Maria could not marry a poor fellow who was utterly penniless, whose elder brother claimed he would give him nothing!"

"I did it for the best, madam," said George, still blushing.

"And so thou didst, O thou hypocrite!" cried the old lady.

"Hypocrite, madam! And why?" asked Mr. Warrington, drawing himself up with considerable state.

"I know all, my infant!" the Baroness replied in French. "Thou art very like thy grandfather. Come, let me embrace thee! Harry has told me all, and that thou hast divided thy little patrimony with him!"

"It was but natural, madam. We have shared common hearts and purses since birth. I merely feigned hard-heartedness to test those people yonder," said George, his eyes filling with emotion.

"And thou wilt divide Virginia with him too?" asked the Bernstein.

"I do not say so. It would not be just," replied Mr. Warrington. "The land must go to the eldest-born, and Harry would not have it otherwise; moreover, I may die, or my mother may outlive both of us. But half of what is mine is his. And he, it must be remembered, was only extravagant because he was mistaken about his position."

"But he is a knight of old, a Bayard, the grandfather come to life!" cried Madame de Bernstein to her attendant as she prepared to retire for the night.

That evening, when the lads departed, it was to poor Harry that she bestowed the two-fingered salute, and to George she gave the rouged cheek. George blushed, for his part, almost as deeply as the often-dyed rose, at such a mark of favor from his ancient kinswoman.

Although Harry Warrington was the least envious of men and honored his brother as his chief guide and superior in every respect, it is no wonder that a profound sense of humiliation and disappointment oppressed the young man following his deposition from the status of Fortunate Youth and heir to boundless Virginian territories. Their friends in Kensington might promise and vow to love him all the better after his fall; Harry bowed low and professed himself very thankful. Yet, he could not help noticing, as he accompanied his brother to the state entertainment hosted by my Lord Castlewood for his newly discovered kinsman, that George was all-in-all to his cousins. All the conversation, compliments, and *petits soins* were directed at George, while Harry received no notice whatsoever save from poor Maria. She followed him with wistful glances, pursued him with eyes conveying dismal reproaches, and, as it were, blamed him for accepting her offer.

*"Ah!"* her eyes seemed to whisper, *"It is mighty well of you, Harry, to have accepted the freedom I granted you; but I had no intention, sir, that you should be so pleased at being let off."*
