As Mrs. Henry van der Luyden settled into her chair, a delicate, almost imperceptible tremor seemed to pass through the high-ceilinged, white-walled Madison Avenue drawing-room. The air was heavy with the scent of old books and the soft sheen of the ormolu mantel ornaments, their delicate patterns barely visible beneath the gauzy veil that still shrouded them. Above the fireplace, a beautifully rendered portrait by Huntington, in rich black velvet and intricate Venetian point, gazed down upon the scene, its subject a vision of elegance and refinement. The painting depicted Mrs. van der Luyden's lovely ancestress, Lady Angelica du Lac, in a pose that seemed to embody the very essence of serenity.

To the left of the portrait hung a more recent work, a likeness of Mrs. van der Luyden herself, taken from life by the same masterful hand. Though twenty years had passed since its creation, the portrait remained a "perfect likeness," capturing the subtle nuances of her features with uncanny accuracy. As she sat listening intently to her cousin's words, Mrs. van der Luyden might have been the twin sister of the young, fair woman portrayed in the painting, her raven-black hair parted in flat, overlapping points on her forehead, her straight nose dividing her pale blue eyes with a subtle precision.

Newland Archer, seated across from her, couldn't help but notice the way Mrs. van der Luyden seemed to be suspended in time, her very existence a testament to the power of habit and tradition. Like all her family, he esteemed and admired her, but he found her gentle, bending sweetness somewhat inaccessible, like the quiet strength of a still pond that seemed to hold its breath in anticipation. In contrast, his mother's old aunts, fierce and unyielding, spoke their minds without hesitation, their firm "no" a shield against the unknown.

Mrs. van der Luyden's response, however, was always a studied one, her lips curving into a faint, enigmatic smile as she replied, "I shall first have to discuss this with my husband." It was a phrase that had become a familiar refrain, a gentle reminder that decisions were rarely taken lightly in the van der Luyden household. And yet, as she spoke, something unexpected happened – a small, decisive movement of her hand toward the bell-rope, a gesture that broke the spell of silence that had fallen over the room.
