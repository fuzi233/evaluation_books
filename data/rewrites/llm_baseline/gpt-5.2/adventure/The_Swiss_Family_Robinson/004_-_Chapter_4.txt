The next morning found us awake with the first clean rinsing of daylight. The tree that had become our home held the night’s coolness in its bark, and the leaves overhead still trembled with the small, watery sounds of dawn—dew loosening, a distant surf breathing in, and the bright piping of birds rehearsing the day.

The children were in motion at once, springing and clambering among the branches with the heedless energy of young creatures newly certain of life.

“What shall we begin to do, father?” they cried from above. “What do you want us to do today?”

“Rest, my boys,” I answered. “Rest.”

“Rest?” they echoed, as if the word itself were a dull stone. “Why should we rest?”

“‘Six days shalt thou labour and do all that thou hast to do, but on the seventh, thou shalt do no manner of work.’ This is the seventh day. On it, therefore, let us rest.”

Jack’s face lit as though Sunday were a toy. “Sunday? How jolly! Then I won’t work at all. I’ll take a bow and arrow and shoot, and we’ll climb and have fun all day.”

“That is not resting,” I told him gently. “That is not the way you are accustomed to spend the Lord’s day.”

“No—but there’s no church here,” he protested, “and nothing else to do.”

“We can worship here as well as at home,” I said.

“But there is no church, no clergyman, and no organ,” Franz put in.

I looked up into the green roof of our great tree, where light lay in wavering panes and the air was sweet with sap and leaf-milk. “The leafy shade of this tree is more beautiful than any church,” I said. “There we will worship our Creator. Come, boys—down with you. Turn our dining hall into a breakfast-room.”

One by one they slid down the ladder into our hollowed dwelling. To my dear Elizabeth I said, “This morning we will devote to the service of the Lord. I will speak to them by a parable. But without books, without the usual Sunday occupations, we cannot keep them quiet the whole day; afterward, therefore, let them pursue any innocent recreation. In the cool of evening we will walk.”

She agreed at once; and after breakfast—plain fare, yet eaten with the gratitude of people who have known hunger breathing near them—we gathered on the soft grass in the shade. The island had its own Sabbath music: not the measured peal of bells, but the layered calls of birds, the creek’s light chatter, the sea’s steady, solemn hush. I felt, as I often had since our wreck, how a place’s sounds settle into the heart like salt into food—too harsh, and the spirit tightens; balanced, and the mind grows spacious.

We sang hymns as best we could, voices a little rough from open air and new labours, yet warmed by one another. We prayed—heartfelt, plain words rising under leaves as naturally as breath. Then I told the children I would speak to them in a parable instead of a sermon.

“Oh, that would be delightful!” Franz cried. “I like the parables in the Bible better than anything. When can we hear you read out of the Bible again, father?”

His question struck me with quiet shame. In our eager hunt for what would feed our bodies—tools and powder, rope and flour—I had neglected the Bread of Life.

“Ah, my little boy,” I said, “your words reproach me. I blush to think I have neglected the word of God. On my next return to the wreck I will search for a Bible. Though our own books were nearly all destroyed, I am sure to find one.”

At that, my wife rose without a word and went to her bag—the boys called it her magic bag, for from it she drew what we most needed as if by miracle. She returned with a copy of the Holy Scriptures and placed it in my hands. I received it with such gratitude as I cannot fit into language; for it seemed to me that in an island where even familiar speech began to feel like a dwindling coin, the old words of home still held their full weight.

I read aloud, and then I spoke:

“A Great King, ruling in power and splendour over a vast realm of light and love, possessed within its boundaries a desolate and unfruitful island. This spot he made the object of his special care; and lavishing on it all the varied resources of his might and goodness, it bloomed in beauty, and became the happy residence of a band of colonists, who were charged not only with the cultivation and improvement of the soil, but each, individually, was bound to cherish in his soul the spirit of love and true allegiance to his Sovereign.

“While this faithful union was maintained, the colony flourished; and the noblest virtues exalted and rendered happy the existence of every member of the race.

“That a discontented and rebellious spirit should ever have infected these fortunate subjects of so loving a master seems incredible, yet it was so; disobedience and pride brought misery and punishment, the fair prospects of the colony were blighted, the labours of the colonists were unblessed, and total separation from the parent kingdom seemed inevitable.

“A message of pardon—of free forgiveness—was nevertheless accorded to these rebels; and to all who, humbly accepting it, moulded their future lives to the will of the Great King (now revealed in a character even more gracious than before), was held out the promise of removal at last from among the ruins caused by the great rebellion, to the glory and undimmed splendour of the realm of Light and Blessedness.”

The boys listened with faces that held, for once, more inward light than outward mischief. Leaving allegory, I pressed simply to their young hearts the truths I wished them to carry. A short prayer ended our little service.

Then, after a thoughtful pause, we separated, each to what suited him. Even our idleness had tasks hidden in it, as flour hides its strength until water draws it into dough.

I took reeds for arrows and tried to fit porcupine quills as points. The work had a pleasing fineness: the rasp of thread over reed, the tiny resistance as a spine bit into place. Jack helped, quick-fingered and eager, binding a sharp spine at one end of each reed with pack-thread. He began to wish aloud for glue to keep everything firm.

“Oh, Jack!” cried Franz, eyes bright with invention. “Mamma’s soup is as sticky as anything. Shall I run and ask for a cake of it?”

“No, no, little goose!” Jack laughed. “Better look for real glue in the tool-box.”

“He will find glue there, to be sure,” I said. “And the soup would scarcely have answered your purpose. But Jack, my boy, I do not like to hear you ridicule your little brother’s idea. Valuable discoveries have come from thoughts that first appeared no wiser than his.”

While we talked and worked, a shot cracked above us—so sudden it startled the leaves into a rain of little tremors. Two small birds dropped dead at our feet. Looking up, we saw Ernest among the branches, his face shining with triumph.

“Well hit! Well hit!” he cried. “A good shot, wasn’t it?”

He slipped down, picked up the birds, and brought them to me. One was a kind of thrush; the other an ortolan—a small dove, famed even in Europe as a delicacy for the richness of its flavour. I held it in my hand, feeling the warmth ebbing from the soft body, and already my mind—like my wife’s—moved toward the questions of keeping and cooking. The figs these birds came to feed on were only just beginning to ripen; if the flocks arrived as I expected, we could take many and preserve them for the rainy season by half-cooking them and laying them in casks, sealed beneath melted lard or butter, so that the fat cooled into a smooth, pale lid of safety against air and decay. Even on an island, the old arts of the larder—salt, smoke, fat—were a kind of language: each method saying, in its own untranslatable way, This will last.

By then Jack had made a good supply of arrows and was practising in earnest. I finished a small bow and arrows for Franz. I hoped to be left in peace; but the young hunter immediately demanded a quiver. So I invented that too, to complete his equipment: I stripped bark from a small tree, shaped it into a tube, fitted a flat side and a bottom, and added a string so it could hang at his shoulder. When he had filled it with arrows and gone off, his mother laughed that he looked like an innocent little Cupid bent on conquest.

Not long after, we were called to dinner, and none needed to be asked twice. Hunger makes even a rough meal musical: the clink of cups, the soft tear of bread, the satisfied silence between mouthfuls. As we ate, I proposed that we give names to the places we had visited.

“It will become more and more troublesome to explain what we mean unless we do so,” I said. “Besides, we shall feel more at home if we can talk as people do in inhabited countries, instead of saying ‘the little island at the mouth of our bay where we found the dead shark,’ or ‘the large stream near our tent across which we made the bridge.’ Let us begin by naming the bay in which we landed. What shall we call it?”

“Oyster Bay,” said Fritz at once.

“No, no—Lobster Bay,” cried Jack, rubbing his leg as if the memory still pinched, “in memory of the old fellow who took a fancy to it!”

My wife, whose gratitude was always practical, said, “In token of our escape we should call it Safety Bay.”

Safety Bay pleased us all, and so it was fixed. Our first abode became Tentholm; the islet in the bay, Shark’s Island; the reedy swamp, Flamingo Marsh. A name for our leafy castle took longer. At last we settled on Falconhurst. Then we quickly named the remaining landmarks: Prospect Hill for the first eminence we had climbed; Cape Disappointment for the rocky height where we had strained our eyes in vain for the ship’s company; and Jackal River for the great stream at our landing place.

In the afternoon the boys returned to their occupations. Fritz finished the cases he was contriving from the tiger-cat skin. Jack begged my help with a plan that delighted him: to make a cuirass for Turk from the porcupine skin. We cleansed the inside thoroughly—scraping, washing, and airing it until the smell of animal was diminished, though never entirely gone—then cut and fitted it round the patient dog’s body. When we had sewn on the strings and the hide had dried enough to hold its shape, Turk was armed in a coat of mail both defensive and offensive, the quills standing like an army of small spears.

He became, at once, a creature out of a fable. Juno wanted none of him; she slipped away from his friendly approaches as if the very air around him pricked. It was plain Turk could put to flight the fiercest animal, so equipped. I determined to make a helmet for Jack from the remainder of the skin, and did so quickly, to his boundless delight.

Evening came down softly. We took a walk among the sweet glades near our abode, where the air cooled and the insects began their minute, ceaseless fiddling. We closed the Sabbath with prayer and a glad hymn, and went to rest with peaceful hearts, the tree cradling us in its living timbers.

The next morning I proposed an expedition to Tentholm, for I wished to reach it by a different route. We left Falconhurst well armed: I and my three elder sons carried guns and game-bags; little Franz was equipped with bow, quiver, and arrows. Fritz, with his belt of margay skin, and Jack with his extraordinary headdress looked like young savages out of some traveller’s tale.

My wife walked beside me, the only one unarmed; she carried a jar to fetch butter from Tentholm. The dogs went before us—Turk in his porcupine cuirass, Juno keeping a respectful distance from so formidable a companion.

Master Knips had fully intended to ride his charger as usual. But when he saw Turk seemingly arrayed in a new skin, he approached with caution. Touching him with one paw, he discovered instantly that such a hide would make anything but an agreeable seat. The grimace he made was so comical we could not help laughing. Chattering in protest, he bounded toward Juno, hopped upon her back, seated himself, and soon looked perfectly satisfied with his change of steed.

The flamingo, much petted in the last day or two, decided he was entitled to accompany us. For some time he walked beside the children, following first one and then another as they darted into the wood. Their zig-zagging at last disgusted him; abandoning them, he settled into a dignified pace at my side, his long legs making a quiet, regular tapping on the ground, as if he approved of steadier company.

We followed the stream. The boys ranged ahead, full of exploration. Then I heard a joyful shout, and Ernest came running toward me with his brothers behind him, all breath and excitement. In Ernest’s hand was a plant.

“Potatoes! Potatoes, father!” he panted, eyes sparkling.

“Yes,” Jack cried, “acres and acres of potatoes!”

There was no mistaking leaf and flower, nor the light clear-green bulbous roots. “My dear Ernest,” I said, “you have indeed made a discovery. With the potato we shall never starve.”

“But come and look!” Jack urged. “Come and feast your eyes on thousands of potatoes.”

We hurried to the spot and found a great tract of ground covered with the precious plant.

“It would have been rather difficult,” Jack observed, “not to have discovered such a great field.”

“Very likely,” Ernest replied with a smile, “but I doubt if you would have discovered that it was a potato field.”

“Perhaps not,” Jack conceded. “You’re welcome to the honour. I’ll take the honour of being first to get a supply.” With hands and knife he dug up plants and filled his game-bag with roots. The monkey followed, scratching with nimble paws until he had a heap beside him.

So delighted were we, and so eager to secure a store, that we did not stop until every bag, pouch, and pocket was stuffed with potatoes. Some wanted to return at once to Falconhurst to cook and taste them. But I overruled this, and we continued our march, heavily laden and happy, the weight at our hips and shoulders as pleasing as coin.

“How,” I said, “can we thank the Giver of all these blessings sufficiently?”

“Oh,” said Franz readily, “we can say, ‘We thank thee, O Lord, for all thy goodness and mercy; and bless us for Jesus Christ’s sake. Amen.’”

“That would not be sufficient,” Fritz said. “Do you think it would be enough just to say to father and mother, ‘Thank you for all you do,’ and not show that we were really thankful by loving them and doing what we can to please them?”

“You are quite right, Fritz,” I answered. “Franz did not say all that was necessary. He should have added, ‘Give me grace to do Thy will, and to obey Thee in all things.’”

As we talked, we reached the head of our streamlet, where it spilled from the rocks in a bright cascade, sparkling and splashing with a voice like laughter. We crossed and pushed into tall grass on the other side. The reeds were thick and tangled; we forced our way through with difficulty, their dry blades hissing against our clothes.

Beyond them the landscape opened in a richness that seemed almost too lavish for our need. Tall palms rose like pillars; luxuriant ferns unrolled beside them. Brilliant flowers and graceful creepers poured colour over everything; prickly cactus thrust up amid softer leaves; aloe stood like a patient sentinel; jasmine and sweet-scented vanilla loaded the evening air with perfume. The Indian pea was there, and—most enticing to boys—a profusion of pineapples. Their scent carried a sharp sweetness, like warm honey cut with green acidity.

The boys fell upon the fruit with such eager mouths and sticky fingers that my wife had to caution them, “Remember, there are no doctors here. If you make yourselves ill, you must cure yourselves as best you may.”

Her warning did little. They showed Knips what they wanted, and sent him off to fetch the ripest fruit. He climbed with the smugness of a practised thief, returning with prizes as if he had always lived in this bright, noisy pantry of the wild.

While they were thus employed, I examined the shrubs. Among them I noticed a plant I knew from description: long pointed leaves and a beautiful red flower.

“Come here, boys,” I called. “Here is something far more valuable than your pineapples. Do you see that plant? It is the karatas. The filaments of the leaves make capital thread. The leaves themselves, bruised, form an invaluable salve. The pith may be used either for tinder or for bait for fish.

“Suppose, Ernest, you had been wrecked here alone—how would you have made a fire without matches, or flint and steel?”

“As the savages do,” he replied. “I would rub two pieces of wood together until they kindled.”

“Try it,” I said, “but do it when you have a whole day before you and no other work to be done; I am certain it would be night before you succeeded. But see here.”

I broke a dry karatas twig, peeled off the bark, and laid the pith on a stone. Striking two pebbles over it, I coaxed out a spark; the pith caught at once, and a small flame lifted like a living thing born from patience. The boys’ faces brightened as if the fire itself had spoken a new word into their minds.

Then I drew threads from the leaves—fine, strong fibres—and gave them to my wife. She took them with the look she wore whenever she saw a new means of keeping house and history alive: thread meant net, snare, stitching, binding—measured, repeatable acts that outlast mere luck.

“But what,” Fritz asked, “is the use of all these other prickly plants except to annoy one? Here, for instance, is a disagreeable little tree.”

“That is an Indian fig,” I told him. “It grows best on dry rocky ground, for much of its nourishment is derived from the air. Its juice is used medicinally, I believe, and its fruit is pleasant and wholesome.”

At the word fruit Jack was off. He tried to gather it, but the sharp thorns defied him, and he returned with bleeding hands and a face pulled long with disappointment.

I removed the thorns and made a sharp wooden skewer. Thrusting it into a fig, I twisted the fruit from its branch and split it open with my knife while still holding it on the skewer. The rest followed my example, and we ate. The fruit was excellent—cool, mildly sweet, with a faintly earthy undernote that spoke of sun on stone.

Ernest examined his fig closely. “What are these little red insects?” he exclaimed. “They cling all over it. Can they be cochineal?”

He handed it to me. I looked carefully. “You are right, my boy. It is cochineal—worth its weight in gold to traders, though of little use to us unless any of you care to appear in gay colours. It makes the loveliest scarlet dye.”

“No, thank you,” Jack said. “But we’ll take a lot when we go home again. Now let us find something more useful.” And from then on they pelted me with questions about every shrub and tree.

“Stop, stop,” I said at last, laughing. “The most learned naturalist would be puzzled by many of these, and I, who have never seen them before and know them only by description, cannot pretend to name or explain a quarter of them.”

Talking still of those plants I did know, we reached Tentholm. Everything was safe. We set to work for what we wanted. I opened the butter cask, and my wife filled her pot. Fritz looked after ammunition. Jack and Ernest ran to the beach to capture the geese and ducks we had left there.

This proved no easy task. Left alone so long, the birds had grown shy; they would not come on shore to be caught. Ernest at last devised an ingenious plan. He tied pieces of cheese to long strings and threw the bait into the water. The hungry ducks snapped at it at once; with careful, patient drawing he lured them shoreward. There Jack and he seized and tied the rebels together by the feet, the birds protesting with furious quacks.

Meanwhile we procured a fresh supply of salt. We packed it on Turk’s back, first relieving him of his porcupine armour. The birds were fastened to our game-bags. We closed the tent-door carefully and started home by the sea-shore.

The walk was cheerful; the sea laid down its endless sound beside us like a road of breath, and the sand, strewn with shells and weed, glittered in places as if it had swallowed bits of sky. At Falconhurst I released the birds, clipped their wings so they could not leave us, and established them on the stream.

Then came supper—one of those meals that become a landmark in memory. Potatoes, newly unearthed, were washed and cooked until their skins split and their insides turned to steam and softness. With milk and butter—fresh, rich, and fragrant—it was delicious beyond any feast of our former life, for it tasted of security as much as of food. We ate until our bodies felt settled and warm, and then climbed to our hammocks and slept.

Having noticed much driftwood on the sands the previous evening, it occurred to me that we should collect it and make a kind of sledge, to lessen the labour of fetching supplies from Tentholm. I woke early and roused Ernest, wishing to encourage him to overcome his natural indolence.

He stretched and yawned, then rose more cheerfully than I expected, pleased with the idea of an expedition while the others slept. We took the donkey with us; I brought a large broad bough I meant to use for hauling our load.

As we walked I said, “I suppose you are rather sorry for yourself, Ernest, and grudged leaving your cosy hammock and pleasant dreams at this hour.”

“Oh, father, do not laugh at my laziness,” he replied. “Indeed I mean to cure myself of it. I am glad to go with you. I intended to shoot more ortolans this morning, but there will be time afterwards. The boys will be shooting at them, I daresay, but I don’t expect they will have great luck.”

“Why not?” I asked.

“I don’t believe they will know what shot to use at first,” he said, “and they will most likely shoot upwards and miss, because of the height and the thickness of the branches and foliage.”

“You certainly possess prudence and reflection, as well as observation,” I told him. “These are valuable. But sudden action is so often necessary in life that you must cultivate the power of instantly perceiving and deciding what must be done in emergencies. Presence of mind is precious; though natural to some, it may be acquired by all who train themselves.”

On the shore our work went quickly. We selected the wood I thought suitable and laid it across the broad leafy branch. With our help the donkey dragged a fair load homeward, and I added to it a small chest that lay half-buried in sand.

As we drew near Falconhurst we heard the boys’ guns popping away at the birds. They ran to meet us, eager to know where we had been. Their eyes fixed on the chest, and I allowed them to open it while I asked my wife to excuse our absence without leave. She reprimanded me gently, and I explained my plan for the sledge, which pleased her greatly; she already pictured it loaded with her butter hogshead and gliding from Tentholm to Falconhurst like a patient beast.

The chest proved to be a common sailor’s, containing only clothes, sodden and salted by sea water.

At breakfast the boys displayed several dozen birds and recounted their failures and successes. Ernest had guessed their mistakes, but practice was improving them, and they were ready to continue their sport. Their mother, however, said she could not use more birds than those already killed and asked if I did not think a snare might be contrived; we would waste much powder and shot if they continued so.

I agreed. The lads laid aside their guns. The younger ones set about making snares from the long threads of karatas leaves in a simple method I showed them, while Fritz and Ernest helped me with the sledge.

We were busy when a tremendous commotion among our fowls made us suppose a fox or wild cat had broken in. The cocks crowed defiantly; the hens fluttered and cackled in wild excitement.

We hurried toward them, but Ernest noticed Master Knips slipping away as if guilty. He followed and caught the monkey in the act of eating a newly laid egg, which he had carried off and hidden among grass and roots. Ernest found several more.

They were very welcome to my wife, for as yet the hens had given us no eggs. From that day she determined to imprison the monkey each morning until the eggs were collected.

Soon after, as Jack was setting the newly made snares among the branches, he discovered a pair of our own pigeons building in the tree. It was much to be desired that we should increase our stock of these pretty birds. I cautioned the boys against shooting near our tree while nests were there, and warned them also about their snares, which were meant only for the wild fig-eaters, not for the birds that had already begun to make Falconhurst their home.
