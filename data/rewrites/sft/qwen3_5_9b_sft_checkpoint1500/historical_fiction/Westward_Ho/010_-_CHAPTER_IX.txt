"The sound of the horn calls to the hunter,
And the drum beats for the war;
Stand firm, my brave companions,
And guard the gate with care.
For though the night be dark and dreary,
And winds may howl around,
I will stand by your side,
Until the battle is won!"

It was the blessed afternoon of Christmas. The light was failing, the church bells had ceased their song, and the citizens of Bideford were gathering in joyful clusters—the fathers with their children, the lovers with their sweethearts—to partake of cakes, ale, and the festive games of the season. Yet, amidst this merriment, there was one solitary figure. Wrapped tightly in a black muffler and trailed by a single maid, a lady walked swiftly, yet sorrowfully, toward the long causeway leading to Northam.

Sir Richard Grenville and his wife intercepted her. "Come home with us, Mrs. Leigh," Lady Grenville said kindly, "and spend a pleasant Christmas?"

Mrs. Leigh offered a sweet, faint smile. She placed one hand on Lady Grenville's arm and pointed westward. "I cannot well spend a merry Christmas night while that sound is in my ears."

The company fell silent, glancing in the direction she indicated. Above them, the soft blue sky faded into gray, pierced only by a few misty stars. But to the west, where the downs of Raleigh merged with those of Abbotsham, the heavens were webbed with delicate white flakes. Irises marks of the setting sun danced across the clouds, displaying the dying hues of a dolphin, while low on the horizon lay a band of grassy green.

"What sound troubles you, Mrs. Leigh?" Sir Richard asked. "There has been wind somewhere."

"There is wind now, where my boy is, God help him!" she replied. Her words struck a chord of shared dread in everyone present. Though the air was dead calm, lacking even a breath to stir a blade of grass, it was filled with a low, deep roar. It hovered over the downs, the woods, the salt marsh, and the river, sounding like the rolling of a thousand wheels or the tramp of endless armies—a mighty surge battering the pebble ridge.

"The ridge is noisy tonight," Sir Richard observed. "Perhaps there has been wind."

"There is wind now, where my boy is, God help him!" Mrs. Leigh repeated, her voice trembling. "To-morrow those pebbles, now rattling down with each retreating wave, may leap to the ridge-top and be hurled like round-shot far ashore by the force of the advancing hurricane."

"God help my boy!" she whispered again.

"God is as near him by sea as by land," Sir Richard comforted.

"True, but I am a lone mother," she said, tears welling in her eyes. "My heart has no room for joy now but to go home and pray."

Thus, Mrs. Leigh continued up the lane, listening all night to the thunder of the surge until it was drowned, long before sunrise, in the overwhelming roar of the storm.

And where was Amyas on this same Christmas afternoon?

Amyas sat bare-headed in the stern of a boat in Smerwick Bay, the spray whistling through his curly hair. He shouted cheerfully to his men, "Pull, and with a will, my merry men all, and never mind shipping a sea! Cannonballs are a cargo that doesn't spoil by taking salt-water."

His mother's premonition had proven true. Christmas Eve had marked the end of the still, dark, steaming nights of early winter. For the last twelve hours, the western gale had been roaring along the Irish coast. The short winter light faded rapidly. Behind him lay a leaping line of billows, lashed into mist by the tempest. Beside him, green foam-fringed columns rushed up the black rocks and crashed down in a thousand cataracts of snow.

Before him stretched the deep, sheltered bay, though visibility was limited. Some four miles out at sea, a sloping roof of thick gray cloud began, stretching overhead and inland, cutting off the Kerry mountains at mid-height and darkening the distant firths into the blackness of night. Underneath this awful roof, the storm howled inward, sweeping before it great foam-sponges and gray salt spray, rendering all the land hazy, dim, and dun.

Let it howl! For there was more mist than ever salt spray produced, and more thunder than ever a sea-surge echoed among the cliffs of Smerwick Bay. Along the sand-hills, red sparks flashed in the evening gloom—sparks that never came from heaven. These were from Fort Del Oro, now christened by the invaders, where the hated golden flag of Spain flew proudly. Within its walls held San Josepho and eight hundred foes. Just three nights prior, Amyas and Yeo, alongside the finest hands of Winter's crew, had hauled four culverins from the Admiral's main deck, floated them ashore, and dragged them up to the battery among the sand-hills. Now, it would be seen whether Spanish and Italian condottieri could hold their ground against the men of Devon.

Amyas was likely thinking not of his lonely mother at Burrough Court, but of the quick, bright flashes on the sand-hill and the fort where Salvation Yeo was hurling eighteen-pound shots with deadly aim. He watched with a cool, bitter smile of triumph as the sand flew and the gabions crashed. Amyas and his party had risked their lives aboard ship to secure a fresh supply of shot, for Winter's battery was out of ammunition and had been firing stones for four hours. They ran their boat onto the shore through the surf, scrambling over the sand-hills with each man carrying a brace of shot.

Amyas leaped into the trenches and shouted cheerfully to Salvation Yeo, "More food for the bulldogs, Gunner, and plums for the Spaniards' Christmas pudding!"

"Don't speak to a man at his business, Master Amyas," Yeo snapped. "Five mortal times have I missed, but I will have that accursed Popish rag down, as I'm a sinner."

"Down with it, then! Nobody wants you to shoot crooked. Take good iron to it, not footy paving-stones."

"I believe, sir, that the foul fiend is there, turning my shot aside," Yeo muttered, though gratitude flickered in his eyes. "If one could but cast a silver one! Now, stand by, men!"

Once again, Yeo's eighteen-pounder roared, and away it went. Glory! The great yellow flag of Spain, streaming in the gale, lifted clean into the air, flagstaff and all, then pitched wildly down head-foremast, far to leeward.

A hurrah from the sailors, answered by the soldiers of the opposing camp, shook the very clouds above them. Ere the echoes had died away, a tall officer leaped upon the parapet of the fort, seizing the fallen flag in his hand. He reared it as best he could upon his lance point, holding it firmly against the gale, while the fallen flagstaff was raised again within.

In a moment, a dozen longbows were bent at the daring foe. But Amyas shouted from behind, "Shame, lads! Stop and let the gallant gentleman have due courtesy!"

So they paused. Amyas sprang onto the rampart of the battery, took off his hat, and bowed to the flag-holder. The man, relieved of his burden, returned the bow courteously and descended.

By this time, it was nearly dark, and the firing began to slacken. Salvation and his brother gunners, having covered their slaughtering tackle with tarpaulings, retired for the night. Leaving Amyas, who had volunteered to take the watch till midnight, the rest of the force lay down under arms among the sand-hills, grumbling themselves to sleep over their scanty supper of biscuit, for provisions were running very short.

Amyas had paced up and down in the gusty darkness for an hour or more, exchanging passing words with the sentinel, when two men entered the battery, chatting busily. One wore complete armor; the other was wrapped in the plain short cloak of a man of pens and peace. Yet, their conversation concerned neither sieges nor sallies, catapults nor culverins, but simply English hexameters.

"Do not imagine, gentle reader, that the two were fiddling while Rome burned," Amyas reflected later. "For the commonwealth of poetry and letters, in that critical year 1580, was in far greater danger from those same hexameters than the woes of Ireland were from the Spaniards."

Imitating classic meters, "versifying," as it was called, had become the fashion among the learned. Stonyhurst and others had attempted hexameter translations from Latin and Greek epics, which seemed doggerel enough. Ever and anon, some youthful wit would break out in iambics, sapphics, elegiacs, and what not, to the great detriment of the Queen's English and her subjects' ears.

Whether Mr. William Webbe had yet published fragments of his "Reformation of English Poetry" remained uncertain, but Gabriel Harvey, the Cambridge Malvolio, had successfully argued Spenser, Dyer, Sidney, and the whole clique of beaux-esprits into following his model.
