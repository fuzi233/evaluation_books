To truly grasp the architecture of this early exile, one must first confront the absolute poverty of the universe in which I found myself. We existed within a microscopic empire of stone, subjugated by a regime that had reduced the world to a singular, suffocating purpose. This was the House of Stone in Omdurman, a noisome prison encircled merely by a thorn zareeba—a fragile barrier against a landscape of pure desolation. It stood upon the eastern border of a city that claimed the title of the most squalid capital any empire had ever known, a brown and stony plain scorched by an unrelenting sun. Here, nature had been systematically erased; not a flower bloomed in a corner, no grass rooted itself, and the green shade of a tree was a myth. Instead, a straggling, narrow city of hovels crawled with vermin and poisoned by invisible diseases, a testament to a system designed to crush the human spirit.

Between this fortress of despair and the Nile, the land was bare. Yet, even in this purgatory, the mechanics of survival dictated a grim routine. By day, the prisoners were permitted to stumble in their chains down the half-mile of broken, sloping earth to the riverbank. There, amidst the clamor of the living, they drew water and performed their ablutions. For the native or the negro, escape was a tangible possibility; along that bank, dhows were moored in numbers, the river traffic finding its harbor there, and the wide foreshore served as a bustling marketplace. Captives rubbed elbows with friends, conspiring in whispers, plotting routes to the first blacksmith where the price of iron outweighed the risk of betrayal. Even on their journey to the smithy, their fetters demanded no special notice in Omdurman. Slavery was woven into the fabric of daily life; hardly a street in that long, brown, treeless squalor was free from the clink of chains, a constant auditory reminder of the caste system's permanence.

But for the European, escape was a different calculus entirely. Each white prisoner was a marked man, isolated by the color of his skin and the weight of imperial expectation. Evasion required more than just the procurement of camels and the stationing of drivers; it demanded immense wealth, long preparations, and, crucially, devoted natives willing to risk their lives against the machinery of the state. The camels could be secured, but their drivers might vanish; the preparations could be made, only to be undone by the lash of the gaoler, who might flog a captive within an inch of death on a mere suspicion of hidden funds. The loyal servant might shrink at the critical moment. Colonel Trench began to lose all hope. He knew his friends worked for him—the boy bringing food would sometimes bid him be ready; a passerby might jostle his camel during a parade of the Khalifa's troops and whisper encouragement. But these gestures dissolved into the ether. Nothing ever came of them. He watched the sun rise daily beyond the bend of the river, burning across a sky that offered no solace, while the months dragged on, one agonizing iteration after another.

On an evening towards the end of August, in that year when Durrance returned blind from the Sudan, I sat in a corner of the enclosure, watching the sun drop westward toward the plain with an agony of anticipation. The heat and burden of the day were intolerable, yet they were nothing compared to the horrors that each night renewed. Twilight arrived, and with it came Idris es Saier, the great negro of the Gawaamah tribe, accompanied by his fellow jailers.

"Into the House of Stone!" he cried.

Praying and cursing, the prisoners jostled and struggled at the narrow entrance, the sound of pitiless whips falling perpetually upon the backs of the hindmost. Already, the interior was occupied by some thirty captives, lying upon the swamped mud floor or propped against the walls in the last extremities of weakness and disease. Two hundred more were driven in at night, penned there until morning. The room was perhaps thirty feet square, dominated by a solid pillar rising from the center to support the roof. There was no window; a few small apertures near the ceiling made a pretense of ventilation, yet into this foul, pestilent hovel the prisoners were packed, screaming and fighting. The door slammed shut, and utter darkness replaced the twilight, so profound that a man could not distinguish even the outlines of the heads of neighbors who wedged themselves into the gaps.

Colonel Trench fought like the rest. There was a corner near the door which he coveted with a fierce desperation he had never felt in the days of his freedom. Occupying that spot meant shelter from the blows, the stamping feet, and the bruising pressure of neighboring shackles; it provided a support against which to lean his back during the ten interminable hours of suffocation.

"If I were to fall! If I were to fall!"

That fear haunted him like a drug inducing madness. In that yelling, struggling throng, once a man went down, he never got up again—he was trampled out of shape. Trench had seen such victims dragged from the prison each morning. Being a small man, he fought for his corner in a frenzy like a wild beast, kicking with his fetters, thrusting with his elbows, diving under arms, burrowing between bodies, tearing at clothes, using his nails, his fists, and even striking at heads with the chain dangling from the iron ring about his neck. He reached the corner in the end, streaming with sweat and gasping for breath, spending the rest of the night holding it against all comers.

"If I were to fall!" he gasped. "O God, if I were to fall!" He shouted aloud to his neighbor—for in that clamor, nothing less than a shout was audible—"Is it you, Ibrahim?" A like shout answered him, "Yes, Effendi."

Trench felt a sliver of relief. Between Ibrahim, a great tall Arab of the Hadendoas, and Trench, a friendship born of their common necessities had sprung up. There were no prison rations at Omdurman; each captive depended on his own money or the charity of friends outside. Money came to Trench from time to time, brought secretly by a native ascending from Assouan or Suakin, but there were long periods of silence, forcing him to rely on the charity of Greeks who had sworn conversion to the Mahdist faith, or to starve with such patience as he could muster. Sometimes Ibrahim faced the same scarcity. Thus, each man helped the other in his need. They stood side by side against the wall in the black darkness.

"Yes, Effendi, I am here," Ibrahim murmured, groping with his hand in the void to steady Trench against the rough stone.

Elsewhere in the prison, a fight of violent intensity raged in an extreme corner. So tightly packed were the prisoners that with each advance of one combatant and retreat of another, the entire jostling crowd swayed in a rhythmic oscillation, from end to end, from side to side. They swayed, fighting to keep their feet, fighting even with their teeth. Above the din of hard breathing, the clank of chains, and their imprecations, rose now and then a wild sobbing cry for mercy, or an inhuman shriek, stifled as soon as uttered, revealing that a man had gone down beneath the stamping feet. Missiles were flung across the prison—even the foul earth gathered from the floor—and since no one knew from what quarter they came, heads were battered against heads in the frantic effort to avoid them. All this occurred in the blackest darkness imaginable.

For two hours, Trench stood in that black prison, ringing with noise, rank with heat. Eight hours remained before the door would open, allowing him to stumble into the clean air of the zareeba and sleep. He stood on tiptoe to lift his head above his fellows, but even so, he could barely breathe; the air was moist and sour. His throat was parched, his tongue swollen and stringy like a dried fig. It seemed to him that the imagination of God could devise no worse hell than the House of Stone on an August night in Omdurman. He thought, *If I were to fall*, and suddenly his hell was made perfect.

The door opened. Idris es Saier appeared in the aperture.

"Make room! Make room!" he cried, throwing fire among the prisoners to drive them from the threshold. Lighted tufts of dried grass blazed in the darkness, falling upon the bodies of the captives. They were so crowded they could not avoid the missiles; in places, they could not lift their hands to dislodge them from their shoulders or heads.

"Make room!" Idris commanded. The whips of his fellow jailers enforced the order, lashes falling upon all within reach, clearing a little space within the door. Into that space, a man was flung, and the door closed again.

Trench stood close to the door. In the dim twilight filtering through the entryway, he caught a glimpse of the new prisoner: a man heavily ironed, slight of figure, and bent with suffering.

"He will fall," Trench muttered, "he will fall tonight. God! If I were to!" Suddenly, the crowd swayed against him, and the curses rose louder, shriller than before.
