*"Through cross to crown!—And though thy spirit’s life Trials untold assail with giant strength, Good cheer! good cheer! Soon ends the bitter strife, And thou shalt reign in peace with Christ at length." — Kosegarten*
*"Ay sooth, we feel too strong in weal, to need Thee on that road; But woe being come, the soul is dumb, that crieth not on ‘God.’" — Mrs. Browning*

That afternoon, Margaret walked swift as the wind down the narrow Milton lanes to the Higgins cottage. Mary was waiting at the door, her face half pinched with wary doubt, and Margaret smiled soft and steady into her eyes to reassure her. They slipped past the cluttered house-place and up the steep, rickety stairs in silence, stepping into the small upper room where Bessy lay at last still. The sight made Margaret’s throat tighten, and she thanked God she had come. The face that had so often been drawn tight with coughing fits, restless with the ache of unspoken hopes, now wore a faint, soft smile of perfect, unbroken rest. Slow, warm tears welled in her eyes, but a deep, quiet calm settled deep into her bones. This was death, she thought, and it looked far gentler than the harsh, weary life Bessy had led. Verses she had not thought of in years rose unbidden to her mind: *They rest from their labours. The weary are at rest. He giveth his beloved sleep.*

Slowly, Margaret turned away from the bed. Mary was huddled in the corner, sobbing quiet and humble behind her apron. They trundled back down the stairs without a single word passing between them.

Nicholas Higgins stood planted in the middle of the kitchen floor, one calloused hand braced hard against the wooden table, his great dark eyes blown wide. He had only just stepped through the door, the news of his daughter’s death shouted at him by a dozen neighbours out in the court, and his eyes were dry and sharp, fixed hard on making the impossible fact real, forcing himself to accept Bessy’s place would know her no more. For she had lingered sick and half-dying for so many months, he had talked himself into believing she would beat the illness, that she would “pull through” one more time.

Margaret felt like an intruder, a stranger who had no business standing witness to this raw, private household grief that had only just crashed over the father. She paused for a heartbeat on the crooked stairs, then tried to slip past his unseeing, absorbed gaze to leave him alone in the hush of his loss.

Mary dropped onto the first chair she reached, threw her apron over her head, and broke into loud, shuddering sobs.

The noise jolted Higgins out of his trance. He caught Margaret’s arm in a bruising grip, holding fast while he fumbled for words, his throat thick and dry, his voice coming out hoarse and choked.
“Were yo’ with her? Did yo’ see her die?”
“No,” Margaret answered, standing perfectly still, quiet and patient now that he had registered her presence. He did not let go of her arm for a long stretch, his gaze still distant.
“All men must die,” he said at last, with a heavy, strange gravity that made Margaret wonder if he had stopped for a quick drink on his way home — not enough to make him drunk, but enough to muddle his thoughts. “But she were younger than me.” He fell quiet again, turning the fact over and over in his head, before he looked up at her with a wild, searching stare. “Yo’re sure and certain she’s dead? Not in a dwam, a faint? She’s slipped off that way many a time before.”
“She is dead,” Margaret said. She felt no fear of him, even when his grip dug deep enough to leave purple marks on her arm, and wild, unhinged glints flickered across his dulled, grief-struck eyes. “She is dead.”

The searching look faded slowly out of his face. Suddenly he wrenched his hand away from her, threw his full weight half across the table, and great, heaving sobs burst out of him that shook the whole table and every pot and pan on it, every wobbly chair in the room. Mary stumbled towards him, trembling.
“Get thee gone! — get thee gone!” he roared, lashing out blindly at her. “What do I care for thee?” Margaret pulled Mary close, tucking the girl’s cold hand into her own. Higgins tore at his hair, beat his head against the hard wooden table edge until he collapsed back in the chair, spent and vacant. Neither Margaret nor Mary moved. Mary shook so hard her teeth chattered.

After what might have been fifteen minutes, what might have been an hour, Higgins lifted himself heavily to his feet. His eyes were swollen and bloodshot, and he seemed to have forgotten anyone else was in the room until he spotted them, and scowled dark at their presence. He shook himself like a shaggy dog fresh from a rainstorm, gave them one last sullen glare, and said not a word as he made straight for the front door.
“Oh father, father!” Mary cried, throwing herself against his arm. “Not to-night! Any night but to-night. Oh, help me — he’s going out to drink again! Father, I’ll not leave yo’. Yo’ may strike me all yo’ want, but I’ll not stir. She told me last of all to keep yo’ fro’ drink!”

But Margaret was planted firm in the doorway, silent, unyielding. He looked up at her, defying her with every line of his hunched frame.
“It’s my own house. Stand out o’ the way, wench, or I’ll make yo’!” He had shoved Mary away so hard she fell, her face cutting against the sharp edge of a chair. He looked ready to strike Margaret, but she did not flinch, did not shift a muscle, never took her steady, serious eyes off his face. He stared back, fierce and gloomy, daunted little by little by her unshaking calm.
“What are yo’ looking at me in that way for?” he demanded at last. “If yo’ think yo’ can keep me from going where I choose, just because she loved yo’ — and in my own house too, where I never asked yo’ to step foot — yo’re mista’en. It’s cruel hard on a man that he can’t go to the only comfort left him after all this.”

Margaret knew he had acknowledged her power, however grudging. What was there left she could do? He dropped back onto a chair right next to the door, half beaten, half still angry, fully intent on slipping out the second she moved from the doorway, but too ashamed to lash out at her like he had at Mary. She laid a soft hand on his arm.
“Come with me,” she said, her voice low and solemn, no trace of fear in it. “Come and see her one more time.”

He rose sullenly, his face set in dogged, stubborn resistance. She waited for him there, quiet and patient, until he finally tramped slowly towards the stairs. The two of them stood side by side by Bessy’s bed.
“Her last words to Mary were, ‘Keep my father fro’ drink,’” Margaret told him.
“It canna hurt her now,” he muttered. “Nought can hurt her now.” Then his voice broke into a wail. “We may quarrel and fall out — we may make peace and be friends — we may clem to skin and bone — and nought o’ all our griefs will ever touch her more. Hoo’s had her full portion of ’em. What wi’ back-breaking work first, and wasting sickness at the last, hoo led the life of a half-starved dog. And to die without ever knowing one good piece o’ rejoicing in all her days! Nay, wench, whatever hoo said, hoo can know nought about it now, and I mun ha’ a sup o’ drink just to steady me against this sorrow.”
“No,” Margaret said, softening as his grief spilled over. “You shall not. If her life was as hard as you say, at least she never feared death like so many do. Oh, you should have heard her speak of the life to come — the life hidden with God, that she has gone to join now.”
He shook his head, glancing up sideways at her, his face haggard and pale enough to make her chest ache. “You are sorely tried, are you not? Where have you been all day? You have not been at work, have you?”
“Not at work, sure enough,” he said, with a short, bitter laugh. “Not at what you call work. I were at the Union Committee all morning, till I were sickened clean through wi’ trying to make fools hear reason. I were fetched to Boucher’s wife afore seven this morning. She’s bed-fast, but she were raving and raging to know where her dunner-headed brute of a husband had got to, as if I could keep him on a leash, as if he were ever fit to be ruled by me. The damned fool, who’s put paid to all our strike plans! And I’ve walked my feet raw going round to see men who wouldn’t answer their doors, now the law’s turned against all of us. And I were sore-hearted too, which is worse than sore-footed. And if I did see a friend who offered to stand me a drink, I never knew hoo lay a-dying here. Bess, lass, thou’d believe me, thou wouldst — wouldstn’t thou?” he added, turning to the still, quiet form on the bed in a wild, desperate appeal.
“I am sure,” Margaret said. “I am sure you did not know. It was all so sudden. But now — now you do know. You do see her lying there. You hear what she said with her last breath. You will not go to the gin-shop?”

He did not answer. Where else was a man to turn for comfort, after he had lost the one person who had always understood him?
“Come home with me,” Margaret said at last, taking a bold, nervous risk, half trembling at her own proposal. “At the very least, you shall have a plate of warm, proper food, which I can see you need badly.”
“Yo’r father’s a parson?” he asked, the thought striking him out of nowhere.
“He was,” Margaret said shortly.
“I’ll come and take a dish o’ tea with him, since yo’ve asked me. I’ve many a thing I’ve always wanted to say to a parson, and I don’t care much whether he’s preaching now or not.”

Margaret’s stomach twisted. Nicholas turning up unannounced for tea, when her mother was so unwell, her father totally unprepared for the visitor, seemed like madness — but she knew if she drew back now, he would head straight for the nearest pub, and all her work to hold him back would be for nothing. She decided that getting him across her own threshold was a victory big enough to trust to whatever might happen next.
“Good-bye, ou’d wench! We’ve parted company at last, we have! But thou’st been a blessing to thy father ever sin’ thou wert born. Bless thy white lips, lass — they’ve a smile on ’em now! And I’m glad to see it once again, even if I’m lone and forlorn for evermore.”
He stooped down and kissed Bessy’s forehead tenderly, pulled the sheet up over her face, and turned to follow Margaret. She hurried downstairs first to explain the plan to Mary, tell her it was the only way she could think of to keep her father from the gin-palace, and urge her to come with them too — but Mary said neighbours were already knocking at the door to sit with her, that she would be fine. Nicholas followed close behind, he had shaken off his tears and was half ashamed of breaking down, putting on a harsh, bitter kind of mirth that sounded like dry twigs crackling under a pot.
“I’m going to take my tea wi’ her father, I am.”
He pulled his cap low over his brow as they stepped out into the street, staring straight ahead, not looking left or right, as he tramped along beside Margaret. He was terrified that a sympathetic neighbour’s word, or even a glance, would break the fragile hold he had on his self-control. They walked all the way to the Hales’ house in total silence.
When they neared the street where Margaret lived, he glanced down at his ragged clothes, his grime-streaked hands, his caked clogs. “I should m’appen ha’ cleaned mysel’ first.”
It certainly would have been for the best, and Margaret reassured him he could use the pump in the yard, that there was soap and a clean towel waiting for him, and she would not let him slip away from her now. She sent the servant to lead him down the back passage to the kitchen, and he stepped so carefully over every dark pattern on the oil-cloth floor trying to hide his dirty footprints he looked almost comical.

Margaret ran straight upstairs, and met Dixon on the landing.
“How is mamma? Where is papa?”
Missus was worn out, Dixon told her, and had gone to lie down on the sofa in her room, not bed — Dixon had persuaded her that taking tea there would be better than fretting over being cooped up in bed too long. Good so far. Mr Hale was in the drawing room. Margaret hurried in, breathless, spilling the whole messy, hurried story out in a rush. Mr Hale was very much taken aback at the idea of a rough mill worker who had been drinking a little waiting for him in his quiet study, expecting to share tea, the man Margaret was pleading for so anxiously. The gentle, kind-hearted Mr Hale would have been happy to comfort any grieving soul, but Margaret had dwelt so hard on the fact that he had been drinking, that she had brought him home as a last resort to stop him from going to the gin-shop, that she noticed a flicker of repugnance cross her father’s face.
“Oh papa! He is a man you will not dislike at all, if you don’t get shocked at the very start.”
“But Margaret, to bring a drunken stranger home — when your mother is so ill!”
Margaret’s face fell. “I am sorry, papa. He is very quiet — he is not tipsy at all. He was only a little strange at first, and that’s just the shock of poor Bessy’s death.” Her eyes filled with tears. Mr Hale cupped her sweet, pleading face in both his hands, and kissed her forehead.
“It is all right, dear. I will go and make him as comfortable as I can. You go attend to your mother. Only, if she falls asleep, do come join us in the study. I would be glad of you there.”
“Oh yes — thank you.” But as Mr Hale turned to leave the room, Margaret ran after him. “Papa — you must not wonder at whatever he says. He — he does not believe much in the kind of faith we follow, you see.”
“Oh dear! A drunken infidel weaver!” Mr Hale thought to himself, dismayed. But to Margaret he only said, “If your mother falls asleep, come right down to us.”

Margaret went into her mother’s bedroom. Mrs Hale lifted herself out of a doze.
“When did you write to Frederick, Margaret? Yesterday, or the day before?”
“Yesterday, mamma.”
“Yesterday, and the letter went off?”
“Yes. I took it to the post myself.”
“Oh Margaret, I’m so afraid of his coming! If he should be recognised! If he should be taken! If he should be executed, after all these years that he has kept away and lived in safety!” she burst out. “I keep falling asleep and dreaming he is caught, standing trial right in front of me.”
“Oh mamma, don’t be afraid,” Margaret soothed her. “There is some risk, of course — but we will make it as small as possible. And it is so little risk! If we were at Helstone, it would be a hundred times worse. Everyone there remembers him. If a stranger was known to be staying in the house, they would all guess it was Frederick at once. But here in Milton, no one knows or cares enough about us to notice what we do. Dixon will guard the front door like a dragon, won’t you, Dixon, while he is here?”
“They’ll be clever if they get past me!” Dixon said, baring her teeth at the very idea.
“And he need never go out, except after dark, poor fellow.”
“Poor fellow!” Mrs Hale echoed, her voice softening. “But I almost wish you had never sent that letter. Would it be too late to stop him if you wrote again, Margaret?”
“I’m afraid it would, mamma,” Margaret said quietly, remembering how urgent she had been in her note, begging Frederick to come at once if he wanted to see his mother alive.
“I never like rushing into things like this,” Mrs Hale fretted.
Margaret kept quiet.
“Come now, ma’am,” Dixon said, with cheerful, no-nonsense authority, “you know seeing Master Frederick is the very thing you’ve been pining for most of all. I’m glad Miss Margaret sent that letter straight off, no shilly-shallying. I’d half a mind to do it myself. And we’ll keep him snug, depend on it. There’s only Martha in the house that wouldn’t do all she could to protect him in a pinch — and she’s been saying for weeks she’d love to go visit her mother, who had a stroke last month, only she didn’t like to ask for time off. I’ll see she’s got a ticket to go stay with her the very day Frederick arrives, God bless him. No one will suspect a thing. Now drink your tea, ma’am, and rest easy, leave all the worrying to me.”
Mrs Hale trusted Dixon far more than she trusted Margaret, and the housekeeper’s words quieted her for the time being. Margaret poured out her mother’s tea in silence, trying to think of something cheerful to say — but her own imagination kept circling back to all the terrible fates that could befall Frederick. She was glad when her filial duties were done, and she could slip downstairs to the study, wondering how on earth her gentle father and the rough grieving weaver had got on.

She found the two of them deep in earnest, gentle conversation.
