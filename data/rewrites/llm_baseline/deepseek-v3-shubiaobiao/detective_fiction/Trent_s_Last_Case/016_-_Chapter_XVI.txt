The evening air was crisp as Trent and Mr. Cupples stepped out of the towering flats into the bustling London street.

“Did you say we had an appointment at half-past seven?” Cupples inquired, adjusting his spectacles. “I don’t recall any such arrangement.”

Trent grinned, clapping a hand on his friend’s shoulder. “We do now. You’re dining with me. And since I’ve just untangled a case that’s haunted me for over a year, I’d say that calls for celebration. No arguments—I’m paying.” He flagged down a cab with a flourish. “And we’re not going to my club. Tonight demands something better than their eternally dull menu. We’re going to Sheppard’s.”

“Sheppard’s?” Cupples echoed, bemused.

“A place where the mutton is so good it’s made Americans question their life choices,” Trent declared as they climbed into the cab. “But more importantly—” He lit a cigarette, exhaling smoke with theatrical satisfaction. “—I’m getting married. To the most extraordinary woman alive.”

Cupples’ eyes widened. “Mabel? My dear boy, this is splendid news!” He seized Trent’s hand, shaking it vigorously. “I’ve hoped for this for ages. She’s endured so much unhappiness—she deserves every joy. And you—well, I’ve known your feelings for some time.” A sly smile crept across his face. “Ever since that dinner at my house where you spent the entire evening glaring at poor Professor Peppmuller while stealing glances at her.”

Trent groaned. “Mabel claims she knew even before that. And here I thought I was the picture of indifference.” He sighed, then brightened. “Ah, well. If I was a fool then, I’ll be a happy fool now.”

The cab rolled to a stop outside Sheppard’s, and they were soon seated in a cozy corner of the lively dining room. After ordering—Trent with gusto, Cupples with his usual abstemious precision—their conversation turned to the day’s revelations.

“The irony isn’t lost on me,” Cupples mused, dissecting his mutton with meticulous care. “We held the key to Manderson’s madness all along—his jealous obsession. Yet Marlowe will never know how close he came to being condemned for a crime born of delusion.” He shook his head. “How many lives are shaped by the hidden judgments of others?”

Trent leaned in. “You think Marlowe’s story holds up?”

“Perfectly,” Cupples replied. “A man in peril devises an audacious escape—hardly unheard of. The true marvel is how rarely such schemes unfold in the world of crime. Most criminals lack the strategic depth to weave them.”

“And yet,” Trent countered, “had Marlowe walked blindly into Manderson’s trap, he’d have hanged. How many innocent men have met that fate?”

Cupples’ expression darkened. “Too many. It’s why I’ll never again support capital punishment on circumstantial evidence.”

Their debate meandered through historical cases of wrongful condemnation before Trent circled back. “Still, Marlowe’s defense would have been torn apart in court. No jury would believe Manderson engineered his own death.”

A quiet smile played on Cupples’ lips. “Ah, but there *was* evidence to support Marlowe’s story.” He set down his fork. “Mine.”

Trent froze. “What?”

“I would have testified,” Cupples continued, unperturbed. “You said one piece of evidence could change everything. Well, I possess it.” He took a sip of water. “Because, my dear Trent, *I* was the one who shot Manderson.”

The clatter of the restaurant faded into silence as Trent stared, the weight of the revelation crashing over him.
