Is a person truly happy when they possess every earthly comfort? Consider Madam Esmond Warrington: she held an ancient name, a vast estate, and a fortune sufficient for all desires. Her home at Castlewood was stocked with necessities and luxuries alike, attended by a bustling household of eager black and white servants. She enjoyed robust health, affectionate children, and the humble blessings of a good cook, cellar, and library. In her morning and evening prayers, she meticulously cataloged these advantages, convinced that such a life warranted the title of "very decently happy."

Yet, despite this princely existence, the little Princess Pocahontas, as she was known, was far from content. Enthroned in her Westmorland County dominion, she ruled with an iron fist. The local gentry fawned over her; her sons bowed to her will; the poor whites accepted her medicine without question, and the lesser nobility invariably lost at backgammon to her. Her husband, cut off in early life, was perhaps fortunate to be gone; had he lived, the marriage would have ended in fierce quarrels or the humiliation of a henpecked spouse. Madam Esmond never sought companionship, only domination. She was a tyrant who demanded obedience, offering friendship only to those who submitted, and fighting relentlessly against anyone who resisted. While she preached the sins of despotism with a clear voice on Sundays, she rarely acknowledged her own faults, save for the occasional amusement her father derived from watching her haughty streaks. Without her father's restraining influence, her reign became absolute, and one can only imagine the misery of living under her rule in Virginia.

Her management of the estate was rigorous. She had replaced her father's English factor, expanded the tobacco plantations, hired a new overseer, and imported a strict tutor. Though she loved her father, she rejected his wisdom, believing that a dutiful daughter must override parental counsel. She discarded her husband's surname, adopting "Madam Esmond" to assert her seniority over her half-brother, Lord Castlewood. She claimed precedence over all colonial officials except Lord Fairfax, engaging in legendary battles of etiquette with the wives of judges and governors. To her, the Warringtons were nothing; she had married an English baronet's younger son merely to satisfy her parents, a union she viewed as a sacrifice she was bound to make.

Her behavior toward her English kin was equally cold. Annual letters to the Warrington family and the Hampshire Esmonds often went unanswered. Once, a judge's wife returning to London recounted Madam Esmond's disparaging remarks about her relatives to Lady Warrington, who then shared the tale with Lady Castlewood. The correspondence ceased, leaving Madam Esmond in a state of wounded pride.

Strife soon erupted within her own household. The dismissal of Mr. Dempster, the late Colonel's secretary and the boys' tutor, marked the beginning of the end. Madam Esmond distrusted scholars, fearing they instilled dangerous principles. Suspicious that Dempster was a Jesuit in disguise, she forced him to leave the house, where he built a cabin to teach and practice medicine among the sparse settlers. Master George, loyal to his mentor, refused to abandon him, while Harry, preferring fishing and sport to books, had never been close to the Dominie.

A second, more financial rift followed the deaths of an aunt and the Colonel. George Warrington, as heir, was entitled to six thousand pounds held in trust by his mother and a London lawyer. Madam Esmond, however, viewed herself as the sole proprietor. Furious when the lawyer refused to release the funds prematurely, she berated the agent and insulted Mr. Draper, the trustee, calling him an insolent pettifogger. She believed the money should be used immediately to buy Boulter's estate and slaves, thereby securing an annual income for Harry. Neither George nor his friend, Mr. Washington of Mount Vernon, could persuade her that the lawyer was acting correctly.

George sided with the lawyer, stating he did not desire the money and would gladly give it to his mother if he could. But Madam Esmond saw only a betrayal. She lamented that her younger son, Harry, was left with a mere pittance while his brother inherited everything. She poured out her grievances in tears, scorn, and passionate appeals to Heaven, convinced that the London lawyers were mean-spirited wretches denying her child his fortune. This conviction hardened her resolve to save every penny for Harry's future, halting the grand building projects her husband had begun and ordering the cessation of wine shipments. She mourned the cost of a fine carriage she had imported from England, groaning in church that she wished she had saved the three hundred and eighty guineas for Harry instead.

"You will give me plenty while you live, and George will give me plenty when you die," Harry replied cheerfully.

"Not unless he changes in spirit, my dear," the lady retorted, casting a grim look at her elder son. "Not unless Heaven softens his heart and teaches him charity, for which I pray day and night. Does Mountain know?"

Mrs. Mountain, the widow of Ensign Mountain and Madam Esmond's companion, shook her head. "Humph! You are always disturbing yourself over this legacy. I see no need."

"Oh, no need!" the widow cried, rustling her silks. "Because my eldest is a disobedient son and an unkind brother, and my poor Harry, bless him, has nothing but a mess of pottage."

George looked at his mother with despair, tears welling in his eyes. "I wish you would bless me, too, O my mother!" he cried, bursting into weeping.

Instantly, Harry threw his arms around his brother's neck, kissing him repeatedly. "Never mind, George. I know you are a good brother. Don't mind what she says; she doesn't mean it."

"I do mean it, child," the mother wailed. "Would to Heaven..."

"HOLD YOUR TONGUE, I SAY!" Harry roared. "It is a shame to speak so to him, ma'am."

"And so it is, Harry," Mrs. Mountain agreed, shaking his hand. "You never said a truer word in your life."

"Mrs. Mountain, do you dare to set my children against me?" the widow shrieked. "From this very day, madam—"

"Turn me and my child into the street? Do," Mrs. Mountain declared calmly. "That will be a fine revenge because the English lawyer won't give you the boy's money. Find another companion who will tell you black is white and flatter you; it is not my way. When shall I go? I shan't be long packing. I brought little into Castlewood House, and I shall take little out."

"Hush! The bells are ringing for church, Mountain. Let us try to compose ourselves," the widow said, though her eyes softened with affection as she looked at her sons. George kept his head down, while Harry sat close, his arm wrapped protectively around his brother's neck throughout the sermon.

Later, Harry continued his narrative, interspersed with youthful exclamations and answers to his listener's questions. The old lady seemed inexhaustible in her attention, declining rides, walks, tea, or cards in favor of listening to him. When the Castlewood family was present, she redoubled her attentions, insisting Harry speak close to her ear and commanding the others to be silent so she could hear their cousin. They would retreat to the corners of the room, feigning pleasure.

"Are you my cousin, too?" the honest boy asked. "You see kinder than my other cousins."

Their conversation took place in the wainscoted parlour, hung with portraits of the family for two centuries. Above Madam Bernstein's great chair hung a brilliant painting by Kneller, depicting a young lady of Queen Anne's time with auburn hair and a lovely neck.

"That picture is by Sir Godfrey," she explained. "He thought himself the greatest painter in the world, but he was not so good as Lely, who painted your grandmother—my Lady Castlewood, Colonel Esmond's wife; nor as Van Dyck, who painted your great-grandfather yonder. Some of us are painted blacker than we are. Did you recognize your grandmother? She had the loveliest fair hair and shape of any woman of her time."

"I fancied I knew the portrait from instinct, perhaps, and a certain likeness to my mother," Harry replied.

"Did Mrs. Warrington—though I think she calls herself Madam or Lady Esmond now—ever tell you of another daughter her mother had in England before she married your grandfather?"

"She never spoke of one."

"Nor your grandfather?"

"Never. But in his picture-books, which he constantly made for us children, he used to draw a head very like that above your ladyship. That, and Viscount Francis, and King James III., he drew a score of times, I am sure."

"And the picture over me reminds you of no one, Harry?"

"No, indeed."

"Ah! Here is a sermon!" the lady sighed, her face falling. "Harry, that was my face once—yes, it was—and then I was called Beatrix Esmond. And your mother is my half-sister, child, and she has never even mentioned my name!"
