Before Deronda was called again to his mother, a day slipped by in which she sent only a brief message: she was not yet well enough to see him. The delay had the strange effect of repeating itself in his nerves, like a refrain heard from a far room—now louder, now dying away—until, on the third morning, a note came with a quick, decisive stroke in the words: “I leave to-day. Come and see me at once.”

He was led into the same chamber as before, but it had been turned into a kind of twilight cell: blinds drawn, curtains thickened, daylight filtered and made solemn, as if even the sun had been asked to lower its voice. The house’s ordinary sounds—footsteps in a passage, a door’s soft click—seemed sharpened by the hush, and Deronda felt how silence itself can press upon the mind like a weight. There was no homely comfort of tea-service clinking, no friendly domestic stir—only that muffled quiet which, in great rooms, makes the heart listen too much.

The Princess was not there at first. Then she entered, and the dimness gathered around her as if it belonged to her. She wore a loose wrap of soft silk, dusky orange like the smoulder of an ember under ash, and black lace floated about her head. Her arms showed bare beneath wide sleeves, and in that sombre light her face looked more commanding than ever—eyes enlarged by shadow, lines more resolute, the whole countenance having the authority of someone who has long ago refused to plead.

One might have fancied her a woman who could brew marvels—philtres of youth or courage—yet would not spend the least drop on herself; as if she had tasted enough of all sweet potions and found them, in the end, thin.

She came straight to him, put her arms on his shoulders, and kissed him on both cheeks. Then she seated herself among her cushions with a composed firmness that differed from her former fitfulness, and with a gesture bade him sit near.

“You are quite relieved now, I trust?” he said.

“Yes, I am at ease again.” Her voice was steady, queenly rather than maternal. “Is there anything more that you would like to ask me?”

“Can I find the house in Genoa where you used to live with my grandfather?” Deronda asked.

“No,” she answered, with a slight, dismissing movement of her arm. “It is pulled down—not to be found. But about our family, and where my father lived at various times—you will find all that among the papers in the chest, better than I can tell you. My father, I told you, was a physician. My mother was a Morteira. I used to hear all those things without listening. You will find them all. I was born amongst them without my will. I banished them as soon as I could.”

He felt the words as one feels the cutting of a thread that had been held taut in hope. Yet he tried to keep his pain from showing.

“Anything else that I should desire to know from you,” he said quietly, “could only be what it is some satisfaction to your own feeling to tell me.”

“I think I have told you everything that could be demanded of me,” she said, her gaze fixed, coldly meditative, as if she were closing a case and sealing it.

To her, the confession had been a storm already spent. She had resolved not to be flung into that weather again; she would keep herself, now, in order. But to Deronda the moment was cruel in a different fashion: it was the end of a pilgrimage where he had expected—if not tenderness—at least some shrine-lamp of emotion to burn. Instead, he found a sacred place emptied.

All the womanly yearning she denied seemed to wake in him, and it trembled in his voice. “Then are we to part and I never be anything to you?”

“It is better so,” she said, and though her tone softened, it did not yield. “There could be nothing but hard duty for you, even if it were possible for you to take the place of my son. You would not love me. Don’t deny it.” She lifted her hand sharply, forbidding any protest. “I know what is the truth. You don’t like what I did. You are angry with me. You think I robbed you of something. You are on your grandfather’s side, and you will always have a condemnation of me in your heart.”

Deronda felt himself struck dumb—not because he had no answer, but because any answer seemed either false or a weapon. He rose, preferring to stand rather than sit under that command to be untender.

Then she looked up at him, and admiration—unexpected, almost alarming—came into her eyes.

“You are wrong to be angry with me,” she said. “You are the better for what I did.”

After a pause, she added, with abruptness, “And now tell me what you shall do?”

“Do you mean now, immediately,” he said, “or as to the course of my future life?”

“I mean in the future. What difference will it make to you that I have told you about your birth?”

“A very great difference,” Deronda answered, with an emphasis that surprised even himself. “I can hardly think of anything that would make a greater difference.”

“What shall you do then?” Her sharpness returned. “Make yourself just like your grandfather—be what he wished you—turn yourself into a Jew like him?”

“That is impossible. The effect of my education can never be done away with. The Christian sympathies in which my mind was reared can never die out of me.” His voice grew tenacious, like a hand tightening on a truth. “But I consider it my duty—it is the impulse of my feeling—to identify myself, as far as possible, with my hereditary people; and if I can see any work to be done for them that I can give my soul and hand to, I shall choose to do it.”

She watched him with a strange, searching attention, as if his face were written in a dialect she could not read but would master by force of staring. He bore her gaze, steadied by opposition which was not defiance but fidelity to the deepest part of himself.

She bent toward him. “You are in love with a Jewess.”

The color rose in his face. “My reasons would be independent of any such fact.”

“I know better. I have seen what men are.” Her tone was final, as if it pronounced sentence. “Tell me the truth. She is a Jewess who will not accept any one but a Jew. There are a few such,” she added with scorn.

To argue with certainty is to waste breath; it is like trying to soften iron by speaking warmly to it. And besides, this was a point on which Deronda felt a repugnance either to deny or affirm. He remained silent.

She took his silence for confession and went on: “You love her as your father loved me, and she draws you after her as I drew him.”

The comparison struck through him—filial imagination kindled, tenderness rose unbidden. His glance softened; she read it as assent.

“But I was leading him the other way,” she said, passion swelling. “And now your grandfather is getting his revenge.”

“Mother,” Deronda said, and the word itself was an appeal, “don’t let us think of it in that way. I will admit that there may come some benefit from the education you chose for me. I prefer cherishing the benefit with gratitude, to dwelling with resentment on the injury. I think it would have been right that I should have been brought up with the consciousness that I was a Jew; but it must always have been a good to me to have as wide an instruction and sympathy as possible. And now—you have restored me my inheritance—events have brought a fuller restitution than you could have made—you have been saved from robbing my people of my service and me of my duty: can you not bring your whole soul to consent to this?”

He paused, pleading with voice more than with argument. She listened as if the cadence itself worked on her—sound can sometimes enter where meaning is barred—yet she shook her head slowly.

He began again, more urgent, as if time were thinning. “You have told me that you sought what you held the best for me: open your heart to relenting and love toward my grandfather, who sought what he held the best for you.”

“Not for me, no,” she said, shaking her head with an absolute denial, folding her arms tightly, closing herself like a gate. “I tell you, he never thought of his daughter except as an instrument. Because I had wants outside his purpose, I was to be put in a frame and tortured. If that is the right law for the world, I will not say that I love it. If my acts were wrong—if it is God who is exacting from me that I should deliver up what I withheld—who is punishing me because I deceived my father and did not warn him that I should contradict his trust—well, I have told everything. I have done what I could. And your soul consents. That is enough. I have after all been the instrument my father wanted.—‘I desire a grandson who shall have a true Jewish heart. Every Jew should rear his family as if he hoped that a Deliverer might spring from it.’”

As she spoke these last sentences her eyes narrowed; she waved her head slowly up and down, the words coming in a new, reluctant chest-voice, as if she were quoting an old chant she hated yet could not forget.

“Were those my grandfather’s words?” Deronda asked, as if he had heard a key-note struck to something vast.

“Yes, yes; and you will find them written. I wanted to thwart him,” she burst out, the old fire flashing. Then, more slowly: “You would have me love what I have hated from the time I was so high”—she held her left hand a yard from the floor—“That can never be. But what does it matter? His yoke has been on me, whether I loved it or not. You are the grandson he wanted. You speak as men do—as if you felt yourself wise. What does it all mean?”

Her scorn came like a sudden harsh note in the chamber’s hush, and Deronda felt how words can bruise when the air is too still. Under the solemn urgency of parting he had to clutch at the mere fact of their relationship, lest his speech turn cruel.

“Mother,” he said, in a deep entreating tone, “don’t say that I feel myself wise. We are set in the midst of difficulties. I see no other way to get any clearness than by being truthful—not by keeping back facts which may—which should carry obligation within them—which should make the only guidance toward duty. No wonder if such facts come to reveal themselves in spite of concealments. The effects prepared by generations are likely to triumph over a contrivance which would bend them all to the satisfaction of self. Your will was strong, but my grandfather’s trust which you accepted and did not fulfill—what you call his yoke—is the expression of something stronger, with deeper, farther-spreading roots, knit into the foundations of sacredness for all men. You renounced me—you still banish me—as a son—” indignation moved in his voice despite him—“but that stronger Something has determined that I shall be all the more the grandson whom also you willed to annihilate.”

She watched him fixedly, and again admiration gathered on her face, as if strength—any strength—was the one thing she could still prize without surrender.

After a silence she said, low and persuasive, “Sit down again.” He obeyed and took his place beside her.

She laid her hand on his shoulder; it trembled, though she tried to hold it steady. “You rebuke me. Well—I am the loser. And you are angry because I banish you. What could you do for me but weary your own patience? Your mother is a shattered woman. My sense of life is little more than a sense of what was—except when the pain is present. You reproach me that I parted with you. I had joy enough without you then. Now you are come back to me, and I cannot make you a joy. Have you the cursing spirit of the Jew in you? Are you not able to forgive me? Shall you be glad to think that I am punished because I was not a Jewish mother to you?”

“How can you ask me that?” Deronda said, shocked into protest. “Have I not besought you that I might now at least be a son to you? My grief is that you have declared me helpless to comfort you. I would give up much that is dear for the sake of soothing your anguish.”

“You shall give up nothing,” she said hurriedly, agitation breaking through her command. “You shall be happy. You shall let me think of you as happy. I shall have done you no harm. You have no reason to curse me. You shall feel for me as they feel for the dead whom they say prayers for—you shall long that I may be freed from all suffering—from all punishment. And I shall see you instead of always seeing your grandfather. Will any harm come to me because I broke his trust in the daylight after he was gone into darkness? I cannot tell:—if you think Kaddish will help me—say it, say it. You will come between me and the dead. When I am in your mind, you will look as you do now—always as if you were a tender son—always—as if I had been a tender mother.”

The strange plea—half command, half beseeching—filled the room more than any loud cry could have done. Deronda’s compassion rose until it shut in speech; words felt poor and blunt. He put his arm around her and drew her head gently under his, as if sheltering her from the very air. They sat so for some moments, in that dense hush where even breathing seemed a kind of utterance.

Then she lifted her head and rose with a great sigh, as if she dismissed a multitude of thoughts in a single breath. Deronda stood before her, and felt the parting near like the approach of a cold dawn.

One of her swift changes came again.

“Is she beautiful?” she asked abruptly.

“Who?” he said, changing color.

“The woman you love.”

There was no time for careful explanation; he could only answer, “Yes.”

“Not ambitious?”

“No, I think not.”

“Not one who must have a path of her own?”

“I think her nature is not given to make great claims.”

“She is not like that?” The Princess drew from her wallet a miniature set round with jewels and held it out.

It was herself in the fire of youth: a face made for gaze and music, for the bright tyranny of applause. Deronda looked at it with admiring sadness; in that painted bloom he could almost hear the old theatre’s rustle—the eager coughs, the tuning strings, the roar that rises when a voice fills a hall like warmed wine poured into cold mouths. Yet all that brilliance was now in his mother’s hand like a relic of a life already entombed.

“Had I not a rightful claim to be something more than a mere daughter and mother?” she said. “The voice and the genius matched the face. Whatever else was wrong, acknowledge that I had a right to be an artist, though my father’s will was against it. My nature gave me a charter.”

“I do acknowledge that,” Deronda said, looking from the miniature to her face, which even in worn pallor had a force beyond what any pencil could catch.

“Will you take the portrait?” she asked more gently. “If she is a kind woman, teach her to think of me kindly.”

“I shall be grateful for the portrait,” Deronda said; then, compelled by honesty even in this hour, he added, “but—I ought to say, I have no assurance that she whom I love will have any love for me. I have kept silence.”

“Who and what is she?” The question fell like an order.

“She was brought up as a singer for the stage,” he said, reluctance tightening his throat. “Her father took her away early from her mother, and her life has been unhappy. She is very young—only twenty. Her father wished to bring her up in disregard—even in dislike of her Jewish origin, but she has clung with all her affection to the memory of her mother and the fellowship of her people.”

“Ah, like you,” the Princess said peremptorily. “She is attached to the Judaism she knows nothing of. That is poetry—fit to last through an opera night. Is she fond of her artist’s life—is her singing worth anything?”

“Her singing is exquisite. But her voice is not suited to the stage. I think that the artist’s life has been made repugnant to her.”

“Why, she is made for you then,” said the Princess. “Sir Hugo said you were bitterly against being a singer, and I can see that you would never have let yourself be merged in a wife, as your father was.”

“I repeat,” Deronda said, and his emphasis had the steadiness of someone bracing himself against a gust—“I repeat that I have no assurance of her love for me, of the possibility that we can ever be united. Other things—painful issues may lie before me. I have always felt that I should prepare myself to renounce, not cherish that prospect. But I suppose I might feel so of happiness in general. Whether it may come or not, one should try and prepare one’s self to do without it.”

“Do you feel in that way?” She laid her hands on his shoulders and studied his face, speaking slowly, with pauses, as if each thought were heavy to move. “Poor boy!—I wonder how it would have been if I had kept you with me—whether you would have turned your heart to the old things against mine—and we should have quarrelled—your grandfather would have been in you—and you would have hampered my life with your young growth from the old root.”

“I think my affection might have lasted through all our quarrelling,” Deronda said, sorrow deepening, “and that would not have hampered—surely it would have enriched your life.”

“Not then, not then—I did not want it then—I might have been glad of it now,” she said bitterly, “if I could have been glad of anything.”

“But you love your other children, and they love you?” he asked anxiously, as if clinging to any possibility of warmth in her world.

“Oh, yes,” she answered, as if it were a matter of course, folding her arms again. Then, in a deeper tone: “But I am not a loving woman. That is the truth. It is a talent to love—I lack it. Others have loved me—and I have acted their love. I know very well what love makes of men and women—it is subjection. It takes another for a larger self, enclosing this one”—she pointed to her bosom. “I was never willingly subject to any man. Men have been subject to me.”

“Perhaps the man who was subject was the happier of the two,” Deronda said, not smiling—only grieving, as one grieves over a hunger that has never even learned to name itself.

“Perhaps—but I was happy—for a few years I was happy. If I had not been afraid of defeat and failure, I might have gone on. I miscalculated. What then? It is all over. Another life! Men talk of ‘another life,’ as if it only began on the other side of the grave. I have long entered on another life.”

With the last words she raised her arms till they were bare to the elbow; her brow contracted into one deep fold; her eyes closed; her voice grew smothered. In her dusky, flame-coloured garment, with black lace like mourning smoke about her, she looked like a dreamed visitant from a region where the dead still remember the taste of earthly triumph and find it ashes.

Deronda’s feeling rose to a point where he was no longer master of himself. A sob broke from him—audible, helpless, like the voice of something young inside him that had at last understood.

She opened her eyes, and once more let her hands rest on his shoulders.

“Good-bye, my son, good-bye. We shall hear no more of each other. Kiss me.”

He clasped his arms round her neck, and they kissed each other—an embrace that was less reunion than final recognition.

Deronda did not know how he got out of the room. He went away with the sensation of having crossed an inner boundary: as if some boyish part of him, that had lived on yearning and unanswered questions, had been left behind in that darkened chamber. The anxious pilgrimage was ended; the shrine had not given him what he had once imagined, but it had given him something weightier—a tragic knowledge which would solemnize his life, and deepen forever the meaning of every bond he chose to make.
