Every traveler familiar with the countryside of Eastern England is acquainted with the numerous small country houses scattered throughout the region. These are typically rather gloomy structures, often constructed in the Italian style, encircled by parks ranging from eighty to a hundred acres. To me, they possess an enduring allure: the weathered gray pales of split oak, the majestic trees, the meres fringed with reeds, and the distant lines of woodland. I hold a special affection for the pillared portico—often clumsily affixed to a red-brick Queen Anne residence, disguised with stucco to align with the "Grecian" fashion of the late eighteenth century. Inside, I admire the soaring halls, ideally equipped with galleries and small organs. I enjoy the libraries, where one might encounter everything from a thirteenth-century Psalter to a Shakespeare quarto. Of course, the paintings are delightful, but above all, I delight in imagining the lives lived within these homes during their inception, in the golden eras of landlord prosperity, and equally in contemplating their current state, where, despite diminished wealth, tastes are more diverse and existence remains undeniably intriguing. I aspire to possess one such estate, coupled with sufficient funds to maintain it and host my friends with modest dignity.

However, this is merely a preamble. What follows concerns a peculiar sequence of events that unfolded within a house fitting precisely this description: Castringham Hall in Suffolk. While significant renovations have altered the building since the era of my narrative, the core characteristics remain intact—the Italianate portico, the stark white façade masking an older interior, the surrounding park bordered by woods, and the mere. One distinctive feature, however, has vanished. From the vantage of the park, one would formerly observe, on the right, a colossal ancient ash tree rising within a dozen yards of the wall, its branches brushing against or nearly enveloping the structure. It is presumed this sentinel stood there since Castringham transitioned from a fortified stronghold to an Elizabethan dwelling, filling the drained moat. By the year 1690, it had reached nearly its full, awe-inspiring dimensions.

That specific year witnessed a wave of witch trials in the district. It will likely remain elusive for centuries to ascertain the precise degree of rational basis underlying the pervasive dread of witches in antiquity. Did the accused truly believe themselves imbued with supernatural powers? Did they harbor the intent, if not the ability, to harm their neighbors? Or were the myriad confessions extracted solely through the brutality of the witch-finders? These questions remain unsolved. The following narrative compels reflection; I cannot dismiss it as mere fabrication. The judgment rests with the reader.

Castringham yielded a victim to the *auto-da-fé*. Her name was Mrs. Mothersole. She differed from the typical village witch only in her relative affluence and social influence. Several reputable local farmers attempted to secure her release, offering vigorous testimony to her character and expressing deep concern regarding the jury's verdict.

Yet, the woman's fate was sealed by the testimony of Castringham Hall's proprietor, Sir Matthew Fell. He swore that on three separate occasions, under the full moon, he observed her climbing into the branches of the ash tree adjacent to his house. Clad merely in her shift, she cut small twigs with a strangely curved knife while appearing to converse with herself. Each time, Sir Matthew endeavored to apprehend her, but every attempt was thwarted by an accidental noise that caused her to flee. When he descended to investigate, all he witnessed was a hare darting across the path toward the village.

On the third instance, Sir Matthew pursued her at top speed directly to her cottage. He waited fifteen minutes, pounding on her door, before she emerged, visibly irritable and groggy, as if freshly awakened. She offered no satisfactory explanation for his nocturnal intrusion.

Predominantly based on this evidence, supplemented by lesser testimonies from other parishioners, Mrs. Mothersole was convicted and sentenced to death. She was executed a week later alongside five or six other unfortunate souls at Bury St. Edmunds.

Sir Matthew Fell, serving as Deputy-Sheriff, attended the hanging. It was a damp, drizzly March morning as the cart ascended the rough grassy hill outside Northgate, where the gallows stood. The other victims appeared apathetic or utterly broken by despair; Mrs. Mothersole, however, displayed a temperament distinctively different from her life. As a contemporary chronicler noted, her "poisonous rage" affected the bystanders so profoundly—even the hangman—that she was universally regarded as presenting the "living aspect of a mad devil." Remarkably, she offered no resistance to the law officers. Instead, she fixed her gaze upon those who seized her with such a direful, venomous expression that one officer later confessed the mere thought of it haunted his mind for six months thereafter.

All that she reportedly uttered were seemingly nonsensical words: "There will be guests at the Hall." She repeated this phrase multiple times in a low murmur.

Sir Matthew Fell was deeply unsettled by her demeanor. Later, traveling home with the Vicar of his parish after the assizes concluded, he discussed the matter extensively. Though his testimony at the trial had been reluctant, as he was not particularly susceptible to the witch-hunting fervor, he insisted he could provide no alternative account of his observations and maintained absolute certainty in his perception. The entire ordeal repelled him, for he valued harmonious relations with his community, yet he felt compelled to fulfill his civic duty. This sentiment, the Vicar agreed, was the essence of the situation, and any reasonable person would concur.

Weeks later, under the full moon of May, the Vicar and the Squire reunited in the park, proceeding together toward the Hall. Lady Fell accompanied her ailing mother, leaving Sir Matthew alone at home; thus, the Vicar, Mr. Crome, was readily persuaded to accept a late supper at the Hall.

Sir Matthew was a poor conversationalist that evening. Discussion centered on family and parish affairs. Fortuitously—or perhaps providentially—Sir Matthew drafted a written memorandum detailing certain intentions regarding his estates, a document that would prove invaluable later.

As Mr. Crome prepared to depart around half-past nine, Sir Matthew and he took a brief stroll along the gravel path behind the house. The sole incident that captured Mr. Crome's attention occurred when they approached the ash tree previously described as looming near the windows. Sir Matthew halted abruptly and asked, "What is that scurrying up and down the trunk of the ash? Is it not a squirrel? They should all be nestled by now."

The Vicar glanced upward and discerned a moving form, though the moonlight rendered its color indistinguishable. However, the sharp silhouette, visible for a fleeting instant, burned itself into his memory. He swore, albeit sounding somewhat absurd, that the creature possessed more than four legs.

Nevertheless, little significance was attached to this ephemeral vision, and the two men parted ways. They may have encountered one another subsequently, but not for a decade.

The following morning, Sir Matthew Fell failed to descend to the ground floor at his customary hour of six, nor at seven, nor at eight. Servants, growing anxious, pounded on his chamber door. Their frantic knocking eventually yielded to a response from the outside; the door swung open to reveal their master dead and blackened. Violence played no apparent role in his demise, yet the window stood ajar.

One servant fetched the parson, who then dispatched a rider to notify the coroner. Mr. Crome hastened to the Hall and was led to the deceased's room. His surviving papers reflect the genuine respect and sorrow felt for Sir Matthew, containing a crucial passage illuminating the circumstances and the prevailing superstitions of the era:

"There was not the slightest trace of forced entry to the chamber; the casement stood open, as my poor friend invariably preferred during this season. Beside him lay his evening draught of small ale in a silver vessel of approximately a pint capacity, untouched. This beverage was examined by the physician from Bury, Mr. Hodgkins, who, upon oath before the coroner's inquest, could detect no poisonous substance within it. Naturally, amidst the great swelling and blackening of the corpse, rumors of poison circulated among the neighbors. The body was severely disordered, twisted in such an extreme manner as to suggest my worthy friend expired in immense agony and torment. Furthermore, an inexplicable detail, hinting at some horrid and artful design by the perpetrators of this barbaric murder, arose concerning the women entrusted with laying out and washing the corpse. Both highly respected in their mournful profession, they approached me in profound distress of mind and body. Confirmed immediately upon viewing the body, they stated that the moment their bare hands touched the breast of the corpse, they experienced an intense, violent smarting and acheing in their palms and forearms. Within a short time, these areas swelled immoderately, the pain persisting until, as later proven, they were forced to abandon their calling for many weeks. Strangely, no marks were visible on the skin.

'Upon hearing this, I summoned the physician still present in the house. With the aid of a small crystal magnifying lens, we conducted as careful an examination as possible of the skin in question. We detected nothing of consequence beyond a couple of minute punctures or pricks, which we initially concluded were the spots through which the poison had been introduced, recalling the infamous ring of Pope Borgia and other known specimens of the horrific arts employed by Italian poisoners of the previous age.'
