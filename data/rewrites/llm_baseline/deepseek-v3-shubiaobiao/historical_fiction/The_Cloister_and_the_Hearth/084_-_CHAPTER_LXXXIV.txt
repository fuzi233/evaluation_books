The following Sunday, after Mass, the house of Catherine in the Hoog Straet hummed with activity. The shop stood ready, its sign freshly painted—a white lamb suckling a gilt sheep—bearing the names of Cornelis and Sybrandt, who were to open their venture the next day. Eli had arrived, bringing additional wares from his own stores to ensure their sons began on prosperous footing. Pride warmed the parents’ hearts as they watched their boys stroll the garden, murmuring vows to forsake their idle past for the steadier rewards of trade. Catherine, ever sharp-eared, caught their words through an open window and relayed them to Eli with quiet triumph.

That evening, a celebratory supper was to unite the family—Margaret and her little one included—toasting the new enterprise with advice from the elders and wine to bless their conversion from waywardness to commerce. Margaret, seeking respite from her lingering melancholy, had come early to assist Catherine. Both women’s cheeks glowed from the heat of the hearth and their labors.

Joan arrived bearing a salad from Jorian’s garden. "He picked it for you, Margaret," she said with a playful frown. "You’re all his chatter now—I’ll grow jealous! When I told him of today’s feast, he near stripped his plot bare. Oh, but you should’ve heard the sermon at the new kirk! Never have I heard such preaching."

Margaret’s eyes brightened. "If only I had been there. My soul feels parched, thirsting for such dew."

Joan nodded. "He speaks again this afternoon. Unless you’re needed here—"

Catherine waved a hand. "Go, child. You’ll be better company warmed by a sermon than sighing over the stewpot."

Margaret hesitated, then glanced at her plain cap. "But the women will all be in their fine mutches..."

"Take my handkerchief from the basket," Catherine insisted. "The boy stays with me—Kate needs him today. Poor lamb, it’s one of her bad spells."

Margaret carried the child upstairs, where Kate lay abed, her face pale but serene. "Ah, sweeting," Margaret murmured, seeing the girl’s forced smile. "I’ve brought you your little sunbeam."

"Two sunbeams, I think," Kate whispered, though pain pinched her brow.

Margaret laughed softly. "In your lap? Nay, he’s too heavy for you now."

Kate’s arms tightened. "Love lightens his weight."

After a hurried toilette, Margaret paused at the door. "I’m off to the kirk. This preacher—they say his words are balm."

Kate sighed. "Bring me every word back? Oh, that I could go myself!" Her eyes shimmered.

Margaret kissed her brow. "I’ll try." She glanced at the boy, longing to kiss him too, but held back. "Your father’s rule—never wake a sleeping child. When he stirs, teach him those new words for Aunt Kate."

Downstairs, Catherine and Joan peeled vegetables with brisk efficiency. "Mind this, Joan," Catherine said. "Those who help cook may also feast. That’s a law in this house."

Joan grinned. "Better than most laws in Holland! My Luke’s supper is in the wind today—he’s digging ditches, thank the saints."

When Margaret descended, fresh-faced and tidy, Catherine caught her chin. "Look at you! Rotterdam’s finest couldn’t hold a candle." She pressed a kiss to her cheek. "Now heed—Eli won’t delay supper for the Duke himself. Don’t linger after the service."

The two women stood at the door, watching Margaret until she turned the corner. Catherine’s voice softened. "Joan, it’s like seeing my own daughter off to kirk again. My heart aches for her. She’ll hear no ill of Gerard, though if he lives, he’s treated her cruelly. That’s why I mean to see her wed to your good Luke—a steady man, no grand passions, but a quiet hearth. What’s one grandchild more or less?"

Joan chuckled, and the door closed on the bustling house, its warmth a small fortress against the world’s chill.
