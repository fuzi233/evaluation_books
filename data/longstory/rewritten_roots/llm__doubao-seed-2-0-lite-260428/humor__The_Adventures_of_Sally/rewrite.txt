1
Sally leaned over the long dinner table, grinning so hard her cheeks ached, and decided this party was a total win.
It had teetered on the edge of disaster for the first hour, she knew perfectly well, all thanks to her brother Fillmore’s stupidly pristine white evening waistcoat. That thing had glowed so bright under the dining room chandelier it had made every other patron of Mrs. Meecher’s Select Boarding House (transient and residential) feel like they’d showed up to a royal banquet in a bathrobe. Nobody dared laugh too loud or pass the mashed potatoes too fast, scared they’d slop a spot on the sacred garment and get a look so snooty it would curdle the gravy. But now that awkward ice had fully shattered, everyone was yelling over each other, cackling, spilling wine on the tablecloth, and having the exact raucous good time Sally had planned.
Over at her end, the conversation had circled right back to everyone’s favorite new hobby: dictating how Sally should spend her new inheritance. Turns out the second-best thrill in the whole world, right after getting a big windfall of your own, is playing rich person budget boss for someone else. Rumor had inflated Sally’s legacy to a wild 400 grand, so all their suggestions were as sprawling as a billionaire’s private island itinerary.
“Let me tell you what I’d do if I were you,” barked Augustus Bartlett, sharp as a tack and 100% full of it. He occupied a glorified gofer spot at Wall Street brokerage Kahn, Morris and Brown, and talked like he shared a morning golf cart with J.P. Morgan. “Sink 200k in a rock solid bond issue—we just dropped one you’d be an idiot to pass up—and play with the rest. Multiple Steel’s gonna hit 150 a share before next Saturday, I’ve got the inside line.”
Elsa Doland, the big-eyed pretty chorus girl on his left, rolled her eyes. “Please. Buy a theatre, Sally. Put on actual good shows instead of whatever garbage they’re shoving at Broadway crowds these days.”
“Lose every single cent you own,” a soft, mild guy with a surprisingly deep voice called across the table. “If I had a couple hundred grand? I’d dump every last dollar on Benny Whistler for the heavyweight championship. I got private info: Battling Tuke’s been paid off, he’s gonna go down in the seventh round no questions asked.”
“Wait a second, listen—”
“Nah, if I had four hundred grand first thing I’d do is pay my hotel bill that was due this morning,” Elsa cut in, winking at Sally.
Sally popped up so fast her chair scraped the floor, flitted around the table, and wrapped an arm around her friend’s shoulder. “Elsa, are you actually broke? If you are, you know I’d—”
Elsa cackled and squeezed her hand. “You’re a total angel, Sally, I’m fine! Just got back from a tour, saved a fortune. I only said that to mess with you, you sweetheart.”
Sally trundled back to her seat, relieved, to find the crowd had already split into two warring finance camps. Team Augustus the Prudent had earmarked 300k for Liberty Bonds and the rest for fancy real estate, no fun allowed. Team Fight Fix Gossips had already portioned out Sally’s cash to bet on Benny Whistler, doling it out in tiny sums so they didn’t tip off the bookies and tank the three-to-one odds.
Sally decided it was time to burst their collective bubble.
“Guys, I hate to kill the buzz,” she said, “but all those numbers are way off. I only got twenty-five thousand dollars total.”
The room went dead quiet for a full three seconds. Those people had just mentally set aside 25 grand as the petty cash for her hypothetical private yacht’s gas money. Then they snapped back to reality, already rearranging their budgets. Twenty five grand might be pocket change to a millionaire, but you could still do plenty of dumb fun stuff with it.
“If I had twenty five grand,” Augustus spluttered, the first one to recover, “I’d buy Amalgamated—”
“If I had twenty five grand,” Elsa started.
“If I’d had twenty five grand in 1900,” mumbled a gloomy guy with spectacles who’d been brooding in the corner this whole time, “I could’ve started a full-on revolution in Paraguay.” He slumped back, clearly grieving the lost opportunity.
“Alright, no more hypothetical what-ifs,” Sally laughed. “I’ll tell you exactly what I’m doing. First, I’m booking a trip to Europe, France first—everyone says France is great. As soon as my passport comes through, I’m off. I’ll lounge around there for a few weeks, come back, and find some cute little low-effort business to sink money into that pays me enough to never have to eat Mrs. Meecher’s overcooked liver and onions ever again. Any complaints?”
“Even two grand on Benny Whistler,” the mild boxing fan wheedled.
“Not a chance,” Sally said. “I wouldn’t take Benny Whistler as a gift. If I’m gonna lose money on purpose, I’m gonna do it properly at Monte Carlo.”
“Monte Carlo!” The gloomy guy perked right up. “I was there in ‘97, I was 50 bucks short, just 50, and I would’ve—”
At the far end of the table, a chair scraped loudly across the floor. Up rose Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, the boarding house’s oldest long-term resident, moving with the dramatic grace of a 1890s stage actor who’d never once forgotten a curtain speech.
“Ladies,” he boomed, bowing so deep his white eyebrows wiggled, then flicking a sharp glare at the two new acrobat transients, the Marvellous Murphys, who were already squirming like they were about to heckle. “…and gentlemen. I cannot let this occasion pass without saying a few words.”
Nobody was surprised. Life in New York served up all kinds of wild events, but not a single one in the three years any of them had known Maxwell Faucitt had ever been boring enough for him to skip a speech. They’d all braced for this the second Sally announced it was her farewell dinner, and with full stomachs and decent wine in their systems, they settled in to listen. The smaller Murphy started to stand up to heckle, but one sharp look from Sally shut him right down. The two lissom acrobats, who could balance on one hand on an overturned ginger ale bottle while spinning a barrel on their feet, both adored Sally to bits, and they knew better than to mess with her.
To be fair, who wouldn’t adore Sally? She was a tiny, trim little wisp of a girl with the daintiest hands and feet you ever saw, a smile that lit up the whole room, and a dimple that popped in her rounded chin when she laughed, which was almost all the time. Her bright hazel eyes crinkled shut when she was amused, her brown hair was soft and messy, and she carried herself with a warm, easy charm that was missing from 90% of Mrs. Meecher’s guests. The Marvellous Murphys had great taste, that was for sure.
“I have been asked,” Maxwell Faucitt droned on, stroking his beard, “though I know there are others far worthier than I—Brutuses next to whom I am just a bumbling Marc Antony— I have been asked to propose the health of our charming hostess, coupled with her brother, our old friend Fillmore Nicholas!”
Fillmore, perched at the head of the table, nodded like he was accepting a Nobel Prize. It was a condescending little bob, the kind a king gives a loyal peasant who handed him a handkerchief. Sally’s hand closed around an orange sitting right next to her plate. That sparkly white waistcoat was the perfect target. She’d had to beg Fillmore for over an hour to drag his new rich self out of his fancy Riverside Drive apartment to come to this dinner, and the second he walked through the door in that flawless tux, he’d made every other person in the room look like a hobo who’d raided a thrift store formal bin. But she restrained herself. A hostess can’t go throwing fruit at the guest of honor, even if he is a pompous jerk. Chaos would ensue.
“Speaking as an Englishman,” Faucitt went on, “I took out my citizenship papers decades ago, but I still remember my first trip here. The two greatest things about this country are the over-the-top American hospitality, and the charm of the American girl. Tonight we’ve watched our Sally host a full-on banquet! We’ve even had alcohol, don’t ask where it came from, I won’t snitch. For three years she’s been flitting around this boarding house like a ray of sunshine! She made all our grumpy Bohemian days so much brighter. And now that she’s turning twenty one and got her little fortune, she’s off to bigger and better things. But she’ll never forget us, right? She’ll always save a little spot in her heart for all us Meecher’s boarders!”
The whole table cheered so loud the chandeliers rattled. Sally looked over at Fillmore, waiting for him to get up and give the speech he’d promised—back in his college days he was a champion debater, and she’d figured that after Faucitt’s big dramatic monologue, he’d smooth things over perfectly so the old guy didn’t feel like his speech was wasted.
Fillmore heaved his bulky self out of his chair, smoothed a wrinkle out of that stupid white waistcoat, rested a podgy hand on the table, and glanced down at the crowd with his nose tilted so high he was almost looking at the ceiling.
“I’m sure you don’t want a speech,” he said. “Very good of you to drink our health. Thank you.”
He sat right back down.
The whole room paused, then erupted into quiet cheers of relief. Everyone had been bracing for a 20 minute ramble about Fillmore’s brilliant finance plans. That was the perfect after-dinner speech, as far as they were concerned.
But Maxwell Faucitt? He looked like someone had just yanked the chair out from under him mid-sit. His face was that exact shocked, disappointed hurt you get when you think there’s one more stair at the bottom of a flight and there isn’t.
Sally saw his face and bolted around the table in two seconds, threw her arms around his neck. She shot a death glare at Fillmore, who was pretending he was very interested in the ceiling.
“My brother didn’t… didn’t quite say everything I wanted him to,” she stammered, close to tears. “I can’t do big speeches, but I love all of you, I’m never gonna forget you, and…” She kissed Faucitt on the top of his head, and promptly burst into full happy-sad tears.
“There, there, dear,” Faucitt said, patting her arm. It wasn’t eloquent by any means, but he didn’t feel a single bit of anti-climax. That was way better than any boring formal speech Fillmore could have dragged out.

Ten minutes later, out on the sidewalk outside the boarding house, Sally finished telling Fillmore exactly what a gigantic pompous pig he was. He’d snuck out the side door with his hat and coat, trying to escape without anyone noticing, and she’d run him down before he could get to the subway. Her rant had lasted a solid ten minutes, punctuated by Fillmore’s quiet little whiny protests.
Fillmore, who was usually strutting around like he owned half of Wall Street, was completely cowed. He’d never been able to stand up to Sally when she got mad, not ever. Even their grim old guardian uncle, who’d raised them after their parents died, couldn’t win an argument with her. She was sweet 99% of the time, but the remaining 1% was a full cyclone.
When Sally finally paused to catch her breath, Fillmore puffed himself back up like a deflated rubber balloon.
“What did I even do?” he whined.
“Do you want me to list it all over again?”
“No no,” he yelped. “Listen, Sally, you don’t get it. All this boarding house stuff? It’s over. We’re past it now! Why do I have to go around reliving the days I was broke? I’m about to be a very important, very rich man, I’ve got a huge deal in the works right now—”
“You’re about to be a very fat man,” Sally said flatly. Fillmore had no right to be offended, she was 100% correct. He’d been a cute seven year old, then he’d gone completely downhill. Twenty five now, newly rich, he ate way too many fancy steaks and oysters, and he was already starting to look like a well-fed walrus.
“Anyway,” he huffed, “I don’t see why I have to be tied to a whole group of people that call themselves my friends just ‘cause I was broke enough to live in this boarding house three years ago! You can’t stop people making ‘I Knew Him When’ clubs, but you don’t have to go to their stupid meetings.”
“These people helped you pay your rent when you lost your whole week’s salary at poker,” Sally said.
“I always paid them back!”
“Yeah, and I paid half the loans too, don’t act like a hero,” she said. “If you’re so big and important, why do you act like you’re too good for everyone who was nice to you when you were poor?”
Fillmore sighed. “You just don’t believe in me. If I’d gotten my inheritance at 21 like normal people instead of waiting till 25, I’d be a millionaire by now.”
“You’d be in the poorhouse,” Sally said. “You’d blow every cent on a stupid bad investment before the week was out. You know what you’re missing, Fillmore?”
“What?” he said, scanning his own mental list of wonderful, brilliant qualities, very confused what he could possibly be lacking.
“A full time nurse to remind you to put your socks on and not bet your whole fortune on some guy’s ‘inside tip’.”
Fillmore’s feelings were so hurt he couldn’t even talk back. He sulked, kicking a pebble on the sidewalk. “I’m gonna make my mark on the world, you’ll see.”
“Sure you will,” Sally grinned. “I’ll come visit you in the fancy mansion, bring you jelly, and read you the newspaper on visitor days. Oh, hi there!”
The last line was for a tall, lean guy strolling up the sidewalk from Broadway, who stopped when he saw them. It was Gerald Foster, the new English boarder who’d moved in a couple weeks prior.
“Good evening, Miss Nicholas.”
“Gerald, this is my brother Fillmore. He left this boarding house right before you moved in, back when he was still a broke schmuck just like the rest of us. Mrs. Meecher still thinks of him like a wayward son.”
The two men shook hands. Fillmore, who thought he was a tall guy, suddenly felt tiny next to Gerald, all broad shoulders and sharp, friendly eyes. Fillmore, who’d been taking that silly “sum up every stranger as soon as you meet them” finance bro class, decided this guy was definitely going places too.
They chatted for a minute about nothing much, and Fillmore was thrilled to have an excuse to leave. He mumbled a quick goodbye, scurried off down the street before Sally could start yelling at him again.
Sally leaned against the lamppost, grinning, happy as anything. The night had turned out perfect, even with Fillmore being a total idiot.

Sally was propped against a fluffy dune of golden sand, half-lidded eyes wandering over the usual Roville-sur-Mer beach chaos. Like every French seaside resort worth its overpriced iced coffee, Roville’s morning crowd turns the shore into a chaotic playground: whiskered papas in neon swim trunks dotted the foreground like misplaced tropical birds, clusters of ladies huddled under parasols bright enough to give a seagull a sunburn, and spade-wielding urchins paused mid-sandcastle dig every three minutes to thump their nearest friend or sibling over the head with their trusty trowel before getting back to work like nothing had happened. One poodle, cropped so tight and bristly he looked like a retired army sergeant major, trundled over, sniffed out Sally’s half-eaten box of nougat and chocolates, and parked himself at her feet to wait for handouts like he had a legal right to them.

Her vacation had been the sort of lazy, sun-soaked magic she’d daydreamed about back when she was hoofing it in Broadway chorus lines, drifting slow from one cute French town to the next until Roville’s blue skies, glittering promenade hotels, and the casino’s waltzing band made her decide to stay forever. Too bad the letter from her producer Gerald had landed two days prior: *The Primrose Way* was getting its Detroit previews before New York, and if she wanted to make the opening, she had to sail home immediately. She was leaving tomorrow.

Today, though, was still hers. She basked in the warm sun, listening to the tiny waves gurgle against the shore, and was this close to dozing off – that classic vacation sin of napping before 10 a.m. – when English voices jerked her right back to the land of the awake. English, on the Roville beach? As rare as a waiter who didn’t side-eye you for ordering tap water. Two men had plopped down on the sand three feet away from her.

Sally’s favorite pastime, ever since she’d started travelling alone, was making up elaborate backstories for every stranger she passed, even if she guessed wrong 99% of the time. She sized the first man up out of the corner of her eye: tall, dark, jawline so sharp it could cut glass, mouth a thin tight line like he’d never laughed at a bad joke in his entire life. He looked like the kind of guy who shaved twice a day and never swore, even when he nicked himself, just smiled a nasty little smile that made you want to hide your wallet. “Hard,” Sally diagnosed. “Definitely a lawyer. The kind who’d send you a formal notice for stepping on his lawn.”

She shifted her gaze to the second man, and found he was staring straight at her, mouth slightly agape like he’d just found a winning lottery ticket half-buried in the sand. He was short, stocky, freckled all over his face, with hair so bright red it could guide fishing boats home in a fog, a square chin with a tiny fresh nick on the edge that definitely came from a dumb bar brawl, not a genteel gardening accident. “Rather a dear,” Sally decided. “Got a temper that blows over in ten minutes, not the sharpest tool in the shed, but he seems nice.” She looked away fast, his fascinated stare making her cheeks warm.

The lawyer guy lit a cigarette in the face of a gale that should have blown the match straight out of his hand, flicked the dead stick away, and picked up their interrupted conversation. “How’s Scrymgeour?”

“Eh? Oh, fine,” the redhead mumbled, still half-looking at Sally.

“Surprised he’s here. Told me he was staying in Paris.”

Sally slipped the sergeant major poodle a piece of nougat.

“I say,” the redhead announced suddenly in a booming, unfiltered voice that could have been heard all the way to the promenade, “that’s the prettiest girl I’ve ever seen in my life!”

Sally was startled, but far from offended. A girl who’d spent years on the New York stage had long ago learned to take a genuine compliment for what it was. The lawyer, though? He looked like he’d just watched his parish priest cuss out a nun.

“My good man!” he yelped, horrified.

“Relax,” the redhead said, not even phased. “No one here speaks a word of English. If I didn’t remember three random bits of high school French, I’d have starved to death three days ago. That girl?” He went on, completely unrepentant, “Absolute topper! Never seen anyone that pretty. Look at those hands, those feet. Classy. Though, her mouth is a bit wide, I guess,” he added reluctantly, like it pained him to admit even a tiny flaw.

The lawyer relaxed a little, reassured that no one nearby could understand them, and went back to Scrymgeour. “How’s his dyspepsia holding up?”

The redhead clearly could not care less about Scrymgeour’s upset stomach. “Did you notice how her hair curls all soft over her ears?” he asked. “Eh? Oh, same as usual, I guess.”

“Where are you staying?”

“Normandie Hotel.”

Sally nearly dropped her chocolate. She was staying at the Normandie too, that rickety little budget spot right by the train station, not one of the fancy glittering hotels on the promenade. She figured the redhead had just arrived, since she hadn’t seen him around the lobby before.

“The Normandie?” The lawyer blinked, confused. “I know Roville pretty well, never heard of it. Where is it?”

“Little dump near the station. Nothing fancy, but it’s cheap, and the fries are great.” The lawyer’s frown deepened. “What on earth is a guy like Scrymgeour doing there? He’s fussy about hotels, insists on the best of everything. Should be at the Splendide, not some hovel by the tracks. I want to see him again – tell him to dine with me at the Splendide tonight, eight sharp.”

Sally now had three extra dogs at her feet: a scruffy mongrel, and a little white terrier with a black patch over one eye who looked like a tiny, very grumpy pirate. She couldn’t see the redhead’s face, but his voice went weird, airy, like he was trying to lie his way out of a speeding ticket.

“Oh, Scrymgeour’s not in Roville.”

“Then where is he?” The lawyer’s voice sharpened, like he was cross-examining a witness in court. “Paris, I suppose? Then why aren’t you there? What are you doing here? Did he give you time off?”

“Sort of.”

“When do you go back to work for him?”

“I don’t.”

“What?”

The redhead set his jaw. “If you must know, the old curmudgeon fired me the day before yesterday.”

The lawyer shot to his feet so fast he kicked sand everywhere. Sally, so shocked she wasn’t paying attention to her candy distribution, gave the sergeant major poodle the nougat that was meant for the pirate terrier. She snuck a side glance: the lawyer was standing in a dramatic pose straight out of a 1920s melodrama, like he was seconds away from disowning his lead and kicking her out into a snowstorm. The redhead didn’t even flinch, just stared down the beach at a very fat man in an orange bathing suit who’d been wading in and out of the water for ten minutes, and had just finally summoned the courage to dunk his head under.

“Are you seriously telling me,” the lawyer seethed, “that after the whole family pulled every possible string to get you that perfectly easy sinecure job, the one with endless raises if you just didn’t mess up, you deliberately threw it all away? Good God, you’re hopeless!”

The redhead said nothing, still watching the fat bather. It was a great show, all right, but he wasn’t enjoying a second of it.

“It’s maddening! What do you plan to do now? Do you expect the rest of us to spend the rest of our lives getting you jobs you’ll immediately get fired from? It’s monstrous! It’s sickening! Good God!” He gave a dramatic huff, turned on his heel, and stormed off up the beach – his big dignified exit immediately ruined when a gust of wind snatched his straw hat off, and a passing toddler stomped right on it, squishing it flat into a floppy pancake.

The quiet after he left only lasted a minute. Then, with one low, gurgling snarl, the most spectacular dog fight Roville had seen all summer exploded right at Sally’s feet.

It was the pirate terrier who started it, obviously. Even his own mother wouldn’t have denied that. His only crime? Sally had cheated him out of three nougat pieces in a row, too busy listening to the two men argue to notice. Every dog on the beach, big and small, every breed imaginable, came sprinting over to join the fun. Half of them didn’t even know what they were fighting about, they just started brawling for the sake of it. The terrier had the sergeant major poodle by the left back leg, the scruffy mongrel was attempting to chew a Sealyham stranger’s ear, and the French bystanders were screaming advice in rapid, incomprehensible French, waving their arms and hopping up and down in their swim trunks, no one brave enough to step in.

Until the redhead stepped into the middle of the chaos.

He might have gotten fired by Scrymgeour, might have been a constant pain to his entire family, but that man knew how to stop a dog fight. Ten minutes later, he’d sorted every snarling pooch apart, calmed them all down, and handed most of the confused, suddenly sheepish dogs back to their very apologetic, chattering owners. The whole brawl was gone like it never happened, save for the pirate terrier, who was quietly licking a tiny chewed leg like nothing had occurred.

He turned to Sally, suddenly shy, fumbling like an Englishman about to butcher a foreign language for the first time. “J’espère,” he stammered, swallowing hard, “J’espère que vous n’êtes pas – oh, dammit, what’s the word – J’espère que vous n’êtes pas blessée?”

“Blessée?”

“Wounded! Bitten, don’t you know. J’espère–”

“Oh, bitten!” Sally giggled, dimpling. “No, I’m fine. And that was the bravest thing I’ve ever seen, you just stopped an entire canine war single-handedly.”

The compliment flew right over his head. He stared at her, eyes huge, his face turning bright red as a fire truck. His jaw dropped.

“Oh my sainted aunt!” he yelped. Then he spun on his heel and bolted, walking so fast it was nearly a run, vanishing up the beach before Sally could even say her name. She was left sitting there, still wondering what on earth he’d done to get fired by the mysterious Scrymgeour.

Roville’s bedtime rules are simple: the fancy promenade hotels party till dawn with nonstop jazz, but cheap little spots like the Normandie roll up the shutters by 11 p.m. The only person ever on duty is Jules, the stout young night clerk/lift operator who seems to be awake 24/7, working so hard Sally had long felt bad for him.

She stumbled back to the hotel just before midnight, after a trip to the casino to listen to the band and a moonlit stroll along the promenade. The lobby was dim, the only light a tiny bulb over the front desk where Jules was slumped in his chair, snoring so loud the potted palm next to him shook. She didn’t want to wake him – until the front door banged open, and in walked the redhead.

“Good evening,” Sally said, warmly.

He stopped dead, shuffling his feet so tangled together he nearly tripped. His face was still that same bright tomato red, like he’d never stopped blushing since that morning. “Er – good evening. Bon soir, I mean.” He dropped his hat, then tripped over it when he stooped to pick it up.

Jules, sleepwalking on automatic pilot, stumbled over to the lift, rattled the iron cage door open, and Sally stepped inside, patting his shoulder. “Sorry I woke you up, Jules.” He didn’t reply. He was still fully asleep, operating the lift by muscle memory, yanking the rope to take them up.

The tiny lift seat was only big enough for two, so they sat side by side, the only sound Jules’ soft snores. When they got to the third floor, Sally prodded Jules in the ribs, her go-to method for getting anything done in France (far better than trying to conjugate verbs). The lift jolted to a halt – but Jules didn’t open the gate. He just stood there, frozen, staring at the lock like it had personally insulted his entire family.

“Looks like we’ve got a hitch,” Sally said to the redhead. “Can you ask him what’s wrong? My French only goes as far as ‘ooh la la!’”

He coughed, bracing himself. “Oh, esker… esker vous… pourquoi vous ne… que est le raison–”

Jules cut him off, launching into a rapid-fire, five-minute explanation that sounded like a geyser of French words, none of which Sally could pick apart. “Stop him!” Sally ordered. “Yell something loud. Blow a whistle!”

Some random schoolboy French word floated up from the deep dark recesses of his memory. “ZUT!” he barked.

Jules shut up instantly, like someone had hit a mute button. The silence was so thick you could chew it. “Great!” Sally said. “Now tell him to speak slow, really slow.”

“Lentement,” the redhead said. “Parlez lentement. Pas si… pas si fast!”

Jules caught on, and explained the whole mess at a snail’s pace. The redhead winced, turning back to Sally. “The idiot half-asleep when we walked in. Shut the lift door, completely forgot he left the keys to the whole contraption back down on the front desk. We’re stuck in here.” He groaned. “I really wish I knew better French right now. I’d cuss him out so good – wonder what the word for ‘blighter’ is in French, anyway.”

By six o’clock the next evening, Sally had to concede defeat on her plan to give Ginger Kemp a well-meaning life-coaching lecture: she hadn’t spotted him all day, and in sixty minutes flat she was due to hop the 7:15 express for Paris, then Cherbourg to catch her New York-bound liner. She’d dropped all her luggage at the station into the care of a very cheery porter, and drifted over to the Casino Municipale for one last desperate sweep, hating the thought of leaving Ginger un-uplifted. Like most sharp, energetic girls, Sally had a bad case of busybody syndrome — or, as her brother Fillmore rudely called it, messing with other people’s private business — and Ginger struck her as the exact kind of lost puppy who’d benefit from a friendly shove in the right direction. So she was absolutely chuffed when she spotted a shock of bright red hair glowing like a traffic beacon through the horde packed around a roulette table.

For the uninitiated, Roville has two casinos. The one on the promenade serves mild sea air and an even milder game called boule, for tourists who don’t care if they win or lose a couple of francs. The Casino Municipale, down near the station by the Palace Massena, is where the serious gamblers camp out, the ones who turn up ready to make or break their month’s rent. And it was immediately obvious to Sally that Ginger wasn’t just there to observe — he was absolutely cleaning up. He was camped behind a towering, opulent stack of square counters, and even as she watched, the deadpan croupier raked another huge pile of winnings straight over to his side of the table.

“Epatant!” sighed a goggling man at her elbow, yanking his elbow out of her ribs to gesticulate wildly. Sally’s French was terrible, but she got the gist: this guy was equal parts shocked and awed. The whole crowd was. Continental roulette onlookers are weirdly altruistic — they get the same high watching some random stranger win big as if they’d won the cash themselves. The croupier twisted his moustache with one hand, spun the wheel with the other, and the whole room went dead quiet. Sally had shifted to a less crowded spot, and now she could see Ginger’s face clearly — and snort-laughed out loud.

He looked exactly like a terrier glued to a rat hole. His red hair practically bristled with excitement. You could almost swear his ears were pricked straight up. In that dead, tense hush around the spinning wheel, Sally’s cackle cut through the room like a foghorn. The effect was immediate. The crowd around a hot roulette streak carries the same pious, reverent energy as a congregation at a cathedral service — and if she’d guffawed mid-Hail Mary at high mass, she couldn’t have got more pained, scandalized stares. Ginger jolted around at the noise, saw her, and shot to his feet. Right then the ball clicked into a red slot that wasn’t Ginger’s pick, the crowd groaned so hard it sounded like the penitents’ bench at a southern US revival meeting, and every single person in the room agreed: Sally’s dumb laugh had jinxed the golden boy.

Ginger didn’t care a bit. He scooped up every last one of his chips, stuffed them all in his pocket, and elbowed his way over to fish Sally out of the crowd, who by this point was universally treated like a social pariah. Everyone was gutted the big winner was calling it a night — it felt like a A-list movie star had walked off stage mid-monologue, and even a screaming, hair-pulling fight between two gamblers over a disputed five-franc chip couldn’t cheer them up properly.

“Hey!” Ginger said, dragging her clear to a quiet little ante-room off the gambling floor. “This is brilliant! I’ve been hunting for you everywhere.”

“Funny, that’s exactly what I was doing for you,” Sally said.

Ginger beamed, led her over to a quiet corner bench, the only other person nearby being a fancy uniformed attendant by the door who looked like he’d seen every terrible romantic misadventure known to man.

“That’s so nice of you.”
“I had to talk to you before my train left.”

Ginger jolted so hard he nearly fell off the bench. “Your train? What train?”
“The choo-choo,” Sally explained. “I’m leaving tonight, remember?”
“Leaving?” Ginger looked as horrified as the most pious of the roulette congregation had after her jinx laugh. “You can’t be leaving Roville! Where on earth are you going?”
“Back to America. My boat sails from Cherbourg tomorrow.”
“Oh my aunt!”

Sally felt a little soft spot for him, all wide-eyed and distraught. She was a warm-hearted girl, after all, and who doesn’t like being appreciated? “I’m sorry, but — ”
“Look,” Ginger blurted, turning bright scarlet and staring very intently at the uniformed attendant by the door, who was now giving them the indulgent little smile of a guy who’d been exactly this flustered back in his youth. “Look, will you marry me?”

Sally stared at his beet-red profile, completely gobsmacked. She’d figured Ginger was a surprising guy, but not this surprising. “Marry you?”
“You know. The whole wedding and house and so on thing.”
“Uh, yeah, I follow. You’re proposing the holy state of matrimony. Got it.”
“So what d’you say?”

Sally got her wits back, and her sense of humour was cackling. She looked at him very gravely. He wouldn’t meet her eye, still gawking at the attendant, who was so wrapped up in their little drama he started quietly humming a French love ballad under his breath.
“Isn’t this… don’t think I’m being difficult, but isn’t this a tiny bit sudden?”
“It has to be sudden!” Ginger whined. “I thought you were gonna be here for weeks!”
“Come on, my guy, have you noticed we’re basically strangers?” She patted his hand patronizingly, making the uniformed attendant heave a tender, swoony sigh. “You’ve mixed me up with some girl you actually know properly, the one you got properly introduced to. Look at me again, you’ll realise.”
“If I look at you hard enough,” Ginger said, feverish, “I don’t answer for what I’ll do.”
“And this is the man I was gonna give a whole speech about ambition to!”
“You’re the most amazing girl I’ve ever met, curse it!” Ginger babbled, still not looking at her. “I know it’s sudden, I can’t help it! I fell in love with you the second I saw you, that’s just how it went!”
“Wait, have you considered I might already be engaged to someone else?”

Ginger finally snapped around to look at her, and his face was so genuinely upset Sally’s laugh died in her throat. As silly as it all was, this idiot was dead serious.
“As a matter of fact, yeah, I am,” she said gently.

Ginger bit his lip and went quiet for a minute. “Oh. Well. That’s that, then.”

Sally had a weird, soft, maternal feeling, like watching a small puppy beg for a whole chocolate bar it’s not allowed to have, the kind of feeling where you don’t know if you want to laugh or tuck their hair behind their ear. There was no way she could treat this overgrown redhead like a reasonable grown man right now.
“It’s not real,” she said. “Love at first sight doesn’t exist. Love’s when you know someone properly, you — ” She cut herself off. Wait a second, she and Gerald Foster had fallen for each other in two weeks flat, engaged before they even knew what his favourite breakfast was. She ran out of steam fast, ending with a very limp “It’s ridiculous.”

Ginger slumped into melancholy resignation. “I never expected you to say yes, anyway. I’m not exactly a great catch.”

That was the exact in Sally was waiting for. “That’s exactly what I wanted to talk to you about! I was gonna finish that conversation we started in the elevator last night, before we got interrupted. Can I talk to you like a sister? Let’s make me your honorary sister, yeah?”
Ginger didn’t look super thrilled at the sister bit, but he perked up a little. “That’s really nice of you.”
“I’m gonna give you wisdom. Ginger, why don’t you brace up? Stiffen your spine, stick your chin out, and go actually do something with your life! Why do you just flop around waiting for your family to hand you jobs and decide where you live? Why don’t you go out and grab work for yourself? Why do you care so much what the family thinks? Go make yourself independent! I know you had bad luck when you lost all your money, but every single successful person in the world has been broke at some point. It’s part of the fun! You’ll never get anywhere letting your family pick you up like a floppy Newfoundland puppy and dump you wherever’s convenient for them. Go find something you’re good at, go after it like a rabid badger, hold onto it, and forget the family exists!”

Sally stopped, gasping for air. Ginger stared at her for a long, thoughtful second. “When you talk really fast,” he said, eventually, very seriously, “your nose goes all squiggly. Looks great.”

Sally squawked indignantly. “Are you telling me you didn’t listen to a single word I said?”
“Oh no! I listened! Honest!”
“Then what did I say?”
“Uh… and your eyes shine, too.”
“Forget my eyes! What did I say?”
“You told me… er… to get a job,” Ginger said, after a moment’s hard reflection.

Sally sighed. “Well, I phrased it a lot fancier than that, but yeah, that’s the gist. Good. Glad that landed.”
“Hey,” Ginger said, looking all mournful and devoted. “Can I write to you? Letters, I mean? I think it’d keep me motivated, if I could send you updates.”
“Sure, why not. I wanna know how you get on, anyway.” She gave him the address of the New York boarding house she was staying at, and he wrote it down as reverently as if he was copying the Ten Commandments on his shirt cuff.

Then Sally glanced at the clock on the wall, and nearly had a heart attack. “Oh my GOD! That’s the time? My train!” She sprinted for the swing door, nearly knocking over the confused attendant who had no idea their quiet romance drama was about to turn into a track meet. “Goodbye, Ginger! Write me, remember my speech!”

Ginger, surprisingly fast for a guy who spends half his time drunk and the other half gambling, bolted after her. He’d been a rugby scrum-half back in his university days, after all, so he knew how to run. They sprinted side by side across the square to the platform, just as the train was starting to slowly chug out of the station. Ginger dove for the nearest open door, wrenched it wide open, picked Sally up neatly, and hurled her inside the carriage. She landed square on the toes of the guy in the corner seat, bounced off, and lunged for the window. Ginger was trotting alongside the speeding train, waving.
“Ginger! My porter! Tip him! I forgot!”
“Got it!”
“Don’t forget what I said! Death to the overbearing family!”
“Right ho!”

The train pulled smoothly out of the station. Sally waved until Ginger was a tiny red speck in the distance, then turned around to apologize to the man whose toes she’d just crushed. “I’m so sorry!” she panted. “I hope I didn’t hurt you too bad.”

She was staring straight at Bruce Carmyle. The dark, stuffy cousin she’d run into on the beach the day before. The one who’d assumed she was a random French tourist.

Mr. Carmyle was not the kind of man who let little life surprises ruffle him, but right now he was very, very confused. He’d pegged her as a local French girl yesterday, and now she was chattering to him in perfect, fluent English? When had she learned that? And how on earth had she, a total stranger yesterday, got close enough to his cousin “Ginger” — a nickname he’d always thought was tacky and vulgar — to sprint down the platform with him and yell out the window like they were old college pals? If Sally hadn’t been so pretty, Bruce would have given her a very stern talking-to, she’d completely shattered his strict sense of public propriety. But right now, flushed and panting from the run, she was the prettiest girl he’d ever seen, so he forced a smile.

“Not at all,” he lied. His left big toe was throbbing like it had been hit with a hammer. Even a girl with tiny feet can do some damage when a former rugby scrum-half hurls you straight at it. He hobbled a little, then gestured to the empty seat. “Close call. You nearly missed the train.”
“Yeah, lucky Ginger — Mr. Kemp — was there. He throws really well, doesn’t he?” Sally said, collapsing into the seat to catch her breath.

A waiter popped into the corridor, announcing in surprisingly good English that dinner was being served in the dining car.
“Shall we?” Bruce offered. Sally, who was starving, agreed immediately, and told herself off for judging him earlier for being all grim and stern. He seemed perfectly nice, for a stuffy Brit — until they sat down at the dinner table, and his entire mood soured.

This was a man who took dining extremely seriously, and thought waiters should be seen and not heard. He winced at a tiny spot on the tablecloth, frowned at the menu, and acted like the cheerful, friendly old waiter who doted on Sally was a personal insult. The waiter and Sally were getting on like a house on fire, even though neither spoke the other’s language — he treated her like his favourite granddaughter — until Bruce snapped his order out in that weird loud half-French half-English dialect British tourists use. The waiter chirped “Boum!” happily and vanished.
“Nice old guy!” Sally said.
“Far too familiar,” Bruce grumbled.

Sally changed the subject. “By the way, my name’s Nicholas. I think it’s polite to get names out of the way first, don’t you?”
“Mine’s Carmyle,” he said, then stiffened up. “Though I believe you already heard that from my cousin, apparently.”
Sally didn’t like his cold, judgemental tone. “Why ‘apparently’? I told you we got stuck in an elevator together for half the night. You can’t spend that long trapped with someone without getting to know them. Mr. Kemp’s very nice, and very interesting.”
“Interesting?” Bruce raised a very judgemental eyebrow. “My cousin can play rugby quite well, and I’m told he’s a decent amateur boxer, but we never found him particularly entertaining. The whole family thinks he’s a little dull.”
“Last time I checked, only royalty gets to call themselves ‘we’ when they’re talking about their relatives,” Sally shot back, sharp as a tack. The mention of the overbearing Kemp family made her want to throw a bread roll at his head. “He told me about Mr. Scrymgeour, the guy he punched for beating his dog.”
Bruce stared at a loaf of bread on the table like it had personally offended him. “He has absolutely no sense of privacy.”
The waiter came back and slammed the soup down, grinning at Sally like he’d just pulled a magic trick off. Sally didn’t grin back. She was furious. “I think he got treated terribly unfairly,” she said.
“I would prefer not to discuss my family’s private affairs,” Bruce said, icy as the north sea. “A man who throws away a perfectly good, well-paid job just because — ”
“Oh, for heaven’s sake!” Sally snapped. “Stop being so stuffy! You look exactly like one of those boring 18th century portraits in a heavy gold frame, staring out at everyone with fishy eyes like they’re an unpleasant inconvenience.”

The waiter popped up behind them. “Rosbif!” he announced cheerily, dropping the roast beef on the table. Bruce attacked his beef like it personally wronged him.
“I’m sorry,” he said, stiff as a board. “I never had anyone tell me my eyes looked fishy before.”
“Bet if you’d had sisters, they would have told you years ago,” Sally said.

He went silent and offended until the coffee showed up. Sally stood up, pushing her chair back. “I think I’ll head back to my carriage. I don’t want any coffee, and if I sit here any longer I’ll say something properly rude. I was gonna try and talk you into going easy on Ginger, but clearly that’s impossible. Goodbye, Mr. Carmyle. Thanks for the dinner.”

She stormed off down the corridor, leaving Bruce staring after her, both indignant and weirdly, completely fascinated. No one had ever called him stuffy, or said his eyes looked like a fish’s, in his entire life. And now he couldn’t stop thinking about it.

A handful of days later, Ginger—who’d been deliberately dodging every last member of his meddling extended family like they were a plate of lukewarm brussels sprouts a nosy great-aunt kept trying to force on him—was staring so hard at a street cart selling candy that he didn’t spot his cousin Bruce Carmyle on Piccadilly until it was far too late. They’d returned from Roville by entirely separate routes, on purpose, and Ginger had been quietly congratulating himself on stretching that peaceful separation out for at least another three months. He was just about to grunt a half-hearted nod and speed-walk off like he was late for a very urgent appointment watching paint dry, when Bruce clamped a hand on his arm like a bouncer nabbing someone sneaking a flask into a rugby match.

“Just the man I was looking for,” Bruce said.

“Oh, hullo,” Ginger replied, approximately as thrilled as someone who just stepped in a puddle wearing brand new socks.

“I was actually heading over to your club to hunt you down.”

“Yeah?”

“Yep. Cigarette?”

Ginger peered at the proffered fancy cigarette case with the exact wide-eyed suspicion of a guy who let himself get roped up onto a stage by a magician and is now being handed a “harmless” card trick prop. He was completely bewildered. In all the years they’d known each other, he couldn’t remember Bruce ever being even remotely genial to him. What’s more, he’d fully assumed Bruce would never speak to him again after that whole messy Scrymgeour affair, the unhealed family wound that had the entire brood still convening like a cranky supreme court handing down life sentences for minor misdemeanors.

“Been back in London long?” Bruce asked.

“Couple days.”

“Heard by complete accident you were back crashing at your club. Oh, by the way—thanks for introducing me to Miss Nicholas.”

Ginger jolted so hard he nearly dropped his own lit cigarette. “What!”

“I was in that train compartment at Roville Station, remember? You tripped and sent her flying right on top of me. We agreed to count that as a proper introduction. Very attractive girl, that.”

Bruce hadn’t fully made up his mind what he thought about Sally yet, but he was dead set on not letting her vanish from his life if he could help it. Her abrupt departure from the French station had left him all baffled and cranky, a feeling that’s not quite love at first sight but often makes people act exactly like it is. She’d gotten the better of their last run-in, and he was itching to meet her again to prove he wasn’t the boring stick-in-the-mud she’d clearly pegged him for. Bruce, in a word, was thoroughly piqued: he still couldn’t decide if he liked or disliked Sally, but he knew for a fact a future without her popping up to surprise him would be hopelessly dull.

“Absolutely charming chat we had,” he added.

“Bet you did,” Ginger mumbled, green with envy.

“Say, did she happen to give you her address by any chance?”

“Why?” Ginger snapped immediately, suddenly as possessive of that information as a art collector who just found a one-of-a-kind painting no one else knows exists. He wanted to hoard that address all to himself, gloat over it when he was bored.

“Well, I… er… I promised to send her a stack of books she’s been dying to read…”

“I doubt she’s got much free time to sit around reading,” Ginger argued.

“Books that haven’t been published in America yet.”

“C’mon, pretty much every book gets published in America, right?”

“These particular ones don’t,” Bruce said shortly. He was already annoyed by Ginger’s stubbornness, and kicking himself for not coming up with a more believable lie. “Just hand over the address, would you?”

“Give the books to me and I’ll post them to her,” Ginger offered.

“Good Lord, man!” Bruce snapped. “I am perfectly capable of mailing a couple of books across the Atlantic. Where does she live?”

Ginger caved eventually, because there was no shaking a pest as persistent as his cousin, and spat out the street and number of the lucky New York block that got to be Sally’s home. He gave it up like he was handing over a family heirloom he’d sworn to protect, grumbling the whole time.

“Thanks.” Bruce jotted the info down with a fancy gold pencil in a crisp little morocco-bound notebook. He was the sort of man who always had a pencil on him, so old crumpled envelopes never even entered his line of vision. He never once had to scribble a phone number on the back of a fish and chips receipt.

There was an awkward pause. Bruce coughed.

“Ran into Uncle Donald this morning,” he said. His whole nice-guy act vanished immediately, no point wasting charm on Ginger when he already had what he wanted. His voice went cold, that familiar sharp reproving edge he only pulled out when he was doing the Family’s dirty work.

“Yeah?” Ginger said, glum. Uncle Donald was the head honcho of the whole Family syndicate, the guy who’d employed Ginger in that soul-sucking office job right after he flunked out of art school. A highly respected member of the National Liberal Club, and easily the closest thing to a human blister that had ever walked the Earth as far as Ginger was concerned.

“He wants you to dine with him tonight at Bleke’s.”

Ginger’s mood plummeted even further. A dinner with Uncle Donald would’ve been a total buzzkill even if it was held at the wildest Arabian Nights banquet. That man had a personality so dour it could’ve sobered up Emperor Tiberius’s most chaotic raves in Capri in under two minutes. To dine with him at Bleke’s, that moldy old relic of a coffee house where all the regulars were over 90 and hadn’t missed a night there since 1922, was scraping the absolute bottom of the barrel of bad evenings. Ginger was pretty sure no ordinary human flesh and blood could survive it.

“Tonight?” he said. “Oh, tonight? Well…”

“Don’t be an idiot. You know full well you have to show up.” Uncle Donald’s invitations were royal decrees as far as the Family was concerned. If Ginger had other plans, he was expected to cancel them.

“Fine, all right.”

“Seven thirty sharp.”

“All right,” Ginger moped.

The two split up after that: Bruce headed east to his lawyer’s chambers in the Temple to meet boring clients, Ginger trundled west just to get far away from his cousin as fast as possible. They didn’t have a single scrap of sympathy between them, but funny enough, both of them were thinking the exact same thing as they walked. Bruce was weaving briskly through the Piccadilly Circus crowds daydreaming about Sally, and Ginger was loafing aimlessly towards Hyde Park Corner, so zoned out he kept bumping into pedestrians like a confused goldfish that wandered out of its bowl.

Since he got back to London, Ginger had been a total mess. He wandered through the days half-asleep, and tossed and turned all night. If there’s one thing worse than anything else in an already pretty garbage world—one thing that makes you want to throw your hat in the Thames and give up—it’s hopeless love. Hopeless love had turned his usually chill, go-with-the-flow self completely upside down. Even that big financial crash that wrecked all his plans earlier hadn’t phased him much, he’d just shrugged and said “right ho” and carried on. Now? Every tiny little petty thing made him seethe, stuff he never would’ve noticed before. Like Uncle Donald’s ridiculous bushy moustache, which he used during meals like a little defensive hedge to keep soup from touching his upper lip.

“By gad!” Ginger stopped dead across from Devonshire House, fuming. “If he uses that damned shrubbery as a soup strainer tonight, I’m gonna whack him with a fork!”

Hard thoughts. Hard, angry thoughts, getting sharper by the second, because rebellion spreads faster than a forest fire. The spark had been lit in Ginger, and by the time he made it to Hyde Park Corner he was fully ablaze, crackling with rage at every last stuffy relative he’d ever been forced to be polite to. By the time he got back to his club, he was basically a one-man menace to society—specifically the segment of society that included Uncle Donald, and his other boring uncles George and William, and his tedious aunts Mary, Geraldine, and Louise.

His mood didn’t cool off even when he started getting dressed for the miserable “festivities” at Bleke’s. He scowled as he fought with a stubborn tie that refused to tie properly, like the fabric had personally insulted his favorite pair of socks. There was no getting around it: Ginger was all fired up, working himself up to something properly chaotic.

And just then, as if Fate had been waiting for the exact perfect moment to give him the final nudge, there was a knock at his door. A waiter walked in holding a telegram.

Ginger stared at the envelope, which had been forwarded on from the Hotel Normandie in Roville. It was a wireless, sent from on board the White Star liner Olympic. It read:

Ginger plopped down onto his bed so hard the mattress springs squeaked in protest. The taxi driver who pulled up to the grimey little entrance of Bleke’s Coffee House on the Strand at 7:25 stared at his fare for a second. Determined sort of bloke, he thought, like he’s not heading to a stuffy family dinner, he’s heading straight into a bare-knuckle brawl. He made a mental note not to try and short-change the guy on his change later.

Sally’s whole plan the second her ship docked in New York had been to hole up at the St. Regis for a week, gorging on caviar room service and rolling around in silk sheets to properly revel in her newfound wealth, before hunting down a cozy little permanent apartment of her own. The second she leaned out the taxi window at the pier to give the driver the St. Regis address, though, the whole scheme reeked so strongly of her insufferably performative brother Fillmore that she almost gagged. Why would she blow off the boarding house that had been her home for three years just to flex for random hotel strangers, before she’d even found a new place to live? The only decent call, per her very strict internal morality cop, was to head straight back to Mrs. Meecher’s establishment — prune overload at Sunday dinners and all — and catch up with all her old pals.

The crisp blue-sky amber autumn New York morning was so nice the whole city felt like it was throwing a welcome home party just for her, even if not a single soul on the street knew she was back. Ash cans gleamed in the sun like fancy decor pieces, streetcar conductors were waving happily at commuters, cops directed traffic with the jaunty pep of a vaudeville emcee, and the street cleaners twirled their brooms like they were about to break into a chorus line.

The first buzzkill hit the second she walked through Mrs. Meecher’s door. The landlady was all smiles to hear Sally wanted her old room back, before dropping the first bit of bad news: Gerald Foster had left town that very morning. “Gone straight to Detroit, him and Elsa Doland both!” she yelped, pausing mid-sentence to snap a caustic rebuke at the handyman who was currently using Sally’s trunk as a battering ram to scrape half the wallpaper off the hallway wall. “His new play’s having its tryout there opening Monday, you know — they’ve been rehearsing nonstop since you sailed off.”

Sally was a little miffed she didn’t get to surprise Gerald, but the day was too nice to mope. She could just hop a train to Detroit tomorrow, easy! “Oh, Elsa’s in the cast too? That’s fun.”
“Sure is, and everyone says she’s brilliant,” Mrs. Meecher purred, keeping up with all the theatrical gossip as a former Florodora chorus girl (though, for the record, she was not one of the iconic original Sextette, a fact she would bring up unprompted at every dinner party). “Mr. Faucitt came to see a rehearsal, said she was stellar, and you know how hard he is to impress.”

Sally asked after the old ex-actor, and Mrs. Meecher’s face immediately lit up with the glee of someone who gets to share very juicy bad news. “Poor old dear’s not doing great! Went to bed last night with a splitting headache, and this morning he’s all washed out. That new Spanish flu that’s been going around? Half the papers say people are dropping dead left and right from it!” she chirped, so cheerily you’d think she was announcing a free pie giveaway.
“Good heavens, you don’t think —?”
“Well, he hasn’t turned black yet,” Mrs. Meecher admitted, sounding a little regretful the worst case scenario hadn’t come to pass. “Papers say the patients turn black, you know. Could still happen later, I suppose! Toto’s in there sitting with him, he’s all cozy.”

Sally’s stomach dropped. Toto was the boarding house’s famous woolly little demon dog, whose yap was high-pitched enough to shatter glass, who’d chewed through three boarders’ best neckties in one week, and who definitely had no business hovering within 10 feet of a sick man. Mrs. Meecher, however, remained fully convinced Toto was a certified therapy pet whose yap was a soothing lullaby.
“I’m going up to see him right now!” Sally said, grabbing a cracker from the side table.

One look at the old man’s tired, miserable face when she stepped in told her everything, but he lit up like a Christmas tree the second he saw her. She held up the cracker, lured Toto trotting out into the hallway, tossed the snack down the corridor, and shut the door firmly on him. The two of them could hear Toto yapping outside, equal parts confused and furious, and Mr. Faucitt let out a massive, relieved breath.
“Sally, you’re an absolute angel. Mrs. Meecher means well, God love her, but that little whelp of hers’s yap feels like someone’s beating a tin drum directly against my skull. I thought I was going to lose my mind before you showed up.”

They caught up on her travels fast — she told him England was so damp it looked like the whole country forgot to towel off after a shower, Paris was a blast, and the seaside cottage she’d stayed at was so perfect she almost didn’t come home. Then the old actor groaned about the rehearsal he’d sat through.
“The play itself is great, top notch! But the lead actress, Mabel Hobson? That woman has the acting range of Mrs. Meecher’s infamous Friday suet pudding, no joke. She’s the worst star I’ve ever seen in my 40 years in the business. Reminds me of that 1894 show we did in Wigan, where some millionaire dumped his no-talent mistress in the lead role. She got booed so hard the show closed the very next day. Gerald’s a fool for casting her, mark my words — that boy’s so selfish and wrapped up in his own head he couldn’t see a bad actor if they tripped him on the street.”

Then he dropped a second bombshell that hit Sally even harder: “Oh, speaking of selfish idiots, your brother Fillmore’s engaged! Ran into him on Broadway the other day with the girl, a Miss Winch who works in the theater. She was sweet, quiet, perfectly sensible, no fuss at all.”

Sally stared at him, flabbergasted. “That can’t be Fillmore’s fiancée! The last time I saw him he was dating a girl with crimson hair who yelled at waiters for bringing her the wrong champagne. You must have mixed him up with someone else. I can’t leave New York for a few weeks and come back to Mr. Faucitt sick, my favorite director out of town, and my insufferable brother accidentally engaged to a nice person? That’s absurd.”

A knock at the door interrupted her rant, and in trooped the doctor, spectacles perched on his nose, black bag in hand. Mrs. Meecher followed, hovering, already gearing up to tell the doctor about the black death symptoms she was convinced he’d find.
“Would you mind running down to get me a tiny glass of water?” the doctor asked her, all politeness. “Not a big one, just a little one, let the tap run a few seconds, don’t spill on the stairs.”
The second she was gone, he snickered. “That trick never fails. Gets them out of your hair for ten minutes, and they think you’re gonna do some fancy medical magic with the water. I just want to drink it.”

His exam was fast. “Yep, it’s that Spanish flu, mild case. He just needs to stay warm in bed, no stress, and he’ll be fine. You two got someone to nurse him?”
“Of course I’m his nurse!” Sally said immediately. “All I have to do is fluff pillows, right? That’s easy.”
“Two main job requirements, actually,” the doctor told her, grinning. “One: keep that overly chatty landlady out of this room so she doesn’t scare the poor man half to death with her flu death statistics. Two: if that fluffy little devil dog I passed downstairs tries to bust in, throw a book or an old shoe at it. If you can handle that, he’ll be 100% in no time.”

Five minutes later, Sally was in the Western Union office down the block, scribbling out a telegram to Gerald in Detroit: Sorry, can’t make opening night. Stuck back here nursing Mr. Faucitt through the flu. Don’t panic about the lead actress, I’ll sort everything out as soon as I get there.

It wasn’t till the following Friday that Sally finally pried herself loose from nursing Mr. Faucitt to head for Detroit, and she rolled into the city at 8 a.m. Saturday feeling like she’d spent three days sleeping on a pile of wadded train tickets. She cabbed straight to the Hotel Statler, confirmed Gerald was checked in, yelled up his room line that he better drag himself down for breakfast stat, and parked herself in the dining room booth.

She was moping a little waiting for her food. The Faucitt nursing gig had left her running on fumes, the train berth had been lumpy enough to make a sack of potatoes jealous, and worst of all—Gerald’s voice on the phone had been flatter than a day-old pancake. He’d greeted the news of her showing up after weeks apart like she’d told him the hotel ran out of maple syrup, not like he was glad to see his fiancée. Sally was half-hurt, half-convinced she must have accidentally swapped her number for a random plumber’s in his contacts.

One sip of scalding black coffee fixed 70% of her bad mood, though. Duh, men are all total zombies before 10 a.m. and a cold shower. Give him 10 minutes and he’d come bounding in bright as a firecracker, ready to gush. Until then, she had breakfast to demolish.

She was halfway through her second coffee when she spotted a stout, harried young man wandering through the visible sliver of the hotel lobby, squinting like he was hunting for a lost diamond ring in the potted ferns. Sally had done a double take the second she glimpsed him—could that guy be any more identical to her brother Fillmore? Turns out, it *was* Fillmore.

Sally was properly confused. She’d left him firmly planted in New York, preening around his office acting like a big shot man of affairs who lunched at fancy steak places. But sure, tycoons flit around, right? She waved him over, and he stood staring at every other table in the room for a full minute before he spotted her and shuffled over, looking as guilty as a dog caught eating a whole batch of cookies off the counter.

“Uh—Sally?” He fidgeted with the edge of the tablecloth so hard he was about to tear it. “What are you doing here? I thought you were still in Europe.”

“Got back a week ago, but I’ve been playing nurse to poor old Mr. Faucitt who got sick. I’m here to catch Gerald’s new play *The Primrose Way*, you know? It’s a smash hit, right? Opened last Monday.”

Fillmore coughed so hard he turned pink. “Er. About that. They shut every theatre in town. All because of that dumb Spanish influenza bug. No shows playing this week. Didn’t you see it in the papers?”

“I haven’t read a paper in two weeks. Oh, that’s rotten luck!”

“You can say that again. Everyone in the company is climbing the walls. It’s been total chaos, let me tell you.”

“Wait a second—what on earth are you doing tangled up with this show?”

Fillmore shifted in his seat like he was trying to melt into the booth cushions. “Oh, you know. Cracknell—my old college buddy? He suggested I come out and take a look at the production. I might put some money into it, y’know, get in on the ground floor of a good thing.”

Sally blinked. “I thought Cracknell was richer than the Rockefellers.”

“Sure he is! But guys like him love to cut their old pals in on a great deal.”

“Is it actually a great deal?”

“The play’s fine! But that Mabel Hobson girl? The star? Total nightmare. Can’t act for peanuts, and throws a fit over every little thing. The other day we had a whole screaming match over a stupid paper-knife prop…”

“Wait, a fight over a paper-knife? How does that even happen?”

Fillmore’s voice went all wobbly. “It got mislaid! Definitely not my fault, but you know how it is when an angry star is on the warpath—she yells at the first person she sees, right?”

Mr. Faucitt had said Elsa Doland was the good actress in the show, so Sally switched topics to that, and Fillmore lit up like someone flipped a switch. “Oh, Elsa’s fine, but you haven’t seen *Gladys Winch*. She plays the maid! Only in the first act, all her lines are stuff like ‘Did you ring, madam?’ but the way she says it? That girl’s a genius! Best character actress to hit the stage in 10 years. Mark my words, in no time her name’ll be up in electric lights on Broadway. Charm? She wrote the textbook on it. Personality? You could power a whole theatre on it—”

“Whoa, hold on,” Sally snickered. “You don’t have to sell me on her. Faucitt told me you two got engaged, and I want to know why you didn’t call me to ask for my official sisterly approval first.”

Fillmore turned so red he looked like a ripe tomato. “Uh. You already knew?”

“Yep. And I can’t even be mad, honestly. You’re way less of a pompous windbag than you used to be. If Gladys did that, she’s a miracle worker.”

“I’ll introduce you two later, promise,” he mumbled, checking his watch and scrambling out of his seat. “Got to run, I’m supposed to track down Bunbury the producer. He’s probably still holed up eating breakfast in his room.”

He scurried off, and Sally leaned back, no longer miffed about Gerald’s grumpy phone greeting. Of course he sounded off! His big career break was hanging by a thread, poor guy.

A few minutes later Gerald trundled into the dining room, and his vibe was exactly as depressing as his phone voice had been. He looked like he’d just watched his favourite sports team lose the championship, twice.

“Total garbage luck,” he moped, dropping into the booth and stabbing his grapefruit with a spoon like he personally blamed the fruit for the flu. “Only thing that could ever happen to me, right? Shutting all the theatres over a stupid cold scare? They let hundreds of people cram into department stores all day long, but going to a play is some sort of public health hazard? It’s all a fake, everyone’s just got a head cold and is acting like they’re dying.”

Sally shook her head. “It’s not a fake, poor Mr. Faucitt had it so bad I thought he was going to croak, that’s why I couldn’t come out earlier.”

Gerald didn’t even react to that news, or the fact that Sally had traveled cross-country to see him. He kept prodding his scrambled eggs like they’d personally offended him. “We’ve been hanging around bored out of our minds for days. The whole cast is falling apart. They rehearsed all their lines to perfection a week ago, and now they’re all going slack. This is my first big shot at making it, and it’s getting thrown right in the trash.”

Sally’s heart sank. She was trying to be understanding, he was obviously crushed—but self-pity was one of her least favourite traits in a man, and he was oozing it so thick you could spread it on toast. She hated that the old loyal little voice in her head was popping up, agreeing with everything Faucitt had said about Gerald being a little too self-centred.

“And that Hobson woman is already starting trouble,” he went on, glum as ever. “She never should have gotten the lead role, she’s terrible at it. Elsa Doland could play it ten times better. I wrote Elsa a quick little note the other day, and Hobson blew up so bad I spent an hour kissing up to her to stop her from quitting the show entirely.”

“Why not just let her quit?”

“Are you crazy?” Gerald snapped, all irritable. “You think Cracknell would keep funding the play if the star walked? He’d shut it down in two seconds flat, and I’d look like the biggest fool on Broadway. This is my one chance, I can’t blow it.”

Sally went quiet, suddenly feeling wretched. Maybe traveling abroad had addled her brain? She felt shut out, useless. Gerald wanted all her sympathy, but wouldn’t even bother to say he was glad she was there. Then, out of nowhere, she realized the real problem: she was still grubby from the train ride, her hair was sticking up in 17 different directions, and she hadn’t had a bath in 36 hours. That’s why everything felt off. She softened, putting a hand on his arm.

“I’m sorry, I’m being a total brute. I do get how stressful this is. But—you didn’t even say you were happy to see me.”

“Of course I’m happy to see you,” he mumbled.

“Then say it, you poor grumpy fish! And you didn’t even ask if I had fun in Europe.”

Gerald mumbled that he was glad she was there, asked about her trip, and the little awkward cloud between them cleared. He ranted on for another 15 minutes about how the flu was obviously a personal plot by Providence to ruin his acting career, before he glanced at his watch and jumped up. “Rehearsal’s starting, I better go. You coming along?”

“Just let me go unpack and fix my hair, I’ll meet you at the theatre,” Sally said, heading for the lift.

2

The rehearsal was already in full swing when she walked into the dark auditorium. Voices echoed around the empty room all thin and reedy, the way they only do when half the building is unoccupied. She sat at the back, let her eyes adjust to the gloom, and spotted Gerald up in the front row next to a bald guy with a fringe of bright orange hair that Sally immediately pegged as Mr. Bunbury, the notoriously high-strung producer. Half the cast was scattered around the seats, killing time waiting for their scenes, and up on stage Elsa Doland was running a scene with a guy in a bowler hat.

“Why, what do you mean, father?” Elsa said, going through her lines.

“Tiddly-omty-om,” the guy in the bowler hat mumbled, half-skipping a page. “Tiddly-omty-om… big long speech that ends with ‘you’ll find me in the library.’ And exit.”

Mr. Bunbury grabbed the walking stick leaning against his seat and hurled it across the room so hard it bounced off a chair. “FOR GOD’S SAKE!”

“Whoa, what’s the problem?” the bowler hat guy paused halfway off stage, completely confused.

“Speak the lines, Teddy!” Gerald yelled. “Don’t slur through half of them like you’re half-asleep!”

“You want me to say the whole entire long speech?” Teddy gaped at him, like he’d just been asked to recite the entire encyclopedia front to back.

“Yes!”

“Not the whole damn thing?”

“This is a rehearsal!” Bunbury snapped. “If we’re not going to do it properly, what’s the point of even being here?”

Teddy huffed, delivered the whole long speech like he was being forced to eat brussels sprouts, and shuffled off. The tension in the room was so thick you could cut it with a knife. Sally realized the theatre world was just a giant overgrown nursery full of fretful toddlers who’d skipped naptime. Weeks of waiting around in a strange hotel, rehearsing the same lines over and over when no one even knew if the show would ever open, had worn every last nerve thin. It wouldn’t take more than a tiny spark to set the whole place exploding.

Elsa moved to the door, rang the bell on the prop table, and sat down flipping through a magazine. A second later, a square, cheerful-looking girl with a faint permanent smirk at the corner of her mouth wandered on stage, and Bunbury bellowed so loud the chandeliers shook: “MISS WINCH!”

Gladys Winch stopped and peered out over the footlights, looking about as bothered as someone who’d been asked to pass the salt. “Hi there!” she said, amiable as ever.

“Miss Winch! I explicitly told you no chewing gum during rehearsals!”

“Yep, that’s right, you did,” Gladys agreed, chummily.

“Then why are you chewing gum right now?”

Gladys swished the gum around her mouth for a second before answering. “Bit of character business. I thought it up myself. Maids chew gum, right? Makes the role more real.”

Bunbury ran his palm through his bright orange hair so hard he nearly ripped the strands out. “Have you ever met a *proper parlour maid* who works in a fancy house like the one in this play? Do you seriously think she’d be allowed to march into the drawing room champing gum like a racehorse?”

Gladys thought for half a second, then lit up. “Oh! Great idea! We can get Gerald to write Elsa a line yelling at me for chewing gum, then I give her a snappy comeback, we build it out into a whole big five minute comedy bit, nothing but laughs. The crowd’ll go wild.”

Bunbury was so stunned he forgot how to talk for a whole ten seconds. Before he could recover, a dazzling vision in blue velvet and a hat so fancy Sally was instantly seething with envy, stormed out of the wings. It was Mabel Hobson, the star—drop dead gorgeous, spun gold hair, figure perfect, but when she opened her mouth she sounded exactly like a very indignant peacock.

“Miss Hobson, please,” Bunbury wheezed. “You’re interrupting rehearsal.”

“Interrupting? You bet your sweet life I’m interrupting rehearsal!” Mabel yelled. “And if you want to make some easy cash, bet every guy in the lobby that I’ll interrupt it a hundred more times before the day’s over, if anyone even *thinks* about writing up anyone else’s part in this show but mine! Write up other people’s scenes? Not while I’m still breathing!”

A pale, butter-haired guy who was obviously Mr. Cracknell scurried after her, patting her arm. “Now, sweetie—”

“Oh, can it, Reggie!” Mabel snapped. Cracknell immediately zipped his mouth shut, buried his face in his high collar like a tortoise hiding in its shell, and started gnawing on the knob of his walking stick.

“I AM THE STAR!” Mabel went on, vehement as ever. “If so much as a single extra syllable goes into anyone else’s part, I walk right out of this theatre so fast you won’t even see me leave. You’ll blink and I’ll be gone.”

“Fine! Fine!” Bunbury waved his hands in surrender. “No one’s getting their parts written up, okay? Everyone calm down.”

“Like I said, it was just a random little thought for the good of the show,” Gladys shrugged, totally unbothered.

Mabel let Cracknell pat her back and lead her off to the wings, still grumbling about how no one was going to pull a fast one on her. The rehearsal stumbled back on, but Sally’s heart sank more and more the longer she watched. Mabel Hobson was so beautiful she’d have lit up any scene that let her just stand there looking pretty in fancy dresses—but as the lead in a serious dramatic play? She couldn’t act her way out of a paper bag. Sally could already picture the first night audience booing her off the stage, just like the story Faucitt had told her about the flop actress that got pelted with peanuts in Wigan.

Ten minutes later, the next explosion hit. Mabel was mid-dramatic scene, supposed to be the neglected wife heartbroken that her husband loved his desk more than her, when she suddenly stopped dead and screamed. “OH FOR HEAVEN’S SAKE! WHAT NOW?”

“On that line you’re supposed to pick up the paper-knife on the desk and fidget with it negligently!” Bunbury yelled. “We went over this at three different rehearsals!”

“How the heck am I supposed to toy negligently with a paper-knife that’s *not even on the desk*?” Mabel squawked. “It’s not there! Don’t yell at me, yell at the idiot who’s supposed to keep track of the props!”

Bunbury threw his head back and howled like a bloodhound calling for its quarry. A second later, a stout, hunched, shrinking little man shambled out from the wings, clutching a crumpled play script, looking like he was about to be marched straight to the guillotine.

It was Fillmore.

3

Poor Fillmore! He stood dead centre of the stage, with Bunbury’s wrath zapping all around his head like lightning bolts, and Sally’s heart flooded with sisterly pity. She never usually pitied Fillmore—his old pompous, big-shot act had been so annoying that watching him scrape by on cheap coffee and buckwheat cakes for a few years had been a positive joy, as far as she was concerned. But this? This was full-on tragedy.

The “big shot investor” lie he’d told her over breakfast was just a dumb bluff to save face. Her brother wasn’t a wealthy playboy cutting his old pal in on a Broadway deal. He was the lowest man on the theatrical totem pole, the gofer whose whole job was making sure stupid paper-knives didn’t go missing, and if he messed up he got screamed at by the star and the producer both. He’d lied about being a financier because he was too embarrassed to admit he’d blown all his money on the show, and now he was stuck working 12 hour days for nothing just to make sure he didn’t lose his entire investment.

Sally sighed, shaking her head. Oh Fillmore. You poor, silly idiot. She already knew she was going to have to step in to get him out of this mess.

If there’s one unifying quirk shared by actors, actresses, and unsupervised seven-year-olds, it’s that they crumple completely at the smallest disaster then bounce right back to delirious joy the second good luck swings their way. Sally could barely wrap her head around the fact that all it took to make every single person in the *Primrose Way* troupe over the moon was her lending her brother Fillmore a measly twenty thousand bucks. If the actual Millennium had rolled into town, the whole company couldn’t have been more chuffed with themselves. The fog of gloom that had hung over them through a whole week of sitting around twiddling their thumbs waiting for the show to fold fell off them faster than a chorus girl’s discarded dance shoe.
The news that the former assistant stage manager — the guy who’d spent his days fetching cold coffee for the cast and ducking flying scripts from temperamental leading ladies — had rocketed up to the fancy new title of show proprietor tickled their theatrical bones perfectly. Most of them had starred in at least three old melodramas where the exact same rags-to-riches beat happened to the persecuted heroine right around the 11pm mark of the final act, so the whole situation felt dramatically foolproof. What’s more, now that the sour, joyless Miss Hobson was gone for good, everyone suddenly agreed she’d been a bigger killjoy than a rainstorm on a beach picnic.
Optimism was contagious, and absurdly cheerful rumors zipped around the cast nonstop. Teddy, the guy who never took his bowler hat off even backstage, had straight from his hotel’s lift boy that the theatre ban would be lifted no later than Tuesday. The actor who played the butler swore he got top-secret intel from the girl working the cigar stand at the Pontchatrain Hotel that Toledo and Cleveland were both opening their theatre doors the very next day. The whole lot of them were fully convinced the sun was blowing through the clouds at last, and Fate could just give up already on its stupid, impossible mission to keep hardworking showfolk down.
Fillmore was fully his ebullient old self again. Everyone has their own silly little way of celebrating a win, and Fillmore’s method involved splurging on a fancy new embroidered waistcoat, a hundred premium half-dollar cigars, and throwing a huge diva-level fuss when his lunch spot informed him they’d run out of his favourite pickled walnuts. It might have been a trick of the light, but Sally swore he packed on at least six whole pounds the first day of his new good fortune regime. Back when he was just a lowly serf in charge of wrangling paper knives and fake teacups for props, he’d been practically svelte for him. Now that he was the big boss, he’d bloomed out into soft, content, billowy curves, and when he loitered on the sidewalk outside the theatre gawking at the new posters blaring *FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS*, passing pedestrians had to detour around him like he was a newly installed public fountain.
It felt deeply unfair that Sally — the fairy godmother who’d made all this joy possible — couldn’t shake a faint, vague unhappiness, and she puzzled over it for days. The little shadow lingering at the back of her mind refused to step out into the light and name itself, no matter how hard she tried to pin it down. It wasn’t until she was out for a drive in a rented car with Gerald one afternoon on Belle Isle that it all clicked.
Ever since Miss Hobson skipped town, Gerald had been his absolute best self, all warm charm and no of the brooding moodiness that had made him such a drag a few weeks prior. Like Fillmore, he positively bloomed in the sunshine of unlooked-for prosperity. And yet — as the car rolled smoothly past the sun-dappled woods and river-side fields, Sally could feel something small and sharp jarring under all his cheer.
Gerald was chatting a mile a minute, gushing about all the huge plans he had for the future. “Once this show catches on — and it’s definitely going to catch on — I’m gonna show every last snob who ever laughed at me! One single hit, that’s all I need, and then you watch me go! I never got a fair shot before, but —”
His voice rambled on, but Sally had stopped listening. This was the time of year when the soft warm glow of afternoon burned off fast, leaving a sharp evening chill right behind it. The sun had dipped behind the treeline, and a cold wind was blowing straight up off the river. All at once, it felt like that wind had blown the fog clean out of her head, and she finally saw what had been niggling at her for days.
For a second the truth stood bare, no hiding spots left, and the whole world suddenly felt a little forlorn. She’d just stumbled on the huge, fundamental difference between how men and women looked at life. Success! Men worshipped the thing like it was a holy relic, and they barely had a scrap of themselves left over for anything else. The irony hit her that this very obsession was the core theme of Gerald’s play, the one she’d just saved from being shut down for good.
How many of the men she knew viewed life as anything other than a mad race they had to sprint full-tilt to win, no matter what small, nice things they trampled past on the side of the road in their hurry? Fillmore, Gerald, every last one of them. Sure, there might be a woman tucked somewhere in the back of their life, but she always came second — an afterthought, something to fit into their spare time after all the important success work was done.
For her, Gerald was everything. His big flashy success would never be more than a side note. She would have been perfectly happy living in a tiny cramped apartment eating tinned beans with him, no hit shows, no fancy producers lining up to beg for his scripts. But she wasn’t enough for him, not on her own. A stupid, sharp little spasm of useless jealousy shook her, and she shivered.
“Cold?” Gerald said, barely pausing his rant. “I’ll tell the driver to turn back. Honestly, there’s no reason this show doesn’t run a full year in New York, everyone’s saying it’s brilliant. Once it blows up, every producer in town’s gonna be chasing me, I —”
Sally stared out at the bleakening world outside the car window. The sky had turned a flat, heavy lead grey, and the wind off the river blew sharp and dismal, cutting right through her coat.

When Sally boarded the Saturday train out of Detroit with her brother Fillmore, bound for New York so he could bag fancy Broadway offices and strut around like he owned half the theatre district, her mood had done a full 180. She’d spent the last couple days kicking herself for being a silly, over-sensitive ninny—obviously guys chased success, that’s their whole job! She was all in for Gerald’s big break, right? No point moping that he was working hard to make them both rich, duh.

The Detroit crowds had been nothing but sunshine, too. The play *The Primrose Way* opened Tuesday just like Teddy promised, and the whole city, starved for fun after their enforced lull, turned up screaming for more. The critics didn’t just agree—they practically high-fived each other, calling Gerald the next big playwright, Elsa Doland the next A-list star, and even tossing Fillmore a quick line as a rising hotshot manager. The only blemish on the whole perfect week? The local critic had spelled Gladys Winch’s name *Wunch* and only mentioned her in a throwaway line listing “other cast members.” Fillmore had practically sputtered into his coffee the next morning. “One of the finest character actresses on the planet, and they misspell her name like she’s a side character in a cheap western!”

But Fillmore was too naturally buoyant to mope for long. Ticket sales were through the roof, Elsa got better every single night, their old disgruntled producer Mr. Cracknell was blowing up their telegram line begging to buy the play back now that his investor Miss Hobson had cooled off, and the biggest boss of all—Ike Schumann, who owned half the theatres in New York and was in town checking on his musical show—had dropped by one night and given *The Primrose Way* the official mogul seal of approval.
Sitting across from Sally on the train, Fillmore was practically glowing with self-importance.

“Absolutely,” Sally said, breaking a long quiet.
Fillmore jolted out of his daydream. “Eh?”
“I said absolutely. You 100% owe it to your new big-shot status.”
“Owe what?”
“Buying that fur coat you’ve been mooning over. Duh.”
Fillmore blushed bright red. “Don’t be an idiot.” He absolutely had been daydreaming about that fur coat. What, a guy can’t keep warm in New York winter?
“With a giant astrakhan collar,” Sally pushed, grinning.
“For your information,” Fillmore huffed, like this petty coat talk was beneath his elevated station, “I was not thinking about fur coats. I was thinking about what Ike Schumann said to me five minutes ago. He’s on this train, you know. I just ran into him in the dining car.”
“Ooh, you’re on first-name terms with the great Ike Schumann?”
“Everyone calls him Ike!” Fillmore squawked, defensive.
“Mmm. He wears a fur coat.”
“Ugh, will you stop harping on the damn coat? What’s wrong with me having a fur coat anyway?”
“Fill! I never said you couldn’t! I’m the president of your official Fur Coat Fan Club. You need giant fuzzy cuffs, too. Then you’ll roll down Fifth Avenue in your fancy new car, and I’ll point at you and yell to everyone around me ‘That’s my brother!’ and they’ll say ‘No way, that’s the legendary Fillmore Nicholas!’ and I’ll say ‘I know, right? I was there when he bought that fur coat.’”
“Stop talking about the coat!”
“‘And it’s not even the coat that’s impressive,’ I’ll tell them, ‘Tucked inside all that fur, behind that giant dollar cigar, is a man we all should be proud of—’”
Fillmore checked his watch, cold. “I have to go meet Ike Schumann right now. He wants to talk about the show. He’s suggesting we run it in Chicago first before we open on Broadway.”
Sally went pale for half a second, thinking that’d mean she’d be separated from Gerald even longer, then relaxed—obviously Gerald wouldn’t have to stick around the Chicago tour after the first few days. “Oh, that’s… an interesting thought?”
“Some people say you should build a New York reputation first, but a Chicago hit would make New York eat it up. Anyway,” Fillmore said, furrowing his brows like he was solving global politics, “I’ll have to think on it. Deeply.”
“Nah, you’re doing the brooding face wrong,” Sally informed him. “Lips compressed, forefinger pressed to your temple like you haven’t slept in three days. You’ve got so much to learn, kiddo.”
“Ugh, quit teasing!”
“Fillmore, if you knew how much it hurts me to roast my only brother, you’d cry. But it’s for your own good. Now run along—poor Ike’s probably chewing his nails waiting for you. I bet ten minutes ago he turned to the porter and went ‘Do you think Fillmore Nicholas will ever show up?’ and the porter said ‘Of course he will, sir, he’s too great to forget a meeting’ but Ike was all ‘But what if he got distracted picking out fur coats?’”
Fillmore didn’t even dignify that with a reply, just marched down the aisle with his back ramrod straight, making it very clear he was suffering extreme sisterly harassment. Sally laughed, grabbed a magazine, and plowed through a ridiculous adventure story that dragged on through 47 pages of ads, only to realize it was part one of two. That’s when a voice spoke right across from her.
“Hello, Miss Nicholas.”

In plopped Bruce Carmyle, of all the people on the entire planet, into the empty seat that used to hold Fillmore, with that stuffy, polite, overly formal vibe he never lost.
Sally nearly jumped out of her skin. Sure, people travel, but she’d 100% left this guy in Europe after that awkward Paris train ride, when she’d been unreasonably rude to him and then forgot he existed entirely. It felt like someone had hit a rewind button on a chapter of her life she’d fully closed the book on.
“Mr. Carmyle! What on earth are you doing in America?”
“Came over for a holiday. My doctor said I was run down, and everyone should visit America at least once. It’s part of a proper education.”
“Cool! What do you think of our great nation?” Sally said, scrambling to recover her composure. The guy was weirdly intimidating, she’d always thought, with that hard, unreadable face.
“Just spent a week in Chicago. Wonderful city. I heard you were in Detroit, by the way.”
Sally blinked. “You heard I was in Detroit? How?!”
“I… stopped by your New York boarding house and asked for your whereabouts,” Carmyle mumbled, like he was slightly embarrassed to admit that.
“But how did you know where I was staying?”
“My cousin Lancelot told me.”
Oh, right—Ginger. Sally suddenly had that exact feeling a detective novel protagonist gets when he slowly realizes someone’s been following him for weeks. This guy didn’t just accidentally run into her on the train. He’d tracked her down. It was a very weird, over-the-top compliment, and Sally hated it. She didn’t care about Carmyle at all, and the idea that he’d crossed an entire ocean just to hang around her was deeply unsettling. So she latched hard onto the Ginger mention to steer the conversation away from her.
“How’s Ginger—Mr. Kemp? You said he was your cousin, right?”
Carmyle’s face darkened immediately. “We haven’t heard a single word from him since I saw you last. He’s disappeared.”
“Disappeared? What do you mean?!”
“He stormed off after insulting his Uncle Donald, the one guy who held his entire future in his hands. Grossly insulted him. The whole family’s washed their hands of him. We think he’s lost his mind, honestly.”
Sally’s temper flared, but she bit it back. She was weirdly Team Ginger for no real reason, but if she wanted to hear the rest of the story, she had to play nice. “What did he do, exactly?”
“Uncle Donald won’t talk about the details. He invited Lancelot to dinner to map out his whole cushy future, and the little fool defied him. Full-on yelled at him. Won a bunch of money gambling at Roville, apparently, and it went to his head. We haven’t heard a peep from him since that dinner.”

Right then, Fillmore loomed over the aisle, staring at Carmyle like he had no idea why some random guy was sitting in his seat chatting up his sister.
“Hey Fill, this is Mr. Carmyle, we met in Europe. Carmyle, my brother Fillmore Nicholas,” Sally said, making the introductions. Fillmore, who judged everyone by how fancy they looked, immediately approved of Carmyle.
“Wild running into you two like this!” he said, friendly. The porter was hovering nearby, getting ready to make up the sleeping berths for the night.
“Y’all should head to the smoking car and chat,” Sally said. “I’m beat, gonna turn in early.” She wanted to be alone, to think.
The two men trundled off, and Sally sat down in an empty corner seat, her brain buzzing. Ginger actually did it! He’d sassed the stuffy uncle! She was so proud she could barely stand it—she was the one who’d told him to stop being a doormat, after all! She’d unleashed the guy! Then the giddy feeling deflated, and the guilt hit. Wait a second. Did she just ruin Ginger’s entire life? What if he was broke, wandering the streets of London with no money, no family, no future? She tossed and turned all night on the bumpy train, imagining him sleeping on the Thames embankment, staring at the dark water…

Daybreak didn’t make the guilt go away. She got off at Grand Central Station feeling 10 times worse, turned down Carmyle’s offer to drive her back to the boarding house, and trundled the few blocks on foot, wallowing in the conviction that she was a one-woman menace to society, who’d deliberately wrecked a poor redheaded guy’s entire career prospects on a stupid impulse.
Mrs. Meecher, the boarding house landlady, greeted her at the door gushing, the family dog Toto yapping at her heels. The newspaper *Variety* had run a whole write-up about their play’s Detroit success, and the whole boarding house was thrilled—she’d even given Toto a chicken dinner and a blue ribbon to celebrate. Sally asked after her old sick roommate Mr. Faucitt.
“Oh, he’s gone!” Mrs. Meecher said cheerfully.
Sally went white, grabbing the banister. “Gone?!”
“To England! His brother died, left him a whole heap of money, so he hopped the very next boat to go sort out the estate. He’s totally healthy now, that little bout of flu he had actually perked him right up! Said to give you his love, that he’d write soon.”

Sally trundled up to her room, cheered up for all of two minutes thinking about nice old Mr. Faucitt being rich now, before the guilt came roaring back. She threw herself on the bed, too tired to sleep, listening to Mrs. Meecher prowl around the house and Toto yap. Then she froze. Sticking out from under her bed was a very masculine leather shoe, and six inches of crumpled grey trouser leg.
Sally jumped to her feet, not even scared. “What the hell are you doing under my bed?”
A muffled sneeze echoed, and the intruder started crawling out. Shoe first, then legs, then a dusty suit, then a head of bright, fire-engine red hair that could only belong to one person.
Ginger Kemp blinked up at her from all fours on her bedroom carpet.
“Hey, Sally!” he said. “Fancy seeing you here.”

Sally didn’t fully grasp how many overactive-imagination doomsday scenarios she’d cooked up for Ginger until he was actually standing right in front of her: hair rumpled like a sparrow had built a nest in it, a great sooty smut parked on the tip of his nose like he’d tried to nose-dive down a factory chimney. For hours she’d been picturing him bobbing along the Thames as a very cold, underappreciated corpse, so the shock of seeing him alive and mostly unharmed got shunted straight aside by a wave of relief so huge she collapsed into a nearby chair cackling so loud it sounded like a feral parrot had escaped her throat.

Ginger, ever the concerned gentleman, hovered looking alarmed. “I say, steady on! Nasty shock, finding some blighter camped out under your bed, isn’t it? Didn’t mean to give you hysterics, what?”

Sally wiped the tears off her cheeks, gurgling. “Oh, don’t be daft, I’m just so glad to see you!”

Ginger preened, gratified, then remembered he was supposed to be apologizing. “Awfully sorry for barging in, by the way. Spot was empty, I thought – never in a million years dreamed it was your room. Had a great nap on the floor, though, ten out of ten for nap quality, zero out of ten for stray dust bunnies that bit my ankle.”

“Speaking of things that bit you,” Sally said, grinning, “that little smut on your nose? It’s doing a great job as a novelty beauty mark, if that’s the vibe you’re going for. If not, though…”

Ginger yelped. “My aunt! It’s there? Let me at the mirror, stat.” He galloped over to the dressing table, scrubbing at his nose with a crumpled handkerchief like he was trying to erase a stain off a prize rug. “Ugh, you’re dead right. Let me tell you, take it from me: camping out under beds is massively overrated. Nothing good comes of it.”

“Right,” Sally said, leaning back. “Which brings me to the obvious question. What on earth were you doing hiding under my bed, anyway? Playing advanced hide and seek that leaves you covered in grime?”

“Old Meecher – the landlady – was hunting me down,” Ginger said, passing a dusty hand over his forehead and promptly leaving a new brown smudge right where his eyebrow was.

Sally winced. “You really shouldn’t encourage her to chase you for childish games, it’ll spoil her. Also, your hands are so dirty you just painted your own forehead. Go wash up, there’s a good chap. Use my soap, Americans are famous for that whole hospitality thing, remember?”

Ginger splashed around at the basin like a sea lion who’d just found a kiddie pool, five minutes later emerging looking almost human again, albeit still slightly ruffled. “Okay, spill. Why were you hiding from Mrs. Meecher?”

Ginger’s face went all hangdog, like a pup that had just chewed up the family heirloom. “That woman’s terrifying, okay? Word came down she was on the prowl, and I knew exactly what she wanted. She was gonna make me walk her dog. Toto.”

“Toto?”

“Toto,” Ginger repeated, with the righteous indignation of a man who has survived a near-miss with a rabid opossum. “Look, I love dogs. Normally. But that thing? It’s a walking woolly dust mop with a yapping problem that looks like someone found a dead rat, fluffed it up, and sewed little legs on it. I can’t be seen parading that thing down a public street! My reputation wouldn’t survive it.”

“Then why not just say no?” Sally asked.

“Ah, there’s the rub,” Ginger said, wincing. “I’m twelve bucks behind on the rent. Hardly easy to stand your ground with the landlady who holds your lease hostage for a rat-dog walk when you owe her cash, right?”

Sally blinked. “Wait, you can’t have been here more than a week! Last time I saw you in Roville you were practically a millionaire, rolling in casino winnings.”

Ginger rubbed the back of his neck sheepishly. “Uh, well. I went back to the tables that last night and lost most of it. Then hopped a boat to America for the whole ‘land of opportunity’ vibe, and somehow the rest of the cash just vanished. Got a job waiting tables on Second Avenue, but I broke so many plates the boss docked almost all my pay last week. I’m making a total hash of every job I apply for, everyone keeps saying they’re perfectly fine running their businesses without my help, the rude sods.”

Sally snapped her fingers. “Oh! I’ve got the perfect solution. My brother Fillmore. He’s a theatrical manager, young, building his business, he definitely needs a right-hand man. We’ll go get him to hire you right now.”

“Wait, hold on,” Ginger said, looking nervous. “I’ve never been a right-hand man before. What if I make a hash of that too? What do right-hand men even do, polish the right hand?”

“Nonsense, you’ll pick it up in ten minutes,” Sally said, shooing him toward the door. “Come on, let’s go. He’s staying at the Astor.”

Ginger took one step toward the door, then froze mid-stride, one foot hanging in the air like he’d been turned to stone. A high, shrill yapping echoed from the hallway outside. He made a long, longing eye contact with the space under the nearest couch, ready to dive for cover.

“Stop being a coward,” Sally said, severely. “I’ll sort it. You think I’m letting that woolly rat ruin your new career before it even starts?”

She herded him out into the hall, right past Toto who was stalking a very imaginary phantom rat, and straight to Mrs. Meecher who was hovering in the wings waiting to pounce. “Oh, Mrs. Meecher! Fancy running into my old friend Mr. Kemp here, we met in France! We’re in such a huge hurry to go meet my super busy brother, he’s expecting us for lunch right this second. Such a gorgeous day for a walk with Toto, isn’t it? You two will have the best time!”

Before Mrs. Meecher could protest, Sally had steered Ginger down the stairs and out the front door. The landlady, who’d never quite managed to boss Sally around even back when she was a broke boarder, was left standing there too flustered to argue.

Ginger drew a huge, gasping breath once they hit the sidewalk, staring at Sally like she’d just parted the Red Sea for him. “You’re a blooming wizard, you know that?”

“Flattery accepted,” Sally said, grinning. “First we’ll take a little walk, then we’ll go corner Fillmore at the Astor. Oh, by the way – I ran into your cousin Donald Carmyle on the train coming from Detroit. He’s in America, did you know that?”

Ginger winced. “Uh, yeah. We had a bit of a falling out, lost touch with the whole family. Bloke named Bruce said he was gonna send you some books, by the way – I accidentally gave him your address when we talked in Piccadilly. Did they ever turn up?”

Sally blinked. “No. Not a single book.” She made a mental note, vaguely unsettled – no girl likes the idea of a bloke she’s not fussed about crossing three thousand miles to track her down over a fake book delivery. “Tell me about that fight with your uncle, then! Carmyle said you insulted him over dinner.”

Ginger turned bright red. “Ah, well. He took me out for dinner, and my old uncle’s got a walrus moustache, right? Thick as a brillo pad. When the mulligatawny soup came, he kept inhaling great globs of it through the whiskers. I made a very polite little suggestion that he maybe slow down a bit, and he blew a fuse. By the time the steak showed up we were yelling at each other, and I told him I was done putting up with the family’s nonsense, and stormed off. Next thing I knew I was on a boat to New York. Here I am.”

Sally’s heart swelled, resolve solid. There was no way she was letting this hapless, good-hearted idiot waste his life breaking plates in a diner. “Right, no more dallying. We’re going to the Astor right now. Ginger – hail that cab, will you? I’m making you Fillmore’s right-hand man, and I’m not taking no for an answer. I regard you as a wayward grandson, and I’m not letting my grandson wind up Toto’s full-time dog walker.”

For weeks after Sally ran back into Ginger Kemp’s orbit, it felt like the universe had rolled out a golden welcome mat for her, no lumps, no crumbs, no surprise puddles to step in. Every corner of her tiny little personal kingdom was running so smooth even the grumpiest professional pessimist could have wandered through for hours hunting for something to complain about and come up empty-handed.

Okay, fine, Gerald was still a thousand miles off in Chicago, stuck running damage control on a bombed-out local comedy the poor guy had been roped into rewriting, plus overseeing the opening of *The Primrose Way*. Sally had been hoping he’d be back in New York in a couple days, but she refused to mope. Life was way too good overall to waste time on that one small wrinkle. Fillmore’s new show was a smash, Ginger wasn’t drinking himself into a ditch anymore, she’d signed a lease on a perfect little apartment, her new hat didn’t make her look like a confused watermelon wearing feathers, and *The Primrose Way* was eating Chicago alive. According to Fillmore’s dispatches, the entire city had dumped every other priority to fight over tickets. City council meetings got canceled because every alderman was camped outside the box office. Husbands were sneaking out of their anniversary dinners to stand in the rush line. The production, Fillmore swore, was the most seismic event Chicago had experienced since the Great Fire.

Of all the little wins piling up, the best one by far was that Ginger’s whole dead-end future problem was solved. He’d joined the staff of the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director: Fillmore Nicholas) — Fillmore had tried to cram 17 more descriptive words onto the brass plate out front before the engraver threatened to throw him out a window. Most days you could find Ginger parked at the desk outside Fillmore’s private office, his official duties 90% reading the evening papers and 10% physically blocking hordes of desperate producers from storming in to pitch bad scripts to the semi-regal, scheme-obsessed man perched behind the inner office desk.

Ginger was never 100% sure what his job title even was, half the time he felt like he was the guy steering the whole enterprise, half the time he felt like a glorified (very glorified, on a good day) office boy. But the $50 he got every Friday cashed out real, and he never stopped thanking Sally for pulling the strings to get him the gig. They were meeting constantly lately, too, because Ginger had volunteered to help her furnish the new apartment, and he swore hauling couches around kept him in tip-top shape.

“— and what I’m trying to say is,” Ginger panted, pausing mid-heave with a massive overstuffed armchair, “if I didn’t break my back helping you out after you got me that job, I’d be the lowest rotter walking the face of the earth.”
“Ginger, cease and desist immediately,” Sally said.
“But honestly—”
“If you don’t quit the thank-you speech, I’m making you drag that chair all the way to the next apartment down the hall.”
Ginger rubbed his blistered palms and got a new grip on the chair leg. “Anything you say. You want me to knock on their door and ask if they need a new armchair?”
“Silly, of course not. The only other rooms I have are the bedroom, bathroom and kitchen. What would I do with a giant armchair in there, eat toast over the sink while sitting in it? Wait — actually, you’re right, the first spot we put it was better.”
“Back it goes, then?”

Sally frowned, tapping her chin. Setting up house was turning out to be the most mentally taxing project she’d taken on all year.
“No,” she decided. “By the window is better. Sorry, I’m making you haul this all over the place, aren’t I?”
“Trouble?!” Ginger staggered across the room, sweat dripping off his freckled forehead. “C’mon, you got me a steady job where no one yells at me for showing up late three times a week, the way I see it—”
“Right ho… still, you did, y’know.”

Sally plopped down in the newly placed armchair, feeling vicariously tired just from watching Ginger work. She glanced around the room proudly — pictures hung straight, carpet laid smooth, all the bits and bobs felt like they belonged. For the first time since she’d been stuck in crummy boarding houses eating burnt toast for dinner, she had that warm, quiet at-home feeling where she could lock the front door and the whole world outside couldn’t bother her. The apartment was tiny, but it was a perfect little haven. Wait… hold on. Something was missing.
“Hey!” she said. “Where’d my photo go? I swear I put that fancy touched-up portrait of me on the mantel yesterday.”

Ginger’s face went bright, beet-red all of a sudden. He leaned in to squint at the mantel like he was inspecting for hidden fingerprints.
“Nope. No photo there.”
“I know there isn’t! But I could’ve sworn I set it out. It’s the greatest thing ever, the editors in the dark room smoothed out all my wonky bits so I look like some sort of glamorous movie star. Worth every penny.”
“I never got a fancy nice photo taken of me,” Ginger said, all solemn and regretful.
“Cheer up, maybe next year.”
“Oh I don’t mind, I just thought I’d mention—”
“Ginger,” Sally said, cutting in, “sorry to interrupt this very compelling life story, but this chair is still wrong. It needs to go back to the very first spot we had it. Can you be a very good pack mule just one more time? After that I’ll make you tea. Assuming I remembered to buy tea. And milk. And cups.”
“Cups are fine,” Ginger said. “I’d know, I smashed two of them the other day. I’ll run around the corner for milk right now.”
“Please do. All this hard work hauling furniture has drained me of every last calorie.”

Over the tea mugs, Sally leaned in curious. “One thing I can’t figure out about your new job — which I was, as you were about to point out, a total hero to get you — is why you have so much free time. How are you able to skip out on Fillmore’s payroll all day to rearrange my furniture?”
“Oh, I can usually sneak away easy,” Ginger said, stirring his tea.
“Shouldn’t you be at your post doing… whatever it is you do, exactly?”
Ginger thought for a second. “Well, I mess around, y’know. I kick all the annoying producers out when they try to barge in to see Fillmore, write down their names so we can throw their scripts away later… all that sort of thing.”
“Does Fillmore ever ask you for your big important opinion on stuff?”
“He makes me read half the terrible new plays people send in, most of them are utter garbage. Sometimes he sends me to vaudeville houses at night.”
“As a treat?”
“Nah, to check out acts and write a report on ‘em. He says he might want to steal the good bits for his new big revue.”
“Wait, what revue?” Sally’s eyes went wide. She knew her brother. He was the kind of guy who’d win a dollar on a scratch ticket the next day decide he was gonna build the Empire State Building. “That sounds way too big. He just had his first small hit, he should be playing it safe, not dumping a fortune on a giant lavish revue.”
“Ah, he’s an ambitious bloke, your brother. Total Napoleon of the theatre scene,” Ginger said.
“I’m marching over to talk to him right now,” Sally decided, annoyed. Everything had been so perfectly stress-free, and now Fillmore was over there plotting to blow all their good luck on a wild scheme.

---
Sally’s anxiety got way worse a couple days later when a telegram from Miss Winch in Chicago showed up at her apartment, reading: *Have you been feeding Fillmore meat?*
Sally had zero clue what that was supposed to mean, but the vibe of the thing was so deeply sinister she grabbed her coat and hightailed it over to Fillmore’s theatre headquarters immediately.

Ginger was nowhere to be seen at his usual desk, replaced by a pimply 17-year-old temp who went from his stiff official guard post to starstruck the second he heard Sally’s name, and waved her straight back to the inner office. Fillmore was propped with his feet all over a messy desk, flipping through sparkly costume designs, looking very preoccupied with very important showbiz thoughts. The double chin that had been just a faint little hint a few weeks back was now out in full unapologetic force, and his brand new waistcoat was unbuttoned, gaping open like he’d just polished off three slices of apple pie.
“Ah, Sally!” he said, in that faraway, busy voice of people who think they’re a titan of industry. “Great to see you, but I’m very swamped. Hundred things to do today.”
“Make it a hundred and one. Fill, what’s all this about a giant new revue?”

Fillmore looked exactly like a small boy caught red-handed with his hand in the jam jar. He’d been dreading Sally finding out about the revue, he’d been hoping to get all the contracts signed before she heard a whisper so she couldn’t yell at him and spoil all his fun.
“Oh, the revue! That’s just—”
“Don’t ‘oh the revue’ me. It’s a crazy idea.”
“Really, these are complicated business matters, you don’t—”
“I don’t want to run your company for you, but my money makes me a silent partner, right? I get to yell very loud when I see you about to dump it all down a drain.”
Fillmore puffed up all lofty, looking relieved to have an excuse to sound authoritative. “Your money is 100% tied up exclusively in *The Primrose Way*, which is a massive smash. You don’t have a single thing to worry about with my new projects.”
“I’m not worried about the money! I’m worried about you. You just started as a manager, you can’t afford to blow a fortune on a revue that could flop immediately.”
“My dear girl, women never understand finance. A man in my position can always find investors to back a good new show.”
“Wait, you actually found someone stupid enough to give you money for this?” Sally said, shocked.
“Certainly. Your friend Mr. Carmyle, Bruce, has signed on to invest in several of my upcoming productions.”
Sally’s jaw dropped. Bruce Carmyle. The guy she’d been dodging for months, the rich guy who kept popping up everywhere she went like a unkillable weed. Now he was invested in her brother’s business? She couldn’t escape him if she tried.

Before she could say anything more, the doors to the outer office blew open. The temp guard had been trying and failing to block someone from storming in, and in marched Miss Winch, all fire, chewing gum so aggressively her jaw was working overtime.
“Fillmore, you poor dummy,” she said, no beating around the bush. “Stop picking at your hair and listen to me. You’re completely dippy.”

Last time Sally had met Fillmore’s fiancée, she’d seemed as unflappable as a block of marble, nothing could ruffle her. To see her all charged up and furious meant something very bad had gone down.
“Ah, Gladys!” Fillmore stood up, half-indignant, half-pleased.
“Yes, here I am!” Miss Winch dropped into a swivel chair, heaving a huge sigh. “Fillmore, darling, you’re the sweetest man alive and I love you more than my mother’s apple pie, but right now you’re one step away from checking into Bloomingdale and getting the royal suite reserved all for you.”
“Dear girl, that’s hardly—”
“Guess what he’s planning to do!” Miss Winch turned to Sally.
“I was just telling him his giant new revue idea is totally absurd,” Sally said, thrilled to have an ally.
“Revue?” Miss Winch froze mid-chew, threw her hands up. “I’m gonna have to swallow this gum now. My head’s spinning too fast to chew. He’s doing a revue *too*? On top of his other insane plan?”

Fillmore was fidgeting so much he was buttoning and unbuttoning his waistcoat so fast his fingers were blurring.
“What other plan?” Sally said.
“This poor, addled man,” Miss Winch said, “is trying to make *me* a leading star. Me! Star of his brand new Broadway show.”

Fillmore waved a hand like he was protesting very nobly. “I’ve seen a lot of acting, Gladys, I think you’d make a fantastic character lead, you have natural talent—”
“Bullshit!” Miss Winch said, grinning and leaning over to give him a quick, loud kiss on the cheek. “You’re a sweetheart for saying that, but wake up from your daydream for two seconds. I can do one thing on a stage really well: walk on with a tray, say ‘Yes, madam’ perfectly, and walk off without spilling a drop of champagne. That’s it! Ellen Terry couldn’t touch my tray-carrying skills, Sarah Bernhardt would be jealous of my ‘Yes, madam’ delivery. I can’t carry a whole star role, I don’t want to, and if you tried to make me do it every critic in New York would line up to tear me to shreds and waste all that money you saved up for our little house with the stationary wash tubs. The only thing I’m actually a genius at is cooking. I can make a chicken casserole so good you’d leave your entire family to eat it, my English pork pies would make you cry. Acting? Not a chance.”

Fillmore sank back in his chair, looking like Napoleon after Waterloo. His big grand plan to turn his fiancée into a star had been his favorite project in months, and it had just crumbled right in front of him.
“Fine,” he said, glum. “If that’s how you feel, there’s nothing to say.”
“Great! Now we’re talking about your revue. It’s off.”

Fillmore shot to his feet, slamming his fist on the desk hard enough to make all the costume designs jump. A man can only take so much nonsense.
“It is NOT off! For heaven’s sake, this is too much! I will not be hounded and interfered with in my own office! I am a man of broad vision! I make grand plans, I shape big schemes, and then a whole horde of girls burst in and yell at me while I’m trying to do important business! I won’t stand for it! Advice is fine, but interference? Absolutely not! Mark my words!”

He stormed out the door, slammed it behind him, you could hear his angry footsteps all the way down the hallway. Sally stared at the empty doorway, stunned. She’d never seen Fillmore throw a tantrum before.

Miss Winch pulled her stick of chewing gum out of her pocket, unwrapped it, and popped it in her mouth.
“Isn’t he the cutest thing?” she said, smiling. “He’ll be back in 20 minutes with a box of that horrible soft creamy candy I hate, even though I keep telling him I like the hard kind. He’s completely hopped up on big ideas right now, we’re gonna have our work cut out for us to get his head back down to earth. Not that it even matters, y’know? I’d live in a two-room kitchenette apartment with him and be perfectly happy, no fancy house required. I’m so dippy about that chump it’s ridiculous. Chumps make the best husbands, honestly. All the miserable marriages are the ones where the husband has too many brains. Brains just make people miserable, what’s the point of that?” She paused, squinting at Sally’s face enviously. “Hey, what’s your secret for perfect skin? My freckles are taking over my whole face, I added like 12 new ones last summer. Lemon juice doesn’t do a thing, they just drink it down and ask for more. The only way to get rid of them is to saw your head off at the neck, as far as I can tell.”
“Fillmore adores freckles,” Sally laughed. “You can see it in his eyes every time he looks at you.”
“Really? Well that’s a relief,” Miss Winch said, grinning. “Those face cream ads keep telling me my husband’s gonna stop loving me because of my ‘unsightly blemishes’ — but so far, Fillmore hasn’t seemed to mind a bit.”

If you’d asked Ginger Kemp to jot down a list of his good qualities, he’d have stared at you like you’d just asked him to recite all the kings of England backwards after three pints of bitter. The only bullet point he could nail without squinting for half an hour was “I generally mean well”, and even that was a generous stretch. Tact? Delicacy? Those were words he’d previously assumed referred to fancy imported beer brands.

Yet somehow, he pulled off a masterclass in giving Sally space in the days after her heartbreak, no small feat for a guy whose default setting is showing up uninvited with a bag of salted peanuts and a terrible joke about pigeons. He figured when your life’s gone pear-shaped, the last thing you want is someone hovering over you clucking like a nervous mother hen. He’d had his share of what he called “nasty jars” before — lost entire weeks’ pay betting on racehorses that stopped mid-run to sniff a patch of clover, gotten tossed out of pubs for accidentally knocking over a bartender — and all he’d ever wanted in those moments was to mope alone eating cold baked beans, zero spectators.

By Saturday, though, he decided the coast was clear. No office shift that day, for one. Even better: payday. Mrs. Meecher, his landlady with the fingers so sticky she could relieve you of half your cash before you noticed, had taken her weekly cut, but enough was left to fund a proper princely consolation mission: rent an open-top car, drive Sally up the Boston Post Road to one of those fancy roadhouses he’d heard about that served steak big enough to feed a small village. The weather was doing him a huge solid too, that magical soft Indian Summer he’d never encountered in stuffy old England, the kind of golden sun that wraps around you like a nice warm blanket no one’s stolen off you yet.

He was loitering on the Maison Meecher roof that Saturday morning, weaving his way through the landlady’s jungle of damp laundry strung between clotheslines, when he spotted it: the *Mauritania*. The exact boat he’d sailed over on from England, chugging down the Hudson, its four funnels poking over the Manhattan rooftops like the whole giant steamship was cruising straight down Ninth Avenue. He gawked at it till the skyscrapers ate it from view, then trotted back down to his garret to grab his hat, ready to go sweep Sally off for that perfect drive.

Fifteen minutes later he’s in the lobby of Sally’s apartment building, and his smile dies so fast it’s like someone hit it with a frying pan. There’s his cousin Bruce Carmyle, the human wet blanket himself, in his crisp sergeant’s uniform, chatting to the janitor. Ginger’s mood did a full 180 faster than a hobo spotting a cop next to a free pie stand. Even back in Piccadilly, running into Bruce was like stepping on a slug in your sock. Finding him here? Nauseating. Then he spots the shiny, overpriced luxury car idling outside, and his stomach sinks. Oh no. Bruce had the exact same bright idea for a golden sunny drive, the showoff.

Bruce turns around, scowl firmly in place like the janitor had just spent ten minutes explaining his weird stamp collection to him, and lights up exactly zero percent when he sees Ginger. “Hullo.”
“Hullo,” Ginger mumbles. The silence that follows is thick enough to spread on toast.
“You here to see Miss Nicholas?” Bruce asks.
“Uh, yeah.”
“She’s not here.” Bruce’s scowl almost twitches into a smile, like he’s thrilled to share the garbage news.
“Not here? Where’d she go?”
Bruce looks like someone just stole his favorite top hat. “Apparently, for no reason any sane human can fathom, she decided to hotfoot it all the way to England.”

Ginger almost faceplants into the lobby potted fern. The news hits him like a runaway fruit cart. He follows Bruce out onto the sidewalk in a daze. Bruce waves off his fancy hired car, tells the driver he doesn’t need it. The driver, a guy who looked like he’d seen every trick in the book, opened one eye, spat with the precise drama of a millionaire signing off on a multi-million dollar deal, and said “You still gotta pay the full day’s rate, buddy.” Bruce huffs, hands over the cash, and the car rumbles off.
“Gone to England?” Ginger blinks, still not processing.
“Yep. Sailed this morning on the *Mauritania*.”

Oh. Oh no. The *Mauritania*. The exact boat he was just gawking at from the roof 20 minutes prior, watching it drift down the river like a total idiot while Sally was on it, sailing away. Ginger doesn’t even say goodbye to Bruce, he just wanders off, the sun suddenly feeling way too cold. The whole city’s garbage now, right? No Sally, New York’s just a big drab pile of bricks, not even the petty joy of watching Bruce burn a full day’s car hire fee can cheer him up. He loiters in Central Park, he loiters on the sidewalk, nothing’s fun.

Then night falls, he trundles back to Maison Meecher, and there’s a letter in his pigeon hole with the *Mauritania*’s fancy crest on it. He snatches it like a seagull stealing a french fry, bolts up the stairs to his tiny room. Normally his room’s so bleak it could make a monk cry — Mrs. Meecher’s idea of luxury is adding a rickety Morris chair next to the bed and sticking a random Bible verse print over the sink — but tonight? He’s over the moon, he’s got a letter from Sally.

He drags out the stolen photo of Sally he swiped from her apartment a few weeks back, props it up by the basin, takes off his shoes, his collar, packs his pipe, arranges his matches and tobacco pouch on the arm of the Morris chair, pulls the chair up so his feet are propped on the bed, and makes a whole ritual out of opening the letter, like a little kid savoring the last scoop of ice cream so it lasts as long as possible. He weighs the envelope, stares at the handwriting, lights his pipe twice for good measure, and finally rips it open.

First line: “Ginger, dear.” He has to pick up the photo and stare at it for a full five minutes after that. Then he keeps reading. She apologizes for running off without saying goodbye, says it’s cowardly but she couldn’t handle all the reminders of what went wrong in New York, that he was the only person nice enough to leave her alone to mope the past few days, and she appreciated it more than he knew. She says she got a letter from Mr. Faucitt, their old friend, who inherited his dead brother’s fancy dress shop Laurette et Cie in Regent Street — he’s totally lost running it and begged her to come over and help. She also got a letter from Elsa Doland, the actress Sally helped break into the business, all full of gushy gratitude, and a letter from Gerald, her ex-fiance. She couldn’t bring herself to open Gerald’s letter, just burned the whole thing on the bathroom floor till it was nothing but black ash and a stain on the tile. She rambles about how stupid it is that you can be the world’s best relationship advisor for every idiot you meet, but when it’s your own heartbreak you’re just a sniveling mess, how her pride is more broken than her actual heart, how she knows Gerald bailed on her for Elsa ‘cause Elsa’s a big star now who can help his career, how there’s nothing for her left in New York but ghosts. She tells him not to make a mess of his new job, that he’s her favorite pal, she’ll miss him like crazy, and there wasn’t time to mail him that photo he was looking for.

Ginger sets the letter down, making a noise half sigh, half curse, and for a second he seriously considers taking the next train to Chicago to break Gerald Foster’s stupid neck. Not street cleaning, not delivering parcels, something where you end up sitting in a big fancy office chair as the president of something, right? Think.”
“Er. Well. If I had a little bit of startup capital…”
“Startup capital! Ooh, big business tycoon moves. What would you do with your little startup capital, Mr. Carnegie?”
“Run a dog thingamajig.”

Sally blinked. “A dog what?”
“Y’know. A place for dogs. Breed ‘em, sell ‘em, win dog shows, all that stuff. Makes great money. I talked to a guy who ran one out in Long Island when I was working for Fillmore, he was looking to sell the whole place off cheap.”
Sally stared at him, then burst out laughing. “A kennels! You couldn’t just say kennels, could you? Had to call it a dog thingamajig. But wait — that’s perfect! Remember that day in Roville, when a whole pack of stray dogs started a riot in the street? You strolled in, whispered something to them, and they all sat down like perfect little angels? You’re basically a dog whisperer. That’s the job for you.”
“Apparently it pays 100% returns on your initial investment, too,” Ginger said, brightening up.
“100%? That sounds like the kind of scam Fillmore pitches to producers. Let’s say 99% to be safe. You’re going to be the Dog King of Long Island. But what’s the price tag on that place you mentioned?”
“Ah. The catch. He wants five thousand bucks. Total.”

“Done. I’ll lend you the five grand.”
“Absolutely not,” Ginger said, so firm he almost split his swollen lip.
Sally groaned. “Ginger, don’t be ridiculous. This is a business deal! I’m not handing you charity money, I’m investing in a very sure thing. Queen Isabella didn’t make Columbus feel weird about taking her money to sail to America, did she? You have to pay me interest, obviously.”
“25% interest a year,” Ginger said immediately.
“Absolutely not. 3%.”
“Three percent? That’s pocket change! 20% minimum.”
“5%. That’s my offer.”
“10%.”
“6%!” Sally yelled, holding up six fingers. “That’s final. No haggling, no negotiating. 6% a year.”
Ginger did the math in his head. 6% of five grand was $300 a year. “That’s way too little —”
“Take it or leave it,” Sally said, grinning. “Now go call that guy right now, before some other dog lover snaps the whole place up. Go go go!”
Ginger paused at the door, still looking uncertain, but Sally shooed him out.

Sally carried her paper over to the window after he left, to re-read the fight report for the third time and people-watch the quiet street below her apartment building. It was a perfect sunny day, everyone looked happy, and she was half-convinced this whole dog kennel plan was going to make Ginger a millionaire before the year was out.

Then a taxi pulled up outside, and out stumbled her brother Fillmore. She was about to wave and holler at him through the open window, when his behavior got so weird she froze. He looked like he was rehearsing a new dance craze no one had heard of yet. First he stormed purposefully towards the front door, then skidded to a halt and ran back to the sidewalk. He did that three whole times, stood there frowning like he was arguing with a ghost, made one last mad dash for the door, then turned around and sprinted off down the street like someone had lit his coat on fire.

Sally was so confused she almost forgot to breathe. Why take a taxi all the way over just to run away at the front step? Then the phone rang, and Fillmore’s voice came over the line, hoarse like he’d been eating sawdust.
“Sally? You saw that, didn’t you.”
“Hi Fill! What do you call that new dance you invented? The Doorstep Shuffle? I was very impressed.”
“Ugh. I came over to talk to you, but — er — I couldn’t work up the nerve. Sally, Gladys is coming over to see you right now. I told her to head straight over. Will you be home?”
“Sure, I’ll stay in. Is everything okay? You sound like you’ve seen a ghost.”
“Everything’s — well. Not great. She’s got bad news to tell you. I’ll — I’ll leave you two to it.” And he hung up before Sally could ask any more questions.

Ten minutes later the doorbell rang, and Gladys was there, hugging Sally so tight her ribs creaked. She still had that same no-nonsense, warm personality she’d had before marrying Fillmore, but there was a little frown on her face that Sally had never seen before.
After a minute of casual chit-chat about Sally’s trip to England, Gladys leaned forward, sighed, and said the thing Sally had been dreading. “Sally, who’s this rich guy Carmyle over in England that Fillmore says proposed to you? You couldn’t have changed your mind about saying no, right? Because —”

And that’s when Sally realized the bad news wasn’t about her. It was about Elsa.

There’s a specific breed of man – and Bruce Carmyle was a prize specimen – that comes with a bounce-back factor so unbreakable it could outlast a rubber ball dropped off the Eiffel Tower. The sort of stubborn oaf who refuses to admit defeat even when defeat has smacked him square across the chops with a cricket bat, a trait that serves him just as well chasing a lover as it does chasing a business deal or a particularly stubborn golf ball in the rough.

As a suitor, Bruce was exactly that tough old pugilist who doesn’t even bother putting in effort till he’s taken one solid, numbing wallop to a tender spot. Sally had turned down his proposal flat as a pancake at Monk’s Crofton, but it never once crossed his mind that the episode was closed. He’d spent 30 years of his life getting every single thing he set his sights on, and he wasn’t about to break the streak now. That tiny, nagging sliver of doubt he’d occasionally felt about her – that whiff of unruly Bohemian spirit that had clashed so hard with his neat, colour-coded schedule of a life? Gone. Wiped clean. All he could think of was the brightness of her eyes, the jaunty little tilt of her chin, and the way she carried herself like she’d laugh out loud if a duke tried to order her around. Her bright, sharp prettiness was prodding him awake at 3 a.m. like a mischievous sprite with a whip, lashing him to go after her. Quiet, methodical, about as subtle as a distinguished gentleman wolf who’s packed a valet and a first-class transatlantic ticket, he set off on the trail. Delicacy and tact might have slowed a lesser man down, but Bruce had never had much time for either. You can’t expect a man who’s made of very little but grit and inherited wealth to come with every optional extra, can you?

He tried to pack his bags fast and sneak off without anyone noticing, but he forgot about the Carmyle family grapevine. Every extended English family of that vintage runs on a gossip network that makes African jungle drum telegraphs look slow: no group chats, no carrier pigeons, no texts, but somehow news of Bruce’s secret trip spread to every cousin, aunt, and great-uncle within a 50-mile radius faster than you could say “he’s chasing a girl who won’t have him”.

On his last night in London, the family’s chosen ambassador turned up at his South Audley Street flat: Uncle Donald himself. Two hundred and forty pounds of him, to be exact. The poor armchair he lowered his full weight into creaked so loud it sounded like it was making a last dying plea for mercy. Sally had once made her brother Fillmore’s whole morning miserable by pointing out that Uncle Donald was the exact spitting image of what Fillmore would look like if he didn’t straighten up, and Fillmore had moped for three straight days. A childhood superstition that he had a dicky heart had led Uncle Donald to avoid every single form of exercise for nearly 50 years, and paired with one of the three most ravenous appetites in the whole of southwest London, at 62 he was not the sort of man you wanted parked in your favourite armchair.

Bruce, normally the very picture of polite poise, eyed him with something very close to loathing. Uncle Donald’s walrus moustache heaved up and down with his huffing breath after climbing the stairs, bobbing like seaweed on a rolling tide.

“What’s this? What’s this?” he panted out eventually. “You packing?”
“Yes,” Bruce said, shortly. For the first time in his whole life, he got that weird, furtive guilty feeling he’d always assumed only his cousin Ginger got whenever he snuck out to play poker or stole a jam tart from the larder in front of this big, mackerel-eyed old man.
“You going away?”
“Yes.”
“Where you going?”
“America.”
“When you going?”
“Tomorrow morning.”
“Why you going?”

This back and forth might read like snappy vaudeville banter, but in reality it stretched on for a full three whole minutes: Uncle Donald would huff, sigh, snuffle his moustache up his nose at a glacial pace after every reply, before he even thought about asking the next question, and Bruce’s nerves were fraying so thin he was half-convinced he’d snap and scream any second.

“You’re going after that girl,” Uncle Donald accused, jabbing a fat finger at him.

Bruce flushed so dark red he looked like a boiled lobster. For half a second he found himself thinking that time Ginger hid under a table at Bleke’s Coffee House to avoid Uncle Donald hadn’t been nearly as cowardly as Bruce had called it at the time. The family’s golden boy was, it turned out, absolutely insufferable.

“Would you like a whisky and soda, Uncle Donald?” Bruce said, trying valiantly to change the subject.

“Never turn down a free drink,” Uncle Donald said, sticking to a vow he’d made back in 1882. “Gimme.”

You’d think that would soften the old man up, wouldn’t you? Not a bit of it. He knocked the whole restorative back in two greedy sips, then launched right back into the attack as grumpy as before. “Never thought you were a fool before,” he said severely.

Bruce’s pride chafed. These dressing-downs were a normal part of daily life for Ginger, but Bruce had never once had anyone question a single thing he did in his whole life. “I’m not a fool.”
“You are a fool. A damn fool,” Uncle Donald clarified. “Don’t like the girl. Never did. Not a nice girl. Took a dislike to her the second I laid eyes on her.”
“Must we really discuss this?” Bruce said, pulling out his stuffy grand-heir manner like a protective blanket.

Uncle Donald snorted so hard his moustache flapped. “Must we discuss it? D’you think I climbed three flights of blasted stairs with my weak heart for the fun of it? Gimme another drink.”

Bruce poured him a second one.
“Terrible business,” Uncle Donald moaned once he’d drained that glass too. “Shocking bad. If your poor old dad was alive right now, d’you think he’d let you go tearing across the Atlantic after some girl who won’t even look at you? I’ll tell you what he’d say – what’s this whisky, anyway?”
“O’Rafferty Special.”
“New one on me. Not bad. Quite good. Mellow. Where’d you get it?”
“Bilby’s in Oxford Street.”
“Must order a case. Mellow. Your dad’d be spitting feathers. None of us get what you’re doing. Puzzles your uncle George. Baffles your aunt Geraldine. Everyone says she’s only after your money. Anyone with half a brain can see that.”
“Pardon me, Uncle Donald,” Bruce said, stiff as a board, “but that’s absurd. If she was only after my money, why did she turn me down flat at Monk’s Crofton?”
“Drawing you on!” Uncle Donald said, as bright as a button. “Luring you in. Oldest trick in the book. There was a girl back when I was at Oxford in 1881 who tried that on me, and if my weak heart hadn’t stepped in to save me I’d have been trapped forever. What d’you even know about this girl, anyway? Who is she? Where’d you meet her?”
“At Roville, in France.”
“Travelling with her family?”
“Travelling alone,” Bruce admitted, reluctantly.
“Not even with that brother of hers? Bad!” Uncle Donald wheezed. “Bad, bad!”
“American girls are used to far more independence than English girls.”
“That boy’s going to be as fat as I am one day if he doesn’t lay off the cream buns,” Uncle Donald said, nodding to himself. “Travelling alone, eh? Did you catch her eye on the pier or something?”
“Really, Uncle Donald!”
“Well, you had to meet her somehow.”
“Lancelot introduced us. She was a friend of his.”

Uncle Donald quivered all over like a jelly that’d just been smacked with a spoon at the loathed name. “Lancelot! That useless, lacy little fop? That settles it! Any girl who’s friends with that waste of space – unpack your bags! Right now! This foolery stops here. Find yourself a nice respectable girl who’ll make you a proper wife. Your aunt Mary just met the Bassington-Bassingtons, related to the Kent branch, eldest daughter’s a perfect little lady, right up your street.”

Outside of old-fashioned Victorian melodramas, no one ever actually grinds their teeth, but Bruce came closer to doing it right then than any man in living memory. He scowled so dark his face looked like a thundercloud, all his usual suavity gone out the window. “I won’t do any such thing. I sail tomorrow.”

Uncle Donald had defied exactly one nephew before (Ginger, obviously) but no one had ever defied him back before. He suddenly felt horribly, embarrassingly powerless. There’s no worse feeling than standing there with no idea what to do next when a younger generation tells you to get stuffed. “Eh?” he said.

Bruce, now fully committed to being defiant, decided to go all the way. “I’m over 21. I’ve got my own money, no one tells me what to do. I’m going to do exactly as I please.”
“Now, hold on, think about it!” Uncle Donald pleaded, sounding terribly wimpy. “Reflect! Your position in the county –”
“Thought about it.”
“You could marry literally anyone you wanted!”
“Exactly. And I’m going to marry her.”
“So you’re determined to go running off to God-knows-where after this girl whose name I can’t even be bothered to remember?”
“Yes.”
“Have you forgotten,” Uncle Donald said, leaning forward portentously, “that you have a duty to the Family?”

Bruce’s patience snapped clean in two. He sank straight to Ginger levels of plain speaking, no fancy manners left. “Oh, damn the Family!” he yelled.

Silence. The only sound was the relieved groan of the armchair as Uncle Donald heaved his two hundred and forty pounds off it. “After that,” Uncle Donald said, “I’ve got nothing more to say.”
“Good,” Bruce said, rudely, no trace of shame left.
“’Cept this. If you come back married to that girl, I’ll cut you dead in Piccadilly. Swear to God I will.”

He waddled to the door. Bruce just stared at his shoes, not saying a word. Tense moment. “Hey,” Uncle Donald said, his fingers on the door handle, “what’d you say that whisky was called again?”
“O’Rafferty Special.”
“And where’d you get it?”
“Bilby’s in Oxford Street.”
“Right,” Uncle Donald said. “I’ll make a note of that.” And he was gone.

“And after every single blasted thing I’ve done for her!” wailed Mr. Reginald Cracknell, voice wobbling like a jelly on a bouncy castle, eyes glistening from a potent mix of romantic heartbreak and one too many snifters of his famous top-shelf private moonshine. “After all that, she dumps me flat!”

The Flower Garden’s orchestra blared so loud you’d need a foghorn to make yourself heard over the brass section, and she was far too busy doing advanced parkour just to keep her own feet from getting squashed under Mr. Cracknell’s unpredictable, drunken dance steps to pay attention to much else. Draped in old gold and black, she looked so regal you half expected the crowd to curtsy as you passed, and poor smitten Reggie let out a sorrowful gurgle as he shambled past her line of sight.

Nearly a month had dragged by since she’d gone back to working for Mr. Abrahams. It had been a dull, leaden stretch of days, the sort of monotonous slog that feels like a bad dream you can’t wake up from. She’d barely seen a single familiar face in weeks, none of her old boarding-house pals had crossed her path, Fillmore was obviously avoiding her out of guilty conscience, and Ginger was off doing his vaudeville bit somewhere on Long Island’s south shore.

She lowered her hands and blinked out at the crowd. The Flower Garden was packed, per usual. One of the thousands of new dance clubs that had popped up riding New York’s dance craze wave, and unlike half its rivals that had gone bust after two weeks, it was thriving—Abrahams swore blind it was “a nice place, run nicely,” and the crowd ate that line up. The ads called it a “supper club for post-theater dining and dancing,” bragged it was “spacious, sumptuously appointed, one of the city’s greatest wonders, with an unbeatable dance floor, heavenly music, five-star food and luxury service.” You didn’t even have to meet Abrahams to know he thought this joint was the eighth wonder of the world.

Sally had liked it once, back when she first started working there, found it fun and chaotic and full of silly little bits of entertainment. But back then she didn’t get splitting headaches every night, or that heavy, listless depression that made every shift feel like hauling bricks uphill.

“Miss Nicholas?”

The orchestra, which never shut up for longer than 90 seconds max at the Flower Garden, started up again, and Lee Schoenstein had arrived with her next dance partner. She stood up on autopilot.

“First time I’ve ever stepped foot in this sort of place,” the guy said as they bounced and bumped across the dancefloor. He was huge, clumsy, the sort of man who moves through crowds like a wrecking ball. “Swanky joint! I’m from upstate, we don’t have nothing half this fancy back home.” He plowed a path through the crowd using Sally as a human battering ram, and Sally, who’d thought dancing with Reggie was bad, found herself missing Reggie actively. This man was unequivocally the worst dancer on the entire North American continent.

“Give me old New York, huh!” he said proudly. “Nothing beats it. I’ve seen every show in town since I got here. You catch this year’s Follies?”

“No,” Sally said.

“You gotta go! S’the best thing you’ll ever see. You seen *Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath*?”

“I don’t get to the theater much,” Sally said.

“Go! It’s a scream! I’ve been to a show every single night since I got into the city. All of ‘em great, except last night’s dud. Took a chance, y’know, it was a brand new opening. Thought I could tell all the folks back home I went to a New York opening night! Cost me two seventy-five with tax, and I’d rather have flushed the cash down the toilet. Called it *The Wild Rose*,” he said, sneering like he’d just caught the management trying to sell him a glass of tap water as champagne. “*The Wild Rose*! It sure made me wild, all right. Before the second act even hit halfway, the whole audience was sprinting for the exits, and if someone hadn’t yelled ‘women and children first’ there would’ve been a full stampede.”

Something clicked in Sally’s head. *The Wild Rose*. Oh, of course. That was Gerald’s new play. She’d been dodging the thought of running into him in their shared apartment building for weeks, until she’d spotted a tiny newspaper note saying he was out on tour with a new production.

“Dunno who wrote it, but that guy ought to get ten years hard labor for writing it,” the upstate guy grumbled. “They’ve got guys breaking rocks up at Ossining prison who did way less to deserve a sentence.”

“Miss Nicholas.”

Isadore Abrahams was a man of his word. He named his club the Flower Garden, so he’d done his level best to make a stuffy, overcrowded Broadway dance joint feel like an actual garden. He leaned in, whispering in her ear. The room was hotter than ever, but the bird was singing again, and all she could think was *take me out of this*. Did anything else even matter? How, or where, or who took her, none of it mattered. As long as she got to get out of here.

Monk’s Crofton looked so cool, so green, so quiet.

“All right,” Sally said.

Bruce Carmyle, newly minted accepted suitor, found himself completely stumped. He’d barely survived that night, and he hated being reminded of it. He shifted in his chair, annoyed, and tried to get back on the romantic track.

“Darling,” he murmured, shifting his chair two feet to the right and leaning far over so only she could hear him. “You have made me so—”

“BATTI BATTI! PRESTO RAVIOLI HOLLANDAISE!” the first waiter shrieked right in his ear.

“LA DONNA E MOBILE SPAGHETTI NAPOLI TETRAZZINA!” the second waiter yelled back, firing back.

“—You have made me so—”

“INFANTA ISABELLA LOPE DE VEGA MULLIGATAWNY TORONTO!” the first waiter fired back, wheezing a little but still going strong.

“—so happy—”

“FUNICULI FUNICULA VINCENTE Y BLASCO IBAÑEZ VERMICELLI RISOTTO!” the second waiter yelled, landing the knockout blow.

Bruce gave up, lit a very moody cigarette. He’d never felt so strongly that this whole situation was completely wrong.

The blood seeped back into Sally’s cheeks at the sound of that familiar voice, and her heart, which had briefly spiked like she’d just spotted a spider the size of a dinner plate, settled right back into its regular rhythm. The shock wore off fast, and she found herself weirdly, completely calm. She’d run this hypothetical meeting a hundred times in her head before, every version ending with her panicking, stammering, bursting into tears or running for the fire escape. But after the absolute garbage night she’d just had? Not a single violent emotion in sight.

“Hey there, Sally!” Gerald slurred, swaying in the doorway with one hand glued to the frame like it was the only thing keeping him from tipping over. He was shirt-sleeved, collarless, his face puffy and pale, and the whole vibe coming off him was less tragic romantic hero and more guy who’d just stumbled out of a dive bar 3 hours after last call, trailing a haze of stale beer and bad life choices.

Sally didn’t say a word. She’d been too exhausted to think straight ten minutes prior, but now her overtaxed nerves had flipped into that weird hyper-alert mode you get when you’ve been up 18 hours and suddenly everything is crystal clear. She looked at him like he was a random stranger who’d wandered into the wrong apartment.

“Uh… hey,” Gerald said again, the same wobbly grin still plastered to his face.
“What do you want?” Sally asked, steady as a judge.
“Heard your voice. Saw your door was open. Figured I’d pop in.”
“What do you want?”
The dopey smile fell right off Gerald’s face. A fat tear rolled down his cheek. He’d hit the sloshed, soppy stage of drunk.
“Sally… S-Sally… I’m so miserable.” He stumbled over the syllables like they were marbles scattered on the floor. “Heard your voice. Saw your door open. Figured I’d pop in.”

A little flicker of recognition pricked at Sally’s brain. She’d seen this exact act before. That time Reginald Cracknell had gotten tossed from the dinner party and blubbered on her couch for 45 minutes about how the world didn’t appreciate his poetry.
“Gerald, I think you should go to bed,” she said evenly. She didn’t feel a single twinge of pity for him, not at his slobby state, not at his shameless self-pity.
“What’s the point? Can’t sleep. No use. Sally, you have no idea how stressed I am. I finally see what an idiot I’ve been.”

Sally lifted a hand to cut him off, fully braced to listen to a teary, belated apology for the way he’d strung her along and nearly wrecked her whole life. Turns out that wasn’t the thing eating at his soul at all.
“I was a fool ever to try writing plays,” he babbled. “Got lucky the first time, can’t do it again. Total flop tonight. Useless. Should’ve stuck to newspaper work. I’m good at that. Gotta go back to the grind, damn it.” He sniffled, fully immersed in feeling bad for the poor hard-done-by brilliant him. “So miserable.”

He lurched a step into the room, wobbled, and scrambled back to hang onto the doorframe for dear life. For half a second Sally felt a sharp, hot jolt of contempt. Then it passed, and she was back to that cool, indifferent armor.
“Go to bed,” she repeated. “You’ll feel better in the morning.”
Maybe the faint memory of how brutal a hangover hits filtered through Gerald’s booze fog, because he winced, and his mood got even more dramatic.
“Might not be alive in the morning,” he declared solemnly. “Got half a mind to end it all. End it all!” He waved a hand grandly and almost face-planted when he had to catch himself on the door again.

Sally was not in the mood for this cheap melodrama. “Oh, go to bed,” she huffed, annoyed. That frozen calm was melting fast, turning into sharp resentment – mad at Gerald for making such a pathetic mess of himself, mad at her younger self for ever thinking there was anything glamorous or special about this guy. She could practically taste the humiliation of how she’d once let him walk all over her, and it made her hard and unforgiving. Normally, the sight of any creature even a little bit in distress would make her jump to help, but Gerald crying about having to go get a regular job? Not even a tiny spark of sympathy. Which he noticed immediately, of course.
“You’re very unsymp… unsympathetic,” he whined.
“Sorry,” Sally said, and she walked over to the door and shoved it. Gerald, still clinging to the doorknob, went staggering out into the hallway like the whole planet had just tilted under his feet. He let go of the handle, wobbled across the hall, tripped over his own threshold, and vanished into his own apartment. Sally watched him make it inside safely, then turned and decided she was done with this chaotic night, time to crash.

Spoiler: sleep was not on the menu. She was wired, jittery, full of restless energy. She threw on a kimono and headed to the kitchen to make herself a hot glass of milk, only to remember she’d given the very last of her milk that morning to a sandy stray cat that had sauntered through her window like it owned the place, staring her down until she handed over the goods. She was still bemoaning her bad life choices when a muffled crash boomed from across the hall.

She froze. More crashes came, one after another, loud enough to rattle the pictures on her own walls. Even her detached, 2AM brain jumped straight back to Gerald’s dramatic “I’m gonna end it all” line, and suddenly she was panicking. That guy was too drunk to know what he was doing. The crashes picked up, louder, unmistakeable. She didn’t hesitate anymore, ran across the hall, and banged on his door.

A minute later, the door creaked open. Gerald was fine. He was still swaying, still grinning that wobbly grin, still looking like a disaster, but 100% alive.
“Hey Sally!”

Sally’s panic evaporated immediately, replaced by that old, annoyed resentment. He’d scared her half to death for no reason?
“What was all that noise?” she demanded.
“Noise?” Gerald blinked at her like he had no clue what she was talking about.
“Yes, noise,” Sally snapped.
“I was cleaning house,” Gerald said, dead serious, like this was a totally normal 2AM activity for a grown man.

Sally pushed past him into his apartment. It was basically the exact copy of her place, except his wife Elsa had spent ages decking it out with all her favorite little frilly knickknacks – dainty plates on shelves, tiny porcelain statuettes, mismatched glass lamp shades, pictures crammed wall to wall.
Past tense.
Sally skidded to a halt just inside the door, staring. A hand grenade could not have made more of a mess. Every single one of Elsa’s little decorations was in pieces. The floor was ankle deep in shards of china and glass. The pictures had been ripped off the walls and looked like a herd of wildebeest had chewed on them. Off in the corner, a golf niblick lay tossed to the side, the obvious weapon of mass destruction.

Gerald wobbled over to a tiny, flimsy side table and plopped down on it, immediately starting to blubber again, fully back in his poor little hard-done-by self-pity mood.
“Well!” Sally gasped. “You certainly did a number on this place.”
There was a sharp, loud crack. The tiny side table, which had never been designed to hold the full weight of a 180lb drunk man, splintered into a dozen pieces under him, and he landed hard in a pile of old watercolor paintings.

That was the breaking point for Sally. All the tension, all the old heartache, all the lingering weird pining she’d carried for Gerald for years just popped, and she burst out cackling so hard she had to lean against the wall to stay upright. She was laughing at herself, mostly – at the stupid, romantic girl who’d once thought this ridiculous, tantrum-throwing man-child was some sort of deep, tragic genius.

Gerald picked himself up out of the broken wood, staring at her like she’d lost her mind. “No sympathy,” he said, offended.
“I can’t help it!” Sally wheezed. “It’s too funny.”
“Not funny,” Gerald mumbled, his brain fogging over again.
“Why did you even do all this?”
Gerald got heated for half a second, indignant. “She went off and left my show to go act in another play! Ruined my whole career! Why shouldn’t I smash her stuff? Why should I put up with that?”
“Sure, you showed her,” Sally said dryly. “Very manly. Very independent.”
“Damn right! Manly independent.” He waggled a finger at her. “Don’t care if she ever comes back. That woman-”

Sally did not want to have this conversation about Elsa at 4AM. The funny bit was wearing off fast, replaced by tired disgust at how sordid the whole mess was. She wanted Gerald gone. “Alright, enough. I’m going to clean this up. You go hang out in my apartment. I’ll come get you when it’s safe to come back in.”
“NO!” Gerald protested, but he was too tired and too hungover to fight her for long. He shuffled out the door and into her place, and Sally sighed in relief, grabbed a broom from the kitchen, and got to work. Half an hour later, she’d swept all the shards into neat little piles against the walls, flung the windows open, and realized the sun was coming up. The sky was soft pale blue, the street below was quiet and cool, and she suddenly got so tired her knees gave out. She sank into an armchair, and was asleep before her head even hit the cushion.

Sally woke up to bright sunshine streaming through the window, car horns honking outside, people yelling on the sidewalk, the whole city wide awake and going about its day. She rubbed her aching head, splashed cold water on her face in the bathroom, then trundled back to her own apartment.

Gerald was passed out in her armchair by the window, snoring so loud the curtains rattled, legs splayed out, head lolling over the arm. Sally stood there for a second, staring at him, and she realized something huge. That whole chapter of her life was closed. The ghost was gone. All that old pining, all that weird regret for the relationship that never was? Vanished. She could look at this pathetic mess of a man and feel nothing but a tiny, faint twinge of half-pity, half-amused contempt. The glamour was 100% gone.

She shook him awake. He jolted up, blinking, staring at her like he had no clue where he was. “Oh God,” he moaned, pressing both hands to his throbbing head. “My head. It hurts so bad.”
“Go have a shower,” Sally suggested. “I’ll make you some breakfast.”
“NO,” Gerald croaked, and tottered off to the bathroom like a zombie.

Sally was still sitting there staring out the window when the doorbell rang. She opened it, and there was Bruce Carmyle, in his fancy driving coat, grinning so wide his face almost cracked, trying way too hard to be charming. He’d woken up that morning feeling guilty for ditching her the night before, so he’d showed up early to surprise her with that drive he’d promised.
“Hey! Ready to go?” he said cheerfully.

Sally had completely forgotten about the drive. She stood there blankly, no words coming out. Bruce was just about to lean in for a chivalrous good morning kiss when the bathroom door swung open. Gerald stepped out, half washed, hair sticking up, pale as a sheet, squinting like someone was drilling holes into his skull.

Bruce froze. His grin died. All the stupid little doubts he’d ever had about Sally instantly became 100% certain fact. His Uncle Donald’s “I told you so” voice was ringing so loud in his ears he could hear it.
“So…” Bruce said, his voice ice cold, staring daggers at Gerald.
“Get out,” he said.

Gerald, who had a headache so bad he couldn’t even summon the energy to argue, tried to draw himself up to be indignant, immediately got a stabbing pain behind his eyes, and thought better of it. He shuffled past Bruce and out the front door without a single protest.

Bruce turned back to Sally, so furious he was practically vibrating. “This is very amusing,” he said, bitterly. “I should have expected this.”
“Would you like me to explain?” Sally asked, quiet.
“There’s no explanation that could make this right,” Bruce snapped.
“Alright,” Sally said.
“Goodbye.”
“Goodbye.”

Bruce stormed out, the door slamming behind him so hard the walls shook. He climbed into his fancy sports car and peeled off down the street, seething.

Sally didn’t even run after him. She walked straight to her telephone, dialed Ginger Kemp’s number, and woke him up from a dead sleep. The second he realized it was her on the line and she was asking him to take her out for breakfast and a drive, the sleep fell right out of his voice, and he started whooping so loud the line crackled. Five minutes later, he was splashing around in his bathtub singing terrible off-key jazz songs, already halfway out the door in his head.

Darkness crept over the countryside with all the awkward politeness of a party guest who’s just realized he’s outstayed his welcome, almost apologetic for having to snuff out what had been a perfect, sun-warmed summer day. West of the treeline, a faint pink afterglow still lingered like a half-empty glass of lemonade, and the new moon glinted sharp as a silver sickle bobbing over the big red barn roof.

Sally stepped out onto the porch gravel and bowed three times very gravely for luck, then breathed in deep lungfuls of sweet clover and cut grass. Life, she decided, was pretty much unbeatable right now. The skittish darkness, having dragged its feet at the start, soon got down to business, painting the sky a rich dark blue dotted with soft, twinkling stars. The pale smear of the state road leading off to Patchogue and Babylon vanished into the dark, and little yellow windows began to glow in the farmhouses scattered across the meadows. A sleepy single bark drifted over from the kennels, and the tiny fluffy white dog that had scampered out at Sally’s heels puffed out its chest and let out a ferocious squeak that would have terrified a dandelion, if dandelions had a sense of self-preservation.
