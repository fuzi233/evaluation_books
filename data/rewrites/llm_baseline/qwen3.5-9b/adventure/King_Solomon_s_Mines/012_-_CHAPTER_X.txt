Upon reaching our hut, I signaled for Infadoos to join us inside.

"Now, Infadoos," I said, "we wish to speak with you."

"Speak freely, my lords," he replied.

"It appears to us, Infadoos, that King Twala is a cruel ruler."

"So it is, my lords. Alas! the land itself cries out in protest against his tyranny. Tonight, you shall witness the proof. It is the Great Witch-Hunt, and many will be exposed as sorcerers and slaughtered. No man's life is secure. Should the King covet a man's cattle, or desire his wife, or suspect a man of plotting rebellion, then Gagool—or one of the witch-finders she has trained—will scent him out as a wizard, and he shall die. Many must fall before the moon turns pale tonight. It is always thus. Perhaps I too shall meet my end soon. Thus far, I have survived because of my skill in warfare and the affection of the soldiers, but I cannot fathom how long my days remain. The land groans under Twala's cruelty; it is weary of him and his bloody ways."

"Then why do the people not overthrow him?"

"Nay, my lords. He is the King. Were he slain, Scragga would ascend the throne, and Scragga's heart is darker than his father Twala's. Under Scragga, the yoke upon our necks would be heavier than under Twala. Had Imotu never been killed, or had his son Ignosi lived, perhaps things might differ. But both are dead."

"How do you know Ignosi is dead?" a voice asked from behind us. We turned in astonishment to see Umbopa standing there.

"What means this, boy?" Infadoos demanded sharply. "Who gave you leave to speak?"

"Listen, Infadoos," Umbopa answered calmly, "and I shall tell you a tale. Long ago, King Imotu was killed in this land, and his wife fled with the young boy, Ignosi. Is that true?"

"Yes, that is true."

"It was said that the woman and her son perished upon the mountains. Is that correct?"

"Yes, exactly so."

"Well, it happened that the mother and the boy did not die. They crossed the mountains and were guided by a tribe of wandering desert folk across the sands beyond, until finally they found water, grass, and trees once more."

"How do you know this?"

"Listen. They traveled on and on for many months' journeys until they reached a land inhabited by a people called the Amazulu, who, like the Kukuana, belong to the same stock. There they stayed for many years until the mother eventually died. Then the son, Ignosi, became a wanderer again, journeying into a land of wonders where white men dwell. For many more years he learned the wisdom of the white people."

"That is a charming story," Infadoos said, sounding deeply skeptical.

"For years he lived there as a servant and a soldier, yet keeping in his heart everything his mother had told him of his homeland, constantly planning how he might return to see his people and his father's house before his own death. For long years he waited, and at last, as it always comes to those patient enough to wait, he encountered white men seeking this unknown land. He joined them. The white men traveled onward, searching for someone lost. They crossed the burning desert, traversed the snow-capped mountains, and finally arrived in the land of the Kukuana, where they found *you*, O Infadoos."

"You are surely mad to speak thus," the old soldier exclaimed in amazement.

"You think so? Well, I shall prove it to you, O my uncle."

"I am Ignosi, the rightful King of the Kukuana!"

With a single fluid motion, Umbopa removed his "moocha" or girdle and stood completely naked before us.

"Look," he said, pointing to a tattoo of a great snake inked in blue around his midsection, its tail vanishing into its own open mouth just above where the thighs meet the torso.

Infadoos stared, his eyes bulging from their sockets. Then he fell to his knees.

"_Koom! Koom!_" he gasped. "It is my brother's son; it is the King."

"Did I not tell you so, my uncle? Rise. I am not yet King, but with your aid and the support of these brave white men, my friends, I shall become one. Yet the old witch Gagool was right: the land must run with blood first, and hers must run with it, if she possesses any and can die, for she killed my father with her words and drove my mother into exile. Now, Infadoos, choose. Will you place your hands between mine and become my man? Will you share the dangers ahead and help me overthrow this tyrant and murderer, or will you refuse? Decide."

The old man placed a hand to his head in contemplation. Then he rose, walked forward to where Umbopa—or rather, Ignosi—stood, knelt before him, and took his hand.

"Ignosi, rightful King of the Kukuana, I place my hand between yours and pledge myself to you until death. When you were a babe, I rocked you upon my knees; now, in my old age, my arm shall strike for you and for freedom."

"It is well, Infadoos. If I conquer, you shall be the greatest man in the kingdom after the King. If I fail, you can only die, and death is not far from you. Rise, my uncle."

"And you, white men, will you aid me? What can I offer you? The white stones! If I conquer and locate them, you shall take as many as you can carry. Shall that suffice?"

I translated this offer.

"Tell him," Sir Henry responded, "that he misunderstands an Englishman. Wealth is good, and if it crosses our path, we shall take it; yet a gentleman does not sell himself for riches. Still, speaking for myself, I must say this: I have always liked Umbopa, and as far as lies within my power, I will stand by him in this matter. It will be quite pleasant for me to attempt to settle accounts with that cruel devil Twala. What say you, Good, and you, Quatermain?"

"Well," said Good, adopting the language of hyperbole common among these people, "you can tell him that a row is surely good and warms the cockles of the heart, and that so far as I am concerned, I'm his boy. My only stipulation is that he permits me to wear trousers."

I conveyed the essence of these replies.

"It is well, my friends," said Ignosi, formerly Umbopa. "And what sayest thou, Macumazahn? Art thou also with me, old hunter, cleverer than a wounded buffalo?"

I pondered for a moment, scratching my head.

"Umbopa, or Ignosi," I said, "I dislike revolutions. I am a man of peace and, admittedly, a bit of a coward"—here Umbopa smiled—"but, on the other hand, I stand by my friends. Ignosi, you have stuck to us and acted like a man, so I will stand by you. However, remember I am a trader and must earn my living, so I accept your offer regarding those diamonds should we ever reach a position to utilize it. Furthermore, as you know, we came to search for Incubu's (Sir Henry's) lost brother. You must assist us in finding him."

"That I will do," Ignosi answered. "Wait, Infadoos, by the sign of the snake about my middle, tell me the truth. Has any white man, to your knowledge, set foot within the land?"

"None, O Ignosi."

"If any white man had been seen or heard of, would you have known?"

"I certainly would have known."

"Then listen, Incubu," Ignosi addressed Sir Henry. "He has not been here."

"Well, well," Sir Henry sighed. "So it is. I suppose he never got so far. Poor fellow, poor fellow! All for naught. God's will be done."

"Now for business," I interjected, eager to escape the painful topic. "It is fine to be a King by divine right, Ignosi, but how do you intend to become a King in fact?"

"Nay, I know not. Infadoos, do you have a plan?"

"Ignosi, Son of the Lightning," his uncle replied, "tonight is the great dance and witch-hunt. Many shall be exposed and perish, and in the hearts of many others, there will arise grief, anguish, and fury against King Twala. Once the dance concludes, I shall approach some of the great chiefs. If I can win them over, they will address their regiments. I shall speak to the chiefs gently at first, bringing them to realize that you are indeed the King. I believe that by tomorrow's dawn, you will command twenty thousand spears. Now I must go to think, listen, and prepare. After the dance, if I am still alive—and we are all alive—I will meet you here, and we can discuss further matters. At the best, there must be war."
