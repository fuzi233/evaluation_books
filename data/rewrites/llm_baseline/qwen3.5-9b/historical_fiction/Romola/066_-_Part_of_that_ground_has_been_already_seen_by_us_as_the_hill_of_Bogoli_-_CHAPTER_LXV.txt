Little more than a week later, on the seventh of April, the great Piazza della Signoria staged a spectacle stranger even than the infamous Bonfire of the Vanities. A larger multitude had gathered than had ever attempted to fit themselves into the vast square, surpassing even the crowds of San Giovanni Day. By midday, the scene was teeming with humanity. Since dawn, people had swarmed every available perch: men sat precariously on iron rods jutting sharply from walls, clung to slender pillars with arms and legs, balanced astride the necks of rough statues crowning grand entrances, and squeezed onto mere palm-widths of architraves or footholds on rustic stonework, gripping the heavy iron rings and staples driven into the masonry. They endured cramped limbs and scraped flesh as minor inconveniences compared to the promise of a miracle unfolding before them. While mankind often hears of miracles and believes in them vaguely, the Florentines were about to witness one—or at least half of one. If the monk emerged unscathed, it would be a true miracle; if he entered the flames and vanished, the inference would be equally damning: he was burned in the middle.

There could be no doubt the fire would be lit and the monks summoned. Before their eyes lay a long platform, eight feet wide and twenty yards long, backed by a terrifying grove of fuel. Great branches of dry oak formed the base, topped by crackling thorns, well-anointed tow, and rags chosen specifically to create fierce flames in Florentine illuminations. The platform began at the corner of the marble terrace before the Old Palace, near the Marzocco, the stone lion whose ancient face frowned upon the fuel stretching obliquely across the square. Flanking the scene were three massive bodies of armed men: five hundred hired soldiers of the Signoria before the palace; five hundred *Compagnacci* under Dolfo Spini on the opposite side; and three hundred armed citizens led by Marco Salviati, Savonarola's ally, guarding the loggia where the Franciscans and Dominicans were to stand. Much money and labor had been expended, involving high dignitaries. The stakes were clear: if the two monks were simply burned, God would speak plainly that Fra Girolamo was not His prophet.

Time was short. Near midday, half the monks were already in position. The half of the loggia facing the palace was filled with grey habits, while the section divided by boards remained empty save for a small altar. The Franciscans had entered in silence. Then, from the other side of the piazza, loud chanting rose from two hundred voices, signaling the arrival of the Dominicans. The repetition of "Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered" struck some impartial ears as a display of arrogant confidence designed to incite dismay. Leading the procession was Fra Domenico, dressed in a flame-colored velvet cope, holding a cross. His simple mind was genuinely exalted by faith, intending truly to enter the flames for the glory of God and Fra Girolamo. Behind him walked Savonarola in white priestly vestments, carrying a vessel with the consecrated Host. Both chanted loudly, appearing firm and confident. Under the scrutiny of eager, anxious, and malignant eyes, Savonarola's countenance flashed with an intensified energy.

Human nature dictates that the false persona we dread in private solitude becomes effortless under the stimulus of expectant audiences. Few possess the innate need to dominate that Savonarola held; this need grows imperious as life complicates the separation of Self from Purpose. Consequently, on the day of the Trial by Fire, the duplicity inherent in public careers was sharper in Savonarola's consciousness than ever before. He struggled not against impending martyrdom, but against impending ruin. Thus, he projected thorough confidence while a leaden weight of foreboding pressed his heart, caused not only by the trial's probable outcome but by a recent event spreading satisfaction through the mind of a man watching him from a window of the Old Palace.

Two evenings prior, news arrived that the Florentine courier of the Ten had been arrested and robbed of his dispatches. Savonarola's letter was now in the hands of the Duke of Milan and soon the Pope's—a development heightening rage and justifying extreme measures. Tito Melema felt no malice toward the friar; it was merely the mild self-gratification of a skilled player winning a game. To Tito, the bundle of desires and contrivances known as human nature, molded into a plain-faced preacher, was not a pathetic figure but a puzzle solved. Fra Girolamo was immensely clever, mixing absurd superstitions with remarkable political notions, capable of keeping secrets. Tito harbored no spite; rather, Fra Girolamo's existence had been conveniently useful, providing Tito with a rung on the ladder from which he intended to leap to a smoother footing. With preparations complete, Tito awaited the setting sun to depart Florence. He had amused himself with today's comedy, a feat the thick-headed Dolfo Spini could never have achieved without his guidance.

The chanting swelled to a deafening roar, taken up by the *Piagnoni*, who carried red crosses and sang prayers for the confusion of God's enemies, expecting a signal response from Fra Domenico. Now kneeling before the altar where the Sacrament rested, the flame-clad friar waited for his summons. Across the loggia, the Franciscans offered only silence and grey robes. However, they held a counterbalance: two champions. Fra Giuliano was paired with Fra Domenico, while the original challenger, Fra Francesco, reserved his challenge solely for Savonarola.

Spectators perched on rods and pillars wondered aloud if all were ready, hoping the chanting would cease so the friars might move toward the platform. But the Dominicans did not advance. Pale Franciscan faces peered anxiously over the boards at the flamboyant cope, suspecting enchantment or magic. A monk might emerge whole, yet it could be the Devil's work. Movement began between the loggia and the palace terrace; the chanting quieted as everyone watched more intently. Yet, this movement proved an obstacle, not a beginning. Dignitaries appointed as moderators entered and exited the palace, debating fiercely with the Franciscans. Finally, it became clear that Fra Domenico was being fetched toward the palace. The fire likely burned unseen, and the miracle was imminent.

No. The flame-colored cope disappeared inside the palace, followed shortly by another Dominican. For a long time, the tedious chanting continued, and Fra Girolamo stood motionless in his white vestment. Eventually, something shifted. Fra Domenico emerged, returned to his brethren, and had changed clothes with a fellow monk, flanked by Franciscans to prevent any further enchantment.

"Aha!" thought the distant spectators, forgetting their hunger and cramps. "Fra Domenico does not enter the fire. It is Fra Girolamo after all. We shall see him move, and if he emerges, we shall have a fine view!"

But Fra Girolamo did not move, save for the gestures accompanying speech. His words were bold, firm, and perhaps ironically remonstrant, like Elijah challenging the priests of Baal. Yet, speech is the most irritating argument for those cramped in limbs and empty in stomachs. Why argue? If the miracle failed, it was Fra Girolamo's fault. He could end the delay by offering himself now that the fuel was ready, just as he had been eager when none existed.

More movement, more debate, and the afternoon slipped away as clouds gathered, casting a chill through the hungry crowd. Now, Fra Domenico insisted on carrying the crucifix into the fire, fearing its profanation. Reluctantly, Savonarola conceded, gaining a concession but immediately placing the vessel with the Host into Fra Domenico's hands. An idea flickered in his mind: perhaps the sacred Mystery could avert the fire's effects in extremis. But his real calculation was different. Taking up the Host, he spoke quietly, as if fulfilling a preordained role: "Since they refuse you entry with the crucifix, my brother, enter simply with the Sacrament."

New horror seized the Franciscans; new resolve hardened in Savonarola. "It was impious presumption to carry the Sacrament into the fire," they protested. "If burned, the scandal among the weak and ignorant would be immense."

"Not at all," Savonarola replied. "Even if burned, only the accidents would be consumed; the substance remains."

Here lay a theological quagmire, elastic and endless, unsuitable for resolution by trial. The only path was for both sides to remain firm: the Franciscans refusing the Host's entry, and Fra Domenico refusing to enter without it. Meanwhile, the darkened clouds and chilling air deepened the gloom. The chanting faded into inaudible argument, replaced by confused murmurs from the piazza. Expectation relaxed, replaced by an irritating sense that nothing decisive would occur. Scattered shouts broke out, then grew frequent and scornful: "Light the fire and drive them in!" "Let us have a smell of roast—we want our dinner!" "Come, Prophet, tell us if anything happens before the twenty-four hours are over!" "What is your last vision?" "He has a dozen visions in his belly; they're the small change for a miracle!" "Where are you, Brother? Stop wasting the fuel!"
