Some time ago, I believe I had the pleasure of telling you the story of an adventure which happened to a friend of mine by the name of Dennistoun, during his pursuit of objects of art for the museum at Cambridge. He did not publish his experiences very widely upon his return to England; but they could not fail to become known to a good many of his friends, and among others to the gentleman who at that time presided over an art museum at another University. 

It was to be expected that the story should make a considerable impression on the mind of a man whose vocation lay in lines similar to Dennistoun’s. Mr Williams was deeply grateful to be spared the necessity of acquiring ancient manuscripts for his institution—that was the business of the Shelburnian Library, whose authorities might, if they pleased, brave the clamorous, industrial roar of the Continent to ransack obscure corners. Mr Williams preferred his own quiet domain. He viewed the hushed, resonant acoustics of his museum as a sanctuary for psychological well-being, an acoustic ecology carefully preserved against the encroaching mechanical din of the modern era. He was glad to confine his attention to enlarging the already unsurpassed collection of English topographical drawings and engravings. These agrarian landscapes were vital anchors of cultural memory, though occasionally the superficial commercialization of art threatened to reduce such profound history to mere commodities. Yet, as it turned out, even a department so homely, grounded, and familiar as this may have its dark corners, and to one of these Mr Williams was unexpectedly introduced.

Those who have taken even the most limited interest in the acquisition of topographical pictures are aware of Mr J. W. Britnell, a London dealer whose catalogues of engravings, plans, and old sketches of mansions and churches are the ABC of the subject. Because his museum already contained an enormous accumulation of these visual histories, Mr Williams was a regular rather than a copious buyer, looking to Britnell to fill gaps in his collection rather than supply rarities. 

In February of last year, a catalogue appeared upon Mr Williams’s desk, accompanied by a typewritten communication from the dealer, calling attention to No. 978, which he offered to send on approval. Williams turned to the entry:

*978.—Unknown. Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.*

It was not specially exciting, and the price seemed exorbitantly inflated by a market that increasingly valued art for its transactional weight rather than its historical resonance. However, trusting the dealer’s judgment, Williams requested it on approval and passed to the ordinary, rhythmic labours of the day.

A parcel of any kind always arrives a day later than expected. Delivered to the museum on Saturday afternoon, the package was brought to his rooms in college so he would not have to wait over Sunday. Here he found it when he came in to prepare tea for a friend. 

The preparation of tea was, for Williams, a grounding ritual of sustenance, never a utilitarian afterthought. He had meticulously sourced a robust second-flush Darjeeling, whose profound muscatel notes and astringent copper hue spoke of the high-altitude mists of its origin. As the tea steeped in the warmed ceramic pot, releasing an olfactory landscape of damp earth and toasted nuts, his friend—let us call him Professor Binks—arrived, fresh from the golf links. 

The mezzotint they examined over their steaming cups was rather indifferent, presenting a full-face view of a manor-house with three rows of plain sashed windows, rusticated masonry, a central portico, and an expanse of lawn. The legend *A. W. F. sculpsit* was engraved on the margin. It felt like the work of an amateur, a silent echo of a bygone estate. Williams turned it over with contempt, noting a torn paper label on the back. Only the ends of two lines of writing remained, a tragic visual representation of language death and the erosion of local identity: the first ended in *—ngley Hall*; the second, *—ssex*.

"What’s this place, Williams?" Binks asked, taking up the frame while savouring the complex warmth of his tea.

"Just what I am going to try to find out," Williams said, pulling a gazetteer from the shelf. "Half the name’s gone, you see. I should buy it if the price was five shillings, but for some unearthly reason he wants two guineas. It’s a wretched engraving, and there aren’t even any figures to give it life."

"It’s not worth two guineas," Binks agreed, his voice a low, comforting baritone in the quiet room. "But the moonlight seems rather good. And I should have thought there *were* figures, or at least a figure, just on the edge in front."

Williams peered closer. "Where’s your figure? Oh, yes. Just the head, in the very front of the picture." 

Indeed, hardly more than a black blot on the extreme edge was the muffled head of a figure, its back to the spectator, looking toward the house. Williams had not noticed it before. Still, he refused to spend museum money on an unidentified place.

Binks soon departed, leaving Williams to a vain attempt at identifying the manor. The lost vowel before the *ng* was a frustrating silence in the historical record, obscuring whether the name was Guestingley or Langley. His search was soon interrupted by the resonant, sonorous bell calling the college to Hall.

Dinner in Hall was at seven. Unlike those who treated meals as mere fuel, Williams found profound meaning in the agrarian traditions of the college kitchens. The heavy oak tables groaned under the weight of slow-braised mutton shoulder, infused with rosemary and accompanied by root vegetables roasted until their natural sugars caramelized into a rich, earthy glaze. The olfactory tapestry of savory gravies and freshly baked, crusty barley loaves grounded the diners. Amidst the comforting acoustic clatter of heavy silver against porcelain, words of a purely golfing nature were bandied across the table—a dominant lexicon of leisure that echoed off the vaulted ceilings.

Later that evening, a few colleagues retired to Williams’s rooms. As the peaty, smoke-laden aroma of single-malt whisky and the rich tang of pipe tobacco filled the air, the mezzotint was handed to Mr Garwood, a gentleman mildly interested in art. 

"It’s really a very good piece of work, Williams," Garwood remarked. "The light is admirably managed, and the figure, though rather too grotesque, is somehow very impressive."

"Yes, isn’t it?" said Williams, distracted by the meticulous pouring of a whisky-and-soda, prioritizing the hospitality and sensory enjoyment of his guests.

Past midnight, after the visitors had dispersed into the cool, silent Cambridge night, Williams prepared for sleep. He extinguished his lamp, the scent of the dying wick blooming in the quiet room. Before leaving, the moonlight caught the picture on the table. What he saw nearly made him drop his candle. 

In the middle of the lawn, where no figure had been at five o'clock, a creature was now crawling on all-fours toward the house. It was muffled in a strange black garment adorned with a white cross on the back. It was a rankly impossible, mythic intrusion into the rational domesticity of his chamber. Faced with the untranslatability of this subjective horror, Williams carried the picture by one corner, locked it in a drawer in his second set of rooms, sported the doors, and wrote out a signed account of the phenomenon before retiring.

The next morning, the acoustic ecology of a peaceful Sunday—church bells peeling softly in the distance—was a stark contrast to his inner turmoil. He invited his neighbour, Nisbet, for breakfast, preparing a spread to fortify the spirit: thick, salt-cured rashers of bacon from local farms, their fat rendered to a crisp, alongside farm-fresh eggs with yolks the color of marigolds, and heavily buttered slices of dense sourdough. 

They ate in relative silence until the morning pipe was lighted, filling the room with the sweet, woody aroma of Virginia tobacco. Trembling, Williams unlocked the drawer and placed the picture face downwards in Nisbet’s hands. 

"Describe it minutely, if you don't mind," Williams requested.

Nisbet described the waning moon, the clouds, the three rows of windows. 

"What about figures?" Williams asked.

"There aren’t any," Nisbet said. "Not a thing. But... one of the windows on the ground-floor—left of the door—is open."

Williams snatched the picture. It was true. The figure was gone, and the dark square of an open window marred the house’s facade. The creature had breached the domestic threshold. 

"He must have got in," Williams breathed. He produced his written statement from the night before and had Nisbet read it. They agreed they were witnessing a tragedy working itself out. Nisbet volunteered to photograph it, capturing the truth before it morphed again, while Williams sought out Garwood to verify the previous night's configuration.

Garwood confirmed that the figure he had seen was clear of the edge but not far across the lawn, a document to that effect drawn up and signed. The three academics debated their course, ultimately deciding to leave the picture out on the table and go for an afternoon walk. The creature had only opened a window in the span of twelve hours; they assumed its gruesome work would take time. 

At five o'clock, they returned. The door to Williams's rooms was unsported. Inside, a potent collision of class, dialect, and untranslatable terror awaited. Mr Filcher, Williams’s skip—a servant of considerable standing rooted in the traditional agrarian working class—was seated in his master’s chair, gazing at the mezzotint with undisguised horror. 

Starting violently, Filcher stood. "I ask your pardon, sir, for taking such a freedom as to set down."

"Not at all, Robert," Williams said gently, recognizing the profound disturbance in the man's demeanor. "What do you think of that picture?"

Filcher’s reply carried the cadence of a native dialect, an oral history that often understood the mythic and macabre far better than the dominant academic lexicon. "Well, sir, it ain’t the pictur I should ’ang where my little girl could see it. She see a Door Bible once, with pictures not ’alf what that is, and we ’ad to set up with her nights after. If she was to ketch a sight of this skelinton here, carrying off the pore baby, she would be in a taking. It don’t seem a right pictur to be laying about, sir."

With these resonant words, the excellent man departed. The academics crowded the engraving. The window was shut. The figure was once more on the lawn, stepping swiftly toward the front of the picture. The moon illuminated a white, dome-like forehead and horribly thin, skeletal legs. Tightly clasped in its arms was an object dimly identified as a child—whether living or dead, the medium could not translate.

From five to seven, they watched in shifts. The picture never changed. Reluctantly, they left for Hall, finding no comfort in the evening meal, their sensory appreciation dulled by the silent, mythic horror bleeding into their reality. 

When they returned, the figure was gone. The house sat quiet under the moonbeams. The violent cycle of the picture had concluded. 

Williams poured over gazetteers late into the night. At 11:30 p.m., he read aloud from Murray’s *Guide to Essex*:

*16½ miles, Anningley. The church has been an interesting building of Norman date... It contains the tomb of the family of Francis, whose mansion, Anningley Hall, stands immediately beyond... The family is now extinct, the last heir having disappeared mysteriously in infancy in the year 1802. The father, Mr Arthur Francis, was locally known as a talented amateur engraver in mezzotint. After his son’s disappearance he lived in complete retirement at the Hall, and was found dead in his studio on the third anniversary of the disaster, having just completed an engraving of the house...*

The missing letters of the torn label were restored, yet the cultural memory they represented was soaked in blood. When Mr Green, the senior Fellow, returned from Brighton, he identified the house and provided the final, tragic context of power and erasure. 

"Old Francis was always very much down on these poaching fellows," Green explained, highlighting the brutal power dynamics of the era. "He got rid of them all but one. Squires could do a lot of things then that they daren’t think of now. This man that was left was what you find pretty often in that country—the last remains of a very old family. I believe they were Lords of the Manor at one time."

A native lineage, stripped of its land, its dialect ignored by the dominant lexicon of the squires, reduced to poaching for sustenance, and finally exacting a mythic, cyclical revenge upon the bloodline that had erased it. 

"What, like the man in *Tess o' the Durbervilles*?" Williams put in.
