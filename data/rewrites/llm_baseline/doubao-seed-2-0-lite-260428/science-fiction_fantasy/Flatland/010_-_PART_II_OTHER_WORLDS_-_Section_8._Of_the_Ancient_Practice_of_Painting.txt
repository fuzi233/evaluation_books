If you’ve kept pace with my accounts of life in two-dimensional Flatland, you won’t be shocked to hear that day-to-day existence can be gratingly dull on the aesthetic front. Don’t get me wrong—we have our fair share of clashing border skirmishes, shadowy political conspiracies, rioting street factions, and all the chaotic spectacle that fills the pages of your Spaceland history books. Even better, our lives are stitched through with a weird, thrilling blend of everyday practical problems and mathematical puzzles that let us test wild guesses against hard, provable fact moments after we make them—a rush you three-dimensional folks can barely imagine. But strip all that away, and when you look at our world through an artistic, creative lens? It’s unbearably, painfully plain.
How could it be anything else? Every landscape, every historical mural, every portrait, every blooming wildflower, every still life of household goods—all of it unfurls as nothing more than a single straight line, the only variation between them being different degrees of light and shadow.

But this flat, monochrome drudgery wasn’t our lot forever. Old, unbroken tradition tells us that for six full centuries and more, color spilled bright, transient splendor across the lives of our most distant ancestors. The first person to unlock that magic was a private citizen, a Pentagon whose name gets muddled in different surviving texts—he stumbled on the formula for simple pigments and a rough, workable painting method by total accident, so the story goes. First he daubed color on the walls of his house, then on the edges of his domestic servants, then on his elderly father, his sons, his grandsons, before finally painting his own edges.
The results were every bit as practical as they were beautiful, and the whole country fell in love with the idea almost overnight. The most reliable historical records all agree on his name: Chromatistes. Wherever his newly multicolored polygon frame drifted, every eye turned to him, brimming with instant respect. No one had to reach out and feel him to know what he was, no one ever mixed up his front edge with his back edge, his neighbors could track every movement he made without expending a single scrap of mental math effort. No one jostled him in the street, everyone automatically stepped aside to let him pass. Even his voice got a break from the exhausting, constant work that us modern colorless Squares and Pentagons still have to do, shouting our unique shapes out loud every time we push through a crowd of uneducated, sharp-angled Isosceles workers to prove who we are.

The trend spread faster than wildfire. Before the week was out, every Square and Triangle in Chromatistes’ home district was copying his example, only a handful of deeply traditional, stuffy old Pentagons refusing to touch paint. A month or two later, even the sleek 12-sided Dodecagon nobles had caught the innovation bug. Before a full year rolled around, the custom had spread to every class in Flatland except the absolute highest tier of the aristocracy. It didn’t take long for the fad to leak past the borders of Chromatistes’ home district, either. Within two full generations, every single resident of Flatland wore color on their edges—every single one, that is, except the Women and the Priests.

Nature herself seemed to throw up a hard barrier here, begging the population not to extend the new paint custom to these two specific groups. The folks pushing the color revolution had latched onto a convenient sophism: “Nature designed distinct sides to correspond to distinct colors.” That catchphrase spread from mouth to mouth faster than gossip, converting entire towns to the new colorful way of life in days. But for our Priests and our Women, that rule didn’t hold water. Our Women are straight lines—so to be pedantic, plural, formal about it, they have zero sides at all. The Priests, meanwhile, are supposed to be perfect Circles, not just very high-sided polygons with infinitesimally tiny edges. They love to brag (a point our Women openly admit and bemoan) that they also have no sides at all: their whole shape is just one single smooth perimeter, one unbroken curved circumference.
So neither of these two classes could see any logic in that so-called universal axiom about distinct sides demanding distinct colors. When every other group in the realm had caved to the thrill of decorating their own bodies, the Priests and the Women alone stayed completely free of paint’s supposed “pollution.”

Call that era immoral, licentious, anarchic, unscientific—use every harsh label you want. But from an artistic, aesthetic point of view, the days of the Great Colour Revolt were the glorious, giddy childhood of all art in Flatland. A childhood, sad to say, that never grew to maturity, never even bloomed into adolescence. Back then, just being alive was a delight, because living meant you could instantly see who and what everyone around you was. Even a small dinner party was a feast for the eyes. The riot of rich, varied hues at a packed church service or public theatre was so distracting, we have records of our greatest orators and actors stumbling over their lines mid-speech, too busy staring at the crowd. But nothing, the old tales say, ever matched the unspeakable, staggering magnificence of a full military review.
Imagine a 20,000-strong line of Isosceles soldiers, all turning in perfect unison at once, swapping the flat, inky black of their wide bases for the bright orange and purple of the two sharp sides that met at their tiny acute angle. Picture the Equilateral Triangle militia, decked out in bold tricolor red, white, and blue. Imagine the Square artillerymen, their edges painted mauve, ultramarine, gamboge, and burnt umber, spinning fast beside their vermilion-colored cannons. Then there’s the flash and dash of five-colored Pentagons and six-colored Hexagons, their edges streaked with bright distinct hues, careering across the parade field in their roles as field surgeons, battle geometricians, and aides-de-camp.
It’s no wonder the famous old legend feels so plausible: the story of a high-ranking, illustrious Circle commander, so overcome by the sheer artistic beauty of the forces under his command, that he tossed his marshal’s baton and his royal crown aside, shouting that he was trading both forever for an artist’s paintbrush. The sensuous, creative richness of that long-lost era still echoes down to us today, in our very language and vocabulary. The most throwaway lines of the most common working citizen in the Colour Revolt era were steeped in richer, more vivid wordplay and imagery, and to that far-off golden age we still owe all our finest surviving poetry, and every scrap of beautiful rhythm that lingers even in the dry, precise scientific writing of our modern day.
