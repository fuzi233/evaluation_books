The air inside the Academy of Music was thick with the scent of beeswax and suppressed anxiety, a heavy atmosphere that clung to the red and gold upholstery of the boxes. Outside, the snow fell in a relentless, white silence, muffling the city's frantic pulse, while inside, the world of fashion reassembled itself around the familiar, shabby grandeur of the old institution. It was a sanctuary for conservatives who prized its inconvenience as a shield against the "new people" rising in the distant districts beyond the Forty-second Street, and for sentimentalists who clung to its ghosts, and musicians who tolerated its flawed acoustics as a necessary tax on tradition.

Christine Nilsson sang. Her voice, a brilliant instrument cutting through the hush, drifted down from the stage where the garden scene unfolded. The audience, transported through private carriages and humble Brown coupes alike, leaned forward in a collective suspension of disbelief. Newland Archer stood at the rear of the club box, his posture relaxed, a deliberate delay in his movements. He had dined alone with his mother and sister hours prior, lingering over a cigar in the Gothic library—a room defined by its black walnut bookcases and the singular rule that permitted smoke. Yet, in New York, punctuality was a performance; to arrive too early was to break the rhythm of the evening. Furthermore, Newland was a dilettante by nature, deriving a sharper, more refined pleasure from anticipating a delight than from consuming it.

He watched the stage. Madame Nilsson, dressed in white cashmere slashed with pale blue satin, performed the Daisy Song. She sang of love and rejection, translating the German text into the fluid Italian required by convention, her large eyes fixed on the disheveled Faust, Capoul, who struggled in his purple velvet to appear pure amidst his artifice. As she reached the climax—"M'ama!"—she pressed a daisy to her lips, her voice triumphant.

Newland's gaze shifted from the stage to the opposite side of the house. There, in the box of the Mingotts, sat a tableau of brocaded matrons and a young girl in white. Her name was May Welland. A flush of pink mounted her cheeks, spreading from her chin to the roots of her fair braids, illuminating the modest slope of her breast where a single gardenia pinned a tulle tucker. Her eyes, ecstatic and fixed on the lovers, softened as she glanced down at the immense bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley resting on her lap. Her gloved fingers touched the stems gently, a gesture of innocent absorption.

Newland breathed out, a sigh of satisfied vanity mixing with a tender reverence. He saw in her an abysmal purity, untouched by the complexities of the world he inhabited. "We'll read Faust together... by the Italian lakes..." the thought floated through his mind, a hazily constructed vision of a future where he would guide her through the masterpieces of literature, revealing the world to her as a man reveals a treasure. Only that afternoon had May hinted that she "cared"—the consecrated phrase of maiden avowal—and now his imagination raced ahead of the engagement ring and the march from Lohengrin, picturing her beside him in scenes of old European witchery.

He did not wish for her to remain a simpleton forever. His plan was to cultivate in her a social tact, a readiness of wit that would allow her to navigate the "younger set," attracting homage while playfully deflecting it. Deep down, perhaps dangerously so, he wished for her to possess the worldly wisdom of the married woman whose charms had once captivated him, though he carefully excluded any trace of the frailty that had ruined that previous life. How such a creature of fire and ice might survive the harsh realities of New York society was a problem he had never bothered to solve; he preferred to hold his idealized view without dissecting it, trusting that it aligned with the consensus of the other gentlemen in the box—their white waistcoats, their buttonholes, their shared doctrine of morality.

Suddenly, Lawrence Lefferts, the arbiter of "form" in New York, broke the spell. With a sharp turn of his head, he lowered his opera-glass, his expression shifting from appreciation to scrutiny. From the slant of his bald forehead to the careless elegance of his patent-leather shoes, Lefferts embodied the knowledge of propriety as if it were written in his bones. "My God!" he exclaimed, handing his glass to Old Sillerton Jackson.

Newland followed the direction of Lefferts's gaze. Standing in the center of the Mingott box was a new figure: a slim young woman, slightly shorter than May, with brown hair curled tightly against her temples and secured by a narrow band of diamonds. The "Josephine look" suggested by her headdress was echoed in the dramatic gathering of her dark blue velvet gown under a girdle with a large, antique clasp. She seemed unaware of the attention she commanded, pausing briefly to discuss seating arrangements with Mrs. Welland before yielding her spot to sit alongside Mrs. Lovell Mingott.

Mr. Jackson returned the opera-glass to Lefferts, and the entire club box turned in unison, waiting for the verdict. Jackson was the keeper of New York's genealogy, a living map of cousinships and forbidden connections, from the stingy Leffertses of Long Island to the mentally unstable Chiverses of Albany. Between his silver hair and hollow temples, he carried the weight of fifty years of scandals and secrets, including the mysterious disappearance of handsome Bob Spicer and the tragic fate of Medora Manson. He scrutinized the newcomers with his filmy blue eyes, his expression unreadable, before giving his moustache a thoughtful twist.

"I didn't think the Mingotts would have tried it on," he said simply. The sentence hung in the air, heavy with implication, sealing the moment in a silence that matched the falling snow outside.

Newland Archer found himself suspended in a peculiar suspension of embarrassment. It was an irritating anomaly that the box commanding the undivided attention of masculine New York should contain his betrothed, seated between her mother and aunt. For a fleeting instant, the figure in the Empire dress remained an indistinct silhouette, a mystery he could not solve until clarity descended like a sudden, sharp light. With it came a rush of indignation, however brief. No, certainly; no one would have presumed the Mingotts would attempt such a maneuver. Yet they had. The low murmurs drifting from the corridor confirmed it beyond doubt: the young woman was May Welland's cousin, the perpetual subject of pity known to the family as "poor Ellen Olenska."

Archer knew she had arrived from Europe only days prior. He had even heard from Miss Welland—a report delivered without disapproval—that she had visited her cousin, who was currently residing with the elderly Mrs. Mingott. Archer possessed a genuine approval for familial solidarity; one of the traits he most revered in the Mingotts was their resolute defense of the few black sheep their otherwise impeccable lineage had produced. There was nothing base or stingy in the young man's heart. He was content that his future wife should not be shackled by false prudery from offering kindness, in private, to her unfortunate relative. To receive Countess Olenska within the domestic sphere was one thing; to parade her publicly at the Opera, specifically in the box occupied by the very girl whose engagement to him was imminent, was quite another.

No, he felt precisely as old Sillerton Jackson must feel: the Mingotts would never have tried it on. Of course, he acknowledged that whatever a man dared within the bounds of Fifth Avenue, old Mrs. Manson Mingott—the Matriarch of the line—would inevitably dare. He had always held admiration for the formidable old lady. Despite her origins as Catherine Spicer of Staten Island, a background marked by a father mysteriously discredited and a lack of wealth sufficient to erase that stain, she had allied herself with the head of the wealthy Mingott dynasty. She had married two of her daughters to "foreigners"—an Italian marquis and an English banker—and capped her audacity by constructing a massive house of pale cream-colored stone in an inaccessible wilderness near Central Park, a stark contrast to the brown sandstone that served as the only acceptable attire for afternoon wear.

Old Mrs. Mingott's foreign daughters had become a local legend. They never returned to visit their mother. And the mother, like many minds of active will and dominating nature, remained sedentary and corpulent at home, philosophically accepting her confinement. Yet the cream-colored mansion, supposedly modeled after the private hotels of the Parisian aristocracy, stood as tangible proof of her moral courage. Within it, surrounded by pre-Revolutionary furniture and souvenirs of the Tuileries under Louis Napoleon—where she had shone in her middle age—she reigned as placidly as if living above Thirty-fourth Street or possessing French windows that swung open rather than sliding up were entirely normal phenomena.

Every observer, including Mr. Sillerton Jackson, concurred that old Catherine lacked beauty—a gift that, in New York's eyes, justified every success and excused numerous failures. The unkind whispered that, like her Imperial namesake, she had secured her triumphs through sheer force of will, a hardness of heart, and a haughty effrontery that was somehow sanctified by the extreme decency and dignity of her private life. Mr. Manson Mingott had died when she was merely twenty-eight, having "tied up" the money with an additional caution born of the general distrust of the Spicers. But his bold young widow proceeded fearlessly. She mingled freely in foreign societies, married her daughters into heavens-know-what corrupt and fashionable circles, hobnobbed with Dukes and Ambassadors, associated familiarly with Papists, entertained opera singers, and maintained intimate friendships with figures like Mme. Taglioni. All the while, as Sillerton Jackson was the first to proclaim, there had never been a breath against her reputation. The only distinction, he noted, lay in the fact that unlike the earlier Catherine, Mrs. Manson Mingott had successfully untied her husband's fortune and lived in affluence for half a century.

However, memories of her early straits had rendered her excessively thrifty. While she ensured that any dress or piece of furniture purchased was of the highest quality, she could not bring herself to expend much on the transient pleasures of the table. Thus, for entirely different reasons, her food matched Mrs. Archer's in poverty, and her wines failed to redeem either. Her relatives deemed this penury a discredit to the Mingott name, historically synonymous with good living. Yet people continued to flock to her despite the "made dishes" and flat champagne. In response to the remonstrances of her son Lovell—who attempted to restore family credit by employing the finest chef in New York—she would laugh and declare, "What's the use of two good cooks in one family, now that I've married the girls and can't eat sauces?"

As Newland Archer mused on these matters, he turned his gaze back to the Mingott box. He observed that Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law faced their semicircle of critics with the Mingottian aplomb instilled in them by old Catherine. Only May Welland betrayed, through a heightened flush—perhaps exacerbated by the knowledge that he watched her—a sense of the situation's gravity. As for the source of the commotion, she sat gracefully in her corner, eyes fixed on the stage. As she leaned forward, she revealed a fraction more shoulder and bosom than New York was accustomed to witnessing in ladies who wished to remain invisible.

Few things seemed more awful to Newland Archer than an offense against "Taste," that distant divinity of whom "Form" was merely the visible representative. Madame Olenska's pale, serious face appealed to his imagination as suitable for the occasion and her unhappy circumstances. But the manner in which her dress, lacking a tucker, sloped away from her thin shoulders shocked and troubled him. He hated the thought of May Welland being exposed to the influence of a young woman so careless of the dictates of Taste.

"After all," he heard a younger voice begin behind him, cutting through the Mephistopheles-and-Martha scenes, "just what happened?"

"Well—she left him; nobody attempts to deny that."

"He's an awful brute, isn't he?" continued the young inquirer, a candid Thorley, clearly preparing to enter the lists as the lady's champion.

"The very worst; I knew him at Nice," said Lawrence Lefferts with authority. "A half-paralysed white sneering fellow—rather handsome head, but eyes with a lot of lashes. Well, I'll tell you the sort: when he wasn't with women he was collecting china. Paying any price for both, I understand."

There was a general laugh, and the young champion pressed on. "Well, then—"

"Well, then; she bolted with his secretary."

"Oh, I see." The champion's face fell. "It didn't last long, though: I heard of her a few months later living alone in Venice. I believe Lovell Mingott went out to get her. He said she was desperately unhappy. That's all right—but this parading her at the Opera's another thing."

"Perhaps," young Thorley hazarded, "she's too unhappy to be left at home."

This was greeted with an irreverent laugh, and the youth blushed deeply, attempting to look as though he had intended to insinuate what the cognoscenti called a "double entendre."

"Well—it's queer to have brought Miss Welland, anyhow," someone said in a low tone, casting a side-glance at Archer.

"Oh, that's part of the campaign: Granny's orders, no doubt," Lefferts laughed. "When the old lady does a thing she does it thoroughly."

The act concluded, and a general stir rippled through the box. Suddenly, Newland Archer felt himself impelled to decisive action. The desire to be the first to enter Mrs. Mingott's box, to proclaim to the waiting world his engagement to May Welland, and to stand beside her through whatever difficulties her cousin's anomalous situation might entail, had abruptly overruled all scruples and hesitations. It sent him hurrying through the red corridors to the farther side of the house.

Upon entering the box, his eyes met Miss Welland's. He saw that she had instantly understood his motive, though the family dignity both considered a supreme virtue prevented her from voicing it. The persons of their world lived in an atmosphere of faint implications and pale delicacies, and the fact that he and she understood each other without a word seemed to the young man to bring them nearer than any explanation ever could. Her eyes said: "You see why Mamma brought me," and his answered silently: "I would not for the world have had you stay away."

"You know my niece Countess Olenska?" Mrs. Welland enquired as she shook hands with her future son-in-law.

Archer bowed without extending his hand, adhering to the custom of introduction to a lady; and Ellen Olenska bent her head slightly, keeping her own pale-gloved hands clasped on her huge fan of eagle feathers. After greeting Mrs. Lovell Mingott, a large blonde lady in creaking satin, he sat down beside his betrothed and said in a low tone, "I hope you've told Madame Olenska that we're engaged? The pattern held fast, immutable as the architecture of the house itself. On the night of the annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort invariably appeared at the Opera, a ritual designed to signal her total emancipation from domestic drudgery and her command over a staff capable of orchestrating chaos in her absence. The Beaufort mansion stood as a monument to this separation; it possessed a ballroom, a relic predating even the Mingotts and the Chiverses, a cavern of gilt chairs stacked in corners and chandeliers swaddled in dark cloth, dormant for three hundred and sixty-four days of the year. This architectural excess compensated for the shadows of the Beaufort past. Mrs. Archer, architect of social maxims, had once declared, "We all have our pet common people," a sentiment that, however daring, found a guilty admission in many an exclusive heart. Yet the Beauforts transcended the label of "common"; whispers suggested they were worse. Mrs. Beaufort was Regina Dallas, a Southern beauty rendered penniless by circumstance, introduced to New York by her cousin Medora Manson, whose motives were always noble but whose actions perpetually disastrous. In a city where lineage granted a *droit de cité*, the marriage to Julius Beaufort seemed a forfeiture of status. Who was he? An Englishman, agreeable and handsome, yet ill-tempered and bitter, arriving with recommendations from an old banker and quickly ascending the ladder of worldly power despite dissipated habits and mysterious origins. When Medora announced the engagement, it was dismissed as another act of Medora's folly. But folly, like wisdom, bears fruit. Two years later, the miracle was undeniable: the Beauforts owned the most distinguished house in New York. How it happened remained a mystery. Indolent, passive, labeled dull by the caustic, she reigned in her brownstone palace, draped in pearls and growing younger with each season, drawing the entire world without lifting a jeweled finger. The knowing observed that Beaufort himself managed the machinery—the training of servants, the selection of flowers, the brewing of punch, the drafting of notes—while presenting himself to the world as a carefree millionaire strolling into his own drawing-room with the detached amusement of a guest. "My wife's gloxinias are a marvel," he would say, unaware that the flowers were his doing. He carried rumors of his questionable English departure with the ease of a man carrying a hat, sweeping them aside until New York itself swept them into his drawing rooms. For over twenty years, invitations to the Beauforts carried the same weight of security as visits to the Mingotts, promising hot canvas-back ducks and vintage wines rather than tepid champagne and reheated croquettes.

As usual, Mrs. Beaufort appeared in her box just before the Jewel Song. When she rose at the end of the third act, wrapping her opera cloak around her lovely shoulders to vanish, New York knew the clock had struck for the ball. Their house was a badge of pride for foreigners, especially on this night. They were pioneers: the first to roll their own red velvet carpet down the steps under their own awning, the first to allow ladies to remove their cloaks in the hall rather than shuffle to the hostess's chamber to re-curl their hair—a courtesy attributed to Beaufort's assumption that all his wife's friends possessed maids for such tasks. The house was boldly planned, featuring a grand vista of enfiladed drawing rooms—sea-green, crimson, and bouton d'or—leading to a conservatory where camellias and tree-ferns arched their costly foliage over seats of black and gold bamboo.

Newland Archer arrived late, as befits a young man of his station. Archer was distinctly nervous. He had not retreated to his club after the Opera, as the young bloods usually did, but had walked up Fifth Avenue in the fine night before returning. He feared the Mingotts might be pushing too hard, perhaps acting on Granny Mingott's orders to bring the Countess Olenska to the ball. The gravity of such a mistake had been clear in the tone of the club box earlier. Though determined to "see the thing through," his chivalrous eagerness to champion his betrothed's cousin had waned since their brief exchange at the Opera.

Archer paused. It was his express wish that the announcement be made, yet he would have preferred a different stage. The next day, the ritual unfolded with the rigid precision of a clockwork mechanism. Newland Archer accompanied his mother and sister to pay their respects to Mrs. Welland, and subsequently, the trio drove to the ancestral estate of Mrs. Manson Mingott. For Archer, this visit was a familiar performance, a scripted encounter with history itself. The Mingott house was a document of its era, distinct from the grim, harmonious architecture of University Place—a deliberate rejection of the heavy mahogany and cabbage-rose garlands of the 1830s in favor of the frivolous, Second Empire upholstery that defined her taste.

Mrs. Mingott presided over the ground floor, seated in a window as if observing the slow, inevitable march of civilization toward her solitary doors. She possessed a patience matched only by her certainty that the chaotic sprawl of the city—the hoardings, the quarries, the ragged gardens—would eventually yield to the stately order of her own domain. She viewed her geographic isolation not as a deficit, but as a strategic advantage; she filled her rooms with visitors without expanding her household budget. Time had transformed her physically, burying her former vitality beneath an immense accumulation of flesh, turning her into a vast, natural phenomenon. Yet, she accepted this metamorphosis with philosophical indifference, presenting to the mirror an expanse of firm, pink skin where only the ghost of a small face awaited excavation. Her reception rooms, located upstairs, were accessible only through a breach in propriety: a door left ajar revealed a bedroom with a low, sofa-like bed and a vanity table, a layout that suggested the architectural incentives to immorality found in French fiction rather than American convention.

Archer found amusement in imagining her blameless life staged within a set designed for adultery, convinced that if a lover had existed to match her desires, the intrepid woman would have claimed him. Fortunately, the Countess Olenska was absent during the betrothal visit. Mrs. Mingott dismissed her absence lightly, noting she had gone out shopping—a detail that seemed indelicate for a woman of her reputation, yet conveniently spared the guests the shadow of her unhappy past.

The next evening, Old Mr. Sillerton Jackson arrived to dine with the Archers.

Mrs. Archer was a woman of shrinking temperament, retreating instinctively from the noise of society, yet she possessed a voracious hunger for its mechanics. She treated the investigation of her neighbors' lives with the patient taxonomy of a collector and the rigorous classification of a naturalist. Her sister, Miss Sophy Jackson, who resided with him and was tolerated by everyone else solely because they could not secure the company of her formidable brother, supplied the minor gossip fragments necessary to fill the gaps in his data sets.

Consequently, whenever Mrs. Archer required information, she issued a summons for Mr. Jackson to dine. Since she honored few guests with such invitations, and since she and her daughter Janey constituted an exemplary audience, Mr. Jackson invariably attended personally rather than delegating to his sister. Ideally, if he held the power of dictation, he would have selected evenings when Newland was absent—not because the young man was disagreeable (they operated with capital efficiency at their club)—but because the old chronicler occasionally sensed in Newland a propensity to scrutinize his evidence with a severity the ladies of the household never displayed.

If perfection were achievable on earth, Mr. Jackson would also have stipulated that Mrs. Archer's provisions be superior. Yet New York, as far as the collective consciousness could trace, had bifurcated into two fundamental clans: the Mingotts and Mansons and their progeny, obsessed with sustenance, attire, and currency; and the Archer-Newland-van-der-Luyden lineage, devoted to travel, horticulture, and high fiction, regarding the grosser pleasures of the table with disdain.

One cannot possess all advantages. Dining with the Lovell Mingotts yielded canvas-back ducks, terrapin, and vintage vintages; conversing at Adeline Archer's offered discourse on Alpine topography and *The Marble Faun*; fortunately, the Archer Madeira had circumnavigated the Cape. Thus, when a friendly summons arrived from Mrs. Archer, Mr. Jackson, a true eclectician, would typically inform his sister: "I have suffered a bout of gout since my last repast at the Lovell Mingotts'; it will serve me well to diet at Adeline's."

Mrs. Archer, a widow for many years, occupied an upper floor of West Twenty-eighth Street with her son and daughter. A separate wing was allocated to Newland, while the two women compressed themselves into narrower quarters below. In a cloudless harmony of aesthetic sensibilities, they cultivated ferns within Wardian cases, constructed macramé lace and wool embroidery on linen, amassed collections of American Revolutionary glazed ware, subscribed to *Good Words*, and consumed Ouida's novels for the atmospheric quality of their Italian settings. (They favored tales of peasant life for their scenic descriptions and benevolent sentiments, though generally preferring narratives of society where motives were transparent; they criticized Dickens harshly for failing to capture the essence of a gentleman, and deemed Thackeray less at ease in the grand world than Bulwer, who, however, was increasingly viewed as antiquated.) Both Mrs. and Miss Archer were ardent devotees of landscape. It was the primary object of their pursuit and admiration during their sporadic excursions abroad; they relegated architecture and painting to the domain of men, specifically scholars who read Ruskin.

Mrs. Archer was born a Newland. Mother and daughter, resembling sisters in every feature, were universally acknowledged as "true Newlands": tall, pallid, and slightly hunched, with elongated noses, sweet smiles, and a specific drooping distinction reminiscent of faded Reynolds portraits. Their physical similarity would have been total were it not for an elderly corpulence that strained Mrs. Archer's black brocade, whereas Miss Archer's brown and purple poplins hung with increasing slackness upon her virgin frame as the years advanced.

Mentally, the resemblance was less absolute than their identical mannerisms suggested. The prolonged habit of cohabitation in mutual dependency had endowed them with a shared lexicon and the reflexive habit of prefacing opinions with "Mother thinks" or "Janey thinks," depending on which party sought to assert dominance. In reality, while Mrs. Archer's serene unimaginativeness found comfort in the accepted and familiar, Janey was susceptible to sudden aberrations of fancy, springing from wells of suppressed romance.

Mother and daughter adored one another and revered their son and brother. Archer loved them with a tenderness rendered compunctious and uncritical by the weight of their exaggerated admiration, bolstered by his secret satisfaction in it. After all, he reasoned, it was beneficial for a man to have his authority respected within his own household, even if his sense of humor occasionally led him to question the force of his mandate.

This evening, the young man was convinced that Mr. Jackson would prefer he dine elsewhere, yet he had his own rationales for remaining.

Certainly, Old Jackson desired to discuss Ellen Olenska, and certainly Mrs. Archer and Janey craved his insights. All three would experience a degree of embarrassment given Newland's presence, now that his prospective alliance with the Mingott clan had been announced; the young man awaited with amused curiosity to observe how they would navigate this friction.

They commenced, obliquely, with Mrs. Lemuel Struthers.

"It is a pity the Beauforts invited her," Mrs. Archer said softly. "Yet Regina invariably obeys his commands; and *Beaufort*—"

"Certain nuances elude Beaufort," Mr. Jackson observed, cautiously examining the broiled shad and wondering for the thousandth time why Mrs. Archer's cook invariably charred the roe to ash. (Newland, having long shared this observation, could always detect it in the elder man's expression of melancholic disapproval.)

"Oh, necessarily; Beaufort is a vulgar man," Mrs. Archer conceded. "My grandfather Newland always instructed my mother: 'Whatever you do, do not introduce that fellow Beaufort to the girls.' But at least he has the advantage of association with gentlemen; in England, they say. It is all quite mysterious..." She glanced at Janey and paused. She and Janey knew every crease of the Beaufort enigma, yet in public, Mrs. Archer maintained the pretense that the subject was inappropriate for the unmarried.

"But this Mrs. Struthers," Mrs. Archer resumed; "what did you say *she* was, Sillerton?"

"From a mine: or rather, from the saloon at the head of the pit. Subsequently, with Living Wax-Works, touring New England. After the police dismantled *that* enterprise, they claim she resided—" Mr. Jackson, in his turn, glanced at Janey, whose eyes began to bulge beneath their prominent lids. There remained hiatuses in her understanding of Mrs. Struthers's past.

"Then," Mr. Jackson continued (and Archer noted he was pondering why no one had instructed the butler never to slice cucumbers with a steel blade), "then Lemuel Struthers appeared. They say his advertiser utilized the girl's head for shoe-polish posters; her hair is intensely black, you know—the Egyptian style. In any case, he... eventually... married her." There were volumes of innuendo in the spacing of the word "eventually," each syllable receiving its due emphasis.

"Oh, well—in the pass we have reached nowadays, it matters not," Mrs. Archer stated indifferently. The ladies were not genuinely interested in Mrs. Struthers at that moment; the subject of Ellen Olenska was too fresh and too absorbing. Indeed, Mrs. Struthers's name had been introduced merely to facilitate Mrs. Archer's subsequent query: "And Newland's new cousin—the Countess Olenska? Was *she* at the ball as well?"

A faint touch of sarcasm colored the reference to her son, and Archer perceived it, having anticipated it. Even Mrs. Archer, rarely overly pleased with human events, had been wholly gratified by her son's engagement. ("Especially following that silly affair with Mrs. Rushworth," she had remarked to Janey, alluding to what had once seemed to Newland a tragedy bearing a permanent scar upon his soul.)

There existed no superior match in New York than May Welland, regardless of the angle of examination. Naturally, such a union was precisely what Newland was entitled to; yet young men are so foolish and incalculable—and some women so ensnaring and unscrupulous—that it was nothing short of a miracle to see one's only son survive the Siren Isle and reach the harbor of blameless domesticity.

All this Mrs. Archer felt, and her son knew she felt; but he also knew she had been disturbed by the premature announcement of his engagement, or rather by its cause. It was for this reason—that on the whole he was a tender and indulgent master—that he had remained at home that evening. "It is not that I do not approve of the Mingotts' *esprit de corps*," Mrs. Archer grumbled to Janey, the sole witness of her slight deviations from perfect sweetness; "but why Newland's engagement should be entangled with that Olenska woman's comings and goings, I fail to comprehend."

She had conducted herself beautifully—and in beautiful conduct she was unsurpassed—during the call upon Mrs. Welland; yet Newland knew (and his betrothed undoubtedly surmised) that throughout the visit, she and Janey had been nervously vigilant for Madame Olenska's potential intrusion; and when they departed the house together, she had permitted herself to remark to her son: "I am thankful that Augusta Welland received us alone."

Newland Archer ascended the stairs to his study, the movement deliberate, a physical manifestation of the weight settling upon him. The room waited for him, preserved in a state of suspended animation by a vigilant hand that tended the fire and trimmed the lamp. Rows of books lined the shelves, silent sentinels alongside bronze and steel statuettes of fencers on the mantelpiece and photographs of famous paintings. It was a sanctuary, meticulously curated to feel singularly home-like, yet as he sank into the leather armchair near the hearth, the atmosphere shifted.

His gaze landed on a large photograph of May Welland. It was a gift from the early days of their courtship, a token that had since usurped all other portraits on the table. He studied the image with a growing, unsettling awe: the frank forehead, the serious eyes, the gay, innocent mouth. This was the face of the young creature whose soul he was destined to guard. Yet, staring back at him through the familiar features, he saw something else—a terrifying product of the social system he inhabited and revered. The girl who knew nothing and expected everything looked like a stranger. Marriage, he realized with a jolt, was not the safe anchorage his upbringing had promised; it was a voyage onto uncharted seas.

The memory of the Countess Olenska churned through his mind, stirring old convictions until they drifted dangerously. His own declaration—"Women should be free"—struck the root of a problem his world insisted did not exist. "Nice" women, regardless of grievance, would never claim the specific liberty he envisioned. Generous men like himself were merely performing a chivalrous charade, using verbal generosity to mask the iron grip of convention. Here, he was pledged to defend conduct on behalf of his betrothed's cousin that, if committed by his own wife, would invite the thunders of Church and State. The dilemma was theoretical, absurd in its application to his station, yet Newland Archer possessed an imagination that refused to dismiss the possibility. In his union with May, the tie might gall for reasons far less gross than those affecting a Polish nobleman.

He considered what they truly knew of one another. As a "decent" fellow, he concealed his past; as a marriageable girl, she had none to hide. What if, driven by subtle, unseen currents, they tired of each other? He surveyed the marriages of his peers—the supposedly happy unions—and found none that resembled the passionate comradeship he had pictured. Such a vision presupposed experience, versatility, and freedom of judgment, qualities May had been carefully trained not to possess. A shiver of foreboding ran through him. He saw his future marriage crystallizing into what surrounded him: a dull association of material and social interests, cemented by ignorance on one side and hypocrisy on the other.

Lawrence Lefferts surfaced in his thoughts as the archetype of this enviable ideal. As the high priest of form, Lefferts had shaped his wife entirely to his convenience. During his frequent affairs with other men's wives, she wandered about in smiling unconsciousness, claiming "Lawrence was so frightfully strict." She blushed indignantly when anyone mentioned Julius Beaufort's "other establishment," a foreigner of doubtful origin. Archer tried to comfort himself with the notion that he was not quite such an ass as Larry Lefferts, nor May such a simpleton as poor Gertrude. But the distinction was one of intelligence, not of moral standards.

They all existed in a hieroglyphic world where the real thing was never spoken, acted, or even thought, but only represented by arbitrary signs. Mrs. Welland knew precisely why Archer had urged her to announce her daughter's engagement at the Beaufort ball, yet she felt compelled to simulate reluctance, feigning that her hand had been forced. It was akin to the savage bride dragged from her parents' tent, shrieking, as described in the books on primitive man that cultured people were beginning to read. The result was that the young girl at the center of this elaborate mystification remained the most inscrutable for her very frankness. She was frank because she had nothing to conceal; she was assured because she knew of nothing to fear. With no better preparation, she was to be plunged overnight into the facts of life.

Newland was sincerely, placidly in love. He delighted in her radiant beauty, her health, her horsemanship, her grace, and the shy intellectual curiosity blooming under his guidance. She had progressed far enough to ridicule the *Idyls of the King*, though not yet to grasp the beauty of *Ulysses* or the *Lotus Eaters*. She was straightforward, loyal, brave, possessing a humor proven chiefly by her laughter at his jokes. He suspected, deep within her innocently gazing soul, a glow of feeling that would be a joy to awaken.

Yet, when he completed this circuit of observation, he returned discouraged. All this frankness and innocence was merely an artificial construct. Untrained human nature was not innocent; it was bristling with the twists and defenses of instinctive guile. He felt oppressed by this creation of factitious purity, cunningly manufactured by a conspiracy of mothers, aunts, grandmothers, and long-dead ancestresses. It was produced because it was what he wanted, what he had a right to demand, so that he might exercise his lordly pleasure in smashing it like an image made of snow.

These reflections carried a certain triteness, habitual to young men approaching their wedding day. Usually, they were accompanied by a sense of compunction and self-abasement, a regret that he lacked a blank page to offer his bride in exchange for her unblemished one. Newland felt no such trace. He could not deplore, as Thackeray's heroes often did, that he had not offered a slate of white paper. He could not escape the fact that if he had been raised as she had, they would have been no more fit to navigate the world than the Babes in the Wood. Nor could he, despite his anxious cogitations, discern any honest reason—unconnected to his own momentary pleasure or the passion of masculine vanity—why his bride should not have been granted the same freedom of experience as himself.

Such questions drifted through his mind at such an hour, inevitable and persistent. Their uncomfortable precision was due to the inopportune arrival of the Countess Olenska. Here he was, at the very moment of his betrothal—a time for pure thoughts and cloudless hopes—pitchforked into a coil of scandal that raised all the special problems he had preferred to leave lying dormant.

"Hang Ellen Olenska!" he grumbled, covering the fire and beginning to undress. He could not fully comprehend why her fate should bear upon his own, yet he dimly sensed he had only just begun to measure the risks of the championship his engagement had forced upon him.

A few days later, the bolt fell. The Lovell Mingotts issued cards for a "formal dinner"—three extra footmen, two dishes per course, Roman punch in the middle—headlined with the words "To meet the Countess Olenska," adhering to the hospitable American custom of treating strangers as royalty or ambassadors. The guest list displayed a boldness and discrimination recognized by the initiated as the firm hand of Catherine the Great. Alongside immemorial standbys like the Selfridge Merrys, the Beauforts, and Mr. Sillerton Jackson with his sister Sophy, were the Lawrence Leffertses, Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth, the Harry Thorleys, the Reggie Chiverses, and young Morris Dagonet and his wife, a van der Luyden. The company was perfectly assorted, comprising the little inner group who, throughout the long New York season, disported themselves together with undiminished zest.

Forty-eight hours later, the unbelievable occurred. Every single guest refused the Mingotts' invitation save for the Beauforts and old Mr. Jackson and his sister. The intended slight was emphasized by the presence of the Reggie Chiverses, of the Mingott clan, among those inflicting it, and by the uniform wording of the notes. Each writer simply "regretted that they were unable to accept," omitting the mitigating plea of a "previous engagement" that ordinary courtesy prescribed. New York society was far too small, its resources too scant, for everyone—from livery-stable keepers to butlers and cooks—to know exactly on which evenings people were free. Thus, the recipients of Mrs. Lovell Mingott's invitations could cruelly clarify their determination not to meet the Countess Olenska.

The blow was unexpected, but the Mingotts met it gallantly, as was their way. Mrs. Lovell Mingott confided the case to Mrs. Welland, who confided it to Newland Archer. Aflame at the outrage, he appealed passionately and authoritatively to his mother. She had prepared herself for this moment, aligning her internal compass with the rigid geography of her own character: silent by nature, kind by training, yet perpetually distant. Beneath the portrait by Huntington, Mrs. van der Luyden sat. In black velvet and Venetian point, she mirrored the fair ancestress above her. He admired her, as his family did, but found her gentle sweetness impenetrable, colder perhaps than the fierce, principled "No"s of his mother's aunties. Mrs. van der Luyden offered neither affirmation nor denial. She and Mr. van der Luyden were two halves of a single equation, merged after forty years of conjugality. How they managed to separate themselves enough to deliberate on matters as controversial as the current crisis remained a mystery to Archer. New York had reached a consensus: the Countess Olenska had lost her looks. The memory of her arrival persisted, however—a brilliant, painted doll of nine or ten, a child of wandering parents who had vanished, leaving her to the care of Aunt Medora Manson. Medora, a perpetual refugee herself, returned to the city only to "settle down," usually in a cheaper apartment, bringing a new husband or an adopted child before inevitably discarding them both. Yet, amidst this cycle of dispossession, New York tolerated the eccentricities of the Chivers-Manson line. But when Medora returned with her orphaned niece, dressed in crimson merino and amber beads while wearing a crape veil too short for propriety, the city's indulgence wavered. Only a few old ladies shook their heads at the gaudy display; the rest were charmed by the child's high color and high spirits. Little Ellen Mingott was fearless, asking disconcerting questions, performing Spanish shawl dances, and singing Neapolitan songs to a guitar.

This chaotic upbringing continued until Chivers died in a madhouse, and Medora, draped in strange weeds, departed once more with Ellen. Years later, news arrived of Ellen's marriage to a Polish nobleman of legendary wealth, a union consummated in a sulphurous apotheosis at the Tuileries. When Medora eventually returned, impoverished and mourning a third husband, the city wondered why her rich niece had not sent word. Then came the report of the marriage's collapse and Ellen's return to seek rest among her kinsfolk.

These facts drifted through Newland Archer's mind a week later as he watched the Countess Olenska enter the van der Luyden drawing-room. The occasion was solemn, a dinner attended by New York's most chosen company, now augmented by the presence of a Duke. Archer noted her lateness, her ungloved hand, the hurried fastening of a bracelet, yet she entered without haste or embarrassment. In the center of the room, she paused, surveying the assembly with a grave mouth and smiling eyes. In that instant, Archer rejected the general verdict. True, her early radiance was gone; the red cheeks had paled, she was thin and worn, nearly thirty. But there existed a mysterious authority of beauty, a sureness in the carriage of the head, a movement of the eyes that was not theatrical but highly trained, full of conscious power. She was simpler in manner than the others, quieter in voice and motion. New York expected resonance from a woman with such a history, but she offered silence instead.

The dinner proceeded with formidable gravity. The crystal and porcelain were displayed; Mrs. van der Luyden resembled a Cabanel painting, Mrs. Archer an Isabey miniature. Old Miss Lanning wore her mother's cameos. Among the smooth, plump elderly faces adorned with diamonds and ostrich feathers, the Countess appeared curiously mature. The Duke of St. Austrey, seated at the hostess's right, was the nominal chief figure, yet he was almost invisible. His evening clothes were shabby and baggy, worn with an air of homespun indifference, his vast beard spreading over his shirt-front. He spoke rarely, in low tones lost to everyone except his neighbors.

After dinner, the Duke approached the Countess immediately, ignoring protocol to pay respects to the elder ladies. They retreated to a corner, plunging into animated talk for twenty minutes, seemingly unaware of the social breach. Then the Countess rose and walked alone across the wide drawing-room to sit beside Newland Archer. It was not customary for a lady to leave one gentleman to seek another; etiquette demanded she remain immovable as an idol while suitors rotated. "I want you to talk to me about May," she said.

"Oh, yes—we used to see him every winter at Nice. He's very fond of gambling—he used to come to the house a great deal." She said it simply, as if noting a preference for wildflowers. After a pause, she added candidly, "I think he's the dullest man I ever met."

Archer felt a surge of excitement. To meet a lady who found the van der Luydens' Duke dull and dared to speak it was thrilling. He longed to question her further, to probe the life hinted at in her careless words, but fear of distressing memories held him back. Before he could formulate a query, she shifted topics.

"May is a darling; I've seen no young girl in New York so handsome and so intelligent. Are you very much in love with her?"

Archer reddened and laughed. "As much as a man can be."

She considered him thoughtfully, missing no shade of meaning. "Do you think, then, there is a limit?"

"To being in love? If there is, I haven't found it!"

She glowed with sympathy. "Ah—it's really and truly a romance?"

"The most romantic of romances!"

Archer looked at her incredulously. "Have you forgotten," he asked with a smile, "that in our country we don't allow our marriages to be arranged for us?"

A dusky blush rose to her cheek, and he instantly regretted his words. "Yes," she answered, "I'd forgotten. You must forgive me if I sometimes make these mistakes. I don't always remember that everything here is good that was—that was bad where I've come from."

She looked down at her Viennese fan of eagle feathers, and he saw her lips tremble. "I'm so sorry," he said impulsively; "but you _are_ among friends here, you know."

"Yes—I know. Wherever I go I have that feeling. That's why I came home. I want to forget everything else, to become a complete American again, like the Mingotts and Wellands, and you and your delightful mother, and all the other good people here tonight."

Her eyes turned from the door, where May Welland was arriving in a dress of white and silver, looking like a Diana fresh from the chase, to rest on Archer's face. "Ah, here's May arriving, and you will want to hurry away to her," she added, remaining still.

Madame Olenska's dwelling was distinguished from its surroundings only by a marginally fresher coat of paint around the window-frames. In London, nothing captivated her except the theatres and the shops. She found the theatres less exciting than the Paris _cafés chantants_, where, beneath the blossoming horse-chestnuts of the Champs Élysées, she had enjoyed the novel experience of looking down from a restaurant terrace onto an audience of "cocottes," while her husband interpreted the songs for her bridal ears.

Archer had reverted to all his old inherited ideas about marriage. It was less trouble to conform with tradition and treat May exactly as all his friends treated their wives than to try to put into practice the theories with which his untrammeled bachelorhood had dallied. There was no use in trying to emancipate a wife who had not the dimmest notion that she was not free; and he had long since discovered that May's only use of the liberty she supposed herself to possess would be to lay it on the altar of her wifely adoration. Her innate dignity would always keep her from making the gift abjectly; and a day might even come, as it once had, when she would find strength to take it altogether back if she thought she were doing it for his own good. But with a conception of marriage so uncomplicated and incurious as hers, such a crisis could be brought about only by something visibly outrageous in his own conduct; and the fineness of her feeling for him made that unthinkable. Whatever happened, he knew, she would always be loyal, gallant, and unresentful; and that pledged him to the practice of the same virtues.

All this tended to draw him back into his old habits of mind. If her simplicity had been the simplicity of pettiness, he would have chafed and rebelled; but since the lines of her character, though so few, were cast in the same fine mold as her face, she became the tutelary divinity of all his old traditions and reverences.

Such qualities were scarcely of the kind to enliven foreign travel, though they made her so easy and pleasant a companion. He saw at once how they would fall into place in their proper setting. Now, walking home from Mrs. Mingott's, he felt a growing distaste for what lay ahead. There was nothing unknown in the path he was supposed to tread; indeed, he had walked it before as a free man, accountable to no one, lending himself with amused detachment to the game of precautions, prevarication, concealment, and compliance required by society. This procedure was termed "protecting a woman's honor," and the best fiction, combined with the post-prandial discourse of his elders, had long initiated him into every nuance of its code.

Now, viewed anew, his role seemed singularly diminished. It was, in fact, the performance he had watched Mrs. Thorley Rushworth enact toward a fond, unperceiving husband: a smiling, bantering, humor-ing, watchful, and incessant lie. A lie by day, a lie by night, a lie in every touch and every glance; a lie in every caress and every quarrel; a lie in every word and every silence.

It was easier, and less dastardly overall, for a wife to play such a part. A woman's standard of truthfulness was tacitly lower; she was the subject creature, versed in the arts of the enslaved. She could always plead moods and nerves, claiming the right not to be held strictly to account; and even in the most rigid societies, the laughter was invariably against the husband.

But in Archer's little world, no one laughed at a deceived wife, and a certain measure of contempt attached to men who continued their philandering after marriage. In the rotation of crops, there was a recognized season for wild oats, but they were not to be sown more than once.

Archer had always shared this view: in his heart, he thought Lefferts despicable. But to love Ellen Olenska was not to become a man like Lefferts. For the first time, Archer found himself face-to-face with the dread argument of the individual case. Ellen Olenska was unlike any other woman; he was unlike any other man. Their situation, therefore, resembled no one else's, and they were answerable to no tribunal but that of their own judgment.

Yes, but in ten minutes he would be mounting his own doorstep. There were May, and habit, and honor, and all the old decencies he and his people had always believed in...

Archer possessed the nocturnal perspective of Fifth Avenue almost to himself. "Ellen," he said in a low voice as she reached the pavement.

A mean desire not to have Madame Olenska seen at the Beauforts' door vanished as he felt the penetrating warmth of her hand.

"I shall see you now—we shall be together," he broke out, hardly knowing what he said.

"Ah," she answered, "Granny has told you?"

While he watched her, he became aware that Lefferts and Chivers, upon reaching the far side of the street corner, had discreetly struck away across Fifth Avenue. She wavered, moving toward the carriage.

"But I shall be at Granny's—for the present that is," she added, as if conscious that her change of plans required some explanation.

"Somewhere where we can be alone," he insisted.

She gave a faint laugh that grated on him.

She turned away without answering and quickly entered the carriage. As it drove off, she leaned forward, and he thought she waved her hand in the obscurity. He stared after her in a turmoil of contradictory feelings. "She'll come!" he said to himself, almost contemptuously.
