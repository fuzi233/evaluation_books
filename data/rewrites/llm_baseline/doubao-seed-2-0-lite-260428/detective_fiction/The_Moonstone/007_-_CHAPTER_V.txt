The last of the summer stragglers had drifted out of sight along the coast, and the second the two of us were left alone on the gritty edge of the Shivering Sand, I made my third clumsy attempt to heave myself up off my seat on the shore. Mr. Franklin’s hand came down light but firm on my shoulder to stop me.
“One blessing at least of this godforsaken place,” he said, “we’ve got the whole stretch of it to ourselves. Stay right where you are, Betteredge. I’ve got something weighty to say to you.”
While he spoke I was squinting at him, trying to dig up a trace of the scruffy rosy-cheeked boy I’d known running wild about the estate years before. The man standing in front of me baffled me at first look. No sign of that round boyish face, no trace of his old trim little play jacket. His complexion had paled to a faint, nervous clear white, and the lower half of his face was covered in a thatch of curly brown beard and moustache that took me completely by surprise. He had that quick, jumpy, charming touch-and-go way about him, I’ll grant you, but nothing that matched the free-and-easy rascal manners of the lad I remembered. Worse still, he’d promised to grow well over middle height, and he’d failed to keep that promise: neat, slim, well-made, but a good inch or two short of what we’d all expected. For a minute I thought the years had rubbed every last scrap of his old self away — until I met his eyes. That bright, straightforward, unflinching glint was exactly the same. There was my boy again, and I stopped poking at my memories of him right there.
“Welcome back to the old place, Mr. Franklin,” I said. “All the warmer welcome, sir, seeing as you’ve turned up hours before we were all expecting you.”
“I’ve a very good reason for beating the timetable,” he answered. “Betteredge, I’ve been tailed and watched in London for the last three or four days. I swapped the afternoon train for this morning’s one expressly to shake off a certain dark, scowling stranger who’s been dogging my every step.”
Those words didn’t just surprise me. They jolted me straight back to the three wandering Indian jugglers we’d had hanging around the gate that very morning, and Penelope’s silly little notion that they meant no good to Mr. Franklin Blake.
“Who’s the man watching you, sir? And what on earth for?” I asked.
Instead of answering, he turned the question back on me. “Tell me about those three Indians you had at the house today. It’s just possible that my mysterious stranger and your three jugglers are pieces of the very same puzzle.”
“How do you know about the jugglers already, sir?” I blurted out, piling one bad-mannered question on top of the other — but you don’t expect perfect politeness from an old servant like me, do you?
“I ran into Penelope at the front door the second I arrived,” he said, grinning. “Your daughter promised to grow into a pretty girl, Betteredge, and she kept that promise. Small ear, small foot — your late wife Mrs. Betteredge had those little charms too, didn’t she?”
“Late Mrs. Betteredge had her share of faults, sir,” I told him. “One of them, begging your pardon for saying so, was that she could never stick to a topic to save her life. She was more like a fly than a woman, darting from one thing to the next and never settling anywhere long.”
“She’d have been my perfect companion,” Mr. Franklin laughed. “I never stick to anything either. Penelope told me you’d be the one to give me all the juggler details, by the way. ‘Father’s a wonderful man for his age,’ she said, blushing bright pink, ‘and he expresses himself beautifully.’ Don’t look cross — I knew her when she was seven and running around the garden barefoot, so you can’t begrudge me a quick kiss on the cheek. Now let’s be serious. What did those jugglers do?”
I was a little huffy at Penelope for spilling that silly story of hers second-hand, but there was no use dragging my feet now, so I laid the whole incident out for him. All the laughter drained right off his face as I talked. He sat frowning hard, twisting at the end of his beard between his fingers, and when I finished he repeated the two questions the head juggler had asked the small boy he’d stopped, like he was carving them into his memory.
“‘Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?’ ‘Has the English gentleman got It about him?’” He fished a small, sealed paper parcel out of his coat pocket, his face deadly serious now. “I suspect ‘It’ means this. And this, Betteredge, is my uncle Herncastle’s famous Moonstone Diamond.”
“Good Lord, sir!” I burst out. “How in the world did you end up in charge of that wicked Colonel’s cursed rock?”
“The old sinner’s will left that diamond as a birthday present to my cousin Rachel,” he said. “My father, as the Colonel’s executor, handed it straight over to me to bring down here safe and sound.”
I doubt I could have been more shocked if the tide creeping smooth over the Shivering Sand had suddenly turned to dry land right under my backside. “The Colonel’s diamond left to Miss Rachel? And your father the Colonel’s executor? Why, I’d have laid any wager you cared to name, Mr. Franklin, that your father wouldn’t have touched that man with a pair of tongs!”
“Strong words, Betteredge! What was there against the Colonel, anyway? He belonged to your generation, not mine. Tell me everything you know about him, and I’ll tell you how my father ended up as his executor, and the rest of the ugly secrets I dug up about him and that diamond in London. You called him the wicked Colonel just now. Dig into that old memory of yours, and tell me why.”
I saw he was dead earnest, so I leaned in and told him. Let me pause here a second to beg you to pay full attention. Clear your head of your dinner, your new bonnet, your horse bets, your club grievances, all the nonsense that makes your eyes glaze over. Forget politics, forget City share prices, forget whatever triviality is prancing through your mind right now. If you skip this part, you’ll be totally lost the moment we go deeper into the story. Don’t take that as me being rude — haven’t I seen you readers drift off even when you’re holding a great author’s book in your hands, the second the page stops being a person talking right to you?
I told him about the old lord, my lady’s father, short-tempered and sharp of tongue, who had five children total. First two sons, then three little girls in a rush years later, my mistress the youngest and kindest of the lot. The oldest son Arthur inherited the title and all the estates. The second, the Honourable John Herncastle, got a huge fortune left to him by a distant relative and joined the army.
They say it’s an ill bird that fouls its own nest, and the Herncastles are my nest, so I won’t dwell on every last rotten thing about the Honourable John. But I will say he was one of the biggest blackguards that ever drew breath. He joined the Guards first, and got kicked out before he was twenty-two for reasons no decent man would name. He shipped off to India to see if they were laxer out there, and get himself some active service. For all his faults, he was brave — a cross between a bulldog, a gamecock, and a dash of the wildest savage you ever saw. He was at the sacking of Seringapatam, then swapped regiments twice more, until he made Lieutenant Colonel, got a brutal sunstroke, and came home to England.
He came back with a reputation so black every single door in his own family slammed shut in his face. My lady, newly married by then, led the charge, declaring with my lord’s full approval that her brother would never set foot in her house. There were half a dozen unspeakable slurs attached to his name, but the stain of the Moonstone is the only one that matters here. Everyone said he’d stolen that huge yellow gem from a Hindu temple during the Seringapatam looting, by means so vile he never dared admit to them. He never tried to sell it — he had no need for money, and never cared about profit. He never gave it away, never showed it to a single living soul. Some said he was afraid it would get him in trouble with the military authorities. Others, fools who didn’t know the man at all, said he was afraid showing it would get him killed.
There was a grain of truth in that last rumour. He wasn’t afraid, not him. But his life had been threatened twice in India, and everyone knew the Moonstone was the reason for it. When he came home and found every last person shunning him, they said the Moonstone was the reason for that too. The mystery of that diamond turned him into an outlaw among his own people. Men wouldn’t let him into their clubs. The women he tried to marry all turned him down. Friends and relations pretended they were too short-sighted to recognise him on the street.
Any other man would have tried to clear his name. But the Honourable John never gave an inch, even when he was wrong, even when all society was against him. He’d kept that diamond in India, defying every assassin that came for him. He kept that diamond in England, defying every bit of public opinion that said he shouldn’t. That was the man: brute, stubborn, daring everything, his handsome face twisted with that look like the devil himself was sitting behind his eyes.
He lived a vicious, solitary, underground life. Rumours drifted to us now and then: he was smoking opium and collecting old rare books, he was messing around with strange dangerous chemistry, he was carousing in the worst slums of London with the dregs of society. Only once, two years before this very day, a year and a half before he died, did I ever see him face to face.
It was the night of Miss Rachel’s birthday, the 21st of June, and we had a big party going on in my lady’s London house in her honour. A footman came to fetch me, saying a gentleman was waiting in the hall to see me. I walked out, and there stood the Colonel, haggard, shabby, old and worn, wild and wicked as he’d ever been.
“Go up to my sister,” he said, “and tell her I’ve come to wish my niece many happy returns of the day.”
He’d sent letters before trying to reconcile with my lady, though I’m convinced he only did it to annoy her — but this was the first time he’d ever turned up at the door in person. I almost told him my lady had a full party tonight, but that devilish glint in his eye daunted me, so I trundled upstairs with his message, and left him waiting in the hall like he asked. All the servants were clustered at the far end of the corridor staring at him, like he was a walking loaded cannon that could go off and blow them all to bits at any second.
My lady had a sharp streak of the family temper, same as the rest of them. “Tell Colonel Herncastle,” she said, when I gave her the message, “that Miss Verinder is engaged, and I decline to see him.” I tried to beg her for a softer answer, knowing the Colonel didn’t play by the rules that govern normal gentlemen — but it was no use. The temper flared straight at me. “When I want your advice,” she snapped, “you know I ask for it. I don’t ask for it now.”
I trundled back downstairs, and took the liberty of softening her message to something more polite: “My lady and Miss Rachel regret that they are both engaged, Colonel, and beg to be excused from having the honour of seeing you.”
I expected him to fly into a rage. To my total shock, he didn’t. He was unnaturally quiet, those glittering pale grey eyes fixed on me for a long minute, and he laughed — not a normal laugh out loud, but a soft, quiet, chuckling, horribly mischievous little laugh he kept right inside his chest. “Thank you, Betteredge,” he said. “I shall remember my niece’s birthday.” And with that, he turned on his heel and walked right out the front door.
The next birthday rolled around, and we heard he was bedridden, ill. Six months after that, half a year before today, we got a letter from a very respectable clergyman saying two miraculous things: first, that the Colonel had forgiven his sister on his deathbed, and second, that he’d forgiven everyone else and died a most pious, edifying death. I’ve got all the respect in the world for the Church, but I know for a fact the devil kept full possession of John Herncastle right up to his last breath, and that his final abominable trick was fooling that poor clergyman completely.
That was all I had to tell Mr. Franklin. He leaned in closer the longer I talked, and the part about the Colonel being turned away on Rachel’s own birthday seemed to hit him like a bullet right on target. He didn’t say it, but I could see plain as day he was made uneasy by it.
“You’ve told your side, Betteredge,” he said. “Now it’s my turn. But before I tell you what I dug up in London, and how I got tangled up in this diamond mess, I want to know one thing. You look like you haven’t the faintest clue why we’re sitting here on the sand going over all this. Are your eyes lying?”
“No sir,” I said. “They’re telling the plain truth.”
“Then let me lay out my point of view for you, before we go a step further.” He leaned back, that bright boyish little flash of pride at how clever he was coming back for a second, just like when he was a lad. “There are three very serious questions tied up in the Colonel’s birthday gift to Rachel. Count them off on your fingers if it helps you follow. First: was the Colonel’s diamond the centre of a lifelong conspiracy among the Hindu priests in India who swore to get it back to their temple, no matter what it took? Second: did that conspiracy follow the diamond all the way to England? Third: did the Colonel know exactly they were following it, and did he deliberately leave that cursed rock as a legacy of trouble and danger, to get his revenge on his sister, using her own innocent daughter as the tool? That’s what I’m driving at, Betteredge. Don’t let me frighten you.”
Too late. He’d already frightened me. If he was right, our quiet little English country house had just been invaded by a devilish Indian diamond, dragging after it a gang of ruthless living thieves, set loose on us all by the petty vengeance of a dead man. Who ever heard the like of it? In the nineteenth century, mind you, the age of progress, in a country that boasts of the blessings of British law and the constitution? No one ever did — so no one will believe it, I don’t doubt. But I’ll tell the story all the same.
When you get a shock like that, it always hits you right in the stomach. My gut was twisting itself into knots, I was fidgeting on the sand, my mind wandering all over the place. Mr. Franklin noticed me squirming, stopped himself mid-sentence about to launch into his own story, and snapped: “What do you want?”
I didn’t tell him, but I’ll tell you in confidence. What I wanted more than anything in the whole world was to get back to the house, light my old clay pipe, and lose myself in the pages of my tattered, dog-eared copy of Robinson Crusoe.
