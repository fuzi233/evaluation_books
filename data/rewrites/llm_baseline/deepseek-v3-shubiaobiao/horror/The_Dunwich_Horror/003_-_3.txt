Old Whateley’s madness took shape in wood and nails. Though his herd never grew, cattle still vanished into his hands—paid for in tarnished gold coins that whispered of older, darker treasuries. And though his words often unraveled into senseless babble, his hands worked with grim purpose.

The house, a sagging skeleton half-swallowed by the hillside, began to change. The lower rooms had always sufficed for him and his slack-jawed daughter, Lavinia. But now the upper floor—long abandoned to dust and shadows—was being reclaimed. Boards groaned under his hammer, sealing every window with a craftsman’s precision. No one understood why he bothered. Fewer still understood why, when the work was done, one window in the east gable had been replaced with a heavy door, bolted from within. A rough wooden runway led up to it from the ground, as if something needed to come and go unseen.

Inside, another room took shape—this one for the child. Shelves rose like the ribs of some long-dead beast, carefully fitted to cradle the moldering books that had once littered the house. Old Whateley would crouch by the stove, stirring paste in a rusted pot, painstakingly repairing pages whose black-lettered secrets had crumbled with age.

*“The boy’ll use ’em better,”* he muttered to no one. *“He’s got the mind for it.”*

And the boy did.

By the time Wilbur was a year and seven months old, he spoke like a scholar and moved like something older. His limbs stretched too fast, his voice too deep for his years. He roamed the fields with his mother, but his true schooling came in the dim-lit room below the sealed upper floor, where the old man drilled him in words that twisted the air.

The tool-shed, locked tight since Wilbur’s birth, stood empty again. When Earl Sawyer stumbled inside after a fruitless haggle over cattle, he reeled back, gagging. The stench was unholy—thick as rotting meat, sharp as spoiled milk, but underneath it all, something worse. Something that made his skin prickle as if the earth itself had exhaled.

The hills, too, grew restless. On May Eve, tremors shivered through Dunwich, felt even in Aylesbury. By Halloween, the ground groaned in time with flickering lights atop Sentinel Hill—lights that danced where no fire should burn.

Wilbur, at four, looked ten. At four and a half, his face darkened with coarse stubble, his voice cracking between boy and man. He carried a pistol now. Dogs howled at his approach, their teeth bared in mindless terror. Farmers learned to chain them when the Whateley boy passed.

Visitors to the house found Lavinia alone, her fingers knotting in her apron as muffled thuds shook the ceiling above. Once, when a peddler jangled the locked stairway door, her face went gray.

*“What’s up there?”* he teased.

She didn’t answer. But later, in the village, the peddler swore he’d heard the sound of hooves.

The war came in 1917, and Dunwich’s sons were too sickly, too inbred, to fight. Government men arrived, sniffing out degeneracy. Reporters followed, their pencils scratching tales of a four-year-old who read Latin, of gold that stank of burial vaults, of a second floor no one could enter.

And through it all, the hills kept their secrets.

But the locked door creaked.

And something in the dark listened.
