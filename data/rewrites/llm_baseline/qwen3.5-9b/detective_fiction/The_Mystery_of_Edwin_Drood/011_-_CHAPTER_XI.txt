Deep behind the oldest section of Holborn, where gabled houses centuries old stare listlessly onto the public road as if searching vainly for the dried-up Old Bourne, lies a secluded enclave known as Staple Inn. Entering this narrow courtyard from the chaotic street above grants the weary traveler a sudden sense of peace, as though one has just inserted cotton into their ears and velvet soles into their boots. Here, smoky sparrows chatter among smoky branches, pretending to play "country" amidst mere scraps of garden soil and gravel—a delightful deception for their tiny minds. Furthermore, it is a legally sanctioned nook, housing a modest hall topped with a single lantern; the purpose of this structure and who pays for it remain unknown to history.

Long ago, when the distant railway threatened to offend the sensitive constitution of our nation's sacred institutions, no towering buildings overshadowed Staple Inn. Then, the setting sun cast bright glances upon it, and the southwest wind swept through unimpeded.

However, neither sun nor wind favored the inn on a particular December afternoon around six o'clock. That day, it was choked with fog, and candles inside the occupied chambers cast murky, blurred light. Most notably, this dim glow emanated from a corner suite in the inner quadrangle, marked above its grim doorway with the cryptic inscription: **P J T 1747**.

Inside this suite, a man named Mr. Grewgious sat by his fire, rarely pondering the inscription above him, save for fleeting thoughts that it might signify *Perhaps John Thomas* or *Perhaps Joe Tyler*.

To judge Mr. Grewgious by his appearance, one could not determine if he had ever known ambition or suffered disappointment. Trained for the Bar, he had aimed for chamber practice, intending to draft deeds and "convey the wise," as Pistol might say. Yet, Conveyancing and he had married poorly; they parted by mutual consent, though technically, they had never truly united.

Conveyancing refused to court Mr. Grewgious. She was wooed, not won, and they parted ways. However, an unexpected gust of wind blew an Arbitration toward him, in which he earned significant credit for his tireless pursuit of justice. Following this, a substantial Receivership drifted into his possession via a wind with a clearer origin. By sheer chance, he had found his niche. Now serving as Receiver and Agent for two wealthy estates, delegating their legal affairs to a firm of solicitors downstairs, he had extinguished his ambitions (had he ever lit them?) and settled down permanently beneath the protective vines of P.J.T., established in 1747.

His room was cluttered with accounts, ledgers, correspondence files, and strongboxes, yet these items were arranged with such meticulous orderliness that they hardly seemed to crowd the space. The fear of dying suddenly, leaving even a single fact or figure incomplete or obscure, would have driven Mr. Grewgious mad. Absolute fidelity to a trust was the lifeblood of this man. While other forms of life-blood flow faster, more gaily, or more attractively, none circulated with greater virtue.

Luxury was absent from his quarters. Comforts were limited to warmth, dryness, and a cozy, albeit faded, fireplace. His private life revolved around the hearth, an easychair, and an antique round table kept hidden in a corner during business hours, revealed only after work ended. Behind this table lay a closet, typically stocked with something refreshingly alcoholic. An adjacent room served as the clerk's office; Mr. Grewgious slept upstairs; and he retained some cellarage at the bottom of the stairs. For at least three hundred days a year, he crossed to Furnival's Inn for dinner and returned afterward to maximize his solitude until the next business day arrived under the banner of P.J.T., dated 1747.

While Mr. Grewgious wrote by the fire, so did his clerk. This attendant was a pale, puffy-faced man of thirty, with dark hair, large dull eyes, and a sullen complexion that seemed perpetually begging to be sent to the baker's. Possessed of some strange influence over Mr. Grewgious, he adhered tightly to his master's stool, despite the obvious inconvenience he caused. With tangled locks and an aura suggesting he had grown up under the shadow of the infamous Java tree—which shelters more falsehoods than the entire botanical kingdom—he was nonetheless treated with inexplicable kindness by Mr. Grewgious.

"Now, Bazzard," Mr. Grewgious said as his clerk entered, looking up from his papers as he organized them for the night. "Besides the fog, what is blowing?"

"Mr. Drood," replied Bazzard.

"What of him?"

"He has called," said Bazzard.

"You should have shown him in."

"I am doing it now," Bazzard retorted.

The visitor entered accordingly.

"My goodness!" exclaimed Mr. Grewgious, surveying his pair of office candles. "I thought you had merely dropped your name and departed. How do you do, Mr. Edwin? My heavens, you're choking!"

"It's this fog," Edwin replied. "It makes my eyes sting like cayenne pepper."

"Is it truly that severe? Please remove your wraps. Fortunately, I possess a fine fire, though Mr. Bazzard has already attended to me."

"No, I haven't," Bazzard interjected from the doorway.

"Oh! Then it follows that I must have taken care of myself without realizing it," Mr. Grewgious observed. "Please sit in my chair. No, I beg! After entering such an atmosphere, you shouldn't occupy *my* chair."

Edwin took the easychair in the corner. The fog he carried in with him, along with that released by removing his greatcoat and scarf, was swiftly consumed by the eager flames.

"I appear," Edwin smiled, "as though I intended to stay."

"—By the way," cried Mr. Grewgious, interrupting himself. "Stay. Excuse my interruption; please remain. The fog may clear in an hour or two. We can fetch dinner from just across Holborn. You'd better enjoy your cayenne pepper here than outside; please stay and dine."

"You are very kind," Edwin said, glancing around as if tempted by the prospect of a novel and hearty meal.

"Not at all," Mr. Grewgious replied. "*You* are very kind to join a bachelor in chambers and accept potluck. Besides," he added, lowering his voice with a twinkle in his eye, as if struck by a brilliant idea, "I'll invite Bazzard. He might otherwise object.—Bazzard!"

Bazzard reappeared.

"Dine shortly with Mr. Drood and me."

"If I am ordered to dine, naturally I will, sir," the gloomy clerk answered.

"Save the man!" cried Mr. Grewgious. "You are not ordered; you are invited."

"Thank you, sir," Bazzard said. "In that case, I don't mind."

"That is settled. And perhaps you wouldn't mind," Mr. Grewgious continued, "crossing over to Furnival's Inn and requesting they send provisions for the table. For dinner, we shall have the hottest, strongest soup available, the finest made-dish recommended, a joint—perhaps a haunch of mutton—and a goose, turkey, or any stuffed delicacy currently on the menu. In short, we shall consume whatever is available."

Issuing these generous instructions with his usual air of reciting a lesson or reading an inventory, Mr. Grewgious spoke as if memorized. Bazzard, after pulling out the round table, withdrew to execute the orders.

"I was somewhat hesitant," Mr. Grewgious whispered later, after his clerk's departure, "about employing him in the commissariat department. He might dislike it."

"He seems to have his own way, sir," Edwin remarked.

"His own way?" Mr. Grewgious countered. "Oh, no! Poor fellow, you misunderstand him entirely. If he had his own way, he wouldn't be here."

"I wonder where he would be!" Edwin thought silently. He voiced only the thought because Mr. Grewgious rose, positioned himself with his back to the opposite corner of the fire, pressed his shoulder blades against the mantelpiece, and gathered his skirts to facilitate comfortable conversation.

"I assume, without needing prophetic gifts, that you visited to inform me you are heading down there—where I can assure you you are awaited—and to offer to handle any commissions for my charming ward, or perhaps to assist me in certain proceedings? Eh, Mr. Edwin?"

"I called, sir, earlier today as an act of courtesy."

"Courtesy!" Mr. Grewgious exclaimed. "Ah! Naturally, not impatience?"

"Impatience, sir?"

Mr. Grewgious intended to be arch—not expressing that meaning remotely—but had leaned so close to the fire to burn the full effect of his wit into himself, like subtle impressions burned into hard metal. However, his archness vanished instantly before the composed demeanor of his visitor, leaving only the fire. Startled, he rubbed himself.

"I recently visited down there," Mr. Grewgious said, adjusting his skirts. "That is what I referred to when I mentioned you are expected."

"Indeed, sir! Yes; I knew that 'Pussy' was watching for me."
