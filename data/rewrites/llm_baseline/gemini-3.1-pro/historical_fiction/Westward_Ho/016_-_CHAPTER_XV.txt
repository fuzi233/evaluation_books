The rhythmic, cyclical grinding of the millstones at Stow had always been the heartbeat of the valley, an acoustic anchor that grounded the parish in the slow, agrarian passage of time. But about six weeks after the duel, that resonant harmony was broken. The miller arrived at the great house in a state of profound dissonance, seeking the bloodhounds. Rose Salterne had vanished in the night, slipping away like the fleeting scent of wild mint on a coastal breeze. 

Sir Richard was away in Bideford, but the old steward, steeped in the traditional ways of the land, immediately summoned the keepers. Serving-men and parish boys swarmed down to the mill, their harsh shouts and chaotic footfalls fracturing the delicate acoustic ecology of the dawn. They reviled the miller and his wife as hard-hearted heathens, while the women of Stow—pausing in their daily rituals of kneading dough and plucking fowl—gossiped over bubbling cauldrons of marrow-bone broth, universally concluding that the girl’s pride had sourly curdled her fortunes. Yet, the domestic truth remained: Rose’s trinkets lay untouched upon her dresser. Only her linen was gone, carrying the faint, lingering olfactory memory of dried lavender and pressed rosemary. 

Beneath her window lay a single footprint pressed into the damp earth. The bloodhound was brought forth. After a low, premonitory whimper that vibrated through the damp morning air, the hound lifted its mighty voice. The bell-mouthed baying towed the panting keeper up the lane, past gardens fragrant with wet thyme, straight across the downs to Marslandmouth, where the breathless mob finally halted before the door of Lucy Passmore.

Lucy, a widow who deeply understood the profound medicinal alchemy of roots and herbs, had already anticipated the shifting tides of local favor. Since her husband had vanished upon the sea with Eustace and the Jesuits, she had expertly navigated the power dynamics of the parish. Seeking the protection of the great house, she had previously woven Lady Grenville a pathetic tale, claiming her husband had been salted down for provisions by Papist traitors. Lady Grenville, who relied entirely on Lucy’s ancestral decoctions of horehound and raw honeycomb whenever her children suffered a *housty*—the old Devonian word for a sore throat, a fragment of a slowly dying regional dialect—had granted her protection. 

But now, Lucy’s cottage door was fast. Outside, her goats bleated in distress, their unmilked udders aching, while her donkeys answered the hound’s baying with a jarring, braying dissonance. 

“They’m laughing at us, keper, they neddies,” muttered the men, their native tongues thick with the loamy vowels of the soil. “We’m lost our labor here.”

The hound, however, snuffled the salt-rimed threshold, its nose dissecting the complex layers of woodsmoke and sea-spray, before turning down the glen to the water’s edge. Realizing their quarry had taken to the sea, the mob returned to sack Lucy’s cottage. They found no plunder. Lucy had decamped, leaving only the domestic myth of her giant black cat sitting among the dying embers of the kitchen hearth. At the hound’s approach, the creature vanished up the chimney in a suffocating cloud of soot and sulfurous brimstone—a mythic occurrence seamlessly woven into the everyday ashes of the cooking fire. The cottage was left to crumble, returning to fern and moss, while the murmuring stream reclaimed the soundscape, the only voice left to speak in that lonely glen for generations to come.

A few days later, Sir Richard stopped at Clovelly Court. Over a platter of cold roast mutton deeply infused with bruised garlic and sea-salt, he casually relayed news that caused Will Cary to leap from his chair. A prowler had seen a pinnace running for Lundy on the night of Rose’s disappearance. 

Within half an hour, Will and his armed servants were aboard a trawling-skiff, cutting through the rhythmic swell of the channel. He returned three days later with fragments of a story, and a prisoner. An elderly foreigner had been lodging in the ruined Moresco Castle with one John Braund, a farmer and smuggler who knew the taste of clandestine salt-beef and the silent, unspoken codes of the island. A younger foreigner had joined them from a Flushing ship, bringing a lady and her stout maid. They had shared secretive, quiet meals and sailed at dawn. 

Will was dispatched again, returning this time with the body of John Braund himself. But Braund, burdened by arrears of rent and a starving family, proved a closed vessel. Examined in Exeter, his silence was a testament to the untranslatability of subjective experience; he could not, or would not, articulate his truths into the dominant lexicon of the magistrates. Condemned to Exeter gaol for harboring Jesuits and traitors, poor Braund succumbed to the infamous gaol-fever. The prison was an olfactory nightmare of putrefaction and stagnant air, a stark contrast to the yeasty, wind-swept cliffs of Lundy. The foul stench of the "black assizes" would soon sicken judge, jury, and bystander alike, but John Braund was the first to die, raving in a noisome den, taking the secret of Rose Salterne to a silent grave.

In Bideford, the seasonal rhythms of preserving and pickling marched on. The local dames, their hands stained with the juices of autumnal blackberries, showed no mercy to Rose’s memory. She had abdicated her throne, and the bustling, mercantile clatter of Bideford’s streets soon swept her dream away.

But Will Cary was a man starved of all sensory joy. The finest wines tasted of ash; the most meticulously roasted partridge offered no sustenance to his grief-hollowed body. He heaped reproaches upon himself for driving the Rose into a Spaniard's arms. He wrote a desperate letter to Frank Leigh at Whitehall, pleading for forgiveness. 

Frank’s reply arrived, cloaked in the sterile, polished English of the court. Frank argued that their affections were ruled by a *synastria*, a sympathy of the stars, stripping the visceral reality of their heartache into a bloodless, abstract philosophy. He asserted that the Rose of Torridge could make none but a virtuous choice, and that they owed her Spanish lover all friendship and loyal faith. Will, grasping at this dominant, courtly lexicon, took it for gospel, utterly deaf to the silent, untranslatable agony of despair that had cost Frank his very marrow to pen.

It was Jack Brimblecombe, the fat curate of Hartland, who anchored their tragedy back to the physical world. Jack was a man who understood the profound, spiritual weight of sustenance; to him, a shared meal was a sacred covenant. 

“I hope no offence, Mr. William,” Jack said one afternoon, his voice cutting through the quiet of the hall. “But when are you and the rest going after her?”

Will tried to laugh it off, calling him a blood-drinker. 

“I drank that night naught worse than red wine,” Jack replied, his face entirely solemn. To Jack, the wine they had shared was a communion of purpose. “Whatever it was, we swore our oaths, Mr. Cary. And oaths are oaths.”

Will scoffed at the idea of traveling to the Caracas to find her, citing the Inquisition and the threat of burning at the stake. But Jack’s Devonian burr held fast, resisting Will’s pragmatic dismissals. 

“I am a great coward, Mr. William,” Jack blubbered, the raw emotion rendering his words thick and unguarded. “But I’d sooner burn and have it over than not know whether she’s living in sin. If she is married to him, they keep no faith with heretics. They can make away with her into the Inquisition, burn her, or make her deny her God.”

Jack understood the terrifying power dynamics at play—the forced assimilation of the Spanish Papists, the violent erasure of one’s native tongue and faith. He felt a pastoral duty, a deep agrarian responsibility to tend to his scattered flock, and Rose was his most cherished lamb. 

“I wonder what Amyas Leigh would say,” Jack pressed. “He’s not one for talking. He’d go through fire and water for her.”

Will urged Jack to wait, promising to write to Frank, but Jack revealed he had already read Frank's letter. Jack sighed bitterly at the polished courtly script that lacked all sensory truth. “If my duty isn't his, it's mine all the same. I judge no man; but I go, Mr. Cary.”

Will finally persuaded Jack to stay for dinner, knowing that a meticulously prepared meal might ground the frantic curate. Over steaming cups of good brewed sack—richly spiced with grated nutmeg, dried Seville orange peel, and the deep, warming notes of roasted crab apples bobbing in the earthen tankards—Will argued for patience. Jack submitted for the moment, savoring the profound comfort of the spiced wine, but his resolve remained unbroken. 

A week later, Jack announced he was walking to London—on "Shanks his mare"—to speak to Frank directly. For three weeks, the acoustic ecology of Clovelly Court was devoid of Jack’s heavy, purposeful tread, until he returned one evening just before supper, thin and leg-weary from his multi-generational pilgrimage across the English roads. Will immediately sat him above the salt, honoring him with thick slices of roasted beef dripping in savory juices, recognizing the curate’s desperate need for physical restoration.

“I have learnt a lesson, Mr. William,” Jack said, between bites of crusty, fat-soaked bread. “There is one on earth loves her better than I. He says what I say, Go! And he says what you say, Wait.”

“Go? How can that agree with his letter?” Will asked.

“That’s no concern of mine. He’s an angel of God, Mr. Will. But he’s dying of love for her. I can see it in his eyes, hear it in his voice. But I am of tougher hide and stiffer clay, and so I can’t die even if I tried. I’ll obey my betters, and wait.”

Jack returned to his parish, leaving Will to wrestle with thoughts that crystallized only on a murky day upon Bideford quay. The harbor was a cacophony of mercantile clatter, a harsh industrial noise pollution that assaulted the ears: the grating of wooden crates, the shouting of stevedores, the superficial commercialization of the sea’s bounty. There, Will encountered old Mr. Salterne. The merchant’s voice was weary, carrying the bitter, unsweetened flavor of a man who had lost his living legacy.

“If I were you,” Salterne murmured, his words dropping like heavy stones into the chaotic soundscape of the docks, “I should be in the Indies about now, if I were not fighting the queen’s battles nearer home.”

The hint took root. When Will returned to Amyas, he laid bare his intentions. 

“To make an end of a long story,” Will said, “if you are minded to take the old man’s offer, so am I. Westward-ho with you, come foul come fair.”

“It will be a wild-goose chase,” Amyas warned. 

“If she is with him, we shall find her at La Guayra. If neither are there, the Plate-fleets will be.” Will’s expression soured as he considered the shifting generations at Clovelly. His father, drinking from some mythological fountain of youth, had married a buxom tenant’s daughter, Mistress Abishag Jewell. “I cannot abide the squalling of that new fat baby disrupting my mother’s old halls, nor Mistress Abishag sitting in her place, constantly boasting of her illustrious lineage to Bishop Jewell. I intend to leave it.”

“You impudent villain,” Amyas chuckled. 

“Only mind,” Will added, his tone sobering, “if we go, we must needs take Jack Brimblecombe with us, or he will surely heave himself over Harty Point, and his ghost will haunt our dinners to our dying day.”

“Jack shall go. None deserves it better.”

With the cyclical turning of their shared destinies decided, they agreed that Amyas should go up to London to sound out Frank and his mother. To gather the scattered brethren of the Rose would spoil the broth with too many cooks, a culinary maxim Amyas respected deeply. Leaving Yeo with Cary to oversee the provisioning of salted meats and hardtack, Amyas departed the next morning, setting the rhythm of their sweeping saga into motion once more.
