The Virginian Colonel sat in silence within one chamber of the tavern, his thoughts heavy and his preparations grim. Across the hall, his adversary occupied himself with matters of a more solemn kind—writing a farewell letter to his mother, dictated by his younger brother and secretary, Harry Warrington. The words flowed with a grandeur that belied the trembling hand beneath them.

“…I scarcely suppose you will carry out the intentions you have at present in view,” George read aloud, emphasizing each syllable as if it were a judgment. “After this morning’s event, should I fall—as probably will be the case—your plans may well be abandoned.”

“Don’t say that, Georgy!” cried Harry, his voice cracking. “You’ll not fall! You can’t mean it!”

“‘As probably will be the case,’” George repeated, his tone unyielding. “You know what a good shot Colonel Washington is. And though I myself am no novice with a pistol, ‘tis likely one or both of us will fall.”

He paused, then added with cold irony: “I scarcely suppose you will carry out the intentions you have at present in view.” The bitterness in his voice was sharper than any blade.

Harry wept openly as he scribbled down the words. His fingers shook, and the ink blurred across the page. He had written “in view” as “in vew,” and a tear, salt and hot, smudged the line beneath.

“I can’t think about spelling now,” he whispered, burying his face in his hands. “I’m too miserable. Perhaps—perhaps it’s all nonsense. Maybe Colonel Washington never meant to take Castlewood. Never pretended to be our superior there. Never told Mother to have me flogged. Never intended to marry her. Never insulted me before the king’s officers. Never wrote to his brother saying we’d be better off under his rule?”

George snapped his fingers sharply. “The paper is here!” he said, slapping his breast-pocket. “If anything happens to me, Harry Warrington, you’ll find it on my corpse!”

“Write it yourself,” Harry begged, wiping his eyes with his elbows, ruining the entire draft. “I can’t do it anymore.”

So George took up a fresh sheet and began again. This time, he poured into the letter every ounce of learning and feeling he possessed—the long Latin phrases, the lofty sentiments, the quiet satire that only a young man with a scholar’s mind could muster. He asked that his trusted negro servant, Sady, be freed. That his copy of Horace be preserved. That his tutor, Mr. Dempster, receive a modest provision. That his silver fruit-knife, music books, and harpsichord go to little Fanny Mountain. And that Harry keep a lock of his hair—“as a token of my ever fond and faithfully attached George.”

With a flourish, he sealed it with the family crest—his grandfather’s seal—and placed it aside.

Then he pulled out his grandfather’s gold watch. “Two hours and a half gone,” he murmured. “Sady should be back with the pistols.”

“Take the watch,” he said softly. “It’s yours.”

“No good!” Harry cried, throwing his arms around him. “If he fights you, I’ll fight him too. If he kills you, I’ll kill him! Let him have a shot at me!”

The words spilled from his lips like curses—words so raw, so full of passion, that even the angels above might have winced as they recorded them in their celestial ledgers.

Meanwhile, General Braddock’s new aide-de-camp had already penned five letters in his bold, decisive hand. One to his mother at Mount Vernon. One to his brother. One addressed simply “M. C.” One to Major-General Braddock. And the fifth—delivered by a boy who overheard—was for Madam Esmond.

At this, Harry let out another cry of rage, and the recording angel once more fled across the heavens.

It was the tavern keeper who relayed these tidings. The Captain, dressed in his old militia uniform for the occasion, informed the boys that the Colonel was pacing in the garden, awaiting them. The regulars, he added, were nearly sober now.

A small plot near the log cabin had been fenced with shingles and cleared for a kitchen garden. There, beneath the pale afternoon sun, stood Colonel Washington—hands behind his back, head bowed, his face grave and sorrowful. Negro servants crowded at the palings, watching. Officers under the porch stirred from their sleep, waking to the gravity of the moment.

Captain Waring walked slowly beneath the sloping roof, while Captain Grace leaned over the railing, eyes wide but unfocused. Benson’s Tavern was no stranger to violence—cockfights, horse races, boxing matches—all drew the Virginia countryside together. Many a man had left there with broken ribs or blinded eyes. Gentlemen, farmers, slaves—they all came to witness the spectacle.

Now, the Colonel paced alone, lost in thought. There was no escape. No way out but through the duel—the brutal, accepted path of honor. He had spoken harshly to a young man, provoked by youthful insolence. The boy had demanded reparation. And though the Colonel regretted the heat of his words, he knew he must answer.

He did not wish to believe that George Warrington’s jealousy had festered so long. But the wrong was his, and the price must be paid.

Suddenly, a roar erupted from the distance—a cacophony of voices, loud and joyous. The negroes, always lovers of noise, especially when riding, shrieked and howled. Then came the clatter of hooves.

Mr. Sady burst into the courtyard on a steaming horse, firing a pistol into the air. Another shot rang out. The horse, familiar with such displays, remained calm. Sady rode in amid flapping chickens, squealing pigs, and bellowing comrades. He grinned, pulled the pistols from their holsters, snapped the locks, and pointed them at a fleeing pig.

“Comin’, mas’r,” he called. “Everybody a-comin’.”

And indeed, they were.

Horsemen approached rapidly. Little Mr. Dempster spurred his pony forward, digging his heels in. Behind him rode a woman in riding habit—on Madam Esmond’s grey mare. Could it be Madam Esmond? No—she was too stout. It was Mrs. Mountain.

“O Lor! O Golly! Hoop! Here dey come! Hurray!” the negroes cried.

Dr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain clattered into the yard, dismounted, pushed through the crowd, and rushed into the house. They tore through the rooms, burst onto the porch, where the British officers stared in dazed confusion, and hurried down the steps into the garden.

There stood George and Harry, facing their tall opponent. Before George could speak, Mrs. Mountain threw her arms around him.

“Oh, George, my darling! It’s a mistake! A terrible mistake—and it’s all my fault!”

“What mistake?” George asked, stepping back.

“That paper—I found it in his portfolio. The one where he says he’s marrying a widow with two children. Who else could it be but your mother? But it’s not! It’s not your mother at all! It’s Miss Custis—the wealthy widow the Colonel intends to wed. He always chooses rich ones. I knew he would. And he told Madam Esmond just before he left—said the marriage would happen after the campaign. And your mother—she’s furious, boys! When Sady came for the pistols and told the whole house you were going to fight, I told him to fire them off. I galloped after him, nearly broke my bones getting here!”

“I’ve a mind to break Sady’s,” George growled. “I ordered him not to say a word.”

“Thank God he didn’t,” Harry whispered.

“Thank God he didn’t,” echoed George.

“But what will Colonel Washington think?” George muttered, still angry. “That my servant told my mother I was going to duel?”

“You’ve shown your proof,” Harry said gently. “And thank Heaven, you’re not going to fight our old friend—our grandfather’s old friend. It was a mistake. There’s no quarrel now, is there? You acted under a false impression.”

“I certainly did,” George admitted. “But—”

“George! George Washington!” Harry suddenly shouted, springing across the cabbage patch toward the bowling green, where the Colonel stood.

We cannot hear his words, but we see them—the boy’s hands outstretched, his face alight with urgency and love. We imagine the tale spilling forth—of mistaken papers, jealous hearts, and a misunderstanding that could have ended in blood.

And when he finished, something rare happened. In those days, such gestures were common, but they are forgotten now. The Colonel stepped forward, took Harry in his arms, and held him tightly. His voice trembled. “Thank God, thank God for this!”

“Oh, George,” Harry said, tears in his eyes, “how I wish I could go with you on the campaign!”

The Colonel squeezed his hands, a bond forged in trust and shared history. Then he turned to George Warrington, extended his hand, and offered a formal greeting—no embrace, no warmth, only dignity.

“I find I have done you a wrong, Colonel Washington,” George said. “I must apologize—not for the error, but for my behavior since.”

“The error was mine,” Mrs. Mountain interjected. “I found the paper and showed it to George. All women are jealous.”

“Tis a pity you could not have kept your eyes off my private paper, madam,” Washington replied coolly. “A great deal of harm has come because I chose to keep secrets that concerned only myself and another. For a long time, George Warrington’s heart has been black with anger toward me. My feelings toward him have not been much kinder. All this pain might have been avoided had my papers stayed where they belonged.”

He paused. “I shall say no more, lest my emotions betray me again.”

Then, turning to Harry, he said, “Heaven bless thee, Harry.”

To George, he added, “Farewell. And take a true friend’s advice—try not to assume evil in those you call friends.”

“We shall meet again at the camp,” he continued. “And we’ll save our weapons for the enemy.”

With a stately bow to the English officers, the Colonel turned and rode away—swift, silent, and free of burden.

Behind him, the world breathed again.
