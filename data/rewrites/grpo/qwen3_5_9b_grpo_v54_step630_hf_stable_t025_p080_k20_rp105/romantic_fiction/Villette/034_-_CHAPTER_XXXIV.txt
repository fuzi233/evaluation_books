On a Thursday afternoon, the air thick with the promise of rain, Madame Beck summoned me from my studies. "Lucie," she said, her voice cutting through the humid silence, "are you free to run a few errands for me in the city?"

I was at liberty, so I accepted her service with alacrity. She handed me a list of silks, wools, and embroidery threads required for the pupils' work. Clad in a coat suited for the gloomy, sultry weather, I stepped onto the threshold of the street door, only to be recalled instantly.

"Pardon, Miss Lucie!" Madame Beck cried, rushing back into the salon. "I have just remembered one more commission. Will your good nature not be overburdened?"

"By no means," I assured her, ready to oblige.

She returned with a small, exquisite basket filled with hothouse fruit—rosy, perfect, and tempting, nestled among dark green leaves and pale yellow exotic flowers. "Take this to Madame Walravens," she instructed, her tone shifting to something more intimate. "Leave it at her house, Numéro 3, Rue des Mages, in the old town. It is not heavy, and it will not mar your neat appearance. Tell her I send my felicitations on her fête. The walk is long, but you have the whole afternoon; do not hurry. If you are late for dinner, I will save a portion, or Goton will toss you a trifle. You shall not be forgotten, my dear."

"And one more thing!" she added, catching me as I turned. "Insist on seeing Madame Walravens herself. Give the basket into her own hands. She is a punctilious personage who values propriety above all. Adieu!"

Thus dispatched, I set out. The shopping for fabrics was tedious, a slow process of matching colors and textures, but eventually, the list was complete. Only the fruit and the message remained.

I welcomed the prospect of a long walk into the grim, ancient Basse-Ville. The sky above Villette was settling into a mass of black-blue metal, heating at the rim and inflaming slowly to a heavy, ominous red. I feared a high wind, for storms demand strength and action, things I yield to with pain. But the threat of rain, the sullen downpour, or the dark rush of deluge requires only resignation—a quiet abandonment of self to be drenched. In return, the rain sweeps the capital clean, transforming Villette into a phantom city of Tadmor. Let the floods descend; I must first deliver this basket.

I arrived at the address just as an unknown clock tolled the third quarter past five. The street was not a street but a square, quiet and overgrown with grass between broad grey flags. The houses were large and ancient, looming behind trees that hinted at gardens. Antiquity brooded here; business had long fled. Once, this quarter belonged to the rich and grand, centered around the church of the Magi, whose dark, half-ruinous turrets still overlooked the square. But wealth had flown, leaving behind only the potential for penury or empty, mouldering silence.

Crossing the deserted place, where raindrops the size of five-franc pieces darkened the pavement, I saw only one sign of life: an infirm old priest, bending and propped on a staff, the very type of decay. He emerged from the very house to which I was directed. As I paused before the door and rang the bell, he turned to look at me. He did not avert his gaze; perhaps he saw in my basket of summer fruit and my youthful lack of dignity an incongruous figure in such a scene.

When the door finally opened, it was not by a young *bonne*, but by a very old woman dressed in an antique peasant costume. Her cap was hideous yet costly, with long flaps of native lace; her petticoat and jacket were of coarse cloth, and her sabots looked like little boats. Her appearance was soothingly in character with the setting, though her expression was anything but. She was cantankerous, barely replying to my inquiry about Madame Walravens, ready to snatch the fruit from my hand.

However, the old priest, hobbling up, checked her. Addressing the aged woman in the aboriginal tongue of Labassecour, he persuaded her to let me enter. He then escorted me upstairs, ushering me into a salon that was large, with a fine old ceiling and church-like windows of colored glass. Yet, the room was desolate, cast in the shadow of the coming storm, looking strangely lowering.

Inside, a smaller room awaited, its single window blind drawn tight. Through the deep gloom, few details were visible, save for the outline of a picture on the wall. As I stared at it, bewildered, the image seemed to give way. It shook, sank, and rolled back into nothingness, revealing an arched passage leading to a mystic winding stair of cold, uncarpeted stone.

Down this donjon stair descended a *tap, tap* like a stick striking the steps. Soon, a shadow fell upon the stairs, followed by a substance that approached me. Was it actual matter? An obstruction partially darkening the arch?

It drew near, and I saw it clearly. I began to comprehend where I was. This old square was indeed the Quarter of the Magi; the three towers overlooking it were the godfathers of three mystic sages of a dead and dark art. Hoar enchantment prevailed here. A spell had opened an elf-land: the cell-like room, the vanishing picture, the arch, and the stone stair were all parts of a fairy tale. Standing distinct against this backdrop was the chief figure—Cunegonde, the sorceress; Malevola, the evil fairy.

She might have been three feet tall, yet she had no shape. Her skinny hands rested upon each other, pressing the gold knob of a wand-like ivory staff. Her face was large and set, positioned before her breast rather than upon her shoulders; she seemed to have no neck. There were a hundred years in her features, and more perhaps in her eyes—her malign, unfriendly eyes, framed by thick grey brows and livid lids. They viewed me with a sort of dull displeasure.

She wore a gown of brocade dyed a bright blue, full-tinted as the gentianella flower, covered with a large pattern of satin foliage. Over it, a costly shawl gorgeously bordered, so large for her that its many-colored fringe swept the floor. But her chief adornments were her jewels: long, clear earrings blazing with a lustre that could not be false; rings on her skeleton hands with thick gold hoops and stones of purple, green, and blood-red. Hunchbacked, dwarfish, and doting, she was adorned like a barbarian queen.

*"Que me voulez-vous?"* she asked hoarsely, with the voice of male rather than female old age. Indeed, a silver beard bristled her chin.

I delivered my basket and my message.

*"Is that all?"* she demanded.

*"It is all,"* I replied.

*"Truly, it was well worth while,"* she answered. *"Return to Madame Beck and tell her I can buy fruit when I want it, and as for her felicitations, I mock them!"* With that, the courteous dame turned her back.

Just as she turned, a peal of thunder broke, and a flash of lightning blazed broadly over the salon and boudoir. The tale of magic proceeded with the due accompaniment of the elements. The wanderer decoyed into the enchanted castle heard the spell-wakened tempest rising outside.

What was I to think of Madame Beck? She owned strange acquaintances; she offered messages and gifts at a unique shrine, and the bearing of the uncouth thing she worshipped seemed inauspicious. There went that sullen Sidonia, tottering and trembling like palsy incarnate, tapping her ivory staff on the mosaic parquet and muttering venomously as she vanished.

Down washed the rain; the welkin lowered deep. The clouds, ruddy a moment ago, had now turned deadly pale through their blackness, as if in terror. Despite my earlier boast about not fearing a shower, I hardly liked to go out under this waterspout. The lightning was fierce, the thunder crashed nearby; this storm had gathered immediately above Villette, bursting at the zenith. Forked, slant bolts pierced athwart vertical torrents; red zigzags interlaced a descent blanched as white metal, all breaking from a sky heavily black in its swollen abundance.

Leaving Madame Walravens' inhospitable salon, I retreated to her cold staircase. There was a seat on the landing, and I waited. Someone glided along the gallery above; it was the old priest.

*"Indeed, Mademoiselle shall not sit there,"* he said. *"It would displease our benefactor if he knew a stranger was so treated in this house."*

He begged me so earnestly to return to the salon that, without discourtesy, I could not refuse. The smaller room was better furnished and more habitable. Thither he introduced me. Partially withdrawing the blind, he disclosed what seemed more like an oratory than a boudoir—a very solemn little chamber dedicated to relics and remembrance rather than present comfort.

The good father sat down as if to keep me company, but instead of conversing, he took out a book, fastened his eyes on the page, and employed his lips in whispering what sounded like a prayer or litany. A yellow electric light from the sky gilded his bald head; his figure remained in deep, purple shade. He sat still as sculpture, seemingly forgetting me for his prayers, looking up only when a fiercer bolt or harsher rattle told of nearing danger. Even then, it was not in fear, but in seeming awe, that he raised his eyes. I too was awe-struck. Being, however, under no pressure of slavish terror, my thoughts and observations remained free.

Truth be told, I began to fancy that the old priest resembled Père Silas, before whom I had knelt in the church of the Béguinage. The idea was vague, for I had seen my confessor only in dusk and profile, yet I seemed to trace a likeness. I thought also I recognized the voice. While I watched him, he betrayed, by one lifted look, that he felt my scrutiny. I turned to note the room; it too held a half-mystic interest.

Beside a cross of curiously carved old ivory, yellow with time and sloped above a dark-red *prie-dieu* furnished with a rich missal and ebon rosary, hung the picture whose dim outline had drawn my eyes before—the picture that moved, fell away with the wall, and let in phantoms. Imperfectly seen, I had taken it for a Madonna; revealed by clearer light, it proved to be a woman's portrait in a nun's dress. The face, though not beautiful, was pleasing: pale, young, and shaded with the dejection of grief or ill health. It was not intellectual; its amiability was that of a weak frame, inactive passions, acquiescent habits. Yet I looked long at that picture and could not choose but stare.

The old priest, who at first had seemed so deaf and infirm, must have retained his faculties in tolerable preservation. Absorbed in his book, without once lifting his head, he perceived the point towards which my attention was drawn. In a slow, distinct voice, he dropped these four observations concerning it:

"By that aged lady, Madame Walravens?" I inquired, fancying that I had discovered in the incurable grief of bereavement a key to that same aged lady's desperate ill-humor.

The father shook his head with a half-smile.

"No, no," he said. "A grand-dame's affection for her children's children may be great, and her sorrow for their loss lively; but it is only the affianced lover, to whom Fate, Faith, and Death have trebly denied the bliss of union, who mourns what he has lost, as Justine Marie is still mourned."

I thought the father rather wished to be questioned, so I inquired who had lost and who still mourned "Justine Marie." I received, in reply, a little romantic narrative told not unimpressively, accompanied by the now subsiding storm. I must say it might have been made much more truly impressive if there had been less French, Rousseau-like sentimentalizing and wire-drawing, and rather more healthful carelessness of effect. But the worthy father was obviously a Frenchman born and bred—a true son of Rome. When he did lift his eyes, he looked at me out of their corners with more and sharper subtlety than one would have thought could survive the wear and tear of seventy years. Yet, I believe, he was a good old man.

The hero of his tale was a former pupil of his, whom he now called his benefactor. This man had loved Justine Marie, the daughter of rich parents, at a time when his own worldly prospects justified his aspiring to a well-dowered hand. The pupil's father, once a rich banker, had failed, died, and left behind only debts and destitution. The son was then forbidden to think of Marie, especially since that old witch of a grand-dame, Madame Walravens, opposed the match with all the violence of a temper which deformity made sometimes demoniac. The mild Marie had neither the treachery to be false nor the force to be quite staunch to her lover; she gave up her first suitor but, refusing to accept a second with a heavier purse, withdrew to a convent and died in her noviciate.

Lasting anguish had taken possession of the faithful heart which worshipped her, and the truth of that love and grief had been shown in a manner which touched even me as I listened.
