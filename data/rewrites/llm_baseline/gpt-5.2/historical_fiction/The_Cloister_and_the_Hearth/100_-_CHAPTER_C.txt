In obedience to a custom I have always disliked, yet never quite had the stubbornness to refuse, I loiter a moment longer on the threshold and gather up the smaller lives I have dropped along the road—those crumbs of humanity that prove Gerard did not journey from Holland to Rome through a toy-world of carved dolls, but through breathing folk, each with a mouth to be fed, a tongue to speak, and a fate to taste.

Eli and Catherine lasted into a great age—so long that, in their dimming rooms, Gerard and Margaret became like old sayings: repeated less for exactness than for comfort. Their house kept its habits. The day still began with the small domestic music of it: a latch lifted, a pail set down, the soft chide of a hen, the scrape of a knife on a board. Bread still rose and browned; porridge still thickened with patient stirring; herbs still hung to dry where the air moved cleanly through them. If time thinned their sight, it sharpened, oddly, the other senses—Catherine could tell, by the first crackle in the hearth, whether the wood was too green; she could judge, by the sound of boiling, whether a pot wanted skimming. In such homely acoustics a life can root itself deeper than in any speech of pride.

Giles, too, was long-lived: he went to the court of Bavaria and was known there at ninety—yet by then had somehow turned into bones and leather, all angles and straps, his very voice grown trumpet-toned, as if it had been cured in smoke and hung in a draught. He was the sort of old man whose laugh startled birds from hedges, and whose step, though slow, still struck the floor like a signal.

Cornelis, rid at last of rivals and forgiven long since by his mother—who clung to him more and more now all her brood was scattered like seed—waited and waited and waited for his parents’ decease. He waited as some men watch a loaf in an oven, not with love but with appetite, counting minutes that belong to Heaven. Yet Catherine’s shrewd word came true: before she and her mate wore out, this worthy rusted away. At five-and-sixty he lay dying of mere old age in his mother’s arms—she a hale woman of six-and-eighty, with hands that had kneaded and scrubbed and tended and did not yet forget their work. He had lain unconscious awhile, but came to himself in articulo mortis; and seeing her close, he told her—there, on the edge of silence—how he would transform the shop and premises as soon as they should be his.

“Yes, my darling,” said the poor old woman, soothing him as one soothes a fevered child that begs for sweetmeats, and in another minute he was clay. And that clay was followed to the grave by all the feet whose shoes he had waited for—an odd procession, for in a town people remember a man not by his prayers but by what he measured and sold them, by the small tyrannies he practiced at the counter, by the way his words pinched like a tight welt. So the last sound he made in the world—the last breath leaving his throat—was answered by the common, indifferent sound of many soles on a road: life stepping over death, as it must.

Denys, broken-hearted at his comrade’s death, was glad to return to Burgundy. There, a small pension the court allowed him kept him until, quite unexpectedly, he inherited a considerable sum from a relation—fortune coming late, like a dish set down when appetite is already dulled. For many years he was known in his native place as a crusty old soldier, who could tell good stories of war when he chose: tales in which the clink of harness and the cough of powder still lived in his mouth, though the battlefields were far behind him. But he was also a bitter railer against women—his speech, once meant to command, curdled into complaint, as milk will if you leave it too long by the fire. Yet even he, in some winter evenings when the wind combed the eaves and the room held only the honest noises of a household—spoon against bowl, log settling, a child’s small breath—would grow quiet, and the very silence showed what no boasting could: that a man’s heart has its own language, and sometimes loses it.

Jerome, disgusted with northern laxity, retired to Italy; and having high connections became at seventy a mitred abbot. He put on the screw of discipline. His monks revered and hated him. Under his rule the cloister grew sharp-edged: days were divided by bells that cut the air cleanly, prayers marched in their appointed ranks, and every fault—whether of flesh, of tongue, or of mind—was hunted down like a rat in a granary. He ruled with iron rod ten years. And one night he died, alone; for he had never found the way to a single heart. The Vulgate was on his pillow, and the crucifix in his hand, and on his lips something more like a smile than was ever seen there while he lived; so that, at that awful hour, methinks he was not quite alone.

Requiescat in pace.

The Master he served has many servants, and they have many minds; and now and then a faithful one will be a surly one, as it is in these our mortal mansions. A rule may keep a house standing, yet it cannot make it warm; words may govern a tongue, yet they cannot always translate the secret hunger beneath it. There are dialects of tenderness that no dominant lexicon can compel, and silences that rise, like steam from a pot, when a soul has been too tightly covered.

The yellow-haired laddie, Gerard Gerardson, belongs not to Fiction but to History. She has recorded his birth in other terms than mine. Over the tailor’s house in the Brede Kirk Straet she has inscribed: “HAEC EST PARVA DOMUS NATUS QUA MAGNUS ERASMUS,” and she has written half-a-dozen lives of him. But there is something left for her yet to do. She has no more comprehended magnum Erasmum than any other pigmy comprehends a giant, or partisan a judge. First scholar and divine of his epoch, he was also the heaven-born dramatist of his century. Some of the best scenes in this new book are from his mediaeval pen, and illumine the pages where they come; for the words of a genius so high as his are not born to die. Their immediate work upon mankind fulfilled, they may seem to lie torpid; but at each fresh shower of intelligence Time pours upon their students, they prove their immortal race: they revive, they spring from the dust of great libraries; they bud, they flower, they fruit, they seed, from generation to generation, and from age to age.
