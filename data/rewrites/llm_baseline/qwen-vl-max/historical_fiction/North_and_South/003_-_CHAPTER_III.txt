“Learn to win a lady’s faith
Nobly, as the thing is high;
Bravely,5 as for life and death—
With a loyal gravity.
Lead her from the festive boards,
Point her to the starry skies,
Guard her, by your truthful words,
Pure from courtship’s flatteries.”

“Mr. Henry Lennox.”

Margaret had just been thinking of him—of his quiet inquiry into how she might spend her days at home—when his name was announced. It was as if the sun had suddenly broken through clouds: her face lit up with warmth and surprise as she set down her drawing board and stepped forward to greet him.

“Tell mamma, Sarah,” she said. “Mamma and I have so many questions to ask you about Edith. I’m truly grateful you’ve come.”

“Did not I say I would?” he replied, lowering his voice slightly. “But I heard you were far away in the Highlands, and never imagined Hampshire could be within reach.”

“Oh!” he answered, more lightly now, “our young couple were behaving like children—climbing mountains, sailing on lakes, risking everything. I thought they needed a guide, a mentor, someone to keep them out of trouble. And indeed they did—my uncle was nearly driven mad, fearing for them every hour. Once I saw how reckless they were, I felt it my duty not to leave until I’d seen them safely aboard the ship at Plymouth.”

“Have you been to Plymouth? Oh! Edith never mentioned that. Of course, she’s been writing in such haste lately. Did they really sail on Tuesday?”

“They did,” he said, “and I’m relieved to say it. I’ve brought messages from Edith—here’s a little note.” He pulled out a folded slip of paper.

“Oh, thank you!” Margaret exclaimed. Then, half wishing to read it alone, she made an excuse: “I must go tell mamma again—Sarah must have mistaken something—that Mr. Lennox is here.”

As she left the room, he began to look around, his eyes sharp and observant. The small drawing-room glowed in the morning sunlight. The bow window stood open, framing climbing roses and scarlet honeysuckle. Outside, the lawn sparkled with verbenas and geraniums in vivid hues. Yet the brightness outside only made the room seem duller—the carpet worn, the chintz faded, the whole space smaller and humbler than he had expected. For Margaret, who seemed so regal in spirit, this setting felt inadequate.

He picked up one of the books on the table. It was *The Divine Comedy* by Dante, bound in old Italian vellum and gold. Beside it lay a dictionary and several pages of handwritten notes—words copied out neatly in Margaret’s hand. They were dry, scholarly entries, yet somehow they pleased him. He sighed softly as he laid them down.

“The living is clearly as modest as she described. Strange, though—Beresford is a family of standing.”

Meanwhile, Margaret found her mother in the hall, wrapped in her usual apron, looking tired and preoccupied. It was one of Mrs. Hale’s restless days, when even simple tasks felt like burdens.

“It’s most unfortunate,” she said. “We’re dining early, and all we have is cold meat—so the servants can finish their ironing. Still, of course, we must invite him. He’s Edith’s brother-in-law, after all. And your father—he’s in such low spirits today. I went into his study earlier, and he had his head buried in his hands. I told him Helstone air didn’t agree with him any more than it does with me, and he suddenly looked up and begged me not to speak against Helstone. Said it was the one place he loved on earth. But I know it’s the damp and sluggish air.”

A chill passed over Margaret, as if the sun had been dimmed. She listened quietly, hoping her mother might find relief in speaking. But soon it was time to turn her thoughts back to Mr. Lennox.

“Papa likes Mr. Lennox,” she said gently. “They got on well at the wedding breakfast. His visit might cheer papa up. And don’t worry about dinner, dear mamma. Cold meat will do perfectly well—especially since Mr. Lennox likely sees a two o’clock meal as a light lunch.”

“But what shall we do with him till then? It’s only half-past ten.”

“I’ll ask him to sketch with me,” Margaret suggested. “I know he draws. It will take him away from you, mamma. Please come in now—he’ll think it odd if you don’t.”

Mrs. Hale removed her apron, smoothed her hair, and greeted Mr. Lennox warmly, as one might welcome a near relation. He clearly expected to stay the day, and accepted the invitation with such cheerful eagerness that Mrs. Hale wished she could offer more than cold beef. He admired everything—the house, the garden, the idea of sketching together. He insisted on not disturbing Mr. Hale, knowing they would meet soon at dinner.

Margaret brought out her art supplies. After selecting paper and brushes, the two set off, full of good humor.

“Now, please stop here for a moment,” Margaret said. “These cottages haunted me during the rainy fortnight—like reproaches for not having drawn them.”

“Before they vanished,” he replied. “If they’re worth sketching—and they are very picturesque—we should not wait until next year. Where shall we sit?”

“Oh!” she laughed. “You could have stepped straight from chambers in the Temple, after two months in the Highlands! Look at this tree trunk—left just right for the light. I’ll put my plaid over it—it’ll make a fine forest throne.”

“And your feet in that puddle for a royal footstool!” he teased. “Wait—I’ll move. You can come closer this way. Who lives in these cottages?”

“They were built by squatters fifty or sixty years ago. One is empty—foresters plan to tear it down once the other passes on. Poor old man! There he is—I must go speak to him. He’s deaf, so you’ll hear all our secrets.”

The old man stood bareheaded in the sun, leaning on his cane. His weathered face softened into a slow smile as Margaret approached.

Mr. Lennox quickly sketched the two figures, adding them as secondary elements to the landscape—something Margaret noticed only when they packed up. She laughed and blushed. He watched her face closely.

“Now, I call that treacherous,” she said. “I never dreamed you were making us subjects when you asked me to talk to Isaac.”

“It was irresistible,” he admitted. “You wouldn’t understand how tempting it was. I hardly dare say how much I’ll treasure this sketch.”

She walked to the brook to wash her palette. He wasn’t sure if she heard the last part. When she returned, her cheeks were flushed, but she looked innocent and unaware. He was glad—because the words had slipped out unguardedly, rare for a man as deliberate as Henry Lennox.

Back at the house, the atmosphere was bright again. The clouds on Mrs. Hale’s brow had lifted, thanks to a neighbor’s gift of two carp. Mr. Hale had returned from his morning walk and waited just outside the garden gate. He looked like a gentleman—though his coat was threadbare, his hat worn. Margaret was proud of him, always moved by how strangers respected her father. Yet her quick eye caught traces of unease beneath his calm.

Mr. Hale asked to see their sketches.

“I think you’ve made the thatch too dark,” he said, handing back Margaret’s. “Don’t you?”

“No, papa,” she replied, peering over his shoulder. “The house-leek and stone-crop turned darker in the rain. Is it like, papa?”

“Yes, very like,” he said. “Your figure and posture are excellent. And that’s exactly how poor old Isaac holds himself—stooped, stiff with rheumatism. What’s hanging from that branch? Not a bird’s nest?”

“Oh no,” she smiled. “That’s my bonnet. I can’t draw with it on—it makes my head too hot. I wonder if I could manage figures. There are so many people here I’d love to sketch.”

“I should say that a likeness you deeply wish to capture would always succeed,” Mr. Lennox remarked. “I have great faith in the power of will. I believe I’ve succeeded fairly well in yours.”

Mr. Hale had already entered the house. Margaret lingered, picking roses to adorn her gown for dinner.

“A London girl would catch the meaning behind that,” Mr. Lennox thought. “She’d read between the lines, detect the compliment. But I don’t believe Margaret—no, I won’t believe it!” He stopped himself. “Let me help you.” He reached above her, gathering velvety crimson roses, then placed two in his buttonhole before sending her inside, smiling and content.

Dinner passed pleasantly. Questions flowed—news of Mrs. Shaw in Italy, shared memories. In the warmth of conversation, Mr. Lennox forgot the slight disappointment he’d first felt upon seeing the truth in Margaret’s description of her father’s modest income.

“Margaret, my child,” Mr. Hale said, as wine was poured, “you might have gathered some pears for dessert.”

Mrs. Hale was taken aback. Desserts were rare at the parsonage. Yet behind Mr. Hale, biscuits and marmalade sat neatly arranged on the sideboard. But the idea of pears had seized him.

“There are brown beurrés against the south wall—worth any foreign fruit. Run, Margaret, and gather some.”

“I propose we move into the garden,” Mr. Lennox suggested. “Nothing beats biting into crisp, sun-warmed fruit. Though the worst is, the wasps are bold enough to challenge you at the very peak of pleasure.”

He rose, waiting for Mrs. Hale’s permission. She preferred to finish dinner properly—especially since Dixon had fetched the finger-glasses from storage. But Mr. Hale stood, ready to go, so she yielded.

“I shall arm myself with a knife,” Mr. Hale declared. “Such primitive eating is beyond me. I must pare and quarter it first.”

Margaret made a plate from a beetroot leaf, which brought out the golden hue of the pears beautifully. Mr. Lennox watched her more than the fruit. Her father, meanwhile, chose the ripest pear with care and sat on the bench to savor it slowly.

Margaret and Mr. Lennox strolled along the terrace under the south wall, where bees hummed among the hives.

“What a perfect life you lead here,” he said. “I used to mock poets who dreamed of ‘a cot beside a hill.’ Now I feel like a true Cockney. Twenty years of law study might be repaid by one year of this peace—these skies!” He looked upward. “This amber and crimson foliage, still as stone!”

“You must remember,” she said gently, “our skies aren’t always this blue. We have rain. Leaves fall and grow wet. Still, I think Helstone is one of the finest places in the world. Do you recall how you once dismissed it as ‘a village in a tale’?”

“Scorned, Margaret? That’s a strong word.”

“Perhaps. But I felt I had so much to say, and you—what shall I call it?—spoke disrespectfully of Helstone.”

“I will never do so again,” he said, earnestly.

They turned the corner of the path.

“I could almost wish, Margaret—” he hesitated. So unusual for a man of his fluency that she looked up, startled.

In an instant, she wished she were anywhere else—back with her parents, safe. She sensed he was about to say something she couldn’t answer. But pride surged within her. She would not shrink. She could respond—calmly, firmly. To fear such a moment was weak.

Then he took her hand—suddenly, decisively—forcing her to stop.

“Margaret,” he said, “I wish you didn’t love Helstone so much—didn’t seem so happy here. For three months, I’ve hoped to find you missing London—missing its friends—enough to listen kindly to one who has little to offer—only hopes—but who loves you, Margaret, almost despite himself.”

She tried to pull her hand free, quietly but firmly.

“Margaret,” he continued, seeing her lips tremble, “have I frightened you?”

She fought to control herself. When she could speak, she said: “I was startled. I didn so know you cared in that way. I’ve always thought of you as a friend. I prefer to keep it so. I don’t like being spoken to like this. I cannot answer as you want. Yet I’d grieve if I hurt you.”

“Do you”—he began, looking into her eyes—“love anyone else?”

He stopped. It felt like an insult to those clear, honest eyes.

“Forgive me,” he said. “I’ve been too sudden. I am punished. But let me hope. Tell me—you’ve never seen anyone whom you could—”

He paused. He couldn’t finish.

Margaret felt deep regret. If only he hadn’t formed this feeling!

“If only you’d never thought this,” she said. “It was such joy to think of you as a friend.”

“But may I hope,” he asked, “that someday you’ll think of me as a lover? Not now—there’s no rush—but someday—”

She was silent, searching her heart. Then: “I’ve never thought of you but as a friend. I like that. I’m certain I could never think of you otherwise. Let us both forget this conversation.”

He paused. Then, coldly: “Of course. Your feelings are clear, and this has clearly upset you. It’s best forgotten. But in theory, forgetting pain is noble. In practice—well, it will be hard for me.”

“You’re disappointed,” she said sadly. “Yet how can I help it?”

Her sorrow touched him. He struggled, then spoke more cheerfully, though with stiffness: “You should consider the mortification—not just of a lover, but of a man not given to romance. Prudent, worldly, as some say. Carried out of habit by passion—well, let’s say no more. But in the one outlet for deeper feeling, he meets rejection. I’ll console myself by mocking my own folly. A struggling barrister dreaming of marriage!”

She couldn’t reply. His tone annoyed her—highlighting every difference that had ever distanced her from him. Yet he had been her most understanding friend in London.

A faint scorn curled her lip.

Thankfully, they came upon Mr. Hale, still enjoying his pear, peeled in one long, delicate strip.

Margaret felt stunned, unable to join the trivial talk that followed.

She remained grave, wondering when Mr. Lennox would leave—when she could return to her thoughts.

He was eager to go, too. But he stayed, forcing light conversation—a sacrifice to his wounded pride.

He glanced at her often—her sad, thoughtful face.

“I am not indifferent,” he thought. “Not as she believes. I do not give up hope.”

Within minutes, he fell into sarcasm—comparing city and country life, aware of his own mockery.

Mr. Hale was puzzled. This man was different—lighter, sharper, more worldly—dissonant to his own nature.

All were relieved when Mr. Lennox said he must leave to catch the five o’clock train.

They walked back to the house to bid Mrs. Hale farewell.

At the last moment, Henry Lennox’s true self broke through.

“Margaret,” he said, “don’t despise me. I have a heart—despite all this careless talk. Proof? I love you more than ever—if I don’t hate you—for the disdain in your eyes these past thirty minutes. Good-bye, Margaret—Margaret!”
