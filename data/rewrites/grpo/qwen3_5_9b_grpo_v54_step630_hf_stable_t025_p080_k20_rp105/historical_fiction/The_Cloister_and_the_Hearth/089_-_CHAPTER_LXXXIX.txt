The turmoil that swept through Clement's sensitive mind, his tender conscience, and his loving heart within mere hours of such a sudden and tremendous shock defies the power of words to describe. Imagine yourself in his place, bearing the weight of such a revelation. If I were to write a volume on this internal storm, this very scene would be its conclusion. Instead, I shall recount only his next two overt acts, which reveal the state of his spirit once the first fierce tempest had subsided.

After spending the night with the dying hermit, offering and receiving holy consolations, Clement did not head for Rotterdam. His destination was Tergou. He went there to confront his fatal enemy, the burgomaster, armed with a parchment whose history was itself a romance, intending to force the man to disgorge the stolen goods and restore them to Margaret.

He arrived heated and dusty, stopping at a fountain where he began to eat his black bread and drink the cool water. Midway through this frugal meal, a female servant ran up, begging him to come and hear the confession of her dying master. Clement returned the bread to his wallet and followed her without a word.

She led him to the Stadthouse. He drew back with a slight shudder as she entered, but he quickly recovered his composure and followed her inside, up the stairs. There, propped up by pillows, lay his deadly enemy, Ghysbrecht, looking already like a corpse.

Clement eyed him from the doorway for a moment, recalling the tower, the wood, and the letter. Then, his voice trembling slightly, he spoke in a low tone, "Pax vobiscum!"

The sick man welcomed him with eager gratitude, limited only by his weakness. "Thank Heaven, thou art come in time to absolve me from my sins, father, and pray for my soul, thou and thy brethren."

"My son," Clement replied, "before absolution comes confession. In this act, there must be no reservation, if you value your soul's welfare. Consider carefully wherein you have most offended God and the Church, while I offer a prayer for wisdom to guide you."

Clement knelt and prayed. When he rose, he addressed Ghysbrecht with apparent calm, "My son, confess thy sins."

"Ah, father," the sick man sighed, "they are many and great."

"Great, then, be thy penitence, my son; so shalt thou find God's mercy great."

Ghysbrecht clasped his hands and began to confess with every appearance of contrition. He admitted to eating meat during mid-Lent, frequently missing Mass on Sundays and saints' days, and trifling with other religious observances, listing them with scrupulous fidelity.

When he finished, the friar said quietly, "'Tis well, my son. These are faults. Now, to thy crimes. Thou hadst done better to begin with them."

"Why, father, what crimes lie to my account if these be none?" Ghysbrecht asked.

"Am I confessing to thee, or thou to me?" Clement asked somewhat severely.

"Forgive me, father! Surely, I confess to you. But I know not what you call crimes."

"The seven deadly sins, art thou clear of them?"

"Heaven forefend I should be guilty of them. I know them not by name."

"Many do them all who cannot name them. Begin with that one which leads to lying, theft, and murder."

"I am quit of that one, any way. How do you call it?"

"AVARICE, my son."

"Avarice? Oh, as to that, I have been a saving man all my days; but I have kept a good table and have not altogether forgotten the poor. But alas, I am a great sinner. Mayhap the next will catch me. What is the next?"

"We have not yet done with this one. Bethink thee, the Church is not to be trifled with."

"Alas! Am I in a condition to trifle with her now? Avarice? Avarice?" He looked puzzled and innocent.

"Hast thou ever robbed the fatherless?" the friar inquired.

"Me? Robbed the fatherless?" Ghysbrecht gasped. "Not that I mind."

"Once more, my son, I am forced to tell thee thou art trifling with the Church. Miserable man! Another evasion, and I leave thee, and fiends will straightway gather round thy bed to tear thee down to the bottomless pit."

"Oh, leave me not! Leave me not!" shrieked the terrified old man. "The Church knows all. I must have robbed the fatherless. I will confess. Who shall I begin with? My memory for names is shaken."

The defense was skilful, but in this case, it failed.

"Hast thou forgotten Floris Brandt?" Clement asked stonily.

The sick man reared himself in bed in a pitiable state of terror. "How knew you that?"

"The Church knows many things," Clement said coldly. "And by many ways that are dark to thee. Miserable impenitent, you called her to your side, hoping to deceive her. You said, 'I will not confess to the cure, but to some friar who knows not my misdeeds. So will I cheat the Church on my deathbed and die as I have lived.' But God, kinder to thee than thou art to thyself, sent to thee one whom thou couldst not deceive. He has tried thee; He was patient with thee, warning thee not to trifle with Holy Church; but all is in vain. Thou canst not confess, for thou art impenitent as a stone. Die, then, as thou hast lived. Methinks I see the fiends crowding round the bed for their prey. They wait but for me to go. And I go."

He turned his back, but Ghysbrecht, in extremity of terror, caught him by the frock. "Oh, holy man, mercy! Stay. I will confess all, all. I robbed my friend Floris. Alas, would it had ended there; for he lost little by me. But I kept the land from Peter his son, and from Margaret, Peter's daughter. Yet I was always going to give it back; but I couldn't, I couldn't."

"Avarice, my son, avarice. Happy for thee 'tis not too late."

"No; I will leave it her by will. She will not have long to wait for it now; not above a month or two at farthest."

"For which month's possession thou wouldst damn thy soul forever, Thou fool!"

The sick man groaned and prayed the friar to be reasonable. The friar, however, persisted firmly yet gently and persuasively. With infinite patience, he detached the dying man's grip from another's property. There were times when his patience was tested to the limit, and he stood on the point of thrusting his hand into his bosom to produce the deed he had brought for that purpose. But after yesterday's outbreak, he was on his guard against anger. To conclude, he conquered his impatience; he overcame a personal repugnance to the man so strong that it made his own flesh creep while he struggled with this miser for his soul. At last, without a word about the deed, he won upon him to make full and prompt restitution.

How the restitution was made will be briefly related elsewhere, along with certain curious effects produced upon Ghysbrecht by it, and the terms on which Ghysbrecht and Clement parted.
