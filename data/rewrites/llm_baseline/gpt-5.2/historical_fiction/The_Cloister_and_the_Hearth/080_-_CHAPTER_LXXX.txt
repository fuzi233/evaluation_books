Brother Clement had taught and preached in Basle more than a twelvemonth, when, one raw morning, the door of the schoolroom opened and in came Brother Jerome—powdered with road-dust as with flour, his cloak grey at hem and shoulder, and yet with a light in his eye as though he carried a banner under his ribs.

“Give the glory to God, Brother Clement,” said he; “thou canst now wend to England with me.”

Clement’s face, that could be tender as warmed wine and keen as vinegar, brightened at once. “I am ready, Brother Jerome; and expecting thee these many months, have in the intervals of teaching and devotion studied the English tongue somewhat closely.”

“’Twas well thought of,” Jerome replied; and, as they broke their fast—black bread, a scrap of cheese sharp with age, and the thin beer of the house—he told him why the delay had been so long. He had waited until he could win from Rome extraordinary powers: to collect moneys for the Church’s need in England, and to hear confession in the secular monasteries.

“So now,” said Jerome, setting down his cup as if it were a seal upon the table, “gird up thy loins, and let us go forth and deal a good blow for the Church, and against the Franciscans.”

They went out together, two black figures on the long river-road; and the Rhine went beside them with its own perpetual sermon—now a lap and suck at stones, now a broad hush like a held breath, now the slap of oar-blades under a ferry, and, in the towns, the clang of smiths and the crying of women in markets. Clement, who had a quick ear for a place, would listen to its sound as other men read a book: if the alehouse door clacked all day, if the street-song was coarse, if at vesper-time the bell was drowned by shouting, he knew something of the town before he ever saw its faces.

Down the Rhine they preached for England. In the larger places they mounted the same pulpit in turn; in the smaller they often divided, taking different sides of the river, and meeting again at some appointed stone-cross or ferry. Both were able orators, yet of unlike craft.

Jerome’s preaching was noble, grave, and impressive; it marched like a file of armed men—step after step the same, and each step sure. His voice, too, held one note longer than most men would dare; it made a roof over the hearers’ heads, and under that roof they sat still. Clement’s was another manner: full of turn and variety, sometimes homely as talk in a bakehouse, and then suddenly lifting, as a wind lifts smoke straight up from a chimney. In its common flow it was gentle and winning; it curled round the understanding and the heart as the smell of good broth steals into a house and makes even the sour-tempered child think of sitting down. Yet when the thought rose, he rose with it; and then, for moments, he thundered and flashed and swept men off their feet as a torrent in spring.

Clement had also a habit—quiet as the salting of meat—that Jerome scorned. Before he preached he would contrive, without seeming to do so, to learn what had lately befallen a town, and what sin lay nearest its daily hand. He would question a lay-brother at the convent gate, a woman drawing water, a boy selling eels, a host in an inn; and, like a careful cook who tastes the pot before he adds pepper, he would know what spice the souls before him required. Jerome, unbending, would go straight to his chosen theme as a plough to its furrow, and thought it beneath him to bend aside to men’s particular mire.

Once, at a great town some leagues from the Rhine, they mounted the same pulpit in turn. The church was full—oil lamps staling the air, wet cloaks steaming, and the breath of a hundred bodies mixed with the faint sweet rot of rushes on the floor. Outside, through a door left ajar, came the market’s life in scraps: a donkey braying, a knife hammered on a stall, the far-off singing of a drinker already rehearsing the night.

Jerome preached against vanity in dress, a favourite quarry of his. He was eloquent and satirical; he pricked the pride of ribbons and sleeves and painted faces, and the people listened with complacency, for the barbs flew over them. It was a vice they were little given to; they could enjoy the lash when it fell on another’s back.

Then Clement rose, and the very shifting of the crowd told Jerome that the key had turned in another lock. Clement preached against drunkenness.

Here the air changed. The sin was a besetting one and, in those parts, almost fenced round from rebuke by custom; for the clergy themselves were infected, and a broad popular indulgence stood guard like a brawny porter at a tavern door. Clement struck at it without mercy, with blows taken from Holy Writ and from the plain records of village talk. A crime in itself, he called it the nursing mother of crimes—especially theft and murder. He named, without flinching, a parricide lately committed in that very town by an honest man in liquor; he told how a band of drunkards had roasted one of their comrades alive in a neighbouring village, as if man were pig at a feast and the devil the cook.

“Your last prince,” cried he, “is reported to have died of apoplexy; but well you know he died of drink. And of your aldermen, one perished miserably last month—dead drunk, suffocated in a puddle. Your children’s backs go bare that ye may fill your bellies with that which makes you the worst of beasts—silly as calves, yet fierce as boars—and drives your families to need and your souls to hell.”

The church muttered like a pot beginning to simmer.

“I tell ye,” he went on, and his voice took that strange mingling of pity and scourge which made women press their hands to their mouths, “your town—ay, and your very nation—would sink to the bottom of mankind, did your women drink as you do. And how long will they be temperate, and, contrary to nature, resist the example of their husbands and fathers? Vice ne’er yet stood still. Ye must amend yourselves, or see them come down to your mark. Already in Bohemia they drink along with the men.”

The whispering rose; the men scowled; the women’s eyes widened, not at the men’s guilt, but at the picture he painted.

“How shows a drunken woman?” he demanded. “Would you love to see your wives drunken, your mothers drunken?”

At this there broke forth a shout of horror; for those audiences had not learned to sit mute when the preacher drove a nail home. Clement caught the sound as a man catches the crackle of kindling.

“Ah, that comes home to you!” he cried. “What madmen! Think you it doth not more shock the all-pure God to see a man—His noblest work—turned to a drunken beast, than it can shock you creatures of sin and unreason to see a woman turned into a thing no better nor worse than yourselves?”

Then, with a skill that was almost household-craft, he set before them two pictures as plainly as a table laid: a drunkard’s house, and a sober man’s.

He made them smell the sour ale that had spilled and dried on boards; see the bread so thinly cut it was shame; hear the child’s thin crying at night because there was no milk, no pottage, no peace; see the wife’s face, once fresh as new churned butter, now pinched and grey; and, above all, hear the house’s sound—the drunken foot against the bench, the quarrel like a dog-fight, the sudden dead hush after a blow. Then he turned, and made them taste the other dwelling: the clean smell of wood-smoke; the pot that had something in it besides water; the loaf baked honest and shared; the ale taken in measure, as a seasoning not a flood; the children’s laughter, the wife’s voice not trembling, the husband’s steps steady; and in that house, even the silence was a good silence—the kind that lets a man sleep and a woman breathe.

He spoke so truly, with such domestic exactness, that the wives fell all to “ohing” and “ahing,” and “Eh, but that is a true word,” as if he were recounting their own pantries and bedsides.

The discourse made uproar. When they poured out, the crowd did not break cleanly like water; it clotted into knots. The men were indignant, their pride stung where it was tenderest; and then—strange commerce of household power—the women, who had cried out at his pictures, flattered their men and took their part openly against the preacher. A married man had a right to a drop; he needed it, working for all the family. And for their part, they said, they did not care to change their men for milksops. Thus did they defend with their tongues what their eyes had condemned.

Yet the very evening gave Clement his answer. A band of women—near a hundred—found him, crowded round him, and with a kind of hungry gratitude poured gifts into his wallet: the best they had, as if they were laying up provision against a famine of the soul. One pressed on him a little loaf, another a lump of butter wrapped in leaf, another a string of dried apples; some offered him the roses off their heads as though giving up an ornament for a remedy. They kissed his frock, blessed him “for taking in hand to mend their sots,” and spoke low and fast, like people afraid the men might hear and forbid their hope.

Jerome, watching, felt a chill disapproval, as of a monk tasting spice and calling it worldly. “Drunkenness is not heresy, Clement,” said he; “that a whole sermon should be preached against it.”

But as they went on down the river-road, town after town, Jerome found—much to his surprise—that Clement’s words sank deeper than his own. Men listened, thought, cried; sometimes they even amended their ways. Clement, in confession, would draw out a man’s shame as patiently as bones are picked from a fish; he would name the sin in the sinner’s own dialect and cadence, so that it was no Latin ghost but the very thing that stank on the man’s breath. Jerome saw it, and could not deny it.

“He hath the art of sinking to their peg,” thought Jerome. “Yet he can soar high enough at times.”

Upon the whole it puzzled him. He had a secret sense of superiority to his tenderer brother; and after about two hundred miles of such travelling, that puzzle began to displease him as well. He checked the feeling as petty and unworthy; for he was stern first with himself.

“Souls differ like locks,” said he within, “and preachers must differ like keys, or the fewer should the Church open for God to pass in. And certes, this novice hath the key to these northern souls, being himself a northern man.”

So they came slowly down the Rhine—sometimes drifting a few miles by boat, the water slapping the timbers and the oars creaking like old doors; but in general walking the banks, barefoot, preaching and teaching and confessing, the soundscape of each place entering their bodies as surely as its bread and air.

At length they reached the town of Düsseldorf.

There was the little quay. There were the same waters licking the posts. There were the streets, the familiar angles of houses, the smell of river-mud and tarred rope, the calls of boatmen that a man might recognise blindfold. There stood “The Silver Lion.”

Nothing had changed—save he who looked on it.

Brother Clement landed and walked through it barefoot, his heart calm and cold, his hands crossed on his breast, his eyes bent meekly on the ground: a true son of Dominic and of Holy Church, passing through the world like a shadow that had once been flesh.
