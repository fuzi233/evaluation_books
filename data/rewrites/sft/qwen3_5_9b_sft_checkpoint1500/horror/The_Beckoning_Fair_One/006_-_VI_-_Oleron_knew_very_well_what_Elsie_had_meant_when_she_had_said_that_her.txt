Oleron understood perfectly what Elsie had intended when she mentioned the postcard preceding her next visit. He realized, with a crushing finality, that she knew—and knowing, she simply did not want him. A miserable, hollow ache tore through him when she arrived again within a week, knocking without announcement. Standing on the landing, she declared she had no intention of staying, yet he found himself pressing her until she begrudgingly entered.

Her pretext was a query regarding short-story commissions, something he should perhaps pursue. He offered thanks, though he saw through the lie immediately; her business concluded, she seemed desperate to flee. As he accompanied her downstairs, the atmosphere shifted from polite evasion to genuine terror.

Halfway down the staircase, a sharp, splintering crack echoed. Elsie stopped mid-step, a stifled scream catching in her throat. Oleron knew the timber was ancient, having traversed it countless times without incident. Now, however, Elsie Bengough met disaster. She had driven her heel through a stair tread.

Springing forward, he reached for her side. "Oh, my poor girl!" he gasped.

Instead of fear, she erupted into a hysterical laughter. "It's my weight—I know I'm getting fat—"

"Be quiet!" he hissed, teeth clenched, as he began to pry away the jagged wood. "Let me clear these splinters."

Her laughter and sobs mixed, fixated on her perceived bulk. "I shall be too heavy for anything soon..."

He forced his hands downward, struggling to free her foot. The torn boot revealed the extent of the abrasion on her ankle—a ghastly, raw wound. "Good God—good God!" he muttered, the words tumbling over each other.

"I'll be too heavy," she sobbed, the delusion of vanity warring with physical pain.

Refusing to climb back up to inspect the injury, she demanded release. "No, let me go quickly—let me go quickly."

"But it's a frightful gash!"

"Not so bad—let me escape. I'm... I'm not wanted."

Those words struck Oleron like a physical blow, dropping his gaze as if she'd slapped him. "Elsie!" he choked out, shock breaking his composure.

She made a violent gesture, as if discarding something repulsive. "Oh, Paul, not *that*—not *you*! Of course I mean that too, in a sense—you know what I mean!... But if the other cannot be, spare me this now! I tried to keep away, oh, I *did* try!"

It was unbearable, a heartbreak that twisted his gut. What could he say? He did not love her.

"Let me go—I'm not wanted—take away what's left of me—"

"Dear Elsie, you are very dear to me—"

Again, she waved her hand, rejecting his comfort. "No, not that—not anything less! Don't offer me anything less! Leave me a shred of pride!"

"Let me get your hat and coat. Let me take you to a doctor," he pleaded.

She refused everything, refusing even the support of his arm. Another unsteady laugh bubbled up. "I'm sorry I broke your stairs, Paul. Will you go and see about the short stories, won't you?"

He groaned, the irony bitter. "Then, since you refuse a doctor, will you walk across the square and let Mrs. Barrett look at you? Look, Barrett is passing now—"

Barrett, with his long nose, peered curiously down the alley, but as Oleron opened his mouth to call him, the man vanished without a word. Elsie was frantic to leave the premises, promising eventually to see a physician but insisting on doing so alone.

"Good-bye," she whispered.

Oleron watched her depart, lingering until she passed the "To Let" sign outside, which resembled a hatchet poised to strike. He felt a superstitious dread that even the boards might fall and maim her.

That night, Oleron ate nothing. His mind was consumed by Elsie's haunting cry echoing in his ears: *"I'm not wanted—don't offer me anything less—let me take away what's left of me."* Oh, if only he could convince himself he loved her!

Pacing his flat, he wandered from room to room, unable to escape the phantom of her distress. Later, after twilight fell, he lit no candles, stoked the fire, and collapsed into a chair.

"Poor, poor Elsie..."

Yet, even as his heart ached, reason held firm. If only he had noticed sooner! If only he had applied common sense! Those walks, those moments where he'd taken her arm as a sisterly friend—he had been a fool. Too late now. It was her burden to bear, her duty to distance herself. He would aid her from afar, ensuring he never sat in her presence. If she returned, he would usher her out instantly.

Darkness swallowed the room; the fire died down to embers. Sitting in the gloom, he winced as fresh tortures of memory played in his mind. Suddenly, a new anxiety seized him—not for her rejection, but for her safety. A horrific vision formed: perhaps she was already standing on an embankment, staring down into dark water, or hanging from the hook above the door. Women did strange, fatal things. An inquest would follow, requiring him to identify a body bearing an ill-healed hand wound and a severe ankle abrasion. Barrett would testify that he saw her leaving his house.

Realizing his thoughts were morbid, he exerted a will to push them aside, listening to the faint creaks and ticks within the paneling. *If only I had married her!* The image of her face rose again, drawn tight with pain, ugly and swollen with tears. Ugly, yes—positively blubbered. If tears were women's weapons, hers had been turned inward, a suicide note written in fluid.

Then, abruptly, his focus shifted to her two accidents. They were extraordinary. He could not possibly have left that old nail protruding; he had fetched tools specifically to remove it, convinced the step had been as sound as any other. These events were inexplicable. If such things could occur, anything could. No beam, no jamb, no plank was safe; every piece of furniture, every nail, threatened to become a trap. The whole house teemed with a malevolent life, a chorus of noises filling the silence like a microphone amplifying a crowd.

Consciously identifying the sources of most sounds—the settling wood, the wind, the street below—he missed one persistent anomaly. It had begun minutes prior and continued now: a soft, sweeping rustle, holding within it an almost inaudible, dry crackle. For a fleeting moment, it captured his attention, then faded into the background of his grief-stricken thoughts.

In that instant, he felt closer to loving her than ever before. To some men, their beloveds were cherished precisely because of those mortal flaws that reminded us of our transience, of the shared fate awaiting us all. Strangling sobs, blurred vision, bodies battered by sickness, minds hardened by the world's cruelty—love would be diminished if these barriers existed. In truth, he did love Elsie Bengough. Her happiness had never stirred him, but her sorrow nearly awakened him.

Suddenly, the meditation shattered. His ear, previously dormant, snapped back to consciousness. The soft, repeated noise returned. A long, sweeping motion, punctuated by that insistent, microscopic cracking. It persisted with a curious urgency, growing slightly louder as he paid it heed.

Startled upright, Oleron sat rigid, straining to locate the source. The silky rustle came again. He tried to anchor it to a material object, but found nothing.

The next instant, he leaped from his chair, unnerved and terrified. The wooden seat hung suspended for a heartbeat before crashing down, sending the iron fire-tools clattering across the floor. There was only one noise in the world like that which had caused him to spring.

Moments later, the sound recurred. This time, Oleron reached blindly behind him, grasping at empty air, backing slowly until his spine pressed against the wall.

"God in Heaven!" The cry burst from his lips. Silence followed.

Immediately, a high-pitched shriek escaped him. "What is it? What's there? *Who's* there?"

A scuttling sound made his knees buckle, but he knew it was a rat. A creature that turned his stomach sick did not belong here. The other sound—the impossible, hair-brushing rustle—had ceased completely. Again, he called out.

He shouted repeatedly, and then a new terror seized him: the horror of hearing his own voice echo in the void. He dared not speak again. His shaking hand plunged into his pocket for a match, finding only emptiness. Perhaps they were on the mantelpiece?

He navigated carefully around a small recess, keeping his back to the wall, until his fingers brushed the cold stone of the mantel. Groping frantically, his hand closed around a box. It tumbled onto the hearth. In the flickering firelight, he could just discern the scattered matches, but his trembling fingers could not retrieve them until he cornered them deep within the fender.

Finally, he rose and struck a light.
