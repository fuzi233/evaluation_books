It is not needful that I set down every mile of that tramp to Loo, nor each halt and start along Solomon’s Great Road, for the road itself did the talking—straight as a knife-cut, hard-beaten, and old with a kind of age that seems to hum under the foot. For two full days we travelled, and the farther we went the more the country fattened, as though the earth had been well tended by patient hands for generations. Round every kraal lay belts of cultivation—patches of mealies with their tassels whispering, beans climbing their poles, gourds swelling in the sun—so that the air held the green smell of bruised leaf and warm soil. And everywhere there were soldiers. In Kukuanaland, as among other fighting nations, every able-bodied man was a man under arms: war lay in the daily routine like a pot always on the edge of the fire.

All day companies of warriors overtook us—thousands upon thousands hurrying toward Loo for the great yearly review and feast. Their shields flashed in the sun, their plumes nodded like grass in wind, and the road rang with the measured thud of feet and the dry click of spear-hafts, a great moving music that rose and fell with the land. There is a comfort in a clean soundscape—wind, footfall, distant cattle, a song started and taken up properly—very different from the harsh clatter and ceaseless grinding that civilised places have taught us to endure. Here the noises belonged to life.

At sunset of the second day we rested on the crown of some heights where the road ran. Before us, spread out like a mat, lay Loo upon a rich plain—an enormous town for a native place, five miles round at least, with outlying kraals like fingers, built for regiments to camp in on great occasions. Northward, two miles off, stood a curious hill shaped like a horseshoe, and I noted it because it sat there like a thing waiting to be understood.

Through the middle of the grass city ran a river, dividing it into two halves, bridged here and there; it was the same stream we had seen from the slopes of Sheba’s Breasts. And far away—sixty or seventy miles by my guessing—three great mountains, snow-capped and set like the points of a triangle, sprang sheer and harsh from the level. They were nothing like Sheba’s Breasts with their rounded softness. These were abrupt, cut and shadowed, like black teeth with white crowns.

Infadoos saw us looking and spoke, as though giving a plain direction about a footpath.

“The road ends there,” he said, pointing. “At the Three Witches.”

“Why does it end?” I asked.

He shrugged in that way of his that is half ignorance, half refusal. “Who knows? The mountains are full of caves, and there is a great pit between them. There the wise men of old went to get what they came for. There now our kings are buried—in the Place of Death.”

“And what was it they came for?” I pressed him.

“Nay, I know not.” His eyes flicked quick and sharp. “My lords who have dropped from the Stars should know.”

“You are right,” I said, taking his hint as one takes salt—sparingly. “In the Stars we learn many things. I have heard that the wise men of old came there to find bright stones—pretty playthings—and yellow iron.”

“My lord is wise,” he answered, suddenly cold. “I am but a child. My lord must speak with Gagool the old at the king’s place. She is wise even as my lord,” and he turned away.

When he had gone I pointed the mountains out to the others. “There are Solomon’s diamond mines,” I said.

Umbopa stood near, sunk in one of his heavy silences, and my words struck him awake.

“Yes, Macumazahn,” he put in, in Zulu, “the diamonds are surely there, and you shall have them, since you white men are so fond of toys and money.”

“How dost thou know that, Umbopa?” I asked sharply. I liked neither his knowing nor his way of choosing when to show it.

He laughed softly. “I dreamed it in the night, white men,” and turned his heel as Infadoos had.

Sir Henry watched him go. “Now what is our black friend driving at?” he said. “He knows more than he says. Has he heard anything—anything of my brother?”

“Nothing,” I answered. “He has asked everyone he can get friendly with. They swear no white man ever was seen in this country before.”

“Do you suppose he got here at all?” Good said. “We’ve only reached it by a miracle. Could he do it without the map?”

Sir Henry’s face hardened. “I don’t know,” he said, “but I think I shall find him.”

The sun went down, and the change was as sudden as a knife: no long dying of light, no kindly dusk, but darkness rushing upon the land like something with weight. Then, as quickly, the west glowed and the moon rose—clean, white, and masterful—flooding the plain with silver until the fires of the town pricked out below us like seeds sown in ash. We stood a while and let that quiet brightness wash over us. The world seemed to hold its breath, and the ordinary sounds—the murmur of insects, the far-off lowing of cattle, the faint chorus of marching feet—fell into a kind of order under the moon, as if the night itself were conducting them.

Infadoos broke in at last.

“If my lords are rested, we will go on to Loo. A hut is made ready. The moon is bright; we shall not fall by the way.”

So we went down and in, and in an hour reached the outskirts of the town. Its size, mapped by thousands of fires and the dark circles of hut-walls, seemed without end; Good, who cannot see a large thing without trying to make it small by joking, called it “Unlimited Loo.”

We came to a moat with a drawbridge. Arms rattled; a sentry challenged hoarsely. Infadoos answered with a password I did not catch, and we passed through into the central street, tramping on and on past endless huts, the air thick with smoke, crushed grass, sweat, and the rich, tempting scent of roasting meat. Here and there women moved in and out of the firelight with pots on their heads, and the soft chink of earthenware carried differently than metal—it has a domestic sound, comforting even in a strange place.

At last Infadoos halted at the gate of a small group of huts set round a courtyard powdered white with limestone, and told us these were our “poor” quarters.

Each of us had a hut, and they were better than any we had yet seen. In each stood a bed of tanned skins laid on a mattress of sweet-smelling grass—aromatic as thyme when you crush it between your fingers. Water waited in earthen jars; we washed the dust from ourselves and watched it run away grey. Then young women—handsome, composed, untroubled by the shyness Good expected—brought in food: roasted meat, browned and shining, and mealie cobs served neatly on wooden platters. The mealies smelled of smoke and warm starch, and the fat on the meat carried that deep, animal sweetness that makes a hungry man forget to talk.

We ate and drank. Then, because we had learned caution on this road, we asked that all the beds be moved into one hut. The girls smiled at that, as though at children afraid of shadows, but they obeyed. We slept like dead men.

When we woke, the sun was high. The same attendants stood inside the hut, ready to “make us ready,” as though we were infants to be dressed.

“Make ready, indeed,” growled Good. “When one has only a flannel shirt and a pair of boots, that does not take long. Ask them for my trousers, Quatermain.”

I asked. The answer came back that these sacred relics had already been taken to the king, who would see us in the forenoon.

We sent the young women outside, to their astonishment and, I suspect, their disappointment, and did the best toilet possible. Good shaved again—only the right side of his face—leaving the left, where whiskers had begun to grow, untouched as we had urged. Sir Henry’s yellow hair was nearly to his shoulders; he looked more like some sea-king from an old tale than a modern English gentleman. My own grizzled scrub had reached a full inch, which I consider excessive.

After breakfast and a pipe, Infadoos himself brought word that Twala the king was ready to see us, if we would be pleased to come.

We returned that we would wait until the sun was a little higher—we were weary, and so forth. It is useful with uncivilised people not to hurry; they take politeness for fear. So we sat an hour and prepared such presents as we could manage—Ventvögel’s Winchester rifle and some beads. The rifle and ammunition we meant for the king; the beads for his wives and courtiers. We had given a few to Infadoos and Scragga and seen how their eyes caught fire at the colours, as though the very glass held imprisoned light.

At length we rose, and, guided by Infadoos, went to the audience. Umbopa carried the rifle and beads.

After a few hundred yards we came to an enclosure like our lodging-court, only vast—six or seven acres at least. A fence circled it, and just inside the fence stood a ring of huts: the houses of the king’s wives. Facing the gateway on the far side stood a very large hut, built alone, where the king lived. The open ground between should have been empty, but it was filled—company after company—by warriors, seven or eight thousand of them, standing as still as carved posts. Plumes lifted and fell with breathing; spearpoints caught the light; iron-backed ox-hide shields made a repeating pattern like scales. The sight was grand, but the greater impression was the hush of it—the massed silence of men trained to become part of a single will.

The space in front of the great hut was clear, and before it were placed stools. At Infadoos’s sign we sat—three stools for us, Umbopa behind. Infadoos took his place by the hut door.

Ten minutes passed, perhaps more, in dead quiet. Eight thousand eyes pinned us. It is a hard thing to sit still under such a gaze, but we did it.

At last the hut door opened. A gigantic figure stepped out, a tiger-skin karross flung across his shoulders. Behind him came Scragga, and then something that at first glance looked like a withered monkey wrapped in fur. The giant sat upon a stool; Scragga stood behind; the monkey-thing crawled on all fours into the shade and squatted.

Silence held.

Then the giant slipped off the karross and rose, and we saw the king fully. He was enormous, built like a bull, but his face was the most repulsive I have ever looked upon—thick lips, flat nose, one gleaming black eye, the other a hollow. Cruelty and appetite sat on his features as plainly as grease on a platter. A splendid plume of white ostrich feathers rose from his head. He wore a shirt of shining chain armour; white ox-tail garnishes bound waist and right knee. In his right hand he held a great spear; round his neck a thick torque of gold; on his forehead, bound there, a single enormous uncut diamond shone dully, as though it drank light rather than gave it back.

Still he said nothing. Then he lifted his javelin.

Instantly eight thousand spears rose, and eight thousand throats rang out the royal salute: “Koom!”

Three times it was shouted, each time as deep as thunder, and the very ground seemed to tremble with it. The sound struck the chest and shook the teeth; it was a noise that could rule men by its weight alone.

“Be humble, O people,” piped a thin voice from the shadow—monkey-like, yet sharp as a bird’s cry—“it is the king.”

“It is the king,” boomed the multitude back. “Be humble, O people, it is the king.”

Then again silence, thick enough to choke on.

It was broken by a clatter: a soldier on our left dropped his shield; it struck limestone.

Twala turned his one eye.

“Come hither, thou,” he said.

A fine young man stepped out and stood before him.

“It was thy shield that fell, thou awkward dog. Wilt thou make me a reproach in the eyes of these strangers from the Stars? What hast thou to say?”

The poor fellow went pale beneath his dark skin. “It was by chance, O Calf of the Black Cow,” he murmured.

“Then it is a chance for which thou must pay,” said the king, as calmly as a man ordering a pot to be moved from the fire. “Thou hast made me foolish; prepare for death.”

“I am the king’s ox,” came the low answer.

“Scragga,” roared Twala, “let me see how thou canst use thy spear. Kill me this blundering fool.”

Scragga stepped forward with an ugly grin and lifted his spear. The doomed man covered his eyes and stood still. We sat frozen. Sir Henry’s hands clenched, and I heard his breath go harsh.

Scragga poised—once, twice—and drove the spear home. It stood a foot out behind the man’s back. He flung up his hands and dropped dead. A murmur rolled like wind through dry grass, circled, and died. The body lay there, and for a moment my mind refused to accept that I had just watched a man killed as one kills a chicken for supper.

Sir Henry sprang up and swore, a great oath that would have startled an English drawing-room; then the heavy silence pressed down and he sat again, white with fury.

“The thrust was a good one,” said the king. “Take him away.”

Four men lifted the corpse and carried it off.

“Cover up the blood-stains, cover them up,” piped the thin voice from the shade. “The king’s word is spoken, the king’s doom is done!”

A girl came forward with a jar of powdered lime and scattered it over the red mark until the blood vanished into white dust, as though memory itself could be smothered with a handful of chalk.

Sir Henry shook with anger. I leaned close.

“Sit still, for heaven’s sake,” I whispered. “Our lives depend on it.”

He yielded, though it cost him.

Twala waited until the stain had disappeared. Then he addressed us.

“White people,” he said, “who come hither, whence I know not, and why I know not, greeting.”

“Greeting, Twala, King of the Kukuanas,” I answered.

“White people, whence come ye, and what seek ye?”

“We come from the Stars; ask not how. We come to see this land.”

“Ye journey from far to see a little thing.” His eye shifted to Umbopa. “And that man with you—does he also come from the Stars?”

“Even so,” I said. “There are people of thy colour in the heavens above; but ask not of matters too high for thee, Twala the king.”

“Ye speak with a loud voice, people of the Stars,” he answered, and his tone rasped. “Remember, the Stars are far off, and ye are here. How if I make you as him whom they bore away?”

I laughed aloud, though my stomach tightened.

“O king,” I said, “walk warily over hot stones lest thou burn thy feet; hold the spear by the handle lest thou cut thy hands. Touch but one hair of our heads, and destruction shall come upon thee. Have not these”—I nodded toward Infadoos and Scragga, who was wiping the dead man’s blood from his spear with a bored care—“told thee what manner of men we are? Hast thou seen the like of us?” And I pointed to Good, certain enough that Twala had never seen any creature like Good’s present half-shaved, half-bearded countenance.

“It is true,” said the king, studying him. “I have not.”

“Have they not told thee how we strike with death from afar?” I went on.

“They have told me, but I believe them not. Let me see you kill. Kill me a man among those who stand yonder, and I will believe.” He pointed to the opposite side.

“Nay,” I answered. “We shed no blood of men except in just punishment. But if thou wilt see, bid thy servants drive in an ox through the kraal gates, and before he has run twenty paces I will strike him dead.”

“Nay,” he laughed, “kill me a man.”

“Good, O king,” I said, keeping my voice cool, “so be it. Do thou walk across the open space, and before thy feet reach the gate thou shalt be dead; or, if thou wilt not, send thy son Scragga.”

At that Scragga gave a sort of yelp and bolted into the hut like a whipped cur.

Twala frowned. The joke sat badly with him.

“Let a young ox be driven in,” he ordered.

Two men ran off.

“Now, Sir Henry,” I murmured, “you shoot. I want him to see I am not the only magician.”

Sir Henry took up his heavy “express” and tried to set his face. “I hope I shall make a good shot,” he groaned.

“You must,” I said. “If you miss with the first barrel, give him the second. Sight for a hundred and fifty yards. Wait till the beast turns broadside.”

We waited. The great enclosure held its breath; even the distant sounds seemed to thin out, as if the air itself were listening. Then an ox came running straight for the gate, shoved through, saw the mass of men, and stopped stupidly, turning and bellowing.

“Now,” I whispered.

Up went the rifle.

Bang—then the solid thud of bullet striking flesh. The ox toppled and kicked on its back, shot through the ribs. The semi-hollow bullet had done its work quickly. A sigh rose from the thousands, one great intake of breath like surf.

I turned to the king as though nothing remarkable had happened.

“Have I lied, O king?”

“Nay,” he answered, and there was awe under his roughness. “It is the truth.”

“Listen then, Twala,” I said. “We come in peace, not in war. See”—and I held up the Winchester repeater—“here is a hollow staff that shall enable thee to kill even as we kill. Only I lay this charm upon it: thou shalt kill no man with it. If thou liftest it against a man, it shall kill thee. Stay—I will show thee. Bid a soldier step forty paces and set the shaft of a spear in the ground with the flat blade towards us.”

It was done in moments.

“Now,” I said, “I will break yonder spear.”

I took careful aim and fired. The bullet struck the blade full and shattered it into fragments.

Again that great sigh, half fear and half delight at power.

“Now, Twala, we give this magic tube to thee,” I said, and the lie had to be wrapped carefully, like meat carried through flies. “By-and-by I will show thee how to use it. But beware how thou turnest the magic of the Stars against a man of earth.” I handed him the rifle.

He took it as gingerly as a man takes a live coal and set it at his feet.

As he did so, the monkey-like figure crept out from the hut’s shadow. It came on all fours, then rose, and threw back its fur covering. What stood revealed was a woman’s face—if face it could be called—so shrunken it seemed no bigger than a child’s, yet seamed all over with deep yellow wrinkles like sun-cracked mud. A slit of a mouth lay sunk beneath them; the chin curved outward to a point; there was hardly a nose. The skull projected, bare and parchment-coloured, and under snow-white brows two large black eyes burned with life, bright as coals in a hearth. The scalp moved and contracted like a cobra’s hood.

She stood a moment, and a chill ran through me that had nothing to do with air. Then she stretched out a claw-like hand, nails nearly an inch long, laid it on Twala’s shoulder, and began to speak in a thin, piercing voice that seemed to cut straight through flesh and lodge in the bone.

“Listen, O king! Listen, O warriors! Listen, O mountains and plains and rivers, home of the Kukuana race! Listen, O skies and sun, O rain and storm and mist! Listen, O men and women, O youths and maidens, and O ye babes unborn! Listen, all things that live and must die! Listen, all dead things that shall live again—again to die! Listen, the spirit of life is in me and I prophesy. I prophesy! I prophesy!”

Her words faded into a faint wail. Dread took the air, and even the warriors seemed to tighten, as though a wind had shifted to blow from graves.

“Blood! blood! blood!” she cried. “Rivers of blood—blood everywhere. I see it, I smell it, I taste it—it is salt! It runs red upon the ground; it rains down from the skies.

“Footsteps! footsteps! footsteps! The tread of the white man coming from afar. It shakes the earth; the earth trembles before her master.

“Blood is good—the red blood is bright; there is no smell like the smell of new-shed blood. The lions shall lap it and roar, the vultures shall wash their wings in it and shriek with joy.

“I am old! I am old! I have seen much blood—ha, ha!—but I shall see more ere I die, and be merry. How old am I, think ye? Your fathers knew me, and their fathers knew me, and their fathers’ fathers’ fathers. I have seen the white man and know his desires. I am old, but the mountains are older than I. Who made the great road, tell me? Who wrote the pictures on the rocks, tell me? Who reared up the three Silent Ones yonder, that gaze across the pit, tell me?” She pointed, and though her arm was thin as a stick, the gesture seemed to drag the eye toward the Three Witches.

“Ye know not, but I know. It was a white people who were before ye are, who shall be when ye are not—who shall eat you up and destroy you. Yea! yea! yea!

“And what came they for, the White Ones, the Terrible Ones, skilled in magic and all learning, strong, unswerving? What is that bright stone upon thy forehead, O king? Whose hands made the iron garments upon thy breast, O king? Ye know not, but I know—I the Old One, I the Wise One, I the Isanusi, the witch doctress!”

Then she turned that vulture-head upon us.

“What seek ye, white men of the Stars—ah, yes, of the Stars? Do ye seek a lost one? Ye shall not find him here. He is not here. Never for ages upon ages has a white foot pressed this land—never, except once, and I remember that he left it but to die. Ye come for bright stones; I know it—I know it. Ye shall find them when the blood is dry; but shall ye return whence ye came, or shall ye stop with me? Ha! ha! ha!

“And thou—thou with the dark skin and the proud bearing,” and she stabbed a finger at Umbopa, “who art thou, and what seekest thou? Not stones that shine, not yellow metal that gleams; these thou leavest to ‘white men from the Stars.’ Methinks I know thee; methinks I can smell the smell of the blood in thy heart. Strip off the girdle—”

Her face twisted; her body convulsed, and she fell foaming, struck down by an epileptic fit. They carried her into the hut.

Twala rose trembling and waved his hand. At once the regiments began to file away, and in ten minutes—save for ourselves, the king, and a few attendants—the great space lay empty, whitened with limestone, as though nothing had happened there at all.

“White people,” he said, and his voice had lost its swagger, “it passes in my mind to kill you. Gagool has spoken strange words. What say ye?”
