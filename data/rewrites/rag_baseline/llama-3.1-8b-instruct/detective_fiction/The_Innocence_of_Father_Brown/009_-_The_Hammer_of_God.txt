The village of Bohun Beacon clung precariously to the crest of a hill so steep that the church spire seemed to pierce the sky like the peak of a small mountain. Below, the smithy's forge glowed like a fiery ember, casting a warm orange glow across the rough cobblestones that crisscrossed the village square. The air was heavy with the scent of freshly baked bread wafting from the nearby bakery, mingling with the acrid tang of hot metal and coal smoke from the forge. It was here, at the heart of Bohun Beacon, that the Bohun family's legacy lived on – in the worn flagstones, the rustic wooden beams, and the intricate carvings that adorned the church's stone façade.

As the sun began to rise, casting a golden light over the village, two brothers emerged onto the cobblestones. The Reverend and Honorable Wilfred Bohun, a man of unwavering devotion, made his way towards the church, his footsteps quiet on the stones. His brother, Colonel Norman Bohun, a man of refined taste and questionable morals, sat on a bench outside the Blue Boar Inn, sipping a glass of wine. The colonel's eyes, a piercing blue that seemed almost black in the morning light, were fixed intently on the church, his gaze a mixture of curiosity and disdain.

The Bohuns were a family with a storied past, their ancestors having fought alongside the Crusaders in the Holy Land. Yet, despite their noble heritage, they had long since abandoned the traditions of their forebears, embracing instead the fashionable excesses of their time. The colonel, in particular, had made a name for himself as a reckless and dissolute figure, known for his pursuit of pleasure and his disdain for the conventions of society.

Wilfred, on the other hand, was a man consumed by his faith. His love of Gothic architecture was matched only by his devotion to the mysteries of the divine. Some said he spent more time in the church's crypts and galleries, lost in contemplation, than he did in the company of his family and friends. The blacksmith, a stout Presbyterian, had even accused him of being more enamored with the beauty of the church than with the God it served. Yet, Wilfred's piety was genuine, a flame that burned brightly within him, guiding him through the complexities of life.
