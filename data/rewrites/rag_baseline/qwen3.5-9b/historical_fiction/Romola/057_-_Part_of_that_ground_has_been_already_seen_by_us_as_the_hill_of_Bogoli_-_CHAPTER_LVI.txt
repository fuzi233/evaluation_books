The morning sun had already begun to press heavily upon Romola, the heat becoming an oppressive weight as she navigated the intersecting streets beyond the gate of Santa Croce. The silence of the Borgo La Croce was absolute, broken only by the rhythmic tap of her own footsteps on the pavement. As she rounded a bend, a small figure emerged from the dust—a toddler, no older than three, clad solely in a white shirt. He halted mid-waddle, turning to regard her.

At first, she saw only the back of a sturdy, square-shouldered boy crowned with a cloud of reddish-brown curls. Then he turned, revealing dark eyes swollen with tears and a lower lip protruding in a silent tremor. His fat brown fists gripped the fabric of his shirt with desperate, useless force. A passing shadow cast by a taller figure seemed to trigger a climax of terror, erupting into a loud, wailing sob that sent great beads of moisture rolling down his cheeks.

Instinctively, driven by a maternal compassion that was a hidden wellspring of her own passion, Romola removed her hood. She dropped to her knees on the hot stones, wrapping her arms around the child and pressing her cheek against his. Initially, his cries intensified, but he made no attempt to flee. Soon, the outburst ceased with the abrupt, illogical finality characteristic of childhood emotion. His distorted features smoothed out, replaced by a wide-eyed, open-mouthed stare fixed upon her.

"You have wandered off, little one," she said softly, kissing his forehead. "Fear not; we shall locate your home. Perhaps your mother awaits us."

She deduced he had slipped away during a moment when his guardian's attention had been diverted, and surmised he would soon be pursued.

"Oh, what a heavy, heavy boy!" she murmured, attempting to hoist him. Finding him immovable, she gestured. "Come, then. You must toddle alongside me."

His parted lips remained sealed in awed silence, one fist still clutching the shirt with tenacious grip, while the other yielded readily to her surprisingly strong yet gentle hand.

"Do you have a mother?" Romola asked, gazing down at the boy with a mixture of curiosity and longing. He offered no verbal response. Under different circumstances, a girl might have chattered endlessly; this square-shouldered miniature man, however, possessed a solemn reserve.

Yet, he was acutely aware of his destination. At the turn near San Ambrogio, he dragged Romola toward it, lifting his head to signal recognition.

"Ah, that is the path home, is it?" she smiled. He merely thrust his head forward and pulled, a silent command to hasten their pace.

Another corner awaited his scrutiny before Romola arrived at a short lane opening onto a garden. Here, the boy stopped, tugging her toward a flight of stone stairs. He showed no inclination to release her hand, and she, unwilling to part with him without ensuring safe delivery to his guardians, ascended. The sudden withdrawal from sunlight rendered visibility dim, but at the final landing, a beam of light spilled from an open doorway. Passing through a narrow antechamber, they entered the main room, where Romola paused. No sound greeted her arrival.

In the far corner, bathed in the light, Tessa sat on a low chair beside a cradle, her head lolling to one side, fast asleep. By a window, with her back turned to the entrance, Monna Lisa worked silently, preparing a salad in a state of deafened obliviousness. Romola allowed herself a fleeting second to register the stillness before Lillo snatched his hand away and bolted to his mother's side. He leaned his head back against her arm, regarding Romola with a serious, assessing gaze, making no overt move to disturb her rest.

When Lillo nudged her, Tessa stirred, blinking up in confusion. Her glance landed on the stranger at the threshold, and she jolted upright, flushing deeply and trembling, unable to speak or advance.

"Ah! We have met before," Romola said, stepping forward with a reassuring smile. "I am delighted it was your son. He was weeping in the street; I presumed he had fled. So we walked together briefly until he recognized his surroundings and led me here. You had not noticed his absence? Good, otherwise you would have been terrified."

The shock of discovering Lillo missing momentarily eclipsed all other sensations for Tessa. Her complexion drained, then flushed again. Seizing her son's arm, she rushed toward Monna Lisa, whispering loudly in the old woman's ear with a sob caught in her throat:

"Oh, Lisa, you are wicked! Why stand with your back to the door? Lillo ran far into the street."

"Holy Mother!" Monna Lisa exclaimed in her meek, thick voice, dropping the spoon from her grasp. "Where were *you*, then? I believed you were present, watching him."

"But you *know* I drift off to sleep while rocking," Tessa retorted pettishly.

"Well, well, we must keep the outer door barred or bind him tight," Monna Lisa advised, "for before long he'll be as cunning as Satan, and that is the holy truth. But how did he return, then?"

This query snapped Tessa back to the awareness of Romola's presence. Without answering, she turned, blushing and shrinking once more, while Monna Lisa's eyes tracked her movement. The elder woman performed a low reverence.

"Doubtless the most noble lady brought him back." Advancing slightly, she added, "It is my shame that he was found in only his shirt. He kicked and refused his garments this morning, and the mother, poor soul, will never permit him to be beaten. But what can an old woman do without a stick when the lad's legs grow so strong? Let your nobility inspect his legs."

Conscious of the scrutiny, Lillo hitched his shirt higher, examining his olive-toned calves with a dispassionate curiosity. Romola laughed, bending to give him a affectionate shake and a kiss. This gesture bolstered the reassurance Tessa had gleaned from Monna Lisa's deference. Earlier, when Naldo recounted the Carnival incident and Tessa asked who the celestial lady—who appeared precisely when needed and vanished swiftly—might be, whether perhaps the Holy Madonna herself, Naldo had replied, "Not exactly, my Tessa; only one of the saints," refusing further elaboration. Thus, within the dreamlike synthesis of Tessa's limited experience, Romola remained vaguely associated with church iconography. Upon her reappearance, the memory of her protection was tinged with religious awe, albeit lightly, for Tessa's primary dread concerned ugly and malevolent entities. It seemed improbable that benevolent forces would anger or punish her, as Nofri and the Devil were wont to do. With Monna Lisa speaking freely of Lillo's legs and Romola laughing, Tessa relaxed considerably.

"Ninna sleeps in the cradle," she noted. "_She_ is lovely too."

Romola moved to observe the sleeping infant, while Monna Lisa, one of the exceptionally meek deaf who rarely anticipated speech directed at them, resumed her salad preparation.

"Ah! She is waking; her blue eyes have opened," Romola observed. "You must lift her, and I shall occupy this chair—may I?—to nurse Lillo. Come, Lillo!"

She settled into Tito's chair, extending her arms toward the boy, whose gaze had followed her. He hesitated, pointing small fingers at her with a mix of puzzlement and indignation. "That is Babbo's chair," he declared, seemingly trapped by the dilemma of what to do if Babbo returned to find Romola occupying his seat.

"But Babbo is absent, and I depart shortly. Come, allow me to nurse you as he does," Romola said, wondering internally for the first time the nature of this father whose wife dressed in peasant garb yet possessed a certain daintiness indicative of idleness and abundance. Lillo consented to be lifted, finding the lap remarkably comfortable, and began exploring her dress and hands, searching for ornaments beyond the rosary.

Tessa, previously engaged in soothing Ninna's waking irritability, now seated herself on a low stool near Romola's knee, arranging the infant with jealous precision. She resented that the strange lady seemed to favor the boy as much as Naldo did.

"Lillo intended to be angry with me for sitting in Babbo's chair," Romola remarked, bending to kiss Ninna's tiny foot. "Will he return soon and claim it?"

"Ah, no!" Tessa assured her. "You may sit therein for a long while. I shall regret your departure. When you first arrived to care for me at the Carnival, I deemed it marvelous; you came and departed so quickly. Naldo suggested you might be a saint, which caused me to tremble somewhat, though I know saints are very good. You were kind to me, and now you have tended Lillo. Perhaps you will always visit to care for me. That is how Naldo behaved long ago; he came to protect me when I was frightened, one San Giovanni. I could not discern his origin—he was so beautiful and good. And so are you," Tessa concluded, gazing upward at Romola with devout admiration.

"Naldo is your husband. His eyes mirror Lillo's," Romola stated, observing the boy's darkly penciled eyebrows, unusually prominent for his age. She spoke not as an interrogator, but with a quiet certainty of inference that remained enigmatic to Tessa.
