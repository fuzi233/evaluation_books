CHAPTER LXVI. In which Pen begins his Canvass — Or, How a Young Gentleman Learned to Smile at the World (and Why It Wasn’t as Easy as It Looked)

The great house at Clavering Park had once been a monument of grandeur—before it became a monument of bankruptcy. Now, under Sir Francis Clavering’s stewardship, it was merely a monument of melancholy. The mansion stood like a ghost in its own grounds: most of the rooms shuttered, the corridors echoing with silence, and only a handful of chambers occupied by the Baronet and his two loyal servants from the lodge gate—women who, though not exactly cheerful, were at least experienced in cooking game that had been shot during mornings spent in dreary solitude.

Lightfoot, Sir Francis’s man, had moved on—upward, socially speaking—to become a domestic in Lady Clavering’s service. And according to Mr. Smirke, the man of ceremony who performed the rites, Lightfoot had also performed the even more significant rite of marrying Mrs. Bonner, the elderly lady’s maid, whose heart, it seemed, had been captured not by her years, but by Lightfoot’s youth. Their ambition? To run the Clavering Arms. They would remain in service until quarter-day, when they’d officially take over the inn, complete with dreams of ale, pewter mugs, and the occasional scandalous guest.

Arthur Pendennis, meanwhile, graciously promised he would host his election dinner at the Clavering Arms once Sir Francis vacated his seat. Arthur came down in September, ostensibly for a visit—but really to begin what he called “the canvass.” He arrived just as Sir Francis was preparing to flee from his own home, and within days, Arthur had supplied him with enough ready money to make that escape possible. As soon as the cash was in hand, Sir Francis declared he must attend to business in Chatteris and various nearby watering-places—places known primarily for their racecourses and billiard tables, where "business" usually meant betting and bad decisions.

Arthur, left alone, found himself quite content. He could walk with the gamekeeper in the morning, read in the evening, and enjoy a cigar while scribbling away at his next literary masterpiece. But after three days, he decided he’d rather be anywhere else than in the same room as Sir Francis. So when the Baronet asked for a loan—his usual custom—Arthur handed it over with a smile so wide it might have been carved into stone. It was, in fact, a relief to see Sir Francis go.

But Arthur wasn’t just there to escape loneliness—he was there to win votes. And winning votes required something he hadn’t mastered before: charm. He remembered how unpopular he’d been in Clavering ten years ago—a haughty young dandy who looked down on everyone. Now, he vowed to be different. He would be warm, jovial, accessible. He would be Captain Strong reincarnated.

And so he became Captain Strong.

He laughed at everything, shook hands like a politician handing out free pamphlets, and made appearances at market days and farmers’ dinners as if he’d been born to them. At the Clavering Athenaeum—the Liberal club, established to oppose the aristocratic old reading-room where tradesmen were barred and *Edinburgh Review* copies were considered subversive—he mingled with the opposition preacher, Mr. Simcoe; the two partners of the Chatteris cloth factory; and the Independent minister. He invited the younger partner to dine at the Park, sent hares and partridges to Mrs. Simcoe with a request to read her husband’s latest sermon, and when he felt unwell, he let Mr. Huxter—local apothecary—send medicine and pay a visit. All this was done with such artful ease that even the old gentleman himself might have thought Arthur was genuinely ill.

Old Pendennis would have been proud. Arthur, for his part, found the whole thing deeply amusing. He enjoyed being the kind of man people liked—even if he knew full well he wasn’t one. There was a wicked joy in pretending to be good-natured, when you knew all along you were playing a role.

Yet, on quiet nights, when he walked back from the Athenaeum or from an evening with the Simcoes, something stirred inside him. As he crossed the old bridge over the rushing Brawl, the sound of the water beneath reminded him of childhood. The stars shone bright, the trees whispered secrets, and far off, across the fields, he saw Fairoaks—his mother’s cottage—its windows still lit, as if someone waited there.

He didn’t look directly at it. That light felt like a reproach—like a ghostly eye watching him, warning him. He remembered the room where his mother had sat, praying, worrying, loving him. He remembered the terrace where, as a boy, he had walked untroubled, pure, untouched by the world’s cynicism. Now he was here, pretending, smiling, scheming.

The clock in the town tolled midnight. He turned toward Clavering Avenue, passing under the dark arches of the limes, and entered the park.

The next day, he visited Fairoaks again. This time, the sun was setting. The windows were open. His tenant, Captain Stokes of the Bombay Artillery (whose mother lived in Clavering), greeted him warmly. They toured the grounds, admired the new pond, discussed repairs, and Captain Stokes begged Arthur to name a day when he might do himself and Mrs. Stokes the honor of hosting a dinner.

Arthur apologized for not visiting sooner. “I couldn’t bring myself to,” he said.

“I understand,” said the Captain.

Mrs. Stokes came down in her best dress, children trailing behind her. The youngest clambered over furniture—specifically, the armchair that had once belonged to Arthur’s father. Arthur froze. That chair had been sacred. He wouldn’t have dreamed of sitting in it as a child. Now, a boy was bouncing on it like a trampoline.

“Can Miss Stokes play?” Arthur asked, trying to sound casual.

She played. The piano was old, the notes slightly flat. But Arthur wasn’t listening to the music. He heard his mother singing, saw Laura at the keyboard, and felt the warmth of a past that no longer existed.

Dinner was dull—until the waiter, Hodson, dropped a plate over Arthur’s shoulder. Mr. Hobnell, the local squire, chuckled. “I suppose your hands are slippery with bear’s grease, Hodson. Always dropping crockery, that fellow is—haw, haw!”

Hodson blushed. Arthur burst out laughing. The mood shifted. Soon, the table was alive with jokes. When Arthur suggested Hodson should cut the hare, the Vicar of Tinckleton took up the joke, then the Captains, then Hobnell, arriving late but roaring with laughter. For a moment, it felt like life.

Meanwhile, in London, Blanche Clavering—Lady Clavering’s daughter—had come up from Tunbridge Wells with Mrs. Bonner, her childhood nurse. Mrs. Bonner, about to retire from service and marry Lightfoot, wanted to leave a token of affection. She bought a Bible for Blanche, who insisted on getting a large Johnson’s Dictionary for her mother. Then she splurged on linen for the Clavering Arms and a red-and-yellow neckerchief—for Lightfoot, naturally. Mrs. Bonner adored him—more than a wife, more than a mother. She already imagined him bedecked in jewelry, pins, rings, chains, seals—all purchased at her expense.

They shopped in the Strand. After finishing, Blanche smiled sweetly at a shopman and said, “Pray, sir, will you show us the way to Shepherd’s Inn?”

“Shepherd’s Inn? What on earth for, miss?” Mrs. Bonner asked.

“To see Captain Strong,” Blanche replied. “But I’m not going to see him. I want to visit Fanny Bolton—the girl who nursed Mr. Arthur last year and saved his life. I’ve brought dresses—look!” she added proudly, as if offering charity was a feat of heroism.

Blanche, resplendent in feathers, flowers, silk, and a parasol that probably cost more than Fanny’s entire wardrobe, arrived at Shepherd’s Inn. Mrs. Bolton, scrubbing the floor, stared in awe. Betsy-Jane and Ameliar-Ann gaped.

“My good woman! I wish to see Fanny Bolton—is she here?” Blanche asked.

Mrs. Bolton, suspicious, hesitated. “What do you want with her?”

“I am Lady Clavering’s daughter. You’ve heard of Sir Francis Clavering?”

“Step in, miss. Betsy-Jane, where’s Fanny?”

Fanny was upstairs—in Captain Strong’s rooms.

“In Captain Strong’s rooms!” Blanche exclaimed. “Oh, let’s go there! I know him very well.”

As they climbed, Costigan, a swaggering old gentleman from the court below, muttered to himself: “That’s a devilish foine gyurl, bedad, goan up to Sthrong and Altamont. They’re always having foine gyurlls up their stairs.”

Then—shrieks.

Costigan, thinking a woman was in distress, rushed up. He found the outer door of Strong’s chambers open and began banging the knocker.

After many knocks, the inner door cracked open. A head appeared.

“It’s oi, me boy. Hwhat’s that noise, Sthrong?”

“Go to the d——!” came the reply. The door slammed shut.

Costigan stomped down muttering threats.

But the reader, unlike Costigan, knows the truth.

It had been said that Colonel Altamont was generous. When he had money, he spent it freely. He loved company, especially drinking with others. On this particular day, he had invited Miss Delaval of the Theatre Royal and her mother, Mrs. Hodge, for a river outing. They were to meet at Chambers and walk to a wharf in the Strand.

So when Blanche and Mrs. Bonner arrived at Strong’s door, Grady, Altamont’s servant, welcomed them in. Two bouquets of flowers sat on the table—gifts from Altamont, purchased earlier that morning. Blanche sniffed one, twirled around, looked behind curtains, examined books, prints, and even the plan of the Clavering estate hanging on the wall.

She nearly forgot why she was there.

Grady disappeared into the bedroom with a pair of enormous varnished boots.

“The women’s come,” he said, helping Altamont into them.

“Did you ask ’em if they’d like a drink?” Altamont asked.

Grady returned. “He says, will you take anything to drink?”

Blanche giggled. “Shall we take anything to drink?”

“Well, you may take it or lave it,” Grady grumbled, offended.

“Will we take anything to drink?” Blanche repeated, laughing.

“Grady, my coat!” roared a voice from the bedroom.

“Why, that’s not Mr. Strong’s voice,” Blanche said.

“Grady, my coat!” the voice bellowed again.

Bonner stared, terrified.

The bedroom door opened. A man stepped out—no coat.

He nodded at the women, walked across the room.

“I beg your pardon, ladies. Grady, bring my coat down, sir! Well, my dears, it’s a fine day, and we’ll have a jolly lark at——”

He stopped.

Mrs. Bonner screamed: “Amory! Amory!”

She fainted.

The man rushed to Blanche, seized her, kissed her.

“Yes, Betsy,” he said. “By G—it is me. Mary Bonner knew me. What a fine gal we’ve grown! But it’s a secret, mind. I’m dead, though I’m your father. Your poor mother don’t know it. Kiss me—kiss me close, my Betsy? D—— it, I love you. I’m your old father.”

Blanche shrieked.

The screams echoed through the building.

The man—Amory—rushed to his dressing-case, pulled out a bottle of eau-de-Cologne, and began spraying the fainting women.

Strong appeared from upstairs, saw the scene, and said: “Grady, go wait in the court. If anyone comes—you understand.”

“Is it the play-actress and her mother?” Grady asked.

“Yes—confound you—say there’s nobody in chambers. Party’s off for today.”

“Shall I say that, sir? After I bought them bokays?”

“Yes,” Amory snapped.

Strong reached the door just in time to stop Costigan from entering.

The theatre ladies never made it to Greenwich. Blanche never saw Fanny Bolton. And Costigan, asking Grady what the hell was going on, was told: “It’s another woman. One of them. Women, sir—they’re the cause of most mischief in the world.”
