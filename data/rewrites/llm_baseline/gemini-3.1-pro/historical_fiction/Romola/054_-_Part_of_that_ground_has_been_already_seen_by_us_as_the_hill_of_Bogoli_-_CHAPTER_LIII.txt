“I would speak with you,” said Baldassarre. His voice lacked the resonant timbre it must have once possessed, worn down now to a frayed, eroded dialect of grief. Romola looked at him in silent expectation. It was plain that he had followed her through the narrow, echoing streets, waiting for the precise moment to approach. She was going, at last, to know the secret about him.

“Yes,” she said, submitting to the inevitable with the quiet resignation of one beginning a long, arduous fast. “But you wish to go where no one can hear us?”

“Where *he* will not come upon us,” said Baldassarre, glancing behind him timidly, as if the oppressive, mercantile clatter of Florence were a physical predator. “Out—in the air—away from the streets.” 

“I sometimes go to San Miniato at this hour,” said Romola. She longed to escape the superficial commercialization of the city below, its harsh, metallic din grating against her psychological well-being. “If you like, I will go now, and you can follow me. It is far, but we can be solitary there.”

He nodded assent, and Romola set out. To some women, retreating to an isolated spot with a man exhibiting the outward signs of madness—the very madness Tito had so smoothly diagnosed—might have seemed an alarming risk. But Romola possessed no personal fears; she only welcomed the physical distance, the steady, rhythmic exertion that would delay the next bitter drop of truth from falling onto her tongue. The afternoon was far advanced, the sun sinking low, when she paused on the rough ground. The acoustic ecology of the hill was a profound sanctuary; the wind sifted through the ancient cypress trunks, carrying the pungent, sun-baked aromas of crushed wild thyme, drying clay, and the sharp, resinous tang of the earth. 

When Baldassarre reached her, he sank heavily onto a ridge of stony ground. His thickset frame had lost the sturdy, agrarian vigor it possessed when he first appeared bound in the Duomo; the steep ascent left his breathing a harsh, shallow rasp, and his eyes carried the helpless vagueness of a man whose internal compass had been shattered. 

“The hill is steep,” said Romola with compassionate gentleness, seating herself beside him, attuned to the grounding scent of the soil. “And I fear you have been weakened by want?”

He fixed his eyes on her in a silence that spoke of profound untranslatability. The moment for speech had arrived, but he seemed unable to seize the vocabulary to bridge the chasm between his subjective horror and the physical world. She remained motionless, allowing the quiet hum of the hillside to calm him. He looked like nothing more than a neglected, common-bred elder, but Romola had learned to find profound meaning in the presence of those intimately tied to the earth and its rawest sufferings. Gradually, a definite expression gathered in his gaze, and he spoke with abrupt emphasis.

“Ah! you would have been my daughter!”

The words landed like a drop of concentrated rue on Romola’s palate. A swift flush of blood rose in her face and vanished just as swiftly, leaving her lips parted and white, a marble image of horror tasting the astringent bite of absolute betrayal. The revelation was complete. She divined the sprawling, cyclical tragedy that lay behind that single phrase. 

The sensitive, physical response of her face acted as a stimulus to Baldassarre; his fragmented words had finally wrought their right effect. He pressed on with gathering eagerness, laying his rough hand upon her arm. “You are a woman of proud blood—is it not true? You go to hear the preacher; you hate baseness—baseness that smiles and triumphs. You hate your husband?”

“Oh God! were you really his father?” Romola asked in a low voice, too entirely possessed by the interwoven timelines of her past to answer his question. “Or was it as he said? Did you take him when he was little?”

“Ah, you believe me—you know what he is!” Baldassarre exulted, tightening his grip as though her physical solidity anchored him. “You will help me?”

“Yes,” she said, though her definition of the word belonged to an entirely different lexicon. She laid her palm gently over his weathered hand, her eyes welling with tears. “Oh, it is piteous! Tell me—you were a great scholar; you taught him. How is it?” She broke off. Tito’s allegation of madness echoed like an acoustic pollutant in her mind, a jarring noise against the quiet truth of the old man’s grief. She sat perfectly still.

“It is gone!—it is all gone!” said Baldassarre, a mournful testament to the erosion of his cultural memory. “And they would not believe me, because he lied, and said I was mad; and they had me dragged to prison. And I am old—my mind will not come back. And the world is against me.” He paused, sinking under a wave of despondency, before looking up with renewed, fiery urgency. “But *you* are not against me. He made you love him, and he has been false to you. Yes, he made *me* love him: he was beautiful and gentle, and I was a lonely man. I took him when they were beating him. He slept in my bosom when he was little. I became his elder, his provider. I pressed the sweetest oils from the first harvest to heal his skin; I baked the coarse, sustaining bread of the earth to build his flesh. I poured out my meticulous histories, the rich, simmering broths of antiquity, feeding him the very marrow of my knowledge, and everything that was mine I meant to be his.”

Baldassarre’s voice trembled with the grief of a passionate historian whose deepest offerings had been discarded. “But to him, my sustenance was mere utilitarian fuel. He possessed no sensory reverence for what I gave. He had my gems—the crystallized heritage of the ancients—and he sold them, trading profound art for superficial commercial coin. He left me in slavery. He never came to seek me, and when I returned poor and in misery, he denied me. He wielded the dominant lexicon of this city against me. He said I was a madman.”

“He told us his father was dead—was drowned,” Romola said faintly, the sour, metallic taste of Tito’s deceit flooding her mouth. “Surely he must have believed it then. Oh! he could not have been so base *then*!” The mythic purity of her early love, a time when she thought no more of poison in him than a child suspects toxicity in a sweet, edible flower, rose up and clashed violently with her present reality. With one great sob, her tears rushed forth.

“Ah, you are young, and the tears come easily,” said Baldassarre with a chef's impatience for a spoiled reduction. “But tears are no good; they only quench the fire within, and it is the fire that works, that reduces the bitter root to justice. Tears will hinder us. Listen to me.”

Romola started slightly, the threat of his madness darting again through her thoughts. But her deep belief in his history remained firm, and it was out of sympathy for his profound language death that she asked, as gently as she could, “Tell me, how did you lose your memory—your scholarship?”

“I was ill. I can’t tell how long—it was a blank,” he said, describing the brutal starvation of his mind. “The rich vocabulary of my life suffered a sudden death. I remember nothing, only at last I was sitting in the sun among the stones, and the syntax of my existence was darkness. Slowly, I felt a longing for something that never came. On the ship, on the waters, I began to know what I hungered for: it was for the Boy to come back, to restore the native tongue of my thoughts, for I was locked away outside them all. And I am outside now. I feel nothing but a wall and darkness.” 

His head sank between his hands. Romola’s pity swelled. Had she not daily seen her own father sitting in his library, aching for Dino, starving for the future he had meant to cultivate in his son? 

“It all came back once,” Baldassarre went on, his tone hardening. “I was master of everything. I saw all the world again, the rich ingredients of my past, my gems, my books. I thought I had him in my power, and I went to expose him where the lights and the trees were. But he lied again. He used his smooth, hollow words to label me a madman, and they dragged me to prison... Wickedness is strong; and he wears armour.” 

The fierce intensity flared. He grasped Romola’s arm again. “But you will help me? He has been false to you too. He has another wife, and she has children. He makes her believe he is her husband, and she is a foolish, helpless thing. I will show you where she lives.”

The shock that passed through Romola tasted like ash. The sacred, everyday domestic routine of her life had been a fiction, secretly interwoven with a hidden, parallel timeline. The mythic horror of this duality ignited a fierce, protective anger within her. Baldassarre instinctively felt her sympathetic resonance.

“You hate him,” he said. “Is it not true? There is no love between you. I know women can hate; you have proud blood. You hate falseness, and you can hunger for revenge.”

Paralyzed by the sensory overload of conflicting realities, Romola was barely conscious of the bruising grip on her arm. 

“You shall contrive it,” Baldassarre whispered eagerly. “I have learned by heart that you are his rightful wife. You are a noble woman. You go to hear the preacher of vengeance; you will help justice. My mind goes—the lexicon fails sometimes—all but the fire. The fire is God: it is justice: it will not die. You believe that—is it not true? If they will not hang him for robbing me, you will strip him. You will take away the clanking, unnatural noise of his armour, that acoustic pollution that shields his hollow core, and I will stab him. I have a knife, and my arm is still strong enough.”

He reached beneath his tunic and drew the hidden blade, running his thumb abstractedly along its edge, needing the grounding physical sensation to keep the fractured ideas in his mind alive. It was a simple, agrarian tool, primed to pare away the rot. 

To Romola, every fresh hour of life tasted more complex and difficult than the last. Her judgment was too vigorous to offer futile, deprecatory words to a man locked in such an untranslatable state of grief. She needed to manipulate the pacing, to let the boiling pot of his excitement cool.

She spoke tremulously. “You say she is foolish and helpless—that other wife—and believes him to be her real husband. Perhaps he is: perhaps he married her before he married me.”

“I cannot tell,” said Baldassarre, pausing his rhythmic stroking of the blade, bewildered by the tangled timelines. “I can remember no more. I only know where she lives. You shall see her. I will take you; but not now. *He* may be there. The night is coming on.”

“It is true,” Romola said, starting up with the sudden consciousness that the sun had vanished, leaving the hills bathed in a cool, dark silence. “But you will come and take me—when?”

“In the morning,” Baldassarre muttered, dreaming she shared his appetite for immediate vengeance. “Come to me, then, where you came to me to-day, in the church. I will be there at ten; and if you are not there, I will go again towards mid-day. Can you remember?”

“Mid-day,” Romola said.

“Only mid-day. The same place, and mid-day. And, after that,” he added, rising to grasp her arm with his left hand while his right held the knife, “we will have our revenge. He shall feel the sharp edge of justice. The world is against me, but you will help me.”

“I would help you in other ways,” Romola offered, making a timid effort to introduce a gentler dialect to his obsession. “I fear you are in want; you labour for so little. I should like to bring you rich, warming sustenance, to make you feel again that there is someone who honors you.”

“Talk no more about that,” Baldassarre snapped fiercely. “I will have nothing else. Help me to wring one drop of vengeance on this side of the grave. I have nothing but my knife. It is sharp; but there is a moment after the thrust when men see the face of death,—and it shall be my face that he will taste.”

He released her and sank back down to the stony earth. Romola felt utterly helpless; she must defer all action to the morrow.

“Mid-day, then,” she said, her voice cutting clearly through the quiet acoustic space.

“Yes,” he answered, exhaling a breath of profound exhaustion. “Go; I will rest here.”

She hastened down the steep path. Turning at the last bend where the fragrant cypress trees parted, she looked back and saw him seated still, a solitary elder waiting in the fading light.
