Since Briarmains lay closer than the Hollow, Mr. Yorke had escorted his young companion there. He saw the boy laid in the finest bed of the house, tended with a care that mirrored the affection of a true father. Seeing the blood welling from the treacherous wound, Mr. Yorke felt a surge of paternal love; the sight of the tall, proud figure suddenly prostrate upon the road, the handsome southern head bowed low in the dust, and the vibrant youth rendered pale, lifeless, and helpless—this was a combination of circumstances designed to capture Mr. Yorke's deepest sympathy.

With no other hand to offer aid, no other voice to ask questions, and no other mind to devise a plan, Mr. Yorke was forced to act entirely alone. This total dependence of the speechless, bleeding boy on his benevolence secured that benevolence completely. Mr. Yorke enjoyed having power, and using it; now, he held the power over a fellow creature's life in his hands. It suited him perfectly.

Equally, it suited his saturnine wife. The incident was precisely to her taste. While some women might have been terrified by a bloody man dragged over their threshold and laid in their hall during the witching hour, Mrs. Yorke remained composed. She reserved her hysterics for trivial matters, such as Jessie refusing to leave the garden for her knitting or Martin proposing a trip to Australia to escape her tyranny. An attempted murder near her door, however—a half-dead man in her best bed—calmed her spirit, brightened her mood, and gave her cap the look of a fashionable turban.

Mrs. Yorke was the type of woman who would reduce a simple maid to misery yet nurse a hospital full of plague victims like a heroine. She almost loved Moore. Her tough heart yearned toward him when she found him entrusted to her care, left in her arms as dependent as her youngest child in a cradle. Had she seen a servant or daughter give him water or smooth his pillow, she would have boxed their ears. She chased Jessie and Rose from the upper floors and forbade the housemaids to enter the room.

If the accident had occurred at the rectory gates and Old Helstone had taken in the martyr, neither Yorke nor his wife would have pitied him; they would have deemed him rightly punished for his tyranny. Instead, he became the apple of their eye.

Strangely, Louis Moore was permitted to sit on the edge of the bed, lean over the pillow, hold his brother's hand, and press his pale forehead with fraternal kisses. Mrs. Yorke endured this well, allowing him to stay half the day and even letting him sit up all night once. She rose at five on a wet November morning, lit the kitchen fire herself, cooked breakfast, and served it to the brothers. Dressed majestically in a flannel wrapper, shawl, and nightcap, she sat watching them eat with the complacency of a hen observing her chicks. Yet, she warned the cook severely for bringing up a basin of sago-gruel and scolded the housemaid for helping Moore with his coat, accepting only a smile, a "thank you," and a shilling in return. When two anxious ladies begged to see Moore, Mrs. Yorke hardened her heart and sent them away with opprobrium.

But how did she react when Hortense Moore arrived? Surprisingly well. The entire Moore family seemed to suit Mrs. Yorke better than any other. Hortense and she shared an endless topic of conversation: the corrupt tendencies of servants. They viewed this class with identical suspicion and severity. From the start, Hortense showed no jealousy of Mrs. Yorke's attention to Robert, allowing her to remain the nurse with little interference. Instead, Hortense kept herself busy fidgeting around the house, monitoring the kitchen, reporting news, and proving generally useful. Together, they excluded visitors from the sickroom, keeping the young mill-owner captive, hardly letting air or sunlight reach him.

Dr. MacTurk, the surgeon, declared Moore's wound dangerous but not hopeless. Initially, he wanted to assign a nurse of his own choosing, but neither Mrs. Yorke nor Hortense would agree. They promised to follow his instructions faithfully, leaving the patient in their hands.

They certainly performed their duty to the best of their ability, though something went awry. Bandages were displaced or tampered with, leading to significant blood loss. Dr. MacTurk, summoned in haste, arrived foaming at the mouth. He was a surgeon dangerous to anger—one abrupt in his best moods and savage in his worst. Upon seeing Moore's condition, he released his frustration in florid language unnecessary to record here. A few choice bouquets fell upon the unperturbed head of Mr. Graves, his stony young assistant; another nosgay went to a second young gentleman in his train, an interesting facsimile of himself and his own son; but the full basket of blushing blooms fell to the lot of meddling womankind, en masse.

For most of one winter night, Dr. MacTurk and his satellites worked and argued over Moore's exhausted frame beside the bed, shut off from the world. Three against one, they battled Death until dawn, when the balance seemed so equal that either side could claim victory.

At first light, Graves and the younger MacTurk stayed with the patient while the senior doctor sought additional strength, securing Mrs. Horsfall, the best nurse on his staff. He charged her with strict responsibility. She accepted this burden stolidly, taking her position in the easy chair at the bedhead immediately. That moment marked the beginning of her reign.

Mrs. Horsfall possessed one supreme virtue: she obeyed Dr. MacTurk's orders to the letter. The Ten Commandments meant less to her than his dictum. In every other respect, she was no woman but a dragon. Hortense Moore faded before her; Mrs. Yorke withdrew, crushed. Yet both were dignified figures in their own eyes and substantial presences to others. Completely cowed by Mrs. Horsfall's breadth, height, bone, and brawn, they retreated to the back parlor. She sat upstairs when she pleased and downstairs when she preferred it, drinking three drams daily and smoking four pipes of tobacco.

Regarding Moore, no one dared inquire. Mrs. Horsfall had him under dry-nurse supervision. It was she who was to do for him, and the general consensus was that she did exactly that.

Twice a day, Dr. MacTurk visited. Complicated by this new mishap, his case became a matter of professional interest. He viewed Moore as a broken piece of clockwork that it would be creditable to his skill to repair. Graves and the young MacTurk—the only other visitors—regarded him as they might the current occupant of the dissecting room at Stilbro' Infirmary.

Robert Moore had a peculiar existence: in pain, in danger, too weak to move or speak, attended by a giantess of a keeper, with the three surgeons as his sole company. Thus he lay through the dwindling days and lengthening nights of a dreary November.

Initially, Moore feebly resisted Mrs. Horsfall. He hated her rough bulk and dreaded her hard hands, but she taught him docility instantly. She ignored his six-foot stature and manly muscles, turning him in bed as one turns a babe in a cradle. When he was good, she called him "dear" and "honey"; when he was bad, she shook him. If he attempted to speak while Dr. MacTurk was present, she raised her hand and bade him "Hush!" like a nurse silencing a forward child. If she hadn't smoked and drunk gin, he thought, it might have been better, but she did both. Once, in her absence, he hinted to Dr. MacTurk that "that woman was a drinker."

"Pooh, my dear sir, they are all so," the surgeon replied dismissively. "But Horsfall has this virtue: drunk or sober, she always remembers to obey *me*."

Eventually, the late autumn passed. Its fogs and rains withdrew from England, replaced by winds sighing over distant lands. Deep winter followed, bringing clarity, stillness, and frost.

A calm day settled into a crystalline evening. The world wore a North Pole coloring; lights and tints resembled the reflections of white, violet, or pale green gems. Hills appeared lilac-blue; the setting sun held purple within its red; the sky was ice, a silvered azure. When the stars rose, they were of white crystal, not gold, casting cool, pure, transparent hues of gray, cerulean, or faint emerald across the landscape.

In a wood stripped of green and russet, appearing merely in neutral tones, what was this dark blue moving object? It was a schoolboy—a Briarfield grammar-school boy—who had left his companions trudging home by the highway to seek a specific tree with a mossy mound at its roots, convenient as a seat. Why linger? The air was cold, and the time was late. He sat down. What occupied his thoughts? Did he feel the chaste charm of Nature tonight? A pearl-white moon smiled through the gray trees; did he care for her smile?

Impossible to say, for he was silent, his countenance a mask rather than a mirror of sensation. This boy was fifteen, slight and tall for his age.
