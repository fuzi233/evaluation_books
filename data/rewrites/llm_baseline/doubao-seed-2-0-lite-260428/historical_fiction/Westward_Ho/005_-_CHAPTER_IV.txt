All this while, what has become of that fair, notorious heartbreaker I have not yet formally introduced to you? None other than Rose Salterne, the wealthiest merchant’s daughter in Bideford, who found herself cooped up in a tiny whitewashed farmhouse tucked beside a watermill, sunk deep in the green, mist-draped folds of Combe Valley, halfway between Stow and Chapel. She sulked as deeply as her naturally sweet, sunny disposition would allow, furious to be barred from all the grand feasts, dances, and pageantry unfolding down in Bideford town, stuck observing something like an unasked-for Martinmas Lent in that far-flung western glen.

The valley was so lonesome, in fact, that though she felt no small aversion to Eustace Leigh, and as a devout Protestant, a great deal of quiet suspicion of his hidden Papist sympathies, she could not bear to turn away his visits. He found some half-baked errand to call at the mill nearly every day, lingering to chat. At first, her aunt and uncle—poor distant cousins tasked with chaperoning the rich heiress—frowned stiffly on these calls, and the aunt made a point of acting as third party to every conversation. But Eustace was a local squire’s son, a respected neighbor, and a steady customer for their milled flour and dairy; they dared not be rude to him. Nor did they dare cross Rose, who had a will of iron hidden behind her soft smiles, and who had wisely begged her aunt never to leave her alone with him, saying “I cannot abide the sight of Mr. Eustace, but I must have someone to talk to out here, else I shall go mad.” Her simple country aunt, not wanting to second-guess the ways of “town-bred folk,” soon let the visits go unchallenged.

Eustace knew full well that the rift between his Catholic faith and Rose’s Protestant upbringing was the greatest obstacle to winning her, so he hid his true opinions deep beneath a mask of mild conformity. Instead of trying to convert the mill household, he bought milk and flour from them daily, and gave the goods away to the poor widows of Moorwinstow, who all agreed that for a Papist, he was a godly enough young man. At last, after conferring with the Jesuit priests Campian and Parsons over the political plots brewing across the realm, he and the two men resolved to attend the local parish church the following Sunday. The pair, travelling under the aliases Evan Morgans and Morgan Evans, had memorized every line of the Anglican rubrics beforehand, and behaved with such unremarkable orthodoxy that no one could find fault with them. Even Eustace played the part so well that all the good Protestant folk of the parish were thoroughly astonished—though every housewife whispered to her neighbor after the service, “He turn Protestant? The devil turn monk! That young hypocrite is only chasing Mistress Salterne.”

But if the two Jesuits found it prudent to pretend conformity to Elizabeth’s government for the sake of their holy cause, they worked none the less furiously in private to plot its overthrow. They had been darting across England in hiding since the previous April, lying low now only until they received word from Ireland that would signal the launch of a great “Rising of the West,” meant to sweep that “stiff-necked, persecuting, excommunicate, illegitimate usurper” calling herself Queen Elizabeth off her throne forever.

They had persuaded themselves, as many of their order would go on persuading themselves for centuries after, that the entire heart of England secretly sided with them, that the nation was mere days from flocking back to the bosom of the Catholic Church and handing Elizabeth over to be led in chains to the Pope’s feet in Rome. That fair hope danced always a step ahead of them, as unreachable as the spot where a rainbow touches the ground, and they spoke of it in terms that left old Mr. Leigh, Eustace’s father, trembling with fear. One day, Eustace arrived home after his usual visit to the mill to hear raised voices in his father’s private study: Parsons blustering as usual, old Mr. Leigh peevishly protesting, and Campian, the gentlest of the three, trying to pour oil on the roiling waters. Eustace stopped outside the door to listen, for what he told himself was the good of the cause.

“My good sir,” old Mr. Leigh was saying, “does not my very protection of you here prove my devotion to the Catholic faith? But I cannot forget, for all that, that I am an Englishman.”

“England?” Parsons thundered back. “It is nothing but a heretic, schismatic Babylon, of which it is written, ‘Come out of her, my people, lest you be partaker of her plagues.’ A country is no more than an arbitrary line drawn on the map by mortal princes, who rise and fall to nothing. All their authority comes only from our Holy Father the Pope, who holds all power in heaven and earth. Strip away his sanction, and what is a king? A creation of the people, who can unmake him just as easily as they made him.”

“Sir, I have sworn allegiance to Queen Elizabeth!”

“And you were absolved of that oath the moment Pius V’s bull declared her a heretic, excommunicate, and stripped of all right to rule. Since 1569, England has no queen, no magistrates, no lawful laws at all. To swear loyalty to her officials, or to plead in her courts, is to defy the apostolic command: ‘How dare you go to law before the unbelievers?’ Rebellion is not merely permitted now. It is a sacred duty.”

“Take care, man, for God’s sake take care!” Mr. Leigh begged. “Right or wrong, I will not have such treason spoken in my own house, not for the sake of my wife and children!”

“Peace, brother Parsons, be gentler with the flock,” Campian interposed. “Your view, while widely held among our order, is too bold to put to good men here. The opposite view, that Catholics may still keep their oaths of peace to the crown, is far kinder to tender consciences, and we are sent here to ease their burdens, not add to them.”

The argument raged on, until old Mr. Leigh broke down in tears, furious that the Jesuits had used his devotion to the Church to take his money, his son, and now threatened his very life to push their plots forward. In a burst of loyalty he had not expected from himself, Eustace stepped to his father’s side, declaring he would not stand by and see his family ruined, and that he was still an English citizen, not a vowed servant of the order. The two Jesuits were stunned, realizing their most promising young acolyte had slipped their grasp—until Parsons spotted the flush on Eustace’s face, and guessed the truth: a woman was the distraction.

They resolved to change their tactics, and soon Campian manipulated the household chaplain into letting him hear Eustace’s confession. Through a series of careful, probing questions, he wormed the truth out of the lovesick young man: Eustace was utterly besotted with Rose Salterne, the 18-year-old heiress of Bideford’s richest merchant. Blind with passion, Eustace swore that if the Church would only let him court Rose, for even a single day, he would devote every penny of her fortune, every breath of his life, to the cause.

That was all Campian needed. He absolved Eustace of his sin of passion, and handed him a secret coded mission: the next day, the 25th of November, he was to go to the shore at Clovelly at dead low tide, and wait for a boat rowed by a red-bearded Portuguese man. If he asked the sailor “How many?” and got the answer “Eight hundred and one,” he was to take the secret dispatches the man carried, which held the final plans for the Catholic rising.

The two set out together for the mill farm, and had just reached the top of the hill between Chapel and Stow mill when Rose tripped up the lane, wearing a brand new scarlet hood, her long dark curls spilling out from beneath it to her waist, her dark eyes glowing soft and bright. Even Campian paused to stare at her innocent beauty as she curtsied and passed by. Eustace, shaking from head to toe, begged leave to run after her, to speak just a few words. Campian nodded, and Eustace darted across the field to catch her at the next turn in the road.

She shrieked softly in surprise. “Mr. Leigh! I thought you had gone on ahead.”

“I came back to speak to you, Rose—Mistress Salterne, I mean. I must tell you all, or die!” He pressed close to her, and she shrank back, frightened. “Do not run, I beg you! Only hear me!” He seized her hand fiercely, and poured out every wild, passionate declaration of love he could summon, heaping her with every extravagant compliment he had ever read or heard.

Rose had heard such words a hundred times from the gallants of Bideford, but the raw, hungry fire in his eyes made her recoil. “Let me go at once, sir! You are far too rough!” She struggled to pull her hands away.

“Rougher than the soft lads who serenade you and write you sonnets?” he said, grabbing both her wrists now. “But far more true, Rose! Do not turn away! I shall die if you take your eyes from me! Tell me—here, now, this very moment—before we part—may I love you?”

“Go away!” she sobbed, fighting to free herself. “This is unforgivably rude! I may be only a merchant’s daughter, but I am God’s own child. I am alone here. Leave me, or I will call for help!”

Eustace had read somewhere that such protests from a woman were no more than pretty modesty, signs she did not truly want to be left alone, and he tightened his grip on her wrists, leaning in closer. But Rose was in deadly earnest, and drew in a breath to scream as loud as she could, to call for help from the farmhands not far off. To save his own reputation, he knew he must flee—but one final, desperate question burned in his throat, and he would risk everything to hear her answer.
