*P. Henry. Why, what a rascal art thou, then, to praise him so for running! Falstaff. O' horseback, ye cuckoo! but a-foot, he will not budge a foot. P. Henry. Yes, Jack, upon instinct. Falstaff. I grant ye, upon instinct.*
— *Henry IV, Part I*

They slipped past Grenada’s southern headland under cover of dark, and at last crossed the threshold of that fabled ring of islands where Nature had lavished every shade of her beauty, and man had heaped up every sin of greed and cruelty. If Barbados had glowed like something half-magical to these English newcomers, how much more did the stretch of sea they now sailed feel like a dream: calm almost year-round, untouched by the hurricanes that raged hundreds of miles north, sky and water and far green islands bleeding into one soft, endless rainbow. The hard-bitten veteran sailors scarcely noticed the grandeur, their heads full only of Spanish gold and pearl harvests; even Amyas, long used to tropical skies, was busy calculating how he might root out every last Spaniard and claim the whole of the West Indies for Queen Elizabeth. But for all their unpoetic pragmatism, none of the men could look on these famous, barely-touched lands without a jolt of awe and fire: they were sailing waters scarcely ever ploughed by an English keel, no more than four or five other English voyages having ventured here before. The penalty for failure here was not just death, but the slow, searing torments of the Inquisition. Aboard the *Pelican* and *Minion*, old mariners who had sailed with John Hawkins himself were badgered all day for tales of every hidden cove, every strange fish and bright-plumed bird, while Frank stood apart, quiet and serious, a look of wonder softening his face.

That wonder clung to him like a veil, not sad, but luminous: he seemed to drink in the glow of the landscape around him, murmuring to himself now and then that this was the very gate of heaven, too caught up in watching to think of food or rest. Every new league the ship sailed unveiled another fantastical sight: islands and headlands hung suspended in mid-air, their inverted reflections glowing in the glassy water beneath, long sand dunes rolling and warping in the heat haze. Bright yellow flower beds and gnarled giant cacti like candelabra twisted and flicker on the shore slopes, the whole scene shifting into a blazing phantom world, the sun itself warping into strange oval and pear shapes as it sank through banks of crimson mist.

While Frank marveled, Yeo the old sailor rejoiced. Far to the south of the setting sun, a line of tall blue peaks jutted from the water: by his reckoning, they were the mountains of Macanao, at the western tip of Margarita — the Isle of Pearls, whose name was famous across every Mediterranean city and every great trade fair in Germany, second only in richness to the Panamanian pearl isle where John Oxenham had met his death fifteen years prior.

By the next dawn, they were cruising along Margarita’s north shore, having slipped unseen past the Spanish fort built on the island’s eastern tip to guard the pearl fisheries. At last they rounded a headland to find a deep, quiet cove, thick with trees right down to the waterline, and a single Spanish caravel riding at anchor, three small boats bobbing beside her. In an instant every man of the crew was on deck, shouting out his own plan of attack. Some wanted to sail straight into the cove, the fresh afternoon trade wind blowing fair toward the shore, but after spotting jagged reefs breaking the water at the bay mouth, they agreed it was safer to slip past out of sight of the caravel, then send in the pinnace and the longboat to board her. Yeo suggested they run up Spanish colors to avoid alarming the crew, but Amyas refused flat. “I count it a mean, cowardly trick to tell such a lie unless we’re in mortal peril, or fighting for the crown itself,” he said.

They held their course till the next headland hid them from the caravel’s view, then launched their boats: Will Cary took the larger longboat with twenty men, Amyas the smaller pinnace with fifteen, including John Brimblecombe — who insisted on coming along in his clerical cassock and bands, armed with his great-uncle’s old broadsword that he treasured above all his possessions.

When they reached the cove mouth, they found exactly the reefs they had feared, stretching for acres under the clear turquoise water, forcing them to skirt the edge for half an hour hunting for a narrow channel. The new men to the Indies leaned over the gunwales, gawking at the living coral flower beds and sunlit underwater gardens of the sea gods, when a dark school of sharks came gliding up below them, some as long as the boat itself, their wicked slitted eyes staring up at the men hungrily.

“Jack,” Amyas teased, sitting right next to him, “look how that great brute is eyeing you. He’s got a taste for that plump hide of yours, thinks you’ll eat far sweeter than any suckling pig.”

Jack’s face blanched white, but he said nothing. Just then, the very shark Amyas had pointed to lunged up at a parrotfish darting out of a coral crevice, two more right behind him. The poor fish had nowhere to run, so it leaped straight out of the water, nearly landing in the boat, while three great rough brown shark snouts clashed together less than two yards from where Jack sat, baring their rows of saw-sharp teeth before sinking back down to glower for new bait. Jack whispered so quiet no one but Amyas could hear, *“Into thy hands, O Lord,”* and turned his eyes firmly back to the boat’s interior, no desire left to look at sharks ever again.

Through the channel they rowed, the caravel now less than musket-shot away. Cary laid her alongside before the Spanish crew could even reach their guns, standing in the stern sheets to shout for them to yield. The captain called boldly back, demanding to know in whose name they made the attack. “In the name of common sense, you dogs!” Will laughed. “Don’t you see you’re fifty men to our twenty?” He scrambled over the gunwale before they could answer, the captain firing a pistol straight at him. Cary knocked him unconscious rather than kill him, and the rest of the crew surrendered at once, some falling to their knees, others leaping overboard to swim for shore.

While that was happening, Amyas had pulled round to the caravel’s stern to board the second of the small boats, whose crew surrendered without a blow, screaming *“Mercy!”* while their enslaved oarsmen dived overboard and swam for shore like sea-dogs. The third boat, no more than an oar’s length away, turned to flee at once. That was when the great farce befell Jack Brimblecombe: desperate to prove his courage, he grabbed a boat-hook, clawed it hard into the stern of the fleeing Spanish boat, and yelled, “Stop, you Papists! Stop, you Spanish dogs!” — and of course, with ten Spaniards yanking the other way, he was hauled right over the gunwale, landing flat on his face in the sea, the hook still stuck fast in the boat’s stern.

Panic seized him at once, his imagination filling the whole water with the sharks he had just seen. He roared like a raging town bull, and so flustered he never thought to turn back for his own boat, but struck out swimming furiously after the Spanish craft, his cassock billowing out behind him like the fins of some great black monk-fish, howling and spitting salt water: “Stop, you Spanish dogs! Help, all good fellows! Don’t you see I’m a dead man? They’re nibbling at my toes already! One of them has my leg! My right thigh is bitten clean off! Oh, that I was back preaching in Hartland pulpit! Stop, you Papist cowards, yield before I mince you to bits, and pull me aboard! Yield, I say, or my blood is on your heads! I’m no Jonah — if he swallows me, he’ll never spit me back up! It’s far better to fall into the hands of men than devils with three rows of teeth apiece. Into thy hands, O Lord — pray for my soul —”

He ranted on, more frantic than Panurge at his most seasick, till the English crew, convinced he would be snapped up by a shark or brained by a Spanish oar any second, fired a volley over the heads of the fleeing boat. Every Spaniard in the boat leaped overboard at once, and Jack scrambled over the empty craft’s gunwale, drew his sword with one hand while wiping salt water from his eyes with the other, and laid about him like a raging lion, slashing empty air as he yelled for the idolaters to surrender. When he finally caught his breath and realized he was the only man on board, he plopped down panting in the stern sheets and started yanking off his hose. Amyas, half-convinced the poor fellow had gone mad from sunstroke, pulled alongside to ask what on earth he was doing. Jack swore through puffed, salt-stung lips that his right thigh had been bitten clean through to the bone, that his hose was full of blood, and would have fainted dead away from the imaginary injury had not his friends argued with him for five minutes till he agreed he was completely unharmed.

They set to ransacking their first prize then, finding the caravel loaded with hides and salt pork — but that was not all. Tucked in the captain’s cabin, and stowed in the stern of the little boat Jack had captured, they found neatly wrapped baskets of palm leaves, stuffed full of lustrous, slightly browned pearls. The Spaniards had damaged their shine, rushing to pry the oysters open over fire rather than letting them rot slowly in the old Arab way to preserve their luster, but their value was still immense. A mischievous hand set fire to the caravel before they left, and its load of smoldering hides filled the whole cove with a thick, reeking stench.

Amyas was furious at the senseless waste, pointing out that Drake himself would never have done such a thing — but Cary had a jest ready. “Ah, but we’re ‘Lutheran devils’ to these Spaniards, aren’t we? Of course we have to vanish like any other fiend, leaving an evil stink behind us.”

As soon as they were back aboard their own ship, Amyas took Jack aside and told him he’d do better to fight bravely and save his breath, rather than roar before he was hurt, if he ever wanted the crew to take his sermons seriously. Frank, overhearing, said he would deal with Jack himself, and led him off to tease him gently.

“Come here, recreant Jack, lily-livered Jack, hysterical Jack. You’ve read Plato’s dialogues and Aristotle’s logic, haven’t you?”

Jack meekly said yes.

“Then you must agree the greater must contain the less?”
Jack nodded.
“And that which is more than a part must contain that part?”
Jack agreed again.
“Then tell me — is a priest not greater than a layman?”

Jack, who was always very loud about the dignity of the priesthood, puffed up and said “Of course, it is.”
“Then a priest contains a man, and is a man, plus something extra: his holy office. Which means if a priest proves he is no man, he proves he is no priest either, doesn’t he?”

Jack crossed his arms. “You may be right by logic, Frank, but sharks don’t care a fig for logic, and they sure don’t understand it.”

Frank laughed, and prodded him further till Jack admitted he had no defense for howling like a stuck pig at the sight of a non-existent bite, then teased him that he was a Manichee heretic for calling sharks evil fiends’ spawn, when God had made them to eat useless dead carcasses like his own. As a brother of their secret Rose fellowship, Frank declared, he was bound to face danger joyfully — so for his cowardice, they stripped him of his chaplain post for the brotherhood, and warned him that unless he performed a deed worthy of the Seven Champions of Christendom within seven days, he would be forbidden to carry a sword or dagger ever again, allowed no more iron than the pen nib he used for writing his sermons.

Amyas piled on, telling Jack it was no wonder young men laughed off the gospel when their preachers were soft, cowardly lads who were parsons before they were men, that a priest of the Church of England was meant to make good men first, before he made good Christians, and that his one minute of screaming fear had undone every good word he had ever preached. They badgered him from both sides till Jack clapped his hands over his ears and ran away, and Frank laughed so hard his usual quiet, serious face lit up like a boy’s.

The next day was Sunday. They barely convinced Jack to lead divine service, he was so shamefaced, and he flatly refused to give a sermon. After the service, Amyas read out the ship’s shared articles of agreement, then spotted a sloping beach nearby with a clear stream running down to the sea, and announced they would land to wash their clothes and refill their water casks, having found fresh water scarce at Barbados. Jack insisted on coming along, strapped with his old sword and a heavy arquebuse, saying he’d dreamed the previous night Spaniards had ambushed him, and he was sure the dream would come true. He added he’d almost rather die than be the crew’s laughing stock forever, badgered with Plato’s “devilish dialectic” and called a heretic at every turn.

They landed without trouble, washed their clothes, and cast their seine net in the shallows, catching all manner of strange bright fish, plus a manati fully seven feet long, its back crusted with barnacles like a piece of driftwood. The old chronicler noted the manati was a gentle, almost pious creature, that would watch over a corpse in the water for days till it decayed, carrying its young tucked under its flipper to suckle like a human mother, crying out with a human wail when wounded. Frank swore he heard the far-off, melodious song of the sirens while they were anchored there, which the rest of the crew joked was just the manati’s cry. One of the men shot two great black curassow birds with a crossbow, bigger than eagles, and that evening they feasted on roasted and boiled manati meat, tender and sweet as four-month-old veal.

They were hauling their full water casks back to the boat when they heard a shout: the Spaniards were on them. Fifty armed arquebusiers burst out of the treeline less than twenty yards from where Jack had sat down alone, half hidden under a great tree, reading his Bible, sword girt at his waist and arquebuse at his side. Jack calmly slipped a leaf into his Bible to mark his page, set it down on the roots, grabbed his arquebuse, and ran straight at the mounted Spanish captain at the head of the troop. He shot the man dead through the chest, hurled his arquebuse at the nearest soldier’s head, then drew his sword and laid about him like the mythic giant Colbrand, cutting through the ranks of arquebusiers with such wild, ferocious strokes that the Spaniards were convinced he was the devil himself, or Martin Luther risen from the dead to plague them. They fired half a dozen shots at him at once, but were so panicked they only grazed his thigh, a single shallow gash that left him limping for weeks. He kept advancing, sword flashing, while the rest of the Englishmen rallied behind Amyas and charged. The Spaniards turned and fled into the woods as fast as they had come, Cary laughing that “the dogs got such a taste of the parson, they didn’t dare wait for the rest of the congregation.”

Jack never stopped running till his foot caught on a root, and he pitched headlong to the ground, knocking all the breath out of his body. When he came to, he blinked up at Amyas and yelled “Glory be to God! How many did I kill? How many?”

“Nineteen at least,” Cary joked, “seven of them felled by a single backhand stroke.” He pointed to the dead Spanish captain at Jack’s feet, two dead arquebusiers, and three more wounded Spaniards limping away into the trees.

“There!” Jack panted, wiping sweat from his brow. “Will you laugh at me now, Will Cary, or say I can’t fight just because I’m a parson?” Cary grabbed his hand and apologized for all his earlier teasing, saying Jack had been the bravest man on the shore that day. Jack laughed, and reminded him of the old prank at Bideford School when Cary had stuffed great noisy drone beetles into his school desk. They pulled back to the ship thanking God for their narrow escape, Jack growing faint from the slow bleeding of his real thigh wound, the very wound he had screamed about the day before as imaginary. That evening, when Frank sat by his bunk to ask what had made him so suddenly brave, Jack just grinned, and said he had remembered the teasing about no man being no priest — and he’d be damned if he’d let a crew of Spaniards prove his friends right.
