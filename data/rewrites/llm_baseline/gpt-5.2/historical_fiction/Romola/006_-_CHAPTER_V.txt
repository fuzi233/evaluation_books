The Via de’ Bardi lay on the Oltrarno side, a long, river-breathed artery between the Ponte Vecchio and the open space by the Ponte alle Grazie. In the later hours the Arno softened everything: it took the harsh teeth of old stone and returned them as a wavering tenderness—window after window broken into the water’s slow, darkened gold. Yet the street had a memory that refused to be drowned in reflection. Even as carts creaked and sandals scuffed and the river murmured under the bridges, the place seemed to listen for older noises: the clang of iron on iron, the thud of stones hurled from a hill, the roar of an armed people pressing into narrow inlets between walls.

Once, those walls had been the Bardi’s—proud hands that had known, with equal appetite, the sword-hilt and the glittering edge of coined metal. Their eyes had been Florentine in the keenest way: able to measure power not only in blood but in contracts, in warehouses, in wool bales and galleys. They rose, purchased lands and feudal dignities, and stood, for a time, like a great banking engine driving Europe’s wars. Then came the splendid debtor’s splendid default; the sudden fear of creditors; the crash that ran along Mediterranean coasts like a storm through rigging. Even that did not bend their necks as much as the people’s rage did in 1343, when the street between the bridges became a trap and a fortress together. They held it like panthers at bay until the assault came from the hill behind. Twenty-two great houses by the river were sacked and burned, and many who bore the name were driven out.

But Florentine families were rooted like old vines: cut them back, scorch them, and still they sent up shoots. The Bardi rose again and again in altered shapes—now honorable, now disgraced, now rich, now shamed into hiding. Yet the old street by the water passed into other hands; and by 1492 it had long been thick with other names, especially the Neri, who owned a stretch of the hillward side.

In one of the Neri houses there lived, however, a Bardi still—a descendant from the branch that had been Counts of Vernio. The pride remained; the hunger to leave a track on the fast-whirling earth remained. But the instrument had changed. This Bardi did not stalk the streets with armed men, did not hang vassals from feudal rights, did not plot voyages of credit and cargo. His hand, deep-veined and cramped, had been shaped by years of copying manuscripts; his dinners had grown spare, first by choice, then by necessity; his clothes had worn into threadbare honesty. He sat among books and marble fragments—among the dead—seeing them now only by the light of remembered sight. He was blind, moneyless, and famous in a small, fierce way: Bardo de’ Bardi, to whom Nello the barber had promised to introduce a young Greek named Tito Melema.

Bardo’s house stood on the hillward side of the street, one of those sombre, massed stone buildings with narrow windows and a roofed terrace above, as if the structure itself mistrusted the world and chose to watch it through slits. The main door—grim, with scrolled hinges—opened on a groined entrance-court, empty but for a massive lamp-iron hanging in the center like a black fruit. A smaller door admitted to a stone stair and to ground-floor rooms used as warehouse by the proprietor. The first floor too was stacked with precious stores meant for roads and seas: perhaps to the Scheldt, perhaps down the African coast, perhaps to islands in the Aegean or the shores of the Euxine. Even in the morning quiet, those lower stories had their own hushed music: the faint shift of packed goods, the smell of hemp rope and dried skins and spice dust that had settled into the mortar. When Maso, the old serving-man, returned from the Mercato with his small load of cheap vegetables, the damp green scent of them—cabbage leaves, onions with their papery bite, herbs bruised by handling—rose with him as he climbed slowly, step after step, until at last he reached the second storey and the door of his master.

We follow him across the ante-chamber to the door on the left. He opened it and merely looked in and nodded, as if words were another weight to carry.

“Ah, you are come back, Maso. It is well. We have wanted nothing,” said a clear young voice from the far end of the room.

The room was long and spacious, its order so scrupulous that it seemed an act of devotion, the kind that makes a household ritual out of dusting and placing. Shelves ran around the walls, packed with books and antiquities arranged as if each had its appointed seat in a choir. Here and there, set on stands before the shelves, the past displayed itself in fragments: a beautiful feminine torso; a headless statue with a muscular arm uplifted, gripping a sword that had lost its blade; rounded infant limbs severed from their trunk, the marble dimples inviting a tenderness the stone could never return; well-preserved Roman busts; two or three vases from Magna Graecia. A large table in the center bore antique bronze lamps and small vessels in dark pottery. The colors were mostly pale or sombre: vellum bindings with deep-ridged backs; marble livid from long burial; bronze waiting for sun to coax out its greenish tinge; a once-splendid patch of carpet worn down to a dim, tired pattern. The narrow windows looked on the Via de’ Bardi, and the sun was not yet high enough to send any cheer into the room.

The brightest color was a girl’s hair.

She stood before a carved leggio, a churchlike reading-desk, and her hair—reddish gold—lay in an unbroken ripple, like sunset clouds on a clear autumn evening. A black fillet held it above small ears; it flowed forward again, a natural veil for her neck above a square-cut gown of black rascia. Her eyes were bent on a large volume. One long white hand rested on the desk; the other clasped the back of her father’s chair, not with languor but with a steadiness that looked like habit trained into duty.

The blind father sat with his head uplifted and turned a little toward her, as if sight had become a posture rather than a faculty. His delicate paleness under a black velvet cap made the likeness between them more evident: both had the same refined brow and nostril, and both lacked any rose in the cheek. Yet in both, too, the full firm mouth and powerful chin gave a proud tenacity, a latent impetuousness. In her it showed in the backward poise of the head and the grand line of neck and shoulders. One could not tell whether such a face would win love or only admiration edged with dread; everything depended on the eyes—the direct messengers. The father’s eyes had long been silent. The daughter’s hazel eyes were fixed on the Latin pages of Politian’s Miscellanea, and her voice, clear and distinct, carried the words across the room like a controlled, steady chant.

“There was a certain nymph of Thebes named Chariclo, especially dear to Pallas; and this nymph was the mother of Teiresias. But once when in the heat of summer, Pallas, in company with Chariclo, was bathing her disrobed limbs in the Heliconian Hippocrene, it happened that Teiresias coming as a hunter to quench his thirst at the same fountain, inadvertently beheld Minerva unveiled, and immediately became blind. For it is declared in the Saturnian laws, that he who beholds the gods against their will, shall atone for it by a heavy penalty... When Teiresias had fallen into this calamity, Pallas, moved by the tears of Chariclo, endowed him with prophecy and length of days, and even caused his prudence and wisdom to continue after he had entered among the shades, so that an oracle spake from his tomb: and she gave him a staff, wherewith, as by a guide, he might walk without stumbling... And hence, Nonnus, in the fifth book of the Dionysiaca, introduces Actreon exclaiming that he calls Teiresias happy, since, without dying, and with the loss of his eyesight merely, he had beheld Minerva unveiled, and thus, though blind, could for evermore carry her image in his soul.”

As she reached the end of the passage, her hand slipped from the back of the chair and met her father’s, which had risen at that moment as if in answer to the story’s touch. She did not look around; she began to shape the Greek quotation with a voice subtly altered, tightened by something she refused to let rise. But the old man spoke, and his words cut in with quick, nervous authority.

“Stay, Romola; reach me my own copy of Nonnus. It is a more correct copy than any in Poliziano’s hands, for I made emendations in it which have not yet been communicated to any man. I finished it in 1477, when my sight was fast failing me.”

Romola crossed the room with the queenly step that belonged to her tall, finely-wrought frame—a simple motion, as if she had never learned to think of grace because she had always possessed it.

“Is it in the right place, Romola?” Bardo asked. He was perpetually seeking that assurance, like a man touching the edge of a table in the dark to prove it still stood where memory had built it.

“Yes, father; at the west end of the room, on the third shelf from the bottom, behind the bust of Hadrian, above Apollonius Rhodius and Callimachus, and below Lucan and Silius Italicus.”

In the clearness of her diction, a fine ear might have caught a faint weariness pushing against habitual patience—like a low note under a melody. But when she turned with the volume and saw his arms lifted a little, stretched out with eager, tremulous need to seize it, pity brimmed in her eyes. She hastened to lay the book on his lap, then knelt close by him, looking up as if love itself were a light strong enough to press through the barrier of blindness. Pride and passion, which often seemed to tremble in her features as if ready to harden, were softened now into womanliness by that mingled pity and affection. Something deeper than her proud mouth and firm chin found its outlet chiefly through her eyes.

Bardo, unaware of her radiance, looked flushed and agitated as his hand explored the edges and back of the large book.

“The vellum is yellowed in these thirteen years, Romola.”

“Yes, father,” she said gently. “But your letters at the back are dark and plain still—fine Roman letters; and the Greek character”—she opened the book on his knee—“is more beautiful than that of any of your bought manuscripts.”

“Assuredly, child,” Bardo said, moving his finger across the page as if he could still feel line and margin. “What hired amanuensis can be equal to the scribe who loves the words that grow under his hand, and to whom an error or indistinctness in the text is more painful than a sudden darkness or obstacle across his path? And even these mechanical printers who threaten to make learning a base and vulgar thing—even they must depend on the manuscript over which we scholars have bent with that insight into the poet’s meaning which is closely akin to the mens divinior of the poet himself; unless they would flood the world with grammatical falsities and inexplicable anomalies that would turn the very fountain of Parnassus into a deluge of poisonous mud. But find the passage in the fifth book, to which Poliziano refers—I know it very well.”

Romola drew a low stool close to his knee, laid the book on her lap, and read the four verses that held Actreon’s exclamation.

“It is true, Romola,” Bardo said when she had finished. “It is a true conception of the poet; for what is that grosser, narrower light by which men behold merely the petty scene around them, compared with that far-stretching, lasting light which spreads over centuries of thought, and over the life of nations, and makes clear to us the minds of the immortals who have reaped the great harvest and left us to glean in their furrows? For me, Romola, even when I could see, it was with the great dead that I lived; while the living often seemed to me mere spectres—shadows dispossessed of true feeling and intelligence; and unlike those Lamiae, to whom Poliziano, with that superficial ingenuity which I do not deny to him, compares our inquisitive Florentines, because they put on their eyes when they went abroad, and took them off when they got home again, I have returned from the converse of the streets as from a forgotten dream, and have sat down among my books, saying with Petrarca, the modern who is least unworthy to be named after the ancients, ‘Libri medullitus delectant, colloquuntur, consulunt, et viva quadam nobis atque arguta familiaritate junguntur.’”

“And in one thing you are happier than your favourite Petrarca, father,” Romola said, with the affectionate tact of one who knows how to humor without mocking. “For he used to look at his copy of Homer and think sadly that the Greek was a dead letter to him: so far, he had the inward blindness that you feel is worse than your outward blindness.”

“True, child; for I carry within me the fruits of that fervid study which I gave to the Greek tongue under the teaching of the younger Crisolora, and Filelfo, and Argiropulo; though that great work in which I had desired to gather, as into a firm web, all the threads that my research had laboriously disentangled, and which would have been the vintage of my life, was cut off by the failure of my sight and my want of a fitting coadjutor. For the sustained zeal and unconquerable patience demanded from those who would tread the unbeaten paths of knowledge are still less reconcilable with the wandering, vagrant propensity of the feminine mind than with the feeble powers of the feminine body.”

“Father,” Romola said, flushing suddenly, her tone wounded, “I read anything you wish me to read; and I will look out any passages for you, and make whatever notes you want.”

Bardo shook his head and smiled with a bitter sort of pity. “As well try to be a pentathlos and perform all the five feats of the palaestra with the limbs of a nymph. Have I forgotten thy fainting in the mere search for the references I needed to explain a single passage of Callimachus?”

“But, father, it was the weight of the books, and Maso can help me; it was not want of attention and patience.”

Bardo shook his head again. “It is not mere bodily organs that I want: it is the sharp edge of a young mind to pierce the way for my somewhat blunted faculties. For blindness acts like a dam, sending the streams of thought backward along the already-travelled channels and hindering the course onward. If my son had not forsaken me, deluded by debasing fanatical dreams, worthy only of an energumen whose dwelling is among tombs, I might have gone on and seen my path broadening to the end of my life; for he was a youth of great promise. But it has closed in now,” he continued after a pause; “it has closed in now;—all but the narrow track he has left me to tread—alone in my blindness.”

Romola rose abruptly, stung beyond stillness. She carried the large volume back to its place and set it with exactness, as though order could be an answer to pain. Then she stood at some distance, arms stretched down, fingers tightly clasped. Her gaze moved over the lifeless company around her—the parchment backs ranked like silent jurists, the unchanging mutilated marble, the obsolete bronze and clay. The room had its own quiet sound: the faint creak of the leggio settling, the far murmur of the street filtered through stone, the river’s low, persistent breath. In that quiet, her father’s words rang with a sharper edge than any noise outside. If Florence was full of cries and bells and bargaining calls, here the silence itself pressed like a hand; it held memory in place and made every reproach echo longer.

Bardo, usually quick to notice her movements, was now too full of rankling remembrance to attend to her.

“Yes,” he went on, “with my son to aid me, I might have had my due share in the triumphs of this century: the names of the Bardi, father and son, might have been held reverently on the lips of scholars in the ages to come; not on account of frivolous verses or philosophical treatises, which are superfluous and presumptuous attempts to imitate the inimitable, such as allure vain men like Panhormita, and from which even the admirable Poggio did not keep himself sufficiently free; but because we should have given a lamp whereby men might have studied the supreme productions of the past. For why is a young man like Poliziano (who was not yet born when I was already held worthy to maintain a discussion with Thomas of Sarzana) to have a glorious memory as a commentator on the Pandects—why is Ficino, whose Latin is an offence to me, and who wanders purblind among the superstitious fancies that marked the decline at once of art, literature, and philosophy, to descend to posterity as the very high priest of Platonism, while I, who am more than their equal, have not effected anything but scattered work, which will be appropriated by other men? Why? but because my son, whom I had brought up to replenish my ripe learning with young enterprise, left me and all liberal pursuits that he might lash himself and howl at midnight with besotted friars—that he might go wandering on pilgrimages befitting men who know of no past older than the missal and the crucifix?—left me when the night was already beginning to fall on me.”

His voice had risen in protest, then sank into a tremulous, plaintive reproach. Romola turned back, and her heart swelled with forgiving pity. Whatever pride stood erect in her, love knelt down easily. She returned to his side, seated herself near, and placed her hand on his knee—too proud to offer consolations that might sound like pleading for her own worth, yet eager that he should feel she was there.

“Yes, Romola,” Bardo said, and his left-hand—heavy with massive prophylactic rings—fell a little too heavily on the delicate blue-veined back of her right. She bit her lip to keep from starting. “If even Florence only is to remember me, it can but be on the same ground that it will remember Niccolò Niccoli—because I forsook the vulgar pursuit of wealth in commerce that I might devote myself to collecting the precious remains of ancient art and wisdom, and leave them, after the example of the munificent Romans, for an everlasting possession to my fellow-citizens. But why do I say Florence only? If Florence remembers me, will not the world remember me? ... Yet,” he added after a pause, his voice dropping into sadness, “Lorenzo’s untimely death has raised a new difficulty. I had his promise—I should have had his bond—that my collection should always bear my name and should never be sold, though the harpies might clutch everything else; but there is enough for them—there is more than enough—and for thee, too, Romola, there will be enough. Besides, thou wilt marry; Bernardo reproaches me that I do not seek a fitting parentado for thee, and we will delay no longer, we will think about it.”

“No, no, father; what could you do? besides, it is useless: wait till some one seeks me,” Romola said quickly, as if the words had the taste of iron and she wished them done and swallowed.

“Nay, my child, that is not the paternal duty. It was not so held by the ancients, and in this respect Florentines have not degenerated from their ancestral customs.”

“But I will study diligently,” Romola said, and her eyes widened with anxiety, as if she were trying to make her resolve visible. “I will become as learned as Cassandra Fedele: I will try and be as useful to you as if I had been a boy, and then perhaps some great scholar will want to marry me, and will not mind about a dowry; and he will like to come and live with you, and he will be to you in place of my brother... and you will not be sorry that I was a daughter.”

The last words rose into a sob she could not wholly master. Something in them touched the fatherly fibre in Bardo. He lifted his hand upward, searching, and when she bent her head beneath it, he stroked her golden hair gently, leaning toward her as if some glimmer could still be found there.
