The long-awaited test day for Fritz’s hand-built Inuit-style cajack finally dawned bright and warm over Safety Bay. We all gathered on the shingle shore to laugh at his ridiculous getup: his mother’s custom-designed full-body inflatable flotation suit, thick as a quilted mattress, puffed him up the second he blew up its air chambers until he looked exactly like the frog from the old fable, squeezed into the kayak’s tiny cockpit like a overstuffed sausage. Even his mother, who’d stitched every seam of the suit, couldn’t hold back giggles at the sight of him.
I hauled our sturdier support rowboat down to the water right after, Ernest and Jack scrambling aboard with me, ready to dart in the second anything went wrong. The cajack glided light as a leaf across the glassy green bay, and Fritz grabbed his double paddle to show off: darting arrow-fast across the water, wheeling sharp left and right, then tipping the craft almost fully on its side to prove it wouldn’t capsize. His mother shrieked at the sight, but the little skin boat never dipped even a drop of water, and he righted himself whooping, before he drifted straight into the fast, unmarked current of Jackal River. Before we could call a warning, he was being swept fast out past the bay’s headlands toward the open ocean.
My heart jumped. We all hauled on the oars at top speed, his mother yelling after us to hurry, calling the tiny kayak “a horrid soap bubble that’s bound to pop any second.” We rounded the old shipwreck rocks and scanned the empty waves for ten long, panicky minutes before we spotted a faint puff of smoke, followed by the crack of Fritz’s hunting pistol. We fired a signal shot in reply, and within seconds his cheery halloo drifted over the water, the cajack darting out from behind a rocky headland.
“Come ashore!” he waved wildly. “I’ve got something to show you!”
We pulled up on the beach to stare in shock at a full 14-foot young walrus, harpooned clean through and dead at the tide line.
“You killed this?” I asked, half-expecting a whole tribe of Inuit hunters to step out of the trees to claim it.
“Of course I did!” Fritz laughed. “Once the current dragged me out I spotted a whole herd of them, and forgot every rule I ever learned chasing after this one. I harpooned him twice before he dragged himself up on these rocks to die, and I shot him in the head before I got close — remembered that big serpent that almost got Jack, didn’t I?”
I scolded him gently for scaring his half-mother out of her wits, explaining that even the gentle walrus could easily smash a full-sized whale boat to splinters with its tusks if wounded, but we were all far more relieved to see him unhurt than upset. We set to work chopping off the walrus’s massive skull to get at its perfect ivory tusks, and stripping long tough strips of its hide to tan later for tool straps and harnesses. It was then that I noticed the air had gone thick and still, heavy with the scent of coming storm. We rushed to pack up, and Fritz begged to head back alone in the cajack to break the good news to his mother, rather than crowding into our slow rowboat. I let him go.
By the time we’d pushed off from the rocky beach, the sky had turned black as ink. Gales tore over the waves, tossing 10-foot breakers over the gunwales of our little boat, and we lashed ourselves tight to the seats with our swimming belts buckled on, riding out the tempest as rain lashed us half-blind and lightning split the dark all around us. I barely worried about our own boat — it was sturdy enough to weather the storm — every last one of my prayers was for Fritz, who’d long vanished out of sight ahead of us. I’d fully given him up for lost by the time we rounded the headland and glided into Safety Bay.
Then we saw them: his wife and Fritz, kneeling side by side on the shore, praying so hard for our safe return they never noticed we’d pulled up until we called their names. The whole family collapsed in tears of relief, stumbling back to our Rockburg home to peel off our soaked clothes, huddle by the fire, and tuck into a hot supper while we traded tales of the storm.
Later, we cleaned, embalmed, and mounted the walrus’s full skull on the prow of Fritz’s cajack, naming the craft *Sea-Horse*, and the tanned hide made some of the toughest, most useful leather we’d ever had. But the storm had done widespread damage: it washed out half the bridge over Jackal River, flooded half our unharvested crops, and cracked all our rainwater tanks, so we spent weeks repairing everything. We found a thick patch of wild clove berries growing under the cascade that same week, planting saplings in our nursery garden so his wife could flavor their meals with spice instead of their usual overused black pepper, and we laid new clay aqueducts to irrigate our corn fields with runoff from the grain mill. The fishing runs that season were bountiful, the larder stuffed full of salmon, sturgeon, and herring — even if we had to laugh Jack’s ridiculous plan of tethering live salmon to the bay floor by their gills, like we did our turtles, straight out the door.
After weeks of monotonous farm work, the boys grew restless. Fritz put forward a plan: they were almost out of bamboo for making new farm hurdles, and we also needed to restock our supply of fine pottery clay up at the Gap. We agreed on a quick trip: Fritz, Jack, and Franz would take the cart pulled by our faithful oxen Storm and Grumble, plus our ostrich Hurry and the two hunting dogs Floss and Bruno, while Ernest stayed behind with us to help build the new sugar mill I’d been planning for months. Fritz made a whole stock of North American trapper’s pemmican — pounded, dried, cooked bear meat, compressed into dense portable bars — for the trip, though his wife teased him that it would probably taste like sawdust. I spotted Jack sneaking a basket of our homing pigeons under the supplies in the cart, and grinned — the little rascal didn’t trust the pemmican either.
The first evening the boys were gone, we sat on Rockburg’s porch resting after a long day on the sugar mill, when one of the homing pigeons fluttered onto our dovecot. Ernest ran to fetch it, pulling a tiny scrap of paper tied to its leg:
> Dearest Parents and Ernest,
> A brute of a hyena killed our ram and two lambs. The dogs seized it. Franz shot it. It is dead and skinned. The pemmican isn’t worth much, but we are all right. Love to all.
> Fritz
> Woodlands, 15th instant
We laughed at the no-nonsense hunter’s note, even as his mother fretted about the danger they were running. The pigeon post kept us updated the whole trip: we heard how the boys arrived at Woodlands to hear uncanny cackling human laughter drifting from the bushes, the ostrich Hurry panicking and bolting straight into the rice swamp. Franz crept through the thicket with the dogs to find the massive hyena dancing and whirling in circles around its fresh kill, letting loose that ghoulish hysterical cackle. Franz fired both barrels of his gun, breaking its foreleg and wounding its chest, and even though the hyena badly bit both Floss and Bruno before it died, the boys patched up the dogs, skinned the hyena, and moved on.
They spent the next few days mapping the shoreline of Wood Lake, driving tall bamboo marker poles in the ground at every safe, firm crossing spot in the marsh. They fought off angry parent swans to catch three fuzzy baby black swans to bring back to live on Safety Bay, Fritz used his trained eagle to capture a stunning long-necked wild heron, and a huge snuffling tapir burst right through the reed beds next to them before anyone could blink, diving into the lake and vanishing before they could even call the dogs. Franz and Jack even invented a clever looped birdlime arrow to catch live birds without harming them, netting five cranes, two of them the rare, delicate Numidian demoiselle cranes. They tried the pemmican exactly once, then fed the whole batch to the dogs, feasting instead on peccary ham, cassava bread, and roasted sweet potatoes.
When they trekked up to Prospect Hill, a horde of screaming wild monkeys swarmed them, pelting them hard in the head with pinecones. They fired a few warning shots to scatter the apes, only to find our little summer cottage there completely torn apart, wrecked and trashed by the monkey horde. Fritz found a bottle of toxic euphorbia tree sap in the old tool chest, mixing it with sweet milk and crushed millet to make bait, hanging the poisoned coconut shell treats high in the treetops out of reach of their farm animals. That night the whole forest came alive: jackals howled in the distance, they heard deep distant roars they swore were lions, and the weird rumbling snorts of hippos from the far swamp — before the monkeys started screaming, the poison kicking in. The racket lasted until dawn, and when the sun came up, the ground under the trees was littered with dead apes. Fritz felt sick with guilt, and they worked for hours to bury every body deep or dump them far out at sea, scrubbing every last trace of the poison away before they set out for the Gap.
The last pigeon arrived at Rockburg late that same night, the note scrawled in a panic:
> The barricade at the Gap is broken down. Everything laid waste as far as the sugar-brake, where the hut is knocked to pieces, and the fields trampled over by huge footmarks. Come to us, father — we are safe, but feel we are no match for this unknown danger.
I saddled Swift immediately, riding hard under the bright full moon all the way to the Gap, surprising the three boys who’d expected me at dawn the next day. We inspected the damage at first light: thick, heavy barricade posts had been snapped like dry twigs, every single young sugarcane plant eaten or trampled flat, massive footprints of every size scattered everywhere. It was a full herd of elephants, the whole lot of them, that had wandered through. We traced the tracks out of the Gap back to the uninhabited wild forest, so the herd had moved on, no lingering threat left on our land. We sat up that whole night around a roaring bonfire, the boys spilling every last detail of their wild adventures, making plans to build a new, massive, reinforced barricade at the Gap — strong enough to keep even the largest elephant from ever breaking through again.
