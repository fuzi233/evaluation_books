Little had Mrs. Dashwood or her daughters anticipated upon arriving in Devonshire that their time would be consumed by such a relentless barrage of engagements, leaving them with negligible leisure for serious employment. Yet, this was the reality. Upon Marianne's recovery, Sir John executed the schemes of amusement he had previously formulated: private balls at the park commenced, and parties on the water were organized as often as the treacherous October showers permitted. In every gathering, Willoughby was integrated; the inherent ease and familiarity of these occasions were precisely calibrated to deepen his intimacy with the Dashwoods, affording him opportunities to witness Marianne's virtues, mark his animated admiration, and receive, through her behavior toward him, the most explicit assurances of her affection.

Elinor was unsurprised by their attachment; she merely wished it were displayed with greater discretion. On occasion, she ventured to suggest the propriety of self-command to Marianne. However, Marianne abhorred concealment where no genuine disgrace ensued from unreserve. To attempt restraining sentiments that were not inherently flawed appeared to her not merely an unnecessary exertion, but a disgraceful submission of reason to commonplace and erroneous notions. Willoughby shared this sentiment; their conduct served as a perpetual illustration of their convictions. When present, she possessed no vision for anyone else. Everything he did was deemed correct; everything he said was considered brilliant. If their evenings at the park concluded with cards, he rigged the game against himself and the entire party to ensure she held a winning hand. If dancing constituted the night's diversion, they were partners for half the duration; when compelled to separate for a couple of dances, they remained clustered together, speaking scarcely a word to any other soul.

Such behavior inevitably invited derisive laughter, yet ridicule failed to shame them and seemed hardly to provoke a reaction. Mrs. Dashwood embraced their emotions with a fervor that left her no inclination to curb this excessive display. To her, it was merely the natural outcome of a robust affection within a youthful, ardent mind. This era represented the zenith of happiness for Marianne. Her heart was entirely devoted to Willoughby, and the fond attachment she carried from Norland, Sussex, proved more susceptible to softening than she had previously believed, buoyed by the charms his society bestowed upon her current residence.

Elinor's happiness, however, was markedly diminished. Her heart lacked the same tranquility, and her satisfaction in their diversions was impure. None of her companions could compensate for what she had abandoned, nor could any instruct her to view Norland with reduced regret. Neither Lady Middleton nor Mrs. Jennings supplied the conversation she craved, despite the latter's incessant verbosity and initial kindness, which secured her a significant portion of the discourse. Mrs. Jennings had already recounted her own history to Elinor several times; had Elinor's memory matched her capacity for learning, she might have learned early in their acquaintance the specifics of Mr. Jennings' final illness and his dying words to his wife. Lady Middleton was preferable to her mother solely due to her silence. Elinor required little observation to discern that this reserve was merely a placid demeanor devoid of intellectual substance. Toward her husband and mother, she exhibited the same affectation; thus, intimacy was neither expected nor sought. She possessed nothing novel to say one day that she had not articulated the previous. Her insipidity was immutable, for even her spirits remained static. Although she did not oppose the gatherings arranged by her husband, provided affairs were conducted with style and her two eldest children were present, she derived no greater enjoyment from them than she might have experiencing solitude at home. So little did her presence enhance the pleasure of others through participation in conversation that they were occasionally reminded of her proximity only by her anxiety regarding her troublesome sons.

In Colonel Brandon, among all her new acquaintances, Elinor discovered the sole individual capable of commanding respect through ability, inspiring the interest of friendship, or providing genuine pleasure as a companion. Willoughby was off-limits. Her admiration and regard—even her sisterly esteem—were exclusively his; yet he was a lover, his attentions wholly directed toward Marianne, meaning a far less agreeable man might have proven more universally pleasing. Unfortunately for himself, Colonel Brandon possessed no such encouragement to focus solely on Marianne. In conversing with Elinor, he found the greatest solace for the indifference of her sister. Elinor's compassion for him intensified as she suspected, with cause, that the misery of thwarted love had already been etched onto his soul.

This suspicion crystallized following remarks that accidentally escaped him one evening at the park, when they sat together by mutual consent while the others danced. His gaze was fixed upon Marianne. After a silence lasting several minutes, he remarked with a faint smile, "Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments."

"No," replied Elinor, "her opinions are purely romantic."

"Or rather, as I believe, she deems them impossible to exist."

"I believe she does. Yet, how she manages this without reflecting on the character of her own father, who himself had two wives, remains beyond my comprehension. A few years, however, will settle her opinions on the reasonable foundation of common sense and observation; thereafter, they may be easier to define and justify than they currently are, by anyone but herself."

"This will likely occur," he responded, "and yet there is something so amiable in the prejudices of a young mind that one regrets seeing them yield to the reception of more generalized opinions."

"I cannot concur with you there," said Elinor. "There are inconveniences attendant upon such feelings as Marianne's that all the charms of enthusiasm and ignorance of the world cannot atone for. Her systems possess the unfortunate tendency of setting propriety at naught; and a deeper acquaintance with the world is what I anticipate as her greatest possible advantage."

After a brief pause, he resumed the discussion: "Does your sister make no distinction in her objections against a second attachment? Or is it equally criminal in everyone? Are those who have been disappointed in their first choice, whether due to the inconstancy of its object or the perverseness of circumstances, to remain equally indifferent for the remainder of their lives?"

"Upon my word, I am unfamiliar with the minutiae of her principles. I only know that I have never yet heard her admit any instance of a second attachment being pardonable."

"This," he stated, "cannot hold; but a change—a total transformation of sentiments—No, no, do not desire it; for when the romantic refinements of a young mind are compelled to yield, how frequently are they succeeded by opinions that are but too common and too dangerous! I speak from experience. I once knew a lady whose temperament and mind greatly resembled your sister, who thought and judged alike, but who, from an enforced change—from a series of unfortunate circumstances—"

He stopped abruptly, appearing to realize he had spoken too freely, and by his countenance aroused conjectures that might not otherwise have entered Elinor's thoughts. Had the lady passed without suspicion, had he not convinced Miss Dashwood that matters concerning her ought not to escape his lips. As it was, it required but a slight effort of imagination to link his emotion with the tender recollection of past regard. Elinor attempted no further inquiry. But Marianne, in her position, would not have restrained herself so little. The entire narrative would have been swiftly constructed under her active imagination, with every detail established in the most melancholy order of disastrous love.
