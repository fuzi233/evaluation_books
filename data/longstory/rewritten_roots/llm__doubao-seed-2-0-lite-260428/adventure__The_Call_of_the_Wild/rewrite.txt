Old longings nomadic leap,
Chafing at custom’s chain;
Again from its brumal sleep
Wakens the ferine strain.

Buck never skimmed newspaper headlines, or he would have known trouble was brewing not just for him, but for every heavy, long-coated tidewater dog between Puget Sound and San Diego. Men digging in the frozen dark of the Arctic had struck gold by the bucketful, and steamship lines blew the story up across every port, sending thousands of frantic prospectors stampeding north. Those men didn’t want lapdogs. They needed hard-muscled brutes that could haul tons of supplies across ice, thick fur wrapped tight to their bones to fend off sixty-below frost.

Buck ruled every inch of Judge Miller’s sun-drenched estate, tucked back from the road in Santa Clara Valley, half-hidden behind draping oak and poplar boughs. A wide cool veranda wrapped all four sides of the big house, gravel driveways winding through acres of manicured lawn, stables that held a dozen grooms, rows of vine-choked servant cottages, endless stretches of grape arbors, green pastures, orchards, and berry patches. There was an artesian well pumping station, and a big cement swimming tank where the Judge’s boys cannonballed at dawn and lazed through sweltering afternoons.

He’d been born here, four years old, 140 solid pounds of mixed St Bernard and Scotch Shepherd breeding, no soft pampered house pet. He swam the tank, hunted alongside the Judge’s sons, escorted the Judge’s daughters on long twilight rambles, curled warm at the old man’s feet in front of the library fire on winter nights, toted the Judge’s grandsons on his back through the orchard trails. The dozen snippy fox terriers didn’t dare challenge him, the strange tiny pug Toots and hairless Mexican dog Ysabel he completely ignored. He was king of every creeping, crawling, flying thing on the whole property — even the human kids followed his lead half the time.

1897 rolled around, the Klondike gold rush dragging every fortune-seeker on the continent north into the ice, and Buck had no clue that Manuel, one of the gardener’s helpers, was a deadbeat gambling fool hooked on Chinese lottery. His “foolproof” betting system chewed through every cent of his meager wages, leaving nothing for his wife and horde of kids. The night the Judge was off at a raisin growers’ meeting, and the boys were busy setting up their new athletic club, Manuel slipped Buck through the orchard fence on what Buck thought was just a casual walk. No one saw them but a stranger loitering at the tiny College Park flag station, and Buck heard the clink of coins pass between them.

“Wrap the goods up tight before you hand ’em over,” the stranger grunted, and Manuel looped a thick stout rope around Buck’s neck right under his collar. “Twist it, you’ll choke him plenty,” Manuel said, and the stranger nodded.

Buck had always trusted the people who lived on the estate, so he accepted the rope with quiet dignity — right up until the stranger’s hand wrenched it tight, cutting off his breath. He snarled, then lunged, full of rage at the betrayal, but the man caught him by the throat, twisted, and slammed him flat on his back. The rope bit harder, Buck’s tongue lolled out, his great chest heaving useless air. He’d never been so humiliated, so furious in his life. His vision blurred, and he went limp just as a train screeched to a stop, the two men dumping his unconscious body into the baggage car.

He woke up to the rumble of the train, his tongue throbbing, and the full, unbridled rage of a kidnapped king flaring up. He lunged for the stranger’s hand, jaws clamping down hard, and didn’t let go till the rope choked him senseless again. The man lied straight to the baggage handler who heard the struggle, hiding his mangled hand behind his back, claiming Buck was a sick show dog he was taking to a vet in San Francisco.

Later that night, in a rickety shed behind a waterfront saloon, the man complained he only got fifty bucks for the whole mess, his hand wrapped in a blood-soaked handkerchief, his right trouser leg ripped open from the knee to the ankle. The saloon keeper had paid a hundred bucks for Buck, and said he was worth every cent. They pried the heavy brass collar off Buck’s neck while he was half out of it, pulled the rope away, and locked him in a flimsy wooden crate.

Buck lay there the rest of the night, rage and wounded pride eating at him, confused, waiting for the Judge or the boys to come rescue him. Every time the shed door rattled, he lifted his head hopeful, only to see the saloon keeper’s fat face peering in by the guttering tallow candle, and the happy welcome bark in his throat twisted into a savage snarl.

By dawn, four ragged, rough-looking men showed up to haul the crate away. Buck stormed and raged at them through the slats, and they laughed, poking at him with sticks till he figured out that was exactly what they wanted. He went sullen and quiet, as the crate passed from wagon to ferry to express train, jostled along for two full days and nights. Buck refused to eat a bite, refused to take a drop of water. He growled at every messenger who came near, they taunted him back, barking and mewing and flapping their arms like idiots, and his anger built higher and higher, turning to a burning fever from his parched, swollen throat. He swore no one would ever loop a rope around his neck again. By the time they pulled into Seattle, the express workers were so relieved to get him off their car they practically ran to hand him over.

Four men carried the crate into a small, high-walled backyard. A broad, stocky man in a stretched-out red sweater stepped out, signed for the delivery, and Buck knew instantly he was the next tormentor. He threw himself against the slats of the crate, snarling. The red-sweatered man grabbed a hatchet and a heavy wooden club. The four other men scattered fast, scrambling up to perch on top of the wall to watch.

Buck bit and tore at the splintering wood, lunging at every spot the hatchet bit through the crate. The second the opening was big enough for him to squeeze through, the man tossed the hatchet aside, hefted the club. Buck launched himself full force, all 140 pounds of two days’ worth of pent-up fury, straight for the man’s throat. Mid-leap, the club cracked him hard across the side, and he flipped over to hit the ground back first, teeth clipping together so hard his jaws ached.

He’d never been hit by a club in his life. He staggered back up, snarling half bark half scream, and charged again. Again the club knocked him flat. A dozen times he threw himself at the man, and a dozen times the club smashed his charge apart. After one particularly brutal blow, he stumbled around dazed, blood pouring from his nose, mouth, and ears, his once-glossy coat matted with slaver and blood. The man stepped forward, and hit him square on the nose. The pain was worse than every blow before, a white-hot fire, and Buck roared like a lion as he charged one last time. The man twisted his jaw, wrenched him around in a full circle, and Buck crashed head first into the dirt. One final, sharp blow to the skull, and Buck went completely senseless.

“Damn, that guy knows how to break a dog,” one of the men on the wall yelled enthusiastically.
“Still rather break horses any day, twice on Sundays,” the driver called back, climbing back onto his wagon.

When Buck came to, he didn’t have the strength to stand. He lay there in the dirt, watching the man in the red sweater. The man spoke in a soft, genial tone, patting the top of Buck’s throbbing head even though Buck’s fur bristled on instinct. “We had our little fight, that’s all. You learned your place, I learned mine. Be good, and everything’s easy. Be bad, and I’ll beat you within an inch of your life. Got it?”

Buck didn’t snap. He accepted the bowl of water the man brought him, then bolted down a big pile of raw meat straight from the man’s hand. He’d been beaten, but he hadn’t been broken. He knew now he would never win a fight against a man with a club. That club was his first lesson in the brutal, unforgiving law of the primitive wild. He watched every new dog that got dragged through the yard, every one of them that fought, that fawned, that refused to submit and got killed for it. He learned the rule sharp: the man with the club is the lawgiver, you obey him — you don’t have to like him.

His turn to get taken away came at last, when a small, weathered French-Canadian man named Perrault spotted him, spat broken English, and exclaimed Buck was the finest dog he’d ever seen, one in ten thousand. The red-sweatered man sold him for three hundred dollars, a steal with the price of work dogs skyrocketing. Perrault was working for the Canadian government, carrying urgent dispatches north. Buck saw the money change hands, and soon he was being led away alongside Curly, a sweet-natured Newfoundland, down to the docks to board the steamship *Narwhal*. That was the last he ever saw of the man in the red sweater, and the last he ever saw of the sun-warmed California south.

Below decks, Perrault handed them over to François, a half-French, half-Indian breed with a dark, weathered face. Buck soon learned both men were fair, sharp, they knew dogs better than dogs knew themselves, and he grew to respect them, even if he never loved them. They shared the hold with two other dogs: Spitz, a sharp, snow-white dog from Spitzbergen who’d come home with a whaling crew, who smiled right at you while he snuck your food. When Spitz stole Buck’s first meal on the ship, François’s whip cracked across his back before Buck could even lunge, and Buck decided the half-breed was fair, worth his respect. The other dog was Dave, grim, quiet, grumpy, who wanted nothing more than to be left alone to eat, sleep, and yawn. He didn’t care when the ship bucked and rolled across Queen Charlotte Sound, while Buck and Curly panicked at the rough seas, he just lifted his head, glanced at them annoyed, and went right back to sleep.

Day after day the *Narwhal*’s propeller droned on, and Buck noticed the air getting colder and colder, sharp enough to make his nose tingle. One morning, the propeller went quiet, the whole ship hummed with excitement. François leashed all the dogs and led them up on deck. Buck stepped forward, and his paws sank straight down into cold, soft white mush that looked like mud. He yelped and sprang back. More of the white stuff was drifting down from the sky. He shook himself, but it kept landing on his fur. He sniffed it curious, licked a bit up on his tongue. It burned like fire, then vanished instantly. He tried another lick, same result. The sailors watching roared with laughter, and Buck felt a hot, strange shame creep up — no one had ever laughed at him before. He was touching snow for the very first time.

Buck’s first 24 hours on Dyea Beach was a nightmare stitched out of ice, shock, and snarling fur. He had been ripped straight from the soft, sun-drenched heart of Judge Miller’s California estate—where the hardest work he ever did was chase the gardener’s children through orange groves—and hurled into a primordial frozen world where peace, rest, and even a single second of safety did not exist. Every step, every snap of a twig under the snow, every lunging dog was a threat to life and limb, and he learned fast he could never let his guard drop. These men and these hounds were not soft town creatures. They were savages, all of them, who recognized no rule but the unbreakable law of the club and the fang.

He had never seen dogs fight the way these wolfish northern brutes fought, and his first lesson was one he would never forget. He didn’t even have to be the one to take the blow to learn it. Curly, the gentle, playful Newfoundland who had traveled on the boat with him, trotted over to make friends with a husky as big as a full-grown wolf. No warning growl, no stiffened hackles—just a flash of movement, a sharp metallic snap of jaws, and the husky leaped away as fast as he’d struck, leaving Curly’s face ripped wide open from eye to jaw.

That was the wolf way of fighting: strike hard, leap fast, no fair play. But worse was coming. Thirty or forty more huskies swarmed the clearing, forming a tight, silent, staring circle around the two of them, licking their chops in hungry anticipation. Curly lunged at her attacker, who dodged, then slammed his chest into hers hard enough to knock her flat on her back. She never got back up.

The waiting circle of hounds surged in at once, yelping and snarling, and Curly vanished under a writhing, bristling mass of bodies, screaming in agony. It all happened so fast Buck froze, stunned. He saw Spitz, the sharp-faced lead husky, loll his scarlet tongue out like he was cackling with glee. François came charging in swinging an axe, three other men with clubs right behind him, beating the hounds off. Two minutes after Curly went down, the last of the attackers were scattered. But Curly lay limp and lifeless in the trampled, blood-soaked snow, torn almost to shreds, while François stood over her cursing in his rough French patois.

The scene haunted Buck’s nightmares for weeks after. So that was how it worked. No rules. No second chances. Once you went down, you were done for. He swore he would never, ever let himself fall. When he glanced over and saw Spitz laughing at him again, a bitter, unkillable hatred bloomed hot in his chest.

Before he could even shake off the shock of Curly’s death, Buck got his next surprise. François strapped a heavy leather harness of buckles and straps to his back—the exact kind he’d seen the grooms fasten to carriage horses back at the ranch. They hitched him to a sled, and suddenly he was hauling François out to the forest at the edge of the valley and dragging a full load of firewood back to camp. His pride ached, furious that he was being treated like a common draft animal, but he was too smart to rebel. He buckled down and threw all his weight into the pull. François was stern, his whip cracking for instant obedience, and Dave, the veteran wheel dog who ran at the very back of the team, nipped Buck’s hindquarters hard every time he messed up his pace. Spitz, the lead dog, could not always reach him, but he growled sharp, biting rebukes, or threw his whole weight against the traces to jerk Buck back into the right path. Buck learned fast. By the time they got back to camp, he knew to stop dead at the call of “ho,” surge forward at “mush,” swing wide on sharp bends, and leap clear out of the way of the wheel dog when the heavy sled shot downhill fast enough to run the whole team over.

“Three very good dogs,” François bragged to Perrault, the government courier who owned the team. “That Buck, he pulls like hell. I’ll train him faster than any dog I ever had.”

By mid-afternoon, Perrault, in a rush to hit the trail with his top-secret dispatches, showed up with two more huskies, brothers named Billee and Joe. Born to the same litter, they were as different as day and night. Billee was nothing but excessive good nature, while Joe was sour, sharp, perpetually snarling, his eyes glinting with unrepentant malice. Buck greeted them friendly enough, Dave ignored them entirely, but Spitz immediately moved in to thrash both of them into submission. Billee tucked his tail, wagged appeasingly, then ran whimpering when he realized kindness wouldn’t save him, letting Spitz nip a gash in his flank. But no matter how Spitz circled, Joe spun on his heels to face him, hackles bristling, ears flattened back, jaws snapping so fast they blurred. Spitz tried and tried, but Joe wouldn’t back down an inch, and finally Spitz slunk away, embarrassed, to chase the harmless, wailing Billee all the way to the edge of the camp.

That evening, Perrault brought in one last dog: an old, gaunt, battle-scarred husky named Sol-leks, which meant the Angry One. He had only one good eye, and that single eye glinted with so much unspoken brute prowess that every dog who looked at him stepped back automatically. Like Dave, he asked nothing of anyone, gave nothing, expected nothing, and even Spitz didn’t dare pick a fight with him. Buck made the mistake one day of wandering up on his blind left side, without thinking. Sol-leks whirled on him in a flash, slashing his shoulder three inches down to the bone. Buck never got close to that blind side again, and the two never had another run-in. The only thing both Dave and Sol-leks cared about, as Buck would soon learn, was being left alone to do their work.

That night, Buck faced his first big, miserable problem: where to sleep. The team’s tent glowed warm and golden, lit by a candle, buried deep in the snow. He wandered in, as he would have done back at the ranch, and Perrault and François immediately pelted him with curses and flying tin cooking pots until he scrambled yelping out into the freezing dark. A biting wind cut through his fur, stabbing like a knife into his fresh wounded shoulder. He flopped down on the snow to try to sleep, but the frost bit so deep he was shivering too hard to rest, and he got back up to wander. All the other tents were just as cold outside, and savage strange dogs charged at him out of the dark until he bristled his neck fur and snarled, and they left him alone.

Where were his team mates? He’d seen them all disappear a few minutes earlier. He wandered around the camp, confused, until the snow gave way under his front paws and he sank right through the surface. Something warm wriggled under his feet. He sprang back, snarling, ready to fight, until a tiny friendly yelp drifted up. He dug down, and found Billee curled up in a snug little hollow under the snow, his breath warming the small space. That was it. Buck laughed to himself, picked a perfect spot, and dug like crazy until he had a snow burrow just big enough for him to curl up in. His own body heat filled the tiny space in minutes, and he was asleep instantly, deep and heavy, even growling and kicking through bad dreams about Curly and the red sweater man.

He didn’t wake up until the whole camp was moving around him. It had snowed all night, and he was completely buried under a thick layer of white. A wild, ancient fear shot through him—the primal terror of the trap, inherited from thousands of generations of wild ancestors who had never known a peaceful, domesticated life. Every muscle in his body tensed, his hackles stood straight up, and he burst out of the snow in a cloud of flying white, landing on all four paws in the bright sun. He blinked, saw the camp spread out in front of him, and remembered everything. François whooped loud. “See? I told you! That Buck learns faster than any dog alive!” Perrault nodded, pleased—he was in a hurry to get his dispatches north, and a strong smart dog like Buck was worth his weight in gold.

Within an hour, they added three more huskies to the team, making nine total. They harnessed up, and were swinging up the Dyea Canyon trail before another 15 minutes had passed. The work was hard, but Buck didn’t hate it. He was shocked by how eager the whole team was to pull, how the excitement rippled through every dog in the traces. Even Dave and Sol-leks were transformed—all their passiveness fell away the second the harness went on. They were sharp, alert, furious if anything delayed the team’s progress. Pulling the sled was the whole point of their lives, the only thing they lived for. Buck had been placed right between Dave and Sol-leks on purpose, so the two veterans could teach him the ropes. They were tough, no nonsense, nipping him hard every time he messed up the traces or missed a command, and backed up by François’s whip, Buck learned fast. Once, when he tangled the traces and delayed their start, both Dave and Sol-leks flew at him and beat him until he yelped. After that, he never let the traces tangle again. By the end of the day, he had it down perfect, and the other dogs stopped nipping him almost entirely. François cracked his whip far less often, and Perrault even knelt down to lift Buck’s paws and check them for cuts, honored that he was holding up so well.

It was a brutal run: up the canyon, through Sheep Camp, past the timber line, across hundreds of feet of deep packed snow and sliding glaciers, over the forbidding Chilcoot Divide that marked the line between the coastal salt water and the frozen northern interior. They made great time across a chain of lakes filling the craters of long-dead volcanoes, and pulled into the massive camp at Lake Bennett late that night, where thousands of gold miners were chopping out boats to sail when the ice broke in spring. Buck dug his snow nest and slept so hard he didn’t move until the cold dark hours before dawn, when François dragged him back out to harness up again.

That first day they made 40 miles on packed trail, but for days after that, they were breaking fresh trail through untouched snow, working twice as hard to make half the speed. Perrault usually walked a mile ahead of the team, wearing big webbed snowshoes to pack the trail flat for the dogs, and he knew the ice better than any man alive—critical knowledge, because the new fall ice was paper thin, and where the river ran fast, there was no ice at all, just a dark freezing death waiting under the snow.

Days blurred together into endless toil. They broke camp before the sun came up, and were still running the trail long after dark, stopping only to gobble their ration of raw fish before crawling back into their snow nests to sleep. Buck was ravenous. His daily ration of a pound and a half of sun-dried salmon vanished in two bites, and he was hungry every waking minute. The smaller native huskies only got a pound of fish a day, but they were used to it, and stayed fat and strong. He lost all his old fancy California table manners fast. When he paused to savor a bite of fish, the other dogs would finish their own rations and gang up to steal his. He learned to scarf his food as fast as they did, and when hunger gnawed at his stomach so hard his sides ached, he didn’t even feel guilty about stealing food that wasn’t his. He watched a clever lazy dog named Pike sneak a whole slice of bacon out of Perrault’s pack one day when his back was turned, and the next day Buck copied the trick, stealing the entire chunk before anyone noticed. A huge fuss broke out, but no one suspected Buck, and the clumsy, bumbling dog Dub got the beating for it.

That first theft was proof Buck was fit to survive the brutal Northland. It showed he could adapt, that he could ditch the soft civilized rules that would have gotten him killed. Back south, under the law of love and kindness, respecting other people’s property and feelings was right and good. But up north, under the law of club and fang, anyone who tried to live by those soft rules was a fool, and they died fast.

Buck didn’t stop to think it all through. His body just adjusted, his instincts taking over. The man in the red sweater had already beaten the basic code of survival into him, and now he learned fast that running from a losing fight to save his own skin was not cowardice—it was smart. His muscles turned hard as iron, and he stopped feeling ordinary pain. He could eat anything, no matter how disgusting or tough, and his stomach would squeeze every last drop of nutrition out of it. His eyes got sharper, his nose could pick up a scent an hour old, and his hearing was so good he could hear the faintest creak of ice while he was asleep, and tell instantly if it meant danger or peace. He bit sharp ice chunks out from between his toes when they froze, and learned to rear up and smash a thin sheet of ice over a water hole with his front paws to get a drink. His strangest new trick was scenting the wind a whole night in advance—no matter how still the air was when he dug his snow nest, when the wind picked up later, he was always sheltered on the leeward side, warm and safe.

Long dead, half-forgotten instincts woke up inside him, rising up out of thousands of generations of domesticated life. He could almost remember the time before dogs lived with men, when wild wolf packs roamed the primeval forests hunting their prey down. He didn’t have to learn to fight with the fast, slash-and-leap wolf style—those tricks were already in his blood. On quiet, freezing nights, he would lift his nose up to the bright northern stars and howl long, high, and wolfish, and the sound coming out of him was not his alone. It was the howl of every wild ancestor who had run the cold dark North before him, rising up through him to sing their ancient song. He had come home to himself, all because men had found gold in the Yukon, and Manuel the gardener’s helper had needed extra money to feed his wife and his many little children.

Deep in Buck’s bones, the wild old beast stirred and swelled, growing bigger and sharper with every brutal mile of the frozen trail. It grew quiet, unrushed—Buck’s newfound cunning had gifted him a steady poise, a tight control over every flash of temper. He was too busy fighting to adapt to this harsh new world to pick unnecessary fights, going out of his way to avoid clashes whenever possible. Every choice he made was deliberate, no rash lunges, no stupid gambles. Even the seething, bitter hatred he carried for Spitz, the lead husky, never made him snap early. He never struck first, never gave the red-eyed brute an excuse to start a fight that would only end when one of them lay dead.

Spitz, though, had sensed the dangerous rival lurking under Buck’s careful calm from the start. He never passed up a chance to snap at Buck’s heels, to bully him out of his rations, to prod and provoke until he got the death match he craved. They would have torn each other apart early in the trip, if not for a brutal, unforeseen accident. On the shores of Lake Le Barge, they made camp in a hellscape of wind that cut like white-hot knives, stinging driving snow, and pitch black darkness that forced the men to grope blindly for any patch of flat ground. At their backs loomed a sheer, unclimbable rock face, and Perrault and François had no choice but to build their fire and lay their sleeping robes right on top of the frozen lake. They’d dumped their tent back at Dyea to lighten their load, and the tiny stack of driftwood they scavenged burned right through the thin top layer of ice, leaving them huddled in the dark to eat their supper.

Buck carved out his nest right against the shelter of the rock, snug and warm enough that he hated to leave it to go collect his thawed fish ration from François. But when he came back, ready to curl up, a low, warning snarl told him Spitz had stolen his bed. Buck had spent weeks avoiding fights with his enemy—but this was too much. The beast inside him roared. He launched himself at Spitz in a red, blind fury that shocked both of them, and hit Spitz hardest: all his prior experience had led him to write Buck off as a soft, timid Southland dog who only held his own because of his size, not his teeth.

The two tangled fur and snapping teeth went rolling out of the disrupted nest, and François laughed sharp when he realized what had happened. “A-a-ah!” he yelled at Buck, “Give it to him, by God! Give it to that dirty thief!”

Spitz was just as hungry for the fight, circling back and forth snarling for an opening, and Buck matched him, circling just as fast, just as cautious, hunting for an advantage. That was when the world blew up.

A curse from Perrault, the heavy thud of a club cracking against bone, and a high, sharp yelp of pain signaled the start of total chaos. The camp was crawling with skulking, starved huskies—almost a hundred of them, who’d scented the food from a nearby native village and crept in while Buck and Spitz fought. When Perrault and François charged at them with their clubs, they bared their fangs and fought right back. They were half-mad with hunger, drunk on the smell of bread and bacon. Perrault bashed one over the head that was half-buried in the grub box, sending the whole crate spilling across the ice. A swarm of famished dogs scrambled for the crumbs, ignoring the clubs raining down on their backs, yelping and howling as they fought until the last crumb was gone.

The team sled dogs, spooked out of their nests, got swarmed the second they stepped outside. Buck had never seen dogs like these: raw skeletons draped in matted, tattered fur, eyes blazing, fangs slavering, their hunger making them unstoppable. The team got swept back against the cliff in seconds. Three wild huskies set on Buck, tearing gashes in his head and shoulders before he could twist free. Billee cried and cowered, Dave and Sol-leks fought back side by side bleeding from a dozen cuts, Joe snapped like a demon crunching straight through a wild husky’s foreleg, and Pike the shirker leaped on the crippled dog and snapped its neck in one quick jerk. Buck locked his jaws on a frothing attacker’s throat, warm blood spraying over his face as he bit through its jugular, the iron taste on his tongue making him fight harder. He lunged for a second wild dog, and that’s when he felt teeth sink into his own throat: Spitz, stabbing him in the back, treacherous as a snake.

Perrault and François fought their way back from the grub pile to save their team, driving the wild huskies back for a minute—until they had to turn and run to stop the rest of their food from being stolen. The second they left, the wild pack swarmed the sled dogs again. Billee snapped and fled through the circle, sprinting out over the open ice, with Pike and Dub and the rest of the team right on his heels. Buck tensed to spring after them, and caught Spitz rushing at him out of the corner of his eye, clearly planning to knock him off his feet so the wild dogs could tear him apart. Buck braced hard for the impact, shrugged off Spitz’s charge, and bolted after the rest of the team out onto the frozen lake.

Hours later, all nine sled dogs huddled together for shelter in the trees. The wild huskies were gone, but the team was a mess. Every single one of them had at least four or five bleeding cuts, some grievous. Dub had a horrible gash in his hind leg, Dolly— the new husky they’d added at Dyea—had a throat torn almost open, Joe had lost an eye, and the gentle Billee had an ear chewed to ribbons, whimpering nonstop all night. When dawn broke, they limped back to camp to find the wild marauders gone, and Perrault and François furious. Half their grub supply was gone. The huskies had chewed through sled lashings, canvas covers, Perrault’s moose-hide moccasins, chunks out of the leather traces, even two feet off the end of François’s whip. The men spent two hours swearing and patching everything back together, before the stiff, wounded team hit the hardest stretch of trail they’d face all the way to Dawson: the Thirty Mile River.

The river was wide open, its churning waters defying the frost, with thin, crackling ice only holding in the slow eddies. Six days of brutal, backbreaking work got them thirty terrible miles, every single step risking drowning. Perrault broke through the fragile ice bridges a dozen times, saved only by the long pole he carried slung across his chest that caught the edges of the hole before he could sink under. The temperature hung at 50 below zero, and every time he fell through he had to stop and build a fire just to dry his frozen clothes and survive.

He was unstoppable, that was why the government had picked him as their courier. He pushed his gaunt, frostbitten face right into the gale, driving from dim dawn to full dark. Once, the whole sled broke through the ice with Dave and Buck tangled in the traces, and they were half frozen, half drowned by the time the men hauled them out. They coated them solid in ice, so François and Perrault made them run in circles right next to the fire, close enough that the flames singed their fur, until the ice melted off their coats. Another time Spitz fell through the ice, dragging the whole team halfway in behind him, Buck straining every muscle backward with his front paws hooked on the slippery ice edge, Dave straining right behind him, François heaving on the sled straps until his tendons ached. At one point the ice broke away both in front and behind them, and they only escaped by scrambling straight up a sheer cliff face, hoisting the dogs, the sled, and all their supplies up one by one with cut up harnesses as makeshift rope. Night fell before they made it back down to the river, with only a quarter mile of progress to show for the whole day.

By the time they hit the Hootalinqua and solid, packed ice, Buck was completely worn out, and the rest of the team was in the same shape. But Perrault was determined to make up lost time, pushing them from dawn to dusk. They covered 35 miles to Big Salmon the first day, 35 more to Little Salmon the next, and 40 miles the third, bringing them right up to the Five Fingers. Buck’s feet weren’t hard and compact like the native huskies’—generations of domesticated soft living had softened them, and he limped in agony every step. Once they made camp, he collapsed like he was dead, too tired even to get up for his fish ration, so François brought it right to him. Every night after supper the driver rubbed Buck’s sore feet for half an hour, and even cut the tops off his own moccasins to make four soft little booties for Buck. The trick worked so well that one morning François forgot the moccasins, and Buck flopped onto his back waving all four paws in the air, refusing to stand up until he got them, making even gruff Perrault crack a grin. By the time they reached Pelly, Buck’s feet had hardened to the trail, and he threw the worn little booties away.

The next morning, as they were harnessing up, Dolly—who’d never shown a single unusual streak before—suddenly went mad. She let out a long, heartbreaking wolf howl that made every dog on the team bristle in terror, then charged straight for Buck. He’d never seen a mad dog before, but he knew this was pure horror, and he ran for his life. He bolted through the trees, across three separate islands, Dolly frothing and snarling exactly one leap behind him, too crazed to stop, too fast to shake. He doubled back toward camp when he heard François yell his name, gasping for air, and the driver swung his axe right as Buck darted past, splitting mad Dolly’s skull in one clean blow.

Buck staggered against the sled, completely exhausted, too winded to move. That was Spitz’s chance. He lunged at Buck, sinking his fangs twice deep into his unresisting, limp flesh, tearing the skin right down to the bone. Before he could strike a third time, François’s whip cracked down, and Buck got to watch Spitz take the worst beating any dog on the team had ever gotten.

“One devil, that Spitz,” Perrault said, shaking his head. “One day soon he’ll kill that Buck.”

“That Buck is two devils,” François shot back. “I watch him all the time, I know for sure. One day soon he’ll blow up with rage, tear Spitz all to pieces, and spit him out on the snow. You wait.”

From that day on, it was open war between them. Spitz, the established lead dog, could feel his absolute grip on the team slipping, and he knew Buck was the threat. None of the soft Southland dogs he’d ever met before had lasted on the trail, but Buck was different. He endured, he thrived, matching the wild huskies in strength, savagery, and sharp, careful cunning. The club of the man in the red sweater had beaten all stupid, reckless courage out of him, leaving only cold, patient, primitive cunning, the kind that waits for the perfect moment to strike.

Buck wanted the lead. It was in his bones, that fierce, unnameable pride of the trail—the pride that makes dogs pull until their hearts burst in the harness, that makes them fall apart if they’re ever cut loose from their work. That was the pride that drove Dave, that drove Sol-leks, and it was driving Buck to steal Spitz’s spot.

He started openly undermining Spitz at every turn. He’d step right between Spitz and any dog that Spitz tried to punish for shirking. One night a foot of snow fell, and the lazy shirker Pike hid himself deep under his nest, not showing up at harness time. François called for him, dug for him, and couldn’t find him, and Spitz raged through camp snarling, digging in every snow drift until Pike trembled in his hiding spot. The second Spitz dragged him out and lunged to punish him, Buck flew right between them, ramming Spitz so hard he went flying backward off his feet. Pike took heart, and leaped on his toppled leader, and Buck joined him, no rules of fair play left in him. François laughed, then brought his whip cracking down on Buck, hard, until he knocked him back off Spitz, and Spitz got to punish Pike as planned.

But the mutiny didn’t stop. The closer they got to Dawson, the more Buck interfered, careful to do it when François’s back was turned. The whole team started disobeying. Bicker fights broke out nonstop, and François spent every day tense, waiting for the inevitable death match between Buck and Spitz to blow up.

But the big fight never came before they pulled into Dawson one gray, dreary afternoon. The town was full of men, and hundreds of working dogs, hauling logs and mine supplies up the trails, howling their eerie wild wolf songs every night at nine, twelve, and three a.m. Buck joined in the weird, keening chant, and it woke old, old memories in him—memories of his wild, wolf ancestors running through frozen dark forests, long before any man had ever tamed a dog, long before houses or fires existed.

Seven days after they arrived in Dawson, they harnessed back up to make the record run back to Dyea, carrying even more urgent dispatches. The team was rested, the trail was packed hard from other travelers, and the police had stashed food caches along the route so they could travel light. They ran 50 miles to Sixty Mile the first day, and were flying up the Yukon the second, but the team was completely falling apart. Discipline was gone. The old fear of Spitz was gone. Even gentle Billee stopped being placating. Pike stole half a fish from Spitz one night right under Buck’s protection. Dub and Joe ganged up on Spitz and made him back down instead of punishing them. Buck never walked past Spitz without snarling, bristling, swaggering right under his nose like he already owned the lead spot. François swore, tore his hair, lashed the dogs until his arm ached, and nothing fixed it—every time he turned his back, the dogs were fighting again.

One night at the mouth of the Tahkeena, after supper, Dub flushed a snowshoe rabbit, fumbled the catch, and missed. The whole team gave chase, and fifty more huskies from a nearby Northwest Police camp joined the hunt. Buck led the whole pack of sixty dogs, low to the ground, leaping under the pale moonlight, every muscle singing with the ancient joy of the hunt, the lust to run down living meat and tear it apart with his own teeth. That was when Spitz, cold and calculating even in the middle of the chase, cut across a narrow neck of land where the creek looped far around. Buck didn’t see him, rounding the bend with the rabbit still darting ahead—until a bigger pale shadow leaped off the bank right in the rabbit’s path. It was Spitz. His jaws snapped the rabbit’s back clean in midair, and the screaming cry of the dying animal made the whole pack howl in wild, bloodthirsty delight.

“Eh? What’d I tell you? I spoke true when I said Buck’s not one devil, he’s two.”

That was François’s bark of a line first thing the next morning, when he stumbled out of his furs to find Spitz vanished entirely, and Buck crisscrossed with raw, gaping wounds across his pelt. He hauled Buck straight to the campfire, the dancing flames gilding every tear and cut on the dog’s body.
“That Spitz fought like a cornered wolverine,” Perrault agreed, prodding at one of the rips with a gloved finger.
“An’ dat Buck fought like two of ’em,” François shot back, grinning. “We’ll make great time now. No more Spitz, no more brawling mess, sure as snow’s cold.”

While Perrault packed up camp and lashed their overstuffed supplies to the sled, François moved to harness the team. Buck trotted straight to the lead spot—right where Spitz had claimed for months, the position he’d won fair and square in the bloodied snow. But François wasn’t paying attention. He grabbed Sol-leks, the tough one-eyed old husky, and nudged him into that coveted front position. He thought Sol-leks was the strongest remaining lead dog.

Buck exploded. He launched at Sol-leks in a red fury, drove the old dog back with a snarl that rumbled deep in his chest, and planted his paws dead center in the lead traces.
“Eh? Look at dat Buck!” François cackled, slapping his thighs so hard his gloves cracked. “He killed Spitz, now he thinks he gets the job!”
“Go on, git!” he yelled, shooing Buck away. Buck didn’t budge. François grabbed him by the scruff, and though Buck snarled at him with bared fangs, he dragged him back behind Dave and shoved Sol-leks back to the lead. Sol-leks didn’t fight it. He was already plainly terrified of what Buck’s jaws could do.

The second François turned his back, Buck shoved Sol-leks aside again. Sol-leks didn’t even pretend to resist. François’s temper flared.
“By God, I’ll fix you!” he roared, stomping off to grab a heavy, thick oak club.

Buck remembered the man in the red sweater. He didn’t lunge, didn’t run off into the woods. He retreated slowly, circling just out of range of the club, hackles raised, eyes locked on the wood so he could dodge the second it flew. When François called him to his old spot behind Dave, Buck stepped back two paces. François stepped forward. Buck stepped back. The driver thought he was scared of a beating—but that was not what Buck wanted at all. He was in open revolt. He didn’t care about avoiding a thrashing. He wanted the leadership. It was his by right, earned in a fight to the death. He would not settle for less.

Perrault jumped in to help. For the better part of an hour they chased Buck around camp, hurling clubs, cursing him, his parents, every generation of his ancestors, and every last hair in his pelt. Buck answered every curse with a low snarl, stayed just out of reach, and never once tried to run away. He was making it plain: give me the lead, and I’ll be good.

François scratched his head. Perrault checked his watch and swore. They were already an hour late to hit the trail. François shrugged, and the courier nodded to signal they were beaten. He walked over to unclip Sol-leks’s traces, then called Buck. Buck’s doggy, triumphant grin spread across his face, but he stayed out of range until Perrault ordered François to drop the club. The second the wood hit the snow, Buck trotted right over, swung around into the lead spot like he’d been born to stand there, and let them snap his traces into place. The sled lurched out onto the frozen river trail, both men running behind to get up speed.

François had already thought Buck was a miracle of a dog, but he realized before midday he’d still underestimated him. Buck took to leadership like he’d been doing it his whole life. Where quick judgment and split-second decisions were needed to navigate thin ice and hidden drifts, he outperformed even Spitz, a dog François had once thought was unbeatable. But where he truly excelled was laying down the law for the rest of the team.

Dave and Sol-leks didn’t care who was lead. Their only job was to pull till their paws bled, and as long as that work wasn’t interfered with, they were happy. But the rest of the team had grown lazy and unruly in Spitz’s last fading days, and Buck wasted no time whipping them back into shape. Pike, the loafer who never pulled an ounce more than he absolutely had to, was shaken by the scruff over and over until by the end of that first day he was pulling harder than he ever had in his whole life. Joe, the sour, snappy dog Spitz had never managed to break, was pinned under Buck’s superior weight, nipped repeatedly, until he stopped snarling and whined for mercy. The team snapped back into perfect solidarity instantly, leaping through the traces as one single animal.

When they picked up two wild native huskies named Teek and Koona at Rink Rapids, Buck broke them in to team order so fast François could barely believe his eyes. “Never no dog like Buck!” he yelled to Perrault. “Never! He’s worth a thousand dollars, by God!”

Perrault nodded. They were shattering records, day after day. The trail was packed hard and smooth, no new snow to fight, and the temperature held steady at 50 below zero. The men rode and ran by turn, the dogs kept at a steady lope, with barely any stops. They crossed the Thirty Mile River in one day—a route that had taken them ten days to trek when they’d first arrived. They ran a 60-mile straight dash from the foot of Lake Le Barge to White Horse Rapids, and flew across 70 miles of frozen lakes so fast the man whose turn it was to run ahead ended up towed behind the sled on a rope. On the second week’s final night, they crested White Pass and looked down to see the glowing lights of Skaguay and its anchored ships spread out below them.

It was a record run: 40 miles a day, for fourteen days straight. For three days Perrault and François hauled their supplies up and down Skaguay’s main street, drowning in invitations to drinks, while the team drew crowds of adoring dog mushers and gold seekers. Then new official orders arrived. François called Buck over, threw his arms around him, and wept. That was the last Buck ever saw of Perrault and François. The two men vanished from his life for good.

A gruff Scotch half-breed took charge of Buck and his team, and joined up with a dozen other dog sleds to head back over the long, weary trail to Dawson. No more record runs now. This was the mail train, hauling thousands of pounds of letters out to the men hunting gold under the shadow of the Arctic Pole. It was slow, brutal, heavy toil every single day, no shortcuts, no easy patches.

Buck didn’t love the work, but he bore it well, taking pride in the toil the same way Dave and Sol-leks did, and cracking down hard to make sure every one of his teammates pulled their fair share. Life ran like a machine: wake an hour before dawn, build the fires, eat breakfast, harness the dogs, and hit the trail before the first sliver of sun peeked over the horizon. At night they’d make camp, chop wood, lay pine boughs for beds, and feed the dogs first before the men even thought about their own dinner. There were 60 other sled dogs total, plenty of fierce brawlers, but three quick fights with the meanest of them landed Buck as undisputed top dog, and no one dared challenge him again.

His favorite time was after dinner, curled up by the fire, hind legs tucked under him, front legs stretched out, blinking dreamily at the flames. Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller’s big sunlit house in Santa Clara, the cement swimming pool, the silly little Mexican hairless and Japanese pug that used to follow him around the yard. But those memories were faint, far away, no hold on him at all. Far more real were the ancient memories buried deep in his bones, the instincts passed down from a hundred generations of wild ancestors. Sometimes, staring at the flames, he swore he was looking at a different fire, in front of a different man: short-legged, long-armed, knotted stringy muscles, matted long hair, clutching a stone-tipped club, crouched low in perpetual fear of the dark. He could hear the crash of large predators moving through the trees beyond the firelight, see their two-by-two glowing eyes, and the hair would stand straight up along his spine until he whimpered softly, half-asleep, and the half-breed cook would yell, “Hey Buck, wake up!” The vision would vanish, and he’d stretch and yawn, as if he’d only been napping.

The trip was brutal. They’d traveled 1800 miles that winter, dragging sleds the whole way, and even the toughest dogs wore down to skin and bone. When they stumbled into Dawson, they were gaunt, sore, and in desperate need of a week’s rest. But two days later they were loaded up with outgoing mail, heading back down the Yukon, and it snowed every single day. Soft new snow meant far more drag on the sled runners, twice the work for the already exhausted dogs. The drivers were fair: they tended to every dog’s sore feet before they even thought about taking off their own boots, fed the dogs before they ate their own meals. But the dogs’ strength drained anyway. Billee whimpered constantly in his sleep. Joe was sourer than he’d ever been. Sol-leks wouldn’t let anyone near his blind side.

But Dave suffered the worst. Something had gone irreparably wrong inside him. He grew sullen and irritable, crawled straight into his nest the second camp was pitched, and wouldn’t get up until harness time the next morning. Sometimes out on the trail, he’d cry out in agony when the sled jerked to a sudden stop, or when he strained to pull it out of a drift. All the drivers prodded and pressed him, but they couldn’t find any broken bones, couldn’t figure out what was wrong. By the time they reached Cassiar Bar, he was so weak he kept collapsing in the traces.

The Scotch half-breed called a halt, pulled Dave out of the team, and clipped Sol-leks into his old spot. He planned to let Dave run free behind the sled to rest. But Dave could not bear to be separated from his work. The pride of the traces was all he had, even if he was dying. He snarled and snapped when they unclipped his harness, whimpered broken-heartedly when he saw Sol-leks in the spot he’d served for years. When the sled started moving, he floundered through the soft deep snow beside the trail, biting at Sol-leks’s heels, shoving at him to force him out of the traces, crying with grief and pain. The half-breed cracked his whip at him, but Dave ignored the stinging lash, and the man didn’t have the heart to hit him hard. Dave ran himself until he collapsed in the snow, howling, then staggered back after the sled when it stopped, bit clean through both of Sol-leks’s traces, and planted himself dead in his old lead spot, eyes pleading.

The drivers talked it over. They’d all seen dogs die before when you took their work away from them, their hearts broken by the idleness. It was a mercy, they agreed, to let Dave die in the traces, doing the work he loved, happy and content. So they harnessed him back in. Dave pulled proudly, just like old times, even when the pain made him cry out involuntarily, even when he fell and got dragged a few steps behind the sled. They made camp that night, and by morning Dave was too weak to stand. He crawled slowly toward the harness line the dogs were lining up at, dragging himself inch by inch on his forelegs, until his strength gave out. The last the other dogs saw of him, he was lying in the snow gasping, yearning after them. They could hear his mournful howls long after they rounded a bend behind a stand of thick river pines.

The whole sled train halted. The Scotch half-breed walked slowly back toward the camp they’d just left. The men fell quiet. A single revolver shot rang out through the trees. The man came hurrying back, climbed onto his sled, and snapped the whip. The sleds rumbled forward, bells tinkling.

Buck knew. Every single dog on the team knew exactly what had happened behind those pines. No one said a word.

Thirty days out from Dawson, the Salt Water Mail team hauled themselves into Skaguay, Buck and his run-ragged mates at the lead, every one of them a broken shadow of their former selves. Buck’s solid 140 pounds had dwindled down to 115, and even the smaller dogs in the team had lost more of their body weight proportionally. Pike, the lifelong trickster who’d faked a dozen hurt legs to shirk work back in the day, was limping for real now. Sol-leks dragged one stiff hind leg, and Dub moved with a hunch, his wrenched shoulder blade throbbing with every step.

Every single one of them was footsore past the point of bearing. No spring, no leftover rebound lingered in their muscles. Their paws slammed heavy into the packed trail, jarring their bones all the way up their spines, doubling the fatigue of every mile they dragged. They weren’t just the temporary tired you get from a short, brutal burst of effort that wears off after a few hours’ sleep. This was the deep, bone-deep drain of 2500 miles of nonstop toil in less than five months, with only five total days of rest across the last 1800 of those miles. There was no reserve strength left to call on — they’d burned every last scrap of it. Every muscle, every fibre, every cell in their bodies was dead tired. They could barely keep the traces taut, and on the downhills they only just managed to stumble out of the way of the heavy sled sliding behind them.
“Mush on, you poor sore feet,” their Scots half-breed driver called to them as they tottered down Skaguay’s rutted main street. “This is the last stretch. Then we get a long rest, eh? A real good, long rest.”

The drivers were confident they’d earned a weeks-long break. They’d ridden 1200 miles with only two days’ rest themselves, and by all rights they deserved to laze around. But the Klondike gold rush had flooded the north with tens of thousands of men, all their sweethearts, wives, and family members waiting back home, and the backlog of mail had piled up to Alpine heights. Official orders came down immediately: the worn-out mail dogs would be replaced with fresh teams from the Hudson’s Bay stock, and the too-spent-to-trail animals would be sold off. Dogs were worth nothing compared to the dollars tied up in mail delivery.

Three days passed, and Buck and his mates had all the time in the world to feel just how weak and exhausted they truly were. On the fourth morning, two men fresh up from the States bought the entire team, harnesses and all, for a handful of loose change. They called each other Hal and Charles. Charles was middle-aged, fair-skinned, with weak, watery eyes and a bushy mustache that curled fiercely upward, lying straight to the world about the soft, drooping lip it hid. Hal was 19 or 20 years old, a kid with a big Colt revolver and a hunting knife strapped to a belt that bristled so full of extra cartridges it jingled when he moved. That belt was the most noticeable thing about him, a big bright billboard for his raw, untested callowness. Both men were obviously wildly out of place in the frozen north, the kind of greenhorn adventurer no Yukon old-timer could ever make sense of.

Buck heard them haggling, saw the cash pass between his old driver and the government agent, and knew the Scotsman, Perrault, François, and all the rest of his former caretakers were gone from his life forever. When they were herded over to their new owners’ camp, Buck stared at the mess: a half-stretched lopsided tent, piles of unwashed tin dishes, nothing put away where it belonged. And there was a woman with them, the men called her Mercedes. She was Charles’s wife, and Hal’s sister — one very ill-prepared little family party.

Buck watched them with growing dread as they tried to take down the tent and load their sled. They moved with endless useless effort, no sense of practical order at all. They rolled the tent up into an awkward lumpy bundle three times bigger than it ever needed to be, and stuffed all their dishes away still crusted with leftover food. Mercedes fluttered around them nonstop, chattering nonstop with advice and complaints. When they strapped a clothes sack to the front of the sled, she insisted it go on the back. When they moved it to the back and stacked other bundles on top, she remembered a dozen small items that just had to go in that exact sack, and made them unload the whole thing all over again.

Three old-timers from a neighboring tent wandered out to watch, grinning and winking at each other.
“Your load is already way too heavy, and it’s not my place to meddle in your business,” one of them said, “but I wouldn’t drag that tent along if I was you.”
“Unthinkable!” Mercedes gasped, throwing up her hands in dainty dismay. “How on earth am I supposed to survive without a tent?”
“It’s spring now, you won’t get any more freezing weather,” the man replied.

She shook her head firmly, and Charles and Hal heaped every last stray odd and end on top of the already mountainous load.
“Think that’ll ride okay?” the man asked.
“Why wouldn’t it?” Charles snapped, short and defensive.
“Oh, right, it’ll be fine,” the man backpedaled meekly. “I was just wondering, is all. Looks a little top-heavy, that’s all.”

Charles turned his back and pulled the lashings as tight as he could — which was not tight at all.
“Course the dogs can haul that contraption all day,” a second onlooker put in.
“Absolutely,” Hal said, his voice sharp with frozen politeness. He grabbed the steering gee-pole in one hand and swung his whip high with the other. “Mush!” he yelled. “Mush on, you lot!”

The dogs threw themselves against their breast-bands, strained for a few heaving seconds, then went slack. They couldn’t move the sled an inch.
“Lazy brutes, I’ll show you,” Hal snarled, lifting the whip to lash down.
Mercedes screamed and ran to yank the whip right out of his hand. “Oh Hal, don’t you dare! The poor dears! You have to promise not to be harsh to them for the rest of the trip, or I won’t take another single step.”
“You don’t know a thing about dogs,” Hal sneered. “Leave me alone, will you? They are lazy, that’s how sled dogs are, you have to whip them to get any work out. Ask anyone, ask those men over there.”

Mercedes looked at the onlookers pleadingly, pure revulsion at the thought of the dogs being hurt written all over her pretty face.
“They’re weak as water, if you really want the truth,” one man called back. “They’re plumb tuckered out. All they need is rest.”
“Rest be damned,” Hal said under his breath, and Mercedes gasped, hurt at the curse.

But she was fiercely loyal to her family, and jumped immediately to Hal’s defence. “Don’t listen to that man,” she said pointedly. “You’re the one driving our dogs, do what you think is best.”

Hal’s whip came down on the dogs again. They threw themselves against the traces, dug their paws deep into the packed snow, hunkered their whole bodies low, and pulled with every last scrap of strength they had. The sled didn’t move an inch, stuck fast as an anchor. After two tries they collapsed panting, standing still, and Hal’s whip whistled down savagely — until Mercedes ran over again, dropped on her knees right in front of Buck, tears in her eyes, and wrapped both arms around his neck.
“You poor poor dears,” she cooed, “why don’t you pull harder? Then you wouldn’t get hit.” Buck didn’t like her, but he was too miserable to fight her, and just accepted the silly fuss as one more annoying thing in the long day of toil.

One onlooker, who’d been clenching his teeth to hold back sharp words, finally spoke up. “I don’t care a bit what happens to you people, but for the dogs’ sakes I’ll tell you: you can help them a lot if you break the runners free first. They’re frozen solid to the snow. Throw your weight against the gee-pole, rock it side to side, and work it loose.”

They tried a third time, and this time Hal followed the advice, rocking the sled till the ice cracked loose from the frozen runners. The overloaded, wobbly sled finally lurched forward, Buck and his team mates scrambling wildly under a rain of blows. A hundred yards ahead, the trail curved steeply downhill toward Skaguay’s main street. It took an experienced driver to keep that top-heavy load upright, and Hal was nothing of the sort. They swung around the turn, the sled tipped all the way over, and half their stuff spilled right out of the loose lashings into the snow.

The dogs never stopped. They were furious at the unfair beatings and the absurd load, and Buck snapped into a full run, the whole team following his lead. Hal screamed “Whoa! Whoa!” but they didn’t listen. He tripped, got dragged right off his feet, the capsized sled scraped over his legs, and the dogs bolted up the main street, scattering the rest of the family’s belongings all over the road to the glee of every laughing citizen in town.

Kind locals caught the runaways, gathered up all their scattered stuff, and gave them plain advice: if they ever wanted to make Dawson, they needed to cut their load in half and add twice as many dogs. Hal, Charles and Mercedes sulked, pitched their lopsided tent, and started going through their gear. They turned up piles of canned goods that made the old-timers cackle — no one hauled heavy canned food on the 3000 mile Long Trail, that was the stuff of daydreams. “You’ve got enough blankets for a whole hotel,” one helper laughed. “Half that would still be too many. Throw the tent out, throw all those dishes away — who’s going to wash them, anyway? Good Lord, do you think you’re travelling on a fancy Pullman train?”

The trimming down was brutal. Mercedes wailed when her clothes bags got dumped on the snow, crying over every single item someone tossed out. She rocked back and forth on the ground clutching her knees, sobbing, swearing she wouldn’t go an inch further, not even for a dozen Charleses. She begged every bystander for help, then finally wiped her eyes and started throwing out even her own essential warm clothes to lighten the load. Once she was done with her own bags, she tore through Hal and Charles’s belongings like a tornado, tossing out half their spare gear too.

Even after all that, the load was still far too heavy. That evening, Hal and Charles bought six extra Outside dogs from the waterfront. Adding those six to the six remaining original team members plus the two huskies they’d picked up at Rink Rapids, the team came to 14 dogs total. But the new Outside dogs weren’t much use. Three were short-haired pointers, one was a Newfoundland, the other two were random stray mongrels. None of them knew the first thing about sled work. Buck and his old team mates looked at them in disgust. Buck quickly taught them their places in the traces, but he couldn’t teach them how to pull. The pointers and Newfoundland were completely bewildered by the harsh wild north, and their already bad treatment had broken their spirits. The mongrels had no spirit left at all — you could barely break anything on them but their bones.

With the new dogs useless, and the old team worn paper-thin from 2500 miles of nonstop travel, their prospects were terrible. But the two men were cheerful, even proud. They thought they were doing the trail in high style, 14 dogs on one sled, something no one else had ever seen heading over the pass to Dawson. What they didn’t know was that there was a very good reason no one ever did that: no single sled could ever carry enough food to feed 14 dogs for the full trip. But Hal and Charles had never thought about that. They’d scribbled the whole trip out with a pencil on a scrap of paper, so much food per dog, so many days of travel, simple as that. Mercedes peered over their shoulders and nodded, like it all made perfect sense.

Late the next morning, Buck led the huge ragtag team up Skaguay’s main street. No one had any spring left in them. Buck had already made the run between the coast and Dawson four times before, and facing that same brutal trail again while he was this dead tired filled him with quiet bitterness. His heart wasn’t in the work, and none of the other dogs cared either. The new outsiders were scared and confused, and the old huskies had no faith at all in their bumbling new masters.

Buck soon realized they couldn’t count on Hal, Charles or Mercedes for anything. The three of them had no idea how to do even the simplest tasks, and as the days dragged on it became obvious they would never learn. They were lazy, unorganized, had zero discipline. It took them half the night to pitch a messy, half-done camp, then half the morning to take it down and load the sled so badly they had to stop and rearrange the load half a dozen times for the rest of the day. Some days they didn’t make 10 whole miles. Some days they couldn’t even get the sled moving at all. They never once hit half the daily mileage they’d written down to calculate their dog food rations.

It was inevitable they’d run out of food fast, but they made it worse by overfeeding the dogs at first, racing toward the day when the food would run completely out. The outsiders, their stomachs never trained to stretch every last scrap of nutrition out of tiny rations like the local huskies, were ravenous. When Hal saw the old huskies pulling weakly, he decided the standard ration was too small, and doubled it. When Mercedes couldn’t cajole him into giving the dogs even more food, she snuck into the fish sacks at night and fed them extra scraps behind his back. But what Buck and the old huskies needed wasn’t more fish — it was rest. They were making such slow time, and the heavy load was still draining every last scrap of strength they had left.

Then the underfeeding started. Hal woke up one morning and realized their dog food was already half gone, and they’d barely covered a quarter of the distance to Dawson. Worse, there was no extra dog food to be bought anywhere, no matter how much money they offered. He cut the rations down even further, and tried to force the team to travel longer hours. Charles and Mercedes backed him up, but their overloaded sled and their own incompetence made it impossible. It was easy to cut the dogs’ food, but impossible to make tired starving dogs run any faster, and they never woke up early enough to get extra miles in anyway. Not only did they not know how to drive dogs, they didn’t know how to work themselves.

The first dog to die was Dub. The clumsy old thief who always got caught stealing and got punished for it, who’d still been a faithful worker, had his untreated, unrested wrenched shoulder blade fester till Hal shot him dead with his Colt revolver. The old Yukon saying went that an Outside dog would starve to death on the full ration meant for a tough husky, so there was no hope for the six new dogs surviving on half a ration. The Newfoundland died first, then the three pointers. The two mongrels hung on for a little while longer, but they died too.

By this point, every last trace of soft civility the three of them had brought up from the south was gone. Stripped of all the romance and glamour they’d read about, Arctic travel turned into a harsh, brutal reality none of them could handle. Mercedes stopped crying over the dogs, she was too busy crying over herself, and bickering nonstop with her husband and brother. Quarrelling was the only thing they had the energy for, no matter how tired they were. Their irritability grew with their misery, doubling and redoubling till it swallowed them whole. The quiet, hard-won patience of the trail that lets tough old travelers stay kind and gentle no matter how sore they are never found these three. Their muscles ached, their bones ached, their very hearts ached, and every morning their first words were sharp, every night their last words were angry.

Charles and Hal fought every time Mercedes gave them half a chance. Both men were convinced they did way more than their fair share of the work, and neither of them ever missed a chance to yell about it. Sometimes Mercedes took Charles’s side, sometimes Hal’s. The result was an endless, vicious family fight. They’d start bickering about who should chop a few sticks of firewood, and end up screaming about distant relatives, dead uncles thousands of miles away, Hal’s stupid opinions on art, the trashy plays his uncle wrote, Charles’s dumb political ideas, Mercedes’s annoying sister — none of it had a single thing to do with chopping firewood, but the fight would spiral on for hours. All the while the fire stayed unlit, the camp stayed half pitched, and the dogs stayed unfed.

Mercedes nursed a special grudge all her own. She’d been pretty and soft her whole life, men had always treated her with chivalry back home. But Hal and Charles were so busy surviving up north they had no time to wait on her hand and foot, and she threw a full-scale fit over it. She was used to being helpless, and when they complained about her not pulling her weight, she made their lives absolute hell. She stopped caring about the dogs entirely, and because she was sore and tired she decided to ride on top of the sled. She was pretty and soft, but she weighed 120 pounds — the last crushing straw on top of the load the starving, weak dogs were dragging. She rode for days, till the dogs collapsed in their traces and the sled couldn’t move an inch. Charles and Hal begged her to get off and walk, pleaded with her, and she wept, screaming that they were cruel brutes who were abusing her.

Back the previous December, John Thornton had frozen his feet mid-trail. His travel partners got him settled comfortably at a river-side camp to rest and heal, then headed further up the Yukon to drag a raft of sawn logs down to Dawson. When Thornton first pulled Buck from the brutal traces that had nearly killed him, he still limped faintly, but as the long, sun-warmed spring days dragged on, even that last small limp vanished.

Through those soft golden weeks, Buck stretched out along the riverbank hour after hour, watching the silver current slide past, half-listening to trilling birds and the low hum of insects in the undergrowth, slowly regaining every ounce of his lost strength. A three-thousand mile trail grind leaves no dog unbroken, and Buck grew gloriously lazy as his old wounds knit shut, his muscles swelled thick and hard, and flesh filled back in over his sharp, starved bones. No one hurried them: Buck, Thornton, and the two camp dogs Skeet and Nig, all lazed the days away while waiting for the raft that would carry the whole group downriver to Dawson.

Skeet was a tiny Irish setter that had befriended Buck the moment he was too weak to snap at her pestering. She had the gentle, nursing streak some dogs carry deep in their bones, and tended to his cuts and scrapes as carefully as a mother cat licking her kittens clean. Every morning right after Buck finished his breakfast, she’d show up to perform her self-appointed task, until he began to seek out her soft licks as eagerly as he sought Thornton’s company. Nig, big and quiet and no less friendly, was half bloodhound, half deerhound, with fur as black as pitch and eyes that crinkled like he was laughing at some private joke, good-natured down to his bones.

To Buck’s shock, neither of the two dogs ever showed a flicker of jealousy over the new addition. They shared Thornton’s big, unshakable kindness, and as Buck grew strong enough to run and play, they dragged him into all sorts of silly, rough games, games that Thornton himself could never resist joining in on. It was in these romps that Buck finished his convalescence, and stepped into a life he had never dared to imagine: for the first time, he knew real, burning, all-consuming love.

He had never felt anything like it back at Judge Miller’s sun-dappled Santa Clara estate. With the Judge’s sons, his bond had been a working partnership for hunting and long tramps; with the Judge’s grandkids, a stiff, stately guardianship of their silly games; with the Judge himself, a formal, dignified respect. But this feverish, adoring, almost mad devotion? That was something only John Thornton had woken in him.

Thornton had saved his life, that much was true, but more than that, he was the perfect master. Other men Buck had worked for cared for their dogs out of duty, or out of business sense, to keep their profit-making teams running. Thornton cared for his dogs like they were his own children, because he couldn’t do anything else. He never missed a chance to say a kind word, never passed up the chance to sit and ramble with them for hours—he called it “gas”, and the long quiet talks were just as much a joy to him as they were to the dogs. He had a habit of grabbing Buck’s big shaggy head roughly between his two calloused hands, pressing his own forehead to Buck’s, shaking him back and forth hard while growling silly, gruff insults that to Buck were the sweetest endearments on earth. There was no greater joy for Buck than that rough, warm embrace, the sound of that low murmur of what he knew to be love, and every time Thornton shook him, his heart swelled so big he thought it might burst right out of his chest. When Thornton finally let go, Buck would leap to his feet, mouth stretched wide in a doggy grin, eyes bright with unspoken feeling, throat thrumming with sound he couldn’t quite voice, and Thornton would stare at him in quiet wonder and whisper, “God, you can all but speak.”

Buck had his own funny way of showing love, one that looked almost like a fight. He’d clamp his teeth gently around Thornton’s hand and squeeze hard enough to leave clear, deep tooth prints in the skin that lingered for hours afterward. And just as Buck knew Thornton’s gruff insults were words of love, Thornton knew that fake hard bite was no more than a sloppy, warm caress.

Most of the time, though, Buck’s love showed through quiet adoration. He went wild with joy every time Thornton touched him or spoke to him, but he never begged for attention. Unlike Skeet, who would shove her nose under Thornton’s hand and nudge and nudge till he petted her, or Nig, who would drag his huge body over and rest his head heavy on Thornton’s knee, Buck was happy just to adore him from a little distance. He’d lie at Thornton’s feet for hours, ears pricked, eyes never leaving his face, drinking in every flicker of expression, every tiny twitch of his mouth, every shift of his gaze. Other days he’d stretch out a little further away, off to the side or behind Thornton, watching the outline of his shoulders, the small, casual movements of his body. So deep was the quiet understanding between them that often Buck’s steady, loving gaze would make Thornton turn his head on instinct, and the two would stare at each other without a single word, their hearts shining out of their eyes to meet, no speech needed at all.

For weeks after Thornton had rescued him, Buck refused to let him out of his sight for a second. From the moment Thornton stepped out of the tent in the morning till the moment he ducked back inside at night, Buck trotted right at his heels. All the temporary masters he’d had since he came to the Northland had left him with a gnawing, constant fear that no master would ever stay forever. He was terrified Thornton would vanish out of his life the same way Perrault, François, and the scotch half-breed mail driver had, and that fear haunted him even in his sleep. He’d wake up shivering in the cold dark, creep soft as a shadow to the tent flap, and stand there listening, just to make sure he could hear Thornton’s steady, slow breathing.

But even through that all-consuming love for the man, even as the soft, gentle pull of civilization hummed in his bones, the old wild primitive streak the Northland had woken in him stayed alive, sharp, and bright. He had loyalty and devotion, the kind of soft bonds that only grow by a warm, man-made fire, but he never lost his wildness, his sharp, wolfish cunning. He was a creature that had wandered in from the cold wild to sit by Thornton’s campfire, not a soft, domesticated southland dog bred for generations to live in houses. He would never dream of stealing so much as a scrap of food from Thornton, but from any other man, in any other camp, he’d snatch what he wanted in a heartbeat, and his sharp cleverness would always keep him from getting caught.

His face and body were crisscrossed with old scars from a hundred dog fights, and he fought as fiercely as he ever had, but now far smarter. Skeet and Nig were too good-natured to ever pick a fight, and anyway they belonged to Thornton, so there was no bad blood between them—but any strange dog, no matter how big, how brave, how tough, either bowed immediately to Buck’s undisputed rule, or found themselves fighting for their life against a merciless, unbeatable foe. Buck showed no quarter. He had learned the unbreakable law of club and fang down to his bones, he never gave up an advantage, he never backed away from an enemy he’d already started chasing toward death. He had learned hard lessons from Spitz, from all the toughest lead dogs of the mail and police trails, and he knew there was no middle ground. You either mastered, or you were mastered. To show mercy was weakness. Mercy did not exist in the old primordial wild. Any sign of softness was misread as fear, and that mistake would get you killed. Kill or be killed, eat or be eaten, that was the ancient, unchanging law, passed down from the very first wolves, and Buck obeyed it without question.

He was older than his own years. He bridged the gap between the old wild past and the sharp bright present, and the endless ages of wild ancestors behind him thrummed through his veins in the same steady rhythm that moves the tides and the seasons. He sat by Thornton’s fire, broad chested, long furred, white fangs bared in a happy grin—but behind him loomed the shadows of every kind of dog, half-wolf and full wild wolf, urging him on. They tasted the meat he ate, drank the water he drank, ran their noses over the wind right beside him, listened for the rustle of wild game in the forest, shared his moods, guided his moves, slept in his dreams and filled them with wild, ancient stories.

Those shadowy ancestors called to him so loudly that every day, the pull of human lives and human rules slipped a little further away. Deep in the dark woods, a wild, thrumming call was ringing out, and every time Buck heard that strange, luring cry, he felt an overwhelming urge to turn his back on the campfire, on the packed dirt around the tent, and plunge straight into the trees, running on and on with no idea where he was going, no thought to stop. But every single time he felt the soft unbroken forest earth under his paws, the green shadow wrapping around him, the fierce, burning love for John Thornton would yank him right back to the fire again.

Only Thornton held him. The rest of the human world was nothing. Strangers wandering through the camp might pat his head, praise his size and strength, but Buck stayed cold and distant under their touch, and if anyone got too clingy and demonstrative, he’d just stand up and walk away. When Thornton’s long-awaited partners Hans and Pete finally pulled up on their raft, Buck refused to acknowledge them at all until he’d watched them long enough to know they were Thornton’s closest friends. After that, he tolerated them, passive and calm, accepting their scraps and pets like he was doing them a favor by letting them get close. They were the same big, down-to-earth sort as Thornton, living close to the land, thinking simple and clear, and by the time they steered the raft into the big eddy outside the Dawson sawmill, they knew Buck and his quirks well enough not to push for the same casual affection they got from Skeet and Nig.

But Buck’s love for Thornton only grew bigger, deeper, by the day. He was the only human in the world who could strap a heavy pack to Buck’s back for long summer treks, and there was no task too hard, no burden too heavy, for Buck to carry if Thornton asked him to do it. One day, after the three men had sold their raft for supplies and headed out of Dawson for the remote headwaters of the Tanana river, the whole group stopped to rest on the crest of a cliff that dropped straight down three hundred feet to bare, jagged bedrock below. Thornton sat right near the edge, Buck at his shoulder. A silly, impulsive whim hit Thornton, and he waved Hans and Pete over to show them what he had in mind. “Jump, Buck!” he called, sweeping his arm out over the empty chasm. The next instant, Thornton was wrestling to drag Buck back from the very edge of the drop, while Hans and Pete lunged in to haul both of them to safety on solid ground.

“That’s uncanny,” Pete said once they’d all caught their breath, pale and shaking.

Thornton shook his head. “No. It’s magnificent. But it’s terrifying, too. You know? Sometimes it makes me afraid.”

“I’d never in a million years want to be the fool that lays a hand on you while that dog’s around,” Pete said firmly, nodding at Buck.

“By gosh,” Hans agreed. “Neither would I.”

By the end of that year, at Circle City, Pete’s prediction came true. A mean-tempered, malicious man named “Black” Burton was picking a brutal fight with a clueless new tenderfoot in the local saloon, when Thornton stepped in calmly to pull them apart. Buck, as he always did, was curled up in a corner, head on his paws, eyes never leaving his master. Burton reared back and punched Thornton full in the face without warning, sending him spinning across the room. Thornton only stopped himself from falling by clutching hard to the edge of the bar.

The whole crowd heard a sound that was neither bark nor yelp, a deep, roaring snarl, and watched Buck launch himself full force off the floor straight for Burton’s throat. Burton saved his own life by throwing his arm up in front of his face on instinct, but Buck hit him so hard he was hurled backward onto the floor, Buck on top of him. Buck ripped his teeth out of Burton’s bleeding arm, and drove straight for his throat again. This time Burton only got a partial block, and Buck’s fangs tore clean through the side of his neck. The whole crowd swarmed in to drag Buck off, and while the town surgeon worked to stop Burton’s bleeding, Buck prowled back and forth outside the saloon, growling furiously, trying to charge back in every few seconds, only to be driven back by a wall of clubs. An impromptu miners’ meeting called on the spot ruled that Buck had more than enough reason to attack, and he was let off without punishment. His reputation was made that day, and his name spread to every remote mining camp across all of Alaska.

Later that fall, Buck saved Thornton’s life a second time, in a far more dramatic way. The three partners were guiding a long, narrow poling boat down a stretch of deadly rapids on Forty Mile Creek. Hans and Pete walked along the bank, tying a thin Manila rope from tree to tree to slow the boat down, while Thornton stayed in the boat, guiding it with a long wooden pole and yelling directions to the shore. Buck trotted along the bank right next to the boat, ears pricked, worried, his eyes never leaving Thornton for even a second.

At the worst stretch of rapids, where a ledge of barely submerged rocks jutted out into the river, Hans untied the rope, and while Thornton poled the boat out past the ledge, he ran down the shore with the rope end to snub the boat the second it cleared the rocks. The boat shot free of the ledge, flying downstream in a current as fast as a mill race, when Hans hauled back on the rope to slow it—too hard, too suddenly. The boat tipped sideways, flipped completely over, and smashed against the bank upside down, flinging Thornton straight out into the rapids, swept away toward the deadliest stretch of white water that no living swimmer could survive.

Buck dove into the river the second Thornton went under. He swam hard, and caught up to him three hundred yards downstream, in the middle of a churning, roiling swirl of water. When Thornton grabbed hold of his tail, Buck turned toward the bank, swimming every muscle in his body to the breaking point. But they drifted toward the shore far too slowly, while the current dragged them downstream far faster than they could fight. Down below them they could hear the roar of the worst drop, where the wild water was shredded into spray by rocks that jutted up like the teeth of some giant beast. The pull of the water at the top of that steep final pitch was terrifying, and Thornton knew they could never make it back to shore in time. He scraped hard over one jagged rock, bruised his side against a second, and slammed into the third so hard he almost lost consciousness. He clung to the slippery top of the rock with both hands, let go of Buck, and yelled loud enough to be heard over the roar of the falls: “Go, Buck! Go!”

Buck couldn’t fight the current on his own, and got dragged downstream, struggling wildly, unable to fight his way back. When he heard Thornton yell the order again, he reared half out of the water, head thrown high as if to take one last look at his master, then turned obediently toward the bank. He swam so hard his muscles burned, and Pete and Pete hauled him ashore at the exact point where the current became too strong for any man or dog to swim.

They knew Thornton could only cling to that slippery slick rock for a few minutes at most, so they ran as fast as they could along the bank to a spot far above where Thornton was trapped. They tied the same Manila rope they used for the boat around Buck’s neck and shoulders, careful not to strangle him or slow his stroke, and pushed him out into the current. Buck struck out boldly, but he angled wrong into the water. He realized his mistake too late, when Thornton was only a half dozen strokes away, and the current was dragging him helplessly past.

Hans hauled back on the rope hard, the same way he would snub a runaway boat. The rope went tight in the middle of the current, jerked Buck straight under the surface, and he stayed under till his body slammed against the bank far downstream, where they hauled him half out of the water. He was half drowned, and Hans and Pete threw themselves on top of him, pounding the water out of his lungs and forcing breath back into his body. He staggered to his feet, then fell back down. They heard Thornton’s faint, distant voice over the roar of the rapids, and even though they couldn’t make out the words, they knew he was running out of time. Thornton’s voice hit Buck like an electric shock. He leaped to his feet, and ran up the bank ahead of the two men to the exact spot he’d jumped in before.

They tied the rope to him again, and launched him back into the water. This time Buck didn’t miss. He struck out straight into the current, no mistakes, no miscalculations, no second errors. Hans let the rope out slowly, no slack, while Pete made sure the line never tangled. Buck swam hard till he was positioned in a straight line directly above Thornton, then he turned, and shot down the current at the speed of an express train. Thornton saw him coming, and as Buck slammed into him like a battering ram, the full force of the raging water behind him, Thornton reached up and wrapped both arms tight around Buck’s shaggy neck. Hans tied the rope fast around a thick tree trunk, and Buck and Thornton were jerked under the water together. They twisted and spun, choking, suffocating, first one above the water then the other, dragging over the jagged rocky riverbed, smashing into hidden snags, until the current finally carried them right up against the bank.
