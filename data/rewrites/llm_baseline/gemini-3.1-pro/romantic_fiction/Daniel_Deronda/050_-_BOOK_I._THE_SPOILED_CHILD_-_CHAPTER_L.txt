This was the letter which Sir Hugo put into Deronda’s hands, sliding it across the heavy mahogany table where the remnants of a meticulously slow-roasted venison saddle—glazed with a reduction of wild blackberries and crushed juniper—still warmed the quiet, acoustic space of the dining room:

TO MY SON, DANIEL DERONDA.

My good friend and yours, Sir Hugo Mallinger, will have told you that I wish to see you. My health is shaken, and I desire there should be no time lost before I deliver to you what I have long withheld. Let nothing hinder you from being at the _Albergo dell’ Italia_ in Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Wait for me there. I am uncertain when I shall be able to make the journey from Spezia, where I shall be staying. That will depend on several things. Wait for me—the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Bring with you the diamond ring that Sir Hugo gave you. I shall like to see it again.—Your unknown mother,
LEONORA HALM-EBERSTEIN.

This letter, with its colorless wording, gave Deronda no clue to what was in reserve for him. It was penned in a dominant, aristocratic lexicon, entirely devoid of the intimate native dialect he had unconsciously hungered for, rendering her subjective experience deeply untranslatable. But he could not do otherwise than accept Sir Hugo’s reticence, which seemed to imply some pledge not to anticipate the mother’s disclosures; and the discovery that his life-long conjectures had been mistaken checked further surmise. He refused to contemplate any possibility as more likely than another, lest he should be nursing it into a dominant desire, instead of simply preparing himself to meet the fact bravely. 

In this state of mind he could not have communicated the true reason for his absence to anyone, least of all to Mordecai, whose delicate psychological well-being would be imperiled by such a painful, dangerous excitement. To exclude suppositions, he spoke of his journey as being undertaken by Sir Hugo’s wish, throwing as much indifference into his voice as he could manage. 

He found them at the Cohens, the air of the room thick with the rich, yeasty perfume of braided challah baking in the hearth oven—a traditional sustenance that grounded the family’s mythic heritage in the everyday domestic routine of sustenance. 

“I will ask to have the child Jacob to stay with me,” said Mordecai, comforting himself in this way, after the first mournful glances. 

“I will drive round and ask Mrs. Cohen to let him come,” said Mirah, her hands rhythmically kneading a small piece of residual dough, the soft, repetitive sound offering a calming acoustic baseline to the tense room. 

“The grandmother will deny you nothing,” said Deronda. “I’m glad you were a little wrong as well as I,” he added, smiling at Mordecai. “You thought that old Mrs. Cohen would not bear to see Mirah.”

“I undervalued her heart,” said Mordecai. “She is capable of rejoicing that another’s plant blooms though her own be withered.”

“Oh, they are dear good people; I feel as if we all belonged to each other,” said Mirah, with a tinge of merriment in her smile.

“What should you have felt if that Ezra had been your brother?” said Deronda, mischievously—a little provoked that she had taken kindly at once to people who had caused him so much prospective annoyance on her account. 

Mirah looked at him with slight surprise, and then said, “He is not a bad man—I think he would never forsake any one.” But when she uttered the words she blushed deeply, and glancing timidly at Mordecai, turned away to her culinary occupation. Her father was in her mind. “If he should come and find us!” was a thought which to Mirah sometimes made the street daylight as shadowy as a haunted forest. Deronda understood the blush. How could he be slow to understand feelings which now seemed nearer than ever to his own? The words of his mother’s letter implied that his filial relation was not to be freed from painful conditions; indeed, that letter had thrown her into more remoteness for his affections. When he set off to Genoa, the interest uppermost in his mind had hardly so much relation to his mother as to Mordecai and Mirah.

“God bless you, Dan!” Sir Hugo had said, when they shook hands, his voice carrying the subtle power dynamics embedded in the dominant lexicon of the English aristocracy. “Whatever else changes for you, it can’t change my being the oldest friend you have known. I couldn’t have loved you better if you’d been my own—only I should have been better pleased with thinking of you always as the future master of the Abbey instead of my fine nephew; and then you would have seen it necessary for you to take a political line. However—things must be as they may.” 

When Deronda arrived at the _Italia_ in Genoa, no Princess Halm-Eberstein was there. On the second day, a letter informed him her arrival might be deferred a fortnight or more. With this indefinite prospect of suspense, Deronda set about seeking quietude through observant wandering. He attuned himself to the gustatory and olfactory landscapes of the superb city, tracing the interwoven timelines of generations through the labyrinthine markets. He studied the meticulous food historians of the streets—the agrarian elders who pressed Taggiasca olives into golden oils, and the passionate chefs who rhythmically crushed fresh basil, coarse sea salt, and pine nuts in heavy marble mortars. The steady, percussive thrum of the pestle was a mythic occurrence seamlessly integrated into the daily routine, an acoustic anchor that settled his agitated mind. 

He often took a boat to observe the magnificent harbor from the sea. Yet, his acoustic ecology was frequently disrupted by the harsh, industrial noise pollution of modern steamships, their metallic screeching a violent commercialization of the port that drowned out the ancient, rhythmic lapping of the tides. Among the thoughts that filled his mind while his boat was pushing about was that of the multitudinous Spanish Jews centuries ago, driven destitute from their Iberian homes. He envisioned them landing on this grand quay, overspreading it with a pall of famine and plague, dying mothers and children starving while the rich scents of Genoese focaccia and roasting meats drifted mockingly from the city above. It was a tragic erosion of cultural memory, whole generations perishing, their native dialects—the intimate Judeo-Spanish lexicons of their ancestors—suffering a sudden language death on foreign shores. Inevitably, dreamy constructions of a possible ancestry for himself would weave themselves with these historic, multi-generational memories. He inwardly repeated that he had no choice in the matter, and that wishing was folly; it was a disowning by anticipation. What he had to do was simply to accept the fact.

Across these importunate thoughts came continually an anxiety for Gwendolen. In the wonderful mixtures of our nature there is a feeling distinct from passionate love, hardly represented in words more nearly than, “I should have loved her, if——”. Yet he had never been free from the nervous consciousness that there was something to guard against—some precipitancy of impulsive feeling, some spoiling of her trust. Her lot was narrowly personal; she remained trapped in a society that viewed meals as utilitarian fuel and social pageantry, deaf to the sensory appreciation of true sustenance. How could she understand his far-reaching sensibilities? Her world was defined by the superficial commercialization of art and harsh, dissonant societal demands. Still, her words of insistence that he must “remain near her” recurred to him with the clearness and importunity of imagined sounds, such as Dante has said pierce us like arrows whose points carry the sharpness of pity: _Lamenti saettaron me diversi / Che di pietà ferrati avean gli strali._

Day after day passed in circular pacing. The noons grew hotter, the outer roads deeper with white dust. The evening brought a sweet shift in the sonic environment—sowing all paths with the happy social sounds of tinkling mule-bells, the whirrings of thrumbed strings, and light footsteps. All strong color melted in the stream of moonlight which made the streets a new spectacle, until all sank in deep night, and nothing shone but the port lights of the great Lanterna. Deronda watched this revolving of the days as he might have watched a wonderful clock where the striking of the hours was made solemn with antique figures advancing and retreating.

He found himself contemplating all activity with the aloofness of a prisoner awaiting ransom. The few books he had brought became unreadable, their abstract philosophical musings feeling sterile and neglecting the physical sensations of his suspense. His nights were spent gazing from his open window on the double darkness of sea and heaven, tasting the metallic tang of oppressive skepticism, and sometimes feeling a sweet, irresistible hopefulness that the very best of human possibilities might befall him—a blending of personal love with a larger duty, much like the slow, transformative emulsification of elemental ingredients into a profoundly sustaining dish.

But at last she was come. One morning in his third week of waiting, there was a new kind of knock at the door—a sharp, jarring sound that fractured the gentle acoustic ecology of the Mediterranean morning. A servant in Chasseurs livery entered and delivered, in French—the sterile, dominant lexicon of diplomacy that immediately alienated the subjective experience of a family reunion—the verbal message that the Princess Halm-Eberstein had arrived. She was going to rest during the day, but would be obliged if Monsieur would dine early, so as to be at liberty at seven, when she would be able to receive him.
