“Within the soul a faculty abides,
That with interpositions, which would hide
And darken, so can deal, that they become
Contingencies of pomp; and serve to exalt
Her native brightness, as the ample moon,
In the deep stillness of a summer even,
Rising behind a thick and lofty grove,
Burns, like an unconsuming fire of light,
In the green trees; and, kindling on all sides
Their leafy umbrage, turns the dusky veil
Into a substance glorious as her own,
Yea, with her own incorporated, by power
Capacious and serene.” —WORDSWORTH: _Excursion_, B. IV.

Daniel Deronda stepped out of the narrow house in Chelsea, his mind restless, stirred by emotions he could not yet name—what he called the fumes of temper. He needed motion, something physical to clear the air within him. Heading toward London, he saw the Chelsea Stairs and the boats waiting there, and without hesitation, chose to row rather than ride in a cab. The act of pulling the oar felt like a release—each stroke grounding him in the present, pushing away the weight of thought.

His destination was Ram’s bookshop, where he had missed Mordecai the day before. He’d been told the man returned between five and six. Deronda wanted more than just a conversation—he sought understanding, connection. He didn’t want their meeting to end again as it had before, abruptly, like a drawbridge raised too soon. He hoped this time, they might speak longer, deeper, and perhaps finally unlock something real.

As he rowed, the rhythm of the water and the effort of the oar shifted his thoughts. He began to see himself not just as someone seeking help for Mirah, but as one who might be using others—like a recruiting sergeant who takes no interest in the lives of those he enlists. Was he doing the same? Was he treating Mordecai merely as a tool?

*If I get what I need from him,* Deronda mused, *would I care if he never spoke of himself again? If he had expectations I couldn’t meet? It’s possible we’ve passed each other like ships at sea—both carrying exiles who might have recognized one another, had they seen each other’s faces. But is there such a thing as missing someone you’ve never known? Or longing for absence in a crowded room? Still… I’ve made my choices. I’ve walked into this moment, and now I must face it.*

By the time the wherry neared Blackfriars Bridge, it was half-past four. The sky was dying in splendor—purple streaks fading into saffron gold, calm above, but alive below. On the river, reflections danced: ripples flared, sails glowed, barges slipped from shadow into color. It was a scene both still and stirring, like the quiet before a revelation.

Deronda, warmed now, handed the oar back and pulled his Inverness cape tighter. As he fastened the top button, he looked up—and froze.

There, silhouetted against the western light, was a face he knew well. Pale, thin, yet glowing with intensity—Mordecai stood on the bridge, gazing down. And then, as if drawn by some unseen force, he lifted a white hand and waved—not once, but again and again, urgently.

Deronda had signaled first, eager that Mordecai should recognize him. And now, the answer came swiftly. Mordecai raised his cap and waved back—his whole being alight with fulfillment. For him, this was prophecy fulfilled. Obstacles dissolved. Doubts vanished. This was not mere chance—it was destiny arriving. His joy was like that of a scientist watching the first sign of life in a long-expected experiment. The figure from his dreams had come, and had acknowledged him. The future now lay open.

Three minutes later, Deronda stepped ashore, paid the boatman, and approached Mordecai, who stood motionless, waiting.

“I was glad to see you here,” Deronda said. “I meant to go to the bookshop, but I’m glad you’re already waiting.”

“Yes,” Mordecai replied, voice low and steady. “That’s why I came to the bridge.”

The simplicity of the reply startled Deronda. Was this man truly unbalanced? Had Cohen been right in hinting at something strange?

“You didn’t know I was at Chelsea?” Deronda asked after a pause.

“No,” Mordecai said. “But I expected you to come down the river. I’ve been waiting for you—for five years.”

His eyes, deep-set and dark, held Deronda’s with an almost unbearable tenderness. There was a childlike dependence in them, mixed with solemnity. Deronda felt a pang—not of doubt, but of sorrow. He believed, even as he feared, that this man was living in illusion.

“If I can help you,” he said earnestly, “I will. Shall we take a cab? You’ve walked enough, I think—your breath is short.”

“Let us go to the shop,” Mordecai said quietly. “It will soon be time. But look upriver,” he added, turning his gaze back to the water, speaking in a hushed tone that carried the weight of emotion. “See how the sky fades. I’ve always loved this bridge. As a boy, I stood here. It’s a place where spiritual messengers meet. The Masters were right—the world has its angels, its full messages from afar. Here, I’ve listened to the voices of earth and sky. When I was strong, I watched for stars in the deep night. But sunset—this hour—was always my favorite. It faded slowly, like my own life. It paused. It waited. And now… it has brought me my new life—my new self—who will live when this breath is gone.”

Deronda said nothing. He felt deeply moved. The earlier suspicion—that Mordecai might be deluded, perhaps even mad—gave way to something quieter, more profound. He wasn’t ready to dismiss this man’s vision. He had always believed in depths beyond his own experience. And now, standing here, he felt a call—not to judge, but to listen.

After a moment, Mordecai turned. “Let us go.”

They rode in silence, save for the rattling of the cab. Deronda’s thoughts drifted to Mirah—but now, he sensed that Mordecai would shape the conversation, not he. He found himself wondering: what questions could he ask? What truths might he uncover?

*I suppose,* he thought, *I am in a state of superstition—waiting for fate to reveal itself. But isn’t faith, even when mistaken, more powerful than reason? Expectation, even when disappointed—hasn’t it shaped the world? Will I fulfill this man’s hope—or break it? If I can, I will not fail.*

Ten minutes later, they arrived at the bookshop. The small space was lit by gas, dim and intimate. They stood facing each other, both removing their hats instinctively—as if they wished to see each other clearly, fully.

Mordecai leaned against the counter. Deronda stood across from him, barely four feet away. Their faces were etched in contrast: Mordecai—pale, gaunt, with the sharp lines of a man worn by illness, yet burning with inner fire; Deronda—young, strong, composed, with a quiet dignity that seemed to hold space around him.

Imagine Mordecai: a Jewish face, marked by suffering and intellect, hair and beard still black, skin yellowed by disease, hands thin and fragile. Yet his eyes—bright, yearning, filled with the look of a mother seeing her son one last time. A sense of continuity, of legacy, radiated from him.

And opposite him—Deronda: not exotic, but striking in his natural grace, his presence calm and attentive. He did not look like a man of the world, nor a scholar, but someone who could hold the weight of another’s soul.

This moment tested Deronda’s deepest nature. He didn’t believe Mordecai’s words were true—but he felt the truth of his pain. He felt the cry from the depths of another human heart, and with it, the demand to receive, not reject.

Receptiveness—rare, like courage—had taken hold of him. His face softened, became still, gentle, powerful. And in that stillness, Mordecai found strength.

“I cannot tell you what led me to you,” Mordecai began. “You wonder?”

“I am not impatient,” Deronda said. “I am ready to listen.”

“You see some reasons why I need you,” Mordecai continued, voice quiet, conserving energy. “You see I am dying. I am like one behind bars, unheard, pitied. The day is closing. Soon, we would not have seen each other. But you have come in time.”

“I rejoice that I have come in time,” Deronda said, feeling the truth of it.

“But the deeper reasons begin far back,” Mordecai went on. “When I was young, studying abroad, I found ideas—beloved ideas—because I was a Jew. They were a trust, an inspiration. My life began then. I counted every breath, every meal, every waking hour—only as fuel for the divine flame.”

He pressed a hand to his chest, then stretched his fingers forward.

“But then—care, labor, disease—blocked my path. I was bound by iron that eats into the soul. I asked: How shall I save the life within me, when my breath is stifled?”

He paused, catching his breath. Deronda dared not speak. The silence hummed with awe.

“And yet,” Mordecai continued, “you may think I speak like a dreamer. But I know English—my mother tongue. I was born here. But my soul was fed in Holland, at my mother’s brother’s side—a rabbi of learning. Then Hamburg, Göttingen—I studied widely. I was free then. I had a craft. I said: Let my body dwell in poverty, but let my soul be a temple of memory—where knowledge enters, and hope remains.”

He smiled faintly. “They called me a visionary. I did not deny it. Visions create the world. I see the world as it is—and as it will be. You are not listening to madness.”

“I am not,” Deronda said softly. “I listen to understand.”

“A spiritual destiny embraced willingly—in youth?” Mordecai repeated, correcting gently. “No. It was born in me as a child. It brought its own world—a medieval world, where men revived the ancient language in new psalms of exile. They absorbed Gentile thought into Jewish faith, yet longed for a center for our people. One of their souls was reborn in me. I walked with them—through Spain, Provence. I debated with Aben-Ezra. I sailed with Jehuda ha-Levi. I heard the Crusaders roar, the cries of tortured Israel.”

He paused, then whispered, hoarse but fierce:

“While it is imprisoned in me, it will never learn another.”

“Have you written entirely in Hebrew?” Deronda asked, remembering his own ignorance of the language.

“Yes,” Mordecai said, voice heavy with sadness. “In youth, I wandered into solitude, but I did not feel alone. The great dead surrounded me. The martyrs listened. But soon, the living were deaf. I thought my life would stretch long. I believed in patience, in finding roots where others failed. But then—new messengers came. Family troubles called. I had to work. I was left alone again. And death—his shadow was near.”

He sighed. “I pleaded. I spoke to leaders, scholars, merchants. No one listened. Some mocked. One said: ‘The Book of Mormon would never work in Hebrew. If you want to teach our learned men, you’ll fail.’ He touched a truth.”

A dry irony lingered in his voice.

“But though you write in Hebrew,” Deronda said gently, “few use English better than you.”

Mordecai shook his head. “Too late. I cannot write now. My words would be like this breath—gasping. But the breath may awaken pity. The writing—no. If I wrote now in English, I’d be like one beating a board to summon those who only hear a bell. My soul hears the flaws in its own speech. New writing would be like this body”—he spread his arms—“within it, there might be Ruach-ha-kodesh—the breath of divine thought—but men would smile and say, ‘A poor Jew!’ And the worst smilers—would be of my own people.”

He let his hands fall. His spirit sank. Despair hovered.

“I feel with you,” Deronda said, his voice clear and warm. “But forgive me—if I speak hastily—your writings need not die. Publication is possible. Trust me. I can make it happen.”

“That is not enough,” Mordecai said quickly, lifting his head. “I need more than a hand. I need a soul. One who believes my belief, feels my hope, sees the glory I see! You must be my life—reborn. You must take the inheritance. It has gathered through ages. Generations crowd upon my narrow life—past and future meet here. The bridge is breaking. But I have found you. You have come in time. You will carry the sacred inheritance of the Jew.”

Deronda paled. Fear surged—not of failure, but of harm. He dreaded discouraging this man, yet also feared feeding an illusion. The grip on his arm was urgent, pleading. He could not refuse, not now.

With quiet instinct, he placed his palm gently over Mordecai’s hand—an act of peace, of solidarity. Then he spoke, slowly, carefully.

“Do you forget what I told you before? That I am not of your race?”

“It can’t be true,” Mordecai whispered, unshaken. The touch had fortified him.

A pause. Deronda could not answer. Mordecai pressed on, his voice rising with conviction.

“You are not sure of your own origin.”

“How do you know that?” Daniel asked, withdrawing his hands.

“I know it. I know it. What is my life else?” Mordecai said, almost pleading. “Tell me everything. Why do you deny?”
