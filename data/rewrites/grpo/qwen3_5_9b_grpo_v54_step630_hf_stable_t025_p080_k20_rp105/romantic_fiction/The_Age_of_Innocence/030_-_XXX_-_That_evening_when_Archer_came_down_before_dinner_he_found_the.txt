That evening, when Archer descended the stairs before dinner, the drawing-room stood empty. The household had retreated into silence; every social engagement had been postponed in the wake of Mrs. Manson Mingott's illness, leaving the couple to dine alone. May, usually the more punctual of the two, had not yet joined him. Archer knew she was home—he had heard the soft rustle of her movements in her room while he dressed—but he was struck by a sudden, uncharacteristic delay.

In recent days, he had cultivated a habit of dwelling on such conjectures, using them to anchor his drifting thoughts to the solid ground of reality. Sometimes, he mused, he had glimpsed the key to his father-in-law's obsession with trivialities. Perhaps Mr. Welland, long ago, had experienced his own escapes and visions, conjuring up entire hostilities of domesticity merely to defend himself against them.

When May finally appeared, she looked exhausted. She wore the low-necked, tightly laced dinner dress required by Mingott ceremony even on the most informal nights, her fair hair coiled into its usual intricate accumulation. Yet her face was wan, almost faded, though her eyes retained the brilliant blue dazzle of the previous day. She greeted him with her customary tenderness.

"What became of you, dear?" she asked gently. "I was waiting at Granny's, and Ellen came alone to say she'd dropped you on the way because you had to rush off on business. Is everything alright?"

"Only some letters I forgot," he replied, surprised by her insistence. "I wanted to get them off before dinner."

"Ah—" she murmured, then paused. "I'm sorry you didn't come to Granny's, unless those letters were truly urgent."

"They were," he said, feeling a flicker of irritation at her scrutiny. "Besides, I didn't know you were there."

She turned toward the mirror above the mantelpiece, lifting a long arm to adjust a puff that had slipped from her elaborate hairstyle. Watching her, Archer felt a strange heaviness settle in his chest. There was something languid and inelastic in her posture, as if the deadly monotony of their lives had already begun to weigh upon her. He remembered that this morning, as he left the house, she had called down the stairs that they would meet at her grandmother's to drive home together. He had called back a cheerful "Yes!" and, lost in his own visions, had forgotten his promise. Now, guilt smote him, mingled with annoyance that such a trifling omission should be stored away against him after nearly two years of marriage.

He was weary of living in a perpetual, tepid honeymoon—devoid of the temperature of passion yet burdened by all its demands. If May had voiced her grievances, he suspected she harbored many, he might have laughed them away. But she was trained to conceal imaginary wounds beneath a Spartan smile.

To mask his own unease, he asked how her grandmother was. May answered that Mrs. Mingott was improving but had been disturbed by the latest news regarding the Beauforts.

"What news?" Archer asked.

"It seems they are staying in New York," she said. "I believe he is entering an insurance business or something similar. They are looking for a small house."

The absurdity of the situation was undeniable, and they proceeded to dinner. Their conversation remained within its usual narrow circle, yet Archer noted that his wife made no mention of Madame Olenska, nor of Old Catherine's reception of her. He felt a vague sense of foreboding, though he was secretly grateful for the silence.

Later, in the library, Archer lit a cigar and opened a volume of Michelet. He had recently taken to history in the evenings since May had begun asking him to read aloud whenever she saw him with poetry. It was not that he disliked the sound of his own voice; rather, he could always foresee her comments. In the days of their engagement, she had simply echoed his words, but since he had stopped providing opinions, she had begun to hazard her own, with results destructive to his enjoyment.

Seeing that he had chosen history, she fetched her workbasket, drew up an armchair to the green-shaded student lamp, and uncovered a cushion she was embroidering for his sofa. She was not a skilled needlewoman; her large, capable hands were made for riding, rowing, and open-air activities. Yet, since other wives embroidered cushions for their husbands, she did not wish to omit this final link in her devotion.

She sat so that Archer, by merely raising his eyes, could see her bent over her workframe, her ruffled elbow-sleeves slipping back from her firm arms, the betrothal sapphire shining on her left hand above her broad gold wedding ring, and her right hand slowly, laboriously stabbing the canvas. Bathed in the lamplight, with her clear brow illuminated, he felt a secret dismay rise within him. He realized that he would always know the thoughts behind her eyes; that never, in all the years to come, would she surprise him with an unexpected mood, a new idea, a weakness, a cruelty, or an emotion. She had spent her poetry and romance on their short courtship; the function was exhausted because the need was past. Now, she was simply ripening into a copy of her mother, and mysteriously, through that very process, trying to turn him into a Mr. Welland.

He laid down his book and stood up impatiently. At once, she raised her head.

"What's the matter?"

"The room is stifling," he said. "I want a little air."

He had insisted that the library curtains slide backward and forward on a rod, allowing them to be closed in the evening, rather than remaining nailed to a gilt cornice and immovably looped up over layers of lace as in the drawing-room. He pulled them back and pushed up the sash, leaning out into the icy night. The mere act of not looking at May, seated beside his table under his lamp, of seeing other houses, roofs, and chimneys, of grasping the sense of other lives outside his own and other cities beyond New York, cleared his brain and made it easier to breathe.

After leaning out into the darkness for a few minutes, he heard her say, "Newland! Do shut the window. You'll catch your death."

He pulled the sash down and turned back. "Catch my death!" he echoed, and felt the urge to add, "But I've caught it already. I *am* dead—I've been dead for months and months."

Suddenly, the play of the word sparked a wild suggestion. What if it were *she* who was dead? If she were going to die—to die soon—and leave him free! The sensation of standing there in that warm, familiar room, looking at her, and wishing her dead, was so strange, so fascinating, and overmastering that its enormity did not immediately strike him. He simply felt that chance had offered him a new possibility to which his sick soul might cling. Yes, May might die; people did: young, healthy people like herself. She might die, and set him suddenly free.

She glanced up, and he saw by her widening eyes that there must be something strange in his own.

"Newland! Are you ill?"

He shook his head and turned toward his armchair. She bent over her workframe, and as he passed, he laid his hand on her hair. "Poor May!" he said.

"Poor? Why poor?" she echoed with a strained laugh.

"Because I shall never be able to open a window without worrying you," he rejoined, laughing also.

For a moment she was silent; then, her head bowed over her work, she said very softly, "I shall never worry if you're happy."

"Ah, my dear; and I shall never be happy unless I can open the windows!"

"In *this* weather?" she remonstrated. With a sigh, he buried his head in his book.

Six or seven days passed. Archer heard nothing from Madame Olenska, and he became aware that her name would not be mentioned in his presence by any member of the family. He did not try to see her; to do so while she was at Old Catherine's guarded bedside would have been almost impossible. In the uncertainty of the situation, he let himself drift, conscious, somewhere below the surface of his thoughts, of a resolve that had come to him when he leaned out from his library window into the icy night. The strength of that resolve made it easy to wait and make no sign.

Then one day, May told him that Mrs. Manson Mingott had asked to see him. There was nothing surprising in the request, for the old lady was steadily recovering, and she had always openly declared that she preferred Archer to any of her other grandsons-in-law. May delivered the message with evident pleasure; she was proud of Old Catherine's appreciation of her husband.

There was a moment's pause, and then Archer felt it incumbent on him to say, "All right. Shall we go together this afternoon?"

His wife's face brightened, but she instantly answered, "Oh, you'd much better go alone. It bores Granny to see the same people too often."

Archer's heart beat violently as he rang Old Mrs. Mingott's bell. He had wanted above all things to go alone, for he felt sure the visit would give him the chance to say a word in private to the Countess Olenska. He had determined to wait until the chance presented itself naturally; and here it was, and here he was on the doorstep. Behind the door, behind the curtains of the yellow damask room next to the hall, she was surely awaiting him; in another moment he should see her, and be able to speak to her before she led him to the sick-room.

He wanted only to put one question; after that, his course would be clear. What he wished to ask was simply the date of her return to Washington, and that question she could hardly refuse to answer.

But in the yellow sitting-room, it was the mulatto maid who waited. Her white teeth shining like a keyboard, she pushed back the sliding doors and ushered him into Old Catherine's presence.

The old woman sat in a vast, throne-like armchair near her bed. Beside her was a mahogany stand bearing a cast-bronze lamp with an engraved globe, over which a green paper shade had been balanced. There was not a book or a newspaper in reach, nor any evidence of feminine employment: conversation had always been Mrs. Mingott's sole pursuit, and she would have scorned to feign an interest in fancywork.

Archer saw no trace of the slight distortion left by her stroke. She merely looked paler, with darker shadows in the folds and recesses of her obesity. In the fluted mob-cap tied by a starched bow between her first two chins, and the muslin kerchief crossed over her billowing purple dressing gown, she seemed like some shrewd and kindly ancestress of her own who might have yielded too freely to the pleasures of the table.

She held out one of the little hands that nestled in a hollow of her huge lap like pet animals and called to the maid, "Don't let in anyone else. If my daughters call, say I'm asleep."

The maid disappeared, and the old lady turned to her grandson.

"My dear, am I perfectly hideous?" she asked gaily, launching out one hand in search of the folds of muslin on her inaccessible bosom. "My daughters tell me it doesn't matter at my age—as if hideousness didn't matter all the more the harder it gets to conceal!"

"My dear, you're handsomer than ever!" Archer rejoined in the same tone, and she threw back her head and laughed.

"Ah, but not as handsome as Ellen!" she jerked out, twinkling at him maliciously. Before he could answer, she added, "Was she so awfully handsome the day you drove her up from the ferry?"

He laughed, and she continued, "Was it because you told her so that she had to put you out on the way? In my youth, young men didn't desert pretty women unless they were made to!" She gave another chuckle and interrupted it to say almost querulously, "It's a pity she didn't marry you; I always told her so. It would have spared me all this worry. But who ever thought of sparing their grandmother worry?"

Archer wondered if her illness had blurred her faculties, but suddenly she broke out: "Well, it's settled, anyhow: she's going to stay with me, whatever the rest of the family says! She hadn't been here five minutes before I'd have gone down on my knees to keep her—if only, for the last twenty years, I'd been able to see where the floor was!"

Archer listened in silence, and she went on: "They'd talked me over, as no doubt you know: persuaded me, Lovell, and Letterblair, and Augusta Welland, and all the rest of them, that I must hold out and cut off her allowance, till she was made to see that it was her duty to go back to Olenski. They thought they'd convinced me when the secretary, or whatever he was, came out with the last proposals: handsome proposals I confess they were. After all, marriage is marriage, and money's money—both useful things in their way... and I didn't know what to answer—" She broke off and drew a long breath, as if speaking had become an effort. "But the minute I laid eyes on her, I said: 'You sweet bird, you! Shut you up in that cage again? Never!' And now it's settled that she's to stay here and nurse her Granny as long as there's a Granny to nurse. It's not a gay prospect, but she doesn't mind; and of course I've told Letterblair that she's to be given her proper allowance."

The young man heard her with veins aglow, but in his confusion of mind, he hardly knew whether her news brought joy or pain. He had so definitely decided on the course he meant to pursue that for the moment he could not readjust his thoughts.
