Do not be too quick to forgive him, nor assume with a hasty benevolence that from that day M. Paul underwent a transformation, becoming easy company and no longer prone to radiate unease around you. Nay, he remained a man of small stature and erratic moods. When agitated, a frequent occurrence, he grew acutely irritable; furthermore, his veins ran dark with a livid tincture of belladonna, the essence of jealousy. This was not merely the tender jealousy of the heart, but a sterner, narrower sentiment seated in the mind.

As I sat observing M. Paul, watching him knit his brow or jut out his lip over some exercise of mine—which inevitably lacked the perfection he desired, for he delighted in my blunders, finding a knot of mistakes sweeter than a cluster of nuts—I often thought he possessed striking resemblances to Napoleon Bonaparte. I maintain this belief today.

In a shameless disregard for magnanimity, he mirrored the Great Emperor. M. Paul would have quarreled with twenty learned women, would have unashamedly engaged in petty squabbles and recriminations against entire circles of society, never caring for loss or a lack of dignity. He would have exiled fifty Madame de Staëls if they had annoyed, offended, or rivaled him.

I recall vividly a heated episode involving a certain Madame Panache, a lady temporarily hired by Madame Beck to teach history. She was intelligent, possessing a vast amount of knowledge, and, more importantly, mastered the art of leveraging what she knew. Words and confidence were at her command. Her physical appearance was far from lacking advantage; many would have deemed her "a fine woman." Yet, there were aspects of her robust and ample figure, along with her bustling, demonstrative demeanor, that clashed with the fastidious and capricious tastes of M. Paul. The echo of her voice through the *carré* sent him into a strange trance; her long, free stride along the corridor often caused him to snatch up his papers and flee instantly.

One day, with malicious intent, he decided to interrupt her class. As swift as lightning, he absorbed her teaching method, which differed from his own pet plan. Without ceremony or courtesy, he pointed out what he termed her errors. Whether he expected submission, I know not; instead, he met an acrid opposition accompanied by a sharp rebuke for his unjustifiable interference.

Rather than withdraw with dignity, he threw down the gauntlet of defiance. Madame Panache, bellicose as Penthesilea, snatched it up immediately. She snapped her fingers in the intermeddler's face and rushed upon him with a storm of words. M. Emanuel was eloquent, but Madame Panache was voluble. A fierce antagonism ensued. Instead of laughing secretly at his fair foe, with all her sore pride and loud self-assertion, M. Paul detested her with intense seriousness. He honored her with his earnest fury, pursuing her vindictively and implacably. He refused to rest peacefully in his bed, to enjoy his meals, or to savor his cigar until she was completely removed from the establishment. The Professor conquered, but I cannot say the laurels of this victory graced his temples. Once I ventured to hint as much. To my great surprise, he conceded I might be right, but averred that when confronted with men or women of coarse, self-complacent quality, like Madame Panache, he had no control over his passions; an unspeakable and active aversion compelled him to a war of extermination.

Three months later, hearing that his vanquished foe had met reverses and was likely distressed for want of employment, he forgot his hatred. Active in both good and evil, he moved heaven and earth to find her a position. When she came to thank him for his recent kindness and make up for former differences, the old voice—a little loud—the old manner—a little forward—acted upon him so strongly that within ten minutes he started up and bowled her, or rather himself, out of the room in a transport of nervous irritation.

To pursue a somewhat audacious parallel, in his love of power and eager grasp for supremacy, M. Emanuel was like Bonaparte. He was a man not always to be submitted to. Sometimes it was necessary to resist; it was right to stand still, to look up into his eyes, and tell him that his requirements exceeded reason—that his absolutism verged on tyranny.

The first developments of peculiar talent within his range, curiously excited and disturbed him. He watched their struggle into life with a scowl; he held back his hand, perhaps saying, "Come on if you have strength," but he would not aid the birth.

When the pang and peril of the first conflict were over, when the breath of life was drawn, when he saw the lungs expand and contract, when he felt the heart beat and discovered life in the eye, he did not yet offer to foster.

"Prove yourself true ere I cherish you," was his ordinance. How difficult he made that proof! What thorns, briers, and flints he strewn in the path of feet not inured to rough travel! He followed footprints that, as they approached the bourne, were sometimes marked in blood, following them grimly, holding the austerest police watch over the pain-pressed pilgrim. And when at last he allowed a rest, before slumber might close the eyelids, he opened those same lids wide, with pitiless finger and thumb, gazing deep through the pupil and iris into the brain and heart, searching if Vanity, Pride, or Falsehood, in any subtle form, was discoverable in the furthest recess of existence. If, at last, he let the neophyte sleep, it was but a moment; he woke him suddenly to apply new tests: he sent him on irksome errands when he was staggering with weariness; he tested the temper, the sense, and the health. It was only when every severest test had been applied and endured, when the most corrosive acid had been used and failed to tarnish the ore, that he admitted it genuine, and, still in clouded silence, stamped it with his deep brand of approval.

I speak not ignorant of these evils.

Until the date at which the last chapter closed, M. Paul had not been my professor; he had not given me lessons. But about that time, accidentally hearing me one day acknowledge an ignorance of some branch of education (I think it was arithmetic) which would have disgraced a charity-school boy, as he very truly remarked, he took me in hand. He examined me first, found me, I need not say, abundantly deficient, gave me some books, and appointed me some tasks.

He did this at first with pleasure, indeed with unconcealed exultation, condescending to say that he believed I was "bonne et pas trop faible" (i.e., well enough disposed, and not wholly destitute of parts), but, owing he supposed to adverse circumstances, "as yet in a state of wretchedly imperfect mental development."

The beginning of all effort has indeed with me been marked by a preternatural imbecility. I never could, even in forming a common acquaintance, assert or prove a claim to average quickness. A depressing and difficult passage has prefaced every new page I have turned in life.

So long as this passage lasted, M. Paul was very kind, very good, very forbearing; he saw the sharp pain inflicted, and felt the weighty humiliation imposed by my own sense of incapacity; and words can hardly do justice to his tenderness and helpfulness. His own eyes would moisten when tears of shame and effort clouded mine; burdened as he was with work, he would steal half his brief space of recreation to give to me.

But, strange grief! When that heavy and overcast dawn began at last to yield to day; when my faculties began to struggle themselves free, and my time of energy and fulfillment came; when I voluntarily doubled, trebled, quadrupled the tasks he set, to please him as I thought, his kindness became sternness; the light changed in his eyes from a beam to a spark; he fretted, he opposed, he curbed me imperiously; the more I did, the harder I worked, the less he seemed content. Sarcasms of which the severity amazed and puzzled me, harassed my ears; then flowed out the bitterest innuendoes against the "pride of intellect." I was vaguely threatened with I know not what doom, if I ever trespassed the limits proper to my sex, and conceived a contraband appetite for unfeminine knowledge. Alas! I had no such appetite. What I loved, it joyed me by any effort to content; but the noble hunger for science in the abstract—the godlike thirst after discovery—these feelings were known to me but by briefest flashes.

Yet, when M. Paul sneered at me, I wanted to possess them more fully; his injustice stirred in me ambitious wishes—it imparted a strong stimulus—it gave wings to aspiration.

In the beginning, before I had penetrated to motives, that uncomprehended sneer of his made my heart ache, but by-and-by it only warmed the blood in my veins, and sent added action to my pulses. Whatever my powers—feminine or the contrary—God had given them, and I felt resolute to be ashamed of no faculty of his bestowal.
