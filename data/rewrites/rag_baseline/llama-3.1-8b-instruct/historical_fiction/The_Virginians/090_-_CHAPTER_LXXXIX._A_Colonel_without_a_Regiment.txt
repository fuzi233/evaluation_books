In the stillness of the evening, I sat with Governor Gooch at his country house, near Williamsburg, surrounded by the faint scent of blooming flowers and the distant hush of the James River. Our conversation turned to the escalating tensions between the colonies and Great Britain. Delegates from Virginia, including my brother, had been appointed to attend the General Congress in Philadelphia, where they would join representatives from other colonies in an attempt to find a peaceful resolution. But the atmosphere was charged with anxiety and uncertainty. Loyalists in the room spoke of drawing swords and scattering the rebels, their words a jarring counterpoint to the soft rustle of the leaves outside.

As we talked, I couldn't help but think of my brother's involvement with George Washington, a man of strong convictions and a fierce commitment to the cause. Some saw his influence as a betrayal, a sign of Harry's growing estrangement from the Crown. Others, like myself, believed he sought a path of reconciliation, a last chance to avoid the devastating consequences of revolt. I knew my brother well enough to understand the turmoil he faced, torn between loyalty to his family and his duty to the colonies. I promised to intercede on his behalf, to try and persuade him to reconsider his stance, but deep down, I feared my words would fall on deaf ears.

My mother, ever the devout woman, took a different approach, appealing to Scripture and the weight of tradition. She warned Harry of the consequences of disobedience, pointing to the example of his elder brother and the perceived influence of his wife. Her words were laced with a subtle venom, a reminder that in our family, loyalty to the Crown was not simply a matter of politics, but of blood and honor. Though I didn't see the letter she wrote to him, I knew its contents would be a mix of scripture and stern admonition, a call to return to the fold. As the night wore on, the room remained filled with the weight of unspoken tensions, the delicate balance between loyalty and rebellion hanging precariously in the air.
