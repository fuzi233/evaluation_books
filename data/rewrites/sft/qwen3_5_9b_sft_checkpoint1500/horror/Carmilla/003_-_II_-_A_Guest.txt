II. A Guest

I must confess to telling you a tale so singular, so steeped in the uncanny, that it demands every ounce of your faith in my honesty to accept it as fact. Yet, it is true—a truth witnessed by my own eyes.

It was a sweet summer evening, the air heavy with the scent of blooming woods, when my father invited me to accompany him on a stroll through the forest vista bordering our estate. As we walked, the path winding beneath the canopy, he spoke of a visitor.

"General Spielsdorf cannot arrive sooner than I had hoped," he murmured, his step slowing as the shadows lengthened. "He was to pay us a visit of several weeks, bringing with him his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt. I have never seen her, though I have heard whispers of her charm. For weeks, I have dreamed of this new acquaintance, of the happy days she would bring to our quiet life."

His disappointment was palpable, a heavy weight in his tone. "How soon does he come?" I asked, feeling a pang of shared sorrow.

"Not until autumn," he replied, his voice dropping. "Perhaps two months yet. And I am relieved, my dear, that you never laid eyes on Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt."

"And why?" I pressed, confused and suddenly chilled.

"Because the poor young lady is dead," he said simply. "I neglected to mention it, for you were not in the room when the General's letter arrived this evening."

Shock washed over me. In letters written six or seven weeks prior, the General had merely noted that his niece was unwell, suggesting nothing of mortal peril. Now, the reality was absolute.

"Here," he said, handing me a crumpled sheet of paper. "Read it. I fear he is in a state of profound affliction; the words themselves seem to tremble with madness."

We sat upon a rough bench beneath the sprawling boughs of majestic lime trees. The sun was dipping below the horizon, painting the sylvan landscape in hues of melancholy gold and violet. A gentle stream, flowing past our home and beneath the ancient stone bridge, mirrored the fading crimson of the sky. Unfolding the letter, I found it a torrent of emotion—vehement, contradictory, and torn by grief. I read it twice, then aloud to my father, struggling to find a logical thread within the chaos.

*"I have lost my darling daughter, for as such I loved her,"* the scrawled words began. *"During the final days of Bertha's illness, I could not write. Before then, I knew not of her danger. I have lost her, and now learn _all_ too late. She died in the peace of innocence... The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all. I thought I was welcoming innocence and gaiety into my house, a companion for my lost Bertha. Heavens! What a fool have I been!"*

The General cursed his blindness, his conceit, his obstinacy, all "too late." He vowed to dedicate his remaining days to hunting down a monster, a creature that had slipped past his defenses. He begged us to pray for him, promising to reveal the truth in two months' time, if he lived.

Though I had never seen Bertha Rheinfeldt, tears pricked my eyes at the tragedy. The sun had fully surrendered to the dark, leaving us in a soft, clear twilight. We lingered, speculating on the violence and incoherence of the General's words, nearly a mile from the road leading to the front of the castle. By then, the moon hung brilliantly above, casting a silver glow over the world.

As we reached the drawbridge, we encountered Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine. They had removed their bonnets to enjoy the exquisite luminescence. Their voices bubbled forth in animated chatter as we joined them. Together, we gazed upon the scene: the glade we had traversed lay before us, the narrow road winding away under lordly trees, vanishing into the thickening forest. To our right, the road crossed the steep, picturesque bridge, near a ruined tower that once guarded the pass. Beyond, an abrupt eminence rose, cloaked in trees and shadowed by grey, ivy-choked rocks.

A thin film of mist drifted over the grasslike plains like smoke, veiling the distances, while the river flashed faintly in the moonlight. It was a scene of profound serenity, enchanting in its vagueness, though the news I carried cast a shadow over its beauty.

My father, who relished the picturesque, and I stood in silence. Behind us, the two governesses discoursed passionately on the scenery and the moon. Madame Perrodon, rotund, middle-aged, and deeply romantic, sighed poetry into the night. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, claiming descent from a German line and embracing metaphysics, declared that the moon's intense brilliance signaled spiritual activity. She spoke of the moon's manifold effects: acting on dreams, inciting lunacy, influencing the nerves, and exerting marvelous physical forces. She recounted the tale of her cousin, a sailor, who had fallen asleep on deck under such a moon. Upon waking from a nightmare of an old woman clawing his face, his features had remained permanently twisted, never regaining equilibrium.

"The moon, this night," she concluded, pointing toward the castle, "is full of idyllic and magnetic influence. See how all the windows flash and twinkle with that silvery splendor, as if unseen hands have lit the rooms to welcome fairy guests."

There is a certain pleasure in listening to the idle fancies of others when one feels inclined to listen, and I gazed on, amused by the ladies' conversation. Then, after a pause, my father quoted Shakespeare.

"In truth I know not why I am so sad. It wearies me: you say it wearies you; But how I got it—came by it."

"I forget the rest," he continued, his voice somber. "Yet I feel as if some great misfortune hangs over us. Perhaps the General's afflicted letter has played a part."

Just then, the unwonted clatter of carriage wheels and the rhythmic pounding of many hooves shattered the stillness. Approaching from the high ground overlooking the bridge, an equipage emerged. First, two horsemen crossed the bridge, followed swiftly by a carriage drawn by four horses, with two men riding behind. It appeared to be the vehicle of a person of high rank, and we all watched with growing fascination.

Moments later, the drama unfolded. As the carriage crested the summit of the steep bridge, one of the lead horses took fright. Panic spread instantly to the team, causing them to break into a wild gallop. They dashed between the horsemen, thundering down the road toward us with hurricane-like speed.

The scene was exacerbated by the clear, long-drawn screams of a female voice rising from the carriage window. Driven by curiosity and horror, we advanced. I moved forward in silence, while the others uttered cries of terror.

Our suspense did not endure long. Just short of the castle drawbridge, a magnificent lime tree stood by the roadside, opposite an ancient stone cross. At the sight of the cross, the horses, moving at a terrifying pace, swerved violently. One wheel struck the projecting roots of the tree, sending the carriage crashing onto its side, two wheels suspended in the air. The men scrambled to remove the traces.

A lady, tall and commanding, stepped out, her hands clasped, lifting a handkerchief to her eyes. Through the open door, a young lady was lifted out, appearing lifeless. My father, eager to display his medical knowledge, rushed to the elder lady, offering his aid and the resources of our castle. The lady, declaring herself the mother, did not seem to hear him, her gaze fixed solely on the slender girl being placed against the bank.

I approached cautiously. The young lady appeared stunned, though certainly not dead. My father, having checked her pulse, assured the distraught mother that it was faint and irregular but undeniably present. The lady clasped her hands, looking upward in a momentary transport of gratitude, before breaking out into a theatrical display of distress.

She was a striking woman, handsome and tall, dressed in black velvet, her countenance pale yet proud.

"Who was ever born to calamity?" she cried, her voice trembling. "Here am I, on a journey of life and death, and to lose an hour is to lose all. My child cannot resume her route for hours, perhaps days. I must leave her; I dare not delay. How far, sir, is the nearest village? I must leave her there. I shall not see my darling again, nor even hear of her, until my return in three months."

I plucked at my father's coat, whispering urgently, "Oh, Papa, please ask her to let her stay with us! It would be delightful."

"If Madame will entrust her child to the care of my daughter and our good governess, Madame Perrodon, and permit her to remain as our guest, under my charge, until her return," my father offered, bowing slightly, "it will confer a distinction and an obligation upon us. We shall treat her with all the care and devotion that so sacred a trust deserves."

"I cannot do that, sir," the lady said, her voice ragged. "It would be to task your kindness and chivalry too cruelly."
