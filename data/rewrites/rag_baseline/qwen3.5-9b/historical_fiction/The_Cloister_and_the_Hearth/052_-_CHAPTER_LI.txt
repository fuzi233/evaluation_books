When she descended once more, the transformation was absolute. The storm that had previously convulsed her spirit had settled into a profound, crystalline calm. Her eyes, though glistening with unshed tears, held no longer the feverish glare of bitterness; they were pools of serene resolve.

"Denys," she murmured, her voice soft as falling snow, "I possess the orders. I am to read my lover's letter aloud to his kin."

"And you shall never do that?" Denys challenged, his brow furrowed.

"Aye, I shall."

"I perceive," he observed, searching her face for a crack in her armor, "that some hidden sentiment in the missive has softened your heart toward them."

"Not a jot, Denys. Not a single particle. Yet, though I loathe them as one loathes poison, I shall not disobey my love. Listen to me, Denys: it is a sweetness beyond measure to obey, and the supreme sweetness lies in obeying one who stands afar, whose command carries no weight of force, but rests entirely upon the altar of my devotion. Ah, Gerard, my darling, were you here beside me, I might have sneered at your commands, dismissed your people as I have every reason to despise them. But now, if you had bidden me wade into the raging sea to read your sweet letter to the very jaws of the sharks, I would have done so without hesitation. Therefore, Denys, tell his mother I carry the letter. If she and hers are willing to listen, I am their humble servant; let them set their hour, and I shall seat them with the utmost courtesy, welcoming them as best I may."

Denys departed to deliver this tidings to Catherine. He found the household gathered around the table, the air thick with anticipation. Upon announcing the arrival of a long letter from Gerard, a wave of joy rippled through the room. Seizing the moment, Denys added, "Moreover, her heart has been softened; she intends to read it to you personally. You are to choose your own time."

"What does she suppose that none but she can decipher?" Catherine asked, her skepticism cutting through the festive atmosphere.

"Let her dispatch the letter, and we shall read it ourselves," someone suggested.

"Nay, but, mother," interjected little Kate, her voice trembling slightly, "perhaps she cannot bear to part with it from her grasp; she loves him so dearly."

"Does she think we intend to steal it?" Cornelis scoffed, his cynicism sharp. "She merely seeks to wedge herself into our fold by means of this correspondence."

Denys cast a look of utter disdain upon the speaker. "There speaks a base heart," he declared. "La camarade hates you all like venom. Oh, mistake me not, dame; I defend her not, for so it is. Yet, despite her spleen at a mere word from Gerard, she proffers to read you his letter with her own pretty mouth, possessing a voice like honey—it is indeed a fair offer."

"'Tis so, mine honest soldier," the patriarch, Eli, replied gravely. "It merits a civil response. Hold your tongues, ye women, and I shall answer her. Tell her that I, her father-in-law, setting aside all ancient grudges, do hereby thank her for this grace. Should she desire double thanks, let her send my son's letter by your faithful hand. I shall read it to his flesh and blood, and thereafter, I shall return to her with such surety and fidelity as I am Eli a Dierich, a William a Luke, free burgher of Tergou, like my forbears, and like them, a man of my word."

"Ay, and a man who is better than his word," Catherine cried out, her eyes shining with a mixture of pride and sorrow. "The only one I have ever encountered."

"Hold thy peace, wife," Eli commanded gently.

"Are you a man of sense, Eli, a dirk, a chose?" Denys shouted, unable to contain his frustration. "That she-comrade will be right glad to obey Gerard while avoiding facing you all, whom she detests as wormwood, save for your presence. Bless ye, the world has changed; she is wholly submissive today: 'Obedience is honey,' she quoth. In truth, it is a sweetmeat she cannot resist, consuming so little of it because of her fair face and her mellow tongue. Furthermore, with her capacity to fly into fits that terrify us soldiers to death when thwarted, and to chide us one moment only to pet us like lambs the next, she has forged two of the most crawling slaves ever seen out of two honest swashbucklers. I am the ironing ruffian, and the other washes."

"What comes next?" Eli asked, weary of the domestic turbulence.

"Why, whenever the brat enters the world, I shall rock the cradle, and the other knave shall wash the tucker and bib. So, I shall fetch the letter on the instant. Will you allow me to remain and hear it read?"

"Else our hearts were black as coal," Catherine answered, her tone leaving no room for refusal.

Thus, Denys hastened to retrieve the letter. He returned moments later, crestfallen.

"She will not yield it to me, nor to you, nor to any living soul," he reported.

"I knew she would not," Cornelis muttered.

"Whisht! Whisht!" Eli commanded, silencing the room. "Let Denys recount his tale."

"'Nay,'" Denys insisted, "'be ruled by me.'"

"Not I," she had replied.

"Well, but," Denys continued, recalling her words, "'that same honey Obedience ye spake of.'"

"You are a fool," she had retorted. "Obedience to Gerard is sweet, but obedience to any other body—whoever said that was sweet?"

"At last, she seemed to soften a fraction," Denys recounted, holding up the document. "She gave me a written paper for you, mademoiselle. Here 'tis."

"For me?" little Kate exclaimed, flushing crimson.

"Give that here!" Eli ordered, scanning the handwriting with intense scrutiny. He spoke almost in a whisper, "These be words from the letter. Hearken!"

"And, sweetheart, an if these lines should travel safe to thee, make thou trial of my people's hearts withal. Maybe they are somewhat turned towards me, being far away. If 'tis so they will show it to thee, since now to me they may not. Read, then, this letter! But I do strictly forbid thee to let it from thy hand; and if they still hold aloof from thee, why, then say nought, but let them think me dead. Obey me in this; for, if thou dost disrespect my judgment and my will in this, thou lovest me not."

A heavy silence descended upon the room as Gerard's words, copied faithfully by Margaret, were passed around and inspected.

"Well," Catherine said finally, breaking the quiet, "that is another matter. But methinks 'tis for her to come to us, not we to her."

"Alas, mother! What odds does that make?" little Kate pleaded.

"Much," Eli replied sternly. "Tell her we are too numerous to journey to her, and bid her hither, the sooner the better."

When Denys had withdrawn, Eli confessed privately to his wife that it was a bitter pill to swallow. "When that lass crosses my threshold, all the mischief and misery she hath wrought here will seem to tumble in at the doors in one heap. But what could I do, wife? We must hear the news of Gerard. I saw that in thine eyes, and felt it in my own heart. She is backed by our undutiful but still beloved son, and thus she stands stronger than we, bringing our noses down to the grindstone, the sly, cruel jade. But never heed. We shall hear the letter; and then let her depart unblessed, as she came unwelcome."

"Make your mind easy," Catherine soothed him. "She will not come at all." A tone of deep regret lingered in her voice.

Shortly thereafter, Richart arrived from Amsterdam, awaited hourly. Grave and dignified in his burgher's robe, gold chain, ruff, and furred cap, he was received not merely with affection but with profound respect; for he had risen a step higher than his parents, and such ascents were marked in medieval society almost as visibly as the stairs themselves. Admitted to the family council in due course, he displayed plainly, though not discourteously, that his pride was deeply wounded by their having deigned to treat with Margaret Brandt.

"I see the temptation," he remarked coolly. "But which of us hath not at times wished one way and done another?"

This remark cast a considerable chill over the older generation. Little Kate, sensing the tension, interjected quickly. "Vex not thyself, dear Richart. Mother says she will not come."

"All the better, sweetheart," Richart replied, his relief palpable. "I fear me, if she do, I shall hie me back to Amsterdam."

Just then, Denys popped his head into the doorway.

"She will be here at three on the great dial."

They all looked at one another in silence.
