"Brass within, and steel without,
With beams on his topcastle strong;
And eighteen pieces of ordinance
He carries on either side along."

Thus sang Sir Andrew Barton of the Pelican, a vessel now resting like an ancient warrior on the sedgy banks of the Thames at Deptford. Drawn high and dry, she serves as a monument to the globe-circling voyages of Sir Francis Drake, upon whose mainmast hang epigrams celebrating her triumphs. It was here, amidst the revelry of a great feast hosted by the Lord Mayor, that Elizabeth I had once knighthooded Drake, though the bridge of planks leading to her ship had collapsed under the weight of the crowd, falling harmlessly upon the throng—a sign, the locals whispered, of a lucky planet guiding the vessel.

Today, however, the atmosphere aboard the Pelican had shifted from jubilation to somber reflection. The Lord Mayor, Sir Edward Osborne, a man whose own chivalry had been forged in the desperate leap from London Bridge to save an infant, presided over a table of England's greatest minds. Beside him sat the Earl of Cumberland and Walter Raleigh, the latter debating the mysteries of the North-West Passage with the quiet scholar Richard Hakluyt and the seasoned traveler Anthony Jenkinson. Across the table, Philip Sidney, having relinquished his seat of honor to allow the virtuosi to converse freely, lamented his confinement to court politics while envying the adventurous spirit of Martin Frobisher.

The conversation turned to the perilous icebergs and the frozen steppes of Siberia, a stark contrast to the wine-filled banquets of the present. Yet, the mood fractured when a waiter announced the arrival of a gentleman seeking Sir Walter Raleigh. Amyas Leigh entered, hesitating in the doorway, his presence commanding silence. "Captain Leigh!" cried the assembly. Osborne, recognizing the delay, asked why he had not simply walked in, but Amyas offered a look to Raleigh that spoke volumes. Pale with shock, Raleigh rose and followed Amyas into a private cabin.

Five minutes later, Amyas emerged alone. In brief, devastating words, he delivered the news: Sir Humphrey Gilbert was dead. The feast dissolved instantly. Osborne, recalling the Egyptian custom of placing a corpse among guests to remind them of mortality, declared the gathering over. "None here can drink with the loss of so great a man still ringing in his ears," he said. The guests departed hurriedly, each whispering, "What will the Queen say?"

Raleigh, Sidney, and Cumberland left in one boat, leaving Amyas and his brother Frank to walk back to Whitehall. The journey was heavy with unspoken grief. Upon reaching their mother's lodgings, they found her absent, a relief that allowed them to retreat to a bay-window overlooking the river. The fading light illuminated the stark contrast between the two brothers. Frank, the courtier, appeared slender, bloodless, and fragile, his fingers delicate as a woman's. Amyas, conversely, was a statue of hardened experience; his locks cropped short, his face bronzed by sun and storm, a scar crossing his temple, and his hand massive and smith-like.

"You look aged," Amyas observed, noting the pallor and sharpness of his brother's features.
"And you," Frank replied with a wry smile, "look like a man who has seen too much of the world."

The conversation drifted to the nature of their lives and the pull of the West. Frank spoke of the ancient poets' belief that Elysium lay beneath the setting sun, a divine instinct driving men to flee eastward toward peace. "I should rather have my face turned to the West when I die," he confessed, admitting his longing to join the Argonauts subduing the earth for God and the Queen.

Amyas, realizing the depth of Frank's resolve, finally voiced the purpose of his journey to London. "I came not merely to see you and my mother, but to ask your advice and her permission to take you with me."
Frank's reaction was immediate and fierce. "Dearest man, a month of it would kill you!" he cried, holding his brother at arm's length, feeling the terrifying thinness of his own frame. Yet, he countered with the philosophy of Thales, claiming the ocean was the mother of all life, and he felt no repugnance in returning to her bosom.

"My mother?" Amyas protested.
"My mother knows all," Frank insisted, citing the prophecies of Doctor Dee. The astrologer had warned that Frank, born under a cold, watery planet, needed the vivifying heat of the sun to survive. He had been told to spend no more winters in the East but to return to the sea-breezes to warm his frozen lungs. Furthermore, stories of men renewed in youth by traveling to Italy and Spain had softened his mother's heart. "She believes," Frank said, his voice trembling. "And I must go. It is not merely love, Amyas—tyrannous and uncontrollable—but honor. My oath."

As Frank paused, gasping for breath from a violent cough, a gentle voice interrupted. "Fools, fools that we were—to take that fantastical vow!" Amyas exclaimed, but the voice belonged to their mother. She had entered silently. "No, my sons," she corrected, her eyes shining with a fierce, maternal resolve. "You vowed for the sake of peace on earth... Blessed are the peacemakers."

Amyas, horrified, begged her not to hate him for taking her first-born away. "God takes him, and not you," she replied, laying her head upon Amyas's chest. She spoke of exceeding honors awaiting them in the West, comparing their destinies to Esau and Jacob. Then, with a winning smile, she proposed joining them. "I could nurse the sick," she said, "or perhaps win back that poor girl you love. I could mend and wash for you. Is it not as easy to play the good housewife afloat as on shore?"

Amyas looked from one to the other, realizing the gravity of her offer. "Mother! You know not what you ask. This is a sterner matter than either of you fancy; one that must be worked out with sharp shot and cold steel."
"How?" they cried in unison, aghast.
"I must pay my men," Amyas explained, his voice hardening. "I must pay them with Spanish gold. And as a loyal subject, I cannot go to the Spanish Main with a clear conscience unless I do all the harm my hand finds to do to her enemies."

Mrs. Leigh shuddered at the thought of Frank becoming "a man of blood," but her gentle heart eventually yielded to the logic of duty. "Frank must at least ask the Queen's leave," she conceded.

The days that followed were a blur of anxiety for Frank. Terrified of the Queen's frown, he spent his time courting Leicester, Hatton, and Sidney, acting as if he were suing for a reprieve from the scaffold. Finally, he secured an audience. He returned pale and exhausted, recounting the Queen's initial anger and her accusations of disloyalty. But when he threw himself at her mercy, pleading that exile from her presence was already a sufficient punishment, she was moved. With angelic pity, she dismissed him, having granted his request.

News of this triumph reached the family through Edmund Spenser, who arrived with Mrs. Leigh. He revealed that the Queen had informed Frank that by undertaking this quest, he had voluntarily forfeited the highest earthly honor available: knighthood. "She told him this day," Spenser said, "that by going on this quest he deprived himself of that highest earthly honor."

Frank smiled, unfazed. "I have lost nothing after all. There is still an order of knighthood in which I may win my spurs, even though her majesty refuse me the accolade."
"What, then? You will not take it from a foreign prince?"
Frank laughed softly. "Have you never read of that knighthood which is eternal in the heavens? Of those true cavaliers whom John saw in Patmos, riding on white horses, clothed in fine linen, knights-errant in the everlasting war against the False Prophet and the Beast? Let me but become worthy of their ranks hereafter, what matter whether I be called Sir Frank on earth?"

With the Queen's permission secured and Spenser's poetic encouragement given, the preparations for the voyage began in earnest. Frank mortgaged his farm; Will Cary did the same. Old Salterne, grumbling at the expense, forced the adventurers to accept his two-hundred-ton ship and five hundred pounds toward outfitting her. Mrs. Leigh worked day and night, sewing clothes and preparing comforts, while Amyas contributed his time and intellect, overseeing every rope and nail with the old merchant.

Recruitment was led by Cary and the enthusiastic John Brimblecombe, who preached a crusade against the Spaniards through the local towns. Amyas, wary of haste after previous disasters, selected only the finest men. Among them was a peculiar character named Parracombe, a former schoolfellow who had drunk his inheritance and now sought redemption at sea. His presence brought a tale of confusion involving an "Egyptian" fortune-teller and a suspicious Popish ostler who had vanished from the Mariners' Rest inn, hinting at deeper conspiracies against the Queen, though nothing could be proven.

Also joining was Salvation Yeo, nearly wild with anticipation to fight the Spaniards once more. He brought with him a letter from Sir Francis Drake, detailing his own projects to drain the silt from Plymouth harbor, and a list of practical naval hints from Captain John Hawkins regarding ordnance and the prevention of scurvy. These notes proved invaluable to Amyas.

By November 15th, 1583, the tall ship *Rose* dropped anchor at Appledore Pool. She was a formidable vessel, carrying a hundred men, ample provisions, and a heavy armament of culverins, swivels, muskets, and pikes. The following Sunday, the entire crew received communion at Northam Church amidst a massive crowd. As the ship hove anchor and sailed out over the Bar before a soft east wind, accompanied by the music of sacbut, fife, and drum, and the discharge of all ordnance, the cliffs and quays erupted in cheers and tearful blessings.

Mrs. Leigh, having kissed her sons one last time at the altar steps, climbed to a rocky knoll outside the churchyard. She watched the *Rose* glide out between the yellow denes, shrinking hour by hour until her hull sank below the dim horizon and her white sails faded into the gray Atlantic mist. Bowing her head in worship, she returned home to loneliness and prayer, leaving her sons to their chivalrous adventure in the boundless West.
