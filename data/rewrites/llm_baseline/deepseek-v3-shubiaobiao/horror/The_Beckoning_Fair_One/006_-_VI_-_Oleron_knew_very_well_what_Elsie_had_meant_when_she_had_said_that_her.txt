Oleron understood. The postcard Elsie had promised would never come now—because she knew. She knew, at last, that he did not want her.

Yet she came anyway.

A week had not passed before her unannounced knock rattled the door. Her voice, strained and thin, carried from the landing. She would not stay, she insisted. It took pleading to draw her inside.

Her excuse was flimsy—some market for short stories he might consider. He thanked her, hollowly. Then, duty done, she was already retreating, eager to escape. Oleron did not stop her. Even he could see through the lie. He followed her down the stairs.

But the house had other plans for Elsie Bengough.

A sharp crack—splintering wood—a choked cry. Her foot had punched through a stair. Oleron moved swiftly, alarm tightening his throat.

Her laughter was frayed at the edges. *"Too heavy, that’s all. Getting fat—"*

He dropped to his knees, fingers scraping at broken edges. The wood was rotten, though he’d passed over it a hundred times without incident. Her boot was torn; beneath, the skin would be raw.

*"I’ll be too much for anything soon,"* she muttered, half-sobbing, half-laughing.

She refused to climb back up. Refused to let him see the damage.

*"No—just let me go. Let me leave. I’m not—"* Her voice fractured. *"I’m not wanted here."*

The words struck like a slap. His head jerked back.

Her hand cut the air between them, sharp as a blade.

*"Don’t. Not like that. Not some half-hearted kindness. If it can’t be real, then let me keep what little pride I have left."*

What could he say? He didn’t love her.

*"Let me go,"* she whispered. *"Let me take what’s left of me out of this place."*

He offered his coat, a doctor, his arm—all refused. She limped away, past the jagged *"To Let"* signs, as if the very boards might lash out again if she lingered.

---

Night fell. Oleron did not eat. He paced, haunted by her voice—

If only he could force himself to love her. But it was impossible. She would have to stay away now. For her sake, he would make sure of it.

Yet as darkness thickened, his thoughts twisted. Where was she now? Staring at black water? Testing the weight of a rope? His mind conjured inquests, Barrett’s testimony, the wound on her hand, the ruined ankle—

No. Morbid fantasy. He forced his thoughts elsewhere.

The house creaked around him, alive with whispers.

Then—a sound.

Soft. Persistent. A slow, silken drag, faint as a breath.

At first, he dismissed it. But it returned, again and again, growing louder, more deliberate.

His body went rigid.

He knew that sound.

He was on his feet before he realized it, chair crashing behind him.

The rustling came once more—unmistakable now.

A woman’s hair being brushed.

His breath locked in his throat. He stumbled back, hands groping for the wall.

Silence.

Then—movement. A skittering? A mouse, surely. But the other sound—that other *thing*—was gone.

He shouted into the dark.

No answer. Only his own voice, trembling back at him.

Matches. He needed light. His hands shook as he fumbled along the mantel, sending a box clattering to the floor. The candle flared.

The room was empty.

But it wasn’t.

Something had been here. Something *was* here.

Oleron had once laughed at the idea of the house shaping its own ghost, weaving itself into a haunting. But now he knew—the past had already done the work.

The tenant was not just him.

And tonight, it had announced itself.

With the sound of a woman, unseen, brushing her hair in the dark.
