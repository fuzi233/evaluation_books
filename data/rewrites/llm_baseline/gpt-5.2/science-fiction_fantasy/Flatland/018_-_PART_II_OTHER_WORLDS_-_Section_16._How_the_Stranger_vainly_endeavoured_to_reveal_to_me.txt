The last tremor of my Wife’s Peace-cry slid down the corridor and thinned into the house’s familiar hush—the domestic hush I had always trusted, stitched together from the soft rasp of linen, the tiny tick of cooling metal in the kitchen range, and the far-off, well-mannered murmur of our street. In that brief calm I meant only to do what courtesy required: approach the Stranger, offer him a seat, and keep the evening from becoming any stranger than it already was.

Yet the moment I turned fully toward him, the room’s soundscape seemed to tilt.

He stood there without the least hint of angularity, smooth as an oil-slick in still water—only his size and brightness would not hold steady. He shifted by such minute gradations—fatter, thinner; duller, whiter—as if some unseen hand were turning a lantern wick while kneading a circle of dough at the same time. No Figure I had ever met behaved so. The effect was not merely visual; it pricked the ear, too, like a silent wrong note. The air around him felt overquiet, the way a kitchen feels when the hearth has gone out and you suddenly notice how much your comfort depended on flame’s low roar.

A thought, sharp as a cleaver: Irregular. Burglar. Cut-throat Isosceles masquerading behind a Circle’s voice, admitted by my Wife’s flustered propriety, now waiting to drive an acute angle into me.

Fogless evenings are a danger to the cautious. In a sitting-room stripped of its usual haze—and this season was uncommonly dry—Sight Recognition grows unreliable at close distances, as if a cook, deprived of aroma, were forced to judge a stew by color alone. Fear rose in me with an ugly taste, like burnt fat.

“You must permit me, Sir—” I blurted, and, desperate, I did what no well-bred Square should ever do to a Circle: I reached out and felt him.

My Wife had spoken true. There was no angle to catch the touch, no roughness, no unevenness, no deceitful edge. The surface under my hand was continuous perfection—like the rim of a well-thrown pot, or the smooth curve of a ladle polished by generations. I walked around him, starting from his eye and returning to it again, my fingers following the contour with the slow attention of one who tests a pastry crust for hidden bubbles. Always the same flawless roundness. A Circle, and a better one than most.

Shame flooded me—hot, immediate, like the embarrassment of tasting a dish before the guest has been served. I stammered apologies until the Stranger’s patience frayed.

“Have you felt me enough by this time?” he said. “Are you not introduced to me yet?”

“Most illustrious Sir,” I replied, forcing my voice into the respectful register, “excuse an awkwardness born not of ignorance but of surprise. And I beseech you—reveal my indiscretion to no one, least of all to my Wife. But before your Lordship continues, may I ask—whence do you come?”

“From Space, from Space, Sir: whence else?”

The word struck my ear like a foreign spice—familiar in name, ungrasped on the tongue. “Pardon me, my Lord,” said I, “but are we not already in Space—you and I—this very moment?”

“Pooh! what do you know of Space? Define it.”

“Space,” I said, clinging to what I knew as one clings to a family recipe, “is height and breadth indefinitely prolonged.”

“Exactly: you see you do not even know what Space is. You think it is of Two Dimensions only; but I have come to announce to you a Third—height, breadth, and length.”

“Your Lordship is pleased to be merry. We also speak of length and height, or breadth and thickness—four names for Two Dimensions.”

“But I mean not only three names, but Three Dimensions.”

“Then,” I pressed, “would your Lordship indicate—if it be possible—where this Third Dimension lies? In what direction does it run?”

“I came from it,” he said, with a weariness that sounded like a man forced to explain to a child why smoke rises. “It is up above and down below.”

“Northward and Southward, my Lord?”

“I mean nothing of the kind. I mean a direction in which you cannot look, because you have no eye in your side.”

At that, irritation and incredulity mingled in me. “My Lord, a moment’s inspection will show that I have a perfect luminary at the juncture of two of my sides.”

“Yes,” he answered, “but to see into Space you ought to have an eye not on your Perimeter, but on your side—what you would probably call your inside; but we in Spaceland should call it your side.”

“An eye in my inside?” The notion tasted of nonsense, like sweetened vinegar. “An eye in my stomach! Your Lordship jests.”

“I am in no jesting humour. I tell you I come from Space—or, since you will not understand what Space means, from the Land of Three Dimensions—whence I but lately looked down upon your Plane, which you call Space forsooth. From that position of advantage I discerned all that you speak of as SOLID—your houses, your churches, your very chests and safes—yes, even your insides and stomachs, all lying open and exposed to my view.”

Such words are easily spoken in a stranger’s voice. I kept my tone civil. “Such assertions are easily made, my Lord.”

“But not easily proved, you mean. But I mean to prove mine. When I descended here, I saw your four Sons, the Pentagons, each in his apartment, and your two Grandsons the Hexagons. I saw your youngest Hexagon remain a while with you and then retire to his room, leaving you and your Wife alone. I saw your Isosceles servants—three in number—in the kitchen at supper, and the little Page in the scullery. Then I came here, and how do you think I came?”

My mouth went dry. The kitchen. Supper. I could almost hear it now in memory: the clink of bowls, the hush of disciplined chewing, the cautious whispering of servants who know their place. Those small, domestic sounds—the honest ecology of a household at rest—he had named as though he had stood among them. It unsettled me more than any boast about “insides.” If he had truly “seen” the kitchen without passing through it, what did that make of our walls, our privacy, our old family habits—everything we keep safe by doors and custom?

“Through the roof,” I said at last, grasping for the ordinary.

“Not so. Your roof, as you know very well, has been recently repaired and has no aperture by which even a Woman could penetrate. I tell you I come from Space. Are you not convinced by what I have told you of your children and household?”

I rallied to skepticism, because skepticism is sometimes the only lid one can clamp on fear. “Your Lordship must be aware,” I said, “that such facts might be ascertained by any neighbour with means and leisure to pry.”

He seemed to turn inward, muttering. “What must I do? Stay; one more argument suggests itself.”

Then, as if changing from command to instruction, he asked, “When you see a Straight Line—your wife, for example—how many Dimensions do you attribute to her?”

My ears burned at his casual use of domestic language, as though my Wife were an exhibit. Still, I answered with the pride of a trained mind. “Your Lordship would treat me as if I were one of the vulgar who suppose a Woman is really a Straight Line, and only of One Dimension. No, my Lord; we Squares are better advised. A Woman, though popularly called a Straight Line, is really and scientifically a very thin Parallelogram, possessing Two Dimensions like the rest of us—length and breadth.”

“But the very fact that a Line is visible implies it possesses yet another Dimension.”

“I have just acknowledged her breadth,” I said, trying not to let my voice sharpen. “We see her length; we infer her breadth—slight though it be, it can be measured.”

“You do not understand me,” he said. “When you see a Woman, you ought—besides inferring her breadth—to see her length, and to SEE what we call her HEIGHT, though that last Dimension is infinitesimal in your country. If a Line were mere length without height, it would cease to occupy Space and become invisible.”

I felt as though he were taking words I had eaten all my life—Space, visible, dimension—and grinding them into a paste that no longer resembled food. “My Lord,” I confessed, “I do not in the least understand you. When we see a Line, we see length and BRIGHTNESS. If the brightness disappears, the Line is extinguished and, as you say, ceases to occupy Space. Am I to suppose that your Lordship gives to brightness the title of a Dimension, and that what we call ‘bright’ you call ‘high’?”

“No,” he said, almost sharply. “By height I mean a Dimension like your length—only, with you, height is not easily perceptible, being extremely small.”

I gathered myself the way one gathers ingredients back into measured order. “My Lord, your assertion is easily put to the test. You say I have a Third Dimension called height. Dimension implies direction and measurement. Do but measure my height—or merely indicate the direction in which it extends—and I will become your convert. Otherwise your Lordship’s own understanding must hold me excused.”

He fell silent. In that silence I heard my own house again: the faint settling of the beams, the soft distance of the servants’ quarters, the way quiet in a well-run home has layers. His voice returned, lower, as if he were speaking not to my ear but to something beneath it.

“I can do neither,” he muttered to himself. “How shall I convince him? Surely a plain statement of facts followed by ocular demonstration ought to suffice.”

Then, aloud: “Listen to me. You are living on a Plane. What you style Flatland is the vast level surface of what I may call a fluid, on, or in, the top of which you and your countrymen move about, without rising above it or falling below it. I am not a plane Figure, but a Solid. You call me a Circle; but in reality I am not a Circle, but an infinite number of Circles, of size varying from a Point to a Circle of thirteen inches in diameter, one placed on the top of the other. When I cut through your plane as I am now doing, I make in your plane a section which you, very rightly, call a Circle. For even a Sphere—which is my proper name in my own country—if he manifest himself at all to an inhabitant of Flatland, must needs manifest himself as a Circle.”

The words “fluid,” “cut,” “section” landed with an odd physicality, like talk of slicing a loaf. I pictured—against my will—a stack of round flatbreads, from smallest to largest, piled with perfect alignment. Yet the taste of the concept remained wrong, because there was no oven in my mind for such bread.

He pressed on. “Do you not remember—” and at that the air seemed to stir with the memory of the previous night’s dream, Lineland written like an afterimage upon my brain—“how, when you entered the realm of Lineland, you were compelled to manifest yourself to the King, not as a Square, but as a Line, because that Linear Realm had not Dimensions enough to represent the whole of you, but only a slice or section of you? In precisely the same way, your country of Two Dimensions is not spacious enough to represent me, a being of Three, but can only exhibit a slice or section of me—which is what you call a Circle.”

I did remember: the King’s thin tyranny, his cramped vocabulary, the way his language seemed built to keep experience small. And I felt, uneasily, the same power at work here but reversed—this Stranger’s grand lexicon pressing down upon my native terms until they bent. Even “Space,” our old honest word, was being annexed and repurposed, as if a conqueror had slapped his own labels on our pantry jars.

“The diminished brightness of your eye indicates incredulity,” he said. “But now prepare to receive proof positive. You cannot see more than one of my sections at a time, for you have no power to raise your eye out of the plane; but you can at least see that, as I rise in Space, so my sections become smaller. See now—I will rise.”

I saw no rising. No footstep, no drift. There was no accompanying creak of floor or sigh of displaced air—none of the honest sounds by which a body announces movement in a room. Yet the Circle before me shrank. Not by stepping away, not by fading like a lamp; he diminished as a coin might diminish in a tale told badly, becoming smaller and smaller until he was a mere bead of light, and then—nothing.

I blinked hard, once, twice, as if to clear steam from a cook’s spectacles. The room remained. The chair legs stood where they stood. The faint household noises continued, indifferent. But the Stranger was gone.

And then, from a nowhere that had no direction, a hollow voice emerged—so near it seemed to sound inside my ribs, close to the heart’s own percussion.

“Am I quite gone? Are you convinced now? Well, now I will gradually return to Flatland and you shall see my section become larger and larger.”

A Circle returned—first a point, then a small bright ring, swelling with steady certainty until he regained his former size. If sound is the soul of place, then his voice had committed a violence: it had entered my most private acoustic chamber without crossing any threshold. My skin crawled with it.

When he was full-sized again, he heaved a deep sigh, and in that sigh I heard the fatigue of a teacher whose pupil cannot taste what is being offered. My silence told him all.

To me, though I had witnessed the facts, the causes remained black as an unlit hearth. I had seen a Circle diminish, vanish, and return. But “upward,” “below,” “solid”—these were seasonings I could not dissolve into my understanding. And so, because the mind hates a vacuum, I began to fill the gap with older, homelier superstitions: perhaps he was no Circle at all, but a clever juggler; perhaps the nursery tales of enchanters and magicians, whispered over kneading boards and washbasins, were true after all.

After a long pause he muttered, as if bargaining with himself, “One resource alone remains, if I am not to resort to action. I must try the method of Analogy.”

Again he held silence for a long while, letting the room’s ordinary noises reassert their frail authority. Then he spoke.

“Tell me, Mr. Mathematician; if a Point moves Northward and leaves a luminous wake, what name would you give to the wake?”

“A straight Line,” I answered at once—thankful, absurdly, for the familiar.

“And a straight Line has how many extremities?”

“Two.”

“Now conceive the Northward straight Line moving parallel to itself, East and West, so that every point in it leaves behind it the wake of a straight Line. Suppose it moves through a distance equal to the original straight Line. What name will you give to the Figure thereby formed?”

“A Square,” I said, with the relief of one who has found again the taste of bread he knows.

“And how many sides has a Square? How many angles?”

“Four sides and four angles.”

“Now stretch your imagination a little,” he said, and the phrase itself felt like being asked to stretch dough beyond its strength, “and conceive a Square in Flatland moving parallel to itself upward.”

“What? Northward?”

“No, not Northward; upward—out of Flatland altogether. If it moved Northward, the Southern points in the Square would have to move through positions previously occupied by the Northern points. But that is not my meaning. I mean that every Point in you—for you are a Square and will serve my illustration—every Point in you, that is to say in what you call your inside, is to pass upwards through Space in such a way that no Point shall pass through the position previously occupied by any other Point; but each Point shall describe a straight Line of its own. This is all in accordance with Analogy; surely it must be clear to you.”

My patience thinned. It is one thing to be instructed; it is another to be told that the failure of comprehension is a moral fault. I restrained the urge—sudden, violent—to rush at him and fling him out of my house, out of my world, out of the very vocabulary by which he bullied my senses.

“And what may be the nature of the Figure,” I asked, “which I am to shape out by this motion you denote by ‘upward’? I presume it is describable in the language of Flatland.”

“Oh, certainly,” he said. “It is all plain and simple, and in strict accordance with Analogy—only you must not speak of the result as a Figure, but as a Solid. But I will describe it to you. Or rather not I, but Analogy.

“We began with a single Point, which—being itself a Point—has only ONE terminal Point. One Point produces a Line with TWO terminal Points. One Line produces a Square with FOUR terminal Points. Now you can give yourself the answer: 1, 2, 4 are evidently in Geometrical Progression. What is the next number?”

“Eight,” I said, though the word sat on my tongue like a name from a dead dialect—correct, yet unmoored from lived experience.

“Exactly. The one Square produces a SOMETHING-WHICH-YOU-DO-NOT-AS-YET-KNOW-A-NAME-FOR-BUT-WHICH-WE-CALL-A-CUBE with EIGHT terminal Points. Now are you convinced?”

“Has this Creature sides,” I demanded, “as well as angles—or what you call ‘terminal Points’?”

“Of course; and all according to Analogy. But, by the way, not what YOU call sides, but what WE call sides. You would call them SOLIDS.”

“And how many solids,” I pressed, “or sides will appertain to this Being whom I am to generate by the motion of my inside in an ‘upward’ direction, and whom you call a Cube?”

“How can you ask? And you a mathematician!” His impatience cracked, like a pot set too quickly on high heat. “The side of anything is always—if I may so say—one Dimension behind the thing. Consequently, as there is no Dimension behind a Point, a Point has 0 sides; a Line, if I may say, has 2 sides (for the Points of a Line may be called by courtesy its sides); a Square has 4 sides; 0, 2, 4—what progression do you call that?”

“Arithmetical.”

“And what is the next number?”

“Six.”

“Exactly. Then you see you have answered your own question. The Cube which you will generate will be bounded by six sides—that is to say, six of your insides. You see it all now, eh?”

Six of my insides.

The phrase landed like a desecration. My mind flashed—not with diagrams—but with the kitchen again: the servants’ suppers, the careful saving of crusts, the inherited dishes whose flavors carried the memory of mothers and grandmothers. The home is made of boundaries—of walls, of doors, of permitted sounds, of words that mean what they have always meant. And here was a Stranger, claiming new dominion over direction itself, over the very grammar of the body, as though my “inside” were merely a panel to be counted and repurposed.

“Monster!” I cried, and my voice was raw, stripped of polite tone. “Be thou juggler, enchanter, dream, or devil, no more will I endure thy mockeries. Either thou or I must perish.”

And with that I hurled myself upon him.
