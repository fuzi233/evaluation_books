“Your cousin the Countess called on mother while you were away,” Janey Archer announced to her brother on the evening of his return. 

The dining room was a sanctuary of acoustic balance, insulated from the abrasive, metallic grind of the encroaching city streets by heavy damask draperies. At the head of the table, Newland meticulously portioned a crown roast of spring lamb, its crust redolent of toasted coriander, wild mint, and crushed garlic—a recipe salvaged from a fading lineage of Knickerbocker cooks, whose rich culinary dialects were rapidly being erased by the bland, utilitarian palates of the modern era. He glanced up in surprise, the silver carving knife pausing over the delicate meat, and saw Mrs. Archer’s gaze demurely bent on her plate of butter-basted asparagus. Mrs. Archer did not regard her seclusion from the world as a reason for being forgotten by it; and Newland guessed she was slightly annoyed by his surprise at Madame Olenska’s visit.

“She had on a black velvet polonaise with jet buttons, and a tiny green monkey muff; I never saw her so stylishly dressed,” Janey continued, her voice cutting through the rhythmic, soothing clink of sterling silver against bone china. “She came alone, early on Sunday afternoon; luckily the fire was lit in the drawing-room. She said she wanted to know us because you’d been so good to her.”

Newland laughed, savoring a tender morsel of the lamb, letting the complex, gamey notes ground him. “Madame Olenska always takes that tone about her friends. She’s very happy at being among her own people again.”

“Yes, so she told us,” said Mrs. Archer, speaking the dominant lexicon of Old New York—an unspoken dialect of raised eyebrows and pursed lips that held immense power over those who understood it. “I must say she seems thankful to be here.”

“I hope you liked her, mother.”

Mrs. Archer drew her lips together. “She certainly lays herself out to please, even when she is calling on an old lady.”

“Mother doesn't think her simple,” Janey interjected, her eyes screwed upon her brother’s face.

“It's just my old-fashioned feeling; dear May is my ideal,” said Mrs. Archer.

“Ah,” said her son, swirling the deep, garnet-colored Bordeaux in his glass, “they're not alike.”

Archer had left St. Augustine charged with many messages for old Mrs. Mingott, a woman who reigned over her family like a traditional agrarian elder. A day or two after his return to town, he called on her. Her lower-Manhattan house was a fortress of cultural memory, smelling deeply of cured tobacco, rendering beeswax, and the sharp, bright tang of winter preserves. The old lady received him with unusual warmth; she was grateful to him for persuading the Countess Olenska to give up the idea of a divorce. When he told her that he had deserted the office without leave, rushing down to St. Augustine simply because he hungered for the sight of May, she gave an adipose chuckle, her puff-ball hand patting his knee.

“Ah, ah—so you kicked over the traces, did you? And I suppose Augusta and Welland pulled long faces, and behaved as if the end of the world had come? But little May—she knew better, I'll be bound?”

“I hoped she did; but after all she wouldn't agree to what I'd gone down to ask for.”

“Wouldn't she indeed? And what was that?”

“I wanted to get her to promise that we should be married in April. What's the use of our wasting another year?”

Mrs. Manson Mingott screwed up her little mouth into a grimace of mimic prudery. “‘Ask Mamma,’ I suppose—the usual story. Ah, these Mingotts—all alike! Born in a rut, and you can't root 'em out of it. When I built this house you'd have thought I was moving to California! Nobody ever *had* built above Fortieth Street—no, says I, nor above the Battery either, before Christopher Columbus discovered America. No, no; not one of them wants to be different; they're as scared of it as the small-pox. Ah, my dear Mr. Archer, I thank my stars I'm nothing but a vulgar Spicer; but there's not one of my own children that takes after me but my little Ellen.” 

She broke off, and asked, with the casual irrelevance of an elder who measures time in generational harvests rather than minutes: “Now, why in the world didn't you marry my little Ellen?”

Archer laughed. “For one thing, she wasn't there to be married.”

“No—to be sure; more's the pity. And now it's too late; her life is finished.” She spoke with the cold-blooded complacency of the aged throwing earth into the grave of young hopes.

The young man's heart grew chill. “Can't I persuade you to use your influence with the Wellands, Mrs. Mingott? I wasn't made for long engagements.”

Old Catherine beamed on him approvingly. “No; I can see that. You've got a quick eye. When you were a little boy I've no doubt you liked to be helped first at the dinner table.” She threw back her head with a laugh that made her chins ripple. “Ah, here's my Ellen now!” 

The portières parted, and the sonic environment of the room instantly shifted. Madame Olenska came forward, her footsteps light against the heavy rugs, her face vivid and happy. She held out her hand gaily to Archer while stooping to her grandmother's kiss. 

“I was just saying to him, my dear: ‘Now, why didn't you marry my little Ellen?’”

Madame Olenska looked at Archer, still smiling. “And what did he answer?”

“Oh, my darling, I leave you to find that out! He's been down to Florida to see his sweetheart.”

“Yes, I know.” She still looked at him, her voice carrying the rich, resonant timbre of a foreign dialect that New York society deemed untranslatable. “I went to see your mother, to ask where you'd gone. I sent a note that you never answered, and I was afraid you were ill.”

He muttered something about leaving unexpectedly, the power dynamics of their unspoken words constraining his tongue. He wanted to thank her for visiting his mother, but under the ancestress's malicious eye, the native vocabulary of his gratitude failed him. 

“Look at him—in such hot haste to get married that he took French leave and rushed down to implore the silly girl on his knees!” cried the old lady scornfully. “That's something like a lover—that's the way handsome Bob Spicer carried off my poor mother. It's only my poor Ellen that has kept any of their wicked blood; the rest of them are all model Mingotts.”

Archer was aware that Madame Olenska was still thoughtfully scrutinising him. The gaiety had faded from her eyes. “Surely, Granny, we can persuade them between us to do as he wishes,” she said with a gentle acoustic softness.

Archer rose to go, and as his hand met Madame Olenska's, he felt the heavy silence of her unanswered letter hanging between them. “When can I see you?” he asked at the door.

“Whenever you like; but it must be soon if you want to see the little house again. I am moving next week.” 

A pang shot through him at the memory of his lamplit hours in that low-studded drawing-room, a space where mythic occurrences seemed to integrate seamlessly into everyday domestic routines. “Tomorrow evening?”

She nodded. “Tomorrow; yes; but early. I'm going out.”

The next day was a Sunday. If she were going out, it could only be to Mrs. Lemuel Struthers's—a house where she was sure to meet Beaufort. Archer felt a movement of annoyance; Beaufort represented the worst of modern commercialization, a man who viewed art and sustenance as mere superficial commodities to be purchased. Archer resolved he would not go early, planning to arrive late enough to prevent her departure.

Yet, a singular restlessness drove him to her door by half-past eight. Entering Madame Olenska's hall, he found the domestic harmony disrupted by the presence of unfamiliar hats and overcoats. Beside them, the air hung heavy with the moist, sawdusty scent of prolonged sessions against bar-room walls and the deep, yeasty odor of old wool. Nastasia took his own coat with a fatalistic “Gia!”—a sudden burst of native dialect that reminded Archer of the untranslatability of this household’s true nature.

In the drawing-room, his hostess was absent. Instead, a lady clad in intricately looped and fringed raiment stood by the fire. Beside her, wreathed in a cloud of cigar-smoke, stood Ned Winsett and a gigantic older man with a feebly leonine head. The remnants of a bohemian meal lingered in the room’s olfactory landscape: the earthy, charred aroma of roasted root vegetables and the robust scent of a coarse, hand-torn sourdough, reflecting a deliberate, if chaotic, appreciation for physical sustenance. The three guests stood around an extraordinarily large bouquet of crimson roses on the sofa.

“What they must have cost at this season—though of course it's the sentiment one cares about!” the lady was saying in a sighing staccato. She turned and advanced. “Dear Mr. Archer—almost my cousin Newland! I am the Marchioness Manson.”

Archer bowed. “My Ellen has taken me in for a few days,” she continued. “I came from Cuba, where I have been spending the winter with Spanish friends—the highest nobility of old Castile! But I was called away by our dear great friend here, Dr. Carver. You don't know Dr. Agathon Carver, founder of the Valley of Love Community?”

Dr. Carver inclined his massive head. The Marchioness continued: “Ah, New York—how little the life of the spirit has reached it! But I see you do know Mr. Winsett.”

“Oh, yes—I reached him some time ago; but not by that route,” Winsett said with his dry smile.

“How do you know, Mr. Winsett?” she reproved. “The spirit bloweth where it listeth.”

“List—oh, list!” interjected Dr. Carver in a stentorian murmur that violently disrupted the delicate acoustic ecology of the room. “But do sit down, Mr. Archer. We four have been having a delightful little dinner together. My child expects you; she will be down in a moment.”

Winsett remained on his feet. “I'm afraid I must be off. Please tell Madame Olenska that we shall all feel lost when she abandons our street.” After a brief, caustic exchange with the Marchioness and Dr. Carver, Winsett slipped out. 

Dr. Carver, noting the time on his ponderous gold watch, also reluctantly gathered his mighty limbs to depart for a lecture, leaving Archer his card. When the door closed, Mrs. Manson waved Archer to a seat, sighing.

“Ellen will be down in a moment; and before she comes, I am so glad of this quiet moment with you. I know everything, dear Mr. Archer—my child has told me all you have done for her. Your wise advice: your courageous firmness—thank heaven it was not too late!”

Archer listened with considerable embarrassment. “Madame Olenska exaggerates; I simply gave her a legal opinion.”

“Ah, but in doing it you were the unconscious instrument of Providence!” she cried, dropping her voice. “Little did you know that at that very moment I was being approached from the other side of the Atlantic! By the Count himself—my poor, mad, foolish Olenski; who asks only to take her back on her own terms.”

“Good God!” Archer exclaimed, springing up.

“You are horrified? Yes, of course. He does not defend himself—he casts himself at her feet: in my person.” She tapped her emaciated bosom. “I have his letter here.”

“Has Madame Olenska seen it?” 

“Time—time; I must have time. I know my Ellen—haughty, intractable.”

“But, good heavens, to forgive is one thing; to go back into that hell—”

“Ah, yes,” the Marchioness acquiesced. “But on the material side, Mr. Archer; do you know what she is giving up? Those roses there on the sofa—acres like them, under glass and in the open, in his matchless terraced gardens at Nice! Jewels, sables—but she cares nothing for all these. Art and beauty, those she does care for. Pictures, priceless furniture, music, brilliant conversation—an entire lexicon of sensory and artistic mastery that you've no conception of here! She had it all; the homage of the greatest. Her portrait has been painted nine times by the greatest artists in Europe. Are these things nothing? And the remorse of an adoring husband?”

As she rose to her climax, her face assumed an expression of ecstatic retrospection. She seemed to him to come straight out of the hell from which Ellen Olenska had just escaped, a ghost mourning the erosion of a cultural memory that New York could never replace. 

“She knows nothing yet—of all this?” he asked abruptly.

“Nothing directly. The truth is, Mr. Archer, I have been waiting to see you. I hoped it might be possible to count on your support—to convince you...”

“That she ought to go back? I would rather see her dead!” cried the young man violently.

“Ah,” the Marchioness murmured. She sat in her arm-chair, opening and shutting her ivory fan, the clicking sound rhythmic and sharp. Suddenly she lifted her head, tuning into the subtle acoustic shift in the house. “Here she comes,” she whispered rapidly. Pointing to the garish, commercially cultivated bouquet of crimson roses on the sofa, she added, “Am I to understand that you prefer *that*, Mr. Archer? After all, marriage is marriage... and my niece is still a wife...”
