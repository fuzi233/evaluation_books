Gwendolen’s shoulders slumped with a quiet, unnameable relief when she walked away from Klesmer’s studio—she had weathered his brutal, unvarnished verdict on her actress ambitions before she had to face her aunt and uncle, and the new, sharp-edged clarity of her circumstances had settled over her like a lead shawl. She had steeled herself to dogged, unflinching calm now; every path stretched before her was lined with small, unavoidable humiliations, and she would not break, no matter what burdens they tried to lay at her feet.

The meeting with the Gascoignes was delayed until Monday, when Gwendolen walked to the rectory arm-in-arm with her mother. They detoured first to Sawyer’s Cottage, the drafty little home the rest of her family would soon be moving into, and wandered every narrow inch of its cramped rooms under a harsh midday sun, unsoftened by blinds or curtains that had not yet been salvaged from the rectory’s old stores. The bare walls, scuffed floorboards, tiny garden dotted with wilted cabbage stumps, and yew arbor stuffed full of dust and cobwebs made the space feel more like a discarded closet than a home.

“How will you ever bear it, mamma?” Gwendolen burst out once they were out on the lane again, her voice tight with the frustration she had swallowed the whole time they toured the cottage. “You and the four girls all crammed into that one tiny sitting room, with that garish green-and-yellow wallpaper pressing on your eyes? And I won’t even be there to help you manage it.”

“All that matters is that you won’t have to suffer it too, dear heart,” her mother soothed.

“If I didn’t need to earn money to send home to you,” Gwendolen said sharply, “I would rather squeeze into that cottage with all of you than go be a governess.”

“Don’t set your heart against it before you’ve even tried, Gwendolen,” her mother chided gently. “They say the Bishop’s household is a palace of luxury compared to falling up and down those rickety cottage stairs, listening to crockery rattle at every turn, and the girls chattering over mending. You’ve always loved nice things—this won’t feel half so harsh as you think.”

“It’s all a terrible, wrong dream,” Gwendolen burst out, hot with sudden anger. “I can’t believe Uncle Henry would let you all be shunted off to a hovel like that. He should have found some other way to fix things.”

“Don’t be unreasonable, darling. What else could he possibly have done?” her mother sighed.

“That was his job to figure out!” Gwendolen snapped. To her, who had always lived in a world where her future was pre-written to be bright, admired, and full of ease, the idea that her entire family could sink so far, so fast, felt like a cruel trick played on her personally. Her words were sharper than her heart was, though—she had always cared far more about sounding clever than sounding virtuous, a flaw that softened even her own awareness of how selfish her frustration sounded.

But when her uncle and aunt greeted her with a warmer, softer kindness than they had ever shown her in all the years she’d lived with them, the hot edge of her anger melted into quiet guilt. She watched, humbled, as they spoke of the coming household economies with steady, uncomplaining cheer: Mr. Gascoigne’s quiet goodness, once a little hidden by the small vanities of a comfortable rectory life, shone bright now that fortune had stripped them bare. He had already given up his carriage, sorted through his worn old suits to mend what he had, cut meat from his breakfast, cancelled all the expensive periodicals, pulled his younger sons out of boarding school to teach them himself, and trimmed every unnecessary expense down to the bone. Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna, who had always taken the rector as their model, did not even mourn the small luxuries they had lost; to them, the worst part of their misfortune was how hard the fall had been for Gwendolen’s mother and her sisters.

Anna even set aside her old quiet resentment on Rex’s behalf, her heart softening entirely for the cousin she had once adored. Neither she nor Mrs. Gascoigne had any urge to lecture Gwendolen on the value of hardship; instead, they spent their days rummaging through old linen cupboards for spare curtains and blinds for the cottage, careful not to rub the family’s misfortune in her face, and starting the visit by asking her about her travels, and how good it was for her mother to have her home again.

There was nothing for Gwendolen to reasonably rebel against here, and she found herself sitting straighter, as if she had been called to line up with the rest of the household to face the hard days ahead, when her uncle leaned forward and spoke to her in his steady, kind voice about the work he had done to secure her a position. He had thought of Grandcourt, of course, but a vague, uncertain hope of a wealthy suitor was not something a practical, responsible man like the rector could hang all his niece’s future on. He would do the best he could for her with the hand life had dealt them right now.

“I knew we could not waste a single day, Gwendolen,” he told her. “A good position, where you will be treated with proper respect, does not fall into one’s lap. After looking everywhere, I found you a spot that could not be better for you in Bishop Mompert’s household. I have known the bishop and his wife for years, and that reference will serve you well. Our correspondence has gone very smoothly, though Mrs. Mompert has asked to meet you in person before she makes the offer official. She will be passing through Wanchester on her way to London soon, and has arranged for you to meet her there. I know it will feel like a trying interview, my dear, but you have a little time to prepare.”

“Does she have a specific reason she needs to meet me, uncle?” Gwendolen asked, her mind already racing through a dozen unpleasant possibilities, all of them casting her as a specimen to be inspected and graded before she was deemed acceptable to care for three privileged girls.

The rector smiled reassuringly. “No need to be nervous, love. She simply wants a clearer sense of who you are than my letter could give her. Any mother would be careful about who she hires as a companion for her daughters. I told her you are very young, but she supervises her girls’ education herself, so your age is no problem for her. She’s a woman of great taste and strict principle, and prefers not to hire a French governess for the girls. I am certain your manners and accomplishments will far exceed what she is looking for. The bishop and his wife will oversee all the religious and moral elements of the girls’ education themselves, too.”

Gwendolen could not find the words to reply, but the effort of swallowing down her sharp, immediate hatred of the whole prospect sent a deep, hot flush creeping up her neck and across her face. Anna squeezed her cousin’s hand tight, full of gentle sympathy, and the rector, seeing how hard the blow had landed, hurried to paint a brighter, easier picture of the position.

“I think so highly of this spot that I would have tried to secure it for Anna, if it suited her at all,” he said easily. “It is a proper home, not a grueling job. The word ‘governess’ makes it sound far worse than it is. The bishop’s views are a bit more strictly Low Church than my own—he is a close friend of Lord Grampian’s, you know—but he is never narrow or unkind in public. He has always been a good friend to me, ever since he was a local incumbent here and we had a silly little disagreement about the Bible Society years ago.”

To the rector, his words were a list of all the good, respectable perks of the position—but to Gwendolen, they felt like a handful of snakes being loosed in the room. “Strictly Low Church”… close supervision… moral oversight… the whole prospect loomed before her like a penitentiary, not a home. Panicked, she scrambled for any other option.

“Mamma mentioned there was another position, at a school?” she said, forcing herself to sound calm and reasonable.

“Yes,” the rector said, his tone turning a little apologetic, “but that is nowhere near as good a fit for you. The work is far harder, the hours far longer, and there is no guarantee you would get the spot at all. You might not even have a bedroom of your own there.”

Gwendolen’s memories of strict, crowded schoolrooms made that “alternative” feel no less of a nightmare. “When is Mrs. Mompert expected to send for me?” she asked, as if she had already accepted the plan.

“Any time in the next fortnight, most likely. She promised me she will not consider any other candidate before she has met you. She has been very kind, and fully understands your family’s situation,” the rector said cheerfully, standing to leave. “I’m off now, to rent out a plot of the glebe land for a very good price.” He left the room fully convinced that Gwendolen, sensible girl that she was, would adapt gracefully to her new circumstances.

“Henry is such a rock for all of us,” Mrs. Gascoigne sighed once he was gone.

“He truly is,” Gwendolen’s mother agreed warmly. “Cheerfulness is a fortune all its own. I only wish I had half of his.”

“And Rex is just like him,” Mrs. Gascoigne went on, pulling a letter out of her pocket. “We got a letter from him the other day, and it has been such a comfort. Let me read you a bit of it—” Anna paled, nervous, for they had all avoided speaking of Rex in front of Gwendolen since their broken engagement. But Mrs. Gascoigne scanned the page and tucked it back into her pocket before she could read any of the lines that referenced the pain of what had happened between Rex and Gwendolen. “He says our family’s troubles have turned him into a man. He’s determined to win a fellowship, take on private pupils, get his little brothers set up in life, make something remarkable of himself. He jokes, ‘Tell mother she advertised for a hard-working, reliable son just in time to stop me running off to sea, and I’m applying for the job.’ We haven’t seen Henry that moved since the day Rex was born. It feels like such a gift to balance out all we’ve lost.”

That letter was the very reason the Gascoignes had been so gentle and forgiving to Gwendolen lately, and she smiled at Anna, pinching her cousin’s chin playfully to signal that there was no hard feelings left between them. She had never taken any pleasure in hurting people who cared for her; she only hated it when other people tried to make her life miserable.

But when the talk turned to arranging old bits of furniture for the cottage, Gwendolen could not summon even a flicker of interest. She thought she had already done more than enough that morning, holding the raging, terrified war inside her quiet. The recoil from the only future laid out for her was far sharper than she had ever imagined it would be. The idea of standing before Mrs. Mompert to be judged, accepted or rejected, felt like pressing on a fresh, throbbing bruise. Even after she forced herself to accept the strict bishop and his wife, they still got to decide if she was good enough. Every moment of every day would be spent under inspection, expected to perform lessons she did not care about, piety she did not feel, for three girls she had no desire to “edify.” Only six weeks prior, she had been the belle of every local party, her quick, playful wit making everyone laugh, the very last person anyone would have pitied. Now the life ahead of her felt like a prison.

Wild, giddy thoughts of running away to be an actress still darted through her head, luring her with the promise of freedom—but Klesmer’s harsh, cutting words still hung heavy on her soul, scaring her pride and her sense of herself as a lady. She could not bear the thought of being thrown among vulgar strangers, men who would leer and make rude, familiar remarks, no polite social barrier between her and their rudeness. Even her daydreams of winning big at the gaming table had always assumed that everyone would still treat her with the deference a lady deserved. The strict, stuffy life at the bishop’s house, where at least she would be treated with the basic respect due to a gentlewoman, was less terrifying than that uncertain, unprotected freedom.

Her rebellion curdled into a heavy, world-weary nausea. She was the one person in the whole world who had been built, all her life, to shine, to be admired, to be the center of attention—and now she was expected to fade into the background, to take orders, to be patronized. She could not bring herself to believe things would ever get better. Even the cheap solace of a governess romance, the kind she had read about in novels where the quiet, pretty teacher catches the eye of a wealthy heir, could not cheer her. There was no space for hope, no space for dreams. She saw no reason at all to want to go on living.

The feeling sank into her bones, making her numb. She could not bring herself to do a single small chore. The slightest demand, even that she sit down to eat a meal, felt like an irritation. She avoided the rectory entirely, because she could not bear to pretend to be compliant and grateful. She would not even feign interest in arranging furniture for the horrid little cottage. Miss Merry had come to stay to help, and Jocosa and the other girls were happy to sort through linens, so her mother had to make excuses for her absence even when Anna came to call. The steeled calm she had promised herself had melted into a listless, heavy apathy. She thought to herself, *I’ll pretend soon enough. Why bother now?*

Her mother watched her with silent, tender distress, and fell back into the old habit of indulging her every wish, desperate to do anything at all to ease her daughter’s pain. One afternoon, while they were alone in the black and yellow bedroom, her mother lingering under the pretense of sorting through Gwendolen’s old dresses, Gwendolen suddenly roused herself to drag out the little casket that held all her old trinkets and ornaments.

“Mamma,” she said, rummaging through the top tray, “I forgot all about these. Why didn’t you remind me? Take them to be sold. You don’t mind parting with them, you gave them all to me years ago.” She lifted the top tray to get at the layer beneath, and her eyes fell on the torn handkerchief she had stuffed in next to her turquoise necklace, the one that had been the only thing to save her necklace from being tossed into the river by Deronda back at the casino.

“Darling, if we don’t need the money that badly, I would rather keep them for you,” her mother said, relieved that Gwendolen was finally talking about something. “Why, how did that old torn handkerchief end up in here?”

“It just got tossed in with the necklace, I was in a hurry,” Gwendolen said, pulling the handkerchief out and tucking it into her pocket. “Don’t sell the turquoise necklace, mamma,” she added, a strange, soft new feeling rising in her chest for that small, fateful souvenir she had once resented so bitterly.

“Of course I won’t, dear. It was made from your dear father’s old watch chain. I would never sell that. None of these trinkets are worth much anyway—all my own best jewels were taken from me years ago, by your stepfather.” Mrs. Davilow colored, avoiding Gwendolen’s eye, for she never liked to speak of the old shame of her late husband stealing and selling off all her jewelry. “These haven’t been budgeted for any expenses. You can take them with you, to the Momperts’.”

“That would be useless,” Gwendolen said coldly. “Governesses don’t wear pretty ornaments. You might as well buy me a gray frieze uniform and a poke bonnet like the ones the charity children at the rectory school wear.”

“Don’t look at it that way, dear,” her mother said, distressed. “I’m sure the Momperts will love to see you graceful and elegant.”

“I have no idea what they will want me to be. All I know is I am expected to be exactly what they like,” Gwendolen said bitterly.

“If there is any other path you would rather take—anything at all that would be less hard than going to the bishop’s house—tell me, Gwendolen,” her mother begged, taking her hand. “Tell me what is in your heart. I will do anything I possibly can to make it happen. Don’t shut me out. Let’s bear this together.”

“There’s nothing to tell,” Gwendolen said, not looking at her mother, twisting the turquoise necklace between her fingers. “There is no better option for me. I’m lucky if they even agree to hire me. All I have to think about is sending money home to you. I won’t spend a single penny of the eighty pounds a year they pay me. You can have all of it, to help with the housekeeping. You won’t have to stitch your fingers raw, or cry your eyes out over mending late at night.” She did not lean in to kiss her mother, the way she used to, her eyes fixed on the glint of the turquoise stones.

“Bless you, my sweet, good child,” her mother said, tears running down her cheeks. “Don’t despair now, just because there are clouds. You are so young. There is great happiness still waiting for you somewhere, I just know it.”

“I see no reason to count on that,” Gwendolen said, her voice hard. Her mother fell quiet, wondering, as she had so many times before, what exactly had passed between Gwendolen and Mr. Grandcourt that day he had come to visit.

“I will keep this necklace, mamma,” Gwendolen said, tucking it to one side before closing the casket. “But sell all the other trinkets, even if they don’t fetch much. Ask uncle what to do with them. I will never wear any of them again. I’m as good as taking the veil. I wonder if every other poor soul who ever did that felt the same hollow, dead feeling I do right now.”
