No man can foresee the future. Love does not speak in prophecy. Fear often conjures up shadows that are not real. Those three years of separation—how I had dreaded them! The pain they would bring seemed as certain as death itself. I knew the path they would take; I felt sure of every ache they would leave behind. Like a colossal Juggernaut rolling forward, heavy and unstoppable, I saw it looming ahead—its broad wheels crushing all beneath. And I, kneeling low, already felt the crushing weight before it came. Yet—strange though it may seem, and yet true, as so many lives have shown—the worst torment was not the passage of time, but the fear of it. The great chariot moved on, loud and solemn, yet passed silently, like a shadow gliding across the noonday sky. Only a chill dimness remained. I looked up—chariot and driver were gone. The worshipper still lived.

M. Emanuel was away for three years. Reader, those were the happiest years of my life. You may think this odd—but hear me out.

I began my school. I worked—oh, how I worked! I took upon myself the care of his estate, determined, if God would allow, to give an honest account. Pupils came first from modest homes, then from more distinguished ones. Midway through the second year, fate offered me an unexpected gift: one hundred pounds, delivered by letter from England. It came from Mr. Marchmont, cousin and heir to my dear departed mistress. He had just recovered from a serious illness, and this sum was meant as penance—a quiet offering to his conscience, troubled by papers found after her death—papers that named or praised Lucy Snowe. Mrs. Barrett had given him my address. How deeply he had wronged himself, I never asked. I accepted the money without question, and put it to good use. With it, I bought the house next door. I could not abandon the home M. Paul had chosen, where he had left me, and where he expected to find me again. My small boarding school grew into a full-fledged pensionnat—and it thrived.

But my success was not due only to my own effort, nor to any special talent. It was born of a changed world, a transformed life, a heart set free. The force that stirred me came from afar—from an island in the Indian Ocean. At our parting, I had been left with a legacy—not of gold, but of hope. That promise shaped my present, guided my future. It gave me strength to endure, to work, to strive, to be brave and patient. Nothing could easily shake me now. Few things held power to disturb, frighten, or sadden me. Most things brought joy—even the smallest details sparkled with charm.

Do not suppose this warmth lasted only because of a distant dream or a farewell vow. A generous hand kept it burning. I was spared coldness, scarcity, anxiety. I was never made to fear poverty or doubt. With every ship that crossed the sea, he wrote. He wrote as freely as he gave, as fully as he loved—without restraint, without pretense. He sat down to write simply because he wanted to. Because he loved Lucy. Because he had so much to say. Because he was faithful, thoughtful, tender, true. There was no falsehood in him. No hollow words. No excuses disguised as kindness. Apology never touched his lips—never crept into his letters. He gave no stone for bread, no scorpion for honey. His words were nourishment, living water, sustenance for the soul.

And did I feel gratitude? Heaven knows! I believe no creature who has been so steadfastly cared for, so nobly treated, could fail to be grateful—through every breath, until death.

He honored his faith, yet respected mine. He was not one to turn lightly from belief. But he never sought to change me. “Remain a Protestant,” he said. “My little English Puritan—I love your Protestantism. I admire its strength, its dignity. Though I cannot accept its rites, it is the only creed for ‘Lucy.’” Even Rome could not make him bigoted, nor the Church’s influence mold him into a rigid zealot. He was born honest, not deceitful; simple, not cunning; free, not enslaved. His tenderness had made him pliable under a priest’s guidance; his affection, devotion, and fervent piety sometimes blinded him—causing him to sacrifice fairness to himself for the sake of duty, or to serve selfish ends. But these flaws, rare and costly, may one day be counted among virtues rather than sins.

Now the three years are over. M. Emanuel’s return is fixed. It is autumn. He will be here before November’s mists descend. My school flourishes. My home is ready. I have built him a small library, filling its shelves with the books he left in my care. I have nurtured plants he loved—though I was never skilled in gardening—and some still bloom in honor of him.

I thought I loved him when he left. Now I love him deeper, more truly. He is more mine than ever before.

The sun crosses the equinox. Days grow shorter. Leaves turn brittle. But—he is coming. Frosts touch the night. November sends its fog ahead. The wind sighs with autumn’s sorrow. But—he is coming.

The skies grow dark and heavy. Clouds form strange shapes—arches, radiant lines. Dawn rises in splendor—purple and golden, like a king in regal robes. The heavens blaze with fire, wild as battle at its fiercest, crimson as victory in its pride.

I know the signs of the sky. I have read them since childhood. God, watch that sail! Protect it! The wind turns westward. Peace, peace, Banshee—do not wail at every window! It will rise… it will grow louder… it screams long and loud. No matter how I wander through the house tonight, I cannot silence the storm. As hours pass, it grows stronger. By midnight, all who wait awake hear it—and fear it. This storm raged for seven days. It ceased only when the Atlantic was littered with wreckage. It calmed only when the deep had drunk its fill. Not until the angel of tempest had done his terrible work would he fold his thunderous wings, still trembling with fury.

Peace, be still!

Oh, a thousand hearts on waiting shores wept and prayed for that voice—but it was not spoken. Not until the silence came—until the sun returned—did some realize it was not night, but dawn.

Here pause. Pause now. Enough has been said. Let no gentle heart be disturbed. Let hopeful minds imagine what they will. Let them see reunion—joy reborn from terror, rescue from danger, deliverance from dread, the fulfillment of longing. Let them picture happiness restored, love renewed, life unfolding in peace.

Madame Beck lived well and prospered. Père Silas lived long and peacefully. Madame Walravens reached ninety before she passed.

Farewell.
