Mr. Moore awoke the next morning in high spirits, as he had done the night before. He and Joe Scott had both spent the evening at the mill, making use of makeshift sleeping quarters tucked away in the front and back counting-houses. The master, always an early riser, was up even earlier than usual. As he dressed, he sang a French song to wake his man.

"Ye're not custen dahn, then, maister?" Joe called out.

"Not a stiver, mon garçon—meaning, my lad. Get up, and let’s take a turn through the mill before the hands arrive. I’ll tell you about my plans. We’ll have the machinery yet, Joseph. You’ve never heard of Bruce, perhaps?"

"And th’ arrand (spider)? Yes, but I hev. I’ve read th’ history o’ Scotland, and happen knaw as mich on’t as ye; and I understand ye to mean to say ye’ll persevere."

"I do."

"Is there mony o' your mak’ i’ your country?" Joe asked, folding up his bedroll and putting it away.

"In my country? Which is my country?"

"Why, France—isn’t it?"

"Not it, indeed! Just because the French seized Antwerp, where I was born, doesn’t make me a Frenchman."

"Holland, then?"

"I’m not Dutch. Now you’re confusing Antwerp with Amsterdam."

"Flanders?"

"I scorn the insinuation, Joe! I a Flamand? Have I a Flemish face—the clumsy nose standing out, the mean forehead falling back, the pale blue eyes ‘à fleur de tête’? Am I all body and no legs, like a Flamand? But you don’t know what they’re like, those Netherlanders. Joe, I’m an Anversois. My mother was an Anversoise, though she came from French stock, which is why I speak French."

"But your father war Yorkshire, which maks ye a bit Yorkshire too; and onybody may see ye’re akin to us, ye’re so keen o’ making brass, and getting forrards."

"Joe, you’re an impudent dog; but I’ve always been used to this kind of boorish insolence since youth. The *classe ouvrière*—the working people in Belgium—treat their employers brutally. And by *brutally*, Joe, I mean *brutalement*—which, when properly translated, might be *roughly*."

"We allus speak our minds i’ this country; and them young parsons and grand folk fro’ London is shocked at wer 'incivility;' and we like weel enough to gi’e 'em summat to be shocked at, 'cause it's sport to us to watch 'em turn up the whites o' their een, and spreed out their bits o' hands, like as they're flayed wi' bogards, and then to hear 'em say, nipping off their words short like, 'Dear! dear! Whet seveges! How very corse!'"

"You _are_ savages, Joe. You don't suppose you're civilized, do you?"

"Middling, midding, maister. I reckon that us manufacturing lads i’ th’ north is a deal more intelligent, and knaws a deal more nor th’ farming folk i’ th’ south. Trade sharpens wer wits; and them that's mechanics like me is forced to think. Ye know, what wi' looking after machinery and sich like, I've getten into that way that when I see an effect, I look straight out for a cause, and I oft lig hold on't to purpose; and then I like reading, and I'm curious to knaw what them that reckons to govern us aims to do for us and wi' us. And there's many 'cuter nor me; there's many a one amang them greasy chaps 'at smells o' oil, and amang them dyers wi' blue and black skins, that has a long head, and that can tell what a fooil of a law is, as well as ye or old Yorke, and a deal better nor soft uns like Christopher Sykes o' Whinbury, and greet hectoring nowts like yond' Irish Peter, Helstone's curate."

"You think yourself a clever fellow, I know, Scott."

"Ay! I'm fairish. I can tell cheese fro' chalk, and I'm varry weel aware that I've improved sich opportunities as I have had, a deal better nor some 'at reckons to be aboon me; but there's thousands i’ Yorkshire that's as good as me, and a two-three that's better."

"You're a great man—you're a sublime fellow; but you're a prig, a conceited noodle with it all, Joe! You need not think that because you've picked up a little knowledge of practical mathematics, and because you've found some scantling of chemistry at the bottom of a dyeing vat, that therefore you're a neglected man of science. And you need not suppose that because trade doesn't always run smooth, and you and such as you are sometimes short of work and bread, that therefore your class are martyrs, and that the whole form of government under which you live is wrong. Moreover, you need not insinuate that virtue has fled to cottages and abandoned slated houses. Let me tell you, I particularly abominate that sort of talk, because I know human nature is human nature everywhere—whether under tile or thatch—and that in every breath of life, vice and virtue are blended, in greater or lesser proportions, and that proportion isn't decided by station. I've seen villains who were rich, and villains who were poor, and villains who were neither, but lived in fair and modest comfort. The clock is going to strike six. Away with you, Joe, and ring the mill bell."

It was mid-February, and by six o'clock dawn was just beginning to steal into the night, piercing its brown shadows with a pale ray and giving a faint translucence to the dark. That morning, the light was weak—no colour tinged the east, no warmth flushed it. To see how slowly day lifted its heavy lid, how wan its glance swept the hills, one might have thought the sun’s fire had been quenched in the night’s rain. The air was cold as its appearance; a raw wind stirred the mass of night-cloud, revealing, as it rose, not blue sky but a layer of paler vapour around the horizon. The rain had stopped, but the earth was soaked, and the pools and streams overflowed.

The mill windows were lit, the bell still rang loudly, and now the children began to stream in—too eager, perhaps, to feel the chill. By contrast, the morning seemed almost pleasant to them, for they had often come to work through snowstorms, heavy rain, and hard frost.

Mr. Moore stood at the entrance, watching them pass. He counted them as they went by. To those who arrived late, he gave a gentle word of warning, which Joe Scott repeated more sharply when they reached the workrooms. Neither master nor overseer spoke harshly. They were not cruel men, though both were firm. One boy who came significantly late was fined—a penny down before entering, with a warning that another offence would cost him twopence.

Rules, of course, were necessary. Coarse and cruel masters made coarse and cruel rules, and enforced them tyrannically in those times. But while I describe imperfect characters—every character in this book being more or less flawed—I have chosen not to portray utterly degraded ones. Child-torturers, slave-drivers—I leave them to the jailer. The novelist may be forgiven for avoiding such horrors.

Instead of describing lashes and beatings, I am pleased to say that neither Mr. Moore nor Joe Scott ever struck a child in the mill. Joe had once severely flogged his own son for lying and refusing to admit it—but like his employer, he was too calm, too reasonable, to resort to physical punishment regularly. It remained an exception.

Mr. Moore wandered through the mill, the yard, the dye-house, and the warehouse until the sickly dawn strengthened into daylight. The sun rose—at least, a white disc, clear and tintless, almost icy in appearance, peered over the dark crest of a hill, turning the edge of the cloud silver, and casting a solemn gaze down the length of the narrow valley where we are now confined. It was eight o'clock. The mill lights were extinguished. The signal for breakfast sounded. The children, released for half an hour, hurried to their small tin cans of coffee and baskets of bread. Let us hope they had enough to eat. It would be a shame if they didn’t.

At last, Mr. Moore left the millyard and walked toward his cottage. It was only a short distance, but the hedge and high bank on either side of the lane gave it a sense of quiet seclusion. The house was small, whitewashed, with a green porch over the door. Brown stalks showed in the garden soil near the porch and beneath the windows—buds and flowers absent now, but promising creepers in summer. A grassy plot and flower borders lay in front. The borders held only dark earth, except where snowdrops or crocuses poked through, green as emerald. Spring was late. Winter had been severe and long. The last deep snow had vanished only yesterday; on the hills, patches still gleamed, dotting hollows and crowning peaks. The lawn was not green, but bleached, as was the grass on the bank and under the hedge. Three trees, gracefully grouped, rose beside the cottage. Not tall, but unchallenged in their spot, they looked noble and strong.

This was Mr. Moore’s home—a snug place for peace and reflection, though one where ambition could not rest for long.

Yet the cottage seemed to hold no special charm for its owner. Instead of entering, he fetched a spade from a shed and began digging in the garden. For nearly fifteen minutes, he worked without pause. Then a window opened, and a woman’s voice called:

"Eh, bien! Tu ne déjeûnes pas ce matin?"

He replied in French, and the conversation followed in the same tongue. Since this is an English tale, I will translate.

"Is breakfast ready, Hortense?"

"Certainly. It’s been ready for half an hour."

"Then I am ready too. I have a canine hunger."

He dropped his spade and stepped inside. The narrow passage led to a small parlour, where coffee, bread and butter, and stewed pears—unusual in England—were laid out. Overseeing the meal was the woman who had spoken from the window. Let me describe her.

She seemed slightly older than Mr. Moore—perhaps thirty-five—tall and stoutly built. Her hair was very black, now twisted up in curl-papers. She had a high colour in her cheeks, a small nose, and bright black eyes. The lower half of her face was larger than the upper; her forehead was small and slightly wrinkled. Her expression was fretful, though not unkind—something about her made one want to be both annoyed and amused. Most striking was her dress: a rough petticoat and striped cotton camisole. The petticoat was short, revealing feet and ankles that lacked symmetry.

You might think I’ve painted a slovenly woman, reader. Not at all. Hortense Moore—Mr. Moore’s sister—was orderly and economical. The petticoat, camisole, and curl-papers were her morning attire, worn in her native land. She refused to adopt English customs simply because she lived here. She clung to Belgian ways, believing them superior.

Mademoiselle had a high opinion of herself—not entirely unjustified, for she possessed solid virtues. But she exaggerated their worth and ignored her flaws. She could never be convinced she was prejudiced, narrow-minded, overly sensitive to her dignity, or quick to take offense. Yet these traits were true. Still, when no one challenged her, and no prejudice was wounded, she could be kind and warm-hearted. She loved her two brothers—Robert and Louis—deeply. As the last of their family line, they were sacred to her. But she knew little of Louis. He had been sent to England as a child, educated in an English school. Unsuited to trade, perhaps by nature, he had become a teacher—first an usher, now tutor in a private household. Hortense said he had "des moyens," but was too quiet and reserved. Her praise of Robert was different—full of pride. She saw him as the greatest man in Europe. Everything he said or did was remarkable in her eyes. She expected others to see him the same way. Opposition to Robert—or to herself—was unthinkable.

So, as soon as Robert sat at the table, and she had served him stewed pears and a generous slice of Belgian tartine, she burst into horror at the night’s events—the destruction of the frames.

"Quelle idée! To destroy them. Quelle action honteuse! One could see clearly that the workers here are both beasts and wicked. Exactly like English servants—especially the maids! Nothing so unbearable as Sara, for instance!"

"She looks clean and industrious," Mr. Moore remarked.

"Looks! I don’t care how she looks. I won’t say she’s dirty or idle, mais elle est d'une insolence! Yesterday she argued with me for half an hour about the beef—said I boiled it to rags, that English people couldn’t eat our bouilli, that the broth was nothing but greasy warm water, and the choucroute—she insists she can’t touch it! That barrel in the cellar—prepared by my own hands—she called a tub of hog-wash, meaning pig food. I’m tormented by the girl, yet I cannot dismiss her, lest I find someone worse. You’re in the same boat, pauvre cher frère!"

"I’m afraid you’re not very happy in England, Hortense."

"It is my duty to be happy where you are, brother. But otherwise, there are a thousand things that make me regret our native town. Everyone here seems ill-bred (mal-élevé). My habits are ridiculed. If a girl from your mill comes into the kitchen and finds me in my jupon and camisole cooking dinner—well, you know I can’t trust Sarah to cook anything—she sneers. If I accept a tea invitation, I notice I’m put aside; I don’t receive the attention I deserve. What a fine family the Gérards are—we know that! And the Moores too! We have a right to respect. When it’s withheld, it wounds. In Antwerp, I was always treated with distinction. Here, it seems everyone thinks I speak English with a ridiculous accent, though I assure you I pronounce it perfectly."

"Hortense, in Antwerp we were known to be rich. In England, we were known only as poor."

"Precisely. And how mercenary mankind is! Again, dear brother, last Sunday was wet. So I went to church in my neat black sabots—not something one wears in a fashionable city, but common in the countryside for walking in mud. As I walked up the aisle, composed and tranquil—as I always am—four ladies and four gentlemen laughed and hid behind their prayer-books."

"Well, well! Don’t wear the sabots again. I told you before I thought they weren’t quite suitable here."

"But, brother, they’re not ordinary sabots—like peasants wear. I tell you, they are sabots noirs, très propres, très convenables. At Mons and Leuze—cities near Brussels—they’re commonly worn by respectable people in winter. Try wading Flemish roads in Paris brodequins, and you’ll hear news!"

"Never mind Mons and Leuze. Do as the Romans do. And the camisole and jupon—I’m not sure about those either. I’ve never seen an English lady dressed like that. Ask Caroline Helstone."

"Caroline! _I_ ask Caroline? _I_ consult her about my dress? It is _she_ who should consult _me_. She is a child."

"She is eighteen, or at least seventeen—old enough to know about gowns, petticoats, and shoes."

"Do not spoil Caroline, I entreat you, brother. Do not make her more important than she ought to be. She is modest and unassuming—let us keep her so."

"With all my heart. Is she coming this morning?"

"She will come at ten, as usual, for her French lesson."

"You don’t find she sneers at you?"

"She does not. She appreciates me better than anyone here. She has intimate knowledge of me—she sees I have education, intelligence, manners, principles—all that belongs to a person well born and well bred."

"Are you fond of her?"

"For _fond_, I cannot say. I do not take sudden fancies. Thus, my friendship is reliable. I regard her as my relative. Her position inspires interest. And her conduct as my pupil has been such as to strengthen, rather than weaken, the attachment arising from other causes."

"She behaves well at lessons?"

"To _me_, she behaves very well. Though you know, brother, I have a manner that discourages familiarity, wins esteem, and commands respect. Yet, having insight, I see clearly that Caroline is not perfect—there is much to desire in her."

"Give me another cup of coffee, and while I drink it, entertain me with her faults."

"Dear brother, I’m glad to see you eating with appetite after your exhausting night. Caroline is defective—but with my guidance, almost maternal care, she may improve. There is something about her—an occasional reserve—that I dislike, because it lacks girlish submission. And I see glimpses of restless haste in her nature, which unsettles me. Yet she is usually calm—sometimes too dejected, too thoughtful. With time, I doubt not, I shall make her uniformly serene and proper, without being inexplicably pensive. I disapprove of what is unclear."

"I don’t understand your description at all. What do you mean by 'unsettled hurries,' for example?"

"An example may clarify. I sometimes make her read French poetry for pronunciation practice. She’s studied much of Corneille and Racine—steadily, soberly, as I approve. Occasionally, she showed languor—more apathy than sobriety—and apathy I cannot tolerate in those under my instruction. The other day, I gave her a volume of short poems. I sent her to the window to learn one by heart. When I looked up, I saw her flipping through the pages impatiently, curling her lip in scorn as she glanced at the verses. I rebuked her. 'Ma cousine,' she said, 'tout cela m'ennuie à la mort.' I told her that was improper language. 'Dieu!' she exclaimed, 'il n'y a donc pas deux lignes de poésie dans toute la littérature française?' I asked what she meant. She apologized with proper humility. Soon she was silent. I saw her smiling to herself over the book. She began to study diligently. Half an hour later, she stood before me, handed me the volume, folded her hands—just as I require—and recited Chénier’s 'La Jeune Captive.' If you had heard her delivery, and her incoherent remarks afterward, you’d understand what I meant by 'unsettled hurry.' One would think Chénier moved more deeply than all of Racine and Corneille combined. You, brother, with your wisdom, will see that this disproportionate passion reveals an unbalanced mind. But she is fortunate in her preceptress. I will give her a system, a method, a set of beliefs. I will guide her feelings completely."

"Be sure you do, Hortense. Here she comes. I believe I saw her shadow pass the window."

"Ah! Truly. She is too early—half an hour ahead of time. My child, what brings you here before I’ve finished breakfast?"

This question was addressed to a young girl who entered the room, wrapped in a winter cloak, its folds gathered with grace around a slender figure.

"I came quickly to see how you were, Hortense, and how Robert was too. I was sure you’d both be upset by what happened last night. I didn’t hear until this morning. My uncle told me at breakfast."

"Ah! It is unspeakable. You sympathize with us? Your uncle sympathizes?"

"My uncle is very angry—but he was with Robert, I believe. Did he go with you to Stilbro’ Moor?"

"Yes, we set out in martial style. But the prisoners we went to rescue met us halfway."

"Of course, nobody was hurt?"

"No—only Joe Scott’s wrists were a little bruised from being bound too tightly behind his back."

"You weren’t there? You weren’t with the wagons when they were attacked?"

"No. One seldom has the fortune to be present at events one wishes to witness."

"Where are you going this morning? I saw Murgatroyd saddling your horse in the yard."

"To Whinbury. It’s market day."
