"Truth will fail thee never, never!
Though thy bark be tempest-driven,
Though each plank be rent and riven,
Truth will bear thee on for ever!"

For Margaret, the burden of existing "better than likely" was a crushing weight. Sometimes, amidst the cheerful facades she maintained with her father, a sudden, jagged thought pierced through her: she was alone. She had lost her mother, and now, perhaps, she was losing everything else. The silence regarding Frederick was a physical ache. The Sunday post had failed to deliver news from London, and by Tuesday, the absence of a letter left her in a state of paralyzed dread. Frederick's future was a blank, and her father's misery manifested in a restlessness that shattered his recent habits. Where he used to sit quietly for hours, he now paced the floor, traversed the rooms, and flapped the bedroom doors open and shut with a frantic, aimless energy. Margaret, exhausted from trying to soothe him with books she could not read, breathed a sigh of relief when Mr. Thornton arrived. His presence acted as an anchor, forcing her father's wandering mind back to solid ground.

Mr. Thornton entered the drawing-room with a directness that bypassed formalities. He stepped immediately to Mr. Hale, grasping his hands and holding them tight, a silent, profound communication of sorrow passing between them that needed no words. His gaze, heavy with empathy, softened as he turned to Margaret. He had intended to maintain the cool reserve of his previous visits, yet the sight of her shattered composure broke his resolve. She stood apart, her stately beauty dimmed by sleepless nights and weeping. Her face bore the marks of gentle, enduring sadness, yet beneath it lay a raw, present suffering. As he approached, his voice dropped to a tender whisper, causing tears to spill instantly. She turned away, hiding her face, and sat down, picking up her work as if it were a shield. To Mr. Thornton, watching her, the harsh realities of Outwood Lane vanished; his heart beat fast, consumed entirely by the girl before him. He spoke to Mr. Hale with a warmth and authority that made the older man feel secure, a feeling Margaret observed with grateful relief.

Suddenly, the door opened, and Dixon stood there, his face pale and flustered. "Miss Hale, you are wanted." The alarm in Dixon's voice sent a chill through Margaret's stomach. Something had happened to Frederick. She prayed silently that her father and Mr. Thornton were too engrossed in conversation to notice her departure.

"What is it, Dixon?" she asked, her voice barely audible.

"Come this way, miss," Dixon urged, ushering her into what had become her chamber following Mrs. Hale's death. "It's nothing, miss," he choked out, struggling for composure. "Only a police-inspector. He wishes to speak with you. I... I told him it might be trivial."

"Do you know why?" Margaret whispered, stepping past him.

"No, miss. He merely asked if you resided here. Martha admitted him, showing him into Master Thornton's study. I offered to explain, but he insisted on seeing you."

As Margaret reached for the handle of the study door, she paused. "Take care, Papa does not come down. Mr. Thornton is with him."

Inside, the air felt thick and oppressive. The inspector, a man of stern demeanor, was momentarily daunted by her entrance. Yet, as she faced him, her countenance transformed. Indignation, carefully suppressed, lent her features a superb, disdainful air. There was no flicker of surprise, no trace of curiosity. She awaited his words with a stillness that bordered on the supernatural. She offered no questions, only a silent, iron vigilance.

"I beg your pardon, ma'am," the inspector began, his tone professional yet grave. "My duty compels me to ask a few plain questions. A man has died at the Infirmary following a fall at Outwood Station, occurring Thursday evening, the twenty-sixth, between five and six o'clock. Initially, the fall appeared minor, but physicians attribute the fatality to an underlying internal condition and the deceased's habitual consumption of alcohol."

Margaret's large, dark eyes widened slightly, locking onto his face. Her lips tightened into a severe, unyielding line. She did not blink, nor did she tremble. When he hesitated, waiting for her reaction, she spoke, her voice flat and devoid of emotion, as if reciting a script rather than answering a charge. "Well—go on!"

"There is reason to believe an inquest will be necessary," the inspector continued, his voice steady. "Evidence suggests the fall resulted from a blow, a push, or a scuffle. It appears the deceased was provoked by the insolent remarks of a half-intoxicated individual toward a young lady accompanying a gentleman. An eyewitness on the platform observed the altercation, though dismissed it as inconsequential given the slight nature of the initial impact."

"And the witness identified the lady?" Margaret asked, her tone challenging.

"There is strong probability," the inspector replied, leaning forward slightly. "The gentleman and lady were seen near the platform at the relevant hour. The lady was described as remarkably handsome. A local grocer's assistant, present at the scene, identified her as Miss Hale of Crampton. Furthermore, the aggressor, Leonard, fled the scene in a fury, seeking solace in a gin-palace. Witnesses there recall him cursing himself for failing to utilize the electric telegraph—a grievance he attributed to a 'Cornish trick' that had cost him a hundred pounds. Overcome by pain and drink, he collapsed in the road, where he was discovered by the police. He remained delirious, unable to coherently recount the events, save for ramblings of ships and captains, ending with a curse against the Cornish deception."

The inspector paused, studying her composed face. "Thus, madam, I seek confirmation of your denial regarding your presence at the station, accompanying the gentleman who delivered the fatal blow?"

A sharp, searing pain lanced through Margaret's brain. *If only I knew Frederick was safe.* For a fleeting second, the torture of truth twisted her features, a glimpse of agony visible to a keen observer. But the inspector, practical and shallow, missed the depth of her distress. He noted only the mechanical, unvaried quality of her response, as if she were trapped behind a wall of stone, unable to shift her words.

"I was not there," she said, slowly, heavily. Her eyes remained fixed on him, glassy and dreamlike, refusing to close or soften.

The inspector's suspicions sharpened. This was not the natural cadence of a truthful denial; it sounded like a rehearsed falsehood, a statement forced out of her despite the obvious contradiction. He closed his notebook deliberately, looking up at her. She stood like an ancient statue, unmoving, her color unchanged, her pride erect.

"I trust you will not deem me impertinent," he said, "if I inform you that I may need to summon you to appear at the inquest. If my sole witness persists in identifying you, you will be required to provide an alibi." He looked at her sharply, expecting a flicker of guilt, a flush of shame. Instead, she remained perfectly still. He was taken aback by her sheer composure. It must be a mistake of identity.

"It is highly improbable I shall require further action," he concluded politely. "I hope you will forgive me for fulfilling my duties, however tedious they may appear."

As the inspector moved toward the door, Margaret bowed her head, her lips dry and stiff, incapable of forming a farewell. Suddenly, a passionate impulse seized her. She rose, walked to the study door, and unlocked it, allowing him to pass. She followed him to the front door of the house, throwing it wide open for his exit. She watched him leave with that same hollow, fixed gaze until he crossed the threshold. Only then did she turn back, locking the study door from within.

She retreated to the study, pausing, then tottering forward. She stopped again, swaying violently for a heartbeat, and collapsed to the floor, sinking into a dead swoon.
