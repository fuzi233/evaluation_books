The cool church, checkered with shafts of sunlight and crowned with a canopy of heavenly purple, soothed Father Clement as it had soothed Margaret long ago. It carried his mind directly to the Creator of all good things. His gaze swept over the great aisle, crammed with his country folk—a thousand snowy caps filigreed with gold. Many leagues he had traveled, yet he had seen nothing comparable, save perhaps the vast, silent snowfields. In the morning, he had thundered; but this sweet afternoon seemed entirely out of tune with threats. His heart yearned over that multitude; he felt compelled to tell them of God's love, for poor souls heard almost as little of it from the pulpit in those days as the heathen did. He spoke the glad tidings of salvation, and the people hung upon his gentle, earnest tongue.

Clement was not one of those preachers who gyrated in the pulpit like a weathercock on a steeple; he moved the hearts of others far more than his own body swayed. Yet, he did not neglect those in need. Warm with this theme, determined that none of the multitude should miss the joyful news of Christ's love, he turned toward the south aisle.

There, in a stream of sunshine pouring through the window, stood the radiant face of Margaret Brandt. He gazed at it without immediate emotion; it simply numbed him, soul and body alike. But soon, the words died in his throat, and he trembled as he stared.

There, with her auburn hair bathed in beams and glittering like the halo of a saint, her face glowing doubly with its own beauty and the light that framed it—stood his dead love. She leaned lightly against a white column, listening with tender, downcast lashes. He had seen her listen to him so a hundred times. There was no change in her; this was the blooming Margaret he had left, only a shade riper and more lovely.

He started at her with monstrous eyes and bloodless cheeks. The people faded from his sight. He heard, as in a dream, a rustling and rising throughout the church, yet he could not tear his prodigy-stricken eyes from that face—all life, bloom, beauty, and that wondrous auburn hair glistening gloriously in the sun. He gazed, thinking she must vanish. She remained.

Suddenly, she looked at him, fully.

Her own violet eyes!

At this, he was beside himself, his lips parting to shriek her name, when she turned her head swiftly and soon vanished. Yet, not without one final glance. Though rapid as lightning, it encountered his, leaving her couching and quivering with her mind in a whirl, and him panting and gripping the pulpit convulsively. For this glance, though not recognition, was the startled, inquiring, nameless look that precedes it. He made a mighty effort, muttering something no one could understand, then feebly resumed his discourse. He stammered and babbled for a while until, forcing himself to regard her as a vision from another world since she was gone, he rose into a state of unnatural excitement. He concluded his sermon in a style of eloquence that electrified the simple, bordering on rhapsody.

The sermon ended. He sat down on the pulpit stool, terribly shaken. Presently, an idea very characteristic of the time took possession of him. He had sought her grave at Sevenbergen in vain. Now she had been permitted to appear to him, showing him that she was buried here—probably hard by that very pillar where her spirit had shown itself. Once adopted, this idea settled on his mind with the certainty of a fact. He felt he had only to speak to the sexton, whom he had seen working during the sermon to his great disgust, to learn the spot where she lay.

The church was now quite empty. He descended from the pulpit and stepped through an aperture in the south wall onto the grass, approaching the sexton. He knew him instantly. But Jorian never suspected the poor lad, whose life he had saved, in this holy friar. The loss of his shapely beard had wonderfully altered the outline of his face, changing him even more than his tonsure, his short hair sprinkled with premature grey, and his cheeks thinned and paled by fasts and vigils.

"My son," said Friar Clement softly, "if you keep any memory of those whom you lay in the earth, prithee tell me: is any Christian buried inside the church, near one of the pillars?"

"Nay, father," said Jorian, "here in the churchyard lie buried all that are buried. Why?"

"No matter. Pray tell me then where lieth Margaret Brandt."

"Margaret Brandt?" Jorian stared stupidly at the speaker. "She died about three years ago and was buried here."

"Oh, that is another matter," said Jorian. "That was before my time; the vicar could tell you, likely, if so be she was a gentlewoman, or at least rich enough to pay him his fee."

"Alas, my son, she was poor—and paid a heavy penalty for it—but born of decent folk. Her father, Peter, was a learned physician; she came hither from Sevenbergen—to die."

When Clement had uttered these words, his head sank upon his breast, and he seemed to have no power nor wish to question Jorian further. He appeared lost in the past.

Jorian put down his spade, standing upright in the grave with his arms akimbo, and said sulkily, "Are you making a fool of me, holy sir, or has some wag been making a fool of you!" Having relieved his mind thus, he proceeded to dig again with a certain vigor that showed his somewhat irritable temper was ruffled.

Clement gazed at him with a puzzled but gently reproachful eye, for the tone was rude and the words unintelligible. Good-natured, though crusty, Jorian had not thrown up three spadefuls ere he became ashamed of himself. "Why, what a base churl am I to speak thus to thee, holy father; and thou standing there, looking at me like a lamb. Aha! I have it; 'tis Peter Brandt's grave you would fain see, not Margaret's. He does lie here, hard by the west door. There; I'll show you." And he laid down his spade, putting on his doublet and jerkin to go with the friar.

He did not know there was anybody sitting on Peter's tomb. Still less did he know that she was watching for this holy friar. Pietro Vanucci and Andrea did not recognize him without his beard. The fact is, that the beard which has never known a razor grows in a very picturesque and characteristic form, becoming a feature of the face; so that its removal may, in some cases, be an effectual disguise.
