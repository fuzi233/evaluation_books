Let anyone who’s ever had the unadulterated joy of rubbing shoulders with London’s fanciest, most exclusive drawing rooms admit it: Major Pendennis pulled off a sacrifice so grand, it practically ought to have a marble plaque in his club’s smoking room. He abandoned London in *May*, for heaven’s sake—his morning papers, his afternoons of drifting between club couches and sipping sherry with widowed ladies who thought he was the wittiest man alive, his trots around Rotten Row, his opera stall where every lord and duke nodded at him so reliably his name was guaranteed a line in the *Morning Post* the next day, those secret little 8-person dinner parties that were too select for even the Post to hear about. All of it, he tossed aside to lock himself up in a draughty little country house with a soft-hearted widow, a starry-eyed teenage son, a whiny curate, and a 10-year-old girl who still picked daisies in the front garden.

Nobody but the Major realized how monumental this self-denial was, of course. He’d get his pre-franked letters from town, heave a world-weary sigh, and show Helen the piles of gilded party invitations like a martyr displaying the nails from his cross. Helen just smiled politely, not getting the whole overblown pathos of the situation, but young Pen gaped at the fancy peerage names scrawled on the envelopes and hung on every one of his uncle’s gossipy stories about high society like they were holy writ.

The Major’s memory was a walking archive of aristocratic tea and scandal, and he poured it all into Pen’s eager ears nonstop. “My dear boy,” he’d say, deadly serious, “you can’t start studying Debrett soon enough. Don’t bother much with the fake historical bits, half those families made up their royal descents last Tuesday—our family’s the only one with a perfectly clean, unbroken line, mark my words. Study the *alliances*. Who’s married to whose cousin, who hates whose son-in-law. I’ve seen a man’s whole career get blown up because he was too thick to learn this one simple rule. Just last month, at Lord Hobanob’s dinner, that new scribbler Mr. Suckling decided to crack a whole set of jokes about Admiral Bowser switching political sides. Turns out the lady right next to him was Bowser’s daughter, and the man across the table was Bowser’s son-in-law. By the end of his bit the whole room was so quiet you could hear a crumb fall off the tablecloth, and Suckling was still snickering like he’d told the funniest joke ever. He will never darken a South Street doorway again. I promise you that.”

He rambled on like this for hours on their two-hour daily terrace walks, or after dinner over their port, mourning that Sir Francis Clavering still hadn’t moved into the local park to give the gentry someone to socialize with, wailing that Lord Eyrie was out of town so he couldn’t drag Pen over to introduce him to the nobleman’s two pretty daughters. “Who knows? You could’ve married Lady Emily or Lady Barbara Trehawk, had a title and a fortune to boot. But those dreams are gone, my lad. You’ve made your bed, now you’ve got to lie in it.”

Pen ate it all up. It was far more thrilling than any schoolbook he’d ever read, hearing stories about the Prince Regent, famous beauties, and all the behind-the-scenes silly business of the rich and powerful, even if it bored his mother Helen half to death. The Major’s morality wasn’t exactly built to get anyone a spot in heaven, but it was perfectly calibrated to make sure you never got snubbed at a party, never got stuck with the worst table at a restaurant, and never missed an invitation to the year’s most exclusive picnic. He fully believed his way was the only *correct* way, and he was determined to pry his naive, lovestruck nephew’s eyes wide open to the real world.

For instance, no man in the whole parish went to church more regularly than the old bachelor. “Doesn’t matter so much in town, Pen,” he’d say, “the men there all skip and no one notices. But down on your own land, a gentleman’s got to set an example. If I could carry a tune I’d even sing out of the family pew—Duke of Saint David’s does it every Sunday, and the villagers stare at him like he’s a saint come down from heaven. As long as the Claverings are away, you’re the most important man in the parish. You could run for Parliament one day, if you play your cards right. Your dad would’ve done it, if he’d lived. You can too—*if* you don’t go and marry some girl the whole neighborhood refuses to call on, no matter how nice she is. Well, well, painful subject. Let’s change it.” He’d change it, then bring it back up again 17 times before dinner was over, the moral of every story being: Pen was throwing his whole life down the drain over this actress.

It didn’t take much to make a soft teenaged boy think he was a very important young man, so Pen lapped up all his uncle’s advice. Suddenly Captain Costigan’s sloshed, punch-reeking company wasn’t fun anymore. He couldn’t imagine dragging that unshaven, perpetually half-drunk old man home to meet his mother, and every time the Major prodded him about his beloved Miss Fotheringay he stammered. “Is she accomplished?” No, not really. “Clever?” Well… average, definitely not clever. “Show me her letters.” Pen had to admit he only had three silly little notes from her, all just inviting him over for tea.

“Cautious little thing,” the Major said dryly. “She’s older than you, my poor boy.” Then he immediately apologized, all soft and humble, said he was just a silly old uncle who only cared about the family’s honor, because Pen would blow up instantly if anyone so much as breathed a rude word about Emily, swearing he’d never part with her, never let anyone talk about her lightly.

He swore the same thing over at Chatteris, too, in front of Emily, her friend the actress Miss Rouncy, and Costigan himself. The Captain pointed to the old fencing foils hung up on his wall, grumbled that he wouldn’t advise any man to go messing with his daughter’s affections, then hugged Pen so tight he could smell the whisky on his breath, wiped a fake tear out of his eye with a dirty paw, and called him his own dear son. Pen shuddered in his grasp, thinking of his spotless, respectable mother back home. He’d even written Costigan a letter recently, slipped a sovereign inside because the old boy was broke, and signed it “your affectionate son Arthur Pendennis.” He could not get out of Chatteris fast enough that day, riding his horse Rebecca back home, grumbling to himself that Emily had no idea what huge sacrifices he was making for her, the poor girl.

What Pen didn’t know was that the Major had a whole surveillance operation running. No matter how sneaky he thought he was being, sneaking over to Chatteris to see Emily, the Major found out every time without fail. He never stopped Pen, never forbade him from going, but the constant awareness that his uncle knew exactly where he’d been made Pen far less eager to go gallivanting off than he used to. Once he snuck half a mile down to the Fairoaks Inn to catch the coach to Chatteris, only to find his uncle’s valet Mr. Morgan perched on the coach roof, apparently running a message for the Major. Morgan rode all the way to Chatteris and all the way back with him, not saying a single word. Pen had full run of the house, every bit of “decent liberty” he could ask for, but he always felt like someone was watching him, even when he was sitting at Emily’s feet.

Morgan, it turned out, was a master spy. He’d made multiple trips to Chatteris already, interviewing every waiter, ostler, and barmaid at The George inn, and found out no one there had ever seen Costigan pay for a single drink. He’d gotten himself chummy with the barracks’ servants, who told him the Captain had been banned from the officers’ mess for getting too drunk and trying to duel a 19-year-old cornet over a bad hand of cards. He sat backstage with the Chatteris theatre’s supporting actors, buying them cigars and punch, and every last one of them agreed Costigan was broke, drunk, and perpetually in debt, but no one had a single bad word to say about Emily. She never went anywhere without her father, even when he was half blackout, he’d still stand guard outside her dressing room. Morgan even went to see her perform, and admitted privately to the Major she was a spectacularly pretty woman.

Doctor Portman even grilled the pew-opener Mrs. Creed after church one Sunday, threatening her with all the wrath of the church if she didn’t tell him everything. Mrs. Creed said Emily never let Pen be alone with her for even a minute—she always made Mrs. Creed, or one of her sons, or Emily’s old piano teacher, hang around the whole time. Pen would even turn up during Sunday church services, hoping everyone else was out, but Emily still dragged a chaperone out of the kitchen to sit with them.

The Major and Doctor Portman groaned together when they heard the news. They couldn’t dig up a single speck of dirt on the girl’s reputation! The Major sighed, “If this was just a casual fling, we could let it slide. Boys have to sow their wild oats, after all. But a *virtuous* attachment? That’s the worst possible thing. All that daft romantic nonsense they pick up being raised by women.”

“Now Major, you’re being far too cynical,” said the Doctor. “A virtuous attachment to a girl of his own rank and fortune would be splendid. But this infatuation? I’d tell him straight to break it off.”
“That’s exactly how you’d make him elope with her the very next day,” the Major said. “We just need to bide our time, that’s all.”
“Wait a second,” Portman said, halfway out the door. “I don’t go to the theatre normally, obviously—but why don’t we just go see her perform ourselves?”

The Major cackled. He’d been at Fairoaks two whole weeks, and he’d never thought of that. So they arranged for Pen to have a quiet dinner with his mother that night, then hopped in Portman’s chaise and drove over to Chatteris, had a very jolly bachelor dinner at The George, and trundled off to the theatre to watch Hamlet. It was the exact same Ophelia performance they’d heard about a hundred times—Emily didn’t change a single gesture, line delivery, or curtsy from one night to the next. The Major lifted his fancy opera glass, stared at her for a long minute, and muttered under his breath, “Hang it all, the little rascal didn’t even pick an ugly one.”
Portman applauded loudly. “She’s a very fine actress, Major, and undeniably very pretty.”
“Notice that young dragoon in the stage box?” the Major said, pointing to the man they’d shared the dinner room with earlier. He was clapping so hard his gloves were splitting, and Emily gave him a soft little simper in return. The Major snapped his opera glass shut, stuffed it in his pocket, and didn’t watch another minute of the show. They left before the silly afterpiece started, Portman heading back to his wife at the Deanery, and the Major wandering back to his reserved room at The George, his head already full of a dozen new scheming plots.
