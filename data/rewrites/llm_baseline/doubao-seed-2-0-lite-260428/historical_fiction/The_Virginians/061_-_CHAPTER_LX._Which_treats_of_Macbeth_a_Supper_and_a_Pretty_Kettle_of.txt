The theatre curtain had barely fallen for the final time that night before our whole party bundled into hired coaches bound for Mr. Warrington’s London lodgings, where the two Virginian brothers had laid out a lavish, warmly welcomed supper spread. George was determined to play the host to his guests in the most generous style he could manage, and General Lambert and his wife accepted the invitation of the two bachelors with unfeigned pleasure, glad to return the kindness that had made their young new friends feel so at home in the city.

The full company sat down soon after: the General and Mrs. Lambert, their college-aged son Jack, their two bright blooming daughters Theo and Hetty, and Mr. Spencer of the Temple, the new friend George had struck up a conversation with over a dish of coffee only a few days prior. They laughed and dug into the landlady’s best cooking with hearty good cheer. I cannot swear to the exact order of every chair at the table, but somehow Miss Theo found herself settled snugly between the platter of roasted spring chickens and George Warrington, while Hetty divided her attention and her wit equally between a honey-glazed ham and Harry. Mrs. Lambert sat to George’s right hand, leaving only three spots to account for: the General, young Jack, and the Templar, which hardly leaves room for any great mystery over who sat where.

Mr. Spencer had been at the Drury Lane theatre on a prior night, and had even escorted George backstage to the green room once before, so the conversation turned naturally back to the tragedy they had all watched that evening, half the party swearing the piece was utterly sublime.
“As for what those two men at the far end are muttering,” Mrs. Lambert declared directly to Mr. Spencer, “do not believe a word of it. It is a perfectly lovely play, and my husband and Mr. George behaved shockingly badly through the whole of it.”
“We laughed at the wrong bits, when we ought to have been dabbing at our eyes with our handkerchiefs,” the General confessed, grinning. “That is the plain truth.”
“You made every person in the surrounding boxes turn around and hiss ‘Hush!’ at you! You set the pit calling out for silence in the boxes up there! I never saw such outrageous conduct in all my days, I was quite ready to hide my face for shame, Mr. Lambert!”
“Your mamma thought it was a tragedy, you see, and we thought it was a great bit of fun,” the General said. “George and I behaved perfectly well, did we not, Theo?”
“Not when I was looking your way, papa!” Theo shot back at once.
“Did anyone ever see such a saucy little baggage?” the General laughed.
“I did not speak until I was spoken to, you know, sir,” Theo added, with a modest little tilt of her chin. “I own I was very much moved by the play, and by Mrs. Woffington’s beauty and acting. My heart ached so for that poor mother who found her long-lost child only to lose him again. I am sorry if I ought not to have been sorry!”
“Women are not half so clever as men, you know, Theo,” Hetty called from her end of the table, throwing a sly look across at Harry. “Next time we go to the play, brother Jack, do pinch my arm when it is time to cry, and nudge my elbow when it is proper to laugh, will you?”
“I wish they had added the fight scene,” General Lambert put in. “The brawl between little Norval and the giant Norwegian – that would have been rare sport. Jack, you ought to write to Mr. Rich the manager and suggest it at once.”
“I have not seen that play, but I did see Slack and Broughton box at Marybone Gardens once,” Harry said gravely, and blinked in surprise when the whole table burst out laughing. “It would not take any giant at all to knock over that little fellow in the red boots,” he added. “For my part, I could throw him right over my shoulder.”
“Mr. Garrick is a small man in height, but there are moments when he looks every inch a giant,” Mr. Spencer said. “How grand he was in *Macbeth*, Mr. Warrington! That dagger scene was so awful it made your hair stand on end. You should have seen our host here, ladies! When I brought him back to the green room, I introduced him to both Mr. Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard, and the actress playing Lady Macbeth did him the very great honor of taking a pinch of rappee straight out of his snuff box.”
“Did the Thane of Cawdor’s lady sneeze after?” the General boomed out in a deep, tragic voice.
“She thanked Mr. Warrington in tones so hollow and dramatic that he stumbled back in shock, and must have spilled half the rest of his snuff over the green room floor – for Macbeth sneezed three times right after!”
“Macbeth! Macbeth! Macbeth!” the General chanted, thumping the table lightly. “And that great philosopher Mr. Johnson who was standing close by called out, ‘Mind your nose, Davy, lest your sneeze wake Duncan!’ – who, by the way, was leaning against the wall chatting with the three witches while they waited for their next scene.”
“Oh! I would give worlds to go behind the scenes at a play!” Theo cried out, her eyes sparkling.
“And see the stagehands yank the ropes, smell the thick tallow candle smoke, peer at the pasteboard gold crowns and tinsel jewels and the painted old actresses? No, do not look too close,” George said, the playful scepticism soft in his voice as he sipped his glass of hock. “You were cross with your papa and me, you know.”
“Nay, George!” the girl protested.
“Nay? I say yes! You were angry with us for laughing when you were all ready to weep. If I may speak for both of us, sir,” he added, bowing to the General, “I think we could not bring ourselves to grieve like the ladies, because we had already peeked behind the scenes of that kind of performance, as it were. We stood close enough to the tragic young hero to see half his grand speeches were just rant and tinsel; and that the pale grieving mother’s deathly pallor was nothing but white chalk, and all her great sobbing was just for show, done with her handkerchief. Own it now, Theo, you thought me quite unfeeling last night, did you not?”
“If you have already worked that out without me confessing, what use is there in me saying so?” Theo smiled back.
“Suppose I were to die,” George went on, “and you saw Harry grieving for me – that would be real affliction, a true tragedy, and you would grieve too. But you would not feel any of that real grief if you saw the undertaker parading around in his weepers and long black cloak, would you?”
“Indeed I would, sir!” Mrs. Lambert declared. “And so would any daughter of mine, I promise you.”
“Perhaps we would find weepers of our own, Mr. Warrington,” Theo said softly, and at that George’s cheeks flushed bright red, and hers did too, for both of them noticed Hetty’s bright curious eyes fixed on the pair of them from across the table.
“Our older writers understood very little of true pathos,” Mr. Spencer, the Temple wit, remarked.
“What of Sophocles and Antigone?” Jack Lambert called out.
“Faith, most of us wits about town trouble ourselves very little about him, unless an Oxford gentleman comes along to remind us he exists! I did not mean to go back further than Mr. Shakespeare, who I think we can all agree does not understand elegant pathos half as well as our modern playwrights. Has he ever written anything that comes close to Belvidera, or Monimia, or Jane Shore? Can you find any of his comic heroines with the elegance of Congreve?” The Templar held out his snuff box to the guests on either side of him.
“I wager Mr. Spencer has tried his own hand at a play, has he not?” someone asked.
“Many a gentleman of leisure has. I will own Mr. Garrick had a copy of one of mine a while back, though he saw fit to send it back to me.”
“And I confess I have four full acts of a tragedy tucked away in a box somewhere,” George added.
“I will wager it is better than any of those modern things,” Harry whispered to Hetty, his neighbour.
“Is it a tragedy or a comedy?” Mrs. Lambert asked.
“Oh, a tragedy, with at least two or three dreadful murders, I promise you,” George laughed.
“The tragedy! Fetch the tragedy at once, we shall read it aloud!” Mrs. Lambert called to Gumbo, George’s Black servant. Gumbo bowed low. “Tragedy? Yes, madam.”
“It is in the great cowskin trunk, Gumbo,” George said, deadpan. Gumbo bowed again, perfectly grave. “Yes, sir.”
“But that trunk is at our agent’s warehouse all the way in Bristol, stacked at the bottom under piles of linen, books, boots and trinkets,” George went on. “Gumbo would have to hire post-horses, ride all the way down there, and we could keep our supper going until he comes back the day after tomorrow.”
The ladies all groaned a comical disappointed “Oh!” and the General, more practical, spoke up. “Let us all be thankful for our escape. It is high time we made for home too. Our young gentlemen have treated us nobly tonight – let us drink a parting bumper to Madam Esmond Warrington of Castlewood, Virginia. Tell me, boys, what if you came home to find a tall handsome new stepfather waiting for you? Women of her years have been known to marry again, after all.”
“To Madam Esmond Warrington, my old schoolfellow!” Mrs. Lambert toasted. “I shall write to her straight away to tell her what a lovely supper her sons have given us. And Mr. George, I shall not breathe a word about how shockingly he behaved at the play last night!”
With that final toast, the whole company took their leave, the General’s coach waiting outside with a footman holding a blazing flambeau to light their path home.

After such a pleasant gathering, what could be more natural than George paying a call the very next day, to inquire after whether the Lamberts had made it home safe and slept well? Their coach could have taken the dark unfenced road behind Montague House towards Oxford Road, and been set upon by footpads in the fields. The ladies could have caught a chill after the excitement of the play, or tossed and turned all night. There was no excuse he needed to make to himself for turning his steps towards Dean Street, so he shut his books early at the Sloane Museum, and found he could not for the life of him recall a single word he had read that day.
What young man who has ever been in love does not recognise that giddy eagerness, that foolish hesitation, that huffing “psha, what nonsense” to himself as he fumbles with his gloves, and his heart hammers so hard he can hear it as he raps at the door of the house he is half afraid to enter? Every old man who ever was young remembers that throbbing in the chest, long after the years have worn the spring out of his steps. We old worn chargers turned out to grass, if we hear a trumpet blow over the hedge, we still kick up our heels and gallop a little way across the paddock, before we remember our old bones are not made for running any more.
We follow the footman up the stairs to the General’s apartments, and enter right behind George Esmond Warrington, who makes a very grand formal bow the moment he steps over the threshold. There is only one lady in the room, seated by the window – there is seldom much bright sunshine in Dean Street, but the light gilds Theo’s cheeks like a fresh bloom of rose, and her eyes shine as soft as violets. A hundred years ago, poets in the old magazines would have written acrostics about her, called her Lambertella, praised her neck as lily-white and her form as nymph-like, and printed the verses right between a map of Prague and a chart of the Delaware counties.
Theo blushes exactly like that rose when she sees him. Mrs. Lambert had insisted only an hour earlier that her daughter was pale and tired, and ought not to come out with the rest of the party that morning, when they went to pay their respects to Lord Wrotham’s family to thank them for the use of their house. Papa is at the Horse Guards, in wonderful spirits, for he expects a new military command soon, though Mrs. Lambert is in a constant fret lest he be ordered to serve overseas again.
“Harry and my brother Jack have gone off to visit my little brother at his school at the Chartreux,” Theo told him, her head bent over the little boy’s shirt she was busy hemming. “They say my two older brothers are bound for the clergy, I think. Harry comes over almost every day now,” she added, looking up at him. “Poor fellow, he likes our company far better than the fine society folks who do not care for him now that he is no longer rich and fashionable himself. Why have you not set up for the ton yourself, Mr. Warrington? You never frequent the chocolate houses or the racecourses.”
“Has Harry gotten so much good out of all those haunts and grand friends that I ought to go and follow his example?”
“You might at least visit Sir Miles Warrington, your uncle – I am sure his doors are wide open to receive you. Her ladyship called on us this morning in her chair, and did nothing but sing your praises! She declares you are certain to rise fast in the world now. Says his Royal Highness the Duke spoke very highly of you at court. When you are a great man, will you forget all of us, Mr. Warrington?”
“Yes, when I am a great man I will, Miss Lambert.”
“Well then, Mr. George –”
“Mr. George? When papa and mamma are not even in the room, you surely do not need to stand on ceremony with a ‘mister’,” Theo said, looking out the window, a little flustered. “What have you been up to all day? Reading books, or writing more of your tragedy? Is it going to be the kind that makes us cry, the kind we like, or just the scary full of murders kind you favour?”
“There is plenty of killing, I fear, but very little of the soft pathos. I have not spent much time around women, you see. I am told I have a sharp, sarcastic way about me that displeases most of them.”
“Perhaps you never much cared to try to please them?” Theo said, blushing.
“I displeased you last night, did I not?”
“Yes, but it was not really displeasure. Afterwards, I thought you and papa were quite right.”
“Did you think about me at all when we went our separate ways last night, Theo?”
“Yes, George – that is, Mr. – well, George! I thought you were right about the play, that all the great grief we were weeping over was nothing but show and affectation. I wonder if it is a blessing or a curse to be able to see the tricks behind the show so clearly? Hetty and I agreed we would be far more careful from now on about how we let ourselves get swept up by staged tragedies. So be careful when yours is finished! What is its name?”
“It has not been christened yet. Will you be its godmother? The name of the main character is –”
But just at that very moment, Mrs. Lambert and Hetty walked in the door from their walk, and Mrs. Lambert broke into a flutter of exclamations, saying she never expected to see Mr. Warrington that day – well, that she had half thought he might come, that it was so very good of him to call, and the play and the supper had all been perfectly charming, only Theo had a little headache that morning.
“I am sure it is all better now, mamma,” Hetty said, with a little grin.
“Indeed it was never anything to fuss over,” Theo added, tossing her head.
Then they got to talking about Harry, and how low he had been of late. He needed something to occupy him, some purpose. He hung around the Military Coffee-House from dawn to dusk, poring over every map of Frederick the Great’s campaigns he could get his hands on. It was cruel to keep him lingering in London after he had squandered his own fortune, living off his brother. He never complained, but everyone could see how ashamed he was of his old useless life, and how he avoided all his former rakish haunts and companions. Only the other day, walking past the guard post at St James’s with Jack Lambert, he had leaned over and said, “Why may I not be a soldier too? I am as tall as that sentry over there, and I can shoot a fowling-piece as well as any man alive. But I cannot earn sixpence a day. I wasted all my own money, and now I am eating half my brother’s bread. He is the best of brothers, but that makes it all the more shameful for me to live on him. Do not tell him I said that, Jack Lambert.”
“And my boy Jack promised he would not breathe a word,” Mrs. Lambert said. The girls had stepped out of the room a few minutes before she spoke, to give the two of them privacy. George told her he had already written home to his mother, that half his own patrimony was at Harry’s disposal, and the other half too if he needed it, to buy an army commission, or fund any project that might give him occupation and advancement.
“He has the best brother in the world, that is for certain. Let us hope good times come for him soon,” Mrs. Lambert sighed.
“Good things come to those who wait, madam,” George said softly.
“Ah, George! My Theo is – well, never mind what Theo is, George Warrington!” the lady cried, her eyes brimming over with happy tears. “Foolish of me, I know, I am going to make a silly gaby of myself, just as I did at the tragedy last night.”
George had been turning over a clever little private plan in his head for weeks now, one he thought would secure Harry’s future.
