As the autumnal hues of Diplow's rolling hills began to unfold, Sir Hugo's arrival brought a welcome warmth to the neighborhood, weaving a tapestry of connections between the stately homes of Brackenshaw and Quetcham, and the respectable shop-parlors in Wanchester. A Liberal of good breeding, Sir Hugo delighted in displaying his affability, yet his true motives lay in the preservation of social hierarchies, which he believed reform would not disrupt. His invitations to the local solicitors and village curates were carefully curated, ensuring a harmonious balance of guests who would appreciate the distinction. Easy-going Lord Brackenshaw, for instance, would not object to meeting Robinson the attorney, but Robinson would be taken aback if invited to mingle with those of his supposed equals. Sir Hugo's tactful approach earned him popularity and pleased others, aligning with his affable nature.

The rector of Pennicote found Diplow's atmosphere a far cry from the haughty disdain he had endured under Grandcourt's rule. Sir Hugo's courteous treatment of Mr. Gascoigne stemmed from his growing admiration for Mrs. Grandcourt, whose charms had captivated him. Though he would not reveal his feelings to anyone, including his wife, his discretion spoke volumes about his respect for women's secrets.

Meanwhile, Daniel Deronda, now betrothed to Mirah, felt compelled to share his plans with Sir Hugo, sensing that his fatherly friend might be disappointed by this turn of events. Writing a letter allowed him to convey the news without risking an impulsive reaction that might later be regretted. Deronda anticipated Sir Hugo's possible dismay, knowing that verbal confrontations could leave lasting impressions.

Upon receiving the letter, Sir Hugo's initial response was one of vexation. To soften the blow, he decided to confide in Lady Mallinger, trusting that her reaction would be an exasperated yet justifiable astonishment. When she expressed her distress that Daniel's remarkable talents and presence in the household had been eclipsed by his fixation on Judaism, Sir Hugo countered with reassuring words:

"Ah, nonsense, my dear! Depend upon it, Dan won't make a fool of himself. He has grand notions about Judaism—political views that you can't grasp. No fear but Dan will keep his wits about him."
