Beyond the great arteries of London, certain roads stretch far into the countryside, lingering as attenuated ghosts of streets. Buildings give way to vast gaps, yet the line persists: a cluster of shops, a fenced paddock, a renowned public house, a market garden, a solitary grand estate, another field, another inn, and so on. Walking along such a road, one might encounter a house that arrests the gaze, though the observer struggles to articulate why. It is a long, low structure running parallel to the road, painted in white and pale green, featuring a veranda with sun-blinds and porches topped by quaint, umbrella-like cupolas. It is undeniably old-fashioned, quintessentially English, and deeply suburban in the manner of wealthy Clapham. Yet, it possesses an air of being constructed specifically for the heat. Its white walls and blinds evoke vague associations with turbans and palm trees. Perhaps it was built by an Anglo-Indian.

Any passerby would feel a nameless fascination for this dwelling, sensing that a story awaited within. That intuition was correct. This is the chronicle of the extraordinary events that transpired there during the Whitsuntide of eighteen—eighty-something.

On the Thursday preceding Whit-Sunday, around half-past four in the afternoon, a visitor would have witnessed the front door swing open. Stepping forth was Father Brown of the small church of St. Mungo, smoking a large pipe, accompanied by his tall French associate, Flambeau, who smoked diminutive cigarettes. While these men might seem peripheral to the narrative, they were not the sole points of interest revealed by the opening door. To comprehend the tragedy that unfolded, one must first understand the peculiar architecture of the house.

The residence was designed in the shape of a "T," albeit with an exceptionally long cross-piece and a remarkably short tail. The long cross-piece formed the street-facing facade, two stories high and housing nearly all significant rooms, centered around the front door. Extending directly backward from this center was the short tail, a single-story wing consisting of two connected rooms. The first was the study of the celebrated Mr. Leonard Quinton, where he penned his wild Oriental poems and romances. Beyond lay a glass conservatory bursting with tropical blooms of unique, almost monstrous beauty, aglow under the afternoon sun. Consequently, whenever the hall door opened, passersby often halted in stunned awe, gazing down a perspective of rich interiors toward a scene resembling a transformation tableau in a fairy play: purple clouds, golden suns, and crimson stars that were simultaneously scorchingly vivid and ethereally distant.

Leonard Quinton had meticulously curated this visual spectacle, arguably expressing his personality more profoundly in its arrangement than in his verse. He was a man who immersed himself in color, indulging his passion for hue at the expense of form—even good form. This obsession turned his genius toward Eastern art and imagery: bewildering carpets and blinding embroideries where colors fell into a chaotic fortune, teaching nothing and typifying nothing. He attempted, with acknowledged imagination if not complete artistic success, to compose epics and love stories reflecting a riot of violent, even cruel color. His tales depicted tropical heavens of burning gold or blood-red copper; Eastern heroes riding atop elephants painted purple or peacock green, wearing twelve-turbaned mitres; and gigantic jewels so massive a hundred negroes could scarcely carry them, yet burning with ancient, strange hues.

To put it plainly, Quinton dealt largely in Eastern heavens, worse than most Western hells; in Eastern monarchs who might be termed maniacs; and in Eastern jewels that a Bond Street jeweler might deem counterfeit. He was a genius, albeit a morbid one, and his morbidity manifested more in his life than in his work. Temperamentally weak and waspish, his health had deteriorated significantly due to Oriental experiments with opium. His wife—a handsome, hardworking, and overworked woman—objected to the opium, but protested even more vehemently against a living Indian hermit dressed in white and yellow robes, whom her husband insisted on hosting for months as a spiritual guide through the Eastern heavens and hells.

It was from this artistic household that Father Brown and Flambeau emerged onto the doorstep, stepping out with evident relief. Flambeau, having known Quinton in wild student days in Paris, had renewed their acquaintance for a weekend. However, aside from Flambeau's recent maturation, he did not fare well with the poet. Choking on opium and scribbling erotic verses on vellum was not his conception of how a gentleman should descend into damnation. Pausing on the step before venturing into the garden, the front gate was flung open violently. A young man, wearing a billycock hat askew on the back of his head, tumbled up the stairs in eager haste. He was a dissipated-looking youth with a gorgeous red necktie disheveled as if worn to bed, constantly fidgeting and brandishing a small jointed cane.

"I say," he panted, "I wish to see old Quinton. I must see him. Is he gone?"

"Mr. Quinton is in, I believe," replied Father Brown, cleaning his pipe, "but I'm unsure if you may see him. The doctor is currently with him."

The youth, appearing imperfectly sober, stumbled into the hall. Simultaneously, the doctor exited Quinton's study, closing the door and beginning to don his gloves.

"Seek Mr. Quinton?" the doctor asked coolly. "No, I fear you cannot. In fact, you must not on any account. No one must disturb him; I have just administered his sleeping draught."

"No, but listen, old chap," interjected the youth in the red tie, attempting affectionately to seize the doctor by his coat lapels. "Look here. I'm completely screwed, I tell you. I—"

"It is futile, Mr. Atkinson," the doctor retorted, forcing the boy back. "When you can counteract the effects of a drug, I shall reconsider my decision." Settling his hat, he stepped into the sunlight alongside the others. He was a bull-necked, good-natured little man with a small moustache, seemingly ordinary yet radiating competence.

The young man in the billycock, lacking tact beyond the instinct to grab coats, stood dazed outside the door, watching the trio walk away through the garden.

"That was a sound, spanking lie I told just now," remarked the medical man, laughing. "In truth, poor Quinton hasn't received his sleeping draught for nearly half an hour. But I refuse to let him be bothered by that little pest, who merely wishes to borrow money he'd never repay. He's a dirty little scamp, though he is Mrs. Quinton's brother, and she is as fine a woman as ever walked."

"Yes," agreed Father Brown. "She is a good woman."

"Therefore, I intend to loiter in the garden until this creature departs," continued the doctor. "Then I shall return to Quinton with the medicine. Atkinson cannot gain entry, for I have locked the door."

"In that case, Dr. Harris," suggested Flambeau, "we might as well stroll around the rear via the end of the conservatory. There is no entrance there, but it is worth viewing from the outside."

"Yes, and I might catch a glimpse of my patient," chuckled the doctor. "He prefers to lie on an ottoman at the very end of the conservatory amidst those blood-red poinsettias; it gives me the creeps. But what are you doing?"

Father Brown had paused, picking up from the long grass—where it had been almost entirely concealed—a peculiar, crooked Oriental knife, exquisitely inlaid with colored stones and metals.

"What is this?" Father Brown asked, regarding it with distaste.

"Oh, presumably Quinton's," said Dr. Harris casually. "He has all manner of Chinese knick-knacks about the place. Or perhaps it belongs to that mild Hindoo he keeps on a leash."

"What Hindoo?" Father Brown queried, still fixated on the dagger.

"Oh, some Indian conjurer," the doctor dismissed lightly. "A fraud, of course."

"You do not believe in magic?" Father Brown asked, without lifting his gaze.

"Oh, crickey! Magic!" exclaimed the doctor.

"It is very beautiful," murmured the priest in a low, dreamy tone. "The colors are exquisite. But the shape is incorrect."

"For what purpose?" asked Flambeau, staring intently.

"For anything. It is the wrong shape in the abstract. Have you never felt this regarding Eastern art? The colors are intoxicatingly lovely, yet the shapes are mean and bad—deliberately so. I have encountered wickedness in Turkish carpets."

"Mon Dieu!" cried Flambeau, laughing.

"They are letters and symbols in a language I do not know, yet I know they signify evil words," the priest continued, his voice dropping. "The lines go astray intentionally—like serpents coiling to escape."

"What devilry are you discussing?" demanded the doctor with a loud laugh.
