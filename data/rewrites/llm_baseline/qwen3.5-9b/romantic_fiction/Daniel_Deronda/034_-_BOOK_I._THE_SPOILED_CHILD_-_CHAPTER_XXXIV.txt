*"He is named Israel. Upon him the witch's spell hath changed him into a dog. * * * * * Yet every Friday evening, in the twilight hour, suddenly the magic wanes, and the dog anew becomes a human creature."* —HEINE: *The Princess Sabbath*.

When Deronda arrived at five o'clock, the shop was already closed. The door was unlatched for him by a Christian servant, who led him into the room situated behind the counter. To his surprise, he found the interior surprisingly charming. The house was old and extended somewhat at the rear; likely, the large chamber he now occupied would have been gloomy under the harsh glare of daylight, but now it glowed with a warm, agreeable radiance. A magnificent antique brass lamp, holding seven oil-lights, hung suspended above a table draped in snow-white cloth. The ceiling and walls bore the soft, smoky patina of age, creating shadows that rendered the human figures within a luminous, Venetian-like hue.

The grandmother was dressed in shades of yellowish-brown, adorned with a heavy gold chain serving as a necklace. Under this light, her yellow complexion, marked by dark eyebrows and framed by a roll of gray hair, possessed a striking, picturesque beauty. Beside her, young Mrs. Cohen wore red and black, encircling her neck with a thick string of large artificial pearls. The infant slept peacefully in a cradle beneath a scarlet coverlet. Adelaide Rebekah was attired in braided amber fabric, while Jacob Alexander wore black velveteen paired with scarlet stockings.

As the four pairs of dark eyes gleamed with welcome upon Deronda's arrival, he felt a sudden flush of shame regarding the haughty disdain these cheerful-looking individuals had previously inspired in him during the day. The reception was nothing short of cordial. Both mother and grandmother seemed to derive an additional sense of dignity from hosting a guest within the privacy of their home, displaying a warmth that felt genuinely hospitable.

Deronda glanced around with mild wonder at the antiquated furnishings. The oak bureau and the tall side-table appeared to be acquired through necessity rather than aesthetic choice. On the side-table stood a large dish of blue and yellow ceramic ware, flanked by two aged silver vessels. Before them rested a massive volume bound in darkened vellum with a deeply ribbed spine. At the far end of the room, an open door revealed a glimpse of another illuminated chamber. While absorbing these details in passing glances, Deronda focused his attention on Jacob's earnest inquiries regarding a knife. Jacob had gone to considerable trouble to procure one featuring a hook and a white handle, presenting it eagerly. "Is this the sort you require, Jacob?" he asked.

Jacob subjected the item to a rigorous inspection, opening the hooks and blades, before producing the cork-screw knife intended for trade. "Why prefer a hook over a cork-screw?" Deronda asked. "'Cause I can grab things with a hook," Jacob replied seriously. "A cork-screw fits nowhere but corks. But it suits you better; you can pull corks."

"Do you agree to the exchange, then?" Deronda asked, noting the grandmother watching with evident delight. "What else do you carry in your pockets?" Jacob pressed, his demeanor grave.

"Hush, hush, Jacob, dear," the grandmother chided gently. Mindful of propriety, Deronda responded, "I believe I ought not to disclose that. Our purpose concerns only the knives."

Jacob scanned Deronda's face intently for a moment, seemingly satisfied with his assessment, before nodding solemnly. "I'll swap," he declared, handing over the cork-screw knife. Deronda accepted it with equal gravity, slipping it into his pocket.

Immediately, the youngest son of Shem darted into the adjacent room, his voice rising in rapid chatter, only to return moments later. Spotting his father, the child snatched a small velveteen hat from a chair and donned it before approaching. Mr. Cohen retained his own hat, ignoring the visitor initially, but stood still as the two children rushed forward to clasp his knees. He placed his hands upon each child in turn, uttering a Hebrew benediction. His wife, having recently lifted the sleeping baby from the cradle, brought the infant forward, holding it beneath her husband's outstretched hands to receive the blessing even in slumber.

For a fleeting instant, Deronda perceived that this pawnbroker, proud of his calling, was not entirely devoid of spiritual depth.

"Well, sir, you seem to have found a warm welcome in my household," Cohen said, removing his hat and resuming his usual composure. "And you were punctual. There is nothing quite like a bit of pressure here," he added, tapping his side pocket as he settled onto a seat. "It benefits us all in rotation. I've felt it when I needed to arrange payments. I started fitting various boxes. It's invigorating for the mind. Now then! Let us see, let us see."

"That is the ring I mentioned," Deronda said, lifting it from his finger. "I believe it cost a hundred pounds. It should serve as adequate security for fifty, I trust. I expect to redeem it within a month or so."

Cohen's glistening eyes narrowed slightly as they met the innocent gaze of this unsophisticated young gentleman, who apparently assumed that redemption was a source of satisfaction for pawnbrokers. Cohen took the ring, inspected it briefly, and returned it with an air of indifference. "Good, good. We shall discuss it after our meal. Perhaps you will join us, if you have no objection. My wife and I shall feel honored, and so will Mother; won't you, Mother?"

The invitation was enthusiastically echoed by both women, and Deronda gladly accepted. Everyone turned and gathered around the table. Aside from a single dish concealed beneath a napkin, no food was visible yet. Mrs. Cohen had positioned a china basin near her husband so he might wash his hands. However, after placing his hat back on his head, Cohen paused and called out loudly, "Mordecai!"

Could this be part of a religious rite? Deronda wondered, unsure what ancient customs might be expected. He heard a "Yes" from the next room and turned toward the open doorway. To his astonishment, he beheld the enigmatic Jew he had encountered earlier that morning in the bookshop. Their eyes met, and Mordecai appeared just as surprised as Deronda; neither offered a sign of recognition. Yet, as Mordecai took his seat at the far end of the table, he merely bowed his head toward the guest in a cold, distant manner, as if the disappointment of the morning lingered as an unpleasant association with this new acquaintance.

Cohen now washed his hands, reciting Hebrew words throughout the process. He then removed the napkin from the dish, revealing two long, flat loaves sprinkled with seeds—a remembrance of the manna that sustained the wandering ancestors. Breaking off small pieces, he distributed them to each family member, including Adelaide Rebekah, who stood on a chair, her entire height displayed in her amber gown, her little Jewish nose appearing elongated by the compression of her lips as she strained to maintain a proper posture. Cohen pronounced another Hebrew blessing, after which the men uncovered their heads, everyone seated themselves, and the meal commenced without any particular feature that captured Deronda's immediate interest.

He was barely conscious of the dishes before him, preoccupied with a desire to steer the conversation toward questions that might yield answers. Simultaneously, his thoughts drifted to Mordecai, between whom and himself there existed an exchange of fascinated, half-furtive glances. Mordecai lacked a handsome Sabbath garment; instead of the threadbare, rusty black coat worn the previous morning, he wore a light drab jacket that appeared to have once been a handsome, loose paletot, now shrunk tight by repeated washings. This change in attire accentuated his dark-haired, eager face, which possessed a prophetic intensity reminiscent of Ezekiel, certainly not fashionable in contemporary eyes.

It was notable that the slender tails of the fried fish were reserved for Mordecai, and generally, the portions allotted to him reflected those given to a poorer relative—a survival of prehistoric custom, perhaps, no longer widely acknowledged as superstitious. Mr. Cohen maintained a lively conversation, introducing topics always chosen with tact (for the Jew prides himself on his loyalty): the Queen and the Royal Family, the Emperor and Empress of France. Both the grandmother and wife participated with genuine enthusiasm. Young Mrs. Cohen demonstrated an accurate memory of distinguished birthdays, while the elder assisted her son in recounting events from when the Imperial couple visited the city ten years prior.

"I daresay you know all about it better than we do, sir," Cohen remarked repeatedly, prefacing his detailed accounts. These interesting narratives continued in a spirited trio.

"Our baby is named Eugénie Esther," announced young Mrs. Cohen, speaking vivaciously. "It is wonderful how the Emperor resembles a cousin of mine in the face," added the grandmother. "It struck me like lightning when I first saw him. I couldn't have imagined it."

"Mother and I went to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace," Mr. Cohen stated. "I had a difficult task managing Mother; she might have been crushed flat—though she was nearly as robust then as she is now.
