If this jumble of words, the fruit of two frantic years' labor, fails to please you artistically—as it surely must, given how difficult true art is—it at least offers the virtue of a certain raw honesty. A polished novel might lose this edge, smoothing out the rough truths until nothing remains but sugar-coated lies. By speaking directly to you, I am forced to drop my mask and blurt out exactly what my heart dictates. There are likely a thousand typos, hasty phrases, and printer's errors scattered through here that I'd love to scrub away if I could see the whole thing again without blinking. Think of this less as a published masterpiece and more as a late-night confession, a rambling chat where I inevitably expose my own foibles, vanities, and quirks.

Just as you wouldn't judge a friend solely by one bad mood or a single offhand remark, you shouldn't judge me by this draft alone. The question isn't "Is this perfect?" but rather, "Does the fellow behind the page mean to tell the truth?" Am I just a charlatan shouting sentimental nonsense to sound deep? Or am I chasing fame with cheap tricks? I cannot pretend otherwise. Fate has thrown me a lucky break; I've attracted far more readers than I ever dreamed possible. But I won't tell you to shut your eyes to flaws or let your eyelids droop over boring passages. All I ask is that you trust me when I say I'm trying to get it straight. Without truth, there is no story worth telling.

Now, for the thrill-seekers among you: yes, this book *did* start with a grand, blood-chilling scheme. Ladies and gentlemen, you were going to read about high-stakes drama! Imagine a ruffian with a charming streak of virtue hanging out in St. Giles's, constantly shadowed by a debutante from Belgravia! Picture the clash of worlds—the street slang battling against polite drawing-room chatter, the hair-raising chases, the fistfights, and potentially some murder! Even as recently as nine this morning, my dear old pal Colonel Altamont was scheduled for the gallows. I only spared his neck at the last minute when he popped up at the window, looking rather cheerful about it.

Alas, I scrapped that plan (much to the relief of the publishers, who are usually quite eager for a bit of sensationalism). Why? Because I tried to write it and realized I knew absolutely nothing about convicts. Having never spent time with a jailbird or a criminal, I couldn't possibly compete with the likes of Monsieur Eugène Sue. To paint a real scoundrel, you have to make him so terrifyingly vile that you can't bear to print his face. Unless you are brave enough to show the ugly truth, perhaps you should stay home and draw flowers instead.

And then there's the matter of "Gentlemen" like myself. This story attempts to sketch one such soul—not better, not worse, just average. Yet, we dare not show them as they truly are, with their glaring selfishness and the petty vices bred by their education. Since the great Henry Fielding wrote *Tom Jones*, no decent novelist in England seems allowed to portray a real man with full force. Society demands we wrap our heroes in silk, forcing them to wear that fake, polite smile known as the "conventional simper." Several gentlewomen have sent angry letters, and some subscribers have dropped their names, simply because I dared to describe a young man struggling with temptation.

My original aim was simple: to show that a man possesses the passions to feel deeply, yet also the courage and kindness to master them. I promise you, you won't hear the gritty, messy details of what really drives people in clubs, colleges, or mess rooms. You certainly won't learn the actual conversations of your sons. I have tried to inject a little more bare-knuckle frankness than is usual, hoping for no harm and with no evil intent. If the truth sometimes tastes bitter, well, it is still the best medicine. Whether you sit in a library reading serious philosophy or sitting by the fire listening to a storyteller wind down, the goal remains the same: to speak plainly and bid you farewell.
