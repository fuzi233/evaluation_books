Returning from the hospital, an hour later, Romola chose a path that widened toward the river, reaching the banks far from the bustle of the Ponte Vecchio. Her destination was clear: she intended to hasten to San Stefano to find Baldassarre. Yet, a dread held her back; she feared the knowledge he might bring, yet felt that to leave him behind would be to sever a vital claim upon her soul.

As she neared the junction where the Por' Santa Maria lay to her right and the Ponte Vecchio to her left, she stumbled into a throng that suddenly sank to their knees. Without hesitation, Romola joined them. The Great Cross of the Duomo was passing, leading a solemn procession. Being late, she was forced to wait until the sacred burden had cleared the intersection.

When the Cross vanished and she rose, the sudden stillness and the weight of standing made her fatigue acute. Beside her, a shopkeeper spoke kindly, "Madonna Romola, you will tire standing here. Gian Fantoni would be glad to offer you a seat in his house; his door is right there. Let me open it. He loves God and the Frate as we do. His home is yours."

Romola, accustomed to such fraternal greetings from citizens whose faces she knew from the Duomo, felt a shift in her understanding of home. It was no longer the lonely chamber in the Via de' Bardi, but the very circuit of Florence itself, where every street corner seemed to offer a look of welcome. Gladly, she accepted the invitation, passing through the open door to be led by the hose-vendor to an upstairs window. There, a stout woman, dressed in the plain garb of the Piagnoni, made reverent space for her among bright draperies. From this vantage point, Romola watched the procession pour slowly between the houses on the bridge, while the river and the Lung' Arno stretched out toward the Santa Trinita.

The procession moved in sadness and silence. No wailing chant broke the air; only the tramp of feet and the faint sweep of woolen robes. First came a long train of Florentine youth, their faces fresh and beardless, bearing high the white image of the youthful Jesus, crowned with golden glory, standing beside the tall cross where thorns and nails awaited.

Following them were the Companies of Discipline, bound by secret vows of self-chastisement and piety. Their garments concealed all but their eyes, transforming sons, husbands, and fathers into anonymous symbols of a common vow. Each company bore its color and badge, yet all wore a shroud-like garb that expressed only fellowship.

In contrast, the multitude of monks appeared as distinct individuals despite their shared tonsure and frocks. First came a white stream of reformed Benedictines, followed by the vast host of the Frati Minori, or Franciscans, clad in grey with knotted cords and wooden sandals—perhaps the most numerous order in Florence, zealous in their love for mankind and their hatred of the Dominicans. After the grey came the black of the Augustinians of San Spirito, men who had inherited Boccaccio's library and formed Florence's most learned company. Then the white-over-dark of the Carmelites, and finally the unmixed black of the Servites, founded by seven merchants who renounced their gains to adore the Divine Mother.

The hearts of the onlookers quickened as a stream of black and white emerged from the bridge—black mantles over white scapularies. The Dominicans were coming. Those from Fiesole led the way, their mantles coarse and often threadbare, a testament to the Prior of San Marco's strict reduction of poverty and discipline. Yet, in that long line, one mantle stood out, slightly less worn than the rest, topped by a tonsured head recognizable to those who had seen it on bronze medals, guarded by armed men, or transfigured by the inward flame of the orator.

As Savonarola's approach became certain, no one dared break the stillness with sound, though a mingled hiss of curses and murmurs of blessing drifted through the air. Perhaps it was the sense that the hissing prevailed that caused two or three disciples in the foreground to fall to their knees as if something divine passed. This silent homage spread like a shockwave along the streets, bending the most stubborn heads, while hatred gathered an intense expression elsewhere. As Savonarola ascended the Por' Santa Maria, Romola saw someone at an upper window spit upon him.

Monks returned—the Frati Umiliati from Ognissanti, heirs to a glorious wool-trading tradition, followed by Vallombrosan and other Benedictines, their varied forms marking a change of spirit through a change of garb. Finally, the shaven crowns ended, giving way to the train of untonsured secular priests.

Then came the twenty-one incorporated Arts of Florence, arrayed in long lines with banners declaring their distinct functions, from bakers to judges. After them marched the secondary officers of State, ascending to the greater, until the line of secularities was broken by the Canons of the Duomo. They carried a sacred relic: the head of San Zenobio, immortal bishop of Florence, enclosed in silver, whose virtues were believed to have saved the city a millennium prior.

This was the nucleus of the procession. Behind the relic came the archbishop in a gorgeous cope, with a canopy held high above him. Following him was the mysterious, hidden Image. First veiled by rich brocade curtains enclosing an outer painted tabernacle, and within that, by an ancient shrine never opened in living memory, lay the image of the Pitying Mother, found ages ago in the soil of L'Impruneta, uttering a cry as the spade struck it. Usually, this unseen Image was preceded by rich gifts, lists of precious offerings, veils, and mantles woven by emulous communities. The richest, it was said, had been crafted by a poor abbess and her nuns, who wove a mantle of gold brocade with their prayers, embroidered it with devotion, and saw their work presented to the Blessed Virgin by two youths who spread white wings and vanished into the blue.

But today, no gifts were carried before the tabernacle; no donations were offered except to the poor. This was the counsel of Fra Girolamo, whose preaching insisted not on gifts to invisible powers, but on aid to visible need. Altars had been raised at various points for the oblations of the needy. Not even a torch was carried. Surely the hidden Mother cared less for torches and brocade than for the wail of the hungry. Florence was in extremity; she had done her utmost and could only wait for a divine intervention beyond her own power.

The Frate in the torn mantle had promised help would come, and many faint-hearted clung to his word rather than the virtues of the unseen Image. Yet, some fierce-hearted souls rejoiced secretly that the Frate's promise might prove false.

Slowly the tabernacle moved forward, knees bent in profound stillness. The train of priests and chaplains from L'Impruneta stirred no passion. The procession was about to close with the Priors and the Gonfaloniere; the long train of companies and symbols, with their silent music that stirred the mind like a chorus, was passing out of sight. A faint yearning hope struggled against the accustomed despondency.

Romola, whose heart had swelled with foreboding and an enthusiasm of fellowship made habitual by the last two years, gave a deep sigh as if releasing a long mental tension. She remained on her knees, overcome by languor. Suddenly, something bright-colored flashed from between the houses onto the distant bridge. Romola started up, stretching her arms and leaning from the window. The black drapery fell from her head, and the golden gleam of her hair and the flush in her face seemed the effect of a single illumination.

A shout arose instantly. The last troops of the procession paused, all faces turning toward the distant bridge. But the bridge was already passed. A horseman pressed at full gallop along the Arno; the sides of his bay horse, streaked with foam, looked white from swiftness. His cap flew loose from his red *becchetto*, and he waved an olive-branch in his hand. It was a messenger—a messenger of good tidings! The blessed olive-branch spoke afar off.

But the impatient people could not wait. They rushed to meet the oncomer, seizing the horse's reins, pushing and trampling. Now Romola saw that the horseman was her husband, Tito, sent to Pisa a few days prior on a private embassy. The recognition brought no new flash of joy to her eyes. She had checked her first impulsive attitude of expectation; her governing anxiety remained fixed on what news of relief had come for Florence.

"Good news!"
"Best news!"
"News to be paid with hose!" (*novelle da calze*)

These were the vague answers Tito met with as the crowd importuned him, until he pushed his horse to the meeting of the ways where the Gonfaloniere and the Priors awaited. There he paused, bowed low, and declared, "Magnificent Signori! I have to deliver to you the joyful news that the galleys from France, laden with corn and men, have arrived safely in the port of Leghorn, by favor of a strong wind which kept the enemy's fleet at a distance."

The words left Tito's lips and seemed to vibrate up the streets. A great shout rang through the air, rushing along the river, then another, and another, spreading along the line of the procession toward the Duomo. Fainter answering shouts followed, like the intermediate splash of distant waves in a great lake obeying one impulse.

For some minutes, no one attempted to speak further. The Signoria themselves lifted their caps, standing bare-headed in the presence of a rescue that had come from outside the limits of their own power—from that region of trust and resignation called divine.

Finally, as the signal was given to move forward, Tito said with a smile, "I ought to say that any hose to be bestowed by the Magnificent Signoria in reward of these tidings are due, not to me, but to another man who rode hard to bring them. He would have been here in my place if his horse had not broken down just before he reached Signa. Meo di Sasso will doubtless be here in an hour or two, and may all the more justly claim the glory of the messenger, because he has had the chief labor and has lost the chief delight."

It was a graceful way of stating a necessity. After a brief reply from the *Proposto*, the dignified extremity of the procession passed on, and Tito turned his horse's head to follow in its train. The great bell of the Palazzo Vecchio began to swing, giving a louder voice to the people's joy. At that moment, when Tito's attention was no longer imperatively directed, it might have been expected that he would look round and recognize Romola. Yet he appeared engaged with his cap, which, as the eager people led his horse, he seized and placed on his head, his right hand still encumbered by the olive-branch. He possessed a becoming air of lassitude after his exertions.

Romola, instead of making any effort to be recognized by him, threw her black drapery over her head again and remained perfectly quiet. Yet she felt almost sure that Tito had seen her; he had the power of seeing everything without seeming to see it.
