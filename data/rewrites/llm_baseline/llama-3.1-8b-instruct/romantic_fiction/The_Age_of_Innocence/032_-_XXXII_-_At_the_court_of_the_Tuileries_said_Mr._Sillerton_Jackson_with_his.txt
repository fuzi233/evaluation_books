In the opulent dining room of the van der Luydens' mansion on Madison Avenue, the evening air was alive with the soft murmur of conversation and the delicate scent of Skuytercliff carnations. Mr. Sillerton Jackson, with his characteristic reminiscent smile, leaned forward in his chair, his eyes twinkling with amusement. "At the court of the Tuileries," he began, "such liberties were taken for granted, and the boundaries of propriety were often pushed to the limit."

The evening's conversation had turned to the scandalous affair of Beaufort's financial collapse, and the moral implications that followed. Mrs. van der Luyden, her voice laced with a mixture of concern and disapproval, addressed May Archer directly. "I was told, dear, that your grandmother Mingott's carriage was seen at Mrs. Beaufort's doorstep. Can this possibly be true?"

May's cheeks flushed, and her mother intervened quickly, "If it was, I'm certain it was without Mrs. Mingott's knowledge or consent." The tension in the room was palpable, and Mr. van der Luyden added his thoughts, "Perhaps Madame Olenska's kind heart led her into the imprudence of calling on Mrs. Beaufort."

Mrs. Archer's voice dripped with irony as she observed, "Or perhaps her taste for unconventional company led her astray." Her eyes met those of her son, Newland, who sat quietly observing the exchange, his thoughts elsewhere. The discussion had reached a delicate point, and Mr. Jackson sensed the opportunity to share a telling anecdote. "At the Tuileries," he said, his voice low and measured, "the lines between virtue and vice were often blurred, and the sources of wealth and influence were rarely questioned."

As the conversation flowed, the dinner party moved through the meal, the sequence of courses unfolding with precision, each guest mindful of the social hierarchy that governed their world. Outside, the city pulsed with life, but within these walls, the atmosphere was one of polite restraint, the unspoken rules of etiquette governing every gesture and every word.
