It was November 17, 1494—more than eighteen months since Tito and Romola had stood together beneath the joyous light of Easter, united in marriage, as comfits rained down upon them in ancient Greek fashion, symbolizing a life blessed by heaven’s own sweetness. Since that springtime union, Florence had changed beyond recognition. Like a tree whose blossoms depend on the flow of sap from its roots, so too were the fates of Tito and Romola bound to the shifting currents of politics and power sweeping across Italy.

Just over a week earlier, the spirit of old Florence had stirred anew. The great bell in the palace tower had tolled with fierce urgency, summoning the people to arms. Men gathered with rusted weapons, farming tools, and makeshift clubs, driven by rage and pride, to expel the Medici from their city. The gates of San Gallo closed behind Piero de’ Medici, fleeing toward Bologna with his hired horsemen in disarray; his younger brother, the cardinal, slipped away disguised as a Franciscan monk. A price was set on both their heads. In the aftermath, homes were looted in accordance with tradition, the dishonorable portraits of past traitors were erased from public walls, and exiled foes of the Medici were welcomed back. The tyrants were gone, and the Republic had reclaimed its will.

Now, seven days later, the grand palace on Via Larga was being readied for a new guest—one whose arrival would stir both hope and unease. Draperies of rich color stretched across the streets, banners fluttered from windows, carpets and tapestries covered every step and stone where noble feet might tread. This was no ordinary welcome—it was a spectacle of celebration, though not without tension beneath the surface. From the palace, the river of color wound through the Cathedral, past the Piazza della Signoria, across the Ponte Vecchio, and ended at Porta San Frediano, the gate facing Pisa. There, a platform and canopy had been erected for the Signoria. Messer Luca Corsini, doctor of law, felt his heart quicken—he was to deliver a Latin oration. Every elder of note prepared himself: clean-shaven, silk robes neatly pressed. Young noblemen admired their newly tailored French-style tunics, meant to impress the visitor with Florentine elegance. And the clergy—archbishop in radiant vestments, monks in black, white, and grey—gathered early to plan their procession, bearing relics, sacred banners, and consecrated jewels, all timed precisely for the king’s expected arrival at three o’clock.

This was no ordinary guest. He had crossed the Alps with an army unlike any seen before: thousands of Swiss mercenaries, hardened warriors who fought for honor as much as coin; noble knights proud of their lineage; infantry lines where one in every hundred bore an arquebus; and cannons of bronze, firing iron balls instead of stones, drawn by horses, not oxen, capable of firing again before a wall could be repaired. Some compared him to Charlemagne, the legendary restorer of Florence, conqueror of degenerate kings, guardian of Christendom. Others saw him as Cyrus, liberator of Israel, redeemer of temples. For he came with grand designs: to march through Italy amid jubilant crowds, settle disputes in Rome, claim Naples by right and force, and then launch a crusade against the Turks—some to be slain, others converted to Christianity.

Such ambitions seemed fit for the Most Christian King, ruler of France, now the strongest monarchy in Christendom, thanks to the cunning of Louis XI, who had died ten years prior, haunted by fears of death and damnation. This heir to that shrewd king was Charles VIII—youthful, ambitious, and now advancing into Italy with divine purpose in his eyes.

To many, this invasion was not merely political—it was providential. It was believed that marble statues wept, phantasmal warriors clashed in the skies, and beasts gave birth to strange creatures—signs that this was no ordinary event, but a divine intervention, a turning point in history. And such belief did not stem only from fear of war, but from deeper moral tremors in the soul of the age—a sense that justice was about to descend.

At the heart of this conviction stood Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of the Dominican convent of San Marco in Florence. On a September morning, as news spread of the French army entering Italy, he preached in the Cathedral from the words: “Behold, I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth.” He claimed it was no accident that he had reached this passage during Lent—this “flood” was the French army, a divine judgment, both punishing and purifying. His audience—among them some of the most learned minds in Florence—listened in stunned silence, moved by awe and dread.

Savonarola possessed a rare power: to move men’s hearts, to make them believe what he believed. Four years earlier, he had warned from the same pulpit that a scourge was coming to cleanse Italy and the Church. Many had scoffed—but now they listened. He saw himself as a prophet, and many believed he was one. While visions and omens were common enough in those times, Savonarola stood apart. Where others saw fleeting glimpses, he saw a beacon—illuminating the path of God’s justice.

In 1492, when Lorenzo de’ Medici died and Tito Melema arrived in Florence as a wanderer, Italy appeared peaceful. Crops were bountiful, palaces rose in cities, villas dotted hillsides. The wealthy feared nothing, for armies were hired like goods, and violence was kept at bay. Even the threat of the Turks had faded—the Pope preferred bribes to battle. The world seemed content under a false peace, a realm of luxury built on hypocrisy, greed, and sin. Art flourished, yes, but also served vanity—painters depicted saints and sinners alike with equal skill, while scholars composed flattery in elegant Latin. The Church, though corrupt, prospered. Heaven smiled; earth showed no cracks.

Yet Savonarola had spoken for two years, warning of doom. He had seen a sword hanging in the heavens—the sword of divine justice. As a young man in Ferrara, he had been struck by the contradiction between faith and life, and at twenty-three, he entered the cloister. He believed God had entrusted the Church with the lamp of truth—but now it was buried beneath corruption. Scandals multiplied; lies became bold. Was there no justice? No God?

No, he insisted. Scripture revealed the pattern: when the chosen people sinned, God punished. Reason itself demanded retribution. Unless the Church repented, how could the world be saved? He had seen these truths in visions, but his strength lay in passion—his fury at injustice, his unwavering trust in a just and pure God. To him, divine intervention was not a distant hope, but an imminent reality.

Beneath the glittering façade of Italian courts, tensions simmered. Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan, patron of Leonardo da Vinci, desired the crown for himself and feared the Neapolitan royal family—King Ferdinand and Prince Alfonso—who despised treachery and opposed poisoning a relative for personal gain. The Neapolitans, in turn, feared Pope Alexander Borgia. All watched Florence, wary of its strategic position. Venice, cautious and powerful, sought to expand across the Adriatic and along the western coast. Lorenzo de’ Medici had balanced these forces, maintaining alliances while keeping peace. But young Piero, rash and vain, shattered that balance. Ludovico, sensing danger, devised a plan: invite the French king to invade Italy and claim Naples as heir to the House of Anjou.

Ambassadors—called “orators”—moved between courts. A recusant cardinal, refusing to recognize a pope elected through bribery, added fervent rhetoric to the plea. Charles VIII listened—and agreed.

By 1493, rumors grew louder: the French king would cross the Alps with a vast army. Italians, long accustomed to foreign powers arbitrating their fate, began to see him as a judge, a deliverer. And Savonarola heard it as confirmation: the prophecy was unfolding. What filled the prophets of old but the sound of marching armies sent by God? He no longer looked to the horizon—he pointed to the storm approaching.

The French army was the new flood. Charles VIII was the instrument of God, like Cyrus of old. Let Italy repent—especially Florence, beloved by God, for it had received the warning first. Turn from sin, as Nineveh once did, and the storm would pass, leaving only cleansing rain.

But now, after three months in Italy and nearing Florence’s gates, the mood was not one of pure joy. Charles had not yet redressed wrongs. He held Florence’s frontier fortresses—given up by Piero without ransom or honor. He had done nothing to quell Pisa’s revolt, which had grown bolder because of his presence. Diplomats, even with a prophet among them, could extract no promise—only that everything would be settled once he entered the city.

Still, there was satisfaction: Piero had been cast out, humiliated. The Republic had regained its fire.

And so, beneath the festive drapes and banners, Florence made other preparations. Hidden within walls were soldiers hired by the Republic, summoned hastily from nearby lands. Old weapons were polished, tools sharpened, cudgels ready. Boards and stakes lay in wait to form barricades. Stones were stockpiled, to be hurled from upper windows if needed. Above all, the people were eager to fight—having recently tasted resistance with fierce delight.

Their mood darkened further with each sight of Frenchmen arriving ahead of the king—some with hawks on their wrists, others with chalk in hand, marking doors for lodging. Historians suggest that many Frenchmen carried themselves with a swagger, a arrogance that only inflamed Florentine pride.

Thus, on the morning of November 17, 1494, Florence stood poised—between celebration and defiance, hope and fear—waiting for the king who might be both savior and conqueror.
