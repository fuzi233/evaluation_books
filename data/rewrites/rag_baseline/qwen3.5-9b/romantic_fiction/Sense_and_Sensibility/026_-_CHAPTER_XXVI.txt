Elinor could not reconcile the image of herself seated beside Mrs. Jennings in a carriage, embarking on a journey to London as her guest, with the reality of their acquaintance. Their association had been brief, their dispositions diametrically opposed, and only days prior had Elinor voiced strenuous objections to such a measure. Yet, driven by the same youthful ardor that animated Marianne and her mother, those objections had been swept aside. Even as doubts flickered in her mind regarding Willoughby's constancy, Elinor watched the radiant anticipation filling Marianne's soul and beaming from her eyes. In stark contrast, Elinor's own prospects appeared bleak and cheerless. She envied Marianne's animating hope, wishing fervently to possess the same object of desire, the same possibility of triumph.

Time, however, was a sharp-edged instrument; a mere handful of days would reveal Willoughby's true intentions. Given Marianne's eagerness to depart, it was evident she believed him already in town. Elinor resolved to gather every scrap of intelligence regarding his character, observing his behavior toward her sister with relentless scrutiny to determine his nature and motives before further meetings occurred. Should her findings prove unfavorable, she intended to awaken her sister to the truth; should they prove favorable, she would strive to suppress all selfish comparisons and banish every regret that might dim her joy in Marianne's happiness.

Three days passed on the road. Marianne's conduct during the travel served as a testament to her future compliance and companionship with Mrs. Jennings. She remained silent, lost in meditation, speaking only when a picturesque vista elicited an exclamatory delight addressed solely to her sister. To compensate for this reserve, Elinor assumed the role of civility, attending meticulously to Mrs. Jennings, conversing, laughing, and listening whenever opportunity arose. Mrs. Jennings, in turn, showered them with kindness, anxious for their comfort, save for her frustration that she could not dictate their meals or extract a preference for salmon over cod.

They arrived in London at three o'clock on the third day, relieved to escape the carriage's confinement and eager for the luxury of a roaring fire. The house was elegant, furnished with taste, and the sisters were installed in a spacious apartment. Above the mantelpiece hung a landscape painted in colored silks—a relic of Charlotte's seven-year tenure at a prestigious academy. Dinner was not due for two hours, prompting Elinor to sit and compose a letter to her mother. Moments later, Marianne joined her.

"I am writing home, Marianne," Elinor stated. "Had you not better defer your correspondence for a day or two?"

"I am *not* going to write to my mother," Marianne replied sharply, clearly intent on avoiding further inquiry.

Elinor said nothing. Instantly, the deduction crystallized: Marianne was writing to Willoughby. The conclusion, though tinged with uncertainty, brought a surge of pleasure to Elinor, who resumed her letter with renewed vigor. Marianne's note was dispatched in minutes; it was brief, folded, sealed, and directed with frantic speed. Elinor discerned a prominent 'W' in the address. Before the task was complete, Marianne rang the bell, instructing the footman to deliver the letter via the two-penny post. The evidence was irrefutable.

Marianne's spirits soared, yet a palpable flutter agitated her, failing to bring genuine joy to her sister. This agitation intensified as evening fell. She barely touched her dinner and, upon returning to the drawing-room, seemed to strain her ears against the sound of every approaching carriage. Elinor found solace in Mrs. Jennings's absorption in her own room, which shielded her from witnessing the scene. When tea was served, Marianne had already been thwarted by several knocks at neighboring doors until a resounding rap echoed unmistakably from their own threshold. Elinor felt certain it signaled Willoughby's arrival. Marianne sprang up and hastened toward the door.

Silence reigned. Unable to endure it, she opened the door and stepped onto the landing, listening intently for half a minute before retreating into the room, trembling with the conviction that she had heard him. Overcome by ecstatic emotion, she exclaimed, "Oh, Elinor, it is Willoughby, indeed it is!" poised to throw herself into his embrace, when Colonel Brandon appeared.

The shock was too profound to withstand calmly, and Marianne fled the room. Elinor felt a pang of disappointment, yet her respect for Colonel Brandon ensured his welcome. She was acutely wounded that a man so fond of her sister perceived only her grief and despair in his presence. She realized immediately that he had noticed Marianne's departure, marked by such astonishment and concern that he momentarily forgot the dictates of civility toward herself.

"Is your sister ill?" he asked.

Elinor answered with distress, attributing Marianne's behavior to headaches, low spirits, and exhaustion, listing plausible causes for her erratic conduct. He listened with earnest attention but, seemingly recalling himself, offered no further comment on the subject, shifting instead to express his pleasure at seeing them in London, asking routine questions about their journey and departed acquaintances. In this subdued manner, lacking mutual interest, they conversed, both listless and distracted.

Elinor desperately wished to inquire if Willoughby was in town but feared causing him pain by questioning his rival. Finally, seeking conversation, she asked if he had been in London since their last meeting.

"Yes," he replied, with visible embarrassment, "almost ever since. I visited Delaford once or twice for a few days, but it has never been in my power to return to Barton."

This response, delivered with such hesitation, resurrected the memory of his abrupt departure from that estate and the unease it had caused Mrs. Jennings. Elinor feared her question had betrayed a curiosity far exceeding her actual feelings.

Mrs. Jennings soon entered. "Oh! Colonel," she cried with her customary boisterous cheerfulness, "I am monstrous glad to see you—sorry I could not come before—beg your pardon, but I have been forced to look about me and settle my affairs. It has been ages since I was at home, and you know one always has a world of little oddities to attend to after an absence. Besides, I have had Cartwright to settle with. Lord, I have been as busy as a bee ever since dinner! But pray, Colonel, how came you to conjure out that I should be in town today?"

"I had the pleasure of hearing it at Mr. Palmer's, where I have been dining."

"Oh, you did; well, and how do they all do at their house? How does Charlotte do? I warrant you she is a fine size by this time."

"Mrs. Palmer appeared quite well, and I am commissioned to tell you that you will certainly see her tomorrow."

"Ay, to be sure, I thought as much. Well, Colonel, I have brought two young ladies with me—you see, that is, you see but one of them now, but there is another somewhere. Your friend, Miss Marianne, too—which you will not be sorry to hear. I do not know what you and Mr. Willoughby will do between you about her. Ay, it is a fine thing to be young and handsome. Well! I was young once, but I never was very handsome—worse luck for me. However, I got a very good husband, and I don't know what the greatest beauty can do more. Ah! poor man! he has been dead these eight years and better. But Colonel, where have you been to since we parted? And how does your business go on? Come, come, let's have no secrets among friends."

He responded to her inquiries with his accustomed mildness, offering no satisfying answers. Elinor then prepared the tea, forcing Marianne to reappear. Upon her entrance, Colonel Brandon grew more thoughtful and silent than before, and Mrs. Jennings could not persuade him to remain long. No other visitors arrived that evening, and the ladies unanimously agreed to retire early.

Marianne rose the next morning with restored spirits and a joyful demeanor. The previous evening's disappointment seemed erased by the anticipation of the day's events. Hardly had they finished breakfast when Mrs. Palmer's barouche halted at the door. Soon she burst into the room, laughing, so delighted to see them that it was difficult to discern whether she derived more pleasure from meeting her mother or the Miss Dashwoods. She was surprised by their visit to town, though she had suspected it; she was angered that they accepted her mother's invitation after declining her own, yet she claimed she could never forgive them had they not come.

"Mr. Palmer will be so happy to see you," she said. "What do you think he said when he heard of your coming with Mama? I forget what it was now, but it was something so droll!"

After an hour of what her mother termed "comfortable chat"—essentially a barrage of inquiries into Mrs. Jennings's circle punctuated by baseless laughter on Mrs. Palmer's part—the latter proposed visiting some shops where she had business. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor readily agreed, having their own purchases to make. Marianne, initially reluctant, was persuaded to join them.

Throughout their outing, she remained constantly vigilant. In Bond Street, her eyes scanned the crowd in perpetual search. In every shop, her mind drifted from the items before them, fixated on something else entirely.
