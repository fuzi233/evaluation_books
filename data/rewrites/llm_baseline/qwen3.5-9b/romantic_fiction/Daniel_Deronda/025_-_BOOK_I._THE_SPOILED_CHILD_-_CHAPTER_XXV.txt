To map the contours of a mind stripped bare by a fastidious egoism—one where every direct desire has dulled, shrinking from motive into a wavering hope of motivation—is a difficult task. It is a psyche composed entirely of shifting moods, where erratic impulses sprout like weeds among the general decay. Such a state often befalls a life lived too freely, untouched by the shaping pressure of duty. *Nam deteriores omnes sumus licentiæ*; or, in plainer terms, "Do as you please" is a terrible signpost. Rulers announce their wills and shift markets with minimal speech. Similarly, when Grandcourt learned Gwendolen had fled Leubronn, he casually dismissed that fashionable retreat as a "beastly hole," worse than Baden. To Mr. Lush, this remark was a definitive signal: his patron intended immediately to return to Diplow.

Of course, action rarely matches intent instantly. Grandcourt indeed loitered the following day, offering no clear orders. Perhaps he sensed Lush's anticipation. He delayed over his grooming, descending finally with a faded aura of perfect distinction that made fresh complexions and flushed hands appear crude and vulgar. He drifted through the terraces, gambling rooms, and reading areas, cultivating an indifference to everyone and everything. Yet, when encountering Lady Mallinger, he exerted effort—raising his hat, pausing, and feigning interest in her suggestion regarding the local waters. "Yes," he replied, "I heard someone say how providential it was that springs always exist near gambling houses."

"Oh, that was a joke," said the innocent Lady Mallinger, misled by his languid gravity, "a parody of the old tale about towns and rivers."

"Ah, perhaps," Grandcourt murmured, his face unchanged.

Lady Mallinger deemed this worthy of reporting to Sir Hugo, who chuckled. "Oh, my dear, he isn't a fool. Do not imagine he cannot see a jest. He plays his cards as well as most of us."

"He has never struck me as particularly sensible," Lady Mallinger offered, defending herself. She harbored a secret aversion to Grandcourt; to her, he was merely a living monument to her own failure as a wife—the fact that she had failed to produce a son for Sir Hugo. She constantly wondered if her husband regretted his choice, fearing that if he weren't exceptionally kind, he might treat her roughly, as men naturally dislike being disappointed.

Deronda, too, received a nod from Grandcourt, one he did not appreciate yet returned with flawless politeness. No amount of philosophical debate could erase the deep-seated belief that his birth had been marred by his father's error. Since Grandcourt's fortune might otherwise have belonged to him, Deronda was fiercely resolved not to display any irritation born of that grievance. He recognized the easy slide into petty, irrational resentment that others felt when thwarted; determined not to descend into that ugly abyss, he clung to kinder affections within himself. Pride aided him—a pride in refusing to acknowledge disadvantages that vulgar minds exaggerate, such as the shabby trappings of poverty. He would not allow a man like Grandcourt to suspect he was envied.

However, one cannot guard against interpretation. Grandcourt believed Deronda, the unfortunate cousin by the father's side, inwardly winced at their shared predicament. Therefore, Deronda's presence became more tolerable to him. The imagined envy of others—that they feel their own lack—is the standard retinue of egoism; and Grandcourt's pet dogs were not the only creatures he delighted in making jealous. Consequently, he was civil enough to converse with Deronda on the terrace about the hunt at Diplow, even suggesting, "You'd better come over for a run or two when the season starts."

Lush, pleased by the delay, amused himself gossiping with Sir Hugo and answering inquiries about Grandcourt's affairs, specifically regarding his willingness to sell his interest in Diplow. On a bright autumn day, there was nothing more pleasant than discussing a tyrannical patron behind his back. Sir Hugo eagerly listened to these "traits of manners," though he kept such whispers strictly from ears that might take them too seriously. Whatever secrets he knew of his nephew, he never shared them with Deronda, who viewed Grandcourt as a pale-blooded mortal and preferred not to learn how the red corpuscles had been washed from him.

Lush's policy was to please everyone when he had no reason not to. The baronet always treated him well, regarding him as one of those versatile figures who, while not quite gentlemen themselves, serve the company of gentlemen with comfortable utility—like second-best garments worn without anxiety.

"Well, keep me posted," Sir Hugo said. "If this marriage looks likely, or if money becomes tight, let me know. My plan would suit him better than burdening Ryelands."

"That's true," Lush agreed. "But it mustn't be forced upon him. Just place it in his path so the scent tickles him. Grandcourt isn't a man to be led solely by self-interest, especially if he senses you're doing it for your own gain. I'm devoted to him, of course. I've sacrificed everything for fifteen years to stay by his side. He wouldn't easily replace me. He's a peculiar character, Henleigh Grandcourt, and he's grown on me lately. Still, I'm steady. I've been his guardian since he was twenty. He was fascinating then, and could be now if he chose. I'm attached to him; it would be far worse for him if I weren't at his elbow."

Sir Hugo offered no explicit sympathy, nor did Lush expect his confession of motives to be analyzed precisely. How can a man avoid becoming the subject of his own conversation? One must dress oneself decently in words as in cloth. His listener, a Member of Parliament, allowed for the necessities of verbal attire, and the dialogue proceeded without friction.

Yet, Lush's hope for indefinite postponement ended abruptly the next morning. Grandcourt greeted him with, "Are you making all the arrangements for our departure via the Paris train?"

"I didn't realize you planned to leave," Lush said, unsurprised.

"You should have known," Grandcourt snapped, staring at the burnt stub of his cigar, speaking in that low, disgusted tone reserved for peremptory commands. "See to everything. Ensure no brute shares our carriage. Leave my P.P.C. at the Mallingers'.

They arrived in Paris the following day. Lush was gratified when instructed to proceed directly to Diplow to oversee preparations, leaving Grandcourt and the valet behind. Days passed before Lush received the telegram summoning the carriage to Wanchester station. During this interval, he worked diligently, executing orders regarding the stud and household while gathering intelligence on Gwendolen and the situation at Offendene. He calculated the probable impact of the family's misfortune on Grandcourt's stubborn nature. If Gwendolen's poverty removed the fear of rejection, it might prompt Grandcourt to act despite his reservations; conversely, the certainty of acceptance might render him as fickle as a moth.

Having observed his patron closely for years, Lush knew Grandcourt better than anyone, yet knowing him meant doubting his actions in any specific instance. Could he suddenly display magnanimity, like a tragic hero awakening to virtue only to relapse? Would he decline the heiress to marry the penniless girl? Lush held general certainties—for instance, that Grandcourt possessed no capacity for genuine generosity—but these were useless when trying to predict the internal stimuli driving him along a network of possibilities. Lush was uncertain about the outcome, yet desperately desired the confidence that they would *never* marry. He would have welcomed a union with an heiress or even Mrs. Glasher; the latter would ensure gratitude, a debt Lush felt entitled to collect. He did not mind social exclusion for the bride, nor did he object to no marriage at all. But he felt justified in obstructing a match that promised only trouble for her husband and potential ruin for his old companion, whom he wished to protect. He sought what he perceived as good, unaware he was wishing ill upon the inconvenient Gwendolen.

Accustomed to being seen as agreeable, Lush was not prepared to be insulted by more than one person. Armed with this fragile strategy, he awaited Grandcourt's arrival, wondering how the campaign would commence.

Grandcourt spent the first day with the stables, ordering a groom to saddle Criterion and demonstrate its gait. This signaled his purpose. Lush reconsidered whether to volunteer information about Gwendolen's dire circumstances while still confident no compromise had occurred. The next morning, he resolved to drop the news if Grandcourt seemed in a conversational mood. But Grandcourt offered no talk. Even asked about his own comfort, he maintained an unwinking silence. After reading his letters, he issued various orders, then gestured for Lush to leave. Before exiting, Grandcourt turned slightly and emitted a broken, languid "Oh."

"What is it?" Lush asked, noting he did not carry his dusty pudding with excessive reverence.

"Shut the door. I can't speak in the corridor."

Lush complied, sat down, and waited. Finally, Grandcourt asked, "Is Miss Harleth at Offendene?" He assumed Lush had investigated and took pleasure in believing Lush did not want *him* to ask.
