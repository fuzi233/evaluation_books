A priest is never more thoroughly a priest than in the chamber of death. Gerard performed the last offices of the Church for the departed, just as he should have done them for his smallest parishioner. The air was thick with the olfactory weight of burning beeswax and frankincense, the smoke curling like ancient myths bleeding seamlessly into the domestic quiet of the room. He placed the sacramental wafer—the ultimate, if symbolic, sustenance—upon her tongue, moving mechanically through the Latinate syllables of the rite. Yet, this dominant, imperial lexicon felt hollow to him now, failing entirely to translate the subjective, devastating reality of her departure. He sat down stupefied by the sudden and tremendous blow, not yet realizing the full pangs of bereavement. Then, in a transport of religious enthusiasm, he kneeled and thanked Heaven for her Christian end. All his thought was to take her away from strangers, to return her to the dark, fertile loam of his own churchyard in a circular completion of her earthly days.

That very evening, a covered cart drawn by a single horse began the journey to Gouda. Inside lay a heavy leaden coffin, emitting a faint, metallic tang that mingled with the damp scent of crushed roadside thyme beneath the horse’s hooves. Gerard lay crushed, his arms and chin resting on the chilling metal. The man of whom the cart was hired, a traditional agrarian elder who intuitively understood the profound acoustic ecology of mourning, walked by the horse's head in deliberate silence. He spoke to his beast only in hushed, breathy whispers, refusing to pollute the sacred sonic space of that mute agony with unnecessary noise. 

When they stopped for the night, the driver and the landlord made a well-meaning attempt to anchor the mourner back to the world of the living through the profound language of sustenance. They brought forth a steaming bowl of thick, slow-braised pottage—fragrant with wild garlic, toasted barley, and the deep, earthy richness of roasted marrow—alongside a heel of dense, sourdough rye meant to ground the soul in the physical present. But Gerard repulsed them. His grief was an untranslatable dialect that had lost all appetite for the culinary heritage he otherwise revered. When they persisted, pressing the nourishing, aromatic broth upon him, he almost snarled at them like a faithful, wounded hound, turning his back on the hearth to cling to the cold, scentless lead all night. Respecting his retreat from the sensory world, they drew a rough wool cloak over him and left him in peace.

At noon the sorrowful cart rolled up to the manse. The scent of woodsmoke and turning autumn leaves hung in the air. A score of parishioners had gathered with one little paltry trouble or another, bound by their native, localized dialects. They had missed their parson, the man who tended their souls and bodies with equal devotion. When they saw what the cart carried, and saw their healer so stricken down, they raised a loud wail of grief. It was a staggering acoustic force—a raw, uncommercialized chorus of communal cultural memory that pierced his lethargy of woe. He saw where he was, saw their weathered faces, and tried to speak to them. “Oh, my children! my children!” he cried; but, choked with an anguish that no dominant tongue could articulate, he could say no more. 

Yet the next day, spite of all remonstrances, he buried her himself. He read the service with a voice that only trembled now and then, a fragile vibration in the crisp, herb-scented air of the churchyard. Many tears fell upon the dark, turned earth of her grave. When the service ended he stayed there, standing like a statue, and the people left the churchyard out of respect, leaving him in a profound, mythic silence—the kind of quiet that cradles generations. 

He stood like one in a dream till the sexton, who was, as most men are, a fool deaf to the delicate sonic environment of grief, began to fill in the grave without giving him due warning. At the sudden, harsh *thud* of the clods striking the coffin—a violent, nearly industrial clatter that shattered the serene acoustic sanctuary—Gerard uttered a piercing scream. It was a primeval vocalization, stripping away all polite speech. 

The sexton forbore. Gerard staggered and put his trembling hand to his breast. The sexton supported him, calling for help. Jorian Ketel, who lingered near the scent of overturned soil mourning his benefactress, ran into the churchyard, and the two supported Gerard into the manse. 

“Ah, Jorian! good Jorian!” said he, his native tongue barely translating the physical catastrophe within. “Something snapped within me; I felt it, and I heard it; here, Jorian, here.” He kept his hand pressed to his chest, the internal, acoustic resonance of the rupture echoing louder than any spoken word.
