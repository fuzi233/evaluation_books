It’s often observed that women hold a near-innate, instinctive gift for reading men’s characters — no slow process of reasoning, no piles of gathered evidence required, just a snap, unshakable verdict that often flies in the face of years of collected male observation. What’s less often noted is that this gift, fallible as every human trait is, almost never revises itself. When that initial bad hunch is later proven wrong by every logical measure, it hardens into plain old prejudice, refusing to budge an inch. Even the remote possibility that their judgment could be disproven turns nine out of ten of these female diviners into biased witnesses: they’ve tied their own pride so tightly to their first call, they can’t back down without feeling like they’ve lost a part of themselves.

“Ma dear,” said the Minor Canon Septimus Crisparkle one afternoon, leaning back in his cluttered little bookroom where his mother sat knitting by the fire, “don’t you think you’re being a little hard on Mr. Neville Landless?”

“Certainly not, Sept,” the old lady replied firmly, her knitting needles never pausing.

“Let’s talk it through, then.”

“I’ve no objection at all to talking it through, my dear. I trust I’m always open to discussion.” Her cap wiggled just a little, like her unspoken addendum was loud enough to shake the frills: *and I’d like to see any discussion that changes my mind*.

“Good, that’s the spirit,” her conciliatory son said.

“Of course it is,” she returned, clearly not open to it at all.

“Right. Neville got out of hand that awful night, yes, but he was provoked.”

“And he was drunk on mulled wine,” his mother added.

“I won’t argue about the wine. Though I’d say both young men were about equally far gone that night.”

“I don’t agree,” she said.

“Why not, Ma?”

“Because I don’t.” She nodded sharply. “Still, I’m fully open to discussion, as I said.”

“Ma, we can’t very well debate if you take that line right out of the gate.”

“Blame Neville for that, not me,” she said, stately and severe. “He came home stumbling drunk, disgraced this entire house, and insulted every last one of us under this roof.”

“That’s true, no denying it. He’s sorry for it, still, to this day.”

“If Jasper hadn’t been so well-mannered as to find me after service the next day, still in his surplice, right there in the cathedral nave, asking if the noise the night before had startled me and spoiled my rest, I’d probably never have heard a word of that shameful incident,” she said.

“To be honest, Ma, I was hoping to keep it from you if I could. I was halfway out the door after Jasper to talk it over, agree we’d both hush the whole mess up for everyone’s sake, when I found him already speaking to you. Too late by then.”

“Far too late. He was still as pale as well-burnt ash over what had happened in his own rooms the night before.”

“If I’d hidden it from you, it would only have been to keep you peaceful, to do right by the two boys, and do my job as best I knew how.”

His mother immediately stood, walked over, and kissed his cheek. “Of course I know that, my dear Sept.”

“But word got out all over town anyway,” Crisparkle said, rubbing the back of his ear as she sat back down to her knitting. “It was out of my hands entirely.”

“And I said back then that I thought Neville was bad news, Sept, and I say it now. I hope he turns out good, but I don’t believe he ever will.” Her cap vibrated so hard with that line, the frills wobbled.

“I’m sorry to hear you say that, Ma—”

“I’m sorry to say it, too, dear,” she cut in, knitting faster, “but I can’t help what I feel.”

“—and it’s a shame, because Neville’s been nothing but hardworking and attentive with his studies, improving every day, and I’d even say he’s grown very fond of me.”

“That last part’s no credit to him,” she shot back. “If he goes around bragging about being fond of you, I think even worse of him.”

“Ma, he’s never said a word of the sort.”

“Maybe not,” she said, unimpressed, “but it doesn’t matter much either way.”

Crisparkle looked at her fondly, amused, like she was a dainty old piece of china that was no use arguing with too closely.

“Besides, Sept — what do you think Neville would be without his sister Helena? You know how much sway she has over him, how sharp she is. Every page he reads with you, he goes over again with her after. Give her all the credit she’s due, and there’s barely anything left to praise Neville for.”

Crisparkle fell quiet, drifting into thought. He pictured the pair of them huddled over his old college textbooks on frosty dawns, when he walked out to Cloisterham Weir to clear his head; then on dark, wind-bitten evenings, when he climbed his favourite perch, that jagged crumbling fragment of old monastery ruin at the cliff edge, and watched Helena and Neville walk along the riverbank below, the town’s gaslights glinting on the dark water to make the dusk feel even bleaker. He realized slowly, without even noticing, that when he taught Neville, he was effectively teaching both of them — adjusting his explanations to land for the sharp, fierce sister too, whom he only ever saw through her brother. He thought of the gossip from the Nuns’ House, that Helena — proud, hot-tempered Helena — had submitted to quiet little Rosa Bud to learn everything she could from her, the two unlikely girls thick as thieves. And he wondered, half charmed, how all of this had only started a few weeks back, and already felt like it had been part of his whole life.

Whenever Reverend Septimus drifted off into a daydream, his mother took it as a sure sign he needed a little pick-me-up. She bustled off to the dining room closet, the famous, much-loved closet of Minor Canon Corner, to fetch him a glass of sweet Constantia wine and a homemade biscuit. This was no ordinary hinged door that swung open all at once: it had two sliding panels that met in the middle, one sliding down, one pushing up, so the contents revealed themselves little by little. Pull the top slide down first, and you found shelves lined with pickle jars, jam pots, tins of spices, and pretty blue and white porcelain pots holding preserved ginger and tamarind, every one labelled neatly on its side. The pickles were labelled in bold printed capitals, like portly gentlemen: Walnut, Gherkin, Onion, Mixed. The jams were scrawled in soft, feminine handwriting, like quiet whispers: Raspberry, Gooseberry, Apricot, Damson. Slide the bottom panel up, and you uncovered piles of bright oranges, a huge japanned sugar box, home-made biscuits, a slab of plum cake, and thin, delicate ladyfingers perfect for dipping in sweet wine. Right at the very bottom, a lead-lined nook held the sweet wines and cordials, smelling of Seville orange, lemon, almond, and caraway seed. The whole closet felt like it had been infused for decades with the sound of the cathedral bells and the organ, until every last jar was brimming with slow, warm sweetness — everyone who rummaged in it came out looking soft and happy, like they’d been blessed with a little sugar coating.

Crisparkle was just as obedient to his mother’s other favourite closet, the herbal remedy cupboard on the upper landing. How many horrid bitter infusions of gentian, peppermint, sage, dandelion, and rue had he drunk without a murmur? How many times had he wrapped his face in layers of dried leaves when she thought he had a toothache, or stuck a poultice of crushed herbs on his cheek for a tiny pimple no one else would ever have noticed? He’d let her lead him to that low, whitewashed little nook, strung with bunches of drying herbs hanging from rusty ceiling hooks, like a lamb to the slaughter — the only difference being, he never complained, just splashed his hands and face in the bowl of dried rose petals and the bowl of dried lavender to get rid of the bitter aftertaste, and trundled off to the weir, confident a long walk and a clear mind would fix whatever ailed him.

That evening, Crisparkle drank his glass of Constantia gratefully, to his mother’s full satisfaction, and got on with the rest of his duties. The hours ticked by neatly until it was time for evening vespers, and the light had faded to twilight. The cathedral was bitterly cold that night, so after the service he set off at a brisk trot, heading straight for his favourite crumbling monastery ruin, planning to charge up the slope without stopping to catch his breath.

He made it to the top perfectly, winded a little, and leaned over the edge to look down at the river. Cloisterham’s river sits close enough to the sea that the tide often washes up great clumps of seaweed — that night, there was far more than usual, the water choppy and wild, gulls dipping and squawking loudly overhead, and a stormy, angry glow far out to sea beyond the brown-sailed barges that were already turning black as the dark crept in. He was thinking of how wild the sea looked compared to the quiet, safe little corner of Minor Canon Corner, when he spotted Helena and Neville Landless walking along the river path below. He’d been thinking about the two of them all day, so he carefully climbed down the rough, uneven slope to catch up with them, nimble as ever on the tricky footing.

“Wild night, Miss Landless!” he called, joining them. “Aren’t your usual walks a little too exposed and cold now that the sun’s gone down, and the wind’s blowing in hard off the sea?”

Helena smiled, and shook her head. It was their favourite walk, completely private, she said.

“It is private, that’s for sure,” Crisparkle agreed, seizing his chance to talk. “The perfect place to say what I need to, no interruptions. Neville, I assume you tell your sister everything that passes between us when we talk?”

“Everything, sir,” Neville said.

“Good. So she’s aware I’ve urged you over and over to make some sort of apology for that awful fight your first night here.” He looked at Helena, not Neville, so it was her who answered.

“Yes.”

“I call it unfortunate, Miss Helena,” Crisparkle went on, “because it’s bred a terrible prejudice against Neville. The whole town thinks he’s a dangerously hot-tempered, violent young man no one can trust. People cross the street to avoid him, for no real reason.”

“I don’t doubt it,” Helena said, her face softening with proud compassion for her brother. “You’ve confirmed what every quiet little hint I get every day has already told me — everyone’s treating him unfairly.”

“So this is something we can fix, right?” Crisparkle said, gentle but firm. “Neville’s only just moved to Cloisterham. He could outwait this gossip eventually, but why wait? Acting now is smarter, and it’s the right thing to do. We all know Neville was in the wrong that night.”

“He was provoked,” Helena pushed back.

“He was the one who threw the first blow,” Crisparkle countered.

They walked in silence for a few paces, before Helena lifted her eyes to his face, almost reproachful. “Oh Mr. Crisparkle — you wouldn’t ask Neville to throw himself at young Edwin Drood’s feet, or Jasper’s, when Jasper poisons everyone against him every chance he gets? You couldn’t ask that, if Neville’s hurt was your hurt.”

“Helena, I’ve told Mr. Crisparkle before,” Neville cut in, deferential to his tutor, “that if I could make that apology and mean it, I would. But I can’t. I refuse to pretend to be sorry when I’m not. But you can’t put his position as if he did what I did — his whole life is nothing like mine.”

“I’m sorry,” Helena said, flushing.

“See?” Crisparkle said gently. “Both of you already admit Neville was in the wrong. Why not take that last step, and make that apology official?”

“Isn’t there a difference,” Helena asked, her voice faltering a little, “between submitting to a generous, kind person, and submitting to someone petty or cruel?”

Before Crisparkle could reply, Neville burst out: “Help me make him understand, Helena. I can’t be the first one to offer peace — it would be a lie, a mockery. I was deeply, deliberately insulted that night, and I’m still angry about it. I’m just as furious now when I think back as I was that night.”

“Neville,” Crisparkle said, steady, “you just made that same clenching fist gesture I told you I hate seeing, the one you made that night.”

“I’m sorry, sir, it was involuntary. But I meant what I said.”

“I’d hoped you’d have cooled off by now,” Crisparkle said, a little sad.

“I don’t want to lie to you, that would be worse. Your influence has done so much for me, but it hasn’t softened that anger yet. It might, one day — you know what a terrible childhood I had, how hard it is to break those hot, angry habits. But it hasn’t today. Isn’t that true, Helena?”

Helena, her dark eyes fixed on Crisparkle’s face, nodded. “It’s true.”

Neville took a deep breath. “I never had the courage to tell you this before, sir — I was too embarrassed, afraid you’d laugh. But I admire Miss Rosa Bud so completely, I can’t stand to see her treated like a doll, like Edwin Drood treats her. Even if Edwin hadn’t hurt me that night, I’d hate him for how he wastes that sweet girl, that beautiful, wonderful girl.”

Crisparkle stared at him, stunned, looked to Helena for confirmation — and her face told him every word was true, and she was begging him for advice.

“The young lady you speak of is soon to be married, Neville,” he said, grave. “That sort of feeling is wildly misplaced. It’s monstrous for you to appoint yourself her champion against her chosen fiancé. You’ve barely even met them both once. She’s your sister’s new friend, Helena — I’m shocked you haven’t talked him out of this ridiculous, wrong infatuation.”

“I tried,” Helena said, quiet. “It was no use.”

“Doll or no doll, fiancé or no fiancé,” Neville shouted, face blazing, fists clenched again, “Edwin Drood doesn’t deserve her! He doesn’t have an ounce of the love she deserves! She’s being sacrificed marrying him — I love her, and I hate him!”

Helena darted over to his side, grabbed his arm, whispering his name to calm him down. Neville came back to himself immediately, horrified, and covered his face with his hand, miserable and ashamed.

Crisparkle walked on in silence for a few paces, thinking through what to say next. “Neville, Neville — I’m grieved to see these angry, wild bursts still come out of you, matching the storm closing in around us. This feud with Edwin can’t go on any longer, not while you live under my roof. I know Edwin — he’s frank, good-natured, no hidden malice. He can be reasoned with. So here’s my offer: I will make sure Edwin meets you halfway. He will make the first move, the first peace offering. Once he does that, you will give me your solemn word as a Christian gentleman that the quarrel is over for good, on your side. God alone knows what’s in your heart when you shake his hand — but there will be no good in your life if you hide treachery behind that handshake.

“As for this infatuation of yours: this secret stays between the three of us, no one else. Not Rosa, not Edwin, not Jasper, not a single other soul in Cloisterham. You will do everything in your power to erase that feeling from your mind, and you will never act on it, never hint a word of it to anyone. That is the second pledge I ask of you.”

Neville tried twice to speak, and couldn’t.

“I’ll leave you with your sister now, it’s time you two headed home,” Crisparkle said. “Come find me in my study later.”

“Please don’t go yet,” Helena begged him. “One more minute.”

“I wouldn’t even have needed a minute,” Neville said, his voice thick, “if you weren’t so patient, so kind, so unassuming and true. If I’d had a guide like you when I was a little boy —”

“Follow him now, Neville,” Helena whispered. “Follow him straight to heaven.”

Crisparkle’s throat tightened, he couldn’t speak to protest her praise. He just laid a finger on his lips, and looked at Neville.

“I give you both pledges, from the bottom of my heart,” Neville said, voice shaking. “There is no treachery in me. Forgive me for losing my temper just now.”

“I’m not the one you need forgiveness from,” Crisparkle said. “Helena, you two are twins. You grew up with the same hard, terrible circumstances, and you tamed that same hot temper in yourself. You know exactly the danger Neville is walking towards. Who else can steer him clear but you?”

“Who else but you, sir?” Helena said, reverent, and raised his hand to her lips to kiss it.

“Tut,” Crisparkle said softly. “I’m far overpaid for that little favour.” He turned and headed back into the dark Cathedral Close.

As he walked, he turned over in his head the best way to make good on his promise. “I’ll probably be the one to marry Edwin and Rosa,” he thought, “and I wish to God they’d hurry up and get married and move away! But this Neville business comes first.” He debated writing Edwin a letter, or going straight to Jasper. He knew he was well-liked by everyone at the cathedral, and when he saw Jasper’s gatehouse windows glowing with warm light, he made up his mind. “I’ll strike while the iron’s hot,” he said to himself. “I’ll go see him right now.”
