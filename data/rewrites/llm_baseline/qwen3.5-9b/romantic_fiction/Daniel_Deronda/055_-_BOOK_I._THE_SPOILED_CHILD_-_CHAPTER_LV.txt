*"Ritorna a tua scienza / Che vuol, quanto la cosa è più perfetta / Più senta il bene, e così la doglienza."* — Dante.

When Deronda encountered Gwendolen and Grandcourt upon the staircase, his mind was already deeply preoccupied. He had just concluded a second, harrowing interview with his mother. Only two hours prior to meeting them, he had learned that the Princess Halm-Eberstein had departed the hotel. Logically, he should have immediately begun his journey to Mainz to deliver the letter from Joseph Kalonymos and secure the family chest. Yet, a complex web of unresolved emotions anchored him to Genoa. A heavy weight of retrospective feeling held him passive long after his farewells.

In his solitude, Deronda relived the recent scenes with a renewed, piercing intensity, as if they existed merely as a prelude to their full impact within his soul. He allowed himself to weep, with a compassion perhaps sharper than most women's, over a life so intimately connected to his own yet so tragically distant. He perceived the world shifting beneath him, altered by the certainty of bonds that disrupted his balance of hopes and fears, granting him a profound sense of fellowship. It felt as though, hidden under the night's cover, he had mistakenly joined a band of wanderers, only to discover at dawn that his true kin's tents lay miles away. He possessed a trembling, imaginative awareness of a close bond with a grandfather whose strong impulses and loving thoughts might now be stirring within his own dormant heart. Throughout this passionate meditation, Mordecai and Mirah remained ever-present, silent companions clasping hands with him in shared understanding.

Deronda was forged of such quick, responsive fiber, draped in a mantle of self-controlled reserve that had absorbed much of his youthful strength through early hardship. When the persistent ringing of a bell signaled the hour, he considered consulting the railway guide (*Bradshaw*) to make the necessary arrangements for the next train to Mainz. He thought of it, but took no action. His desires drifted between Mainz and its treasures, and London and the people there who formed the deepest attachments of his life. Yet, other wishes tethered him to Genoa—the force that compels one to linger during an interview carrying the shadow of a final farewell or overwhelming sorrow.

He did not consciously decide, "I shall stay tonight because it is Friday, and I wish to attend the synagogue service where they surely gathered; moreover, I might encounter the Grandcourts again." Instead, he simply ceased packing and rang for his bill, sitting motionless while his mind wandered to the synagogue. There, he envisioned faces scarcely different from those of his grandfather's era, hearing the Spanish-Hebrew liturgy endure through centuries of wandering generations like a plant with drifting seeds, binding distant lands to the exile's home. Simultaneously, his thoughts turned to Gwendolen, filled with anxious recollection of the past and a half-formed conviction that it would be cruel to depart immediately without attempting, despite Grandcourt's likely displeasure, to demonstrate his continued sympathy following their abrupt separation.

In this state of suspended animation, he delayed leaving, eating dinner without tasting its flavor, then rising abruptly to seek the synagogue. Passing the porter, he inquired if Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt remained in the hotel and requested their room number. The porter supplied the number but added that they had gone out boating. This piece of information exerted a powerful influence on Deronda, dividing his focus between the memories awakened by the sparse *talithim* and the solemn, dark faces of worshippers who approached their prayers with an ease that seemed like Hebrew culture dyed Italian. These reflections led him to consider that his grandfather, based on the Princess's descriptions, must have been nearly as exceptional a Jew as Mordecai himself. Were not men of ardent zeal and boundless hope inherently exceptional? They were the visionaries Mordecai spoke of—the creators and nourishers of the world, shaping the passive lives that, without them, would wither into the narrow instinct of insects, blind to anything beyond their antennae.

A mournful impatience mingled with his concern for Gwendolen, fueled by his current freedom from immediate anxieties, urging him to leave the synagogue and take his customary evening walk along the quay. The spot was usually his favorite, and tonight it held extra allure due to the possibility of intercepting the Grandcourts returning from their boating trip. Resolving to approach them deliberately and disregard any reason Grandcourt might have for excluding him, Deronda stepped out.

The sun had dipped behind a bank of clouds, casting only a faint yellow farewell upon the waves, which churned under an active breeze. Sauntering slowly near the strand, Deronda observed groups gathering their gaze on a sailing-boat moving swiftly toward shore, propelled by two men. Amidst the clamor of voices in various tongues, Deronda decided against asking questions directly. Instead, he pushed forward to stand as an unobstructed witness. Telescopes were raised, and loud speculations erupted: one claimed the boat carried someone who had drowned; another insisted it was the *milord*; a Frenchman, lacking a glass, suggested the *milord* had likely taken his wife out to drown her—a notion an English skipper immediately dismissed as nonsense, comparing it to ore that had failed a mining process, before decisively stating the reclining figure was a woman.

For Deronda, terrified by these fluctuating fears, the rhythmic stroke of the oars was punctuated by rapid visions of possible and impossible outcomes, hinting at a tragedy with a darker half concealed—if the woman seemingly rescued from the water was indeed Mrs. Grandcourt. Soon, however, doubt vanished. The boat was drawn to land, and Deronda saw Gwendolen half-rising on her hands, aided only by her own effort, encased in a heavy layer of tarpaulins and pea-jackets. She appeared pale as one of the sheeted dead, shivering violently, her wet hair streaming down her face. Her eyes held a wild, amazed consciousness, as if she had awakened in a world awaiting judgment, surrounded by figures ready to seize her.

The first rower leaped ashore, drenched and running off. The sailors crowding the boat blocked Deronda's path, allowing him only to watch as Gwendolen cast frightened glances, shrinking in terror as she was gently but firmly extracted and guided away by the strong, bronzed arms of the rough men. Her wet garments clung to her limbs, hindering her weak movements. Suddenly, her wandering eyes landed on Deronda, standing before her. Instantly, as if she had been waiting for him, she attempted to stretch out her arms, restrained by her supporters, whispering in a muffled voice, "It is come, it is come! He is dead!"

"Hush, hush!" Deronda commanded, his tone authoritative. Turning to the assistants, he declared, "I am a connection of this lady's husband. If you will get her onto the *Italia* as quickly as possible, I will handle everything else."

He remained briefly to learn from the remaining boatman that her husband had sunk irretrievably and that his boat floated empty nearby. The crew recounted how they had heard a cry, arrived in time to see the lady leap in after her husband, and managed to rescue her before severe injury occurred. Deronda then hurried to the hotel to ensure the finest medical aid was secured. Once satisfied, he telegraphed the news to Sir Hugo, requesting his immediate arrival, and contacted Mr. Gascoigne at the rectory, knowing this was the quickest way to inform Gwendolen's mother. As he acted, certain words Gwendolen had spoken in the past returned to him with the force of inspiration: in moments of agitated confession, she had mentioned her mother's presence as a potential help, had she only been able to obtain it.
