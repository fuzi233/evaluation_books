Gerard walked in silence beside Teresa, his mind drifting into the contemplative haze reserved for artists, speculating on the trajectory of her intentions regarding him rather than voicing the inquiry. Eventually, she broke the quiet, revealing her plan of her own volition. A confidante had apprised her of a goldsmith's wife seeking a scribe; "Her establishment lies nearby; your journey shall be brief."

Thus, they hastened to the goldsmith's dwelling.

"Madam," Teresa addressed the woman, "Leonora informs me you require a writer. I have procured one of exceptional beauty for you—a man who rescued my child from the treacherous sea. Pray, regard him with favor."

The goldsmith's wife acknowledged the request in spirit alone. Her gaze locked onto Gerard's handsome visage, reluctant to sever its hold. Yet her verbal response was dismissive. "No, I hold no need for a writer. Ah! Now I recall, it was my gossip, Claelia, the sausage-maker, who sought one. She mentioned it to me, and I relayed it to Leonora."

Teresa offered a polite retreat and withdrew.

Claelia resided at a considerable distance. Upon reaching her threshold, she was absent. Teresa declared calmly, "I shall await her return," settling into a posture so still, so dignified, and so statuesque that Gerard began to surreptitiously sketch her profile. Just as Claelia reappeared, the tableau shifted.

"Madam, I learn from the goldsmith's wife, the virtuous Olympia, that you seek a writer," Claelia announced, seizing Gerard's hand and drawing him forward. "I have brought you a paragon of beauty; he delivered my child from the cruel embrace of the waves. For the sake of our Lady, cast your eyes upon him with benevolence."

"My good dame, my fair Sir," Claelia replied, "I possess no use for a writer. However, you jog my memory; it was my friend Appia Claudia who requested one but recently. She is a tailor residing in the Via Lepida."

Teresa retired with serene composure.

"Madam," Gerard interjected, "this pursuit seems destined to become tedious for you."

Teresa opened her eyes wide. "What endeavor has ever succeeded without a measure of patience?" she added softly. "We shall knock upon every door in Rome, and justice shall be ours."

"But, Madam, I perceive we are being shadowed. I noted a man trailing us, sometimes at a distance, sometimes in close proximity."

"I have observed it," Teresa replied coolly, though a faint blush stained her cheeks. "It is my poor Lodovico."

She paused, turned, and beckoned with a single finger. A figure approached, moving with evident reluctance.

Upon arrival, Teresa fixed her gaze upon him until he appeared sheepish.

"Lodovico mio," she commanded, "acquaint yourself with this young Sir, of whom I have frequently spoken. Know him and cherish him, for he is the very man who saved your wife and child."

These final words transformed Lodovico. Having previously bowed and grinned with artificial cheerfulness, his countenance instantly softened into an expression of profound gratitude, and he embraced Gerard with fervent warmth.

Yet, beneath this display, a dissonance lingered. The man's initial demeanor, coupled with his clandestine following of his wife, rendered Gerard uneasy under the embrace. Nevertheless, he asked, "Shall we accept your company, Sir Lodovico?"

"No, signor," Lodovico responded, "I do not travel that side of the Tiber."

"Then, Adieu," Teresa said, her tone laden with significance.

"When shall you return home, Teresa mia?"

"When I have completed my errand, Lodovico."

They resumed their journey in silence. Teresa now bore a somber, almost gloomy air.

Briefly stated, Appia Claudia proved merciful. She did not direct them across the river again, but merely a hundred yards down the street to Lucretia, keeper of the glove shop. Lucretia also required a writer, though Appia Claudia could not discern the purpose. Lucretia was a jovial little dame who welcomed them warmly, declaring she needed no scribe, keeping all her accounts memorized. "It was for my confessor, Father Colonna; he is utterly infatuated with them."

"I have heard of his excellence," Teresa remarked.

"Who has not?"

"But, good dame, he is a friar; he has taken vows of poverty. I cannot permit this young man to write without remuneration. He saved my child at sea."

"Did he indeed?" Lucretia cast an approving glance at Gerard. "Well, rest assured; a Colonna never lacks funds. The good father needs only utter the word, and the princes of his order will shower a thousand crowns into his lap. And what a confessor, dame! The finest in Rome. His mind wanders leagues and leagues away; he scarcely heeds your words. Indeed, I confess my sins to him no more readily than I would whisper them to that wall. Once, to test him, I confessed alongside the crowd that I had slain my lodger's infant daughter and baked her inside a pie. Well, when my voice ceased its confession, he jolted awake from his reverie, summoning a grave demeanor: 'My erring sister, recite three Paternosters and three Ave Marias kneeling, abstain from butter and eggs next Wednesday, and pax vobiscum!' Then off he went, hands clasped behind his back, appearing as though I were nonexistent."

Teresa waited patiently, then gently steered this digressive lady back to the objective: "Would you be so kind as accompany this worthy youth to the friar and advocate for him?"

"Alas! How can I abandon my shop? And what necessity exists? His door stands perpetually open to writers, painters, scholars, and all such creatures. Indeed, once he refused to receive the Duke d'Urbino because a learned Greek was secluded with him; the friar's head and the scholar's were pressed so closely over a dusty parchment newly arrived from Greece that one could overlay both cowls upon the pair. My wench Onesta told me. She frequents here primarily for conversation during her errands."

"This is the man for thee, my friend," Teresa affirmed.

"All you must do," Lucretia continued, "is proceed to his lodgings—my boy shall guide you—and inform Onesta that you come from me and are a writer. She will escort you to him. If you place a piece of silver in the wench's palm, it causes no harm; it stands to reason."

"I possess silver," Teresa said warmly.

"But wait," Lucretia cautioned, "heed one thing. Whatever this young man claims he can accomplish, he must be capable of doing, or let him avoid the good friar like pestilence. He is a ferocious beast against all bunglers. Indeed, but yesterday, someone presented him with a poorly carved crucifix. He exclaimed, 'Is this how you present the *Salvator Mundi*? Who died for you in mortal agony, and you dare begrudge Him careful craftsmanship? This slovenly trinket, a crucifix? But since it is a crucifix of some sort, and I am a holy man, I would dust your jacket with your crucifix!' Onesta heard every syllable through the keyhole; therefore, beware."

"Fear not, Madam," Teresa replied loftily. "I warrant his ability; he saved my child."

Gerard lacked the subtlety to grasp this assurance. Far from sharing Teresa's confidence, he pleaded for a postponement. Would it not suffice to delay until tomorrow?

"Here is a coward for you," Lucretia chided.

"No, he is not a coward," Teresa snapped, inflamed; "he is modest."

"I dread this high-born, fastidious friar," Gerard admitted. "Consider he has witnessed the handiwork of every writer in Italy, dear Dame Teresa. If you would merely allow me to craft a superior piece of work than I have thus far produced, I shall face him tomorrow."

"I consent," Teresa agreed.

They walked homeward together.

Not far from his own lodging stood a shop selling vellum. A magnificent white skin hung in the window. Gerard gazed at it wistfully, knowing he could not afford it, yet he hurried past nonetheless. Soon, however, he resolved where to procure vellum. Parting ways with Teresa at his doorstep, he rushed upstairs, retrieving the bond he had carried all the way from Sevenbergen, laying it upon the table with a sigh. He then prepared his chemicals to erase the ancient script; yet, recognizing this as his final opportunity to read it, he conquered his visceral aversion to illegible handwriting and proceeded to decipher the deed despite its detestable abbreviations. The document revealed that Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was to advance funds to Floris Brandt concerning a parcel of land, to be repaid via rental income.

On this realization, Gerard deemed it imprudent and improper to destroy the deed. Instead, he vowed to decipher every word at his leisure. Descending the stairs, he resolved to purchase a small sheet of vellum with his share of the card-money.
