Days drifted by, and it became evident that Paulina possessed little capacity for affection within the confines of the house. She was not unruly, nor did she defy command; yet, to behold her was to witness something profoundly unsettling. She offered no comfort, no tranquility. Instead, she moped with a gravity that belied her years, bearing upon her infant face the deep, legible grooves of adult exile—the kindling sorrow of a soul longing for Europe while stranded at its antipodes. To me, Lucy Snowe, who felt guilty of possessing an overheated and wandering imagination, she appeared ancient and unearthly. Whenever I opened a door to find her seated alone in a corner, her chin resting on her fist, the room did not seem occupied by a child, but haunted by a spirit of desolation.

On moonlit nights, awakening from sleep, I would often catch sight of her figure, stark white in her nightdress, kneeling upright in bed. She prayed with the fervor of a zealot, a precocious fanatic, or an untimely saint. I confess I could not discern the thoughts swirling behind those eyes, but I suspected they were no healthier or more rational than the mind of the child herself. Her prayers were usually whispers, lost to the air, sometimes unspoken entirely save for the recurring, crushing burden: "Papa, my dear Papa!" It was a singular obsession, a monomania that betrayed the most unfortunate flaw in human nature—the inability to exist without a single, consuming idea.

If this fretting had continued unchecked, its conclusion would have been a tragedy of conjecture. However, a sudden turn arrived. One afternoon, Mrs. Bretton coaxed her from her usual corner of gloom and lifted her onto the window-seat. To occupy her, the governess asked her to watch the passersby and count the ladies descending the street. Paulina sat listlessly, eyes glazed, failing to count. Yet, as I watched her closely, I witnessed a startling transformation within her irises. These "sensitive" natures offer curious spectacles to the cool-hearted. Her fixed, heavy gaze swam, trembled, and suddenly glowed with fire. Her clouded brow cleared; the dejection vanished, replaced by a sudden, intense eagerness. "It *is*!" she cried.

Like a bird taking flight or a shaft leaving the bow, she vanished from the room. How she managed to unlock the door remains a mystery; perhaps it stood ajar, or perhaps Warren, who happened to be nearby, obeyed her impulsive summons. From the window, watching calmly, I saw her—clad in a black frock and a tiny braided apron, a garment she detested—dart halfway down the street. Just as I prepared to announce her madness to Mrs. Bretton, urging immediate pursuit, I saw her snatched away. A gentleman had intercepted her, draping his cloak over her small form, and advanced to return her to the house whence she had fled.

I assumed he would entrust her to a servant and depart, but instead, he ascended the stairs. His reception by Mrs. Bretton revealed his familiarity; she recognized him, yet she was fluttered, surprised, and taken unawares. Her manner was nearly expostulatory. In response to her silent questioning, he declared, "I could not help it, madam: I found it impossible to leave the country without seeing with my own eyes how she settles."

"But you will unsettle her," she warned.

"I hope not. And how is Papa's little Polly?" he asked, addressing the child as he sat and gently lowered her to the floor.

"How is Polly's Papa?" she replied, leaning heavily against his knee and gazing up into his face.

The scene was not loud, nor wordy, yet it was brimful with a feeling that oppressed the weary observer. There is a certain disdain or ridicule that comforts a spectator during scenes of unrestrained emotion, but I have always found the most burdensome that sensitivity which bends of its own volition, a giant slave to the sway of simple, unfeeling reason.

Mr. Home was a stern, hard-featured man. His forehead was knotty, his cheekbones marked and prominent, giving his countenance a distinctly Scotch quality. Yet, there was depth in his eye and genuine emotion in his now agitated features. His Northern accent harmonized with his physiognomy; he was simultaneously proud and homely. Laying his hand upon the child's uplifted head, he heard her whisper, "Kiss Polly." He kissed her. I wished, in my heart, for some hysterical cry, some display of drama to relieve the tension, but she made wonderfully little sound. She seemed to have obtained everything she desired, falling into a trance of contentment. In neither mien nor feature was this creature like her sire, yet she carried his strain within her, as a cup receives wine from the flagon.

Indisputably, Mr. Home possessed manly self-control, though secrets may lurk beneath the surface. "Polly," he said, looking down at his daughter, "go into the hall. You will see Papa's great-coat lying on a chair. Put your hand into the pockets; you will find a pocket-handkerchief there. Bring it to me."

She obeyed, moving with deftness and nimbleness. As she returned, Mr. Home was conversing with Mrs. Bretton, seemingly unaware of her presence until she stood at his knee. Seeing that he continued to talk, apparently oblivious, she took his hand, opened his unresisting fingers, slipped the handkerchief inside, and closed them around it. For a time, he remained unconscious of her return, but eventually, he lifted her to his knee. She nestled against him, and though neither spoke to the other for an hour following, I suppose both were deeply satisfied.

Later, during tea, the minute movements of this little creature demanded full attention. First, she directed Warren, as he arranged the chairs. "Put Papa's chair here, and mine near it, between Papa and Mrs. Bretton: *I* must hand his tea." Taking her seat, she beckoned to her father. "Be near me, as if we were at home, Papa." Intercepting his cup as he passed, she stirred the sugar and poured the cream herself. "I always did it for you at home, Papa," she insisted. "Nobody could do it as well, not even your own self."

Her attentions throughout the meal were somewhat absurd. The sugar tongs were too wide for her hands, requiring two to wield; the weight of the silver cream ewer, the bread-and-butter plates, and the cups taxed her insufficient strength and dexterity. Yet, she lifted and handed with determined effort, miraculously breaking nothing. Candidly, I thought her a busy-body, but her father, blind like other parents, seemed perfectly content to let her wait on him, soothed by her offices.

"She is my comfort!" he exclaimed to Mrs. Bretton. That lady, who possessed her own "comfort" and nonpareil on a much larger scale, was momentarily absent, so she sympathized with his foible.

A second "comfort" appeared later that evening. I knew the day had been fixed for his return, and that Mrs. Bretton had awaited him all day. After tea, we sat round the fire when Graham joined our circle—or rather, broke it up, for his arrival caused a bustle, necessitating refreshments for the fastening guest. Mr. Graham and Mr. Home met as old acquaintances, though Graham initially ignored the little girl.

Once his meal was finished and numerous questions from his mother answered, Graham turned from the table to the hearth. Seated opposite him was Mr. Home, and at his elbow, the child. If I used the term "child," it is an inappropriate descriptor; it suggests any picture rather than that of the demure little person in a mourning frock and white chemisette, resembling a large doll perched on a high chair beside a stand holding her toy work-box of white varnished wood. She held a shred of a handkerchief, professing to hem it, boring perseveringly with a needle that seemed almost a skewer in her fingers. She pricked herself repeatedly, marking the cambric with tracks of minute red dots, occasionally starting when the weapon swerved and inflicted a deeper stab, yet remaining silent, diligent, and absorbed, strangely womanly.

Graham was then a handsome, faithless-looking youth of sixteen. By "faithless-looking," I do not mean a perfidious disposition, but because the epithet strikes me as proper to describe the fair, Celtic character of his beauty: his waved light auburn hair, his supple symmetry, his frequent smiles, destitute neither of fascination nor subtlety. He was a spoiled, whimsical boy.

"Mother," he said, after eyeing the little figure in silence for some time, relieved from the half-laughing bashfulness of Mr. Home's presence, "I see a young lady in the present society to whom I have not been introduced."

"Mr. Home's little girl, I suppose you mean," said his mother.

"Indeed, ma'am," replied her son, "I consider your expression of the least ceremonious: Miss Home *I* should certainly have said, in venturing to speak of the gentlewoman to whom I allude."

"Now, Graham, I will not have that child teased. Don't flatter yourself that I shall suffer you to make her your butt."
