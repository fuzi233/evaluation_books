As the first light of dawn crept over the horizon, I rose from my slumber, driven by a sense of purpose, and began my day by surveying the dormitory. The night's remaining embers cast a faint glow, allowing me to make out the dim outlines of my fellow students, their faces shrouded in shadows. I knelt on the cold stone floor, surrounded by the quiet stillness, and devoted myself to crafting a gift for Monsieur's fête.

My materials, a collection of beads and silken threads, were rapidly dwindling, but I worked with precision, aware that the intricate design required a delicate balance of texture and hue. I had fashioned the ornament double the length I desired, following the ancient principle of opposing forces, to create a visually striking effect. A small gold clasp, salvaged from my own discarded necklace, served as the finishing touch, securing the chain with a satisfying click. The completed ornament lay coiled within a shell-box, its nacarat hue glowing softly in the morning light. I inscribed a personal message within the box's lid, my scissors' point scratching out the initial letters with deliberate care.

Madame Beck's annual fête, with its attendant gifts and tributes, was well-known to us all. Each year, the school collectively contributed a thoughtful present, a gesture of esteem reserved exclusively for Madame and, in a modified form, for her esteemed advisor, Monsieur Emanuel. His distinction was a testament to the regard in which he was held, despite his prickly nature and occasional irritability. Unlike Madame, who received a tangible token of appreciation, Monsieur Emanuel preferred a more subtle acknowledgment – a flower, a drawing, or a simple gesture of goodwill. His preference spoke to his character: a man attuned to the world around him, yet grounded in a deep sense of tradition and respect for the past.

Monsieur's fête coincided with the first Thursday of March, a day blessed with fine weather and a half-holiday. The morning's attendance at mass was followed by a leisurely afternoon, during which the townspeople took advantage of the opportunity to stroll, shop, or pay social calls. The occasion was marked by a sense of festivity, with students donning their finest attire, exchanging their usual woollen dresses for lighter, more vibrant garments. Mademoiselle Zélie St. Pierre, in particular, made a striking impression, wearing a silk robe that was considered a rare indulgence in our humble community.
