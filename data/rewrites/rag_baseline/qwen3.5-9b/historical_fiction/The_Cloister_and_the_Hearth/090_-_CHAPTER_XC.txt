The day following that catastrophic rupture, the small dwelling on the Hoog Straet stood as a mausoleum of silence, heavier than any tombstone. Within it, Catherine, once the very embodiment of bustling vitality, moved with the sluggish grace of a ghost. Her eyes, rimmed with the raw red of weeping, betrayed a spirit crushed under the weight of recent tragedy. Driven by a desperate, final hope, she ventured to the convent to seek Gerard, attempting to lay the cornerstone of a reconciliation before the mortar of time could harden forever. Yet, the gatekeeper met her with the cold indifference of bureaucracy; Gerard had departed in the dead hours of the previous night, vanished into the city's labyrinthine shadows with no promise of return.

Sighing, a sound that seemed to deflate her very lungs, Catherine returned to find Margaret. There sat the younger woman, a figure of profound desolation, clutching her child so tightly it appeared she sought to fuse flesh and bone, terrified that the world might snatch him away as easily as it had taken everything else. Life had lost its flavor for Margaret, reduced to a bitter ash in her mouth.

"Catherine pleaded with her to accompany her back to the Hoog Straet, hoping her presence might bridge the chasm between them."

"Why?" Margaret sighed, her voice hollow. "You must admit, she is the architect of this ruin."

"Nay, nay," Catherine insisted, her tone pleading rather than argumentative. "We are not so devoid of charity. Eli loves you dearly; perhaps your touch will soften his hardened heart."

"If you believe I can effect a change," Margaret murmured, lifting her weary head, "then I shall come."

Upon their arrival, they found Eli engaged with a carpenter, hammering a new signpost into the timber. The name of Cornelis and Sybrandt had been effaced, replaced by a fresh inscription: *Margaret Brandt*. The irony cut deeper than any blade. Despite her enduring affection for Margaret, the sight of her name etched in permanent ink felt like a physical laceration to Catherine.

"The bane of one becomes the sustenance of another," Catherine whispered, the ancient proverb tasting like bile.

"Can he justify spending my inheritance unjustly?" Margaret retorted, her voice icy despite the tremor in her hands.

"You are a soul of goodness," Catherine replied, searching for comfort in the impossible.

"Ay, precisely," Margaret answered, her bitterness sharp as glass. "Which makes him the stronger, for he holds the purse strings."

The following day, Giles arrived, bringing with him the usual current of gossip. Catherine spun a tale woven entirely in favor of the ostracized siblings, painting them as victims of fraternal betrayal and paternal tyranny, begging for his pity. But Giles's prejudices flowed in a contrary river. He listened patiently, then delivered his verdict with blunt clarity: the scoundrels had escaped punishment far too lightly; they deserved execution at Margaret's very doorstep. Dismissing them with scornful laughter, he crowed triumphantly about the return of his favorite nephew.

"I shall demonstrate to him," Giles declared, his eyes gleaming with vindictive joy, "the true value of having a brother at court—one with a heart loyal to friendship and a mind capable of charting the path to justice."

"Bless thee, Giles," Margaret murmured softly, her gaze fixed on the floor.

"Thou hast always been his steadfast ally, dear Giles," little Kate added, her young voice tinged with helplessness. "But alas, I fear nothing you can offer will reach him now."

When Giles departed, the house settled back into its mournful silence. Just then, a well-dressed stranger entered, asking quietly if Margaret Brandt resided within.

"Do you hear, lass? You are summoned," Catherine announced briskly, her gossip instinct overriding her grief.

"Well, mother," Margaret said, rising with a listless resignation, "here I am."

A shuffle of feet echoed near the threshold, and a pale, trembling figure was ushered into the room. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. At the sight of him, Catherine screamed, throwing her apron over her head in horror, while Margaret recoiled, turning her face away as if looking upon a plague.

From the stranger's lips emerged a voice weak as dry leaves: "Good people, a dying man comes to beg your forgiveness."

"To witness the fruits of your labor, you mean," Catherine spat, removing her veil and bursting into fresh sobs.

"There, there, she faints! Look to her, Eli, quickly!"

"Nay," Margaret whispered, her strength failing, "the sight of him has struck me blind. That is all. Pray, let him speak and depart; for he is the murderer of me and my lineage."

"Alas," Ghysbrecht gasped, struggling to maintain his composure, "I am too frail to stand, and no one bids me sit."

Eli, who had followed the intruder inside, stepped forward, his voice a mixture of sullen resentment and forced apology. "Well, Burgomaster, 'tis not our custom to leave a guest standing while we sit. But man, man, you have wrought us unspeakable woe." Even as he spoke, the honest man's voice began to quake with suppressed rage, fighting the urge to unleash fury upon the man who had destroyed his home.

Then, a voice arose that rarely spoke in vain within those walls—that of little Kate. "Father, Mother," she said gently, "my duty to you is clear, yet this course is wrong. Death balances all ledgers. Do you not see death written across his face? I shall not live long, good friends; and his time is shorter still."

Moved by her plea, Eli hurried to set a chair for their dying adversary with his own hands. Ghysbrecht's attendants helped him settle into it.

"Fetch the boxes," Ghysbrecht commanded.

Two chests were brought in, and the attendants withdrew, leaving their master alone amidst the family he had so grievously wounded. Every eye turned toward him save Margaret's. With unsteady fingers, Ghysbrecht unlocked the lids. From one, he produced the title deeds to properties in Tergou.

"This land and these houses belonged to Floris Brandt, and by right, they belong to thee, his granddaughter. I usurped them for a debt long since paid in full with interest. Now, with penitent tears, I restore them to their rightful owner." He paused, his voice thick with emotion. "In this other box lie three hundred and forty golden angels—the rents and fines I collected from that land exceeding Floris Brandt's debt to me. I kept them accounted for, intending to remain just one day longer, but Avarice held me fast, tempting me against my conscience. I was not born dishonest; yet, you see the proof."

"Well, truly!" Catherine exclaimed, her greed momentarily overriding her sorrow. "And you, the Burgomaster! You have whipped countless thieves in your day. However," she amended, reconsidering, "'tis better late than never.' What, Margaret, are you deaf? This good man has restored your birthright. You are a wealthy woman. Alack, what a mountain of gold!"

"Keep the land and the gold," Margaret said, her head still averted, her voice barely audible. "Give me back my Gerard, whom he stole from me through his treachery."

"Alas!" Ghysbrecht lamented. "Would that I could! What can I do? It cost me a fierce internal battle. I rose from my sickbed to deliver this personally, lest some misfortune intervene between you and your rights."

"Old man," Margaret said, her voice breaking, "since thou, whose idol is coin, hast done this, may God and the Saints, as I hope, forgive thee. As for me, I am neither saint nor angel, but merely a poor woman whose heart thou hast shattered. Speak to him, Kate, for I am like the dead."

Kate pondered for a moment, then her soft, silvery voice drifted through the room like a soothing melody. "My poor sister bears a sorrow that riches cannot heal. Give her time, Ghysbrecht; 'tis not in nature for her to forgive thee completely. Her boy is fatherless; she is neither maiden, wife, nor widow; and the blow fell but two days hence that laid her heart open to bleed."

A single, heavy sob escaped Margaret, the only response to these words.

"Therefore, grant her time!" Kate continued, her voice gaining strength. "And ere thou diest, she will forgive thee all, yea, even to please me, who may not linger long behind thee, Ghysbrecht. Meanwhile, we, whose wounds are sore but not as deep as hers, pardon thee, a penitent and a dying man. And I, for one, will pray for thee from this hour. Go in peace!"

Her oracle had spoken; it was sufficient. Even Eli invited him to share a crust of bread and a cup of wine to strengthen him for his journey. But Ghysbrecht declined, declaring that his act of restitution was already a cordial to his soul.

"Man sees but a short distance ahead, neighbor," he said, his voice fading. "This land I clung to with such desperation was a bed of nettles to me all along. It is gone, and I feel happier, lighter, for the loss."
