"I question things but do not find One that will answer to my mind: And all the world appears unkind."

Gwendolen breathed a sigh of relief that the interview with Klesmer lay behind her before she faced the inevitable confrontation with her parents. Her resolve had hardened into a defensive shell; she knew only discomfort awaited her now, and she felt a grim capacity to endure whatever humiliation her uncle and aunt might propose.

The meeting did not occur until Monday, when Gwendolen accompanied her mother to the rectory. Their path had led them first to Sawyer's Cottage, where they inspected every cranny of the narrow rooms bathed in the harsh, unfiltered light of midday. Blinds and curtains were absent, exposing the bare walls and floors to the scrutiny of their gaze. The furnishings, intended to be scavenged from the rectory's stores, had not yet arrived.

"How *shall* you endure it, mamma?" Gwendolen asked as they turned back toward the road. She had kept silent during the tour of the dilapidated space—the little garden choked with cabbage stalks, the yew arbor clogged with dust and cobwebs. "You and the four girls confined to that closet of a room, with that sickly green-and-yellow wallpaper pressing against your eyes? And without me?"

"It will be some comfort that you need not share it, dear."

"If it were not for the necessity of earning money, I would sooner be there than go to serve as a governess."

"Do not pre-judge the situation, Gwendolen. If you secure the position at the palace, every luxury will surround you. You know how deeply you have always cherished such things. Do not mistake the hardship of climbing those steep, narrow stairs, listening to crockery clatter through the halls, and overhearing the girls' conversations, for the lesser evil."

"It feels like a nightmare," Gwendolen exclaimed impulsively. "I cannot believe Uncle will allow you to submit to such a fate. He ought to have taken other measures."

"Do not be unreasonable, my child. What else could he have done?"

"That was for him to discover. It strikes me as a peculiar world where people of our standing must sink so precipitously," Gwendolen remarked, her mental landscape shifting instantly to accommodate this new reality. In her imagination, other worlds were constructed with a logic that ensured her future remained agreeable, even amidst ruin.

It was her temperament that shaped her sentences under this crushing weight of adversity. She possessed the rhetorical skill to speak fittingly of vicissitudes in others' lives, though virtue was never her aspiration; she sought only to appear clever—a distinction that often rendered her words sharper than her intentions warranted. Yet, despite the acute awareness of her own bruises, she retained a capacity for contrition when her uncle and aunt greeted her with a warmth they had previously withheld. She was struck by the dignified cheerfulness with which they discussed the necessary economizing of their lifestyle and the education of the boys. Mr. Gascoigne's inherent worth, slightly dimmed by worldly exigencies much as a woman's poetic beauty is obscured by the demands of fashion, shone brightly under this sudden reduction of fortune. Prompt and methodical, he had resolved not only to retire his carriage but to scrutinize his worn suits, to forego meat at breakfast, to abandon periodicals, to withdraw Edwy from school, and to assume direct oversight of the boys' studies, ordering the entire household onto the sparest footing imaginable. For healthy souls, economy possesses its own pleasures, and the rector's spirit had permeated the domestic sphere. Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna, who always held their father as their ideal, genuinely missed nothing they truly valued, and in sincere sorrow, identified the most tragic aspect of their losses as the displacement of Mrs. Davilow and her children.

For the first time, Anna could merge her resentment regarding Rex with her sympathy for Gwendolen. Mrs. Gascoigne hoped that trouble might prove salutary for her niece, sparing herself the duty of adding bitterness to the medicine. Both women had busied themselves devising ways to procure blinds and curtains from the household stores, yet with delicate tact, they relegated these practicalities to the background, focusing instead on Gwendolen's journey and the comfort her presence brought to her mother.

Indeed, there was nothing for Gwendolen to latch onto that would justify extending her discontent toward those immediately surrounding her. Feeling shaken into a state of hyper-alertness, as if summoned to a drill everyone else was obediently attending to, she listened intently when her uncle began, in a voice of firm kindness, to outline the efforts he had undertaken to secure her a situation offering maximum advantage. Mr. Gascoigne had not forgotten Grandcourt, but the vagueness of any potential advances from that quarter was insufficient to dictate his actions to a man of his sound judgment; such uncertainties could not slacken his diligence in providing for his niece under current conditions.

"I felt there was no time to lose, Gwendolen," he said. "A position in a respectable family, where you will receive proper consideration, does not present itself at a moment's notice. However long we wait, we could scarcely find one where you would fare better than at Bishop Mompert's. I am known to both him and Mrs. Mompert, and that is an asset to you. Our correspondence has proceeded favorably, yet I am not surprised that Mrs. Mompert desires to see you before finalizing an engagement. She intends to arrange for you to meet her at Wanchester on her way to town. I daresay you will find the interview somewhat trying, my dear; but you will have time to prepare your mind."

"Do you know *why* she wishes to see me, uncle?" Gwendolen asked, her mind rapidly cataloging the various reasons an imaginary Mrs. Mompert, burdened with three daughters, might entertain—all reasons of a distinctly disagreeable nature for someone presenting herself for inspection.

The rector smiled gently. "Do not be alarmed, my dear. She seeks a more precise impression of you than my report can convey. A mother is naturally scrupulous regarding a companion for her daughters. I have assured her you are very young. However, she exercises close supervision over her daughters' education, which renders her less anxious about age. She is a woman of taste and strict principle, and she objects to having a French person in the house. I am certain she will deem your manners and accomplishments adequate, and regarding the religious and moral tone of your education, she—and indeed the bishop himself—will preside."

Gwendolen dared not respond, but the suppression of her vehement dislike for the entire prospect sent a deep flush racing over her face and neck, receding as swiftly as it arrived. Anna, filled with tender fears, slipped her small hand into her cousin's, and Mr. Gascoigne, too kind-hearted to ignore the trial this sudden shift posed for a girl like Gwendolen, continued in an easy, reassuring tone, addressing her as if dispelling hypothetical objections.

"I hold this position in such high regard that I would have been tempted to pursue it for Anna, had she been likely to meet Mrs. Mompert's requirements. It is truly a home, offering a continuation of education in the highest sense; 'governess' is a misnomer. The bishop's views lean decidedly Low Church compared to my own—he is a close friend of Lord Grampian's—but though privately strict, he is not narrow in public matters. Indeed, he has aroused as little dislike in his diocese as any bishop on the bench. He has remained friendly to me throughout, although prior to his promotion, when he served as an incumbent in this diocese, we engaged in a slight controversy concerning the Bible Society."

The rector's words were laden with meanings that satisfied his own conscience, leading him to underestimate their impact on his niece. "Continuation of education"—"bishop's views"—"privately strict"—"Bible Society"—these phrases landed like loose snakes released for the instruction of ladies who viewed them all as equally equipped with poison bags, ready to bite or sting according to convenience. To Gwendolen, already recoiling from the prospect opening before her, such terms felt like the intensifying heat of a burning glass, rather than the comforting links of persuasive reflection they formed for her good uncle. Desperate, she began to search for an alternative.

"There was another situation, I think, mamma spoke of?" she said, exercising determined self-mastery.

"Yes," the rector replied in a deprecating tone. "But that is in a school. I would derive less satisfaction from your undertaking that role. It involves much harder work, as you are aware, and offers fewer benefits in other respects. Furthermore, you do not possess an equal chance of securing it."

"Oh dear no," Mrs. Gascoigne interjected. "It would be far harder for you, my dear—and far less appropriate. You might not even have a bedroom to yourself." Memories of school flooded Gwendolen's mind, suggesting other particulars that forced her to admit to herself that this alternative offered no relief. Turning back to her uncle, she appeared to accept his proposal.

"When is Mrs. Mompert likely to send for me?"

"That is somewhat uncertain, but she has promised not to entertain any other proposal until she has seen you. She has entered deeply into your position. It will likely be within the next fortnight. But I must depart now; I am going to lease part of my glebe at an exceptionally favorable rate."
