For the time being, it seemed I would be living within the royal pyramid. The glittering procession came to a halt in the vast paved square before the structure, forming clusters of color and motion. The mammoth was brought to a stop just before the entrance, and when a stairway was rolled into place, the trumpets blared, signaling our arrival. We three—those who had ridden beneath the golden half-castle draped in serpent banners—descended to the ground.

It was clear we were moving from the open sky into the hidden depths of the pyramid’s stone labyrinth. Without thinking, instinct took over—the deep-rooted reverence bred into me by years of tradition caused me to turn toward the Sacred Mountain, its jagged peaks looming above the city like ancient sentinels. I bowed my head, offering the customary prayer of gratitude to the gods, especially to our Lord the Sun, for the air we breathed.

I did this without conscious thought—purely as habit—but as I rose, I swear I heard a soft, mocking titter ripple through the crowd. My gaze snapped toward the source, frowning, ready to demand punishment for such insolence. But then I turned to Phorenice, expecting to see her still in the act of bowing. Instead, she stood upright, untouched by any gesture of submission. And she watched me with an expression I couldn’t read.

Whatever she was thinking, she kept silent before the gathered people. “Come,” she said simply, then turned toward the doorway and called out the secret word for the day. The massive stone blocks groaned on their hinges and swung inward. With stately grace, she stepped into the cool shadows beyond, followed closely by her fan-girl attendant. A heavy weight settled in my chest as I followed, and behind us, the great doors slammed shut with a final, echoing thud.

We didn’t go far. Phorenice stopped in the Hall of Waiting—a place I remembered well. The red walls bore portraits of past kings, and at the center, a jet of earth-breath burned steadily from a bronze spout, casting flickering light across the floor. This was where the late king had bid me farewell twenty years prior, before I set off for my governorship in Yucatan. But the atmosphere was different now. Once pure and fresh, the air now carried a thick, cloying perfume that made my limbs feel sluggish and my thoughts slow.

“My minister,” Phorenice said, “I forgive you any intention of insult. But I think the colonial life has made you… simple. That kind of obeisance you just offered the mountain hasn’t been seen since I came to rule.”

“Your Majesty,” I replied, “I belong to the Priests’ Clan. I was raised in their teachings. Before entering any dwelling, I thank the gods—for the breath they give, and for the sun that warms the land. I’ve been chased through mountains by rivers of fire and poisonous fumes more than once. So when I pray, it comes from the heart.”

“Times have changed since you left Atlantis,” she said. “Now, thanks are not given to those old gods.”

I understood her meaning—and recoiled. To reject the divine? To replace them with something new? If this was the future, I could not serve it. I might face fate as it came, but I would not betray my faith. I would not bow to a mere woman, no matter how powerful, as if she were a deity. I chose to challenge her.

“Phorenice,” I said, “I’ve been a priest all my life. I revere the gods and know their mysteries. Until I see proof they are false, I will stand by them. If there is a cost for that loyalty, I will pay it.”

She smiled slowly. “You’re a strong man, Deucalion.”

I bowed.

“I’ve known others just as stubborn,” she continued. “But they were converted.” She tossed her fiery hair, turning so the glow of the earth-breath caught the lines of her face and body. “I’ve found it easier to convert the stubborn than to burn them. In fact, there’s been little need for burning. They’ve rushed to conversion, whether I wanted them to or not. But today… perhaps my charm fails me.”

“Phorenice is Empress,” I said flatly. “And I am her servant. Tomorrow, if she allows, I’ll clear the rebels from the city gates. I must prove my worth.”

“I’ve heard you’re a skilled fighter,” she said. “And I have some skill myself. I fancy I can judge a man’s courage. Tomorrow, we’ll spar together. But tonight, I must complete the honor I’ve planned for you, Deucalion. The feast is nearly ready. You’ll want to prepare. Chambers have been prepared for you—filled with everything you’ll need. Ylga will show you the way.”

We waited until Phorenice had slipped through a shadowed doorway and disappeared from the light. Then, with Ylga leading and a lamp in hand, we wound our way through the twisting corridors of the pyramid.

Everywhere, perfumes hung in the air. The passageways bent and doubled through solid rock, so complex that even a seasoned traveler might wander for days. These builders had been master architects—designed to keep kings safe from assassins who might slip in from outside. Some say the king himself ordered the deaths of every worker who helped construct it, so no one would ever know its secrets.

Yet Ylga moved with ease. She turned here, paused there, pressed a stone in the wall—it slid aside, revealing a hidden passage. Once, she tapped the corner of a flagstone, which lifted under her foot, exposing a narrow staircase. We descended, then climbed again along a sloping path, until finally we reached the chamber assigned to me.

“There’s clothing in each chest along the walls,” she said. “Jewels and ornaments in that bronze coffer. Phorenice says these are her first gifts—just a taste of what’s to come. My lord, you may drop your simplicity now. Dress yourself in finery. Fit the fashion.”

“Girl,” I said sharply, “be respectful. Spare me such advice.”

“If you find that rude,” she replied, “you can tell Phorenice. I’ll be whipped. Or stripped and scourged before you. She’ll do much for you.”

“Girl,” I said, “you’re closer to that whipping than you think.”

She narrowed her eyes. “I have a name. They call me Ylga. You might’ve heard it when we rode on the mammoth—if you hadn’t been too busy staring at Phorenice.”

I studied her. “You’ve never seen me before. Yet your first words could bring trouble. There’s purpose behind this.”

She stepped to the heavy stone door, then pulled me back from the airshaft. “I’m Zaemon’s daughter,” she whispered. “The man you knew.”

“You carry a message from him?”

“How could I? He lives among the priests on the Sacred Mountain. I haven’t seen him in two years. But when I saw you step from that red pavilion at the harbor… I felt pity. I remembered you were my father’s friend. And I knew what Phorenice plans. She’s been plotting this for two months.”

“I cannot listen to slander against the Empress.”

“And yet—”

“What?”

She stamped her sandal on the stone. “You’re either very blind or very brave, Deucalion. But I won’t interfere—yet. Still, I’ll watch. If you ever need a friend, I’ll help.”

“I thank you for your friendship.”

“You take it lightly. Why, sir, you don’t even know my power—or my reason. You may be the most powerful man in the kingdom, but I rank second among women. And remember—women hold high favor in Atlantis now. Believe me, my friendship is sought after.”

“And I’m grateful,” I said. “You seem to undervalue it, Ylga. But let me say—this is the first time I’ve ever offered such gratitude to a woman. You should treasure it.”

She smiled faintly. “Well, my lord, you have much to learn.” She left, showing me how to summon slaves. For a moment, I stood stunned by what I’d said. Who was this daughter of Zaemon—this girl—who could make me break a lifetime of custom?

The slaves arrived at my call, eager to dress me in elaborate robes and smear me with perfumes and ointments. Their manner annoyed me. I was already clean-shaven, my hair trimmed, my clothes spotless. Their attentions felt intrusive. I ordered them to beat each other—threatening to hand them over to the brander if they didn’t do it thoroughly. It’s strange how even the lowest servant can provoke irritation.

That day had brought many wonders, but nothing shocked me more than how Phorenice summoned the guests to the feast. Not just surprised—shocked. As I sat in my chamber, a sound began to rise from nowhere—soft at first, then swelling into a deafening clangor. The entire pyramid trembled, groaned, shook—as if it were a toy being kicked by a giant.

This was the sacred portent—the annual ritual performed by the Priests’ Clan atop the Sacred Mountain. A warning: man was born from fire and earthquake; he would end the same way. A reminder of sin, mortality, and divine justice. But here, Phorenice had twisted it into a summons to gluttony, laughter, and indulgence.

How had she gained such power? Who was she to command forces that stirred the earth itself? Had a priest betrayed his vows? Had she uncovered a secret herself?

If she’d discovered it alone, I could serve her in silence. She was Empress—my duty was obedience. But if she’d corrupted a priest… then it was a different matter. Our law demanded that both betrayer and recipient die. Secrets were sacred.

With uneasy resolve, I answered the trembling call. Slaves guided me through the winding paths to the banqueting hall. The scene was dazzling. The immense chamber, carved with intricate designs, was filled with guests dressed in every color imaginable. No one cared about the portent, the rebellion outside, the starving people, or the neglected gods. They lived only for the present.

Flames of earth-breath lit the hall like daylight. When I entered, trumpets blared. But no roar of welcome. “Deucalion,” they murmured, bowing low, their silks jingling. Even when Phorenice appeared, the greeting was stiff, artificial. Heartfelt joy was considered barbaric now.

Two smirking chamberlains escorted me to the dais. I was to sit beside her, eat from her dish.

“No greater honor could be shown,” I said, kneeling.

She gave me a sidelong glance. “Deucalion may have more—if he asks prettily. He may have what every man in the world has dreamed of—and none have earned. But I’ve given enough. He must ask warmly.”

“I ask,” I said, “to clear the city’s borders of the rebels.”

“Pah,” she scoffed. “Only stern pleasures for you? My dear Deucalion, your colony must have been primitive. Well, tell me—how delicious is this feast?”

Dishes and goblets were placed before us. I ate little—spices too sharp, food too refined. But the others feasted wildly, savoring each bite as if it were communion with the gods. Women and men alike lingered over their meals, lost in ecstasy.

Phorenice watched, occasionally tossing a word to a favorite, calling someone forward to receive a scrap from her plate. The honored guest would devour it dramatically—or sometimes tuck it away, as if it were too sacred for human lips.

To me, it was revolting. But she accepted it as natural. There was a flaw in her—though none could see it. Her face was strong, intelligent, beautiful. The court praised her beauty endlessly. Though I wasn’t one for admiration, I had to admit she was striking. Yet I found myself drawn more to Ylga, standing behind us—her face honest, unpretentious.

They didn’t eat to live. They ate to excess. New customs emerged with each course. One guest—a flamboyant governor from a rebellious province—approached the divan, flushed and swaying. “Most glorious Empress!” he cried, “fairest of goddesses, queen of my soul! Hail!”

Phorenice handed him her cup. I expected him to pour it at her feet. Instead, he drank it down. “May all your troubles vanish as easily—and leave such a sweet aftertaste!”

She turned to me. “You disapprove of this new custom?”

“To pour drink at one’s feet is respect,” I said. “To drink it is self-indulgence.”

“Our new code says enjoy life first,” she replied. “Order comes later.”

The feast continued—gluttony, absurdity, laughter. Guards stood rigid around the hall, statues of stone. But suddenly, a stir broke the revelry. At the far door, guards crossed spears—but a man passed through unharmed, walking calmly across the pavement.

All turned to look. A few laughed. Others shivered as he passed—like a cold wind had swept through.

He stood before Phorenice’s divan, staring at her, not bowing.

An old man. Thin, white-haired, beard ragged. Mud clung to his legs. Bare except for a single cloth wrapped about him. In his hand, a staff tipped with the glowing symbol of the Sun—marking him as a priest.

I thought he was one of the ascetics—those who abandoned the safety of the Sacred Mountain to live among the burning hills, enduring fire, poison, beasts, and earthquakes. They lived close to the gods, humbled by nature’s fury, and learned mysteries no scholar could grasp.

Silence fell. Phorenice spoke first. “Those two guards failed. Remove them.”

Spears struck. Both guards collapsed—one screamed, the other died silently.

The old man’s voice cracked. “Another crime added to your record, Phorenice. You could not have stopped me if you tried. I come with your last warning. The gods have favored you—gave you merit to rise. They allowed you the throne. But now, you must stop your evil. Feed the starving—even if it means taking from these gluttons. Restore Atlantis to its former order. Or you will be removed. The country cannot fall back into chaos. You hear me. Now answer truly.”

“Speak gently,” whispered Ylga from behind us. “For your sake, speak gently.”

But Phorenice ignored her. She leaned forward, brows furrowed.

“Do you dare threaten me, old man—knowing who I am?”
