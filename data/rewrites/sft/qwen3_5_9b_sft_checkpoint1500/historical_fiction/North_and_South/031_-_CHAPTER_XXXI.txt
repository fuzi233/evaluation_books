The chill, shivering morning of October descended upon Milton not with the gentle, silvery mist of the countryside, which clears before the sun to reveal its vibrant hues, but with the heavy, oppressive fog described by Milton, where sunlight struggles to pierce the gloom, revealing only long, dusky streets. Inside the Hale household, the air was thick with a silence broken only by the occasional sob. Margaret moved through the rooms with a languid, weary step, her eyes constantly blurred by unshed tears. Yet, she found no time to surrender to grief; the weight of responsibility pressed heavily upon her shoulders. With her father and brother consumed by mourning, the practical necessities of the funeral—the arrangements, the guests, the logistics—devolved entirely onto her.

As the fire roared brightly and the tea-kettle sang its cheerful song, signaling readiness for breakfast, Margaret paused to inspect the room one final time. She desired the space to appear as joyful and orderly as possible, a stark, painful contrast to the devastation swirling within her soul. As she knelt beside the sofa, burying her face in the cushions to stifle a cry, a gentle hand touched her shoulder. It was Dixon.

"Come, Miss Hale—come, my dear!" Dixon urged, her voice unusually tender for the stern servant. "You must not give way. If you do collapse, who shall guide us? There is so much to arrange: the funeral details, the invitations, the location. Master Frederick is beside himself with weeping, and his father, poor soul, wanders about as if lost. It is a terrible burden, I know, but death claims us all. You have been spared this loss until now."

Perhaps Dixon was right. Yet, to Margaret, this specific bereavement felt unique, a wound that defied comparison with any other tragedy in the world. Finding no solace in the old woman's words, she was nonetheless moved by Dixon's unexpected gentleness. Driven by a need to repay this kindness, she rose, wiped her cheeks, and offered a fragile smile. "Breakfast is ready," she announced softly, moving to summon Mr. Hale and Frederick.

Her father entered as if in a trance, his movements slow and disconnected, as though his mind resided somewhere beyond the physical realm. Frederick, however, strode in with a forced vitality, seizing Margaret's hand and gazing into her eyes before bursting into fresh tears. Throughout the meal, Margaret struggled to keep the conversation light, filling the air with trivialities to prevent the lingering shadows of their shared memories from surfacing—the strained silence, the desperate listening for a sign from the sickroom, the last meal together.

Afterward, Margaret resolved to discuss the funeral arrangements with her father. He nodded to her suggestions, though they often contradicted one another, offering no real guidance. As she prepared to retreat to consult Dixon, Mr. Hale gently gestured for her to return.

"Ask Mr. Bell," he whispered, his voice hollow.

"Mr. Bell?" she queried, startled. "Of Oxford?"

"Yes," he murmured, sinking back into listlessness. "He was once my groom's-man."

Margaret understood the connection immediately. "I will write today," she decided.

For the remainder of the morning, she labored in a whirlwind of melancholic tasks, longing for rest but finding none. Towards evening, Dixon approached her.

"I have acted, miss. I was truly afraid for Master Frederick; grief threatened to strike him down. He spent the entire day with the late mistress, speaking to her as if she were breathing. When I entered, he was silent, a confused maze. I thought it vital to rouse him. I told him it is unsafe for him to remain here. Furthermore, on Tuesday, while out, I encountered a man from Southampton—the first local visitor I have seen since arriving. It was young Leonards, the draper's son, a notorious scamp who drove his father to madness before fleeing to sea. I never cared for him. He served aboard the *Orion* with Frederick, though I am unsure if he participated in the mutiny."

"Did he recognize you?" Margaret asked, leaning forward eagerly.

"That is the trouble," Dixon replied. "Had he not called out my name, I am certain he would not have known me. Being a stranger here, I might have feigned kinship, but I am not so foolish. He addressed me as 'Miss Dixon,' and I reminded him politely that I am still unmarried, though I have had chances if I had not been so particular. He was civil enough, asking after my father whom I had helped to employ George, despite having thrown him out. To spite me, seeing how civil we were, he began to inquire about Master Frederick. He spoke of Frederick's scrapes, suggesting he would be hanged for mutiny, mentioning the hundred-pound reward, and calling him a disgrace to his family. He did this merely to annoy me, for I had previously helped secure employment for his father."

"But you did not reveal our whereabouts? About Frederick?"

"Not a word," Dixon assured her. "However, before he departed, he turned back and said, 'If you can assist in trapping Lieutenant Hale, Miss Dixon, we shall go partners in the reward. I know you would like to be my partner, would you not?' Then he boarded the bus, leaving me with a leering, wicked smile."

Margaret felt a sharp pang of discomfort. "Have you told Frederick?"

"No," Dixon admitted. "I was uneasy knowing Leonards was in town, but the funeral preparations consumed my thoughts. Seeing Master Frederick so rigid and glum, I realized that the threat of Leonards might finally snap him out of his stupor. He must leave before Mr. Bell arrives."

"Oh, I am not afraid of Mr. Bell," Margaret said, her voice tightening. "I am terrified of this Leonards. I must inform Frederick. What does he look like?"

"A disreputable figure," Dixon described. "Red whiskers that would shame anyone, dressed in coarse fustian like a laborer, despite claiming a confidential position."

It became evident that Frederick must depart. He had already claimed his rightful place within the family, vowing to support his father and sister, yet now the specter of Leonards loomed. As Margaret sat by the drawing-room fire, watching her father pace restlessly under the pressure of this new fear, Frederick entered. The frantic violence of his grief had subsided, replaced by a dim, somber resolve.

"How pale you look, Margaret," he said quietly. "You have thought for everyone, and no one has thought for you. Lie down on this sofa; there is nothing left for you to do."

"That is the hardest part," Margaret whispered sadly. Yet, she obeyed, lying down as Frederick covered her feet with a shawl and sat beside her. In the subdued tones of their conversation, Margaret recounted Dixon's encounter. Frederick's lips tightened in dismay.

"I would dearly like to confront that young fellow," he muttered. "A worse sailor never sailed, nor a worse man. Consider the circumstances: when every decent sailor was indignant at our captain, he sought favor. Pah! And here he is. If he knew I was within twenty miles, he would ferret me out to settle old scores. I would rather anyone possessed the hundred pounds they claim I am worth than that rascal. What a pity Dixon could not be convinced to provide for her old age!"

"Hush, Frederick!" Margaret pleaded.

Just then, Mr. Hale approached, his face aglow with tremors and eagerness. Having overheard their discussion, he seized Frederick's hand.

"My boy, you must go. It is perilous, but necessary. You have done all you could; you have been a comfort to her."

"Papa, must he go?" Margaret begged, fighting against the conviction rising within her.

"I declare, I have a mind to face the music and stand my trial. If I could only gather my evidence! I cannot bear to be in the power of such a blackguard as Leonards. In other circumstances, I might have enjoyed this stolen visit—it possesses all the charm the Frenchwoman attributed to forbidden pleasures."

"One of the earliest memories I have," Margaret interjected gently, "is yours being in great disgrace for stealing apples. We had trees laden with fruit, yet someone told you stolen fruit tastes sweetest. You took it literally and went a-robbing. Your feelings have not changed much since then."

"Yes, you must go," Mr. Hale insisted, his focus singular, struggling to follow his children's zigzagging thoughts.

Margaret and Frederick exchanged a glance. A fleeting, profound sympathy passed between them, a bond that would vanish if he departed. So much was understood without words. Frederick broke the spell first.

"Do you know, Margaret, I was nearly frightened out of my wits this afternoon? I was in my bedroom, hearing a ring at the front door. Thinking the caller had long gone, I intended to emerge, but opening my door, I saw Dixon descending the stairs. She frowned and kicked me back into hiding. I kept the door ajar and heard a man receive a message in Father's study, then depart. Who could it have been? Perhaps some shopmen?"

"Likely," Margaret said indifferently. "There was a small man who came for orders around two o'clock."
