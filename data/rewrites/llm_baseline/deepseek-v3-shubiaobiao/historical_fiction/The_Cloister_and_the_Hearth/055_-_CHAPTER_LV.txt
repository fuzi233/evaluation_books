Two months before the events in Eli’s home, the inhabitants of a small coastal village between Naples and Rome gathered on the shore, their eyes fixed on a ship struggling against a fierce gale. The vessel, ill-suited for such a tempest, pitched violently in the waves, now seeming to escape danger, now lurching perilously closer to destruction. The crowd watched with a morbid fascination—part pity, part selfish relief that it was not their own lives at stake.

Onboard, chaos reigned. Sailors scrambled across the deck, shouting contradictory orders, their prayers and curses lost in the howling wind. Passengers huddled together, some kneeling, others sprawled in terror, clutching at whatever they could as the ship groaned beneath them. Among them stood Gerard, his face pale but his jaw set, gripping a shroud with white-knuckled determination.

Around him, the air was thick with desperate invocations—saints and deities of every name called upon in a cacophony of fear. The sailors pleaded with the Virgin Mary under her many titles, while the landsmen bargained with local patron saints, each promising lavish offerings if only they might be spared. One merchant vowed a waxen effigy of St. Christopher’s own weight, only to whisper afterward that he had no intention of fulfilling such an absurd vow.

A crack like thunder split the air—the mast had splintered, the sail torn away by the wind. The ship, now unmanageable, was at the mercy of the waves. The captain, his face ashen, ordered everything thrown overboard to lighten the vessel. Passengers frantically tossed their belongings into the sea—until one man, an aged Jew, shrieked in protest as his sack of silver was wrenched from him and cast into the depths. He howled curses, shaking his fist at the heavens, his grief as wild as the storm itself.

Amidst the panic, two figures stood apart—a Roman woman, silent and pale, cradling her child with unnatural calm, and a towering Dominican friar, his voice steady as he recited scripture and offered absolution to those who sought it. Gerard, drawn to the friar’s unshaken resolve, edged closer, as if proximity to such courage might lend him strength.

When the crew abandoned ship in the lone boat, leaving the rest to their fate, Gerard acted. He lashed the wooden figure of the Virgin to the Roman woman, giving her and her child a chance to float. She looked at him with dark, liquid eyes and whispered a single word: *"Thyself?"* But he shook his head. *"I am a man,"* he said, *"and have no child to care for."*

Then, with the friar’s help, they heaved the broken mast into the waves and clung to it as the sea hurled them toward shore. The woman paddled fiercely with one arm, her child clutched tight in the other. The friar, feeling sand beneath his feet, abandoned the mast and strode through the surf like a colossus, while Gerard followed, battered but unbroken.

They were dragged ashore by fishermen, the friar offering a blessing before striding off toward Rome without a backward glance. Gerard, shivering and spent, watched as others washed up—some alive, most not. The Roman woman found him on the beach, took his hand, and pressed it to her lips without a word. Her gratitude needed none.

The next day, Gerard entered the Eternal City—no longer a boy, but a man who had faced death and survived.
