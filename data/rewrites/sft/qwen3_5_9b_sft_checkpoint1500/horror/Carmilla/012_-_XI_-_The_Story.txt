"The Story"

"With every ounce of strength I possess," the General rasped, his voice cracking under the weight of the memory. After a heavy, agonizing pause to gather his shattered thoughts, he launched into a narrative so strange, so chilling, that it remains etched in my mind forever.

"My dearest child gazed forward with radiant joy toward the visit you so graciously arranged for her to meet your own daughter." Here, he offered me a bow that was simultaneously gallant and deeply mournful. "Meanwhile, however, we received an invitation from my ancient friend, Count Carlsfeld. His estate lies roughly six leagues beyond Karnstein. We were summoned to witness the series of festivities he hosted in honor of his illustrious guest, Grand Duke Charles."

"Yes," my father murmured, "and I recall them as truly splendid."

"Royal, yes. Yet his hospitality transcends mere royalty; it possesses a regality that borders on the supernatural. He holds a lamp akin to Aladdin's. That fateful night—the dawn of my eternal sorrow—was consumed by a magnificent masquerade. The grounds were unveiled to the world, the trees draped in a kaleidoscope of glowing lamps. The fireworks erupted with a brilliance that Paris itself had never witnessed. And the music... oh, the music. Forgive me, for music is my fatal weakness. It was ravishing, soul-stirring. The finest instrumental ensemble the world knows, paired with the most exquisite singers gathered from the great opera houses of Europe. As you wandered through those fantastically illuminated gardens, the moonlight casting a rosy, spectral glow from the château's endless rows of windows, you would suddenly hear these haunting, ethereal voices slipping from the shadows of some grove or rising from the silent waters of the lake. In that moment, standing amidst the light and sound, I felt myself transported back to the romance and poetry of my youth, a time thought dead and buried."

"When the pyrotechnics faded and the ball finally began, we retreated to the noble suites of rooms opened to the dancers. A masked ball is a vision of beauty, yet I had never beheld a spectacle of such dazzling brilliance before."

"It was an assembly of the highest aristocracy. I, myself, was nearly the sole 'nobody' among them."

"My darling child looked absolutely radiant. She wore no mask. Her excitement and sheer delight lent an indescribable charm to her features, always lovely to behold. I noticed a young lady, dressed in magnificent splendor yet concealed behind a mask, who appeared to be studying my ward with extraordinary, piercing interest. I had spotted her earlier in the grand hall and again, just moments prior, walking near us beneath the castle windows, engaged in the exact same scrutiny. She was accompanied by another masked lady, dressed richly and with grave dignity, possessing a stately air that marked her as a person of high rank—a chaperone if ever there was one.

If that young lady had not been hidden behind her veil, I could certainly have resolved the mystery of whether she was truly watching my poor darling. I am now fully convinced that she was.

We found ourselves in one of the salons. My beloved child, having danced tirelessly, sat resting in a chair near the entrance. I stood close by. The two women I had noted approached; the younger one took the seat beside my ward, while her companion stepped up to stand beside me. For a brief moment, the older woman spoke to her charge in a hushed, intimate tone.

Then, availing herself of the freedom granted by her disguise, she turned to me. In the voice of an old confidante, addressing me by name, she opened a conversation that sparked a fierce curiosity within me. She referenced countless scenes where our paths had crossed—at Court, at distinguished estates. She alluded to trivial incidents I had long dismissed as forgotten dust, yet which, at her touch, instantly leapt back to life in my memory.

"I grew increasingly desperate to uncover her identity, moment by moment." She deflected my inquiries with adroitness and pleasantness. The depth of her knowledge regarding the passages of my life seemed almost unaccountable. She appeared to derive a peculiar, cruel pleasure in frustrating my curiosity, watching me flounder in eager perplexity, jumping from one wild guess to another.

Meanwhile, the younger lady, whom her mother occasionally addressed by the singular, odd name of Millarca, engaged my ward with the same ease and grace. She introduced herself by claiming her mother was an old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the agreeable audacity masks afford, conversing like a friend. She admired her dress and, with insinuating sweetness, expressed admiration for her beauty. She amused her with witty, laughing critiques of the crowds filling the ballroom, mocking my poor child's own fun. She was sharp and vivacious when she chose, and soon they had become fast friends. Eventually, the young stranger lowered her mask, revealing a face of remarkable, arresting beauty. Neither I nor my dear child had ever seen it before. Yet, though entirely new to us, the features possessed such an engaging, magnetic loveliness that resistance was impossible. The attraction was powerful, immediate. My poor girl fell under its spell instantly. I had never witnessed such instant infatuation unless, perhaps, it was the stranger herself, who seemed to have completely lost her heart to her.

While this unfolded, I used the license of the masquerade to interrogate the elder lady.

"You have utterly baffled me," I said, laughing nervously. "Is that not enough? Will you not, now, consent to stand on equal footing and grant me the kindness of removing your mask?"

"Can any request be more unreasonable?" she replied coolly. "To ask a lady to yield an advantage! Besides, how do you know you would recognize me? Years bring about changes."

"As you see," I said, bowing and offering what I hoped was a melancholy little laugh.

"As philosophers tell us," she countered. "And how do you know that seeing my face would aid you?"

"I would wager my life on it," I answered. "It is futile trying to pass yourself off as an old woman; your figure betrays you."

"Nevertheless, years have passed since I last saw you, and far longer since you saw me—that is what weighs on me. Millarca, there, is my daughter. I cannot therefore claim to be young, even in the eyes of those whom time has taught to be indulgent. And I dislike being compared to what you remember me as. You wear no mask to remove. You can offer me nothing in exchange."

"My petition rests upon your pity; remove it."

"And mine rests upon yours; keep it where it is," she replied firmly.

"Well, then, at least you will tell me whether you are French or German; you command both languages with perfect fluency."

"I do not think I shall tell you that, General. You intend a surprise, and you are meditating the particular angle of attack."

"At any rate, you cannot deny this," I pressed, "that being honored by your permission to converse, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I say Madame la Comtesse?"

She laughed, and surely, if I could treat any event in an interview whose circumstances I now believe were prearranged with profound cunning as merely liable to modification by accident, she would have met me with another evasion.

"As to that..." she began, but her words were cut short almost the moment they left her lips. A gentleman entered, dressed in stark black. He looked particularly elegant and distinguished, save for one terrible flaw: his face was the most deadly pale I have ever witnessed, resembling nothing so much as the countenance of a corpse. He wore no costume, only the plain evening attire of a gentleman. Without a smile, yet with a courtly and unusually low bow, he spoke:

"Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to say a few words which may interest her?"

The lady turned swiftly to him, touching her lips in a gesture of silence. Then she whispered to me, "Keep my place for me, General. I shall return when I have spoken a few words."

And with this playful injunction, she walked slightly aside with the gentleman in black. They conferred for several minutes, their tones appearing intensely earnest. Then, they strolled away slowly together into the throng, and I lost them from view for a time.

I spent the interval frantically scouring my brain for a conjecture regarding the identity of the lady who remembered me so kindly. I considered turning around to join the conversation between my pretty ward and the Countess's daughter, hoping to learn her name, title, château, and estates before she returned, thus catching her off guard. Just as I resolved to act, she returned, accompanied by the pale man in black.

"I shall return and inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage is at the door," the man announced.

He bowed and withdrew, leaving me alone with the terrifying aftermath of his presence.
