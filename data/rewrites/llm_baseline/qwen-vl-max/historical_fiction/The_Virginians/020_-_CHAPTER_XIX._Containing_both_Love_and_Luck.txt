At the next meal, the household gathered as usual, but there was no trace of the morning’s storm in Madame de Bernstein’s demeanor. Her face beamed with warmth, and her manner toward every guest—especially Harry—was gracious and affectionate. She praised the cook, declaring the fricassee divine, and insisted that no eels compared to those in Castlewood’s moats. When someone suggested the wine might be corked, she laughed it off, refusing to allow a fresh bottle for “a useless old woman” like herself. Instead, she raised her glass to Madam Esmond Warrington of Virginia, hoping Harry had secured his mother’s blessing to bring back an English bride.

Harry, though he could not recall his grandmother, found himself drawn into the Baroness’s tender recollections of her own dear mother—the beauty, kindness, and joy she’d known in her second marriage, despite the age difference between her and Colonel Esmond. “To see them together,” she mused, “was a sight to warm the heart. Their love was spoken of throughout the land. What matter if one is older than the other? Such devotion makes all differences vanish.”

My Lady Castlewood and her children remained silent, listening politely as the Baroness continued her charming reminiscences. Harry, captivated, felt his heart swell with admiration. Maria, beside him, looked on with quiet surprise. Lord Castlewood, meanwhile, could only wonder what sudden whim had transformed his aunt so completely—but he masked his curiosity with a serene expression, as though everything were perfectly natural.

The Baroness’s good humor spread through the room like sunlight. Not a single person at table escaped a kind word or a gentle jest. When Mr. Will, ever sincere, voiced his astonishment at her change of mood, she smiled and said, “Complimentary, my dear? Of course I am. I’m making amends for being dreadfully rude this morning. When I was a child, and my parents lived here, I used to behave just the same. If I’d been naughty during the day, I’d try to win them over at night. I remember sitting right here, at this very table—oh, centuries ago!—pleading with my father, my mother, and your grandfather, Harry Warrington. And there were eels for supper, just as tonight. That dish brought it all back. I was just as stubborn then, at seven, as I am now, at seventy. So I confess my sins and beg forgiveness, like a good girl.”

“I absolve your ladyship!” declared the chaplain, who sat among them.

“But you don’t know how cross I was,” she continued. “I scolded my sister, Castlewood. I scolded her children. I even boxed Harry Warrington’s ears—because he refused to go with me to Tunbridge Wells.”

“Then I shall go, madam,” said Mr. Warrington promptly. “With the greatest pleasure.”

“You see, Mr. Chaplain,” she added, “they are all so dutiful. Only I was unkind. Oh, how cruel I was to treat them so! Maria, I ask your pardon, my dear.”

“Surely, madam,” replied Maria, “you’ve done me no harm.”

“Indeed, I have—great harm,” the Baroness insisted. “Because I was weary of myself, I told you your company would be tedious. You offered to come, and I refused you rudely.”

“Nay, ma'am,” Maria said gently, “if you were unwell, and my presence troubled you…”

“But it will not trouble me now,” the Baroness interrupted. “You were kind to offer. I beg, I pray, I implore you—come with me.”

Lord Castlewood sipped his wine, his face calm and unreadable. The truth dawned upon him: this sudden affection was no mere whim—it was a plan. A maneuver.

“Anything that pleases my aunt,” said Maria, striving to appear cheerful, “will surely delight me.”

“You must stay with me, my dear,” the Baroness urged. “And I promise to be sweet and pleasant. My lord, will you spare your sister to me?”

“Lady Maria is of age,” his lordship replied with a bow. “She may judge for herself.”

“If any of us can serve you, madam,” he added, “you ought to command us.”
Which, in plain speech, meant: *Curse the old woman—she’s stealing Maria away from this Virginian boy.*

“Oh, Tunbridge will be delightful!” sighed Lady Maria.

“Mr. Sampson will arrange everything,” said my lord.

Harry traced patterns on the table with his finger. His mind swam with visions—long walks beneath ancient trees, rides through sun-dappled lanes, whispered conversations under moonlit skies, music shared in secret corners, stolen glances, tender words… The days ahead promised bliss. Soon, everyone would depart—Lady Castlewood to visit friends, Madame Bernstein to the healing waters—and he would be left alone with Maria. Alone with her, and with a rapture too vast for words.

The thought nearly overwhelmed him. He had feared this moment would never come. Now it was near. And yet—something stirred within him. A whisper: *It won’t last. Something will tear her from me.*

“Will you ride with us to Tunbridge, nephew Warrington?” asked Madame de Bernstein. “To keep us safe from highwaymen?”

Harry felt his cheeks flush. He forced his voice steady. “Yes, madam. I’ll ride with you. I’m sure you need fear no danger.”

Danger? He would welcome it. He would face a hundred thieves to protect her. He would ride beside her carriage, watch her eyes at the window, press her hand at the inn, lead her to the coach each morning. Two full days to Tunbridge—and perhaps a few more spent at the Wells. Was not a condemned man grateful for even two extra days of life?

We do not dwell long upon Harry Warrington’s state of mind. It was not madness, nor mere infatuation—it was a deep, consuming passion, such as some men carry to their graves. Some fall in love gently; others burn with it until they are consumed. And though we might laugh at such folly, let us not scorn him. For who among us has not acted foolishly when love struck? Even if the object is twice his age—have we not read of young men undone by passion for women who turned out to be their grandmothers? Let those who have never brayed mock him. But those who understand the donkey’s burden—let them pity him.

“You’ll stay with us for a day or two at the Wells,” Madame Bernstein went on. “You’ll help settle us in. Then you may return to Castlewood and the partridge-shooting, and all the fine things you and my lord will discuss.”

Harry bowed. A whole week—perhaps more—of pure happiness. Life was not without hope after all.

“And since there will be plenty of company at the Wells,” the Baroness added graciously, “I shall introduce you.”

“Company!” Harry sighed. “Ah, I shan’t need company.”

He corrected himself quickly. “I mean—I shall be quite content with just you two ladies.”

No doubt Mr. Will blinked at his cousin’s declaration.

As it was to be Harry’s final evening at Castlewood, Will proposed that he, the chaplain, and Harry meet later to settle accounts. Harry, having won several hands at cards, saw no objection. So, when the ladies retired and my lord withdrew to his chambers, the three men gathered in Harry’s small room around the punch bowl—a fixture of Will’s nightly routine.

But Will’s idea of settling debts was to draw fresh packs of cards and suggest doubling Harry’s winnings—or letting fate decide whether they were forgiven. The chaplain had little cash, and neither did Lord Castlewood’s younger brother. Harry wanted nothing from either. To take advantage of Maria’s kin, or wound the brother of the woman he adored—such thoughts were unbearable. He agreed to play, not to win, but to give them fair chance.

They began. Dice rattled. Cards flew. Harry’s mind wandered far from the game.

*Right now,* he imagined, *she sits with her golden hair spread across her shoulders, her maid brushing it. Happy maid. Now she kneels in prayer, that angelic creature, speaking to the heavens where angels like her dwell. Now she rests behind her damask curtains. Bless her, bless her.*

“Double us all round?” Will called. “I’ll take a card on each. Ten—then queen. Two natural vingt-et-uns. Since you doubled, you owe me…”

Imagine the curses from Will, the furious imprecations from the chaplain. Yet Harry remained calm. He had not played to win—he had played because he could not refuse. And fortune, ever fickle, favored him. Perhaps because his heart was elsewhere—perhaps because he was thinking of another nymph, equally capricious.

Will and the chaplain, eager to regain their losses, pushed harder. They believed the wealthy Virginian wished to let them recover. But Harry had no such intention. At home, he had played many such games—though he kept such matters quiet from his mother—and always played fairly. He expected fairness in return.

“The luck seems to be with me, cousin,” he said, in response to Will’s grumbles. “And I don’t wish to push it. But I’m not going to throw it away. I’ve already got a pile of your IOUs. If we continue, let’s put real money on the table—or its value.”

“Always the way with rich men,” Will muttered. “Never lend unless you get security. Always win because you’re rich.”

“Faith, cousin,” Harry replied, “you’ve been throwing my riches in my face lately. I have enough for my needs—and my creditors.”

“Oh, if only we could say the same!” groaned the chaplain. “How happy we’d be—and how happy the duns would be!”

“What can we bet against our conqueror?” he asked. “Here’s my new gown, Mr. Warrington. Will you set five pieces against it? I can preach in wool if I lose. Wait—I have Chrysostom, Foxe’s Martyrs, Baker’s Chronicle, and a cow and calf. What shall we wager?”

“I’ll bet one of cousin Will’s twenty-pound notes,” Harry announced, pulling out a document. “Or—my brown mare. I’ll back her red against your notes—though I prefer ready money.”

“I have my horse,” Will bellowed. “I’ll bet it against you for fifty!”

Harry accepted both bets. Within ten minutes, the horse and the bay mare had changed hands. Will swore louder than ever. The chaplain flung his wig to the floor and roared curses with the vigor of a seasoned veteran.

Yet Harry remained composed. He had played because they asked. He had known he would win. And he did—not because he sought victory, but because his soul was already conquered.

*O beloved slumbering angel,* he thought, gazing out his window toward the casement opposite—her room—where a faint light still burned. *Am I not sure of triumph when you are kind to me?*

He forgot the noise, the complaints, the losers’ rage. Under the flickering starlight, behind that quiet casement, lay his joy, his heart, his treasure.
