The sea roared against the base of the cliff, driven by a brisk wind, while the sun poured a dappled, life-giving light across the landscape. Trent, having slept poorly, descended before eight to a secluded pool among the rocks. Following directions he had received, he plunged into the clear water, swimming out between massive grey boulders into the churning surf. He fought briefly against the coastal current before returning to his rocky refuge, battered but revitalized. Ten minutes later, he was scaling the cliff once more. His mind, momentarily cleared of the heavy disgust he felt for the case, began to organize his plans for the day.

It was the day of the inquest, the day after his arrival. After his encounter with the American on the road to Bishopsbridge, he had made little progress. In the afternoon, accompanied by Mr. Cupples, he had walked into town to purchase chemicals, confer privately with a photographer, send a reply-paid telegram, and make inquiries at the telephone exchange. He had spoken little of the case to Cupples, who seemed incurious, revealing nothing of his investigation's results or his intended next steps. After returning from Bishopsbridge, Trent had drafted a lengthy dispatch for the *Record*, sending it via the newspaper's local representative. He had dined with Cupples and spent the remainder of the evening in meditative solitude on the veranda.

As he climbed the cliff that morning, Trent reflected that he had never undertaken a case he disliked so little, yet one that consumed him so completely. The more he contemplated it in the golden sunlight, the more malevolent and challenging it appeared. Hours of sleepless contemplation had filled his mind with suspicion and near-knowledge. Even washed in the fierce purity of the sea, the glorious light only sharpened his vision of the darkness of the guilt he believed existed, repelling him bitterly from the motive he suspected. Yet, his zeal had awakened; the hunt quickened. He would slacken for nothing. By day's end, he hoped, his net would be complete. He had work to do in the morning and awaited the answer to the telegram he had dispatched the previous day with vivid expectancy, though little serious hope.

The path back to the hotel wound along the cliff's edge. Near a spot he had marked from the sea level, where the face had long since crumbled away, he approached the precipice. He looked down, hoping to witness the delicate dance of water washing over broken rock. But there was no rock below. Instead, a broad ledge stood out—a rough platform the size of a large room, thick with wiry grass and walled in steeply on three sides. Close to the sheer drop, a woman sat with her arms around her drawn-up knees, her eyes fixed on the trailing smoke of a distant liner, her face lost in a dream.

To Trent, whose training demanded he live through his eyes, she was the most beautiful picture he had ever seen. Her face possessed a southern pallor, touched by the wind's kiss with color on her cheeks, presenting a profile of delicate regularity devoid of hardness. Yet, her black brows bent downward toward a point of near-meeting, giving her a look of severity in repose, strangely redeemed by the open curve of her mouth. Trent mused that the absurdity of a lover writing sonnets to a mistress's eyebrow depended entirely on the quality of that eyebrow. Her nose was straight and fine, exquisitely avoiding the perdition of excessive length. Her hat lay pinned to the grass beside her, while the lively breeze played with her thick dark hair, blowing backward the two broad bandeaux that should have covered her forehead and agitating a hundred tiny curls from the mass gathered at her nape. Everything about this lady was black—from her suede shoes to the discarded hat; lustreless black covered her to her bare throat. All she wore was fine and impeccably arranged. Dreamy and delicate as her looks suggested, it was plain she was long-practiced in the oldest of arts: dressing well, possessing a primal joy in the excellence of a body admirably curved in her attitude of embraced knees. With a suggestion of French taste in her attire, she made a very modern figure seated there, until one looked at her face and saw the glow and triumph of all vigorous beings facing sun, wind, and sea together in the prime of the year. One saw a womanhood so unmixed and vigorous, so unconsciously sure of itself, as scarcely to be English, still less American.

Trent, who had halted only for a moment in surprise at seeing the woman in black, passed by on the cliff above her, perceiving and feeling the details set down. At all times, his keen vision and active brain took in and tasted details with a swiftness marvelous to men of slower chemistry; he held that the need to stare was evidence of blindness. Now, the feeling of beauty was awakened and exultant, doubling the power of his senses. In these instants, a picture was printed on his memory that would never fade.

As he went by unheard on the turf, the woman, still alone with her thoughts, suddenly moved. She unclasped her long hands from her knees, stretching her limbs and body with feline grace, then slowly raised her head and extended her arms with open, curving fingers, as if to gather to her all the glory and overwhelming sanity of the morning. This was a gesture not to be mistaken: a gesture of freedom, the movement of a soul's resolution to be, to possess, to go forward, perhaps to enjoy.

So he saw her for an instant as he passed, and he did not turn. He knew suddenly who the woman must be, and it was as if a curtain of gloom were drawn between him and the splendour of the day.

At breakfast, Mr. Cupples found Trent little inclined to talk, excusing himself on the plea of a restless night. Cupples, conversely, was in a state of bird-like alertness. The prospect of the inquest seemed to enliven him. He entertained Trent with a disquisition on the history of the coroner's court, remarking on its enviable freedom from the shackles of rule and precedent. From this, he passed to the case awaiting the tribunal that morning.

"Young Bunner mentioned to me last night," he said, "the hypothesis he puts forward regarding the crime. A very remarkable young man, Trent. His meaning is occasionally obscure, but in my opinion, he possesses a clear-headed knowledge of the world quite unusual for one of his apparent age. Indeed, his promotion by Manderson to the position of his principal lieutenant speaks for itself. He seems to have assumed with perfect confidence the control, as he expresses it, of the complicated business situation caused by the death of his principal. He has advised very wisely regarding the steps I should take on Mabel's behalf and the best course for her to pursue until the will is executed. I was accordingly less disposed than I might otherwise have been to regard his suggestion of an industrial vendetta as far-fetched. When I questioned him, he was able to describe numerous cases in which attacks of one sort or another—too often successful—had been made upon the lives of persons who had incurred the hostility of powerful labor organizations. This is a terrible time in which we live, my dear boy. There is none recorded in history, I think, in which the disproportion between the material and the moral constituents of society has been so great or so menacing to the permanence of the fabric. But nowhere, in my judgment, is the prospect so dark as it is in the United States."

"I thought," said Trent listlessly, "that Puritanism was about as strong there as the money-getting craze."

"Your remark," answered Mr. Cupples, with as near an approach to humor as was possible for him, "is not in the nature of a testimonial to what you call Puritanism—a convenient rather than an accurate term; for I need not remind you that it was invented to describe an Anglican party which aimed at purging the services and ritual of their Church from certain elements repugnant to them. The sense of your observation, however, is none the less sound, and its truth is extremely well illustrated by the case of Manderson himself, who had, I believe, the virtues of purity, abstinence, and self-restraint in their strongest form. No, Trent, there are other and more worthy things among the moral constituents of which I spoke; and in our finite nature, the more we preoccupy ourselves with the bewildering complexity of external apparatus which science places in our hands, the less vigor have we left for the development of the holier purposes of humanity within us. Agricultural machinery has abolished the festival of the Harvest Home. Mechanical travel has abolished the inn, or all that was best in it. I need not multiply instances. The view I am expressing to you," pursued Mr. Cupples, placidly buttering a piece of toast, "is regarded as fundamentally erroneous by many of those who think generally as I do about the deeper concerns of life, but I am nevertheless firmly persuaded of its truth."

"It needs epigrammatic expression," said Trent, rising from the table. "If only it could be crystallized into some handy formula, like 'No Popery', or 'Tax the Foreigner', you would find multitudes to go to the stake for it. But you were planning to go to White Gables before the inquest, I think. You ought to be off if you are to get back to the court in time. I have something to attend to there myself, so we might walk up together. I will just go and get my camera."

"By all means," Mr. Cupples answered, and they set off at once in the ever-growing warmth of the morning. The roof of White Gables, a surly patch of dull red against the dark trees, seemed to harmonize with Trent's mood; he felt heavy, sinister, and troubled. If a blow must fall that might strike down that creature radiant of beauty and life whom he had seen that morning, he did not wish it to come from his hand. An exaggerated chivalry had lived in Trent since the first teachings of his mother; but at this moment, the horror of bruising anything so lovely was almost as much the artist's revulsion as the gentleman's. On the other hand, was the hunt to end in nothing? The quality of the affair was such that the thought of forbearance was an agony. There never was such a case; and he alone, he was confident, held the truth of it under his hand. At least, he determined, that day should show whether what he believed was a delusion. He would trample his compunction underfoot until he was quite sure there was any call for it. That same morning he would know.

As they entered the gate of the drive, they saw Marlowe and the American standing in talk before the front door. In the shadow of the porch was the lady in black.

She saw them and came gravely forward over the lawn, moving as Trent had known she would move, erect and balanced, stepping lightly. When she welcomed him on Mr. Cupples's presentation, her eyes of golden-flecked brown observed him kindly. In her pale composure, worn as the mask of distress, there was no trace of the emotion that had seemed a halo about her head on the ledge of the cliff. She spoke the appropriate commonplace in a low and even voice. After a few words to Mr. Cupples, she turned her eyes on Trent again.

"I hope you will succeed," she said earnestly. "Do you think you will succeed?"

He made his mind up as the words left her lips. He said, "I believe I shall do so, Mrs. Manderson. When I have the case sufficiently complete, I shall ask you to let me see you and tell you about it. It may be necessary to consult you before the facts are published."

She looked puzzled, and distress showed for an instant in her eyes. "If it is necessary, of course you shall do so," she said.

On the brink of his next speech, Trent hesitated. He remembered that the lady had not wished to repeat the story already given to the inspector—or to be questioned at all. He was not unconscious that he desired to hear her voice and watch her face a little longer, if it might be; but the matter he had to mention really troubled his mind. It was a queer thing that fitted nowhere into the pattern within whose corners he had by this time brought the other queer things in the case. It was very possible that she could explain it away in a breath; it was unlikely that anyone else could. He summoned his resolution.

"You have been so kind," he said, "in allowing me access to the house and every opportunity of studying the case, that I am going to ask leave to put a question or two to yourself—nothing that you would rather not answer, I think. May I?"

She glanced at him wearily. "It would be stupid of me to refuse. Ask your questions, Mr. Trent."

"It's only this," said Trent hurriedly. "We know that your husband lately drew an unusually large sum of ready money from his London bankers and was keeping it here. It is here now, in fact. Have you any idea why he should have done that?"

She opened her eyes in astonishment. "I cannot imagine," she said. "I did not know he had done so. I am very much surprised to hear it."

"Why is it surprising?"

"I thought my husband had very little money in the house. On Sunday night, just before he went out in the motor, he came into the drawing-room where I was sitting. He seemed to be irritated about something and asked me at once if I had any notes or gold I could let him have until next day. I was surprised at that, because he was never without money; he made it a rule to carry a hundred pounds or so about him always in a note-case. I unlocked my escritoire and gave him all I had by me. It was nearly thirty pounds."

"And he did not tell you why he wanted it?"

"No. He put it in his pocket, and then said that Mr. Marlowe had persuaded him to go for a run in the motor by moonlight and he thought it might help him to sleep. He had been sleeping badly, as perhaps you know. Then he went off with Mr. Marlowe. I thought it odd he should need money on Sunday night, but I soon forgot about it. I never remembered it again
