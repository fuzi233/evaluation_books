The storm rolled in over the valley, a bruising mix of olive and silver clouds that pressed down upon the end of the world. Father Brown, wrapped in a grey Scotch plaid, stood before Glengyle Castle. It was a structure of steep, seagreen slate roofs, rising like the sinister hats of witches from fairy tales, surrounded by pine woods that swayed like black flocks of ravens. There was a dreamy, sleepy devilry about the place, a heavy cloud of pride and mysterious sorrow that seemed to cling to Scottish nobility more than anywhere else. Scotland, Brown mused, suffered a double dose of heredity: the aristocrat's obsession with blood and the Calvinist's sense of doom.

He had snatched this day from his duties in Glasgow to join Flambeau, the amateur detective, and Inspector Craven from Scotland Yard. They were investigating the disappearance of the late Earl of Glengyle, the last of a race known for valour, insanity, and violent cunning. The countryside rhymed their fate: *As green sap to the simmer trees / Is red gold to the Ogilvies.* For centuries, the castle had produced no decent lords; now, the latest Earl had simply vanished. He was listed in the Peerage and the church register, yet no one saw him under the sun.

The only witness was a solitary man-servant named Israel Gow. A gaunt, red-haired labourer with a dogged jaw and blank blue eyes, Gow was deaf enough to be considered dumb and half-witted enough to be ignored. Yet, his energy digging potatoes and his regular disappearances into the kitchen suggested he was feeding a hidden master. When the provost and minister were summoned, they found the gardener, groom, and cook acting as undertakers, having nailed the "noble master" into a coffin. The body lay in the churchyard, waiting for the official inquiry that Flambeau had arrived to conduct.

As Brown passed through the dim garden, the air grew damp and thundery. Against the last stripe of sunset, he saw a black silhouette: a man in a chimney-pot hat carrying a spade. It looked like a sexton, but Brown recognized the economy of the Scotch peasant; even in mourning, one did not waste an hour of digging. The man started suspiciously, his vigilance matching the priest's own wariness.

Inside, Flambeau greeted them alongside Inspector Craven. The entrance hall was stripped bare, save for the pale, sneering faces of the wicked Ogilvies looking down from black periwigs. In an inner room, the three men sat around a long oak table cluttered with scribbled notes, whisky, and cigars. Between them lay a bizarre collection of objects: a heap of glittering broken glass, a mound of brown dust, and a plain stick of wood.

"You seem to have a sort of geological museum here," Brown remarked, gesturing to the debris.

"Not a geological museum," Flambeau replied dryly. "Say a psychological museum."

"Don't let's begin with such long words," Craven laughed.

"Don't you know what psychology means?" Flambeau asked with friendly surprise. "It means being off your chump."

"Well," Flambeau decided, "we've only found out one thing about Lord Glengyle. He was a maniac."

The silhouette of Gow passed the window, dimly outlined against the darkening sky. Brown stared passively. "I can understand there must have been something odd about the man, or he wouldn't have buried himself alive—or been in such a hurry to bury himself dead. But what makes you think it was lunacy?"

"Listen to the list of things Mr. Craven has found," Flambeau said.

"We must get a candle," Craven suddenly announced. "A storm is getting up, and it's too dark to read."

"Have you found any candles among your oddities?" Brown smiled.

Flambeau raised a grave face. "That is curious, too. Twenty-five candles, and not a trace of a candlestick."

As the wind rose, Brown moved to the table and bent over the heap of red-brown dust. A sharp sneeze cracked the silence. "Hullo!" he said. "Snuff!"

He took a candle, lit it carefully, and stuck it into the neck of a whisky bottle. The unrestful night air waved the long flame like a banner, while outside, the black pine woods seethed like a black sea around a rock.

"I will read the inventory," Craven began gravely. "The place was generally dismantled and neglected, but one or two rooms had plainly been inhabited in a simple style by someone who was not the servant Gow. First item: a considerable hoard of precious stones, nearly all diamonds, loose and unset. Second: heaps of loose snuff, lying on mantelpieces and pianos, as if the owner wouldn't take the trouble to look in a pocket. Third: curious little heaps of minute pieces of metal, like steel springs or microscopic wheels, as if he had gutted a mechanical toy. Fourth: the wax candles, which have to be stuck in bottle necks because there are no candlesticks."

Craven paused. "Now I wish you to note how very much queerer all this is than anything we anticipated. We are prepared for a murder, or a disappearance, or a trick. But suppose the worst: the servant killed the master, or the master isn't dead, or the servant is dressed up as the master. Invent a Wilkie Collins tragedy, and you still cannot explain a candle without a candlestick, or why an elderly gentleman of good family should habitually spill snuff on the piano. The core of the tale we could imagine; it is the fringes that are mysterious. By no stretch of fancy can the human mind connect together snuff and diamonds and wax and loose clockwork."

"I think I see the connection," the priest said. "This Glengyle was mad against the French Revolution. He was an enthusiast for the *ancien régime*, trying to re-enact the family life of the last Bourbons. He had snuff because it was the eighteenth-century luxury; wax candles for the eighteenth-century lighting; the mechanical bits represent the locksmith hobby of Louis XVI; the diamonds are for the Diamond Necklace of Marie Antoinette."

Both men stared at him with round eyes. "What a perfectly extraordinary notion!" cried Flambeau. "Do you really think that is the truth?"

"I am perfectly sure it isn't," answered Father Brown. "Only you said that nobody could connect snuff and diamonds and clockwork and candles. I give you that connection off-hand. The real truth lies deeper."

He paused to listen to the wailing wind. "The late Earl of Glengyle was a thief. He lived a second and darker life as a desperate housebreaker. He did not have any candlesticks because he only used these candles cut short in the little lantern he carried. The snuff he employed as the fiercest French criminals have used pepper: to fling it suddenly in dense masses in the face of a captor. But the final proof is in the curious coincidence of the diamonds and the small steel wheels. Surely that makes everything plain? Diamonds and small steel wheels are the only two instruments with which you can cut out a pane of glass."

A broken pine branch lashed against the windowpane, but they did not turn. Their eyes were fastened on Brown.

"Diamonds and small wheels," repeated Craven ruminating. "Is that all that makes you think it the true explanation?"

"I don't think it the true explanation," replied the priest placidly. "But you said that nobody could connect the four things. The true tale is something much more humdrum. Glengyle had found, or thought he had found, precious stones on his estate. Someone bamboozled him with those loose brilliants, saying they were found in the castle caverns. The little wheels are some diamond-cutting affair. He had to do the thing very roughly, with the help of a few shepherds. Snuff is the one great luxury of such Scotch shepherds; it's the one thing with which you can bribe them. They didn't have candlesticks because they didn't want them; they held the candles in their hands when they explored the caves."

"Is that all?" asked Flambeau after a long pause. "Have we got to the dull truth at last?"

"Oh, no," said Brown.

As the wind died with a long hoot of mockery, Brown, with an utterly impassive face, continued: "I only suggested that because you said one could not plausibly connect snuff with clockwork or candles with bright stones. Ten false philosophies will fit the universe; ten false theories will fit Glengyle Castle. But we want the real explanation. Are there no other exhibits?"

Craven laughed, and Flambeau rose to stroll down the table. "Items five, six, seven... A curious collection, not of lead pencils, but of the lead out of lead pencils. A senseless stick of bamboo, with the top rather splintered. It might be the instrument of the crime. Only, there isn't any crime. The only other things are a few old missals and little Catholic pictures, which the Ogilvies kept from the Middle Ages—their family pride being stronger than their Puritanism. We only put them in the museum because they seem curiously cut about and defaced."

The heady tempest drove a dreadful wrack of clouds across the room as Brown picked up the little illuminated pages. He spoke before the drift of darkness had passed, his voice that of a man ten years younger.

"Mr. Craven, you have a legal warrant, haven't you, to go up and examine that grave? The sooner we do it the better. If I were you, I should start now."

"Now?" repeated the astonished detective. "And why now?"

"Because this is serious," answered Brown. "This is not spilt snuff or loose pebbles. There is only one reason I know of for this being done; and the reason goes down to the roots of the world. These religious pictures are not just dirtied or torn. In every place where the great ornamented name of God comes in the old illuminations, it has been elaborately taken out. The only other thing removed is the halo round the head of the Child Jesus. Therefore, let us get our warrant and our spade and our hatchet, and go up and break open that coffin."

"What do you mean?" demanded the London officer.

"I mean," answered the little priest, his voice rising slightly in the roar of the gale, "that the great devil of the universe may be sitting on the top tower of this castle at this moment, as big as a hundred elephants, and roaring like the Apocalypse. There is black magic somewhere at the bottom of this."

"Black magic," repeated Flambeau in a low voice. "But what can these other things mean?"

"Oh, something damnable, I suppose," replied Brown impatiently. "Perhaps you can make a torture out of snuff and bamboo. Perhaps lunatics lust after wax and steel filings. Perhaps there is a maddening drug made of lead pencils! Our shortest cut to the mystery is up the hill to the grave."

They obeyed him like automata. Craven found a hatchet in his hand and the warrant in his pocket; Flambeau carried the heavy spade of the strange gardener; Father Brown carried the little gilt book from which the name of God had been torn.

The path up the hill was crooked but short, though the wind made it laborious. Far as the eye could see, the pines sloped one way under the wind, a gesture as vain as if the wind were whistling about an unpeopled planet. Through the infinite growth of grey-blue forests sang an ancient sorrow, the cry of lost pagan gods who would never find their way back to heaven.

"You see," said Father Brown in a low but easy tone, "Scotch people before Scotland existed were a curious lot. In fact, they're a curious lot still. But in prehistoric times I fancy they really worshipped demons. That is why they jumped at the Puritan theology."

"My friend," said Flambeau, turning in a kind of fury, "what does all that snuff mean?"

"My friend," replied Brown, with equal seriousness, "there is one mark of all genuine religions: materialism. Now, devil-worship is a perfectly genuine religion."

They reached the grassy scalp of the hill, a bald spot clear of the crashing forest. A mean enclosure of timber and wire rattled in the tempest. At the foot of the grave grew great tall thistles, grey and silver in their decay. Once or twice, when a ball of thistledown broke under the breeze and flew past, Craven jumped as if it had been an arrow.

Flambeau drove the blade of his spade through the whistling grass into the wet clay. Then he stopped, leaning on it as on a staff.

"Go on," said the priest very gently. "We are only trying to find the truth. What are you afraid of?"

"I am afraid of finding it," said Flambeau.

The London detective spoke suddenly in a high, crowing voice meant to be cheery. "I wonder why he really did hide himself like that. Something nasty, I suppose; was he a leper?"

"Something worse than that," said Flambeau.

"And what do you imagine," asked the other, "would be worse than a leper?"

"I don't imagine it," said Flambeau.

He dug for some dreadful minutes in silence, and then said in a choked voice, "I'm afraid of his not being the right shape."

"Nor was that piece of paper, you know," said Father Brown quietly, "and we survived even that piece of paper."

Flambeau dug on with blind energy. The tempest had shouldered away the choking grey clouds, revealing grey fields of faint starlight before he cleared the shape of a rude timber coffin. Craven stepped forward with his axe; a thistle-top touched him, and he flinched. Then he took a firmer stride, hacking and wrenching until the lid was torn off, and all that was there lay glimmering in the grey starlight.
