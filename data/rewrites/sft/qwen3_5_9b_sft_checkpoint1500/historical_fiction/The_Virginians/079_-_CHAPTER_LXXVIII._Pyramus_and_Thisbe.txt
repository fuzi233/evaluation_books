Years later, sorting through the dusty archives of my home, I stumbled upon a packet labeled with Mother's precise, familiar script: "From London, April, 1760. My son's dreadful letter." When it finally became mine, I burned it without hesitation. I could not allow a chronicle of such domestic ruin and estrangement to linger in our family history, perhaps to serve as a grim warning to future generations of wayward sons. With equal resolve, I have since consigned to the flames the reply she sent me during this season of tyranny, rebellion, and irritation.

Blinded by the agony of separation from my mistress, and rightly identifying Mrs. Esmond as the architect of the deepest misery I had ever known, I penned a letter home to Virginia. Though I could have written with greater moderation, I strove to maintain an outward facade of extreme respect and silence. I spoke of not understanding her motives, yet I laid the burden of my future wretchedness squarely at her feet, accusing her of deliberately ruining my life. I detailed how she had severed me from a virtuous, innocent youth, casting down her hopes, health, and joy forever. I feigned ignorance of her actions, claiming they were matters for God alone to judge, yet I could not hide the truth: the wound she had inflicted was mortal. I told her that the bond of allegiance I owed her was irrevocably broken; I could never again be the dutiful, respectful son I had been.

Madam Esmond's reply arrived in a vessel of profound dignity. Her penmanship remained as elegant as ever, yet her tone was chilling. She offered no reproaches, no harsh words. Instead, she calmly referred the case between us to that awful Tribunal of God, asking for counsel. She stated humbly that regarding her conduct as a mother, she stood ready to face judgment. Then she turned the question back upon me: Could I, as a son, answer for myself? Could I demonstrate, when the Great Judge demanded it, that I had fulfilled my duties and honored my parents?

*O popoi!* My grandfather, in his memoirs, often cited a line of Homer illustrating how, in trouble and grief, men invariably summon the gods. When pride, avarice, ambition, and the lust to dominate stir within us, do we not endlessly beset Heaven to rule in our favor? Consider our great American quarrel: did not both sides appeal to the skies regarding the justice of their causes, sing *Te Deum* for victory, and confidently declare that righteousness would prevail? Was America right merely because she triumphed? If so, surely Poland was wrong because she fell?—Yet, how am I drifting into this tangent about Poland and America, while my thoughts return to a little woman, now gone, who appealed to Heaven and confronted it with a thousand scriptures, simply because her son desired a marriage contrary to her wishes?

We plead, we curse, we kneel, we beg for blessings, we scream for legal sentences; meanwhile, the vast current of the world rolls on. We pant, strive, struggle; we hate, rage, weep passionate tears; we reconcile, race, and win—or lose. We pass away, and other little strugglers rise in our wake. Our days are spent, our nights arrive, and another morning breaks, shining on us no more.

I recall sending a duplicate of my letter to Madam Esmond, announcing my revolt and disobedience (perhaps I was secretly proud of the composition). I showed it to Mr. Lambert, wishing him to understand the state of my relations with my mother and my determination to view my separation from Theo as nothing short of a forced exile. I promised her, *in secula seculorum*, that whenever I could see her again, I would. I implied that Theo was bound to me just as firmly. Her poor father understood this truth. He might have separated us, yes—he might even have administered poison, and the gentle, obedient creature would have swallowed it and died—but the guilt would have been his alone. Let him answer for that. Yet, he was tender-hearted, weak in resolve, and unable to subject either of his children, especially this one, to torture. We had tried to part; we could not. He sought to divide us, but it was beyond his power. The barriers were raised, yet the maid within and the knight without continued to love each other. The wall stood firm, but Pyramus and Thisbe whispered across it. Amidst his grief and confusion, Uncle Lambert possessed a keen sense of humor, recognizing the sorry nature of his role. Light began to pierce the rough lime-plaster of the wall; the lovers were working their hands through, then their heads—it was high time for the wall to retreat.

I cannot recall the stages of this transition day by day, nor does it greatly concern the instruction of future ages. When my descendants encounter their own love-scrapes, they will find their own remedies. I believe I ceased visiting Dean Street, as the open-air exercise was deemed most salutary for Miss Lambert. I acquired a fine horse and rode beside her carriage. The elderly woman at Tottenham Court soon recognized us both, nodding and winking with friendly familiarity as we passed. I suspect the old goody was not unaccustomed to lending the hospitality of her roadside cottage to young couples.

The physician and the country air wrought a prodigious cure upon Miss Lambert. Hetty invariably served as duenna, listening with exemplary patience to the endless amatory conversations that occurred, sometimes even during holidays, when Master Charley rode my horse once I had entered the carriage. Oh, the quantity of love-making Hetty heard! When we traveled in a closed carriage, she would kindly glance out the window. How many letters were exchanged! How much running to and fro! Gumbo's bandy legs were perpetually on the trot between my quarters and Dean Street, while Mrs. Molly, the girl's maid, constantly returned answers to Bloomsbury. By the time autumn leaves turned pale, Miss Theo's roses were in full bloom again, and Doctor Heberden pronounced his cure complete.

During this blessed interval, Mr. Warrington completed his grand tragedy, *Pocahontas*. This time, it was not only accepted by Mr. Garrick, following favorable remarks from his friend Dr. Johnson, but my friend and cousin, Hagan, was engaged by the manager to play the hero, Captain Smith. Hagan's engagement was secured only when needed. I had assisted him and his family with means disproportionate to my resources, particularly given my feud with Madam Esmond. Her reply to my angry missive of April arrived towards autumn, sent from Virginia with war for war, countermand for countermand. However, these menaces frightened me little; my poor mother's thunder could not reach me. My conscience, or perhaps my casuistry, supplied alternative interpretations for her biblical texts, rendering her oracles weightless in scaring me from my purpose. I told no one of my new love, nor any member of my maternal or paternal families, who had bitterly opposed the union. Why wrangle with them? It was better to seize the day (*carpere diem*) with its sweet loves and pleasures, leaving the railers to grumble and the elders to advise at their leisure.

Besides Madam Esmond, I must admit, in the frantic rage of my temporary separation, I sent notes of wondrous sarcasm to Uncle Warrington, Aunt Madame de Bernstein, and Lord or Lady of Castlewood (I forget which individual specifically). I thanked them for the trouble they had taken in preventing the dearest happiness of my life, promising a corresponding gratitude from my obliged relative. Business brought the jovial Baronet and his family to London somewhat earlier than usual, and Madame de Bernstein was never sorry to return to Clarges Street and her cards. I saw them. They found me perfectly well. Concluding the match was broken off, I chose not to undeceive them. The Baroness took heart at my cheerfulness, making many sly jokes about my philosophy and prudent behavior as a man of the world. As ever, she was bent upon securing a rich match for me, and I fear I paid many compliments at her house to a wealthy young soap-boiler's daughter from Mile End, whom the worthy Baroness wished to place in my arms.

"You court her with infinite wit and *esprit*, my dear," my pleased kinswoman observed, "but she does not understand half you say, and the other half, I think, frightens her. This *ton de persiflage* is very well in our society, but you must be sparing of it, my dear nephew, amongst these roturiers."

Miss Badge married a young gentleman of royal dignity, though his fortunes were shattered, from a neighboring island; and I trust Mrs. Mackshane has ere this pardoned my levity. There was another person at my aunt's house who did not understand my irony much better than Miss Badge herself; she was a lady who had seen the reign of James the Second, and who lived and breathed worldly wisdom in King George's time. I loved to be with her, though, lest my little readers access this volume, I shall omit a hundred stories of the great old folks she had known—the great old days of George the First and his ladies, of St. John and Marlborough, of the reigning Majesty and the late Prince of Wales, and the causes of their quarrel—but my modest muse pipes for boys and virgins. So, my dear wife, you need not shake your powdered locks at me! Papa is not going to scandalize his nursery with old-world gossip, nor bring a blush over our chaste bread-and-butter.
