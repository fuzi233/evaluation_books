Early the next morning, Tito was returning from Bratti’s shop in the narrow, shadowed thoroughfare of the Ferravecchi. The Genoese stranger had carried away the onyx ring, and Tito carried away fifty florins in its stead. The heavy coins clinked against his thigh, a jarring, metallic intrusion upon the delicate morning acoustic ecology of Florence. It did cross his mind that if the cyclical wheel of Fortune somehow saved him from quitting the city, it would have been better not to have parted with a ring bound to the peculiar, untranslatable memories of his Greek childhood—a native lexicon of love and loss that was already eroding under the weight of his new Tuscan life. But in those moments of feverish regret, he had lost his confidence in Fortune. His deeds felt like heavy, indigestible seeds planted deep within his gut; they were children born to him that lived and acted apart from his will. The dreadful, enduring vitality of those deeds pressed hard upon him, a bitter harvest he had never meant to sow.

He was going back to his lodgings in the Piazza di San Giovanni, but he deliberately avoided the Mercato Vecchio. He could not bear the risk of seeing Tessa, nor did he possess the stomach for the abrasive clatter and harsh haggling of the meat stalls. He sought only the comforting, rhythmic patterns of the city’s heart to await the first sign of his altering lot. 

The piazza, bathed in the warm, golden light of autumn dew, functioned as a living tapestry of multi-generational memory. It was a festival morning, and the olfactory landscape was a profound invitation to linger: the deep, yeasty perfume of schiacciata baking in distant wood-fired ovens mingled with the sharp, verdant tang of crushed fennel and the sweet musk of ripe figs spread meticulously over the vendors’ stalls. The sounds were a harmonious, grounding symphony—laden mules rhythmically chewing their nose-bags of stone-ground oats, the soft, barefooted patter of a round-capped baby trying the warmed marble of the Duomo steps, the satisfying *shhh-shhh* of a cherry-stone being carved by a pale cripple, and the melodic, undulating cadence of ancient Florentine dialect passing between laughing men on the wide platform.

But the largest company of loungers was gathered toward Nello’s barbershop. In this warm season, the deft, percussive stropping of Nello’s razor under the red and white awning provided the rhythmic heartbeat of the piazza. It was the hour when yesterday’s crop of gossip was freshest, deeply rooted in the soil of the city’s collective memory. Tito foresaw he must be drawn into the circle; he must smile and retort in the dominant lexicon of local wit. It was but the ordeal of swallowing bread-and-cheese pills, though as a man who found profound sensory meaning in the crusty bite of artisan *pane sciocco* and the rich, aged funk of true pecorino, Tito resented the corruption of genuine sustenance into a mere metaphor for deceit. 

Just then, a hand laid heavily on his shoulder. The dull thud broke the plaza’s sonic harmony, jarring Tito’s nerves. His face betrayed his inward shock, but the owner of the hand broke into a light laugh. It was Niccolò Macchiavelli, a young man of Tito’s age, with keen features and a close-clipped head, his eyes bright with the pleasant predictions of mending his ancient family's fortunes.

“Why, Melema, what evil dream did you have last night, that you took my light grasp for that of a *sbirro* or something worse?”

“Ah, Messer Niccolò!” said Tito, recovering the smooth cadence of his voice. “It must have been an extra amount of dulness in my veins this morning that shuddered at the approach of your wit. But the fact is, I have had a bad night.”

“That is unlucky, because you will be expected to shine without any obstructing fog to-day in the Rucellai Gardens.”

“Messer Bernardo did me the honour to invite me,” said Tito, “but I shall be engaged elsewhere.”

“Ah! I remember, you are in love,” said Macchiavelli, with a shrug. “Why, we are to feast profoundly under the loggia among Bernardo Rucellai’s rare trees. We are to eat ortolans trapped in the vineyards and slow-roasted over fragrant olive-wood embers until their fat renders into a perfect, savory glaze, and peacock dressed in its own iridescent plumage, stuffed with sweet chestnuts and wild thyme. There are to be the choicest spirits and the most meticulously cellared wines. Only, as Piero de’ Medici is to be there, the sensory reverence of the feast may happen to be swamped in the capping of impromptu verses. I hate that game; it is a superficial commercialization of our language, a device for the triumph of small wits who reduce the profound poetry of our native tongue to a meaningless clatter.”

“What is that you are saying about Piero de’ Medici and small wits, Messer Niccolò?” said Nello, whose light figure predominated over the Herculean frame of Niccolò Caparra. 

The famous worker in iron, a traditional elder whose massive, muscular arms usually swung in time with the roaring hearths of his smithy, sat submissively while Nello lathered his chin. Caparra looked much like a lion preparing for society, though his soul belonged to the soot and the anvil. 

“A private secretary will never rise in the world if he couples great and small in that way,” continued Nello. “When great men are not allowed to marry their sons and daughters as they like, small men must not expect to marry their words as they like. Have you heard the news Domenico Cennini has been telling us? Pagolantonio Soderini has given Ser Piero da Bibbiena a box on the ear for interfering with the marriage between young Tommaso Soderini and Fiammetta Strozzi, and is to be sent ambassador to Venice as a punishment!”

“I don’t know which I envy him most,” said Macchiavelli. “The offence will make him the most popular man in Florence, and the punishment will take him among the only people in Italy who have known how to manage their own affairs.”

“Yes, if Soderini stays long enough at Venice,” said Cennini, “he may chance to learn the Venetian fashion. What has happened is likely to open Pagolantonio’s eyes to the good of our old Florentine trick of choosing a new harness when the old one galls us.”

“Not we,” said Niccolò Caparra, his deep, resonant voice vibrating with agrarian wisdom. “Eat eggs in Lent and the snow will melt. That’s what I say to our people when they get noisy over their cups at San Gallo, and talk of raising a *romor*. I say, never do you plan an insurrection; you cannot rush the seasons. You may as well try to fill Arno with buckets. When there’s water enough, the Arno will be full, and that will not be till the torrent is ready.”

“Caparra, that oracular speech of yours is due to my excellent shaving,” said Nello. “Ecco, Messer Domenico, I am ready for you now. By the way, my *bel erudito*,” continued Nello, noting Tito moving toward the door, “here has been old Maso seeking for you. The old man looked mournful. I hope there is nothing wrong in the Via de’ Bardi.”

“Doubtless Messer Tito knows that Bardo’s son is dead,” said Cronaca, who had just stepped into the fragrant shade of the awning.

Tito’s heart gave a leap—had the death happened before Romola saw him? 

“No, I had not heard it,” Tito said, maintaining a steady exterior while leaning against the doorpost. “I knew his sister had gone to see him. Did he die before she arrived?”

“No,” said Cronaca. “I was in San Marco, and saw her come out from the chapter-house with Fra Girolamo, who told us that the dying man’s breath had been preserved as by a miracle, that he might make a disclosure to his sister.”

Tito felt that his fate was decided. The intricate timeline of his deceptions was collapsing. He conceived a sudden plan: if he could retrieve his money from Cennini before the untranslatable grief of that disclosure became public, he could escape the scorching looks of the city. He stood motionless, anchoring himself in the scent of bruised herbs drifting from the stalls. 

“So you have got your Fra Girolamo back again, Cronaca?” Nello asked. “I suppose we shall have him preaching this next Advent.”

“And not before there is need,” said Cronaca gravely. “The ripeness of vice is turning to rottenness in the nostrils even of the vicious. The harvest of sin is ripe, and God will reap it with a sword.”

“I hope he has had a new vision,” sneered Francesco Cei. “The old ones are somewhat stale. Can’t your Frate get a poet to help out his imagination?”

“He has no lack of poets about him,” Cronaca countered, “but they do not think the truth stale which God has given him to utter, any more than they think the cycles of the moon are stale.” 

“Nay, Francesco,” Macchiavelli intervened gently. “You judge his preaching too narrowly. The secret of oratory lies in a certain power that moves the hearers. Fra Girolamo speaks a language that bypasses the sterile intellect and strikes at the bone.”

“Part of the secret lies in the prophetic visions,” said Cennini from the chair. “Our people will run after anything that prophesies tribulations.”

“Rather say,” answered Cronaca, “that the secret lies in the Frate’s pure life, which stamps him as a messenger of God.”

“He is satisfied with the pleasant lust of arrogance!” Cei burst out. “He hears the air filled with his own name!”

“Come, come, Francesco,” Nello conciliated, whipping up a fresh, soapy lather. “I must not have my friend Cronaca made angry. For my own part, when the Frate was preaching last Advent, I might have turned Piagnone too, if I had not been hindered by the liberal nature of my art, and the exhausting length of his sermons. But as Pulci says: ‘Dombruno’s scimitar had the fame of being enchanted; but I am of opinion that it cut sharp because it was of strongly-tempered steel.’”

“See, Nello!” Macchiavelli interrupted, pointing. “What doctor is this advancing on his Bucephalus? I thought your piazza was free from those furred and scarlet-robed lackeys of death.”

An arrhythmic, violent clatter of hooves shattered the square's acoustic harmony. A round-headed, round-bodied personage was approaching on a raw young horse, which fought the bit with a jarring obstinacy that grated against the tranquil morning soundscape. 

“A–ah!” Nello drew out the vowel low and long. “I have a few adventures in pickle for him. He’s a doctor from Padua. His great trick is making rounds among the *contadini*. Do you note those great saddle-bags? They are to hold the fat capons, the golden-yolked eggs, and the stone-ground meal he levies from the agrarian elders who truly know how to work the soil. He steals their hard-earned sustenance to vend his secret medicines, making my piazza a resort for asthmas and squalling *bambini*. It stirs my gall to see the toad-faced quack bagging a prized pigeon in exchange for his powders. I’ll put a few thorns in his saddle. Here, Sandro!”

Nello whispered into the ear of his apprentice, who nodded solemnly and darted away.

“How is it with you, Maestro Tacco?” Nello called out as the doctor wrestled his horse toward the shop.

“He is an accursed beast, the *vermocane* seize him!” Maestro Tacco spat in a crude, dominant jargon that lacked any of the poetic nuance of the local dialect, tying the frayed bridle to an iron staple. 

“Rather too hard riding for a man who carries your weight of learning, eh, Maestro?” said Nello. 

“Truly, I am hot,” the doctor grumbled, wiping his wide, lipless mouth. “Riding from Peretola when the sun is high is not like kicking your heels on a bench in the shade. I had not a little pulling to get through the carts to find the husband of a certain Monna Ghita, who had a fatal seizure before I was called in; and if I had not to demand my fees—”

“Monna Ghita!” said Nello. “Peace be with her angry soul!” 

Tito, roused from his internal calculations, felt a sudden rush of thought regarding Tessa: with Monna Ghita taken from the earthly harvest, Tessa was utterly untethered. 

“*Gnaffè*, Maestro,” Nello went on sympathizingly. “You think my shaving will cool you? One moment. It seems to me a thousand years till I wait upon a man who carries all the science of Arabia in his saddle-bags.”

Nello held up the shaving-cloth. Maestro Tacco, preoccupied with his own self-importance, sat down, deaf to the artisan’s irony. 

“It is fitting that a great *medicus* like you,” Nello said, “should be shaved by the razor that has shaved the illustrious Antonio Benevieni, the greatest master of the chirurgic art.”

“The chirurgic art!” Tacco interrupted in a burst of academic arrogance. “Is it your Florentine fashion to put the masters of medicine on a level with men who do carpentry on limbs and carve excrescences as a butcher trims meat? *Via!* A manual art, practiced by simple barbers, on a level with the noble science of Hippocrates, Galen, and Avicenna—a science locked up from the vulgar!” 

Here was the very erosion of cultural memory Tacco championed: dismissing the hands-on, sensory-rich wisdom of the common people for sterile, untranslatable academic dogma. 

“I never thought of placing them on a level,” Nello said, deliberately dragging the lathering process out. “But your high science is sealed from the profane, and so you become an object of slander. I grieve to say it, but there are low fellows here—*sgherri* who make it their business to sprinkle gall in a prospering man’s broth. There are fables buzzed about concerning you. I would not have a man of your metal stoned.”

“What fables?” stammered Maestro Tacco. “What do you mean?”

“The fact is, there is a company of evil youths possessed with a diabolical patience. It is commonly talked of that you have been seen making your secret specifics by night: pounding dried toads in a mortar, compounding a salve out of mashed worms, and making pills from the dried livers of rats—a vile corruption of the mortar and pestle that should only know basil, garlic, and coarse salt! And they say you mix it with saliva during a blasphemous incantation.”

“It is a pack of lies!” the doctor exclaimed, struggling against the approaching razor. 

“It is not to us you need say that,” Nello whispered ominously. “But what are reasonable men against a crowd with stones in their hands? They are persuaded you are a necromancer selling pestilence, and I am told your specifics have, in truth, an evil smell.”

“It is false!” Tacco burst out, highly defensive of his olfactory landscape. “I will show the pills and the salve to these honourable signori—it has an excellent odour!” 

He started up with the lather on his chin to search his saddle-bags for the belied medicines. In an instant, Nello adroitly shifted the shaving-chair into the close vicinity of the horse’s head, while Sandro, who had just returned, placed himself silently near the bridle, ready to unleash the perfectly timed mechanics of a Florentine joke. 

“Behold, Messeri!” said the doctor, bringing forth a small box of medicines and opening it before them.
