George Warrington managed his legal studies with a singular disregard for the rigors of the profession, ensuring they never impinged upon his comfort, his pleasures, or, most critically, his precious health. Madam Esmond, in her correspondence, had repeatedly admonished him that while he donned the student's gown and sat among nameless commoners in the hall, he possessed a name of ancient lineage to uphold. She insisted he study as a gentleman, not as a mere professional drudge. The young man complied with sufficient obedience to avoid the rank and file of the law, positioning himself instead as a volunteer in her service, akin to the young gentlemen of the era who treated the law as a secondary pursuit. Though the law a century prior was a jealous mistress demanding exclusive attention—allowing her disciples far more leisure, punch, and coffee-house sociability than she permits today—George treated her with decent respect. Yet, his heart belonged elsewhere; the black-letter of Chaucer held infinitely more charm for him than the Gothic pages of Hale and Coke.

In those quaint times, letters were revered, and authors held sway as authorities. Gentlemen appealed to Virgil or Lucan in Courts and Parliament. What did Statius or Juvenal decree on such matters? Their reign has long since passed; the worship of Jupiter and Juno is no more fashionable than the cultivation of Pagan poetry. The age of economists and calculators has superseded the ancients; Tooke's Pantheon is deserted and ridiculed. Occasionally, a Stanley may kill a kid, a Gladstone a wreath, or a Lytton burn incense in honor of the Olympians, but who cares at Lambeth or Birmingham for ancient rites? Who are the Muses, and what use is Greek rubbish? What is Elicon, and who seeks Thalia? As for Melpomene, does her name bear three or four syllables? And from whose design did I steal the figure of Tragedy that adorns the initial G of this chapter?

It is recorded that in his youth, during the long leisure of his home and his imprisonment in the French fort on the Monongahela, George courted Melpomene. The union produced a tragedy, omitted from Bell's Theatre, though I suspect it rivals the worst pieces printed there. Young men often pay their respects to the Tragic Muse first, falling in love with women older than themselves. Let the candid reader admit that, possessing a literary turn, his ambition was of the highest order. In his riper age, he might have settled for translating Horace or versifying Waller, but his green, unknowing youth engaged only in tragedy and epic, deeming no prize but the highest worthy of him.

Upon arriving in London, George attended performances at both houses, frequented theatrical coffee-houses, and absorbed the opinions of critics. He might be seen at the Bedford between plays or supping at the Cecil with wits and actors. Here, he acquainted himself with the players and poets. The tough old Macklin, the frolicsome Foote, the vivacious Hippisley, and the sprightly Garrick himself occasionally graced these circles. Through his wit, modesty, and evident wealth, George became beloved in coffee-house society. Actors drank punch with him; critics supped at his expense with affability. To hob-nob with Bobadil or Henry the Fifth, to accept a pinch from Aristarchus's box, or to escort Juliet and Monimia, were privileges delighting to the poetic young man. George loved the theatre, finding satisfaction in the fact that his mother only half-approved of such forbidden fruit. He hosted elegant entertainments for the players, and it was rumored that distinguished geniuses had condescended to borrow money from him.

As he polished his masterpiece, seeking advice from friends, he received applause and counsel. Mr. Spencer, a new acquaintance of the Temple, hosted a breakfast in Fig Tree Court where George read portions of his play. The gentlemen pronounced it of uncommon merit. Even the learned Mr. Johnson, invited to the gathering, conceded the piece showed talent. It warred against the unities, to be sure, but these had been violated by others; Mr. Warrington might sacrifice them as well. The tragedy contained elements reminiscent of *Coriolanus* and *Othello*. "Two very good things, sir!" the author pleaded. "Well, well," Mr. Spencer replied, "your catastrophe is terrible, just, and being partly true, is not the less awful."

The plot was replete with battle and murder. A favorite book of George's grandfather had been the life of George Frundsberg of Mindelheim, a colonel at Pavia and during the wars of the Constable Bourbon. One of Frundsberg's companions, Carpzow, became the tragedy's hero. The first act, as it stood in Sir George Warrington's manuscript, took place before a convent on the Rhine besieged by Lutherans under Carpezan. These godless men had pulled friars' beards and torn nuns' veils. Inside, a score of religious women trembled; the garrison promised surrender unless succours arrived by midday. An armistice prevailed as soldiers gambled on the grass. Twelve o'clock struck. No succours came. The warder opened the gates to the famous Protestant hero, the terror of Turks and Papists—Colonel Carpezan. Clad in steel, hammer over shoulder, flags displayed, trumpets blowing, he entered. "No rudeness, my men," Carpezan declared. "The wine is yours, the larder good, the church plate to be melted. Any garrison member joining Gaspar Carpezan is welcome. No insult to the religious ladies! I promised them safe-conduct; he who touches them hangs." The Provost Marshal, a huge fellow in a red doublet, nodded.

"We shall see more of that Provost Marshal," Mr. Spencer explained to his guests. "A very agreeable acquaintance," Mr. Johnson added, wagging his head over his tea. "This scene of mercenaries and wild sports is novel and stirring. The Colonel enters the convent?"

The Abbess and her oldest ladies appeared before the conqueror. Despite his title, they heard him in their sacred halls, recalling his violent behavior in other establishments. His hammer had smashed sacred images; his melting of plate was legendary. The Abbess-Princess of St. Mary's, a lady of violent prejudices and noble birth, disliked the lowborn heretic lordling her convent. She challenged Carpezan, a scene the audience at the Temple greatly enjoyed. Though terrible in war, Carpezan was initially shaken by the Abbess's brisk verbal charge, seeming conquered by his prisoner. Yet, an old soldier was not to be beaten ultimately by a woman. "Madam," he asked, "how many ladies are there for whom my people shall provide conveyance?" The Abbess, troubled and angry, counted twenty-three. "Ha! Why this hesitation?" asked Captain Ulric, one of Carpezan's gayest officers.

The dark chief pulled a letter from his pocket. "I require the body of the noble lady Sybilla of Hoya," he said sternly. "Her brother was my favorite captain, slain by my side in the Milanese. By his death, she becomes heiress. It is said a greedy uncle immured her here. She shall pronounce her fate: remain a cloistered sister or return to liberty." The Abbess, haughty, denied any Lady Sybil existed, claiming Sister Agnes had been professed and her wealth reverted to the Order. "Give me straightway the body of Lady Sybilla!" roared Carpezan. "If not, I signal my Reiters and give you and your convent up to war."

"Faith, if I lead the storm," said Captain Ulric, "I'd choose some plump, smiling, red-lipped maid rather than your Lady Abbess." As he looked under the veils of two attendant nuns, the stern Abbess cried, "Silence, fellow! The lady you seek is passed away from sin three days since; our Sister Agnes died." The chaplain confirmed the burial. Too much! In his coat of mail, Carpezan found a letter from Sister Agnes announcing she was to be buried in an oubliette, to starve or live on water and bread. Seizing the Abbess by the arm, while Ulric choked the chaplain, the Colonel blew his horn. Furious Lanzknechts crashed the convent walls. Amidst flames and screams, Sybilla herself was brought in, fainting on Carpezan's shoulder, having been guided to the dungeon by the very nun with the red lips.

"The convent is suppressed with a vengeance," Mr. Warrington noted. "We end the first act with the burning, the roars of triumph, and the nuns' outcries. They must change dresses immediately; they will be court ladies in the next act." The gentlemen cast the roles: Mrs. Pritchard as the Abbess, Miss Pritchard as Sybilla, Miss Gates as the nun, and Mr. Garrick, despite his slight height, as Carpezan. Mr. Johnson deemed Woodward excellent as Ulric. The audience already saw the play on the boards.

In the second act, Carpezan married Sybilla. Ennobled by the Emperor and living in state on the Danube, he was not happy. Perhaps remorse haunted him for his wild life as a condottiere; perhaps his rough manners clashed with his imperious, highborn bride. She taunted him with his low birth and vulgar companions, leading him to wish he had never rescued the quarrelsome vixen from the oubliette. This quiet act passed in quarreling until horns announced the young King of Bohemia and Hungary's approach, offering preferment: Count to Carpezan, General-in-Chief to his wife.

"Oh, stay—I have it," Mr. Johnson interjected. "'Tis in *Meteranus*, in the *Theatrum Universum*. Carpezanus or Carpzoff..." "That is the fourth act," corrected Mr. Warrington. The King's attentions toward Sybilla grew marked, but her husband, battling jealousy, refused to yield until her criminality was proven. The act closed with the terrible tragedy that actually happened: convinced of her guilt, Carpezan ordered the executioner to slay her in her own palace. The curtain fell in a side-chamber illuminated by moonlight through an oriel window, where the King played a song—the signal between him and his guilty victim. Mr. Johnson praised this song as a happy imitation of Waller, noting its gay repetition deepened the tragic gloom.

"But whatever came afterwards?" Johnson asked. "I recall Carpezan being taken into favor by Count Mansfield." Here, the poet departed from history. In the fifth act, King Louis, terror-stricken by the sanguinary end of his intrigue, received word of the Ottoman invasion. Two noblemen related how the injured Carpezan rushed into the royal presence, broke his sword, and threw it at the King's feet along with a glove he dared the King to wear. The rebel fled Prague, reportedly joining the Turks and assuming the turban. The King, preparing for battle at Mohacz, dismissed his generals, keeping an aged knight by his side to express repentance for his past crimes and determination to fight like a man.

"What is this field called?"
"Mohacz, my liege!" the old warrior replied. "Ere set of sun, Mohacz will see a battle bravely won."

Trumpets sounded; the cymbals and barbaric music of the Janissaries signaled the Turkish camp. There walked the Sultan's friend, the conqueror of Rhodes, the Grand Vizier. Who was the warrior in Eastern habit with a glove in his cap? It was Carpezan. Solyman knew his courage. Carpezan prayed for a command near the traitor King Louis. "Tis well," said the grim Vizier. "Our invincible Emperor surveys the battle from yonder tower."

Signal-guns fired. Christian knights and Turkish warriors clashed. Carpezan, glove in cap and hammer in hand, raged about the field, calling for King Louis. About to slay a warrior, he recognized young Ulric, his ex-captain, dropped his hammer, and bid him fly. Softened by memories of their shared Protestant conquests, Ulric urged him to return, but Carpezan knew it was impossible. They fought; Ulric fell under the hammer. The renegade melted in sight of his wounded comrade until King Louis appeared, plumes torn, sword hacked. The guilty monarch tried to turn away, but Carpezan was on him. All softness gone, he roared, "An equal fight! A traitor against a traitor! Stand, King Louis!" He tore the token from his cap and flung it at the King.

They set to, and the monarch fell under the terrible arm of the man he had injured. Dying, he uttered incoherent words of repentance. Carpezan, leaning on his mace, spoke a heartbroken soliloquy over the corpse. The Turkish warriors gathered. The dark Vizier surveyed the renegade. "Christian renegade!" he said. "Allah has given us a great victory. The Christian King is slain by you."
"Peace to his soul! He died like a good knight," gasped Ulric, dying himself.
"In this day's battle," the Vizier continued, "no man hath comported himself more bravely than you. You are made Bassa of Transylvania! Advance bowmen—Fire!"

An arrow quivered in Carpezan's breast.
"Bassa of Transylvania, you were a traitor to your King, who lies murdered by your hand!" the Vizier declared. "You contributed more than any soldier to this victory. 'Tis thus my sublime Emperor meetsly rewards you. Sound trumpets! We march for Vienna to-night!"

The curtain dropped as Carpezan crawled to his dying comrade, kissing his hands and gasping, "Forgive me, Ulric!"

When Mr. Warrington finished reading, he turned to Mr. Johnson, modestly asking, "What say you, sir? Is there any chance for me?" But the eminent critic had been asleep and frankly admitted losing the latter part of the play. The little auditory stirred.
