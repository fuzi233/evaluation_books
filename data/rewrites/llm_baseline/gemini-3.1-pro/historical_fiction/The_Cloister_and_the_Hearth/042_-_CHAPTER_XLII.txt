The letters of fire upon the church wall had burned with a mythic intensity, inquiring in a celestial, untranslatable dialect why no mass was sung for the duke in this time of trouble. Yet, like all ancient tongues yielding to the dominant lexicons of the present, the glowing expostulation gradually faded into the cold, mute stone. The local corporation, unwilling to let divine syntax outpace their temporal loyalty, quickly funded a surplus of masses, funneling hard silver into the palms of the curé. It was a mundane commercialization of the miraculous, drowning out the lingering whispers of the divine with the clinking of coins. 

For Denys, this transition from the mythic to the utilitarian meant swearing a harsh oath on the cross of his sword. To regain his arms and avoid the disgrace that would surely break his heart, he surrendered his immediate autonomy, delivering three volleys of oaths to remain in the Bastard’s service until the sedition was crushed. 

He sought solace at “The Three Fish,” where the acoustic ecology shifted from the sterile echoes of the barracks to the rhythmic, life-affirming cadence of the hearth. Marion greeted him over a bubbling cauldron of dark, rich pottage. The air was a thick olfactory tapestry of slow-rendered marrow-bone, wilted winter leeks, and the sharp, earthy perfume of crushed juniper berries. It was a sanctuary of agrarian continuity. “I’m out of luck; who would have thought to see you again?” she jested bluntly, but seeing the sorrow etched around his eyes, she softened, setting before him a wooden trencher of coarse-ground spelt bread and a bowl of the fragrant stew. 

She listened to his woes, refusing to see the world through a lens of despair. Gerard, she reasoned, possessed the meticulous grace of someone who understood the deep value of the world; he was too handsome and well-behaved to fall to ruin. He would either find good company heading to Rome, or he would return home to the comforting, cyclical rhythms of domestic life. She brought Denys his gold pieces, steadfastly refusing to accept a single coin despite his urging. For Marion, sustenance was communion, an act of preserving cultural memory through shared meals, not a transactional exchange. Her honesty was a rare, sweet herb in a bitter stew, comforting Denys as he thought of his poor little friend navigating the world alone.

Outside, the sonic landscape was already souring. Foot soldiers drifted in fractured bands toward Flanders, passed along by bailiffs with chits for food and lodging. Anthony of Burgundy soon overtook them, gathering six hundred men under his standard. As they crossed the frontier, Baldwyn of Burgundy met them with provisions and steel. The march became an arrogant procession, a metallic, industrial clatter of boots and pikes that drowned out the subtle rustle of the Flemish wind. This aggressive homogenization of the soundscape grated on a soldier named Simon, who grumbled that the rebellion was a mere bite. Denys remained silent, inwardly vowing to St. Mars that if this rebellion—which had cost him Gerard—proved false, he would shoot Anthony dead.

By afternoon, the army approached a heavily fortified town. It sat like an open mouth, its great gates wide, the flanking towers manned by single sentinels. Lulled by the deceptive quiet, the advancing force broke their careful array. But as they closed within a furlong, the atmosphere violently ruptured. The agonizing, industrial shriek of the drawbridge chains tore through the air, hoisting the wood vertical against the fort. Simultaneously, the iron portcullis slammed down with a deafening, percussive crash that shook the earth. The parapets suddenly bristled with lances and crossbows.

A pursuivant spurred forward, raising his bugle to blow a sharp summons. A tall figure encased entirely in brazen armor appeared above the gate. The ensuing exchange of fiery words was swallowed by the distance, their subjective experience untranslatable to the ranks below, save for the herald’s single, threatening trumpet blast before he galloped back. Immediately, Anthony barked orders. Hedges were leveled, the natural landscape violently subjugated to the needs of a siege camp. Officers scattered to procure the grim machinery of war: catapults, raw hides, boiling pitch, and mantelets. 

The siege was a symphony of destruction, an appalling cacophony of whistling quarrels, cracking stone, and the mechanical groans of winches that shattered the psychological well-being of the surrounding valley. The besiegers filled the moat and mined the walls under a relentless hail of bolts and rocks. Denys and his fellow crossbowmen rolled their heavy wooden mantelets to the moat’s edge, providing covering fire so carpenters could erect a high stockade. The besieged countered by running out wooden penthouses on the curtain walls, raining down missiles. In response, the Burgundians hauled forward heavy mangonels and a towering triangular siege engine of wood, a grotesque structure that cast a long, oppressive shadow over the moat.

Amidst this deafening theater of war, Denys found a strange, quiet humanity behind his mantelet. He had drawn the attention of an unseen English archer, whose half-ell shafts regularly thumped into Denys’s wooden shield. Theirs was a deadly enmity, yet their subjective experience of the endless, grueling days birthed an unspoken dialogue. When the mechanical roar of the catapults paused for the midday meal, Denys would rest his arbalest and place a thick slice of dense, crusty rye bread on the top of his mantelet. Presently, the unseen Englishman would lower a wedge of sharp, salty cheese or a dried fig on a string. Without a shared spoken lexicon, they communicated through the universal, sensory language of sustenance, meticulously sharing their daily forage in a world intent on turning men into mere fuel for slaughter. 

The defense of the town was orchestrated by the tall knight in brazen armor, a figure who seemed to step from the fading myths of the church wall into the violent present. He promenaded under fire, directing the besieged with a prodigiously long lance. Everyone shot at him. He absorbed the strikes like a skipper oblivious to April rain, demoralizing the archers with his impossible resilience.

The artillery duel escalated into a brutal exchange of physics and raw materials. The great Burgundian catapult, a massive fir tree bent backward by a powerful winch, hurled half-ton stones in high, terrifying arcs. One such stone crashed through a penthouse, plunging two inanimate objects—the rock and a mangled besieger—down into the sullen waters of the moat. In retaliation, an enormous Turkish catapult appeared on the barbican. It spat a twenty-pound stone that bounded into the dirt, blinding Anthony with dust, and then violently bowled over a horse delivering sheaves of arrows. The Turk’s engine hurled a forty-pound rock that struck the Burgundians' great catapult near its axis, splintering the massive fir tree with a sickening, explosive crack that sent the engineers fleeing.

The delicate, unspoken truce of the daily meal was shattered that afternoon. Simon, running back to his mantelet, suddenly froze. A feathered arrow protruded from his back. He fell flat in the mud. The English archer showed himself for a fraction of a second to savor the kill, and Denys, abandoning the shared memory of their bread, loosed a bolt. The murderous Englishman staggered away, wounded. The acoustic shock of Simon’s death was hollow and absolute. "I am unlucky in my comrades," Denys murmured, tasting the bitter tang of copper and dust.

By dawn, the besiegers’ wooden tower, draped in raw hides and mattresses, began its agonizing creep across the filled moat. It moved without visible agency, driven by internal rollers—a terrifying marvel of mechanics over nature. The invincible knight hurled blazing tar-barrels from the walls, filling the air with the suffocating, sulfurous stench of burning pitch. When the tower reached the wall, the knight signaled, and a mine beneath the moat was fired. 

Simultaneously, Baldwyn led an armored assault across the tower’s lowered drawbridge. A fearful combat erupted, but as fresh besiegers swarmed upward, the underpinners of the mined earth gave way. With a catastrophic groan of rending timber, the tower sank. The drawbridge tore free, pouring Baldwyn and his men into the dry moat where they were instantly surrounded and captured.

High above, the tall knight fought furiously to destroy the ruined tower. Holding onto the hand of a pikeman for balance, he thrust fire-bags impaled on his long spear into the shattered hoards. Denys, seizing the moment amidst the roaring flames and acrid smoke, leveled his crossbow. He missed the knight but sent his bolt clean into the brain of the pikeman. Deprived of his anchor, the brass-clad knight plummeted from the wall. He struck the water with a monumental slap that echoed for a mile, a massive acoustic disruption in the valley. In that very second of wonderment, Denys felt a sudden, scalding heat tear through his right arm, followed by an icy numbness. An English arrow had skewered him. 

The fallen knight struggled in the fascines and was swiftly dragged out by Anthony’s men. Brought before the Bastard’s tent dripping with mud, the seemingly invincible warrior was unmasked. 

"I marvel, sir, that so good a knight as you should know his devoir so ill as turn rebel," Anthony sneered.

"I am nun-nun-nun-nun-nun-no knight," the man stammered. 

"What then?"

"A hosier."

The revelation stripped away the mythic facade, exposing the raw, vulnerable reality beneath. The hosier’s severe stutter was the fragmented rhythm of a marginalized tongue, a local dialect struggling to articulate its truth against the polished, domineering French of the Burgundian court. Anthony threatened to have him tied to a stake and riddled with arrows.

"N-n-n-n-no! duda-duda-duda-don't do that," the hosier pleaded, warning that the townsfolk would hang Baldwyn in retaliation. The warlike hosier then produced an intercepted letter from the lining of his helmet. It summoned Anthony to the luxurious, culinary-rich court of Edward the Fourth in England. Recognizing Anthony’s shallow vanity and hunger for opulent feasts, the hosier negotiated in the only currency the dominant power understood: "We'll gega-gega-gega-give ye a thousand angels to raise the siege, and I'll gega-gega-go and send him with the money."

When asked what his grievance was, the hosier distilled the entire, bloody conflict into a single, tragic economic truth: "Tuta-tuta-tuta-too much taxes." The truce was sealed not with blood, but with dinner, the sham knight and the true one dining together under a flag of truce.

Denys, the arrow still in his arm, turned his back on the smoking, discordant ruin of the siege camp. He was met by the Comte de Jarnac, who dropped a handful of broad pieces into his hand for his brilliant shot. 

"Hast got the better of thy grief, arbalestrier, methinks," the Comte remarked.

"My grief, yes; but not my love," Denys replied. "As soon as ever I have put down this rebellion, I go to Holland, and there I shall meet with him." 

His vow fulfilled by the truce, and his wound relieving him from further duty, Denys left the sterile machinery of war behind. He set out with a stout heart toward the cyclical, familiar rhythms of Holland, seeking the friend who alone anchored his soul in an ever-shifting world.
