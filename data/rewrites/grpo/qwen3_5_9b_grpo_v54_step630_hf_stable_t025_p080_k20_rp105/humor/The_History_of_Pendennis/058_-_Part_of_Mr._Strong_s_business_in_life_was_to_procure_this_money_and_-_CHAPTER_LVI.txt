Helen Pendennis watched her son with a mother's sharp, silent vigilance. Though she never spoke a word of the young woman, Fanny, and though she pretended to ignore her existence entirely, her eyes tracked every movement of Master Arthur. Under the guise of his fragile health, she kept him under her gaze, determined to spare him the trouble of correspondence for the present. Arthur, sitting at the family table, felt the weight of her scrutiny even when her eyes were fixed on her teacup. He waited for a letter that never came. Each day, the silence grew heavier. Was he annoyed that Fanny had parted from him without a single tear, without a "Help, Arthur?"? Or was he relieved? Self-love and pride are stubborn things; we rarely accept sudden bankruptcy gracefully. We pawn our jewels to keep the show going a little longer. But eventually, the shutters must go up. As for Arthur Pendennis, the reader will soon decide whether he belongs to the class of men who sink or those who swim.

While Arthur was too weak to walk, Helen could not stop Mr. Warrington from going to London. Indeed, she suspected his errand before he left: he was there to inquire about Fanny. Arthur had confessed his feelings to Warrington with a gravity that worried the older man. "It is hard to struggle, Arthur, and it is easy to fall," Warrington warned. "The best courage is to fly from danger." When Arthur pressed him for the story of Warrington's own ruined fortunes, the older man deflected with a warning: "Take the moral without the fable."

Meanwhile, in the chaotic world of London's lower classes, Mr. Huxter was making a name for himself. He was a man of mimicry, a disciple of Saint Bartholomew's who thrived on the Back Kitchen club. There, he delighted in imitating an Irish officer of distinction, Captain Costigan. Huxter would draw the General out with brandy-and-water, coaxing stories of his daughter's ingratitude and his own military exploits until the old man was a blur of laughter and tears. The club members loved the General's eccentricities, and the landlord tolerated his foibles because the General brought in the young swells who drank his liquor.

Costigan, however, was a man of secrets. He lived in a state of perpetual flux, moving from garret to garret, often avoiding questions about his lodgings. Legends sprang up that he slept in watch-boxes or under the Duke of York's Column. But on one brilliant September morning, the legend ended in reality. Huxter, fresh from Vauxhall and nursing a few coins, spotted the General reeling down Henrietta Street, pursued by a gang of blackguard boys. The old man, glazed-eyed and drunk, stumbled blindly toward home.

Just as he reached the gate of Shepherd's Inn, a piece of orange peel sent him tumbling backward. Huxter rushed to his aid. Despite the General's refusal to admit his location, the young surgeon insisted on accompanying him. They reached the ancient gate of the inn, where the porter, Mr. Bolton, scowled at the intrusion. Inside, Huxter helped the disoriented Captain to his room, applying compresses to a torn knee and shin. The old warrior lay ill for days, plagued by fever and pain, while Huxter visited him with cheerful chatter and medical skill.

It was during these days of convalescence that Huxter became intimately acquainted with the household of Shepherd's Inn. He bought toys for the children, brought apples, and once frightened them with a mask, causing a rare smile on the face of the pale, wan Fanny Bolton. Her head was bowed, her face ghostly, her heart heavy with the sorrow of the last three weeks. Huxter's kindness was a balm, and he soon learned from Mrs. Bolton the full extent of Arthur's "misconduct" and Fanny's suffering.

Huxter wrote to his brother-in-law, Clavering, reporting Arthur's narrow escape from fever and his current occupation with a compound fracture, but he omitted any mention of Fanny. However, his true feelings were reserved for the musician, Mr. Bows. In a heated conversation, Huxter labeled Arthur a "conceited blackguard" and vowed to punch his head. But Bows, the old fiddler, had a different story. He recounted how Arthur had acted with manliness, how Mrs. Pendennis had been an "aristocratic" brute in turning Fanny out, and how Fanny herself was the one who lacked a heart, now seeking solace in the arms of a new doctor from Saint Bartholomew's.

When Warrington arrived at Shepherd's Inn to deliver Arthur's thanks to Bows, he found the musician in a foul mood. Bows, irritable and bitter, confirmed what Huxter had hinted: Fanny was indeed consoling herself with a new suitor, a "sawbones" who grinned through a horse-collar for her sisters. Warrington, witnessing this scene from the doorway, saw Fanny laughing languidly at Huxter's farces. A bitter thought crossed his mind: "Are all women like that?"

Later, at Piccadilly, Warrington encountered Major Pendennis. The Major, a man of philosophy, agreed with Warrington's observation that "all women are the same," citing the myth of Calypso. He was delighted to hear that Fanny had found a new lover and predicted that Arthur would be furious. "We must break it to him gently," the Major advised, promising to handle the delicate matter with his wife.

Back at the inn, Warrington finally delivered the news to Arthur. Using the metaphor of Ariadne abandoned on Naxos, he revealed the truth. Arthur started up, his face turning red, shouting, "It's a lie!" But Warrington gently pushed him back into his seat. "Sit down, stupid," he said. "It's better for you as it is." The young man, who had hoped for a different ending, was forced to confront the reality of his situation.
