Waiting. The sun-drenched days stretched on, offering neither the reunion Romola craved nor the confirmation of her deepest fears. Baldassarre remained silent; despite the government's intensified surveillance, no evidence surfaced regarding the suspected conspiracy. Yet, the season delivered other calamities that pierced the veil of her anxiety, forcing her into the crucible of immediate action. The Black Death spread across Florence, and Savonarola faced formal excommunication. These twin disasters severed her drifting detachment from the Friar, anchoring her once again to the man who had ignited her vocation. She could not navigate the labyrinth of plague-ridden misery without acknowledging the debt she owed to Fra Girolamo for the moral fortitude to endure it. Nor could she stand idly by as a man distinguished solely by his fierce commitment to Christian reality was silenced, without feeling the magnetic pull toward his cause.

In mid-June, the bull of excommunication, delayed for weeks from Rome, was read aloud within the Duomo. Romola attended, seeking to bolster her resolve through the spectacle of resistance. The scene stood in stark, chilling contrast to the vibrant mornings she had known. Gone were the crowds of citizens, their faces upturned in hopeful anticipation, forming a living amphitheater around the aging friar. Gone was the thunderous voice that once animated the masses with visions of tangible and spiritual grandeur. Instead, the vast nave echoed with the somber flicker of taper-light against bare stone walls. A black-and-grey congregation of monks and secular priests sat with bowed heads, their expressions expectant yet subdued. Only the intermittent chiming of small bells broke the monotony of a voice reciting a sentence already suspended in church tradition. As the candles were extinguished, the heavy, shuffling retreat of monastic footsteps faded into a dim silence.

Romola's fervor for the Frar was doubled by the cruel irony she observed: the gleeful triumph etched onto the coarse features of his enemies, juxtaposed against the terrified confusion in the eyes of his staunchest allies. The boundary between the duty of obedience and the right of resistance was inherently precarious, but the stakes were elevated to cosmic proportions by the conviction that the Church was not a mere political compromise, but a living organism imbued with divine power to bless or curse. For most devout Florentines, this belief was an immutable truth, as undeniable as the curvature of the sky. Consequently, Savonarola's logical rebuttals—that the excommunication was unjust and therefore void—only served to heighten their terror, appearing as a reckless challenge to a mystical force against which humanity possessed no defense.

Romola, however, viewed the institution of the Church exclusively through the lens of Savonarola. Having grown up outside the traditional communal fabric of her father's secluded household, she recognized no authority above his moral force. To her, the excommunication represented not theological nuance, but the naked aggression of vice. On one side stood a man dedicated to public virtue and spiritual purity; on the other, the predatory coalition of alarmist selfishness led by Rodrigo Borgia, now crowned as the infamous Pope Alexander VI. While neutral observers might perceive the finer shades of truth softening the edges of such binary oppositions, Romola required a clarity that neutrality could not provide. This excommunication stripped away the petty local antagonisms that plagued Savonarola in the heat of the pulpit, elevating his struggle to a universal appeal against the corruption of ecclesiastical power. He became a standard-bearer leaping into the breach, and in witnessing this generous self-sacrifice, Romola found her doubts dissolving. She felt the exhilaration of certainty, convinced of the soul's highest prize and her own capacity to achieve it.

This renewed enthusiasm carried her through the arduous summer months. She held back from Tito any mention of her clandestine meeting with Baldassarre or the disturbing revelations he had imparted. Such confessions would yield nothing but cold sarcasm regarding her sympathy for his alleged assassin. Baldassarre appeared paralyzed; the greater threat was not his retaliation against Tito, but the possibility that Tito, driven by paranoia, would devise a new scheme to eliminate the man who haunted his conscience. Romola knew she could take no decisive action until she saw Baldassarre again and uncovered the full truth concerning the "other wife"—determining if she was the woman to whom Tito was originally bound. The implications of this mystery, striking at the core of her inherited pride, poisoned her lingering memories of her husband. Her absolute faith in Baldassarre's testimony bred a visceral revulsion toward Tito, a horror so profound it might have provoked a desperate confrontation had he not been conspicuously absent from the city. Like many affluent citizens fleeing the plague, Tito spent his leisure in the countryside, where the villa of Melema awaited. Since Romola had refused to abandon the city, he had no need to establish a separate rural retreat.

Finally, in late July, the crushing weight of public crisis began to lift. The plague receded, and Savonarola's fortunes improved as a sympathetic magisterate penned urgent appeals to Rome for the annulment of the excommunication. Yet, Romola's robust physique succumbed to the inevitable lethargy following prolonged exertion. Her restless mind, however, demanded constant engagement, compelling her to walk the cooling streets of Florence every morning and evening, altering her route with the faint hope of intercepting Baldassarre. Perhaps a sudden illness had induced a paralysis of memory, causing him to forget her address—or her very existence. This was her most optimistic hypothesis for his silence. The probability she clung to with trembling certainty was far darker: that he had succumbed to the plague.
