The weeks following Sally's reunion with Ginger Kemp unfolded with a deceptive, golden serenity. Her small kingdom existed in a state of polished equilibrium, a world so meticulously ironed that even the most cynical observer struggled to locate a fault line. Peace reigned on every frontier.

Gerald remained distant, a thousand miles away in Chicago, summoned by a frantic theater manager to salvage a brother's failing comedy for a production titled "The Primrose Way." His absence was a disappointment, yet Sally refused to let it mar the perfection of her immediate reality. Life, in its entirety, was satisfactory. Fillmore's enterprise thrived; Ginger's conscience was clear; a new apartment awaited; her hat was impeccable; and the play was a sensation. Chicago, according to Fillmore's reports, had narrowed its entire consciousness to the logistics of securing tickets. National anxieties evaporated; local grievances cooled. The populace was riveted solely on the mechanics of entry, treating the staging of the play as a cataclysmic event surpassing even the Great Fire.

Among these satisfactions, the resolution of Ginger's future stood paramount. He had secured employment with Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.—a title Fillmore deemed unnecessarily verbose for a brass plaque—and was stationed in the outer office. His duties were vague, oscillating between the driver of destiny and a glorified errand boy, mostly involving the prevention of mobs from disturbing Fillmore, who pondered grand schemes in semi-regal isolation behind a closed door. Though Ginger occasionally questioned the nature of his role in exchange for fifty dollars a week, his gratitude toward Sally was absolute. He sought to thank her constantly, especially as they collaborated on furnishing her new flat. He worked with frenetic energy, claiming the labor kept him in shape.

"If I didn't sweat a bit and help you after the way you got me that job..." Ginger paused, hauling a massive armchair toward the third spot Sally had designated in the last ten minutes.

"Ginger, desist," Sally commanded.

"Yes, but honestly..."

"If you don't stop, I'll make you move that chair into the next room."

"Shall I?" Ginger rubbed his blistered palms and adjusted his grip. "Anything you say."

"Silly! Of course not. The only other rooms are my bedroom, the bathroom, and the kitchen. What on earth would I want a great lumbering chair in them for? All the same, I believe the first we chose was the best."

"Back she goes, then, what?"

Sally frowned, reflecting on the mental tax of setting up house.

"No," she decided. "By the window is better." She looked at him with feigned remorse. "I'm giving you a lot of trouble."

"Trouble!" Ginger, accompanied by the chair, staggered across the room. "The way I look at it is this." He wiped a bead of perspiration from his freckled forehead. "You got me that job, and..."

"Stop!"

"Right ho... Still, you did, you know."

Sally sank into the armchair, stretching her limbs. Observing Ginger's exertion induced a vicarious fatigue. She surveyed the room with pride. Pictures hung, carpets lay, furniture stood in neat rows. For the first time in three years of boarding-house drift, she possessed the restful sensation of a settled abode—a place to lock the door and exist alone. The apartment was small, yet undeniably a haven. She scanned the corners for flaws... except... A sudden void opened in her perception.

"Hullo!" she exclaimed. "Where's that photograph of me? I'm sure I put it on the mantelpiece yesterday."

Ginger's exertions had flushed his face a deep crimson. He inspected the mantelpiece narrowly.

"No. No photograph here."

"I know there isn't. But it was there yesterday. Or was it? I know I meant to put it there. Perhaps I forgot. It's the most beautiful thing you ever saw. Not a bit like me; but what of that? They touch 'em up in the dark-room, you know. I value it because it looks the way I should like to look if I could."

"I've never had a beautiful photograph taken of myself," Ginger said solemnly, with gentle regret.

"Cheer up!"

"Oh, I don't mind. I only mentioned..."

"Ginger," Sally interrupted, "pardon my breaking your valuable remarks, but this chair is—not—right! It ought to be where it was at the beginning. Could you give your imitation of a pack-mule just once more? And after that I'll make you some tea. If there's any tea—or milk—or cups."

"There are cups all right. I know, because I smashed two the day before yesterday. I'll nip round the corner for some milk, shall I?"

"Yes, please nip. All this hard work has taken it out of me terribly."

At the tea table, Sally's curiosity sharpened.

"What I can't understand about this job of yours, Ginger—which as you are just about to observe, I was noble enough to secure for you—is the amount of leisure that seems to accompany it. How is it that you are able to spend your valuable time—Fillmore's valuable time, rather—juggling with my furniture every day?"

"Oh, I can usually get off."

"But oughtn't you to be at your post doing—whatever it is you do? What do you do?"

Ginger stirred his tea thoughtfully, engaging the question.

"Well, I sort of mess about, you know." He pondered. "I interview divers blighters and tell 'em your brother is out and take their names and addresses and... oh, all that sort of thing."

"Does Fillmore consult you much?"

"He lets me read some of the plays that are sent in. Awful tosh most of them. Sometimes he sends me off to a vaudeville house of an evening."

"As a treat?"

"To see some special act, you know. To report on it. In case he might want to use it for this revue of his."

"Which revue?"

"Didn't you know he was going to put on a revue? Oh, rather. A whacking big affair. Going to cut out the Follies and all that sort of thing."

"But--my goodness!" Sally recoiled. It was characteristic of Fillmore, she felt, to branch out into expensive schemes when he ought to be consolidating his precarious success. His life operated on a scale of millions where a prudent man would settle for hundreds. An inexhaustible fount of optimism bubbled eternally within him. "That's rather ambitious," she said.

"Yes. Ambitious sort of cove, your brother. Quite the Napoleon."

"I shall have to talk to him," Sally declared. She was annoyed with Fillmore. Everything had been peaceful, prosperous, and anxiety-free until he had spoiled the arrangement. Now she would have to resume worrying.

"Of course," argued Ginger, "there's money in revues. Over in London fellows make pots out of them."

Sally shook her head.

"It won't do," she said. "And I'll tell you another thing that won't do. This armchair. Of course it ought to be over by the window. You can see that yourself, can't you."

"Absolutely!" said Ginger, patiently preparing for action once more.

Sally's anxiety regarding her ebullient brother was compounded shortly thereafter by a telegram from Miss Winch in Chicago.

The message carried a sinister undertone that compelled Sally to abandon her furnishing projects immediately. She marched to the headquarters of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. Without delay.

Upon arrival, she found Ginger absent from his customary post, replaced by a pimpled youth of tender years who melted into compliance upon hearing Sally's name, ushering her inside. She entered the inner sanctum to find Fillmore with his feet propped on an untidy desk, scrutinizing what appeared to be costume designs.

"Ah, Sally!" he said in a distracted, weary voice that spoke of vast preoccupations. Prosperity was performing its silent, deadly work on the Hope of the American Theatre. What had been merely a smooth fullness around the angle of his jaw was now frankly, undisguisedly a double chin. He wore a new waistcoat, unbuttoned. "I am rather busy," he continued. "Always glad to see you, but I am rather busy. I have a hundred things to attend to."

"Well, attend to me. That'll only make a hundred and one. Fill, what's all this I hear about a revue?"

Fillmore looked as guilty as a small boy caught stealing jam. He had wondered in darker moments what Sally would say about the project and had hoped to delay the revelation until preparation rendered interference impossible. Fond though he was of Sally, he knew her cautionary nature might lead to criticism. How could a man of affairs operate if women buzzed around critiquing constantly? He picked up a pen, put it down; buttoned his waistcoat, unbuttoned it; and scratched his ear with a design.

"Oh yes, the revue!"

"It's no good saying 'Oh yes'! You know perfectly well it's a crazy idea."

"Really... these business matters... this interference..."
