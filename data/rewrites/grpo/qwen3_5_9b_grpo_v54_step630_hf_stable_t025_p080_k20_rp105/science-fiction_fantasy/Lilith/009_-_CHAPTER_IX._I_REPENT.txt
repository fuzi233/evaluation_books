I placed the manuscript down, a heavy comfort settling in my chest to know my father had glimpsed that mysterious realm and recognized Mr. Raven. Yet, the silence of his passing echoed louder than any revelation. I had never learned the cause of his death, and a terrible suspicion took root: he must have followed Mr. Raven, crossing over without return. Shame, hot and sudden, burned through my regret for fleeing. How vast the truths I had missed! What wonders lay beyond the veil of ordinary sight! Surely the Ravens were benevolent souls; a night in their sanctuary would have been harmless, perhaps even holy. They were strange, yes, but their peculiarity was faculty, whereas mine was merely beauty. I had rejected them, treating them as threats rather than guides. The more I weighed my own cowardice, the more I loathed myself. Why fear the dead? To share their sacred rest was an honor I had spurned. What harm could a sleeping king or a wounded lady inflict? I longed for the stillness of their faces, and tears streamed down my cheeks. Collapsing onto a couch, I surrendered to sleep.

I awoke abruptly, as if summoned by a voice. The house stood silent, hollow as an empty church, save for a blackbird singing on the lawn. "I must go," I told myself, "and ask forgiveness. I will do whatever they require." Rising, I climbed the stairs to the garret.

The wooden chamber awaited, unchanged since my first arrival, the mirror dimly reflecting the room. Noon approached; the sun would be higher. I needed to raise the hood and adjust the mirrors. If only I had seen Mr. Raven perform this ritual!

I pulled the chains, letting light spill onto the first mirror. Turning to the second, I saw the shapes of my previous vision—distinct yet trembling like a landscape disturbed by a gentle breeze. I touched the glass; it remained impermeable. Suspecting polarization, I shifted the mirrors, altering their angles until, by chance, the alignment clicked. Suddenly, the mountains appeared blue, steady, and clear. Stepping forward, my feet found the heather.

I knew only that the path to the cottage led through a pine forest. I waded through thickets and small fir-woods, convinced I recognized the terrain, yet no forest appeared. As the sun dipped toward the horizon and the air grew chill with the coming winter, a small black figure emerged toward me: the raven.

I hastened to meet him. "Sir, I beg your pardon for my rudeness last night," I said. "Will you take me with you now? I confess I do not deserve it."

"Ah!" he replied, looking up. After a brief pause, "My wife does not expect you tonight. She regrets that we encouraged your stay last week."

"Take me to her," I pleaded humbly, "that I may tell her my sorrow."

"It is of no use," he answered. "Your night had not come then, or you would not have left. It is not come now, and I cannot show you the way. The dead rejoiced beneath their daisies, lying among the roots of heaven's flowers, thinking of your delight when winter passes. But ere you left them, they shivered in their beds. When the spring of the universe arrives—though not for ages, and I do not care to count them—they will wake."

"Tell me one thing, Mr. Raven: is my father with you? Have you seen him since he left the world?"

"Yes; he is with us, fast asleep. That was the figure you saw with his arm on the coverlet, his hand half-closed."

"Why did you not tell me? I should have been so near him!"

"And turn your back on him!" the raven corrected.

"I would have lain down at once had I known!"

"I doubt it. Had you been ready to lie down, you would have known him. Old Sir Up'ard and your twice great-grandfather are gone long ago. Your great-grandfather has been with us for years; he will soon begin to stir. You saw him last night, though you did not know him."

"Because he is so much nearer waking than you. No one who refuses to sleep can ever wake."

"I do not understand you!"

"You turned away, and chose not to understand!" I fell silent, fearing he would depart.

"And my grandfather?" I asked.

"No; he remains in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead."

"Where is the Evil Wood? I must find him."

"You will not find it, but you will hardly miss it. It is the place where those who refuse to sleep wake at night to kill their dead and bury them."

"I cannot understand you!"

"Naturally not. Neither do I understand you; I cannot read your heart or your face. When my wife and I fail to understand our children, it is because there is not enough of them to be understood. Only God understands foolishness."

Feeling naked and worthless, I said, "Will you show me the nearest way home? I know there are many paths, for I have taken two already."

"There are indeed many ways."

"Please, tell me how to recognize the nearest."

"I cannot," the raven answered. "You and I use the same words with different meanings. We often cannot tell people what they NEED to know because they WANT to know something else, leading to misunderstanding. Home is always within the palm of your hand; telling you how to reach it is useless. But you will get there; you must get there; you have to get there. Everyone who is not at home must go home. You thought you were at home where I found you: if that had been your home, you could not have left. Nobody can leave home. And nobody ever is or will be at home without having gone there."

"Enigma treading on enigma!" I cried. "I did not come here to be asked riddles."

"No; but you came, and found the riddles waiting! Indeed, you are the only riddle. What you call riddles are truths, seeming so only because you are not true."

"Worse and worse!" I shouted.

"And you MUST answer the riddles!" he continued. "They will ask themselves until you understand yourself. The universe is a riddle trying to escape, and you are holding your door hard against it."

"Will you not, in pity, tell me what I must do?"

"How should I tell YOUR to-do, or the way to it?"

"If I am not to go home, at least direct me to my kind."

"I know of none. The beings most like you lie in that direction." He pointed his beak. I saw only the setting sun, blinding and bright.

"Well," I said bitterly, "I feel hardly treated—taken from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and denied instruction on where to go or what to do!"

"You forget," the raven said, "that when I brought you and you declined my hospitality, you reached what you call home safely. Now you have come of your own accord. Good night."

He turned and walked slowly away, his beak lowered. I stood dazed. True, I had come of my own volition, but had I not intended atonement? My heart ached, my mind void of quest or purpose, hope or desire. I gazed after him, wanting to follow, yet feeling it futile.

Suddenly, he pounced on a spot, driving his entire body weight into his bill, digging vigorously for moments. Then, with a flutter of wings, he threw back his head, and something shot from his bill, soaring high into the air. Just then, the sun set, and the air plunged into dusk. Yet, the object opened into a soft radiance, pulsing toward me like a firefly, but larger and yellower. It flew over my head. I turned and followed it.

Here I must interrupt my narrative to remark on the constant struggle to articulate the unsayable. The events recorded are, by their nature and the nature of the creatures involved, so inexpressibly alien to the economy of this world that I can present them only by translating their effects upon me into the forms and language of earthly life—not the things themselves, but the feelings they awaken. Even this translation is plagued by a continuous sense of failure. I can capture but one phase of a multitudinously complex significance, or one concentric sphere of a graduated embodiment. A single thing often seems to be and mean many things, with an uncertain identity at its heart that constantly alters its appearance. I am often driven to record what I know is but a clumsy, doubtful representation of the mere feeling aimed at, for no medium in this world is fit to convey its peculiar strangeness with even an approach to clarity. Even to one who knew the region better than myself, I offer no assurance of transmitting the reality of my experience. While without a doubt I was actually regarding a scene of activity, I might simultaneously be aware, in my consciousness, that I was perusing a metaphysical argument.
