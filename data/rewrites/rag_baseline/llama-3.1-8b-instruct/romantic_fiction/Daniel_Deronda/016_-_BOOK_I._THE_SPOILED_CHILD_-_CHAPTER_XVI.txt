In the hushed stillness of the cloistered courtyard, where sunlight struggled to penetrate the Gothic arches, Daniel Deronda lay prone on the lush grass, his curly head propped on his arms as he devoured the pages of Sismondi's _History of the Italian Republics_. The warm breeze rustled the leaves of the nearby roses, their delicate petals unfolding like tiny pink stars scattered across the verdant expanse. His tutor, Mr. Fraser, sat on a camp-stool beneath the shelter of a Gothic arcade, engrossed in the intricacies of political economy. As Daniel delved deeper into the annals of history, his mind wandered to the question that had long fascinated him: the curious case of the popes and cardinals, whose ranks seemed to swell with an endless supply of nephews.

With a sudden movement, Daniel let down his left arm and turned to face his tutor, his voice bursting forth in a pure, boyish tone. "Mr. Fraser, how was it that the popes and cardinals always had so many nephews?" The tutor, an able young Scotchman, roused himself from his studies with a hint of reluctance, his eyes flashing with a clear, emphatic light as he replied, "Their own children were called nephews." Daniel's eyes widened in inquiry, and Mr. Fraser added, with a slight inflection of impatience, "It was simply a matter of propriety; for, as you well know, priests do not marry, and the children were illegitimate."

As the truth sank in, Daniel's face contorted in a mixture of surprise and pain. He had always called Sir Hugo Mallinger his uncle, and the thought of his own parentage had been shrouded in mystery. When he had once asked about his father and mother, the baronet had replied, "You lost your father and mother when you were quite a little one; that is why I take care of you." Daniel's memories of that distant past were hazy, fragmented images of being kissed and surrounded by thin, scented drapery, until his fingers caught on something hard, causing him to cry out in pain. Every other memory he possessed was of the idyllic world he inhabited now, with Sir Hugo as his guardian, a fine man in the full bloom of life, whose estate, Diplow, stood as a testament to the family's storied past.
