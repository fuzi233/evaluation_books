I want to tell you about my old friend Bobbie Cardew. It’s a cautionary tale—one of those stories that makes you nod sagely and say, *"There but for the grace of God go I."* If you’re a man, take heed. If you’re a woman, well, you’ll probably enjoy watching a fellow human being hoist by his own petard.

Now, Bobbie wasn’t always the forgetful sort—or rather, he was, but in those days it was charming. A lovable scatterbrain, the kind of chap who’d lose his head if it weren’t fastened on. If you wanted him for dinner, you’d need to send a telegram, a letter, and a messenger in a taxi just to be sure. And even then, half the time he’d already left town.

Then he got married.

Marriage, as they say, changes a man. In Bobbie’s case, it turned him from an affable dolt into a man perpetually one misstep away from disaster. His wife, Mary, was a hospital nurse—practical, sharp, and possessed of a will that could bend steel. She adored him, mind you, but she also expected him to *remember things*. A fatal flaw in their otherwise picturesque union.

The trouble began with their first anniversary. Bobbie, in his usual fashion, forgot it entirely. Not just forgot to buy flowers—forgot the *date itself*. He’d arranged tickets for the opera, a nice dinner, the whole romantic works. But when the evening arrived, he wandered home with no recollection of the occasion until Mary’s icy stare jogged his memory.

You’d think that would’ve been lesson enough. But no. Bobbie’s memory was like a sieve with particularly large holes. Birthdays, errands, promises—all slipped through unnoticed. Mary, being both patient and terrifying, endured it. Until she didn’t.

One morning, Bobbie burst into my club like a man pursued by wolves.

*"She’s gone!"* he gasped.

*"Who?"* I asked, though I knew.

The note was brief and brutal:

> *"Dear Bobbie—I’ve gone away. When you remember my birthday, I’ll come back. Until then, you’ll find me at Box 341, London Morning News."*

Bobbie stared at me, wild-eyed. *"I don’t know her birthday."*

There followed a frantic detective saga—checking theater programs, rifling through old receipts, even consulting astrological guides (*"February women are determined and expect full return from their companions"*—which, frankly, described Mary perfectly). At last, by sheer dumb luck (and a forgotten cheque to the Ritz), we pinned it down: May 8th.

I rang Mary at her hotel.

*"He’s remembered,"* I said.

A pause. Then, softly: *"Was he very worried?"*

I grinned. *"Worried? My dear woman, he’s been in such a state that if anxiety were currency, he’d own the Bank of England."*

Silence. Then the faintest laugh. *"Tell him… tell him I’ll be home tomorrow."*

And that, dear reader, is how Bobbie Cardew learned the hard way that even the most absent-minded of men must, on occasion, *remember*.
