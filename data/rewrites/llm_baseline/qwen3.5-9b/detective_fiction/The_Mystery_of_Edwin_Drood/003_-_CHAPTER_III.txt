For reasons that this tale will reveal in due course, the ancient cathedral town requires a fictional designation. Let us call it Cloisterham. It likely bore different names for the Druids, the Romans, the Saxons, and the Normans, but over centuries, a name holds little weight against the dust of history. Cloisterham is an ancient city, ill-suited for anyone craving the noise of the modern world. It is a monotonous, silent place, imbued with an earthy flavor by its cathedral crypt and overrun with traces of monastic graves. Here, children play "salad" in the dust of abbots and abbesses, fashioning dirt pies from nuns and friars. In the surrounding fields, every plowman pays tribute to former Lords Treasurers, Archbishops, and Bishops, grinding their bones into bread just as the ogre in fairy tales ground the bones of his unwanted guests.

Cloisterham is a drowsy city, inhabited by people who strangely believe that all its changes belong to the past and that none remain for the future—a peculiar moral inherited from antiquities older than recorded history. Its streets are so silent (yet prone to echoing the slightest provocation) that on a summer day, the shop blinds barely flutter in the southern breeze. Sun-browned tramps hurrying through the town quicken their pace merely to escape the confines of its oppressive respectability. Escaping is easy, however, for Cloisterham consists of little more than one narrow artery leading in and out; the remainder are disappointing yards with pumps and no throughfares, save for the Cathedral Close and a paved Quaker settlement that resembles a Quakeress's bonnet tucked away in a shady corner.

In essence, Cloisterham is a city frozen in a bygone era. With its hoarse cathedral bell, the cawing rooks circling the tower, and the even hoarser birds nesting deep below, fragments of old walls, chapels, chapter-houses, and monasteries have been clumsily incorporated into its modern homes and gardens, much like conflicting ideas clutter the minds of its citizens. Everything here belongs to the past. Even the sole pawnbroker accepts no pledges anymore, vainly offering an unredeemed stock of dim, pale antique watches sweating in the heat, tarnished sugar tongs with useless legs, and grim volumes of forgotten books. The only signs of vibrant life in Cloisterham are the flourishing vegetable gardens; even its drooping, despairing little theater maintains a patch of soil, welcoming the devil when he flees the stage into the underworld, hiding among scarlet beans or oyster shells depending on the season.

Dominating the center of Cloisterham stands the Nuns' House, a venerable brick structure whose current name likely stems from legends of its conventual past. On the gate enclosing its courtyard shines a brilliant brass plate reading: *Seminary for Young Ladies. Miss Twinkleton.* The house front is so weathered and the plate so glaringly new that it reminds visitors of a battered old beau wearing a modern monocle in his blind eye. One wonders if the original nuns, being of a submissive rather than stubborn breed, bowed their heads to avoid hitting the low ceilings; if they sat in long, low windows counting beads for penance rather than jewelry; or if some were ever walled alive in the building's odd angles for possessing too much of the restless spirit of Mother Nature. These mysteries haunt the ghosts (if any exist) but do not appear in Miss Twinkleton's half-yearly accounts. Neither the lady running the poetry department nor her staff concerns themselves with such unprofitable inquiries.

Like those suffering from alcoholism or mesmerism, Miss Twinkleton possesses two distinct states of consciousness that never collide. By day, she is the stern seminary mistress. But every night, once the students retire, she smooths her curls, brightens her eyes, and transforms into a sprightly version unknown to the girls. In this nocturnal phase, she resumes conversations from the previous night, discussing Cloisterham's tender scandals and referencing "The Wells"—a euphemism for Tunbridge Wells—where a certain "Foolish Mr. Porters" once declared his undying love. Miss Twinkleton knows absolutely nothing of this during the day. Her constant companion in both moods is Mrs. Tisher, a deferential widow with a weak back, a chronic sigh, and a suppressed voice, tasked with managing the wardrobes and leading the girls to believe she has seen better days. Servants whisper that the late Mrs. Tisher was a hairdresser, perhaps explaining her adaptability.

The favorite pupil of the Nuns' House is Miss Rosa Bud, universally known as "Rosebud." She is wonderfully pretty, wonderfully childish, and wonderfully whimsical. An awkward, romantic interest surrounds her, for it is common knowledge that a husband has been chosen for her by will, binding her guardian to marry her upon reaching majority. During the day, Miss Twinkleton feigns sorrow over this fate, shaking her head behind Rosebud's dimpled shoulders, but to no avail. The students simply unite in declaring her a "pretending old thing." The house is never more agitated than when this destined suitor arrives. It is understood that he has a legal right to visit, and if Miss Twinkleton dared dispute it, she would be instantly removed. When his bell rings, every student peers out a window; every girl "practicing" plays out of tune; and the French class descends into chaos, marking time as frantically as bottles clinking at a party.

On the afternoon following a dinner served at the gatehouse, the bell rings with its usual effect. "Mr. Edwin Drood to see Miss Rosa," announces the head parlourmaid. Miss Twinkleton, wearing an air of exemplary melancholy, turns to the sacrifice and bids her descend. Rosebud follows all eyes into the hall.

Edwin Drood waits in Miss Twinkleton's parlour, a dainty room containing nothing overtly academic except a terrestrial and a celestial globe. These globes suggest to parents that even in private, Miss Twinkleton remains ready to scour the earth for knowledge. A new maid, having never met the fiancé, stumbles guiltily down the stairs as a charming apparition, face hidden by a silk apron draped over her head, glides into the room.

"Oh! It *is* so ridiculous!" the apparition squeaks, shrinking back. "Don't, Eddy!"
"Don't what, Rosa?"
"Don't come any nearer, please. It *is* so absurd."
"What is absurd, Rosa?"
"The whole thing! It's absurd to be an engaged orphan, absurd to have the girls and servants scurrying around like mice in the wainscoting, and absurd to be 'called upon'!" The girl keeps a thumb in the corner of her mouth while complaining.

"You give me an affectionate reception, Pussy, I must say."
"Well, I will in a minute, Eddy, but I can't just yet. How are you?" (Very briefly.)
"I'm unable to say I'm much better for seeing you, Pussy, inasmuch as I see nothing of you."
This remark draws a dark, bright, pouting eye from behind the apron, which vanishes instantly as the girl exclaims, "Oh good gracious! You've had half your hair cut off!"
"I'd have preferred my head cut off, I think," Edwin replies, ruffling the hair in question with a fierce glare at the mirror and an impatient stamp.

"Shall I go?"
"No; you needn't just yet, Eddy. The girls would all ask why you left."
"For once, Rosa, will you uncover that ridiculous little head and give me a welcome?"
The apron is pulled aside. "You're very welcome, Eddy. There! I'm sure that's nice. Shake hands. No, I can't kiss you; I've got an acidulated drop in my mouth."
"Are you at all glad to see me, Pussy?"
"Oh, yes, I'm dreadfully glad.—Go and sit down.—Miss Twinkleton."

As per custom, Miss Twinkleton appears every three minutes, either as herself or as Mrs. Tisher, to offer a token of propriety while searching for a lost item. Passing by, she says, "How do you do, Mr. Drood? Very glad indeed. Pray excuse me. Tweezers. Thank you!"
"I got the gloves last evening, Eddy, and I like them very much. They are beauties."
"Well, that's something," the fiancé replies, half-grumbling. "The smallest encouragement is thankfully received. And how did you spend your birthday, Pussy?"
"Delightfully! Everyone gave me presents. We had a feast and a ball at night."
"A feast and a ball, eh? These occasions seem to go off tolerably well without me, Pussy."
"De-lightfully!" cries Rosa spontaneously, without reserve.
"Hah! And what was the feast?"
"Tarts, oranges, jellies, and shrimps."
"Any partners at the ball?"
"We danced with one another, of course, sir. But some of the girls made game to be their brothers. It *was* so droll!"
"Did anybody make game to be—"
"To be you? Oh dear, yes!" laughs Rosa with great delight. "That was the first thing done."
"I hope she did it pretty well," says Edwin doubtfully.
