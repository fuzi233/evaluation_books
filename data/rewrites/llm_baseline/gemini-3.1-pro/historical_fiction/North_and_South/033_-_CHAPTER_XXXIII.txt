The house on Crampton Crescent settled into an unnatural, hollowed-out quiet after the noisy commotion of terror had passed. To Margaret, the acoustic landscape of their mourning felt besieged from without by the relentless, low-frequency thrum of Milton’s steam engines—an oppressive industrial vibration that rattled the teacups and seemed intent on grinding their fragile, domestic rituals into the soot-stained cobblestones. The house’s silence was not peaceful; it was a sudden, devastating vacuum left by the eradication of her mother’s gentle, southern cadence, a localized language death that left the rooms feeling profoundly empty. 

Within the kitchen, however, a different ecology prevailed, deeply rooted in the preservation of the senses. Dixon had commandeered Mary Higgins, and the elder woman’s scolding hissed in fierce, breathy whispers. Dixon was meticulously clarifying a restorative bone and root-vegetable pottage—a traditional agrarian Helstone recipe demanding an exacting, slow-simmered extraction of marrow and the precise, late incorporation of bruised thyme. To speak above a whisper would be irreversibly irreverent while her mistress lay dead in the parlor, but to let the broth boil and cloud the rendered fat was an equal insult to the life-giving properties of the sustenance. 

Margaret found a profound, sensory grounding in the aroma of caramelized onions and the earthy, humid steam of the broth. It was a tangible anchor, pulling her back from the cyclical, mythic terror of the railway station. She had resolved not to mention that crowning affright to her father. The lingering threat of Leonards securing enough coin to hunt Frederick down in London hung in the air, a phantom timeline threatening to intersect with their own, but Margaret refused to let the anxiety sour the domestic space. Frederick would be on his guard; the ingredients for his escape were set, and she would not torment herself over a brew she could no longer stir.

“I suppose we shall hear from Mr. Bell to-morrow,” Margaret said, setting down a cup of the revitalizing broth before her father. 

“Yes,” replied Mr. Hale, staring into the dark liquid as though lost in a waking dream. “I suppose so.”

“If he can come, he will be here to-morrow evening, I should think.”

“If he cannot come, I shall ask Mr. Thornton to go with me to the funeral. I cannot go alone. I should break down utterly.”

“Don’t ask Mr. Thornton, papa. Let me go with you,” Margaret said, impetuously. 

“You! My dear, women do not generally go.”

“No; because they are told they cannot control themselves. Women of our class don’t go because they are conditioned to view mourning as a sterilized, hidden thing. Poor women go, and don’t care if they are seen overwhelmed by the raw, physical reality of grief. But I promise you, papa, if you will let me go, I will be no trouble. Don’t have a stranger, whose language and life are so foreign to ours, and leave me out. If Mr. Bell cannot come, I shall go.”

Mr. Bell could not come. His letter, redolent of the sharp apothecary ointments required for his gout, expressed genuine regret. He hoped to visit soon, though the missive was heavily laced with the transactional vocabulary of his Milton property and his agent’s demands—a lexicon of commerce that felt abrasive against the tender margins of their grief. 

Margaret had all the difficulty in the world persuading her father not to invite Mr. Thornton. The night before the funeral, a stately note arrived from Mrs. Thornton. The heavy vellum carried the sterile scent of commercial sizing and factory smoke, devoid of any human warmth. It stated that, at her son’s desire, their carriage should attend the funeral. 

Margaret tossed the note to her father. “Oh, don’t let us have these forms,” she said, tasting the bitterness of the gesture on her tongue. “Let us go alone—you and me, papa. They don’t care for us, or else he would have offered to go himself, instead of proposing this hollow commercialization of mourning, sending an empty carriage as if grief were mere cargo.”

“I thought you were so extremely averse to his going, Margaret,” said Mr. Hale in some surprise.

“And so I am. But this seems such a mockery of mourning that I did not expect it from him.” The untranslatability of her subjective experience—the clash between the deeply felt, communal agrarian traditions of her youth and this northern, industrialized detachment—overwhelmed her. She startled her father by bursting into tears, crying all the more when he tried to comfort her with a tenderness she felt she did not deserve. 

She passed a restless night, haunted by the cyclical recurrence of memories, leaving her ill-prepared for the metallic tang of anxiety brought by Frederick’s letter the next morning. The paper smelled of London coach-dust. Mr. Lennox was out of town; his clerk expected him by Tuesday. Consequently, Frederick was remaining in London a day or two longer. He had been tempted to return to Milton, but the alarm at the station had anchored him in place. 

Margaret was thankful she received this letter while her father was absent. To read it aloud to him would require translating an unspeakable terror into a language that would surely destroy his fragile constitution. The letter contained allusions to the recognition at the station, making Margaret’s blood run cold. She battled hard against the bitter self-reproach of having urged the consultation with Mr. Lennox. At the time, the recipe for their actions had seemed sound, but the resulting dish was proving unpalatable, steeped in the dangerous flavors of detection. 

Her father was too depressed to struggle healthily. He trembled pitifully as the undertaker’s man arranged the dry, rustling crape draperies around him—a grating, artificial sound that deeply disturbed the acoustic ecology of the room. Mr. Hale looked wistfully at Margaret, tottering toward her. 

“Pray for me, Margaret,” he murmured. “I have no strength left. I cannot pray. I give her up because I must. I know it is God’s will. But I cannot see why she died. Pray for me, that I may have faith to pray. It is a great strait, my child.” His native dialect of faith, the lush agrarian prayers of his past, had eroded entirely in this harsh, smoke-filled air.

Margaret sat by him in the coach, supporting him and feeding him the noble, archaic verses of holy comfort. She offered the texts like nourishing sustenance, her voice never faltering, providing a rhythmic, sonic environment that buffered him against the clatter of the Milton streets. Her father’s lips moved after hers, attempting to ingest the resignation he had not the strength to swallow on his own. 

Margaret’s fortitude nearly gave way when Dixon directed her notice to Nicholas Higgins and his daughter. Nicholas stood aloof but deeply attentive, wearing his usual fustian clothes—the visual dialect of the working class—now altered by a sewn bit of black stuff around his hat. It was a traditional mark of mourning he had never even worn for his own daughter, Bessy. 

Mr. Hale saw nothing. He mechanically repeated the funeral service after the officiating clergyman, a fragile echo against the distant, oppressive thud of the town's power-looms. When it was ended, he mutely put his hand on Margaret’s arm, asking to be led away like a blind man relying on a faithful guide. 

Dixon sobbed aloud, so absorbed in the physical release of her grief that she did not notice the crowd dispersing until someone spoke close at hand. It was Mr. Thornton. He had been present all the time, standing in the acoustic shadow of the crowd, his head bent, absorbing the auditory landscape of their sorrow unrecognized. 

“I beg your pardon,—but, can you tell me how Mr. Hale is? And Miss Hale, too? I should like to know how they both are.” 

“Of course, sir,” Dixon replied. “They are much as is to be expected. Master is terribly broke down. Miss Hale bears up better than likely.”

Mr. Thornton would rather have heard that Margaret was consumed by the natural, incapacitating starvation of sorrow. There was a dark selfishness in him that hungered for the chance to let his great love serve as her sole sustenance. But this delicious vision of what might have been was miserably poisoned by the recollection of the Outwood station. 

He was haunted by the image of the handsome young man with whom she had stood in such familiar confidence. The memory shot through him like a physical agony, an acidic bile in his throat that made him clench his hands to subdue the pain. At that late hour, so far from home! It took a monumental effort to galvanize his trust in Margaret’s purity back into life; as soon as the effort ceased, his trust starved and dropped dead, leaving wild fancies to chase each other through his mind. 

Here was a miserable, gnawing morsel of confirmation. *She bore up better than likely.* She had, then, some alternate source of nourishment, a hope so bright it could lighten the dark hours of a newly motherless daughter. He knew her sensory capabilities, the profound depths of her passion. Her soul would walk in glorious sunlight if a man were worthy of feeding that fire. Even in mourning, she would rest upon his sympathy. *Whose?* That other man’s. The subtle power dynamics of his dominant position in Milton meant nothing if he could not translate his heart to her. His pale, grave face grew doubly wan and stern at Dixon’s answer. 

“I suppose I may call,” he said coldly. “On Mr. Hale, I mean. He will perhaps admit me after to-morrow or so.” 

He spoke as if the answer were a matter of indifference, but he was starving for the very atmosphere she breathed. He was caught in the Charybdis of passion, a circular pacing that drew him ever nearer to the fatal center, unable to sever himself from the sensory memory of her presence. 

“I dare say, sir, master will see you. He was very sorry to have to deny you the other day; but circumstances was not agreeable just then.”

For reasons she kept entirely to herself, a guarded piece of domestic history, Dixon never named this interview to Margaret. And so it was that the secret of his presence remained an untranslated whisper in the margins of their grief, and Margaret never knew that he had stood in the smoke-dimmed graveyard to honor her mother’s passing.
