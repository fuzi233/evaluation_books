"All thoughts, all passions, all delights, Whatever stirs this mortal frame, All are but ministers of Love, And feed his sacred flame." —COLERIDGE.

Deronda's eagerness to confess his love burned with a ferocity that Hans's assurance regarding Mirah's need for relief from jealousy had kindled. He returned to Ezra with a singular resolve: to demand, nay, to seize the opportunity for a private audience with her. Should she accept his devotion, he felt a surge of courage that transcended mere romance; as her betrothed, he would wield a protective authority, a shield against the inevitable storms brewing within her household. Though he detected no visible tremors of restlessness in Lapidoth, nor any waning ambition in the father's attempts to court favor, Deronda harbored a gnawing dread. He foresaw a coming struggle, a mortification, or a crescendo of domestic discord that would render Ezra and Mirah helpless victims unless he intervened. These forebodings would have been sharper had he known the tempest raging in Lapidoth's mind. The same restlessness that tormented Ezra was to Lapidoth a suffocating submission to restraint, endured only because he viewed it as a temporary cage leading to a future, well-conditioned freedom.

Lapidoth had intended to wait for a providential opening, perhaps a chance to extract a substantial sum from Deronda, yet his curiosity had already begun to probe the architecture of his daughter's security. He searched frantically for the location of Mirah's funds and her keys. The imperious gambling instinct that animated every fiber of his being, weaving a continuous net of imagination around his existence, demanded release. Yet Mirah, armed with a practical clarity that bordered on the prophetic, had safeguarded all her assets except immediate necessities in Mrs. Meyrick's keeping. To Lapidoth, this created an irritating completeness of supply, akin to confinement in a lunatic asylum where every avenue of escape was meticulously blocked. In his mind, prying open a desk or drawer to pocket a banknote was not theft but a domestic appropriation, a liberty men often claim with others' property long before the law intervenes—a reason why stolen silverware is a safer investment than speculative mining shares. He chafed under his daughter's guardianship, convinced he deserved access to her earnings as readily as he had to her apple tarts. Yet, despite his simmering resentment, he remained outwardly submissive. The temptation that truly gnawed at him was not direct confrontation with Mirah, but a calculated appeal to Deronda. Those clever souls who possess nothing else to sell often command a high price for their absence. Lapidoth's desperate search for leverage birthed the notion that his family would thrive without him, and that Deronda, eager to be rid of such a burden, would advance a fortune to facilitate his departure.

However, even hardened by experience, Lapidoth retained a profound, almost superstitious awe of Ezra's formidable friend. Thus, he deferred his scheme indefinitely. On this particular day, Deronda arrived buoyed by a gladdened consciousness that radiated from his very posture and speech. Lapidoth, trapped in a crisis of discontent and longing, found his mind cluttered with fantasies of liberation, and Deronda's newfound amiability fed these delusions. This obsession became so consuming that it disrupted his usual performance of interest; he sat listlessly beside them, unable to follow the reading, until finally rising to smoke and pace the square. The atmosphere between the two friends lightened. Mirah was absent, but Deronda knew she would return before his departure, and his eyes flared with a secret anticipation. He imagined that upon seeing her again, he would witness a sweetness of recognition previously denied to him. A playful tenderness softened his demeanor toward Ezra.

"This little room is too close for you, Ezra," Deronda declared, breaking off his reading. "The weekly heat here surpasses that of Genoa, where one retreats into the shaded coolness of vast halls. You deserve a better home now. As the stronger half, I shall dictate our arrangements." He smiled at Ezra, who replied, "I am straitened for nothing but breath. You, who might dwell in a spacious palace surrounded by verdant fields, find this a narrow prison. Yet, I cannot say 'Go.'"

"Oh, the country would be a banishment while you remain here," Deronda countered, rising to traverse the cramped double room, fanning his handkerchief vigorously. "This is the happiest room in the world to me. Moreover, I shall imagine myself in the East, preparing for my eventual journey. Only, I shall not don a cravat or a heavy ring there," he concluded emphatically, pausing to strip off these accoutrements and place them on a small table behind Ezra, whose surface was cluttered with books and papers. "I have worn my memorable ring since returning home," he continued, resettling himself. "But I am such a Sybarite that I constantly cast it off as a burden during any task. I comprehend why the Romans possessed summer rings—if they did. Now, I shall function far better."

Soon, they immersed themselves in work. Deronda read passages of rabbinical Hebrew under Ezra's correction and commentary, ignoring the subtle intrusion when Lapidoth re-entered and settled into the background. His wandering gaze swiftly locked onto the ring sparkling on the dark mahogany. During his walk, his mind had rehearsed a fiction of an advantageous overseas opportunity requiring immediate capital. He envisioned presenting this need privately to Deronda, prompting a query about the necessary sum. This calculation was the most precarious part of his plan; asking too much risked rejection, while asking too little yielded insufficient reward. His own desire knew no bounds, and he lacked any metric for Deronda's generosity.

Then, amidst these airy preparations for a receipt that remained indefinite, the ring, now familiar to Lapidoth's envious eye through Deronda's ownership, suddenly appeared detached and within easy reach. Its value was certainly less than the smallest imaginary sum his mind fluctuated between, yet presented as a solid fact, his desire leaped instantly to a nascent thought—not yet an intention, but a seductive possibility: if he quietly pocketed the ring and walked away, he would secure the means for comfortable escape from current restraint, without trouble and without danger. Any property of Deronda's, accessible without formal consent, belonged to his children's estate; since their father would never prosecute them for taking it, the act was legally moot. These thoughts cascaded rapidly, coalescing into a single, vivid image. Lapidoth had never committed larceny, yet larceny was simply appropriation punished by law. Taking this ring from a virtual relative, who would otherwise offer a much heavier gift, did not constitute larceny. Still, the heavier gift was preferable if he could hasten the request. The internal melody of imagining the theft repeated, only to sink into a rejected idea. He satisfied his urgent craving by resolving to descend, waiting for the moment of Deronda's departure to invite him for a walk and execute his plan.

He rose to stare out the window, yet his vision remained fixed on the brief passage separating him from the door near the table where the ring lay. Resolved to go down, yet dominated by a thirst-like desire, he passed the table. His fingers fell noiselessly upon the ring, and he found himself in the passage clutching it. Consequently, he donned his hat and departed. The possibility of throwing himself on his children receded into an indefinite distance. By the time he reached the square, his sense of haste had crystallized into a singular mission: sell the ring and board a ship. Deronda and Ezra registered his exit; that was all.

Later, Mirah entered, causing a genuine disruption. She wore her hat, and when Deronda rose to greet her, she spoke in a confusion that troubled her deeply. "I only came in to ensure Ezra received his new draught. I must go directly to Mrs. Meyrick's to fetch something."

"Pray allow me to walk with you," Deronda urged. "I must not tire Ezra further; besides, my brains are melting. I intend to go to Mrs. Meyrick's: may I accompany you?"

"Oh, yes," Mirah replied, blushing intensely, sensing something new and electric in Deronda, before turning to pour Ezra's medicine. Meanwhile, Ezra threw back his head, eyes closed, unable to dislodge the swirling ideas occupying his mind during the reading session. For a moment, Deronda stood lost in thought of the walk alone, until Mirah returned with the draught. Suddenly remembering he had discarded his cravat, he exclaimed, "Pray excuse my dishabille—I did not mean you to see it"—and moved to retrieve it. Then, with a violent impulse of surprise, "Good heavens, where is my ring gone?" he began scouring the floor.

Ezra glanced around the corner of his chair. Mirah, swift as thought, approached where Deronda searched and asked, "Did you lay it down?"

"Yes," Deronda replied, unvisited by any explanation other than the ring having fallen into shadow, hidden beneath the variegated carpet. He shifted furniture and searched every conceivable and inconceivable spot with frantic hands and eyes. Another explanation, however, had struck Mirah, draining the color from her cheeks. She leaned to Ezra's ear and whispered, "Was my father here?"
