The dusk came down softly on the hills, but Ethne felt no softness in it. She went into the farmhouse parlour as though she were stepping back into a life that had been paused and now must be resumed—chairs in their places, the clean hearth, the familiar scent of turf and woodsmoke waiting like an old habit. Though the evening was warm by any sane measure of summer, chill sat inside her bones, so she had the fire lit. The first flare caught the iron bars, and soon the coals glowed like pomegranate seeds split open, bright with a heat that promised comfort and offered none.

She sat close, hands folded as if to keep them from trembling, and stared into the heart of the fire. That particular stillness took her—an attitude that in her meant not calm but strain drawn so tight it could hum. The moment she had lived toward, counted down to in her mind as women count the minutes to bread rising, had come and been spent. The half-hour was over. Harry Feversham had gone.

Now the village was itself again: remote, small, set out of the world among the hills, with its own evening noises—distant cattle shifting in a byre, a cartwheel somewhere complaining once and then falling silent, the soft punctuation of a gate latch. All of it sounded too clear in the quiet, as though the air had been rinsed. And in that rinsed air, she was alone—more alone than she had been since Willoughby sailed away down the Salcombe estuary on that August morning.

For months—no, for longer than she cared to measure—she had lived with an inward appointment. From the time of Willoughby’s coming she had looked forward night and day to the one half-hour when Harry would be with her. She had thought she was practical, brisk, capable of managing a farm and a household with firm hands and plain speech; but the truth lay there, undeniable as a pot left to boil over: she had made a life around that half-hour. It had come, it had passed, and the world felt suddenly emptied, as if someone had carried away a table and left the room echoing.

Yet she had known this hour would follow. She had foreseen it the way you foresee the sharpness after a cordial—how sweetness can make the next taste bitter by contrast. She had always looked beyond it. Tomorrow she would be steadier; tomorrow she would compare today’s parting with the parting on the night of the ball at Lennon House, and the comparison would shame this pain into proportion. Today would be a small thing beside that.

The fire snapped, a resinous little crack that sounded in her ear too loud. She wondered—because the mind will busy itself when the heart is raw—what Harry would do now he had returned. She found herself building him a future in the careful way she planned the rebuilding of a wall: stone upon stone, sound work, a great distinction rising from patient effort. The thought warmed her for a moment, and then the warmth went out again.

Dermod’s old collie came padding to her knee with the quiet certainty of animals that read distress more truly than words. His muzzle, greyed with age, nudged under her hand. His breath smelled faintly of the day’s milk and damp grass, and there was comfort in the honest animal scent. Ethne rose, took his head between her hands, and kissed it, pressing her lips into the wiry fur as though she could borrow steadiness from him.

He was very old. He would die soon and leave her. The fact arrived without drama, like a cold draft. After that—years and years perhaps—before she herself lay down and knew that the great moment was near. The thought made her shiver, and she drew closer to the fire, as if warmth could be hoarded against the future.

A knock came at the door.

A servant entered to say Colonel Durrance was waiting.

“Yes,” Ethne said, and the word was clipped, controlled. She rose as he entered, went forward to meet him as though she had not been on the edge of breaking. She did not shirk the part she had set herself. Whatever secret chamber her grief kept, she stepped out of it the instant she was summoned.

She spoke with him as though no unusual thing had happened an hour before. She talked of their marriage, of the rebuilding of Lennon House—rooflines, rooms, the practical matters that could be spoken of without betraying the pulse underneath. Her voice did what she required of it; her face held its lines. Difficult, yes, but she had grown used to difficulty as to weather.

The maid brought in the tea. The little domestic ritual—cup on saucer, the faint rattle of china, the smell of strong leaves steeping—made a frame around the evening, as if a table could hold together what a heart could not. Durrance sat, listening as blind men listen, not merely to words but to the space between them.

Then he asked, with a quietness that was nearly harsh, “Will you play to me the Musoline Overture?”

“Not to-night,” Ethne said at once. “I am rather tired.”

The refusal had hardly left her mouth before she felt it like a retreat, and retreat was one of the small things she had promised herself not to permit. It was in the small daily happenings that she must be most careful. She forced a smile that might have deceived anyone less trained in attention than Durrance.

“Still—I think I can play the overture.”

She took down her violin. The varnished wood was cool under her fingers, the familiar weight settling against her collarbone. She drew the bow across the strings, and the first note rose into the room, thin at first and then full, as though the instrument breathed. Outside, the farm noises seemed to withdraw. Even the fire, for a time, kept its tongue.

She played it through from beginning to end. It was not mere accomplishment—her technique precise, her phrasing controlled—so much as something laid bare without permission. The overture, always, carried with it more than melody: it carried the memory of another place, another hour, another kind of light.

Durrance stood at the window with his back towards her while she played, still as a post. When the last note died, he turned and walked to her side.

“I was rather a brute,” he said quietly, “to ask you to play that overture to-night.”

“I wasn’t anxious to play,” she answered, and laid the violin aside as though it were hot.

“I know.” There was no apology in his tone now, only purpose. “But I was anxious to find out something, and I knew no other way of finding it out.”

Ethne turned a startled face up to him. The blood seemed to drain from her skin, leaving it too pale under the lamplight.

“What do you mean?” Her voice held suspense like a held breath.

“You are so seldom off your guard. Only—indeed—at rare times when you play. Once before, when you played that overture, you were off your guard. I thought that if I could get you to play it again to-night—the overture which was once strummed out in a dingy café at Wadi Halfa—to-night again I should find you off your guard.”

The name of that place, spoken here among the clean Devon hills, struck like a foreign coin dropped into a quiet room. It took her breath away. For a moment she could only look at him, wide-eyed, as though he had reached into a locked drawer.

He could not know. It was impossible. He did not know.

But Durrance went on, gently relentless.

“Well?” he asked, and there was even a faint smile in his voice. “Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?”

The words were incredible. They seemed to hang in the air like smoke that would not disperse. It took a long time before Ethne could be sure they had been spoken and not imagined by a mind rubbed raw by the day. Her senses were overstrained; she distrusted them. But he repeated his question, and disbelief fell away.

“Who told you of any fourth feather?” she asked, and the effort to make the question sound ordinary made it sound sharp instead.

“Trench,” he said. “I met him at Dover. But he only told me of the fourth feather.” The smile was still there, quiet, almost rueful. “I knew of the three before. Trench would never have told me of the fourth had I not known of the three. For I should not have met him as he landed from the steamer at Dover. I should not have asked him, ‘Where is Harry Feversham?’ And for me to know of the three was enough.”

“How do you know?” she cried, and despair slipped through her composure like water through a cracked cup. She moved closer, and he took gently hold of her arm—not possessive, but steadying, as though he feared she might fall.

“But since I know,” he protested, “what does it matter how I know? I have known a long while—ever since Captain Willoughby came to The Pool with the first feather. I waited to tell you that I knew until Harry Feversham came back, and he came to-day.”

Ethne sat down again, stunned. For so long she had guarded her secret with the vigilance of someone watching a small flame in wind; to realise that for a year it had been no secret came like a blow. Yet even in the shock, she understood she must gain time, gather her scattered faculties into command. So she spoke of something unimportant, a small domestic diversion, as one stirs a pot slowly to keep it from sticking.

“You were in the church, then?” she asked. “Or you heard us upon the steps? Or you met—him as he rode away?”

“Not one of the conjectures is right,” Durrance said, and she heard the satisfaction he took in that. He had his vanities like other men; since blindness had come upon him, one vanity had grown particularly keen. He prided himself on the quickness of his perception. It delighted him to make discoveries no one expected a blind man to make, and to announce them unexpectedly. It was an added pleasure to tell the steps by which he had arrived at his conclusion, to let his listener follow the trail by sound and inference.

“Not one of your conjectures is right, Ethne,” he repeated, almost inviting the question.

“Then how did you find out?” she asked, giving him what he wanted, buying herself a few seconds more.

“I knew from Trench that Harry Feversham would come some day, and soon. I passed the church this afternoon. Your collie dog barked at me—so I knew you were inside.” He paused, as if hearing again the bark in his memory. “But a saddled horse was tied up beside the gate. So some one else was with you, and not any one from the village. Then I got you to play, and that told me who it was who rode the horse.”

“Yes,” Ethne said vaguely. She had scarcely listened; the words were like rain striking a window while a room burns. “Yes, I see.”

Then her voice changed—became definite, controlled. She had gathered herself.

“You went away to Wiesbaden for a year,” she said. “You went away just after Captain Willoughby came. Was that the reason why you went away?”

“I went because neither you nor I could have kept up the game of pretences we were playing,” Durrance replied, and there was no bitterness, only plainness. “You were pretending that you had no thought for Harry Feversham—that you hardly cared whether he was alive or dead. I was pretending not to have found out that beyond everything in the world you cared for him. Some day or other we should have failed, each one in turn. I dared not fail, nor dared you.

“I could not let you”—and his voice softened here, as though he touched something fragile—“you who had said, ‘Two lives must not be spoilt because of me,’ live through a year thinking that two lives had been spoilt. You on your side dared not let me, who had said, ‘Marriage between a blind man and a woman is only possible when there is more than friendship on both sides,’ know that upon one side there was only friendship, and we were so near to failing. So I went away.”

“You did not fail,” Ethne said quietly. “It was only I who failed.”

The self-reproach came from deep. She had set herself one task worth doing: to guard him from knowledge that would set the crown upon his calamities. She had failed. He had set himself to guard her from understanding she had failed, and he had succeeded. It was not humiliation that stung—no petty wound of being outwitted. It was the knowledge that her failure, if it had hurt him, would rob him of his last chance at happiness. That was the thorn.

“But it was not your fault,” Durrance said. “Once or twice—as I said—you were off your guard, but the convincing facts were not revealed to me in that way. When you played the Musoline Overture before, on the night of the day when Willoughby brought you such good news, I took to myself that happiness of yours which inspired your playing. You must not blame yourself. On the contrary, you should be glad that I have found out.”

“Glad!” The word broke from her, incredulous.

“Yes—for my sake—glad.” He spoke with a careful gentleness, as if guiding her over rough ground. “Two lives should not be spoilt because of you. Had you had your way, had I not found out, not two but three lives would have been spoilt because of you—because of your loyalty.”

“Three?” The number seemed to accuse her.

“Yours. Yes—yes, yours, Feversham’s, and mine. It was hard enough to keep the pretence during the few weeks we were in Devonshire. Own to it, Ethne! When I went to London to see my oculist it was a relief; it gave you a pause, a rest wherein to drop pretence and be yourself. It could not have lasted long even in Devonshire. But what when we came to live under the same roof, and there were no visits to the oculist, when we saw each other every hour of every day? Sooner or later the truth must have come to me.

“It might have come gradually—a suspicion added to a suspicion and another to that until no doubt was left. Or it might have flashed out in one terrible moment. But it would have been made clear. And then, Ethne? What then? You aimed at a compensation; you wanted to make up to me for the loss of what I love—my career, the army, the special service in the strange quarters of the world. A fine compensation to sit in front of you knowing you had married a cripple out of pity, and that in so doing you had crippled yourself and foregone the happiness which is yours by right. Whereas now—”

“Whereas now?” she echoed, because the old habit of obedience to the flow of conversation still held, though her heart was stumbling.

“I remain your friend,” he said very gently, “which I would rather be than your unloved husband.”

Ethne did not answer. The decision had been taken out of her hands as cleanly as a knife takes a thread. Relief and shame, gratitude and grief, all pulled at her at once, and she could not speak without choosing one and betraying the others.

“You sent Harry away this afternoon,” Durrance said. “You said good-bye to him twice.”

At “twice” Ethne lifted her head sharply. But before she could ask what he meant, he explained, with that quiet precision which made his blindness seem almost a different kind of sight.

“Once in the church—again upon your violin.”

He took up the violin from the chair where she had laid it, handling it with care, as one might hold something living. The instrument’s polished body caught the lamplight; its strings gave a faint, sympathetic whisper.

“It has been a very good friend, your violin,” he said. “A good friend to me—to us all. You will understand that, Ethne, very soon. I stood at the window while you played it. I had never heard anything in my life half so sad as your farewell to Harry Feversham, and yet it was nobly sad. It was true music; it did not complain.”

He set the violin down again.

“I am going to send a messenger to Rathmullen. Harry cannot cross Lough Swilly to-night. The messenger will bring him back to-morrow.”

The day had brought Ethne many shocks, and this one, though it was mercy, struck her with a force that finally broke her restraint. As Durrance bent slightly towards her, he became aware that she was crying—silently, without the least sound, as if even tears must not disturb the house’s quiet. For once they had their way.

He took his cap and walked noiselessly to the door. The restraint in his movements was not caution but respect, as though the very air around her grief should not be jarred. As he opened the door, Ethne rose quickly.

“Don’t go for a moment,” she said, and crossed from the hearth to the centre of the room, as if moving could keep her from sinking. “The oculist at Wiesbaden?” she asked. “He gave you a hope?”

He stood still. In the pause she heard everything—the fire’s low roar, the tick of a cooling kettle, the far-off murmur of night creatures outside, sounds that made the silence more intimate. He seemed to weigh whether he should lie, as people lie out of kindness, or speak truth, as people speak out of dignity.

“No,” he said at length. “There is no hope. But I am not so helpless as at one time I was afraid that I should be. I can get about, can’t I? Perhaps one of these days I shall go on a journey—one of the long journeys amongst the strange people in the East.”

Then he went from the house upon his errand.

He had learned his lesson long ago, and the violin had taught it him. It had spoken again that afternoon, and though with a different voice had offered him the same message. The true music cannot complain.
