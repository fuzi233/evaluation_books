For Martin's grand design to hold, he was compelled to remain within the domestic sphere that very day. Consequently, the prospect of breakfast held no allure for him. Just as the school bell was due to toll, a sharp, piercing agony seized his heart, rendering the journey to the grammar school with Mark entirely inadvisable. Instead, he retreated to his father's armchair by the hearth, surrendering to the warmth and the morning paper. With this tactical retreat secured, Mark departed for Mr. Summer's class, and Matthew and Mr. Yorke retired to the counting-house, leaving Martin with a formidable list of objectives: four distinct exploits remained to be accomplished.

The first, and most urgent, was to consume the breakfast he had skipped—a necessity for a boy of fifteen whose energy reserves were critically low. The subsequent tasks involved successfully removing his mother, Miss Moore, and Mrs. Horsfall from the premises before the clock struck four.

To fuel his ambitious plans, Martin navigated the familiar path to the larder. The servants were already in the kitchen, their solemn meal consumed behind closed doors, while his mother and Miss Moore strolled the lawn, discussing the very doors they had just shut. Safe within the pantry, Martin exercised fastidious judgment over the provisions. Determined that his delayed meal should be *recherché* rather than the usual insipid fare of bread and milk, he curated a feast. He selected three rosy apples from their straw, an apricot puff and a damson tart from the pastry dish, and condescended to a single currant tea-cake. With his trusty clasp-knife, he appropriated a wing of fowl, a slice of ham, and a cantlet of cold custard-pudding. Satisfied with his bounty, he emerged into the hall.

He was already halfway across the threshold—three steps from the sanctuary of the back parlour—when the front door swung open and Matthew stood revealed. Had the Old Gentleman arrived in full regalia of horns, hooves, and tail, it might have been preferable. Matthew, the skeptic and scoffer, had already dismissed Martin's claim of heart pain, muttering phrases like "shamming Abraham" with evident disdain. The sight of the stolen feast—the apples, tarts, fowl, and pudding—only inflamed his conviction of his own superior sagacity.

Martin paused, momentarily *interdit*, before instantly regaining his composure. Possessing the true perspicacity of elite souls, he recognized how this untoward interruption could be turned to his advantage. He saw clearly how the confrontation could serve his second objective: the removal of his mother. He knew that any collision between him and Matthew invariably prompted Mrs. Yorke to stage a fit of hysterics. Furthermore, he understood the principle that calm follows the storm; after a morning of theatrical distress, his mother was destined for an afternoon of bedridden repose. This was precisely the accommodation he required.

The collision occurred immediately in the hall. A dry laugh, an insulting sneer, and a contemptuous taunt met Martin's nonchalant yet cutting retort. They rushed at one another. Martin, who usually moved silently, made a great deal of noise this time. Servants poured in, followed by Mrs. Yorke and Miss Moore; no female hand could separate the combatants. Mr. Yorke was summoned.

"Sons," he declared, "one of you must leave my roof if this recurs. I will have no Cain and Abel strife here."

Martin allowed himself to be led away. He feigned injury, playing the role of the youngest and lightest victim. Yet, he remained cool, devoid of passion, even smiling with contentment that the most arduous phase of his labor was complete.

For a brief interval, he seemed to flag in the morning's progress.

"It is not worth while to bother myself for that Caroline," he remarked.

Yet, a quarter-hour later, he returned to the dining room, staring at the head with disheveled tresses and eyes clouded with despair.

"Yes," he said, "I made her sob, shudder, almost faint. I shall see her smile before I am done; besides, I intend to outwit all these womenites."

Directly after dinner, Mrs. Yorke fulfilled her son's calculation by retiring to her chamber. Now, the focus shifted to Hortense.

That lady was comfortably settled in the back parlour, mending stockings, when Martin—having laid down a book he had been perusing with the voluptuous ease of a callow pacha—lazily introduced a discourse concerning Sarah, the maid at the Hollow. Through much verbal meandering, he insinuated that Sarah had three suitors: Frederic Murgatroyd, Jeremiah Pighills, and John-of-Mally's-of-Hannah's-of-Deb's. He added that Miss Mann had confirmed the girl often entertained these swains to meals, serving them the best the house afforded.

It required no further provocation. Hortense could not endure another hour without rushing to the scene of these nefarious transactions to inspect the state of affairs personally. Mrs. Horsfall remained.

With the field secure, Martin extracted a bunch of keys from his mother's work-basket. Using them, he opened the sideboard cupboard, retrieved a black bottle and a small glass, placed them on the table, and nimbly ascended the stairs. He approached Mr. Moore's door and tapped. The nurse answered.

"If you please, ma'am, you are invited to step into the back parlour for some refreshment. You will not be disturbed; the family is out."

He watched her descend, watched her enter, and then locked the door behind her. She was safe.

The hard work was done; now came the pleasure. He snatched his cap and hastened to the wood.

It was barely half-past three. The morning had been fine, but the sky now looked dark. Snow began to fall; the wind blew cold, making the wood appear dismal and the old tree grim. Yet, Martin approved of the shadow on his path, finding a strange charm in the spectral aspect of the doddered oak.

He had to wait. Walking to and fro as the flakes fell faster, he listened to the wind shift from a moan to a pitiful howl.

"She is long in coming," he muttered, glancing along the narrow track. "I wonder what I wish to see her so much for? She is not coming for me. But I have power over her, and I want her to come that I may use that power."

He continued his pacing.

"Now," he resumed after a further delay, "if she fails to come, I shall hate and scorn her."

It struck four. He heard the church clock far away. A step, so quick and light that, save for the rustling of leaves, it would scarcely have sounded on the wood-walk, checked his impatience. The wind blew fiercely, and the thickening white storm became bewildering, yet she came on, undismayed.

"Well, Martin," she said eagerly, "how is he?"

"It is queer how she thinks of *him*," Martin reflected internally. "The blinding snow and bitter cold are nothing to her, I believe; yet she is but a 'chitty-faced creature,' as my mother would say. I could find in my heart to wish I had a cloak to wrap her in."

Thus meditating, he neglected to answer Miss Helstone.

"You have seen him?"

"No."

"Oh! you promised you would."

"I mean to do better by you than that. Didn't I say *I* don't care to see him?"

"But now it will be so long before I get to know anything certain about him, and I am sick of waiting. Martin, *do* see him, and give him Caroline Helstone's regards, and say she wished to know how he was, and if anything could be done for his comfort."

"I won't."

"You are changed. You were so friendly last night."

"Come, we must not stand in this wood; it is too cold."

"But before I go, promise me to come again tomorrow with news."

"No such thing. I am much too delicate to make and keep such appointments in the winter season. If you knew what a pain I had in my chest this morning, and how I went without breakfast, and was knocked down besides, you'd feel the impropriety of bringing me here in the snow. Come, I say."

"Are you really delicate, Martin?"

"Don't I look so?"

"You have rosy cheeks."

"That's hectic. Will you come—or you won't?"

"Where?"

"With me. I was a fool not to bring a cloak. I would have made you cosy."

"You are going home; my nearest road lies in the opposite direction."

"Put your arm through mine; I'll take care of you."

"But the wall—the hedge—it is such hard work climbing, and you are too slender and young to help me without hurting yourself."

"You shall go through the gate."

"But----"

"But, but—will you trust me or not?"

She looked into his face.

"I think I will. Anything rather than return as anxious as I came."

"I can't answer for that. This, however, I promise you: be ruled by me, and you shall see Moore yourself."

"See him myself?"

"Yourself."

"But, dear Martin, does he know?"

"Ah! I'm dear now. No, he doesn't know."

"And your mother and the others?"

"All is right."

Caroline fell into a long, silent fit of musing, yet she continued walking with her guide. They came in sight of Briarmains.

"Have you made up your mind?" he asked.

She was silent.

"Decide; we are just on the spot. I *won't* see him—that I tell you—except to announce your arrival."

"Martin, you are a strange boy, and this is a strange step; but all I feel *is* and *has been*, for a long time, strange. I will see him."

"Having said that, you will neither hesitate nor retract?"

"No."

"Here we are, then. Do not be afraid of passing the parlour window; no one will see you. My father and Matthew are at the mill, Mark is at school, the servants are in the back kitchen, Miss Moore is at the cottage, my mother in her bed, and Mrs. Horsfall in paradise. Observe—I need not ring. I open the door; the hall is empty, the staircase quiet; so is the gallery. The whole house and all its inhabitants are under a spell, which I will not break till you are gone."

"Martin, I trust you."

"You never said a better word. Let me take your shawl. I will shake off the snow and dry it for you. You are cold and wet. Never mind; there is a fire upstairs. Are you ready?"

"Yes."

"Follow me."

He left his shoes on the mat and mounted the stair unshod. Caroline followed with a noiseless step. There was a gallery, and there was a passage; at the end of that passage, Martin paused before a door and tapped. He had to tap twice—thrice. A voice, known to one listener, finally said, "Come in."

The boy entered briskly.

"Mr. Moore, a lady called to inquire after you. None of the women were about. It is washing-day, and the maids are over the crown of the head in soap-suds in the back kitchen, so I asked her to step up."

"Up here, sir?"

"Up here, sir; but if you object, she shall go down again."

"Is this a place or am I a person to bring a lady to, you absurd lad?"

"No; so I'll take her off."

"Martin, you will stay here. Who is she?"

"Your grandmother from that château on the Scheldt Miss Moore talks about."

"Martin," said the softest whisper at the door, "don't be foolish."

"Is she there?" Moore inquired hastily, having caught an imperfect sound.

"She is there, fit to faint. She is standing on the mat, shocked at your want of filial affection."

"Martin, you are an evil cross between an imp and a page. What is she like?"

"More like me than you; for she is young and beautiful."

"You are to show her forward. Do you hear?"

"Come, Miss Caroline."

"Miss Caroline!" repeated Moore.

And when Miss Caroline entered, she was encountered in the middle of the chamber by a tall, thin, wasted figure who took both her hands.

"I give you a quarter of an hour," said Martin as he withdrew, "no more. Say what you have to say in that time. Till it is past I will wait in the gallery; nothing shall approach; I'll see you safe away. Should you persist in staying longer, I leave you to your fate."

He shut the door. In the gallery, he was as elated as a king. He had never been engaged in an adventure he liked so well, for no adventure had ever invested him with so much importance or inspired him with so much interest.

"You are come at last," said the meagre man, gazing on his visitress with hollow eyes.

"Did you expect me before?"

"For a month, near two months, we have been very near; and I have been in sad pain, and danger, and misery, Cary."

"I could not come."

"Couldn't you? But the rectory and Briarmains are very near—not two miles apart."

There was pain and there was pleasure in the girl's face as she listened to these implied reproaches. It was sweet, it was bitter to defend herself.

"When I say I could not come, I mean I could not see you; for I came with mamma the very day we heard what had happened. Mr. MacTurk then told us it was impossible to admit any stranger."
