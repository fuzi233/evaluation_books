The first stretch of their journey away from Norland unfolded under a heavy, cloud of quiet grief that made every mile drag slow and bitter. Elinor twisted a frayed linen handkerchief her mother had given her in her hands, Marianne’s cheek was streaked with silent tears as she stared unseeing at the passing hedgerows, and even Margaret, the youngest, was too heavy-hearted to tease her sisters. But as the road wound deeper into Devonshire, their new home crept into the edges of their attention, softening their dejection little by little. When Barton Valley first came into view, the sun broke through the thin late-summer cloud cover to gild the rolling green pastures, the ancient oak woods heavy with leaves, and the silver thread of a stream snaking through the fields, and the whole family breathed out at once. This was no cold, unwelcoming place.
A mile of winding lane later, their coach pulled to a stop before their new home. A tiny lush green courtyard made up the whole of the cottage’s front grounds, and a neat little wicket gate, painted soft cream, swung open to admit them. Barton Cottage was small, compact, and cozy enough to wrap around a person like a well-loved shawl, though it fell short of the dreamy ideal of a rustic cottage many might picture: its roof was neat and tiled, its window shutters were plain dark wood rather than the fashionable green, and no wild honeysuckle climbed its plaster walls, no matter how much they wished it would. A narrow front passage ran straight through the center of the house to the garden out back, with two sitting rooms each no more than sixteen feet square flanking the front door. Beyond those lay the kitchen and scullery, and the narrow staircase that led up to four small bedchambers and two garret rooms tucked under the eaves. The house was barely a dozen years old, and in perfect, solid repair.
Next to the grand, sprawling halls of Norland, it was impossibly tiny, impossibly humble — and for a heartbeat after they stepped over the threshold, the memory of their old home pricked hard enough to bring fresh tears. But the sound of their longtime servant’s warm, relieved laugh as she set down a tray of spiced cider dried those tears quickly. Each of them resolved, for the sake of the others, to make a happy home here. It was early September, the air sweet with ripening blackberries and clover, and the golden late summer sun flooded every room with warm light on their first day, giving them a gentle first impression that would make the cottage feel beloved long after the weather turned cold.
The location could hardly have been better. High, soft hills rose directly behind the cottage, rolling out to either side: some open, windswept downlands bright with gorse, others thick with trees and planted wheat fields. The village of Barton clung to the slope of one of the nearest hills, its little white church spire peeking over the rooftops to make a lovely view from every front window. From their front doorstep, the valley stretched wide for miles, rolling out into wilder, more distant country beyond.
Mrs. Dashwood, for all her soft heart, was already glowing with eager plans. Though her old life at Norland had made her used to far more grand furnishings, the prospect of decorating and improving their little home was pure joy to her, and she had enough saved to add every small touch of elegance the rooms currently lacked. “It’s true, it’s far too small for our little family,” she said, laughing as she ran a hand over the mantel in the front parlor, “but we’ll make it perfectly comfortable for now. It’s too late in the year for building work, but come spring, if I’ve set a little extra coin aside — and I’m sure I will — we can make all sorts of changes. These parlors are far too small for the dinner parties and musical evenings I hope to host here. We can knock out the old front passage, merge half of it into one parlor, add a bright new drawing room at the back, and a pretty new bedchamber and garret above it, and it will be the coziest little home in all Devonshire. I do wish the stairs were a little wider, of course, but we can’t have everything, and I daresay that’s an easy fix. We’ll map out all our plans once we see how the year’s savings add up.”
And for the weeks before that dream could ever be realized, on an income of only five hundred pounds a year for a woman who had never been good at pinching pennies, they were all perfectly happy to settle in to the house as it was. They spent the rest of the day unpacking, tucking their favorite books onto the small shelves, arranging their small personal treasures on the mantels to make the place feel like theirs. Marianne had her beloved pianoforte unpacked and pushed right up to the front window where the afternoon sun fell soft on the keys, and Elinor pinned all her carefully rendered watercolor sketches of Norland’s woods and wildflowers to the sitting room walls, so a little piece of their old home lived here too.
They were mid-breakfast the next morning when the front door was thrown open without ceremony, and their landlord stepped in to welcome them. Sir John Middleton was a handsome, rosy-cheeked man of around forty, all broad smiles and easy warmth. He had visited their old family home of Stanhill many years before, though the Dashwood girls had been far too small to remember him. His face was nothing but good humor, and his manners were every bit as friendly as the warm welcome letter he had sent them before they arrived. Their arrival seemed to give him genuine joy, and their comfort was his top priority. He talked at length of how much he hoped they would be the closest of neighbors, and pressed them so hard to dine at his home at Barton Park every single night until they were fully settled that even his over-eager persistence felt far more kind than rude.
His kindness did not end at words. Barely an hour after he left, a huge wicker basket arrived from the park, stuffed full of fresh garden peas, ripe plums, crisp apples, and sun-warmed strawberries. Before the sun set that same day, a second delivery arrived, full of game he had shot that morning. He insisted on carrying all their outgoing letters to the village post for them, and refused to let them turn down his daily newspaper, which he promised to send over every evening without fail.
He had also brought a very polite message from his wife, Lady Middleton, who said she would call on them the very first moment she was sure her visit would not be an inconvenience. Mrs. Dashwood sent back an equally warm invitation, and the very next day, Lady Middleton arrived at the cottage. They had all been curious to meet the woman whose goodwill would shape so much of their new life, and her tall, elegant figure, handsome face, and graceful manners met every bit of their hopes at first. She was no more than twenty-six or seven, dressed in a fine silk gown, with perfect polished poise. But that poise edged into cold reserve: for all her good breeding, she had none of her husband’s frank, warm spirit, and very little to say beyond the most generic, polite platitudes.
Conversation would have run dry very quickly, if she had not brought along her eldest child, Harry, a curly golden-haired little boy of six years old, who hung shyly behind his mother’s skirts with his face pressed to her leg. The ladies had no shortage of things to talk about then: they cooed over his beauty, asked his age, laughed at the silly rhymes Sir John insisted he knew by heart, and spent ten pleasant minutes playfully debating whether the boy favored his jolly, round-faced father or his slender, elegant mother, every one of them disagreeing on the point and pretending to be shocked by the others’ opinions.
By the end of the visit, Sir John would not leave without extracting a firm promise from the whole Dashwood family to come dine at Barton Park the very next day, and not a single one of them had the heart to say no.
