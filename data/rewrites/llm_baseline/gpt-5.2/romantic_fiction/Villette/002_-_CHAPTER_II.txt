Some days went by, and still the little stranger would not be won. She was neither turbulent nor wilful; she obeyed, as if obedience were another sort of silence. Yet comfort could scarcely thrive in the room where she sat. She moped with a skill that would have done credit to a grown exile: no furrowed face, turned from its own hearth and set down under an alien sky, could have worn more plainly the ache of home than did that infant countenance. In her black frock and neat white chemisette, she seemed at once too small for sorrow and too full of it; and when I, Lucy Snowe, opened a door and found her curled in some corner, her head propped in its pygmy hand, the place took on the hush of a chapel after the last footfall—inhabited, indeed, yet as if by a presence that would not be spoken to.

At night, the house had its accustomed sounds: the settling of coals; the faint click of a shutter; the distant step of Warren going his rounds. But above these, there was often another sound that made the ear strain—so slight it could hardly be called sound at all: a child’s breath, caught and measured, as if she feared to disturb even the darkness with grief. More than once I woke in moonlight and saw her in her night-dress, white as the sheet behind her, kneeling upright in bed. The very act had a strange ardour in it—like a little votary who had found no language in the day fit for her need and was trying, in the night, to pray without words. I seldom caught more than a thread of whisper, and sometimes even that was swallowed back into her throat; yet when any syllables reached me, they always came weighted with one name, repeated as bread is broken and shared—“Papa; my dear papa!” It was a mind with one idea, and that single seed had filled the whole field.

What end might have come of such fretting, had it been left to feed on itself, I cannot tell. The turn came suddenly, in the plain course of an afternoon.

Mrs. Bretton, wishing to coax the child from her habitual corner, lifted her into the window-seat and tried to occupy her with a little game—bidding her watch the passers-by and count how many ladies went down the street within a certain time. The child sat listless, her gaze not fastening, her lips not counting; her eyes were like two dark pools that refused the light. Then, while my own attention rested on her face, I saw a change begin in that small iris and pupil—a dangerous quickening, as if some hidden string had been plucked and the whole creature answered.

The heavy stare swam; trembled; then caught fire. The cloud upon her brow broke. The dejected little features—so pinched and old of late—lit as if a lamp had been struck within. Eagerness took the place of desolation; expectancy, intense and breathless, replaced her dull patience.

“It is!” she cried.

And like a bird loosed from a hand, she was gone.

How she contrived the house-door I cannot say. Perhaps it stood ajar; perhaps Warren, encountered in her rush, yielded to her sharp, imperative petition. I watched from the window. In her little braided apron—she had an aversion to pinafores—and mourning frock, she darted half the street’s length, straight as a thrown arrow. I was just turning to tell Mrs. Bretton, with my usual coolness, that the child had run out in a sort of madness and must be pursued at once, when I saw her caught up—caught as quickly as a gust snatches a fallen leaf.

A gentleman had seized her out of the way of danger. He wrapped her in his cloak—sheltering that fragile black frock from the street’s draught and glare—and carried her back towards the house whence he had seen her burst. I thought he would restore her to a servant and withdraw; but he entered. After pausing below, he came upstairs.

The manner of his reception explained everything: he was known to Mrs. Bretton. She recognised him at once; greeted him—yet with a flutter, a surprise, a look half expostulation, as though his coming were at once boon and peril. In answer to that look, rather than to any words she found at first, he said, with a blunt sort of feeling struggling under self-command—

“I could not help it, madam. I found it impossible to leave the country without seeing with my own eyes how she settled.”

“But you will unsettle her.”

“I hope not. And how is papa’s little Polly?”

He addressed the question to the child as he sat down and placed her gently on the floor before him. She leaned at once against his knee, as naturally as if she had never leaned elsewhere.

“How is Polly’s papa?” she answered, gazing up into his face.

It was not a loud scene. There were no cries, no dramatic overflow; and for that very reason it pressed on the spectator with a weight almost intolerable. In noisy transports there is often relief, even a touch of contempt to keep one’s footing. Here there was only feeling held down by discipline—like a strong creature stooping of its own will, a giant slave obedient to sense.

Mr. Home was stern-featured—hard-featured, perhaps: knotty in the forehead, with cheekbones marked and prominent; the whole cast of his face Scotch. His accent, when he spoke, matched that northern mould. Yet there was emotion in his eye, and now, despite his manly restraint, agitation showed in the set of his mouth. Proud-looking he was, and homely-looking too, as if he belonged equally to some harsh hillside and some well-swept hearth.

He laid his hand on the child’s uplifted head.

“Kiss Polly,” she said.

He kissed her.

She made wonderfully little noise; she seemed to have got what she wanted—everything she wanted—and to be sunk in a trance of content. Nothing in her features resembled his; yet she was plainly of his strain. Her mind had been poured from his, as wine from a flagon fills the cup.

“Polly,” he said, looking down, “go into the hall. You will see papa’s great-coat on a chair. Put your hand into the pockets; you will find a pocket-handkerchief there. Bring it to me.”

She went and returned deftly, silent, nimbly precise. He was speaking to Mrs. Bretton when she came back and stood at his knee with the handkerchief in her small hand—waiting, without fidget or complaint, like a little housewife at table. Seeing he still talked on, seemingly unconscious she had returned, she took his hand, opened the unresisting fingers, slipped the handkerchief into his palm, and closed the fingers over it one by one, as carefully as if she were sealing a promise. He still did not look down; yet by-and-by he lifted her to his knee. She nestled against him; and though for an hour neither spoke nor looked much at the other, I suppose both were satisfied.

During tea, her movements gave, as usual, full occupation to the eye—so much intention packed into so small a body. She directed Warren where to put the chairs.

“Put papa’s chair here, and mine near it—between papa and Mrs. Bretton. I must hand his tea.”

Then she took her own seat and beckoned with her fingers to her father, as if arranging a household ceremony.

“Be near me, as if we were at home, papa.”

When his cup passed, she intercepted it and would have the stirring of the sugar and the pouring of the cream for herself.

“I always did it for you at home, papa. Nobody could do it as well—not even your own self.”

Her zeal made the service almost comical. The sugar-tongs were too wide for her small grasp; she had to use both hands to command them. The silver cream-ewer, the bread-and-butter plates, even the cup and saucer taxed her scant strength; she lifted, handed, steadied—watching the trembling brim with an anxious seriousness, as if spillage would be a sin. Yet she broke nothing; she contrived, through sheer devotion, to make awkwardness look like duty.

Candidly, I thought her a little busy-body; but her father—blind as other parents—seemed soothed and content to be waited on, as if each small office fed him with comfort more sustaining than tea.

“She is my comfort,” he could not help saying to Mrs. Bretton.

Mrs. Bretton had her own “comfort” and nonpareil on a larger scale, and for the moment absent; so she sympathised with his weakness, perhaps the better because she too had been listening all day to the house’s soundscape for one particular footstep. It came at last, in the course of the evening.

We were seated round the fire after tea when Graham joined our circle—broke it up, rather; for, of course, his arrival made a bustle. There were the quickened voices, the chair pushed back, the fresh coal laid on; and as Mr. Graham had been fasting, there was refreshment to be provided. The clink of plate and the stir of preparation rose like a domestic welcome; it struck me how a home announces its loves not only by words but by its noises—the softened hurry, the brightened rattle, the very tones lifted a key.

He and Mr. Home met as old acquaintances. Of the little girl he took no notice at first. When he had eaten, and answered his mother’s numerous questions, he turned from the table to the hearth. Opposite where he placed himself sat Mr. Home, and at his elbow the child.

And when I say child, I use a term too loose for her: it calls up any picture rather than that demure small person in mourning, perched beside a stand on a high chair, with a toy work-box of white varnished wood at hand. She held a shred of handkerchief and professed to hem it, boring with a needle which, in her fingers, looked almost a skewer. Ever and anon she pricked herself; the cambric showed a track of minute red dots; sometimes she started when the perverse weapon, swerving from her control, drove in deeper than usual. Yet she remained silent, diligent, absorbed—strangely womanly in her patience, as if pain were only another stitch to be taken and hidden.

Graham at that time was a handsome, faithless-looking youth of sixteen. I call him faithless-looking not because he was truly perfidious, but because the word seems proper to the fair Celtic cast of his good looks: waved light auburn hair; supple symmetry; a smile frequent, fascinating, and not without subtlety—subtlety, however, in no bad sense. In those days he was spoiled and whimsical.

After watching the little figure in silence awhile, and when Mr. Home’s temporary absence relieved him from the half-laughing bashfulness which was all he knew of timidity, he said—

“Mother, I see a young lady in the present society to whom I have not been introduced.”

“Mr. Home’s little girl, I suppose you mean,” said Mrs. Bretton.

“Indeed, ma’am,” replied her son, “I consider your expression of the least ceremonious. Miss Home, I should certainly have said, in venturing to speak of the gentlewoman to whom I allude.”

“Now, Graham, I will not have that child teased. Don’t flatter yourself that I shall suffer you to make her your butt.”

“Miss Home,” pursued Graham, undeterred, “might I have the honour to introduce myself, since no one else seems willing to render you and me that service? Your slave, John Graham Bretton.”

She looked at him. He rose and bowed quite gravely. The fire made a soft roar; the clock kept its careful beat; outside, the street’s murmur came faint, as if the house were holding it back. The child, after a pause as solemn as if she weighed his worth like flour, put down thimble, scissors, and work. She descended with precaution from her perch, and curtsying with unspeakable seriousness, said—

“How do you do?”

“I have the honour to be in fair health, only in some measure fatigued with a hurried journey. I hope, ma’am, I see you well?”

“Tor-rer-ably well,” replied the little woman, ambitious of a grand pronunciation.

She essayed to regain her former elevation; but the climbing and straining required would have been a sacrifice of decorum not to be thought of, and she was utterly disdainful of aid in the presence of this strange young gentleman. She relinquished the high chair and took a low stool instead. Toward that low stool Graham drew his own chair, as if he meant to conduct a courtship of conversation—absurd in its object, yet sincere in his amusement.

“I hope, ma’am, the present residence—my mother’s house—appears to you a convenient place of abode?”

“Not par-tic-er-er-ly; I want to go home.”

“A natural and laudable desire, ma’am; but one which, notwithstanding, I shall do my best to oppose. I reckon on being able to get out of you a little of that precious commodity called amusement, which mamma and Mistress Snowe there fail to yield me.”

“I shall have to go with papa soon. I shall not stay long at your mother’s.”

“Yes, yes; you will stay with me, I am sure. I have a pony on which you shall ride, and no end of books with pictures to show you.”

“Are you going to live here now?”

“I am. Does that please you? Do you like me?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“I think you queer.”

“My face, ma’am?”

“Your face and all about you. You have long red hair.”

“Auburn hair, if you please. Mamma calls it auburn, or golden, and so do all her friends.”

And he waved his mane with a sort of triumph—tawny it was, and he was proud of the leonine hue.

“But even with my ‘long red hair,’ I cannot possibly be queerer than is your ladyship.”

“You call me queer?”

“Certainly.”

After a pause, she announced, as if pronouncing sentence—

“I think I shall go to bed.”

“A little thing like you ought to have been in bed many hours since; but you probably sat up in the expectation of seeing me?”

“No, indeed.”

“You certainly wished to enjoy the pleasure of my society. You knew I was coming home, and would wait to have a look at me.”

“I sat up for papa, and not for you.”

“Very good, Miss Home. I am going to be a favourite—preferred before papa soon, I daresay.”

She wished Mrs. Bretton and myself good-night. She hesitated whether Mr. Graham’s deserts entitled him to the same civility; and in that instant he caught her up with one hand and held her poised aloft above his head.

She saw herself thus lifted, small and affronted, in the glass over the fireplace—the suddenness, the freedom, the disrespect of it all flashing upon her like cold water.

“For shame, Mr. Graham!” she cried, indignant beyond her years. “Put me down!”

When her feet touched the floor again, she drew herself up and lifted her hand—mighty in her own eyes.

“I wonder what you would think of me if I were to treat you in that way—lifting you with my hand—as Warren lifts the little cat.”

So saying, she departed.
