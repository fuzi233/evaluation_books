Before ever Lydia Van den Bosch’s little foot had tapped out its music along the boards of our tale, there is a marriage—like a sudden clap in clear weather—that must be owned and set in its place; else Mr. Warrington’s letter, and that casual line in it touching Lady Maria Esmond, hangs in the air like steam without a pot.

Lady Maria—of ancient blood, practiced manners, and an experience the town chose to call “mature”—had, in the teeth of her kin and in the teeth of the world (or, to speak more truly, behind its back), taken for husband Mr. Geoghegan, commonly called Hagan, the young Irish actor who had lately blazed in George’s tragedy. Parson Sampson, at his little Mayfair chapel, fastened the knot privily, as if it were a ribbon on a bodice and must not be seen.

And why should a woman, because she has once loved amiss, be sentenced to starve ever after? People will forgive a man his second appetite as readily as they forgive him his first; but a lady, if she but look twice at the same dish, is cried upon as if she had stolen it. Yet the truth of such hearts—clinging hearts, ivy-hearts that must have a wall—is that when the first support is pulled away, they will wind themselves upon another. It is not philosophy; it is hunger. It is the body remembering warmth; it is the mind craving a familiar voice in the room, the kettle’s homely murmur, the chair drawn close to the hearth. We may talk as we will of constancy; but the blood keeps its own calendar, and the hand will reach out again, even after it has been burned.

Only—this time the reaching was a scandal, because the hand reached down.

Hagan, in his gilt leather armour and buckled periwig, had spoken the poet’s lines as if they were his own breath: his tones struck through the house like a bow drawn across a string; his eye shone; his bearing had that brave counterfeit of nobility which the stage can lend a man so convincingly that real lords look like extras beside him. Lady Maria, who had once yielded her heart as readily as she yielded her tears, gave herself up to him with a headlong tenderness, and he—young, flattered, half frightened at the greatness of the prize—took her too, though perhaps he would have preferred the same ladyship in a younger edition.

When the thing could no longer be hidden, and her furious relations disowned her, she did not take a fine lodging and brood in velvet; she went straight into the actor’s poor rooms at Westminster, and sat down—like some high-born bird that has missed its migration—among common sparrows and common crumbs.

General Lambert brought the news home from Whitehall, and, being a good soldier of that homely age, made a jest of it at his own table, where there was always honest noise enough—the clink of a spoon on a plate, the scrape of a chair, the rustle of a gown as mamma rose to order something, the crackle of coals—those domestic sounds that keep a household in tune. He ran on with broad waggeries, the merrier for being a trifle rude: George’s share in it; Barry’s rage when he should hear; the notion of George standing as groomsman; the first baby christened after some hero or heroine of the tragedy; dragons hired from Mr. Rich’s theatre to draw my lady’s chariot; and a christening done in Macbeth’s cauldron, if the parents had a taste for it.

Little Hetty, who had been brought up among kitchens, gardens, and plain kindnesses, broke in with her quick, right-minded heat.

“Why shouldn’t she marry him if she likes him?” she asked. “Why should he not love her because she is a little old? Mamma is a little old, and you love her none the worse. When you married my mamma, sir, you have said you were very poor; and yet you were very happy, and nobody laughed at you!”

So spoke the child, because youth measures scandal by the heart and not by the heralds’ book.

“So her family has deserted her?” Hetty went on, indignation rising in her like yeast.

George told them how wrathful they were; how Lady Castlewood had gone into mourning as though for a death; how Mr. Will swore he would have the rascal’s ears; how Madame de Bernstein—most fierce of all—flamed and stormed as if the marriage were a personal affront to the Almighty.

“It is an insult to the family,” said Hetty, with that little dignity she had, “and I can fancy how ladies of that rank must be indignant at their relative’s marriage with a person of Mr. Hagan’s condition; but to desert her is a very different matter.”

“Indeed, my dear child,” cried mamma, alarmed, “you are talking of what you don’t understand. After my Lady Maria’s conduct, no respectable person can go to see her.”

“What conduct, mamma?”

“Never mind,” mamma said quickly, with that frightened severity which hides more than it corrects. “Little girls ought not to be curious. Suffice it, miss, that I am shocked her ladyship should ever have been here; and I say again, no honest person should associate with her!”

“Then, Aunt Lambert, I must be whipped and sent to bed,” said George, with mock gravity, “for I own to you—though I did not confess sooner, seeing the affair was not mine—that I have been to see my cousin the player, and her ladyship his wife.”

And he described, with that half-laughing tenderness which made his sarcasm less bitter, the dirty lodgings in Westminster; the shabbiness of it all; the old mother living there too; the little brother whom Hagan put to school. Yet George confessed that he came away liking the fellow, almost respecting him, though the marriage was as improvident as a cook who has barely flour enough for a loaf and yet invites a regiment to supper.

General Lambert growled, “Improvident, if they marry such spendthrifts as you. Master George found his relations, and I’ll be bound he left his purse behind him.”

“No, not the purse, sir,” George said, smiling in that way of his which always seemed to warm the room. “Theo made that. But I am bound to own it came empty away. Mr. Rich is in great dudgeon. He says he hardly dares have Hagan on his stage, and is afraid of a riot—like Mr. Garrick had about the foreign dancers. This is to be a fine gentleman’s riot. The macaronis vow they will pelt Mr. Hagan, and have him cudgelled afterwards. My cousin Will, at Arthur’s, has taken his oath he will have the actor’s ears. Meanwhile, as the poor man does not play, they have cut off his salary; and without his salary, this luckless pair of lovers have no means to buy bread and cheese.”

Theo’s eyes worshipped him the more because charity sat on him as naturally as a well-cut coat. “And you took it to them, sir? It was like you, George!”

“It was your purse took it, dear Theo,” he answered.

“Mamma, I hope you will go and see them to-morrow!” Theo pleaded.

“If she doesn’t, I shall get a divorce, my dear!” cried the General, roaring with laughter; and called for kisses, and dubbed his future son-in-law a fiddlestick; and so, amid clattering plates and bright talk, the great scandal of the town was reduced, for that hour, to a family jest and a household resolution to be kind.

But in the world outside—the world that listens less to the kettle and more to the coffee-house—the marriage rang like a cracked bell.

Any man who has made an imprudent match knows the gauntlet: the frozen calls, the sermons, the pity that is only another form of scorn. And if decent people cry out because you marry the curate’s daughter, or a young barrister with nothing but his reports and his debts, what must Lady Maria Hagan’s friends have felt, and even what must Mr. Hagan’s have muttered, when a fine lady with courtly habits came to take her seat at a table where the joints were counted and the tea measured by spoonfuls?

Begin with old Mrs. Hagan. Her son, since he left Trinity College at his father’s death and made himself Romeo at Crow Street, had kept her dutifully: fire in the grate, a bit of mutton on Sundays, tea that smelt of real leaf, not of hay; a coat for the little brother; a little help for the sister gone out as companion. There is a kind of household economy which is not merely arithmetic but ritual: the same pot rinsed and set to dry; the same small loaf cut carefully so the last slice may look like the first; the same silence at night broken only by the ember’s sigh and the far-off patrol. Into such a place Lady Maria descended—bringing, with her soft hands and grand manner, needs and expectations as real to her as hunger is to a poor woman. Love, especially romantic love, never counts the chairs at the table before it invites itself to sit; never asks whether there is cream enough for two.

So there she was, taking, innocently enough, the cream off the milk, the wings of the chicken, the best half of everything where there had been barely enough before. No wonder the poor old mother-in-law grumbled. Yet her private growling was nothing beside the public howl at Kensington.

Think of the acoustic storm of it—the whispering in doorways, the titter like knives on china at Court where Lady Fanny attended; the jokes at the officers’ table; the waggeries in prints and magazines; the outraged prudes clacking like geese; the laughter of clubs, loud as a drum. Madame de Bernstein fell into fits and fled to the solitude of Tunbridge Wells—where she only saw forty people of a night at cards, and called that retirement. My lord refused to see his sister. Lady Castlewood, in mourning, went to a gracious Princess who was pleased to condole with her upon the disgrace. For three whole days the town kept the marriage on its tongue as it keeps a new sauce—tasting it, praising it, spitting it out; and then other news came—victory in Germany, doubtful accounts from America, a general officer home to take his trial, an exquisite new Italian soprano—and the town, greedy for the next dish, forgot Lady Maria in her garret, eating the hard-earned meal of the actor’s family.

George Warrington wrote to his brother of a visit paid to the newly married pair. The letter had that same interweaving of war and hearth, of distant cannon and near tea-cups, which makes a family’s life, in any century, one long braid.

“My dearest little Theo,” he said, “was eager to accompany her mamma upon this errand of charity; but I thought Aunt Lambert’s visit would be best under the circumstances, and without the attendance of her little spinster aide-de-camp. Cousin Hagan was out when we called; we found her ladyship in a loose undress, and with her hair in not the neatest papers, playing at cribbage with a neighbour from the second floor, while good Mrs. Hagan sate on the other side of the fire with a glass of punch, and the Whole Duty of Man.

“Maria—your Maria once—cried a little when she saw us; and Aunt Lambert, you may be sure, was ready with her sympathy. While she bestowed it on Lady Maria, I paid the best compliments I could invent to the old lady. When the conversation between Aunt L. and the bride began to flag, I turned to the latter, and between us we did our best to make a dreary interview pleasant.

“Our talk was about you, about Wolfe, about war; you must be engaged face to face with the Frenchmen by this time, and God send my dearest brother safe and victorious out of the battle! Be sure we follow your steps anxiously—we fancy you at Cape Breton. We have plans of Quebec, and charts of the St. Lawrence. Shall I ever forget your face of joy that day when you saw me return safe and sound from the little combat with the little Frenchman? So will my Harry, I know, return from his battle. I feel quite assured of it; elated somehow with the prospect of your certain success and safety. And I have made all here share my cheerfulness. We talk of the campaign as over, and Captain Warrington’s promotion as secure. Pray Heaven, all our hopes may be fulfilled one day ere long.

“How strange it is that you who are the mettlesome fellow (you know you are) should escape quarrels hitherto, and I, who am a peaceful youth, wishing no harm to anybody, should have battles thrust upon me! What do you think actually of my having had another affair upon my wicked hands, and with whom, think you? With no less a personage than your old enemy, our kinsman, Mr. Will.

“What or who set him to quarrel with me, I cannot think. Spencer (who acted as second for me, for matters actually have gone this length—don’t be frightened; it is all over, and nobody is a scratch the worse) thinks some one set Will on me, but who, I say? His conduct has been most singular; his behaviour quite unbearable.

“We have met pretty frequently lately at the house of good Mr. Van den Bosch, whose pretty granddaughter was consigned to both of us by our good mother. Oh, dear mother! did you know that the little thing was to be such a causa belli, and to cause swords to be drawn, and precious lives to be menaced? But so it has been.

“To show his own spirit, I suppose, or having some reasonable doubt about mine, whenever Will and I have met at Mynheer’s house—and he is for ever going there—he has shown such downright rudeness to me, that I have required more than ordinary patience to keep my temper. He has contradicted me once, twice, thrice in the presence of the family, and out of sheer spite and rage, as it appeared to me.

“Is he paying his addresses to Miss Lydia, and her father’s ships, negroes, and forty thousand pounds? I should guess so. The old gentleman is for ever talking about his money, and adores his granddaughter, and as she is a beautiful little creature, numbers of folk here are ready to adore her too. Was Will rascal enough to fancy that I would give up my Theo for a million of guineas, and negroes, and Venus to boot? Could the thought of such baseness enter into the man’s mind? I don’t know that he has accused me of stealing Van den Bosch’s spoons and tankards when we dine there, or of robbing on the highway. But for one reason or the other he has chosen to be jealous of me; and as I have parried his impertinences with little sarcastic speeches (though perfectly civil before company), perhaps I have once or twice made him angry. Our little Miss Lydia has unwittingly added fuel to the fire on more than one occasion, especially yesterday, when there was talk about your worship.

“‘Ah!’ says the heedless little thing, as we sat over our dessert, ‘’tis lucky for you, Mr. Esmond, that Captain Harry is not here.’

“‘Why, miss?’ asks he, with one of his usual conversational ornaments, and a volley of oaths.

“‘O fie!’ says she, putting up the prettiest little fingers to the prettiest little rosy ears in the world. ‘O fie, sir! to use such naughty words. ’Tis lucky the Captain is not here, because he might quarrel with you; and Mr. George is so peaceable and quiet, that he won’t. Have you heard from the Captain, Mr. George?’

“‘From Cape Breton,’ says I. ‘He is very well, thank you; that is—’ I could not finish, for I was in such a rage I scarce could contain myself.

“‘From the Captain, as you call him, Miss Lyddy,’ says Will. ‘He’ll distinguish himself as he did at Saint Cas! Ho, ho!’

“‘So I apprehend he did, sir,’ says I.

“‘Did he?’ says our dear cousin; ‘always thought he ran away; took to his legs; got a ducking, and ran away as if a bailiff was after him.’

“‘La!’ says Miss, ‘did the Captain ever have a bailiff after him?’

“‘Didn’t he? Ho, ho!’ laughs Mr. Will.

“I suppose I must have looked very savage, for Spencer, who was dining with us, trod on my foot under the table. ‘Don’t laugh so loud, cousin,’ I said, very gently; ‘you may wake good old Mr. Van den Bosch.’ The good old gentleman was asleep in his arm-chair, to which he commonly retires for a nap after dinner—one of those deep, satisfied sleeps a man takes when his cellar is full and his table well served.

“‘Oh, indeed, cousin,’ says Will; and he turns and winks at a friend of his, Captain Deuceace, whom Will has introduced into this simple family as a man of the highest fashion. ‘Don’t be afraid, miss,’ says Mr. Will, ‘nor my cousin needn’t be.’

“‘Oh, what a comfort!’ cries Miss Lyddy. ‘Keep quite quiet, gentlemen, and don’t quarrel, and come up to me when I send to say the tea is ready.’ And with this she makes a sweet little curtsey, and disappears—leaving behind her, like a spice-trail, the faint promise of Bohea and buttered bread.

“‘Hang it, Jack, pass the bottle, and don’t wake the old gentleman!’ continues Mr. Will. ‘Won’t you help yourself, cousin?’—being particularly facetious in the tone of that word.

“‘I am going to help myself,’ I said; ‘but I am not going to drink the glass; and I’ll tell you what I am going to do with it, if you will be quite quiet, cousin.’ (Desperate kicks from Spencer all this time.)

“‘And what the deuce do I care what you are going to do with it?’ asks Will, looking rather white.

“‘I am going to fling it into your face, cousin,’ says I—and did so, very rapidly.

“‘By Jove, and no mistake!’ cries Mr. Deuceace; and as he and William roared out an oath together, good old Van den Bosch woke up, and, taking the pocket-handkerchief off his face, asked what was the matter.

“I remarked it was only a glass of wine gone the wrong way; and the old man said, ‘Well, well, there is more where that came from! Let the butler bring you what you please, young gentlemen!’ and he sank back in his great chair, and began to sleep again—money and meat lulling him better than any sermon.

“‘From the back of Montagu House Gardens there is a beautiful view of Hampstead at six o’clock in the morning; and the statue of the King on St. George’s Church is reckoned elegant, cousin!’ says I.

“‘D— the statue!’ begins Will; but I said, ‘Don’t, cousin! or you will wake up the old gentleman. Had we not best go upstairs to Miss Lyddy’s tea-table?’

“We arranged a little meeting for the next morning; and a coroner might have been sitting upon one or other, or both, of our bodies this afternoon; but, would you believe it? just as our engagement was about to take place, we were interrupted by three of Sir John Fielding’s men, and carried to Bow Street, and ignominiously bound over to keep the peace.”
