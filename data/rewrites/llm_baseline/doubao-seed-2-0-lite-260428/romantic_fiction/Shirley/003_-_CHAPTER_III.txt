Cheerfulness, it turns out, relies just as much on the fire burning in a man’s breast as it does on the sun in the sky above him. I note this obvious little truth only because I happen to know full well that Messrs. Helstone and Moore trotted out of the mill-yard gates at the head of their tiny rescue party in the highest of spirits. When the glow of one of the three pedestrian party’s lanterns caught Mr. Moore’s face, you could spot a rare, lively spark dancing in his dark eyes, a new, bright vivacity warming his sharp, dusky features. And when the light fell on the rector, his normally hard, severe face was split wide with a grin, all ashine with unrepentant glee.

You’d think a drizzling, bone-chilling night and a foolhardy, perilous expedition wouldn’t be circumstances to liven up men soaked to the skin and riding straight into trouble. Any of the rioters who’d been working the frame-breaking job on Stilbro’ Moor would have been only too happy to shoot both leaders dead from behind a stone wall, and both men knew it perfectly. The fact of it, instead of damping their mood, stoked their high spirits all the higher: they were both made of steely nerves and steady, unshaking hearts, and the risk of it all made them feel more alive than they had in months.

I know, reader—you needn’t remind me that a parson is meant to be a man of peace, not a warrior. I haven’t forgotten a clergyman’s mission, who he serves, whose message he carries. But if you hate parsons, don’t expect me to follow you down that bitter, narrow road of blind rage against the cloth. Mr. Helstone was not diabolical, not by a long shot. He had simply missed his calling: he ought to have been a soldier, and circumstance had made him a priest instead. For the rest, he was conscientious, hard-headed, brave, stern, loyal to a fault, and almost entirely without soft, yielding sympathy—ungentle, prejudiced, rigid, but unwaveringly true to his principles, honourable, sharp, and sincere. Men aren’t always carved perfectly to fit their professions, and there’s no sense cursing a man because his vocation sits a little awkwardly on his shoulders.

Helstone was cursed by half his parishioners, though, and adored by the other half—the common fate of men who love fiercely and hate fiercely, who cling as hard to their prejudices as they do to their core beliefs. You’d think two men in such good spirits, united in a single cause, would ride side by side chatting amicably. Not these two. Both with sharp, bilious natures, they rarely came into contact without chafing each other’s tempers raw. Their favourite point of contention was the war. Helstone was a staunch high Tory (Tories were still thick on the ground in those days), and Moore was a bitter, unapologetic Whig, at least on the question of the war that was ruining his mill and starving his workforce—he cared nothing for other British political debates otherwise. He loved to infuriate Helstone by declaring Bonaparte unbeatable, taunting England and Europe for their useless efforts to stand against him, arguing it made more sense to yield sooner than later when Bonaparte would inevitably crush all his enemies and rule the continent.

Helstone could barely stand to hear the words. The only reason he didn’t cane Moore on the spot was the knowledge that Moore was a half-foreign outcast, only half British, the rest of his blood made sharp and strange by the foreign roots he couldn’t shake. He also had a sneaking, grudging respect for the dogged, unyielding tone with which Moore stated his unpopular opinions.

As the party turned onto the Stilbro’ road, the wind hit them full in the face, rain lashing hard against their cheeks. Moore had been teasing his companion all the while, and now, braced by the raw chill of the breeze, he began to goad him in earnest.
“Does your Peninsular news still sit well with you?” he asked.
“What do you mean?” the rector snapped, surly.
“I mean, do you still have faith in that Baal you call Lord Wellington?”
“And what on earth are you on about now?”
“Do you still believe that wooden-faced, pebble-hearted idol of England has the power to send fire down from heaven to burn up the French army you’re so eager to throw yourself against?”
“I believe Wellington will flog Bonaparte’s marshals straight into the sea the day he lifts a finger to do it,” Helstone growled.
“My dear sir, you can’t be serious. Bonaparte’s marshals are great men, guided by a master spirit that’s omnipotent. Your Wellington is nothing but a plodding, commonplace martinet, his slow, mechanical movements further crippled by an idiot home government back in London.”
“Wellington is the soul of England. He is the champion of a just cause, the perfect representative of a powerful, resolute, sensible, honest nation.”
“Your just cause, as far as I can tell, is just putting that filthy, useless Ferdinand back on a throne he disgraced. Your representative of an honest people is a dull-witted drover, taking orders from a duller-witted farmer—and against them you have victory and unbeatable genius arrayed on the other side.”
“Against legitimacy stands usurpation. Against modest, righteous, brave resistance to greed stands boastful, double-tongued, selfish ambition to steal what is not theirs. God defends the right!”
“God often defends the powerful.”
“What? Do you think the handful of Israelites standing dryshod on the far side of the Red Sea were more powerful than Pharaoh’s army drawn up on the other? More numerous? Better armed? They were a ragged, overwrought band of bondsmen, oppressed for four hundred years, their ranks thinned by women and children. Their masters, roaring to follow them through the divided flood, were pampered, brutal men, armed, horsed, riding chariots, while the Hebrews had nothing but shepherds’ crooks and masons’ tools. Their own great leader carried nothing but a rod. But right was on their side. The God of battles was on their side. Pharaoh’s army drowned to the last man. ‘The depths covered them, they sank to the bottom as a stone.’ The Lord’s right hand dashed the enemy to pieces.”
“You’re all right—only you got the parallel backwards. France is Israel. Napoleon is Moses. Europe, with its bloated old empires and rotten dynasties, is corrupt Egypt. Gallant, young France is the Twelve Tribes, and her vigorous ‘usurper’ is the shepherd of Horeb.”
“I scorn to answer you.”
Moore answered himself anyway, muttering under his breath. “Oh, in Italy he was as great as any Moses! He was exactly the man to organize the regeneration of nations. It still puzzles me how the conqueror of Lodi could stoop to become a petty, stupid emperor, a vulgar fraud. And how a people who once called themselves republicans could sink back so shamelessly into slavery. I despise them. If England had marched as far towards civilization as France did, she’d never have retreated that far.”
“You don’t mean to say that besotted imperial France is any worse than bloody republican France?” Helstone demanded, furious.
“I don’t have to say anything,” Moore smiled dryly. “I can think what I please, you know—about France, about England, about revolutions, regicides, restorations, the divine right of kings you love to preach about, the duty of non-resistance, the utter insanity of this whole war, and—”

His sentence was cut short by the rapid rumble of a gig rolling up fast, stopping dead in the middle of the road. Both men had been so wrapped up in their argument they hadn’t heard it approaching until it was almost upon them.
“Nah, maister—did th’ wagons hit home all safe?” a voice called from the vehicle.
“Is that Joe Scott?”
“Ay, ay!” a second voice called back. The three pedestrians with lanterns had fallen far behind, the two riders having outpaced them easily, and the gig’s lamp cast a warm glow over two men huddled inside. “Ay, Mr. Moore, it’s Joe Scott, and I’m bringing him back to you in a right bonny pickle. I found him up on the moor, him and three of his lads. What’ll you give me for restoring him safe and sound?”
“Why, my heartfelt thanks,” Moore laughed. “I can ill afford to lose a man as good as Joe. That’s you, Mr. Yorke, isn’t it? I’d know that voice anywhere.”
“Ay, lad, it’s me. I was coming home from Stilbro’ market, whipping the pony on as fast as he’d go—they say these aren’t safe times, thanks to a useless government!—when I heard a groan right out of the heather. Some folks would have spurred faster, but I’ve naught to fear. Not a lad in these parts would harm me, and if they tried I’d give them as good as I got. I called out, ‘Is there something wrong there?’ ‘Deed there is,’ someone answered, like he was speaking out of the ground. ‘What’s happened? Speak sharp,’ I told them. ‘Nobbut four of us lying in a ditch,’ says Joe, as calm as you like. I told them they ought to be ashamed of themselves, bid them get up and move or I’d lash them with my gig whip, for I could see they weren’t hurt bad. ‘We’d ha’ been gone an hour sin’, but our legs are tied tight with bands,’ Joe says. So I got down, cut all their bonds loose with my penknife, and Scott rode back with me to tell me the whole tale. The other three are trundling along behind as fast as their feet can carry them.”
“Well, I’m deeply obliged to you, Mr. Yorke.”
“Are you, my lad? You know you’re not, but no matter. Look, here come the rest of your lads. And by the Lord, there’s another group coming with their lights bobbing like Gideon’s army, and we’ve got the parson with us—good evening, Mr. Helstone—we’ve got eleven strong men between us, horses and a gig and all. If we run into those starved ragamuffin frame-breakers, we could win a grand victory. Every last one of us could be a Wellington, just like you want, Mr. Helstone! We’d write such a grand spread for the *Stilbro’ Courier* that Briarfield would be famous. I dare say we’ll get a full column and a half out of this night’s work at the very least.”
“And I’ll promise you no less,” the rector grinned. “I’ll write the article myself.”
“Sartain you will! And mind you recommend that the lot who broke your frames and tied Joe Scott’s legs up hang without benefit of clergy. It’s a hanging offence, no two ways about it.”
“If I was judging them, I’d give them short shrift,” Moore put in. “But this time, I mean to leave them well alone, give them enough rope to hang themselves before long.”
“Leave them alone, do ye, Moore? Do you promise that?”
“Promise? No. I only mean I won’t go out of my way to hunt them down. But if one of them happens to stumble across my path—”
“You’ll snap him up, of course. You’d rather they do something worse than just stop a wagon before you settle scores. Well, we’ll say no more about it now. Here we are at my door, gentlemen. You and your lads will all step in and have a bite and a warm drink, won’t you?”

Moore and Helstone tried to protest, but Yorke pressed the invitation so warmly, the night was so raw and miserable, and the soft golden glow spilling out of his muslin-curtained windows looked so inviting, that they gave in at last. Mr. Yorke hopped down from the gig, left the pony in the care of a man who came out of an outbuilding, and led the way inside.

You might have noticed Mr. Yorke slips between broad Yorkshire brogue and crisp, perfect formal English without even noticing. His manners flip just as fast: one moment blunt and rough as a moor stone, the next warm, affable, and refined. His station wasn’t easy to guess from his speech, but the inside of his house told the whole story. He sent the workmen off to the kitchen, promising them a hearty supper, then led Helstone and Moore through the front door into a matted hall lined almost to the ceiling with paintings, then into a great parlour with a magnificent roaring fire blazing in the grate. There was no gaudy show of wealth, but taste was everywhere—the kind of taste belonging to a travelled man, a scholar, a gentleman. Italian pastoral landscapes decked the walls, each a genuine, carefully selected work of art, their sunny skies and soft blue distant hills glowing even in candlelight. A guitar and a stack of sheet music rested on the sofa, cameos and beautiful miniatures lined the mantel, a set of elegant Grecian vases sat at either end, and two polished bookcases held rows of well-loved, neatly arranged books.

Yorke bade them sit, rang for wine, and gave the servant orders to feed the men in the kitchen. The rector stayed standing, stiff as a board, clearly not comfortable in his host’s home, and refused the glass of wine offered him.
“Suit yourself,” Yorke shrugged. “I reckon you’re thinking of old Eastern customs, Mr. Helstone, that you won’t eat or drink under my roof for fear we’d be forced to be friends. But I’m not that superstitious. You could drink that whole decanter, give me a bottle of the very best claret from your own cellar, and I’d still fight you tooth and nail at every vestry meeting and magistrate’s session we happen to cross paths at.”
“That’s exactly what I’d expect of you, Mr. Yorke.”
“Does it suit you, a man your age, to be tramping out after rioters on a wet night like this?”
“Doing my duty always suits me. And this particular duty is a pleasure. Hunting down vermin is a noble occupation, fit even for an archbishop.”
“Fit for you, at any rate. But where’s your curate? Did he run off to visit some sick parishioner, or is he out hunting vermin in another direction?”
“He’s on garrison duty down at Hollow’s Mill.”
“I hope you left him a dram of wine to keep his courage up, Bob,” Yorke said, turning to Moore, who had flopped into a deep old oak chair by the fireside. “Move, Robert! Get up, lad! That seat’s mine. Take the sofa, or any three other chairs you like, but not that one. It belongs to me, and nobody else.”
“Why are you so fussy over that one chair, Mr. Yorke?” Moore laughed, lazily standing up to oblige him.
“My father sat in it before me. That’s all the answer you’ll get, and it’s a better reason than Mr. Helstone has for most of his silly little opinions.”
“Moore, are you ready to leave?” the rector asked.
“Nay, Robert’s not ready, and I’m not ready to let him go. He’s a bad lad, and needs a good talking to.”
“Why, sir? What have I done?”
“Made enemies of every last soul within ten miles of you.”
“What do I care? What difference does it make if your Yorkshire louts hate me or like me?”
“Ay, there it is. The lad’s a foreigner through and through, outcast among us. His father would never have talked like that. Go back to Antwerp, where you were born and bred, mauvaise tête!”
“Mauvaise tête vous-même; je ne fais que mon devoir; quant à vos lourdauds de paysans, je m'en moque!”
“En revanche, mon garçon, nos lourdauds de paysans se moqueront de toi; sois en certain,” Yorke replied, his French almost as perfect and natural as Moore’s own, the accent spotless.

They bantered back and forth in that quick, rolling tongue, old, half-forgotten grievances from Moore’s childhood in Antwerp rising to the surface, until Yorke fell quiet, leaning back in his heavy carved three-cornered oak chair. And now, while he sits there, warm firelight gilding his sharp, witty face, I will pause to paint the full portrait of this contradictory, French-speaking Yorkshire gentleman.
