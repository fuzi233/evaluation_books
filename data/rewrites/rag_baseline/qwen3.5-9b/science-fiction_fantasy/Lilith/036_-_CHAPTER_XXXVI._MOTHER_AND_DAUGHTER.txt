"Disgusted," Lona spat, the word tasting like bile. "With these people, and specifically the women. I want to burn this place to the ground and ride out before the sun sets."

Her desire for flight cut through the air like a knife, sharp and desperate. I held her gaze, my expression hardening into something she would not recognize as maternal tenderness, but rather as the cold resolve of a commander facing a breached fortress.

"To retreat now is not escape, Lona. It is surrender. It is a voluntary failure where victory remains within our grasp," I said, my voice low and steady. "Far worse, it shatters the spirit of the Little Ones. Their morale collapses, and they become prey. If we withdraw, the Princess will hunt us down without hesitation. If we flee, the prophecy dies with us."

I stepped closer, forcing her to confront the gravity of the moment. "Mother and Daughter *must* meet. Perhaps your beauty will strike the Princess with such force that her heart turns against her own ambition. If she draws steel, I shall stand between you both. Should I find myself powerless to stop her, I will strike her hand with a blade, severing the threat to ensure your safety. I know the outcome is sealed; fate has decreed that our doom arrives today. But we fight until the last breath."

Without a flicker of doubt regarding the ancient bond binding me to the Princess—a truth I kept hidden from her—I laid out the strategy. Lona's eyes widened, the disgust fading into a dawning realization of duty. She nodded, agreeing instantly to follow me to the palace.

High atop one of the great towers, the Princess had watched the dawn break over the sleeping city. She had seen the dust clouds rising—the approach of the Little Ones' army. Terror, primal and overwhelming, seized her. Her attempt to annihilate them had failed; they were upon her. The prophecy was fulfilling itself in real-time.

When reason returned, she descended to the Black Hall, seeking the North Focus of the ellipse beneath the roof's opening. She needed to think. Thinking, for her, required a specific state of being: not the reality of her current form, but the idealized version she chose to inhabit. To facilitate this delusion, she had suspended a mirror above her, invisible in the gloom, angled to catch the direct rays of the meridian sun.

She waited. Shadows danced in the periphery, but she possessed an inner eye that allowed her to ignore them. Even the Shadow that accompanied her walks remained unseen to her, too absorbed in its own obscurity. Outside, the city crumbled into panic; the inhabitants cowered as the Little Ones and their strange cavalry encamped in the square. The sun climbed higher, bathing the Princess in a false glory. For a fleeting minute, the vision took hold, and she saw herself radiant and untouchable. Then the light shifted, and the vision shattered.

Night fell, cloaking the glass ceiling in darkness. Yet she did not move. A suffocating gloom, teeming with spectral entities, filled the hall. Servants shivered, paralyzed by the beasts outside, refusing to leave the chamber where the Princess sat motionless, starving for the sun's return. Courage and will had drained from her; they could no longer sustain her.

At dawn, we selected twelve of the Little Ones—tall, brave boys—to escort us. We mounted our great horses, alongside the smaller mounts and the war-elephants. The Princess remained in the hall, waiting for the sun to grant her the illusion of her own divinity. The tide of light crept up the eastern horizon, but until the sun stood directly overhead, no ray could penetrate the black stone.

Then, it happened. The sun rose, ascending swiftly toward zenith. As we climbed the steep path to the palace, the celestial body climbed the dome of the great hall. It peered through the central aperture—and suddenly, the hall flooded with brilliance.

The Princess screamed. She sprang to her feet, a cry of absolute despair tearing from her throat. "Alas!" she wailed. "My whiteness!"

A massive black spot, covering half her side, was revealed against the marble floor. It was as dark as the stone itself. Clutching her robes, she collapsed back into her chair. The Shadow glided silently across the floor and vanished into the darkness.

We found the gate open as usual, passing through the paved grove to the palace door and entering the vestibule. There, in her cage, lay the spotted leopardess, seemingly lifeless. The Little Ones paused, mesmerized. Suddenly, the beast leaped, rampant against the bars. The horses reared and plunged; the elephants recoiled. In the next instant, the leopardess fell supine, writhing in quivering spasms before going utterly still.

We rode into the Great Hall. The Princess still leaned back in her chair, bathed in the shaft of sunlight, when the sound of hooves erupted from the courtyard. She started, listening, shaking. Never had such a cacophony been heard within her sanctuary. She pressed a hand to her side and gasped. The trampling drew nearer, invading the hall itself. Figures that were not shadows emerged from the darkness, approaching her.

For us, the scene transformed into a spectacle of splendor. A glorious woman centered the darkness, radiating power. Lona spurred her horse forward, bounding from the mount to reach her. I followed suit, springing from my steed.

"Mother! Mother!" Lona cried, her clear, lovely voice echoing off the domed ceiling.

The Princess shivered violently. Her face darkened with hate, her eyebrows knitting together until they touched. Rising to her feet, she stood rigid.

"Mother! Mother!" Lona shouted again, leaping onto the dais and throwing her arms around the Princess.

In that split second, I would have reached them—but I saw Lona lifted high, then dashed against the marble floor. The sound was horrific, a crack of bone meeting stone. She fell at my feet, lying still.

The Princess sat down, wearing the smile of a demoness. I dropped to my knees beside Lona, lifting her from the cold stones and pressing her to my bosom. Indignant hate burned in my eyes as I glanced at the Princess, but she answered with her sweetest, most venomous smile. Love for the child overwhelmed my hatred for the mother. I tightened my grip on my precious burden. Her arms hung limp; blood trickled over my hands, falling to the floor in soft, slow plashes.

The horses smelled it first—my mount, then the others. Mine reared, shivering and wild-eyed, thundering blindly down the dark hall, dragging the smaller horses in its wake. Lona's horse stood frozen, gazing down at his mistress, trembling uncontrollably. The boys threw themselves from their saddles, failing to see the black wall ahead, and crashed against it along with my own mount, reduced to fragments.

The elephants advanced to the foot of the dais, stopping abruptly, trumpeting wildly. The Little Ones sprang upon them, standing in horror. The Princess slumped back into her seat, her face that of a corpse, her eyes alone alive, wickedly flaming. She was withered and wasted, returning to the state I had found her in the woods, the black brand on her side unmistakable.

But Lona saw nothing. I saw only Lona.

"Mother... mother..." she sighed, her breathing ceasing.

I carried her into the courtyard. The sun illuminated a pale face, marked by the pitiful shadow of a ghostly smile. Her head hung back. She was dead as earth.

I forgot the Little Ones. I forgot the murdering Princess. I forgot the body in my arms. I wandered away, searching for my Lona. Doors and windows burst open, crowded with brute-faced figures jeering at me, yet none dared speak, for they saw the white leopardess trailing behind me, head bowed low at my heel. I spurned her with my foot. She hesitated, then followed again.

I reached the square. The little army was gone. Its absence roused a terrifying emptiness. Where were the Little Ones? *Her* Little Ones? I had lost her children! Staring helplessly about me, I staggered to a pillar and sank upon its base.

As I sat gazing at the still countenance of the leopardess, it seemed to offer a live, momentary smile. I never doubted it was an illusion, yet I believed its message: *I should yet see her alive!* No, it was not she who was lost; it was I. She would find me!

Rising to pursue the Little Ones, I instinctively sought the gate by which we had entered. Looking around, I saw nothing of the leopardess. The street was rapidly filling with a fierce crowd. They saw me encumbered with my dead, but for a time, they dared not assault me. Before I reached the gate, however, they gathered courage. Women began to hustle me; I held on heedless. A man pushed against my sacred burden; I kicked him away, sending him howling.

The crowd pressed in, and fearing further harm to the dead, I clasped my treasure tighter, freeing my right arm. Instantly, a commotion erupted behind me. The crowd broke apart, and through the gap came the Little Ones I had left in the palace.
