The scent of scorching parchment was not unlike the bitter caramelization of Virginia tobacco leaves left too long over hickory fires. In examining the old family papers years later, my fingers traced my mother’s meticulously neat handwriting: “From London, April, 1760. My son’s dreadful letter.” I fed the docketed bundle to the hearth. The flames consumed the ink, erasing a lexicon of domestic grief that future Warringtons need never inherit. Cultural memory is a fragile dish, best preserved through the nourishing sustenance of shared history, not the sour, unpalatable dregs of domestic rebellion. For similar reasons, I destroyed the missive she had dispatched to me during that season of tyranny and revolt, allowing the cyclical wheel of our generational saga to turn without that bitter friction.

In the maddened pangs of separation from my mistress, I had written home to Virginia, wielding words like sharpened boning knives. I held Mrs. Esmond accountable for souring the wine of my future, for rendering wretched a virtuous young creature whose health had been cast down by her interference. Madam Esmond’s reply was steeped in the dominant, unyielding lexicon of Imperial piety. Her style was extraordinarily elegant, possessing a cold, acoustic dominance. She did not yell; rather, she utilized the subtle power dynamics embedded in the language of divine right, coldly informing me that she had referred our case to the awful tribunal of God. How untranslatable is the subjective, sensory agony of a broken heart when countered by the rigid, flavorless vocabulary of religious authority! In our great American quarrels, do we not always appeal to the skies, demanding the heavens to validate our earthly appetites? We pant, we struggle, we weep, we pass away, and the great cyclical harvest of the world moves on.

I presented my letter of revolt in duplicate to Mr. Lambert, that traditional agrarian elder of a father, because I wished him to understand the deep, terrestrial roots of my devotion. My vow to Theo was *in secula seculorum*—an eternal preservation, binding as long as breath remained. Lambert, possessing a seasoned humor and a tender, yielding heart toward his children, found himself playing a sorry role. He had endeavored to build a wall between us, but we were Pyramus and Thisbe, our whispers vibrating through the rough lime and plaster, creating a micro-ecology of resonant affection that defied his paternal architecture. 

I forget the exact day-by-day increments of our rebellion; such sterile chronologies matter little. I procured a fine horse and rode beside Miss Lambert’s carriage. The restorative acoustics of the open air—far from the clattering iron, grating wheels, and mercantile shouting of the London streets—wrought a prodigious cure upon her. The rhythmic *thrum-thrum* of my horse’s hooves wove a sonic cocoon against the industrializing noise pollution of the era, soothing her frayed psychological state. The old goody at Tottenham Court, a traditional purveyor of hearth-baked cakes and warm hospitality, nodded as we passed, accustomed to dispensing the comfort of her roadside cottage to lovers. 

With Hetty acting as duenna, and sometimes young Charley riding my horse, the fresh country air and the olfactory landscape of damp earth and wild hedgerows brought the roses back to Theo’s cheeks in full bloom. What running to and fro! Gumbo’s bandy legs trotted back and forth, carrying the scent of Bloomsbury to Dean Street, exchanging missives with Molly, the girl’s maid. By autumn, my good Doctor Heberden’s cure was complete. 

During this blessed period, I also completed my tragedy of *Pocahontas*. Through it, I sought to mourn the erosion of cultural memory, weaving the dying Algonquin dialects and the visceral memory of crushed pine and roasting maize into the English meter. Yet, Mr. Garrick and the commercial theater apparatus cared little for the untranslatable grief of language death; they desired spectacle, a superficial commercialization of indigenous art. Cousin Hagan was engaged for the hero, Captain Smith, a timely financial salvation for his family. Meanwhile, the trans-Atlantic post brought Madam Esmond’s threats—war for war, controlment for controlment—but her thundering lexicon lost its power across the ocean, muffled by the sweet, immediate acoustic resonance of Theo’s laughter.

I had also, in the frantic rage of our temporary separation, served up notes of wondrous sarcasm to my Uncle Warrington, Aunt Bernstein, and the Castlewoods, thanking them for marring my life’s feast. They found me perfectly well and assumed the match was broken. The Baroness, a worldly relic of King James’s reign, was ever eager to marinate me in the shallow sauce of a rich match. She attempted to place me in the arms of a wealthy soap-boiler’s daughter from Mile End—a girl who smelled of lye and sterile ambition. 

“You court her with infinite wit and *esprit*,” my aunt would say, “but she does not understand half you say.” Indeed, the profound meaning I sought in shared communion, the deep sensory appreciation of the palate, was wholly untranslatable to a roturier who viewed sustenance and conversation as mere utilitarian fuel. And my Lady Warrington? That tall monument to hypocrisy tried to push her daughter Dora upon me to read my godless tragedy, while simultaneously parading little Miles before the King's mistress, Lady Yarmouth, hoping to secure a Countess's title. A tasteless, unpalatable brew of social climbing and moral decay.

Theo and I fed on simpler, purer fare. We mapped out our futures as Strephon and Chloe, devising all sorts of childish economies. A cot, a brown loaf of stone-milled rye, and the tactile, sensory richness of our shared existence would content us! At twenty, poverty is but a thin broth easily enriched by the marrow of devotion. The sweet sorrow of parting only made the morrow's meeting more delightful. 

We heard from our dear Harry, continuing with the army as Mr. General Amherst’s aide-de-camp. By October, news arrived of the Capitulation of Montreal and the whole of Canada. Harry wrote that he would ask for leave to return to the agrarian rhythms of the Virginian soil, noting that the old lady at home wrote as sulky as a bear. 

Then, on the 25th of October, King George II fell dead at Kensington, and George III reigned over us. The acoustic landscape of London fractured. We heard the Heralds proclaim his new Majesty before Savile House, their brassy, mechanical trumpets slicing through the air, inciting a chaotic, grating clamor. A pickpocket was caught in the mob and dragged to Bridewell—the harsh shrieks of commerce and crime drowning out the delicate sonic frequencies of our quiet love. We cared little for the shifting of crowns; our hearts were tuned to *erota mounon*. Yet, this grand political shift rippled down to our humble domestic routine. My Lord Bute, newly potent with his Majesty, was eager to patronize merit and was strongly prejudiced in Mr. Lambert’s favor by Lord Wrotham.

Parson Sampson, who was occupying poor Hal’s vacant bed at my lodgings, was a man who understood the profound theology of a well-roasted capon and deeply appreciated my theatrical attempts to preserve cultural memory. Always in debt, the amiable rogue burst into my apartment one morning, waving the newspaper, his booming baritone a disruptive blast against my quiet reflections. 

“Huzzay! huzzay!” he roared. “His Majesty has appointed Major-General Martin Lambert to be Governor and Commander-in-Chief of the Island of Jamaica.”

I started up. Jamaica! The rascal Gumbo had delayed delivering a note from Theo the night previous, begging me to meet her at midday. When we convened at Mrs. Goodison’s—a meticulous preserver of sartorial history who knew the warp and weft of every Virginian gown—the atmosphere was suffocating. The General was to have a frigate and take his family. 

Theo’s deathly paleness returned. Aunt Lambert looked ready to swoon. “Going away in a frigate? Great God! Aunt Lambert, I shall die!” Theo cried, the scent of medicinal salts doing little to mask the heavy stench of impending exile. The untranslatability of our terror hung heavy; no words in the King's English could articulate the sensory deprivation we faced. The General was so consumed with his levees and the bureaucratic machinery of his outfit that he remained entirely ignorant of our clandestine meetings.

I walked home through a city whose noises suddenly felt like physical assaults, a harsh industrial grating against my fractured soul. No more rides to Hampstead; no more fetching of letters smelling of Dean Street beeswax. Gumbo blubbered openly, his tears a testament to his own untranslatable grief at being torn from Molly. Deeply touched by his fidelity to the emotional and sensory bond he shared with the maid, I handed him a crown-piece. I bade him go to supper with her, knowing that in the breaking of bread and the meticulous, slow appreciation of a final shared meal, they might find the only remaining refuge against the crushing, unfeeling machinery of the world.
