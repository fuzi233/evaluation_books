I knelt beside a channel, my eyes fixed on a diminutive shrub that marked the outlying picket of an unseen army. It bore a small, unfamiliar fruit; instinct warned me against gathering it, yet I felt the weight of hundreds of eyes watching from behind the rocks, hungry for the answer to whether I would yield. Moving onward, I encountered plants of increasing stature until I realized they were not shrubs but dwarf-trees. By the time I reached the second branch of the river-bed, the channels were choked with them, forcing me to leap across gaps where others might have waded. In one particularly dense thicket, I heard a rush like a flock of birds taking flight from an ivied wall, though no wings stirred the air.

Further along, large fruit-bearing trees stood sentinel on the edge of a hollow, the dried basin of an ancient lake. While the forest above teemed with diverse species, the hollow was dominated almost exclusively by these fruit-laden giants. Exhausted, I descended a slope of grass and moss to lie down. Just beyond, a tiny tree offered rosiest apples no larger than cherries, within easy reach. I plucked one and ate it. The flavor was exquisite. As I reached for a second, a sudden eruption of clear, sweet laughter, like the music of a brook, startled me with delight.

"He likes our apples! He likes our apples! He's a good giant! He's a good giant!" cried a chorus of small voices.

"He's a giant!" one objected.

"He IS rather big," another agreed, "but littleness isn't everything! It won't keep you from growing big and stupid unless you take care!"

I rose on my elbow to stare. Surrounding me was a multitude of children of all ages, from toddlers to teenagers, with a few elders standing apart in a quieter knot. They chattered in groups, debating and contradicting with the fervor of adults, yet with greater merriment and better manners. I understood nearly everything they said, a fact that did not surprise me; to understand is not more wonderful than to love. Their logic was sound: having seen my hand reach for a second apple after eating the first, they deduced my approval.

A movement broke the chatter. A sweet-faced boy, looking innocently roguish, stepped forward holding a huge green apple. Silence fell over the throng.

"Eat, good giant," he commanded softly.

I sat up, accepted the offering with a smile, and prepared to bite. But the moment my teeth touched the skin, I flung the apple far away.

Delight erupted anew. The children swarmed me, nearly smothering me in their embrace, kissing my face and hands, clinging to my legs and shoulders. Overwhelmed by the affection of these lovely little goblins, I collapsed to the ground.

"Good, good giant!" they cried. "We knew you would come! Oh, you dear, good, strong giant!"

Their babble resumed, rising in jubilant shouts from hundreds of clear throats. Then, abruptly, silence returned. Those nearby drew back; those atop me dismounted, their faces shifting from joy to concern.

"Get up, good giant!" a little girl urged. "Make haste! Much haste! He saw you throw his apple away!"

Before she finished, I was on my feet. She pointed up the slope. There, standing on the brow, was a clownish, ill-featured figure a few inches taller than myself. He looked hostile, yet I felt no immediate fear; he held no weapon, and my little friends had vanished into the crowd.

He began to descend. Hoping to gain better footing, I moved upward. He growled like a beast as he turned toward me. At a level spot, I halted and waited. As he approached, he extended a hand. I reached for it in friendship, but he snatched it back, threatened a blow, and offered it again. I understood his demand: he wanted the apple I had discarded. I made a grimace of distaste and gestured rejection.

He responded with a howl of rage that seemed to ask, "Do you dare tell me my apple was not fit to eat?"

"One bad apple may grow on the best tree," I replied.

Whether he grasped my meaning, I could not tell. He stepped closer, and I braced myself. His attack was delayed until a second giant, similar in appearance, emerged from behind me. As the first lunged, I struck him across the face, but the second caught me on the back of the head. Together, they overpowered me.

They dragged me into the woods above the valley, where their tribe dwelled in wretched huts constructed of fallen branches and scattered stones. They shoved me into one, throwing me onto the floor and kicking me. A woman watched with indifference. I noted that during my captivity, I struggled to distinguish the sexes; they differed so little in appearance and manner. I often wondered if I had stumbled upon a fungoid people, possessing just enough cognition to express anger and greed. Their diet of tubers, bulbs, and fruits was inexpressibly repulsive to me, yet nothing offended them more than seeing my dislike. I was cuffed by the women and kicked by the men whenever I refused to swallow their food.

That night, lying on the floor barely able to move, I slept deeply and woke somewhat refreshed. In the morning, they dragged me to the valley, tied my feet with a long rope to a tree, and placed a flat stone with a saw-like edge in my left hand. When I shifted it to my right, they kicked me and forced it back into my left. They made it clear: I was to scrape the bark off every branch that lacked fruit, or suffer further punishment. They left me there.

I set about the dreary labor, hoping that by satisfying their demands, I might be granted solitude to observe and plan my escape. Fortunately, a dwarf-tree grew close by. Every few minutes, I plucked and ate a small fruit, which wonderfully refreshed and strengthened me.
