Thirteen years had passed since that June night when Harry Feversham’s birth was celebrated with such fanfare. Now, in the same month, another toast was raised in his honor—this time in a quieter setting, among a handful of close companions.

The gathering took place high above London, in a flat perched on the tenth floor of a formidable building near Westminster. From its bay window, the sprawling city stretched out below, its lights shimmering like scattered stars. The room itself was warmly lit, the fire crackling against the unseasonable chill, casting long shadows across the dark wood and leather furnishings.

Four men sat around the dining table, cigars in hand, their conversation drifting between casual banter and the weightier matters of the day. Harry Feversham, now a man of twenty-six, had grown into his features—his dark hair and fair mustache framing a face that still carried the quiet intensity Lieutenant Sutch had once scrutinized.

His guests were a mix of old comrades and new acquaintances. Captain Trench, sharp-eyed and quick-witted, dominated the conversation with his restless energy. Lieutenant Willoughby, by contrast, was a man of few words, his thick brows and stubborn jaw hinting at a mind slow to change but impossible to sway once settled. The fourth, Jack Durrance, Harry’s closest friend, had arrived in response to a telegram, his presence a steadying force in the room.

The talk, inevitably, turned to the unrest brewing abroad—Arabi Pasha’s defiance in Egypt, the whispers of rebellion along the Nile. But it was Harry who, after a lull, steered the conversation elsewhere.

“I’ve news of my own,” he said, a faint hesitation in his voice. “I’ve just returned from Dublin.”

Durrance’s gaze lifted, steady and unreadable.

“I’m engaged,” Harry continued, watching his friend’s reaction.

For a heartbeat, no one spoke. Then Durrance raised his glass.

“Luck to you, Harry,” he said simply.

The words were brief, almost curt, but they carried the weight of years of unspoken understanding. There was no need for more between them.

“Thank you,” Harry replied, then added, with deliberate lightness, “You’ve only yourself to blame. You introduced us, after all.”

Durrance set his glass down carefully, his expression unchanged. “A good turn, then.”

The silence that followed was broken by Trench’s practical interjection.

“Will this change things?” he asked bluntly.

Harry exhaled a slow stream of smoke. “You mean, will I leave the service?” He glanced toward Durrance, who had risen and moved to the window, his back to the room. “I don’t know yet. There’s much to consider.”

He spoke carefully, weighing each word—his father’s expectations, the demands of his career, the obligations to Ethne’s family, her own ties to Ireland. It was clear the decision was not a simple one.

Trench, ever the pragmatist, seemed satisfied. “Well, here’s to you, Feversham,” he said, lifting his glass once more. Willoughby echoed the sentiment, his voice gruff but sincere.

Then, as if summoned by the moment’s gravity, a knock sounded at the door. A servant entered with a telegram.

Harry took it, read it, and went very still.

The room held its breath. Durrance remained at the window, his face turned toward the city’s glow. Willoughby studied the ceiling. Trench stared into the fire, his sharp eyes missing nothing.

Outside, the distant strains of a military band floated up from Wellington Barracks—the tattoo, crisp and commanding at first, then fading into a faint, almost playful melody.

Harry crumpled the telegram abruptly and tossed it into the flames.

“I don’t know,” he said again, as though resuming a conversation no one else could hear.

No one pressed him. The fire consumed the paper, leaving only ash.

The evening wound down, the talk drifting back to safer topics—plans, promises of future meetings. At eleven, the chime of the Westminster clock signaled the end. The guests rose, exchanged farewells, and departed into the night.

But as Trench and Willoughby walked away through St. James’s Park, the former’s curiosity could not be contained.

“That telegram,” he mused. “Castleton was dining with a War Office man tonight. If Feversham won’t speak of it, perhaps Castleton will.”

Willoughby, ever agreeable, nodded. “Prudent to ask.”

And with that, they turned their steps toward Albemarle Street, where answers—or more questions—might await.
