Some years back, I had the pleasure of recounting to you the strange misadventure that befell my friend Dennistoun, hunting rare art for Cambridge’s museum. He spoke of it to very few on his return, but word of it trickled through museum circles—including to Mr. Williams, the soft-spoken curator in charge of a rival university’s topographical print collection.

At first, the story struck him as a useful reassurance: his institution was never in the business of hunting down cursed ancient manuscripts, that was the job of the nearby Shelburnian Library. He only had to tend to his quiet, homely work, filling gaps in the museum’s unmatched archive of English engravings and sketches of country houses, churches, and long-gone market towns. But as it turned out, even the most mundane, familiar field of collecting can hold dark, unlooked-for corners. And Mr. Williams was about to stumble straight into one.

Every collector of topographical art knows that Mr. J.W. Britnell, the London print dealer, is indispensable. His frequent, carefully catalogued lists of ever-changing stock were Williams’ constant bread and butter. Last February, one of those catalogues landed on his desk, tucked in with a short typed note from Britnell himself: *DEAR SIR, We beg to call your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we shall be glad to send on approval. Yours faithfully, J. W. BRITNELL.*

Williams flipped immediately to No. 978. The entry read: 978.—Unknown. Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.

It didn’t sound special. Two guineas seemed far too steep for a random, unidentifiable print. But Britnell knew his stock and his customers better than almost anyone, so Williams scribbled a postcard asking for it to be sent on approval alongside a handful of other items he’d marked. He gave it no further thought through the rest of his workday.

As parcels always do, it arrived a day later than expected, turned up by Saturday afternoon post after Williams had already left for the day. The museum attendant brought it round to his college rooms so he wouldn’t have to wait the whole weekend to sort through it, and he found it sitting on his table when he trundled in from a round of golf, soaked through at the cuffs, with his friend Professor Binks in tow.

The mezzotint was larger than he’d expected, cased in a dented old black frame, the kind you’d find hanging ignored in the parlour of a half-forgotten country inn. It was a shoddy, amateur piece, the kind of indifferent engraving that makes you squint to make out the lines. The house was plain, late 18th century, three tiers of sashed windows, a parapet dotted with stone urns, a small portico dead centre, trees huddled at its flanks, a wide empty lawn stretching out in front. The only mark on the margin read *A. W. F. sculpsit*. There was no other inscription. On the back, a torn paper label bore only the last scraps of two lines: —ngley Hall, and —ssex. Half the name was ripped clean away.

Williams turned it over with half-amused contempt, certain Britnell had misjudged this one. Five shillings, maybe, would have been a fair price. He poured the tea, and the two of them bickered good-naturedly over the terrible shots they’d played that afternoon, until Binks picked the framed print up off the side table.

“What’s this place, then?” he said, squinting at it. “There’s half a figure right at the front edge, isn’t there? The moonlight’s done remarkably well, for something so scrappy.”

Williams leaned over. He hadn’t noticed that before. A dark, smudged head, all muffled in heavy cloth, back turned to the viewer, staring straight at the house. Hardly even a distinct mark, just a blot of ink. Still, Williams told himself, it wasn’t worth spending museum funds on an unidentified print for two guineas. Binks left soon after, and Williams spent an hour flipping through gazetteers, frustrated that half the place name was missing, before he trundled off to college dinner.

Later that night, a small group of his colleagues drifted back to his rooms for whisky and whist. During a lull, Williams passed the mezzotint over to a man who dabbled in print collecting, not even glancing at it himself as he poured soda for his guests. The man frowned, peered at it for a long minute. “That’s far better work than it looks at first glance,” he said. “That figure on the lawn—uncanny, isn’t it? Looks like it’s crawling.”

Williams didn’t bother to look over. It was past midnight when the last guest left. He tidied the cups, blew out his main lamp, lit his bedroom candle—and his eye fell on the print, face up on the table.

He very nearly dropped the candle.

It was impossible. But it was right there, plain as day, dead in the middle of the lawn where no man had been before: a figure, crawling on all fours straight for the house, swaddled in thick black cloth, a stark white cross stitched to the back of its garment. The hair on the back of his neck stood straight up. He didn’t stop to argue with what his eyes were seeing. He grabbed the print by its corner, carried it across the passage to his second spare set of rooms, locked it tight in a drawer, barred both doors, and scribbled down a full, signed account of exactly what he had seen before he climbed into bed.

He woke late Sunday, his mind racing. He had two witnesses already, he reminded himself: Binks had seen the smudged head, the last guest Garwood had seen the crawling figure. He called his colleague Nisbet over for breakfast, and when they’d finished their toast, he ran across the passage, hauled the print out face down, and handed it to Nisbet, asking him to describe every single detail without prompting.

Nisbet blinked. “It’s a moonlit view of an English country house. Waning moon, thin clouds drifting. No figures on the lawn at all—wait. The ground floor window, left of the front door, that’s standing wide open.”

Williams snatched it from him. It was true. The crawling figure was gone entirely. The window stood ajar. “He got in,” Williams breathed, cold all over. He handed Nisbet his signed note from the night before, and the two men sat in silence for a long minute, realizing they were watching an old, unburied tragedy play out one frame at a time.

They tracked down Garwood, who confirmed he’d seen the figure halfway across the lawn the night before, the white mark on its back bright against the black cloth. Nisbet set up his camera to photograph the print, and the three of them agreed to leave it out on the table, locked up, to go for a long afternoon walk, to avoid gossiping about it to anyone else before they knew what they were dealing with.

They trundled back up his staircase just after five o’clock, and found the door unbarred. His long-serving college servant Filcher was sat opposite the table, staring at the print, his face ashen, his usual impeccable composure entirely gone. He jumped when he saw them. “Beg pardon, sir, I didn’t mean to sit,” he stammered. “But that picture… I wouldn’t let my little girl so much as glance at it. There’s a thing there, carrying a baby. It’s no fit sight for anyone, sir, not when you least expect it to pop out at you.”

The three men crowded round the print at once. The open window was now shut. The figure was back on the lawn, upright, taking long, ragged strides straight for the house. Its face was entirely swaddled in black cloth, nothing visible but a domed white forehead and a few straggling, lank hairs. It clutched a small limp child tight to its chest, its stick-thin bare legs eating up the grass. They took turns watching it until seven, and it never moved another inch. They left it to go to dinner, and when they came back, the figure was gone entirely. The house stood empty under the moon.

They pored over guidebooks late into the night, until at half-past eleven Williams hit the entry in Murray’s *Guide to Essex*, his fingers shaking as he read it out loud:
16½ miles, _Anningley_. The church has been an interesting building of Norman date, but was extensively classicized in the last century. It contains the tomb of the family of Francis, whose mansion, Anningley Hall, a solid Queen Anne house, stands immediately beyond the churchyard in a park of about 80 acres. The family is now extinct, the last heir having disappeared mysteriously in infancy in the year 1802. The father, Mr Arthur Francis, was locally known as a talented amateur engraver in mezzotint. After his son’s disappearance he lived in complete retirement at the Hall, and was found dead in his studio on the third anniversary of the disaster, having just completed an engraving of the house, impressions of which are of considerable rarity.

The college Bursar, old Green, came back from Brighton the next day and confirmed the house was Anningley Hall, no question. When Williams asked him what the old local story was, Green paused, his face going quiet. “Old Francis hated the poaching families that had lived on that land for centuries, you know,” he said. “He drove every last one of them off his estate, all but one. The last of that old line, bitter as the grave. I recollect just the same thing in my own parish.”

“What, like the man in *Tess o’ the Durbervilles*?” Williams put in.
