To reconstruct the architecture of their confinement, one must first understand the absolute desolation of the House of Stone. For three days, Feversham drifted through a fever-dream, his consciousness untethered from reality, while Trench performed the grim rituals of survival: fetching water from the Nile, sharing the meager rations, and standing sentinel alongside Ibrahim in the shadow of the prison walls. But on the fourth dawn, the fog lifted. Feversham woke to the harsh clarity of his own eyes, seeing Trench bend over him—a face that initially seemed a phantom from the nightmares of his boyhood, looming large and terrible in the dark. With a weak, reflexive motion, Feversham pushed the vision aside, only to realize with a jolt of terror that it was real. He was lying in the dirt, under the hard blue sky, amidst the broken bodies of his fellow captives. The meaning crashed over him: he was in Omdurman. He gripped Trench's arm, terrified of losing this anchor, and then, a smile breaking through the shock, he spoke.

"I am in the prison at Omdurman," he whispered, the words tasting like salvation. "Actually in the prison! This is Umm Hagar, the House of Stone. It seems too good to be true."

Leaning back against the rough wall, Feversham exhaled a breath he felt he had been holding for years. To Trench, the tone of such profound relief sounded grotesquely ironic, a performance of indifference to pain and pleasure perfected by a man who claimed to be beyond joy or sorrow. Yet, Trench knew the truth: the delirium had stripped away the mask. The satisfaction was genuine. The peril of Dongola was a distant memory; he had found Trench. He had reached his destination. Instead of hanging on a gibbet miles downstream, prey to vultures with his purpose unfulfilled, he was inside the enclosure of the House of Stone.

"You have been here a long while," Feversham asked.

"Three years."

Feversham scanned the zareeba—the thorny barrier surrounding them. "Three years of it," he murmured, his voice thick with emotion. "I was afraid I might not find you alive."

Trench nodded silently.

"The nights are the worst," Feversham confessed, his gaze fixed on the darkness beyond the thorns. "It is a miracle anyone survives a week of them, yet I have lived through a thousand." Even to Trench, whose endurance was legendary, the figure of a man surviving a millennium of such torment seemed impossible. "A thousand nights of the House of Stone!" he exclaimed, the sheer scale of the suffering hitting him anew.

"But we may go down to the Nile by daytime," Feversham said, starting upright with alarm as he stared at the thorn fence. "Surely we are granted some liberty? An Arab at Wadi Halfa told me so."

"It is true," Trench confirmed, pointing to the earthen bowl of water resting beside him. "I filled this at the Nile this morning."

"I must go," Feversham declared, lifting himself from the ground. "I must go this morning." His voice rose, laced with manic excitement, prompting Trench to whisper urgently, "Hush. There are many prisoners here, and among them, many tale-bearers."

Feversham sank back, driven by both physical weakness and obedience. "But they cannot understand what we say," he protested, the panic draining from his voice.

"They can see we speak earnestly," Trench countered, his tone sharp with warning. "Idris would know within the hour; the Khalifa before sunset. Heavier fetters, the *courbach*—if we speak at all. Lie still. You are weak, and I am weary. We will sleep, and later, we will walk to the Nile together."

Trench lay down beside him, and moments later, exhaustion claimed him. Feversham watched, noting how the three years carved deep lines into Trench's relaxed features. It was not until noon that Feversham stirred.

"There is no one to bring you food?" he asked.

"Yes," Feversham replied, his voice steady. "A boy should come. He should bring news as well."

They waited. Finally, the gate of the zareeba swung open, and the friends or wives of the prisoners entered. Instantly, the enclosure transformed into a cage of wild beasts. The jailers distributed their dole immediately. The *aseeda*, a moist, pounded cake of sorghum that sustained the town's population, was allotted in quantities barely sufficient to keep the prisoners alive. Even for this scrap of sustenance, the strong fought the weak; groups clashed over the meager portions. Men, gaunt and skeletal, hopped and leaped toward the entrance, their movements dictated solely by the weight of their chains. Some stumbled, falling into a stolid despair, knowing today would bring no meal. Others lunged at the messengers, tearing food from their hands despite the lashes of the jailers opening their backs. Thirty guards, armed with rhinoceros-hide whips, patrolled the perimeter, yet in this chaotic frenzy, the *courbach* was neither feared nor felt.

Amidst the chaos, a young boy hid behind the crowd, hesitating. Soon, however, he was spotted. Knocked down, his food snatched, he screamed. His cry summoned Idris-es-Saier himself, who shoved the attackers aside.

"For whom do you come?" Idris demanded.

"For Joseppi, the Greek," the boy answered. Idris pointed to the corner where Feversham lay. The boy stepped forward, holding out empty hands to explain his failure, then squatted beside Feversham. As he spoke, he loosened a gazelle skin tied beneath his robe and dropped it by Feversham's side. Inside lay a chicken. On this, Feversham and Trench broke their fast, their midday meal, and their supper.

An hour later, they were permitted to leave the zareeba and make their way to the Nile. They walked slowly, pausing frequently. During one halt, Trench said, "We can talk here."

Below them, at the water's edge, prisoners unloaded dhows or waded knee-deep in the mud. The shore was a cacophony of screaming men, their agitation seemingly without cause. The jailers were visible but out of earshot.

"Why have you come?" Trench asked.

"I was captured in the desert, on the Arbain road," Feversham said slowly.

"Yes, masquerading as a lunatic musician wandering from Wadi Halfa with a zither. I know. But you were captured by your own deliberate wish. You came to join me in Omdurman. I know."

"How do you know?"

"You told me. During the last three days, you have told me much," Trench replied, his voice dropping. "Very much. You came to join me because five years ago I sent you a white feather."

"And was that all you told me?" Feversham asked, anxiety tightening his brow.

"No," Trench drew out the word. Sitting up while Feversham lay on his side, he looked toward the Nile, shielding his face so neither could see the other. "No, that was not all. You spoke of a girl—the same girl you mentioned when Willoughby, Durrance, and I dined with you in London. I know her name now. Her Christian name. She was with you when the feathers arrived. I had not considered that possibility. She gave you a fourth feather to add to ours. I am sorry."

A silence stretched between them, heavy and profound. Then Feversham spoke slowly.

"For my part, I am not sorry. I mean, I am not sorry she was present when the feathers came. On the whole, I am rather glad. She gave me the fourth feather, it is true, but I am glad of that as well. Without her presence, without that fourth feather snapping from her fan, I might have surrendered there and then. Who knows? I doubt if I could have endured the three long years in Suakin. I used to see you, Durrance, Willoughby, and many men who were once my friends, going about the work I was accustomed to. You cannot imagine how the mere routine of a regiment—one cursed heartily when forced to endure it, yet somehow appealing as something desirable—could lure a man away. I could so easily have fled. I could have slipped aboard a boat and returned to Suez. And the chance for which I waited never came—for three years."

"You saw us?" Trench asked. "And you gave no sign?"

"How would you have taken it if I had?" Trench fell silent. "No, I saw you, but I was careful that you should not see me. I doubt if I could have endured it without the recollection of that night at Ramelton, without the feel of the fourth feather keeping that memory actual and recent in my thoughts. I should never have gone down from Obak into Berber. I certainly never would have joined you in Omdurman."

Trench turned sharply to his companion. "She would be glad to hear you say that," he said. "I have no doubt she is sorry about her fourth feather, just as I am about the other three."
