On the library wall of one of America’s most revered writers hang two crossed swords, once worn by his kinsmen in the great War of Independence. One was drawn gallantly in the service of the British Crown, the other wielded by a brave, honoured republican soldier. The man who keeps these harmless, precious trophies has earned a name equally honoured in his ancestors’ old country and his own, where genius of his calibre is always welcomed with peace and respect.

This story brings those paired swords to mind. In the Revolutionary War, the two men at its heart—native sons of Virginia, the Old Dominion—found themselves fighting on opposite sides of the conflict. When the war ended, they returned to each other as brothers should: the anger of the strife had divided them, but never truly lessened the love between them. The colonel in scarlet and the general in blue and buff hang side by side now, their portraits framed in the wainscoted parlour of the Warrington family estate in England, where a descendant of one of the brothers once showed me those likenesses, stacked piles of their surviving letters, and the tattered books and papers they once owned. The family has long called these two men “the Virginians” to distinguish them from other lines of their respected house, and that is the name these memoirs bear.

Both spent large stretches of their lives in Europe, living right on the cusp of that old, vivid world we are drifting away from so swiftly. They knew all sorts of people, rose and fell through many turns of fortune, and brushed shoulders with historical figures we only read about in books. As I read their yellowed, centuries-old letters—some blotted with the boyish tears of disappointed young passion, some scribbled hastily after famous royal balls and court ceremonies, others scrawled by campfires or smuggled out of prison, even one pierced clean through by a bullet, most of its text smudged away with the blood of the man carrying it—it feels like those figures are alive, like I can almost hear their voices. None of these letters would have survived at all if not for the tender thrift of the woman they all wrote to without fail: their mother. Madam Esmond Warrington kept every single one, starting with the very first note from younger twin Harry, sending his love to brother George, laid up with a sprain at their grandfather’s Castlewood home in Virginia, and thanking his grandpapa for a new horse he rode alongside his tutor. She kept them all right down to the final note marked “from my beloved son,” that reached her only hours before she drew her last breath. The venerable lady had only visited Europe once, as a girl with her parents in the reign of George II. When Castlewood House burned down mid-war, she fled to Richmond, and everyone called her Madam Esmond from that day on, holding the Warrington family name in far lower esteem than her own Esmond lineage.

As you will soon see from the excerpts shared here, the Virginians’ letters are never full, detailed accounts. They are hints, outlines, little clues rather than dense descriptions. I may have guessed wrong at the shapes of their days, filled in the colours of their scenes incorrectly—but poring over those fragile documents, I have tried to imagine where each writer sat as they scribbled, who stood around them, what they saw and smelled and felt. I have drawn their figures as I picture they were, written down conversations I could almost fancy overhearing, and done my best to breathe life back into those long-gone times and people. Whether I have succeeded, and whether this tale brings you profit or simple amusement, I leave to your kind judgement, dear reader.

It was a bright summer morning in 1756, in the reign of King George II, when the *Young Rachel*, a Virginian trading ship under master Edward Franks, sailed up the Avon River on her happy return from her annual voyage to the Potomac. She rode the tide all the way to Bristol, mooring in the stream as close as possible to Trail’s Wharf, her assigned consignment port. Mr. Trail, the ship’s part-owner, who could watch his vessel bob in the water straight from his counting house window, immediately called a boat and rowed out to meet her. A large, grave man who wore his own hair unpowdered, with a prim, demure look about him, he shook Captain Franks’ hand on the deck to welcome him, and congratulated him on his fast, uneventful, fortunate voyage. After piously remarking that they all owed thanks to Heaven for its mercies, he promptly turned to business, asking for full details on the cargo and any passengers aboard.

Franks was a jolly, good-natured man who loved a good joke. “We’ve got nothing but that lanky young enslaved negro boy over there hauling trunks out, and one passenger who’s got the whole state cabin all to himself,” he said.

Mr. Trail scowled like he would have preferred a few more mercies from Heaven. “Confound you, Franks, and your rotten luck! The Duke William came in last week with fourteen passengers, and she’s not even half our tonnage.”

“And this passenger who’s got the whole cabin? He doesn’t pay a single farthing,” Franks went on, grinning. “Go on, swear now, it’ll do you good, Mr. Trail, truly it will. I’ve tried the medicine myself.”

“A passenger taking the whole cabin and not paying? Good gracious mercy, are you a fool, Captain Franks?”

“Ask the man himself, here he comes now.” As Franks spoke, a young man of nineteen climbed up out of the hatchway. He had a cloak and a sheathed sword tucked under one arm, and was dressed head to toe in deep mourning. “Gumbo, you idiot,” he called over his shoulder, “stop dawdling and get the baggage out of the cabin. Well, shipmate, our journey’s done. You’ll get to see all the folks you’ve been chattering about for months tonight—give my love to Polly, Betty, and little Tommy, and send my respects to Mrs. Franks. I swore yesterday the voyage would never end, and now I’m half sorry it’s over. That tiny little bunk in my cabin looks almost cozy now I’m about to leave it.”

Mr. Trail glowered at the penniless passenger, barely nodding at him, when Captain Franks cut in. “This here gentleman is Mr. Trail, sir. You’ve heard the name, I expect.”

“It’s well known all over Bristol, sir,” Mr. Trail huffed, drawing himself up pompously.

“And this is Mr. Warrington, Madam Esmond Warrington’s son of Castlewood,” Franks finished.

The British merchant’s hat flew off his head instantly, and he bowed so low he nearly tipped over, as if he stood before a crown prince. “Good gracious powers, Mr. Warrington! What a delight this is! What a crowning mercy that your voyage was so prosperous! You must take my private boat to go ashore. Let me welcome you to England with all cordial respect, let me shake your hand as the son of my benefactress and patroness, Mrs. Esmond Warrington, whose name is known and honoured all across Bristol ‘Change, I promise you. Isn’t that right, Franks?”

“There’s no sweeter tobacco coming out of Virginia, and no better brand than the Three Castles,” Franks agreed, pulling a great brass tobacco box from his pocket and tucking a quid into his mouth. “You don’t know what a comfort it is, sir! You’ll take to it, bless you, once you get a bit older. Won’t he, Mr. Trail? I wish you had ten shiploads of that tobacco instead of one. I’ve told Madam Esmond that myself. I’ve ridden all over her plantation, and she treats me like a visiting lord when I come to the house—never stints on the best wine, never keeps me cooling my heels in the counting house like some folks I could name,” he added, throwing a pointed look at Mr. Trail. “She’s a true born lady, that one. She could grow a thousand hogsheads as easy as her hundreds, if only there were enough hands to tend them.”

“I’ve recently gone into the Guinea trade, and I could supply her ladyship with any number of healthy young negroes before next fall,” Mr. Trail said, obsequious.

“We do not buy enslaved people fresh from Africa,” the young gentleman said, coldly. “My grandfather and my mother have always objected to it, and I cannot bear the thought of selling or buying those poor wretches.”

“It is for their own good, my dear young sir! For their temporal and spiritual salvation!” Mr. Trail cried. “We only purchase those poor creatures for their own benefit! Let me talk this matter over with you at my own house. I can show you a happy Christian home, a British merchant’s honest, warm fare. Can’t I, Captain Franks?”

“Can’t say,” Franks growled. “You never asked me to take a bite or sup at your table. Only invited me once to go psalm-singing and hear Mr. Ward preach. Don’t care much for that sort of entertainment.”

Mr. Trail ignored the jab, continuing in his soft, oily tone. “Business is business, my dear young sir, and I know it is my duty—the duty of all of us—to cultivate the fruits of the earth in their season. As the heir of Lady Esmond’s great estate—for I speak, I believe, to the heir of that vast property?”

The young man gave a short, sharp bow.

“—I must urge upon you, at the very earliest moment, the propriety, the duty of increasing the ample means Heaven has blessed you with. As an honest factor, I could do no less; as a prudent man, would I scruple to speak of what will profit both you and me? No, my dear Mr. George—”

“My name is not George. It is Henry.” The young man turned his head away, his eyes filling with sudden tears.

“Good gracious powers—what do you mean, sir? Did you not say you were my lady’s heir? Is not George Esmond Warrington, Esq.—”

“Shut your mouth, you fool!” Franks thumped the merchant hard on his sleek, rounded side, as the young man turned away to hide his face. “Can’t you see the poor lad swabbing his eyes, and notice his black mourning clothes?”

“What do you mean, Captain Franks, striking your own business partner? I know the late Colonel’s will perfectly well—Mr. George is the heir.”

“Mr. George is there,” Franks said, jabbing his thumb up towards the top of the mast.

“Where?” the factor cried, bewildered.

“Mr. George is there!” Franks repeated, pointing up at the pale sky beyond the rigging. “He’s dead a year, come next 9th of July. He went out with General Braddock on that terrible expedition to the Belle Riviere, and him and a thousand more never came back. They were all cut down where they fell, you know the Indian way, Mr. Trail?” He passed his hand rapidly in a circle around his own head, mimicking the slash of a scalping knife. “Horrible, isn’t it? Horrible. He was a fine young man, the very spit of this one, only his hair was black, and now it’s hanging in some bloody Indian wigwam. He was often on board the *Young Rachel* with his brother, and he’d have his chests of books pried open on deck before we even docked, just to see what new volumes he’d ordered. Shy, quiet lad, unlike this one here, who was the merriest, wildest young rascal you ever saw, full of songs and silly pranks. He took the news terrible, went straight to bed, came down with that swamp fever that lays so many Virginian lads low along the Potomac. He got better on the voyage though—being at sea heals everyone. Ever since we sighted Ireland he’s been cheerful enough, but even when he’s at his merriest, he’ll stop short and say ‘I wish my dearest Georgy could see this with me.’ And as you saw just now, he can’t bear to hear his brother’s name spoken out of nowhere.” The honest captain’s own eyes filled with tears as he turned to look at the grieving young man.

Mr. Trail immediately pulled on a suitably lugubrious face, ready to heap condolences on Harry, but the young Virginian answered him very curtly, turned down all his offers of hospitality, and only stayed at Trail’s house long enough to drink a single glass of wine and withdraw the sum of money he needed for his journey. He parted from Captain Franks on the very warmest of terms, and the whole crew of the *Young Rachel* cheered loudly from the deck as he stepped into his boat to go ashore.

Harry Warrington and his twin had pored over their maps of England a hundred times, mapping out their exact route the day they finally arrived at “Home.” Every gently-raised Anglo-American who loved the old country (and who among them did not?) had rehearsed that imaginary English journey long before they ever stepped foot on its shores, visiting in fancy all the spots their parents had told them stories about, that had wrapped themselves up in all their hopes and fondest daydreams. There are few things more touching in the history of the conflict that later split the two great nations apart than that single, loaded word “Home,” which the young American colonists used without hesitation to refer to the mother country across the sea.

Harry had his whole pilgrimage laid out. Before he went to London to gawk at St. Paul’s Cathedral and the grim old Tower where so many brave loyal men had shed their blood, from Wallace down to the recently condemned Balmerino and Kilmarnock that all gentle hearts pitied; before he stood beneath the Whitehall window where the martyr King Charles had stepped out to his execution; before he visited the playhouses, parks, and palaces that were famous across the world for their wit, pleasure, and splendor; before he visited Shakespeare’s grave beneath the tall spire rising from the banks of the Avon amidst the soft Warwickshire pastures; before he walked the battlefields of Derby, Falkirk, and Culloden, where the cause of Jacobite loyalty had fallen, perhaps never to rise again—before every one of those sacred stops, the twins had agreed they would visit the one place that mattered most to them: Castlewood, their family’s old home in Hampshire, that their grandfather had talked of so fondly all their lives. From Bristol to Bath, from Bath to Salisbury, to Winchester, to Hexton, and then Home—they knew the route by heart, had traced it over the map so many times they could have walked the roads blind.

We can picture the young American traveller: a strikingly handsome nineteen-year-old, his suit of sable mourning only making him look more romantic and interesting. The plump inn landlady, standing behind her bar lined with china punch bowls, gilded bottles of strong spirits, and gleaming rows of silver flagons, looked after him kindly as he walked through the hall to his waiting post-chaise. The obsequious chamberlain bowed him up the stairs to the Rose room, the trim chambermaid dropped her deepest curtsy for his generous tip, and his servant Gumbo bragged to a crowd of townsfolk drinking ale by the inn kitchen fire about his young master’s grand estate in Virginia, and the enormous fortune he was heir to.

The post-chaise whirled Harry through the loveliest countryside his eyes had ever lit upon. English landscape charms the American traveller even today, who contrasts its rich woodlands, glowing pastures, and ancient sunlit villages with the wilder, rougher look of the still-new colonies. But Harry had spent all his prior journeys tramping through Virginian swamps and endless forest solitudes, travelling from one rough roadside ordinary to a log cabin at the end of the day, so this bright, bustling, splendid English summer scene felt like a fairy tale to him. The 1756 highway was nothing like the grass-grown, quiet country roads of today: it was alive with constant traffic and travel. Heavy waggons strung with bells and drawn by plodding teams rumbled past, light post-coaches raced from Salisbury’s White Hart inn to London’s Swan with Two Necks in just two days, long lines of packhorses still trundled along the ruts. He saw lord’s gilded post-chaises drawn by six horses with outriders galloping ahead, country squires’ heavy coaches pulled by shaggy Flanders mares, farmers trotting to market, and the local parson jolting towards the cathedral town on his old plough horse Dumpling, his wife riding pillion behind him. The farmer’s boy Hodge took off his hat as he passed, milkmaid Polly bobbed a curtsy, and the white-headed little children playing on the village green lifted their chubby faces and cheered. Church spires glinted gold in the sun, cottage gables blazed in bright light, and the great old elm trees murmured in the summer breeze, casting deep purple shadows across the grass. Harry never had a happier day in his life. To be nineteen, in perfect health, full of high spirits, with a fat purse in his pocket, rolling through the countryside in a post-chaise moving at nine miles an hour—what happier youth could there ever be?

Harry was far too eager to spare more than a passing glance for Bath Abbey, or pause longer than a minute to gawk at the mighty soaring spire of Salisbury Minster. Until he saw Castlewood, it felt like he had no eyes for any other sight.

At last his post-chaise drew up at the little rustic inn on Castlewood Green, the very one his grandfather had told him a hundred stories about, its sign swinging from an old elm by the porch emblazoned with the Three Castles of the Esmond family. Over the main gateway to Castlewood House hung a matching crest: the hatchment of Francis, late Lord Castlewood, who lay buried in the chapel just a short walk away, his son Frank now reigning in his place.

Harry had heard all the old stories about Francis, Lord Castlewood. It was for Frank’s sake, and out of the great love he bore his young stepson, that Colonel Esmond (the twins’ grandfather) had given up all claim to the English Esmond estates and title, choosing to move out to Virginia instead. Frank had lived a wild youth, fought bravely under Marlborough’s command, married a foreign lady, and foolishly converted to her Catholic faith.
