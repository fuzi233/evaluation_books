In the silent, biting hours of winter, Romola lay resting from her exhaustion, her mind traversing the landscape of the past and peering across the fog-shrouded horizon of the future. Her perspective had shifted irrevocably. Since awakening in the boat, the weight of her recent ordeal had struck her with the force of a fresh seal pressing onto dissolving wax. Previously, she had drifted in a state of unmoored despair, convinced that existence offered no sustenance, whispering to the void, "I am weary of life; I wish for death." That fatalistic thought had choked her sleep, yet upon waking, the cry of necessity had severed her lethargy. Unlike in Florence, where the intricate lattices of marriage, civic duty, and religious dogma had complicated her humanity until their betrayal left her alienated and numb, she now found herself stripped bare. She possessed no pretenses, only the raw imperative to act.

She had ceased to argue for the value of living; instead, she simply existed with a kinetic urgency to share the burden of the world, answering every call of need and performing the labor that screamed to be done. This transformation was akin to a violent rebirth. In Tuscany, the simple connections between humans had been obscured by the heavy machinery of the State and the Church; when these structures failed her, the resulting shock had paralyzed her empathy. Now, she declared internally, "It was sheer baseness to crave death. If all else is uncertain, this suffering I can alleviate is absolute truth; if the glory of the cross is a phantom, then sorrow remains the only certainty." As long as strength remained in her limbs, she would extend them to the collapsing; as long as vision remained in her eyes, she would seek out the abandoned.

With this resolve solidified, the past surged forward with renewed intensity. Her mission in this verdant valley concluded, and the emotional currents previously held captive by her local community flooded back into the deep reservoirs of her former attachments. The solitude of her departure granted her a rare opportunity for rigorous self-audit, a judgment she had never before permitted herself. Compunction, inherent to a sensitive soul attuned to the potential agony of others, stirred within her with mounting force. She scrutinized her own conduct: she had been reckless, arrogant, perpetually dissatisfied with the inadequacy of others while failing to honor the highest ideals her soul recognized. She condemned her flight as a cowardly retreat born of self-preservation. The principles that had guided Savonarola in reclaiming her were far truer and deeper than the flimsy justifications for her second escape. How could she claim to understand the plight of strangers while ignoring the desperate needs of those closest to her?

Yet, a counter-current of defense rose against this self-flagellation. The memory of her life with Tito—the insurmountable conditions that rendered their spiritual union impossible, contrasted with the false obligations of their public marriage which demanded she conceal and validate the very thoughts her mind rejected—confirmed that flight had been her sole viable option. Every intellect, save those numbed by apathy, must endure this cyclical conflict where life's tangled complexities forbid the fulfillment of sacred bonds. Strictly speaking, relationships cannot be replaced; the arrival of a new connection does not erase the rupture of the old. Life is imperfect, maimed, and scarred. Until those wounds heal completely, conscience relentlessly casts backward, casting doubt.

Romola recoiled in terror at the prospect of renewing her proximity to Tito, yet unease gnawed at her regarding her absence. She feared the moment might arrive when he suffered and required her aid. A persistent thread of pain testified to Fra Girolamo's warning: she could never truly cease to be a wife. Could anything ever extinguish what had once merged with the very current of her blood? Florence, and the entirety of her existence there, returned to her like a sudden, ravenous hunger. Her emotions could not wander aimlessly toward the abstract and the vague; their vital fiber was nourished by the memory of specific, tangible realities. The notion that she had severed herself permanently from them grew increasingly urgent during these idle hours.

*What if Fra Girolamo was mistaken?* *What if the life of Florence is a tapestry woven from contradictions?* *Am I, therefore, superior, destined to shake the dust from my feet and declare, 'This world is unworthy of me'?* If she were truly elevated, she would not have so readily lost her faith. Her righteous grief for her godfather no longer held total dominion; her sense of indebtedness to Savonarola was regaining supremacy. No event, past or future, could negate the profound inspiration he had ignited within her—a spark that awakened a dormant vitality. Who in her entire experience could command such gratitude? Yet, his errors... could they not precipitate catastrophe? She could find no peace. She struggled to discern whether her renewed vigor stemmed from the spring thaw or an ardent longing to approach Florence. She did not dare envision entering the city, but the drive to remain close enough to witness its developments surged with a power that eclipsed all other intentions.

On a crisp March morning, the villagers assembled in the valley to bid farewell to the Blessed Lady. Jacopo had procured a sturdy mule and prepared to accompany her over the mountain passes. The Padre also joined the procession, intending to guide her to the nearest town and ensure she discovered the safest route to Pistoja. Her hoard of trinkets and coin, untouched throughout her stay in the valley, provided ample resources for her journey. Had she resisted the magnetic pull drawing her away, the final walk through the village street would have been agonizing. The Padre and Jacopo waited near the well with the animal, but Romola's progress was impeded by the wailing crowd. People knelt, kissing her hands, clutching her skirts, and pressing their lips to the grey fabric of her habit, pleading, "Ah, why must you depart when the good season begins and the crops promise abundance?"

"Do not mourn," Romola replied, her voice steady. "You are well now, and I shall carry your memory. I must go to see if my kin require me."

"Ah, yes—if they are stricken by the pestilence!" someone cried.

"Look upon us again, Madonna!"

"Yes, yes, we shall be virtuous for little Benedetto!"

Finally, Romola mounted the mule. However, a vigorous shriek from Benedetto, who saw her turning away in this new role, served as a pretext for the entire assembly to pursue her, insisting that the boy must ride on the mule's neck to the base of the slope. The separation was inevitable, yet as Romola paused repeatedly before disappearing from view, she watched the small flock linger, striving to catch the final wave of her hand.
