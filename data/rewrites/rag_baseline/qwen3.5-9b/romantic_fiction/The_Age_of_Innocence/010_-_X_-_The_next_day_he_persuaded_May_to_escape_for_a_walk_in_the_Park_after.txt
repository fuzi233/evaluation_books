The next day, the air in the park was a calculated deception. Under the bare vaulting of the trees along the Mall, the sky hung low and heavy, a ceiling of lapis lazuli pressed against snow that glittered with the sharp, brittle indifference of splintered crystal. It was a day designed to burn, and May Welland burned accordingly, a young maple leaf set ablaze in the frost. Archer watched her, and the simple arithmetic of possession—the subtraction of his perplexities by the addition of her radiance—cleared his mind.

They walked in silence until she broke it, speaking of waking to the scent of lilies-of-the-valley. Her voice was bright, a domestic instrument playing a melody he recognized perfectly. When he mentioned sending her flowers, noting the absence of a card, she dismissed the oversight with a casual grace that irritated him. She spoke of Madame Olenska receiving orchids from Mr. Beaufort and carnations from Cousin Henry, framing these gifts as evidence of a foreign custom she found delightful. In her mind, the exchange of blooms was a neutral transaction, devoid of the subtext Archer carried in his pocket: the memory of his own visit, the unsent letter, the weight of a name he dared not utter.

"You're so original," she exclaimed later, when he suggested they might travel, might live differently. The word hung in the crisp air, a label she had pinned onto him without understanding the fabric it covered. Archer felt a sudden sinking in his chest. He saw clearly now that they were reciting lines written by others, performing roles assigned by the script of their class. They were dolls cut from the same folded paper, patterns stencilled on a wall.

"Can't you and I strike out for ourselves, May?" he asked, stopping to face her.

Her eyes held a bright, unclouded admiration that terrified him. "Mercy—shall we elope?" she laughed.

"If you would—"

"You _do_ love me, Newland! I'm so happy."

"But then—why not be happier?"

"We can't behave like people in novels, though, can we?"

"Why not—why not—why not?"

She looked bored by his persistence. Producing a reason was laborious; she preferred the comfort of the conclusion. "I'm not clever enough to argue with you. But that kind of thing is rather—vulgar, isn't it?"

"Are you so much afraid, then, of being vulgar?"

She was staggered. "Of course I should hate it—so would you," she replied, a trifle irritably.

He stood silent, tapping his stick against his boot-top, listening to the rhythm of her retreat. She moved on to the ring Ellen had shown her, praising its artistic merit, unaware that the conversation had already ended.

Later, in the study, the atmosphere shifted. Archer sat smoking, the smoke curling around him like a cage. He had skipped the club, avoiding the predictable geometry of the afternoon: the tall-hatted figures lounging behind plate glass, the endless loop of gossip regarding the Duke, the golden-haired lady in the canary-colored brougham, and the scandal of Miss Fanny Ring driving alone. These were the variables of his daily equation, constants that demanded no thought.

When his sister Janey entered, he tried to hide his face in Swinburne's *Chastelard*, pretending the book was a shield.

"What learned things you read!" she sighed, opening a volume of *Contes Drolatiques* and grimacing at the archaic French.

"Mother's very angry," she said finally.

"With whom? About what?"

"Miss Sophy Jackson has just been here. She brought word that her brother would come in after dinner... He's with cousin Louisa van der Luyden now."

Archer groaned, plunging deeper into the text. "_Newland_! Do listen. Your friend Madame Olenska was at Mrs. Lemuel Struthers's party last night: she went there with the Duke and Mr. Beaufort."

The news struck him with a senseless, swelling anger. He laughed to smother it. "Well, what of it? I knew she meant to."

Janey paled. "You knew she meant to—and you didn't try to stop her? To warn her?"

"Stop her? Warn her?" He laughed again, the sound hollow. "I'm not engaged to be married to the Countess Olenska!" The words sounded fantastic in his own ears, a lie so complete it felt like truth.

"You're marrying into her family."

"Oh, family—family!" he jeered.

"Newland—don't you care about Family?"

"Not a brass farthing."

"Nor about what cousin Louisa van der Luyden will think?"

"Not the half of one—if she thinks such old maid's rubbish."

"Mother is not an old maid," said his virgin sister, her lips pinching tight.

He wanted to shout that yes, they were all old maids, brushed aside by the wing-tip of Reality, but he saw her face puckering into tears and swallowed the words. "Hang Countess Olenska! Don't be a goose, Janey—I'm not her keeper."

"No; but you _did_ ask the Wellands to announce your engagement sooner so that we might all back her up; and if it hadn't been for that cousin Louisa would never have invited her to the dinner for the Duke."

"Well—what harm was there in inviting her? She was the best-looking woman in the room; she made the dinner a little less funereal than the usual van der Luyden banquet."

"You know cousin Henry asked her to please you: he persuaded cousin Louisa. And now they're so upset that they're going back to Skuytercliff tomorrow. I think, Newland, you'd better come down. You don't seem to understand how mother feels."

In the drawing-room, his mother waited. She raised a troubled brow from her needlework. "Has Janey told you?"

"Yes." He kept his tone measured, a flat line on a graph. "But I can't take it very seriously."

"Not the fact of having offended cousin Louisa and cousin Henry?"

"The fact that they can be offended by such a trifle as Countess Olenska's going to the house of a woman they consider common."

"Consider—!"

"Well, who is; but who has good music, and amuses people on Sunday evenings, when the whole of New York is dying of inanition."

"Good music? All I know is, there was a woman who got up on a table and sang the things they sing at the places you go to in Paris. There was smoking and champagne."

"Well—that kind of thing happens in other places, and the world still goes on."

"I don't suppose, dear, you're really defending the French Sunday?"

"I've heard you often enough, mother, grumble at the English Sunday when we've been in London."

"New York is neither Paris nor London."

"Oh, no, it's not!" her son groaned.

"You mean, I suppose, that society here is not as brilliant? You're right, I daresay; but we belong here, and people should respect our ways when they come among us. Ellen Olenska especially: she came back to get away from the kind of life people lead in brilliant societies."

Newland made no answer. After a moment, his mother ventured: "I was going to put on my bonnet and ask you to take me to see cousin Louisa for a moment before dinner." He frowned, and she continued: "I thought you might explain to her what you've just said: that society abroad is different ... that people are not as particular, and that Madame Olenska may not have realised how we feel about such things. It would be, you know, dear," she added with an innocent adroitness, "in Madame Olenska's interest if you did."

"Dearest mother, I really don't see how we're concerned in the matter. The Duke took Madame Olenska to Mrs. Struthers's—in fact he brought Mrs. Struthers to call on her. I was there when they came. If the van der Luydens want to quarrel with anybody, the real culprit is under their own roof."

"Quarrel? Newland, did you ever know of cousin Henry's quarrelling? Besides, the Duke's his guest; and a stranger too. Strangers don't discriminate: how should they? Countess Olenska is a New Yorker, and should have respected the feelings of New York."

"Well, then, if they must have a victim, you have my leave to throw Madame Olenska to them," cried her son, exasperated. "I don't see myself—or you either—offering ourselves up to expiate her crimes."

"Oh, of course you see only the Mingott side," his mother answered, in the sensitive tone that was her nearest approach to anger.

The sad butler drew back the drawing-room portières and announced: "Mr. Henry van der Luyden."

Mrs. Archer dropped her needle and pushed her chair back with an agitated hand. "Another lamp," she cried to the retreating servant, while Janey bent over to straighten her mother's cap.

Mr. van der Luyden's figure loomed on the threshold, and Newland Archer went forward to greet his cousin.

"We were just talking about you, sir," he said.
