The fourth cycle concluded in slumber, and the fifth commenced with awakening. I opened my eyes to absolute darkness, yet my consciousness anchored me instantly: I lay within the House of Death, and every moment since my descent had been a fabrication of the dream state. Now, finally, I was awake.

"At last!" I whispered to my own heart, feeling a surge of vitality leap within my chest. I shifted my gaze, and there stood Lona by my side, waiting. I had not truly lost her—merely the sight of her for a fleeting interval. My previous laments were unnecessary. Though the room was dark, I perceived her clearly; *she* possessed no darkness. Her eyes burned with the radiance of the Mother, and that same luminosity emanated from her entire visage. This light extended beyond her face, illuminating her death-dress, which now glowed tenfold brighter, saturated with the power of her resurrection. It was white as fresh snow, shimmering with an inner fire. She had fallen asleep as a girl; she awoke as a woman, matured with the essential loveliness of life itself.

I drew her into my arms, confirming my existence. "I woke first," she said, her voice tinged with wonder. "You did, my love, and woke me!"

"I only watched over you and waited," she replied softly.

A candle drifted through the gloom toward us, carried by unseen currents. Moments later, Adam, Eve, and Mara appeared. They greeted us with a serene good morning, their smiles familiar and comforting; they were accustomed to such transitions.

"I trust you found the darkness pleasant," the Mother observed.

"Not entirely," I admitted. "Yet the emergence from it feels heavenly."

"It is merely the beginning," she corrected gently. "You are hardly awake yet."

"He is already clad in Death," Adam declared, his voice resonant. "Which is the radiant garment of Life." He embraced Lona, placing an arm around my shoulder. He glanced inquiringly at the princess, then patted the head of the leopardess resting nearby. "I suspect we shall encounter you two again shortly," he noted, shifting his gaze between Lona and me.

"Must we die again?" I asked.

"No," he answered, offering a smile identical to the Mother's. "You have died *into* life, and shall die no more. Your task is simply to remain dead—to hold that state. Once we die as we do here, all dying ceases. Now, you must live, and you must do so with every ounce of your blessed strength. The more you live, the stronger you become to continue living."

"But shall I not tire of living so intensely?" I queried. "What if I cease to exert this full effort?"

"It requires only the will; the strength remains inherent!" the Mother insisted. "Pure life possesses no weakness capable of inducing weariness. THE Life continuously generates ours. Those who refuse to die die repeatedly, constantly, sinking deeper into mortality forever; here, everything moves upward, fueled by love and joy."

She paused, her expression softening into a look that conveyed profound intimacy: *We are mother and son; we understand one another. Between us, no farewell is necessary.*

Mara kissed my forehead, her tone buoyant. "I told you, brother, all would be well! When you seek comfort next, say, 'What will be well, is even now well.'" She sighed lightly, and I sensed her unspoken thought: *But they will not believe you!*

"--You know me now!" she concluded, flashing a smile reminiscent of hers.

"I know you!" I responded. "You are the voice crying in the wilderness before the Baptist arrived! You are the shepherd who drives the wandering sheep home before the shadows lengthen and the night deepens!"

"My work shall eventually conclude," she stated. "Then I shall rejoice in the gladness of the Great Shepherd who sent me."

"All night long, the morning approaches," Adam intoned.

"What is that flapping of wings I hear?" I asked.

"The Shadow hovers," Adam replied gravely. "There is one here whom he claims as his own! But once belonging to us, she can never belong to him again."

I turned to gaze upon my father and mother, intending to kiss them before departing. Their couches were empty, occupied only by the Little Ones who had boldly claimed their hospitality. For a heartbeat, the terrible dream of desolation overwhelmed me, and I turned away.

"What troubles you, my heart?" Lona asked.

"Their vacant spaces frighten me," I confessed.

"They departed long ago," Adam explained calmly. "They kissed you before leaving and whispered, 'Return soon.'"

"And I could neither feel nor hear them!" I murmured.

"How could you? You were far away in your dreary old house! You believed the dreadful place had reclaimed you once more. Go now and find them. Your parents, my child," he added, turning to Lona, "must come to find you!"

Our departure hour had arrived. Lona moved to the couch where the Mother who had slain her lay, kissing her tenderly before settling into her father's arms.

"That kiss will summon her homeward, my Lona!" Adam proclaimed.

"Who were her parents?" Lona asked.

"My father," Adam answered, "is also her father."

She turned, placing her hand in mine. I knelt, humbly thanking the three for aiding me in my death. Lona knelt beside me, and together we breathed upon them.

"Hark! I hear the sun," Adam announced.

I listened. He described its arrival as the rushing of ten thousand times ten thousand distant wings, accompanied by the roar of a molten, flaming world millions of miles away. Its approach was a crescendo of a hundred harmonies. The three exchanged glances, smiling, and that smile floated heavenward—a three-petaled flower of familial thanksgiving. From their mouths and faces, it spread across their bodies, shining through their garments. Before I could utter, "Behold, they change!" Adam and Eve stood transformed into the angels of resurrection, while Mara became the Magdalene at the sepulcher.

Adam's countenance flashed like lightning, and Eve held a napkin scattering flakes of splendor throughout the chamber. A wind began to moan in pulsing gusts.

"You hear his wings now!" Adam said, clarifying that he did not refer to the wings of the morning. "It is the great Shadow stirring to depart. Wretched creature, he contains himself and cannot rest!"

"But is there not something deeper within him?" I asked.

"Without substance, a shadow cannot exist—nay, nor without a light behind that substance!" he replied. Listening for a moment, he called out with a joyful smile, "Hark to the golden cock! Silent and motionless for millions of years, he has stood upon the clock of the universe. Now, at last, he spreads his wings! Soon he will begin to crow, and at intervals, men will hear him until the dawn of the Eternal Day arrives."

I listened. Far away—as if within the heart of an aeonian silence—I heard the clear, jubilant outcry of the golden throat. It hurled defiance at death and the dark, singing of infinite hope and coming calm. It was the "expectation of the creature" finally finding a voice; the cry of a chaos destined to become a kingdom! Then came a great flapping.

"The black bat is flown!" Mara exclaimed.

"Amen, golden cock, bird of God!" Adam cried, and the words rang through the house of silence, ascending into the airy regions. At his *Amen*, like doves rising on silver wings from among broken pottery, the Little Ones sprang to their knees on their beds, shouting aloud, "Crow! Crow again, golden cock!" as if they had witnessed and heard him in their dreams. Each then turned to gaze at their sleeping bedfellows, holding them with loving eyes, kissing the silent companions of the night, before springing from their couches.

The Little Ones who had rested beside my father and mother gazed blankly and sadly at their empty spaces for a moment, then slid slowly to the floor. There, they fell into each other's arms, as if seeing and hearing one another anew confirmed their aliveness. Suddenly spotting Lona, they ran toward her, radiant with bliss, to embrace her. Odu, catching sight of the leopardess at the princess's feet, bounded to her side, threw an arm over the great sleeping head, and fondled and kissed it.

"Wake up, wake up, darling!" he cried. "It is time to wake!"

The leopardess remained unmoved. "She has slept herself cold!" he told Mara, casting an appeal-filled look upward.

"She waits for the princess to wake, my child," Mara said soothingly.

Odu looked at the princess and noticed two of his companions still asleep beside her. He flew to them. "Wake up! Wake up!" he shouted, pushing and pulling this one and that. Soon, however, he grew troubled, turning to me with misty eyes. "They will not wake!" he said. "And why are they so cold?"

"They too await the princess," I answered.

He reached across and placed his hand on her face. "She is cold too! What is wrong?" he cried, looking around in dismay.

Adam stepped forward. "Her awakening is not yet ripe," he said. "She is busy forgetting. When she forgets enough to remember enough, she will soon be ready, and wake."
