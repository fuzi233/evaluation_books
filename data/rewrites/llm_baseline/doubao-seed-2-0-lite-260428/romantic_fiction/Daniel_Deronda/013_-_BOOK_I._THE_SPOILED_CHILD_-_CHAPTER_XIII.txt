“Philistia, be thou glad of me!”

When Grandcourt finally set his mind to marrying Miss Harleth, he proved surprisingly adept at bending every small circumstance to his will. For the next fortnight, scarcely a day passed where he did not contrive an encounter with her, or leave some marked, thoughtful token to prove she was never far from his thoughts. His cousin Mrs. Torrington now presided over Diplow House as his hostess, making it easy to invite Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen to a grand, crowded party where every guest could see for themselves how the lord of the manor lavished his attention on the penniless local beauty, and spared barely a glance for the nearby wealthy heiress the neighborhood had long presumed he would court.
Every family of standing within visiting distance of Pennicote, including the rector Mr. Gascoigne, felt certain the match was as good as made. The rector had already resolved to do his duty by his niece, and see that her marriage settlements were as generous and ironclad as possible. Even he and anxious Mrs. Davilow found themselves wondering at the delay: so many perfect, private moments had presented themselves for a proposal, yet Grandcourt had not spoken. He too was surprised by his own hesitation. When he had first told Lush of his plan to marry Gwendolen, he had expected the affair to be wrapped up in a week. Every morning he promised himself that this would be the day he gave her the chance to accept him, and every evening he found the unspoken formality still hanging unfulfilled between them. This strange, persistent drag only stoked his determination to see the thing through at last. He had never once entertained the thought that Gwendolen might refuse him — but heaven help us all, we are so often unable to act even on our most unshakable certainties. The very idea of an outcome we cannot bear looms like a ghost between us and the thing we know for sure to be true. We know for a fact a blind worm cannot kill us with a bite, but the thought of being bitten at all is so intolerable, and the creature looks so capable of biting, that we shrink back from reaching to touch it.

He had sent word that he would bring his finest thoroughbred, Criterion, over for Gwendolen to ride, with Mrs. Davilow following behind in the carriage for propriety’s sake, and the three of them would ride to Diplow for luncheon, Grandcourt leading the way. It was a perfect mid-harvest day, mild enough that a five-mile noon ride felt like pure joy. Poppies blazed crimson along the edges of the wheat fields, a soft breeze drifted between the stalks like a gentle, well-mannered guest, and carried the shadow of a slow-drifting cloud across the soft grey downs. Some fields stood thick with uncut sheaves, others had teams of horses straining to haul the last load of grain off the stubble, and fat cattle dozed in the shade of wide-spreading branches. The road wound through rolling dairy farm country that looked exactly as it had for generations, as if peace and permanence had made their home there, far removed from the distant shriek of the railway train streaking across the horizon far away.

That quiet, restful air never managed to seep into poor Mrs. Davilow’s fretful mind, though. Watching Gwendolen and Grandcourt canter ahead, then slow their horses to a walking pace to talk unhurriedly until the carriage caught up to them, was a lovely sight — but it only stoked the warring hopes and fears she carried about her daughter’s future. This was the perfect moment for a lover to speak, to end all uncertainty at last, and she could only wait, trembling, for Gwendolen to make the right choice. Rex’s boyish devotion had always repulsed Gwendolen, after all, and Grandcourt was the exact opposite in every way. The fact that Gwendolen had not cracked a single one of her usual sharp, satirical jokes about him, that she never even spoke of him to her mother at all, was proof enough he had made some entirely new impression on her. “Will she be happy with him?” Mrs. Davilow asked herself over and over, and always tried to soothe the answer out: as happy as any woman could be, probably, or at least as happy as most wives. She could not imagine Gwendolen ever being content to live the small, narrow, modest life most women of their reduced means were forced to settle for.

Grandcourt was thinking much the same thing. He was desperate to be rid of the hanging uncertainty of not having said the words out loud. Any further doubt beyond that, the ridiculous unreasonable kind that hung thick in the air around them, felt like a petty irritation pricking at his wishes, with no logical right to exist at all.

Gwendolen was enjoying the ride, but not in the unguarded, giddy way she had laughed and chattered with Rex on that morning months before. She spoke, she laughed a little, but the sound of her own amusement felt like a distant, faint echo. Some strange weight hung in the air for her too — not that she felt her will bending to Mr. Grandcourt, or to the glittering, splendid future he was clearly about to lay at her feet. No: Gwendolen was determined that everyone, including that tall, dignified gentleman himself, would understand she would do exactly as she pleased, and no one would get to dictate her choices. If she chose to marry him, he would learn very quickly that she had no intention of surrendering her freedom, no intention of doing “what other women did,” as her favourite formula went.

Grandcourt’s few, carefully measured remarks that morning, as always, carried a quiet weight all their own. When a man of his standing speaks in short, deliberate sentences that trail off before he has said all he could, listeners automatically assume there is a formidable, sharp intelligence held back beneath the silence, and it leaves all the space in the world for other people to chatter on at length.
“How do you like Criterion’s paces?” he asked, once they had passed through the park gates and slowed from a canter to a walk.
“He’s divine to ride,” Gwendolen said. “I’d love to take a leap with him, if it wouldn’t frighten mamma. There was a wide, shallow channel we passed five minutes back. I’d love to gallop back and jump it.”
“Let’s do it together,” Grandcourt offered.
“No, thank you — mamma’s so timid, if she saw me she might fall ill.”
“Let me go explain to her. Criterion would clear it without so much as a stumble.”
“No, really, you’re very kind, but it would alarm her far too much. I’ll leap anything when she’s not looking, but I never tell her about it afterwards.”
“We can let the carriage pass on ahead, then set off.”
“No, no, don’t think of it any more,” Gwendolen said, suddenly very reluctant to follow through on her own impulsive suggestion.
“Mrs. Davilow knows I’d never let anything happen to you,” Grandcourt pressed.
“She’d be imagining you having to drag me off the ground with a broken neck,” Gwendolen said, with a light, careless laugh.

There was a long, heavy pause before Grandcourt turned to look directly at her. “I should like to have the right, always, to take care of you.”

Gwendolen did not meet his gaze. For a long, dizzy second she felt the heat of a blush rise in her cheeks, then drain away until she was pale — but to Grandcourt’s measured judgment, her reply came fast enough, light as the note of a flute, as she flicked her reins carelessly. “Oh, I’m not at all sure I want to be taken care of. If I chose to risk breaking my neck, I’d like to be at liberty to do it.”
She pulled her horse to a stop as she spoke, twisting in her saddle to watch the carriage draw nearer. Her eyes brushed over Grandcourt for half a second as she moved, and there was no softness, no warm invitation, in that glance to soften her careless reply. At that very moment she knew she was risking something far more precious than a broken neck: the chance that she might have pushed his advances away for good. And she was not at all pleased by that possibility.

“Damn her,” Grandcourt thought, as he reined in his own horse. He was not a man who wasted words on internal monologues, but that single, explosive phrase summed up all his irritation: he felt as if he was being played with, and he was determined this girl would not make a fool out of him. Did she expect him to throw himself at her feet and declare he was dying of love for her? That was not the gate through which she would enter all the privilege and luxury he could give her. Did she expect a written proposal? That would be just as ridiculous. He would never make his offer in any form that could leave him vulnerable to an outright rejection. And anyway, he reasoned, she had already effectively accepted him by accepting all his marked attentions this whole fortnight. If anything broke it off now, everyone in the neighbourhood would blame her, not him. It was just coquetry, he decided.

By then the carriage had caught up, and there was no chance for another private tête-à-tête before they reached the house, where a crowd of guests waited. Gwendolen, still in her dust-spattered riding dress, hat pushed back from her wind-tousled hair, basked in the quiet, admiring gaze of everyone who watched her — for every one of them already accepted she was the woman Grandcourt had chosen to be his wife. The odious Mr. Lush was nowhere to be seen, and the thrill of all that admiring attention lifted her spirits, chasing away for a little while that uneasy feeling that she was fighting against her own wishes, that she might soon regret her careless reply. There was no way to tell if Grandcourt was offended: his manner was exactly as calm, as unchanging as it had always been. Gwendolen was sharp enough to know by now that his manner gave her no clue at all to what he was thinking — and the fact that nothing had changed in his behaviour only made her more afraid of him.

She had only ever visited Diplow for formal dinners before, so after luncheon, when many of the guests had drifted off, Lady Flora Hollis suggested the remaining party wander the grounds to see the pretty views from the garden windows. Again and again, Grandcourt had a perfect chance to pull Gwendolen aside, to speak to her where no one could hear. But he did not. He barely spoke to anyone else, but what he said to her was no more eager, no more intimate than it had been on the night of their very first meeting. He looked at her as often as he always did, and some of her old defiant spirit rose up in her, so she looked right back at him, unflinching, almost preferring that his handsome, still face gave nothing away at all.

At last, it seemed as if he had settled on a plan. The whole party had gathered by the ornamental pool to laugh at Fetch, the spaniel, who was determined to bring a water lily back to the bank like Cowper’s famous Beau, and refused to give up even after his first attempt ended with him splashing about in the reeds. Grandcourt stood just outside the little crowd with Gwendolen, turned slowly, and fixed his gaze on a small knoll planted full of bright American shrubs, with a winding path leading up to a flat little platform at the top.
“This is tedious,” he said, in his lazy, drawling tone. “Shall we go up there?”
“Oh, of course — we came to explore, after all,” Gwendolen said. She felt a little flicker of pleasure, and a little flicker of fear, all at once.

The path was far too narrow for him to offer her his arm, so they walked up in silence. When they reached the little flat clearing at the top, Grandcourt said: “There’s nothing to see here. It wasn’t worth the climb at all.”
Gwendolen did not laugh. She stood perfectly silent, holding the folds of her riding dress up out of the grass like a marble statue, her hand tightening hard around the handle of her whip, which she had snatched up automatically when the group had set off on their walk.
“What sort of place do you prefer?” Grandcourt asked.
“Different places are nice in their own way,” she said. “On the whole, I like open, cheerful places best. I don’t care for anything dark or sombre.”
“Offendene is far too sombre for you,” he said.
“It is, a little.”
“You won’t stay there much longer, I hope.”
“Oh, I expect I will. Mamma likes being close to her sister, the rector’s wife.”
There was another short stretch of silence.
“Surely you’re not going to live there forever, even if Mrs. Davilow does,” he said.
“I don’t know,” Gwendolen replied, her words tumbling out a little nervously, as she flicked her whip against the leaves of a rhododendron bush in front of her. “We women don’t get to go off adventuring, do we? We can’t go looking for the North-West Passage, or the source of the Nile, or hunt tigers in the East. We have to stay where we grew up, or where other people choose to transplant us. We’re raised just like potted flowers, trained to look as pretty as we can, and be bored without complaining. That’s what I think about plants, anyway. Half of them are dreadfully bored, and that’s why so many of them turn poisonous. What do you think?”
“I quite agree,” Grandcourt said, drawn off his intended train of thought by her odd, whimsical little speech. “Almost everything is a bore.” After a short pause, he continued, in his slow, refined drawl: “But a woman can get married.”
“Some women can,” Gwendolen said.
“And you certainly can, unless you choose to be obstinately cruel,” he said.
“I’m not sure I’m not both cruel and obstinate,” Gwendolen said, suddenly turning her head to look full at him, for she had felt his eyes on her the entire conversation. She wanted to see what effect the look would have on her, not just on him.

He stood perfectly still, half a yard away from her, and for a split second she thought of that lotus-eater’s drowsy stupor that hung around him, and that was now beginning to wrap around her too.
“Are you as uncertain about yourself as you make everyone else feel about you?” he asked.
“I’m completely uncertain about myself,” Gwendolen said. “I don’t know how uncertain anyone else is.”
“And you want everyone to think you don’t care at all?” Grandcourt said, a faint new edge of hardness in his tone.
“I didn’t say that,” Gwendolen said, hesitating, turning her eyes away to whip the rhododendron bush again. She wished she was back on horseback, so she could gallop away as fast as she liked. She could hardly run down the knoll, after all.
“So you do care, then?” Grandcourt said, his tone softening back into that lazy drawl.

“Ah — my whip!” Gwendolen yelped, in a little flutter of distress. Her agitation had made her fumble it, and the gold-handled whip flew out of her grasp, bouncing through the shrubbery to lodge firmly in the branches of an azalea halfway down the slope. She could run down after it now, laughing prettily, and Grandcourt was forced to follow — but she was faster than him, snatched the whip back, and kept walking down to the flat level of the lawn, pausing when she reached it to look back at him, cheeks flushed, eyes bright, as if she had just won some small, secret victory. That bright, excited flush was still visible on her face when the two of them rejoined the rest of the party, and Mrs. Davilow noticed it immediately.
“All just coquetry,” Grandcourt thought to himself. “The next time I beckon, she’ll come.”
He felt sure that final moment would come the very next day, at the archery picnic planned for Cardell Chase, the same event they had first agreed on the night of the ball.

Even in Gwendolen’s own mind, two possible outcomes were circling each other: two different choices she could make, as if she stood on the line between two countries, and she had no idea which side she would tumble over to at any second. This strange feeling that she could not predict her own choices filled her with quiet astonishment and terror. Her favourite rule for life — “do as you like” — seemed to be failing her, and she could not guess what she would want to do a minute from now. The prospect of marrying Grandcourt was far more appealing than she had ever imagined any marriage could be. The dignity, the luxury, the power to do almost every little thing she pleased that was now so close she could almost touch it, wrapped around her like the rich scent of something she had dreamed of for years but never got to smell. And Grandcourt himself? There seemed no flaw at all in him, as far as a lover and husband went. Gwendolen wanted to climb into the gilded chariot of his fortune and drive the plunging, powerful horses herself, with a husband at her side who would simply fold his arms, nod, and let her do as she liked, without looking ridiculous. For all her sharp wits and all the books her mother thought far too daring, Gwendolen had to admit her judgment of Grandcourt was still a little uncertain. He was adorably quiet, completely free of every silly affectation and absurd quirk she hated in other men — he was the perfect husband to stand beside her and make her look her very best. But what else was he? He had travelled everywhere, seen everything. That was very desirable, and it was especially flattering that after seeing all there was to see in the world, he had chosen her above everyone else. He never seemed to enjoy anything very much. That hardly mattered, she thought — and the fewer little passions and petty desires he had of his own, the more freedom his wife would have to follow hers. She was fully confident that once they were married, she would be able to manage him completely.

But why did he make her feel so unusually constrained now? Why was she less playful, less bold with him than she had ever been with any other admirer? That total lack of demonstrativeness she had once found so attractive was a charm in more ways than one — it was slightly numbing, as well.
