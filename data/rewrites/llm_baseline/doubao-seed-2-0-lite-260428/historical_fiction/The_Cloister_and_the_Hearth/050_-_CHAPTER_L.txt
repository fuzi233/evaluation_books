Among strangers in the foreign city, Margaret Brandt found a quiet, comparative peace, and soon a new, unexpected stroke of good fortune seemed to light their path. A wealthy civic dignitary, ill and peevish as only a man of means with a stubborn fever can be, ran from one doctor to the next, bled and purged so thin he was nearly a shadow of himself, until he found himself at death’s door and sent in desperation for Peter Brandt.
Peter took one look at the wasted nobleman, heaved a whole battalion of his overzealous colleagues’ medicine bottles straight out the open window, beat fresh egg yolks smooth with neat Schiedam gin, dosed the patient in tiny gentle sips, then followed it with tender meat stewed in red wine and spring water, shredding in mild harmless febrifugal herbs that did no harm and nourished where the other doctors had only drained. Before long the dignitary was walking again, looking something between a living man and a limp pillowcase, and being a voluble, grateful sort, he spread Peter’s fame through every street in the city. The old healer, who had deserved a reputation for decades in vain, earned it overnight by pure luck. For a little while, things looked bright. Peter’s long-starved pride was stoked, his purse began to fill, and he spent almost every coin he earned on rare sovereign herbs and choice imported drugs – until Margaret, with her soft but firm wit, wheedled a good portion of his gains away to set aside for a rainy day.
That burst of happy excitement came too late. One evening, leaning in to bid her good night, Peter’s voice came thick and slurred, almost unintelligible. The next morning, Margaret found him motionless in his bed, speechless, only barely conscious. She, who had been his attentive student for years, saw at once he had suffered a paralytic stroke. Still, she did not trust her own judgment fully, and ran for a doctor – one of the very men Peter had once stopped from bleeding the dignitary to death – who cheerfully confirmed her fears. He immediately proposed bleeding the half-paralyzed old man, but Margaret refused flat. “He always said never to bleed the old and weak,” she told him.
Peter lived, but he was never the same man again. His memory was frayed to ribbons, he could not be trusted to write a single prescription, and the line of patients who had waited eagerly to see the famous new healer all melted away. Misery stared her straight in the face. With no income, two helpless men to house and feed, and her own pregnancy fast approaching, she swallowed her pride and decided to go for aid and comfort to her cousin William Johnson, a relation she had avoided out of shame for her own poverty until now.
She arrived to find him and his supposed servant sitting in the same room, neither of them sober. Margaret mastered her surprise, gave them her greetings, and said she had come to speak of private family business, glancing pointedly at the woman to hint for a moment of privacy. The woman took the hint, but not as Margaret expected.
“Oh, speak free before me, can’t she, old man?” she cackled, slapping William on the shoulder.
Margaret’s face burned scarlet. “I crave your pardon, mistress. I did not know my cousin had fallen into this town’s loose customs. I shall come back at a fitter hour,” she said, rising to leave.
“I know not what you mean by the town’s customs,” the woman snapped, bouncing to her feet. “But I know this: a faithful servant’s duty is to keep her master from being preyed on by his beggarly kin.”
“You do yourself too little credit, mistress,” Margaret retorted sharply. “You are no servant. Your own tongue betrays you. It is only when the ape climbs a tree that she shows her tail so plain. There sits the real servant, poor soul! And as long as things stand so, never fear his kin will be so poor in spirit as to come begging where the likes of you can flout their sorrow.” She cast one look of mute, stinging reproach at her cousin, who had sat passive and silent through the whole exchange, too cowardly to speak up for her, and swept haughtily out of the house. She did not shed a single tear until she got home, and realized how completely alone she was.
Yet even in that dark hour, the coming child proved to be her greatest strength. The wild, stubborn maternal instinct that makes every bird, every beast, every lowly insect cunning in providing food and shelter for their unborn young woke strong in Margaret Brandt. She sat brooding for hours, turning every possible scheme over in her head, determined to outrun destitution. Even with five gold pieces still left from their journey from Sevenbergen, she could see starvation creeping closer, step by inevitable step.
When a woman thinks with the sharp, focused intensity usually reserved for men, her plans are as practical and unflinching as any man’s. Margaret rose, bade Martin carry the half-paralyzed Peter to a back room, tidied her own front parlor spotless, polished her father’s old glass alchemy globe until it glowed, hung it from the ceiling, dusted the stuffed crocodile Peter had nailed to the wall years before, and fixed it to the outer front of their house. She gave Martin careful instructions, then set him to loitering like a casual sentinel by the street door, to tell every curious passerby that a great, ancient, learned alchemist dwelt within, who in his rare moments of rest from searching for the fabled elixir of life, deigned to amuse himself by curing mortal ills.
Patients began to trickle in almost immediately. Margaret received them all, and told them no one could see the great alchemist, for not even princes were allowed to disturb his studies. They must tell her their symptoms, and return in two hours. When they came back, their medicine was always waiting for them. For wealthier, more worshipful patients, she would scan their faces, take their pulse, listen long to their complaints, then slip quietly back to Peter’s room, sit and puzzle out how her father, whose every method she had studied for years, would have treated the case. Then she would scrawl an illegible, cabalistic-looking note, bring it down reverently, and show it to the patient, asking if they could read the sacred prescription. None could. “Aye, but I can,” she would tell them. “It is sovereign. You may look on yourself as cured.” If she had the herbs and simples in stock, she would even let the patient watch her mix the medicine, glancing every few moments at the scribbled “prescription” as if afraid to make a mistake and offend the great alchemist’s science. They left with their purses light of coin, their pockets full of harmless, healing medicine, their heads full of faith, completely satisfied. The people wanted to be fooled, and Margaret gave them foolery that did far more good than harm.
After every patient left, she would take down the two little carved boxes Gerard had made her, one labeled *To-Day* and the other *To-Morrow*, tuck the small coins into the first for immediate expenses, and the larger coins and remaining gold pieces into the second, to save for the rainy day she knew was coming. Still, her conscience pricked her sore for running this little fraud, and every night after her last patient left, she knelt and prayed God to forgive her, for the sake of her unborn child. Then cure after cure was reported back to her, and her conscience softened, then hardened, as she saw she was doing more good than ill.
Old Martin Wittenhaagen had grown stiff and slow lately, after a lifetime of hard campaigning, but he was still as strong as an ox, and in this new strange business he showed all the qualities of a good soldier: unreasoning obedience, punctuality, speed, and a very bad weakness for strong drink. He fell in with a crowd of drinking “good fellows,” who plied him with Schiedam gin until he was babbling and bragging. He thought Margaret’s little scheme was not science at all, but pure brilliant strategy, and he boasted loud and long in a mixed tavern company how “me and my General, we are biting the burghers for their coin.”
Word of the joke spread through the city, and before long, Margaret found two constables at her door. They led her, trembling and begging for mercy, before the burgomaster, where a whole row of real licensed physicians in long robes and square caps stood waiting to accuse her of practising unlawfully on the bodies of the duke’s lieges. At first she was too frightened to speak, the very name of the law paralyzing her. But when they questioned her, not unkindly – for no man could be harsh to a pretty, tearful pregnant girl – she told them the whole truth. She was her father’s lifelong pupil, she had only followed his methods, she had cured dozens of people, and she was not doing it for profit for vanity. “Sirs, I have two poor helpless honest old men at home, and a child coming, and how else am I to feed them?” she pleaded. “You will not stop a poor girl from earning her bread honestly, but you wink at idleness and shame all around us. You are all husbands and fathers; you must see I have no other choice.” The moment the words left her lips, she would have given everything to take them back, covering her face with one hand while tears trickled down beneath her fingers.
The burgomaster was unmoved by her plea. “What have we to do with that?” he said. “If you will vow never to break this law again, I will spare you imprisonment, and only exact a fine.”
Margaret clutched her hands together, and vowed most penitently that she would never cure a man or beast again, and was dismissed with the constables to pay her fine. The trouble was, the fine could only be paid from the *To-Morrow* box, which she had nailed shut tight weeks before, to stop herself from dipping into the emergency savings on a trivial temptation. Now she could not pull the nails out. The constables grew impatient, and offered to break it open for her, but she refused. “You will break it worse than I shall.” She picked up a hammer, struck it too softly, lost all strength for a moment, and wept hysterically, until finally she brought the hammer down hard. A little broken cry bubbled from her throat as the carved wood split apart. The fine took every last coin of her “unlawful gains” and two of her precious gold pieces to boot. When the constables left, she gathered the broken splinters of Gerard’s hand-carved box and the few pitiful coins left to her on the table, wrapped her arms around them, let her long hair fall loose over the wreckage, and sobbed, “My love’s box is broken, and my heart with it.”
Martin came in to find her prostrate, and when he heard what had happened, he was not so sad as furious. Some traitor gossiper had betrayed him. He swore he would hunt the man down and run an arrow through his throat.
“Idle chat, idle chat!” Margaret moaned, not lifting her head from the table. “When you have slain every gossip in this town, can we eat them? Tell me how we are to keep the three of us fed, or hold your peace, and let the saints leave me a moment to think.” Martin held his tongue, and looked uneasily at his defeated little general.
Towards evening, Margaret washed her face, pinned her hair back up, and bade Martin take the stuffed crocodile down off the wall, and set out a wicker laundry basket in its place.
“I can do finer linen work than any washerwoman on this street,” she said, “and you will carry the basket of linen for me.”
“That I will, for your sake,” Martin said.
“Good Martin, forgive me for speaking sharp to you just now.”
Even as they spoke, a man who had come looking for the alchemist knocked at the door. Margaret told him the mayor had forbidden her to sell medicine. “But I will gladly iron and starch your linen for you, and come fetch it from your house myself.”
“Are you mad, young woman?” he huffed. “I came for a leech, and you offer me a washerwoman?” and he stormed out in a huff.
“Stupid creature,” Margaret said sadly.
Presently a woman came, to describe the symptoms of her sick little boy. Margaret stopped her before she could finish. “The bailiff has forbidden me to sell drugs. But I will wash, iron, and starch all your linen for you – and I will come fetch it from your house.”
“Oh, aye,” the woman said. “I have a pile of smocks and ruffs all foul. Come for them, and when you are at the house, you can look in on the boy for me.”
An introduction was an introduction. Two or three of the former patients who had come to her for medicine hired her as their washerwoman, soon after.
“Martin, you must help,” Margaret said. “I will not keep more cats than can catch mice.”
“Mistress, I have the stomach for the work, but not the knack, worst luck,” he said.
“Oh, I do not mean the starching and ironing, that takes a deft woman’s hand. But the scrubbing? Any man can manage that. Why, a mule has wit enough to scrub linen with his hooves, if you drove him to the tub. Come, off with your doublet, and try.”
“I am your man,” the old soldier said, stripping his sleeves up for the unwonted toil. “I’ll risk my arm in the soapsuds, if you’ll risk your good name as a washerwoman.”
“My what?”
“Your glory and honour as a washerwoman.”
“Gramercy! If you are man enough to bring me half-washed linen to iron, I am woman enough to fling it straight back in the suds.”
And so the brave girl and the brave old soldier worked side by side, cheerfully, and kept the wolf from their door. They could pay the rent every week, and keep a pot boiling over the fire, but nothing more. Margaret glued the broken *To-Morrow* box back together, and her tears fell into the glue as she worked, cementing her exiled lover’s broken little box with them. When it was whole again, it stood empty, for there was no spare coin left to tuck inside it.
Word of her good work spread, and eventually she was recommended as a washerwoman to the burgomaster himself. One day, as she brought his clean linen home to his kitchen, she heard the servants gossiping that his only daughter, eighteen years old, was stricken with a mysterious wasting illness, and not expected to live. Margaret cross-questioned them gently, gathered snippets of the girl’s symptoms from one servant and the next, and pondered every detail.
A few days later, she met the burgomaster on the street. He recognized her at once. “Why, you are the unlicensed doctor.”
“I was,” Margaret said. “Now I am your worship’s washerwoman.”
The dignitary coloured. “That is a sharp come-down.”
“I bear no malice,” Margaret said. “Your worship could have been far harsher to me. I would rather do you a kindness. Sir, you have a sick daughter. Let me see her.”
The burgomaster shook his head. “That cannot be. The law I enforce on others I cannot break myself.”
Margaret’s eyes went wide. “Alack, sir, I seek no payment for curing folk now! I am only a washerwoman. I think one may heal all the world, if the world will but let you starve in return.”
“That is no more than just,” the burgomaster said, and added, “If you do not trade on it, there is no offence in it.”
“Then let me see her.”
“What good would it do? The most learned leeches in Rotterdam have all tended her, and done her no good at all. Her illness is inscrutable. One says it is the spleen, one the liver, one the blood, one the stomach, and one says she is possessed of a demon. In truth, she acts like it: shuns all company, pines alone, eats no more than a sparrow, speaks to no one, and wastes thinner and paler every day, drawing closer to the grave.”
“Sir,” Margaret said, “if you took your velvet doublet to six different traders in Rotterdam, and asked each what it was worth, they would all give you the exact same answer, because they know their trade. But all your leeches give six different answers. That is because they do not know theirs. My father used to say every physician is infatuated with one particular ailment, and sees it in every patient, like a bat seeing only its own favourite prey. Most of them would have given you those same six diagnoses without ever laying eyes on your daughter. Let me see her. We are both women, and that counts for much.”
When he still hesitated, the frustration of her pregnancy broke through, and two violet eyes flashed fire. “Saints of heaven! Is this how men love their own flesh and blood? Her mother would have taken me by the arm and carried me straight to the sick room before now.”
“Come with me,” the burgomaster said hastily.
He stepped into his daughter’s room ahead of Margaret. “Mistress, I have brought you a new doctor.”
The pale, sullen eighteen-year-old girl wrenched her shoulder away in contempt, and turned even closer to the fire she was huddled over.
Margaret came in softly, and sat down beside her. “But this one will not torment you.”
“A woman!” the young lady exclaimed, half surprised, half scornful.
“Tell her your symptoms,” the burgomaster urged.
“What for? You will be no wiser.”
“You will be none the worse.”
“Very well. My food has no taste, and I have no heart for anything at all. Cure me now, and go.”
“Patience a little while longer,” Margaret said gently. “Is your food tasteless on your tongue, no matter what you eat?”
“Aye. How did you know that?”
“I did not, until you told me. I think you would be the better for a little good company.”
“I think not. What use is their silly chatter to me?” Margaret asked the father to leave them alone, and put a few more soft, practical questions, then sat and thought a minute.

“Nay… aye. How knew you that?”
