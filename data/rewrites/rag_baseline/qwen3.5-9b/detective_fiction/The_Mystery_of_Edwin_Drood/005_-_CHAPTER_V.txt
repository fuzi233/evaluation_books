The moonlight fell flat across the Close, illuminating a scene of static violence. John Jasper stepped from the shadow of the arches into the pale glare, halting abruptly. Before him leaned Stony Durdles, a figure encrusted in mortar and lime, his dinner bundle resting heavily against the iron railing of the burial ground. Opposite him, a small boy in rags hurled stones with the rhythmic indifference of a machine. Some struck Durdles; others missed. The boy cared for neither outcome, save for the specific joy of impact. Upon hitting his mark, the boy blew a sharp, triumphant whistle through a jagged gap in his gums, where several teeth were missing. On a miss, he yelped, "Mulled agin!" and recalibrated his aim with renewed ferocity.

"What are you doing to the man?" Jasper demanded, his voice cutting through the silence.

"Making a cock-shy of him," the boy replied.

"Give me those stones in your hand."

"Yes, I'll give 'em you down your throat, if you come a-ketching hold of me," the boy retorted, shaking loose and backing away. "I'll smash your eye, if you don't look out!"

"Baby-Devil that you are, what has the man done to you?"

"He won't go home."

"What is that to you?"

"He gives me a 'apenny to pelt him home if I ketches him out too late," the boy explained, chanting like a primitive incantation, stumbling through the tatters of his boots. "Widdy widdy wen! I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—ten, Widdy widdy wy! Then—E—don't—go—then—I—shy— Widdy Widdy Wake-cock warning!" The chant ended with a sweeping gesture and another stone aimed at Durdles.

Jasper extended a beckoning finger, inviting the boy to follow, though he knew dragging or coaxing was futile. He crossed to the railing where Durdles stood, profoundly meditative amidst his creations. "Do you know this thing, this child?" Jasper asked, searching for a definition.

"Deputy," Durdles nodded.

"Is that its—his—name?"

"Deputy," Durdles assented. "I'm man-servant up at the Travellers' Twopenny in Gas Works Garding. All us man-servants at Travellers' Lodgings is named Deputy. When we're chock full and the Travellers is all a-bed I come out for my 'elth." Withdrawing into the road, Deputy resumed his aim.

"Hold your hand," Jasper cried sharply, "and don't throw while I stand so near him, or I'll kill you! Come, Durdles; let me walk home with you to-night. Shall I carry your bundle?"

"Not on any account," Durdles replied, adjusting the weight. "Durdles was making his reflections here when you come up, sir, surrounded by his works, like a poplar Author." He introduced a sarcophagus within the railing, white and cold in the moonlight. "Mrs. Sapsea;" he pointed to the monument of the devoted wife. "Late Incumbent;" he indicated the Reverend Gentleman's broken column. "Departed Assessed Taxes;" he gestured to a vase and towel standing on what might represent a cake of soap. "Former pastrycook and Muffin-maker, much respected;" he marked a gravestone. "All safe and sound here, sir, and all Durdles's work. Of the common folk, that is merely bundled up in turf and brambles, the less said the better. A poor lot, soon forgot."

"This creature, Deputy, is behind us," Jasper noted, glancing back. "Is he to follow us?"

The relationship between Durdles and Deputy was capricious. When Durdles turned with the slow gravity of beery suddenness, Deputy executed a wide circuit into the road, assuming a defensive posture. "You never cried Widdy Warning before you begun to-night," Durdles observed, suddenly recalling—or perhaps inventing—an offense.

"Yer lie, I did," Deputy countered, offering his only form of polite contradiction.

"Own brother, sir," Durdles remarked, turning again and forgetting the slight as quickly as he had conceived it. "Own brother to Peter the Wild Boy! But I gave him an object in life."

"At which he takes aim?" Jasper suggested.

"That's it, sir," Durdles returned, satisfied. "at which he takes aim. I took him in hand and gave him an object. What was he before? A destroyer. What work did he do? Nothing but destruction. What did he earn by it? Short terms in Cloisterham jail. Not a person, not a piece of property, not a winder, not a horse, nor a dog, nor a cat, nor a bird, nor a fowl, nor a pig, but what he stoned, for want of an enlightened object. I put that enlightened object before him, and now he can turn his honest halfpenny by the three penn'orth a week."

"I wonder he has no competitors."

"He has plenty, Mr. Jasper, but he stones 'em all away."

"Now, I don't know what this scheme of mine comes to," Durdles pursued, considering it with sodden gravity. "I don't know what you may precisely call it. It ain't a sort of a—scheme of a—National Education?"

"I should say not," Jasper replied.

"I should say not," Durdles assented; "then we won't try to give it a name."

"He still keeps behind us," Jasper repeated, looking over his shoulder. "is he to follow us?"

"We can't help going round by the Travellers' Twopenny, if we go the short way, which is the back way," Durdles answered. "and we'll drop him there."

They proceeded; Deputy, as a rear-rank element, took open order, invading the silence of the hour and place by stoning every wall, post, pillar, and other inanimate object along the deserted path.

"Is there anything new down in the crypt, Durdles?" Jasper asked.

"Anything old, I think you mean," Durdles growled. "It ain't a spot for novelty."

"Any new discovery on your part, I meant."

"There's a old 'un under the seventh pillar on the left as you go down the broken steps of the little underground chapel as formerly was; I make him out (so fur as I've made him out yet) to be one of them old 'uns with a crook. To judge from the size of the passages in the walls, and of the steps and doors, by which they come and went, them crooks must have been a good deal in the way of the old 'uns! Two on 'em meeting promiscuous must have hitched one another by the mitre pretty often, I should say."

Without attempting to correct the literalness of this opinion, Jasper surveyed his companion—covered from head to foot with old mortar, lime, and stone grit—as though he, Jasper, were becoming imbued with a romantic interest in this weird life. "Yours is a curious existence."

Without furnishing the least clue to whether he received this as a compliment or its reverse, Durdles gruffly answered: "Yours is another."

"Well! inasmuch as my lot is cast in the same old earthy, chilly, never-changing place, Yes. But there is much more mystery and interest in your connection with the Cathedral than in mine. Indeed, I am beginning to have some idea of asking you to take me on as a sort of student, or free 'prentice, under you, and to let me go about with you sometimes, and see some of these odd nooks in which you pass your days."

The Stony One replied generally, "All right. Everybody knows where to find Durdles, when he's wanted." Which, if not strictly true, was approximately so, if taken to express that Durdles may always be found in a state of vagabondage somewhere.

"What I dwell upon most," Jasper continued, pursuing his subject of romantic interest, "is the remarkable accuracy with which you would seem to find out where people are buried.—What is the matter? That bundle is in your way; let me hold it."

Durdles had stopped and backed a little (Deputy, attentive to all his movements, immediately skirmishing into the road), looking about for some ledge or corner to place his bundle on, when thus relieved of it. "Just you give me my hammer out of that," Durdles said, "and I'll show you."

*Clink, clink.* His hammer was handed to him.

"Now, lookee here. You pitch your note, don't you, Mr. Jasper?"

"Yes."

"So I sound for mine. I take my hammer, and I tap." (Here he struck the pavement, and the attentive Deputy skirmished at a rather wider range, as supposing that his head may be in requisition.) "I tap, tap, tap. Solid! I go on tapping. Solid still! Tap again. Holloa! Hollow! Tap again, persevering. Solid in hollow! Tap, tap, tap, to try it better.
