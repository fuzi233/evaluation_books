The harsh, scraping scratch of the town clerk’s quill against dry parchment was an abrasive violation of the room’s quiet—a sterile, rhythmic friction that seemed to scrape away the very humanity of the words being recorded. To Gerard, whose soul was deeply attuned to the resonant, nourishing cadences of hearth-side storytelling and the bubbling hum of a well-tended stewpot, this bureaucratic chamber felt entirely devoid of sustenance.

“In prison, sir?” Gerard asked, his voice strained against the oppressive acoustics of the magistrate’s office. “Good lack, for what misdeed?”

“Well, she is a witness, and may be a necessary one,” the alderman replied, his tone as flat and dry as the unbuttered crusts of pauper’s bread.

“Why, Messire Bailiff,” put in Denys, “you lay not all your witnesses by the heels, I trow.”

The alderman, his ego plumped by the title of bailiff, expanded slightly, though his words remained tethered to the dominant, bloodless lexicon of the law. “In a case of blood we detain all testimony that is like to give us leg bail, and so defeat justice, and that is why we still keep the women folk. For a man at odd times hides a week in one mind, but a woman, if she do her duty to the realm o' Friday, she shall undo it afore Sunday, or try. Could you see yon wench now, you should find her a-blubbering at having betrayed five males to the gallows. But now I think on't, there were other reasons for laying this one by the heels. Hand me those depositions, young sir.” He adjusted his spectacles, peering at a document that reduced a life’s tragedies to mere ink. “Ay! She was implicated; she was one of the band.”

A loud disclaimer burst from Denys and Gerard at once, their voices a sudden, warm harmony of righteous defense against the room’s cold dissonance.

“No need to deave me,” said the alderman, annoyed by the sudden spike in the room’s sonic intensity. “Here 'tis in black and white. 'Jean Hardy, being questioned, confessed that the girl Manon was the decoy, and her sweetheart was Georges Vipont, one of the band; and hanged last month: and that she had been deject ever since, and had openly blamed the band for his death... and it is his opinion she did but betray them out of very spite...'”

“His opinion!” cried Gerard indignantly. The sheer untranslatability of Manon’s profound grief into this vulgar legal shorthand appalled him. How could they flatten the nuanced, Épinal-inflected sorrow of a woman who had lost her love into the crude syntax of a convicted cut-throat? “What signifies the opinion of a felon burning to be revenged on her who delivered him to justice? Revenge—why, 'tis the strongest of passions! What madness to listen to him lying away an honest life!”

“Young man, restrain thy heat in presence of authority,” the alderman countered. “In this land we question all the world. We shut not the barn-door against any man's grain. Only having taken it in, we do winnow and sift it.”

“Why, who but the thieves would breathe a word against her?” Gerard demanded.

“Marry, herself.”

Gerard looked blank. He took Manon’s deposition, his eyes scanning the translated words. The text was a linguistic cage. It stripped away the rich, regional vowels of her native Épinal, flattening her tragic history into a sterile confession: she had left service, been hired at 'The Fair Star', loved a young man, discovered the inn was a slaughterhouse, and wept for the travelers' souls. When her bearded lover died, the sight of Denys’s beard had moved her to intervene. 

The paper almost dropped from Gerard's hands. He saw the subtle power dynamics embedded in this dominant lexicon; it was designed to condemn. He threw himself on his knees, his plea vibrating with a desperate, acoustic resonance that finally pierced the alderman's bureaucratic shell. “Denys and I be true men, and you will rend our hearts if you kill that poor simple girl. What is left for us to do then but cut our throats at her gallows' foot?”

The alderman, tough but mortal, became peevish. He summoned the town clerk—a man composed entirely of rigid impossibilities. The clerk insisted she could not be pardoned as she wasn't a formal accessory, spouting mechanical, legalistic objections. But the alderman, irritated by the grating, repetitive noise of the clerk’s denials, decided to flex his own authority. With Denys depositing five gold pieces and the landlord of “The White Hart” acting as bondsman, Manon was liberated. The alderman eased his conscience by noting, in the crassly commercial terms of the town's ledger, that saving her saved the municipality the expense of a new hanging rope.

As they escorted her to “The White Hart,” the public, swayed by the archers' warm retellings, cheered her. The landlord, smelling the sweet aroma of impending custom and coin, gave her an upper chamber. Yet, alone, her mind fluctuated like a disturbed pond, and she wept.

Bidding the landlord keep her comfortable, Gerard and Denys stepped out into the street and were immediately engulfed in a horrific collision of sound. 

From one side came a large procession. It was an assault on the acoustic ecology of the village—a cacophony of howls, jeers, and those indescribable, guttural cries by which humanity reveals its base relationship to the beasts of the forest. This was the mob escorting the prisoners to exposure. From the opposite side crept a second procession: slow, silent, and thinly attended by a few friars. A profound, rhythmic quiet hung over it. These were the gathered bones of the thieves' victims, making their final, mythic journey to hallowed ground. 

The carts wedged together in the narrow street. The sensory dissonance was unbearable. When a woman shrieked, “Look at your work, ye dogs!” the crowd erupted in a horrible, jagged yell. The culprits groaned, pale and hollow-eyed, staring enviously at the quiet bier of the very souls they had slaughtered. Manon, upstairs, peeked trembling through her fingers at the auditory nightmare below.

The strange meeting parted the friends. Denys yielded to the visceral noise of revenge, while Gerard, seeking the solemn quietude that sustained his spirit, doffed his bonnet and followed the bones. The rhythmic tread of the artisans who joined him became a communal heartbeat, a silent resistance against the town’s ugly industrial clamor, guiding the remains to the churchyard.

After the rites, Gerard approached the curé to purchase a mass. Gerard, son of Catherine, understood the agrarian necessity of frugality, viewing every transaction as a careful weighing of resources. He attempted to haggle the price down. But the good curé, a traditional elder steeped in the rhythmic cycles of his parish, gently and adroitly parried the youth’s ingenuity, blandly screwing him up to the fair market price. 

This collision in the prayer market blossomed into mutual esteem. The curé, recognizing a fellow soul who respected both piety and worldly prudence, invited Gerard into his parlor. 

“I’ll broach a bottle of my old Médoc for thee,” the curé said, his tone softening with reverence. 

While groping in his dark cupboard for the choice bottle, the curé muttered, “At their old tricks again!”

“Plaît-il?” said Gerard.

“I said nought. Ay, here 'tis.” The curé smiled, pulling forth a bottle coated in the sacred dust of the cellar. He poured the wine. It fell into the cups with a rich, heavy plash—a beautiful, resonant sound. The air immediately bloomed with the profound, olfactory landscape of the Gironde: damp earth, sun-baked vines, and the deep, macerated essence of dark plums and ancient oak. It was not merely a beverage; it was generational memory distilled into liquid sustenance. 

The curé added a log to the fire. “And so I said 'At their old tricks!' did I? Come, sip the good wine, and, whilst it lasts, story for story, I care not if I tell you a little tale.”

Gerard’s eyes sparkled. The wine coated his palate, complex and enduring. 

“Once upon a time,” the curé began, his voice carrying the rhythmic cadence of a seasoned storyteller, “in the duchy of Burgundy, there lived a curé. The parish was small, the parishioners greedy. They starved their curé, believing meagre diet would yield holier prayers. At last, he pickled a rod for them.”

The curé recounted how, when the richest farmer brought twins to be baptized, he demanded the burial fees upfront. When challenged by the local miller in the dialect of the village, the curé replied that as sons of Adam, they were bound to die, and it would cost them their lives anyway. The village laughed, but when every subsequent child was charged a burial fee, discontent festered. 

Summoned before the bishop, the curé explained his terroir-specific problem: “I searched the register book and probed the matter. It came out that of those born at Domfront, all, but here and there one, did go and get hanged at Aix. Being ‘pendards,’ they hang in the air and pay no funeral fees to the Church. So, greed against greed, I laid a trap to keep them from hanging.”

The bishop had laughed until he wept, approving the act until the village mended its manners. The ringleaders submitted, and the bishop eventually transferred the curé to this decent parish, where he could offer a glass of old Médoc to the worthy.

A light broke in upon Gerard. Domfront. The very thieves who had nearly ended his life. “Ay!” said his host, seeing Gerard's realization. “I am that curé. My successors have surely been wheedled out of those fees. You are well out of that parish.”

Their communion of wine and memory was interrupted by the sudden entrance of the curé’s little niece. “Uncle, the weighing—la! A stranger!” she cried, before vanishing.

“Wet thy beard once more, and come with me,” the curé instructed.

They moved to the church porch, stepping back into the cyclical, agrarian routines of the village. The sexton stood beside a huge pair of iron scales. A woman stepped forward, cradling a sickly child. 

“Be it heavy, be it light, I vow, in rye meal of the best, whate'er this child shall weigh, and the same will duly pay to Holy Church, an if he shall cast his trouble,” she chanted. Her words were a beautiful, rustic poetry, rooted in the earth. She offered the ultimate sustenance—freshly milled rye, smelling of toasted bran and fields kissed by the sun—in exchange for her child's life. 

The child was weighed, screaming as if the cold iron were the baptismal font. 

“Courage, dame!” cried Gerard, attuned to the vigor in the cry. “This is a good sign. There is plenty of life here to battle its trouble.”

“Now, blest be the tongue that tells me so,” the poor woman murmured, hushing her ponderling. 

Suddenly, a loud, dictatorial voice shattered the porch’s gentle acoustic harmony. “Way there, make way for the seigneur!”

The small folk parted. In marched the lord of the manor, a walking spectacle of commercialized ostentation. He wore a jerkin richly furred, satin doublet, red hose, shoes like skates, and a diamond-hilted sword in a velvet scabbard, a hawk perched aggressively on his wrist. He flung himself onto the scales with a harsh, metallic clatter, expecting the heavens to be impressed. The hawk flapped, its bells jingling sharply, entirely out of tune with the sacred space.

The curé had told Gerard that this lord, having been sick unto death, had vowed his weight in bread and cheese to the poor. Yet, instead of approaching the sustenance with reverence—appreciating the tang of the aged farmhouse curds or the crusty resilience of the baked loaves that would soon balance the iron—the lord attempted to cheat. 

“Permit me, my lord; if your lordship continues to press your lordship's staff on the other scale, you will disturb the balance,” the curé said firmly.

“Mille diables! What am I to do with it, then?” the lord barked. 

When the lord held the staff wide, the curé whispered to Gerard, “Cretensis incidit in Cretensem!”—Diamond cut diamond. He then obsequiously suggested the lord let his lackey hold the bird to save a pound of cheese. The arrogant noble scoffed, declaring he wouldn't grudge his fowl the benefit of the Church. 

While the immense weights of bread and cheese were recorded, the curé urged the lord into the church to inspect a newly discovered artifact of his lineage. Workmen repairing a pillar had found a skeleton erected inside it.

“What, the old dict was true after all?” the lord asked.

“It is he!” The curé explained to Gerard how the great-great-grandfather, a proud man, had sworn his son to bury him standing up within the masonry, declaring, “No base man shall pass over my stomach.” It was a stunning integration of mythic pride into the everyday domestic architecture of the nave. 

Just then, a sharp, hollow *clack* echoed discordantly off the vaulted ceilings. A skull came hopping down the aisle, bowled by a village boy. The harsh noise of desecrated bone skipping over stone was a violent affront to the ancestral silence. The boy, realizing his sacrilege, fled howling.

Instead of showing reverence for his forebear, the lord furiously hurled the skull after the boy. The curé gave a shout of dismay, but it was too late. The violent action triggered a whole ambuscade of children who had been hiding. They clattered out of the church like a covey of partridges, their footsteps echoing loudly. They had pocketed the smaller bones for primitive games, erasing the physical memory of the old noble piece by piece.

“I'll excommunicate them,” roared the curate, “and all their race.”

“Never heed,” the scapegrace lord said dismissively, stroking his hawk. “There is enough of him to swear by. Put him back!”

“Surely, my lord, 'tis your will his bones be laid in hallowed earth, and masses said for his poor prideful soul?” the curé pleaded, desperate to preserve the generational continuity.

But the noble merely stroked his bird. “Nay, the business is too old: he is out of purgatory by this time, up or down. I shall not draw my purse-strings for him. Every dog his day. Adieu, Messires, adieu, ancestor.” 

He sauntered off, whistling a sharp, piercing tune to his hawk, leaving behind the rich, silent history of his bloodline. The good curé stood in the quiet of the nave, looking ruefully after him, mourning the loss of a language of reverence that the lord had already forgotten how to speak.
