Every house, if you listen carefully enough at night when the fires have dropped to a whisper and the boards begin to talk in their old wooden dialect, has something it keeps in a cupboard. It may be an unpaid bill; it may be a portrait with eyes that follow you; it may be an aunt who refuses to die out of mere politeness. At Clavering Park the skeleton wore a ribbon, quoted Lamartine, and could weep on cue with the neatness of a kitchen-maid separating whites from yolks. Blanche Amory—“Missy” at home when people felt brave, “Blanche” when she insisted on being poetry itself—was as pretty as a sugared violet and as soothing, in constant use, as grit in a shoe.

You would have said she must make sunshine in whatever room she entered. And so she did—if you define sunshine as that bright, relentless glare which shows every speck of dust and makes everybody blink.

“I say, Strong,” Sir Francis Clavering drawled one day, after dinner, when the billiard-table had become a confessional and a cigar the priest, “I wish to the doose your wife was dead.”

Captain Strong, who met most of life the way a good cook meets a boiling pot—with a laugh and a towel—blew a calm cloud and answered, “So do I. That’s a cannon, by Jove. But she won’t; she’ll live for ever—you see if she don’t. Why do you wish her off the hooks, Frank, my boy?”

“Because then you might marry Missy. She ain’t bad-looking. She’ll have ten thousand, and that’s a good bit of money for such a poor old devil as you,” said the Baronet, as if proposing a joint of meat at market.

“And gad, Strong,” he added, souring, “I hate her worse and worse every day. I can’t stand her, Strong. By gad, I can’t.”

“I wouldn’t take her at twice the figure,” Strong said, laughing outright. “I never saw such a little devil in my life.”

“I should like to poison her,” Sir Francis observed, with the cool deliberation of a man choosing between port and claret. “By Jove I should.”

“Why, what has she been at now?” asked Strong.

“Nothing particular,” said Clavering; and that “nothing” in a house is commonly the very thing that breaks plates. “Only her old tricks. That girl has such a knack of making everybody miserable that, hang me, it’s quite surprising. Last night she sent the governess crying away from the dinner-table. Afterwards, as I was passing Frank’s room, I heard the poor little beggar howling in the dark, and found his sister had been frightening his soul out of his body, by telling him stories about the ghost that’s in the house. At lunch she gave my lady a turn; and though my wife’s a fool, she’s a good soul—I’m hanged if she ain’t.”

“What did Missy do to her?” Strong asked, still amused, for he was one of those obstinate men whose good-humour has no handle.

“Why, hang me, if she didn’t begin talking about the late Amory—my predecessor,” said Sir Francis with a grin that tried to look manly and came out peevish. “She got some picture out of the Keepsake, and said she was sure it was like her dear father. She wanted to know where her father’s grave was. Hang her father! Whenever Miss Amory talks about him, Lady Clavering always bursts out crying: and the little devil will talk about him in order to spite her mother. Today when she began, I got in a confounded rage; said I was her father; and—and that sort of thing, and then, sir, she took a shy at me.”

“And what did she say about you, Frank?” asked Strong.

“Gad, she said I wasn’t her father; that I wasn’t fit to comprehend her; that her father must have been a man of genius, and fine feelings, and that sort of thing: whereas I had married her mother for money.”

“Well, didn’t you?” Strong inquired, with that blunt honesty which makes the truth sound like a joke told at the wrong time.

“It don’t make it any the pleasanter to hear because it’s true, don’t you know,” Sir Francis said, shifting his cue as if he could rearrange his conscience with it. “I ain’t a literary man and that; but I ain’t such a fool as she makes me out. I don’t know how it is, but she always manages to put me in the hole. She turns all the house round her in her quiet way, and with her confounded sentimental airs. I wish she was dead, Ned.”

“It was my wife whom you wanted dead just now,” Strong reminded him.

“Well; when people bore my life out, I do wish they were dead,” said the Baronet, with his usual candour, “and I wish Missy were down a well, with all my heart.”

Thus, from the frankness of two gentlemen digesting their dinner in smoke, it appears that our little accomplished fairy possessed certain gifts which are not always welcomed in families—especially when they arrive without warning, like a sharp smell from the kitchen at midnight.

Blanche had talent, literary tastes, exquisitely tuned sympathies, and the firm belief that nobody within three counties deserved her. She lived, like many a genius, among relatives who could not comprehend her; and, like many a genius, she took pains that they should never forget it. Her stepfather’s reading—if you may call it that—lay between Bell’s Life and the Racing Calendar; and Lady Clavering wrote in a hand and orthography that suggested her education had been abandoned at thirteen in favour of ribbons. The poor lady would call asparagus “sparrowgrass,” and add an “h” to objects as if trying to give them a little pedigree; and Blanche, who corrected grammar the way a cook corrects seasoning—with a pinch that stings—would smile gently and set her mother trembling into worse mistakes.

If Blanche suffered, as she declared, it was chiefly in her own soul—where the tears were stored, decanted, and poured out at convenience. She had the kind of sorrow that increases with practice; the more she wept, the more accomplished her weeping became, until Mes Larmes would dribble on demand like a well-trained sauce from a ladle. Sentiment, indeed, behaves like certain maladies: indulge it and it grows greedy.

She had begun her “gush” early. Lamartine had been her chosen prophet from the first moment she could feel herself interesting. After that, she nourished her mind—so she said—upon French novels with an appetite that would have done honour to a farm-boy with a loaf. Balzac and George Sand were her daily bread; she devoured them by sixteen, and then, finding her own household too plain, lived with companions in what she called the spirit-world: tender Indiana, passionate Lélia, amiable Trenmor (that angelic convict), fiery Stenio, and all the other high-souled persons who inhabit romances and never have to pay for coal. She had been in love with Prince Rodolph and Prince Djalma while still in pinafores, and had settled the rights of woman with Indiana before she had finished learning to tie her sash. She played at love with heroes as she had once played at maternity with dolls—choosing, neglecting, resuming; to-day the blue-eyed darling, to-morrow the black-eyed one; and sometimes, in a fit of perverse taste, it would be the battered little object with a burnt nose and no eyes at all that received the warmest caresses.

As novelists are expected to know everything—even what a young lady does not know about herself—we may say (with the discretion of people peeping through keyholes in the name of art) that Mademoiselle Betsi, as Blanche had been called in her early Paris days, felt tender emotions at eleven for a Savoyard organ-grinder whom she persisted in believing a prince stolen from his parents; that at twelve an old and hideous drawing-master had “agitated” her heart (for love, like appetite, is not always fastidious); and that at thirteen, at Madame de Caramel’s boarding-school in the Champs Élysées—next door, as everybody knows, to Monsieur Rogron’s pension for young gentlemen—she carried on a correspondence with two youthful pensioners of the College of Charlemagne.

Betsi—yes. For the child had been christened Betsy, and had later, by her own will and fancy, crowned herself Blanche, as if baptism were a hat one could choose in a shop-window. Sir Francis’s one weapon, held always in reserve like a kitchen threat—“I’ll put you on bread and water”—was to call her publicly Betsy. The menace could still bring the Muse to momentary order; no genius enjoys being dragged into the world by its nursery-name.

In her treasure-chest she possessed a little museum of locks of hair, relics of “dear, dear” friendships—some married away, some transferred to other schools, one darling “Leocadie” recovered in horror keeping books in her father’s grocery in the Rue du Bac. Blanche had tasted disappointments, estrangements, and disillusions (always in her pretty French jargon), and felt herself marked for suffering as genius must be. Meanwhile she made her mother as uncomfortable as the day allowed, and drove her stepfather to dreams of wells and poison. Captain Strong alone, by the sheer obstinacy of his cheerfulness, refused to be governed by her tongue.

And while the household at the Park simmered with this delicate domestic acid, the town of Clavering bubbled with curiosity. It was not to be supposed that Madame Fribsby—the milliner, the purveyor of caps and gossip—should remain unmoved. At the first appearance of the Park family in church, she noted every bonnet, ribbon, and brodequin with the attention of a food-historian reading an old recipe: what was trimmed where, what was stitched how, what kind of silk whispered when it moved. Doctor Portman delivered one of his oldest sermons—richly preserved like fruit in brandy—but Madame Fribsby tasted none of it. Her mind was occupied with the rustle of fabrics and the faint squeak of new gloves, and the way modern fashion had begun to sound like commerce.

In a few days she had arranged an interview with Lady Clavering’s confidential attendant in the housekeeper’s room at the Park. Cards in French and English—proclaiming that Madame Fribsby received the newest fashions from Paris through her correspondent Madame Victorine, and that she made court and ball dresses for the nobility and gentry of the shire—were conveyed into the hands of Lady Clavering and Miss Amory, and, to Madame’s satisfaction, received favourably. The first thread had been slipped into the needle; the seam would follow.

Mrs. Bonner, Lady Clavering’s woman, soon became a frequent visitor in Madame Fribsby’s drawing-room, where evenings were stitched together out of green tea, scandal, hot Sally-Lunn cakes, and a little novel-reading. There is a certain kind of talk that rises like yeast when warmed with sugar and a secret. Mrs. Bonner found much time for these entertainments—far more than Miss Amory’s maid, who could seldom be spared; for Blanche worked her attendant harder than any factory.

Blanche loved clothes as some people love language: not for warmth, but for power, for transformation, for the way a new cut could rename a body. With poetic hunger for change, she altered her attire daily. Her maid, Pincott—an English girl apprenticed in Paris, skilled in the whole holy trinity of ripping, remodelling, and making the old look new—was kept at needle and comb from morning until far into night. The girl had respectable parents and a poor home: her father crippled, the family not quite ruined but never quite afloat; if she returned, she would only add weight to the sinking boat. So Pincott stayed where she could support herself and send a little relief back like crumbs from the rich table.

And Blanche, with that candour which is sometimes merely cruelty with good pronunciation, never let her forget it.

“I should send you away, Pincott,” she would say, “for you are a great deal too weak, and your eyes are failing you, and you are always crying and snivelling and wanting the doctor; but I wish that your parents at home should be supported, and I go on enduring you for their sake, mind.” Or: “Pincott, your wretched appearance and slavish manner, and red eyes, positively give me the migraine; I think I shall make you wear rouge, so that you may look a little cheerful.” Or: “Pincott, I can’t bear—even for the sake of your starving parents—that you should tear my hair out of my head in that manner; write to them and say that I dispense with your services.”

And then—after keeping the poor girl an hour trembling over her hair, which Blanche liked combed slowly while she lay reading French novels—she would go to bed at one, and say with royal sweetness, “Pincott, you may kiss me. Good night. I should like you to have the pink dress ready for the morning.” And thus, having dealt out her little blessings like sugared almonds at a funeral, the Muse would turn and sleep.

Blanche could lie abed as long as she chose; Pincott rose early to make the day’s miracle of ribbons and hems. She appeared with the same red eyes and wan face—offending Blanche by the stubborn fact of illness. The servant persisted in being unwell, and the mistress persisted in being surprised by it. Yet Blanche never thought herself harsh. At times she “made quite a friend” of Pincott, wrote pretty verses about the lonely little tiring-maid whose heart was far away, and considered this poetry as payment, or at least as proof of delicacy. She was a superior being and expected service as such. There are tyrannies done with oaths and whips; there are tyrannies done with a soft voice and a well-bred simper. The bruises are quieter in the second case; they are no less real.

But Blanche was a Muse—tremulous with excitability, tearful at the slightest touch, a butterfly who claimed that merely to brush her wing was to crush her. Vulgar people, she implied, did not understand the fine skin of genius.

So Pincott, occupied all day and night with stitching, hemming, ripping, combing, ironing, crimping; reading to her mistress in bed (for the girl knew both languages, and had a sweet voice and manner), took no share in Madame Fribsby’s little entertainments. Nor was she much missed; in society as in kitchens, those who do the hardest work are expected to be invisible.

Another person from the Park, however, became a constant guest of our milliner: the chief of the kitchen, Monsieur Mirobolant, with whom Madame Fribsby soon formed an intimacy.

The rustic inhabitants of Clavering, not being accustomed to Frenchmen except as a word in a war-song, were not so favourably impressed by Monsieur Alcide’s manners and appearance as that gentleman had imagined they must be. On a summer afternoon when his services were not required, he walked down the street in what he considered the easiest, most natural thing in the world: a light green frock or paletot; a crimson velvet waistcoat with blue glass buttons; a pantalon écossais with a check so large and decided it might have been intended as a warning signal; an orange satin neckcloth; jean-boots with shiny leather tips; and, crowning the whole, a gold-embroidered cap and a richly gilt cane. He fancied that, unless the beauty of his person attracted observation, there was nothing remarkable in the ensemble; he believed he showed the air of a gentleman of good Parisian ton. The very street, had it possessed ears educated in acoustic ecology, would have told him otherwise: his cane clicked, his buttons flashed, his colours shouted, and the town’s quiet domestic soundscape—hens, carts, the mild gossip of hedges—received him as it receives a factory whistle.

He went grinning and ogling at every woman he met with glances he meant to be fatal, and peered over railings and into windows at tranquil evening domesticity. Betsy, Mrs. Pybus’s maid, shrank back with a “Lor bless us!” as he ogled her over the laurel-bush. The Miss Bakers and their mamma stared as if an exotic bird had escaped into the lane. Presently children left their dirt-pies to follow him.

For a time Mirobolant believed himself admired, and walked on the more proudly, pleased to think he could confer delight like seasoning scattered with a generous hand. But the small followers were joined by larger ones; lads and girls from the factory, loosed at that hour, swelled the train. Laughter turned sharper. Jeers began to crack like thrown nutshells. “Frenchy! Frenchy!” they cried. “Frogs!” One demanded a lock of his long hair, which fell in rich ringlets. The hooting rose in a harsh chorus, a kind of human machinery, and the poor chef—who understood applause but not this particular music—began to perceive he was being cooked in his own sauce.

At this moment Madame Fribsby spied him with his mob at his heels and heard the scornful shouts. She ran out, crossed the street, held out her hand, and addressed him in his own language—ever so ill spoken, but recognisably home. She invited him into her abode; and when she had housed him within her door, she stood bravely on the threshold, facing the gibing crowd like a small general. She told them they were a pack of cowards to insult a man who could not speak their language and was alone without protection.

The little crowd, with ironical cheers and final hoots, retreated. Madame Fribsby was respected in the place; her oddity and kindness had earned her friends; and even factory girls can recognise a force of character when it speaks plainly.

Poor Mirobolant was grateful to hear French, however battered. Frenchmen, with their national politeness, pardon our faults more readily than we pardon their English; they will endure our blunders through a long conversation without the least propensity to grin. He vowed she was his guardian angel; he declared he had not yet met such suavity among les Anglaises; and he paid her compliments as if she had been the fairest duchess in Europe. Alcide Mirobolant did homage, after his fashion, to all womankind, and never troubled himself with distinctions of rank in the realms of beauty.

Next day one of his aides-de-camp arrived at the milliner’s with a basket, bearing the tributes of the rescued artist: a cream flavoured with pineapple, cool and perfumed as a summer pantry; a mayonnaise of lobster he declared not unworthy of his hand nor of her to whom he offered it; and a box of preserved fruits of Provence, jewel-like and sticky with sun. A gallant note accompanied them to “La respectable Fribsbi.” Her kindness, he wrote, had made “a green place in the desert of his existence”; her suavity would ever contrast in memory with the grossièreté of the rustic population unworthy to possess such a jewel.

Thus sprang up an intimacy of the most confidential nature between the milliner and the chief of the kitchen. Yet Madame did not know whether to receive his friendship with pleasure or mortification; for he persisted in calling her “La respectable Fribsbi,” “La vertueuse Fribsbi,” and in declaring that he should consider her as his mother, while hoping she would regard him as her son. It was not very long ago, she thought, that French words had been addressed to her suggesting another sort of bond. She sighed, and looked up at the picture of her Carabineer. It is astonishing how young some hearts remain when heads have need of a front, or a little hair-dye; and at that moment Madame Fribsby, as she told young Alcide, felt as romantic as a girl of eighteen.

When talk drifted in that direction—and at their first intimacy Madame was rather inclined to steer it there—Alcide always diverged, politely as a man turning a sauce so it shall not catch. It was as his mother he insisted on regarding her. In no other capacity would he recognise her; and with that relationship the gentle lady was forced to content herself, when she found how deeply the artist’s heart was engaged elsewhere.

He was not long before he described to her the subject and origin of his passion.
