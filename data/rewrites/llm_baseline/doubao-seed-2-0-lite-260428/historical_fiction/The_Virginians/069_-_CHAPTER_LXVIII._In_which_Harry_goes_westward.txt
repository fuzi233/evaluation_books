Our soft, tender human hearts shrink from lingering on the sharp ache of parting, so I will waste no time dwelling on every raw, tangled feeling Harry Warrington carried with him as he said goodbye to his brother and all the friends who had grown dear to him across his years in England. His plight was no singular one: thousands of men stood exactly where he did that day, bound for the western military campaigns. Had not the brave General Wolfe paused before his own embarkation to kiss his mother goodbye—his gallant father had already passed away while Wolfe was off fighting the glorious Louisbourg campaign—and wrap his betrothed in one last tight, lingering embrace? Had not stout Admiral Holmes, before weighing anchor with his squadron of the Somerset, the Terrible, the Northumberland, the Royal William, the Trident, the Diana, and the Seahorse (his own flag flying from the Dublin), said his goodbyes to Mrs. Holmes and his daughters? Even Admiral Saunders, who set sail the very day after Holmes, was not exempt from the soft tug of human affection that ties men to the homes they leave behind.

Away the ships go, their crews of jovial, weathered sailors ploughing through tumbling grey waves, while poor Black-eyed Susan lingering on the shore watches the last vessel shrink to a speck against the sunset. It dwindles westward, and the darkling night falls soft over the ocean. The men are gone, but their hearts linger back home for a little while yet. In the quiet of that first night at sea, every man’s heart swells with an unutterable softness, thinking of the loved ones left behind. A chorus of tender, pleading prayers rises up to the Father both at sea and on shore: from the wife kneeling in tears beside her now-empty bedside, from the mother huddled with her children around the home fire pouring out their supplications, and from the seaman leaning on the ship’s rail, gazing up at the unblinking stars as the vessel cuts through roaring midnight waters. By the next dawn, the sun rises on ordinary life once more, and everyone returns to their daily toil and duty.

George Warrington travels with his brother, lingering for some days at Portsmouth waiting for a favourable wind to blow the fleet out of harbour. He shakes Wolfe’s pale hand one last time, looks on his gaunt, determined face, and watches the vessels depart amid the clangour of church bells and the thunder of cannon salutes booming from the shore. The very next day he is back at his London home, throwing himself into the absorbing, self-focused work that nearly every man of thirty years has long since apprenticed himself to. He feels a sharp little twinge of sadness when he pauses by Harry’s old lodgings and glances through the window to the small bedroom his brother once occupied, seeing half-burned scraps of Harry’s notes still scattered in the cold grate. But within minutes he is hurrying on to Dean Street, whispering soft nothings by the fitful firelight to his clinging, adoring sweetheart. Theo is so overjoyed to have him home that she is almost ashamed of it. Is it not heartless to be this giddy, when poor Hetty is still sunk in melancholy? “Don’t, sir!” she murmurs, flushing, when he teases her for it. “Well, I ought to be wretched, and it is very, very wicked of me if I’m not.” No one could blame the soft-hearted girl for that little rush of guilty joy, of course. The dim room fills with light the second the servants enter bearing supper and lamps, the rest of the Lambert family file in, and conversation turns general.
Everyone in town now knows the fleet’s destination: its force is more than large enough to rout every French soldier in Canada, and under an officer as fiery and determined as Wolfe, it will surely undo every blunder and disaster of earlier campaigns. Wolfe looks dreadfully ill, it is true, but a great soul burns bright in that frail body, and both the ministers and the whole country hold their breath in hope of victory. After supper, Mr. Lambert leads his modest household in their regular evening prayers, George counted fully as one of the family, and as they all kneel together to pray for the safety of all travellers by land and sea, Theo and Hetty huddle side by side on the rug.

As the weeks slip by, the ships speed farther and farther west, carrying Harry ever further from home. George’s first tragedy *Carpezan* has now run a successful thirty to forty performances, and the theatre has moved on to new productions. Some loyal fans grumble at how fickle the town is, to tire of such a masterpiece so soon, but the sceptical George takes the fate of his play with perfect philosophy. He pockets the hundred pounds the bookseller Dodsley pays him for the printed edition, and the two hundred plus guineas the theatre manager hands over for its run, all glowing reviews ringing in his ears as he sets straight to work on a new play he hopes will outshine his first.
These studies, and the sweet, all-consuming new happiness of his courtship of Theo, make the months slip past unnoticed. It is the happiest time of youth and life: when love is first spoken and returned, when the dearest eyes greet you every day, and the fondest lips never tire of whispering their sweet secrets, when the parting goodnight look carries a delicious promise of tomorrow, when your heart overflows so full of joy you have kindness left to spare for the whole world, when doubt feels like cowardice, misfortune seems impossible, and poverty feels like no more than a sweet little trial of constancy. Theo’s parents, who remember their own prime all too well, sit soft and quiet by, watching the young people play out their pretty little gentle comedy. On one of the few occasions George is temporarily away from home, he dutifully writes to Theo to note that he paused to look up at the windows of their old happy house in Dean Street, wondering who now sits in the little chamber where the two of them spent so many sweet hours.

We can trace the course of these months, and keep track of all their friends, through a long, lively letter George writes to Harry, dated 20th August 1759, from his old window opposite Bedford Gardens:
> “Why are you gone tramping over rugged rocks, bleak northern shores, sweltering summer heat and nipping winter cold out in the colonies, when you could have been in Germany reaping heaps of military laurels with the rest of the King’s regiments? They come back from Minden strung with honours as covered as a Jack-in-the-Green on May-day. Our six regiments fought absolute wonders, and our cavalry would have wiped the French off the field entirely if that fool Lord George Sackville had just obeyed orders. When Prince Ferdinand shouted ‘Charge!’ he claimed he could not hear, or could not translate the German word for ‘Forward’, so we only beat the French instead of annihilating them, as we surely would have done if Lord Granby or even your humble brother had been in command. My lord is back in town now, shouting for a court martial to clear his name. He held his head high enough in prosperity, and in disgrace he carries on with such unshakable arrogance that one almost finds oneself admiring the man. He looks half like he envies poor Admiral Byng, as if the government refusing to shoot him is a sheer lack of respect.
> “The Duke of Cumberland has had notice he ought to make ready to leave this world of grandeur, victory, and bitter disappointment. A mild palsy attack struck his royal highness, and pale Death popped his head in at the door, as it were, and said ‘I shall be back another day’. Tyrant as he was in his prime, this prince has behaved with noble dignity in disgrace, and no king ever had a truer servant than our late king found in his son. I always do find myself rooting for the losing side, don’t I, and bridling against the men who win all the glory? I have gone to hear that famous Mr. P——, your general’s great patron, speak in the House of Commons two or three times now, and I cannot stand his grandiloquent posturing. I wish some little David would come along and topple that strutting giant over. Every thought and every line he speaks is deliberately posed for effect. I much prefer Barry the actor’s plain, natural manner, even if Mr. P.’s delivery is far more thunderous and imposing.
> “My new play *Pocahontas* is coming on famously. Barry is delighted with his part as Captain John Smith, though he insists Smith ought to wear a red coat with blue facings and an epaulet. I am fighting him tooth and nail to dress the part exactly like the portraits of Queen Elizabeth’s gentlemen up at Hampton Court: a crisp ruff, a neat square beard, square-toed leather shoes. ‘And what of Pocahontas? Are you planning to have her tattooed?’ Uncle Lambert teases me. Hagan has the part of the native warrior who loves her, and who sees her partiality for Captain Smith, then nobly risks his own life to rescue Smith from execution. I am sure his performance will be a great hit. Hagan is a strange old fish: his mouth is forever full of play lines and dramatic rant, but he is good, honest, and brave, if I do not miss my guess. He is furious he has been cast as Sir O’Brallaghan in Mr. Macklin’s new farce *Love A-la-mode*, saying he ‘will not de-grace his tongue with imitations of that rascal brogue’. As if he needs to fake a brogue, when his own natural Irish twang is thick enough to cut with a knife!
> “Shall I tell you a secret? Shall I keep it from you? Shall I hurt your feelings, rouse you to a fit of jealous rage, and make you beg your commanding officer for immediate leave to sail home to Europe? Know then, that though our *Carpezan* closed long ago, cousin Maria Esmond haunts the playhouse every night. Tom Spencer has spotted her again and again in the gallery, always turning up on the nights Hagan is performing. Quick! Burroughs, fetch Mr. Warrington’s boots and portmanteau! Order a chaise and four to Portsmouth at once! That letter I burned that morning when we were both at breakfast, that I told you never to ask about? I may as well spill the cat out of the bag now, when you are thousands of miles away: it was a note from cousin M., begging me not to breathe a word about her little theatre visits to anyone. But I cannot resist whispering just to you that Maria is at this very moment consoling herself for her old heartbreak as fast as she possibly can. Should I spoil her sport? Should I tell her brother? Is this affair any business of ours? What have the Esmonds ever done for you and me but win all our money at the card tables? Yet I still like our noble cousin. It seems to me he would be a good man if he could—at least, he would have been, once, before he was set on a path of vice it is probably far too late to drag him back from now. Oh happy farmers! Our old Virginia plantation was dull, but thank heaven we were raised there. We were kept a little sheltered, but we were never made slaves to gambling, or to the wicked, cruel company of the sort of men who waylaid my poor Harry on the high roads of England and stripped him of all he had. I do not think you are a bit the worse for that robbery, you silly thing. Now you are off to make a great name for yourself, kill more French dragons, and become a famous commander. Our mother will soon be bragging about her son the Captain, then the Colonel, then the General, and have his portrait painted all decked out with stars and epaulets, while poor dawdling old me will be no more than a scribbling poet, or if luck turns my way, a snug government clerk with a little cottage at Richmond or Kew, and half a dozen little picaninnies bobbing curtsies at the garden gate every time their uncle the General rides up on his great war charger, his aide-de-camp’s pockets stuffed full of gingerbread for all his nephews and nieces. You are the one meant to brandish Mars’s great sword. As for me, I look forward to a quiet life: a little home of my own, a little library stuffed full of books, and a little someone dulce ridentem, dulce loquentem sitting on the other side of the fire, smiling and chattering, as I scribble away at my plays. I am so perfectly happy with this prospect, that I catch myself afraid it might slip away from me, and even to my dearest Hal I am almost shy of speaking of how bright my future looks. What do I care for grand ambition, when this happiness is almost within my grasp? What do I care for wars, with this soft, beatific peace waiting right around the corner?
> “Our mother’s old friend Mynheer Van den Bosch has been off on a tour back to Holland to track down his long-lost family, and much to everyone’s surprise he found them. That daughter of his, the one our mother once hoped would make you a perfect wife, has spent the last six months at Kensington School, and is coming out into society with a hundred pretty accomplishments that are supposed to turn her into the perfect fine lady. Her papa brought her to Dean Street to make a curtsy the other day, and it was the most elegant curtsy I ever saw. Though she is barely seventeen, no sixty year old Mayfair dowager could carry herself with more ease. She conversed with Aunt Lambert as if they were equals, treated the girls like silly little chits—to Hetty’s great fury and Theo’s huge amusement—chatted politics with the General, and gossiped about the last routs, new gowns, operas, fashions, and scandals with such perfect poise that, barring one or two small blunders, you would swear Miss Lydia was born and bred in the heart of Mayfair. She says she means to live in the court end of town, and has no patience at all for her father’s choice of lodging in Monument Yard. For anyone who favours a pretty dark beauty, there is no sweeter little mignonne creature alive. But my taste, dearest brother, you know full well…”

The rest of the letter dissolves into a page of rapturous, flowery praise for Theo, full of the sort of poetic quotations and gushings no editor could possibly reprint in polite company. Gentlemen and ladies of a certain age will no doubt remember when they indulged in exactly the same kind of rhapsodic foolishness in their own youth, when they could not stop singing the praises of their beloved, when all the flowers of life were in full bloom and every spring bird was singing loud in their chest. The twigs may be bare now, the leaves long fallen, but we have no reason not to look back soft and fond on that bright vernal time. And for you young people, whose May (or perhaps April?) of life has not even begun yet, you do not need to linger over other people’s love rhapsodies. Rest assured, when your own spring comes, kind Nature will warm all your flowers into full bloom, and rouse your glad young hearts to pour out their own full, happy song.
