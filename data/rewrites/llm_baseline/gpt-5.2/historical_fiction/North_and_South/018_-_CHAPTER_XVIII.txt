When Margaret came back into the little parlour, the table wore its accustomed look of scanty order—work-basket shut, the tea-things pushed aside from the morning—yet two letters lay there with a foreign air, as if they had strayed in from another climate. One was for her mother, plain and English; the other, by the thinness of its paper and its silvery rustle, announced Aunt Shaw before ever the foreign post-marks had done their part. The envelope crackled like dried bay-leaves.

She had taken up her mother’s note, weighing it a moment between finger and thumb, when her father entered quickly, with that light haste he used when he would fain persuade himself that nothing was amiss.

“So your mother is tired, and gone to bed early! I’m afraid such a thundery day was not the best in the world for the doctor to see her. What did he say? Dixon tells me he spoke to you about her.”

Margaret’s eyes dropped. The room seemed full of the day’s heaviness still: the close air after storm, the faint, gritty smell that Milton’s smoke left on curtains and paper, and, beneath all, the restless undertone of the town—wheels in the street, distant hammers, a far-off, never-ending pulse as of machinery breathing. Even here, in a house that tried to keep its peace like a covered dish keeps warmth, sound crept in and altered the temper of one’s thoughts.

Her hesitation drew him on.

“He does not think her seriously ill?”

“Not at present; she needs care, he says. He was very kind, and he said he would call again, and see how his medicines worked.”

“Only care?—he did not recommend change of air?—he did not say this smoky town was doing her any harm, did he, Margaret?”

“No! not a word,” she replied, gravely. “He was anxious, I think.”

“Doctors have that anxious manner; it’s professional,” said he; but his voice betrayed him. The first touch of fear, once admitted, made everything else in him attentive. He could not set it down again, as one lays aside a book. He began to move about on tiptoe, as if quietness itself could keep illness from being named.

Margaret turned, to give him other matter, as one sets a cup of broth before a sick person who refuses the thought of stronger food.

“This letter is from Aunt Shaw, papa. She has got to Naples, and finds it too hot, so she has taken apartments at Sorrento. But I don’t think she likes Italy.”

“He did not say anything about diet, did he?” he broke in, with that eager change of subject which always returned to the same point.

“It was to be nourishing, and digestible. Mamma’s appetite is pretty good, I think.”

“Yes! and that makes it all the more strange he should have thought of speaking about diet.”

“I asked him, papa.”

He stood still, as if that confession had pulled a thread he would rather not have felt in his hands.

Margaret went on, forcing a small smile into her tone because it was easier for him to take comfort from her voice than from any argument.

“Aunt Shaw says she has sent me some coral ornaments, papa: but she’s afraid the Milton dissenters won’t appreciate them. She has got all her ideas of Dissenters from the Quakers, has she not?”

He scarcely heard. The house held him, divided between the desire to go and look at his wife and the shame of seeming to watch her sleep.

“If ever you hear or notice that your mother wishes for anything, be sure you let me know. I am so afraid she does not tell me always what she would like. Pray, see after that girl Mrs. Thornton named. If we had a good, efficient house-servant, Dixon could be constantly with her, and I’d answer for it we’d soon set her up amongst us, if care will do it. She’s been very much tired of late, with the hot weather, and the difficulty of getting a servant. A little rest will put her quite to rights—eh, Margaret?”

“I hope so,” said Margaret; but the sadness that clung to her words was like a taste that will come through spice.

He noticed it. He pinched her cheek with a poor attempt at playfulness, as if colour could be summoned like blush on a painted face.

“Come; if you look so pale as this, I must rouge you up a little. Take care of yourself, child, or you’ll be wanting the doctor next.”

Yet he could not settle. Through the evening he went backwards and forwards, returning again and again to the landing as if drawn by a cord, pausing to listen for the breath above—listening not only for his wife’s, but for the whole sound of the house. Margaret felt, with a kind of bodily ache, his effort to stifle the terror that was rising in him like smoke in a closed room.

At last he came back with a look of relief, as if he had found a door unbarred.

“She’s awake now, Margaret. She quite smiled as she saw me standing by her. Just her old smile. And she says she feels refreshed, and ready for tea. Where’s the note for her? She wants to see it. I’ll read it to her while you make tea.”

Margaret went to the table and began the familiar offices. The kettle was filled; the fire coaxed; the tea caddy opened. In that homely action there was comfort—a small ritual against the vast, unmanageable thing upstairs. The tea-leaves, dry and dark, gave out their faint, sharp scent when she measured them; the cups, warmed, seemed to promise that some part of life could still be kept in order. Even the clink of spoon against china had its own softness, a domestic music that for a moment muffled Milton’s harsher chorus.

The note for Mrs. Hale proved, when it was opened, to be a formal invitation from Mrs. Thornton, to Mr., Mrs., and Miss Hale to dine on the twenty-first instant. Margaret, holding the tray ready, was surprised to hear her father reading it aloud in that clear, careful voice of his, as if the very act of reading were an assertion that their days went on as usual.

She went upstairs with the tea, and found her mother propped among pillows, the shadow on her face softened by the little flush of having something to anticipate. The invitation had already kindled a kind of wilful brightness in her, like a candle lit in a draught.

“It will do me good,” Mrs. Hale said, almost pleading, before Margaret had time to object. “It is so long since I have been anywhere. It will be a change.”

Margaret would have urged her to husband her strength, to spare herself; but her father’s eyes entreated her help in keeping up this new hope, as if the dinner were a medicine.

“Nay, Margaret: if she wishes it, I’m sure we’ll both go willingly. She never would wish it unless she felt herself really stronger—really better than we thought she was, eh, Margaret?” he said, anxious, while Mrs. Hale’s hand lay like a light weight on the paper.

“Eh! Margaret?” he repeated, with a nervous motion of his fingers.

It seemed cruel to deny him the comfort. Besides, his passionate refusal to admit fear almost infected Margaret with a momentary belief.

“I do think she is better since last night,” she said, letting her eyes rest on her mother’s face as if she could will the truth into it. “Her eyes look brighter, and her complexion clearer.”

“God bless you,” said her father, with earnestness that made the words almost a prayer of hunger. “But is it true? Yesterday was so sultry every one felt ill. It was a most unlucky day for Mr. Donaldson to see her on.”

So, the next day, the note of acceptance was prepared, and Mr. Hale went out to his duties, now increased by the labour of lectures he had promised to deliver to the working people at a neighbouring Lyceum. He had chosen Ecclesiastical Architecture—more to his own taste and stored knowledge than to any direct hunger for it among those who came for instruction. Yet the institution, being in debt, caught at the credit of his name and the cheapness of his gift, as one catches at any wholesome food in lean times and does not ask too curiously if it be the dish one would have chosen.

That night, in a very different house, where order was kept like a strict account-book and economies were remembered as if they had a conscience, Mrs. Thornton sat with her son and daughter, and the subject of the dinner was gone over as punctiliously as a bill of fare.

“Well, mother,” asked Mr. Thornton, “who have accepted your invitations for the twenty-first?”

“Fanny, where are the notes? The Slicksons accept, Collingbrooks accept, Stephenses accept, Browns decline. Hales—father and daughter come—mother too great an invalid—Macphersons come, and Mr. Horsfall, and Mr. Young. I was thinking of asking the Porters, as the Browns can’t come.”

“Very good.” His tone was composed, yet something in him tightened. “Do you know, I’m really afraid Mrs. Hale is very far from well, from what Dr. Donaldson says.”

“It’s strange of them to accept a dinner-invitation if she’s very ill,” said Fanny, quick, with that small, nervous laugh she used like a fan to stir her own feelings away.

“I didn’t say very ill,” said her brother, rather sharply. “I only said far from well. They may not know it either.”

And then, as if a thought struck him like the sound of a dropped coin, he remembered that, by Donaldson’s account, Margaret must at least know something nearer to the truth than her father chose to hear.

“Very probably they are quite aware of what you said yesterday, John—of the great advantage it would be to them—to Mr. Hale, I mean, to be introduced to such people as the Stephenses and the Collingbrooks.”

“I’m sure that motive would not influence them. No! I think I understand how it is.”

“John!” said Fanny, laughing again, but this time with the faint malice of one who enjoys setting a pin where it will prick. “How you profess to understand these Hales, and how you never will allow that we can know anything about them. Are they really so very different to most people one meets with?”

She had not meant, perhaps, to vex him; but the words did their work. He walked to and fro, the boards answering his steps with a steady, domestic sound—quiet enough, yet not soothing, for his mind kept its own harsher rhythm.

“They do not seem to me out of the common way,” said Mrs. Thornton. “He appears a worthy kind of man enough; rather too simple for trade—so it’s perhaps as well he should have been a clergyman first, and now a teacher. She’s a bit of a fine lady, with her invalidism; and as for the girl—she’s the only one who puzzles me when I think about her,—which I don’t often do. She seems to have a great notion of giving herself airs; and I can’t make out why. I could almost fancy she thinks herself too good for her company at times. And yet they’re not rich; from all I can hear they never have been.”

“And she’s not accomplished, mamma,” Fanny put in, with childish spitefulness, yet as if she spoke of a fault in a pudding that refused to rise. “She can’t play.”

“Go on, Fanny. What else does she want to bring her up to your standard?” said Mrs. Thornton, dryly.

“Nay! John,” continued Mrs. Thornton, turning to her son, “that speech of Fanny’s did no harm. I myself heard Miss Hale say she could not play. If you would let us alone, we could perhaps like her, and see her merits.”

“I’m sure I never could!” murmured Fanny, sheltering under her mother’s toleration.

Mr. Thornton heard, but did not choose to answer. He kept walking, wishing for candles, wishing for the interruption of work—anything to stop this talk that beat upon a tender place in him like a dull hammer.

But Mrs. Thornton observed her household ways as if they were laws, and he did not meddle in them. The old economies lived in her as naturally as breath.

“Mother,” he said at last, stopping short, and speaking with a candour that startled even himself, “I wish you would like Miss Hale.”

“Why,” asked she, sharp and startled, “you’re never thinking of marrying her?—a girl without a penny.”

“She would never have me,” said he, with a short laugh that cut like a knife through pride.

“No, I don’t think she would,” answered his mother, with blunt certainty. “She laughed in my face, when I praised her for speaking out something Mr. Bell had said in your favour. I liked the girl for doing so frankly, for it made me sure she had no thought of you; and the next minute she vexed me so by seeming to think—well, never mind! Only you’re right in saying she’s too good an opinion of herself to think of you! The saucy jade! I should like to know where she’d find a better!”

“Well, as I’m just as much convinced of the truth of what you have been saying as you can be; and as I have no thought or expectation of ever asking her to be my wife, you’ll believe me for the future that I’m quite disinterested in speaking about her.” He paused, as if weighing the words, then went on more steadily. “I foresee trouble for that girl—perhaps, want of motherly care—and I only wish you to be ready to be a friend to her, in case she needs one. Now, Fanny,” he added, turning with sudden severity, “I trust you have delicacy enough to understand, that it is as great an injury to Miss Hale as to me—in fact, she would think it a greater—to suppose that I have any reason, more than I now give, for begging you and my mother to show her every kindly attention.”

“I cannot forgive her her pride,” said Mrs. Thornton. “I will befriend her, if there is need, for your asking, John. I would befriend Jezebel herself if you asked me. But this girl, who turns up her nose at us all—who turns up her nose at you—”

“Nay, mother; I have never yet put myself, and I mean never to put myself, within reach of her contempt.”

“Contempt, indeed!”—and one of Mrs. Thornton’s expressive snorts did duty for a whole sentence. “Don’t go on speaking of Miss Hale, John, if I’ve to be kind to her. When I’m with her, I don’t know if I like or dislike her most; but when I think of her, and hear you talk of her, I hate her. I can see she’s given herself airs to you as well as if you’d told me out.”

“And if she has,” said he; then he stopped, and when he spoke again his voice had the hardness of a man striking flint. “I’m not a lad, to be cowed by a proud look from a woman, or to care for her misunderstanding me and my position. I can laugh at it!”

“To be sure! and at her too, with her fine notions, and haughty tosses!”

“I only wonder why you talk so much about her, then,” said Fanny. “I’m sure, I’m tired enough of the subject.”

“Well!” said her brother, with a shade of bitterness, as if he tasted something sour and would wash it down with a harsher draught. “Suppose we find some more agreeable subject. What do you say to a strike, by way of something pleasant to talk about?”

“Have the hands actually turned out?” asked Mrs. Thornton, kindling at once; this was a subject she could handle, as she would a joint on the table—firmly, with no shrinking.

“Hamper’s men are actually out. Mine are working out their week, through fear of being prosecuted for breach of contract. I’d have had every one of them up and punished for it, that left his work before his time was out.”

“The law expenses would have been more than the hands themselves were worth—a set of ungrateful naughts!” said his mother.

“To be sure. But I’d have shown them how to keep my word, and how I mean them to keep their’s. They know me by this time. Slickson’s men are off—pretty certain he won’t spend money in getting them punished. We’re in for a turn-out, mother.”

“I hope there are not many orders in hand?”

“Of course there are. They know that well enough. But they don’t quite understand all, though they think they do.”

“What do you mean, John?”

Candles had been brought in at last. Their flames steadied the room’s shadows, and Fanny, relieved to have something to do with her hands, took up her interminable worsted-work. The soft rasp of wool drawn through canvas made a small sound of its own, like quiet rain; yet it did not drown the bigger sound that seemed always near in Milton—industry, anger, wealth, want: a town’s continual grinding.

“Why,” said he, “the Americans are getting their yarns so into the general market, that our only chance is producing them at a lower rate. If we can’t, we may shut up shop at once, and hands and masters alike go on tramp. Yet these fools go back to the prices paid three years ago—nay, some of their leaders quote Dickinson’s price now—though they know as well as we do that, what with fines pressed out of their wages, as no honourable man would extort them, the real rate of wage paid at Dickinson’s is less than at ours.”

His voice rose with the injustice of being thwarted by men he called ignorant; and the very words he used—fools, hands—had in them the old power of a dominant lexicon, sorting human lives into categories fit for ledgers. It made the room feel harder.

“Upon my word, mother, I wish the old combination laws were in force. It is too bad to find out that fools—ignorant, wayward men like these—just by uniting their silly heads, are to rule over the fortunes of those who bring all the wisdom that knowledge and experience, and often painful thought and anxiety, can give. The next thing will be—indeed, we’ve all but come to it now—that we shall have to go and ask—stand hat in hand—and humbly ask the secretary of the Spinner’s Union to be so kind as to furnish us with labour at their own price. That’s what they want—they, who haven’t the sense to see that, if we don’t get a fair share of the profits to compensate us for our wear and tear here in England, we can move off to some other country; and that, what with home and foreign competition, we are none of us likely to make above a fair share, and may be thankful enough if we can get that, in an average number of years.”

“Can’t you get hands from Ireland?” asked Mrs. Thornton, prompt and practical. “I wouldn’t keep these fellows a day. I’d teach them that I was master, and could employ what servants I liked.”

“Yes! to be sure, I can; and will, too, if they go on long. It will be trouble and expense, and I fear there will be some danger; but I will do it, rather than give in.”

“If there is to be all this extra expense, I’m sorry we’re giving a dinner just now,” said his mother; her frugality saw the strike first as waste.

“So am I,—not because of the expense, but because I shall have much to think about, and many unexpected calls on my time. But we must have had Mr. Horsfall, and he does not stay in Milton long. And as for the others, we owe them dinners, and it’s all one trouble.”

He went back to walking, drawing a deep breath now and then as if to throw off some thought that clung like smoke in his lungs. Fanny asked small questions—about ribbons, about morning calls, about nothing at all—each one a little tap at her mother’s attention; and received short answers, for Mrs. Thornton’s mind was set on the coming contest, the way a cook’s mind sets on bread that must be watched through the proving.

At ten, the servants came in, filing quietly for prayers. Mrs. Thornton always read them; first a chapter. They were working steadily through the Old Testament, as steadily as her son worked through figures, and with the same sense that endurance was a virtue in itself. When the prayers were ended, she wished her son good-night with that long, steady look which held no outward softness, but had the force of a benediction.

Mr. Thornton, left alone, did not go to bed. He continued his walk. The check upon his business plans was like a sudden pull on a tightly drawn thread. The forethought of many anxious hours—his careful reckonings, his arrangements—seemed flung aside by what he called their insane folly, which would hurt them even more than him, though he spared no pity for that part of it. The town outside, quieter now, still spoke in undertones: a late cart, a watchman’s step, far away the continuous murmur of great buildings where men and engines had laboured all day.

Hamper had said, only that day, that if he were ruined by the strike, he would begin again, comforted by the conviction that those who had brought it on would be worse off; for he had head as well as hands, while they had only hands, and if they drove away the market they could not follow it, nor turn to anything else.

But such thoughts gave Mr. Thornton no comfort. Whether revenge pleased him little, or whether he valued the hard-earned place he had won too keenly to think of any consequence beyond its peril, he paced up and down with his teeth set, feeling the ground of his authority threatened by men whose speech—Union, prices, rights—had begun to carry weight like a newly-coined currency.

At last the clock struck two. The candles guttered, their grease running, their flames leaning as if tired. He took up one to light his own, and muttered, as if fixing an oath into a plan made it firmer:

“Once for all they shall know whom they have got to deal with. I can give them a fortnight,—no more. If they don’t see their madness before the end of that time, I must have hands from Ireland. I believe it’s Slickson’s doing,—confound him and his dodges! He thought he was overstocked; so he seemed to yield at first, when the deputation came to him,—and of course, he only confirmed them in their folly, as he meant to do. That’s where it spread from.”
