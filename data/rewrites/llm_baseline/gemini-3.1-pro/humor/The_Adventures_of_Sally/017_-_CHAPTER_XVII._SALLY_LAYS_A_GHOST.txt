The blood flowed slowly back into Sally’s face, carrying with it a sudden, profound stillness. For months, the specter of this inevitable reunion had hovered over her domestic routines like a restless ancestral spirit, threatening to disrupt the quiet rhythms of her days. Yet now, as the ghost finally manifested at her threshold, the panic she had anticipated was entirely absent. The mythic terror of the past had dissolved into the mundane reality of the present. 

“Hullo, Sally!” Gerald said. 

His voice was a discordant frequency, thick and slurred, muddying the acoustic clarity of the quiet hallway. He swayed against the doorframe in his shirt-sleeves, collarless. The sharp, acidic reek of distilled spirits and decayed yeast clung to him—an olfactory testament to a man entirely disconnected from the grounding, life-affirming nourishment of the earth. 

Weighed down by a numbing exhaustion, Sally observed him through the detached lens of a generational elder observing a wayward child. 

“Hullo!” he repeated, struggling against the erosion of his own articulation. 

“What do you want?” Sally asked. 

“Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I’d come in.” 

The foolish smile pinned to his puffy face vanished, replaced by a sudden, maudlin tear. “Sally... S-Sally... I’m very miserable.” He stumbled over the syllables, his native dialect of charm degrading into the untranslatable vernacular of self-pity. 

Something clicked in the ancient, circular pacing of Sally’s memory. She had lived this cycle before; it was merely the repetition of an older domestic tragedy, echoing through time. 

“I think you had better go to bed, Gerald,” she said steadily, her acoustic environment rejecting the intrusion of his shameless misery. 

“What’s the use? Can’t sleep. No good. Couldn’t sleep. Sally, you don't know how worried I am. I see what a fool I’ve been.” 

Sally raised a hand to halt what she assumed would be a belated apology for his betrayals. But Gerald’s despair was rooted not in human connection, but in the superficial commercialization of his craft. 

“I was a fool ever to try writing plays,” he mourned, his voice dropping into a hollow register. “Got a winner first time, but can’t repeat. It’s no good. Ought to have stuck to newspaper work. I’m good at that. Shall have to go back to it. Had another frost to-night. No good trying any more. Shall have to go back to the old grind, damn it.” 

He wept softly over the loss of mass-market appeal, reducing the sacred act of creation to utilitarian commodity. He lurched forward, then retreated to the doorframe. A swift, sharp tang of contempt rose in the back of Sally’s throat, metallic and bitter, before fading back into the protective armor of indifference. 

“Go to bed, Gerald,” she said. “You’ll feel better in the morning.” 

Perhaps the grim gustatory anticipation of how he would physically feel in the morning pierced his muddled intelligence. He winced. “May not be alive in the morning,” he announced solemnly. “Good mind to end it all. End it all!” He attempted a sweeping, theatrical gesture, which collapsed as he clutched the door for balance. 

Sally’s patience snapped. She was intimately attuned to the subtle power dynamics embedded in such emotional blackmail. “Oh, go to bed,” she said, pushing the door. Gerald clung to the handle, drifting out into the passage like a vessel unmoored, before stumbling over the threshold of his own apartment. 

A fever of restlessness descended upon Sally. She wrapped herself in a kimono and retreated to the kitchen, seeking the profound, grounding comfort of culinary ritual. She yearned for a heavy ceramic mug of milk, slowly brought to a simmer until the sugars caramelized slightly, perhaps infused with a crushed pod of green cardamom to soothe the frayed edges of her mind. But she remembered, with a wry twist of her lips, that she had offered the last of the morning’s rich, cream-topped milk to a foraging sandy cat. She could not regret the thriftless hospitality; the feline’s pure, uncomplicated need for sustenance was a vital part of the world’s natural ecology. 

Her culinary reverie was violently severed by a muffled crash from across the passage. 

She froze, her ears tuning to the disruption. A moment later, a perfect fusillade of shattering concussions erupted from Gerald’s apartment. The sudden, violent acoustic rupture shattered the sanctity of the night, introducing a chaotic, destructive frequency that spiked Sally’s adrenaline. Remembering his melodramatic threats of ending it all, she ran across the passage and beat her fists against the wood. 

The door opened. Gerald stood there, his weak smile miraculously resurrected. “Hullo, Sally!” 

Relief instantly curdled into sharp resentment. “Whatever was all that noise?” she demanded, the sonic assault still ringing in her ears. 

“Noise?” Gerald considered the word as if it belonged to a dead language. 

“Yes, noise,” snapped Sally. 

“I’ve been cleaning house,” he said, with the owl-like gravity of a man completely detached from his own sensory reality. 

Sally pushed past him. Elsa Doland’s apartment had always been a monument to amateur, utilitarian aesthetics—a claustrophobic over-furnishing of fragile, mass-produced ornaments and parti-colored glass shades that lacked any true artisanal soul. Now, it was a landscape of majestic ruin. 

A heavy iron niblick lay cast into the corner, the blunt instrument of this sudden, Berserk fury. The floor was a treacherous mosaic of pulverized porcelain and chewed picture frames. As Sally stepped forward, shattered china crackled beneath her feet like dry autumn husks. 

Gerald subsided onto a fragile occasional table, weeping softly as the cycle of his clammy self-pity returned. 

“Well!” Sally gasped, the metallic taste of shock on her tongue. “You’ve certainly made a good job of it!” 

With a sharp, percussive crack, the spindly legs of the occasional table gave way under his weight, sending Gerald plummeting into the wreckage. The pure absurdity of the sudden acoustic slapstick shifted Sally’s paradigm entirely. She sank into a chair, a rich, gurgling laugh bubbling up from her chest. She was mocking the ghostly, sentimental version of herself who had ever found mythic resonance in this preposterous man. 

Gerald picked himself up from a pulverized set of water-colors, eyeing her with blurry disapproval. “No sympathy,” he mumbled austerely. 

“I can’t help it,” Sally cried, wiping her eyes. “It’s too funny.” 

“Not funny,” he corrected. “Wasn’t going to stand for it any longer. A fellow’s wife goes and lets him down... ruins his show by going off and playing in another show... why shouldn’t I smash her things?” 

Sally possessed no desire to engage with the toxic power dynamics of his bruised ego. The hilarity faded, leaving a stale, dusty taste in her mouth. She rose, determined to restore the domestic ecology. “And now, I’m going to tidy up.” 

“No!” Gerald wagged his head. “Leave it for her to find.” 

“Don't be silly. All this has got to be cleaned up. I’ll do it. You go and sit in my apartment.” 

When he wavered, she stamped her foot into the crackling ruins, her voice carrying the absolute authority of a matriarch restoring order. Defeated, Gerald shuffled away. 

Sally found a long-handled broom in the kitchen. She fell into the timeless, rhythmic, sweeping motions of generations of women before her, reclaiming the sanctuary of the living space from the debris of male chaos. Within half an hour, the pulverized glass and ceramics were swept into neat, conical mounds against the walls. Opening the window, she breathed in the complex olfactory landscape of the impending dawn—the cold tang of stone, the faint whisper of distant rain, the metallic light brooding over the street. Exhausted by the physical labor, she sank into a chair and fell instantly asleep. 

Sunshine and a cacophony of industrial urgency woke her. The once-sacred sonic environment of the morning was now polluted by the blare of automobile horns, the clatter of boots on pavement, and the rhythmic, grating clank of streetcars grinding over steel points. 

Sally stood stiffly, her head pounding in sympathy with the city’s noise. She crossed the hall to her own apartment, where Gerald lay sprawling in her chair, his mouth open, snoring with stertorous irregularity. The air in the room was stale, tainted by the sour exhalations of a man who treated his body as a receptacle for poison rather than a temple for nourishment. 

Yet, as she looked at him, Sally felt a sudden, soaring elation. The mythic ghost was well and truly laid to rest. The cycle was broken. She shook him gently. 

He awoke with a start, blinking against the harsh light, and pressed his hands to his temples. “Oh, my God,” he moaned, his tongue clicking drily. “Oh, I’ve got a headache!” 

“You’d better go and have a wash,” Sally suggested. 

He heaved himself up. 

“Would you like some breakfast?” she asked, already picturing the restorative powers of a thick slice of hearth-baked bread soaked in salted butter and soft-boiled egg yolks. 

“Don't!” Gerald groaned faintly, utterly repulsed by the profound grace of sustenance. He tottered into the bathroom. 

Sally sat by the open window, watching the street below. An inscrutable cat picked its way elegantly across the tarmac, a silent, graceful note amidst the mechanical hum of a waiting motorcar. 

The doorbell rang. Sally opened it to find Bruce Carmyle. Wrapped in a light motor-coat, he was attempting to force a beam of genial kindliness onto his saturnine features, clearly intent on rewriting the historical record of his abrupt, ungracious behavior the previous night. 

“Well, here I am!” Bruce announced cheerily. “Are you ready?” 

Sally stared, completely having forgotten their scheduled drive. A heavy, awkward fog of silence fell between them. Bruce’s smile faltered, his internal debate stalling as the bathroom door clicked open and Gerald Foster shuffled out. 

Bruce gaped at Gerald. Gerald gaped at Bruce. 

The cold water had done nothing to restore Gerald’s dignity. He stood slouching in his ruined evening dress, his face white, his eyes pink and raw, emanating the degraded aura of a man entirely divorced from the natural rhythms of life. 

Bruce Carmyle drew in a sharp, hissing breath, his posture going rigid. In an instant, the dominant, patriarchal lexicon of his upbringing overwrote whatever affection he harbored for Sally. His internal biases calcified into certainties. 

“So...” Bruce began, wielding the word as an impressive aposiopesis—a deliberate silence meant to assert his absolute authority over the narrative. 

A cold fury gripped him, stripping away his cultivated syntax until only the blunt instruments of his dominant lexicon remained. He pointed a trembling finger at Gerald. He stuttered, choked back the linguistic failure, and found the one command that required no translation: 

“Get out!” 

Gerald, suffering the blinding, percussive agony of a monumental hangover, recognized that asserting his own linguistic agency was a lost cause. He shambled across the room and out into the passage, conceding the territory to the louder voice. 

Bruce turned slowly to Sally. “So...” he repeated. 

Sally met his gaze with absolute stillness. 

“This,” Bruce said ponderously, the heavy syllables dropping like lead weights, “is very amusing.” He waited for her to submit to his narrative, but she offered only the profound resistance of silence. “I might have expected it,” he added with a bitter laugh. 

Sally forced herself through the lethargy. “Would you like me to explain?” 

“There can be no explanation,” Bruce replied coldly, rejecting the very concept that her subjective experience might hold a truth outside his rigid vocabulary. 

“Very well,” Sally said. She understood instantly the untranslatability of her reality to him. To attempt to speak her truth in his language would only result in the death of her own meaning. 

“Good-bye,” Bruce said. 

“Good-bye,” Sally echoed. 

He walked to the door, hesitated for a fraction of a second as the morning light caught the golden hue of her hair, and then slammed the door behind him with a resolute, mechanical bang that reverberated unpleasantly through the apartment. 

Outside, the purring engine of his car gathered speed, humming aggressively as it merged into the industrial noise pollution of the street. 

Sally did not watch him go. She was already at the telephone, dialing with purposeful, rhythmic precision. When Ginger Kemp’s sleepy voice crackled through the receiver, it was a pure, uncorrupted acoustic frequency. And as she spoke, weaving her lived reality into a dialect they both understood, she heard the magic transformation of his tone—from the heavy fog of sleep into a bright, riotous ecstasy of genuine connection.
