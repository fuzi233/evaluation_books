"But work grew scarce, while bread grew dear,
And wages lessened, too;
For Irish hordes were bidders here,
Our half-paid work to do."

Margaret was ushered into the drawing-room. The atmosphere within had settled back into its usual state of dust and heavy drapery, yet the heat pressed against the windows, forcing them half open. Venetian blinds obscured the glass, filtering the daylight into a gray, grim haze that reflected the pavement below. This distorted light, mingling with the greenish tint from above, cast shadows askew and rendered Margaret's own reflection in the mirrors ghastly and wan. She sat, waiting in silence. No one approached. Occasionally, the wind seemed to carry the distant, multitudinous roar of the street closer, only to die away into a profound, suffocating stillness.

Finally, Fanny entered.

"Mamma will be with you directly, Miss Hale," Fanny said, her voice trembling slightly. "She asked me to apologize for the delay. You must understand, my brother has imported hands from Ireland, and it has enraged the Milton people beyond measure—as if he lacked the right to secure labor wherever he could. These foolish locals refuse to work for him, and in their fury, they have terrified these poor Irish starvelings with threats so severe that we dare not let them out. You can see them huddled in the top room of the mill; they are to sleep there to protect them from the brutes who will neither work nor allow them to. Mamma is arranging their food, and John is speaking to them, for some of the women are weeping to return. Ah! Here comes Mamma!"

Mrs. Thornton entered, her face etched with a black, stern severity that made Margaret feel she had arrived at a perilous moment. Yet, she had come only in compliance with Mrs. Thornton's expressed wish to inquire about necessities for her mother's illness. Mrs. Thornton's brow furrowed, her mouth setting hard, as Margaret spoke gently of her mother's restlessness and Dr. Donaldson's recommendation for a water-bed. She paused, expecting a response. None came immediately. Then, Mrs. Thornton sprang up, her composure shattering.

"They are at the gates! Call John, Fanny—call him in from the mill! They are at the gates! They will batter them down! Call John, I say!"

Simultaneously, the gathering tramp she had been listening to, rather than hearing Margaret's words, erupted just outside the wall. An increasing din of angry voices raged behind the wooden barrier, shaking it as if the unseen, maddened crowd wielded their bodies as battering rams. They retreated a short distance only to surge forward again with more united, steady impetus, until their great beats made the strong gates quiver like reeds before a gale.

Women gathered round the windows, fascinated yet terrified by the scene. Mrs. Thornton, the servants, and Margaret were all there. Fanny had returned, screaming upstairs as if pursued at every step, and collapsed onto the sofa in hysterical sobbing. Mrs. Thornton watched for her son, who remained in the mill. He emerged, looked up at the pale cluster of faces, and offered a smile of good courage before locking the factory door. He then called to a servant to unlock his own door, which Fanny had fastened in her panic. Mrs. Thornton went to assist. The sound of his well-known, commanding voice seemed like the taste of blood to the infuriated multitude outside. Until now, they had been voiceless, needing all their breath for the hard labor of breaking down the gates. But hearing him speak inside, they set up a fierce, unearthly groan that made even Mrs. Thornton turn white with fear as she stepped back to let him pass.

Mr. Thornton came forward, flushed but his eyes gleaming with the fire of danger. He possessed a proud look of defiance that made him a noble, if not strictly handsome, man. Margaret, who had always dreaded proving herself a coward in an emergency, forgot her own fear entirely, feeling only an intense, painful sympathy for the moment.

"I am sorry, Miss Hale," Mr. Thornton said frankly, "that you have visited us at this unfortunate moment, when you may be involved in whatever risk we bear. Mother! Had you not better go into the back rooms? I am not certain they have not made their way from Pinner's Lane into the stable-yard, but you will be safer there. Go, Jane!" he commanded the upper servant.

"I stay here!" his mother declared firmly. "Where you are, there I stay." Indeed, retreat was futile; the crowd had surrounded the outbuildings at the rear, sending forth an awful, threatening roar. The servants fled to the garrets with cries and shrieks. Mr. Thornton smiled scornfully at their panic. He glanced at Margaret, who stood alone by the window nearest the factory. Her eyes glittered, her cheeks and lips deepened in color. Sensing his gaze, she turned to him, asking the question that had been forming in her mind:

"Where are the poor imported workpeople? In the factory?"

"Yes," he replied. "I left them cowed in a small room at the head of a back flight of stairs, bidding them flee if they heard an attack on the mill doors. But it is not them—it is me they want."

"When can the soldiers arrive?" his mother asked, her voice low but unsteady.

He consulted his watch with the same steady composure he applied to everything.

"Supposing Williams departed immediately and did not have to dodge through the crowd, it must be twenty minutes yet."

"Twenty minutes!" his mother exclaimed, her terror finally breaking through.

"Shut down the windows instantly, Mother! The gates cannot withstand another shock. Shut that window, Miss Hale."

Margaret closed her window and moved to support Mrs. Thornton's trembling fingers. For several minutes, there was a pause in the unseen street. Mrs. Thornton looked at her son's countenance with wild anxiety, seeking interpretation in his rigid lines of contemptuous defiance, where neither hope nor fear could be read.

Fanny raised herself: "Are they gone?" she whispered.

"Gone!" he replied. "Listen!"

They heard the one great straining breath, the creak of wood slowly yielding, the wrench of iron, and the mighty fall of the ponderous gates. Fanny stood up, tottering, took a step toward her mother, and collapsed into her arms in a fainting fit. Mrs. Thornton lifted her with a strength born of sheer will and carried her away.

"Thank God!" Mr. Thornton said, watching her exit. "Had you not better go upstairs, Miss Hale?"

Margaret's lips formed a "No," but he could not hear her, for the tramp of innumerable steps pounded right under the house wall, accompanied by a fierce growl of low, angry voices that held a ferocious murmur of satisfaction more dreadful than their baffled cries moments before.

"Never mind!" he said, attempting to encourage her. "I am sorry you were entrapped in this alarm, but it cannot last long. A few more minutes, and the soldiers will be here."

"Oh, God!" Margaret cried suddenly. "There is Boucher. I know his face, though he is livid with rage—he is fighting to get to the front! Look! Look!"

"Who is Boucher?" Mr. Thornton asked coolly, moving close to the window to identify the man. As soon as they saw him, the crowd set up a yell that was not human—it was the demoniac desire of a wild beast for food withheld from its ravenous jaws. Mr. Thornton drew back momentarily, dismayed by the intensity of hatred he had provoked.

"Let them yell!" he said. "In five minutes more... I only hope my poor Irishmen are not terrified out of their wits by such fiendlike noise. Keep your courage for five minutes, Miss Hale."

"Don't be afraid for me," she said hastily. "But what in five minutes? Can you do nothing to soothe these poor creatures? It is awful to see them."

"The soldiers will be here directly, and that will bring them to reason."

"To reason!" Margaret snapped. "What kind of reason?"

"The only reason that deals with men who make themselves into wild beasts. By heaven! They've turned to the mill-door!"

"Mr. Thornton," Margaret said, shaking with passion, "go down this instant, if you are not a coward. Go down and face them like a man. Save these poor strangers whom you have decoyed here. Speak to your workmen as if they were human beings. Speak kindly. Do not let the soldiers come in and cut down poor creatures driven mad. I see one there who is. If you have any courage or nobility, go out and speak to them, man to man!"

He turned to look at her while she spoke. A dark cloud gathered on his face as he listened. He set his teeth at her words.

"I will go. Perhaps I may ask you to accompany me downstairs and bar the door behind me; my mother and sister will need that protection."

"Oh! Mr. Thornton! I do not know—I may be wrong—only—"

But he was gone. He was downstairs in the hall, had unbarred the front door, and all she could do was follow him quickly, fasten it behind him, and climb the stairs again with a sick heart and a dizzy head. She took her place by the farthest window. He was on the steps below, visible to a thousand angry eyes, but she could see or hear nothing but the savage satisfaction of the rolling, angry murmur. She threw the window wide open. Many in the crowd were mere boys, cruel and thoughtless; some were men, gaunt as wolves and mad for prey. She understood now; they were like Boucher—men with starving children at home, relying on ultimate success to raise wages, and enraged beyond measure at discovering Irishmen were brought in to rob their little ones of bread. Margaret read this desperation in Boucher's face, forlorn and livid with rage. If Mr. Thornton would but speak—if they heard his voice alone—it seemed it would be better than this wild beating against stony silence. Perhaps he was speaking now; there was a momentary hush in their noise, inarticulate as the sound of a troop of animals. She tore her bonnet off and bent forward to listen. She could only see; for if Mr. Thornton had indeed attempted to speak, the instinct to listen was gone, and the people were raging worse than ever. He stood with arms folded, still as a statue, his face pale with repressed excitement. They were trying to intimidate him, urging each other on to immediate violence. Margaret felt intuitively that in an instant all would be uproar; the first touch would cause an explosion in which even Mr. Thornton's life would be unsafe. Even as she watched, she saw lads in the background stooping to remove their heavy wooden clogs—the readiest missiles they could find. She saw it was the spark to the gunpowder. With a cry no one heard, she rushed out of the room, down the stairs. She had lifted the great iron bar of the door with imperious force, thrown it open wide, and stood there, facing that angry sea of men, her eyes smiting them with flaming arrows of reproach. The clogs were arrested in the hands that held them; the countenances, so fell a moment before, now looked irresolute, as if asking what this meant. For she stood between them and their enemy. She could not speak, but held out her arms until she could recover breath.

"Oh, do not use violence! He is one man, and you are many." Her words died away, for there was no tone in her voice; it was but a hoarse whisper. Mr. Thornton stood a little to one side; he had moved away from behind her, as if jealous of anything that should come between him and danger.

"Go!" she said once more, and now her voice was like a cry. "The soldiers are sent for—they are coming. Go peaceably. Go away. You shall have relief from your complaints, whatever they are."

"Shall them Irish blackguards be packed back again?" asked one from the crowd with fierce threat.

"Never, for your bidding!" exclaimed Mr. Thornton. And instantly the storm broke. The hootings rose and filled the air, but Margaret did not hear them. Her eye was fixed on the group of lads who had armed themselves with their clogs. She saw their gesture, knew its meaning, read their aim. Another moment, and Mr. Thornton might be smitten down—he whom she had urged and goaded to this perilous place. She only thought how she could save him. She threw her arms around him, making her body a shield from the fierce people beyond. Still, with his arms folded, he shook her off.

"Go away," he said in his deep voice. "This is no place for you."

"It is," she said. "You did not see what I saw." If she thought her sex would be a protection—if, with shrinking eyes, she had turned away from their terrible anger hoping they would pause and reflect—she was wrong. Their reckless passion had carried them too far. It was always the savage lads, with their love of cruel excitement, who headed the riot, reckless to what bloodshed it might lead. A clog whizzed through the air. Margaret's fascinated eyes watched its progress; it missed its aim, and she turned sick with affright, but did not change her position, only hiding her face on Mr. Thornton's arm. Then she turned and spoke again:

"For God's sake! Do not damage your cause by this violence. You do not know what you are doing." She strove to make her words distinct.

A sharp pebble flew by her, grazing forehead and cheek, drawing a blinding sheet of light before her eyes. She lay like one dead on Mr. Thornton's shoulder. Then he unfolded his arms and held her encircled for an instant.
