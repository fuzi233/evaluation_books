A chill, sterile air, reminiscent of a subterranean ice-house, greeted me as I crossed the threshold. Behind us, the heavy door slammed shut, severing our connection to the outside world. The sexton muttered something to his wife—a phrase that caused her to pivot slowly toward us. What a transformation had occurred within her! It was as though the brilliance of her gaze had become too intense to contain, sinking inward to ignite a luminosity akin to Beatrice amidst the white rose of the redeemed. From her countenance, life itself—eternal, immortal, unbroken lightning—streamed forth. Even her hands shimmered with a pale radiance; every "pearl-shell helmet" gleamed like a captured moonstone. Her beauty was overwhelming, and I was relieved when she finally turned her attention away from me.

However, the flickering candlelight she held reached only a short distance. Initially, I could discern nothing of the surroundings. Gradually, though, the beam illuminated something glimmering slightly above the floor. Was it a bed? Could any living creature endure such profound, mortal cold? Perhaps it was no surprise that none stirred to awaken! Beyond that point, fainter shines emerged, followed by uncertain glimmers on every side.

A few steps brought us to the first object: a human form beneath a sheet, straight and motionless—I could not determine gender, for the light seemed to shy away from the face as we passed. Soon, I realized we were traversing an aisle of couches. Upon almost every one, aligned head-first toward the passage, lay a sleeper or a corpse, draped in sheets as white as fresh snow. My soul fell silent, consumed by dread. We walked aisle after aisle among countless couches. I could see only a handful at any given moment, yet they surrounded me on all sides, vanishing into an apparent infinity. Was this where my allotted bed lay? Must I succumb to sleep among the unwaking, with no one to rouse me? Was this truly the sexton's library, and were these his books? This chamber of the dead was certainly no halfway house.

"One of the cellars I am charged to watch!" the sexton remarked, speaking in a low whisper as if fearing to disturb his silent guests. "Much wine is laid here to ripen! Yet, it grows dark for a stranger!" he added.

"The moon is rising; she will arrive shortly," said his wife. Her clear, sweet voice echoed with an ancient sorrow long since bid farewell to.

As she spoke, the moon peered through an aperture in the wall, and a thousand gleams of white silver responded to her touch. Still, I could not discern a beginning or an end to the rows of couches. They stretched endlessly, as if prepared for the scattered world to slumber upon. Along the far-receding narrow avenues, each couch stood solitary, hosting a lone sleeper. At first, I mistook their state for death, but I quickly perceived it was something far deeper—an unknown abyss.

The moon climbed higher, casting light through additional openings, yet I could never grasp the full shape or character of the place simultaneously. Now it resembled a vast cathedral nave, then a colossal barn converted into a dwelling of tombs. It felt colder than any moon known during Earth's frostiest nights. Where her beams struck directly, they cast a bluish, icy sheen upon the white sheets and the pallid countenances—but perhaps it was the faces themselves that rendered the moon so frigid!

Among those visible, all shared a brotherhood of death, yet each differed wildly in the histories etched upon them. Here lay a man who had perished in the prime of manly strength; though this was not death, I lacked another name for it. His dark beard flowed like a liberated stream from the glacier of his frozen visage; his forehead was smooth as polished marble. A shadow of pain lingered about his lips, but only a fleeting ghost of it. Beside him rested the form of a girl, passing lovely to behold. The sadness left on her face by departure had not yet dissolved into perfect peace, but absolute submission possessed her placid features. There was no sign of wasting disease, no trace of "killing care or grief of heart"; if pain had ever existed, it was long charmed asleep, never to wake. Many beautiful souls lay very still—some mere children—but I saw not a single infant.

The most beautiful of all was a lady whose white hair, alone, suggested her age when first she fell asleep. Upon her stately countenance rested not submission, but a right noble acquiescence—an assurance, firm as the foundations of the universe, that all was as it should be. On some faces lingered the almost obliterated scars of strife, the marring marks of hopeless loss, the fading shadows of sorrows that had seemed inconsolable. The aurora of the great morning had not yet quite melted them away. Yet, those faces were few. Every bearer of such a brand of pain seemed to plead, "Pardon me: I died only yesterday!" or, "Pardon me: I died but a century ago!" I knew that others had lain dormant for ages—not merely by their unutterable repose, but by some quality for which I possess neither word nor symbol.

We arrived eventually at three empty couches. Immediately beyond them lay the form of a beautiful woman, a little past the prime of life. One of her arms extended outside the sheet, her hand resting palm-upward. In the center of her palm lay a dark spot. Beside her stood the stalwart figure of a middle-aged man. His arm too protruded from the sheet, his strong hand almost closed, as if clenched around the grip of a sword. He must have been a king who died fighting for the truth.

"Shall I bring the candle closer, wife?" the sexton whispered, leaning down to inspect the woman's hand.

"It heals well," he murmured to himself. "The nail found in her causes no harm!"

Finally, I ventured to speak.

"Are they not dead?" I asked softly.

"I cannot answer you," he replied in a subdued tone. "I almost forget what they mean by 'DEAD' in the old world. If I said a person was dead, my wife would understand one thing, and you would imagine another.—This is but one of my treasure vaults," he continued. "And all my guests are not laid in vaults: out there on the moor, they lie thick as the leaves of a forest after the first blast of your winter—thick, let me say rather, as if the great white rose of heaven had shed its petals over it. All night the moon reads their faces, and smiles."

"But why leave them in the corrupting moonlight?" I questioned.

"Our moon," he answered, "is not like yours—the old cinder of a burnt-out world. Her beams embalm the dead, not corrupt them. Observe that here the sexton lays his dead upon the earth; he buries very few beneath it! In your world, he heaps huge stones upon them, as if to pin them down. I wait for the hour to ring the resurrection-bell, to wake those who still sleep. Your sexton consults the clock to know when to ring the dead-alive to church; I listen for the cock on the spire to crow: 'AWAKE, THOU THAT SLEEPEST, AND ARISE FROM THE DEAD!'"

I began to conclude that this self-styled sexton was, in truth, an insane parson; the entire affair was too mad! Yet, how could I escape? I was helpless! In this realm of the dead, the raven and his wife were the only living beings I had encountered. Whither should I turn for aid? I was lost in a space larger than imagination—for if two objects, or even parts thereof, could occupy the same space, why not twenty or ten thousand? But I dared not pursue this line of thought further.

"You seem to perceive differences in your dead beyond my comprehension!" I ventured to remark.

"None of those you see," he answered, "are truly dead yet. Some have barely begun to come alive and die. Others had begun to die—that is, to come alive—long before they reached us. And when such are indeed dead, that instant they will wake and depart. Almost every night, some rise and go. But I shall say no more, for I find my words only mislead you!—This is the couch that has been waiting for you," he concluded, pointing to one of the three.

"Why this specific one?" I said, trembling as anxiety drove me to delay through parley.

"For reasons which one day you will be glad to know," he answered.

"Why not know them now?"

"That also you will know when you wake."

"But these are all dead, and I am alive!" I objected, shuddering.

"Not much," rejoined the sexton with a smile. "Not nearly enough! Blessed be the true life wherein the pauses between its throbs are not death!"

"The place is too cold to allow one to sleep!" I protested.

"Do these find it so?" he returned. "They sleep well—or will soon. Of cold, they feel not a breath: it heals their wounds.—Do not be a coward, Mr. Vane. Turn your back on fear, and your face to whatever may come. Give yourself up to the night, and you will rest indeed. Harm will not come to you, but a good you cannot foreknow."

The sexton and I stood beside the couch, while his wife, holding the candle, knelt at its foot.
