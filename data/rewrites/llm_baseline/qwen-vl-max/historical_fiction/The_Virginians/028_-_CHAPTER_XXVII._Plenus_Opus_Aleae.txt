“Tell me about those children, Harry,” said Maria, as they walked slowly across the common. “The ones I saw running about at the house where you were taken in after your fall—poor dear boy, it broke my heart to see you so hurt.” She spoke gently, her voice soft with concern.

“Oh, that fall!” Harry exclaimed, gripping her arm more tightly than he meant to. “I thought I’d never recover when I saw it! But don’t squeeze like that—you know I don’t care for you?” He laughed, though his tone carried a hint of teasing.

“The people there are the kindest, most wonderful folks I’ve ever met,” he went on, warming to the subject. “Mrs. Lambert was a friend of my mother’s during her time in Europe. Colonel Lambert has seen service all over the world—Scotland, Flanders, Minorca. He’s a gentleman through and through. They treated me like their own son. No natural parents could have been kinder. How can I ever repay them? I must do something—give them a gift, I tell you!” He reached into his pocket, fingers brushing against the crisp banknotes he had just won from Morris and March.

“We could go to the toy shop,” suggested Lady Maria, smiling faintly. “Buy a couple of dolls for the children. Though I warn you—it would be ungracious to offer payment for such kindness.”

“Dolls for Hester and Theo?” Harry chuckled. “Do you think a woman isn’t a woman until she’s forty, Maria?”

Her arm under his gave the tiniest twitch—just a flicker of pain beneath the surface.

“You don’t understand,” he continued, eager now. “Hester doesn’t consider herself a child at all. And Theo—she’s older than her sister, far cleverer. They speak half a dozen languages, read endlessly—books upon books. They play the harpsichord together beautifully, sing duets, and Theo writes songs of her own.”

“Indeed?” Maria replied, surprised. “I barely noticed them. They looked so young. I had no idea they were such remarkable girls.”

“That’s just how women are!” Harry declared, laughing. “Back home, if George or I praised a woman, Mrs. Esmond or even Mountain would find fault with her!”

“I wouldn’t criticize anyone who’s been kind to you, Mr. Warrington,” Maria sighed, her voice low. “Though I know you’re not angry with me for envying them—for being the one who cared for you when you were wounded and ill, while I… I had to leave you behind.”

“You dear good Maria,” he said, squeezing her hand again.

“No, Harry,” she insisted, pulling away slightly. “Don’t be so forward. Look—there’s your black servant walking with a group of others in livery. The wretches are off to the tea-garden, no doubt to drink themselves into oblivion, just like their masters. That dreadful Mr. Morris was utterly drunk when I came to you—he frightened you terribly.”

“I’d just won big bets from both of them,” Harry said, pride swelling in his chest. “What shall I buy for you, my dear cousin?” And he began recounting his triumphs, flushed with victory and high spirits. He laughed, boasted lightly.

“For the honour of Virginia,” he declared, “I was determined to show them what real jumping was. With a little practice, I could leap two feet farther.”

Maria smiled at her young champion’s success. But then, with a note of caution, she added, “Still, you must be careful about gambling, child. You know it’s ruined our family before. My brother Castlewood, Will, poor father, even Aunt Lady Castlewood—each fell victim to it. As for Lord March, he’s the most reckless and yet most successful gambler among the nobility.”

“I don’t intend to fear him—or his friend Jack Morris,” Harry replied, fingers still tracing the edges of his winnings. “What games do you play at Aunt Bernstein’s? Cribbage, all-fours, brag, whist, piquet, quadrille? I’m ready for any of them. What time is it—surely it’s seven?”

“And you want to start now?” Maria said, voice tinged with sorrow. “You don’t care to walk with your poor cousin anymore. Not long ago, you did.”

“Hey! Youth is youth, cousin!” Harry declared, tossing back his head. “A young fellow must have his fun!” And he strutted beside her, confident, happy, hungry for pleasure.

Not long ago, he had enjoyed walking with her. Only yesterday, he had been content in the company of Hester and Theo, and kind-hearted Mrs. Lambert. But life, gaiety, the thrill of winning—these things called to him now, and he seized them without hesitation, like any other young man, blind to the hangover waiting in the morning.

As they spoke, the fiddles from the open orchestra on the Parade began tuning up, squealing in preparation for their evening concert. Maria knew her aunt was awake again—her usual routine had begun—and that she must return to her duties. Harry never asked about this burden, though he must have known it. He never pitied her. His thoughts were elsewhere.

Yet when Harry had been injured, she had suffered far more deeply than he had. He had rarely left her mind, though she buried her feelings beneath smiles and quiet composure, as women often do.

I know, dear Mrs. Grundy, you may think her an old fool. But do you suppose fools’ caps only crown bright hair? Grey locks wear them too. Be gentle with our elderly ladies, O wise Minerva of a woman! Or perhaps you’re so virtuous and enlightened that you never read novels at all. This I know: late harvests of wild oats grow just as well as early ones. From observation of myself and neighbours, I suspect *avena fatua* thrives right up to the final days of the year.

Like parents eager to escape a troublesome child and attend their evening party, Madame Bernstein and her staff had hurriedly put the sun to bed while daylight still lingered, drawn curtains over the sky, and busied themselves with cards, candles, tea, negus, and other delights.

One by one, ladies arrived at the Baroness’s door—some painted, some patched, all dressed in brocade. Gentlemen followed in gala attire. Mr. Poellnitz wore the largest star and the most elaborately embroidered coat of all. When Lord March and Ruglen appeared, he was transformed from the rough jockey Harry had met at the White Horse. His tight brown scratch hat was replaced by a curled feather wig with grey powder; his leather breeches exchanged for a rich French suit. Jack Morris wore nearly identical finery—same wig, same style of coat, tailored to resemble his lordship’s exactly.

Mr. Wolfe entered in attendance upon his beautiful mistress, Miss Lowther, and her aunt, whose passion for cards matched that of the world.

When Lady Maria Esmond arrived, her appearance belied her aunt’s description. Her figure was graceful, her dress revealing much of it. Her complexion was naturally fair, enhanced by a dark frilled ribbon around her neck, clasped with a jewel. Her cheeks bore no more colour than others’, though roses were freely bought at perfumery shops. A few artful patches, it was whispered, added sparkle to her charm. Her hoop was no larger than modern contraptions worn today. In sum, though absurd in parts, the fashion was pleasing overall.

Suppose our ladies took to bangles and nose-rings? We might laugh—but not despise them. Lovers would scarcely hesitate to lift the ring to kiss the lips beneath.

As for the Baroness de Bernstein, when she made the effort to dress grandly, she was still handsome, magnificent—but melancholy, even terrifying. Some faces carry the weight of the past; others fade into meekness. The fire dies in some eyes, as crow’s-feet deepen around them. They no longer flash with scorn, anger, or love. They gaze, but no one melts under their sapphire stare. They look—but no one is dazzled.

My fair young reader, if you are not the peerless Lindamira, Queen of the Ball—if, at night’s end, you admit you danced with only two or three partners while Lindamira drew a crowd—console yourself. At fifty, you will appear as kind and pleasant as you do now at eighteen. You won’t surrender your coach-and-six of beauty to another. You won’t walk through life alone, watching admiration flee. You won’t witness the decline of your smile, nor see fashion abandon your once-grand rooms, leaving only dust, cobwebs, and lonely placards: “To Let.”

You may never have known grandeur—but you’ll never feel abandonment. You may not have inherited millions—but you’ll avoid bankruptcy.

“Our hostess,” murmured Lord Chesterfield to a friend, unaware of his loud whisper, “reminds me of Covent Garden in my youth. Then it was the fashionable quarter. Now, noble houses are gaming dens—or you may enter with a friend and order a bottle.”

“Ah,” said Lord March, shrugging, “a bottle and a tavern have their uses. I was born after the Georges came in, though I hope to live to a hundred. I never knew the Bernstein except as an old woman. If she once had beauty, I confess I don’t know how she spent it.”

“No, hang me, how *did* she spend it?” Jack Morris laughed.

“Here’s a table!” he announced. “Shall we sit? Don’t let the Frenchman join us—he won’t pay. Mr. Warrington, will you take a card?”

Mr. Warrington and Lord Chesterfield found themselves partnered against Morris and Lord March.

“You’re late, Baron,” said the elder nobleman, stopping the other nobleman advancing toward the table. “We’ve already started. Have you forgotten Mr. Warrington of Virginia—the young gentleman you met in London?”

“The young gentleman I met at Arthur’s Chocolate House had black hair, a slight turned-up nose, and wasn’t nearly as fortunate in looks as Mr. Warrington,” the Baron replied with quick wit. “Warrington, Dorrington, Harrington? We of the continent struggle with your insular names. I assure you, this gentleman is not the one I mentioned at dinner.” With a kind glance, he sidled away to the window embrasure, where Mr. Wolfe and Miss Lowther were deep in conversation.

There, the Baron decided to engage the lieutenant-colonel in discussion of the Prussian manual exercise recently introduced into King George II’s army—a subject Mr. Wolfe knew well, and which might have interested him at any other moment. Still, the old gentleman delivered his opinions with confidence, believing himself charming.

At first, the Baroness greeted guests personally, chatting as they arrived. But as the rooms filled and games began, she grew restless and withdrew with three friends to her corner, where a reserved table awaited her. There, seated firmly, she stayed until dawn, never changing position or abandoning her game.

Now, the duty of welcoming guests fell to Lady Maria. Though she disliked cards, she dutifully attended to her aunt’s visitors, often passing by the table where Harry played with his companions.

“Come cut the cards for us,” Lord March called out as she passed. “Bring us luck, Lady Maria! We’ve had none tonight, and Mr. Warrington is winning everything.”

“I hope you’re not playing high, Harry?” she asked timidly.

“Oh, only sixpences,” Lord March replied, dealing.

“Only sixpences,” echoed Mr. Morris, though his face darkened as if losing mere coins were a disaster.

Lord Chesterfield sat opposite Harry, calmly sorting his cards. One could not tell from his expression whether fortune favoured him.

A word slipped from Morris’s lips—something less than joyful—and his partner snapped, “Hang it, Morris, play your cards and keep silent!”

Strange, considering they were only betting sixpences.

Maria lingered near Harry’s chair, hand resting on the back. She could see his cards—dozens of trumps clustered in one corner. She hadn’t taken his luck away. She smiled, pleased she had cut the pack that dealt him such advantage.

As Lord March dealt, he quietly said, “Same bet as before, Mr. Warrington—or shall we double it?”

“Anything you like, my lord,” Harry replied, calm as ever.

“Then let’s say shillings.”

“Shillings,” Harry agreed. The game continued.

The outcome of that night—and several following—can be learned from a letter never sent, but later found among Mr. Henry Warrington’s papers:

Since White’s two bottles of Burgundy and a pack of cards constitute all the joys of your life, I assume you’re currently in London, preferring smoke and faro to fresh air and haystacks. This letter will reach you by a young gentleman I’ve lately met—one you’ll be delighted to know. He plays any game, at any stake, any hour, and drinks any reasonable number of bottles during play. Mr. Warrington is none other than the Fortunate Youth of whom so many tales have appeared in the *Public Advertiser* and similar papers. He owns an estate in Virginia as large as Yorkshire, burdened only by a mother—the reigning sovereign. But since the climate is unhealthy and fevers rampant, let us hope Mrs. Esmond passes soon, leaving this virtuous youth in undisputed possession.

She is aunt to that rogue Castlewood, who never pays his debts unless he’s more honourable with you than he was with me. Mr. Warrington is of noble stock. We must include him in our circle—if only so I may win my money back.

He’s had extraordinary luck here, winning everything while his card-playing aunt loses. A few nights ago, when I first met him, he beat me in jumping (having trained among savages and fled bears in his native woods). He won bets from me and Jack Morris on my weight. That night, at old Bernstein’s, he defeated us all.

If you settle our last Epsom account, please transfer 350 pounds to Mr. Warrington—I still owe him, having emptied my purse. Chesterfield lost six hundred, though he refuses to acknowledge it, having sworn to quit gaming and live cleanly. Jack Morris, less affected than the rest—since he has no estates or retinue to maintain, and all his father’s ill-gotten wealth at hand—roars like a bull of Bashan over his losses.

We had a second night’s play, in private, with Barbeau serving us a fine dinner. Mr. Warrington keeps silence like a gentleman—none of us have spoken of our losses, but the whole place does. Yesterday, Cattarina glared at me in fury because I refused her a diamond necklace, claiming I denied her due to losing five thousand to the Virginian. My old Duchess of Q. confirms the same tale—and knows precisely how much Chesterfield and Jack have lost.

Warrington treated the company to breakfast and music. You should have seen how the women tore him apart. That fiendish Cattarina ogled him from my vis-à-vis, right under my nose, as we drove to Penshurst. I have no doubt she’s already sent him a billet-doux.

He shot Jack Morris to pieces at target practice—we’ll test him with partridges when season arrives.

He is truly fortunate. Youth unspoiled by English excesses, good health, good looks, and good luck.

In short, Mr. Warrington has won our money in the finest gentlemanly way. Since I like him—and wish to win some back—I entrust him to your saintly guardianship. Adieu! I’m heading north and returning for Doncaster.

**Yours ever, dear George,
M. et R.**
