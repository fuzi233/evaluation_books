Rosa scarcely regained consciousness before the terrifying scene of her recent encounter flooded her mind. It haunted her dreams, intruding upon her waking moments with relentless clarity; there had not been a single instant of oblivion between the shock and her return to reality. Her mind, usually a sanctuary of gentle thoughts, was now a chaotic storm. Every fiber screamed for flight. The man who had held her captive, who had spoken with such chilling intensity, was a monster she could not endure. Where could she hide? How could she escape?

For weeks, Rosa's existence had been a kaleidoscope of confusion, a swirling mist of unspoken suspicions. Now, however, the fog lifted to reveal a horrifying truth. For six months, Jasper's obsession with Edwin Drood—the nephew who was now missing—had blinded everyone else. They saw only a devoted uncle mourning a lost boy, a man consumed by grief and justice. But Rosa saw the fissures in his devotion. She remembered the sun-dial in the garden, where he had leaned, his voice trembling with a mixture of rage and desperate love. He had treated Edwin's disappearance as a premeditated murder, a fact he had woven into his public persona ever since the discovery of the watch and shirt-pin.

Yet, something gnawed at her conscience. If Jasper truly feared his crimes being exposed, why did he insist on the narrative of murder? A murderer would want the world to believe his nephew ran away voluntarily. Furthermore, he had spoken of sacrificing his "fidelity" to his dead boy for her sake, pouring his "desolate heart" and "wasted life" into her path. Were these words genuine? Or were they the fabrications of a villain constructing a mask?

The thought that Jasper might have murdered Edwin solely to gain control over Rosa—a motive born of vanity and twisted affection—seemed so monstrous that she nearly covered her face in shame. Could she, a humble governess, conceive of such wickedness? Was it perhaps a product of her own aversion to him, a projection of fear onto a man who appeared so broken? Yet, as she recalled his terrifying gaze and the way he had clutched her wrist, the evidence mounted. He was not merely a grieving uncle; he was a terrible man, a force of nature that defied human understanding. To stay was to invite catastrophe. If she hesitated, if she delayed, Jasper's malice would turn upon Helena and Neville.

Helena, her young friend, believed in Jasper's innocence with blind faith. Rosa had assured her of this belief, shielding Helena from the ugly truth. But Rosa knew better. Helena was innocent of Jasper's designs, unaware of the danger hanging over them both. Rosa could not risk revealing the truth to her; the shock might drive Helena mad, or worse, provoke Jasper into immediate action.

Rosa realized that any place within the vicinity of Cloisterham was unsafe. The walls of the convent offered no protection against a man who could penetrate them. She needed distance, absolute safety. Her thoughts drifted to her guardian, Miss Twinkleton. But the thought of seeking help from her old friend was insufficient; Rosa needed strength, wisdom, and authority. She required someone who understood the depths of human evil. She decided she must flee to London, to Mr. Hiram Grewgious.

With a trembling hand, she wrote a hurried note to Miss Twinkleton, claiming urgent business and assuring her guardian that all was well, lest alarm be caused. She packed a small satchel with the bare essentials—a change of clothes, a trifle of money—and left the convent, stepping quietly through the gate.

It was the first time Rosa had traversed Cloisterham High Street entirely alone. The street, familiar to her from childhood, suddenly felt alien, its winding alleys potentially hiding dangers unknown. She hastened toward the corner where the omnibus stood. Just as she reached it, the vehicle prepared to depart.

"Stop!" she cried, breathless. "Take me, if you please, Joe. I must go to London."

Joe, the driver, paused instantly. Within a minute, Rosa was seated in the carriage, wrapped in a cloak, her small bag tucked securely beneath her. Joe drove her swiftly to the railway station, offering his assistance until she was safely inside the train car. He placed her bag near her, handling it with exaggerated care, as if it were a precious cargo.

"I shall return to tell Miss Twinkleton that you have arrived safely," Joe promised.
"With my love, please, Joe," Rosa whispered, pressing a kiss to his cap.
"Yes, Miss," Joe murmured, his lips curving slightly. "And I wouldn't mind keeping it myself!" Though he did not speak the sentiment aloud, his eyes twinkled with a quiet pride.

As the train lurched forward, carrying her away from the gray skies of Kent toward the bustling heart of London, Rosa allowed herself a moment of reflection. The indignation she felt at Jasper's audacity bolstered her resolve. His declaration of love was a stain upon her honor, one that could only be cleansed by exposing the truth to honest, upright men. Yet, as the landscape shifted from rolling fields to the looming silhouette of the Great City, a wave of doubt washed over her. Was this rash decision wise? Would Mr. Grewgious understand her plight? What if he were absent? The darkness of the tunnel swallowed the daylight, and with it, her courage flickered. The crowd of strangers in the carriage, the endless rumble of wheels, the uncertainty of the future—all threatened to overwhelm her fragile spirit.

Hours later, the train emerged from the tunnels, plunging into the grit of London. The streets were alive with the scurrying of humanity, a sea of shabbiness and grime under the gaslights. Rosa alighted and hailed a cab, directing the driver to Staple Inn, the address of Mr. Hiram Grewgious. The streets were narrow and labyrinthine, echoing with the shuffle of boots and the faint, melancholy strains of street musicians. Their music seemed to resonate with the sorrow of the city, evoking echoes from brick walls and stirring dust from cobblestones.

The cab halted before a heavy, iron-bound gateway, the kind of entrance that suggested a household wary of intruders. Rosa stepped out, clutching her satchel, and knocked timidly. The door opened, and a watchman, alert and sharp-eyed, peered out.

"Does Mr. Grewgious reside here?" she asked, her voice barely above a whisper.
"He does, miss," the watchman replied, nodding toward a specific alleyway. "But it is a private court. Please follow."

Rosa followed him deeper into the shadows, ascending a staircase that creaked underfoot. The air grew cooler, smelling of wax candles and old paper. When she reached the landing, the clock chimed ten. She found herself standing before a door marked with a faded sign bearing the name "P.J.T.," though she suspected the occupant was different. Guided by the painted nameplate, she approached the correct door. It yielded easily to her knock, opening silently.

Inside, the room was dim, illuminated only by a single lamp cast low on a table. Sitting by an open window, bathed in the pale moonlight, was a figure of thin frame and intense intellect. He wore a frock coat that seemed too large for his slender body, and his face, gaunt and angular, bore the marks of deep contemplation.

Rosa moved closer, drawn by an instinctive trust.
"My God!" the man exclaimed, rising abruptly. "Rosa!"

Tears streaming down her cheeks, she threw her arms around his neck, burying her face in his shoulder. He embraced her gently, his movements slow and deliberate, devoid of the frantic energy that usually characterized his interactions.

"My child, my child!" he sobbed, his voice thick with emotion. "I thought you were your mother! What has happened, Rosa? Who has brought you here? Speak, my dear, speak!"
"No one," she choked out, pulling back to look into his weary eyes. "I came alone. I took a sudden resolution. Oh, Mr. Grewgious, poor, poor Eddy!"
Jasper," corrected Mr. Grewgious softly. "Tell me, Rosa. Has he harmed you?"
"No," she said, shaking her head violently. "But he... he made love to me. I cannot bear it, sir! I shudder with horror at the thought of him. I have come to you to protect me, and Helena, and Neville, from him. Will you?"

"I will!" Mr. Grewgious cried, his composure shattering. "By the Holy Ghost, I will! Confound his politics! Frustrate his knavish tricks! On thee his hopes to fix? Damn him again!"

He paced the room, his hands gesturing wildly, his voice rising and falling in a rhythm of righteous fury. He seemed torn between a fit of loyal enthusiasm and a combative denunciation, unable to settle into a single mode of expression. After a moment, he stopped, wiping his brow with a handkerchief.

"I beg your pardon, my dear," he said, his voice regaining a measure of calm. "I feel better already. But tell me no more just now, or I might lose control again. You must be refreshed and cheered. What did you take last? Breakfast, lunch, dinner, tea, or supper? And what shall we take next?"
