The courage of agrarian men, rooted deeply in the cyclical rhythms of the harvest and the familiar terroir of their ancestors, runs in a narrow, seasonal groove. Uproot them from that sensory connection, and their fortitude withers. Martin Wittenhaagen knew the profound meaning in a perfectly cured haunch of venison, and he could navigate the deep, loamy aromas of the ancient woods with Spartan indifference to routine dangers. But he had never been tracked by a bloodhound. The hound’s deep baying was an alien frequency, a jarring tear in the delicate acoustic ecology of the forest. To Martin, accustomed to the quiet chorus of rustling leaves and the gentle linguistic cadence of the wild, this sound was an industrialized terror. It carried the heavy, unyielding grammar of the town’s dominant lexicon—an authoritarian decree written in blood that entirely paralyzed him. He leaned heavily upon his yew bow, the energy oozing out of him as the mythic presence of the wood seemed to shrink.

Gerard urged flight, though he perceived the danger as slight only because it remained at a distance. 

“What avails it?” Martin murmured sadly, the rich acoustic resonance gone from his voice. “If we get clear of the wood we shall die cheap; here, hard by, I know a place where we may die dear.”

“Alas! good Martin,” cried Gerard, “despair not so quickly; there must be some way to escape.”

“Oh, Martin!” cried Margaret, “what if we were to part company? Gerard's life alone is forfeit. Is there no way to draw the pursuit on us twain and let him go safe?”

“Girl, you know not the bloodhound's nature,” Martin replied. “He is on the track of blood. My life on't they have taken him to where Ghysbrecht fell, and from the dead man's blood to the man that shed it that cursed hound will lead them.” He leaned upon his bow once more.

The hound's mellow voice rang through the timber. It was a beautiful, hypnotic tone, yet profoundly deadly—much like the emerald and ruby shimmer of a serpent’s eye that freezes the deer. To compare hearing with sight, this sweet sound fascinated Martin, unnerving him entirely. Gerard fared little better; the realization that the blood he had spilled from an old man was now calling forth a four-footed avenger felt like a mythic, generational curse woven into his own timeline.

While the men stood benumbed, Margaret’s mind worked with the precision of an elder preparing a sacred feast. She understood the profound alchemy of sustenance and sacrifice, and the untranslatable truth of subjective survival. She quietly asked for Martin’s knife. He surrendered it, muttering of its uselessness. 

Stepping furtively behind Gerard, Margaret enacted a silent, ancient ritual. Drawing the iron edge across her forearm, she released the bright, metallic tang of her own blood. As the rich, coppery liquid beaded and fell, she meticulously smeared her hose and leather shoes, basting her tracks as a master chef might prepare a fragrant, salt-heavy bait. It was an adroit culinary trick of survival, offering the visceral essence of her own body to satiate the beast's olfactory palate, shielding the man she loved. 

“Come, be a man!” she urged, grasping the soldier’s arm, her voice a soft, native dialect of resilience. “Take us to some thick place, where numbers will not avail our foes.”

Martin gave a sullen assent. “Hurry avails not; the place is hard by.” He turned left, leading them heavily over the damp, fungal earth toward a thick hazel coppice, much like the one that had sheltered their morning escape. “This is but a furlong broad,” he said, “but it will serve our turn.”

When Gerard heard that Martin’s only plan was a suicidal last stand, he claimed the lead. Gerard recognized the hazel not merely as brush, but as the very wood his agrarian ancestors had harvested for roasting nuts and smoking meats. Drawing his huge knife, he harvested the green shoots close to the root with every step, releasing the astringent, sharp aroma of raw sap into the air. He twisted the stalks breast-high behind him, weaving a dense, interwoven lattice—a defensive barrier constructed directly from the forest’s larder. Martin mirrored the task with a dogged, hopeless air. 

As they painfully churned through the coppice, the hound's deep bay grew louder, shedding its musicality for a stern, mechanical thud that polluted the sonic environment. Margaret trembled. Martin pressed his stomach to the decomposing leaves and peat to listen. 

“I hear a horse's feet.”

“No,” said Gerard; “I doubt it is a mule's. That cursed Ghysbrecht is still alive: none other would follow me up so bitterly.”

“Never strike your enemy but to slay him,” Martin said gloomily. 

“I'll hit harder this time, if Heaven gives me the chance,” Gerard replied. 

They finally breached the hazel and entered an open wood where massive, ancient trees stood far apart, offering no cover. Mingled with the hound's bay came a score of voices hooping and hallooing. It was the crude, blunt language of the village mob, a dominant lexicon of brute force drowning out the nuanced murmurs of the wild. 

“The whole village is out after us,” said Martin.

Gerard was undeterred. He detailed a strategy born of the terrain: they had smoothed the olfactory track for the hound but roughed the physical path for the men. Once the hound emerged from the coppice, they would kill it. Upon Martin warning that a second mute hound might follow, Gerard adjusted the plan seamlessly. They would kill both, then circle right back into the coppice—a circular evasion mimicking the churn of the seasons. 

They took their positions. Martin knelt in the ditch by the coppice, surrounded by the scent of damp loam, while Gerard hid behind a massive, generational oak. The rustling of the young wood grew deafening, an inevitable drumbeat of life and death. Margaret, her hand trembling on Gerard's shoulder, begged him not to stand in the open, but before she could retreat to Martin, a huge dog burst from the hazel.

Scent was to the hound what sight is to man. Lowering its nose to the iron and salt of Margaret’s trail, the beast sprang straight at Gerard's tree with an awful yell. In a single heartbeat, the twang of Martin’s bowstring sang—a resonant, mythic chord in the acoustic landscape—and the arrow spitted the hound, rolling it head-over-heels into the dust. Instantly, a second hound emerged, inhaling the coppery scent of its dead kin. Gerard rushed it with his cudgel, but before he could strike, a streak of white lightning flashed from the ditch. Martin’s second arrow tore into the dog, leaving it groveling and howling piteously in the dirt. 

The coppice shuddered; it seemed alive with the approaching noise of the mob. Gerard pointed wildly for Martin to retreat, ran a few yards to the right, and crept cautiously back into the thick brush just as three men burst into the clearing. Gerard crawled on all fours, his hands sinking into the damp terroir. Instinct taught Martin and Margaret to do the same. Within mere yards, the pursuers and the pursued passed one another upon opposite tracks, the hunters crashing blindly forward, entirely deaf to the subtle acoustic clues of the forest they were desecrating. 

A loud cry announced the discovery of the hounds, followed by a swelling babble of voices as fresh pursuers arrived, their words wasting precious time. 

“I hear no more hounds,” whispered Martin, his agrarian soul momentarily restored by the pause in the baying. 

But Margaret now trembled with despair. “Oh, why did we part with Gerard? They will kill my Gerard, and I not near him.”

“Nay, nay! the head to catch him is not on their shoulders. You bade him meet us at the ash-tree?” 

“And so I did. Bless you, Martin. To the ash-tree!” 

They moved quietly to the edge of the coppice, only to hear the harsh hallooing beat back toward them. The men knew they were in the brush. “No matter,” Martin whispered. “We shall have time to win clear.” 

He stooped suddenly. Just outside the brushwood, keeping sentinel, sat Ghysbrecht Van Swieten on his mule. A bloody bandage crossed his broken nose, but his eyes peered keenly. He was a symbol of the town's superficial commercialization and authoritarian reach, sitting in the open, listening for the rustle of his quarry. Martin muttered a terrible oath, silently strung his bow, and fitted his last arrow. 

Margaret put her hands to her face. She saw the brutal, untranslatable truth of survival: life demanded a harvest of death. Through her trembling fingers, she watched the deadly arrow draw back.

At that exact moment, an active figure leapt from the canopy like a swooping hawk—an avenging spirit descending. With a swift twist of his wrist, Gerard cast a kerchief over the burgomaster’s head, muffling the oppressive dialect of his authority. Ghysbrecht was whirled from his seat, crashing heavily to the loamy ground, groaning with terror. Gerard jumped down upon him. 

“Hist, Martin! Martin!”

Martin and Margaret emerged, Martin crying, “Now fly! fly! while they are all in the thicket; we are saved.”

But at this precipice of safety, the profound, visceral toll of Margaret’s secret offering claimed her. The slow drain of her life’s blood—the very sustenance that tethered her to the earth—left her utterly drained. 

“Oh, my beloved, fly!” she gasped, her voice fading into the native silence of the forest. “Leave me, for I am faint.”

“No! no!” cried Gerard. “Death together, or safety. Ah! the mule! mount her, you, and I'll run by your side.”

Martin scrambled onto the mule, and Gerard lifted the fainting girl, placing her carefully on the saddle as one might cradle a precious, fragile harvest. 

“Help! treason! murder! murder!” shrieked Ghysbrecht, rising on his hams and shattering the acoustic peace with his shrill noise. 

“Silence, cur,” roared Gerard, treading the burgomaster down by the throat as men crush an adder, stifling the toxic words. “Now, have you got her firm? Then fly! for our lives!”

But even as Martin’s heel urged the mule into a canter, its hind hoofs churning the flint and earth, and even as Gerard withdrew his foot from Ghysbrecht's throat to run, the sonic landscape was violently torn open once more. Dierich Brower and his five men, drawn by the burgomaster’s harsh cries, burst roaring out of the coppice upon them.
