I am truly sorry to keep you hanging about with me and my old beehive chair, in the sun at the back of the house. A man of my years, half-asleep, is not much to look at—least of all to anyone impatient for Mr. Franklin Blake. But if you want the truth fit to stand up in court, you must take it as it came: in its own order, with its own interruptions, as obstinate as a fly in the pantry when the sugar’s out.

Penelope had scarcely left me, after her message, when the house gave the signal that sets every servant’s stomach to marching. From the servants’ hall came the bright racket of plates, the clack of knives against trenchers, the little shriek of a teapot lid—music to those who dine at the proper hour, and torture to those kept waiting. I took my meals in my own room, as was my right, and had nothing to do with the servants’ dinner beyond wishing them a good appetite—then easing myself back to my chair, thinking to doze again.

I had just got my legs stretched out, with my stick handy, when out bounced another woman on me.

Not Penelope this time—Nancy, the kitchen-maid. A nice plump young lass, and generally as hearty as the gravy that runs clear when the joint’s properly rested. But now she had a face on her like sour ale; and a sulky face, in a well-ordered household, is like a bad smell in the larder—it must be traced and stopped at once.

“Why are you turning your back on your dinner?” I asked. “What’s wrong now, Nancy?”

She tried to squeeze by without answering. I got up—slowly, as you will when you’ve lived as long as I have—and took her by the ear. It’s my way of showing I bear no malice and mean to set things right. “What’s wrong now?” I said again.

“Rosanna’s late again for dinner,” says Nancy. “And I’m sent to fetch her in. All the hard work falls on my shoulders in this house. Let me alone, Mr. Betteredge!”

Rosanna Spearman was our second housemaid. I had my reasons—good reasons—for not liking that girl to be hauled in by a kitchen-maid with a temper sharpened on hunger. I had nothing particular to do; and a man who knows his servants knows when a word spoken kindly will go farther than a reprimand flung like dishwater. So I said:

“Where is Rosanna?”

“At the sands, of course!” Nancy tosses her head, as if Rosanna had taken a holiday in the moon. “She had another of her fainting fits this morning, and she asked to go out for fresh air. I have no patience with her!”

“Go back to your dinner,” I told her. “I have patience with her, and I’ll fetch her in.”

Nancy, who has a fine appetite and no shame about it, looked pleased at being excused from trudging. When she looks pleased, she looks nice; and when she looks nice, I chuck her under the chin. It’s not immorality—it’s habit, like tasting the broth before you serve it.

I took my stick and was for setting off; and then found myself obliged—again—to stop you. Because the sands are not like other sands; and Rosanna is not like other housemaids; and, worse luck for all of us, the matter of the Diamond touches both them and her so near that you can’t understand what follows unless you’ve smelt the place for yourself and taken the measure of the girl.

Rosanna was the only new servant in the house. About four months before the time I’m writing of, my lady had been in London and had visited a Reformatory—one of those places meant to keep a poor forlorn creature from slipping back into the old mire after prison has let her go. The matron—knowing a compassionate heart when she saw it—told my lady a miserable story about a girl named Rosanna Spearman. I won’t repeat it. There are sorrows that do no good by being served up again; and besides, I have always held that the kitchen is no place for needless waste.

It came to this: Rosanna had been a thief. Not one of the grand rogues who dress their plunder in ledgers and call it “business,” but a small one, caught with her hand where it shouldn’t be. So the law did what the law does—seized her, locked her up, labelled her, and handed her on, like a joint passed from cook to scullery, to reformatory.

The matron’s opinion—spite of everything—was that Rosanna was one in a thousand, and wanted only a fair chance. My lady, being a Christian woman if ever there was one, said at once: “Rosanna Spearman shall have her chance, in my service.”

In a week she entered our establishment as second housemaid. Not a soul was told her story except Miss Rachel and me. My lady consulted me—she honoured me that way—and, having fallen into Sir John’s old habit of agreeing with her in most things, I agreed heartily. The chance was as fair as clean linen: wages, privileges, a roof, and now and then a friendly word from my lady in private, given quietly as you give a child an extra spoonful without making the others jealous.

And Rosanna, I will say for her, worked well. She wasn’t strong—she had those fainting fits—and yet she went about her duty modest and uncomplaining, careful as a woman who knows she has only one good gown and must keep it for Sunday. But she never took among the other maids. Penelope was kind to her, always; never intimate, but never cruel. The rest kept her out.

Why? Not beauty; she had none to make them envious. She was the plainest in the house, and had the misfortune of one shoulder bigger than the other—a thing she tried to hide with a grey cloak when she went out. I think what the women resented most was her silence and her solitary ways. In leisure hours when they sat chattering, she read or worked. When her turn came to go out, nine times out of ten she went alone, bonnet on, hands folded, stepping away as if the very air of company scalded her. She never quarrelled; never snapped back; never took offence—only kept a distance, civilly and obstinately, as if there was an invisible line drawn around her.

And there was another thing—harder to name. A dash of something that wasn’t like a housemaid and was like a lady. In the voice, perhaps; or in the look of the face when she forgot herself. The women pounced on it the first day and said she gave herself airs. Unjust, as most quick judgments are.

Now, having put the Person before the Thing, I must come to the Thing: the sands.

Our house stands high on the Yorkshire coast, close by the sea. Walks there are in every direction but one. That one—if you value your spirits—is a walk you avoid. It takes you for a quarter of a mile through a plantation of firs where the wind makes a sound like a steady hiss over a kettle that will never boil, and where the branches rub and whisper, as if rehearsing some old tale in a dialect no living tongue can translate. Then you come out between low cliffs upon the loneliest, ugliest little bay on our coast.

Here the sandhills slope down to the sea and end in two spits of rock running out opposite each other until the water swallows them: the North Spit and the South. Between them lies—shifting with the seasons—the most horrible quicksand on all the Yorkshire shores.

At the turn of the tide, something stirs in the unknown deeps below. The whole face of the sand begins to tremble—shiver and quiver in a way that turns your stomach. The country folk call it the Shivering Sand; and a fitter name was never given. Half a mile out, a bank breaks the ocean’s force. So when the tide comes in over the quicksand, the waves seem to be left behind out there, and the water rolls in smooth, with a heavy heave—and covers the sand in silence.

Silence, mind you. Not the wholesome quiet of a field at dusk, where you can hear a bird settling and a cow shifting its weight. This is a deadened hush, like a mouth shut tight over a scream. No boat ever ventures into that bay. No children from Cobb’s Hole—the fishing village—come there to play. Even the birds, to my thinking, give it a wide berth, as if the place offends their very hearing.

And yet Rosanna, with dozens of good walks to choose from and company enough if she had only said “Come,” took this miserable place for her favourite. She would sit there, alone, working or reading when it was her turn out. Only now and then she went to Cobb’s Hole to see the one friend she had in our neighbourhood, of whom you shall hear later.

So this was where I now set off, stick in hand, to fetch her in to dinner.

I saw no sign of her in the fir plantation. The firs stood close, dark and sour-smelling, their fallen needles making a carpet that muffled my steps. The air had that resinous tang like pinewood thrown on a fire before the flame has taken—sharp in the nose, and not pleasant long. And above it all, the sea’s far breath—one steady undertone—kept time with my own.

When I got through the sandhills and out upon the beach, there she was at once: little straw bonnet, plain grey cloak, standing alone and looking out upon the quicksand and the sea.

She started when I came up. Then she turned her head away.

Not looking me in the face is another proceeding I never allow, on principle, to pass without inquiry. I took her gently by the arm, turned her my way, and saw she had been crying.

One of my bandanna handkerchiefs—one of six beauties my lady gave me—was in my pocket. I drew it out and said: “Come and sit down, my dear, on the slope of the beach along with me. I’ll dry your eyes first, and then I’ll make so bold as to ask what you have been crying about.”

Sitting down on a slope of sand is a longer business than you think, until your joints begin to argue with you at every bend. By the time I was settled, Rosanna had dried her own eyes with a little cheap cambric thing, poor and thin beside mine. She looked quiet—too quiet—and wretched; but she sat down by me when I told her, like a good girl.

“Now, tell me,” I said, “what are you crying about?”

“About the years that are gone, Mr. Betteredge,” she answered. “My past life still comes back to me sometimes.”

“Come, come,” I said. “Your past life is all sponged out. Why can’t you forget it?”

She took hold of the lappet of my coat. I am a slovenly old man, and a good deal of my meat and drink gets splashed on me. One woman servant or another is always scraping or rubbing at my spots. Only yesterday Rosanna had cleaned a greasy mark from that very lappet with a new composition, warranted to remove anything. The grease was gone; but the cloth showed dull where the nap had been hurt.

She pointed to the place and shook her head. “The stain is taken off,” she said. “But the place shows, Mr. Betteredge—the place shows!”

Now there’s a remark that takes a man unawares by means of his own coat. I didn’t find an answer ready. And there was something in her that made me sorrier for her than I liked to be. She had nice brown eyes, plain though she was; and she looked at me with a respect—as if my comfortable age and my good character were things as far beyond her as a foreign tongue she could never speak, no matter how she listened.

Not knowing how to comfort her, I took the only sensible step: I meant to get her in to dinner.

“Help me up,” I said. “You’re late for dinner, Rosanna—and I have come to fetch you in.”

“You, Mr. Betteredge!” she cried.

“They told Nancy to fetch you,” I said. “But I thought you might like your scolding better from me.”

Instead of helping me up, she slid her hand into mine and gave it a little squeeze. She tried hard not to cry again—and managed it, which I respected.

“You’re very kind, Mr. Betteredge,” she said. “I don’t want any dinner today—let me bide a little longer here.”

“What makes you like to be here?” I asked. “What is it that brings you everlastingly to this miserable place?”

“Something draws me to it,” she said, tracing little shapes with her finger in the sand as if she were writing a language only she could read. “I try to keep away, and I can’t. Sometimes”—she lowered her voice, as if frightened by her own thought—“sometimes, Mr. Betteredge, I think my grave is waiting for me here.”

“There’s roast mutton and suet pudding waiting for you,” I said. “Go in to dinner directly. This is what comes of thinking on an empty stomach!”

I spoke sharp, for her sake. At my time of life, to hear a young woman of five-and-twenty talk of her end sets one against it like vinegar against milk.

She scarcely seemed to hear. She put her hand on my shoulder and kept me where I sat.

“I think the place has laid a spell on me,” she said. “I dream of it night after night; I think of it when I sit stitching at my work. You know I am grateful, Mr. Betteredge—you know I try to deserve your kindness, and my lady’s confidence. But I wonder sometimes whether the life here is too quiet and too good for such a woman as I am, after all I have gone through. It’s more lonely to be among the other servants, knowing I am not what they are, than it is to be here. My lady doesn’t know—the matron doesn’t know—what a dreadful reproach honest people are, just by being honest, to a woman like me.”

She stopped, swallowed, and went on with a quick little pleading that cut me. “Don’t scold me—there’s a dear good man. I do my work, don’t I? Please not to tell my lady I am discontented—I am not. My mind’s unquiet sometimes, that’s all.”

Then, as if the sand itself had spoken into her ear, she snatched her hand away and pointed down.

“Look!” she said. “Isn’t it wonderful? isn’t it terrible? I have seen it dozens of times, and it’s always as new to me as if I had never seen it before!”

The tide was on the turn. The broad brown face of the quicksand began to shiver. It heaved slowly, then dimpled and quivered all over—like a great living thing breathing under a blanket. There was no crash of surf here, no cheerful slap of water on pebbles—only that muffled, dreadful stirring, and the long far sound of the sea beyond the bank, as steady as a drum heard from a distance.

“Do you know what it looks like to me?” Rosanna said, catching my shoulder again, her fingers tight. “It looks as if it had hundreds of suffocating people under it—all struggling to get up, and all sinking lower and lower in the dreadful deeps! Throw a stone in, Mr. Betteredge! Throw a stone in—and let’s see the sand suck it down!”

Unwholesome talk. A hungry body and an unquiet mind making a meal of horrors. I was just about to answer her—as sharp as need be—when my words were snapped short by a shout from among the sandhills.

“Betteredge!” cried a voice. “Where are you?”

“Here!” I shouted back, without knowing who it was.

Rosanna sprang to her feet and stared toward the sound. I was thinking to get on my own legs next, when the girl’s face changed so suddenly it startled me more than the quicksand. A beautiful red rushed into her cheeks—colour I had never seen in them before. She brightened all over with a speechless, breathless surprise.

“Who is it?” I asked.

She gave my own question back to me, softly, more to herself than to me. “Oh—who is it?”

I twisted round on the sand and looked.

Coming out upon us from among the hills was a bright-eyed young gentleman in a beautiful fawn-coloured suit, gloves and hat to match, a rose in his button-hole, and a smile on his face that might have set the Shivering Sand itself smiling if anything could. Before I could right myself, he dropped down by me on the sand, flung an arm round my neck in the foreign fashion, and hugged me till the breath went out of my body.

“Dear old Betteredge!” he cried. “I owe you seven-and-sixpence. Now do you know who I am?”

Lord bless us and save us—four good hours before we expected him, there was Mr. Franklin Blake.

Before I could get a word out, I saw him—surprised a little, as it seemed—lift his eyes from me to Rosanna. I followed his look.

She was blushing deeper than ever, as if his glance had touched a secret place in her; and then, in confusion quite beyond my understanding, she turned and went off without a curtsey to the gentleman and without a word to me—away among the sandhills, her little grey cloak fluttering like a scrap of cloud.

Very unlike her. A civiller, better-behaved servant you never met with.

“That’s an odd girl,” says Mr. Franklin. “I wonder what she sees in me to surprise her?”

“I suppose, sir,” I answered—drolling on his Continental education—“it’s the varnish from foreign parts.”

I set down his careless question and my foolish answer for the comfort of all stupid people. Neither Mr. Franklin, with his wonderful foreign training, nor I, with my age and experience and mother-wit, had the ghost of an idea what Rosanna Spearman’s strange behaviour meant. She was out of our minds before we had seen the last flutter of her cloak.

And what of that? you will ask.

Read on, as patiently as you can—and perhaps you may be as sorry for Rosanna Spearman as I was, when the truth came out.
