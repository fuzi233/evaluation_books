I owe a debt of gratitude to my departed parents, whose habits of order and regularity were drilled into me at a tender age. In those happier days, I was taught to maintain my hair in a perpetual state of tidiness and to fold every garment with military precision upon a specific chair at the foot of the bed before retiring. This ritual was invariably preceded by an entry in my journal, recording the day's events, and followed by the recitation of the "Evening Hymn." Thus, the hymn was succeeded by the sweet slumber of childhood.

Alas, in later life, the Hymn has yielded to sorrowful meditation, and the peaceful sleep of youth has been traded for the broken, troubled dreams of a burdened conscience. Yet, I have retained the habit of folding my clothes and keeping my journal. The former preserves a link to my pre-disgrace childhood; the latter, originally intended to curb the inherited fallibility of human nature, has proven unexpectedly vital to my modest ambitions. It has enabled me to serve the whims of a wealthy relative, specifically Mr. Franklin Blake, into whose family I was brought by the marriage of my late uncle.

For some time, I have been severed from the news of my kin, often forgotten when isolated and destitute. Now, residing in a small town in Brittany—a sort of Patmos amidst the roaring waves of Catholic Europe—I live with a select circle of serious English friends, enjoying the blessings of a Protestant clergyman and affordable provisions. Into this seclusion, a letter from England has finally drifted. Mr. Franklin Blake, my affluent kinsman, remembers me, albeit with a desire that borders on the mercenary. He writes without disguise, seeking my aid in stirring up the scandal surrounding the Moonstone. He wishes me to recount the events I witnessed during my visit to Aunt Verinder's house in London. A pecuniary reward is offered, devoid of sentimentality typical of the wealthy. I am to reopen wounds barely healed by time and relive intense pain, only to be compensated by a fresh laceration in the form of a banker's draft. My nature is frail; it required a strenuous internal battle for Christian humility to subdue sinful pride and accept the check.

Were it not for my journal, I doubt if I could have honestly earned this sum. Without it, I would be a mere laborer insulted by Mr. Blake. With it, I am deemed worthy of my wage. Every detail of my visit to Aunt Verinder was recorded daily, thanks to my early training. Nothing, however trivial, has been omitted. While I hold a sacred reverence for truth, I also possess a profound respect for persons. It will undoubtedly be easy for Mr. Blake to edit any passages that fail to flatter the central figure of this narrative. He has purchased my time, but his wealth cannot buy my conscience.

According to my journal, I was inadvertently passing Aunt Verinder's residence in Montagu Square on Monday, the 3rd of July, 1848. Observing the shutters raised and the blinds drawn up, I deemed it a courteous duty to knock and inquire. The individual who answered informed me that my aunt and her daughter (whom I hesitate to term my cousin) had arrived from the countryside a week prior and intended to stay in London. I dispatched a message declining to disturb them, merely asking if I could render any service.

The messenger, displaying an insolent silence, accepted my note and left me standing in the hall. She is the daughter of an elderly pagan man named Betteredge, who has been tolerated in my aunt's household far too long. Sitting in the hall awaiting a response, I selected a religious tract from my bag, one seemingly divinely suited to the recipient. The hall was soiled, and the chair uncomfortably hard, yet the conviction of returning good for evil elevated me above such minor inconveniences. The tract, titled "A Word With You On Your Cap-Ribbons," was part of a series addressing young women on the sinfulness of fashion, written in a devoutly familiar style.

"My lady is much obliged, and requests that you join us for lunch at two o'clock tomorrow."

I shall omit the manner in which this message was delivered and the terrifying audacity of her gaze. I thanked this young castaway and, adopting a tone of Christian benevolence, asked, "Will you accept a tract?"

She glanced at the title. "Is it authored by a man or a woman, Miss? If by a woman, I prefer not to read it. If by a man, I assure him he knows nothing of the matter." She returned the tract and opened the door. Having to sow the seed somehow, I waited until the door closed upon me before slipping the pamphlet into the letterbox. After dropping another through the area railings, I felt a slight alleviation of the heavy responsibility I bore toward others.

That evening, the Select Committee of the Mothers'-Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society convened. The aim of this virtuous charity, as understood by all serious souls, is to redeem unredeemed fathers' trousers from the pawnbroker and prevent their repossession by the irrepentant parent, thereby tailoring them to fit the innocent son. I was a member of the select committee at that time, mentioning the society here because my cherished and estimable friend, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, was associated with our work of moral and material utility. I had anticipated seeing him in the boardroom that Monday evening, intending to inform him of Aunt Verinder's arrival in London. To my profound dismay, he did not appear. Upon expressing my surprise at his absence, the sisters of the Committee collectively looked up from the trousers piled before them—we faced a significant backlog—and asked in wonder if I had not heard the news. Acknowledging my ignorance, I learned for the first time of an event that serves, in effect, as the genesis of this narrative. Two gentlemen, occupying vastly different strata of society, had become victims of an outrage that had sent a shockwave through London. One was Mr. Septimus Luker of Lambeth; the other, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Living in my current isolation, I lack the means to introduce the newspaper accounts of this outrage directly into my narrative. Furthermore, at the time, I was deprived of the inestimable benefit of hearing the events recounted through the fervid eloquence of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite himself. All I can do is state the facts as they were conveyed to me that Monday evening, adhering to the method I was taught from infancy when folding my clothes: everything neatly arranged, everything in its proper place. These lines are penned by a poor, weak woman. Will anyone be cruel enough to demand more?

The date, thanks to my dear parents' insistence on precision, was Friday, June 30th, 1848.

On that fateful morning, our talented Mr. Godfrey was cashing a cheque at a banking house in Lombard Street. The name of the firm is accidentally smudged in my diary, and my unwavering devotion to truth prevents me from guessing in matters of such delicacy. Fortunately, the name of the firm is immaterial. What matters is a circumstance that arose when Mr. Godfrey concluded his business. Upon reaching the exit, he encountered a gentleman—a complete stranger—who was departing the office simultaneously. A brief contest of politeness ensued regarding who should yield first to pass through the door. The stranger insisted that Mr. Godfrey precede him; Mr. Godfrey offered a few civil remarks, they bowed, and parted ways onto the street.

Superficial and thoughtless observers might dismiss this as a trite, absurdly detailed anecdote. Oh, my young friends and fellow-sinners! Beware of presuming to exercise your limited carnal reason. Be morally orderly. Let your faith be as spotless as your stockings, and your stockings as pure as your faith, both ready for instant donning!

I beg a thousand apologies. I have unconsciously slipped back into my Sunday school persona. Entirely inappropriate for such a record. Let me strive to be worldly; let me assert that trifles, in this instance as in countless others, precipitated terrible consequences. Premising that the polite stranger was indeed Mr. Luker of Lambeth, let us follow Mr. Godfrey home to his residence at Kilburn.

He found waiting in the hall a poorly clad, yet delicate and strikingly handsome little boy. The boy handed him a letter, briefly noting that he had been entrusted with it by an old lady he did not know, who had given no instructions to await a reply. Such occurrences were not uncommon in Mr. Godfrey's extensive experience as a promoter of public charities. He dismissed the boy and opened the letter.

The handwriting was utterly unfamiliar. It requested his attendance, within an hour, at a house in Northumberland Street, Strand, a location he had never visited before.
