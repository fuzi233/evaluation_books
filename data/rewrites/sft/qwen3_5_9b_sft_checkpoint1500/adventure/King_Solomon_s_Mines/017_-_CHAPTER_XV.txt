When the dust of battle finally settled, Sir Henry and Good were dragged into the hut of Twala, where I followed, joining them in their state of utter exhaustion. We were a sorry trio; the exertion and blood loss had drained us dry, and I, usually a man of iron constitution, felt nothing but misery. My old scar, the souvenir of a lion's claw, flared with renewed pain, and the blow to my head from the morning's chaos throbbed relentlessly. Lying there, amidst the carnage, the only solace we possessed was the grim realization that we were lucky enough to feel so wretched rather than lying stiff and silent among the twelve thousand brave souls who had fallen that night.

With the aid of Foulata—the beautiful captive who had become our devoted handmaiden since we spared her life—and particularly that of Good, we managed to remove the chain shirts that had miraculously kept us alive. Beneath the steel links, the flesh was a mess of terrible contusions. The armor had stopped the blades, but it had not prevented the brutal bruising. A remedy arrived in the form of pounded green leaves, fragrant and soothing, which Foulata applied as a plaster, bringing us fleeting relief.

Yet, these bruises paled in comparison to the gaping wounds that truly threatened our lives. Good's "beautiful white leg" bore a hole right through the fleshy part, from which he had bled profusely. Sir Henry, battered and bleeding, sported a deep slash across his jaw, dealt by Twala's battle axe. Fortunately, Good possessed a small box of medical supplies and, despite the dim, flickering light of a primitive Kukuana lamp, proved to be a capable surgeon. With steady hands, he cleansed and stitched Sir Henry's jaw, then tended to his own leg, applying an antiseptic ointment before bandaging both with the remnants of a pocket-handkerchief.

Meanwhile, Foulata brewed a strong broth, for we were far too weary to attempt solid food. Swallowing it greedily, we collapsed onto piles of magnificent *karrosses*—fur rugs scattered across the dead king's great hut. It was a cruel twist of fate, laden with irony, that Sir Henry, the man who had slain Twala, slept that night on the very couch where the tyrant had lain, wrapped in Twala's own particular rug.

Though I say "slept," rest was a distant dream. The air itself seemed thick with sorrow, filled with the wailing of women whose husbands, sons, and brothers had perished. From every corner of the hut drifted the mournful cries for those who would never return. It was heart-rending to lie there and listen, a sonic testament to the horrors unleashed by human ambition. Over twelve thousand men had been destroyed, nearly a fifth of the entire Kukuana army. Towards midnight, however, the endless weeping subsided, leaving only intervals of silence broken by a long, piercing howl emanating from a hut directly behind ours. Later, I learned this terrible keening voice belonged to Gagool, mourning the dead king.

Afterward, I secured only fitful slumber, waking repeatedly with a jolt, reliving the terrors of the last twenty-four hours. In flashes, I saw the warrior I had felled charging down the mountain; I found myself back in the glorious ring of Greys, making our immortal stand upon the mound; and I saw Twala's plumed, gory head rolling past my feet, jaws agape with rage.

Finally, dawn broke, though it offered no comfort. My companions had slept no better than I. Good was burning with a high fever, growing light-headed until he began to spit blood—a sure sign of internal injury from the spear thrust that had pierced his leg armor. Sir Henry, despite his facial wound, seemed surprisingly fresh, though the pain made eating difficult and laughter impossible; he was so sore and stiff he could barely stir.

Around eight in the morning, Infadoos visited us. The old warrior looked remarkably unharmed, despite his exertions, and he expressed profound grief for Good's condition, shaking our hands warmly. I noted, however, that he addressed Sir Henry with a reverence bordering on worship. Indeed, the soldiers spoke of the Great Englishman as a supernatural being, a figure of myth who had slain Twala, the strongest warrior in the land, in single combat. That stroke had become proverbial in Kukuanaland, forever known as "Incubu's blow."

Infadoos reported that Twala's regiments had submitted to Ignosi, and similar surrenders were trickling in from outlying chiefs. With Twala dead and his only legitimate son, Scragga, eliminated, there remained no rival claimant to the throne. The kingdom was secure, at least for the moment. I remarked that Ignosi had ascended to power through blood. The old chief shrugged. "Yes," he answered. "But the Kukuana people can only be kept cool by letting their blood flow sometimes. Many are killed, indeed, but the women remain, and others must soon grow up to take the places of the fallen. After this, the land will be quiet for a while."

Later that morning, Ignosi himself paid us a visit. The royal diadem now rested upon his brow, and an obsequious guard followed in his wake. As I contemplated him, I could not help but recall the tall Zulu who had sought employment at Durban mere months prior, reflecting on the dizzying revolutions of fortune.

"Hail, O king!" I said, rising.

"Yes, Macumazahn," he replied readily. "King at last, by the might of your three right hands."

He assured me that affairs were proceeding smoothly and that he hoped to host a great feast within two weeks to display himself to the people. When I asked what he intended to do with Gagool, his expression hardened.

"She is the evil genius of the land," he declared. "I shall kill her, and all the witch-doctors with her! She has lived so long that none can remember when she was not ancient, and she alone has trained the witch-hunters, making the land wicked in the sight of heaven."

"Yet she knows much," I countered. "It is easier to destroy knowledge, Ignosi, than to gather it."

"That is so," he said thoughtfully. "She, and she only, knows the secret of the 'Three Witches' yonder, where the great road leads to the burial caves of the kings and where the Silent Ones sit."

"Yes, and the diamonds are there," I pressed. "Do not forget your promise, Ignosi. You must lead us to the mines, even if you must spare Gagool alive to guide us."

"I will not forget, Macumazahn, and I will ponder what you say."

Once Ignosi departed, I checked on Good, finding him in a state of near-delirium. The fever from his wound had taken a firm hold, compounded by the internal trauma. For several days, his condition was critical. Had it not been for Foulata's indefatigable nursing, I believed he would have died.

Women are women, wherever they roam, regardless of color. Yet, watching this dusky beauty tend to the fevered man with the swift, gentle efficiency of a trained nurse seemed almost miraculous. For the first couple of nights, I and Sir Henry attempted to assist, but Foulata bore our interference with impatient disdain. She insisted that our movements disturbed the patient, and eventually, she prevailed, demanding we leave him to her care. Day and night, she sat by his side, administering a native cooling drink made of milk infused with tulip bulb juice, and shielding him from the swarming flies. I often saw the scene now in memory: Good tossing restlessly, his features gaunt, his eyes wide and luminous as he jabbered nonsense; and beside him, seated on the earth with her back against the wall, the soft-eyed, shapely Kukuana beauty. Her face, worn thin by long vigils, was illuminated by a look of infinite compassion—or perhaps something deeper.

For two days, we feared he would die, moving about with heavy hearts. Only Foulata refused to accept this doom.

"He will live," she insisted.

For over three hundred yards surrounding Twala's chief hut, there was a hushed silence. By the king's orders, everyone living in the habitations behind it, save for Sir Henry and myself, had been removed to ensure no noise reached the sick man's ears. On the fifth night of his illness, as was my custom, I slipped across to check on him before retiring.

I entered the hut cautiously. A lamp on the floor revealed Good lying perfectly still, no longer tossing.

"So it had come at last!" I thought, a sob rising in the bitterness of my heart.

"Hush—h—h!" a whisper came from the dark shadow behind Good's head.

Creeping closer, I saw the truth: he was not dead, but sleeping soundly. Clamped tightly in his pale, thin hand were Foulata's slender fingers. The crisis had passed. He slept for eighteen hours, and I hesitate to mention it, for fear of disbelief, but during the entire time, this devoted girl sat motionless by his side, terrified that if she shifted and withdrew her hand, it might wake him.
