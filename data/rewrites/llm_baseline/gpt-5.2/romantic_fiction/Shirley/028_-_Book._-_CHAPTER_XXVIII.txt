Shirley must have found Sir Philip’s talk agreeable the night before; for in the morning she came down as if some hidden hearth had been newly kindled in her. The breakfast-room, with its sober light and its sober faces, could not smother that inward flame. She had a brisk word for every one, a smile ready before it was asked, and—rarer still—an invitation.

“Who will walk with me?” she demanded, pushing back her chair with decision. “Isabella—Gertrude—will you?”

So uncommon a summons from Miss Keeldar to her female cousins, that both Misses Sympson stared a moment, as if the offer were a dish set before them whose name they did not know. Their mamma, however, gave assent; bonnets were fetched; and the three went out together.

They were not three who naturally mixed and mellowed by being thrown into one pot. Miss Keeldar liked few ladies’ company, and had cordial pleasure in none but Mrs. Pryor and Caroline Helstone. She could be scrupulously civil, even kind; she could remember little attentions as a careful housekeeper remembers her stores; yet commonly she had little to say to her cousins, and what she said was of the plainest grain—weather, roads, neighbours, the small homely ledger of daily things. But this morning even those ordinary matters took savour from her spirit, as the simplest broth is transformed by a handful of herbs. A sparkle ran along her phrases; she made the damp lane and the dun wood seem almost jocund.

For the day itself gave no help. It was one of those pale, waning autumn mornings when the air hangs heavy as wet wool, when the woods smell of mouldering leaves and soaked bark, when every twig drops coldly on your sleeve, and the sky is a lid of lead. The path through the coppice was dark with moisture; the ground yielded underfoot with a soft sucking sound; there was no sun—only a dim, colourless light, and a steady hush broken now and then by the drip from bough to bough. Yet in Shirley’s heart lived a warmth like southern air; in her grey eye laughed a fervour that did not belong to Yorkshire.

On their way back, as Fieldhead came into view, Shirley was detained. Some direction had to be given to her foreman, John; so she let her cousins go on, while she turned aside to speak of work—of hedges, drains, cattle—things that root a family in land and make the year’s bread. Perhaps twenty minutes passed between their parting and her return. In that interval she did speak with John; and then she lingered in the lane at the gate, as if the wet smell of earth and the soft, far murmur of the moor-wind had something to say that she would not carry indoors.

A summons to luncheon called her in at last. She excused herself from the meal, and went upstairs.

“Is not Shirley coming?” Isabella asked, surprised. “She said she was hungry.”

An hour went by, and still she did not come down. One of the cousins, uneasy, went up to seek her. Shirley was found sitting at the foot of the bed, her head resting on her hand. The colour had left her cheek; she looked not merely thoughtful, but subdued—almost sad.

“You are not ill?” was asked.

“A little sick,” said Miss Keeldar.

She was altered; no one could deny it. Two hours earlier she had been all briskness; now she was pale and still, as if a sudden shadow had crossed her. Yet that shadow did not pass like a light summer cloud. When she joined them at dinner, she talked; she sat with them in the evening; when questioned again, she declared herself perfectly recovered. It had been a mere faintness—not worth thought. But those about her felt a difference, like a change in the air before rain.

The next day, and the week, and the fortnight after, the new cloud remained. A strange quietude settled on her look, her movements, her very voice. It was not so marked as to invite constant question; but it was there, and it would not disperse. To notice it annoyed her. At first she shrank; if pressed, she repelled with that peculiar hauteur which sat on her like armour.

“Was she ill?”

“I am not.”

“Did anything weigh on her mind? Had anything happened?”

She ridiculed the notion with scorn. What did they mean by “spirits”? She had none—black, blue, grey, or white—to be affected.

“But you are so altered.”

She flung back, peremptory, that she supposed she had a right to alter at her ease. She knew she was plainer; if it suited her to grow ugly, why should others fret?

“What is the cause?”

She requested to be let alone.

Then, as if angry with herself for having let her shadow be seen, she would make an effort to appear quite gay; and when she could not wholly succeed, brief self-spurning words broke from her when alone.

“Fool! coward!” she would mutter. “Poltroon! If you must tremble, tremble in secret. Quail where no eye sees you.”

“How dare you,” she would ask her own heart, “show weakness—betray imbecile anxieties? Shake them off. Rise above them. If you cannot, hide them.”

And to hide them she did her best. She became resolutely lively in company; and when she was weary of effort and compelled to let the mask slip, she sought solitude—not the shut-in solitude of a chamber (she refused to mope between four walls), but the wide, raw solitude outdoors, which she could pursue mounted on Zoë. She took rides that lasted half a day. Her uncle disapproved; but he dared not remonstrate. It was never pleasant to face Shirley’s anger when she was healthy and bright; now, with her face grown thin and her large eye hollow, there was something in the darkening of that face and the kindling of that eye which touched as well as alarmed.

To strangers who noticed her changed looks, she gave one answer only.

“I am perfectly well; I have not an ailment.”

And, in truth, health must have been in her to endure what she now endured. Wet or fair, calm or storm, daily she rode over Stilbro’ Moor, Tartar keeping pace beside her with his wolfish gallop, long and untiring. The very sound of them—the ring of hoof on stone, the soft thud on turf, the dog’s swift pads, the rush of wind in the heather—was a kind of fierce music, a counter-chime to the town’s harsher noises. Yet even that open-air harmony could not wholly cleanse the mind. The moor might give clean scents—peat, rain, crushed ling—but it could not always give clean peace.

Twice, thrice, the eyes of gossips—those eyes that are everywhere, in closet and on hill-top—noticed that instead of turning on Rushedge, the top ridge of Stilbro’ Moor, she rode forward all the way to the town. Scouts were not wanting to mark her destination there. It was ascertained she alighted at the door of one Mr. Pearson Hall, a solicitor, related to the vicar of Nunnely—an agent of the Keeldars for generations. The village, hungry for explanations, set its teeth into the matter. Some affirmed Miss Keeldar had been drawn into business speculations connected with Hollow’s Mill, that she had lost money and must mortgage land. Others said, with a sort of relish, that she was going to be married, and that settlements were being prepared.

In the schoolroom Mr. Moore sat with Henry Sympson. The tutor waited for a lesson which the pupil seemed to be preparing with unusual slowness.

“Henry, make haste. The afternoon is getting on.”

“Is it, sir?”

“Certainly. Are you nearly ready with that lesson?”

“No.”

“Not nearly ready?”

“I have not construed a line.”

Mr. Moore looked up. The boy’s tone was odd—flat, yet strained, as if he held something down by force.

“The task presents no difficulties, Henry; or if it does, bring them to me. We will work together.”

“Mr. Moore, I can do no work.”

“My boy, you are ill.”

“Sir, I am not worse in bodily health than usual—but my heart is full.”

“Shut the book. Come hither, Harry. Come to the fireside.”

Harry limped forward. His tutor placed him in a chair. The child’s lips quivered; his eyes brimmed. He laid his crutch on the floor, bent his head, and wept.

“This distress is not occasioned by physical pain, you say? You have a grief. Tell it me.”

“Sir, I have such a grief as I never had before. I wish it could be relieved somehow; I can hardly bear it.”

“Who knows but that talking it over may relieve it? What is the cause? Whom does it concern?”

“The cause, sir, is Shirley; it concerns Shirley.”

“Does it? You think her changed?”

“All who know her think her changed—you too, Mr. Moore.”

“Not seriously—no. I see no alteration but such as a favourable turn might repair in a few weeks; besides, her own word must go for something: she says she is well.”

“There it is, sir. So long as she maintained she was well, I believed her. When I was sad out of her sight, I soon recovered spirits in her presence. Now—”

“Well, Harry, now. Has she said anything to you? You and she were together in the garden two hours this morning. I saw her talking and you listening. If Miss Keeldar has said she is ill and enjoined you to keep her secret, do not obey her. For her life’s sake, avow everything. Speak, my boy.”

“She say she is ill! I believe, sir, if she were dying, she would smile and aver, ‘Nothing ails me.’”

“What have you learned then? What new circumstance?”

“I have learned that she has just made her will.”

“Made her will?”

Both were silent a moment. The rain whispered at the panes; the house held its breath.

“She told you that?” asked Moore at length.

“She told me quite cheerfully—not as ominous, which I felt it to be. She said I was the only person besides her solicitor, Pearson Hall, and Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, who knew anything about it; and to me, she said, she wished specially to explain its provisions.”

“Go on, Harry.”

“‘Because,’ she said—looking down on me with her beautiful eyes—oh! they are beautiful, Mr. Moore! I love them! I love her! She is my star! Heaven must not claim her! She is lovely in this world, and fitted for this world. Shirley is not an angel; she is a woman, and she shall live with men. Seraphs shall not have her! Mr. Moore, if one of the sons of God, with wings wide and bright as the sky—blue and sounding as the sea—having seen she was fair, descended to claim her, his claim should be withstood—by me—boy and cripple as I am.’”

“Henry Sympson, go on, when I tell you.”

“‘Because,’ she said, ‘if I made no will, and died before you, Harry, all my property would go to you; and I do not intend it should be so, though your father would like it. But you will have his whole estate, which is large—larger than Fieldhead. Your sisters will have nothing; so I have left them some money, though I do not love them, both together, half so much as I love one lock of your fair hair.’ And she called me her darling, and let me kiss her. She said she had left Caroline Helstone some money too; that this manor house, with its furniture and books, she had bequeathed to me, because she would not take the old family place from her own blood; and all the rest—about twelve thousand pounds, exclusive of the legacies—she had willed not to me, since I was already rich, but to a good man, who would make the best use of it any human being could—a man, she said, gentle and brave, strong and merciful; a man who might not profess to be pious, but she knew he had the secret of religion pure and undefiled before God. The spirit of love and peace was with him; he visited the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and kept himself unspotted from the world. Then she asked, ‘Do you approve what I have done, Harry?’ I could not answer. My tears choked me, as they do now.”

Mr. Moore waited till the boy’s sobs lessened.

“What else did she say?” he demanded.

“When I had signified my consent, she told me I was generous, and she was proud of me. ‘And now,’ she added, ‘in case anything should happen, you will know what to say to Malice when she comes whispering hard things in your ear—insinuating Shirley has wronged you, that she did not love you. You will know that I did love you, Harry; that no sister could have loved you better—my own treasure.’ Mr. Moore, when I remember her voice and look, my heart beats as if it would break its strings. She may go to heaven before me—if God commands it, she must; but the rest of my life—and my life will not be long, I am glad of that now—shall be a straight, quick, thoughtful journey in the path her step has pressed. I thought to enter the vault of the Keeldars before her. Should it be otherwise, lay my coffin by Shirley’s side.”

Moore answered with a weighty calm that offered strange contrast to the boy’s fervour.

“You are wrong, both of you—you harm each other. If youth once falls under a shadowy terror, it imagines there will never be full sunlight again; its first calamity it fancies will last a lifetime. What more did she say? Anything more?”

“We settled one or two family points between ourselves.”

“I should rather like to know what—”

“But, Mr. Moore, you smile. I could not smile to see Shirley in such a mood.”

“My boy, I am neither nervous, nor poetic, nor inexperienced. I see things as they are; you don’t as yet. Tell me these family points.”

“Only, sir, she asked whether I considered myself most a Keeldar or a Sympson; and I answered I was Keeldar to the core of the heart and to the marrow of the bones. She said she was glad; for, besides her, I was the only Keeldar left in England. And then we agreed—”

“Well?”

“Well, sir, that if I lived to inherit my father’s estate and her house, I was to take the name of Keeldar, and make Fieldhead my residence. Henry Shirley Keeldar I said I would be called; and I will. Her name and her manor house are ages old, and Sympson and Sympson Grove are of yesterday.”

“Come, you are neither of you going to heaven yet. I have the best hopes of you both, with your proud distinctions—a pair of half-fledged eaglets. Now, what is your inference from all you have told me? Put it into words.”

“That Shirley thinks she is going to die.”

“She referred to her health?”

“Not once; but I assure you she is wasting. Her hands are grown quite thin, and so is her cheek.”

“Does she ever complain to your mother or sisters?”

“Never. She laughs at them when they question her. Mr. Moore, she is strange—so fair and girlish—not a man-like woman at all, not an Amazon—yet lifting her head above both help and sympathy.”

“Do you know where she is now, Henry? Is she in the house, or riding out?”

“Surely not out, sir. It rains fast.”

“True; which is no guarantee she is not at this moment cantering over Rushedge. Of late she has never permitted weather to hinder her rides.”

“You remember last Wednesday, Mr. Moore—so wet and stormy she would not permit Zoë to be saddled? Yet the blast she thought too tempestuous for her mare she faced on foot; she walked nearly as far as Nunnely. I asked if she was not afraid of taking cold. ‘Not I,’ she said. ‘It would be too much good luck for me. I don’t know, Harry, but the best thing that could happen to me would be to take a good cold and fever, and so pass off like other Christians.’ She is reckless, sir.”

“Reckless indeed! Go and find where she is; and if you can speak to her without attracting attention, request her to come here a minute.”

“Yes, sir.”

He snatched his crutch and started.

“Harry!”

He returned.

“Do not deliver the message formally. Word it as, in former days, you would have worded an ordinary summons to the schoolroom.”

“I see, sir. She will be more likely to obey.”

“And, Harry—”

“Sir?”

“I will call you when I want you. Till then, you are dispensed from lessons.”

He departed. Mr. Moore, left alone, rose from his desk.

“I can be very cool and supercilious with Henry,” he said to himself. “I can make light of his apprehensions, and look down, du haut de ma grandeur, on his youthful ardour. To him I can speak as if they were both children. Let me see if I can keep up the same rôle with her.

“I have known the moment when I seemed about to forget it—when Confusion and Submission seemed about to crush me with their soft tyranny—when my tongue faltered, and I have almost let the mantle drop, and stood in her presence not master—no—but something else. I trust I shall never so play the fool. It is well for a Sir Philip Nunnely to redden when he meets her eye: he may indulge submission; he may, without disgrace, suffer his hand to tremble when it touches hers. But if one of her farmers were to show himself susceptible and sentimental, he would merely prove his need of a strait waistcoat. So far I have done well. She has sat near me, and I have not shaken—more than my desk. I have encountered her looks and smiles like—why, like a tutor, as I am. Her hand I never yet touched; never underwent that test.

“Her farmer or her footman I am not—no serf nor servant of hers have I ever been; but I am poor, and it behoves me to look to my self-respect—not to compromise an inch of it. What did she mean by that allusion to cold people who petrify flesh to marble? It pleased me—I hardly know why. I would not permit myself to inquire. I never indulge in scrutiny either of her language or countenance; for if I did, I should sometimes forget common sense and believe in romance. A strange, secret ecstasy steals through my veins at moments. I’ll not encourage it; I’ll not remember it. I am resolved, as long as may be, to retain the right to say with Paul, ‘I am not mad, but speak forth the words of truth and soberness.’”

He paused, listening.

“Will she come, or will she not come? How will she take the message—naïvely or disdainfully? Like a child or like a queen? Both characters are in her nature.

“If she comes, what shall I say? How account, firstly, for the freedom of the request? Shall I apologize? I could in all humility; but would an apology place us where we ought to stand? I must keep up the professor—otherwise— I hear a door.”

He waited. Many minutes passed.

“She will refuse me. Henry is entreating; she declines. My petition is presumption in her eyes. Let her only come, I can teach her to the contrary. I would rather she were perverse; it will steel me. I prefer her cuirassed in pride, armed with a taunt. Her scorn startles me from dreams; it makes me stand up myself. A sarcasm from her eyes or lips puts strength into every nerve and sinew I have. Some step approaches—and not Henry’s.”

The door opened. Miss Keeldar came in.

The message, it seemed, had found her with her needle; she brought her work in her hand as if she would not be dislodged from the domestic posture she had chosen. That day she had not been out; she had passed it quietly. She wore a neat indoor dress and a silk apron. This was no Thalestris from the fields; this was a household figure from the fireside, with thread drawn through cloth and a quiet hand to guide it. Mr. Moore had her at advantage; he should have seized it at once and spoken in rigid accents. Perhaps he would have done so had she looked saucy. But never had her air shown less of crânerie. A soft, youthful shyness lowered her eyelid; a faint colour mantled on her cheek. The tutor stood silent.

She stopped between the door and his desk.

“Did you want me, sir?” she asked.

“I ventured, Miss Keeldar, to send for you—that is, to ask an interview of a few minutes.”

She waited, plying her needle with small exact stitches, as if the slight resistance of the fabric steadied her.

“Well, sir,” she said at last, not lifting her eyes, “what about?”

“Be seated first. The subject I would broach is one of some moment. Perhaps I have hardly a right to approach it. It is possible I ought to frame an apology; it is possible no apology can excuse me. The liberty I have taken arises from a conversation with Henry. The boy is unhappy about your health; all your friends are unhappy on that subject. It is of your health I would speak.”

“I am quite well,” she returned briefly.

“Yet changed.”

“That matters to none but myself. We all change.”

“Will you sit down? Formerly, Miss Keeldar, I had some influence with you: have I any now? May I feel that what I am saying is not accounted positive presumption?”

“Let me read some French, Mr. Moore, or I will even take a spell at the Latin grammar, and let us proclaim a truce to all sanitary discussions.”

“No, no. It is time there were discussions.”

“Discuss away, then, but do not choose me for your text. I am a healthy subject.”

“Do you not think it wrong to affirm and reaffirm what is substantially untrue?”

“I say I am well. I have neither cough, pain, nor fever.”

“Is there no equivocation in that assertion? Is it the direct truth?”

“The direct truth.”

Louis Moore looked at her earnestly.

“I can myself trace no indications of actual disease,” he said. “But why, then, are you altered?”

“Am I altered?”

“We will try. We will seek a proof.”

“How?”

“I ask, in the first place, do you sleep as you used to?”

“I do not; but it is not because I am ill.”

“Have you the appetite you once had?”

“No; but it is not because I am ill.”

“You remember this little ring fastened to my watch-chain? It was my mother’s, and is too small to pass the joint of my little finger. You have many a time sportively purloined it. It fitted your forefinger. Try now.”

She held out her hand. The ring slid on—and then, startlingly, dropped off the wasted finger as if the flesh that once filled it had melted away. Louis picked it up and reattached it to the chain. An uneasy flush rose on his brow.

Shirley said again, as if the words were a charm she forced herself to repeat, “It is not because I am ill.”

“Not only have you lost sleep, appetite, and flesh,” proceeded Moore, “but your spirits are always at ebb. Besides, there is a nervous alarm in your eye, a nervous disquiet in your manner. These peculiarities were not formerly yours.”

“Mr. Moore, we will pause here. You have exactly hit it. I am nervous. Now—talk of something else. What wet weather we have—steady, pouring rain!”

“You nervous? Yes; and if Miss Keeldar is nervous, it is not without a cause. Let me reach it. Let me look nearer. The ailment is not physical. I have suspected that. It came in one moment. I know the day. I noticed the change. Your pain is mental.”

“Not at all. It is nothing so dignified—merely nervous. Oh! dismiss the topic.”

“When it is exhausted; not till then. Nervous alarms should always be communicated, that they may be dissipated. I wish I had the gift of persuasion, and could incline you to speak willingly. I believe confession, in your case, would be half equivalent to cure.”

“No,” said Shirley abruptly. “I wish that were at all probable; but I am afraid it is not.”

She paused in her work. Now seated, she rested her elbow on the table and leaned her head on her hand. Mr. Moore, watching, felt he had gained at last some footing on a difficult path: her wish implied an admission; she could no longer affirm that nothing ailed her.

He allowed her minutes for reflection. Once his lips moved to speak, then he checked himself and prolonged the pause. Shirley lifted her eye to his. Had he betrayed injudicious emotion, she might have locked herself into silence; but his look was calm—strong—trustworthy, like a firm hand held out without flourish. Outside, the rain continued, a constant low sound that drowned sharper noises and made the room feel more sealed, more intimate, as if the world were listening elsewhere.

“I had better tell you than my aunt,” she said at last, “or than my cousins, or my uncle. They would all make such a bustle—and it is that very bustle I dread: the alarm, the flurry, the éclat. In short, I never liked to be the centre of a small domestic whirlpool. You can bear a little shock—eh?”

“A great one, if necessary.”

Not a muscle moved; yet his heart beat fast, deep in his chest. What would she confess? Had something been done that could not be undone?

“Had I thought it right to go to you,” she continued, “I would never have made a secret of the matter one moment. I would have told you at once, and asked advice.”

“Why was it not right to come to me?”
