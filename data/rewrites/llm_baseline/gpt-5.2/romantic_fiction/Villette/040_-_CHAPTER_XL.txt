The morning after that strange Midsummer night came in with a brightness so ordinary it seemed, by its very innocence, to mock the ferment within doors. The sun rose as if to preside over nothing more momentous than the ripening of currants and the steady swelling of peas in their pods. Heat already lived in the brickwork; it would soon draw from it that baked, mineral scent which mingles, in enclosed gardens, with the bruised sweetness of herbs when you brush past them. In the Rue Fossette, however, few noses were lifted to the air, and few ears attended to the sparrows and the thin, contented hiss of leaves. The house listened for other sounds—footsteps where none came, a door that had not creaked, a voice that did not answer when called.

At matins there was a gap—an absence seated like a cold plate at a family table. At breakfast, one coffee-cup remained untouched; the steam from the others rose, thinned, and vanished, and that small fading seemed a sign. When the beds were made, a bolster was found laid lengthwise, capped and night-gowned—an effigy stuffed with linen, a jest or a stratagem left in place of a girl. And when the music-mistress arrived, punctual as the striking of a clock, her pupil—so accomplished, so confident of her own claims on the world—failed, for once, to present herself.

Ginevra Fanshawe had disappeared.

The pensionnat was searched as a kitchen is searched when something is missing that must be found before the dish can be served: drawers pulled open, cupboards sounded, corners hunted, the very air suspected. Up-stairs and down, through dormitory, class-room, corridor—no trace; not so much as a scrap of paper; not a pin dropped in haste. The house’s usual soundscape—bells, lessons, the measured step of routine—was broken into sharp consultations, sudden scurries, the hiss of whispers, and, beneath them, Madame Beck’s breathing, altered from its habitual composure to a quick restraint that betrayed fear.

Never had I seen her so pale. Here was not merely a truant: here was a blow to her interest, her reputation, her command. She demanded to know by what outlet the fugitive had fled. Doors were examined, bolts tried, windows tested; no casement was found unfastened, no pane broken. The building presented itself like a sealed pantry—close, locked, irreproachable. Yet something living had been taken out of it in the night.

And still, for me, a particular recollection persisted with the steadiness of a flavor that will not be masked once the palate has recognized it. To facilitate a certain enterprise, a certain great door had been drawn softly to its place—closed, yes, but neither bolted nor made secure. Along with this came back the memory of a thundering carriage-and-pair encountered, and that puzzling signal of a waved handkerchief. From these facts, and from others kept to myself, I could draw but one inference.

It was elopement.

I held my certainty for a time, because certainty, when it carries scandal in its hands, is not to be flung down like a loaf on a bare board. Yet Madame Beck’s dismay deepened; and at length I spoke. I alluded to M. de Hamal’s suit; and as I expected, she was perfectly aware of that affair. She had discussed it long since with Mrs. Cholmondeley, and, with that admirable art of shifting weight from her own shoulders, had contrived to lay her responsibility in the business upon that lady.

Now she had recourse to Mrs. Cholmondeley and to M. de Bassompierre. The Hôtel Crécy, we found, was already alive with the news. Ginevra had written to her cousin Paulina—vaguely, coquettishly, yet with enough meaning to set a family ear pricked—to signify hymeneal intentions. Communications had also been received from the de Hamal side. M. de Bassompierre took up the track with the tenacity of a man who cannot endure to be outwitted in his own house; he followed, he hurried, he questioned. He overtook them too late.

In the course of the week the post brought me a note. It explained more than one point—explained, with that airy insolence which is Ginevra’s way of making mischief seem like a dance.

“DEAR OLD TIM (short for Timon),—

“I am off you see—gone like a shot. Alfred and I intended to be married in this way almost from the first; we never meant to be spliced in the humdrum way of other people; Alfred has too much spirit for that, and so have I—Dieu merci!

“Do you know, Alfred, who used to call you ‘the dragon,’ has seen so much of you during the last few months, that he begins to feel quite friendly towards you. He hopes you won’t miss him now that he has gone; he begs to apologize for any little trouble he may have given you. He is afraid he rather inconvenienced you once when he came upon you in the grenier, just as you were reading a letter seemingly of the most special interest; but he could not resist the temptation to give you a start, you appeared so wonderfully taken up with your correspondent. En revanche, he says you once frightened him by rushing in for a dress or a shawl, or some other chiffon, at the moment when he had struck a light, and was going to take a quiet whiff of his cigar, while waiting for me.

“Do you begin to comprehend by this time that M. le Comte de Hamal was the nun of the attic, and that he came to see your humble servant? I will tell you how he managed it. You know he has the entrée of the Athénée, where two or three of his nephews, the sons of his eldest sister, Madame de Melcy, are students. You know the court of the Athénée is on the other side of the high wall bounding your walk, the allée défendue. Alfred can climb as well as he can dance or fence: his amusement was to make the escalade of our pensionnat by mounting, first the wall; then—by the aid of that high tree overspreading the grand berceau, and resting some of its boughs on the roof of the lower buildings of our premises—he managed to scale the first classe and the grand salle. One night, by the way, he fell out of this tree, tore down some of the branches, nearly broke his own neck, and after all, in running away, got a terrible fright, and was nearly caught by two people, Madame Beck and M. Emanuel, he thinks, walking in the alley. From the grande salle the ascent is not difficult to the highest block of building, finishing in the great garret. The skylight, you know, is, day and night, left half open for air; by the skylight he entered. Nearly a year ago I chanced to tell him our legend of the nun; that suggested his romantic idea of the spectral disguise, which I think you must allow he has very cleverly carried out.

“But for the nun’s black gown and white veil, he would have been caught again and again both by you and that tiger-Jesuit, M. Paul. He thinks you both capital ghost-seers, and very brave. What I wonder at is, rather your secretiveness than your courage. How could you endure the visitations of that long spectre, time after time, without crying out, telling everybody, and rousing the whole house and neighbourhood?

“Oh, and how did you like the nun as a bed-fellow? I dressed her up: didn’t I do it well? Did you shriek when you saw her: I should have gone mad; but then you have such nerves!—real iron and bend-leather! I believe you feel nothing. You haven’t the same sensitiveness that a person of my constitution has. You seem to me insensible both to pain and fear and grief. You are a real old Diogenes.

“Well, dear grandmother! and are you not mightily angry at my moonlight flitting and run away match? I assure you it is excellent fun, and I did it partly to spite that minx, Paulina, and that bear, Dr. John: to show them that, with all their airs, I could get married as well as they. M. de Bassompierre was at first in a strange fume with Alfred; he threatened a prosecution for ‘détournement de mineur,’ and I know not what; he was so abominably in earnest, that I found myself forced to do a little bit of the melodramatic—go down on my knees, sob, cry, drench three pocket-handkerchiefs. Of course, ‘mon oncle’ soon gave in; indeed, where was the use of making a fuss? I am married, and that’s all about it. He still says our marriage is not legal, because I am not of age, forsooth! As if that made any difference! I am just as much married as if I were a hundred. However, we are to be married again, and I am to have a trousseau, and Mrs. Cholmondeley is going to superintend it; and there are some hopes that M. de Bassompierre will give me a decent portion, which will be very convenient, as dear Alfred has nothing but his nobility, native and hereditary, and his pay. I only wish uncle would do things unconditionally, in a generous, gentleman-like fashion; he is so disagreeable as to make the dowry depend on Alfred’s giving his written promise that he will never touch cards or dice from the day it is paid down. They accuse my angel of a tendency to play: I don’t know anything about that, but I do know he is a dear, adorable creature.

“I cannot sufficiently extol the genius with which de Hamal managed our flight. How clever in him to select the night of the fête, when Madame (for he knows her habits), as he said, would infallibly be absent at the concert in the park. I suppose you must have gone with her. I watched you rise and leave the dormitory about eleven o’clock. How you returned alone, and on foot, I cannot conjecture. That surely was you we met in the narrow old Rue St. Jean? Did you see me wave my handkerchief from the carriage window?

“Adieu! Rejoice in my good luck: congratulate me on my supreme happiness, and believe me, dear cynic and misanthrope, yours, in the best of health and spirits,

“GINEVRA LAURA DE HAMAL, née FANSHAWE.

“P.S.—Remember, I am a countess now. Papa, mamma, and the girls at home, will be delighted to hear that. ‘My daughter the Countess!’ ‘My sister the Countess!’ Bravo! Sounds rather better than Mrs. John Bretton, hein?”

So ended her explanation: with titles on her tongue as naturally as sugar, and with that strange confidence that naming a thing—Countess, marriage, happiness—makes it secure against all after-tastes.

Those who delight in moral reckonings will expect, perhaps, that so light a creature should be brought in time to heavy expiation. Doubtless a share of suffering lay ready somewhere in her future, as it lies ready for all; yet, of what I knew, I can only say this.

I saw her towards the close of her honeymoon. She called on Madame Beck, and sent for me into the salon. She rushed into my arms laughing—warm with life, scented with the newness of her condition, bright as a confection dressed for a feast. Her curls were longer, her cheeks rosier than ever; her white bonnet and Flanders veil, her orange-flowers and bride’s dress, became her mightily; she was all airy whiteness and soft rustle, and her voice rang in the room like quick silver struck on glass—too clear, too restless to be called music, yet impossible to ignore. There are sounds that do not nourish; they stimulate like strong coffee taken too late.

“I have got my portion!” she cried at once. Ginevra ever clung to the substantial; there was a trading element in her composition, much as she professed to scorn the bourgeoise. “And uncle de Bassompierre is quite reconciled. I don’t mind his calling Alfred a ‘nincompoop’—that’s only his coarse Scotch breeding; and I believe Paulina envies me, and Dr. John is wild with jealousy—fit to blow his brains out—and I’m so happy! I really think I’ve hardly anything left to wish for—unless it be a carriage and an hotel, and, oh! I—must introduce you to mon mari. Alfred, come here!”

And Alfred appeared from the inner salon, where he had been talking with Madame Beck and receiving from her a mixture of congratulation and reprimand, as if she seasoned her approval with salt that it might keep. I was presented under my various names: the Dragon, Diogenes, Timon. The young Colonel was very polite. He offered me a neatly turned apology for the ghost-visits and the rest, ending with the declaration that “the best excuse for all his iniquities stood there,” pointing to his bride.

Then the bride sent him back to Madame Beck as one sends away a servant for another dish, and took possession of me. She proceeded, in her unrestrained spirits—girlish, giddy, willful—to overwhelm and almost suffocate me with her nonsense. She thrust forward her ring again and again; she called herself Madame la Comtesse de Hamal, and made me listen to the sound of it a score of times, as if repetition could hammer it into truth. I said little. I gave her only the crust and rind of my nature. It sufficed her; she expected nothing finer from me, and indeed seemed to relish my dryness as a condiment to her own froth. The more impassive and prosaic my mien, the more merrily she laughed, like someone delighted to hear a sharp utensil scrape a plate.

Soon after his marriage, M. de Hamal was persuaded to leave the army—the surest way, it was thought, of weaning him from certain associates and habits that cost more than they yielded. A post of attaché was procured for him, and he and his young wife went abroad.

I thought she would forget me then, but she did not. For many years she kept up a capricious, fitful correspondence. At first it was all herself and Alfred; then Alfred receded, as summer fruit recedes when a newer harvest comes in, and she wrote chiefly of herself and a certain newcomer—one Alfred Fanshawe de Bassompierre de Hamal—who began to reign in his father’s stead. There were boastings about this infant, amplifications of miracles of precocity, as if motherhood were a legend she could recite and embellish; and when I received her extravagances with my customary phlegm, she reproached me fiercely. I did not know what it was to be a mother; the sensibilities of the maternal heart were Greek and Hebrew to me; I was an unfeeling thing.

In due course the young gentleman took his degrees in teething, measles, hooping-cough. That was a terrible time for me: her letters became a shout of affliction—never woman so visited by calamity, never human being in such need of sympathy. At first I was frightened and wrote back pathetically. Soon I perceived there was more cry than wool in the matter, and relapsed into my natural, cruel insensibility. As for the boy, he weathered each storm like a hero. Five times he was “in articulo mortis,” and five times he revived.

In later years, ominous murmurings rose against Alfred the First. M. de Bassompierre had to be appealed to; debts had to be paid, some of them of that dismal and dingy order called debts of honour. Complaints and difficulties grew frequent. Under every cloud—whatever its shape—Ginevra still called out lustily for sympathy and aid; she had no notion of meeting distress single-handed. Yet in some fashion, from some quarter, she was pretty sure to obtain her will. And so she went on—fighting the battle of life by proxy; and, on the whole, suffering as little as any human being I have ever known.
