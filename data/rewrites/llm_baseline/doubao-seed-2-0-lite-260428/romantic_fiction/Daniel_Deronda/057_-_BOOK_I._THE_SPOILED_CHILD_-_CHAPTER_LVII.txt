*“The unripe grape, the ripe, and the dried. All things are changes, not into nothing, but into that which is not at present.”* — Marcus Aurelius

Deeds are the pulse of Time, his beating life,
And righteous or unrighteous, being done,
Must throb in after-throbs till Time itself
Be laid in darkness, and the universe
Quiver and breathe upon no mirror more.

The salt wind drifted soft through the open windows at dusk, the hour drawing near to the exact moment the night before when they had carried Gwendolen back, soaked and shivering, from the shore where her husband had drowned. The blinds were drawn low to gild the room in faded amber light, and she sat propping her cheek on her hand, staring unblinking out at the grey rolling waves. She looked less haggard than when Deronda had left her hours earlier, but a deep, heavy melancholy clung to every line of her face, softening into skittish, anxious timidity the second he stepped across the threshold. She did not reach out for his hand, only breathed, “How long it has felt.” Then she added, quiet and pleading, “Will you sit near me a little while again?”

He settled into the chair at her side as he had the night before, and saw her turn to him with that unnameable, wavering expression that said she had something desperate to confess, so he waited, gentle and still, for her to speak. Twice she turned back to stare out the window in silence, then turned again with that same tight, wanting look, the words trapped behind her teeth. Some fear was holding her fast, so Deronda turned his face away to give her space, to ease the weight of his gaze.

It was only a few moments before he heard her voice, trembling and imploring. “You will not tell anyone else? No one else can ever know?”

“Most certainly not,” he said at once. “There is no action that should ever be taken because of this secret. No injury could be mended by spreading it, no retribution any living person could hand down would be just.”

She went so still for a long pause he thought she might be holding her breath, holding back a sob, before she whispered, “But if I had not had that murderous will, just for that one moment — if I had thrown the rope to him right away, would it not have stopped him from dying?”

“No, I do not think so,” Deronda said slowly. “If he could swim as well as you say, he must have been seized by sudden, fatal cramp. Even if you had run as fast as your legs could carry you, thrown the rope with all your strength, there is no way you could have reached him in time. That one dark, murderous thought in your heart never changed a single thing about how he died. Its weight lives only in you, in your own breast. Evil will inside us matters deeply, sooner or later it will leak out of us — sometimes it breeds more terrible wicked acts, but sometimes it stings us with such self-loathing it pushes us to be better, to strive for good we never thought we could reach.”

“I do not have to rob anyone,” she said, voice still shaking. “His family will get everything they are owed, all that they should have. I found that out long before I left London. You do not think I am still greedy for those things, do you?”

“I had not thought of them at all,” Deronda said. “I was far too preoccupied with the hurt you are carrying.”

“Perhaps you do not know how all this began,” Gwendolen said, slow, like she was forcing the words out through a wall of reluctance. “There was another woman he should have married. I knew all about her. I even told her I would never stand between them, that I would step aside. That was the first day you ever saw me, at Leubronn. But then we lost all our money, all at once, and I was so miserable I let myself be tempted. I told myself I could marry him, make all of it right, get everything I ever wanted. But it was nothing like that. It was dreadful, all of it. Full of coldness, and cruelty, and slowly that hatred grew in me, and all those wicked thoughts. I told you once I was afraid of myself. I tried to do what you said, to make that fear keep me safe. I lay awake thinking what would happen if… if I gave in. I imagined how I would dread every morning, wish the dark would last forever, but never get any peace in the dark — because all I would see there was death. If you had not known how miserable I was, you might have… but none of that effort was any use. All I care about now is making sure no one else finds out, least of all my poor mamma, who has never known a day of real happiness.”

Silence stretched long again, before she spoke, her throat tight with a repressed sob. “You cannot bear to look at me anymore, can you? You think I am far too wicked. You do not believe I can ever get better, ever be worth anything, ever be good enough. I will always be too wicked to —” Her voice broke off, helpless, tearful.

Deronda’s heart ached so sharply it felt pierced. He turned his gaze back to her tear-streaked, beseeching face. “I believe you may become far worthier than you have ever been in your life — worthy to lead a life that is a blessing, to yourself and everyone around you. No sin dooms us to hopelessness, except the sin we love, and cling to, and never try to escape. You have already tried to escape. You will keep trying.”

“But you were the one who made me start trying. You must not forsake me,” Gwendolen said, leaning forward with her clasped hands resting on the edge of her chair, her face crumpled with the raw, concentrated pain of the last twenty-four hours, that terrible new reality that waited on the other side of a wish that almost turned into murder. “I will bear any penance. I will live any kind of life you tell me to. Only do not leave me. If you had been near me all along, if I could have told you everything as it happened, I would never have fallen this far. You will not forsake me?”

“It could never be my impulse to turn away from you,” Deronda said, quick and sure, his voice soft and steady — the same voice, the same warm, earnest gaze that always made his ready sympathy feel far more personal, far more special, than he had ever intended it to be. Even in that moment, a quiet, sharp foreboding pricked at him. His overwhelming care for this broken, stricken woman could not hold back a flood of images of future difficulty, of the days ahead. He held her gaze as he spoke, but he was painfully aware that his words to her now sounded like a promise he might not be able to keep one day: he was making an indefinite promise to a hope that had no clear shape. Anxieties, small immediate ones and large distant ones, crowded into his thoughts, and after a short silence he said, “Sir Hugo Mallinger is set to arrive no later than tomorrow night. I have every reason to hope your mother will not be long after him. Her presence will be the greatest comfort possible to you, will it not? It will give you every reason to spare her all unnecessary pain.”

“Yes, yes,” she said. “I will try. But you will not leave before she comes?”

“Not until Sir Hugo has arrived.”

“But we will all go back to England soon?”

“As soon as it is possible,” Deronda said, not wanting to get tangled up in small details.

Gwendolen turned back to the window, her face softening like she was slowly waking up after a hard blow to the head, the twilight deepening fast around them.

“You will always be with Sir Hugo now, won’t you?” she said after a while, turning back to him. “You will always live at the Abbey, or at Diplow?”

“I have no idea where I will end up living,” Deronda said, the heat rising to his cheeks.

She saw his flush at once, realized she had spoken too rashly, and fell quiet. After a little while, staring away again, she said, “I cannot imagine how my life will ever go on now. I think it would be better if I were poor, if I had to work for every bit of bread I eat.”

“New hopes and new purposes will come to you as the days pass,” Deronda said. “When you are back among your friends, you will see new duties laid out before you that you cannot even picture now. Make it your first small task now to get well, to grow calm, to be as much like the woman you were meant to be, before —” he hesitated.

“Before my mother comes,” Gwendolen finished. “Ah, yes. I have to be different. I have not even looked at my own face. Would you have known me,” she added, turning to him, “if you had passed me on the street right now? Would you have known that this was the same girl you saw at Leubronn?”

“Yes, I would have known you,” Deronda said, his voice soft and mournful. “The change to your face is not great. I would have known it was you at once, and seen straight away that you had walked through some terrible, great sorrow.”

“Do not wish now that you had never met me,” Gwendolen begged, tears brimming over her lashes. “Do not ever wish that.”

“I would despise myself for making such a wish,” Deronda said. “How could I ever know what was best for me, before it happened? We have to find our duties in what life brings us, not in the imaginary what-ifs of what could have been. If I were foolish enough to waste time on wishing, I would not wish I had never met you. I would wish I could have saved you from all this pain.”

“You already saved me from far worse,” Gwendolen sobbed. “I would have been far more wicked than I am now if you had never been kind to me. If you had not been good to me, I would have sunk so much deeper.”

“It is time for me to go now,” Deronda said, his spirit worn thin from the hours of holding her grief with her. “Remember the small task we spoke of: get well, grow calm, before your other friends arrive.”

He stood up as he spoke, and she held her hand out to him, soft and submissive. The second the door clicked shut behind him, she sank to her knees, hysterical tears wracking her whole body. The space between them felt as vast as an ocean. She was a banished soul, staring across the waves at a bright, good life she had sinned her way right out of reaching. They found her there, crumpled on the floor, and no one who looked at her thought anything of her grief — it was only the natural, expected sorrow of a poor young lady whose husband had drowned right in front of her eyes.
