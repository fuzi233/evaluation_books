That morning, Mademoiselle Moore had a pupil whose mind wandered frequently. Caroline forgot, time and again, the explanations offered to her, yet she endured the resulting reprimands with a serene and untroubled spirit. Seated in the sunlight near the window, she seemed to absorb a kind of influence from the warmth that made her appear both happy and benevolent. In this state, she looked her best, presenting a vision that was undeniably pleasing.

Nature had not withheld the gift of beauty from her. One need not know her intimately to find her likable; she was fair enough to captivate at first sight. Her figure suited her age perfectly—girlish, light, and supple; every curve was neat, every limb proportionate. Her face was expressive and gentle, her eyes handsome and occasionally flashing with a winning beam that seemed to speak softly to the affections. Her mouth was lovely, her skin delicate, and her abundant brown hair, which she knew how to arrange with taste, fell in picturesque curls around her shoulders. Her attire reflected a refined taste: unobtrusive in fashion, inexpensive in material, yet chosen in colors that harmonized beautifully with her fair complexion and tailored to her slight frame. That day, she wore a winter garment of merino in a soft brown shade matching her hair, accessorized only by a pink ribbon and a pink knot at her neck. No other adornment graced her.

Thus much for Caroline Helstone's appearance. As for her character or intellect, if she possessed any, they would reveal themselves in due course.

Her background was straightforward. She was the daughter of parents who parted ways shortly after her birth due to incompatible temperaments. Her mother was the half-sister of Mr. Moore's father; consequently, although there was no direct blood relation between them, she was, in a distant sense, the cousin of Robert, Louis, and Hortense. Her father was the brother of Mr. Helstone—a man whose character friends preferred not to recall once death had settled all earthly accounts. He had rendered his wife unhappy, and the truthful reports of his failings lent credibility to the false rumors spread about his more virtuous brother. Caroline had never known her mother, who was taken from her in infancy, nor had she seen her since. Her father died relatively young, leaving her under the guardianship of her uncle, the rector, for several years. He was neither by nature nor habit suited to raising a young girl. He exerted little effort regarding her education; perhaps he would have neglected it entirely had she not, sensing the neglect, grown anxious and periodically requested attention and the means to acquire the basic knowledge deemed necessary. Nevertheless, she harbored a depressing sense of inferiority, believing her attainments to be fewer than those of girls of her age and station. Consequently, she welcomed the kind offer made by her cousin Hortense, soon after the latter arrived at Hollow's Mill, to teach her French and fine needlework.

Mlle. Moore, for her part, delighted in the task, as it afforded her a sense of importance. She enjoyed asserting a degree of superiority over a docile yet quick-witted pupil. She viewed Caroline precisely as she estimated herself: an irregularly taught, even ignorant girl. When she discovered that Caroline was making rapid and eager progress, she attributed the improvement not to the student's talent or diligence, but entirely to her own superior teaching methods. Even when she realized that Caroline, untrained in academic routines, possessed a knowledge of her own—desultory but varied—the discovery caused her no surprise. She assumed the girl had gleaned these treasures inadvertently from her conversations, maintaining this belief even when forced to admit that her pupil knew much about subjects where she herself knew little. The logic was flawed, but Hortense possessed absolute faith in it.

Proud of her "positive spirit" and her preference for dry studies, Mlle. Moore kept her young cousin as tightly bound to her lessons as possible. She relentlessly drilled Caroline in French grammar, assigning endless "logical analyses" as the most beneficial exercises. These analyses were far from enjoyable for Caroline; she believed she could have mastered French equally well without them and resented the excessive time spent pondering "principal propositions" and "incidents," debating whether a clause was "determinative" or "applicative," or analyzing if a sentence was "full," "elliptical," or "implicit." Occasionally, she became lost in the labyrinth of syntax. When this happened, and while Hortense rummaged through her upstairs drawers—an inexplicable pastime consuming a large portion of each day as she sorted, unsorted, and re-sorted items—Caroline would take her book to Robert in the counting-house. There, he would smooth out the rough patches of her understanding. Mr. Moore possessed a clear, tranquil mind. Almost immediately upon seeing Caroline's difficulties, they seemed to dissolve under his gaze. Within two minutes, he would explain everything, offering the key to the puzzle in mere words. Caroline often wished that Hortense could teach with such clarity; she imagined how much faster she might learn. In gratitude, she would repay him with an admiring smile, cast more at his feet than toward his face, before reluctantly leaving the mill to return to the cottage. While completing her exercises or working out sums (as Mlle. Moore also taught her arithmetic), she would secretly wish that nature had made her a boy, allowing her to serve as Robert's clerk and sit beside him in the counting-house rather than enduring Hortense's tutelage in the parlor.

On rare occasions, she spent the evenings at Hollow's Cottage. Sometimes Mr. Moore was away attending markets, sometimes visiting Mr. Yorke, and often engaged with male visitors in another room. Yet, sometimes he was at home, free to converse with Caroline. When this occurred, the evening hours flew by on wings of light, vanishing before they could be counted. There was no corner in England more pleasant than that small parlor when the three cousins occupied it. Hortense, when not teaching, scolding, or cooking, was far from ill-humored. By evening, she relaxed and treated her young English kinswoman kindly. Furthermore, there was a way to render her delightful: by persuading her to pick up her guitar and sing. Under such circumstances, she became genuinely good-natured. With skilled playing and a well-toned voice, listening to her was not disagreeable. It would have been wholly agreeable, save for her formal and self-important demeanor, which modulated her songs just as it shaped her manners and molded her countenance.

Released from the demands of business, Mr. Moore, though not particularly lively himself, was a willing spectator of Caroline's vivacity, a complacent listener to her chatter, and a ready responder to her questions. He was someone pleasant to sit near, to hover around, to address, and to admire. Sometimes he was even better than this—almost animated, quite gentle and friendly.

The drawback was that by the following morning, he invariably returned to being "frozen up" again. Regardless of how much he seemed, in his quiet manner, to enjoy these social evenings, he rarely managed to repeat them. This circumstance puzzled the inexperienced mind of his cousin. "If I had a means of happiness at my command," she thought, "I would employ it often. I would keep it bright with use, rather than letting it lie aside for weeks until it grew rusty."

Yet, she was careful not to put this theory into practice. Although she greatly enjoyed evening visits to the cottage, she never paid one uninvited. Often, when pressed by Hortense to come, she would refuse simply because Robert did not, or barely, second the request. That morning marked the first time he had ever invited her of his own unprompted will, speaking so kindly that hearing him had instilled in her a sense of happiness sufficient to sustain her joy throughout the entire day.

The morning passed as usual. Mlle. Moore, perpetually breathless with activity, bustled from kitchen to parlor, alternately scolding Sarah and reviewing Caroline's exercises or hearing her recitation lessons. However flawlessly these tasks were executed, she never offered commendation. It was her maxim that praise was inconsistent with a teacher's dignity, whereas blame, in varying degrees, was indispensable. She believed that constant reprimand, whether severe or slight, was essential to maintaining her authority. If no errors could be found in the lesson, it was invariably the pupil's carriage, air, dress, or demeanor that required correction.

A typical altercation ensued over dinner. When Sarah finally brought the meal into the room, she almost threw it onto the table with a look clearly stating, "I have never served such rubbish in my life; it isn't fit for dogs." Despite Sarah's disdain, it was a savory enough repast. The soup was a purée of dried peas, which Mlle. Moore had prepared amid bitter lamentations that in this desolate country of England, no haricot beans were available. Next came a dish of meat—its nature unknown but presumably miscellaneous—singularly chopped with breadcrumbs, seasoned uniquely though not unpleasantly, and baked in a mold. Oddly bruised greens accompanied it, along with a fruit pâté preserved according to a recipe devised by Madame Gérard Moore's grandmother, from which it appeared probable that molasses had been substituted for sugar.

Caroline had no objection to this Belgian cuisine; indeed, she rather liked it as a change. Well, she did so, for had she shown any distaste, such a display would have permanently damaged her standing in Mlle. Moore's good graces; a positive crime might have been more easily forgiven than a symptom of dislike for foreign food.

Shortly after dinner, Caroline coaxed her governess-cousin upstairs to dress.
