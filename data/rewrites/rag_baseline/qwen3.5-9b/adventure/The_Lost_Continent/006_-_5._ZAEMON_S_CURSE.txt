The royal pyramid held me, for the moment, as a cage of stone and shadow. Outside, the great paved square churned with a glittering cavalcade, massed in shifting clusters of noise and color. A mammoth, a beast of impossible scale, halted before the doorway. Stairs were hoisted, trumpets blew their hollow notes, and we three—the riders of the golden half-castle under the canopy of snakes—descended.

From the open sky to the interior mazes. Without thought, the reflex of custom seized me. I turned toward the towering rocks of the Sacred Mountain, which frowned above the city, and performed the obeisance. I offered the prescribed prayer in silence. It was a mechanical motion, a muscle memory of a life lived among priests. Yet, as I rose, a sound cut through the air—a titter. Not a roar, but a dry, sharp cracking of teeth from somewhere within the bedecked crowd.

I frowned, scanning the faces, seeking the source of the mockery. I turned to Phorenice to demand justice for the disrespect. But she was not kneeling. She stood erect, having never touched her forehead to the dust. Her gaze met mine, carrying a peculiar weight I could not parse.

She said nothing of the laugh. Instead, she simply gestured. "Come," she said, turning toward the doorway and shouting the secret word for the day. The ponderous stone blocks barring the porch swung back on their ancient hinges. With a stately tread, she passed from the hot sunshine into the cool gloom beyond, followed decorously by a fan-girl. A heaviness settled in my chest. I followed. Behind us, the stone doors slammed shut with a sullen thud, sealing the sun out.

We did not travel far. Phorenice halted in the Hall of Waiting. I knew this place intimately: the red walls painted with the ghosts of kings, the central jet of bronze from which the burning fountain of earth-breath erupted, casting a flickering, orange light. Twenty years prior, the old King had walked this far to bid me farewell as I departed for my vice-royalty in Yucatan. The air then had been pure, sweet, breathable. Now, it was thick, laden with a scent that made the lungs feel sluggish and oppressed.

"My minister," the Empress said, her voice cutting through the stagnation. "I acquit you of intentional insult. But I suspect the colonial air has simplified your mind. No one has made such an obeisance to that mountain since I ascended to this throne."

"Your Majesty," I replied, keeping my tone level. "I am of the Priests' Clan. We are taught to thank the Gods, specifically our Lord the Sun, for the air He provides. Having been chased by streams of fire and stinking miasma in the mountains during their boils, I pray this prayer from the heart."

"Circumstances have changed since you left Atlantis," she countered. "Now, thanks are not cast at those old Gods."

The implication struck me like a blow. If this was the new law, I had no part in it. Serving a monarch was one thing; to palter with sacrilege, to bow to a swineherd's daughter as a deity worthy of prayer—that revolted my very manhood. I invited the crisis.

"Phorenice," I said, "I have been a priest from childhood, revering the mysteries until I find them false. Until then, I stand by that allegiance. If there is a cost, I will pay it."

She regarded me with a slow, deliberate smile. "You are a strong man, Deucalion."

I bowed.

"I have heard others as stubborn," she continued, shaking out the ruddy bunches of her hair so the light of the burning earth-breath caught the curve of her face. "But they were converted. It is easier to convert the stubborn than to burn them. Few speak of burning; they rush to conversion whether I will it or not. Perhaps my looks and tongue lack charm today."

"Phorenice is Empress," I stated flatly. "I am her servant. Tomorrow, if permitted, I will clear the rabble clamoring outside the walls. I must begin to prove my utility."

"I am told you fight well," she said. "I possess some skill myself and consider myself a judge. Tomorrow, we shall taste battle together. Today, however, I must complete the honorable reception I have planned. The feast is nearly ready. You will wish to prepare. Chambers have been selected for you, stocked with necessities. Ylga will show you the way."

We waited until she had vanished into the shadows of the farther angle, then the fan-girl took a lamp and led the way. We threaded our path through the narrow, twisting mazes of the pyramid. Everywhere, the air was cloying with perfume. The passages doubled and turned within the solid stone, designed so that a stranger might spend days lost before finding their destination. These forgotten builders possessed a cunning intelligence; they ensured kings could not be slain by vulgar assassins entering from without. Legend says the builder-king killed everyone who had laid the inner stones, ensuring the tomb remained a secret grave.

Ylga, however, knew the way. She doubled, turned, and stopped before a blank wall to push a stone that swung inward. Once, she pressed a corner of a flagstone, causing it to rise and reveal a steep, narrow stair. We descended, emerging onto an inclined way that led upward again, ascending degree by degree until we reached the chamber assigned to me.

"There is raiment in these chests by the walls," the girl said, gesturing to rows of storage. "Jewels and gauds lie in that bronze coffer. They are Phorenice's first presents, a small earnest of what follows. My Lord Deucalion can drop his simplicity and dress in finery suitable for the fashion."

"Girl," I said sharply, "be more decorous. Spare me such advice."

"If my Lord Deucalion finds this rude, he may speak to Phorenice, and I shall be whipped. If he commands it, I can be stripped and scourged before him. The Empress does much for Deucalion just now."

"You are nearer to that whipping than you think," I warned.

"I have a name," she retorted, looking up from under black brows with a sullen intensity. "They call me Ylga. You might have heard it as we rode on the mammoth, had you not been so wrapped in Phorenice."

I studied her. "I have never seen you before. Your first words threaten trouble. There is an objective here."

She pushed the massive stone door aside, then used her jeweled fingers to pull me away from the draft of the airshaft into the far corner. "I am the daughter of Zaemon," she said quietly. "Whom you knew."

"Do you bear a message?"

"How could I? He lives in the priests' dwellings on the Mountain you bowed to. I have not seen him in two years. But when I saw you step from that red pavilion at the harbor, I felt a pity for you, Deucalion. I remembered you were my father's friend, and I knew what Phorenice plotted. She has been scheming for two months."

"I cannot speak ill of the Empress."

"And yet—"

"What?"

She stamped her sandal hard against the stone floor. "You must be either blind or incredibly daring. But I will not interfere—not now. I shall watch. If you ever require a friend, I will serve you."

"I thank you for your friendship."

"You take it lightly. Even now, I do not believe you understand my power, nor my motives. You may be the first man in this kingdom, but let me remind you: I rank as the second lady. In Atlantis, women stand high. My friendship is a commodity sought with frequency and industry."

"As I say, I am grateful. You seem to underestimate my gratitude, Ylga. Credit me: I have never bestowed it upon a woman before. Treasure it for its rarity."

"Well," she said, "there is an education ahead." She left me, demonstrating how to summon slaves, and I stood alone for a full minute, wondering why the daughter of Zaemon should urge me to abandon a lifetime of habits.

Slaves arrived at my command, eager to clothe me in a thousand foolishnesses of robe and gaud, peddling the virtues of scores of perfumes and unguents. Their manner grated on me. I was clean, shaved, my hair trimmed, my robe unsoiled. Considering their attentions an impertinence, I ordered them to beat one another. I promised that if they lacked thoroughness, I would send them to the brander to be marked with stripes that would endure. It is strange, but a common menial can often surpass a rebellious general in the power to ruffle one's temper.

That day brought many strange sights, but nothing shocked me more than Phorenice's method of summoning guests to her feast. It did more than shock; it horrified. Sitting in my chamber, awaiting the call, a sound emerged from nowhere. It grew in volume with infinitesimal graduations until it became a clanging din that hurt the ears. Then, guessing what was coming, the entire massive fabric of the pyramid trembled, groaned, and shook as though it were a child's wooden toy brushed by a giant's sandal.
