The heart’s natural inclinations are not easily subdued, yet the struggle to master them is not in vain. Such efforts, though they may seem small, shape our actions toward the path reason dictates—often against the protests of feeling. They lend life a steadier rhythm, a calmer surface, though what lies beneath remains known only to God. To Him alone should we lay bare the secrets of the spirit, seeking strength for the pains He appoints, light for the darkness, patience for the unendurable. In His time—if not in ours—the waters will stir; in some form, if not the one we longed for, healing will come.

Morning found me weak, my hands trembling as I dressed. Before I could steady myself, Mrs. Bretton swept into the room.

"Absurd!" she declared, though her tone held no real reproach. In moments, she had bundled me back into bed with the brisk efficiency she once reserved for her son’s rebellions. "You’ll stay there till afternoon," she informed me. "Doctor’s orders."

She brought breakfast herself, settling beside me as I ate. Few people possess the gift of easing solitude, but she had always been one of them. Her presence was warmth, her hands a comfort. Even the room seemed brighter when she entered.

Graham had left instructions that I was not to be overtaxed—proof that he had not forgotten me. The thought softened the edges of my loneliness. As the day waned, the quiet soothed me. My room, pale green and white, reminded me of sea foam and depths, the ornaments like shells, the ceiling’s moldings like dolphins. Closing my eyes, I fancied I heard distant waves, their roar muted to a lullaby.

By evening, I felt stronger. I descended to the blue salon unassisted. There, Graham stood by the window, reading in the fading light. The fire glowed, but the lamp remained unlit, and tea had not yet been served. Mrs. Bretton dozed in her chair, exhausted from her day outdoors.

He noticed me at once, stepping forward with care not to wake her. His voice was low, soothing. "This house is quiet, isn’t it?" he murmured, guiding me to the window. "A hidden place—La Terrasse, they call it. The moon suits it well."

And indeed it did. The rising moon, first flushed, then gold, then clear, bathed the scene in silver. It stirred something in him—a sigh, soft and unguarded. I knew at once where his thoughts had flown: to Ginevra.

I might have spoken her name then, but he forestalled me. "This morning," he said, turning practical, "I went to the Rue Fossette. Your absence hadn’t even been noticed."

I smiled faintly. "Goton gave up on me days ago."

"And Madame Beck? Leaving you alone—"

"She couldn’t have known I’d fall ill."

He studied me. "Your nerves bore the worst of it, I think."

"I was low-spirited," I admitted.

"Medicine can’t mend that. You need company, air, movement."

The advice was sound, if unremarkable. Then, abruptly, he asked, "Are you a Catholic?"

The question startled me. "No. Why?"

"Last night—the way you were given into my care—it made me wonder."

I frowned. "I don’t even recall how that happened."

He explained: a priest, Père Silas, had found me near the Béguinage, collapsed in the storm. "He said you’d come to him in distress—confessed something that troubled him enough to follow you."

"Confessed?" I echoed. "No secrets, only despair. I needed—something. Counsel, perhaps."

He shook his head. "You ought to travel. Six months abroad would steady you."

But I brushed aside the notion. The fault, I insisted, lay only with me and fate.

He humored me before shifting back to the night’s events—how Père Silas had helped carry me here, how duty had called them both away before I woke.

"And now," he finished, eyes twinkling, "will you convert?"

"Not yet," I said dryly. "But thank him for me—and if I’m ever rich, I’ll fund his charities."

Just then, Mrs. Bretton stirred, indignantly denying she had slept. Graham teased her mercilessly, their banter affectionate, their mutual devotion clear. Yet I wondered—did she know of his love for Ginevra? If she saw how capriciously the girl tormented him, would she still approve?

The evening passed more sweetly than the last—easier, freer. That night, I did not weep. Instead, I slipped into dreams along a path lined with quieter, kinder thoughts.
