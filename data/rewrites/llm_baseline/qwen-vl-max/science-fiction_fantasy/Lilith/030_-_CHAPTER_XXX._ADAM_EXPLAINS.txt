“We must be vigilant,” he said, “or she will outwit us once more. She could deceive even the chosen.”

“How can we guard ourselves?” I asked.

“Every way,” he replied. “She fears her child—therefore hates her—and is here on a mission to destroy her. To her, childbirth is not a beginning but an ending: each new generation is a foe to the one before. Her daughter represents, in her eyes, a channel through which her own imagined immortality bleeds away. And so, from the moment of her birth, she has pursued her with relentless hatred. Yet her schemes have only led to the opposite: within the realm she claims as her own, a thriving colony of children has emerged—one that looks to her daughter as its heart, its leader, its protector. My Eve longed for this child, would have nurtured her as a mother does her firstborn—but we were not yet ready to guide her. She was taken into the wilds, and for countless ages we knew nothing of her fate. But she was tended by divine hands, played with by young angels, and lived without care—until she found a baby abandoned in the woods, and the maternal instinct stirred within her. Since then, she has gathered others, one by one. That love has never been satisfied. Her family is her entire world, and no children have ever known a better guardian. Her authority over them is absolute, though she herself is unaware of it—except in quiet watchfulness, in silent service. She no longer remembers life before them; she believes she came from the forest, the first of the line.”

“You saved the life of her enemy—and thus, your life now belongs to her and hers,” he continued. “The princess was coming to annihilate the Little Ones. But as she crossed the stream, vengeance caught up with her. She would have died had you not intervened. You did. And if she had dared return across the water, she would now be raging among them. But there was another path—a way into their sanctuary through the three-dimensional world. Yet she had barred herself from it by destroying her former body. She could only re-enter through direct contact with someone bound to that world. You gave her that chance—her first in all her long years. With every step you climbed, her hand, barely touching, brushed against your muffled feet. Now, in that chamber, she waits, watching, ready to escape the moment she can.”

“She cannot know about the door!” I protested. “She can’t possibly know how to open it!”

But my voice lacked conviction.

“Hush,” whispered the librarian, raising a hand. “She hears through anything—through walls, through silence, through thought itself. You must go at once. Make your way to my wife’s cottage. I will stay and guard her.”

“Let me go to the Little Ones!” I cried.

“Beware,” he said gently but firmly. “Go to my wife. Do as she says.”

His words made no sense to me. Why rush toward what might only delay? If not to protect the children, why go at all? Still, I believed him—just enough to ask questions, not enough to obey.

“Tell me first,” I said, “why you chose *that* place to imprison her? The night I fled from your house, I ran straight into that very closet.”

“The closet is no closer or farther from our cottage than any other place,” he replied.

“But,” I insisted, struggling to grasp the logic, “how is it that when you please, you draw a whole book from that same door—when I saw only half of one? The other half, you told me, extends into your library. When you return it to the shelf, won’t it again pass through? Doesn’t that mean the two places where parts of the same book exist simultaneously must lie close together? Or can one part of the book exist in space—somewhere—and the other outside it—nowhere?”

“I regret,” he said, “I cannot explain it to you. There is no capacity in you to comprehend such things. Not only is the phenomenon beyond your understanding—it is fundamentally inaccessible. Even I grasp only fragments. Yet you constantly experience realities you neither understand nor can understand. You think you do, but your so-called understanding is merely familiarity. You accept them not because you grasp them, but because they are unavoidable. They are there, and they affect you. Truth is: no man truly understands anything. When he admits his ignorance, that is not the start of understanding—but the beginning of the ability to one day reach it. These matters are unfamiliar to you, so you do not pretend to understand. Neither I nor anyone else can help you understand here. But perhaps I can help you believe.”

He walked to the closet door, gave a low whistle, and stood listening. A moment later, I heard—or seemed to hear—a soft fluttering of wings. Looking up, I saw a white dove alight briefly on the top shelf above the portrait, then drop gracefully onto Mr. Raven’s shoulder, resting her head against his cheek. Only the subtle movement of their heads betrayed that they were speaking. I stared, transfixed—then, suddenly, the bird was gone. Mr. Raven returned to his seat.

“Why did you whistle?” I asked. “Sound here isn’t sound there, surely.”

“You’re right,” he said. “I whistled so you’d know I called her. Not the sound, but the meaning reached her. There is no time to lose—you must go.”

“I will,” I replied, rising.

“You will sleep tonight at my hostelry,” he said—not a question, but a command, gentle yet firm.

“My heart is with the children,” I answered. “But if you insist—”

“I do.”

“I can do nothing otherwise,” he added. “I’ll accompany you to the mirror and see you off.”

He rose. Then came a sharp jolt from within the closet—the leopardess had thrown herself against the heavy door. I looked at him.

“Come,” he urged. “Now.”

We reached the library door just as a shrieking yell echoed behind us, mixed with the scraping of claws on oak. I hesitated, turning back.

“To think of her lying there alone,” I murmured, “with that terrible wound.”

“That wound will never heal,” he sighed. “It will gnaw at her heart. Annihilation is no death to evil. Only good replacing evil can kill it. An evil thing must live with its nature until it chooses to become good. That is the only true slaying.”

I remained silent until a strange sound reached us.

“If she breaks free!” I gasped.

“Hurry,” he said. “I’ll follow immediately after you leave—and I’ll rearrange the mirrors.”

We ran, breathless, into the wooden chamber. Mr. Raven fastened the chains, adjusted the hood, then set the mirrors in their proper alignment. He stepped beside me before the standing mirror. Already, the mountain range emerged from the mist.

Then, with a demonic roar, the massive form of the spotted leopardess burst between us—leaping through the mirror as if it were air, settling into a swift, steady gallop.

I turned to my companion, horrified. “You need not run,” he called. “You can’t catch her. This is our way.”

As he spoke, he turned in the opposite direction.

“She has more magic at her fingertips than I care to know,” he added quietly.

“We must do what we can,” I said, and ran—but as I watched her shrink in the distance, I stopped, returning to him.

“Doubtless we must,” he replied. “But my wife has warned Mara. She will act. You must sleep first. You’ve given me your word.”

“I intend to keep it,” I said. “But surely sleep isn’t the first priority? Action comes before rest!”

“A man can do nothing he is not fit to do,” he said calmly. “See? Did I not tell you Mara would act?”

I followed his gaze and saw a white speck moving at an angle to the leopardess’s path.

“There she is!” he exclaimed. “The spotted one is strong—but the white is stronger.”

“I’ve seen them fight,” I said. “It didn’t seem decisive.”

“How could eyes that have never slept judge such a contest?” he replied. “The princess did not admit defeat—she never does—but she fled. When she finally confesses her last hope is gone—that it is impossible to resist—then her dawn will begin. Come. Come. He who cannot act must make haste to sleep.”
