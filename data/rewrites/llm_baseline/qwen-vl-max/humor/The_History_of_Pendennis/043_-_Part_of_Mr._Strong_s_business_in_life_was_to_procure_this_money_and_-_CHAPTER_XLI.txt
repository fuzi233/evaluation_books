CHAPTER XLI. A Tale of Two Cities—Richmond and Greenwich

Poor Harry Foker found himself at Richmond, seated at a dinner table that might have been designed by the devil himself to test the patience of a man who’d just lost his last nerve. The meal? A masterpiece of excess: melting ices like tears of sugar, pineapples carved into shapes that made him wonder if they were meant for dessert or sculpture, bottles emptying themselves into glasses with no apparent purpose, and cigar ash scattered across fruit like the fallout of some minor war. And yet, despite it all, he felt nothing but despair.

“I wonder how I ever thought these people were worth my time,” he muttered under his breath, staring blankly at the chaos before him. “Rougemont’s got crow’s-feet like a weathered map, and her cheeks look like they’ve been painted by a clown who ran out of skill. Calverley talking slang? Ugh. I hate chaff in women—especially when it comes with a smirk. And Colchicum! That old relic, riding down here in his brougham with a coronet on top of it, sitting like a stuffed badger between Mademoiselle Coralie and her mother! An English peer, and a horse-rider from Franconi’s circus? It’s not right. Not right at all. I’m not proud—but this is beyond taste.”

“Twopence-halfpenny for your thoughts, Fokey!” Miss Rougemont called out, plucking her cigar from lips as red as a ripe tomato. She was clearly enjoying herself, while Harry looked like he’d rather be buried in a crypt beneath the churchyard.

“Does Foker ever think?” drawled Mr. Poyntz, leaning back with the air of someone who had just discovered the secret of life—and then promptly forgotten it.

“Foker,” said Poyntz, gesturing dramatically toward the young man, “here is a considerable sum offered by a fair capitalist at this end of the table—for the present emanations of your valuable and acute intellect, old boy!”

“What the deuce is Poyntz talking about?” Miss Calverley asked her neighbour, eyes narrowing. “I hate him. He’s a sneering, drawling beast.”

“Oh, what a droll little man!” said Mademoiselle Coralie, in French, her voice rich with the accent of Gascony, where sunburnt cheeks and dark eyes are born of heat and passion. “He doesn’t look twenty years old. What a droll!”

“I wish I were,” sighed Lord Colchicum, turning his purple face toward a large goblet of claret. “C’te Jeunesse. Peuh! je m’en fiche.”

Madame Brack, Coralie’s mother, took a generous pinch of snuff from Lord Colchicum’s delicate gold box. “Je m’aime que les hommes faits, moi,” she declared. “Comme milor. Coralie! n’est-ce pas que tu n’aimes que les hommes faits, ma bichette?”

“My lord,” she added with a grin, “you flatter me.”

“Taisez-vous, Maman, vous n’etes qu’une bete,” Coralie snapped, shrugging her broad shoulders. My lord, catching the tone, quietly pocketed his snuff-box—no doubt wary of Madame Brack’s fingers getting too close to his Mackabaw.

Needless to say, the rest of the banquet was a symphony of idle chatter, half-truths, and pretentious nonsense. Nothing edifying. Nothing memorable. Except perhaps the fact that not every woman of the corps de dance is like Miss Calverley, nor every peer quite so… eccentric as the late Viscount Colchicum. But history has shown us such figures: men who preferred the company of youth over their own age, offering their wisdom like free samples at a department store. And yes, there were respectable men—like Major Pendennis—who didn’t mind the entertainment so much as the *publicity*.

For instance, our dear Major would have gladly joined any party of pleasure—provided it was *en petit comité*, and included gentlemen like Lord Steyne and Lord Colchicum. “Give the young men their pleasures,” he once told Arthur. “I’m not one of those strait-laced moralists. I’m an old man of the world, begad. And I know young men will be young men—as long as it lasts.”

And indeed, he believed seventy years was a reasonable span for sowing wild oats—if you were a man of fashion.

That night, Mr. Foker drove his lovely guests back to Brompton in his drag. But he was silent, brooding, lost in thought. He ignored the jokes from his friends behind him, didn’t crack one of his usual witty remarks, didn’t even glance at the moon. When the ladies alighted at their door, one of them asked if he’d care for a drink.

“No thanks,” he replied, his voice flat, his expression mournful.

They assumed he’d had a quarrel with the Governor—or worse, some catastrophe had struck. He didn’t tell them the truth: that his heart was broken by the sight of Blanche Amory’s window in Grosvenor Place, lit up like a beacon in the night.

He sent the drag home with a groom and walked alone, hands in pockets, staring up at the stars. The moon watched him with quiet pity. And when he reached the house, he stood beneath the windows he imagined belonged to the object of his affection, and moaned like a wounded badger.

Policeman X, drinking beer at a nearby pub after delivering Lady Clavering from the French play, later reported seeing “another chap hanging about the premises”—a small man, dressed like a swell. “Looks like he’s in love,” the officer concluded, though he didn’t know it was poor Harry Foker, reduced to stalking the very person he couldn’t speak to.

And so began the great campaign: dodging Miss Amory through London like a cat avoiding a dog. If Lady Clavering went to the French play, Foker appeared in a stall—despite knowing only three words of French. He somehow discovered her engagements (perhaps Anatole, his valet, had befriended Sir Francis Clavering’s man and glimpsed the engagement book). At every soirée, Foker showed up—much to the surprise of everyone, especially his mother, whom he now ordered to apply for invitations to parties he had previously scorned.

“Why, I go to parties because it’s good for me to see the world,” he told her proudly. “And I go to the French play to improve my language. There’s no better teacher than a comedy or vaudeville.”

One evening, Lady Agnes saw him dancing—and complimented him on his elegance.

“Ah, I learned in Paris,” he lied.

Anatole knew better: his master had been sneaking off to an academy in Brewer Street, studying for hours each morning.

The casino hadn’t been invented yet—so dancing lessons were rare, secretive affairs.

Meanwhile, Old Pendennis rarely missed church. He believed it was a gentleman’s duty to support public worship, and also a fine opportunity to be seen. One Sunday, he and Arthur walked across the park together—Arthur having breakfasted with his uncle and now heading to St. James’s for a charity sermon.

“You know,” said the Major, “we’ll go to church, and then we’ll pop over to the Claverings’ for lunch. Lady Clavering loves being asked. She’s kind, hospitable—monstrous so.”

“We met them last week at Lady Agnes’s,” Pen replied. “The Begum was very kind. But I agree with you about Miss Amory—she’s a flirt, isn’t she?”

“She’s the most confounded little flirt in London,” Pen laughed. “She nearly devoured Harry Foker at dinner—gave him all the talk, though I tried to take her down.”

“Bah!” said the Major. “Harry’s engaged to his cousin—everyone knows it. A fine coup by Lady Rosherville. And Foker senior? He’s a parvenu who adores big men. He’ll make sure his son does as he’s told.”

“And what about Miss Amory?” Pen asked. “Is she really just a flirt?”

“She’s dangerous,” Pen admitted. “I was smitten myself once. Very far gone, too.”

“Were you?” the Major asked, eyes narrowing. “Did she return it?”

Pen laughed. “At one time, I thought I was pretty well in her good graces. But my mother didn’t like her. The affair ended.”

He didn’t mention the full story—the letters, the stolen glances, the near-kiss in the garden.

“A man might go farther and fare worse, Arthur,” said the Major, still watching him closely. “Her father was a ship’s mate, they say. No money.”

“That’s ten thousand pounds and a girl raised like her,” Pen countered, sounding dandified.

“What’s ten thousand pounds?” the Major retorted. “She’s worth more than that. From what I’ve seen—and heard—I’d say she’s clever, accomplished. Would make a fine wife for a sensible man.”

“How do you know about her money?” Pen asked, smiling.

“You seem to know everything,” he added.

“I know a few things,” the Major replied. “But I don’t tell all I know. Mark that.”

“And as for Miss Amory—if I saw her Mrs. Arthur Pendennis, I wouldn’t be surprised. Or sorry. And if you’re worried about ten thousand pounds—what would you say to thirty, forty, fifty?”

Pen stared. Then laughed. “Well, sir,” he said, “make her Mrs. Arthur Pendennis. You can do it as well as I.”

“Psha!” the Major replied, slightly peeved. “You’re laughing at me. And near a church gate, too!”

They arrived at St. Benedict’s. The bells rang. The congregation poured in. Carriages unloaded their fashionable loads. Pen and his uncle entered, ending their worldly conversation with the solemnity of a church door.

Arthur, always respectful in sacred places, felt the incongruity of their talk. But the Major? He was utterly unaware. His hat was brushed. His wig trimmed. His neckcloth perfectly tied. He studied every head in the pews—the bald ones, the bonnets, the flowers and feathers—with the same calm detachment as a man reading a newspaper.

Then Pen glanced toward the servants’ seats—and spotted Henry Foker, Esquire, lurking there like a guilty ghost.

Following Foker’s gaze, Pen saw two bonnets—one yellow, one pink—belonging to Lady Clavering and Blanche Amory.

So poor Harry wasn’t the only one bringing love into church?

After service, Foker rushed out first—but Pen caught up with him as he lingered, unwilling to leave until Lady Clavering’s barouche carried her away.

When the ladies emerged, they found the Pendennises and Foker waiting.

To see and to eat—these were simultaneous acts for the good-natured Begum. “Come to luncheon!” she invited immediately.

Blanche was particularly sweet. “O! Do come,” she said to Arthur. “If you’re not too great a man. I want to talk to you about—but we mustn’t say what here. What would Mr. Oriel say?”

And the young devotee jumped into the carriage after her.

“I’ve read every word of it,” she added, still addressing Pen. “It’s adorable.”

“I know who is,” Arthur replied, making a pert bow.

“What’s the row about?” Foker asked, confused.

“Walter Lorraine,” said the Major, nodding knowingly.

“Oh, yes,” Foker said. “Walter Lorraine died of measles when we were at Grey Friars. I remember his mother coming up.”

“You’re not a literary man, Foker,” Pen laughed, linking arms with him. “I’ve been writing a novel. Some papers have spoken well of it. Perhaps you don’t read the Sunday Papers?”

“I read Bell’s Life regularly, old boy,” Foker replied.

Pen laughed again. They walked off in high spirits.

After luncheon, Miss Amory resumed the topic. She loved poets, artists, men of feeling.

“Some passages made me cry,” she said.

“I’m happy to think I have a part of vos larmes, Miss Blanche,” Pen said, puffing up.

The Major, who’d read only six pages, put on his sanctimonious face. “Yes, there are some passages quite affecting—mons’ous affecting.”

“Oh, if it makes you cry,” Lady Clavering declared, “I won’t read it.”

“Don’t, mamma,” Blanche said, shrugging in French style.

Then she launched into a rhapsody about the novel—its poetry, its heroines Leonora and Neaera, its heroes Walter Lorraine and the young Duke.

“And what good company you introduce us to,” she said archly. “Quel ton! Have you spent time at court, Mr. Arthur? Are you a prime minister’s son?”

Pen laughed. “It’s as cheap for a novelist to create a Duke as to make a Baronet. Shall I tell you a secret? I promoted all my characters at the publisher’s request. The young Duke was a Baron. The Viscount was a commoner.”

“What a wicked, satirical, pert young man you’ve become!” she exclaimed. “Comme vous voila forme!”

“But I still like Arthur Pendennis of the country best,” she added, giving him her full gaze—both the tender look into his eyes and the modest glance downward, which displayed her dark lashes like fans.

Pen protested he hadn’t changed.

She sighed. Then turned to Foker, who had been silently sucking the crook of his cane, wishing he were half as clever.

The Major, thinking he’d discouraged Blanche by mentioning Foker’s engagement to Lady Ann Milton, was wrong. She became *more* attentive. Praised him, his mother, his pony, his watch-chain breloques, his darling cane, the monkey-head buttons on his waistcoat.

Then, having coaxed him into a blush, she turned to Pen.

“I’m afraid Mr. Foker is a sad young man,” she said.

“He doesn’t look it,” Pen sneered.

“I mean—we’ve heard sad stories. Haven’t we, mamma? What was Mr. Poyntz saying about that party at Richmond?”

“O you naughty creature!” the mother cried.

But Harry’s face turned pale. Pen smiled.

“I believe you’re just as bad,” Blanche said. “I believe you’d have liked to be there—wouldn’t you? I know you would. And so should I.”

“Lor, Blanche!” her mother gasped.

“Well, I would,” Blanche insisted. “I’ve never seen an actress. I’d give anything to know one. I adore talent. And I adore Richmond. And Greenwich. And I say—I should like to go there.”

And so, gallantly, the Major broke in: “Why should not we three bachelors beg these ladies to join us at Greenwich? Is Lady Clavering to be hospitable forever, and we make no return? Speak for yourselves, young men—eh, begad! Here’s my nephew with pockets full of money—his pockets full, begad! And Mr. Foker, who’s well-off—how is your cousin, Lady Ann, Mr. Foker?”

“Here are two young men,” the Major continued, “who allow an old fellow like me to speak. Lady Clavering, will you do me the honour of being my guest? And Miss Blanche shall be Arthur’s, if she will be so good.”

“Oh, delightful!” Blanche cried.

“I like a bit of fun too,” said Lady Clavering. “We’ll take a day when Sir Francis——”

“When Sir Francis dines out,” Blanche interrupted. “It will be charming.”

And it was. The dinner at Greenwich was exquisite. Foker, though he didn’t invite Miss Amory, had plenty of chances to talk with her—during the meal, on the balcony, and later in the barouche ride home.

Pen came down with his uncle in Sir Hugh Trumpington’s brougham, borrowed for the occasion.

“I’m an old soldier, begad,” the Major said. “I learned early to make myself comfortable.”

And being an old soldier, he let the young men pay for dinner. On the way back, he teased Pen about Miss Amory’s obvious affection.

“Beautiful girl,” he said. “Good looks, wit, spirit. And in strict confidence—she’s richer than people think.”

And thus ended the chapter—full of laughter, longing, and the eternal folly of young men chasing hearts they cannot claim.
