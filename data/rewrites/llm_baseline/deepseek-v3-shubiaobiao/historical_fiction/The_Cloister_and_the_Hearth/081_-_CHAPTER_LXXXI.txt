Catherine fixed her husband with a steady gaze. “Eli,” she said, “answer me one question like a man, and I’ll trouble you no more today. What is wormwood?”

Eli hesitated, caught off guard by the sudden demand, but soon rallied. “Something bitter as gall,” he replied.

“A fair answer,” Catherine conceded, “but not the one I seek.”

“Then answer it yourself,” Eli grumbled.

“So I shall,” she said. “Wormwood is—to have two idle hands in the house, wearing out thy shoes and mine while they wait for us to die.”

Eli groaned. The barb struck true.

“Methinks waiting for their own father’s grave, with naught to do but brood, is enough to sour better men than those two. Why not set them up in trade? Give them a chance to mend.”

Eli frowned. “And risk them drinking and gaming away even the shelves beneath their wares?”

“Do you take me for a fool?” Catherine retorted. “I mean to watch them sharp at first, then slacken the rein—as the saying goes.”

“Where would you place them? Not here, to steal our own custom.”

“Rotterdam,” said Catherine firmly. “There’s trade enough for all. And I could see them settled.”

*Ah, self-deception!* The truth, unspoken, was that Rotterdam lay nearer to Gerard.

After much debate—and many hollow promises of reform from Cornelis and Sybrandt—Catherine set out for Rotterdam with Kate in tow, scouring the streets for a suitable shop. They found one, well-situated but in sore need of repair. The disarray worked in their favor, lowering the price, and soon they were scrubbing, polishing, and fitting shelves, transforming the neglected space into a respectable trade-house and dwelling.

Meanwhile, Luke Peterson, ever eager to serve Margaret, would ask, “What can I do for you?”

Her answer was always the same, weary and soft: “Nothing, Luke. Nothing.”

“Surely,” he persisted, “a woman of your wit can think of some task for me?”

“Nothing, Luke. Nothing.”

But at last, she relented. “If you could make something to ease my sweet Kate’s burdens…”

Luke, ever the willing servant, set to work with joy. Soon he presented Kate with a small, cushioned cart, sturdy yet light. At dusk, he would harness himself to it and draw her along the boulevard, Margaret and Catherine walking beside. To passersby, they might have seemed a merry party—but the truth was quieter, sadder.

Kate cradled little Gerard in her lap, her thin face alight with tenderness. The sight—a babe like a rosebud held in the cup of a fading lily—drew jeers from the coarse-minded.

“How’s the taste of thistles, Luke?” they called. “Can your mistress not afford a donkey?”

Luke paid them no heed, but Kate flinched for him.

“You’ve done enough, good Luke,” she said softly. “The cart was kindness itself. But I’ll not have you mocked for drawing me. Let us hire some poor soul to do it.”

Luke shook his head. “Their words are wind. If they vexed me, I’d crack their skulls together. I’ll pull you till my mistress bids me stop.”

“Luke,” Margaret said gently, “if you would please me, obey Kate in this.”

“Then I obey,” he said at once.

Not all scorned him. Joan, Jorian Ketel’s wife, spoke kindly. “Strength spent on the weak is strength well spent. And her prayers will bless you—she has not long in this world. See how she smiles through pain?”

But Luke, simple and true, answered, “I want no prayers. I do it for my mistress—for Margaret.”

Still, he pressed her for tasks, hungry to serve. And one day, worn by his persistence, she turned on him with a look and tone that no words could capture.

“Find me my boy’s father,” she said.
