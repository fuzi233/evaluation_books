That evening, the words that could not be believed were spoken.

Ethne sat in the parlour of her farmhouse, wrapped in silence. Though it was summer, a chill had settled over her, and she lit the fire. She stared into the glowing embers, her stillness betraying the storm within. The moment she had longed for—yet feared—had passed. Harry Feversham was gone. And now, alone in this remote village nestled among the hills, she felt more isolated than ever since Willoughby had sailed away from Salcombe on that August morning.

From the day Willoughby arrived, she had lived for one brief half-hour: the time when Harry would come to see her. That half-hour had come and vanished. Now she understood how deeply she had counted on it, how her very life had been shaped around its arrival. Without it, the world seemed hollow.

Yet she had known this emptiness would come. She had foreseen the ache that would follow his departure—the desperate urge to call him back. But she had also known she must endure it. So she sat by the fire, shivering not just from cold, but from sorrow, reminding herself that tomorrow would bring clarity. Tomorrow, she would compare today’s farewell with the night of the ball at Lennon House—and realize how much smaller this parting truly was.

She wondered what Harry would do now. Would he return to duty? Rise again in rank? Build a name for himself? As she imagined his future, Dermod’s old collie dog nudged her hand, sensing her distress. He was ancient, she thought—soon he would die, leaving her entirely alone. And then, perhaps years would pass before she lay down in her bed and knew the final moment had come.

A knock at the door. A servant announced Colonel Durrance was waiting.

“Yes,” she said softly. When he entered, she rose to meet him—not with hesitation, but with quiet resolve. She stepped out of her private grief, as she had promised herself she would. She spoke to him naturally, discussing their marriage, the rebuilding of Lennon House—anything to keep the world spinning forward. It was hard, but she had grown accustomed to bearing burdens.

Only that night did Durrance make her path heavier. After tea, he asked her to play the Musoline Overture on her violin.

"Not tonight," she replied. "I’m rather tired."

But even as the words left her lips, she changed her mind. She had vowed not to shrink from any task—great or small. The little things, especially, demanded care. They revealed the truth most clearly.

"Still," she said, smiling faintly, "I think I can play it." She took down her violin.

She played through the entire overture—from beginning to end. Durrance stood by the window, facing away, until the last note faded. Then he turned and walked beside her.

"I was rather a brute," he said quietly, "to ask you to play that tonight."

"I wasn’t anxious to," she answered, setting the instrument aside.

"I know. But I wanted to find out something. And I knew no other way."

Her face turned toward him, startled. "What do you mean?"

"You’re so rarely off your guard," he said. "Only when you play—only then do you let go. Once before, when you played this overture, you were unguarded. I remembered that. I thought—if I could get you to play it again tonight, the same piece once heard in a dingy café at Wadi Halfa—I might catch you off balance."

The breath left her. Her cheeks drained of colour. He couldn’t know. It was impossible. He didn’t know.

But Durrance continued, calm and steady.
"Well? Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?"

To Ethne, these were the unthinkable words. She stared at him, stunned. Had he really said them? For a moment, she thought her senses had betrayed her. But he repeated the question. There was no escape from the truth.

"Who told you about a fourth feather?" she whispered.

"Trench," he replied. "I met him at Dover. But he only told me of the fourth. I already knew of the three. Trench wouldn’t have mentioned the fourth if I hadn’t known the others. If I hadn’t asked, 'Where is Harry Feversham?' And I knew because Captain Willoughby came to The Pool with the first feather. That was enough."

"How do you know?" she cried, voice breaking. He stepped close and gently took her arm.

"But since I know," he said, "what does it matter how?"

He had known for months—ever since Willoughby brought the first feather. He had waited to tell her until Harry returned. And Harry had come today.

Ethne sank back into her chair. The shock was overwhelming. She had guarded her secret so fiercely, believing it untouched. To learn that it had been known all along—known for a year—was like a blow to the heart.

Yet even in confusion, she knew she needed time to gather herself. So she asked the trivial, to buy moments.

"You were in the church, then? Or did you hear us on the steps? Or did you meet him as he rode away?"

"Not one of those," Durrance said, smiling.

She had chosen the right distraction. He had vanities, like any man—especially one who had lost his sight. Since becoming blind, he had taken pride in his sharp perception. It pleased him to uncover truths others expected him to miss, to reveal them with quiet certainty.

"Not one of your guesses is right, Ethne," he said, inviting her to press further.

"So how did you find out?" she asked.

"I knew from Trench that Harry would return soon. This afternoon, I passed the church. Your collie barked at me—so I knew you were inside. But there was a saddled horse tied to the gate. Someone else was with you—someone not from the village. Then I asked you to play. And that told me who rode it."

"Yes," she murmured, barely listening. "Yes, I see."

Then, in a voice clear and composed, she said:

"You went to Wiesbaden for a year. You left just after Captain Willoughby came. Was that why?"

"I went," he said, "because neither of us could keep up the pretense any longer. You pretended not to care whether Harry lived or died. I pretended not to see that you cared more than anything. One of us would have broken eventually. I couldn’t fail. Nor could you. I couldn’t let you live a year believing two lives had been ruined for my sake—when you’d said, 'Two lives must not be spoilt because of me.' And you couldn’t let me, who said 'Marriage between a blind man and a woman is only possible when there is more than friendship,' know that on one side there was only friendship. We were too close to failing. So I left."

"You didn’t fail," she said quietly. "It was only I who failed."

She blamed herself bitterly. She had sworn to protect him from knowing the truth—that he was being married out of pity. She had failed. He had protected her from knowing she had failed. And that was worse.

It wasn’t shame that cut her—it was guilt. Because by failing, she had robbed him of his last chance at happiness.

"But it wasn’t your fault," he said. "You were off guard a few times—once, when you played the overture. But the real clues weren’t found that way. That night, when Willoughby brought news, your music carried joy. I felt it. But the truth came slowly, through signs, not emotions. You should be glad I found out."

"Glad?" she echoed.

"For my sake," he said. "Two lives shouldn’t be spoiled because of you. But if I hadn’t discovered the truth, not two—but three—lives would have been ruined. Yours. Harry’s. Mine."

"Three?"

"Yes. Yours. Because you would have sacrificed your own happiness to spare me. Harry’s, because he would have watched you marry a man you didn’t love. And mine—because I would have lived knowing I was loved only out of duty. A fine compensation—sitting across from you, knowing you married a cripple out of pity, and that in doing so, you crippled yourself. But now—"

"Whereas now?" she asked.

"I remain your friend," he said gently. "And I would rather be that than your unloved husband."

She said nothing. The decision had been made—for her, by him.

"You sent Harry away this afternoon," Durrance said. "You said goodbye twice."

At "twice," she looked up sharply.

"Once in the church," he explained. "Again—on your violin."

He picked up the instrument from the chair where she had placed it.

"It has been a good friend," he said. "A true friend to all of us. Soon, Ethne, you’ll understand. I stood at the window while you played. I’ve never heard anything so sad—and yet so noble. True music doesn’t complain."

He laid the violin back down.

"I’m sending a messenger to Rathmullen. Harry cannot cross Lough Swilly tonight. But he will return tomorrow."

It had been a day of unbearable emotion. As Durrance bent toward her, he saw tears tracing silent paths down her cheeks. For once, they were allowed to fall.

He took his cap and moved quietly toward the door.

"Don’t go yet," she said, rising from the hearth and stepping into the center of the room. "The oculist in Wiesbaden... did he give you hope?"

Durrance paused, weighing the truth against the lie.

"No," he said at last. "There is no hope. But I am not helpless. I can move about. Perhaps one day I’ll journey far—among strange lands, strange people."

He left the house, carrying his purpose. He had learned his lesson long ago—and the violin had taught it. That afternoon, it had spoken again. Not with the same voice, but with the same message.

True music does not complain.
