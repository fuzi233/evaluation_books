The storm rolled in with the bruised colors of olive and silver, pressing down upon the grey valley until the strange castle of Glengyle loomed like a dead end carved into the earth. It halted the glen abruptly, a blind alley of stone that seemed to mark the edge of the known world. Its steep roofs and spires, clad in seagreen slate, mimicked the architecture of old French-Scottish châteaux, yet to an English eye they resembled the sinister witch-hats of fairy tales. The pine forests rocking around the green turrets looked, by contrast, as black as countless flocks of ravens. This atmosphere of dreamy, almost sleepy devilry was not merely aesthetic; it rested upon the land like a cloud of pride, madness, and mysterious sorrow, weighing heavier on Scottish noble houses than on any other lineage. Scotland possesses a double dose of the poison called heredity: the aristocrat's obsession with bloodline and the Calvinist's crushing sense of doom.

Father Brown had seized a single day from his duties in Glasgow to join his friend Flambeau, the amateur detective, who was stationed at Glengyle alongside Inspector Craven of Scotland Yard. They were investigating the life and sudden disappearance of the late Earl of Glengyle. This enigmatic figure was the last survivor of a race whose valor, insanity, and violent cunning had rendered them terrifying even among the sinister nobility of the sixteenth century. Few understood the labyrinthine ambitions woven into the palace of lies constructed around Mary Queen of Scots.

The local rhymes attested to the motives and results of their machinations: *As green sap to the simmer trees / Is red gold to the Ogilvies.*

For centuries, no decent lord had ever ruled from Glengyle Castle. By Victorian standards, one might assume all eccentricities had been exhausted, yet the last Earl satisfied his tribal tradition by performing the only act remaining for him: vanishing. He did not flee abroad; accounts placed him still within the castle walls, provided he existed anywhere at all. Though his name remained in the church registers and the peerage lists, no living soul had seen him under the sun.

Only a solitary manservant claimed to have witnessed him—a gaunt, red-haired laborer named Israel Gow, described variously as a groom, a gardener, or a mute due to severe deafness. Others suspected him of partial imbecility. Yet, Gow possessed a dogged jaw and blank blue eyes, working with such relentless energy digging potatoes and disappearing regularly into the kitchen that he projected the image of feeding a hidden superior. When society required proof of the Earl's presence, Gow simply insisted the master was not at home. On one occasion, the provost and the minister were summoned to the castle only to discover that Gow, acting as undertaker, had nailed the nobleman into a coffin. Whether this indicated murder or madness remained unclear until Flambeau arrived from London a few days prior. By then, the body—or what passed for it—had lain undisturbed in the hillside churchyard.

As Father Brown traversed the dim garden beneath the looming chateau, the air grew thick and thundery. Against the fading green-gold sunset, a black silhouette emerged: a man in a chimney-pot hat carrying a large spade. The image was strangely reminiscent of a sexton, though Father Brown recognized the practical necessity of the attire for a peasant engaged in an official inquiry, unwilling to waste an hour of digging without proper dress. The man's startled, suspicious glare as the priest passed aligned perfectly with the vigilance typical of such a type.

The great doors were opened by Flambeau himself, accompanied by Inspector Craven, a lean man with iron-grey hair clutching documents. The entrance hall was largely stripped bare, save for the pale, sneering visages of the wicked Ogilvies watching down from black periwigs and decaying canvases. Inside an inner room, the detectives sat at a long oak table cluttered with scribbled notes, whisky, and cigars. Stretching along the remainder of the table were detached objects arranged at irregular intervals, items as inexplicable as any artifact could be. One resembled a small pile of glittering broken glass; another, a high mound of brown dust; a third, a plain stick of wood.

"You seem to have established a sort of geological museum here," Father Brown remarked, settling into a chair and nodding toward the dust and crystalline fragments.

"Not a geological museum," Flambeau corrected with a smile. "Say a psychological museum."

"Oh, for the Lord's sake," Craven laughed, "let's avoid such pretentious jargon."

"Do you not comprehend the meaning of psychology?" Flambeau asked, feigning surprise. "It simply means being off one's chump."

"I still fail to grasp the point," Craven replied.

"Very well," Flambeau decided. "We have deduced only one thing regarding Lord Glengyle: he was a maniac."

At that moment, the black silhouette of Gow passed the window, dimly outlined against the darkening sky. Father Brown watched him passively. "I can understand there must have been something peculiar about the man, or he would not have buried himself alive, nor rushed so urgently to bury himself dead. But what leads you to conclude it was lunacy?"

"Listen to the inventory Inspector Craven has compiled," Flambeau urged.

"We require illumination," Craven interjected suddenly. "A storm is brewing, and visibility is nil."

"Have you located any candles among your curiosities?" Brown asked, smiling.

Flambeau adopted a grave expression, fixing his dark eyes on his friend. "That is equally curious. Twenty-five candles, not a single candlestick in sight."

As the room plunged into rapid darkness and the wind howled outside, Brown moved to retrieve a bundle of wax candles scattered among the other exhibits. Bending accidentally over the heap of reddish-brown dust, he let out a sharp sneeze that shattered the silence.

"Hullo!" he exclaimed. "Snuff!"

He selected a candle, lit it carefully, and jammed it into the neck of a whisky bottle. The restless night air, streaming through the fractured window, waved the long flame like a banner. Beyond the castle walls, miles of black pine forest seethed like a dark ocean surrounding a rock.

"I shall read the inventory," Craven began solemnly, unfolding a document. "These items were found loose and unexplained within the castle. Generally, the premises were dismantled and neglected, yet specific rooms had clearly been inhabited in a simple, non-squalid manner by someone other than the servant Gow. The list follows:"

"Item One: A substantial hoard of precious stones, nearly all diamonds, entirely loose and unset. Naturally, the Ogilvies possess family jewels, yet these are typically set in ornaments. The Ogilvies appear to have stored theirs loosely in their pockets, like copper coins."

"Item Two: Heaps of loose snuff, neither contained in horns nor pouches, but scattered on mantelpieces, sideboards, and pianos. It suggests the occupant lacked the inclination to check pockets or lift lids."

"Item Three: Scattered throughout the house are tiny piles of minute metal fragments—some resembling steel springs, others microscopic wheels, as if derived from dismantled mechanical toys."

"Item Four: The aforementioned wax candles, necessitating insertion into bottle necks due to the absence of holders. Note how profoundly bizarre this entire assemblage is compared to our initial hypotheses. We are prepared for the central riddle; we recognize immediately that something is wrong with the last Earl. We seek to determine if he truly lived here, truly died here, or if the red-haired scarecrow named Gow played a role in his demise. However, consider the most lurid solutions: suppose the servant murdered the master, or the master is not dead, or the master is disguised as the servant, or the servant is interred for the master. Invent a Wilkie Collins tragedy, and you still cannot explain a candle without a holder, nor why a gentleman of good breeding would habitually spill snuff onto a piano. We can imagine the core narrative, but the fringes remain inexplicable. No amount of imaginative stretching connects snuff, diamonds, wax, and loose clockwork."

"I perceive the connection," the priest stated calmly. "Lord Glengyle was a fanatic opposed to the French Revolution, an enthusiast for the Ancien Régime attempting to reenact the domestic life of the last Bourbons. He utilized snuff as an eighteenth-century luxury; wax candles for eighteenth-century illumination; the iron fragments represent the locksmith hobbies of Louis XVI; the diamonds symbolize the Diamond Necklace of Marie Antoinette."

Both men stared at him with widened eyes. "What an extraordinary notion!" Flambeau cried. "Do you genuinely believe this is the truth?"

"I am absolutely certain it is not," Father Brown answered. "Yet you admitted that no one could link snuff with diamonds, clockwork, and candles. I offer that connection instantly. The actual truth lies far deeper."

He paused, listening to the wailing wind in the turrets. "The late Earl of Glengyle was a thief. He led a second, darker existence as a desperate housebreaker. He possessed no candlesticks because he used these candles, cut short, within the small lantern he carried. He employed snuff as the fiercest French criminals utilize pepper: to cast it suddenly in dense masses into the face of a captor or pursuer. The definitive proof lies in the curious coincidence of the diamonds and the small steel wheels. Does that clarify matters? Diamonds and small steel wheels are the only two instruments capable of cutting a pane of glass."
