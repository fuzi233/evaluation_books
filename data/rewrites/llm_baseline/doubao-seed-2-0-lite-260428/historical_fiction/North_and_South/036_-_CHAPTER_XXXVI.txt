At the time they had agreed the day before, Margaret Hale and her father set out to walk to Nicholas Higgins’ cottage. The stiff, unyielding feel of their new mourning clothes reminded both of them of the sharp, fresh loss of Mrs Hale; it was the first time in weeks they had deliberately gone out together, and they drew close without a word, bound by quiet, unspoken sympathy.

Nicholas was sat in his usual spot by the fire, but his pipe was nowhere in sight, his head propped on his hand, his elbow resting heavy on his knee. He did not rise when he saw them, though Margaret could read the faint, tired welcome in his eyes.
“Sit ye down, sit ye down. Fire’s welly out,” he said, giving the embers a vicious poke as if to draw attention away from his own red, puffy eyes. He looked unkempt, his dark beard unshaven for three days making his pale, worn face look even more gaunt, his frayed jacket in sore need of patching.
“We thought we would be sure to find you just after dinner time,” Margaret said softly.
“We have had our own sorrows, too, since last we saw you,” Mr Hale added.
“Ay, ay. Sorrows is far more plentiful than dinners just now. I reckon my dinner hour stretches all o’er the day — yo’re pretty much guaranteed to find me home,” Higgins said.
“Are you out of work?” Margaret asked.
He nodded shortly. After a beat of silence, he looked up for the first time. “I’m not wanting brass, mind. Dunnot you think that. Poor Bess left a little sum stashed under her pillow, ready to slip into my hand at the last, and Mary’s got steady work cutting fustian. But I’m out of work all the same.”
“We owe Mary some money for all her kind help with my wife,” Mr Hale began, before Margaret could dig her elbow into his arm to stop him.
“If she takes it, I’ll turn her out of doors this minute. I’ll bide inside these four walls, and she’ll bide outside. That’s all there is to it,” Higgins snapped.
“But we owe her so much gratitude for her care,” Mr Hale protested.
“I ne’er thanked your daughter there for all she did for my poor lass Bess. I could never find the right words. I’d have to start trying now, if yo’ keep making a fuss over the little work Mary did for your lady.”
“Is it because of the strike you cannot find work?” Margaret asked gently.
“The strike’s ended. O’er for this time. I’m out o’ work because I will not beg for it. And I will not beg, because good words are scarce in this world, and bad words are everywhere.” He was in a surly, half-defiant mood, half-pleased to speak in riddles, but Margaret could see he was eager to be pressed for an explanation.
“Good words, you mean?”
“Asking for work. That’s the best words a man can say. ‘Gi’ me work’ means ‘I’ll do it like a man.’ Those are good words.”
“And the bad words are bosses turning you away when you ask,” Margaret said.
“Ay. Bad words are them saying ‘Aha, my fine chap! You’ve stood true to your union men, and I’ll stand true to mine. You did all you could for the folks who needed help — that’s your way of being faithful to your kind. I’ll be faithful to mine. You’re a poor fool who didn’t know better than to stand by your mates. So go to hell. There’s no work for you here.’ Those are the bad words. I’m no fool — and if I was, folk ought to have taught me how to be wise by their rules. I might have learnt, if anyone had ever bothered to try.”
“Would it not be worth the attempt to ask your old master to take you back?” Mr Hale suggested. “It may be a slim chance, but it is a chance all the same.”
Higgins glanced up at him sharply, then let out a low, bitter laugh.
“Measter! No offence, but let me ask yo’ a few questions in turn.”
“Ask away, you are perfectly welcome,” Mr Hale smiled.
“I reckon you have some way to earn your bread. Folk don’t live in Milton for pleasure, if they can live anywhere else.”
“You are quite right. I have a small independent income, but I settled here to work as a private tutor.”
“Teaching folk. Well, they pay you for that work, don’t they?”
“Yes,” Mr Hale nodded. “I teach to be paid.”
“And the folks who pay you — do they tell you what you can or cannot do with the money they give you as fair payment for your pains? Do they say, ‘You might have a friend as dear as a brother who needs that money for a purpose you both think right, but you must promise never to give it to him. We do not think that use of your money is good, so if you spend it that way, we will stop giving you work entirely.’ Do they say that?”
“Of course not.”
“Would you stand for it if they did?”
“It would take the harshest possible pressure to make me even consider submitting to such dictation,” Mr Hale said.
“There is no pressure on all this earth that would make me,” Higgins declared. “Now you see it. Hampers Mill, that’s where I worked before — they make every man sign a pledge promising he will never give a penny to the union, never feed a starving locked-out turn-out. They can make men sign all the pledges they like,” he scoffed, “all it makes is liars and hypocrites. And that is a smaller sin, in my mind, than making men’s hearts so hard they will not do a kindness to a neighbour in need, or help a cause they know is just, even if it defies the bosses. I will never forswear myself for any work the king himself could offer me. I am a union man. I believe it is the only thing that will ever do working people any good. I have been a turn-out, I have known what it is to starve. If any union man asks me for a shilling, sixpence of it is his. Consequence is, I cannot get a single shilling’s work anywhere in Milton.”
“Is that anti-union pledge rule in force at all the mills?” Margaret asked.
“I cannot say. It’s new at our mill, and I reckon they will find soon enough they cannot keep enforcing it. But it’s law right now. Tyrants always end up making liars of everyone around them, sooner or later.”
There was a short silence. Margaret hesitated, reluctant to irritate a man who was already worn thin with grief and hopelessness, but the thought would not stay buried. She spoke softly, slowly, so he would not take offence.
“Do you remember poor John Boucher saying the union was a tyrant? That it was the worst tyrant of all? I agreed with him, when he said it.”
It was a long time before Higgins replied. He rested his head between his two hands, staring down into the fire, so she could not read his face.
“I will not deny that the union sometimes has to force a man to act for his own good. I will speak the truth. A man leads a dreary, lonely life if he is not in the union. But once he is in, his interests are cared for far better than he could ever look after them by himself. It is the only way working men can get their rights, all joining together as one. The more members we have, the more chance every single separate man has of getting justice. The government takes care of fools and madmen, and if a man is likely to hurt himself or his neighbour, it puts a check on him, whether he likes it or no. That is all we do in the union. We cannot throw people in prison — but we can make a man’s life so heavy to bear that he has to join, and be wise and helpful in spite of himself. Boucher was a fool from the start, and never more a fool than at the end.”
“Did he do you harm?” Margaret asked.
“Aye, that he did. We had public opinion on our side, right up until he and his lot started rioting and breaking windows. The strike was lost, then and there.”
“Then would it not have been far better to leave him alone, not force him to join the union against his will? He did you no good, and you drove him mad.”
“Margaret!” Mr Hale said, a low warning tone, for he saw the dark, angry cloud rise on Higgins’ face.
“I like her,” Higgins said, suddenly looking up. “She speaks plain what is in her mind. She does not understand the union, though. It is a great power — our only power. I once read a bit of poetry about a plough driving over a daisy, that made tears come to my eyes before I had any other reason to cry. But the man never stopped driving that plough, I will warrant, no matter how sorry he felt for the daisy. He had too much common sense for that. The union is that plough, turning over the land to get it ready for the harvest. Men like Boucher — it would be too much of a compliment to call him a daisy, he is more a weed sprawling over the ground — they just have to be torn up and moved out of the way. I am still so angry with him I may not be speaking fair about him. I could drive that plough over him myself right now, and take pleasure in it.”
“Why? What has he done now? Anything new?”
“Aye, of course. He is never out of mischief, that man. First he goes raging like a mad fool and kicks up that riot that lost us the strike. Then he runs off and hides, where he would still be if Thornton had followed through and prosecuted him like I hoped he would. But Thornton got what he wanted out of the strike, so he did not bother chasing Boucher down. So he slunk back home. He did not show his face in the street for a day or two, at least he had that much sense. And then — where do you think he went? Straight to Hamper’s mill. Damn him! He turned up there with his mealy-mouthed face that makes me sick to look at, begging for work, even though he knew full well about the new pledge rule, that you cannot give a penny to starving turn-outs. He would have starved to death months back if the union had not helped him in his pinch. And there he was, offering to promise anything, pledge anything — tell every last secret he knew about our union meetings — the good-for-nothing Judas! But I will say this for Hamper, and thank him for it on my dying day — he drove Boucher away, would not listen to a single word of it. Folk standing by say the traitor cried like a baby when he was turned out.”
“Oh! How shocking! How pitiful!” Margaret exclaimed. “Higgins, I do not recognise you today. Do you not see you made Boucher what he is, by forcing him into the union against his will, without his whole heart going with it? You made him what he is!”

Made him what he is? What was he?
A low, slow, measured tramp of feet drifted down the narrow street, growing louder, forcing itself into their awareness. Many voices were hushed, soft; many footsteps shuffled not forward fast, but circling, clustering around one single spot. There was one distinct, heavy rhythm, the laboured walk of men carrying a great weight.
They were all drawn to the cottage door by some unshakable, solemn impulse, not by idle curiosity, but as if a cold gale of sorrow had blown through the street and pulled them all outside.
Six men walked down the middle of the road, three of them uniformed policemen. They carried a door prized off its hinges across their shoulders, and a dead man lay stretched out on it. Water dripped steadily from the edges of the wood, leaving dark wet splotches on the cobblestones. The whole street poured out to see, and once they saw, they joined the procession, every person questioning the bearers, who repeated their tale over and over until they answered almost reluctantly.
“We found him in the brook in the field past the end of the lane.”
“The brook? There is not enough water there to drown a man!”
“He was a determined chap. Laid face down in the shallow water. He was sick enough of living, no matter what made him that way.”
Higgins crept up to Margaret’s side, his voice thin and reedy.
“It is not John Boucher? He had not the spunk for that. Sure, it is not John Boucher! Why are they all walking this way? There is a singing noise in my head, I cannot hear.”
They set the door down gently on the cobblestones, and everyone could see the poor drowned man. His glassy eyes, one half-open, stared straight up at the sky. His face was swollen and discoloured from being face down in the water, his skin stained dark by the dye waste the mills poured into that brook. The front of his head was bald, his thin long hair at the back soaked through, every separate lock dripping dark water.
Through all that disfigurement, Margaret recognised John Boucher. It felt sacrilegious to stand gaping at that twisted, agonised dead face, and on instinct she stepped forward, pulled her linen handkerchief from her pocket, and gently covered his countenance. The crowd followed her as she stepped back, and their gazes landed on Nicholas Higgins, rooted to the spot where he stood.
One of the men stepped up to him. “Higgins, you knew him. You have to go tell the wife. Do it gentle, man, but do it quick — we cannot leave him here out in the street much longer.”
“I cannot go,” Higgins said, shrinking back towards his door. “Dunnot ask me. I cannot face her.”
“You know her best,” the man said. “We did the hard part bringing him all this way. It is your turn to do your share.”
“I cannot do it,” Higgins repeated, his voice breaking. “I am welly felled just looking at him. We was not friends. And now he is dead.”
“Well, if you will not, you will not. But someone has to. Every minute that passes is a chance for her to hear the news rough and cruel, from some stranger, instead of someone who can break it to her soft.”
“Papa, you go,” Margaret said, turning to her father, but she saw him trembling from head to foot, his face as white as a sheet. He could not do it, not all at once. “I will go,” she said.
“Bless you, miss, that is a kind act. She has been a sickly, fragile thing these past months, and few folks around here know her well,” one of the bystanders said.
Margaret knocked at the Boucher’s closed door, but the noise of half a dozen small, unsupervised children drowned out her knock. She doubted she had been heard, and the longer she waited, the more her courage faltered, so she turned the latch and stepped inside, shutting the door behind her and bolting it unseen, to keep the crowd of gawkers out.
Mrs Boucher sat in her rocking chair on the far side of the cold, messy fireplace; the house looked as if no one had cleaned or tidied it for days. Margaret’s throat was so dry she could barely speak, and the children’s chatter drowned out her first attempt at greeting.
“How are you, Mrs Boucher? I am afraid you are very poorly,” she tried again.
“I have every right to be poorly,” the woman snapped peevishly. “I am left all alone to look after these children, and there is not a scrap of food in the house to keep them quiet. John should never have left me, not when I am this sick.”
“How long has he been gone?” Margaret asked.
“Four days. No one would give him work here, so he set off walking towards Greenfield to look for it. He ought to have been back by now, or sent word he found work. He ought to —”
“Oh, do not blame him,” Margaret cut her off. “He suffered very deeply, I am sure —”
“Will you hush your noise and let the lady speak!” she snapped at a tiny toddler at her feet, then turned back to Margaret apologetically. “He is always whinging for daddy and bread, and I have no bread to give him, and daddy is away and forgotten all of us, I think. He is his father’s darling,” she added, her mood softening all at once, as she dragged the little boy up onto her knee and kissed his hair.
Margaret laid her hand on the woman’s arm to get her attention. Their eyes met.
“Poor little fellow,” Margaret said slowly. “He was his father’s darling.”
“He is his father’s darling,” Mrs Boucher said, standing up so fast her chair scraped across the floor. They stared at each other in silence for a long moment. Then the woman’s voice rose in a low, wild growl, shaking Margaret’s arm as she demanded: “He is his father’s darling, I say. Poor folk can love their children as well as rich folks. Why will you not speak? Why do you stare at me with your great pitying eyes? Where is John?”
A second later the meaning of Margaret’s tearful look crashed over her. She cried out, “Oh my God,” and sank back into the rocking chair. Margaret picked up the toddler and set him in her arms.
“He loved him,” Margaret said.
“Ay,” the woman nodded, tears spilling down her face at last. “He loved all of us. We had someone to love us once. It feels like a long time ago now, but when he was home with us, he loved us. He loved this baby the best, I think. I was calling him a useless fool not five minutes ago. Are you sure he is dead?” She tried to stagger to her feet. “If he is only ill, not drowned for good, they can still bring him round. I am ailing too, I have been for months —”
“But he is dead. He is drowned.”
“Folks get brought back to life after drowning all the time! What am I doing sitting still? Where has all my strength gone? John — my husband —”
Margaret caught her as she fainted, easing her down into the rocking chair, holding her limp form against her shoulder
