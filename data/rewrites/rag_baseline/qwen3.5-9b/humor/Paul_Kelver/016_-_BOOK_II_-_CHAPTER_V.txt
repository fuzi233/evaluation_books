On a sweet grey morning, the future arrived for Paul, not with the thunder of destiny, but with the quiet rustle of a shared breakfast table. Across from him sat Dan, a man whose life had been a kaleidoscope of improbable turns, each fragment reflecting a different facet of the human condition. Over their supper, they exchanged histories, weaving a tapestry of similarities that bridged the gap between their disparate paths. Dan had left school years ahead of Paul, destined for Cambridge, yet fate, in its capricious humor, had intervened. Two years later, the death of his father, compounded by a wound sustained in the Indian Mutiny—a scar that refused to heal—had severed his academic thread prematurely.

"You might not have expected that to grieve me," Dan said, a wry smile playing on his lips, though his eyes betrayed a depth of loss. "But it was a severe blow. At Cambridge, I discovered I was by temperament a scholar. The tragedy of my schooling was that learning had been deliberately rendered uninteresting. We were treated like tourists in a gallery, rushed through exhibits with the sole objective of checking off names rather than comprehending art. We were stuffed with facts like Strasbourg geese, plump and silent, rather than fed on knowledge until we grew strong."

His journey home led him to London, where he secured a banking position through a connection with his late father's friend. Yet, the deadly monotony of the ledger appalled him. Upon his mother's passing, he shook the cloying dust of the City from his brain and threw himself into the chaotic embrace of a small theatrical troupe. For eighteen months, he inhabited every role imaginable—from Romeo to the meddling Paul Pry—and performed auxiliary duties ranging from painting scenery to the perilous art of bill-posting. He recalled the peculiar physics of the trade: the paste-laden posters possessed an innate rebelliousness, frequently recoiling to adhere stubbornly to the amateur's own forehead. Wearied of the stage, he drifted into the circus as Signor Ricardo, the daring bareback rider, and later as one of the Brothers Roscius in a trapeze act. Seeking respectability once more, he served as a waiter in Ostend, then a footman, sampling the flavors of a thousand callings.

"I never know," Dan mused, swirling his tea. "Perhaps I shall become a society novelist; if so, my intimate knowledge of the aristocracy will grant me a distinct edge over my competitors."

His resume expanded further: tramping through Ireland with a fiddle, lecturing on Palestine across Scotland with dissolving views, marking billiards, teaching school, and finally, for the last three months, serving as a dramatic and musical critic for a Sunday newspaper. Paul had often dreamed of such a perch.

"How did you obtain it?" Paul asked.

"The idea struck me," Dan recounted, leaning forward. "Late one afternoon, sauntering down the Strand with my pockets light and my prospects dimmer, I wandered into the first newspaper office I encountered. Without knocking, I ascended to the first floor, pushed open a door into a small antechamber, and stepped into a larger room cluttered with books and papers. Darkness was falling. A gentleman of strikingly youthful appearance was pacing frantically, cursing himself for three calamities: spilled ink, missing matches, and a broken bell-pull. In the gloom, mistaking him for a junior clerk, I decided to play a prank—I assumed the role of the editor. I lit the gas, fetched him fresh ink, and engaged him in conversation. He was a slim youth, barely twenty-six, whose voice carried the timbre of a schoolboy, his position owed entirely to the fact that his aunt owned the paper. He seemed burdened by a general sense of grievance.

"'People flood this office,' he lamented, 'treating it as a shelter from the rain—strangers I know not from Adam. That fool downstairs lets them march straight up, men writing about safety valves or kicking up rows about trivialities. Half my work is done on the stairs.'"

I advised him to require visitors write their business on slips of paper. He agreed. Moments later, another knock echoed.

"I won't see him!" he cried, his composure fracturing. "Send everyone away!"

I moved to the door. An elderly gentleman attempted to sweep past, but I barred his path, closing the editorial door firmly behind him. Surprised, he was informed that the editor was unavailable and presented with a blank sheet for his message. I remained in that ante-chamber for half an hour, dispatching approximately a dozen individuals. Their concerns seemed trivial, for none reached the editor's ear afterward. The final visitor was a weary gentleman puffing a cigarette. When I asked for his name, he looked at me in bewilderment and replied, "Idiot!"

Undeterred, I held my ground. "'It's a bit awkward,'" he retorted. "Couldn't you make an exception for the sub-editor on press night?"

"That contradicts my instructions," I replied.

"Oh, all right," he conceded. "I just wanted to know who is attending the Royalty tonight. It's seven o'clock."

A spark ignited in my mind. If the sub-editor lacked knowledge of the evening's theater-goers, the post of dramatic critic must be vacant.

"Oh, that's settled," I declared confidently. "I shall be in time."

He seemed neither pleased nor annoyed. "Have you arranged with the Governor?"

"I am merely awaiting a brief audience," I lied smoothly. "It will be fine. Do you have the ticket?"

"Haven't seen it," he admitted.

"About a column?" I probed.

"Three-quarters," he preferred, and departed.

The moment he vanished, I descended to meet a printer's boy ascending the stairs. "What is your sub-editor's name?" I asked, describing a tall man with a black moustache looking exhausted. "Oh, you mean Penton," the boy clarified. "That's the name; I couldn't recall it."

Walking straight back into the irritable editor, I demanded the ticket for the Royalty Theatre. When he claimed ignorance of its location, I located it on his desk. "Who is going?" he asked.

"I am," I stated simply, and I went.

To this day, they have never uncovered that I appointed myself. Penton believes I am a distant relative of the proprietress, treating me with marked deference. Mrs. Wallace herself, the owner, thinks I am Penton's discovery, trusting his judgment implicitly; consequently, our relationship flourishes. The paper struggles slightly, and the salary is modest, yet sufficient. Journalism suits my temperament, and I foresee a long tenure here.

"You have been something of a rolling stone thus far," I observed.

He laughed. "From the stone's perspective," he countered, "I never saw the virtue of being smothered in moss. I prefer remaining the stone, unhidden, mobile, and aware of my surroundings."

Turning to my own trajectory, he noted that my opera, ironically dubbed "Goggles" by the gods, would likely run through the winter. "Are you receiving a salary?"

"Thirty shillings a week," I explained, "with full pay for matinees."

"Two pounds," he corrected cheerfully. "With my three, we could establish our own household. I have an original notion. Shall we develop it together?"

My enthusiasm was fervent. "Nothing would please me more."

"There are four delightful rooms in Queen's Square," he continued, his eyes lighting up. "Charmingly furnished: a fine sitting-room facing the street, with two bedrooms and a kitchen behind. The previous tenant was a Polish revolutionary who, three months ago, foolishly ventured back to Russia and now resides rent-free. The landlord is an eccentric old fellow named Deleglise, an engraver who occupies the rest of the house. He has offered us the rooms for thirty shillings weekly—normally worth three or four pounds—on the condition that we manage everything ourselves. He is a singular character; he detests petticoats, particularly elderly ones. He employs only one servant, an old Frenchwoman who was formerly his mother's housekeeper, and the two of them perform all domestic duties, mostly arguing over them. No other female enters the threshold, not even a casual cleaner. However, it is a magnificent Georgian house, adorned with Adams mantelpieces, a stone staircase, and oak-paneled rooms. Our domain would be the entire second floor: no pianos, no landlady. He is a widower with a daughter, perhaps fourteen or fifteen. What do you say? I am a competent cook; will you accept the dual role of householder and parlour-maid?"

Pressed by no urgency, a week later we were installed. For nearly two years, we dwelt there. Risking offense to a certain adorable yet sensitive gender, I must confess, based on undeniable evidence, that without female assistance or supervision, we navigated those two years successfully and emerged intact. Dan had not exaggerated. I have never tasted plain cooking superior to his; a cup of coffee, an omelette, or deviled kidneys prepared by him is a rarity in my culinary experience. Had he confined his efforts to his natural talents, history would record only continuous, monotonous success. But stirred by dangerous ambition at the call of occasional tea parties, and lured by the example of Mr. Deleglise—a man who had cultivated cooking as a hobby for twenty years—Dan occasionally ventured into experimentation.
