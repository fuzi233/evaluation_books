"Work grew scarce, while bread grew dear, And wages lessened, too; For Irish hordes were bidders here, Our half-paid work to do." — *Corn Law Rhymes*

Margaret was ushered into the drawing-room. The room had reverted to its usual state of disarray, draped in heavy coverings. The windows stood half-open against the stifling heat, the Venetian blinds casting a gray, grim light that reflected from the pavement below. This distorted illumination, mixed with the greenish hue filtering from above, rendered even Margaret's own reflection in the mirror ghastly and wan. She sat and waited, but no one appeared. Occasionally, the wind seemed to carry the distant, multitudinous sounds closer, only to die away into a profound, unnerving stillness.

Finally, Fanny entered.

"Mamma will be with you directly, Miss Hale," Fanny began, her voice tight. "She wanted me to apologize for the delay. You likely know my brother has imported laborers from Ireland, which has enraged the Milton locals terribly—as if he lacked the right to hire workers wherever he could. These foolish people here simply refuse to work for him. Consequently, they've terrified these poor Irish starvelings with threats, so we dare not let them out. You'll find them huddled in the top room of the mill; they're sleeping there to protect them from the brutes who won't let them work. Mamma is arranging their food, and John is talking to them, as some of the women are crying to return. Ah! Here comes Mamma!"

Mrs. Thornton entered, her face etched with a black, stern severity that made Margaret feel she had arrived at a disastrous moment. Yet, she had come solely to fulfill her mother's expressed wishes regarding necessities during her illness. Mrs. Thornton's brow furrowed and her mouth set firmly as Margaret spoke gently of her mother's restlessness and Dr. Donaldson's recommendation for a water-bed. Silence stretched between them until Mrs. Thornton abruptly sprang up.

"They're at the gates! Call John, Fanny—bring him in from the mill! They're at the gates! They'll batter them down! Call John, I say!"

Simultaneously, the gathering tramp she had been ignoring in favor of Margaret's words reached the walls just outside. An increasing din of angry voices erupted behind the wooden barrier, shaking it as if the unseen, maddened crowd used their bodies as battering rams. They retreated slightly only to surge forward again with greater unity and steady impetus, their rhythmic pounding making the strong gates quiver like reeds in a gale.

Women gathered round the windows, transfixed by the terrifying scene. Mrs. Thornton, the female servants, and Margaret were all present. Fanny had fled upstairs screaming as if pursued, collapsing onto the sofa in hysterical sobs. Mrs. Thornton scanned the room for her son, who remained in the mill. He emerged, looked up at the pale cluster of faces, and offered a smile of brave encouragement before locking the factory door. He then signaled a servant to unlock his own door, which Fanny had hastily barred in her panic. Mrs. Thornton stepped forward herself.

To the infuriated multitude outside, the sound of his familiar, commanding voice tasted like blood. Until now, they had been voiceless, needing every breath for their strenuous efforts to breach the gates. Now, hearing him speak within, they unleashed a fierce, unearthly groan so intense that even Mrs. Thornton turned white with fear as she led the way into the room. Mr. Thornton entered, slightly flushed, his eyes gleaming with the fire of danger. With a proud look of defiance, he appeared a noble figure, though not necessarily handsome. Margaret, who had always feared her courage might fail her in an emergency, proving her a coward, found herself forgotten in the face of this real, pressing terror. Instead, she felt an intense, painful sympathy for the moment.

Mr. Thornton stepped forward frankly.

"I'm sorry, Miss Hale, you've visited us at this unfortunate hour, when you may be involved in whatever risk we face. Mother! Hadn't you better retire to the back rooms? I'm not certain they haven't breached the path from Pinner's Lane into the stable-yard, but if not, you'd be safer there. Go, Jane!" he commanded the upper servant.

She departed, followed by the others.

"I stay here!" his mother declared. "Where you are, I remain." Indeed, retreat to the back rooms was futile; the crowd had surrounded the outbuildings at the rear, emitting an awful, threatening roar. The servants scrambled into the garrets amidst cries and shrieks. Mr. Thornton smiled scornfully at their panic. He glanced at Margaret, who stood alone by the window nearest the factory. Her eyes glittered, her cheeks and lips deepened in color. Sensing his gaze, she turned to him, voicing a question that had long troubled her mind:

"Where are the poor imported workpeople? Are they in the factory?"

"Yes," he replied. "I left them cowering in a small room at the head of a back staircase, telling them to flee down there if they sensed an attack on the mill doors. But it isn't them they want—it is me."

"When can the soldiers arrive?" his mother asked, her voice low but unsteady.

He consulted his watch with the same steady composure he applied to everything. After a brief calculation, he said, "If Williams left immediately as instructed and didn't have to navigate through the crowd, it will be twenty minutes yet."

"Twenty minutes!" his mother exclaimed, her terror finally breaking through in her tone.

"Shut down the windows instantly, Mother! The gates won't withstand another shock. Close that window, Miss Hale."

Margaret obeyed, then assisted her mother's trembling fingers. For several minutes, there was a tense pause in the unseen street. Mrs. Thornton watched her son's countenance with wild anxiety, seeking an interpretation of the sudden silence. His face was carved into rigid lines of contemptuous defiance; neither hope nor fear could be read there.

Fanny raised herself weakly. "Are they gone?" she whispered.

"Gone!" he replied. "Listen!"

They all heard the single, great strain of breath, the creak of wood slowly giving way, the wrench of iron, and the mighty crash of the ponderous gates falling. Fanny stood up, tottering, took a step toward her mother, and collapsed into her arms in a fainting fit. Mrs. Thornton lifted her with a strength born of sheer will and carried her away.

"Thank God!" Mr. Thornton said, watching her exit. "Would you not prefer to go upstairs, Miss Hale?"

Margaret's lips formed a "No," but he could not hear her, for the tramp of innumerable footsteps pounded right beneath the house wall, accompanied by a fierce growl of low, angry voices possessing a ferocious murmur of satisfaction more dreadful than their baffled cries moments before.

"Never mind!" he said, attempting to encourage her. "I regret that you were trapped in this alarm, but it cannot last long. A few more minutes, and the soldiers will be here."

"Oh, God!" Margaret cried suddenly. "There is Boucher! I recognize his face, though he is livid with rage—he is fighting to reach the front! Look! Look!"

"Who is Boucher?" Mr. Thornton asked coolly, moving close to the window to identify the man causing such concern. Upon seeing him, the crowd set up a yell that defied description—not human, but akin to the demonic hunger of a wild beast denied its prey. Even he drew back momentarily, dismayed by the intensity of hatred he had provoked.

"Let them yell!" he said. "In five minutes more... I only hope my poor Irishmen aren't terrified out of their wits by such fiendish noise. Keep your courage for five minutes, Miss Hale."

"Don't be afraid for me," she said hastily. "But what happens in five minutes? Can you do nothing to soothe these poor creatures? It is awful to witness."

"The soldiers will be here directly, and that will restore their reason."

"To reason!" Margaret snapped. "What kind of reason?"

"The only reason that works on men who turn themselves into wild beasts. By heaven! They've turned to the mill-door!"

"Mr. Thornton," Margaret said, shaking with passion, "go down this instant, if you are not a coward. Go down and face them like a man. Save these poor strangers whom you have decoyed here. Speak to your workmen as if they were human beings. Speak kindly. Do not let the soldiers come in and slaughter poor creatures driven mad. I see one there who is already mad. If you possess any courage or nobility, go out and speak to them, man to man!"

He turned to look at her as she spoke. A dark cloud settled over his face as he listened. He clenched his teeth at her words.

"I will go. Perhaps I may ask you to accompany me downstairs and bar the door behind me; my mother and sister will need that protection."

"Oh! Mr. Thornton! I do not know—I may be wrong—only—"
