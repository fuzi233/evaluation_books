A brief pause is necessary here.

Consulting my own memory—and with Penelope’s journal to assist me—I find little worth recording in the days between Mr. Franklin Blake’s arrival and Miss Rachel’s birthday. With your permission, then, I shall skip ahead, noting only the essential dates before returning to a full account when the matter of the Moonstone takes center stage in our affairs.

Now, let us resume with the peculiar discovery of the sweet-smelling ink bottle on the gravel walk.

The next morning (the twenty-sixth), I showed Mr. Franklin this curious object and relayed the events as I’ve already described them to you. His opinion was clear: the Indians had not only been lurking about in pursuit of the Diamond but were also foolish enough to believe in their own superstitions—the markings on the boy’s head, the ink in his palm, all meant to grant him visions beyond ordinary sight. Mr. Franklin explained that such practices existed even in England, though without the ink—a French-named trick, something to do with *clairvoyance*.

*"They assumed we still had the Diamond here,"* he said. *"That boy was their guide, meant to locate it if they got inside."*

*"Will they return, sir?"* I asked.

*"That depends,"* he replied, *"on whether the boy’s sight is genuine. If he can peer through the iron vault of Frizinghall Bank and spot the Diamond there, we’ll see no more of them. If not, we’ll catch them in the shrubbery before long."*

I expected another attempt, but none came. Whether they learned of Mr. Franklin’s visit to the bank, whether the boy truly *saw* the Diamond (which I refuse to believe), or whether it was mere coincidence, the fact remained—not a shadow of an Indian crossed our grounds again until Miss Rachel’s birthday. They lingered in town, plying their trade, while Mr. Franklin and I waited, careful not to alert them by showing suspicion. And so ends my report on the Indians for now.

On the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin devised an amusement to pass the time—one worth noting, for it bears on events yet to unfold.

Idleness is a perilous thing for gentlefolk. Left with nothing to occupy their hands or minds, they often drift into absurd or even cruel pastimes under the guise of *intellectual pursuit*. I’ve seen ladies and gentlemen alike collect insects, impale them on pins, or dissect flowers with morbid curiosity, all while calling it *natural history*. At least Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel confined their mischief to inanimate objects—though they did ruin a perfectly good door.

Mr. Franklin, ever the dabbler, had taken up *decorative painting* and invented a new medium—a *vehicle*, as he called it—whose odor could make a dog sneeze. Miss Rachel, eager to try, was soon draped in an apron, daubing grotesques and flourishes onto her sitting-room door under his guidance. Slow, messy work—yet they were absorbed in it for hours, like children smearing mud.

By June, whispers began below stairs. Seeing how well Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel suited one another, speculation arose: would there be a wedding before summer’s end? Some said yes. Others (myself included) doubted the groom would be Mr. Franklin Blake.

His affection was plain enough, but Miss Rachel’s feelings were harder to discern. Allow me to paint her for you.

She was small, dark, and exquisitely proportioned—every movement graceful, her figure unadorned by artifice. Black hair, blacker eyes, a mouth that Mr. Franklin declared *a morsel for the gods*, and a complexion warm as sunlight but without its inconveniences. She carried herself with a spirited confidence, her voice clear, her smile beginning in her eyes before it reached her lips.

Yet for all her charm, she had a will of her own. Unlike most girls her age, she formed her own opinions and defied convention when it displeased her. In trifles, this independence was harmless; in greater matters, it led her astray. She confided in no one, asked no advice, and kept her own counsel—her best friend and worst enemy, as my lady often said.

But mark this: for all her secrecy, she was never false. She would sooner take punishment than lie. A creature of contradictions, like all women worth knowing.

On the twelfth, an invitation was sent to London—to the man I suspected held Miss Rachel’s heart: Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

A philanthropist of renowned charm, he stood tall and golden-haired, a fixture at ladies’ charitable committees where his speeches could draw both tears and coin. Next to him, Mr. Franklin—for all his cleverness—stood at a disadvantage.

Godfrey’s reply arrived on the fourteenth: he would come, though his *duties to the Ladies’ Charities* demanded an early return. Enclosed were verses for his cousin’s *natal day*, which Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin laughed over at dinner.

*"Wait,"* I told Penelope. *"The man himself will follow."*

On the sixteenth, a foreign-accented stranger called upon Mr. Franklin. The matter, whatever it was, displeased Miss Rachel, for sharp words were exchanged that evening. Work on the door ceased the next day—then resumed as abruptly. Had some old folly of Mr. Franklin’s resurfaced? I could only guess.

Meanwhile, Rosanna Spearman, our second housemaid, grew increasingly strange—wan, restless, and fixated on Mr. Franklin in a manner that troubled me. When the doctor pronounced it nerves, I foolishly advised keeping her on. A decision I would soon regret.

But that, as they say, is a matter for another day.
