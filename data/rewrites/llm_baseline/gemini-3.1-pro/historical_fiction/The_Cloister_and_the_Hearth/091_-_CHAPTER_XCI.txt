The passing of Margaret Van Eyck drifted through the household like the fading scent of bruised thyme—a slow, inexorable exhalation of memory. By a testament penned a year prior, the elder artist bequeathed her worldly estate to Margaret Brandt, alongside a solemn plea to retain Reicht Heynes within the domestic fold. Margaret inherited a home resonant with the acoustic echoes of a bygone era, and a trove of sketches that stood in stark defiance of the superficial commercialization of modern guild-art. Among these artifacts hung the diptych, “A Betrothal.” The solemnity of the man and the demure complacency of the woman were rendered with a staggering, almost mythic intimacy. The accessories, painted with Jan’s meticulous exactitude, remained an enduring miracle of the eye. Margaret sent word to Reicht to tend the hearth and guard this painting within the little cupboard until her own grief—a heavy, untranslatable weight that tasted of ash on the tongue—softened enough to let her step into a space now devoid of the elder Margaret’s sonic presence.

Then came Luke Peterson, bringing the discordant noise of the town’s chatter: Gerard was a monk. Luke, brimming with a loud, unseasoned joy, offered himself. “If he cannot marry you, I can.”

Margaret turned from the mortar where she ground fennel seed and coarse salt, the rhythmic crackle of the pestle forming a steady, grounding baseline to her anxiety. “You? Why, I have seen him.”

“But he is a friar.”

“He was my husband, and my boy’s father, long ere he was a friar. And I have seen him, I've seen him.”

Puzzled, Luke proposed consulting a lawyer, injecting the sterile, dominant lexicon of municipal courts into her sanctuary. Margaret’s rejection was swift, defending the acoustic purity of her home. “Nay, I shall ask my own heart, not a lawyer. If you seek to make me town talk, oh, fie! Never come nigh me again.”

When Luke departed with bitter words, accusing her of preferring a friar's illicit bed to an honest man’s hearth, the silence he left behind rang hollow. “Farewell, dear Luke; and God forgive you for saying that to me,” she murmured.

Margaret waited. She baked dense, dark loaves of rye, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke filling the void. She reasoned Gerard would come to see his son. But days fermented into weeks. The terrifying thought crept in like rot in a root cellar: the cold-blooded monks, men who consumed tasteless gruel merely as utilitarian fuel, had swallowed him into their silent, unfeeling abbey. 

The oppressive quiet was shattered when Giles came roaring. The dwarf’s voice was a colossal acoustic force, an unbridled baritone that shook the very rafters. “I’ve hit the clout! Our Gerard is Vicar of Gouda!”

Giles’ intellect had blossomed months prior—a mythic awakening triggered on an early June morning when, swinging from a tight-rope amidst the scent of lye and sun-dried linen, something within him cracked open. At the Burgundian court, he navigated the treacherous power dynamics embedded in the courtiers' manicured French by weaponizing his own blunt, rustic dialect. The young Duchess and Princess Marie found him amusing, while the court poet—a purveyor of sterile, commercialized verse—attempted to amplify and enfeeble Giles’ raw wisdom, pouring enough sycophantic praise upon him to stew a Goliath in butter.

But Giles claimed his power through physical and acoustic dominance. Before a grand joust, he challenged the Duke's giant to climb a lofty pole. Giles ascended like a squirrel, anchoring himself by sheer arm strength to form a perfect right angle, before sliding down so rapidly the Princess squeaked, hiding her face. The giant, sweating heavily, managed merely ten feet, blaming his bulk.

“Come,” Giles challenged in his native tongue, a dialect rich with the soil of his heritage, “an ye go to that, I'll wrestle ye, my lad, if so be you will let me blindfold your eyne.”

The giant consented. Giles orchestrated his victory: "Madam, see you yon blind Samson? At a signal from me he shall make me a low obeisance, and unbonnet to me." With the wagers laid, Giles struck the giant squarely in the bread-basket. The giant doubled over, his bonnet falling, while the court erupted in jagged, superficial laughter. Giles escaped through a cunningly painted trick door, leaving behind a posy criticizing the giant's "panting wit." 

Now, Giles strutted before Margaret, recounting how he had manipulated Princess Marie. He spoke of how he bypassed the court's artificial cheer, bringing his heavy heart to her chambers. He recounted the tale of Margaret Van Eyck’s letter, translating the untranslatable sorrow of Gerard's plight into terms the Princess could digest. He reminded her of the late Countess of Charolois's promise. By marrying cultural memory with decisive action—knowing the exact moment to strike, as the Vicar of Gouda had died the week prior—Giles secured the benefice. He described the manse to Margaret with evocative precision: the hawthorn blooming in spring-tide, the dog-roses and eglantine scenting the summer hedges.

Margaret caught him in her arms, her tears soaking his collar. “Oh, Giles,” she wept, kissing him. “I cannot keep my hands off thee, thy body it is so little, and thy heart so great. Thou art his true friend.”

To force the issue, Giles employed the town bellman, sending the acoustic reverberations of the new Vicar’s appointment throughout Rotterdam and neighboring towns. Margaret began preparing in her mind the culinary welcoming—rich broths, slow-roasted meats, the restorative foods of a long-awaited homecoming. Yet, the days rotted on the vine, and Gerard came to neither Rotterdam nor Gouda. 

Despair threatening her sanity, Margaret journeyed to Tergou, seeking out Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. She found the burgomaster in his garden in a wheeled chair, the heavy, fermented scent of fallen autumn apples surrounding him. His voice was feeble, stripped of its former authoritarian boom.

“Gerard no more, but Friar Clement,” Ghysbrecht wheezed, recounting his miraculous encounter. He confessed the whole truth—the forged letter he had penned to part the lovers. The burgomaster explained the friar’s untranslatable act of grace: Clement had forgiven him, not with the sterile absolution of the Church, but with the visceral, bleeding struggle of a son of Eli. “These three days thou hast spent in penitence, I have worn under thy roof in prayer; and I do forgive thee.”

Margaret’s tears flowed, salting her lips. “Ghysbrecht, since he hath forgiven thee, I forgive thee too,” she said, her voice finding resonance in the shared dialect of their mutual suffering. Rejuvenated by this closure, Ghysbrecht called for ink and vellum, dictating missives to every burgomaster in Holland and as far as Basle, casting a wide net to draw the Vicar of Gouda home.

But catastrophe struck Rotterdam shortly after. Sybrandt, flush with Catherine’s secretly gifted gold, plunged into drunken debauchery. The jarring, discordant clatter of his tavern revelry—a noxious noise pollution devoid of any acoustic harmony—ended violently when he stumbled off a stable roof. He fell, hanging momentarily on a paling before crashing into the dirt. 

They carried him, paralyzed and stinking of stale ale, into Margaret’s shop. Margaret, whose hands were stained with the earthy pigments of the kitchen, recognized the sheer destruction in his face. “His back is broken,” she told his drunken companions, her voice a low, steady drone against their panicked dissonance. Sybrandt cried out about the "curse."

Despite his past treacheries, Margaret and Reicht laid him upon the softest bed. Margaret fed him with spooned decoctions of bone marrow and medicinal herbs, channeling her grief into the restorative alchemy of the hearth. “I must do as he would do,” she whispered.

Catherine arrived from Tergou, devastated. The traditional agrarian elder, whose bustling songs and rhythmic household cadences had once been the sonic backbone of her family, was silenced. She rocked for hours beside the bed, her vibrant cultural memory eroding under the weight of her perceived treason to Eli. Her hair turned grey, and her voice lost its song. Sybrandt whined endlessly for Gerard, begging Margaret to find him so the curse might be lifted.

Luke Peterson returned, hollowed and thin, begging to serve her on any terms. Margaret sent him to find her "marriage lines," effectively dispatching him to track down Gerard, whether he was in Holland or Rome. Furnished with money, the slave of love wandered hither and thither, coopering and searching. 

As the months rolled on in their cyclical, multi-generational pacing, Sybrandt became Margaret's lifelong pensioner. Catherine, her nerves shattered, pleaded with Eli to take Cornelis back so she could live in Rotterdam. She longed for the acoustic comfort of little Gerard's prattle and Margaret’s domestic warmth, missing the vibrant hum of a functioning family. 

Meanwhile, the people of Gouda drafted a second petition to the Duke. Their community’s spiritual and physical nourishment was starving; children died unchristened, the dead went unburied. The silence of the absent vicar echoed through their empty manse, an echoing void in the village's daily rhythm that could no longer be maintained.
