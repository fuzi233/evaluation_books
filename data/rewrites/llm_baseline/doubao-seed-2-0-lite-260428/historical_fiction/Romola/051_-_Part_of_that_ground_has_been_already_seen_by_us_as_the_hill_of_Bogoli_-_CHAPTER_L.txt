Down the sun-dappled February lanes leading toward Florence’s Piazza della Signoria that Carnival morning stepped a figure all but glowing in her faded red, green, and white peasant dress: Tessa. She no longer called the hill of San Giorgio home, not after the frightening run-in with the raving stranger Baldassarre there. Tito had arranged their quiet new house, tucked against wide, sunlit garden grounds just north of the Porta Santa Croce, for her and the children, far enough from the bustle that she could raise little Lillo and tottering Ninna undisturbed.

She had not ventured out to see the Carnival without careful planning. The night before, she had held her plea tight in her throat, waiting for the moment Tito’s work-weary scowl softened, the moment he sat in the carved oak armchair he had brought specially for his visits, one arm slung around sturdy curly-haired Lillo, the other resting light on her shoulder as she helped wobbly Ninna find her feet. That was the Naldo she loved, the soft, unguarded version of the charming young man who had stumbled lost under the Loggia de’ Cerchi five years prior, before the weight of lies and debts had hardened the edges of him. Tito himself did not quite understand the relief that washed over him in those small hours with Tessa and the children: no guile was needed there, no pretenses, no hidden agendas. The high, sweet voices calling him *Babbo* were a refuge he had never dared name to himself. Poor Romola, for all her noble self-sacrifice, had only driven him further into layers of deceit with her quiet, cold disappointment, but Tessa’s uncomplicated affection kept the last soft springs of kindness alive in his chest.

“Ninna’s quite content to stay with Monna Lisa for hours now,” Tessa ventured at last, letting the toddler plop down to pat the straw mat on the floor. “If she cries in her cradle, Lillo is so clever he goes right over to thump Monna Lisa’s arm till she wakes, no matter how deep in her spinning she is.”

Lillo, whose dark eyes stood all the brighter against his mother’s same warm light brown curls, promptly hopped off Tito’s knee to prove his point, thumping the old servant’s shoulder so hard she chuckled mid-spin. Tito laughed, and Tessa leaned in, eager. “So I might go see the Carnival tomorrow, just for an hour or two, mightn’t I? I won’t stay late, I promise.”

“Oh, you wicked little pigeon,” Tito teased, pinching her pink cheek. “What use have you for Carnivals now you’re a proper old married woman with two children?”

“Old women like to look at pretty things too,” Tessa pouted, her lower lip drooping. “Monna Lisa says she’d love to go, only she’s so deaf she can’t hear anyone coming up behind her, and she says she’d never be able to keep an eye on both babies in a crowd.”

Tito’s face turned serious. “You must not dream of bringing Lillo or Ninna into those packed streets, Tessa, or I will be very angry.”

“I haven’t stepped foot in the Piazza without leave since that terrible Holy Saturday, and I think old Nofri must be dead now, his poor wife gone too,” Tessa pleaded, her voice soft and scared. “I still remember that one Carnival I saw once, all strewn with roses, a king and queen parading under garlands, people singing. I liked it better than the feast of San Giovanni.”

“There’s nothing like that now, my Tessa. They’re only building a huge bonfire in the Piazza this year for Fra Girolamo’s Bonfire of Vanities. But I won’t let you wander the streets alone after dark, that’s out of the question.”

“I don’t want to go at all in the evening!” she assured him, breathless. “I only want to see the daytime procession. There *will* be a procession, won’t there?”

“Oh, in a fashion,” Tito said, smiling. “Lively as a flock of slow-flying cranes, no doubt. No glittering kings or rose petals, I’m afraid. But there’s the great pile they’ve stacked for the bonfire in the Piazza della Signoria, you might find that interesting. Come home early, be the grave little matron you ought to be, and keep well clear of any men running about with feathers in their hats and swords at their hips: they’re a fierce lot, and like to lop the heads off silly old women who get in their way.”

“Santa Madonna!” Tessa gasped, then saw the crinkle at the corner of his eye. “You’re teasing me. But I’ll stay far away, I promise. Only — what if I take Lillo with me? He’s so sensible now.”

“Who will thump Monna Lisa awake then if she nods off?” Tito countered, pretending to be solemn. “No, Tessa, you’d never keep hold of him in a crowd, and he’s far too heavy for you to carry for long.”

“That’s true,” Tessa sighed, a little sad. “He’s always trying to run off. I’ll go alone then. But look at Ninna, you haven’t even stopped to marvel at her new teeth.”

Tito knelt to coo over the tot, and Tessa leaned in to whisper, “May I buy confetti for the babies? The coloured sweet kind?” He poured a handful of small coins into her palm, and Tessa’s face lit up. “That’s more than enough! I won’t mind leaving Lillo at home half so much if I bring him a whole pocketful of treats.”

So she set out early the next morning, the February breeze soft on her cheeks, no heavy mantle weighing her down. She had no thought of showing off her figure — no one had ever told her she was pretty — but she had no wish to hide the new silver necklace and matching clasp Tito had given her, the only fine ornaments she had ever owned. She arranged her white linen hood just so, to show the silverwork glinting at her throat, certain no contadina in Florence had anything half so pretty, sure it would make passersby respect her as the wife of a man who could afford such gifts.

She tripped along cheerily, planning what sweets to tuck into her small wicker basket, never noticing the ragged man carrying a heavy bundle of yarn who had been watching her door since dawn. Baldassarre had dogged Tito’s steps to this very house dozens of times before, waiting for proof that the young Greek kept a secret wife there, and that morning he had his confirmation. Years in the dungeons had sharpened his vengeful paranoia to a knife’s edge: he dared not confront Tito openly, terrified the younger man would have him dragged back to a dark cell, his knife taken away, his vengeance snuffed out before he could strike. He had lingered in the Duomo a hundred times, waiting for that roaring call for justice from Savonarola’s pulpit that had once roused the crowd, but the voice that might have helped him seemed to have forgotten his wrongs. Now he had settled on a new plan: he would find Tito’s other wife, the noble one who lived in the old house on Via de’ Bardi, who knew of scholarship and treachery, who was already wronged by Tito’s secrets. If he could win her to his side, her anger at his betrayal would be the strength he needed to finally strike.

Tessa wandered the streets for an hour before she spotted any sign of the coming Carnival, only groups of country people in their holiday best, loitering and chatting, passing jugs of wine back and forth. Then she saw a pedlar with a basket slung over his arm, handing out little red paper crosses to every passenger. A red cross would look so nice hung over her bed, she thought, keep Ninna safe from fevers. She crossed the street to ask the price, and recognized the pedlar instantly: Bratti Ferravecchi, the old trader she had known back in the Mercato Vecchio. She tried to slip past unseen, but Bratti’s sharp eyes caught her, and he tapped her on the shoulder with one of the crosses.

“Little Tessa, is it you? As plump and pretty as a ripe peach!” he cackled. “You did well to run off from old Nofri, you know. He’s on crutches now, and a crabbed man on crutches can beat his wife from across the whole room without even standing up. Four quattrini for this blessed cross, the Padre himself sprinkled holy water on the whole batch.”

Tessa fidgeted, worried she was spending too much of her confetti money. Bratti haggled her down to two, then tricked her into revealing exactly where she lived, under the pretense of calling by later to collect the two quattrini and see if she had any old rags to trade. Flustered, Tessa hurried to the confectioner’s shop, her basket soon brimming with coloured sugar almonds, honey cakes, and twisted sweet pastries for the children, forgetting all about Baldassarre trailing her a dozen paces back.

Just as she turned down the narrow Via de’ Libraj, she saw the procession coming: a group of boys all clad in spotless white, green wreaths woven through their hair, a banner of the Madonna and Child held high at their head. Her heart fluttered, half in awe, half delight. But the boys turned straight toward her, their faces serious, and before she could slip away, the tallest of them leaned in.

“Sister, you carry vanity that is anathema to the Lord,” he said. “Yield up that silver necklace and clasp on your belt, that they may burn in the holy Bonfire of Vanities, and save you from the fires of hell.”

Tessa froze, tears rushing to her eyes. She could not bear to give up the gifts Naldo had bought her, not for anything. Her lips trembled, great fat tears rolling down her cheeks, when a soft, warm hand settled on her arm.

“Do not be afraid,” said a gentle, musical voice. Tessa looked up to see a lady in plain black wool, her face soft and kind, hazel eyes brimming with pity. It was Romola. “You must not take these things from her against her will,” she said to the white-robed boys, her tone firm but kind. “Fra Girolamo does not ask for offerings that are not given freely. To force her to give these trinkets up would do no good for her soul.”

The boys, unwilling to argue with the woman everyone in Florence knew as the godfather’s noble, pious daughter, hurried off to join the rest of their procession. Romola led Tessa under a sheltered archway, and helped her tuck the silver necklace and clasp carefully under the sweet parcels in her wicker basket, safe from any other would-be vanities collectors. “You said you were heading toward Porta Santa Croce, right?” she said, taking Tessa’s hand to lead her to the end of the alley. “Go straight down that lane, and you will be on the quiet road straight home. No one will bother you again. *Addio*, and greet your little ones for me.”

Tessa could only whisper a shaky thank you, staring after Romola’s tall, gliding figure as she vanished around a stone corner, certain the lady was an angel sent from the Madonna herself. She hurried home as fast as her feet could carry her, never wanting to go near a Carnival spectacle again.

Baldassarre had seen the whole exchange, lingering half a block back. The two wives of Tito, side by side, stood clear in his mind now. The noble one, Romola, had all the cause in the world to hate the man who had deceived her, stolen her father’s books, betrayed every trust she had ever placed in him. That would be his ally, he thought, shifting his heavy yarn bundle on his back, a faint, terrible smile tugging at his ragged lips. He would bide his time, watch the house on Via de’ Bardi, wait for the perfect moment to tell her the truth, to turn her righteous anger to his long-simmering vengeance. At last, his path was clear.
