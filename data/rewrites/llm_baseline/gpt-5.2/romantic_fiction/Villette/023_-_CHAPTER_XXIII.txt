To wonder sadly, had I said? No. For a space, sorrow was held back, as if some sudden gust had come upon a hollow in a wood—one of those secret dells where the grass lies spongy with mist, and the leaves keep a sickly, pale damp. Let but a storm, or an axe, make room in the boughs; let a slit be opened for air and sun; and what was a cold cup becomes a vessel brimmed with lustre. So, by some rent made in my own close-growing shade, light poured in; and I—who had been living on scant fare—took a new nourishment.

A new creed became mine: belief in happiness.

Three weeks had gone since that strange night in the garret; and now, in my little case, in the locked drawer upstairs, lay the first letter, and with it four others—each traced by the same firm hand, each sealed with the same clear seal, each bringing a draught of comfort so immediate it seemed not ink but cordial. After years, I read them again: kind letters—pleasant, because written by one well pleased; the last had a few lines, half-gay, half-tender, touched by feeling, but not subdued. Time mellowed them to a mild beverage; but then—fresh drawn from a source I honoured—each page was like a vintage poured out for the gods.

How did I answer? Under the dry check of Reason, or with the full impulse of Feeling?

I served two masters. I bowed in Rimmon’s house, and yet turned my heart to another shrine. I wrote two replies to every letter—one for my own relief, one for Graham’s perusal.

Feeling and I would thrust Reason out, shoot bolt and bar against her, spread paper, dip a pen deep, and pour forth, with a kind of hungry joy, our whole sincere heart—gratitude close-clinging, affection strongly adherent, attachment rooted and active. (Once for all, let me say I scorn the mean suspicion of what are called “warmer feelings.” Women do not kindle those flames where, from first to last of an acquaintance, they have never been cheated by even a glimmer of Hope. None launches into Love unless Hope’s star has risen, or been dreamed of, on Love’s troubled waters.) When I had written myself out—had told how gladly I would draw to my own lot whatever was painful in his destiny; how willingly I would stand, if I might, between him and storms—then, at that very point, the doors of my heart would shake. Bolt and bar would give. Reason would spring in, vigorous, revengeful; snatch the warm sheets; read; sneer; erase; tear up; re-write; fold; seal; direct; and send a curt page, proper and composed. She did right.

Nor did I live on letters only. I was visited; I was cared for. Once each week I was taken out to La Terrasse, and always made much of. Dr. Bretton did not fail to tell me why. “To keep away the nun,” he said. He was determined to dispute with her her prey. He professed a thorough dislike to her—chiefly for that white face-cloth and those cold grey eyes; the moment he heard those odious particulars, disgust, he declared, had roused opposition. He only wished she would look in upon me once more when he was present. But she never did. In short, he regarded me as a patient, and practised on me that cordial, attentive treatment where benevolence and skill make one method.

One evening—the first in December—I walked alone in the carré. It was six o’clock. The class-doors were closed; but within, the pupils, let loose for recreation, were making a small riot of laughter, shrieks, and racing feet: a domestic tempest, all noise and no danger, beating against the panels. Outside, the carré itself lay dark, save for the red glow under and about the stove: a low, steady furnace-light, like embers kept for the morrow’s bread. The wide glass-doors and long windows were frosted over, their whiteness stitched with starlight—fine, scattered points pricking through the blanched veil. The air promised clear cold, though there was no moon.

That I could remain there thus, in darkness, showed my nerves had gained tone. I thought of the nun; I hardly feared her; yet behind me the staircase climbed up—blind, black, landing after landing—to the haunted grenier. And still my heart gave one leap, and my pulse sprang, when I heard a breathing, a rustle, and saw, on the steps’ deep shadow, a deeper shadow move and descend. It paused at the class-door, then glided before me. At the same instant, from far off, came the clang of the great bell.

Life-like sounds bring life-like feelings. That figure was too round, too low for my gaunt nun. It was only Madame Beck on duty.

“Mademoiselle Lucy!” cried Rosine, bursting in with a lamp from the corridor. “On est là pour vous au salon.”

Madame saw me; I saw Madame; Rosine saw us both; there was no mutual recognition. I went straight to the salon.

There, as I owned I expected, I found Dr. Bretton—Graham—but in evening dress.

“The carriage is at the door,” said he. “My mother has sent it to take you to the theatre. She meant to go, but an arrival has prevented her; she said at once, ‘Take Lucy in my place.’ Will you go?”

“Just now? I am not dressed,” I cried, glancing at my dark merino as if it had suddenly become a disgrace.

“You have half an hour. I should have given notice, but I only determined since five, when I heard there was to be a genuine regale—a great actress.”

And he named her; and the name—then—was a trumpet through Europe. It is hushed now. The woman who bore it has long been covered by night and oblivion. But then her day, a day of Sirius, stood at its full height of heat and fervour.

“I’ll go. I will be ready in ten minutes,” I vowed—and flew, checked by no scruple such as might have checked another: the thought that to go anywhere with Graham, without Mrs. Bretton, might be objectionable. I could not have conceived it without kindling in myself a tyrannous shame. Besides, my godmother, knowing her son and knowing me, would as soon have chaperoned a sister with a brother, as watched our comings and goings with anxious guard.

There was no need of show. My dun mist crape would suffice. I went to seek it in the great oak-wardrobe of the dormitory, where hung no fewer than forty dresses. But reforms had come: some innovating hand had pruned the crowded press and carried divers garments to the grenier—my crape among them.

I must fetch it. I took the key and went aloft—fearless, almost thoughtless. I unlocked the door; I plunged in.

And then—the reader may believe or not—but that garret was not wholly dark. From one point shone a solemn light, like a star, yet broader; and it shone so plainly it revealed the deep alcove with part of the tarnished scarlet curtain drawn over it. Instantly, silently, it vanished: so did curtain and alcove; the whole end became black as night.

I made no search. I had no time—no will. Snatching my dress, happily hung near the door, I rushed out, relocked with convulsed haste, and darted down to the dormitory.

But I trembled too much to dress. Fingers so shaken cannot manage hairpins or hooks-and-eyes. I called Rosine and bribed her to help me. Rosine liked a bribe. She smoothed and plaited my hair with a practised neatness; set the lace collar mathematically straight; tied the neck-ribbon with exactness; did all like the neat-handed Phillis she could be when she chose. Then, candle in hand, she lighted me downstairs. At the last, I had forgotten my shawl; she ran back; and I stood in the vestibule with Dr. John waiting.

“What is this, Lucy?” he said, looking down at me keenly. “Here is the old excitement. Ha! the nun again?”

I utterly denied it. I was vexed to be suspected of a second illusion. He was sceptical.

“She has been, as sure as I live,” said he. “Her figure crossing your eyes leaves on them a peculiar gleam and expression not to be mistaken.”

“She has not been,” I persisted—for indeed, her apparition I could deny with truth.

“The old symptoms are there,” he affirmed: “a particular pale, and what the Scotch call a ‘raised’ look.”

He was so obstinate I told him what I had seen—the light, the curtain, the sudden blank. With him, of course, it was still the same cause: optics, nerves, malady. I did not believe him—not one bit; but I did not contradict. Doctors are so self-opinionated, so immovable in their dry, material views.

Rosine returned with the shawl, and I was bundled into the carriage.

The theatre was full—crammed to the roof. Royal and noble were there; palace and hotel had poured their inmates into those tiers, thronged and hushed. Even before the curtain rose, the place had its own soundscape: the low sea-swell of many breaths held in one cavern; the rustle of silk like reeds in a winter wind; the occasional thin ring of a dropped glass, startling in that gathered silence. I felt, keenly, the privilege of a place before the stage. I longed to see this being whose fame had made my anticipation severe and austere, yet riveted.

She rose at nine that December night: above the horizon I saw her come. She could shine yet with pale grandeur and steady might; but that star verged already on its judgment-day. Seen near, it was a chaos—hollow, half-consumed: an orb perished or perishing—half lava, half glow.

I had heard her called plain. I expected harsh bone, grim angles, something large, sallow. What I saw was the shadow of a royal Vashti: a queen, fair as day once, now pale like twilight, wasted like wax in flame.

For a while—a long while—I thought it only a woman, though a unique woman, moving in might and grace before the multitude. By-and-by I knew my mistake. There was on her something neither woman nor man. In each eye sat a devil. These forces bore her through the tragedy; they kept up her feeble strength, for she was frail; and as the action rose, they shook her with passions like a pit’s upheaving. They wrote HELL upon her straight, haughty brow. They tuned her voice to torment. They wrung her regal face to a demoniac mask. Hate and Murder and Madness, incarnate, stood there.

Marvellous: a mighty revelation.

Also low, horrible, immoral.

What were swordsmen bleeding out on arena sand? what bulls goring horses?—meeker sights, milder condiments, than this human creature torn by seven devils—devils that cried sore and rent the tenement they haunted, yet would not be exorcised.

Suffering had struck that stage-empress; and she stood before her audience neither yielding nor enduring nor, in any finite measure, resenting. She stood locked in struggle, rigid in resistance—draped, not dressed, in pale antique folds long and regular as sculpture, set against deepest crimson that flung her out white like alabaster—like silver—nay, like Death.

What hurts her becomes embodied at once; she attacks it as prey. Scarcely substance herself, she grappled with abstractions. Before calamity she was tigress: she rent woe, shivered grief in convulsed abhorrence. Pain bore no good fruit for her; tears watered no harvest of wisdom. Sickness, death itself, she faced with a rebel eye. Wicked, perhaps; strong, assuredly. Strength had conquered Beauty, had overcome Grace, and bound both at her side, captives peerlessly fair and docile. Even in frenzy, every movement was royally upborne. Her loose hair, in revel or war, was still an angel’s hair, glorious under a halo; fallen, insurgent, banished, she remembered the heaven where she rebelled. Heaven’s light followed her exile and disclosed the forlorn remoteness.

Vashti was not good, I had been told; and she did not look good—though spirit, a spirit from Tophet. Well: if so much unholy force can rise from below, may not one day an equal efflux of sacred essence descend from above?

What thought Dr. Graham?

For long intervals I forgot to look. Genius drew my heart out of its old orbit, as a fierce, cometary light draws a sunflower away from the south. I had seen acting, but never this: never anything that astonished Hope and hushed Desire; that outstripped Impulse and paled Conception; that showed power like a deep winter river, swollen, thundering in cataract, bearing the soul like a leaf on the steel sweep of its descent.

People had called Dr. Bretton serious and impassioned—too grave, too impressible. I never saw him so. He was impressionable as dimpling water, and as water, almost unimpressible: sun and breeze moved him; metal could not engrave, nor fire brand. He could think—and think well—but he was made rather for action than meditation. He could feel, vividly in his way; but his heart had no chord for enthusiasm. Sweet, bright influences his eyes and lips welcomed; for what belonged to storm—wild, intense, dangerous, flaming—he had no sympathy.

When at last I looked at him, it amused and enlightened me to discover he watched Vashti not with wonder, worship, nor dismay, but with intense curiosity. Her agony did not pain him; her wild moan—worse than a shriek—did not much move him; her fury revolted him somewhat, but not to horror. Cool young Briton! The pale cliffs of his England do not look down on the Channel more calmly than he watched that Pythian inspiration.

I longed to know his exact opinion, and asked.

At the sound of my voice he woke, as from a dream of his own thoughts. “How do you like Vashti?” I wished to know.

“Hm-m-m,” was the first answer—scarce articulate, yet expressive. Then a strange smile wandered round his lips, critical, almost callous. In a few terse phrases he gave his judgment—judged her as a woman, not an artist. It was a branding judgment.

That night was already marked in my book of life, not with white, but with a deep-red cross. Yet it was not ended; other memoranda were to be set down in tint indelible.

Towards midnight, when the tragedy blackened to the death-scene, and all held breath—when even Graham bit his under-lip, knit his brow, sat still and struck—when the whole theatre was hushed, all eyes centred, all ears leaned towards one quarter—nothing seen but the white form sunk on a seat, quivering in conflict with the last, worst-hated, visibly conquering foe—nothing heard but throes and gaspings, breathing mutiny, panting defiance—when it seemed an inordinate will convulsed a perishing frame and fought doom and death foot by foot, drop by drop, resisting to the latest the rape of every faculty—would see, would hear, would breathe, would live up to, within, well-nigh beyond the moment when death says to sense and being, “Thus far and no farther”—

Just then, behind the scenes, a stir ran like a bad omen: feet hurried; voices spoke. What was it? asked the whole house.

A flame, a smell of smoke replied.

“Fire!” rang through the gallery. “Fire!” repeated, re-echoed, yelled. And faster than pen can set it down came panic—rushing, crushing—a blind, selfish, cruel chaos. The sound of it was a new, monstrous thing: not music, not speech, but the raw roar of terror; the theatre’s carefully gathered hush broken into a savage industrial clamour, as if some machine had been loosed among human throats.

And Dr. John? I see him now, with comely courage and cordial calm.

“Lucy will sit still, I know,” said he, glancing down at me with serene goodness—the same repose of firmness I had seen by his mother’s hearth. Thus adjured, I think I would have sat still under a rocking crag. Besides, to sit still was my instinct; and at the price of my life I would not have moved to trouble him, thwart him, or make demands on his attention.

We were in the stalls. For a few minutes there was a terrible pressure round us—hot, suffocating, ruthless.

“How terrified are the women!” said he; “but if the men were not almost equally so, order might be maintained. This is a sorry scene. I see fifty selfish brutes at this moment, each of whom, if I were near, I could conscientiously knock down. I see some women braver than some men. There is one yonder—Good God!”

A young girl who had been clinging quietly to a gentleman before us was struck from his arms by a big, butcherly intruder and hurled under the crowd’s feet. Scarce two seconds lasted her disappearance. Graham rushed forward; he and the gentleman—a powerful man though grey-haired—united their strength to thrust back the throng. The girl’s head and long hair fell back over Graham’s shoulder; she seemed unconscious.

“Trust her with me; I am a medical man,” said Dr. John.

“If you have no lady with you, be it so,” was the answer. “Hold her, and I will force a passage: we must get her to the air.”

“I have a lady,” said Graham; “but she will be neither hindrance nor incumbrance.”

He summoned me with his eye. We had been separated by the press. Resolute to rejoin him, I penetrated the living barrier, creeping under where I could not pass between or over.

“Fasten on me, and don’t leave go,” he said; and I obeyed.

Our pioneer—this iron-grey Englishman—proved strong and adroit. He opened the dense mass like a wedge; with patience and toil bored through the flesh-and-blood rock—so solid, hot, suffocating—and brought us at last to the fresh, freezing night. The cold struck my lungs like a draught of pure water after smoke.

“You are an Englishman!” said he, turning shortly on Dr. Bretton when we reached the street.

“An Englishman. And I speak to a countryman?” was the reply.

“Right. Be good enough to stand here two minutes whilst I find my carriage.”

“Papa, I am not hurt,” said a girlish voice, faint but clear; “am I with papa?”

“You are with a friend, and your father is close at hand.”

“Tell him I am not hurt, except just in my shoulder. Oh, my shoulder! They trod just here.”

“Dislocation, perhaps!” muttered the doctor. “Let us hope there is no worse injury. Lucy, lend a hand one instant.”

I assisted while he arranged her drapery and position for ease. She suppressed a moan and lay quietly and patiently in his arms.

“She is very light,” said Graham, “like a child!” and in my ear he asked, “Is she a child, Lucy? Did you notice her age?”

“I am not a child—I am a person of seventeen,” returned the patient, demurely, with dignity; and then, directly, anxiety broke through: “Tell papa to come; I get anxious.”

The carriage drove up. Her father relieved Graham; but in the change of arms she was hurt and moaned again.

“My darling!” said the father tenderly; then, turning to Graham, “You said, sir, you are a medical man?”

“I am—Dr. Bretton, of La Terrasse.”

“Good. Will you step into my carriage?”

“My own carriage is here: I will seek it, and accompany you.”

“Be pleased then to follow us.” And he named his address: the Hôtel Crécy, in the Rue Crécy.

We followed. Their carriage drove fast. Graham and I were silent. It felt like an adventure written on the instant—one of those turns life takes, sudden as a knife in cloth.

Some little time was lost in finding our own equipage; but we reached the hotel perhaps ten minutes after the strangers. It was an hotel in the foreign sense: not an inn, but a collection of dwelling-houses—a vast lofty pile, with a huge arch to the street-door, leading through a vaulted way into a square built round on all sides. Sound changed there: the street’s alarm was left behind; the enclosed court held only the echo of wheels, the quick commands of servants, the sharp fall of doors—each noise struck clean, as if the stone kept account of it.

We mounted a wide public staircase and stopped at Numéro 2 on the second landing; the first floor, Graham said, was occupied by some “prince Russe.” We rang at a second great door and were admitted to handsome apartments. Announced by a servant in livery, we entered a drawing-room where an English fire glowed—coal snapping, flame steady and domestic—and foreign mirrors made the light double. Near the hearth stood a small group: a slight form sunk in a deep arm-chair, one or two women busy about it, and the iron-grey gentleman anxiously looking on.
