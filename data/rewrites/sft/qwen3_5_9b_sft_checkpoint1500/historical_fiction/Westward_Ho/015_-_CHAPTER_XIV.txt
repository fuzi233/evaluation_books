How Salvation Yeo Slew the King of the Gubbings

"Ignorance and evil, even in full flight, deal terrible backhanded strokes at their pursuers."—HELPS.

It was not safe, I regret to say, for the honor of our nation, to travel from Plymouth northward into Devonshire in those days. Unless one wished to make a wide detour, one had to pass through the dominions of a foreign and hostile potentate who had repeatedly ravaged the realm and defeated Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth's forces. Behind his back, at least, he was known as the King of the Gubbings. "So now I dare call them," wrote Fuller, "secure by distance, which one of more valor durst not do to their face, for fear their fury fall upon him." While scholars have offered various etymologies—from fish shavings to inhabitants of crooked hills—the truth remains that they are a peculiar breed, a Scythia within England, living in ignorance of law, luxury, and God. They dwell in cottages that are merely holes, multiplying without marriage, their language the dregs of the vulgar tongue, and their wealth consisting solely of stolen goods. During the Civil Wars, no soldiers were quartered upon them, lest they be quartered amongst them. Yet, rumors circulated that they were beginning to be civilized, offering their children to baptism and returning to the fold.

With this quip, Fuller ended his story, leaving us to picture Amyas riding out of Plymouth on a spirited horse, fully armored with lance, sword, and two great horse-pistols. Following him were Salvation Yeo and five or six men from North Devon, veterans of the Irish campaign, clad in head-pieces and quilted jerkins, armed with pikes and swords, while two sumpter ponies carried their baggage. They pressed on as fast as possible through Tavistock, hoping to reach Lydford before nightfall, but delays in purchasing mounts meant they did not start until noon. Night descended upon them just as they crossed the frontier into the enemy's country—a dreary expanse of heath, banked by the crags of Dartmoor and sloping toward the great cone of Brent-Tor, which towered like an extinct volcano. Upon its summit stood a tiny church, a votive offering built by a distressed merchant who vowed to construct it to the Blessed Virgin upon the first point of English land he saw. From afar, wisps of blue smoke drifted from the dens of the Gubbings, and more than once the party halted, mistaking distant furze bushes and ponies for patrols of an advancing army.

Upon the down stood a wayside inn, a desolate and villainous-looking lump of lichen-spotted granite with paper-patched windows and rotting thatch held down by stones and straw banks. At the rear sprawled a rambling court-yard of barns and walls, around which pigs and barefoot children grunted in a loving communion of dirt. Standing at the door, seemingly contemplating the glowing mountain peaks but actually watching the movement of sheep on the moor, was the innkeeper. He was a brawny, sodden-visaged brute, six feet of brutishness, holding up his hose with one hand for lack of points and clawing at his elf-locks with the other, upon which a sprinkling of feathers testified to his recent nocturnal exploits. As Amyas and his party crested the hill, the host pricked up his ears, counted them, noted Amyas's armor, and then, uttering a sleepy howl, declared, "Mirooi!--Fushing pooale!"

A strapping lass, wearing only a green bodice and red petticoat, emerged with a fishing rod and basket. She informed the host that the flies—"mun"—were gone. The host, receiving this news with an oath, struck Mary on the head; she dodged the blow and returned it with effect, whereupon the host shuffled off, howling, "Tell Patrico!" Mary quickly combed her hair, slipped on her best gown, and awaited the guests, who, despite making long faces at the "mucksy" nature of the place, preferred spending the night there to bivouacing near the enemy's camp.

The old hen who had swallowed the dun fly was roasted, joined by "black Dartmoor mutton" disguised as red-deer venison. Amyas secured his horses in a lockless shed, returning with a grumble and fear for his steed's safety, while Yeo posted himself at the door. Soon, Yeo announced the arrival of a solitary gentleman. When the stranger failed to appear, Yeo returned in high excitement, claiming to have trapped a Jesuit. He described how the man had approached stealthily, called "Mary!" softly, produced a Popish Agnus, and kissed it before slipping into the stable. Convinced of the man's guilt, Yeo barred the door and blocked the entrance with a cart, leaving the prisoner confined.

Amyas hurried out to retrieve his cousin, Eustace. Eustace appeared with a countenance half foolish, half venomous, revealing his true nature as a fox cornered by terriers. After a tense exchange regarding the theft of horses and the nature of their respective faiths, Amyas, though angry, invited Eustace to supper. Over the meal, they spoke guardedly at first, but eventually, Amyas's straightforward kindness thawed Eustace's frozen heart, leading them to discuss old haunts and boyhood memories. However, the conversation soured when Amyas revealed his intent to seek out Rose Salterne. Eustace, realizing his own love for her and his betrayal by the Brotherhood of the Rose, erupted in despair, accusing Amyas of hunting her down as an outlaw and murdering her lover.

Despite Eustace's protests and claims of being in the power of the Catholics, Amyas dismissed him, resolved to investigate the matter himself. He instructed his men to sleep on the floor, suspecting a plot on Yeo's part, and retired to the settle. About midnight, Amyas was awakened by the terrifying scream of geese, which turned out to be a flock fleeing from a real alarm. A fierce fight was underway in the courtyard. Bursting out, the men found Yeo defending himself manfully against a dozen attackers, backed against the stable door amidst a chorus of screaming animals and a wailing Mary. The ruffians, seeing the captain arrive, took to their heels and vanished over a low hedge.

Yeo reported that he had slain two ringleaders. One, a huge man, lay bleeding against the wall; the other, dead as a herring, lay in the straw. Yeo's account was that he had anticipated a night attack, sleeping in the stable, and had driven off the intruders with his sword and buckler. Hardly had they recovered the bodies when a fresh hubbub arose from within. Amyas rushed forward to find Mr. Evan Morgans, alias Father Parsons, looking like a half-plucked turkey-cock, accompanied by Eustace Leigh. Parsons claimed he was attending to his wounded "lamb," while Eustace acted as interpreter for the dying thief, Ballard, translating his thieves' dialect into Latin for the priest's confession.

Yeo protested vehemently, declaring that a soul was perishing before his eyes and that the Jesuits were impostors. Amyas silenced him, noting that the dying man was responding to the priests, the first who had ever spoken to him as a human being. However, before the words of absolution could follow the dark catalogue of brutal deeds, the man's head fell back, and he was dead. Parsons, undeterred, declared that confession in extremis was sufficient and that the intention would be accepted. Yeo, furious, accused Parsons of bringing the devil into the sheepfold, and Amyas, seeing the impracticality of the situation, decided to move on.

To avoid pursuit by the Gubbings, who knew the country better, Amyas devised a plan. He ordered Eustace to return to his converts and warn them not to interfere, threatening them with deadly force if they did. He then insisted that Father Parsons accompany them as a hostage, arguing that the shepherd must guarantee the safety of his sheep. Reluctantly, Parsons agreed, and Eustace followed. Amyas had Parsons tied to his saddle, and as they rode, Yeo walked beside them, recounting the woful end of Nicholas Saunders the Legate, joking that Parsons could easily find a similar bog to die in nearby. As they approached Lydford Castle, Amyas cast Parsons off, and Yeo and Simon Evans lingered behind to salvage his clothes, throwing the man into a furze bush. Yeo assured Amyas that the Jesuit would likely survive the fall, given his legendary resilience, and they pressed on toward Okehampton.

The journey continued through the mire until sunrise, during which Amyas reflected on the tragedy surrounding Rose Salterne. He realized that Frank, the rival suitor, and the Brotherhood of the Rose were involved, and that his own oath to protect her had been broken by circumstances beyond his control. Arriving in Okehampton, he was detained for hours by the local Justice, Shallow, who refused to take depositions regarding the previous night's affray, citing jurisdictional issues and a reluctance to involve himself in the affairs of the Gubbings. Finally, Amyas traveled to Bideford, where he sought out Sir Richard Grenville, only to find him in Ireland. He then visited the Ship Tavern, where he learned from the host of strange doings involving Mistress Salterne and her father.
