The day after that dreadful outburst, the little house in the Hoog Straet lay under a hush like a churchyard after rain. Even the common street-sounds came softened, as if the very air had been taught to tread gently—no jolly clatter of trenchers, no brisk scold-and-laugh between hearth and table. Only a thin simmering in the pot at noon, a spoon set down too carefully, and then long silences that swallowed the faintest creak of the settle.

Catherine, whose hands were made for bustle and whose tongue was born to stitch neighbours together with talk, went about as if her limbs had forgotten their business. After dinner she wiped her eyes with the corner of her apron—eyes red and swollen, like onions had been peeled without end—and said she would go to the convent and speak with Gerard, if only to lay the first stone of peace.

The convent porter, thick of ear or thick of patience, made her say it twice, then again; and even then stared at her as though the name did not fit his world. At last she got it from him: Gerard had gone late the previous night, and was not looked for back.

Catherine came away with that news in her mouth like bitter herbs, and carried it, sighing, to Margaret.

She found Margaret sitting idle, as if life had lost all savour—like broth after the salt has been forgotten. The child was in her arms so tightly clasped that one might have thought she feared hands would snatch him out of her very lap. The room held its own small noises—the boy’s breathing, the faint tick of cooling cinders—but Margaret’s stillness made them loud, and made Catherine’s heart ache.

“Come to the Hoog Straet,” Catherine begged.

“What for?” Margaret answered, and the words fell without heat, only weary. “You cannot but say to yourselves, she is the cause of all.”

“Nay, nay,” said Catherine quickly, as if haste could mend what sorrow had torn. “We are not so ill-hearted; and Eli is so fond on you. You will maybe soften him.”

Margaret drew a long breath, as though air itself weighed. “Oh—if you think I can do any good, I’ll come.”

They went together, the child bundled close, and when they reached the house they found Eli in the doorway with a carpenter. Hammer and mallet were at their work, and every blow rang sharp in that stripped, unhappy place, each thud like a seal set on a sentence already passed.

They were putting up a name where Cornelis and Sybrandt’s had been.

And the name painted there, bold as a proclamation, was—Margaret Brandt’s.

Though Catherine loved Margaret, that sight went through her like a knife between rib and rib.

“The bane of one is another’s meat,” she whispered, not meaning to wound, and yet wounded herself.

“Can he make me spend the money unjustly?” Margaret replied, and her voice held no triumph. It was cold, as if spoken from a room where the fire has gone out.

“You are a good soul,” Catherine said, and then, with the sour wisdom of the weak, she added, “Ay, so best, sith he is the strongest.”

The next day Giles dropped in. He brought with him the outer world—street-dust on his shoes, a brisker step, and the livelier jangle of a man who has errands and influence. Catherine, eager as ever to patch what was torn, poured out the story, turning it as much as she could in favour of the black sheep—anathematized by their brother and thrust upon the wide world by their father.

But Giles’s prejudice ran the other way. He heard her through, then snapped out his judgment as one snaps a bone to get at the marrow.

“They’ve got off cheap,” he said bluntly. “They deserve to be hanged at Margaret’s door into the bargain.”

Then, dismissing them with contempt, he brightened at once—crowed, almost, at the thought of his favourite’s return.

“I’ll show him,” said he, “what ’tis to have a brother at court with a heart to serve a friend, and a head to point the way.”

“Bless thee, Giles,” Margaret murmured softly, as one might bless a loaf put quietly into a poor hand.

“Thou wast ever his stanch friend, dear Giles,” said little Kate; “but alack, I know not what thou canst do for him now.”

Giles was gone. The house sank again into its sad quiet, broken only by the small domestic sounds that, in happier days, would have been the music of plenty: a pot-lid set straight, a spoon rinsed, a crust folded away. Now each sound felt like a reminder of what could still be done for hunger, and what could not be done for grief.

Then the door opened softly, as if the visitor feared to disturb the air itself. A well-dressed man stood there and asked, cautious and formal, was Margaret Brandt here.

“D’ye hear, lass? You are wanted,” said Catherine briskly; in her, the gossiping nerve was indestructible, twitching even under mourning.

Margaret rose without haste. “Well, mother,” she said listlessly, “and here I am.”

There came a shuffling of feet, and a colourless, feeble old man was helped into the room, his weight borne by others. The sight of him struck the house like a draught that puts out a candle.

It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten.

Catherine gave a shriek and flung her apron over her head, as if cloth could keep out memory. Margaret shuddered so violently her whole frame answered, and she turned her head away swiftly, as though her eyes would burn at the looking.

A thin voice, worn down as a candle-wick, came from the old man’s lips. “Good people, a dying man hath come to ask your forgiveness.”

“Come to look on your work, you mean,” sobbed Catherine, dropping the apron, the tears breaking free as if they had been dammed too long.

“There, there—she is fainting; look to her, Eli, quick.”

“Nay,” said Margaret, and though her voice was weak it was clear. “The sight of him gave me a turn, that is all. Prithee, let him say his say, and go; for he is the murderer of me and mine.”

“Alas,” said Ghysbrecht, and he seemed to gather breath with effort, like a man climbing a steep hill. “I am too feeble to say it standing—and no one biddeth me sit down.”

Eli, who had followed him in, moved uneasily—half sullen, half ashamed at his own harshness, for this was still his house and he was still a host in it.

“Well, burgomaster,” said he, fighting the shake in his voice, “’tis not our wont to leave a visitor standing whiles we sit. But man, man—you have wrought us too much ill.”

And then the advocate rose—one who seldom spoke in vain in that family.

Little Kate.

“Father, mother,” she said, and the voice was soft, but it carried, like a clear bell heard across water. “My duty to you, but this is not well. Death squares all accounts. And see you not death in his face? I shall not live long, good friends; and his time is shorter than mine.”

At that, Eli hurried, and with his own hands set a chair. Ghysbrecht’s attendants settled him into it as tenderly as one props a failing plant.

“Go fetch the boxes,” said Ghysbrecht.

They brought in two boxes—plain enough without ostentation, yet made to hold weight—and withdrew, leaving their master alone among the people he had wronged.

Every eye was fixed upon him now, save Margaret’s, which kept its averted vigil as if looking elsewhere could undo the years.

Ghysbrecht’s fingers trembled over the fastenings. When he opened the first box, the smell of old parchment rose—dry, ink-bitten, faintly moulded, like the breath of a chest that has kept secrets too long. He drew out title-deeds, and spoke slowly, as though each word cost him.

“This land and these houses belonged to Floris Brandt, and do belong to thee of right, his granddaughter. These I did usurp for a debt long since defrayed with interest. These I now restore their rightful owner with penitent tears.”

From the second box he poured out gold—angels, bright and heavy, their edges catching light even in that dim room. The coins made a sound as they shifted: not the rich chime that makes the blood quicken in ordinary folk, but a colder clatter, like hail on a shutter—wealth falling where warmth could not follow it.

“In this other box are three hundred and forty golden angels,” said he, “being the rent and fines I have received from that land more than Floris Brandt’s debt to me. I have kept it compt, still meaning to be just one day; but Avarice withheld me—pray, good people, against temptation! I was not born dishonest: yet you see.”

“Well, to be sure!” cried Catherine, and the scandal in her voice wrestled with relief. “And you the burgomaster! Hast whipt good store of thieves in thy day. However—’tis better late than never. What, Margaret, art deaf? The good man hath brought thee back thine own. Art a rich woman. Alack, what a mountain o’ gold!”

Margaret did not turn.

“Bid him keep land and gold,” she said, and the words came out like something scraped raw, “and give me back my Gerard, that he stole from me with his treason.”

“Alas!” said Ghysbrecht, and for the first time his face seemed to hold something beyond sickness—something like fear, or sorrow, or the shadow of both. “Would I could. What I can I have done. Is it nought? It cost me a sore struggle; and I rose from my last bed to do it myself, lest some mischance should come between her and her rights.”

“Old man,” said Margaret, still turned away, “since thou, whose idol is pelf, hast done this, God and the saints will, as I hope, forgive thee. As for me, I am neither saint nor angel, but only a poor woman, whose heart thou hast broken. Speak to him, Kate—for I am like the dead.”

Kate waited a moment, as if listening—not only to the room, but to what lay behind it: the long echo of wrong, the deeper hum of pity. Then her silvery voice fell, and it did not argue so much as soothe, like music laid over a wound.

“My poor sister hath a sorrow that riches cannot heal. Give her time, Ghysbrecht; ’tis not in nature she should forgive thee all. Her boy is fatherless; and she is neither maid, wife, nor widow; and the blow fell but two days syne, that laid her heart a bleeding.”

Margaret’s answer was a single heavy sob, dragged up from somewhere deep and dark.

“Therefore,” Kate went on, “give her time! And ere thou diest, she will forgive thee all—ay, even to pleasure me, that haply shall not be long behind thee, Ghysbrecht. Meantime we, whose wounds be sore, but not so deep as hers, do pardon thee, a penitent and a dying man; and I, for one, will pray for thee from this hour. Go in peace!”

Their little oracle had spoken, and the room took it as final. Eli even offered him food and drink, as custom bids a householder do—invited him to break a manchet and take a stoup of wine, to give him heart for the journey.

But Ghysbrecht declined.

“What I have done is a cordial to me,” he said, and his voice, though weak, held a strange ease, as if a strap had been loosened from his chest. “Man seeth but a little way before him, neighbour. This land I clung so to—it was a bed of nettles to me all the time. ’Tis gone; and I feel happier and livelier like for the loss on’t.”

He called his men. They lifted him into the litter, and the soft shuffle of their feet faded down the street—an old man carried away with his repentance, leaving the sound of gold behind him.

When he was gone, Catherine hovered over the coins as if their shine could heat the room. She had never seen so much together. The angels lay in a small glittering heap like a harvest of metal, each stamped face turned up to the light, each worth a hundred dinners and a hundred nights under a whole roof.

Catherine was almost angry with Margaret for sitting apart “like an image.” She spoke of what money could do—how it could buy flour when mills were dear, and a side of bacon when winter pinched, and decent cloth for the child; how it could set a pot to boiling with good things and keep a house from going thin.

She teased and coaxed and pressed, until at last she drew Margaret toward the box.

“Better let her be, mother,” Kate said quietly. “How can she relish gold, with a heart in her bosom liker lead?”

But Catherine persisted.

Margaret looked down at her wealth with wide, wondering eyes, as if she had been shown a language she could not speak. For a breath, she only stared—at the yellow heap, at the deeds, at the neat accounting of restitution.

Then, as if something snapped inside her, she wrung her hands and cried out—not in anger, but in a piercing anguish that made the very air feel thin:

“TOO LATE! TOO LATE!”

And she shook off her leaden despondency only to fall into strong hysterics over the riches that had come too late to be warmed by the life she wanted to share them with. A little of this gold, a portion of this land, a year or two ago—when it had been as much her own as now—and Gerard would never have left her side for Italy, or any other place.

“Too late! Too late!”
