The *Pall Mall Gazette* burst onto the scene with all the subtlety of a fireworks display in a library. Immediate success! Whispers swirled that a powerful political faction lurked behind its pages, and illustrious names were tossed about like confetti at a society wedding. Were these rumors true? Well, discretion forbids us from confirming *or* denying—but let’s just say that a certain earth-shattering article on foreign policy, widely attributed to a certain noble lord (whose ties to the Foreign Office were as obvious as his tailor’s bills), was in fact penned by none other than Captain Shandon, hunched over a sticky table at the *Bear and Staff*, a printer’s boy panting at his elbow. Meanwhile, a series of dazzling financial essays, believed to be the work of a towering parliamentary statesman, flowed from the ink-stained fingers of Mr. George Warrington in the Upper Temple.

Coincidence? Perhaps. But then again, Percy Popjoy (son of Lord Falconet, a pillar of the aforementioned political party) was spotted more than once clomping up the stairs to Warrington’s chambers. And the *Gazette* did seem to possess an uncanny knack for insider information—the kind that didn’t just fall off the back of a hansom cab.

Then there were the poems. Ah, the poems! Signed boldly *P. P.*, they thundered across the page with all the delicacy of a drunkard reciting Homer. And lo! Percy’s dreadful novel received such rapturous praise in the *Gazette* that one might suspect either bribery or temporary insanity.

Young Arthur Pendennis, meanwhile, steered clear of politics but dove headfirst into literary criticism with the enthusiasm of a terrier in a rat pit. The *Gazette*’s offices in Catherine Street became his second home, manuscripts bulging from his pockets, his chest puffed with the intoxicating thrill of seeing his name—nay, his *genius*—in print.

Here, too, labored Mr. Jack Finucane, the sub-editor, a man who wielded scissors and paste like a surgeon performing a particularly violent amputation. With the diligence of a bloodhound, he scoured every newspaper in the kingdom for gossip about the aristocracy. Deaths, dinners, scandals—nothing escaped his keen eye. It was a sight to behold: Finucane, munching on a greasy chop from the cookshop, recounting the grandest balls of the season as if he’d waltzed through them himself—though his trousers were frayed and his shirt had seen better days.

Pen found Finucane’s contradictions endlessly amusing. Here was a man who wrote of dukes and duchesses with the familiarity of a butler, yet whose most frequent companions were tavern rats and ink-stained hacks. His prose occasionally stumbled—mistaking a viscount for a baron, perhaps—but the *Ballinafad Sentinel* suffered these blunders, not the lofty *Pall Mall Gazette*, where Finucane’s pen was kept on a tight leash.

As for Pen’s own contributions, he approached them with the solemnity of a scholar composing a dissertation. His reviews were sharp, witty, and—most dangerously—*honest*. This, alas, did not always sit well with Mr. Bungay, the publisher, who viewed journalism as a blunt instrument for crushing rivals, not a forum for fairness.

One day, Shandon confronted him. “Pendennis, what in blazes is this? You’ve gone and *praised* a Bacon publication!”

Pen blinked. “You mean to say I must declare good books bad simply because they’re printed by the enemy?”

Shandon sighed, the sigh of a man who has long since sold his scruples for a steady income. “My dear boy, do you think we run a critical journal to hand out bouquets to the competition?”

“I think we run it to tell the truth,” Pen retorted, chin high.

Shandon laughed. “Ah, the idealism of youth! Reminds me of my own naivety—before I realized that principles are a luxury, like champagne, and most of us drink porter.”

Pen left that day, more determined than ever to cling to his integrity, even as the cynical laughter of his elders rang in his ears.

But fate, ever mischievous, had a lesson in store. The very next issue of the *Gazette* featured a brutal evisceration of the *Spring Annual*—a publication which, unbeknownst to the world, contained Pen’s own verses (hidden under a pseudonym, naturally). Since Pen had refused to review it, Shandon had handed the task to the notorious Mr. Bludyer, a critic whose pen dripped more venom than a cobra’s fang. With savage glee, Bludyer tore the anthology to shreds, then promptly sold his review copy for brandy money.

And so Pen learned two invaluable truths: first, that the literary world is a battlefield where no good deed goes unpunished; and second, that one’s finest verses may one day buy a stranger’s bottle of gin.
