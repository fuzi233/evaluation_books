“The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew;
The furrow follow’d free;
We were the first that ever burst
Into that silent sea.”

—*The Ancient Mariner*

It was too late and too dark the night before to see the old house at Stow. But now, on this bright October morning, we may take a look around while Sir Richard and Amyas Leigh stroll through the terraced garden to the south of the manor, about eleven o’clock. Amyas has slept until luncheon—just an hour ago—but Sir Richard, despite the chaos of the previous night, rose by six, already in the valley with his two terrier dogs, chasing badgers to stir the courage in their own hearts and his.

Old Stow House—once grand, now only memory—stood four miles beyond the Cornish border, perched on the northern slope of the largest and most beautiful of those deep valleys I described in the last chapter. Eighty years after Sir Richard’s time, a massive Palladian mansion arose there, gaudy with every excess of poor taste. Legend says it was built by Charles the Second for Sir Richard’s great-grandson, heir to the famed Sir Bevil, who defeated Parliament’s forces at Stratton and fell bravely at Lansdowne near Bath. But like many things born under the Stuarts, it rose only to fall again. An old man who had watched its foundation stones laid lived long enough to see it torn down, its bricks and timber sold off-site. Since then, the stables became a farmstead, the tennis court a sheep pen, the great quadrangle a hayyard. Civilization, spreading fast elsewhere, retreated from this remote corner of England—though let us hope only for a season.

But I am not writing of that later, showy Stow House, nor of Sir Bevil, the most noble and gallant of his age, whose name Prince honored with a proud epigram:

“Where next shall famous Grenvil’s ashes stand?
Thy grandsire fills the sea, and thou the land.”

I write of an earlier time, a harsher generation, and of the original Stow House—part fortress, part home—standing since ancient times. It was built when the first Sir Richard, son of Hamon Dentatus, Lord of Carboyle, grandson of Duke Robert, son of Rou, settled at Bideford after slaying the Prince of South-Galis and the Lord of Glamorgan. He gave all his conquests in South Wales to the Cistercian monks of Neath. The house itself was a sprawling, half-castle, half-manor, much like Compton Castle near Torquay—the home of Humphrey Gilbert, Walter Raleigh’s half-brother and Richard Grenville’s closest friend, of whom more will come.

On three sides—north, west, and south—the high walls of the old bailey still stood, complete with machicolated turrets, arrow slits, and dark openings for dropping stones and fire upon attackers—remnants of a turbulent past. But the southern courtyard had become a flower garden, adorned with neat terraces, statues, clipped hedges, and intricate floral knots, a testament to the topiary art of the age. To the east, where the valley opened wide, the old walls had vanished, and the ruined Norman keep—shattered in the Wars of the Roses—had been replaced by the rich, elegant architecture of the Tudors. In short, the house reflected the time: caught between eras, embodying the transition from medieval austerity to the blossoming Renaissance, a world awakening to new life across Europe—may it never know autumn or winter.

From the house, the hill sloped steeply downward, and the garden where Sir Richard and Amyas walked offered a truly English view. One moment they could glimpse the blue ocean beyond the western wall, dotted with distant sails; the next, a vast panorama unfolded beneath them—tier upon tier of fertile parkland, stately avenues, golden autumn woods, and purple heather moors rolling up into the valley toward an ancient British earthwork, black and bristling with gorse atop a conical hill. On the eastern ridge, rising against the sky, stood the lofty tower of Kilkhampton Church, adorned with monuments and offerings from five centuries of Grenvilles. A soft yellow haze hung over park and wood, cattle lowed as they brushed flies beside a stream far below, colts played in the horse-park nearby, and their sires answered faintly from the Queen’s Park across the valley. A stag thundered through the woods, challenging another deep in the distance, whose roar echoed back, met in turn by defiance from high, mist-shrouded slopes. And close at hand, on the terrace before the house, sat Lady Grenville herself—the beautiful St. Leger of Annery, the jewel of the place—watching her noble children, then raising her eyes to her noble husband, then sweeping her gaze across this broad paradise of the West, until life seemed too full of joy and heaven too bright.

All the while, Amyas and Sir Richard paced slowly, speaking earnestly and deeply—for both knew this was the turning point in the boy’s life.

“Yes,” said Sir Richard, after Amyas had plainly told him of Rose Salterne and his brother, “yes, lad, you have chosen the better part—and so has your brother. That choice shall not be taken from you. But be strong, and trust in God that He will make a man of you.”

“I do trust,” said Amyas.

“Thank God,” replied Sir Richard, “that you’ve removed from my heart what had troubled me since your father—rest his soul—entrusted you to me. For all good things, Amyas, can become the worst when misused. Love of woman, because it can lift a man’s soul to heaven, can also drag him to hell. But you’ve learned better, Amyas. You know, as our German ancestors did, that *Sera juvenum Venus, ideoque inexhausta pubertas*—the youth’s passion is brief, hence the unspent vigor. And more than that—you understand that the foolish French and Italian fashion of clinging to women’s aprons, where no boy feels manhood unless he can *vagghezziare le donne*, whether maid or wife—this is no less harmful to the soul than open sin. It breeds vanity, waste, envy, bitterness, even hatred and murder. Even if escaped, the true treasure of manly love—meant for one alone—is squandered among many, and the bride receives only the dregs, the caput mortuum, of her husband’s heart, becoming little more than decoration or a means to produce heirs. May God, who saved me from such death-in-life, save you too!”

As he spoke, he looked down toward his wife on the terrace. She, sensing his words, glanced up with a smile so sweet that Sir Richard’s stern face softened into radiant warmth.

Amyas looked at them both and sighed. Then, changing the subject abruptly—

“May I go to Ireland tomorrow?”

“You shall sail in the *Mary* for Milford Haven, carrying these letters to Winter. If the wind favors, the master may lower down the river tonight and set sail. We must not delay.”

“Winter?” asked Amyas. “He’s no friend of mine—he abandoned Drake and us so cowardly at the Straits of Magellan.”

“Duty does not wait for personal quarrels, even just ones, lad. But he won’t be your general. When you reach the marshal or the Lord Deputy, give them this letter—they’ll assign you work—and hard work, I assure you.”

“I want nothing better.”

“Good,” said Sir Richard. “The best reward for doing well is more to do. He who has been faithful over few things will be entrusted with many. That is the true, heroic rest—worthy of gentlemen and sons of God. As for those who seek idleness in this world or the next, hoping God will feed them with a spoon, Amyas, I count them cowards, even if they call themselves saints.”

“I wish you could persuade my cousin of that.”

“He hasn’t yet learned what it means to lose his life to save it. Bad men have taught him—and I fear these Anabaptists and Puritans at home teach nothing else—that the one great task is to save one’s soul after death. Every man for himself. That, not divine self-sacrifice, is the one thing needful—the better part Mary chose.”

“I think men are selfish enough already without being taught it.”

“True, lad. Were I able, I’d hang such a teacher high as an enemy of mankind and corrupter of youth. Is there not enough cowardice and self-interest already in fallen sons of Adam? Must false prophets, with their promises of heaven and threats of hell, elevate our basest vices into heavenly virtues and paths to bliss? Farewell to chivalry, to desperate valor, to patriotism, to loyalty, to England and English manhood—if preachers begin telling every man, as Jesuits do, to care first for the ‘safety of his soul.’ Then no man will dare die at his post, fearing he isn’t fit to die. Do your duty like a man, Amyas—to your country, your queen, your God. Count your life worthless, as the holy men of old did. Do your work, and leave your soul to Him who judges fairly and mercifully. Does God show favoritism? Now come in, take the letters, mount your horse. And if I hear you died at Smerwick fort, wounded frontward, I’ll weep for your mother—but not for you.”

If anyone finds it surprising that a nobleman and warrior like Sir Richard should quote Scripture and speak of faith so freely, they should read the writings of the time. They’ll find how possible it was then for men of the world to be deeply rooted in the Gospel, yet free from superstition or false piety. The religion of that age was one no soldier need have shunned. At least, Sir Richard died as he lived—without fear, without complaint. Fifteen years later, shot through and through, captive among Spanish priests, crucifixes, confession, extreme unction, and all the tools meant to save souls from a loving God, these were his final words:

“Here die I, Richard Grenville, with a joyful and quiet mind; for I have ended my life as a true soldier ought—fighting for my country, queen, religion, and honor. My soul departs willingly from this body, leaving behind the lasting fame of having done what every brave soldier is bound to do.”

These were the last words of Richard Grenville. The pulpits of his day had shaped them.

But let us return. The day was not yet done. When they entered the house, the first person they met was the old steward, searching for his master.

“There’s a kind of rogue at the gate, Sir Richard—a masterless man, bold as brass, insists on speaking with you.”

“A masterless man? Let him speak if he wants gaol or gallows.”

“Well, sir,” said the steward, “I suspect that’s just what he wants. He swears he won’t leave till he sees you.”

“Seen me? By heavens, he’ll see me here and in Launceston too, if he likes. Bring him in.”

“Fegs, Sir Richard, we’re half afraid. With your permission—”

“Hillo, Tony!” called Amyas. “Who ever feared with Sir Richard’s leave?”

“What, does he have horns or a tail?”

“Massy no! But I’m afraid of treason for your honor. The fellow’s covered in strange patterns, brown as a filbert, tall and strong, foreign, and carries a mighty staff. I expect he’s a Jesuit or wild Irishman. The grooms won’t touch him, nor the dogs—otherwise he’d be under the pump already. Those who saw him coming swear fire came out of his mouth!”

“Fire from his mouth?” said Sir Richard. “The men are drunk.”

“Covered in marks? Then he’s a sailor,” said Amyas. “Let me go out and see him. If he needs putting in order—”

“He’s not so big he won’t fit in your pocket. Go, lad, while I finish writing.”

Amyas stepped outside. Leaning on his staff at the back door stood a tall, raw-boned, ragged man—“pinked all over,” as the steward had said.

“Hillo, lad!” said Amyas. “Before we talk, lay down that Plymouth cloak of yours.” He pointed to the cudgel, known among West Country sailors as a “cloak.”

“I’ll warrant,” said the steward, “where he found his cloak, he found a purse not far off.”

“But no hose or doublet—so his staff’s magic didn’t help him much. Put down your staff, man, and speak like a Christian, if you are one.”

“I am a Christian, though I look like a heathen; and no rogue, though masterless. I ask nothing, deserve nothing—only to speak with Sir Richard before I go on my way.”

There was something dignified yet humble in his tone that drew Amyas. He asked gently where he was going and where he came from.

“From Padstow Port to Clovelly town, to see my old mother—if she lives, which God knows.”

“Clovelly man!” cried the grooms in unison. “Why didn’t you say so?” A West Countryman was always kin.

“What’s your mother’s name?” asked the steward.

“Susan Yeo.”

“That one who lived under the archway?” asked a groom.

“Lived?” said the man.

“Aye, sure—she died three days ago, poor soul.”

The man stood silent for a moment. Then quietly, in Spanish, he said: “That which is, is best.”

“You speak Spanish?” asked Amyas, growing more curious.

“I must, young sir. I spent five years in the Spanish Main. Only landed two days ago. If you’ll let me speak with Sir Richard, I’ll tell him something that will make both ears of the listener tingle. If not, I’ll go to Mr. Cary of Clovelly—if he lives—and unburden myself. But I’d rather speak with one who’s a mariner like myself.”

“And you shall,” said Amyas. “Steward, bring this man in—he’s ragged, but a man of sense.” He led him inside.

“I only hope he’s not one of those Popish murderers,” muttered the steward, keeping his distance.

“Popish, old man? There’s little chance of that. Look here!” The man pulled back his rags to reveal a ghastly scar circling his wrist and winding up his forearm.

“I got that on the rack,” he said calmly, “in the Inquisition at Lima.”

“Oh, Father! Why didn’t you tell us you were a poor Christian?” the steward exclaimed.

“Because I’ve had only what I deserved—and barely tasted it, as the Lord knows who delivered me. I wasn’t going to beg or be shown off for it.”

“By heaven, you’re a brave fellow!” said Amyas. “Come straight to Sir Richard’s room.”

They entered the library, where Sir Richard sat amid books, dispatches, state papers, and warrants. Though not yet admiral, general, member of Parliament, or privy councillor—as men would be later—he corresponded with nearly every great man and knew the affairs of the realm.

“Hillo, Amyas—have you tamed the wild man already and brought him in to swear allegiance?”

Before Amyas could answer, the stranger fixed his eyes on him. “Amyas?” he asked. “Is that your name, sir?”

“Amyas Leigh, at your service.”

“Of Burrough by Bideford?”

“Why yes—what do you know of me?”

“Oh, sir, sir! Young minds forget quickly; but old, sorrowful ones remember too long. Do you recall one who served with Mr. Oxenham? A swearing reprobate, God forgive him—yet forgiven, for His Son’s sake—one who gave you a horn, a toy with a chart on it?”

“Soul alive!” cried Amyas, seizing his hand. “You’re him! The horn? I still have it—I’ll keep it till I die. But where is Mr. Oxenham?”

“So, good fellow,” said Sir Richard, rising, “where is Mr. Oxenham? You’re rushing to greet an old acquaintance, Amyas, before we’ve heard if he can give honest account of himself—or of his captain. Many a sailor returns without his captain, as poor Mr. Barker of Bristol discovered. May God spare us any treachery here.”

“Sir Richard Grenville,” said the man, “if I were guilty to my noble captain—as I am to God—I wouldn’t be here today, seeking favor from one who has never welcomed villainy. I know your character well. Trust in the Lord: if you’ll listen, you’ll learn my truth.”

“You’re a well-spoken knave. We shall see.”

“My dear sir,” whispered Amyas, “I’ll wager this man is innocent.”

“I believe him, but this is too serious to rely on guesswork. If he’ll swear—”

The man replied humbly: “If it pleases Sir Richard, I’d rather not be sworn.”

“But it does not please me, rascal! Did I not warn you, Amyas?”

“Sir,” said the man proudly, “God forbid my word should be less than my oath. But it goes against my conscience to swear.”

“What have we here? Some fantastical Anabaptist wiser than his teachers?”

“My conscience, sir—”

“The devil take it and you! I’ve never known a man begin to talk of conscience without planning something cruel or false.”

“Sir,” he answered coolly, “do you sit here to judge me by law, yet break it yourself with profane oaths—fines are levied for such?”

Amyas expected a storm—but Sir Richard merely pulled out a shilling and placed it on the table. “There—my fine to the poor of Kilkhampton. But listen: if you won’t swear, you’ll speak under compulsion. To Launceston gaol you go, accused of Mr. Oxenham’s death and mutiny. We’ve lost too many brave captains to crew treachery. He who refuses to clear himself by oath is guilty in my eyes.”

“My good sir,” said Amyas, unable to abandon his belief, “don’t risk your life, honor, and Mr. Oxenham’s reputation for such scruples. If questioned, you might be forced to lie under torture.”

“Not likely,” the man replied with a grim smile. “I’ve known the rack and strappado. I care little what man can do. I’d gladly swear—if I thought it lawful. But not believing it, I submit to human cruelty—though I hoped for mercy from one who’s seen God’s ways in the sea.”

Sir Richard smiled. “Well, you’re a brave fool, valiant though a fool. See how you’ve sworn a tenfold oath than I’d asked? But this is typical of your kind—hating forms, yet binding yourselves in heavier chains than the law demands. Yet what do they care for the common good, so long as each saves his own soul?”

Still, he nodded. “You’re sworn now—go on. First, who are you, and where from?”

“Well, sir,” said the man, unruffled, “I’m Salvation Yeo, born in Clovelly Street in 1526. My father was a barber-surgeon and preacher—Anabaptist, as they’re called. I thank God for it.”

“Fie!” said Sir Richard. “Thank God your father was an ass?”

“No,” said Yeo, “because he was a barber-surgeon. I learned a bit of that trade—and it saved my life, as I’ll tell you. A good mariner should know all worldly skills—from sewing to shoemaking—so he can adapt to whatever comes.”

“Well said,” said Sir Richard. “Now tell your tale—no comments.”

“Well, sir. I took to the sea young. I sailed with Captain Hawkins on his three voyages to Guinea for slaves, then to the West Indies.”

“Then you went thrice to a bad end,” said Sir Richard. “And the last time, you returned to a worse.”

“No denying the last,” said Yeo. “But the earlier ones? I doubt their unlawfulness. The negroes are of Ham’s line—cursed, reprobate, as Scripture says. Their blackness is Satan’s livery. Among them, none of the elect. So the elect need not treat them as brothers.”

“Ah,” said Sir Richard, “what a pragmatic sea-lawyer we have! And I doubt you’ll preach black robes are Heaven’s livery every Sunday, howling for a parson to wear Geneva fashion. Out upon you! Go on—before you preach at Launceston.”

Yeo continued, undeterred.
