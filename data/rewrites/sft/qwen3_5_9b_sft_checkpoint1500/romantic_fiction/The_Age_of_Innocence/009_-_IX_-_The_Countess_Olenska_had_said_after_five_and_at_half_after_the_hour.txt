The Countess Olenska had suggested meeting "after five," and at twenty minutes past the hour, Newland Archer stood before the peeling stucco facade of her rented house. A massive wisteria vine strangled the feeble iron railing of the balcony, framing a scene that felt worlds away from the polished avenues of Fifth Avenue. This was a strange quarter for a Polish Countess to inhabit; her neighbors included dressmakers, bird-stuffers, and writers, and further down the disheveled street, Archer recognized the dilapidated wooden home of a journalist named Winsett—a residence so humble it sent a chill of unease through his memory of other capital cities. Yet, Madame Olenska's dwelling was redeemed from squalor merely by a fresh coat of paint around the windows. As Archer surveyed the modest front, a grim thought crossed his mind: the Polish Count must have lost not only her fortune but her illusions to some cruel betrayal.

The young man's day had been unsatisfactory. After lunching with the Wellands, he had hoped to whisk May away for a solitary walk in the Park, to express how enchanting she had looked the previous night and to urge her to accelerate their wedding. Instead, Mrs. Welland had firmly reminded him that the rounds of family visits were far from complete. When he hinted at advancing the date, she had raised reproachful brows and sighed, "Twelve dozen of everything—hand-embroidered." Packed into the family landau, they had rolled from one tribal doorstep to another until Archer felt exposed, like a wild animal cunningly trapped. While he attributed this discomfort to his recent readings in anthropology, a damp sorrow fell upon his spirit when he realized the Wellands did not expect a wedding until autumn. For months, his life would be confined to the space between the cast-iron railings of their ghastly greenish-yellow house in East Thirty-Ninth Street.

He had intended to tell May of the Countess's summons, yet in the brief moments of privacy they shared, he had had more pressing matters to discuss. Furthermore, it struck him as absurd to mention it now. May clearly desired kindness toward her cousin; hadn't that wish hastened their engagement? Reflecting on this, he felt a peculiar relief, as if the Countess's arrival had shifted the burden of his future, liberating him to visit his cousin without her knowledge.

Standing on the threshold, curiosity gripped him. He was puzzled by the tone of her invitation, concluding that she was less simple than she appeared. A swarthy maid with a prominent bosom under a gay neckerchief answered the door, welcoming him with a display of white teeth. She led him through a narrow hall into a low, firelit drawing-room, leaving him alone for a significant time to wonder if she had gone to fetch her mistress or misunderstood his purpose entirely. Eventually, she returned with a lamp, and Archer, constructing a polite phrase from his knowledge of Dante and Petrarch, learned that the Signora was out but would return soon.

What he witnessed, illuminated by the flickering light, was a shadowy charm unlike any room he had known. The Countess had brought bits of wreckage from Europe—small, slender tables of dark wood, a delicate Greek bronze on the mantelpiece, and stretches of red damask nailed over discolored wallpaper beneath old Italian paintings. Newland prided himself on his knowledge of Italian art, having devoured the works of Ruskin, Symonds, and Walter Pater. Yet these pictures bewildered him; they defied the conventions he was used to seeing in Italy. He was disturbed by the silence of the house and worried that May might arrive unexpectedly, finding him sitting there with an air of illicit intimacy. But since he had come, he resolved to wait, sinking into a chair and stretching his feet toward the logs.

The atmosphere of the room was so distinct that his usual self-consciousness vanished, replaced by a sense of adventure. Medora Manson's shabby hire, with its background of pampas grass and Rogers statuettes, had been transformed by the Countess's touch into something intimate and foreign, subtly evoking old romantic scenes. Two Jacqueminot roses stood in a slender vase at his elbow, and a pervasive perfume—reminiscent of Turkish coffee, ambergris, and dried roses—drifted through the air, smelling of some far-off bazaar.

His mind drifted to May's future drawing-room. He knew Mr. Welland was eyeing a new estate in East Thirty-ninth Street, a house built in a ghastly greenish-yellow stone, praised for its perfect plumbing but lacking soul. Archer imagined his fate sealed: evenings passing through a Pompeian vestibule into a hall of varnished yellow wood. Beyond that, his imagination could not travel. He knew May would submit cheerfully to the purple satin and yellow tuftings of the Welland drawing-room, filled with sham Buhl tables and gilt vitrines. His only comfort was the hope that she would allow him to arrange his library as he pleased, perhaps with sincere Eastlake furniture.

The round-bosomed maid returned, drew the curtains, and pushed back a log, offering consolation in broken Italian: "Verra—verra." When she departed, Archer rose and wandered about. Was it wise to wait longer? Had he perhaps misunderstood the Countess? Suddenly, the ring of a stepper's hoofs echoed down the cobblestones. A carriage door opened, and through the parting curtains, Newland saw Julius Beaufort's compact English brougham, drawn by a big roan. The banker stepped out, offered a hand to assist the Countess, and after a brief exchange that seemed to negate whatever plans Beaufort had proposed, he jumped back into his carriage.

When the Countess entered, she showed no surprise at seeing Archer. Surprise was an emotion she rarely indulged. "How do you like my funny house?" she asked, tossing aside her velvet bonnet and standing tall in her cloak, her eyes meditative. "To me, it's like heaven."

"You've arranged it delightfully," Archer replied, conscious of the flatness of his words but driven by a desire to be simple and striking.

"Oh, it's a poor little place. My relations despise it. But at any rate, it's less gloomy than the van der Luydens'," she noted. The words delivered an electric shock to Archer; few rebellious spirits would dare call the stately home of the van der Luydens gloomy. Those privileged to enter usually shivered, speaking only of its "handsomeness." But suddenly, a thrill of gratitude ran through him that she had voiced the general shiver.

"It's delicious—what you've done here," he repeated.

"I like the little house," she admitted, lowering her voice. "But I suppose what I like is the blessedness of being here, in my own country and my own town... and then, of being alone in it." She spoke so softly he barely caught the final phrase, but in his awkwardness, he seized it. "You like so much to be alone?"

"Yes; as long as my friends keep me from feeling lonely." She sat near the fire, signing for Nastasia to bring tea, and gesturing for him to return to his armchair, noting that he had already chosen his corner. Leaning back, she folded her arms behind her head and gazed at the flames under drooping lids. "This is the hour I like best—isn't it?"

A proper sense of his dignity compelled him to answer, "I was afraid you'd forgotten the hour. Beaufort must have been very engrossing."

She looked amused. "Why—have you waited long? Mr. Beaufort took me to see a number of houses—it seems I am not to be allowed to stay in this one." She appeared to dismiss both Beaufort and Archer from her mind, continuing, "I've never been in a city where there seems to be such a feeling against living in *quartiers excentriques*. What does it matter where one lives? I'm told this street is respectable."

"It's not fashionable."

"Fashionable! Do you all think so much of that? Why not make one's own fashions? But I suppose I've lived too independently; at any rate, I want to do what you all do—I want to feel cared for and safe."

Touched, as he had been the evening before, by her expressed need of guidance, Archer added with a flash of sarcasm, "That's what your friends want you to feel. New York's an awfully safe place."

"Yes, isn't it? One feels that," she cried, missing the mockery. "Being here is like—like—being taken on a holiday when one has been a good little girl and done all one's lessons."

The analogy was well-meant but failed to please him entirely. He did not mind being flippant about New York but disliked hearing anyone else adopt the same tone. He wondered if she truly grasped the power of the machine she had escaped and how narrowly she had avoided crushing destruction at the Lovell Mingotts' dinner. Either she remained unaware of having skirted disaster, or she had lost sight of it in the triumph of the van der Luyden evening. Archer leaned toward the former theory; he suspected her New York was still completely undifferentiated, a conjecture that nettled him.

"Last night," he said, "New York laid itself out for you. The van der Luydens do nothing by halves."
