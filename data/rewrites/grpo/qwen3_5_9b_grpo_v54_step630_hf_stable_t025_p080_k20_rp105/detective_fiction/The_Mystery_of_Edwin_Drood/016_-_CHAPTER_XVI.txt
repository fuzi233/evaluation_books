When John Jasper emerged from his trance, the room was filled with the scent of hot broth and the anxious presence of Mr. and Mrs. Tope. His visitor sat rigid in a chair, hands resting heavily on his knees, a statue of silent vigilance.

"You've rallied nicely, sir," Mrs. Tope murmured, her voice trembling with relief. "You were utterly drained. No wonder!"

"A man," Mr. Grewgious intoned, his tone carrying the weight of a lecturer delivering a final verdict, "cannot endure the shattering of rest, the torment of the mind, and the exhaustion of the body without being thoroughly spent."

"I fear I have frightened you?" Jasper whispered, accepting the help offered to guide him to an easy chair.

"Not at all," Mr. Grewgious replied, his expression unchanged.

"You are too kind."

"Not at all," the lawyer repeated, the phrase rolling off his tongue like a rehearsed line.

Mrs. Tope moved immediately to the table. "You must drink wine, sir, and eat the jelly I prepared. You refused it at noon, though I warned you of the consequences. And you must have a wing of that roast fowl, which has been reheated twenty times if it has been once. It will be served in five minutes, and this gentleman will likely stay to ensure you eat."

The gentleman responded with a sharp snort—a sound ambiguous enough to mean yes, no, or silence—which Mrs. Tope ignored as she arranged the plates.

"Will you join me?" Jasper asked as the table was set.

"I cannot swallow a morsel, thank you," Mr. Grewgious answered flatly.

Jasper ate and drank with a voracious hunger that bordered on desperation. He consumed the food not for pleasure, but as fuel to stave off another collapse of spirit. Beside him, Mr. Grewgious remained upright, his face a mask of impassive politeness, radiating a refusal to engage in any conversation whatsoever.

"Do you know," Jasper said after pushing his plate away, his eyes fixed on the empty space before him, "that I find a fragment of comfort in what you have told me?"

"Do you?" Mr. Grewgious asked, his voice dry, implying the unspoken *I do not*.

"After the shock of learning that my beloved boy has vanished, destroying every plan I had built for him... yes."

"I shall be glad to hear your reasoning," Mr. Grewgious said.

"Is there not, or is there—if I deceive myself, correct me—hope that, realizing the heavy burden of explanation this new situation places upon him, he chose to flee rather than face the scrutiny of the idle world?"

"Such a thing is possible," Mr. Grewgious mused.

"It has happened," Jasper insisted, his voice gaining strength. "People have vanished to avoid a 'seven days' wonder,' leaving no trace for years."

"I believe such things occur," Mr. Grewgious conceded.

"When I had no suspicion that my boy withheld anything from me," Jasper continued, seizing the thread of hope, "what light pierced the darkness? I assumed his bride was here, his wedding imminent. How could I conceive he would leave voluntarily, in such a capricious and cruel manner? But now, knowing your information, is there not a crack through which daylight enters? If he departed of his own volition, is his absence less cruel to her? The fact that he had just parted from your ward provides a motive. It does not lessen the cruelty to me, but it spares her."

Mr. Grewgious could not deny this logic.

"And even regarding me," Jasper pressed, his face brightening with a fragile optimism, "he knew you were coming to tell me this. If your words have sparked this new thought in my mind, it follows he may have anticipated my conclusions. Grant that he foresaw them; then the cruelty to me—John Jasper, Music Master—vanishes!"

Mr. Grewgious assented again.

"I have harbored terrible doubts," Jasper admitted. "But your disclosure, though overwhelming, kindles a hope. You do not extinguish it when I speak it; you admit it is reasonable. I begin to believe: that he may have left of his own accord, and that he may yet be alive."

At that moment, Mr. Crisparkle entered. Jasper repeated his theory to him. The Minor Canon, already preparing his mind to defend his pupil, found the argument compelling. He recalled the immediate embarrassment Edwin Drood had faced regarding his secret engagement, a circumstance that could indeed drive a man to flight.

"I told Mr. Sapsea," Jasper explained, "that there was no quarrel between the two boys at their last meeting. Though their first encounter was hostile, their final meeting at my house was peaceful. Yet, Edwin was depressed. Now that I know the cause of his depression, I realize it may explain his absence."

"Heaven grant it is so!" Crisparkle cried.

"I pray to Heaven it is so!" Jasper echoed. He then addressed the gravity of his own bias. "You know, and Mr. Grewgious should know, that I held a deep prejudice against Mr. Neville Landless following his violent behavior on that first occasion. I feared for my nephew's life. I even recorded my dark forebodings in my diary. Mr. Grewgious must understand that my current hope exists despite my previous conviction of Landless's guilt."

This admission troubled Crisparkle deeply. He realized he had been withholding the truth of Neville's jealous outbursts and threats. He feared that revealing these facts now would seem like constructing a lie to protect his pupil. Yet, seeing Jasper's model of honesty, he resolved to speak. He testified to Jasper's integrity and declared his absolute confidence in Edwin's innocence, despite knowing Neville's fiery temper and romantic obsession with the girl.

Jasper's hopeful reaction was so intense it turned him pale. He vowed to cling to the possibility that Edwin had fled of his own free will.

Leaving the room, Crisparkle walked uneasily to Cloisterham Weir. The moonlight illuminated the falling water, a familiar sound that suddenly seemed ominous. He reasoned with himself, searching for the source of his unease, but found none. He returned at dawn, his eyes scanning the timbers of the weir.

There, caught in the crevices of the wood, glinting in the frosty air, was a gold watch. He dove into the icy water, retrieving the watch engraved with *E. D.* After a fruitless search for a body, he found only a shirt-pin stuck in the mud.

Returning to town, Crisparkle took Neville Landless to the Mayor. The evidence—the watch, the pin, the blood on Neville's clothes, the destroyed papers in his room, and the jeweler's testimony that the watch had not been wound since Edwin left Jasper's house at midnight—coalesced into a damning narrative. Neville had been seen wandering in a distracted state; he had threatened the missing boy; he had prepared to leave early in the morning.

Suspicion mounted until Neville was detained. The public outcry was fierce, fueled by Neville's known violent nature and history of threats. Yet, as the investigation pressed on, the case against him weakened. The girl from whom Edwin had parted claimed he had planned to wait for Mr. Grewgious, yet he vanished before the lawyer arrived. The watch, found at the weir, suggested it had been discarded shortly after Edwin left Jasper's house, implying a struggle or a hurried departure, but not necessarily murder.

Despite the lack of definitive proof of death, the weight of suspicion forced Neville's release. But the town would not let him go. The Dean intervened, stating that while human justice could err, it must act on available evidence. Sanctuary was no longer an option. Neville was ordered to leave Cloisterham, his reputation blighted by the lingering shadow of suspicion.

Only then did John Jasper return to his place in the choir. His haggard face, red with fever, betrayed the death of his hope. A few days later, while undressing, he pulled a diary from his coat pocket. With a solemn, terrifying expression, he handed the open book to Mr. Crisparkle.

"My dear boy is murdered," Jasper wrote, his voice barely a whisper. "The discovery of the watch and shirt-pin convinces me he was killed that night, his jewelry stolen to prevent identification. All my delusive hopes perish. I swear, and record this oath on this page: I will never discuss this mystery with any human being until I hold the clue in my hand. I will not relax my secrecy or my search. I will fasten the crime of this murder upon the murderer. And I devote myself to his destruction."
