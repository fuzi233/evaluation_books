After leaving Vienna, and long before you come to Budapest, the Danube slips over its paved stone banks and vanishes into a stretch of country no map ever dares render honestly. On official charts the region is smudged in a faint, fluffy blue that bleeds paler and paler the further it stretches from the surveyed shorelines, the word *Sümpfe* – marshes – scrawled across hundreds of unmarked square miles in thin, apologetic ink, as if the cartographers feared to write too loud of what lives there. This is no mere wetland: it is a sprawling, endless swamp choked waist-deep with willow bushes, a sea of green that stretches to the blurred horizon without a single rise, a single sharp edge to break its hunched, low-lying mass.

These willows never grow into trees. They refuse the dignity of rigid trunks, staying forever low, bowed, their supple stems bending like grass under the lightest breath of wind. They shift so constantly, their silver undersides flashing one after another as the gale rolls across the plain, that you get the unshakable, skin-prickling impression that the whole landscape is moving – creeping, slow, in unison, no two bushes ever staying in exactly the same place for more than a second. Wind ripples over them like a tide, rolling green then blazing silver, a living sea of leaves that never rests.

Freed from the hard banks that hemmed it in further upstream, the Danube here wanders wild, splitting into a thousand unmarked channels that tear at the sand, carve new whirlpools into the foam, rip whole clumps of willow out by the roots and carry them away to build new islands that vanish entirely when the high flood peaks. Nothing here stays fixed. Nothing here belongs to humans.

This stretch of river begins not long past Pressburg, and we – two men in a lopsided Canadian canoe, a dented gipsy tent lashed to the stern and a single black frying pan clinking against the gunwale – drifted into it on the crest of a rising flood at mid-July. We had slipped through the sleeping streets of Vienna an hour before sunrise, left it nothing but a wisp of smoke behind us against the distant Wienerwald hills, breakfasted under birches that roared in the rising wind below Fischeramend, and sped past Orth, Hainburg, the crumbled Roman ruins of Carnuntum where Marcus Aurelius once wrote his meditations, before the Carpathian foothills loomed ahead and we crossed the unmarked border into Hungary.

The current drove us at twelve kilometers an hour, the muddy brown flood water yanking us onto shingle beds, spinning us like a cork in sudden belching whirlpools, before the spires of Pressburg broke over the treeline. The canoe leapt beneath us like a spooked horse, we dodged the sunken chain of the old flying ferry, rounded a sharp left turn – and plunged straight over a lip of yellow foam into the land of the willows.

The shift was instantaneous, like a film reel snapping without warning from a crowded city street to a dead, alien world. Less than half an hour later, there was no sign of humanity left anywhere in sight: no fishing hut, no distant wisp of chimney smoke, no other boat, not even a scrap of discarded trash bobbing in the current. The isolation settled over us so thick we could almost taste it, and we laughed uneasily, joking that we ought to have applied for a special passport to cross into this unmarked kingdom – that we had trespassed somewhere no human had any right to be, where unwritten warnings were stitched into every rustle of the leaves, for anyone who had the eyes to see them.

The wind howled so hard by mid-afternoon our arms ached from fighting it, and we began hunting for a place to make camp. Landing was nearly impossible: the flood would hurl us at the shore then yank us back out again before we could grab hold, willow branches snagged at our sleeves and tore at our palms when we reached out to slow the canoe, and we dragged whole handfuls of sandy bank into the water with us before a brutal sideways gust blew us into a sheltered backwater and we beached the bows in a cloud of stinging spray. We collapsed onto the hot, shifting yellow sand panting, and all around us thousands of willow bushes clapped their leaves in the gale, a million tiny rustling claps that sounded far too much like an audience closing in, applauding for the wrong reason.

“What a river,” I said to my friend – the Swede, my traveling companion of years, the man I’d pushed and waded with through the shallow upper reaches of the Danube back in June, where the stream bubbled small and bright out of the Black Forest.

“Doesn’t suffer fools,” he grunted, yanking the canoe further up the sand before he flopped back for a nap, face half turned to the sun.

I lay beside him, and the old familiar sense of the Danube as a living thing wrapped around me, thicker than ever here. We’d followed it for weeks, watched it grow from a tiny trickle that would vanish down holes in the Swabian limestone hills to run under a different name for miles, to the great roiling force that had shoved us through the Passau gorge shoulder to shoulder with the thundering Inn river. We knew all its voices: the sibilant hissing it sang to the moon at night, grinding pebbles along its bed, the gurgle of hidden whirlpools that bubbled up without warning on perfectly calm water, the deep unceasing thunder that rumbled under every other sound, the sound of it tearing chunks of bank apart in the dark. It had never been cruel to us, before. Just vast. Old. Something that held our tiny canoe on its shoulders without ever really noticing we were there.

But here, past Pressburg, the Danube had stopped playing tricks. It had grown serious. Halfway to the Black Sea, far from the towns and bridges and roads, it had dropped all pretense of being a river for human use. It split into three separate arms, none of them marked on our map, and the Hungarian provision seller in Pressburg had grabbed my wrist hard before we left, his face pale, to warn us off. “Take a wrong channel, and when the flood drops, you will be stranded forty miles from any living soul. No farms. No fishermen. No one comes here. The river will leave you high and dry to starve. And the willows –” He’d cut himself off, shaken his head, and refused to say another word. We’d laughed it off, bought extra tins of beans, but we never let that warning slip entirely from our minds.

The sun was still two hours from setting when I left the Snede asleep on the sand to explore our camp. The “island” was barely an acre of shifting shingle, raised no more than three feet above the roiling flood, triangular with the point aimed straight upstream. I stood at that point for minutes, watching the red-stained flood bear down on me, roaring so loud the sand under my boots trembled, the waves throwing spray so high it stung my cheeks. The island swayed under me, or maybe the willows swayed so hard the whole world felt like it was moving, slipping downstream. When I turned to look the other way, the river vanished into the mass of willows, the bushes huddled shoulder to shoulder like a herd of monstrous prehistoric things crowding down to drink, swallowing the water whole, swallowing the light, swallowing any trace of the path ahead.

That was when the unease crept in, uninvited, cold down the back of my neck. It wasn’t awe at the river’s power, wasn’t fear of the gale that could tear willows up by the roots and toss them like chaff. It was the willows. Acres and acres of them, stretching as far as the eye could see, packed so tight there was no space between their stems, all of them turned – I knew it, though I couldn’t see a single face, a single eye – turned towards us, watching. Waiting. They were not neutral. They were not friendly. We had trespassed somewhere that did not belong to mankind, and every leaf rustling in the wind was a quiet note of that fact.

I didn’t tell the Swede. He was practical, no patience for vague unease, he would have laughed and called me fanciful. We set up the tent in the small dip at the center of the island, the willows huddling close to block the worst of the wind, and he grumbled that there was no firewood, no stones to stake the tent pegs deep, that the sand was already shrinking, the flood eating away at the edges of our tiny island foot by foot. “Won’t last the night at this rate,” he said. “I’m sleeping in my clothes. We keep the canoe right by the tent, ready to go at a second’s notice.”

He wandered off along the bank to hunt for driftwood, and a second later I heard him curse, sharp and uncharacteristic, not his usual easy laugh.

“Good God – what is that?”

I ran over to him. He was staring out at the foaming current, pointing. A dark bulk rolled over and over in the waves, drifting fast towards us, disappearing under the foam then bobbing up again. It was no log. When it was level with our position, it lurched, all on its own, and twisted to face us. Its eyes glowed a weird, bright yellow, reflecting the sunset, staring straight into ours for a long, terrible heartbeat before it gave a great gulping dive and vanished.

“Otter,” we both blurted at the exact same time, laughing too loud, our hearts hammering. It was an otter. Alive. Hunting. But neither of us could shake the split second where we’d both been certain it was the face of a drowned man, dead a long time in the flood.

We had turned to head back to the camp, our arms piled high with driftwood, when we saw him. A man, standing upright in a flat-bottomed boat, being swept down the opposite shore at breakneck speed. He was gesticulating wildly, screaming at us, but the wind tore his words away. He was making the sign of the cross over and over, not at us – past us, at the wall of willows at our backs, his face as white as bone. In a breath he was gone, vanished around the bend of the river into the growing mist, no wake left behind him, no trace that he had ever been there at all.

The Swede’s laugh was thin, forced, when he spoke. “Saw our smoke, probably. The locals around here are full of old tales. The shop woman back in Pressburg told no one ever lands in the Sumpfe, that it belongs to beings that aren’t human. He got spooked, that’s all.”

Neither of us believed him. The wind rose higher, tearing at our hair, and a thousand thousand willow bushes hissed around us in the gathering twilight. We realized, all at once, that we could not hear a single bird call. Not one.
