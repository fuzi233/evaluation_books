He stood at the curb, swallowed by misery, watching her until she vanished from sight. Yet the moment she was gone, the weight upon his spirit lightened—almost imperceptibly at first, then with a swift, dizzying rush. She had released him. Or perhaps, in truth, he had never truly been bound. No matter. The path ahead was clear now, and he could move freely.

By the time he was halfway home, his steps had quickened, his mind sharpened by a giddy exhilaration. He knew exactly what came next.

The vicar’s house lay a short walk from the square—a man who might know something of the old house with its faded insurance marks and sagging *To Let* signs. If anyone could offer answers, it would be him. And once that final thread was pulled… Oleron’s lips curled. Then things would *happen*.

But the vicar proved disappointingly cautious.

*Yes, the house was old*—hardly a revelation. *Rumored to be haunted*—as if every crumbling structure didn’t whisper such tales to the gullible. The vicar’s voice was measured, careful, as though weighing each word before letting it slip free.

Oleron smiled thinly. “Spare my nerves. How long has it stood empty?”

“A dozen years, perhaps.”

“And the last tenant?” Oleron’s pulse stuttered as he added, “*Him or her?*”

“Him,” the vicar said. “A man named Madley. An artist. Kept to himself, rarely seen outside the place. And—” Here, the vicar faltered, then exhaled. “Since you’ve come asking, and since truth is preferable to whispers… Madley died there. Under unusual circumstances.”

Oleron stilled. “Oh?”

“The post-mortem found no food in his stomach, though he wasn’t destitute. His body was… withered. Suicide was suggested, but starvation?” The vicar’s mouth twisted. “An open verdict.”

Oleron absorbed this silently. “Is there a history of the parish?”

“Fragments. I’ve notes on its ecclesiastical records, if you’d care to see them. But the parish is large, my time limited…”

The interview dissolved into polite nothings. Oleron thanked him, left, and walked home slowly—deliberately. Twice he turned away mere steps from his gate, prolonging the journey.

What came next required precision. He needed his mind *empty*, receptive, as it had been that first morning when he’d sat down to write *Romilly*. Back then, he’d been cluttered, sluggish. The house’s influence had crept in slowly, saturating him drop by drop.

Now? Now he was unburdened. Free of *Romilly*, free of her ghost.

Now the Beckoning Fair One had his full attention.

———

At half-past two, he turned the key in the lock, stepped inside, and shut the door behind him.

The effect was instantaneous. Triumph surged through him. It was like stepping into another world—one where the air was lighter, the very walls hummed with welcome. The flowers in their bowls, the pale panels, the polished floor—all seemed to *laugh*.

Oleron laughed back. “Oh, you’re lovely,” he murmured.

He stretched on the couch, languid as a convalescent awaiting a beloved visitor. The hours slipped by in a haze of contentment, his gaze drifting lazily over the room until darkness fell.

And with darkness came the noises of the house.

———

Nothing *happened*. Not that night, nor the next. He maintained his watchful stillness, a plate waiting for an impression.

On the third day, understanding dawned. A sly amusement curled in his chest.

“Ah,” he chuckled. “Playing coy, are we?”

He wouldn’t summon Elsie. No need for blunt force.

Instead, he went out. Spent the day in the country. Let the Fair One stew in neglect.

He returned late, well past eleven.

“*Now*,” he murmured, fingers closing around his keys.

Inside, he was methodical. Deliberate. A single candle lit his path as he made his rounds—kitchen, bathroom, study—moving with calculated weariness.

Then, the bedroom.

He set the candle on the chest of drawers, feigned a yawn. His reflection flickered in the mirror above the mantel, alongside the gleam of the candle’s flame.

And then—movement.

A faint shimmer in the glass, a gliding spark. The reflection of his comb, suspended in air, moving in slow, deliberate strokes.

*Something* was being combed.

Oleron’s pulse hammered, but his face remained slack. He wound his watch. Counted coins. All the while, the comb kept its rhythm, sweeping through empty space.

Finally, he turned.

The comb hung midair, untouched.

A laugh caught in his throat. He reached out, plucked it from the air. Just an ordinary comb, cold in his palm.

Then his gaze fell on the manuscript in his chair—the original *Romilly*.

“Hm,” he muttered, tossing it aside.

———

Nights bled into days. Oleron coaxed, taunted, pleaded. His life narrowed to a single hunger: to meet the unseen thing that shared his home.

And still, it eluded him.
