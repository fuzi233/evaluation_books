The village of Bohun Beacon sat atop a hill so precipitous that the church's tall spire appeared merely the summit of a miniature mountain. At the base of this sacred peak stood a smithy, perpetually glowing with fire and cluttered with hammers and scrap metal. Directly opposite, separated by a rough cobblestone crossroads, lay "The Blue Boar," the village's sole inn. It was at this intersection, bathed in the shifting light of dawn, that two brothers encountered one another—one beginning his day, the other concluding it.

The Reverend and Honourable Wilfred Bohun was deeply pious, hastening toward austere devotions at daybreak. His elder brother, Colonel the Honourable Norman Bohun, possessed no such devotion. Dressed in evening wear, he sat on a bench outside the inn, consuming what a philosophical observer might deem either his final drink of Tuesday or his first of Wednesday. The Colonel cared little for distinction.

The Bohuns were among the rare aristocratic families tracing their lineage directly to the Middle Ages; their banner had once flown over Palestine. Yet, it is a grave error to assume such houses remain bastions of chivalric tradition. Traditions survive best among the poor; aristocrats live in fashion. The Bohuns had been Mohawks under Queen Anne and dandies under Victoria, but over the last two centuries, like many ancient houses, they had decayed into mere drunkards and degenerate fops, with whispers of insanity creeping in. There was something inhuman about the Colonel's wolfish pursuit of pleasure, and his habitual refusal to return home until morning bore the mark of a terrible, chronic insomnia. He was a tall, fine creature, elderly yet possessing startlingly yellow hair. He might have appeared merely leonine, but his blue eyes were sunk so deeply into his face they looked black, set slightly too close together. Long yellow moustaches framed his face, flanked by deep furrows running from nostril to jaw, carving a permanent sneer into his features. Draped over his evening clothes was a curious pale-yellow coat resembling a dressing gown more than an overcoat, topped by an extraordinary broad-brimmed hat of bright green—an Oriental curiosity seized at random. He took pride in these incongruities, boasting that he always managed to make them appear congruous.

His younger brother, the curate, shared the yellow hair and elegance but dressed strictly in black, buttoned to the chin, his face clean-shaven, cultivated, and somewhat nervous. He seemed devoted entirely to religion, though some—most notably the blacksmith, a Presbyterian—suspected it was a love of Gothic architecture rather than God. They claimed his ghostly haunting of the church stemmed from a morbid thirst for beauty, mirroring his brother's rage against women and wine. Whether this charge was true was debatable, yet the curate's practical piety was undeniable. Often found kneeling not before the altar but in secluded spots—the crypt, the gallery, or even the belfry—he sought solitude and secret prayer.

At that moment, Wilfred was about to enter the church through the smithy's yard when he paused, frowning slightly as he noticed his brother's cavernous eyes staring in the same direction. Assuming the Colonel was interested in the church, Wilfred offered no speculation. His gaze shifted to the blacksmith's shop. Though the blacksmith was a Puritan and unrelated to the family, Wilfred had heard rumors of a beautiful, celebrated wife. Casting a suspicious look across the shed, he watched the Colonel rise with a laugh to address him.

"Good morning, Wilfred," the Colonel said. "Like a diligent landlord, I watch sleeplessly over my flock. I intend to visit the blacksmith."

Wilfred looked down. "The blacksmith is out. He is at Greenford."

"I know," the Colonel replied, his laughter silent. "Precisely why I am visiting him."

"Norman," the cleric said, fixing his eyes on a pebble in the road, "are you ever afraid of thunderbolts?"

"What do you mean?" the Colonel asked. "Is your hobby meteorology?"

"I mean," Wilfred said, refusing to look up, "do you ever consider that God might strike you down in the street?"

"I beg your pardon," the Colonel said. "I see your hobby is folklore."

"I know your hobby is blasphemy," the religious man retorted, stinging the one living nerve of his nature. "But if you do not fear God, you have ample reason to fear man."

The elder brother raised his eyebrows politely. "Fear man?"

"Barnes the blacksmith is the largest and strongest man for forty miles around," the clergyman stated sternly. "I know you are no coward or weakling, but he could throw you over the wall."

The remark struck home, being true. The deepening line beneath the Colonel's mouth and nose darkened. For a moment, he stood with his heavy sneer fixed. Then, instantly, Colonel Bohun recovered his cruel good humor, laughing to reveal two dog-like front teeth beneath his moustache.

"In that case, my dear Wilfred," he said casually, "it was wise for the last of the Bohuns to emerge partially armored."

He removed the strange round green hat, revealing it was lined inside with steel. Wilfred recognized it as a light Japanese or Chinese helmet, torn down from a trophy hanging in the old family hall.

"It was simply the first hat to hand," his brother explained airily. "Always the nearest hat—and the nearest woman."

"The blacksmith is away at Greenford," Wilfred said quietly. "His return time is uncertain."

Turning with a bowed head, he crossed himself as if dismissing an unclean spirit. He wished to escape the grossness of the scene into the cool twilight of the Gothic cloisters, but fate ensured his morning of prayer would be interrupted by small shocks. Upon entering the church, usually empty at that hour, a kneeling figure rose hastily and approached the daylight of the doorway. The curate froze in surprise. The early worshipper was none other than the village idiot, the blacksmith's nephew, a boy named "Mad Joe" who cared for nothing—not the church, nor anything else. He was a dark, strong, slouching lad with a heavy white face, straight dark hair, and a mouth perpetually open. Passing the priest, his vacant countenance showed no sign of his thoughts or actions. Never known to pray, what prayers was he uttering now? Extraordinary ones, certainly.

Wilfred stood rooted, watching the idiot exit into the sunshine, seeing his dissolute brother hail him with jovial familiarity. The last image was of the Colonel tossing pennies into Joe's open mouth, pretending seriously to aim for it.

This grim tableau of earthly stupidity and cruelty drove the ascetic to seek purification in prayer. He climbed to a gallery pew beneath a colored window he cherished—a blue pane depicting an angel bearing lilies. Here, he tried to push aside the image of the half-wit with his fish-like mouth and his brother, pacing like a starving lion. He sank deeper into the cold, sweet colors of silver blossoms and sapphire skies.

Half an hour later, he was found by Gibbs, the village cobbler, summoned in haste. Rising promptly, Wilfred knew no trivial matter would draw Gibbs to the church. The cobbler, typically an atheist, was a sight even stranger than Mad Joe's presence. It was a morning of theological puzzles.

"What is it?" Wilfred asked stiffly, reaching for his hat with trembling hands.

Gibbs spoke in a tone surprisingly respectful, even huskily sympathetic. "Please excuse me, sir. We felt compelled to inform you immediately. I'm afraid a dreadful thing has occurred, sir. I'm afraid your brother..."

Wilfred clenched his frail hands. "What devilry has he committed now?" he cried in passionate volition.

"Why, sir," Gibbs coughed, "I'm afraid he's done nothing, and won't do anything. I'm afraid he's finished. You'd better come down, sir."

Following Gibbs down a short winding stair, they emerged at an entrance higher than the street. In one glance, Wilfred saw the tragedy laid out flat beneath him like a map. In the smithy's yard stood five or six men, mostly in black, including an inspector. Present were the doctor, the Presbyterian minister, and the Roman Catholic priest, whose parishioner the blacksmith's wife was. The latter was speaking urgently to her as she sobbed blindly on a bench; she was a magnificent woman with red-gold hair. Between these groups, just clear of the main pile of hammers, lay a man in evening dress, sprawled flat on his face. From above, Wilfred could identify every detail of his costume, down to the Bohun rings on his fingers, but the skull was merely a hideous splash of blackness and blood.

Wilfred gave one glance and ran down the steps. The doctor, the family physician, saluted him but received little acknowledgment. Stammering, he said, "My brother is dead. What does it mean? What is this horrible mystery?" An uneasy silence fell until Gibbs, the most outspoken, replied, "Plenty of horror, sir, but not much mystery."

"What do you mean?" Wilfred asked, his face pale.

"It's plain enough," Gibbs answered. "There is only one man for forty miles who could deliver such a blow, and he is the man with the most reason to."
