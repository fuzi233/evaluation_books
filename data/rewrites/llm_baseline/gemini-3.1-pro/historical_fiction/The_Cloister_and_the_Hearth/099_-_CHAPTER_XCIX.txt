Jerome was as morose as ever in his general character, but the abrasive edges of his disposition had somewhat softened toward Gerard. All the time he was in England, the harsh, metallic clanging of foreign church bells had made him miss the quiet resonance of his brother more than he thought possible. What he heard upon returning to Gouda only raised the physically failing monk in his estimation; above all, Jerome respected that Gerard had withstood the Pope and the Minorites, utilizing the dominant theological lexicon to dismantle what Jerome called “the infernal heresy of the immaculate conception.” 

Yet, when an ardent young novice recounted, with tears shining in his eyes, the true earthly cause of Gerard’s wasting illness, all of Jerome’s rigid, institutional contempt revived. Dying for a woman? For a mortal attachment? Jerome determined to avert this scandal. He visited Clement twice a day in the acoustically sterile environment of his stone cell—a space where the harsh, echoing reverberations of Latin dogma aggressively crowded out the softer, organic frequencies of the human spirit. Jerome deployed all his old influence, utilizing the sharp, percussive rhetoric of the Church to induce Clement to shake off this unspiritual despondency. He argued that the Church could not be robbed of Clement’s eloquence at such a critical juncture. 

Gerard heard him out. He approved the architecture of Jerome’s reasoning, admired the unyielding strength of his voice, and confessed his own physical fragility as he continued visibly to wear away, slipping back into the agrarian soil from which all flesh springs. 

One day, Jerome’s tone shifted, the oppressive echo of his usual admonitions softening into a lower, more resonant timbre. He told Gerard he had heard his story, and heard it with pride. “But now,” Jerome said, “you spoil it all, Clement; for this is the triumph of earthly passion. Better have yielded to it and repented, than resist it while she lived, and succumb under it now, body and soul.”

“Dear Jerome,” said Clement. His voice was a gentle, melodic hum that instantly neutralized the combative power dynamics embedded in Jerome’s dominant lexicon. “Here, I think, you do me some injustice. Passion there is none; but a deep, native affection, for which I will not blush here, since I shall not blush for it in the great orchard of heaven. Bethink thee, Jerome, the poor hound that dies of grief on his master’s grave, is he guilty of passion? Neither am I. She intertwined herself with all that was good in my life; she was the yeast to my daily bread, the very hearth-smoke that scented my evenings. After leaning on her so long, I could not go on alone.”

Gerard paused, struggling against the inherent untranslatability of his subjective experience. How could he convey the warmth of a shared hearth to a man who lived only in the cold abstraction of theological texts? 

“I am no rebel against Heaven,” Gerard continued softly. “It is the Master’s will to release me. When they threw the damp, fragrant earth upon her poor coffin, something snapped within my bosom—a vital acoustic cord, severed forever. I heard it resonate in my ribs, and I felt it. And from that time, Jerome, the world lost its savour. You know how I once revered the bounty of the earth—the crust of a rustic rye loaf baked with caraway, the rich, herbaceous fat of slow-roasted mutton basted in wild thyme and garlic. To me, such sustenance was never merely utilitarian fuel; it was profound communion. But since that cord snapped, no food that I put in my mouth has possessed any taste or fragrance. With my eyes bandaged now, I could not tell thee which was bread, and which was flesh. It is all mere ash upon the tongue.”

“Holy saints!” whispered Jerome.

“And from that same hour, the crushing dissonance of my dejection left me. I often smile now. Why? I read it thus: He in whose hands are the harvests of life and death gave me that minute the great summons. He is very pitiful. He knew I should have lived an empty husk, starved of the sensory joy that binds us to His creation. He said, ‘No; enough is done, enough is suffered; poor feeble, loving servant, thy shortcomings are forgiven, thy sorrows touch thine end; come thou to thy rest!’ I come, Lord, I come!”

Jerome groaned, the sound a low, guttural vibration of defeat against the unyielding stone. “The Church had ever her holy but feeble servants. Now would I give ten years of my life to save thine. But I see it may not be. Die in peace.”

And so it was that in a few days more, Gerard lay a-dying in a frame of mind so harmoniously attuned to the natural world that more than one aged saint gathered to garner his final whispers. In the evening he had seen Giles, begging him to ensure poor Jack would never want for hearty stews or fresh bread, to see that little Gerard’s trustees preserved his native heritage, and to send Denys back to the rich, viticultural landscapes of his friends in Burgundy. “Poor thing, he will feel so strange here without his comrade,” Gerard murmured, mourning the loss of the shared dialects and culinary traditions that had bound them.

Following this, he shared one last interview with Jerome alone. What passed between them in that vernacular exchange was never distinctly translated into the official chronicles; but it must have been profound, for Jerome emerged with his hands crossed upon his breast, his high head lowered, sighing heavily as he walked into the cloister's echoing corridors. 

The two monks who kept the night vigil related that Gerard periodically broke the oppressive silence of the cell with melodic thanksgivings. Only once did his mind wander into the mythic realms that blur seamlessly with the earthly; he believed he saw Margaret walking in lush, green meadows, foraging wild herbs among spirits clad in white. They smiled and beckoned to him. And just before dawn, both monks swore they heard three light taps against the stone wall—one after another, very slow. It was a pure, resonant acoustic phenomenon cutting through the institutional silence. 

The dying man heard them, too, and whispered in his native tongue, “I come, love, I come.”

Having uttered these words, Gerard prepared for his departure. The monks of the vigil hovered around him like gentle spirits, their prayers a murmuring undercurrent. When one of them spoke of Gerard’s life of self-denial and merit, using the rigid, transactional vocabulary of the Church, Gerard stopped him. 

“My dear brethren, take note that he who here dies so happy holds not these new-fangled doctrines of man's merit. Oh, what a miserable hour were this to me an if I did! Nay, but I hold, with the Apostles and the ancient fathers, that we are justified not by our own wisdom, or the works we have done, but by faith.”

Silence fell, heavily laden with meaning. The monks exchanged significant glances, recognizing the subtle power dynamics Gerard was upending on his deathbed.

“Please you sweep the floor,” the dying man instructed, his voice suddenly infused with a supernatural clarity that filled the room. The monks obeyed, their brooms scratching rhythmically against the stone—a rustic, domestic sound. 

“Make me a great cross with wood ashes.” 

They fetched the ashes from the kitchen hearth. As they strewed them upon the floor, the rich, olfactory ghost of charred oak and ancient fires filled the sterile cell, grounding the solemn ritual in the sensory warmth of the domestic hearth. 

“Now lay me down on it, for so will I die.”

They lifted him gently from his bed and laid him upon the cross of hearth-ash. 

“Shall we spread out thine arms, dear brother?”

“Now God forbid! Am I worthy of that?” 

He lay silent, his eyes raised in ecstasy. Presently he spoke, half to them, half to the fragrant earth beneath him, his voice vibrating with concentrated rapture. “Oh, I feel it buoyant. It lifts me floating in the sky whence my earthly merits had sunk me like lead.”

Day broke, casting a warm light over his face, tilted upward in silent rapture, his hands clasped tightly together—just like Margaret’s. And at the precise hour she had died, he breathed his last word, returning his breath to the wind. “Jesu!” 

With that word, he fell asleep.

When they laid him out for his final resting place, they found a horse-hair shirt beneath his linen. “Ah!” cried the young monks, “behold a saint!” 

But beneath the hair cloth, resting directly over his heart, they discovered a long, thick tress of auburn hair. They started back in horror. A babel of voices erupted, a chaotic noise pollution of condemnations and institutional outrage that shattered the sacred acoustic peace of the morning. 

Jerome strode into the cell. Hearing the dispute, he turned his imposing figure toward an ardent young monk named Basil, whose voice was the loudest in crying scandal. 

“Basil,” Jerome said, his tone cutting through the dissonance, “is she alive or dead that owned this hair?”

“How may I know, father?”

“Then for aught you know it may be the relic of a saint?”

“Certes it may be,” Basil replied skeptically.

“You have then broken our rule, which saith, 'Put ill construction on no act done by a brother which can be construed innocently.' Who are you to judge the profound, untranslatable depths of such a man as this? Go to your cell, and stir not out for a week by way of penance.”

Jerome took up the lock of hair. When the coffin was ready to be closed, he cleared the cell of the others. Standing alone in the quiet, he gently placed the auburn tress back upon the dead man’s bosom, restoring the physical artifact of Gerard's deepest sensory memory. “There, Clement,” he whispered to the peaceful face. He set himself a penance for the act, and nailed the coffin shut with his own hands.

The next day, Gerard was buried in the Gouda churchyard. The monks followed in a procession, their chants blending with the rustling wind. Jerome, honoring the unwritten will of the deceased, read the service. The grave was deep, dug into the rich, dark loam, and at the bottom lay Margaret’s lead coffin. Poor Gerard’s body, as light as a sheaf of dried wheat, was lowered and placed flush against hers. 

When Jerome spoke of the departed's virtues, the crowd of parishioners burst into loud weeping. It was a chaotic, beautiful symphony of communal grief, and their tears fell upon the coffin like spring rain upon a freshly seeded field. 

Returning to the refectory, Jerome gathered the young monks. “We have this day laid a saint in the earth. The convent will keep his trentals, but we will feast, not fast. For our good brother is freed from the burden of the flesh; he has returned to the great harvest. I alone shall fast, and do penance; for to my shame, I was deaf to his native song, and knew not his worth until the silence fell. And you, young monks, do not inquire whether the lock he bore was a token of earthly affection or a saintly relic. Remember the heart he wore beneath it. Fix your eyes upon his life, and follow it if you may, for he was a holy man.”

Thus, after life's fitful fever, the rhythms of these true lovers were finally aligned in peace. The grave, infinitely kinder and more organic than the rigid structures of the Church, united them forever. 

And now, a narrator from an age of industrial clamor and superficial consumption—touched by their fate—has labored to unearth their memory from the silencing dust of history. I ask for your sympathy, but not your pity. Put this story to wholesome use. Fiction, with its manufactured pacing and sterile tropes, often gives false, bloodless views of life and death. Here, anchored by the cyclical truths of history, she gives you the raw, sensory reality. Let the institutional barriers that kept these lovers apart prepare you for the inevitable dissonance of this world. For if you cannot bear to hear that these died young—who, had they lived a century, would still eventually return to the soil—how shall you bear to see the gentle, the loving, and the true fade from your own hearths? 

The Master of life and death is a careful steward of His garden; He routinely transplants earth's sweetest, most fragrant herbs to bloom in higher pastures. I ask your sympathy for their rare constancy, their profound appreciation for the sustenance they found in one another, and their cruel separation by a vile, institutional mandate of celibacy—a sterile decree that starves the human palate, enforces a tragic language death upon the heart, and severs humanity from the true, organic symphony of creation. But offer no pity for their early, beautiful return to the earth. *Beati sunt qui in Domino moriuntur.*
