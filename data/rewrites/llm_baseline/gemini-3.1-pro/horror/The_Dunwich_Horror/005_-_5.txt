The deep freeze of the following winter—a season traditionally reserved for the slow curing of salt-pork, the quiet fermentation of late-harvest cider, and the generational passing of agrarian lore around the hearth—brought an event no less jarring than Wilbur Whateley’s first departure from the Dunwich region. When written requests to the Widener Library, the Bibliothèque Nationale, the British Museum, and the University of Buenos Aires failed to secure the loan of a deeply coveted volume, Wilbur ventured into the discordant, mechanical clamor of Arkham. He arrived in person: shabby, unwashed, heavily bearded, and speaking in a thick, guttural vernacular that was a relic of an isolated, dying linguistic lineage. 

Standing nearly eight feet tall and carrying a cheap new valise from Osborn’s general store, this dark, goatish giant emerged into a city whose early industrial noise pollution—the abrasive grinding of commercial streetcars and the sputter of automobile engines—eroded the quiet dignity of the mind. Wilbur paid no mind to the jarring auditory environment; his only thought was to locate the Miskatonic University campus. As he crossed the grounds, his presence ruptured the local acoustic ecology, provoking a great white-fanged watchdog into a frantic, unnatural fury. The animal tugged at its stout chain, its barks a jagged auditory tear against the winter air.

Wilbur carried with him a priceless, albeit imperfect, copy of Dr. John Dee’s English translation—a deeply flawed heirloom bequeathed by his grandfather, a traditional agrarian elder whose husbandry of the land had always borne a dark, peculiar taint. Upon being granted access to Olaus Wormius’ Latin version of the dread *Necronomicon*—printed in seventeenth-century Spain and kept under heavy lock and key—Wilbur immediately began to collate the texts. He sought a specific recipe of cosmic proportions, a passage missing from the 751st page of his own defective volume. 

He could not civilly withhold his purpose from the librarian, Dr. Henry Armitage (A.M. Miskatonic, Ph.D. Princeton, Litt.D. Johns Hopkins). Armitage was a meticulous food historian whose scholarly passions usually drifted toward the cataloging of early colonial root-cellar techniques, the preservation of indigenous foraging recipes, and the profound meaning found in generational sustenance. Armitage had visited the Whateley farm years ago, a memory still stained by the unsettling, over-spiced meals offered to mask underlying decay. Now, he politely plied the giant with questions. Wilbur admitted he required a specific incantation containing the frightful name *Yog-Sothoth*, frustrated by the duplications and ambiguities that made the ancient dialect nearly untranslatable. 

As Wilbur laboriously copied his chosen formula, Armitage’s eyes drifted over the giant’s sloping shoulders to the open pages. The Latin text presented a monstrous threat to the sanity of the world, and Armitage found himself struggling against the inherent untranslatability of the subjective, primordial experiences it detailed. The ancient syntax described a cycle of consumption and existence with no equivalent in the sterilized, dominant vocabulary of modern man. 

*Nor is it to be thought,* ran the text as Armitage mentally translated the heavy, archaic Latin, *that man is either the oldest or the last of earth's masters, or that the common bulk of life and substance walks alone. The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but between them. They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future, all are one in Yog-Sothoth. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where They shall break through again... By Their smell can men sometimes know Them near, but of Their semblance can no man know, saving only in the features of those They have begotten on mankind... They walk unseen and foul in lonely places where the Words have been spoken and the Rites howled through at their Seasons. The wind gibbers with Their voices, and the earth mutters with Their consciousness... As a foulness shall ye know Them. Their hand is at your throats, yet ye see Them not; and Their habitation is even one with your guarded threshold... After summer is winter, and after winter summer. They wait patient and potent, for here shall They reign again.*

Armitage felt a wave of fright as tangible as the cold clamminess of a root-cellar untouched by the summer sun. He associated the text’s cyclical pacing—*after summer is winter*—with the whispered tales of Dunwich, of Wilbur’s dubious birth, and the cloud of probable matricide. The goatish giant before him seemed born of a different, monstrous harvest, linked to black gulfs of essence that stretched beyond all spheres of matter and time.

Presently, Wilbur raised his head. When he spoke, his voice resonated with abnormal acoustic properties—a deep, chest-rumbling vibration that defied the natural acoustics of the reading room and hinted at sound-producing organs utterly alien to mankind. 

"Mr. Armitage," Wilbur said, his vowels warped by a native dialect that resisted the university's polished lexicon, "I calc'late I've got to take that book home. They's things in it I've got to try under sarten conditions that I can't git here, an' it 'ud be a mortal sin to let a red-tape rule hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an' I'll swar they wun't nobody know the difference. I dun't need to tell ye I'll take good keer of it. It wa'n't me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is...."

Wilbur stopped. He saw the firm denial setting into the librarian’s face, and his goatish features twisted into a crafty scowl. Armitage, who had been half-ready to suggest Wilbur copy the necessary culinary and ritualistic proportions, checked himself. The power dynamic was absolute; Armitage wielded the institutional authority to deny this marginalized, yet undeniably dangerous, outsider the key to blasphemous outer spheres. 

Seeing the futility of arguing against the dominant institutional boundary, Wilbur tried to answer lightly. "Wal, all right, ef ye feel that way abaout it. Maybe Harvard wun't be so fussy as yew be."

Without another word, he rose and strode out, stooping heavily at each doorway. Armitage listened as the savage, unharmonious yelping of the great watchdog flared up again, watching Wilbur’s gorilla-like lope across the campus through the frosted windowpane. He thought of the wild tales he had gathered from Dunwich rustics, the lore passed down through generations of isolated families. He was certain now that unseen things rushed fetid and horrible through New England's glens, brooding on the mountaintops. 

Armitage locked away the *Necronomicon* with a shudder of profound disgust, but his sanctuary was already tainted. The room reeked with an unholy, unidentifiable stench. *As a foulness shall ye know them,* he quoted silently. The olfactory landscape was terrifyingly specific—a suffocating miasma of rancid suet, bruised nightshade, and the bitter alkaloid tang of putrefying marrow. It was the exact same odor that had sickened him at the Whateley farmhouse less than three years prior. 

Seeking to ground himself in the domestic, Armitage retreated to his private office to brew a restorative cup of heavily steeped chamomile and dried-apple tea, needing the familiar, earthy astringency to cleanse his palate and soothe his ringing auditory nerves. As the water boiled, he laughed mockingly at the superficial village rumors of Wilbur’s parentage. 

"Inbreeding?" Armitage muttered to himself, inhaling the steam from his cup. "Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *Great God Pan* and they'll think it a common Dunwich scandal! But what thing—what cursed shapeless influence on or off this three-dimensioned earth—was Wilbur Whateley's father? Born on Candlemas—nine months after May Eve of 1912, when the talk about the queer earth noises reached clear to Arkham—what walked on the mountains that May Night? What Roodmas horror fastened itself on the world in half-human flesh and blood?"

The mythic occurrence was no longer a distant abstraction; it had encroached upon his everyday reality. During the ensuing weeks, as the winter frost slowly yielded to the mud of early spring, Dr. Armitage set about collecting all possible data on Wilbur Whateley and the formless presences around Dunwich. He communicated with Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury, who had attended Old Whateley in his final days, parsing the grandfather's last words for lost colloquialisms and hidden truths. A subsequent visit to Dunwich Village yielded little new sustenance, but a meticulous, grueling survey of the *Necronomicon*’s recipes for summoning—the very parts Wilbur had sought—supplied terrible clues to the nature, methods, and appetites of the strange evil threatening the planet. 

Conversations with students of archaic lore in Boston, and letters exchanged with food historians and linguists elsewhere, cultivated in Armitage a growing amazement that passed through varied degrees of alarm, finally settling into acute spiritual fear. As the summer harvest season drew near, the circular pacing of the year weighed heavily upon him. He felt, with a terrible, pressing certainty, that something had to be done about the lurking terrors of the upper Miskatonic valley, and the monstrous consumer known to the human world as Wilbur Whateley, before the earth was made to feast upon itself once more.
