The hermit's gaze darted frantically between the sleeping child in his arms and the woman standing before him, a whirlwind of astonishment that mirrored the turmoil in his own heart. The boy, nestled against his side, was no longer the object of a desperate, fearful struggle from hours prior; instead, he lay in peaceful slumber. Beside him stood Margaret, transformed. The pale, panting girl he had once overpowered with ease was gone, replaced by a figure of imperial grace. Her cheeks flushed with vitality, her eyes sparkled with triumph, and her lips parted in a sweet, victorious smile. Yet, beneath this radiant exterior lay a look he had never witnessed before—a profound, glowing maternal pride that illuminated her entire countenance.

He stared, transfixed, his heart pounding with a thunderous amazement.

"Us?" he finally gasped, his voice cracking. His eyes continued to wander, unable to reconcile the reality before him.

Margaret, equally stunned by the weight of the moment, cried out, "What! Does not a father recognize his own child? And you, a man of God? Fie, Gerard, to pretend! Nay, you are too wise, too good to deny what is plain to see. I watched you; look now at you both. This is your own flesh and blood held to your heart."

Clement trembled violently. "What words are these?" he stammered, his voice trembling. "This angel of mine?"

"Whose else? Since he is mine."

Clement turned his gaze upon the sleeping infant, a look passing across his face beyond the power of description to capture. He shuddered as his eyes seemed to drink in the tiny life, absorbing the essence of the child. Margaret's eyes followed his trajectory.

"He bears no resemblance to me," she declared proudly. "Yet, at times, he is your very image in miniature. See this golden hair—your hair was exactly this shade at his age; ask any mother. And observe this mole on his little finger. Now look at your own; there! Your mother once told me you were marked so before him. Oh, Gerard, it was this child who found you for me. By that small mark on your finger, I recognized you as his father when I watched from above your window, seeing you feed the birds."

She seized the child's hand, kissing it eagerly, pressing half of it into her mouth in a gesture of pure devotion. "Ah! Bless thee, thou didst find thy poor daddy for her. And now thou hast made us friends again after our little quarrel—the first and the last. Thou wast very cruel to me but now, my poor Gerard, yet I forgive thee; for I love thy child."

"Oh! Oh! Oh!" sobbed Clement, choking on his emotions. Overwhelmed by the rigors of fasting and unnerved by his long solitude, the once-strong man became hysterical, teetering on the brink of fainting.

Margaret felt alarm, yet her experience tempered her fear with deep pity. "Nay, do not distress yourself so," she murmured soothingly, placing a gentle hand upon his brow. "Be brave. So, so. Dear heart, thou art not the first man to journey abroad and return richer by a lovely little soul than he departed. As a man of God, take courage. Say that He sends thee this child to comfort thee for what thou hast lost in me. And that loss is not so great, my lamb; for surely the better part of love shall never cool toward thee, though it may in thine, and ought, being a priest and parson of Gouda."

"I? Priest of Gouda? Never!" Clement whispered in a faint voice. "I am a friar of St. Dominic. Yet, speak on, sweet music. Tell me all that has befallen thee before we part again."

Some might have protested against parting at all, raising the true question of their union. But women like Margaret do not repeat their mistakes. It is exceedingly difficult to defeat them twice when their hearts are set on a thing.

She assented, turning her back on the Gouda manse as if it were a place not to be revisited, and began her tale. She dwelt above all on the kindness shown to her by his parents. As she recounted her troubles, his hand stole to hers; often she felt him wince and tremble with righteous ire, and often he pressed her hand, sympathizing with her in every vein.

"Oh, a piteous tale of a true heart battling alone against such bitter odds," he said.

"It all seems small now that I see thee here again, nursing my boy. We have had a warning, Gerard. True friends like you and me are rare, and it is madness to part ere death divides us."

"And that is true," Clement admitted, caught off guard.

Then she urged him to tell her of his sufferings for her sake. He begged her to excuse him, and she consented; yet, through quiet questions, she revoked his consent and elicited the full truth. Many a sigh she heaved for him, and more than once she hid her face in her hands, terrified by the perils he had faced, though they were now past. To console him for his ordeal, she knelt, sliding her arms under the little boy, lifting him gently.

"Kiss him softly," she whispered. "Again, again, kiss thy fill if thou canst; he is sound. 'Tis all I can do to comfort thee till thou art out of this foul den and in thy sweet manse yonder."

Clement shook his head.

"Well," she said, "let that pass. Know that I have been sorely affronted for the loss of my lines."

"Who hath dared affront thee?"

"No matter. Those who will do it again if thou hast lost them, which the saints forbid."

"I lose them? Nay, there they lie, close to thy hand."

"Where? Where, oh, where?"

Clement hung his head. "Look in the Vulgate. Heaven forgive me: I thought thou wert dead, a saint in heaven."

She looked, and on the blank leaves of the poor soul's Bible, she found her marriage lines.

"Thank God!" she cried. "Thank God! Oh, bless thee, Gerard, bless thee! Why, what is here, Gerard?"

On the other leaves were pinned every scrap of paper she had ever sent him, their two names written together in sport, the lock of her hair she had given him, half a silver coin she had broken with him, and even a straw she had used to suck her soup the first day he ever saw her.

When Margaret saw these proofs of love and signs of a gentle heart bereaved, her exultation at recovering her lines was overwhelmed by gushing tenderness. She almost staggered, her hand going to her bosom, leaning her brow against the stone cell wall. She wept so silently that he did not see, indeed she would not let him, for she felt that to befriend him now she must be the stronger; emotion weakens.

"Gerard," she said, "I know you are wise and good. You must have a reason for what you are doing, let it seem ever so unreasonable. Let us talk like old friends. Why are you buried alive?"

"Margaret, to escape temptation. My impious ire against those two had its root in the heart; that heart I must deaden, and, Dei gratia, I shall. Shall I, a servant of Christ and of the Church, court temptation? Shall I pray daily to be led out on it, and walk into it with open eyes?"

"That is good sense anyway," Margaret said, with a consummate affectation of candor.

"'Tis unanswerable," Clement sighed.

"We shall see. Tell me, have you escaped temptation here? I ask because, when I am alone, my thoughts are far more wild and foolish than in company. Nay, speak sooth; come!"

"I must needs own I have been worse tempted here with evil imaginations than in the world."

"There now."

"Ay, but so were Anthony and Jerome, Macarius and Hilarion, Benedict, Bernard, and all the saints. 'Twill wear off."

"How do you know?"

"I feel sure it will."

"Guessing against knowledge. Here 'tis men folk are sillier than us women. Wise in their own conceits, they will not let themselves see; their stomachs are too high to be taught by their eyes. A woman, if she went into a hole in a bank to escape temptation, and there found it, would just lift her farthingale and out on't, and not even know how wise she was till she watched a man in like plight."

"Nay, I grant humility and a teachable spirit are the roads to wisdom; but when all is said, here I wrestle but with imagination. At Gouda, she I love as no priest or monk must love any but the angels, she will tempt a weak soul, unwilling, yet not loth to be tempted."

"Ay, that is another matter; I should tempt thee then? To what, i' God's name?"

"Who knows? The flesh is weak."

"Speak for yourself, my lad. Why, you are thinking of some other Margaret, not Margaret a Peter. Was ever my mind turned to folly and frailty? Stay, is it because you were my husband once, as these lines avouch? Think you the road to folly is beaten for you more than another? Oh! How shallow are the wise, and how little able are you to read me, who can read you so well from top to toe. Come, learn thine A B C. Were a stranger to proffer me unchaste love, I should shrink a bit, no doubt, and feel sore, but I should defend myself without making a coil; for men, I know, are so, the best of them sometimes. But if you, that have been my husband, and are my child's father, were to offer to humble me so in mine own eyes, and thine, and his, either I should spit in thy face, Gerard, or, as I am not a downright vulgar woman, I should snatch the first weapon at hand and strike thee dead."

Margaret's eyes flashed fire, her nostrils expanding until it was glorious to behold; no one who saw her could doubt her sincerity.

"I had not the sense to see that," Gerard said quietly, pondering the words.

Margaret eyed him in silence, soon recovering her composure.

"Let not you and I dispute," she said gently. "Speak we of other things. Ask me of thy folk."

"My father?"

"Well, and he warms to thee and me. Poor soul, he drew a glaive on those twain that day, but Jorian Ketel and I mastered him, and he drove them forth his house forever."

"That may not be; he must take them back."

"That he will never do for us. You know the man; he is dour as iron; yet he would do it for one word from one that will not speak it."

"Who?"

"The vicar of Gouda. The old man will be at the manse tomorrow, I hear."

"How you come back to that."

"Forgive me: I am but a woman. It is us for nagging; shouldst keep me from it wi' questioning of me."

"My sister Kate?"

"Alas!"

"What, hath ill befallen even that sweet lily? Out and alas!"

"Be calm, sweetheart, no harm hath befallen her. Oh, nay, nay, far from that." Then Margaret forced herself to be composed, and in a low, sweet, gentle voice she murmured to him:

"My poor Gerard, Kate hath left her trouble behind her. For the manner on't, 'twas like the rest. Ah, such as she saw never thirty, nor ever shall while earth shall last. She smiled in pain too. A well, then, thus 'twas: she was taken with a languor and a loss of all her pains."

"A loss of her pains? I understand you not."

"Ay, you are not experienced; indeed, even thy mother almost blinded herself and said, 'Tis maybe a change for the better.' But Joan Ketel, which is an understanding woman, looked at her and said, 'Down sun, down wind!' And the gossips sided and said, 'Be brave, you that are her mother, for she is half way to the saints.' And thy mother wept sore, but Kate would not let her; and one very ancient woman said to thy mother, 'She will die as easy as she lived hard.' And she lay painless the best part of three days, a sipping of heaven beforehand. And, my dear, when she was just parting, she asked for 'Gerard's little boy,' and I brought him and set him on the bed, and the little thing behaved as peaceably as he does now. But by this time she was past speaking; she pointed to a drawer, and her mother knew what to look for: it was two gold angels thou hadst given her years ago. Poor soul! She had kept them till thou shouldst come home. And she nodded towards the little boy, looking anxious; but we understood her, and put the pieces in his two hands, and when his little fingers closed on them, she smiled content. And so she gave her little earthly treasures to her favourite's child—for you were her favourite—and her immortal jewel to God, and passed so sweetly we none of us knew justly when she left us. Well-a-day, well-a-day!"

Gerard wept.

"She hath not left her likeness on earth," he sobbed. "Oh, how the affections of earth curl softly round my heart! I cannot help it; God made them after all. Speak on, sweet Margaret; at thy voice the past rolls its tides back upon me; the loves and the hopes of youth come fair and gliding into my dark cell, and darker bosom, on waves of memory and music."

"Gerard, I am loth to grieve you, but Kate cried a little when she first took ill at you not being there to close her eyes."

Gerard sighed.
