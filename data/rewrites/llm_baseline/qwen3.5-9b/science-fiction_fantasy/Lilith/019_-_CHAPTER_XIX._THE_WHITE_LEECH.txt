I awoke from a deep slumber to find one hand throbbing with intense pain. The back of it was grotesquely swollen, marked in the center by a triangular wound resembling the bite of a leech. By evening, however, the swelling receded, and the injury had almost vanished entirely. I scoured the cave, flipping over every substantial stone, searching for anything capable of inflicting such harm, but found nothing.

Days dragged by, yet the figure remained motionless, eyes sealed tight. It could not be dead; there was no scent of decay, and the air surrounding it remained pristine. More subtly, I noticed the sharp angles of the bones beginning to soften, the form rounding out, the skin losing its parchment-like translucency. If this transformation was real, life must be returning! The tide that had receded so far toward infinity must be flowing back. Oh, the joy if those rising ripples of life's ocean were indeed reclaiming the lovely shape beneath the bones it had nearly abandoned!

Twenty times a day I scanned for signs of progress; twenty times a day doubt crept in, sometimes morphing into despair. Yet, whenever I recalled the mental image of finding her, hope surged anew. Weeks passed in this oscillation until one night, unable to sleep, I rose to seek the cooler air of the outdoors. Though the cave air was refreshed by the stream, the internal heat often felt oppressive. Outside, the moon hung full, casting a shadowy clarity within the cavern. Naturally, I cast a lingering glance at my treasure before departing.

"Bliss eternal!" I cried aloud. "Do I see her eyes?"

Great orbs, dark as if cut from the sphere of a starless night, seemed to glow with an excess of darkness against the glistening whiteness of her face. I crept closer, my heart pounding so violently I feared the sound might startle her. I leaned down. Alas, her eyelids were firmly shut. Hope and imagination had conspired to create a mutual illusion; my heart's desire would remain unfulfilled.

Turning away, I collapsed onto the cave floor and wept. Then, a thought struck me: her eyes had been slightly open before, and now the terrible slit through which nothingness had peered was gone. Perhaps she had opened them for a fleeting moment and fallen back asleep—perhaps she was awake, holding them closed. In either scenario, life, however diminished, must have caused them to shut. Comforted by this logic, I fell into a heavy sleep.

That night, I was bitten again. I awoke with a burning thirst. Morning brought another thorough search, yet again in vain. The wound was identical to the first, healing completely by evening. I concluded that some large leech-like creature visited the hot stream periodically.

"But if blood is its objective," I reasoned, "so long as I guard her, I need fear little for my treasure!"

That same morning, after peeling a grape as usual and removing the seeds, I placed it in her mouth. Her lips made a slight movement of acceptance. *She lives!* My hope strengthened instantly, and thoughts of clothing for her arose immediately. She must be able to rise the moment she wished! Venturing into the forest, I investigated available materials. Hardly had I begun searching when fibrous skeletons, reminiscent of the prickly pear leaf, suggested themselves as ideal. Gathering a stockpile, I dried them in the sun, separated the reticulated layers, and quickly fashioned two loose garments—one to hang from her waist, the other from her shoulders. Using the stiletto-point of an aloe leaf and various filaments, I stitched three thicknesses of the tissue together.

For the following week, there were no further signs except that she consumed grapes more noticeably. All indicators grew clearer: she was becoming plumper, her skin fairer. Still, she refused to open her eyes. A horrific fear would occasionally seize me—that her growth was some hideous fungal proliferation, the few grapes insufficient to explain it. Once more, I was bitten. Now, whatever entity it was, began visiting regularly at three-day intervals. It typically targeted my neck or arm, delivering exactly one bite, always during my sleep, and never during the day, even when I slept. Hours I lay awake on watch, hearing nothing, seeing no sign of its approach. I likely never felt the actual bite.

Eventually, so hopeless did I become in catching it that I ceased searching by day or waiting by night. I knew from my increasing weakness that I was bleeding dangerously, yet I cared little. Before my eyes, death yielded to life; a soul was gathering strength to rescue me from loneliness. We would depart together, and I would soon recover.

Finally, the garments were complete. Contemplating my handiwork with satisfaction, I proceeded to weave layers of fiber into sandals. One night, I woke suddenly, breathless and faint, craving air. Rising to crawl from the cave, a slight rustle in the leaves of the couch froze me in place.

"I caught the vile thing," a feeble voice whispered in my mother tongue. "I caught it in the very act!"

*She was alive! She spoke!* I dared not yield to my overwhelming transport lest I terrify her.

"What creature?" I breathed, barely forming the words.

"The creature," she answered, "that was biting you."

"What was it?"

"A great white leech."

"How big?" I pressed, forcing calm.

"Not far from six feet long, I should think."

"You may have saved my life, perhaps... but how could you touch such a horror! How brave of you!" I cried.

"I did!" was her sole reply, though I sensed a shudder.

"Where is it? What did you do with such a monster?"

"I threw it in the river."

"Then it will return, I fear!"

"I do not think I could have killed it, even had I known how. I heard you moaning, rose to check the disturbance, and saw the frightful thing at your neck. I pulled it away. But I could not hold it; I was hardly able to throw it from me. I only heard it splash in the water!"

"We'll kill it next time!" I declared, then turned faint, seeking the open air before collapsing.

When consciousness returned, the sun was high. The lady stood a short distance away. Even in the clumsy attire I had crafted, she appeared both grand and graceful. I *had* seen those glorious eyes! They had shone throughout the night. Dark as primordial darkness, they now outshone the daylight. She stood erect as a column, regarding me. Her pale cheek showed no emotion, only inquiry.

I rose. "We must be going!" I said. "The white leech..." I trailed off. A strange smile flickered across her beautiful face.

"Did you find me there?" she asked, pointing to the cave.

"No; I brought you here," I replied.

"You brought me?"

"Yes."

"From where?"

"From the forest."

"What have you done with my clothes—and my jewels?"

"You had none when I found you."

"Then why did you not leave me?"

"Because I hoped you were not dead."

"Why should you have cared?"

"Because I was very lonely, and wanted you to live."

"You would have kept me enchanted merely for my beauty!" she said, her tone laced with proud scorn.

Her words and gaze ignited my indignation. "There was no beauty left in you," I countered.

"Then, again, why did you not let me alone?"

"Because you were of my own kind."

"Of *your* kind?" she shrieked, her voice dripping with utter contempt. "I thought so, but I find I was mistaken!"

"Doubtless you pitied me!"

"Never has a woman had more claim on pity, or less on any other feeling!"

With an expression of pain, mortification, and anger beyond description, she turned from me and stood silent. Starless night lay profound in the gulfs of her eyes; hatred for the one who restored it had extinguished their splendor. The light of life had departed.

"Had you failed to rouse me, what would you have done?" she asked suddenly, unmoving.

"I would have buried it."

"It? What? You would have buried *this*?" she exploded, flashing around to face me in a white fury, arms thrown wide, eyes darting forks of cold lightning.

"Nay; that I saw not! These weary weeks of watching and tending have brought you back," I answered—for with such a woman, I must be blunt. "Had I seen the slightest sign of decay, I would have interred you immediately."

"Dog of a fool!" she screamed. "I was merely in a trance—Samoil, what a fate! Go and fetch the savage woman from whom you borrowed this hideous disguise."

"I made it for you. It is hideous, but I did my best."

She straightened to her towering height. "How long have I been insensible?" she demanded. "A woman could not make that dress in a single day!"

"Not in twenty days," I rejoined, "hardly in thirty!"

"Ha! How long do you pretend I have lain unconscious? Answer me at once."
