The lawn was a flat, bright expanse stretching without interruption to the equally bright sea. It was bordered by a rim of scarlet geranium and coleus, punctuated by cast-iron vases painted in chocolate, standing at intervals along the winding path. These vessels held garlands of petunia and ivy geranium, draped over the neatly raked gravel. Halfway between the cliff's edge and the square wooden house—which shared the chocolate hue but featured a verandah roof striped in yellow and brown to mimic an awning—two large targets stood against a backdrop of shrubbery. Opposite them, a real tent was pitched, surrounded by benches and garden seats. Ladies in summer dresses and gentlemen in grey frock-coats and tall hats populated the lawn or occupied the benches. Periodically, a slender girl in starched muslin emerged from the tent, bow in hand, speeding her shaft toward a target while the spectators paused their conversation to witness the result.

Newland Archer stood on the verandah of the house, looking down at this scene with a mixture of curiosity and distance. Flanking the shiny painted steps were large blue china flower-pots resting on bright yellow stands, each filled with spiky green plants. Below the verandah ran a wide border of blue hydrangeas edged with more red geraniums. Behind him, the French windows of the drawing-rooms offered glimpses, filtered through swaying lace curtains, of glassy parquet floors islanded with chintz poufs, dwarf armchairs, and velvet tables cluttered with silver trifles.

The Newport Archery Club maintained its August meeting at the Beauforts'. This sport, which had previously faced only croquet as a rival, was increasingly being discarded in favor of lawn-tennis. Yet, lawn-tennis was still deemed too rough and inelegant for social display; consequently, the bow and arrow retained their utility as a stage for showcasing pretty dresses and graceful attitudes.

Archer watched the familiar spectacle with a sense of wonder that bordered on unease. It startled him that life continued in the old manner when his own internal reactions had shifted so profoundly. Newport was the catalyst that had first revealed the magnitude of this change to him. During the preceding winter in New York, after settling into the new greenish-yellow house with its bow-window and Pompeian vestibule, he had retreated with relief into the established routines of the office. The repetition of daily professional activity served as a tether to his former self. There had been the pleasurable excitement of selecting a showy grey stepper for May's brougham—a gift from the Wellands—and the consuming interest of arranging his new library. Despite familial doubts and disapproval, the project proceeded as he had envisioned it: dark embossed paper, Eastlake bookcases, "sincere" armchairs, and tables. At the *Century*, he had reunited with Winsett; at the *Knickerbocker*, he had encountered the fashionable young men of his circle. With hours dedicated to law, interspersed with dining out, entertaining friends, and occasional evenings at the opera or theater, the life he inhabited had seemed a fairly real and inevitable enterprise.

Newport, however, represented an escape from duty into an atmosphere of unmitigated holiday-making. Archer had attempted to persuade May to spend the summer on a remote island off the coast of Maine, Mount Desert, where a few hardy Bostonians and Philadelphians camped in "native" cottages. Reports spoke of enchanting scenery and a wild, almost trapper-like existence amidst woods and waters.

Yet the Wellands invariably chose Newport, where they owned one of the square boxes on the cliffs. Their son-in-law could offer no compelling reason why he and May should not accompany them. As Mrs. Welland remarked with a certain sharpness, it was hardly worthwhile for May to exhaust herself trying on summer clothes in Paris if she were not permitted to wear them. This argument was one to which Archer had found no satisfactory response.

May herself could not comprehend his obscure reluctance to embrace such a reasonable and pleasant arrangement. She reminded him that he had always enjoyed Newport during his bachelor years. Since this was indisputable, he could only profess certainty that he would enjoy it better now that they were together. But as he stood on the Beaufort verandah gazing at the brightly peopled lawn, a shiver ran through him: he knew he would not enjoy it at all.

It was not May's fault, poor dear. Should they occasionally fall slightly out of step during their travels, harmony was restored by their return to the conditions she was accustomed to. He had always anticipated that she would not disappoint him; he had been correct. He had married, as most young men do, because he had met a perfectly charming girl at the moment when a series of rather aimless sentimental adventures ended in premature disgust. She represented peace, stability, comradeship, and the steadying sense of an unescapable duty.

He could not claim to have been mistaken in his choice, for she had fulfilled every expectation. It was undeniably gratifying to be the husband of one of the handsomest and most popular young married women in New York, particularly since she was also one of the sweetest-tempered and most reasonable of wives. Archer had never been insensible to such advantages. Regarding the momentary madness that had seized him on the eve of his marriage, he had trained himself to view it as the last of his discarded experiments. The notion that he could, in his senses, have dreamed of marrying the Countess Olenska had become almost unthinkable. She remained in his memory simply as the most plaintive and poignant of a line of ghosts.

However, these abstractions and eliminations left his mind a rather empty and echoing place. He suspected this was one of the reasons why the busy, animated people on the Beaufort lawn shocked him as if they were children playing in a graveyard.

He heard a murmur of skirts beside him, and the Marchioness Manson fluttered out of the drawing-room window. As usual, she was extraordinarily festooned and bedizened, wearing a limp Leghorn hat anchored to her head by numerous windings of faded gauze, and balancing a little black velvet parasol on a carved ivory handle absurdly over her much larger hatbrim.

"My dear Newland, I had no idea that you and May had arrived!" she exclaimed. "You yourself came only yesterday, you say? Ah, business—business—professional duties... I understand. Many husbands, I know, find it impossible to join their wives here except for the weekend." She cocked her head to one side and languished at him through screwed-up eyes. "But marriage is one long sacrifice, as I used often to remind my Ellen..."

Archer's heart stopped with the strange jerk it had given once before, a sensation that seemed suddenly to slam a door between himself and the outer world. Yet this break in continuity must have been fleeting, for he soon heard Medora answering a question he had apparently voiced.

"No, I am not staying here, but with the Blenkers, in their delicious solitude at Portsmouth," Medora replied. "Beaufort was kind enough to send his famous trotters for me this morning, so that I might have at least a glimpse of one of Regina's garden-parties; but this evening I go back to rural life. The Blenkers, dear original beings, have hired a primitive old farmhouse at Portsmouth where they gather around them representative people..." She drooped slightly beneath her protective brim and added with a faint blush: "This week Dr. Agathon Carver is holding a series of Inner Thought meetings there. A contrast indeed to this gay scene of worldly pleasure—but then I have always lived on contrasts! To me the only death is monotony. I always say to Ellen: Beware of monotony; it's the mother of all the deadly sins. But my poor child is going through a phase of exaltation, of abhorrence of the world. You know, I suppose, that she has declined all invitations to stay at Newport, even with her grandmother Mingott? I could hardly persuade her to come with me to the Blenkers', if you will believe it! The life she leads is morbid, unnatural. Ah, if she had only listened to me when it was still possible... When the door was still open... But shall we go down and watch this absorbing match? I hear your May is one of the competitors."

Strolling toward them from the tent, Beaufort advanced over the lawn. He was tall and heavy, too tightly buttoned into a London frock-coat, with one of his own orchids in his buttonhole. Archer, having not seen him for two or three months, was struck by the change in his appearance. In the hot summer light, his floridness seemed heavy and bloated, and but for his erect, square-shouldered walk, he would have looked like an over-fed and over-dressed old man.

Rumors circulated freely regarding Beaufort. In the spring, he had embarked on a long cruise to the West Indies in his new steam-yacht. It was reported that at various points where he had touched, a lady resembling Miss Fanny Ring had been seen in his company. The steam-yacht, built in the Clyde and fitted with tiled bathrooms and other unheard-of luxuries, was said to have cost him half a million. The pearl necklace he presented to his wife upon his return was as magnificent as such expiatory offerings tend to be. Beaufort's fortune was substantial enough to withstand the strain; yet disquieting rumors persisted, not only in Fifth Avenue but in Wall Street.
