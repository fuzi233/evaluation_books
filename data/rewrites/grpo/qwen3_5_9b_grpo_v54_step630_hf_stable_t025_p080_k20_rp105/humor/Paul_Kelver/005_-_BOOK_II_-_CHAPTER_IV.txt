The East India Dock Road, now a cacophony of trams, omnibuses, and ceaseless footfalls, was once a silent, echoing corridor. Bounded by the long, hollow wall of the docks and scattered market gardens, it felt less like a street and more like a boundary line between worlds. At its start stood a single brick cottage, the former toll-house, a landmark that marked the precise moment my childhood fell away.

That afternoon, I had visited the family dentist in Plaistow, a picturesque village then, now largely swallowed by the city. My mother, ever practical, had tucked a half-crown into my knickerbocker pocket, instructing me to return it if the dentist refused it. He was a kind, elderly bachelor, a distant relation perhaps, who treated me with the solemnity of a king in his velvet chair. He would unroll his terrifying array of instruments, recommending the most murderous-looking ones with affectionate praise. But before I could express my terror, a pair of forceps would spring from his sleeve, and the ordeal would end in a shared cup of tea.

His house stood in a great garden, and his housekeeper produced jams of legendary quality. We spoke of my mother, who, he claimed, was once a prankish, wilful little girl—a tale she would laugh off, insisting we gossip about something more sensible, yet always asking, "And what else did he say about me?" After tea, he would produce his great microscope, revealing a fairy world of strange dragons and mighty monsters to me. To a child, he was a harmless magician; to me, looking back, he was a tiny man with a heart as large as any giant.

Leaving the gate, I would hand him the coin with formal thanks. But stepping outside, I would invariably find it still in my pocket. Once, I returned it, but he denied knowing anything of it, suggesting half-crowns were slippery things.

On this particular day, returning from Plaistow, I paused on the bridge watching the lazy barges. It was a hushed summer evening, and as I passed through the white toll-gate, I felt a profound sense of leaving myself behind. I looked back, expecting to see the little boy still leaning over the parapet. He was gone. He passed away from me as a man's body passes, leaving only remembrance. I tried to play his games, to dream his dreams, but the substance was missing. I was a thin ghost making believe.

This realization troubled me deeply. I did not want to grow up; the dark road of adulthood frightened me. But slowly, over months and years, a new consciousness took root. Little Paul faded, and I became someone else. Before letting him go entirely, I must recall the figures who remained on my childhood stage.

First was my Aunt Fan, crouching over the kitchen fire, her skirt rolled up to reveal only her petticoat. She swayed rhythmically, stroking her knees, while I, armed with a paper knife or broomstick, played knight, Indian brave, or Fairy Prince. She was the wicked ogre, Laughing Water, the Sleeping Beauty, or the Cannibal Chief. Her compliance was rarely pure self-sacrifice; often she suggested the game, casting herself as the heavy lead—the dragons, the wicked uncles, the fussy necromancers. As an authoress of a cookery book for giant-land, she would have been brilliant. She planned complicated slaughters with gusto, detailing the sharpening of knives and the cleaning of mops.

"I think, my dear," she would direct, "we'll have him stuffed with chestnuts and served on toast. And don't forget the giblets. They make such excellent sauce."

She would transform the most beautiful damsels into Glyptodons or Hippocrepians, and warriors into plaice or prawns. Her death scenes were conscientious efforts, ending with a final gurgle followed by the lowering of her skirt to signal the fall of the curtain, immediately followed by tea preparation.

Another recurring figure was a small, curly-haired girl I met in the dingy streets. She ran ahead, turning to admire my face, captivated by my praise. She protected me against my baser self, nerving me to face sudden foes. Together, we courted adventure; to me, a simple walk, to her, an expedition full of danger. She held my hand in lonely places and possessed a vocabulary that could silence the blind idiots who sought to walk through us.

"Now, then, where yer coming to, old glass-eye? We ain't sperrits. Can't yer see us?" she would shout, her child's treble rising shrilly.

"If they attempted reply, her child's treble... would only rise more shrill."

She was unwise, introducing me to the shop, for children and dogs are snobbish little wretches. Her father was a fishmonger—a "fried fishmonger," as I noted, which placed us outside the pale of respectable society. Despite her father's trade, she waited for me daily in Stainsby Road, her little face pressed against the iron railings until I came out or shook my head from my bedroom window. Tears would trace paths on her dust-besprinkled cheeks, until the advent of Barbara put an end to those meetings.

So began and ended my first romance. One day, I shall walk into the little grocer's shop and boldly ask to see her. I know she is now the mother of a numerous progeny, likely stout and sharp-tongued. Yet under the clumsy-handed world's realities, I shall see the lithesome little maid with fond, admiring eyes. What help they were to me I never knew till I had lost them. How hard to gain such eyes I have learned since.

Among the remaining figures were the servants of our house, the "generals" of the kitchen. They were mostly discontented or disappointed souls, recruited from the neighborhood. They were poor at "making believe" but would squeeze ten minutes from their work to romp with me. Encouraged by their admiring faces, I would "hold forth," regarding myself as among the Immortals.

One girl, Janet, stayed for months. The daughter of the captain of the barge "Nancy Jane," her conversation was a mix of terror and delight. When my mother exclaimed in agony at Janet's choice of words for the gas man, Janet would defend herself naturally. It was Janet and I who undertook her reformation. I told her bluntly that swearing would send her to hell.

"Then where's my father going?" demanded Janet.

"Does he use language?"

"I am afraid, Janet," I explained, "that if he doesn't give it up—"

"But it's the only way he can talk," interrupted Janet. "He don't mean anything by it."

I sighed, yet set my face against weakness. "You see, Janet, people who swear do go there."

But Janet would not believe. "God send my dear, kind father to hell just because he can't talk like the gentlefolks! Don't you believe it of Him, Master Paul. He's got more sense."

These words comforted me when sinking in sloughs of childish despond. More often than I can tell, when compelled to listen to the sententious voice of immeasurable Folly glibly explaining the eternal mysteries, has it comforted me to whisper to myself: "I don't believe it of Him. He's got more sense."

About that period, I needed all the comfort I could get. As we descend the road of life, the journey becomes more important than the end; but to the child, the terminating hills are clearly visible. What lies beyond is constant wonder. I never questioned my parents directly on the subject of death or the afterlife, shrinking as we all do from discussing matters of most moment. They, on their part, offered vague generalities. But foolish voices about me were less reticent. Literature provided serious-minded youth with blunt brutality: if you did wrong, you burnt in a fiery furnace for ever and ever.

I had been burnt once, rather badly, by live coals falling on me during transit on a shovel. I imagined these burning coals, not confined to a mere part of my body, but pressing upon me everywhere, eating into my flesh and veins. This continued for eternity. You suffered for an hour, a day, a thousand years, and were no nearer to the end; ten thousand, a million years, and yet, as at the very first, it was for ever, and for ever still it would always be for ever!

"Then be good," replied the foolish voices round me; "never do wrong, and so avoid this endless agony."

But it was so easy to do wrong. There were so many wrong things to do, and the doing of them was so natural.

"Then repent," said the voices, always ready.

But how did one repent? What was repentance? Did I "hate my sin," as I was instructed I must, or merely hate the idea of going to hell for it? Because the latter, even my child's sense told me, was no true repentance. Yet how could one know the difference?

Above all else haunted me the fear of the "Unforgivable Sin." What this was I was never able to discover. I dreaded to enquire too closely, lest I should find I had committed it. Day and night the terror of it clung to me.

"Believe," said the voices; "so only shall you be saved." How believe? How know you did believe? Hours would I kneel in the dark, repeating in a whispered scream: "I believe, I believe. Oh, I do believe!" and then rise with white knuckles, wondering if I really did believe.

Another question rose to trouble me. In the course of my meanderings, I had made the acquaintance of an old sailor, one of the most disreputable specimens possible to find; and had learned to love him. Our first meeting had been outside a confectioner's window in the Commercial Road, where he had discovered me standing, my nose against the glass, a mere palpitating Appetite on legs. He had seized me by the collar, hauled me into the shop, dropped me upon a stool, and bade me eat. Pride of race prompted me politely to decline, but his language became so awful that in fear and trembling I obeyed. So soon as I was finished—it cost him two and fourpence, I remember—we walked down to the docks together, and he told me stories of the sea and land that made my blood run cold.

Altogether, in the course of three weeks or a month, we met about half a dozen times, when much the same programme was gone through. I think I was a fairly frank child, but I said nothing about him at home, feeling instinctively that if I did there would be an end of our comradeship, which was dear to me: not merely by reason of the pastry, though I admit that was a consideration, but also for his wondrous tales. I believed them all implicitly, and so came to regard him as one of the most interesting criminals as yet unhanged: and what was sad about the case, as I felt myself, was that his recital of his many iniquities, instead of repelling, attracted me to him. If ever there existed a sinner, here was one. He chewed tobacco—one of the hundred or so deadly sins, according to my theological library—and was generally more or less drunk. Not that a stranger would have noticed this; the only difference being that when sober he appeared constrained—was less his natural, genial self. In a burst of confidence he once admitted to me that he was the biggest blackguard in the merchant service. Unacquainted with the merchant service, as at the time I was, I saw no reason to doubt him.

One night in a state of intoxication he walked over a gangway and was drowned. Our mutual friend, the confectioner, seeing me pass the window, came out to tell me so; and having heard, I walked on, heavy of heart, and pondering.

About his eternal destination there could be no question. The known facts precluded the least ray of hope. How could I be happy in heaven, supposing I eventually did succeed in slipping in, knowing that he, the lovable old scamp, was burning for ever in hell?

How could Janet, taking it that she reformed and thus escaped damnation, be contented, knowing the father she loved doomed to torment? The heavenly hosts, so I argued, could be composed only of the callous and indifferent.

I wondered how people could go about their business, eat, drink and be merry, with tremendous fate hanging thus ever suspended over their heads. When for a little space I myself forgot it, always it fell back upon me with increased weight.

Nor was the contemplation of heaven itself particularly attractive to me, for it was a foolish paradise these foolish voices had fashioned out of their folly. You stood about and sang hymns—for ever! I was assured that my fear of finding the programme monotonous was due only to my state of original sin, that when I got there I should discover I liked it. But I would have given much for the hope of avoiding both their heaven and their hell.

Fortunately for my sanity I was not left long to brood unoccupied upon such themes. Our worldly affairs, under the sunshine of old Hasluck's round red face, prospered—for awhile; and one afternoon my father, who had been away from home since breakfast time, calling me into his office where also sat my mother, informed me that the long-talked-of school was become at last a concrete thing.

"The term commences next week," explained my father. "It is not exactly what I had intended, but it will do—for the present. Later, of course, you will go to one of the big public schools; your mother and I have not yet quite decided which."

"You will meet other boys there, good and bad," said my mother, who sat clasping and unclasping her hands. "Be very careful, dear, how you choose your companions."

"You will learn to take your own part," said my father. "School is an epitome of the world. One must assert oneself, or one is sat upon."

I knew not what to reply, the vista thus opened out to me was so unexpected.
