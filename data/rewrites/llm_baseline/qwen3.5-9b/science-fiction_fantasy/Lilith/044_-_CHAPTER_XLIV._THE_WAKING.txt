The fourth night felt like falling asleep, yet when I opened my eyes, I truly woke. Though darkness swirled around me, I knew with absolute certainty that I lay within the House of Death. Every moment since I entered had been a dream; now, finally, I was awake.

"At last!" I whispered to my own heart, and it leaped in pure joy. I turned my gaze, and there stood Lona by my side, waiting. I had not lost her—only the sight of her for a fleeting moment! My grief had been unnecessary. It was dark, yes, but I saw her clearly. *She* was not dark. Her eyes burned with the radiance of the Mother's, and the same luminous light emanated from her entire being. Even her death-dress, usually somber, now glowed tenfold brighter, filled with the light of her resurrected body, white as fresh snow and shimmering. She had fallen asleep as a girl; she awoke as a woman, ripened with the essential loveliness of life itself.

I wrapped her in my arms and knew, beyond any doubt, that I lived.

"I woke first!" she said, her face wreathed in wonder.

"You did, my love, and you woke me," I replied.

"I merely looked at you and waited," she answered softly.

Then, a candle drifted through the shadows toward us. In moments, Adam, Eve, and Mara appeared. They greeted us with a quiet, serene "good morning" and smiles familiar to those who know the rhythm of such awakenings.

"I hope you found the darkness pleasant," the Mother said gently.

"Not entirely," I admitted. "But the waking from it feels heavenly."

"It has barely begun," she rejoined. "You are hardly awake yet!"

"He is at least clothed in Death," Adam observed, his voice steady. "Which is the radiant garment of Life." He embraced Lona, placed an arm around me, glanced inquiringly at the princess, and patted the head of the leopardess resting nearby. "I think we shall see you two again soon," he said, looking between Lona and me.

"Must we die again?" I asked, fear tightening my chest.

"No," he answered, smiling with the same warmth as the Mother. "You have died *into* life, and you will die no more. Your task now is simply to remain dead—to let go. Once you die as we die here, all dying ends. Now you must only live, and you must do so with all your blessed might. The more you live, the stronger you become to live."

"But what if I tire of living so strongly?" I asked. "What if I stop trying?"

"It requires only the will; the strength is already there!" the Mother declared. "Pure life knows no weariness. *The* Life generates ours. Those who refuse to die die repeatedly, constantly, sinking deeper into death forever. Here, everything moves upward, fueled by love and gladness."

She paused, offering a smile and a look that spoke volumes: *We are mother and son; we understand each other. Between us, no farewell is necessary.*

Mara kissed my forehead, her tone bright and cheerful. "I told you, brother, that all would be well! When you need comfort next, just say, 'What will be well, is even now well.'" She sighed slightly, perhaps sensing my skepticism, then added, "You know me now!"

"I know you!" I responded. "You are the voice crying in the wilderness before the Baptist arrived! You are the shepherd who drives the wandering sheep home before the shadows lengthen and the night deepens!"

"My work will end one day," she said. "And then I shall rejoice with the gladness of the Great Shepherd who sent me."

"All night long, the morning draws near," Adam intoned.

"What is that flapping sound?" I asked, startled.

"The Shadow hovers," Adam replied calmly. "There is one here whom he claims as his own. But once ours, she can never belong to him again."

I turned to my father and mother, wanting to kiss them before we left. Their couches were empty, occupied only by the Little Ones who had boldly claimed their hospitality. For an instant, the terrible dream of desolation overwhelmed me, and I looked away.

"What troubles you, my heart?" Lona asked.

"Their empty spaces frighten me," I confessed.

"They have risen and gone long ago," Adam said reassuringly. "They kissed you before leaving and whispered, 'Come soon.' And you couldn't feel or hear them?"

"How could I? You were far away in your dreary old house! You thought the dreadful place had taken you again! Go now and find them. Your parents, my child, must come to find you!"

Our departure hour approached. Lona moved to the couch where the mother who had slain her lay, kissing her tenderly before settling into her father's arms.

"That kiss will draw her home, my Lona!" Adam promised.

"Who were her parents?" Lona asked innocently.

"My father is her father as well," Adam answered.

She turned, placing her hand in mine. I knelt and humbly thanked the three for guiding me through death. Lona knelt beside me, and together they breathed upon us.

"Hark! I hear the sun," Adam said suddenly.

I listened. He described it as a rushing sound—a thousand times ten thousand distant wings beating, accompanied by the roar of a molten, flaming world millions of miles away. His arrival was a crescendo of a hundred harmonies. The three exchanged glances and smiled. That smile floated heavenward like a three-petaled flower, a family offering of morning thanks. It spread from their mouths and faces over their bodies, shining through their garments.

Before I could speak, "Look, they change!" Adam and Eve stood transformed before me—the Angels of Resurrection. Beside them stood Mara, the Magdalene at the sepulcher. Adam's countenance flashed like lightning, while Eve held a napkin that scattered flakes of splendor throughout the room.

A wind began to moan in pulsing gusts.

"You hear his wings now!" Adam said, clarifying he didn't mean the wings of the morning. "It is the Great Shadow stirring to depart. Wretched creature, he carries himself within him and cannot rest!"

"But isn't there something deeper inside him?" I asked.

"Without substance, there is no shadow—not unless there is a light behind that substance!" he explained.

Listening for a moment, he called out with a joyful smile, "Hark to the golden cock! Silent and motionless for millions of years, he has stood upon the clock of the universe. Now, at last, he spreads his wings! Soon he will begin to crow, and at intervals, men will hear him until the dawn of the Eternal Day arrives."

I listened. Far away—as if in the heart of an aeonic silence—I heard the clear, jubilant outcry of a golden throat. It hurled defiance against death and the dark, singing of infinite hope and coming calm. It was the "expectation of the creature" finally finding a voice; the cry of a chaos destined to become a kingdom! Then came a great flapping.

"The black bat is flown!" Mara exclaimed.

"Amen, golden cock, bird of God!" Adam cried, and the words rang through the house of silence, ascending into the airy realms.

At his Amen, like doves rising on silver wings from broken pottery, the Little Ones sprang to their knees on their beds, shouting aloud, "Crow! Crow again, golden cock!" As if they had seen and heard him in their dreams, they turned to look at their sleeping bedfellows. Gazing a moment with loving eyes, they kissed their silent companions of the night and leaped from their cots.

The Little Ones lying beside my father and mother stared blankly and sadly at their empty spots for a moment before sliding slowly to the floor. There, they fell into each other's arms, as if seeing and hearing one another anew confirmed they were truly alive and awake. Suddenly spotting Lona, they ran toward her, radiant with bliss, to embrace her.

Odu, catching sight of the leopardess standing at the princess's feet, bounded over, threw an arm around the great sleeping head, and fondled it.

"Wake up, wake up, darling!" he cried. "It is time to wake!"

The leopardess did not stir.

"She has slept herself cold!" Odu said to Mara, casting an appeal-filled look upward.

"She is waiting for the princess to wake, my child," Mara replied.

Odu looked at the princess and noticed two of his companions still asleep beside her. He flew to them.

"Wake up! Wake up!" he cried, pushing and pulling this one and that. But soon he grew troubled, turning to me with misty eyes.

"They won't wake!" he said. "Why are they so cold?"

"They are also waiting for the princess," I answered.

He reached out, laying his hand on her face. "She is cold too! What is wrong?" he cried, looking around in dismay.

Adam stepped forward. "Her awakening is not yet ripe," he said gently. "She is busy forgetting. When she forgets enough to remember enough, she will soon be ready to wake."

"And remember?" Odu pressed.

"Yes—but not too much at once," Adam cautioned.
