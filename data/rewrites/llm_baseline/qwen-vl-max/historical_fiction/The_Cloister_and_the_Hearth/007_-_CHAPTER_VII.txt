A bright morning broke over Tergou, and with it came a spectacle unlike any other. Velvet cloaks shimmered in the sunlight, feathers fluttered on hats and headdresses, and the clatter of hooves echoed through the narrow streets as the French ambassador made his way toward the nearby forest for a day of hunting. His procession was grand—accompanied not only by his own entourage but also by several servants lent by the Duke of Burgundy, whose presence added both dignity and splendor to the occasion.

At the head of the cavalcade rode the Duke’s tumbler—a man of grave demeanor and solemn bearing who moved with such stately composure that even the noblemen beside him seemed frivolous by comparison. Yet every now and then, when respect threatened to grow too heavy, he would suddenly tumble from his horse with absurd clumsiness, rolling across the ground in a way that provoked laughter even from the ambassador himself. Then, with equal flair, he’d climb back up by grasping the tail of his steed like some clumsy acrobat, completing his act with a grin that never failed to amuse.

Toward the rear of the procession came an even more remarkable sight: the Duke’s leopard. A huntsman, mounted upon a massive Flemish horse of enormous strength and size, carried a long wooden box strapped securely to his body. Upon this box sat a sleek, golden-spotted leopard, crouched low and alert. She was chained to her rider, and though she appeared calm, her eyes held a wildness that kept the crowd at a respectful distance.

The townsfolk gathered at windows and balconies, drawn by curiosity. Some leaned forward, eager to touch her glossy fur or pull gently at her tail. But the huntsman roared a warning so sharp it silenced them all: “Beware! At Antwerp, one man dared throw dust at her—and the Duke turned him into dust.” He paused, letting the tale sink in. “I speak truth. The good Duke threw him into a dungeon beneath the earth. Rats stripped his flesh from bone in a single night. Justice done, for disturbing the poor beast.”

A murmur of fear passed through the crowd. No one dared approach again.

But just as tension settled, another figure emerged—the Duke’s giant, a Hungarian towering seven feet four inches tall. He brought up the rear, walking with slow, deliberate steps, his voice a high, fluty squeak that belied his immense frame. Proud and unaware of his oddity, he noticed Giles perched atop a balcony and stopped abruptly.

“Hallo, brother!” he chirped, stretching out a hand the size of a mutton leg. “I nearly missed you!”

“You’re plain enough to see,” bellowed Giles, his deep voice cutting through the air like thunder.

“Come sit on my shoulder,” squeaked the giant, grinning. “It’ll be fun!”

“If I do,” thundered Giles, “I’ll punch your ears off!”

The giant chuckled, delighted by the defiance. Encouraged by the laughter from the crowd, he pressed further, teasing the dwarf. But no one realized they were laughing not at wit, but at the sheer absurdity of two voices—one tiny and shrill, the other huge and roaring—clashing like mismatched instruments.

Then, suddenly, Giles lost control. In a fury born of pride and unshakable courage, he leapt from the balcony, landed squarely on the giant’s neck, seized his hair with one hand, and began pounding his head with the other.

The giant laughed at first—but the force of the blows soon turned that laughter into cries of pain. “He! he! Ah! ha! hallo! Oh! Holy saints! Help me! I can’t breathe! I’ll crush this imp—no, I’ll split your skull against—”

And with a wild cry, he ran backward, crashing into the balcony.

Giles, seeing danger, grabbed the railing with both hands and swung himself over just as the giant’s head struck the wood with a sickening crack.

The crowd erupted in cheers. They had witnessed something extraordinary—a small man standing up to a mountain, and winning.

The giant, furious, snatched two nearby men and slammed them together like stones. Catherine screamed and threw her apron over Giles, shielding him from view. Then, still fuming, the giant stomped away after the procession.

No one knew it then, but this moment would echo far beyond the day.

Its immediate effect, however, was joyful. The people of Tergou now looked upon Giles with new respect. When he went about begging parchment for his brother Gerard, they listened more kindly. It had become a kind of trade—Giles collected scraps of paper freely, and Gerard gave him copper coins in return.

That afternoon, Catherine and her daughter Kate sat together, talking softly of their beloved son Gerard—his kindness, his priesthood, the future he promised for their family. Their fortune had come in the form they most desired: not just a benefice, but a holy man within their own home.

“He will marry Cornelis and Sybrandt,” Catherine said. “They may wed now, if they wish. And Gerard will care for us when we are gone.”

“And we can confess to him,” Kate added, “instead of to strangers.”

“Ay,” nodded her mother. “And he can bless us with the sacred oil when our time comes.”

“Oh, mother,” Kate whispered, “not for many years. Please don’t speak of that. It makes me sad. I hope I go before you.”

She smiled then, feeling light and free. “I’m not in pain anymore. It feels strange—so bright and happy. Mother… can you keep a secret?”

“Better than anyone,” Catherine replied, her eyes alight with curiosity.

“Then I’ll show you something beautiful. You’ve never seen anything like it. But Gerard must never know—he’s hiding it, covering it up, sometimes taking it away altogether.”

Kate took her crutches and moved slowly to the corner of the room. She returned with a cloth wrapped tightly around something. She unfolded it carefully.

There, painted with astonishing life and grace, was the Virgin Mary—her face serene, her eyes lifted to heaven as if belonging there, her golden hair flowing loosely over her shoulders, crowned with a tiara of stars and jewels.

Catherine gasped. “It is herself,” she breathed. “The Queen of Heaven. I have never seen her so real.”

“And her eyes,” Kate murmured, “as if they belong to the sky. Her hair—like fire.”

“And to think,” Catherine said, tears in her eyes, “we have a son who can bring the saints back to life on wood.”

“He is a saint himself,” Kate said softly. “Too good for this world. He paints the blessed because he belongs with them.”

Just as they began to admire the image anew, a strange voice cut through the silence.

At the door stood Ghysbrecht Van Swieten—the burgomaster, a man who had not entered their home in ten years.

Both women froze. Their faces told the story before words could form.

“The burgomaster!” they said together, their tone filled with shock.

Ghysbrecht cleared his throat. “Yes. The last time I came here, it was not in friendship. Men follow their interests. Mine and Eli’s were at odds. But today—I come on your behalf, not mine.”

Catherine and Kate exchanged a glance—of suspicion, of disbelief. They knew him well.

“It’s about your son Gerard.”

“Ay,” Catherine snapped. “You want him to work for the town for nothing. We know.”

“No,” Ghysbrecht said sharply. “I come to warn you. Your son has fallen into bad company.”

“Now Heaven forbid!” Catherine cried. “Speak plainly, man. Don’t torment a mother. Speak quickly—before you lie.”

Ghysbrecht paled. Anger mixed with shame. “Then hear it. Your son Gerard is not fit to be a priest. He spends all his time with Margaret—Peter Brandt’s red-haired girl. He loves her like a cow loves its calf.”

Mother and daughter burst into laughter.

Ghysbrecht stared. “What? You knew?”

“Take this news to those who don’t know my son,” Catherine said coldly. “Women mean nothing to him.”

“Other women, perhaps,” Ghysbrecht retorted. “But this one—she’s the apple of his eye. My servant has seen them together—holding hands, gazing into each other’s eyes like fools in love. Come, woman—don’t waste my time. Tell me now—will you stop them?”

“I am ill at ease,” Catherine whispered. “I remember being young. My heart turns sick. Kate—what if it’s true?”

“No!” Kate cried, her voice rising. “Gerard might love a woman—every young man does—but if he did, he’d tell us. He wouldn’t deceive us. You wicked man! No, mother—look at me! Gerard is too pure for earthly love. His heart is for Our Lady and the saints. Look—see this picture! If he loved a mortal, could he paint the Queen of Heaven like this?” She held it up, radiant with conviction.

The burgomaster stared, dumbfounded. “Why—it is herself,” he gasped.

“It is!” Kate insisted. “And you admire it? Then forgive me for fearing you.”

“Am I in a madhouse?” Ghysbrecht muttered. “You show me a portrait of a girl, say he painted it, and that proves he doesn’t love her? Painters always paint their sweethearts!”

“A girl?” Kate exclaimed, horrified. “This is the Blessed Virgin!”

“No,” Ghysbrecht insisted. “It’s Margaret Brandt.”

“Blind fool!” Kate cried. “Look at her crown!”

“Silly child,” he snapped. “See her red hair! Would the Virgin wear red hair? She chose colors ten thousand years before creation!”

At that moment, Peter Buyskens, their neighbor, peeked through the doorway.

“What’s going on?” he whispered. “I can hear you across the street.”

“Here’s the burgomaster accusing Gerard,” Catherine said.

“Stop!” Ghysbrecht interrupted. “Peter Buyskens has come just in time. He knows them both. They cast spells on him.”

“What?” Peter asked, confused.

“They bewitched him,” Ghysbrecht said. “They cured him of colic with unholy magic. Now, Peter—look at the picture. Who is it?”

“Well,” Peter said slowly, “you know as well as I do. But why put a crown on her? I never saw her wearing one.”

“Curse you!” Ghysbrecht shouted. “Can’t you just name the wench?”

“I’d do more to please you than that,” Peter replied calmly. “If it isn’t as natural as life.”

“Curse him!” Ghysbrecht growled.

“Oh, sir,” little Kate said, “tell us—does this face belong to a living woman? To Margaret Brandt?”

“A mirror couldn’t be truer,” Peter said.

“But is it her?” Kate pressed.

“Who else should it be?”

“Why couldn’t you say so at once?” Ghysbrecht snapped.

“I did,” Peter shot back. “As plain as I could.”

They argued fiercely, oblivious to the fact that Catherine and Kate had covered their faces with their aprons and were rocking back and forth in silent anguish.

Then Elias entered from the shop, stopping short at the scene.

Catherine, though her face was hidden, recognized his footsteps.

“That’s my poor man,” she sobbed. “Tell him, Peter Buyskens—I cannot.”

Elias turned pale. The burgomaster’s presence, after years of silence, combined with his wife’s distress, sent a chill through him.

“Richart! Jacob!” he gasped.

“No,” the burgomaster said. “It’s closer to home. No one is dead or dying, old friend.”

“God bless you,” Elias breathed, relief washing over him. “Ah—something has lifted from my chest. Now—what is it?”

Ghysbrecht repeated the accusation and showed the painting.

“Is that all?” Elias asked, calmer now. “What are you shouting about? It’s troubling—yes—but not deadly. Boys will be boys. He’ll grow out of it. It’s skin-deep.”

But when Ghysbrecht added that Margaret was a decent girl, and that such closeness likely meant marriage had been discussed, Elias’s face darkened.

“Marriage?” he said sternly. “That shall never happen. I’ll stop it—by force, if need be. Like old John Koestein did.”

“And what did he do?” Catherine asked, lifting her apron.

The burgomaster answered: “He locked young Albert Koestein in the Stadthouse prison until he submitted. Forty-eight hours—bread and water—cooled his hot blood. ‘Tell my father I am his humble servant,’ he said. ‘Let me out into the sun—sun is worth all the wenches in the world.’”

“Oh, the cruelty of men,” Catherine sighed.

“Nay,” Ghysbrecht said. “It’s the law. If a father says, ‘Lock up my son,’ the burgomaster must obey. It would be chaos if fathers couldn’t protect their children.”

“Well,” Elias said, “it won’t come to that. My son has never disobeyed me. He never will. Where is he, Kate?”

“I don’t know, father.”

“I do,” Ghysbrecht said. “He’s at Sevenbergen. My servant met him on the road.”

Supper passed in silence.

Night fell. Still no Gerard.

Eight o’clock came. Still no sign.

Elias sent everyone to bed, except Catherine.

“You and I will walk abroad,” he said. “Talk over this new trouble.”

“Abroad at this hour?” she asked. “Whither?”

“To Sevenbergen.”

“No hasty words, father,” Catherine pleaded. “Poor Gerard—he never wronged you before.”

“Fear me not,” Elias said. “But it must end. I don’t trust tomorrow with today’s duty.”

They walked hand in hand—strange, perhaps, to modern readers, but in those times, couples walked side by side, not arm in arm. The night was clear and warm, the air soft with memory.

“It’s been many years since we walked so late,” Catherine said softly.

“Ay, sweetheart,” Elias replied. “More than we’ll see again. (I wonder—will he ever come?)”

“Not since our courting days,” she said.

“No. You were a buxom lass then.”

“And you were a comely lad,” she smiled. “I suppose Gerard is with her now—just as you were with me. Nature is strong. It changes nothing across generations.”

“Nay,” Elias muttered. “I hope he’s left her. Or we’ll be here all night.”

“Eli,” Catherine said gently.

“Yes, Kate?”

“I’ve been happy with you, despite all our trials—much happier, I think, than if I had become a nun. Will you not speak harshly to the child? One can be firm without being cruel.”

“Surely,” he said.

“Have you been happy with me, my poor Eli?”

“You know I have,” he said. “Friends I’ve known, but none like thee. Kiss me, wife.”

“A heart to share joy and sorrow is a great comfort,” she said. “Isn’t it, Eli?”

“It is,” he replied. “‘It doth joy double, and halveth trouble.’ That’s how I’ve found it, sweetheart.”

And just then, a voice sang in the moonlight.

“Ah—here comes the young fool.”

Catherine trembled, clutching her husband’s hand.

The moon was bright, but they stood in shadow. Gerard didn’t see them.

He came singing, his face glowing in the silver light.
