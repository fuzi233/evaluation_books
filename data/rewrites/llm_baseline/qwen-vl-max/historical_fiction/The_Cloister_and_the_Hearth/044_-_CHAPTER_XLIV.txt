Experienced women do not panic when another woman faints, nor do they rush to assign blame beyond the body’s natural frailty—something they have seen too often to mistake for anything more profound. Catherine moved swiftly and with purpose: she laid the girl down flat on the floor, her head resting low, opened the window wide to let in fresh air, and loosened the tight folds of her dress as she lay still. Only after all this did she step to the door and call out, her voice firm and clear: “Come here, if you please.”

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht entered at once, finding the young woman lying motionless, while Catherine stood beside her, hands clenched in quiet distress. “Oh, my poor child!” exclaimed Margaret Van Eyck. “What has happened to her?”

“Me?” Catherine replied sharply. “What could I possibly have done?”

“Nothing, madam,” said the other gently, “nothing beyond what is natural given her circumstances.”

A flush rose in Margaret Van Eyck’s cheeks. “You speak so calmly,” she said, “when it was your actions that brought her to this state.”

“That I am not,” Catherine answered bluntly, “nor any woman alive.”

“Was it not you and your husband who kept them apart? And now he has gone to Italy alone—no wife by his side. This is no mere circumstance. You’ve broken her heart between you.”

“Why, madam,” Catherine returned, “who then? In Heaven’s name! To hear you talk, one would think this were Gerard’s own sweetheart. But that cannot be. Look at this fur—five crowns an ell, and more than that in quality. Besides, this young lady is a married woman—or ought to be.”

“Of course she ought,” Margaret Van Eyck replied. “And who is to blame that she isn’t? Who stepped forward before them at the very altar?”

“God forgive whoever it was,” Catherine said gravely, “but it was not I, nor my husband.”

“Well,” the older woman conceded, her tone softening slightly, “now you’ve seen her, perhaps you won’t be so harsh toward her. Thank God, she is coming round.”

“Me bitter against her?” Catherine asked, surprised. “No, that is over. Poor soul—trouble behind her, trouble ahead. And yet I sent her, of all people, to read Gerard’s letter to me. That must have been it—she fainted from the weight of it. She is stirring now. Come, sweetheart—don’t be afraid. There’s no one here but friends.”

They helped her into a comfortable chair. As color slowly returned to her face and lips, Catherine drew Margaret Van Eyck aside. “Will she stay with you tonight?” she asked quietly.

“No, madam,” came the reply.

“I wouldn’t let her return to Sevenbergen tonight,” Catherine insisted.

“It is her choice,” Margaret Van Eyck said firmly. “She still refuses to remain.”

“But you are older than she,” Catherine pressed. “You can insist. See—she is beginning to notice.”

Then, without speaking aloud, Catherine leaned close and whispered something into the other woman’s ear—so briefly that it seemed impossible even a word could pass. Yet between two women such as these, silence could carry more meaning than words. The old lady started visibly, then whispered back, trembling: “It’s false! It’s slander! It’s monstrous! Look at her face—how could anyone accuse such a face?”

“Tut! tut! tut!” Catherine snapped. “You might as well say this is not my hand. I know it well—and I tell you plainly, it is true.”

To Margaret Van Eyck’s astonishment, Catherine then approached the girl, took her in her arms, and kissed her warmly upon the cheek. “I suffered for Gerard,” she said, “and you shed your blood for him—I heard his own words. He told me plainly I had wronged her. Yes, Gerard, my child, I held back from her; but from this hour, I will make amends. You are my daughter now.”

A second embrace sealed their sudden bond, and the impulsive woman turned away, leaving the room.

Margaret sank back into her chair, a fragile smile playing across her lips. Gerard’s mother had kissed her and called her daughter. But in the next breath, she saw her dear friend watching her with a look of deep disapproval.

“I wonder you allowed that woman to touch you,” the elder said, sternly.

“His mother,” Margaret murmured, half in reproach.

“Mother or not,” the other replied, “you would not let her near you if you knew what she whispered to me about you.”

“About me?” Margaret whispered, pale again.

“Yes—about you, whom she never saw until tonight.”

Margaret Van Eyck began to speak, choosing her words carefully, eager to share her outrage—but before she could go further, a sudden interruption silenced her.

The young woman slid from her chair onto her knees, bowing her head in anguish. With trembling voice and tear-filled eyes, she began to beg for forgiveness—not for herself, but for the pain she had caused. Her sorrow was so deep, her humility so complete, that any observer would have believed she had committed some terrible injustice, some unbearable insult, against the noble woman before her.
