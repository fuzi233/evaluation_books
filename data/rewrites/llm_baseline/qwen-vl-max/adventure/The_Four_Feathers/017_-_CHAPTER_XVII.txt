Mrs. Adair made no demand for explanations. Behind her calm, composed face lay a mind sharp and cunning, one that thrived on secrets and preferred to spy through a keyhole rather than walk straight in. To her, knowledge won by cleverness was far more valuable than truth handed over freely. She avoided direct questions as if they were traps, and took pride in coaxing out small revelations from others—trivial bits of news she could have had at breakfast with a simple inquiry. So when Ethne clung to the meaning of a single white feather and reacted so deeply to Captain Willoughby’s news about Harry Feversham, Mrs. Adair said nothing. Instead, she carried on with cheerful conversation during lunch, steering the talk toward light topics. She was good company when she chose to be—and she chose now.

But her efforts were in vain. “I don’t believe you’re hearing a word I’m saying!” she finally exclaimed.

Ethne laughed and admitted it. As soon as the meal ended, she retreated to her room, seeking solitude. Sitting by the window, she replayed in her mind the story Willoughby had told her earlier—the tale of Harry Feversham’s courage, his secret return, his long years of sacrifice. Her heart swelled with emotion, stirred as though by music played from heaven itself. The regret that he hadn’t come home sooner, before she’d become engaged to Durrance, faded into insignificance. What mattered now was the joy that surged within her—a joy so great it eclipsed all sorrow. Her pride, shattered long ago by Harry’s rejection in the hall at Lennon House, had been restored—not by a man who had once wounded her, but by the very man who had done so. And she was profoundly grateful to him for risking everything to reclaim her dignity.

She felt alive again. Youth returned to her in the golden August light. A new warmth hummed through her veins. She unlocked a drawer in her dressing case and pulled out the portrait—the only gift from Harry Feversham she had kept. It was a picture of someone long gone from her life, someone she would never love again. Yet he remained a friend. She looked at it with deep contentment, knowing that Harry had needed no words from her to inspire his courage, no encouragement to keep him true to his purpose. He had acted not for reward, but because it was right.

After replacing the portrait, she slipped the white feather beside it, locked the drawer, and stepped back to the window.

Outside, the breeze danced across the lawn, casting shifting shadows beneath the tall trees. The sunlight softened, turning amber and gold. But Ethne’s thoughts turned northward—to Donegal, to her homeland. It was August, and the first heather would be blooming on the hills. She wished this moment of joy had come there, where the mountains stood guard and the streams sang their ancient songs. There, she believed, the land itself might have shared in her happiness. Great sorrow or great joy always called her home—because endurance was easier, and joy deeper, among familiar places.

Yet she wasn’t entirely alone. Dermod’s old collie dog, now her constant companion, sat quietly by her side. To him she whispered her thoughts, and when her voice trembled with emotion, he listened without judgment. In the dog’s silence, she found comfort.

And in that silence, she pieced together what Willoughby had left unsaid, what Harry himself had never spoken aloud. She imagined those three hidden years in Suakin—how he lived in disguise among crowded streets, watching soldiers march off to battle, returning bloodied and victorious. Each time, he had to slip away, afraid some old friend—Durrance, Willoughby, Trench—might recognize him. She saw the panic in his eyes when he first saw Berber’s dark walls, the desperate night spent in ruined fields, the frantic search for water amid the sand dunes of Obak. These images filled her mind, and each time she thought of them, she asked: *Where was I then? What was I doing?*

She sat wrapped in golden light until dusk painted the creek with fading hues. Rooks wheeled overhead, calling out their evening song, reminding her it was time for dinner.

That night, Ethne brought a radiant spirit to the table. Her eyes sparkled like stars, her cheeks glowed with color. Mrs. Adair watched, amazed. She knew something profound had changed. She had never seen Ethne so bright, so full of life. She was certain Captain Willoughby had delivered startling news—but still, she couldn’t guess its nature. Still, she joined the conversation, and the dinner passed with ease, free of tension for the first time since Durrance returned to Guessens.

Even Durrance seemed lighter. His usual stiffness melted. He laughed readily, matched Ethne’s humor, and the weight of his silence lifted. When they rose from the table, he smiled and said, “You’ll play your violin tonight, I think.”

“Yes,” she replied. “With all my heart.”

He laughed and held the door open.

The violin had been locked away for two months. To Durrance, it had become a barometer of Ethne’s soul. When her world was at peace, the instrument spoke; when it was broken, it stayed silent—for fear of reopening wounds too raw for others to see. Ethne knew it well: the violin was an indiscreet confidant. But tonight, it would speak again.

Mrs. Adair waited until Ethne was out of earshot. “You’ve noticed the change in her?” she asked.

“Yes,” Durrance answered. “One has waited for it, hoped for it, despaired of it.”

“Are you glad?”

He raised his head. “Do you wonder? She’s always been kind, generous, selfless. But tonight… there’s something more. Something new.”

A look of pity crossed Mrs. Adair’s face. She turned and left without another word.

Durrance claimed the transformation as his own.

Mrs. Adair drew up the blinds, opened the window, and let the moonlight flood the room. Then, seeing Ethne unlock her violin case, she stepped out onto the terrace. She could not bear to stay inside.

When Durrance entered the drawing room, Ethne was alone. She sat by the window, tightening the strings of her violin. He took a chair behind her, hidden in shadow.

“What shall I play?” she asked.

“The Musoline Overture,” he said. “You played it the first evening I came to Ramelton. I remember how you played it—so beautifully. Play it again. I want to compare.”

“I’ve played it since.”

“Never to me.”

They were alone. The windows stood wide open. The night was quiet, silvered by moonlight.

Ethne suddenly crossed to the lamp and extinguished it. She returned to her seat. Durrance remained in darkness, leaning forward, hands resting on his knees, listening—not with casual attention, but with a focus so intense he had not shown all evening. He was applying a final test—one that would determine the course of both their lives. The violin would reveal the truth. Would it speak only of friendship—or something deeper?

She began to play.

As the music rose, Ethne forgot Durrance was even there. The garden breathed softly, warm and sweet. On the creek, moonlight shimmered like liquid silver. Trees stood still, dreaming under the stars. And as the melody floated across the lawn, she imagined it traveling down the creek, past Salcombe Bar, across the sea—until it reached a distant shore, where a man slept beneath southern skies, the desert wind brushing his face.

*If he could only hear,* she thought. *If he could wake and know this music is a message of friendship.*

And so, she played with greater skill than ever before. The violin became a voice—full of empathy, longing, hope. Her fantasy grew: the music was a bridge stretched across the world, and for these few moments, she and Harry Feversham could meet, shake hands, and part again—each continuing their separate paths, yet strengthened by the connection.

The chords rang clear and strong. To Ethne, they proclaimed the pride that had returned to her heart. Her fancy deepened into certainty. It was no longer *if* he heard—but *he must* hear!

And then, a wilder hope flared: *What if he could answer?*

She lingered on the final notes, waiting. When the last sound faded into silence, she sat still, violin cradled in her lap, gazing out into the moonlit garden.

An answer came—but not from the sea or sky. It came from the shadows behind her, spoken in Durrance’s voice.

“Ethne,” he said, “where do you think I heard that overture last played?”

She startled, remembering he was there. “You told me—at Ramelton, when you first came to Lennon House.”

“I’ve heard it since. Not played by you. Not really played at all. Just fragments—a melody hinted at, clumsily plucked on a zither, with many wrong notes—by a Greek in a bare, whitewashed café lit by a single harsh lamp, at Wadi Halfa.”

“This overture?” she whispered. “How strange.”

“Not so strange,” he said. “Because the Greek was Harry Feversham.”

The answer had come. Ethne knew it was real. She sat motionless in the moonlight. If anyone had looked closely, they might have seen her eyelids closed.

Silence stretched between them.

She did not question why Durrance had kept this secret so long. She did not ask what Harry was doing in such a place. But she understood—he had spoken to her as she had spoken to him. The music had built a bridge. And it was not strange that he used Durrance’s voice to cross it.

“When was this?” she asked at last.

“In February of this year. I’ll tell you about it.”

“Yes,” she said softly. “Please, tell me.”

And from the shadows, Durrance began to speak.
