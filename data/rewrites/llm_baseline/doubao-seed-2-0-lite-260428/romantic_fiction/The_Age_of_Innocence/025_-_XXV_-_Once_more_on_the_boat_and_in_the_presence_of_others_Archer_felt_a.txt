Back on the boat, surrounded by chattering strangers, Newland Archer settled into a quiet tranquility so unexpected it left him half breathless, as if some soft, warm weight had wrapped around his shoulders to hold him steady.

By every unspoken rule of the world he’d always lived in, the day they’d just spent together in Boston was a ridiculous, wasted failure. He had not so much as pressed his lips to Ellen Olenska’s hand, had not wrung a single whispered promise of future meetings from her. For a man sick with unsatisfied longing, about to part from the woman he loved for an unknown stretch of time, he was almost embarrassingly, humiliatingly calm, almost comforted. It was the perfect, unforced balance she’d struck that did it: she had not betrayed their mutual loyalty to the people who loved them back home, but she had not lied to either of them about the pull thrumming between them. The balance was no calculated performance, he’d seen that in her stammering half-sentences, in the tears she’d swiped away before he could comment. That tender awe swelled in him now that the risk of crossing a line they’d both agreed not to step over was past, and he thanked the fates no silly urge to play the part of a dramatic lover had ever tempted him to push her further than she was willing to go. Even after they’d clasped hands for a final goodbye at Fall River Station, and he’d turned to walk away alone, he carried the quiet, unshakable certainty that he had gained far more from their meeting than he’d ever sacrificed.

He wandered back to his club, settled alone in the deserted library, replaying every single second of their hours together over and over in his head. It grew clearer with every turn of his thoughts: if Ellen finally chose to return to Europe, to go back to her husband, it would not be because her old glittery, empty life tempted her, not even with the new terms the Count was offering. No. She would only go if she feared she was becoming a temptation to Archer, if she worried they would both slip and fall away from the quiet, honorable standard they had set for themselves together. She would choose to stay near him as long as he never asked her to come any closer. It rested entirely with him, to keep her there, safe, close, but never pushed over the edge.

Those thoughts stayed with him all through the train ride home, wrapping him in a soft golden haze that blurred every face around him until his fellow passengers looked like distant, half-seen phantoms. He half believed if he tried to speak to them, they would not understand a single word he said. He woke the next morning to the swelter of a stifling New York September, the heat-wilted faces of the passengers streaming past him still blurred behind that same golden glow — until one face pulled itself free of the crowd, sharp and familiar, forcing its way to the front of his consciousness.

It was the young man he’d spotted the day before, walking out of Boston’s Parker House, the one who did not look like any ordinary American hotel guest. The same odd, faint tug of half-recognition pricked at him now. The young man stood squinting around the station, the dazed, lost air of a foreigner abandoned to the rough unkindness of American transit, then stepped toward Archer, lifted his hat, and spoke in heavily accented English: “Surely, Monsieur, we met in London?”

Archer grasped his hand at once. “Ah, of course! In London! You did make it over, after all?” he exclaimed, staring at the sharp, haggard little face of young Carfry’s French tutor, M. Rivière.

“Oh, I made it, yes,” Rivière smiled, his lips tight. “But not for long. I return the day after tomorrow.” He stood there, gripping his light valise in a neatly gloved hand, his gaze anxious, perplexed, almost pleading as he looked Archer over. “I wonder, Monsieur, since I’ve had the luck to run into you, if I might —”

“I was just about to suggest it,” Archer cut in. “Come to luncheon, won’t you? Downtown — stop by my law office first and I’ll take you to a very decent restaurant nearby.”

Rivière looked genuinely surprised, touched. “You are too kind. I was only going to ask how to find a carriage. There are no porters here, no one seems willing to listen —”

“I know. Our American stations must shock you. You ask for a porter and they hand you chewing gum. Come along, I’ll extricate you, and you really must let me buy you lunch.”

Rivière hesitated for a barely perceptible beat, then thanked him profusely, saying he already had a lunch engagement that he could not break. But by the time they reached the sunlit, comparatively quiet street, he asked if he might call at Archer’s office that afternoon instead. Archer, with nothing but slow midsummer leisure on his hands at the firm, scribbled his address down and set an hour. Rivière thanked him over and over, tipped his hat, and climbed aboard a passing horse-car, leaving Archer to walk the rest of the way to his office.

He arrived sharp on the dot, freshly shaved, his clothes pressed neat, but his face still drawn and serious. Archer was alone in the office, and before he could even offer his guest a seat, Rivière spoke abruptly: “I believe I saw you, sir, yesterday in Boston.”

The line was insignificant, and Archer was about to nod in easy assent, until the strange, urgent intensity in his visitor’s gaze caught him up short, checking the words on his tongue.

“It is extraordinary, very extraordinary,” Rivière continued, “that we should meet under the circumstances I find myself in now.”

“What circumstances?” Archer asked, half wondering if the young man had run out of money.

Rivière studied him for a moment, tentative. “I did not come to New York looking for work, as I mentioned I might when we met in London. I am here on a special mission —”

Archer’s eyes widened. A flash of connection snapped between the two meetings in his mind, the whole scene lighting up like a struck match. He paused, taking in the new information, and Rivière fell silent, apparently aware the half-sentence was enough.

“A special mission,” Archer repeated slowly.

The young Frenchman held his palms open, lifted them an inch from his sides. The two men stared at each other across the desk until Archer roused himself to say, “Do sit down.” Rivière bowed, settled into a chair on the far side of the desk, and waited again.

“It was about this mission that you wanted to consult me?” Archer asked at last.

Rivière nodded. “Not on my own behalf. On that score, I have already made up my mind. I should like — if I may — to speak to you about the Countess Olenska.”

Archer had known the words were coming for minutes, but when they landed, they sent blood rushing to his temples as if he had walked face-first into a bent-back hidden branch in a thicket. “And on whose behalf,” he said, keeping his voice as even as he could, “do you wish to do this?”

Rivière met his gaze unflinchingly. “I might say hers, if it did not sound like an unacceptable liberty. Shall I say instead: on behalf of abstract justice?”

Archer studied him, half ironic. “In other words, you are Count Olenski’s messenger?” He saw his own dark flush reflected in the Frenchman’s sallow face. “You are not here to deliver his message to *you*, Monsieur. If I came to you, it is on entirely other grounds.”

“What right do you, as his emissary, have to be on any other ground?” Archer retorted. “An emissary is an emissary.”

Rivière considered that for a moment. “My mission to the Countess Olenska is over. It has failed.”

“I can’t help that,” Archer rejoined, his tone still sharp with irony.

“No, but you can help —” Rivière paused, twisting his hat in his carefully gloved hands, staring at the lining before lifting his eyes back to Archer’s face. “You can help, Monsieur, I am convinced, to make it equally a failure with her family.”

Archer pushed his chair back and shot to his feet. “Well, by God I will!” he exclaimed. He stood there, hands stuffed in his pockets, glowering down at the little Frenchman, who had also risen, his head still a few inches lower than Archer’s line of sight. Rivière paled even further, to the very edge of what his sallow complexion could manage.

“Why the devil,” Archer exploded, “did you think — if you’re appealing to me because of my connection to Madame Olenska — that I would take a view contrary to the rest of her family?”

Rivière’s face softened into absolute distress, the usual sharp cleverness draining away until he looked entirely defenseless. “Oh, Monsieur —”

“I can’t imagine,” Archer continued, “why you would come to me, when there are others far closer to the Countess to speak to. Still less why you thought I’d be more receptive to the arguments I assume you were sent over to deliver.”

Rivière took the onslaught with a disarming humility. “The arguments I want to present to you, Monsieur, are my own, not the ones I was sent over with.”

“Then I see even less reason to listen to them.”

Rivière stared back at his hat for a long beat, as if considering if the words were enough of a hint to leave. Then he spoke with sudden, sharp decision. “Monsieur — will you tell me one thing? Is it my right to be here that you question? Or do you perhaps believe the whole matter to be already closed?”

His quiet insistence made Archer feel clumsy, loud, foolish for his bluster. He reddened slightly, dropped back into his chair, and gestured for Rivière to sit again. “I beg your pardon. But why isn’t the matter closed?”

Rivière gazed back at him, his face tight with anguish. “You do, then, agree with the rest of the family that, faced with the new proposals I brought, it is hardly possible for Madame Olenska to refuse to go back to her husband?”

“Good God!” Archer exclaimed. Rivière let out a low, quiet murmur of confirmation.

“Before I saw her, at Count Olenski’s request, I met with Mr. Lovell Mingott, spoke with him for hours before I traveled to Boston. I understand he represents his mother’s view, that Mrs. Manson Mingott’s influence guides the whole family.”

Archer went still, feeling as if he was clinging to the edge of a sliding precipice. The realization that he had been entirely excluded from these negotiations, that no one had even bothered to tell him they were happening, barely registered next to the sharper shock of what he was learning. He saw in a flash that the family had stopped consulting him because their deep, unspoken tribal instinct warned them he was no longer on their side. He remembered May’s offhand comment, on the drive home from the archery party at Mrs. Manson Mingott’s: *Perhaps, after all, Ellen would be happier with her husband*. That throwaway line had been nothing more than a test, to see which way the wind blew. He’d exclaimed in outrage at the time, and his wife had never mentioned Ellen’s name to him since. The result of that little test had been reported back to the family, and Archer had been quietly, tacitly locked out of their counsels. He admired the unbreakable family loyalty that made May fall in line, and knew she would never have done it if her conscience protested — but she almost certainly shared the family’s view that Ellen would be better off as an unhappy titled wife than a shunned, separated free woman, and there was no point in discussing the matter with Newland, who had a stubborn habit of suddenly refusing to take the most basic, assumed social rules for granted.

Archer lifted his head, met his visitor’s anxious gaze. “Don’t you know — is it possible you don’t know — that the family is starting to doubt if they even have the right to advise the Countess to refuse her husband’s latest proposals?”

“The proposals you brought?”

“The proposals I brought.”

Archer almost snapped that none of this was any of his business, but the humble, stubborn tenacity in Rivière’s gaze made him hold his tongue, and he asked another question instead: “What is your object in telling me all this?”

Rivière did not hesitate for a second. “To beg you, Monsieur — to beg you with all the strength I have — not to let her go back. Oh, don’t let her!”

Archer stared at him, increasingly stunned. There was no mistaking the sincerity of his distress, the iron of his determination. He had clearly decided he would throw away every last thing he had rather than stay silent on the matter. “May I ask,” Archer said slowly, “if that is what you told the Countess Olenska, when you met with her?”

Rivière flushed, but his gaze did not waver. “No, Monsieur. I accepted my mission in good faith. I truly believed — for private reasons I do not need to trouble you with — that it would be better for Madame Olenska to get back her social position, her fortune, the standing her husband’s name gives her. I would never have taken the job otherwise.”

“Well then —?”

Their eyes locked for a long, long moment.

“Ah, Monsieur, after I saw her, after I sat and listened to her speak, I knew she was far better off here.”

“You knew?”

“Monsieur, I carried out my mission faithfully. I laid out the Count’s arguments, stated all his offers, did not add a single comment of my own. The Countess listened patiently, she saw me twice, considered everything I came to say with perfect impartiality. And it was over the course of those two talks that I changed my mind, that I started to see things differently.”

“What made you change?”

“Simply seeing the change in *her*,” Rivière said.

“The change in her? You knew her before, then?”

Rivière’s color rose again. “I used to see her in her husband’s house in Europe. I have known Count Olenski for many years. You can imagine he would not send a total stranger on a mission this sensitive.”

Archer’s gaze wandered away from him, to the hanging calendar on the wall, its top printed with the rugged features of the President of the United States. That this conversation was happening anywhere under the jurisdiction of that man’s government felt as absurd and impossible as any fantasy he could have dreamed up. “The change — what sort of change?”

“Ah, Monsieur, if I could put it into words!” Rivière paused. “*Tenez* — it was the realization of something I had never considered before: that she is an American. And if you are an American of her kind — of your kind — the sorts of compromises other societies accept, shrug off as part of the give and take of polite life, become unthinkable. Simply unthinkable. If her relations here understood exactly what the Count’s life with her was like, their opposition to her returning would be as absolute as her own. But they seem to think the Count’s wish to have her back is proof of some great, desperate longing for domestic happiness.” Rivière paused, then added softly. “It is not nearly that simple.”

Archer stared back at the calendar, then down at the scattered papers on his desk. For a second or two he could not trust himself to speak. He heard Rivière push his chair back, realized the young man had stood up. When he looked up again, he saw that his visitor was as moved as he was.

“Thank you,” Archer said simply.

“There is nothing to thank me for, Monsieur. It is I who —” Rivière broke off, his voice thick, like speech had become difficult for him too. “I should like to add one thing, though. You asked if I am in Count Olenski’s employ. I am, at this moment. I returned to his household a few months ago, for reasons of private necessity that could happen to any man with sick, elderly dependents to support. But from the moment I walked in here today to say these things to you, I consider myself no longer in his service. I will tell him as much when I return, and give him my reasons. That is all, Monsieur.”

Rivière bowed, stepped back, and Archer stood to meet his outstretched hand. “Thank you,” Archer said again, as their palms clasped.
