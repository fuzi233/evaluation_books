XIV. As Archer stepped into the lobby, he collided with Ned Winsett, the only one among the so-called "clever people" Janey mentioned whom he truly valued for more than idle chatter over cigars or at a club dinner. He’d caught sight of Winsett’s hunched back earlier across the hall and noticed how his gaze had lingered on the Beaufort box. They exchanged greetings, and Winsett suggested a bock at a small German eatery just around the corner. But Archer, not in the mood for their usual round of half-serious banter, declined, citing work waiting at home. Winsett laughed, “Oh, well, so have I—guess I’ll be the Industrious Apprentice too.” They walked together, side by side, until Winsett broke the silence: “Look, I’m really after something—what’s the name of that dark-haired woman in your box? With the Beauforts, wasn’t she? The one Lefferts can’t seem to take his eyes off.”

Archer felt an odd pang—why did Winsett care about Ellen Olenska? And why bring up Lefferts? It wasn’t like him to pry. Still, Archer remembered: Winsett was a journalist. “Not for a story, I hope?” he asked, smiling.

“Not for the press,” Winsett replied. “Just for myself. She lives near me—strange neighborhood for someone so striking—and she was kind to my boy. He tumbled down her front steps chasing his kitten, cut his knee badly. She came rushing out bareheaded, carrying him, bandage already wrapped tight around his leg. So calm, so beautiful—my wife was so overwhelmed, she never thought to ask her name.”

A warmth spread through Archer’s chest. The gesture itself wasn’t rare—any decent woman might’ve done it—but it *was* Ellen. That sudden, unguarded rush, the quiet grace, the way she’d silenced Mrs. Winsett with her presence—it was all unmistakably her.

“It’s Countess Olenska,” he said softly. “Granddaughter of old Mrs. Mingott.”

“Whew—Countess!” Winsett whistled. “Well, I didn’t know they were this neighborly. Mingotts aren’t.”

“They would be,” Archer said, “if you let them.”

They both knew the argument was old and pointless—the endless debate about whether men of talent and taste could ever belong in high society. There was no point in continuing.

“I wonder,” Winsett mused, “how a Countess ends up in our little slum?”

“Because she doesn’t care where she lives—or what we think of it,” Archer answered, proud of the image he painted. “She sees no meaning in our petty rules.”

“H’m,” Winsett muttered. “Been in bigger places, I suppose.” Then he turned toward Broadway. “Here’s my corner.” He disappeared into the street, and Archer stood watching him go, lost in thought.

Winsett had flashes of insight—rare moments when he saw deeper than most. That was what made him compelling. Yet why did such a man accept failure so quietly, at a time when others still fought? Archer knew Winsett had a wife and child, though he’d never met them. Their meetings always happened at the Century Club or in dim corners of restaurants frequented by writers and actors—places where Winsett often proposed a bock. Once, he’d mentioned his wife was an invalid—perhaps true, or perhaps just a polite excuse for not attending parties. Winsett despised social rituals. Archer, who dressed at night because it felt cleaner and easier, saw it as part of a tired Bohemian act—a pose that made fashionable life seem simpler, less pretentious. But despite that, Archer always found himself drawn to Winsett. Whenever he saw that lean face, those melancholy eyes, he’d pull him aside for hours of talk.

Winsett wasn’t a journalist by choice. He was a writer—pure, serious, born too late for a world that no longer needed literature. After publishing a slim volume of literary essays—only 120 sold, thirty given away, the rest destroyed by publishers to make room for fashion spreads and temperance ads—he gave up. He took a sub-editor job on *Hearth-fires*, a weekly filled with paper patterns, sentimental love stories, and moralizing ads. He could talk endlessly about it, but beneath the humor lay a bitterness—of youth wasted, talent unappreciated. His conversations stirred Archer, made him measure his own life and feel its emptiness. But Winsett’s life, in truth, held even less. Still, their shared curiosity kept their talks alive—though always within the bounds of thoughtful dilettantism.

“The fact is,” Winsett once said, “life isn’t much suited for either of us. I’m down and out—nothing to do about it. I’ve got one thing to offer, and no market for it. But you—you’re free, you’ve got means. Why don’t *you* get involved? The only way is politics.”

Archer laughed. That was the gap—between Winsett and men like him. Everyone knew in polite circles: “a gentleman couldn’t go into politics.” But he couldn’t say that aloud. Instead, he evaded: “Look at honest men in American politics! They don’t want us.”

“Who’s ‘they’?” Winsett shot back. “Why don’t you all become ‘they’?”

Archer smiled, condescendingly. It was useless. Everyone knew the fate of gentlemen who entered politics—ruined reputations, ruined clothes. The era was over. The city belonged to bosses and immigrants. Decent people retreated to sport or culture.

“Culture!” Winsett scoffed. “If we had any. Just scattered patches—dying out from lack of care. The last bits of Europe’s tradition your ancestors brought. But you’re a tiny minority—no center, no audience, no competition. You’re like portraits in an empty house: ‘The Portrait of a Gentleman.’ You’ll never matter till you roll up your sleeves and dive into the mud. Or emigrate… God, if I could…”

Archer shook his head silently and steered the conversation to books—where Winsett, uncertain but passionate, was always worth listening to.

Emigrate? As if a gentleman could abandon his country! One couldn’t roll up sleeves and get dirty. A gentleman stayed home and abstained. But you couldn’t make Winsett see that. That was why New York—its literary clubs, its exotic restaurants—seemed dazzling at first, then revealed itself as a smaller, duller box than Fifth Avenue’s polished façade.

The next morning, Archer searched the city for yellow roses—vainly. He arrived late at the office, realized no one noticed, and felt a surge of frustration. Why wasn’t he right now on the beach with May Welland? No one believed in his pretense of work. In firms like Mr. Letterblair’s—focused on estates and conservative investments—there were always young men, comfortably off, doing nothing much. They sat at desks, read papers, performed trivial tasks. The law was acceptable because it was a profession—not business. But none of them hoped to rise. The green mold of indifference had already begun to creep over some. Archer shivered—could it be spreading over him too?

He had other interests—travel, art, the clever set May admired. He tried to “keep up,” as he’d told Madame Olenska. But what would happen when he married? Would this narrow space—his real life—shrink further? He’d seen others dream the same dream, then slowly sink into the comfortable routine of their elders.

From the office, he sent a note to Madame Olenska, asking if he might visit that afternoon, and requested she reply at his club. But there was no message. No letter the next day. The silence stung worse than he expected. Even seeing a perfect bouquet of yellow roses in a shop window the following morning, he walked past without a glance.

Only on the third day did a letter arrive—postmarked Skuytercliff, where the van der Luydens had fled after sending the Duke off to sea.

“I ran away,” it began abruptly, skipping all formalities. “The day after I saw you at the play. These kind friends took me in. I wanted quiet—to think. You were right to say how kind they are. I feel safe here. I wish you were with us.”

It ended with a cold “Yours sincerely”—no mention of when she’d return.

Archer was startled. What was she running from? Why did she need safety? At first, he imagined some threat from abroad. Then he reminded himself—he didn’t know her writing style. Women exaggerated. And she wasn’t fully fluent in English—often speaking as if translating from French. *Je me suis évadée*—that phrase alone suggested she might simply have fled boredom, a tiresome schedule. Likely enough—she seemed capricious, easily weary of the moment’s pleasures.

He chuckled at the idea of the van der Luydens whisking her off to Skuytercliff—again, and for no fixed time. The estate rarely opened its doors; even privileged guests received only chilly weekends. But Archer remembered seeing *Le Voyage de M. Perrichon* in Paris—the stubborn, tender devotion of a man rescuing another from a glacier. The van der Luydens had saved Madame Olenska from a fate nearly as frozen. And though many reasons drew him to her, beneath them all was the quiet, persistent urge to keep saving her.

He felt a pang of disappointment. And then, instantly, he recalled: the day before, he’d refused an invitation to spend Sunday with the Reggie Chiverses at Highbank, just below Skuytercliff. He’d grown tired of their noisy parties—coasting, ice-boating, sleigh rides, snow tramps, light flirtations, mild jokes. He’d just received a new batch of books from London and preferred a quiet day at home.

But now, he went straight to the club’s writing room, scribbled a telegram, and instructed the servant to send it immediately. He knew Mrs. Reggie wouldn’t mind a change of plans. There was always room in her generous house.
