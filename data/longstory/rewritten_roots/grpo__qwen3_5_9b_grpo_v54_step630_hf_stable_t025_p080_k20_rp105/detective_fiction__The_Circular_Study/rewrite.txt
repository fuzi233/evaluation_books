Detective Mr. Gryce was in a state of profound melancholy. He had reached that particular season of life when the natural spirits flag and enthusiasm requires a constant, artificial spur. Lately, there had been a distinct lack of special excitement, leaving him feeling dull and superannuated. He was even contemplating resigning his position on the force to retire to the little farm he had purchased in Westchester; yet this prospect did little to lift his mood, for action was a necessity for him, and the exercise of his mental faculties far more inspiring than any material advantage retirement might offer.

But fate was not destined to let him carry out this impulse just yet. At the height of his secret dissatisfaction, a telephone message arrived at Headquarters that roused the old man to something resembling his former vigor, injecting an unexpected interest into the closing hours of a gray autumn day. The call came from Carter's well-known drug store, reporting that a lady had just dispatched a boy from the street to announce a strange crime committed in a mansion on the corner. The boy, shy about displaying the money the woman had given him, admitted he did not know her, but his fright was evident enough to suggest his story was true. He could be found at Carter's, where he would remain until released by police order.

A *strange* crime! That single word struck Mr. Gryce with quiet insistence, causing him to forget his years in a sudden surge of curiosity regarding its meaning. Meanwhile, the men around him exchanged remarks about the house brought so unexpectedly to their notice. It was one of the few remaining landmarks of the preceding century, made conspicuous by the shops, clubhouses, and restaurants pressing against it on either side, serving as a marked spot for years even to those ignorant of its history.

Now, a crime had taken place within its walls! Mr. Gryce, whose ears caught the word "strange" ringing with persistent urgency, merely needed to catch the eye of the inspector in charge to receive an order to investigate. He started immediately, proceeding first to the drug store. There, he found the boy and took him along to the indicated house. On the way, he tried to draw information from the poor waif, but he could add nothing to the story already transmitted over the phone. The lad persisted in saying that a lady—careful to specify "lady," not "woman"—had approached him while he gazed at toys in a window, handed him a piece of money, and led him down the street to the drug store. There, she produced another coin, promising to add it to the one he already held if he would run to the telephone clerk with a message for the police. He wanted the money; when he reached for it, she instructed him to tell the clerk that a strange crime had been committed in the old house on ---- Street. This terrified him, and he began to slide away, but she caught him, shook him until his wits returned, and he ran into the store to deliver the message.

There was candor in the boy's tone, and Mr. Gryce was inclined to believe him. However, when asked to describe the lady, the lad revealed his observational powers were no better than those of his class. All he could offer was that she was a "stunner," wearing shiny clothes and jewels. Recognizing the boy's limitations the moment he came into view of the target building, Gryce ceased questioning him and focused entirely on the structure before them.

Nothing on the exterior bespoke crime or disturbance. A shut door, a clean stoop, and heavily curtained windows—some further shielded by closely drawn shades—spoke eloquently of inner quiet and domestic respectability. Its calm brick front, trimmed in brownstone, offered a pleasing contrast to the adjoining buildings jutting out on either side, alive with signs and humming with commerce.

"Some mistake," Gryce muttered to himself as the perfect calm reigning over the establishment struck him anew. But before he could decide he was the victim of a hoax, a movement occurred in the area under the stoop. An officer stepped out, his countenance expressing sufficient perplexity for Gryce to motion him back with a hurried inquiry: "Anything wrong? Any blood shed? All seems quiet here."

The officer, recognizing the veteran detective, touched his hat. "Can't get in," he replied. "I've rung all the bells. I'd think the house empty if I hadn't seen something like a stir in one of the windows overhead. Shall I try to make my way into the rear yard through a lower window of Knapp & Co.'s store next door?"

"Yes," Gryce commanded. "Take this boy with you. Lock him up in one of their offices, then break your way into this house by any means necessary. It ought to be easy enough from the backyard."

The officer nodded, took the boy by the arm, and in a trice disappeared with him into the adjoining store. Gryce remained in the area, soon besieged by a crowd of passers-by eager to add their curiosity to the trouble they had so quickly scented. The opening of the door from the inside speedily ended their importunities, allowing him to slip within. He found himself in a place of almost absolute quiet. Before him lay a basement hall leading to a kitchen, which, even at that moment, appeared trimmer than usual for a household doing much work, yet he saw nothing that bespoke tragedy or a break in the ordinary routine of life in houses of such size and pretension.

Satisfied that the scene he sought was not here, he followed the officer upstairs. As they emerged onto the parlor floor, the latter dropped some background information: "Mr. Raffner of the firm next door says the man who lives here is an odd sort of person whom nobody knows; a bookworm, I think they call him. He has occupied the house six months, yet they have never seen anyone about the premises but himself and a strange old servant as peculiar and uncommunicative as his master."

"I know," Gryce muttered. Everyone knew that this house, once the seat of one of New York's most aristocratic families, was currently inhabited by Mr. Adams, noted alike for his uncommon personal attractions, his wealth, and the uncongenial nature of his temperament, which precluded all association with his kind. It was this knowledge that gave zest to the investigation. To enter the house of such a man was an event in itself; to enter it on an errand of life and death—well, it was under the inspiration of such opportunities that life was reawakened in old veins, especially when those veins connected the heart and brain of a sagacious, albeit octogenarian, detective.

The hall in which they now found themselves was wide, old-fashioned, and sparsely furnished in the ancient manner typical of such time-honored structures. Two doors led into this hall, both standing open. Taking advantage of this, they entered the nearest, nearly opposite the top of the staircase they had just ascended, and found themselves in a room barren as a doctor's outer office. There was nothing here worth their attention, and they would have left as unceremoniously as they had entered had they not caught glimpses of richness promising an interior of uncommon elegance behind the half-drawn folds of a portière at the further end of the room.

Advancing through the doorway thus indicated, they took one look about them and stood appalled. Nothing in their experience, and they had both experienced much, had prepared them for the thrilling, solemn nature of what they were now called upon to contemplate.

Shall I attempt its description?

It was a room small and of circular shape, hung with strange tapestries relieved here and there by priceless curios. Although it was still daylight, it was lit by a jet of rose-colored light concentrated not on the rows and rows of books lining the lower portion of the room, nor on the one great picture which at another time might have drawn the eye, but upon the upturned face of a man lying on a bearskin rug. In his heart lay a dagger, and upon his breast rested a cross whose golden lines, sharply outlined against his long, dark, swathing garment, gave him the appearance of a saint prepared in some holy place for burial. Save for the dagger speaking of violent death and the face betraying an anguish for which Mr. Gryce, notwithstanding his lifelong experience, found no name, the expression answered to no sensation of fear, pain, or surprise, or any of the emotions usually visible on the countenances of those who have fallen under the unexpected stroke of an assassin.

Mysteries.

A suspended second, heavy with awe, stretched between the moment of discovery and Mr. Gryce's recovery. The gloom, the suffocating silence, and the setting's romantic antiquity struck a chord that unsettled even the hardened veteran. Before him lay the master of the house, a motionless figure stretched out as if awaiting burial, the sacred symbol pinned to his breast offering a grotesque contradiction to the earthly fury that had driven the dagger home. Such a tableau was enough to momentarily paralyze the mind of an old officer who had solved countless puzzles, yet never one quite like this.

The hesitation lasted only a heartbeat. Before Styles, the junior officer, could voice his own bewilderment, Mr. Gryce stepped forward, positioning himself beside the prostrate form.

There could be no doubt: the man was dead. Yet, professional instinct demanded verification. Gryce laid a hand upon the pulse and examined the eyes, whose gaze seemed to hold a silent reproach that required all his composure to meet. The body was still warm, confirming death beyond question. Convinced, he refrained from removing the dagger, instead studying the wound with clinical detachment. It was no common weapon—a curio from an oriental shop. While the exotic nature of the blade might suggest suicide, the angle of entry and the wound's placement ruled out self-infliction.

The clues were sparse. Despite the violence of the act, the well-appointed apartment bore no signs of a struggle. Aside from scattered rose petals, the room exuded a peace of quiet luxury. Even the massive central table, where the victim had stood moments before his fall, appeared untouched. At first glance, the articles upon it—pipes, pens, portraits, weights, Roman lamps, Venetian glass, rare porcelains, medals, rough metalwork, manuscripts, a scroll of music, and a pot of blooming flowers—stood in perfect order, as if the shock of the tragedy had not registered with his possessions.

Then Gryce noticed the oddity: a row of electric buttons. As his finger brushed one, the red glow of the overhead cage shifted instantly to a sickly green glare, bathing the room in ghastly hues. Gryce quickly pressed another, restoring a mild white radiance that dispelled the immediate horror.

"Childish tricks for a man of his age and station," Gryce muttered, though the thought faltered as he glanced at the dead man's face. The sharply cut features, the intense expression—they spoke of a mind too purposeful for childish folly. The lamp's mechanism, with its scientific apparatus, seemed less a toy and more a testament to the intellect of the deceased.

Shifting his focus to the walls, Gryce noted the tapestries and curios, finding little aid until he spotted a bent nail. It had been wrenched from the wall where a cross had hung; the cross now lay upon the victim's chest. The cord suspending it still clung to the wood, mingling its red threads with the scarlet stain of the wound. Who had torn down that symbol of peace? Not the victim; such a movement was impossible with a wound like that. The nail and the empty hook were positioned far from the body, implying another hand had pulled the cross down, placing it within the dying man's reach—a gesture of religious hope or mad remorse. Gryce dismissed the symbolism for now, too engrossed in the golden solitude of the crime scene.

Behind the table, half-hidden against the wall, hung a portrait of a young girl of striking, pathetic beauty. Dressed in the fashion of the Civil War era, she possessed an intellectual charm that mirrored the man lying prone. Though fair-skinned and he dark-haired, the likeness between them suggested a close kinship. Beneath the portrait rested a sword, a pair of epaulettes, and a medal awarded for valor.

"Mementoes that may prove useful," Gryce mused.

Moving on, he discovered a narrow curtain, dark in hue and blending seamlessly with the adjoining draperies, which had escaped his notice. It did not cover a window, for the apartment's windows were high near the ceiling; rather, it draped an opening to another room. Pushing it aside, he entered a narrow closet containing a narrow cot, a dresser, and a small table. The bed and dresser were immaculate, save for a silver-backed comb missing from the dresser and now lying on the floor at the far end. Nearby, a pearl-handled parasol stood on a small stand. These items indicated a woman's recent presence.

Confirmation came swiftly. A small black sequin lay on the threshold separating the closet from the study. As Gryce stooped to examine it, his eye caught a similar sparkle on the floor beyond, and another a few steps further, leading finally to the large central table.

The trail of these bright, falling drops affected him strangely. As a meticulous observer, he knew that sequins were strung on a thread; once broken, they fell one by one, allowing one to track a woman's path by the glittering debris. Whether it was the delicacy of the clue or the irony of fate using vanity as a trap, a smile played on Gryce's lips. Before inspecting the table further, he sought the break in the thread. Finding no snag in the doorways or furniture, his gaze landed on the cross. Its base featured intricate gold filigree. Could that have caught in the woman's dress as she tore the cross from the wall, initiating the cascade of sequins?

Hastening to the spot where the cross had hung, Gryce searched the floor beneath the rug covering the prostrate man. Amid the long hairs of the bearskin, he found another sequin, confirming that the woman in shimmering attire had stooped there before him.

Satisfied, he returned to the table for a thorough examination. His smile widened as he rubbed his finger across a specific spot near the inkstand and carefully replaced it exactly where he found it. Had he expected to find something concealed beneath? A detective's face rarely reveals its secrets.

Lost in thought before the table, Gryce was startled by a quick step behind him. Styles had returned from his patrol and stood ready to speak.

"What is it?" Gryce asked, turning sharply.

The officer pointed toward the staircase visible through the antechamber door. "Go up!" he indicated.

Gryce hesitated, glancing around the room with deep interest. Styles grew agitated, breaking his silence. "Come! I've found the guilty party. He's in a room upstairs."

"He?" Gryce was surprised by the pronoun.

"Yes; there can be no doubt. When you see him—but what is that? Is he coming down? I'm sure there's no one else in the house. Don't you hear footsteps, sir?"

Gryce nodded; someone was indeed descending.

"Let us retreat," Styles suggested. "Not because he's dangerous, but because you must see him before he sees you. He's a strange-acting man, sir. If he enters here, he'll surely incriminate himself. Where can we hide?"

Gryce recalled the small room he had just vacated and guided the officer inside. He drew the curtain shut, leaving only a small loophole. The approaching footsteps grew louder, accompanied by the intruder's quick, labored breathing.

"Does he know anyone has entered? Did he see you upstairs?" Gryce whispered.

Styles shook his head, pointing eagerly toward the opposite door. The awaited figure had just lifted the portière and stood fully in view.

Gryce and Styles stared. Was this the murderer? This pale, lean servant holding a tray with a single glass of water?

So astonished was Gryce that he looked to Styles for explanation. But the officer, hiding his own surprise at this peaceful apparition, urged patience. Together, they watched as the man advanced, stopped, cast a single look at the figure on the floor, and then dropped the glass with a low cry that instantly turned into a howl.

"Look at him! Look at him!" Styles urged in a hurried whisper. "Watch what he does now. You'll see a murderer at work."

Sure enough, the stranger lost all semblance of his former self, launching into a series of pantomimic actions that seemed to illustrate the crime. With passionate intensity, he contemplated the empty air before him, then, with one hand held back in a cramped position, plunged the other toward the table. He feigned snatching something, closed his hand, and executed a quick side-thrust at the empty air, which seemed to quiver in response to his vigor.

The reaction followed: the slow unclosing of his hand from an imaginary dagger, the tottering backward of his body, and the moment when, eyes wide, he seemed to contemplate the horror of his deed in terror. Succumbing to remorse or panic, he sank lower and lower until, with one arm still stretched out, he collapsed into an inert heap on the floor.

"It is what I saw him do upstairs," Styles murmured. "He has evidently been driven insane by his own act."

Mr. Gryce offered no reply. Here was a problem for which he found no precedent in all his experience.

The Mute Servitor.

As the man who had just mirrored the room's recent tragedy rose to his feet, a profound stillness settled over him. Gone was the violent fury that had animated him seconds prior; in its place stood a figure dazed and hollow-eyed. He bent to retrieve the shattered glass he had dropped, moving toward the exit when Mr. Gryce's voice cut through the silence.

"Wait, man! You needn't take that glass away. We must first understand how your master came to lie here dead."

The command was sharp, designed to jar the senses of any ordinary soul. Yet, this servant remained unnervingly unmoved, turning to pass. It was then that Styles stepped forward, laying a heavy hand upon the man's shoulder.

"Stop!" Styles commanded. "What do you mean by slipping away like this? Do you not hear the gentleman speaking to you?"

The plea finally broke through the man's trance. The glass slipped from his fingers, striking the floor with a final, shattering *clink* that mingled with a sound neither quite a cry nor a scream—a raw, animalistic moan. Having caught sight of the detectives restraining him, his haggard features twisted in terror. He tried to bolt, but Styles gripped him firmly, dragging the struggling figure back to the bearskin rug where the dead master lay.

Instantly, the transformation was complete. At the sight of the recumbent body, a hellish joy illuminated the butler's wandering eyes, distorting his mouth into a grotesque grin. Seeking no disguise for his satisfaction, he let out a low, thrilling laugh that echoed unholy through the study.

Mr. Gryce, though hardened, felt a surge of abhorrence at the irresponsible madness of the creature before him. He waited until the last faint tremors of that diabolical mirth faded into the high corners of the room. Then, locking his rare, glittering gaze with the servant's, he spoke sternly.

"There now! Speak! Who killed this man? You were in the house with him; you should know."

The butler's lips parted, and a string of strange, guttural noises poured forth. With his free hand—Styles holding the other—he frantically touched his ears and his lips, shaking his head violently.

There was only one conclusion. The man was deaf and dumb.

The revelation struck Styles hard. His grip loosened, and the man, suddenly free, retreated to his corner of the room. There, with renewed vigor, he reenacted the murder and the subsequent mourning, a performance that had previously held such a chilling suggestion for the investigators. He stood waiting, but now his eyes burned with that same infernal joy, making his presence in the chamber of death feel like a sacrilege.

Styles could not endure it. "Can't you speak?" he shouted. "Can't you hear?"

The man merely smiled, an expression of evil glee that Mr. Gryce felt compelled to cut short.

"Take him away!" Gryce ordered. "Examine him carefully for bloodstains. I am going upstairs to the room where you first saw him. He is too closely linked to this crime not to carry some trace of it with him."

For once, the seasoned detective found himself at a loss. No marks were found on the old servant, nor did their search of the upper rooms reveal any signs linking this sole remaining occupant to the tragedy within. Mr. Gryce grew thoughtful, returning to a meticulous examination of the two rooms, hoping to find the elusive clues hidden in plain sight.

The results were meager. Just as he began to lose himself in contemplation of the victim's upturned face—the fixed mouth and haunting expression telling a silent story of suffering and resolve—a cry rang out from the dim recesses above.

"Remember Evelyn!"

The words formed with startling clarity, cutting through the silence like a knife. *Remember Evelyn!* Who was Evelyn? And who spoke, in a house already ransacked for other occupants? The voice seemed to emanate from the roof. Sure enough, as Mr. Gryce looked upward, he saw an English starling swinging in a cage strung near the top of a window. Recognizing the attention, the bird craned its neck and shrieked again, with fiercer insistence:

"Remember Evelyn!"

It was the final uncanny touch in a series of bizarre experiences. A nightmare-like sensation seized Mr. Gryce as he leaned forward on the study table to get a better view of the bird. Suddenly, without warning, the white light flooding the room shifted to a sickly green. He realized he had inadvertently pressed a button, activating another slide in the curious lamp overhead.

Annoyed by the shifting hues—a problem he was too absorbed in the case to solve—he moved away from the table, intending to leave the room. As he did, he heard Styles's voice rising from the adjoining antechamber, where he was keeping guard over the butler.

"Shall I let him go, Mr. Gryce? He seems very uneasy; not dangerous, you know, but anxious. As if he had forgotten something or recalled some unfulfilled duty."

"Yes, let him go," Gryce replied quickly. "Only watch and follow him. Every movement he makes is of interest. Unconsciously, he may be giving us invaluable clues."

He approached the door to observe the man's next move.

"Remember Evelyn!" the cry rang out again from above as the detective passed between the curtains. Irresistibly, he looked back and up. Was this appeal addressed to him, or to the man on the floor whose ears were forever closed? It might be a triviality, or it might hold the very secret of the tragedy. Yet, at this juncture, he could afford no distraction.

The old butler emerged from the front hall, having hurried there upon his release. In one hand he carried his master's hat; in the other, the umbrella. Not knowing what this new gesture meant, Mr. Gryce paused, waiting. Seeing the detective's stillness, the mute servant, his face once again bearing the respectful immobility of a trained professional, handed over the articles. Then, with the air of one performing an expected duty, he retreated silently to his old place in the antechamber, sitting down and almost immediately lapsing back into his dazed stupor.

"Hmph! Mind quite lost, memory uncertain, testimony valueless," Gryce muttered to himself as he replaced the hat and umbrella on the hall rack. "Has he been driven to this state by the tragedy that just occurred, or is his present insanity its precursor and cause?" He might have found an answer in his own mind had the fitful clanging of the front door bell—which had hitherto signaled the impatience of the curious crowd outside—not been abruptly silenced by an authoritative knock.

The coroner, or some equally important figure, was at hand. The detective's golden hour had ended.

Detective Mr. Gryce found himself in a predicament more formidable than any he had faced in years. The victim was a solitary man, living alone save for a mute man-of-all-work, in a district where neighbors were nonexistent and friends, it now appeared, were few and far between. Hours had passed since the news of the death spread, yet the door remained silent; no inquisitive neighbor, no concerned friend had knocked. This silence was peculiar, given that the deceased had been a marked figure in the quarter for months, but then, everything about Mr. Adams was peculiar, and his peculiar mode of living precluded the ordinary social associations of his class.

This lack of external witnesses did not dampen Mr. Gryce's interest; rather, it sharpened it. With a profound sense of relief, he watched the procession of reporters, medical experts, and officials file out of the front door just before midnight, leaving him once again, albeit temporarily, the sole master of the scene.

Before claiming his much-needed rest, two matters demanded resolution. The first required immediate attention. Passing a chair in the hall where a small boy sat dozing, Gryce roused him.

"Come, Jake, it's time to wake up. I need you to take me to the exact spot where that lady ran across you today."

The boy, half-dead with sleep, searched frantically for his hat. "I'd like to see my mother first," he pleaded, his voice thick with exhaustion. "She must be devastated. I've never stayed away this long."

"Your mother knows exactly where you are," Gryce replied smoothly. "I sent a message hours ago. She gave a glowing report; she says you're an obedient lad who never tells a lie."

"She's a good mother," the boy declared warmly, his hand falling onto his cap as he pulled it down over his ears. "I'd be just as bad as my father was if I didn't treat her right."

"I'm ready," he said.

Gryce led the way into the cool night air. The city was quiet, save for pockets of lingering activity. They moved into one of these thoroughfares and paused before a darkened shop window. Jake pointed out two stuffed frogs engaged in a miniature sword fight—the very spectacle he had been watching when the lady approached.

Gryce studied the boy more intently than the frogs, though the boy likely believed his gaze was fixed entirely on the wooden duelists.

"Was she a pretty lady?" Gryce asked.

The boy scratched his head, perplexed. "She made me quite afraid. She had splendid clothes—gorgeous!" he exclaimed, as if the answer settled the matter.

"And she was young, carrying a bunch of flowers, looking troubled? What! Not young, no flowers, and not even anxious or trembling?"

The boy shook his head, then stopped, looking nonplussed. "I think she was what you'd call troubled. But she wasn't crying. When she spoke, she gripped my arm harder than she used her voice. She just dragged me to the drugstore. If she hadn't paid me first, I would have wriggled away, but I like money, sir; I don't get too much of it."

Gryce moved on, muttering to himself, "Not young. Some old flame of Mr. Adams, then. They're apt to be dangerous—far more dangerous than the young ones."

At the drugstore, he halted. "Show me where she stood while you went in."

Jake indicated the precise spot with eager precision. "And was she there when you came out?"

"Oh, no, sir. She left while I was inside."

"Did you see her go? Did she walk up the street or down?"

"I kept one eye on her, sir. I was terrified she'd follow me back into the shop, and my arm was too sore to want her to hold on again. So when she started to leave, I stepped closer and saw her move toward the curb, holding up her hand. But it wasn't a car; none came by for several minutes."

The furrow between Gryce's brows smoothed out. "Then it was a cabman or a hack-driver she hailed. Did you see any empty coaches nearby?"

The boy had reached the limit of his observation. No further questioning could extract more, so Gryce handed him over to a duty assistant and returned to the ill-omened house where the tragedy had unfolded.

"Anyone waiting for me?" he asked Styles, who opened the door.

"Yes, sir. A young man named Hines. Says he's an electrician."

"That's the one I want. Where is he?"

"In the parlor, sir."

"Good. I'll see him. But keep everyone else out. Anyone upstairs?"

"No, sir. All gone. Shall I go up or stay here?"

"You'd better go up. I'll watch the door."

Styles nodded and vanished up the stairs. Gryce proceeded to the parlor. A dapper young man with an intelligent eye rose to greet him. "You sent for me," he said.

Gryce nodded, asked a few brief questions, and, satisfied with the answers, led the way into Mr. Adams's study. The body had been moved to an upper room. As they entered, a mild light from a candle placed on a small side table near the door illuminated the space. However, once inside, Gryce walked to the large central table, placed his hand on one of the buttons, and asked Hines to extinguish the candle.

The room plunged into total darkness. Then, with a sudden burst, a marvelous glow of deep violet flooded the space. Both men turned to face each other, their expressions a mixture of inquiry and bewilderment at such a theatrical effect.

"That is but one slide," Gryce remarked. "Press another, and the color changes to pink. This one produces green, this one white, and this one a bilious yellow—not becoming to either of us, I assure you. Now, examine the connection. Is there anything peculiar about it?"

Hines immediately set to work. Beyond the fact that the entire contrivance was the work of an amateur, he found nothing strange, except for its flawless operation.

Gryce expressed his disappointment. "He made it himself, then?"

"Undoubtedly, or someone equally unacquainted with modern wiring methods."

"Look at these books on the shelf. Could an amateur with sufficient knowledge rig up such an arrangement?"

Hines glanced at the shelf without removing the volumes. "A man with a deft hand and a scientific mind could do all this and more using these texts. It requires aptitude."

"Then I'm afraid Mr. Adams possessed that aptitude," Gryce responded dryly. His tone betrayed a deeper concern. "A man with a turn for mechanical contrivances often wastes time and money on useless toys fit only for children. Look at that birdcage. Perched high beyond anyone's reach without a ladder, its utility must depend on a mechanism to raise and lower it. Where is that mechanism? Can you find it?"

The expert located it after some searching. Hidden among the tapestry on the wall was another button. When pressed, it disengaged a latch, and the cage gradually lowered within Gryce's grasp.

"We shall not send this poor bird aloft again," Hines said, detaching the cage. "An English starling is rare here. Listen—he is about to speak."

The sharp-eyed bird, perhaps warned by the detective's emphatic gesture that silence was preferable to his usual cry of "Remember Evelyn," fluttered briefly and then settled into a doze, real or feigned, under the fascinated gaze of the old gentleman holding him. Gryce placed the cage on the floor and, idly or because the play amused him, pressed another button he had previously ignored. He glanced around to see the resulting color.

The yellow glare remained. The previous investigation had likely disarranged the wires. Gryce shrugged and began to move away when he suddenly made a hurried gesture, drawing Hines's attention to something neither had anticipated. The opening leading to the antechamber—the sole exit from the room—was slowly closing. From a yard's breadth to a foot, from a foot to an inch, from an inch to nothing.

"Well, that is certainly the contrivance of a lazy man," Hines laughed nervously. "Seated in his chair, he can close his door at will. No shouting at a deaf servant, no awkward stumbling over rugs. I don't know but I approve of this convenience, only—" He caught the serious expression on Gryce's face. "—the slide seems of a somewhat curious construction. It is not made of wood, as any sensible door should be, but of—"

"Steel," Gryce finished in an odd tone. "This is the strangest thing yet. It begins to look as if Mr. Adams was obsessed with electrical contrivances."

"And as if we are prisoners here," Hines added. "I see no means to draw this slide back."

"Oh, there is another button for that, of course," Gryce remarked casually.

They failed to find it.

"If you don't object," Gryce said after five minutes of futile searching, "I will turn on a more cheerful light. Yellow does not suit the occasion."

"Give us rose," Hines urged. "Unless you have someone on the other side of this steel plate, we seem likely to remain here until morning."

"There is a man upstairs whom we may perhaps make hear, but what does this contrivance portend? It has a serious look, especially when you consider that every window in these two rooms has been bricked up almost under the roof."

"Yes; a very strange look. But before we engage in speculation, I need fresh air. I cannot think while confined. My brain won't work."

Meanwhile, Gryce examined the massive steel plate barring their exit. He discovered it had been crafted at great expense to fit the curve of the walls perfectly. This discovery deepened his thoughts; he eyed the smooth steel with marked distrust.

"Mr. Adams carried his taste for the mechanical to great extremes," he remarked to the increasingly uneasy Hines. "This slide is carefully fitted and, if I am not mistaken, will withstand considerable battering before we are released."

"I wish his interest in electricity had led him to attach something as simple as a bell."

"True. We have encountered no bell."

"It would have seemed too ordinary to please him."

"Besides, his only servant was deaf."

"Try the effect of a blow—a quick strike with this silver-mounted alpenstock. Someone should hear and come to our aid."

"I will try my whistle first; it will be better understood."

Though Gryce whistled and struck resounding knocks against the barrier, it was an hour before he drew Styles's attention, and five hours before an opening large enough for escape could be pried into the wall. The barrier of steel was fixed with such firmness that it seemed to defy all attempts at release.

The ordeal had only sharpened Mr. Gryce's resolve. The mystery of the circular study demanded answers, and the detention of the butler, Bartow, was merely the first hurdle in a labyrinth he intended to map entirely. Upon returning to Headquarters, where his colleagues undoubtedly fretted over his prolonged absence, his first inquiry was sharp and direct: had Bartow regained his faculties during the night?

The reply was a dampening of spirits. The mute remained unchanged, and the asylum specialist summoned to evaluate him had offered a grim prognosis regarding any immediate recovery. Mr. Gryce's face fell into a somber mask. He summoned the officer who had overseen the arrest and asked how the silent man had behaved upon realizing his confinement.

The officer's report was brief but vivid.

"Surprised, sir," the man replied. "He shook his head and performed some peculiar gestures, then launched into his pantomime. It's quite a show, sir. Poor fellow, he keeps holding his right hand back, like this."

Mr. Gryce observed the gesture closely. It mirrored the motion Bartow had made the moment he sensed an audience. The meaning was elusive, yet the mechanics were clear: the mute, clearly right-handed, repeated the movement as if trying to signal a fact that might exonerate him.

"Does he brood? Is his countenance one of terror or rage?"

"It fluctuates, sir. One moment he looks ready to drop off to sleep; the next, he erupts in fury, shaking his head and battering the walls. It's an unsettling sight. He requires constant vigilance."

"Leave him be, but document every shift in his demeanor. His testimony may lack legal weight, yet there is significance in every twitch he makes. Tomorrow, I shall visit him personally."

As the officer departed, Mr. Gryce sat in silence, allowing his thoughts to settle. He reached into his pocket and produced a small packet, emptying five tiny spangles onto his desk. He studied them with intense focus. It was a habit of his; examining trivial objects helped anchor his wandering mind. After a moment of doubtful contemplation, he swept the sequins back into the envelope, glanced at his watch, and moved swiftly to the outer office. He paused before a queue of waiting men, beckoned to a young man whose keen observation of his movements had not gone unnoticed, and led him back to his private room.

"You require a hand in this affair?" Gryce asked, the tone interrogative as the door closed.

"Oh, sir—" the young man began, his face flushing with a glow that highlighted his features, "I do not presume—

"Enough!" Gryce interrupted. "You have spent six months here with no occasion to demonstrate exceptional aptitude. Now, I intend to test your capabilities with a genuine challenge. Can you rise to it, Sweetwater? Do you know this city well enough to locate a needle in a haystack of this magnitude?"

"I would at least like to attempt it," Sweetwater replied eagerly. "Success here would be a greater accolade than living in New York all my life. I have been longing for such an opportunity. Let me assure you, I shall proceed with caution, driven by gratitude."

"We shall see," the veteran detective murmured. "If you crave difficulty, you shall have it in abundance. Indeed, I am asking for the impossible. We are seeking a woman of whom we know nothing but that she wore a dress heavily encrusted with black spangles and carried a parasol during her visit to Mr. Adams, prior to the murder. I can provide you with a view of that parasol before you depart. She came from an unknown origin and vanished into the ether—that is your mission. You are not the sole operative assigned to this task, which is nearly hopeless unless she steps forward voluntarily. I would have despaired entirely were it not for one small fact, which I shall now share as my confidential directive. Just as she was about to vanish from the only eye watching her, she approached the curbstone before Hudson's fruit store on Fourteenth Street and raised her right hand, precisely so. It is a slender clue, but it is all I possess, aside from these five spangles recovered from her dress and my firm conviction that she does not belong to the class of women frequenting police precincts. Yet, do not let this conviction constrain you. Convictions are often impediments, hindering rather than inspiring."

Sweetwater, who might have found the siren's song less alluring, listened with delight and responded with a frank smile.

"I shall do my utmost, sir, but please do not show me the parasol; merely describe it. I would not wish the others to mock me if I fail; I prefer to work quietly and avoid raising false hopes."

"One of those delicate, frivolous affairs made of gray chiffon, with a pearl handle and bows of pink ribbon," Gryce explained. "I doubt it was ever used before. Given the value women place on such ephemera, it could only have been abandoned under the pressure of extraordinary emotion or fear. There was no name on it."

"Nor the maker's mark?"

Gryce anticipated the question and was pleased not to be caught off guard.

"No; that would have aided us too readily."

"And the time she was seen at Hudson's?"

"Half-past four; the exact moment the telephone message arrived."

"Understood, sir. It is the most arduous task I have ever undertaken, but that does not deter me. When shall I report back?"

"Only when you have information to convey. Ah, wait. I harbor a suspicion that this woman's first name is Evelyn. However, consider it merely a hunch."

"As you wish, sir," Sweetwater said, projecting an air of confidence before vanishing.

Mr. Gryce did not share the young man's buoyancy. The fog surrounding the case remained impenetrable. His next avenue of hope lay in the published accounts of the crime, now circulating widely. A man of Mr. Adams's wealth and refinement must have had numerous acquaintances, and the shocking news of his sudden death would naturally draw them forth, especially given the open display of his assets. Yet, as if fate conspired to thwart this sagacious old man, the day passed without a single claimant for the fortune or a friend arriving to lift the veil of secrecy shrouding the deceased's life. His banker and lawyer appeared, revealing only that his financial affairs were orderly and that he seemed to have no family or kin. Even his landlord contributed little. He had first heard of Adams through a deceased Philadelphia attorney who vouched for his client's respectability. When they met, the landlord was struck by the tenant's ascetic appearance and reserved intelligence. Though admirable, Adams remained distant, paying rent with exactitude but offering no warmth to social overtures. The landlord viewed him as a recline scholar, absorbed in experiments conducted with the aid of his deaf-mute servant. When questioned about the servant, the landlord admitted knowing nothing of his character or loyalty, having seen him only twice. Thus, this path was blocked.

Gryce toured the room where Adams had died, staring in awe at the massive steel plate sealing the doorway and the high windows admitting only faint beams of sunlight. He noted where lath and plaster had been inserted to reinforce the barrier, yet he could discern no purpose for such an extravagant expenditure. The lamp, with its erratic illumination, added to the astonishment. He had been unaware of these arrangements until now, having previously been received in the parlor where such oddities were invisible. He was not shown the starling; that talkative bird had been transported to Headquarters for his exclusive amusement. Other inquiries yielded nothing. No leads emerged from the pension office or G.A.R. posts regarding the insignia on the wall, nor was the portrait's artist a recognized figure in New York. Every drawer in the study had been ransacked, finding no will or business documents. It was as if the man had sought to erase his identity or had transcended interest in humanity beyond the walls of his prison.

Late in the afternoon, reports trickled in from tradesmen. They all spoke of the peculiarities of Adams and his mute servant, describing them as hermits who denied themselves nothing but isolation. The master was never seen outside; the servant handled purchases, communicating with strange, significant gestures. He possessed a remarkable power of expression, rarely making mistakes and intolerant of deceit. While his sanity was unquestioned until the tragedy, several witnesses testified to a recent change—a subdued excitement, testiness, and a burning grievance held in check. Mr. Kelly, a grocer, suggested the servant acted like a man ready to vent his spite on someone specific. Without the tragedy, such nervousness might have been excused, but the violent end of the master cast a shadow of malevolence over the servant's glances.

Yet, this was not the decisive evidence the law required. Gryce was preparing to dismiss the day as a failure when Sweetwater reappeared. The spark of life returned to Gryce's eyes.

"What!" he exclaimed. "You have not found her?"

Sweetwater smiled. "Do not ask me, sir, not yet. I have come to inquire if there is any reason I should not borrow that parasol for an hour. I will return it. I merely wish to conduct a specific test."

"What test, my boy? May I ask?"

"Please excuse me, sir; I have little time before respectable businesses close, and this test must be conducted in a respectable establishment."

"Then you shall not be hindered. Wait here; I will bring the parasol. There! Return it soon, my boy. I no longer possess the patience I once had."

"An hour, sir; give me an hour, and then—"

The closing of the door cut off his sentence.

That hour stretched long for Gryce, mercifully shortened only by Sweetwater's return in a state of heightened excitement. He held the parasol in his hand.

"My test failed," Sweetwater admitted, "but the parasol has brought me luck nonetheless. I have found the lady, sir, and—"

He drew a deep breath before continuing.

"And she is exactly as I described," the detective began, though he corrected himself. "A respectable person in a respectable house."

"Yes, sir; very respectable, more so than I anticipated. Quite a lady, sir. Not young, but—"

"Her name, boy. Is it—Evelyn?"

Sweetwater shook his head with a naive expression.

"I cannot say, sir. Indeed, I lacked the courage to ask. She is here—"

"Here!" Gryce took a hurried step toward the door, then turned gravely back. "I can restrain myself. If she is here, she will not leave until I have seen her. Are you certain you have not made a mistake? That she is the woman we seek, the one in Mr. Adams's house who sent the warning?"

"Will you hear my story, sir? It will take but a moment. Then you can judge."

"Your story? It must be a remarkable one. How did you locate this woman so quickly? By using the clue I provided?"

Again, Sweetwater's face softened with innocence.

"I am sorry, sir; but I was too egotistical to follow my own intuition. Hunting up every hack driver in the city would have taken too long, and I could not be certain she used public transport. No, sir; I devised another method. You showed me those spangles. They were part of a rich trimming recently in vogue, expensive and worn chiefly by women of means, sold only in select shops. I had noticed dresses with such trimmings in certain windows and on certain ladies; before you showed me the spangles from the study, I could have described their arrangement perfectly. They were sewn onto black net in scrolls or wreaths, conspicuous due to their brilliance. Any break in the pattern was obvious, especially if worn over a dark color. Recalling that these spangles likely fell from a front breadth, where their loss would be noticed by both the wearer and others, I reasoned: 'What will she do when she discovers her dress thus disfigured?' The answer was immediate: 'If she is the lady Mr. Gryce seeks, she will attempt to repair the damage, especially after a crime scene. But where can she obtain matching spangles? From an extra piece of net. Unlikely to have one on hand, she will be forced to buy a strip. Since only a few are missing, she will purchase the smallest available piece.' This was my lead. I visited the leading stores on Broadway, Twenty-third Street, and Sixth Avenue, gaining the interest of clerks who promised to note any lady purchasing such a fragment. I stationed myself at Arnold's Emporium. Why Arnold's? I do not know; perhaps good fortune guided me. Before afternoon ended, I caught a meaningful glance from a clerk behind the counter. Advancing, I saw him roll a small package to a very pretty, rosy young girl who walked away immediately. 'For one of our leading customers,' he whispered as I approached. Seeing her enter the basement door, I rang the bell above. I bit my lip to suppress my excitement; such an effort could easily end in disappointment. But fortune smiled upon me. The maid who answered was the same merry-eyed lass I had seen leaving the store, holding the identical parcel—the link between the imposing house and the strange murder. I concealed my interest, mastering myself before addressing her. You foresee my question: 'Has your mistress lost a parasol? One has been found...' I stopped, sensing by her look that she had indeed suffered such a loss. 'I will bring it,' I cried. 'If it is hers, all is well,' and I bounded down the steps.

If she recognized the parasol, I could boast of my success. Praying for your loan of the article, I returned and rang the bell again. The same girl answered. Fortune favored me today. "Ah!" The detective leaned forward, his interest piqued instantly. He drew a chair close to her and sat. "You were there!" he repeated, his eyes searching hers. "And when? I do not presume to ask your purpose."

"But I must explain my purpose before I find myself at a disadvantage," she replied, her tone grim and decisive. "Not that I relish displaying my own weakness, but I recognize the exigencies of the occasion. This was no affair of my immediate neighbor; it did not seek me. I sought it, sir, and in this manner. I wish I had gone to Jericho first; it would have cost more and required longer travel, but it would have spared me this humiliation and potential publicity. I have demonstrated my aptitude for detective work and received your approval on several occasions; yet my head was not turned by these accolades—at least, I thought it wasn't—and I was sincere in my determination to stick to my own profession, refusing to be lured by past praise into exercising gifts that had never brought me happiness. Thinking I had succeeded, I congratulated myself on my precautions until that parasol was thrust in my face, revealing that you, if no one else, knew that Amelia Butterworth had been in Mr. Adams's room of death prior to yourself. I saw these tokens of a woman's presence, heard the boy's account of the well-dressed lady who sent him to the drugstore with a message for the police, and drew the conclusion—though I admit it to you—that it was this woman who wielded the assassin's dagger, not the deaf-and-dumb butler who has borne the blame until now. Therefore, I was eager to find her, little realizing the result of my efforts or that I would have to offer my most humble apologies."

"Do not apologize to me. I had no business to be there, or at least, to leave those five spangles on Mr. Adams's miserable floor. I was merely passing by the house; and had I been the woman I once was—a woman who had never dipped into a mystery—I would have continued on my way instead of turning aside. Sir, it is a curious sensation to find oneself, however innocent, regarded by a whole city as the cause or motive of a terrible murder, especially when one has spent time studying crime and pursuing criminals. I confess I do not enjoy the experience. But I brought it upon myself. If I had not been so curious... But it was not curiosity I felt. I will never own that I am subject to mere curiosity; it was the look on the young man's face. But I forget myself. I am rambling when I ought to be telling a consecutive tale. Not my usual habit, sir; you know this; but I am not quite myself at this moment. I declare I am more upset by this discovery of my indiscretion than I was by Mr. Trohm's declaration of affection in Lost Man's Lane! Give me time, Mr. Gryce; in a few minutes I will be more coherent."

"I am giving you time," he returned with one of his lowest bows. "The half-dozen questions I long to ask have not yet left my lips, and I sit here, as you must acknowledge, a monument of patience."

"So you thought this deed perpetrated by an outsider," she suddenly interjected. "Most of the journals—I read them very carefully this morning—ascribed the crime to the man you have mentioned. And there seems to be good reason for doing so. The case is not simple, Mr. Gryce; it has complications—I recognized that at once, and that is why—but I won't waste another moment on apologies. You have a right to any little fact I may have picked up in my unfortunate visit, and there is one which I failed to find included in any account of the murder. Mr. Adams had other visitors besides myself in those few fatal minutes preceding his death. A young man and woman were with him. I saw them come out of the house. It was at the moment I was passing..."

"Tell your story more simply, Miss Butterworth. What first drew your attention to the house?"

"There! That is the second time you have had to remind me to be direct. You will not have to do so again, Mr. Gryce. To begin, then, I noticed the house because I always notice it. I never pass it without giving a thought to its ancient history and speculating on its present inmates. When, therefore, I found myself in front of it yesterday afternoon on my way to the art exhibition, I naturally looked up, and—whether by providence or not, I cannot say—it was precisely at that instant the inner door of the vestibule burst open, and a young man appeared in the hall, carrying a young woman in his arms. He seemed in a state of intense excitement, and she in a dead faint; but before they attracted the attention of the crowd, he had placed her on her feet, taken her on his arm, and dragged her down the stoop into the throng of passers-by, among whom they presently disappeared. I, as you may believe, stood rooted to the ground in astonishment, endeavoring to see their direction, and lingering long enough to take a peep into the time-honored interior of this old house, which had been left open by the young man's forgetfulness. As I did so, I heard a cry from within. It was muffled and remote, but unmistakably one of terror and anguish. Led by an impulse I may live to regret, as it seems likely to plunge me into much unpleasantness, I rushed up the stoop and went in, shutting the door behind me lest others should follow.

"So far, I had acted solely from instinct; but once in that semi-dark hall, I paused to ask what business I had there and what excuse I should give if I encountered the occupants. But a repetition of the cry, coming as I swear from the farthest room on the parlor floor, together with a sharp remembrance of the wandering eye and drawn countenance of the young man who had staggered hence a moment before, with an almost-fainting woman in his arms, drew me on in spite of my feminine instincts. Before I knew it, I was in the circular study before the prostrate form of a seemingly dying man. He lay as you probably found him later, with the cross on his breast and a dagger in his heart; but his right hand was trembling, and when I stooped to lift his head, he gave a shudder and then settled into eternal stillness. I, a stranger from the street, had witnessed his last breath while the young man who had gone out..."

"Can you describe him? Did you encounter him close enough for recognition?"

"Yes, I think I would know him again. I can at least describe his appearance. He wore a checked suit, very natty, and was more than usually tall and fine-looking. But his chief peculiarity lay in his expression. I never saw on any face, no, not on the stage at the climax of the most heart-rending tragedy, a greater accumulation of mortal passion struggling with the imperative necessity for restraint. The young girl whose blond head lay on his shoulder looked like a saint in the clutch of a demon. She had seen death, but he... But I prefer not to be the interpreter of that expressive countenance. It was lost to my view almost immediately, and probably calmed itself in the face of the throng he entered, or we would be hearing about him today. The girl seemed devoid of almost all feeling. I should not remember her."

"And was that all? Did you just look at that recumbent man and vanish? Didn't you encounter the butler? Haven't you some definite knowledge to impart regarding him which will settle his innocence or fix his guilt?"

"I know no more about him than you do, sir, except that he was not in the room by the time I reached it, and did not come into it during my presence there. Yet it was his cry that led me to the spot; or do you think it was that of the bird I afterward heard shouting and screaming in the cage over the dead man's head?"

"It might have been the bird," admitted Mr. Gryce. "Its call is very clear, and it seems strangely intelligent. What was it saying while you stood there?"

"Something about Eva. 'Lovely Eva, maddening Eva! I love Eva! Eva! Eva!'"

"Eva? Wasn't it 'Evelyn? Poor Evelyn?'"

"No, it was Eva. I thought he might mean the girl I had just seen carried out. It was an unpleasant experience, hearing this bird shriek out these cries in the face of the man lying dead at my feet."

"Miss Butterworth, you didn't simply stand over that man. You knelt down and looked in his face."

"I acknowledge it, and caught my dress in the filagree of the cross. Naturally, I would not stand stock still with a man drawing his last breath under my eye."

"And what else did you do? You went to the table..."

"Yes, I went to the table."

"And moved the inkstand?"

"Yes, I moved the inkstand, but very carefully, sir, very carefully."

"Not so carefully but that I could see where it had been sitting before you took it up: the square made by its base in the dust of the table did not coincide with the place afterwards occupied by it."

"Ah, that comes from your having on your glasses and I not. I endeavored to set it down in the precise place from which I lifted it."

"Why did you take it up at all? What were you looking for?"

"For clews, Mr. Gryce. You must forgive me, but I was seeking for clews. I moved several things. I was hunting for the line of writing which ought to explain this murder."

"The line of writing?"

"Yes. I have not told you what the young girl said as she slipped with her companion into the crowd."

"No; you have spoken of no words. Have you any such clew as that? Miss Butterworth, you are fortunate, very fortunate."

Mr. Gryce's look and gesture were eloquent, but Miss Butterworth, with an access of dignity, quietly remarked:

"I was not to blame for being in the way when they passed, nor could I help hearing what she said."

"And what was it, madam? Did she mention a paper?"

"Yes, she cried in what I now remember to have been a tone of affright: 'You have left that line of writing behind!' I did not attach much importance to these words then, but when I came upon the dying man, so evidently the victim of murder, I recalled what his late visitor had said and looked about for this piece of writing."

"And did you find it, Miss Butterworth? I am ready, as you see, for any revelation you may now make."

"For one which would reflect dishonor on me? If I had found any paper explaining this tragedy, I should have felt bound to have called the attention of the police to it. I did notify them of the crime itself."

"Yes, madam; and we are obliged to you; but how about your silence in regard to the fact of two persons having left that house immediately upon, or just preceding, the death of its master?"

"I reserved that bit of information. I waited to see if the police would not get wind of these people without my help. I sincerely wished to keep my name out of this inquiry. Yet I feel a decided relief now that I have made my confession. I never could have rested properly after seeing so much, and..."

"Well?"

"Thinking my own thoughts in regard to what I saw, if I had found myself compelled to bridle my tongue while false scents were being followed and delicate clews overlooked or discarded without proper attention. I regard this murder as offering the most difficult problem that has ever come in my way, and, therefore..."

"Yes, madam."

"I cannot but wonder if an opportunity has been afforded me for retrieving myself in your eyes. I do not care for the opinion of anyone else as to my ability or discretion; but I should like to make you forget my last despicable failure in Lost Man's Lane. It is a sore remembrance to me, Mr. Gryce, which nothing but a fresh success can make me forget."

"Madam, I understand you. You have formulated some theory. You consider the young man with the tell-tale face guilty of Mr. Adams's death. Well, it is very possible. I never thought the butler was rehearsing a crime he had himself committed."

"Do you know who the young man is I saw leaving that house so hurriedly?"

"Not the least in the world. You are the first to bring him to my attention."

Miss Butterworth was a creature of rigid discipline, raised in a school where stillness was virtue and movement was calculated. When she sat, she sat like a statue; when she rose, she moved with the firm, swift economy of a machine. Fidgeting was a sin she had spent her life condemning in others. Yet, trapped in the silence following her interview, the dam broke. The sensible woman within her struggled to suppress the outward tremors of anxiety she usually repressed, her hands betraying the unease her mind tried to hide.

The relief came only when Mr. Gryce returned, his face breaking into a smile that was almost gay.

"Well, that is seen to!" he declared. "All that remains is to wait for the result. Madam, do you harbor any further thoughts? If so, I shall be delighted to hear them."

Her composure snapped back into place instantly.

"You would?" she echoed, her gaze searching his face with a newfound skepticism. "I would prefer to verify the worth of the single idea I have offered before risking another. Let us hold a conference, compare notes. For instance, explain to me why neither of us considers Bartow the guilty party."

"I believed we had drained that well dry," Gryce replied smoothly. "Your suspicions stemmed from the young couple fleeing the house. My own... well, Madam, I must confess to you that the mute's gestures suggest he is rehearsing a scene he witnessed, not one he committed. I see no reason to doubt this."

"I felt the same pull," she admitted. "Even without my other suspicions, his manner spoke of observation, not action. Besides, it is more natural for a man to be driven mad by another's cruelty than by his own."

"Only if he loved the victim," Gryce countered.

"But did not Bartow love Mr. Adams?"

"He does not mourn him."

"Yet he is no longer master of his emotions."

"True. But if we use his actions as a clue, we must accept them wholly. We believe his pantomime is a literal copy of what he saw. So, why does his face light up with infernal joy once the performance ends? It suggests he rejoices in the deed itself."

"Perhaps it is still a copy; the murderer may have smiled. But no," Miss Butterworth insisted, her voice tightening. "There was no joy in the face of the youth I saw rushing away. Quite the opposite. Mr. Gryce, we are in deep water. I feel nearly submerged."

"Keep your head above, Madam. Every flood recedes. If you drown, what becomes of me?"

"You are disposed to humor, Mr. Gryce. A good sign. You never jest when perplexed. Somewhere, you must see the light."

"Let us proceed. Illumination often comes from the most unexpected quarter."

"Very well. Let us file the old man's joy among the mysteries to be solved later. Have you considered him an accomplice?"

"Certainly. But that hypothesis shares the fatal flaw that made him the motive for the murder. One is not driven insane by an expected horror; it requires a shock. He was not expecting his master's death."

"Agreed. That is settled. Bartow was an innocent witness, having nothing to fear, and thus his mimicry should be exact."

"Continue, Madam. Nothing but profit can come from an argument led by Miss Butterworth."

The detective's tone was grave, his manner impeccable. Miss Butterworth, ever vigilant for sarcasm, bristled slightly, though she gave no other sign of displeasure.

"Let us recall his precise movements," she began. "He must have surprised the attacker from the study doorway, viewing the assault over his master's shoulder."

"In other words, directly in front of him. What was his first move?"

"As recorded, he raises his right arm, stretching it behind him while leaning forward to grasp an imaginary dagger. What does that signify?"

"I find it difficult to say. But I did not see that. When I arrived, he was thrusting with his *left* hand across his own body—a vicious motion. That is a critical detail, Mr. Gryce."

"Yes, particularly since the doctors confirm Mr. Adams was struck down by a left-handed blow."

"You don't say! Don't you see the contradiction?"

"The contradiction, Madam?"

"Bartow stood face-to-face with the assailant. In his unreasoning state, he would naturally mimic the opposite arm. If the killer used his right hand, Bartow, mirroring him, would lift his left. Try it yourself. Lift your right hand; instinctively, you raise the one opposite. It is like looking in a mirror. You turn your head right; your reflection turns left."

Mr. Gryce's laugh rang out, involuntary and bright. He was rarely caught off guard, but this woman possessed a peculiar fascination over him, and he found it amusing rather than offensive to be bested.

"Excellent! You have proven your point satisfactorily. But what conclusions follow? That the killer was not left-handed, or that he was not standing where you placed him?"

"Shall we defy the doctors? They insist the blow was left-handed. Mr. Gryce, I would give anything for an hour spent in Mr. Adams's study with Bartow free to move. I believe we would learn more by watching him for a few moments than by talking for an hour."

The suggestion was tentative, tinged with the timidity she claimed she didn't possess. "I don't wish to be disagreeable," she smiled.

She was anything but. Mr. Gryce was taken aback, and for once, he acted impulsively.

"I believe it can be arranged, Madam; perhaps after the funeral. There are too many officials cluttering the house just now, and..."

"Of course, of course," she acquiesced. "I should not dream of intruding at present. But the case is so fascinating, and my connection so peculiar, that I sometimes forget myself. Do you think"—her voice faltered, betraying a rare loss of nerve—"that I have opened myself to a summons from the coroner?"

Gryce grew thoughtful, his eyes softening with a mock compassion as he looked at her folded hands.

"The facts are emerging so slowly that I doubt the inquest will occur for several days. Meanwhile, we may locate those two young people ourselves. If we do, the coroner may overlook your role in bringing them to our attention."

There was a sly emphasis on the word *overlook*, a subtle humor that revealed the detective at his sharpest. Miss Butterworth did not resent it; she was too preoccupied with a fresh confession.

While the news of the paper caused a stir, the revelation of the forced jaw sent a shockwave through the household. Miss Butterworth's composure remained impeccable, yet the secrecy surrounding her reaction fueled endless speculation among the servants. Meanwhile, at Police Headquarters, the detectives were inundated with the usual flood of idle rumors. The second day proved more fruitful than the first, yielding a cacophony of sightings from witnesses of all walks of life. An elderly woman claimed to have seen a lady of genteel bearing admit an elderly gentleman in a great-coat; a fashionable young woman recounted a collision with a pale youth and a ghostly companion wearing a hat adorned with a gray feather. Yet, when pressed for details, these accounts dissolved into vague generalities, offering no concrete leads to unravel the formidable mystery.

On the third day, Mr. Adams was laid to rest. With no relatives or intimate friends to attend, his landlord conducted the rites while his banker served as the chief mourner. Detectives mingled with the crowd outside the house and later at the cemetery, but their surveillance yielded nothing. Realizing the case had to be solved with the material already at hand, Mr. Gryce excused himself from headquarters. He walked alone to the dark, seemingly sealed house where the tragedy had unfolded.

Using a key, he entered and illuminated the interior. "Was the very man you described to me as leaving Mr. Adams's house with her. Tall, finely developed, with a grand air and gentlemanly manners. Even his clothes correspond with what you told me to expect: a checked suit, brown in color, and of the latest cut. Oh, he is the man!"

Mr. Gryce, with a suddenly developed interest in the lid of his inkstand, recalled the lines which Mr. Adams had written immediately before his death, and found himself wholly at sea. How reconcile facts so diametrically opposed? What allusion could there be in these lines to the new-made bride of another man? They read, rather, as if she were his own bride, as witness:

There must be something wrong. Sweetwater must have been led astray by a series of extraordinary coincidences. Dropping the lid of the inkstand in a way to make the young man smile, he looked up.

"I'm afraid it's been a fool chase, Sweetwater. The facts you relate in regard to this couple, the fact of their having been married at all, tally so little with what we have been led to expect from certain other evidences which have come in----"

"Pardon me, sir, but will you hear me out? At the Imperial, where they were married, I learned that the father and daughter had registered as coming from a small place in Pennsylvania; but I could learn nothing in regard to the bridegroom. He had not appeared on the scene till the time for the ceremony, and after the marriage was seen to take his bride away in one carriage while the old gentleman departed in another. On the afternoon of the very day of Mr. Adams's death, this young husband and wife left the hotel on foot and did not come back. Their clothes, which had all been left behind, were taken away two days later by an elderly gentleman who said he was her father and whose appearance coincides with that of the person registering as such at the Imperial. All of which looks favorable to my theory, does it not, especially when you remember that the bridegroom's name----"

"You have not told it."

"Is Adams, Thomas Adams. Same family as the murdered man, you see. At least, he has the same name."

Mr. Gryce surveyed the young man with admiration, but was not yet disposed to yield him entire credence.

"Humph! I do not wonder you thought it worth your while to follow up the pair, if one of them is named Adams and the other Eva. But, Sweetwater, the longer you serve on the force the more you will learn that coincidences as strange and unexpected as these do occur at times, and must be taken into account in the elucidation of a difficult problem. Much as I may regret to throw cold water on your hopes, there are reasons for believing that the young man and woman whom we are seeking are not the ones you have busied yourself about for the last two days. Certain facts which have come to light would seem to show that if she had a husband at all, his name would not be Thomas Adams, but Felix, and as the facts I have to bring forward are most direct and unimpeachable, I fear you will have to start again, and on a new tack."

But Sweetwater remained unshaken, and eyed his superior with a vague smile playing about his lips.

"You have not asked me, sir, where I have spent all the time which has elapsed since I saw you last. The investigations I have mentioned did not absorb more than a day."

"Very true. Where have you been, Sweetwater?"

"To Montgomery, sir, to that small town in Pennsylvania from which Mr. Poindexter and his daughter registered."

"Ah, I see! And what did you learn there? Something directly to the point?"

"I learned this, that John Poindexter, father of Eva, had for a friend in early life one Amos Cadwalader."

"Amos!" repeated Mr. Gryce, with an odd look.

"Yes, and that this Amos had a son, Felix."

"Ah!"

"You see, sir, we must be on the right track; coincidences cannot extend through half a dozen names."

"You are right. It is I who have made a mistake in drawing my conclusions too readily. Let us hear about this Amos. You gathered something of his history, no doubt."

"All that was possible, sir. It is closely woven in with that of Poindexter, and presents one feature which may occasion you no surprise, but which, I own, came near nonplussing me. Though the father of Felix, his name was not Adams. I say was not, for he has been dead six months. It was Cadwalader. And Felix went by the name of Cadwalader, too, in the early days of which I have to tell, he and a sister whose name----"

"Well?"

"Was Evelyn."

"Sweetwater, you are an admirable fellow. So the mystery is ours."

"The history, not the mystery; that still holds. Shall I relate what I know of those two families?"

"At once: I am as anxious as if I were again twenty-three and had been in your shoes instead of my own for the last three days."

"Very well, sir. John Poindexter and Amos Cadwalader were, in their early life, bosom friends. They had come from Scotland together and settled in Montgomery in the thirties. Both married there, but John Poindexter was a prosperous man from the first, while Cadwalader had little ability to support a family, and was on the verge of bankruptcy when the war of the rebellion broke out and he enlisted as a soldier. Poindexter remained at home, caring for his own family and for the two children of Cadwalader, whom he took into his own house. I say his own family, but he had no family, save a wife, up to the spring of '80. Then a daughter was born to him, the Eva who has just married Thomas Adams. Cadwalader, who was fitted for army life, rose to be a captain; but he was unfortunately taken prisoner at one of the late battles and confined in Libby Prison, where he suffered the tortures of the damned till he was released, in 1865, by a forced exchange of prisoners. Broken, old, and crushed, he returned home, and no one living in the town at that time will ever forget the day he alighted from the cars and took his way up the main street. It is to be hoped that the well-dressed lady of uncertain age, lingering late that afternoon in a secluded corner of the Belleville hotel piazza, had not selected a narrative demanding profound mental exertion. Her eyes, sharp yet kindly, seemed far more captivated by the spectacle unfolding before her than by the pages she turned with such industry.

The view was designed to entertain an idle mind. First, the vast blue expanse of the sea, speckled with sails; next, the beach, animated by clusters of youths dressed in garish array; then the village street, and finally, a lawn where young couples wandered with tennis rackets or golf clubs. Children, however, held no fascination for this amiable spinster—her status as one was undeniable. She barely glanced at them twice, whereas a young married couple, or even a tall, imperious elderly gentleman, invariably drew her gaze away from her book, which she held perilously close to her face, a habit a shrewd observer might have noted.

Such a critique, however, could not have been leveled at the sprightly, albeit frail, octogenarian seated at the opposite end of the piazza. He appeared utterly absorbed in the novel he displayed conspicuously, judging by the smiles that occasionally broke the placid serenity of his benevolent features. Yet, midway through his chapter, he abruptly closed the book, unfolded a newspaper to its full width, and began to scan the columns. Simultaneously, the lady at the other end of the piazza lifted her spectacles to scrutinize two gentlemen emerging from the grand door situated between her and the old reader. Did she recognize them, or was it mere curiosity? The manner in which she slammed her book shut and rose suggested she had spotted old acquaintances in the distinguished pair now approaching slowly. Yet, if she did, she offered no joy; after a fleeting sign of their approach, she turned with evident embarrassment and retreated onto the lawn. There, with her back to them, she stroked a small dog—a gesture revealing a total lack of sympathy for the creature, which was clearly her terror whenever her natural instincts allowed her to act freely.

The two gentlemen, displaying an air of total indifference to her presence, walked past until they reached the piazza's edge, then mechanically retraced their steps. Their faces now within view of the elderly man engrossed in his paper, he shifted the sheet a trifle—perhaps due to the sun's glare, or perhaps to catch a glimpse of two remarkable men. If the latter, the opportunity was fortuitous, as they halted mere feet from his chair. One was elderly, matching or surpassing the age of the man observing him, yet belonging to that renowned Scotch stock capable of remaining tough and hale at eighty. His toughness manifested not only in his upright, graceful figure but in the calm, cold gaze of his eye, which surveyed all with unmoved detachment. His younger companion, though equally stalwart, seemed to shrink with acute sensitivity from every person he encountered, pausing only for a brief exchange with the mild old reader nearby.

"I don't believe it serves me any good," the young man remarked gloomily. "I am wretched in her company, and doubly wretched when I attempt to forget myself in her absence. We should perhaps return. I would rather sit where she can see me than have her wonder—Oh, I shall be careful; but you must understand how unnerving the silence I am forced to maintain regarding what destroys us all. I am nearly as ill as she."

They withdrew, and the seemingly disinterested listener turned the page of his paper. Five minutes elapsed before they returned. This time, it was the old gentleman who spoke, his voice lower and more indistinct, driven by discretion or a lesser susceptibility to emotion.

"Escape? South coast—she will forget to watch you for—a clinging nature—impetuous, yet foolishly affectionate—you know that—no danger—discovered—the timing—a cheerful home—courage—happiness—all forgotten."

A gesture from the young man as he moved away indicated he shared no such optimism. Meanwhile, Miss Butterworth—surely you have recognized Miss Butterworth—had her own opportunities. She remained stooping over the dog, which wriggled under her hand without fleeing, perhaps fascinated by that hesitant touch she may or may not have known had never been inflicted upon a canine before. Yet her ears and attention were fixed on two girls chatting freely on a nearby bench, as if they were alone on the lawn. They were gossiping about a fellow-inmate of the hotel, and Miss Butterworth listened intently after catching the name Adams. Among the words she overheard:

"But she is! I tell you she is sick enough to require a nurse and a doctor. I caught a glimpse of her as I passed her room yesterday, and I never saw two such large eyes or such pale cheeks. Then, look at him! He must adore her, for he refuses to speak to another woman and paces that small, stifling room all day. I wonder if they are bride and groom? They are young enough, and if you've noticed her attire—"

"Oh, don't discuss clothes. I saw her on her first day and felt like a dowdy until she suddenly fell ill and could no longer wear those magnificent waists and jackets. I felt inadequate compared to that pale blue—"

"Oh, well, blue suits blondes. You would look dreadful in it. I didn't mind her clothes, but I felt it was all over for us if she chose to speak or smile at the few men brave enough to stay a week here. Yet she isn't a beauty; she lacks a good nose, a handsome eye, or even an impeccable complexion. It must be her mouth, which is lovely, or her walk—did you notice her walk? It was as if she were floating, until she collapsed in that faint. I wonder why she fainted. Nobody was doing anything, not even her husband. Perhaps that was the trouble. I noticed he looked very serious for some reason—and when she tried to attract his attention several times without success, she simply fell from her chair to the floor. That roused him. He hasn't left her since."

"I don't think they appear very happy, do you, for such a wealthy and handsome couple?"

"Perhaps he is dissipated. I've noticed the old gentleman never leaves them."

"Well, well, he may be dissipated; handsome men are prone to it. But I wouldn't care if—"

At that moment, the dog yelped and bolted. Miss Butterworth had unconsciously pinched it, likely in indignation at the direction the conversation was taking. This created a diversion, and the young girls departed, leaving Miss Butterworth without occupation. However, a young man crossing her path provided ample food for thought.

"You recognize them? Is there no mistake?" he whispered.

"None; the one heading this way is the young man I saw leave Mr. Adams's house, and the other is the old gentleman who arrived shortly after."

"Mr. Gryce advises you to return home. He intends to arrest the young man." Sweetwater passed on.

Miss Butterworth strolled to a seat and sat down. She felt weak, envisioning that young wife—sick, overwhelmed, struggling with her great fear—sink under this crushing blow, with no woman nearby capable of offering even the slightest sympathy. The father did not strike her as the man to steady her fainting head or soothe her bruised heart. Beneath his polished exterior lay an icy look that repelled this keen-eyed spinster. Recalling the coldness of his manner, she felt seized by an irresistible impulse to be near this young creature when the blow fell, if only to ease the tension of her own heartstrings, which ached keenly over the role she felt compelled to play in this affair.

But when she rose to seek Mr. Gryce, she found him gone. Searching the piazza for the other two gentlemen, she saw them vanish around the corner toward a small smoking room. Unable to follow, she ascended the stairs. Meeting a maid in the upper hall, she asked for Mrs. Adams. Told that Mrs. Adams was ill, she was shown the door of her room, located at the end of a long corridor. Since all halls terminated in a window beneath which a sofa stood, she felt circumstances favored her and took her seat upon the sofa before her in a state of great complacency. Instantly, a sweet voice drifted through the open transom of the door behind which her thoughts were already concentrated.

"Where is Tom? Oh, where is Tom? Why does he leave me? I'm afraid of what he may be tempted to do or say down on those great piazzas alone."

"Mr. Poindexter is with him," answered a voice, measured yet kind. "Mr. Adams was becoming very tired, and your father persuaded him to go down and smoke."

"I must get up; indeed I must get up. Oh! the camphor—the—"

There was a bustle; this poor young wife had evidently fainted again.

Miss Butterworth cast miserable glances at the door.

Meanwhile, in that secluded smoking room, a terrible scene unfolded. The two gentlemen had lit their cigars and sat in forced attitudes, betraying their lack of enjoyment of the weed, each having taken it out of compliance with the other. An old man, strangely serious, strangely at home, yet as strangely a guest of the house as they, entered and shut the door behind him.

"Gentlemen," he announced immediately, "I am Detective Gryce of the New York police, and I am here—but I see that one of you at least knows why I am here."

One? Both of them! This became evident instantly. No denial, no subterfuge was possible. At the first word spoken in the strange, authoritative tone old detectives acquire after years of such experiences, the young man sank into sudden collapse, while his companion, without yielding so entirely to his emotions, showed he was not insensible to the blow that, in one moment, had destroyed all their hopes.

When Mr. Gryce realized he was fully understood, he no longer hesitated over his duty. Turning his full attention to Mr. Adams, he said, this time with some feeling, for the misery of this young man had impressed him:

"You are wanted in New York by Coroner D----, whose business it is to hold an inquest over the remains of Mr. Felix Adams, of whose astonishing death you are undoubtedly informed. As you and your wife were seen leaving that gentleman's house a few minutes before he expired, you are naturally regarded as valuable witnesses in determining whether his death was one of suicide or murder."

It was an accusation, or so nearly one, that Mr. Gryce was not surprised to behold the dark flush of shame displace the livid terror that but an instant before had made the man before him resemble one of those lost spirits imagined flitting across the open mouth of hell. But he said nothing, seemingly powerless to do so, and his father-in-law was about to make some effort to deflect this blow when a voice in the hall outside inquired for Mr. Adams, stating that his wife had fainted again and required his assistance.

The young husband started, cast a look full of despair at Mr. Poindexter, and thrusting his hand against the door as if to hold it shut, sank on his knees before Mr. Gryce, saying:

"She knows! She suspects! Her nature is so sensitive."

He managed to utter these words in gasps as the detective bent compassionately over him. "Don't, don't disturb her! She is an angel, a saint from heaven. Let me bear the blame—he was my brother—let me go with you, but leave her in ignorance—"

Mr. Gryce, with a vivid sense of justice, laid his hand on the young man's arm.

"Say nothing," he enjoined. "My memory is good, and I would rather hear nothing from your lips. As for your wife, my warrant does in no way include her; and if you promise to come with me quietly, I will even let you bid her adieu, so that you do it in my presence."

The change that passed over the young man's face at these significant words surprised Mr. Gryce. Rising slowly, he took his stand by Mr. Poindexter, who, true to his inflexible nature, had scarcely moved in limb or feature since Mr. Gryce's arrival.

"What have you against me?" he demanded. There was a surprising ring to his voice, as if courage had arrived with the necessity of the moment. "Of what am I accused? I want you to tell me. I had rather you would tell me in so many words. I cannot leave in peace until you do."

Mr. Poindexter made a movement at this, casting a half-suspicious, half-warning glance at his son-in-law. But the young man took no notice of his interference. He kept his eye on the detective, who quietly produced his warrant.

At that instant, the door shook.

"Lock it!" was the hoarse command of the accused man. "Don't let anyone pass that door, even if it is to bring the tidings of my wife's death."

Mr. Gryce reached out his hand and turned the key in the lock. Young Adams opened the paper he had taken from the detective's hand, and while his blood-shot eyes vainly sought to master the few lines written there, Mr. Poindexter attracted the attention of Mr. Gryce. Fixing him with his eye, he formed his lips into three soundless words:

"For murder? Him?"

The detective's bow and a very long-drawn sigh from his son-in-law answered him simultaneously. With a curious lift of his upper lip, which showed his teeth somewhat unpleasantly for a moment, he drew back a step and sank into his previous immobility.

"I am indebted to you," declared the young man. "Now I know where I stand. I am quite ready to go with you and stand trial, if such be deemed necessary by the officials in New York. You," he cried, turning with almost an air of command to the old gentleman beside him, "will watch over Eva. Not like a father, sir, but like a mother. You will be at her side when she wakes, and, if possible, leave her only when she sleeps. Do not let her suffer—not too much. No newspapers, no gossiping women. Watch! watch! as

Thomas explained.

Gryce was a man of finesse. He had declared his intention to shadow Mr. Adams, and he did, but with a precision that bordered on the theatrical. He boarded the same train, claimed the same seat, and watched the young man from a calculated distance. It was a peculiar fascination that gripped the old detective; the boy before him stirred something in Gryce's own conscience, a dissonance that made the veteran feel his age with an acute, unwelcome sharpness just as he had concluded a complex and exhausting pursuit.

From his vantage point, Gryce observed the transformation in the prisoner's face. The handsome countenance, which had recently twisted in agony, now wore a terrifying calm. Gryce wondered at the source of such fortitude. Could it be that courage blooms only in the soil of despair? Or perhaps the boy was so consumed by his internal misery that he failed to see the shadow it cast upon the world around him? His brooding brow and vacant gaze suggested a mind entirely withdrawn from the present. Into what abysses of remorse had he fallen? Even a detective of Gryce's experience, seasoned by decades among criminals of high standing and cultivated appearance, could not fathom the depth of this young man's soul.

At the Brooklyn station, Gryce retook his prisoner, who barely lifted his head as the detective approached. Within an hour, they stood in the interrogation room of Police Headquarters, and the serious questioning of Mr. Adams commenced.

The suspect made no attempt to evade the inquiry. If anything, he seemed eager to speak, driven by a heavy burden he longed to discard. Yet, the nature of this burden differed sharply from the police's expectations. He did not confess to the murder of his brother, but rather admitted to being the incidental catalyst of that death.

"My name is Cadwalader, not Adams," he began, his voice steady despite the gravity of the situation. "My father, a Scot by birth, became a naturalized citizen of Pennsylvania, settling in a town called Montgomery when he was a young husband. He had two children then; one died in infancy, and the other was my brother, Felix—the violent death of whom, under the name of Adams, has brought me here today. I am the product of a later marriage, contracted by my father some years after he left Montgomery. When I was born, he resided in Harrisburg, but since he departed shortly after my third year, I hold no memories of that city. Indeed, my recollections are of scenes vastly different from this country. My mother died while I was still an infant, and I was sent very early to the Old World, from whence my father originally hailed.

"When I returned, which was not until this very year, I found my father dying and my brother a grown man with considerable wealth—a fortune I had been led to believe he intended to share with me. But once my father was laid to rest, Felix—(with visible effort he pronounced the name)—Felix came to New York, leaving me to wander without settled hopes or any definite promise of means to build a future.

"While wandering, I stumbled upon the town where my father had lived in his youth. Hunting up his old friends, I met in the home of one who had emigrated from Scotland with my father a young lady—in whom I quickly became so interested that I desired to marry her. But I possessed no money, no business, no home to offer her, and, as I must acknowledge, no prospects. Still, I could not abandon the hope of making her my wife. So I wrote to my brother, Felix Cadwalader—or, rather, Felix Adams, as he preferred to be known in later years for family reasons entirely unrelated to his sudden demise—and asked him to settle a sum upon me that would allow me to marry her with some semblance of self-respect. My only reply was a repetition of the vague promises he had previously offered.

"Youth is hopeful, even to the point of daring, and I resolved to make her mine without further delay, hoping that her beauty and endearing qualities would win from him, at first sight, the definite concession he had so persistently denied.

"This I did. The fault for which I reproach myself most bitterly is that I entered into this alliance without taking her or her father into my confidence. They believed me well-off, possibly wealthy. While Mr. Poindexter is certainly a man of means, I am sure that if he had known I possessed nothing but the clothes on my back and a mere trifle in pocket money, he would have halted the marriage in its tracks, for he is a man of great ambition and considers his daughter worthy of a millionaire's devotion—as she undoubtedly is.

"Felix—you must pardon me if I display no affection for my brother; he was a very strange man—was notified of my marriage, yet he chose neither to witness it nor to prohibit it. Consequently, the ceremony was conducted quietly, with strangers as witnesses, in a hotel parlor. Then, fueled by vague hopes and certain fears, I prepared to bring my young bride before my brother. Hardened as he was by years of bachelorhood, I reasoned that he could not be so entirely impervious to feminine charms as to fail to recognize my wife as a woman deserving of every consideration.

"But I had counted without my host. Two days after the ceremony that bound us together, when I took her to the house which has since become so unhappily notorious, I discovered that my brother had shown me but one facet of his nature, and that the least obdurate of his many-sided character.

'Brilliant as steel,' he was, and as hard. He not only professed himself unmoved by my wife's many charms but declared himself totally out of sympathy with follies such as love and marriage, which he termed the fruit of unoccupied minds and a pastime wholly unworthy of men boasting of such talents and attainments as ours. Then he turned his back upon us. Angered little short of frenzy, I began an abuse for which he was so ill-prepared that he crouched like a man under blows. Losing his self-control minute by minute, he finally seized a dagger lying close at hand and, crying, 'You want my money? Well, then, take it!' stabbed himself to the heart with one desperate blow.

'I fear I shall not be believed, but that is the story of this crime, gentlemen.'"

Tragedies of this sudden, uncalculated violence had certainly happened before, and human nature had always betrayed its inconsistencies in such moments of impulse. Yet, not a single soul in the room—whether haunted by the memory of Bartow's pantomime or not—failed to cast a look of profound skepticism upon the tale of Mr. Adams's violent end. The story was being poured out with such frantic volubility that it rang hollow to every ear.

The young man, possessing a susceptibility to emotion that was almost painful, felt the weight of their collective disbelief. His head dropped low.

"I shall add nothing to and take nothing from what I have said," he muttered, his voice dogged and flat. "Make of it what you will."

Inspector Gryce, leading the inquiry, glanced sharply at the detective beside him. The silence stretched until Gryce spoke, his tone softening slightly.

"We are sorry you have taken such a resolution, Mr. Adams. There are many threads left untied. For instance, if your brother slew himself in this unforeseen manner, why did you leave the house so precipitately? Why did you not give an alarm, or even proclaim your relationship to him?"

"You need not answer," the inspector interjected, his voice breaking in with a reminder of rights. "No man is called upon to incriminate himself in this free and independent country."

A smile, the saddest ever witnessed, traced a path across the prisoner's pallid lips. Then he lifted his head, his eyes burning with a desperate clarity.

"Incrimination is not what I fear now. From the way you all look at me, I perceive that I am lost. I have no means of proving my story."

This admission, which might easily pass for the cry of an innocent man crushed under suspicion, made his interrogator pause.

"You forget," the gentleman suggested gently, "that you had your wife with you. She can corroborate your words. She will prove herself, no doubt, an invaluable witness in your favor."

"My wife!" he repeated, choking on the word so that it emerged barely intelligible. "Must she be dragged into this? She is so sick, so weak. It would kill her, sir. She loves me—she—"

"Was she with you in Mr. Adams's study? Did she see him lift the dagger against his own breast?"

"No." With this denial, the young man seemed to draw a sudden, fragile courage. "She had fainted several moments previously, while the altercation between my brother and myself was at its height. She did not see the final act. And—gentlemen, I might as well speak the truth, for I have nothing to gain by silence—she finds it as difficult as you do to believe that Mr. Adams struck himself. I—I have tried with all my arts to impress the truth upon her, but oh, what can I hope from the world when the wife of my bosom, an angel who loves me, refuses to see it? She is too conscientious, too true to her own convictions. If she speaks, she will die. I should lose her."

Mr. Gryce tried to interrupt, to calm him, but the young man would not be stopped.

"Spare me, sirs! Spare my wife! Write me down guilty, anything you please, rather than force that young creature to speak!"

The inspector cut short these appeals, which were rending every heart in the room. "Have you read the newspapers for the last few days?" he asked abruptly.

"I? Yes, yes, sir. How could I help it? Blood is blood; the man was my brother. I had left him dying—I was naturally anxious, naturally saw my own danger—and I read them, of course."

"Then you know he was found with a large cross on his breast, a cross which was once on the wall. How came it to be torn down? Who put it on his bosom?"

"I, sir. I am not a Catholic, but Felix was. Seeing him dying without absolution, without extreme unction, I thought of the holy cross, and tore down the only one I saw, placing it in his arms."

"A pious act. Did he recognize it?"

"I cannot say. I had my fainting wife to look after. She occupied all my thoughts."

"I see," the inspector continued, his voice gaining momentum, "and you carried her out, so absorbed in caring for her that you did not observe Mr. Adams's valet."

"He's innocent, sir. Whatever people may think, he had nothing to do with this crime."

"You did not observe him, I say, standing in the doorway and watching you?"

Now the inspector knew that Bartow had not been standing there, but at the loophole above. But the opportunity to entrap the witness was too good to lose.

Mr. Adams was caught in the trap, or so one might judge from the beads of perspiration that suddenly bloomed on his pale forehead. Yet he struggled to maintain the stand he had taken, crying hotly:

"But that man is crazy, and deaf-and-dumb besides! Or so the papers give out. Surely his testimony is valueless. You would not confront me with him?"

"We confront you with no one. We only asked you a question. You did not observe the valet, then?"

"No, sir."

"Or understand the mystery of the colored lights?"

"No, sir."

"Or of the plate of steel and the other contrivances with which your brother enlivened his solitude?"

"I do not follow you, sir." But there was a distinct change in his tone, a tightening of the jaw.

"I see," said the inspector, "that the complications which have disturbed us and made necessary this long delay in the collection of testimony have not entered into the crime as described by you. Now this is possible; but there is still a circumstance requiring explanation; a little circumstance, which is, nevertheless, one of importance, since your wife mentioned it to you as soon as she became conscious. I allude to the half dozen or more words which were written by your brother immediately preceding his death. The paper on which they were written has been found, and that it was a factor in your quarrel is evident, since she regretted that it had been left behind you, and he—Do you know where we found this paper?"

The eyes which young Adams raised at this interrogatory held no intelligence. The sight of this morsel of paper seemed to deprive him instantly of all the faculties he had been using to carry on this unequal struggle. He shook his head, tried to reach out his hand, but failed to grasp the scrap of paper which the inspector held out. Then he burst into a loud, ragged cry:

"Enough! I cannot hold out, with no other support than a wicked lie. I killed my brother for reasons good as any man ever had for killing another. But I shall not impart them. I would rather be tried for murder and hanged."

It was a complete breakdown, pitiful in its contrast with the man's herculean physique and fine, though contracted, features. If it was the end, it was a sad one. Mr. Gryce, whose forehead had taken on a deep line between the eyebrows, slowly rose and took his stand by the young man, who looked ready to fall. The inspector, on the contrary, did not move. He had begun a rhythmic tapping with his fingers on the table, seemingly bound to beat it out, when another sudden cry broke from the young man's lips:

"What is that?" he demanded, his eyes fixed on the door, his whole frame shaking violently.

"Nothing," began the inspector, when the door suddenly opened.

The figure of a woman, white as a wraith and wonderful with a sort of holy passion, darted from the grasp of a man who sought to detain her. She stood before them, palpitating with a protest that for a moment she seemed powerless to utter.

It was Adams's young, invalid wife, whom he had left three hours before at Belleville. She was so frail of form, so exquisite of feature, that she would have seemed some unearthly visitant but for the human anguish which pervaded her look and soon found vent in this touching cry:

"What is he saying? Oh, I know well what he is saying. He is saying that he killed his brother, that he held the dagger which rid the world of a monster of whose wickedness none knew. But you must not heed him. Indeed you must not heed him. He is innocent; I, his wife, have come twenty miles, from a bed of weakness and suffering, to tell you so. He—"

But here a hand was laid gently, yet firmly, on her mouth. She looked up, met her husband's eyes filled with almost frantic appeal, and giving him a look in return that sank into the heart of every man who beheld it, she laid her own hand on his and drew it softly away.

"It is too late, Tom, I must speak. My father, my own weakness, or your own peremptory commands could not keep me at Belleville when I knew you had been brought here. And shall I stop now, in the presence of these men who have heard your words and may believe them? No, that would be a cowardice unworthy of our love and the true lives we hope to lead together. Sirs!"

Each man there held his breath to catch the words which came in faint and fainter intonation from her lips.

"I know my husband to be innocent, because the hand that held the dagger was mine. I killed Felix Cadwalader!"

* * * * *

The horror of such a moment is never fully realized till afterward. Not a man there moved, not even her husband, yet on every cheek a slow pallor was forming, which testified to the effect of such words from lips made for smiles and showing in every curve the habit of gentle thought and the loftiest instincts. Not till some one cried out from the doorway, "Catch her! she is falling!" did anyone stir or release the pent-up breath which awe and astonishment had hitherto held back on every lip.

Then he in whose evident despair all could read the real cause of the great dread which had drawn him into a false confession sprang forward. With renewed life showing itself in every feature, he caught her in his arms. As he staggered with her to a sofa and laid her softly down, he seemed another man in look and bearing. Mr. Gryce, who had been watching the whole wonderful event with the strongest interest, understood at once the meaning of the change which had come over his prisoner at that point in his memorable arrest: the moment he first realized that it was for himself they had come, and not for the really guilty person, the idolized object of his affections.

Meanwhile, he was facing them all, with one hand laid tenderly on that unconscious head.

"Do not think," he cried, his voice ringing clear, "that because this young girl has steeped her hand in blood, she is a wicked woman. There is no purer heart on earth than hers, and none more worthy of the worship of a true man. See! she killed my brother, son of my father, beloved by my mother, yet I can kiss her hand, kiss her forehead, her eyes, her feet, not because I hate him, but because I worship her, the purest—the best—"

He left her, and came and stood before those astonished men.

"Sirs!" he cried, "I must ask you to listen to a strange, a terrible tale."

"It mirrors, yet diverges from, what I have just told you," began young Adams, his voice tight with the weight of unspoken truths. "In my prior confession, I wove truth and fiction together. To clarify my wife's actions and grant you a proper understanding of the tragedy, I must begin anew, speaking as though I have told you nothing."

"Wait!" Mr. Gryce commanded, his tone authoritative. He leaned over the inspector, whispering a few rapid instructions before withdrawing a pencil. With swift efficiency, he jotted down several sentences and slid the paper across to the gentleman.

These notes, appearing as a set of memoranda, served as the inspector's guide as Mr. Adams—now rightfully known as Cadwalader—unfolded his narrative. Glancing at the list repeatedly, the inspector seemed to measure every word against the questions posed therein. For your sake, I shall reproduce these critical inquiries exactly as they were written:

1.  **The Shift from Calm to Frenzy:** How could a woman, composed enough to arrange her hair at the start of an interview, be driven to such a pitch of fury as to kill a man against whom she held no prior grudge? (Recall the comb found on the floor of Mr. Adams's bedroom.)
2.  **The Final Words:** What significance lay in the message written by the deceased just before this meeting: *"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she shall ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"*
3.  **The Pronoun "I":** Why was the first-person pronoun used in this communication? What standing did Mr. Felix Adams possess toward this young girl that qualified him to speak such words after her marriage to his brother?
4.  **The Swallowed Paper:** Having used the pronoun, why did he, upon being attacked, attempt to swallow the very paper bearing these words, dying with it clenched between his teeth?
5.  **Sorrow vs. Hate:** If he was slain in anger and died as monsters do (in her own words), why did his face betray sorrow rather than hate? Why did his expression show a determination removed from the chaotic rush of emotions one might expect from receiving a mortal blow from an unexpected foe?
6.  **The Signal Light:** If he possessed the strength to seize the paper and bring it to his lips, why did he not use that strength to turn on a light calculated to summon assistance? Instead, he left blazing the crimson glow, which, under our current code of signals, means: *"Nothing more required just now. Keep away."*
7.  **The Steel Plate:** What was the purpose of the huge steel plate found between the casings of the doorway, and why did it remain motionless within its socket at this culminating moment of his life?
8.  **Poindexter's Arrival:** Explain how old Poindexter appeared on the scene so soon after the event. His overheard words were: *"It is Amos's son, not Amos!"* Did he not know whom he was to meet in this house? Was the condition of the man lying before him—with a cross on his bosom and a dagger in his heart—less of a surprise to him than the identity of the victim?
9.  **Bartow's Pantomime & The Left Hand:** Recall our conclusions drawn from Bartow's pantomime. Mr. Adams was killed by a left-handed thrust. We must watch for an acknowledgment that the young woman is left-handed, and do not forget to seek an explanation for why, for so long a time, she held her other arm stretched out behind her.
10. **The Bird's Cry:** Why did the bird, whose chief cry is *"Remember Evelyn!"*, sometimes vary it with *"Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?"* For this tragedy to be true, the story must demonstrate that Mr. Adams knew his brother's bride both long and well.
11. **Bartow's Joy:** If Bartow is, as we believe, innocent of all connection with this crime save as a witness, why does he display such joy at its result? This may not reasonably fall within the scope of Thomas Adams's confession, but it cannot be ignored. This deaf-and-dumb servitor was driven mad by a fact which caused him joy. Why?
12. **The Lamp Schedule:** Observe the following schedule, drawn up after repeated experiments with Bartow and the various slides of the strange lamp that cause so many different lights to shine in Mr. Adams's study:

*   **White light:** Water wanted.
*   **Green light:** Overcoat and hat to be brought.
*   **Blue light:** Put back books on shelves.
*   **Violet light:** Arrange study for the night.
*   **Yellow light:** Watch for next light.
*   **Red light:** Nothing wanted; stay away.

The red light was on at the final scene. Note if this fact can be explained by Mr. Adams's account of the same.

With these points firmly in our minds, let us now peruse the history of this crime and the remote, possibly complicated causes that led to it.

Thomas Cadwalader did not tell his story; he suggested it, weaving a narrative of omission and implication. We dare not imitate this reticence, nor would it serve the reader's interest to relate these facts with the baldness and lack of detail that plagued the unhappy man due to the urgency of his situation. Remarkable tragedies are born of remarkable facts, and since such facts are merely the crystallization of human passions, we must delve into those passions to comprehend either the facts or their appalling consequences. In this case, the first link in the chain leading to Felix Adams's violent death was forged before the birth of the woman who would eventually strike him. We must begin, then, with almost forgotten days, recounting the tale as her pleader did: from the standpoint of Felix and Thomas Cadwalader.

Thomas Cadwalader—now known as Adams—never knew his mother; she died in his early infancy. Nor could he be said to have known his father, having been raised in France by an elderly Scotch lawyer, a relative of his mother's. This guardian spoke of her occasionally but never of Thomas's father until the boy reached his fifteenth year. Then, he placed certain books into Thomas's hands with a remarkable injunction:

"Here are romances, Thomas. Read them; but remember that none of them, no matter how thrilling in matter or effect, will ever equal the story of your father's bitterly wronged and suffering life."

"My father!" Thomas cried. "Tell me about him; I have never heard."

But his guardian, satisfied with an allusion he knew would bear fruit in the extremely susceptible nature of this isolated boy, said no more that day. Thomas turned to the books, but nothing thereafter could take his mind away from his father. He had scarcely thought of him for years, but now that this father had been placed before him in the light of a wronged man, he found himself continually hunting back into the deepest recesses of his memory for some long-forgotten recollection of that father's features, calculated to restore his image to his eyes. Sometimes he succeeded, or thought he did; but this image, if image it was, was speedily lost in a sensation of something strange and awe-compelling enveloping it. He found himself more absorbed by the intangible impressions associated with this memory than by the memory itself. What were these impressions, and in what had they originated? In vain he tried to determine. They were as vague as they were persistent: a stretch of darkness, two bars of orange light always shining and always the same, black lines against these bars like the tops of distant gables, an inner thrill, a vague affright, a rush about him as of a swooping wind—all this came with his father's image, only to fade away with it, leaving him troubled, uneasy, and perplexed. Finding these impressions persistent and receiving no explanation in his own mind, he finally asked his guardian what they meant. That guardian was as ignorant as himself on this topic; satisfied with having roused the boy's imagination, he confined himself to hints dropped now and then with a judiciousness that proved the existence of a deliberate purpose: some duty awaiting him on the other side of the water, a duty that would explain his long exile from his only parent and for which Thomas must fit himself by study and the acquirement of such accomplishments as would render a young man a positive power in society, whether that society be of the Old World or the New. He showed his shrewdness in thus dealing with this pliable and deeply affectionate nature. From this time forth, Thomas felt himself leading a life of mystery and interest.

To feel himself appointed for a work whose unknown character only heightened its importance gave point to every effort now made by this young man and lent to his studies that vague touch of romance that made them a delight, rendering him an adept in many things he might otherwise have cared little about. At eighteen, he was a graduate from the Sorbonne and a musical virtuoso. He could fence, ride, and carry off prizes in games requiring physical prowess as well as mental fitness. He was, in fact, a prodigy in many ways and was so considered by his fellow-students. However, he was not perfect; he lacked social charm and, in that regard, failed of being the complete gentleman. This deficiency was made painfully clear to him in the following way:

One morning his guardian came to him with a letter from his father. Along with words of commendation for his present attainments, the father expressed a certain dissatisfaction with his general manner, noting it was too abrupt and self-satisfied with those of his own sex and much too timid and deprecatory with those of the other. Thomas felt the criticism and recognized its justice, but how had his father, proven by the letter to be no longer a myth, become acquainted with defects that Thomas instinctively felt could never have attracted the attention of his far from polished guardian?

His questions on this point elicited a response that confounded him. He was not the only son of his father; he had a brother living, older than himself by some twenty years or more, who had just been in Paris, where, in all probability, he had met Thomas, talked with him, and perhaps pressed his hand.

It was a discovery calculated to deepen the impression already made upon Thomas's mind. Only a purpose of the greatest importance could account for so much mystery. What could it be? What was he destined to do, say, or be? He was not told, but while awaiting enlightenment, he was resolved not to be a disappointment to the two anxious souls who watched his career so eagerly and exacted such perfection from him. Consequently, he moderated his manner, and during the following year acquired by constant association with the gilded youth about him that indescribable charm of the perfect gentleman which he was led to believe would alone meet with the approval of those he now felt bound to please. At the end of the year, he found himself a finished man of the world. How truly so, he began to realize when he noted the blush with which his presence was hailed by women and the respect shown him by men of his own stamp. In the midst of the satisfaction thus experienced, his guardian paid him a final visit.

"You are now ready," he said, "for your father's summons. It will come in a few weeks. Be careful, then. Form no ties you cannot readily break; for, once recalled from France, you are not likely to return here. What your father's purpose concerning you may be I do not know, but it is no ordinary one. You will have money, a well-appointed home, family affection, all that you have hitherto craved in vain, and in return you will carry solace to a heart which has awaited your healing touch for twenty years. So much I am ordered to say; the rest you will hear from your father's own lips."

Aroused, encouraged, and animated by the wildest hopes and most extravagant anticipations, Thomas awaited his father's call with feverish impatience. When it came, he hastened to respond by an immediate voyage to America. This occurred some six months prior to the tragedy in ---- Street. Upon his arrival at the wharf in New York, he was met, not by his brother as he had every reason to expect, but by a messenger in whose face evil tidings were apparent before he spoke. Thomas was soon made acquainted with them. His father, whom he now learned was called Cadwalader (he himself had always been called Adams), was ill, possibly dying. He must therefore hasten, and being provided with minute instructions as to his way, he took the train at once for a small village in northern Pennsylvania.

All that followed was a dream to him. He was hurried through the night, with the motion of the ship still in his blood, to meet—what? He dared not think. He swam in a veritable nightmare. Then came a stop, a hurrying from the train, a halt on a platform reeking with rain (for the night was stormy), a call from someone to hurry, the sight of a panting horse steaming under a lamp whose blowing flame he often woke in after nights to see, a push from a persuasive hand, then a ride over a country road the darkness of which seemed impenetrable, and finally, the startling vision of an open door, with a Meg Merrilies of a woman standing in it, holding a flaming candle in her hand. The candle went out while he looked at it, leaving only a voice to guide him—a voice which, in tones shaken by chill or feeling, he could not tell which, cried eagerly:

"Is that you, laddie? Come awa in. Come awa in. Dinna heed the rain. The maister's been crying on you a' day. I'm glad you're no ower late."

He got down, followed the voice, and, stumbling up a step or two, entered a narrow door, which was with difficulty held open behind him, and which swung to with a loud noise the minute he crossed the threshold. This, or the dreariness of the place in which he found himself, disturbed him greatly. Bare floors, stained walls, meagre doorways, and a common pine staircase, lighted only by the miserable candle which the old woman had relit—were these the appointments of the palatial home he had been led to expect? Were these the surroundings, this the abode of him who had exacted such perfection on his part, and to satisfy whose standard he had devoted years of hourly, daily effort in every department of art and science? A sickening revolt seized him, aggravated by the smiles of the old woman, who dipped and courtesied before him in senile delight. She may have divined his feelings, for, drawing him inside, she relieved him of his overcoat, crying all the while, with an extravagant welcome more repulsive than all the rest:

"O the fine laddie! Wad your puir mither could see you the noo! Bonnie and clever! No your faither's bairn ava! All mither, laddie, all mither!"

The room was no better than the hall.

"Where is my father?" he asked, authoritatively, striving to keep down his strong repugnance.

"Dinna ye hear him? He's crying on ye. Puir man, he's wearying to see ye."

Hear him? He could scarcely hear her. The driving rain, the swish of some great boughs against the house, the rattling of casements and doors, and the shrieking of wind in the chimney made all other sounds wellnigh inaudible. Yet as he listened, he seemed to catch the accents of a far-off voice calling, now wistfully, now imperatively, "Thomas! Thomas!" And, thrilled with an emotion almost superstitious in its intensity, he moved hastily toward the staircase.

But the old woman was there before him. "Na! Na!" she cried. "Come in by and eat something first."

But Thomas shook his head. It seemed to him at that moment as if he never could eat or sleep again; the disillusion was so bitter, his disappointment so keen.

"You will na? Then haste ye--haste ye. But it's a peety you wadna ha'e eaten something. Ye'll need it, laddie; ye'll need it."

"Thomas! Thomas!" wailed the voice.

He tore himself away. He forced himself to go upstairs, following the cry, which at every moment grew louder. At the top, he cast a final glance below. The old woman stood at the stair-foot, shading the candle from the draught with a hand that shook with something more than age. She was gazing after him in vague affright, and with the shadow of this fear darkening her weazen face, she formed a picture from which he was glad to escape.

Plunging on, he found himself before a window whose small panes dripped and groaned under a rain that was fast becoming a torrent. Chilled by the sight, he turned toward the door faintly outlined beside it, and in the semi-darkness seized an old-fashioned latch rattling in the wind that permeated every passageway, and softly raised it.

Instantly the door fell back, and two eyes blazing with fever and that fire of the soul of which fever is the mere physical symbol greeted him from the midst of a huge bed drawn up against the opposite wall. Then two arms rose, and the moaning cry of "Thomas! Thomas!" changed to a shout, and he knew himself to be in the presence of his father.

Falling on his knees in speechless emotion, he grasped the wasted hands held out to him. Such a face, rugged though it was and far from fulfilling the promise held out to him in his dreams, could not but move any man. As he gazed into it and pressed the hands in which the life blood only seemed to linger for this last, this only embrace, all his filial instincts were aroused, and he forgot the common surroundings, the depressing rain, his own fatigue and bitter disappointment, in his lifelong craving for love and family recognition.

But the old man on whose breast he fell showed other emotions than those by which he was himself actuated. It was not an embrace he craved, but an opportunity to satisfy an almost frenzied curiosity as to the appearance and attributes of the son who had grown to manhood under other eyes. Pushing him gently back, he bade him stand in the light of the lamp burning on a small pine table, and surveyed him, as it were, from the verge of his own fast-failing life, with moans of mingled pain and weariness, amid which Thomas thought he heard the accents of a supreme satisfaction.

Meanwhile, in Thomas himself, as he stood there, the sense of complete desolation filled his breast almost to bursting. To have come home for this! To find a father only to be weighed in the scales of that father's judgment! To be admired, instead of loved!

As he realized his position and listened to the shrieking of the wind and rain, he felt that the wail of the elements but echoed the cry of his own affections, thus strangled in their birth. It was a sound that spoke volumes louder than words—a testament to a grief that remained raw and unhealed despite the thirty years that had since washed over the original tragedy.

"From the onset of darkness until the break of dawn, we sat in silent vigil, eyes locked upon one another's faces, sleepless and weary. Then, we sought out John Poindexter.

"If he had chosen to flee, we might have found some measure of pity. Instead, he met us openly, quietly, with the detached indifference of a man who cannot comprehend feeling because he is incapable of experiencing it. His opening words confirmed this diagnosis: 'Spare yourselves, spare me all useless recriminations. The girl is dead; I cannot call her back. Live your lives—eat, drink, spend, and enjoy the fleeting years remaining to you. Why cling to ancient sorrows?' His final remark was a declaration of victory: 'When a man cares for nothing and no one, it is futile to curse him.'

"That was the key. That was the secret of his invincibility. He cared for nothing, not even himself. We felt the impact of his cruelty and bent beneath its weight. Yet, before we departed that town, your father and I swore an oath. We vowed that we would eventually make that cold heart bleed; that some day, in some way, we would force that impassive nature to suffer exactly as we had suffered, regardless of his apparent happiness or the security of his fortune. That was thirty years ago, and the oath remains unfulfilled."

Felix fell silent. Thomas lifted his head, his throat tight, but the old man raised a trembling hand to silence him. "Some men forget within a month; others within a year. Neither I nor Felix have ever forgotten. When you were born—I had remarried, seeking a renewal of joy—I felt, inexplicably, that Evelyn's avenger had arrived. And when, a year later, we learned that God had seemingly forgiven John Poindexter's sins, granting him a child after eighteen years of barren marriage, I looked upon your beautiful face and saw—or perhaps I merely hoped to see—a vessel capable of executing the vengeance to which we had dedicated our lives.

"You grew. Your ardent nature, generous spirit, and quick intellect promised a magnificent adulthood. When your mother died, leaving me a widower for the second time, I hesitated no longer in devoting you to this purpose. Thomas, do you recall the beginning of that journey that would eventually carry you far from me? Remember how I carried you on my shoulders along a dusty road, and when we reached the sight of his home, I lifted you up from the graves, pointing to the distant gables looming black against the twilight's gold, dedicating you to the destruction of whatever happiness might one day bloom under his infant's smile? You remember? I did not think you could forget. Now, as the moment arrives to fulfill that promise, I call upon you again, Thomas. Avenge our griefs. Avenge your sister. *Poindexter's girl has grown to womanhood.*"

At the implication hidden in these words, Thomas recoiled in sheer horror. But the old man misread his terror. Clutching his arm, he spoke with passionate intensity. "Woo her! Win her! They do not know you. To them, you will simply be Thomas Adams, not Thomas Cadwalader. Envelop this budding flower in your embrace, and then—Oh, he must love his child! Through her, we hold the key to his heart. Make her suffer. She is but a country girl, and you have lived in Paris. Make her suffer, and if, in doing so, you cause him to flinch, then believe me as I watch over you from the grave I am about to enter. Be happy, for you will not have lived, nor will I have died, in vain."

He paused, gasping for breath, but his indomitable will conquered his failing body, holding Thomas captive under a spell that defied instinct. It filled him with a hate that had taken his father and brother a quarter-century to cultivate, condensing a lifetime of resentment into a single hour of active vengeance.

"I shall die," the old man panted, his voice thinning. "I will never live to witness your triumph; I will never see John Poindexter's eyes cloud with the suffering that rends the entrails and makes a man question the existence of God. But I shall know it in the realm where I am going. No mound of earth can hold my spirit down when John Poindexter feels his doom. I shall be conscious of his anguish and rejoice. And when, in the depths of darkness to which I descend, he comes faltering along my path—"

"Boy, boy, you were reared for this," he urged, his voice rising again. "God made you handsome; man has forged you strong; you have cultivated your own intelligence and accomplishment. You need only present yourself to this country girl to become the master of her will and affection. Once these are yours, remember *me*! Remember *Evelyn*!"

Thomas had never witnessed such raw passion. It swept him along in a burning torrent against which he struggled uselessly. Raising his hand in a gesture of response, he attempted to speak but found his voice stolen. His father misinterpreted his silence, crying out bitterly, "You are dumb! You dislike the task; perhaps you are virtuous, you who have lived alone and unhampered in Paris. Or perhaps you possess instincts of honor, habits of generosity that blind you to wrongs that have cried aloud to heaven for vengeance for longer than your entire lifetime. Thomas, Thomas, if you should fail me now—"

"He will not fail you," interrupted a voice that was calm, suave, and dangerously insinuating. It was Felix. "I have watched him; I know him. He will not fail you."

Thomas shuddered, having momentarily forgotten Felix's presence. Yet, hearing these words, he could no longer delay turning to the man who had offered to stand surety for the unholy deed his father demanded. He looked upon a figure who, in any setting, would command attention, admiration, and fear. He was not large, certainly not as broad as Thomas, but the frenzy that animated his father's lips manifested as quiet, inflexible resolve in the gray eyes resting upon him with a power Thomas could never evade. As comprehension settled over him, a steel band seemed to compress his heart. Yet, he simultaneously recognized that the personality before him was as elegant as his own, perfectly trained in the ways of men and life. Even the aura of poverty that had shocked him in his father's demeanor was absent here. Felix was impeccably dressed, holding his own as the elder brother in every regard a Parisian gentleman demanded. The long, dark-green serge curtain stretching behind him from floor to ceiling cast his pale features in remarkable relief, and for a fleeting instant, Thomas wondered if it had been placed there specifically to create this dramatic effect. But the demand in his brother's face drew his attention away. Bowing his head, he stammered, "I am at your command, Felix. I am at your command, father. I cannot say more. Only remember that I never saw Evelyn, that she died before I was born, and that I—"

Felix's voice broke in, kind yet measured. "Perhaps there is an obstacle we have not accounted for. You may already love some woman and desire to marry her. If so, it need be no impediment—"

Thomas's indignation found immediate voice. "No," he said sharply. "My heart is whole, save for a few lingering fancies that are fast becoming vanishing dreams."

It seemed he had lived years since entering the room.

"Your heart will not be disturbed now," Felix commented smoothly. "I have seen the girl. I went there on purpose a year ago. She is as pale as a snowdrop and as listless. You will not be obliged to recall the gay smiles of Parisian ladies to remain proof against her charms."

Thomas shrugged.

"She must be made to know the full intoxication of hope," Felix proceeded, his voice clear and cutting. "To realize despair, she must first experience every delight that comes with satisfied love. Do you possess the skill and the heart to play to the end a role requiring patience as well as dissimulation, courage as well as subtlety, and that union of will and implacability which feeds on tears and is strengthened, rather than diminished, by the suffering of its victim?"

"I have the skill," Thomas murmured, "but—"

"You lack the incentive," Felix finished. "Well, well, we must have patience with your doubts and hesitations. Our hate has been fostered by memories of her whom, as you say, you have never seen. Look, then, Thomas. Look at your sister as she was, as she is for us. Look at her, and think of her as despoiled, killed, and forgotten by Poindexter. Have you ever gazed upon a more moving countenance, or one in which beauty contends with a keener prophecy of woe?"

Uncertain of what to expect, anticipating perhaps the ghost herself, Thomas followed the direction of his brother's lifted hand. Where but a minute before the dismal curtain had hung, there was now a blaze of light. In its midst stood a charming yet tragic figure, such as no gallery in all Europe had ever shown him, possibly because no other painted face or form had ever appealed to his heart. It did not appear to be a picture; it seemed her very self—a gentle, loving presence breathing forth all the tenderness he had vainly sought in his living relatives. Falling to his knees before her image, he cried out, "Do not look at me so reproachfully, sweet Evelyn. I was born to avenge you, and I will. John Poindexter shall never go down in peace to his tomb."

A sigh of utter contentment drifted from the direction of the bed.

"Swear it!" cried his father, holding out his arms in the form of a cross.

"Yes, swear it!" repeated Felix, placing his own hand upon those crossed arms.

Thomas drew near and laid his hand beside Felix's.

"I swear," he began, raising his voice above the storm that battered the house gust after gust. "I swear to win the affections of Eva Poindexter, and then, when her heart is entirely mine, to cast her back in anguish and contempt upon the breast of John Poindexter."

"Good!" came a voice from what seemed an immeasurable distance. Then the darkness, which had settled over his senses since the taking of the oath, lifted. Thomas sank insensible at the feet of his dying father.

***

Amos Cadwalader died that night, but not without one final, awful scene. About midnight, he roused from the sleep that had followed the excruciating events he had just recounted. Glancing from Thomas to Felix, who sat on either side of the bed, he fixed his eyes with a strange, gleaming intensity upon the door.

"Ah!" he ejaculated. "A visitor! John Poindexter! He comes to ask my forgiveness before I set out on my dismal journey."

The sarcasm in his tone and the courtesy of his manner caused the hair to stir on the heads of his two sons. There was no doubt that he saw his enemy as plainly as he saw them.

"Does he dread my meeting with Evelyn? Does he wish to placate me before I am joined to that pathetic shade? He shall not be disappointed. I forgive you, John Poindexter! I forgive you my daughter's shame, my blighted life. I am dying; but I leave one behind who will not forgive you. I have a son, an avenger of the dead, who yet lives to—to—"

He fell back. With these words, which seemed to seal Thomas to his task forever, Amos Cadwalader died.

Felix had not inherited his father's incapacity for making money. In the twenty years that had passed since Thomas had been abroad, he had built up a fortune, which he could not induce his father to share, but which that father was perfectly willing to see devoted to their mutual revenge. There was meaning, therefore, in the injunction Felix gave his brother on his departure for Montgomery:

"I have money; spend it; spend what you will, and when your task is completed, there will still be some left for your amusement."

The room hung heavy with a silence thick enough to choke on, charged with the raw aftermath of the final appeal. As the listeners whispered in hushed, frantic clusters and Thomas Adams turned toward his wife with a gaze woven from love and terror, Inspector Gryce returned the memorandum to Mr. Gryce. The document, a grid of questions and answers, stood as the skeleton of the unfolding truth.

Most of the riddles had been solved. The frenzy that drove a calm woman to murder a man without prior grievance was traced to the comb found in the bedroom. The cryptic note—"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"—was decoded through the lens of family history. The use of the pronoun "I" by the deceased clarified his standing toward the young girl even after her marriage to his brother. His desperate attempt to swallow the paper, dying with it clamped between his teeth, spoke of a final, futile grasp at dignity. The sorrow etched on his face, rather than hate, and his failure to summon light despite having the strength to do so, painted a picture of a man overwhelmed not by rage, but by a crushing, quiet despair. Even the strange stillness of the steel plate in the doorway and the sudden arrival of Poindexter, who mistook the victim for someone else, found their places in the narrative. The bird's song, shifting from "Remember Evelyn" to "Poor Eva," confirmed that the dead man knew his brother's bride intimately. And the deaf-mute servant Bartow's inexplicable joy at the tragedy was explained by the hidden scraps of writing revealing a jealousy that bloomed into delight when his master fell.

Yet, two shadows remained. The first was the peculiar pantomime of Bartow, who described a blow delivered by a left hand, yet the woman appeared right-handed. The second was the red light signaling "stay away," which seemed to contradict the urgency of the final moments.

Mr. Gryce, seeing the inspector's lingering doubt, stepped forward. He approached the trembling young man and asked if he could demonstrate the stroke his wife had dealt. The question hung in the air, daring a response. Thomas hesitated, his mind racing through the details he had been forbidden to share. He thought, then thrust out his left hand, snatching an object from the table and lunging across his body toward an imaginary foe at his right. He looked around, his face a mask of anxious hope.

"You would make your wife out left-handed," Mr. Gryce observed gently. "But I have watched her closely since she entered this room, and I have seen no evidence of it."

"She is not left-handed," Thomas replied, his voice breaking slightly. "She thrust with her left hand because her right was fast held in mine. I had seized her instinctively as she bounded for the weapon. The convulsive clutch of our two hands was not loosed until the horror of her act made her faint, and she fell away from me to the floor crying: 'Tear down the cross and lay it on your brother's breast. I would at least see him die the death of a Christian.'"

Relief washed over Mr. Gryce's features. The mystery of the constrained right arm in Bartow's pantomime dissolved under the weight of this confession. With a smile, he picked up the blue pencil and, with decisive strokes, wrote a clear "answered" across paragraph nine. The circle of questions was nearly closed, leaving only the red light to explain itself in the silence that followed.

The outer room buzzed with a low, expectant hum, a gathering of curious onlookers waiting for the storm to pass. Mr. Gryce stood apart, his gaze sweeping over the crowd, driven by a lingering question that refused to settle in his mind. He sought clarity among the faces before him, hoping the answers would surface naturally. They did. In a secluded corner, he spotted Miss Butterworth, her posture deceptively calm, having clearly played a pivotal role in steering the events of the last few hours.

Gryce approached, a polite smile playing on his lips as he gently chided her for her involvement. Miss Butterworth, however, met his amusement with a flash of defensive warmth.

"I was merely present," she insisted, her voice rising slightly. "When the crisis peaked—when this young woman realized her husband had vanished suddenly for New York, accompanied by two strangers—it became undeniable that she needed a guide who understood both her plight and the desperation she faced. I was that guide."

"You are always the woman in the right place at the right time," Gryce replied, his tone lightening. "So, you are familiar with Mrs. Adams' account?"

"Yes," she confirmed, her expression softening into something akin to relief. "Her initial hysterics revealed she was the one who wielded the dagger that struck Mr. Adams. Yet, once she calmed, she offered a narrative that transformed what appeared to be a heinous murder into a tragic necessity. Upon hearing her explanation, Mr. Gryce, my hesitation regarding our respective duties vanished. Do you believe she will be summoned to answer for this blow? Will she face trial and conviction?"

"Madam," Gryce assured her, his voice steady and reassuring, "there are not twelve men in this city so lacking in judgment as to label this act a crime. It was clearly self-defense. No grand jury will find a true bill against young Mrs. Adams. You may rest easy."

The heavy gloom that had shadowed Miss Butterworth's eyes lifted instantly.

"Then I may return home in peace," she exclaimed, sounding almost relieved by the exhaustion of the ordeal. "These past five hours have been a trial by fire. Will you see me to my carriage?"

To Gryce, she appeared far from shaken; indeed, she looked remarkably serene, perhaps even too comfortable for someone who had just navigated such turbulent waters. Yet, he respected her assessment of her own state. Once she confirmed that her charge was recovering under her husband's watchful care, she accepted his offered arm and moved toward the vehicle.

As he helped her inside, the conversation turned to Mr. Poindexter.

"Why is Mrs. Adams' father not here?" Gryce asked. "Did he permit his daughter to embark on such a perilous journey without offering to accompany her?"

The response was curt and cold.

"Mr. Poindexter is a man without heart," she said sharply. "He came with us to New York, but he refused to follow us to Police Headquarters. Sir, you will find that the united passions of three burning souls, fueled by a vengeance I have rarely witnessed, were ultimately wasted on a man incapable of feeling pain. Do not speak of old John Poindexter to me. Now, if you will be so kind, instruct the driver to take me home to Gramercy Park. I have inserted my finger into the police pie for the very last time, Mr. Gryce—positively the last time."

She sank back against the carriage cushions, her face set in an expression of inexorable resolve. Yet, beneath that stern mask, a quiet complacency lingered, suggesting she was not entirely displeased with her own actions or the outcome of her intervention in matters that often clashed with the refined instincts of a lady.

Mr. Gryce suppressed a smile, bowing lower than usual as he watched the carriage depart. Under the shadow of his gesture, the vehicle rolled away. As he walked slowly back toward the station, a sigh escaped him. He wondered, with a touch of melancholy, if a case of such profound interest would ever bring them together again in consultation.
