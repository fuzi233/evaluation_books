The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the Chelsea windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Mirah, like the frantic heartbeat of a city indifferent to the quiet suffocations of its women. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of beeswax and damp wool, a heavy perfume masking the tension of the afternoon. The two rooms had been thrown together, a makeshift sanctuary where the boundaries between domestic duty and artistic aspiration blurred. Across the front room, a great length of embroidery stretched like a tapestry of interrupted dreams, a *pièce de résistance* in needlework that demanded the attention of any clever fingers at liberty. Mrs. Meyrick bent over one corner, Mab in the middle, and Amy at the far end, their movements synchronized in a quiet industry.

In the center of this tableau sat Mirah, perched on a camp-stool that seemed too small for her spirit. Her education in the arts of seamstress had been neglected, her stitches falling on the make-shift level of the tailor-bird's nest, yet today she served a dual purpose. She was the reader, her voice carrying the delicate cadences of Charles Lamb's "Essay of Elia," specifically the "Praise of Chimney-Sweeps," and simultaneously the model for Kate's title-page vignette—a fair public absorbed in the successive volumes of the family tea-table. She spoke with charming distinctness, all smiles over the "innocent blackness" of the chimney sweeps, until the sound of a knock shattered the spell.

It was not the gentle tap of a neighbor, nor the familiar rattle of the muffin-man. This was an imposing signal, a knock accompanied by a ring that magnified the sense of social existence, demanding immediate reverence. The five bright faces lifted in unison, their amusement curdling into anticipation.

"Dear me!" Mrs. Meyrick exclaimed, smoothing her apron. "Can it be Lady Mallinger? Is there a grand carriage, Amy?"

"No—only a hansom cab," Kate replied dryly, her eyes narrowing. "It must be a gentleman. Or perhaps the Prime Minister. Hans says the greatest man in London may get into a hansom cab."

"Oh, oh, oh!" cried Mab, her imagination already spinning tales of royalty. "Suppose it should be Lord Russell!"

The door opened, and the old maid-servant, distracted by the sheer magnitude of the visitor, left it ajar. Bowing toward Mrs. Meyrick was a figure quite unlike the respectable Premier they had imagined. He was tall and physically imposing, dressed in kid and kerseymere that strained against his frame, his face massive and flamboyant, crowned with wild hair and gold spectacles. As Mrs. Meyrick glanced at the card, the name confirmed the visual shock: Julius Klesmer.

Even embarrassment could hardly have made the "little mother" awkward, but her quick perceptions seized the significance of the moment instantly. The great personage had come to Mirah, sparing her the humiliation of approaching him; it was a sign of active interest, a validation of her talent. Yet, as Klesmer entered, the cozy dimensions of the cottage seemed to shrink into closets. The piano, once a centerpiece of domestic harmony, now seemed a ridiculous toy. The entire family existence felt petty and private, like an establishment of mice in the Tuileries, dwarfed by the vast, shadowy presence of the man who carried his consciousness in a theater of thousands.

Klesmer's glance swept the room, suggesting vast areas and a multitudinous audience that formed the usual scenery of his mind. Vanity, yes, but not the petty kind; it was the vanity of a conductor facing a symphony hall, natural to him as the length of his fingers. His grandiose air made Mab feel like a doll beside the cottage piano, yet beneath the performance lay a keen, thoroughly kind sensibility. He remembered homes no larger than this on the outskirts of Bohemia, and in the figurative Bohemia of small incomes, he knew the variety and romance that belonged to limited means.

"I hope I have not taken too great a freedom," he began, addressing Mrs. Meyrick with the utmost deference, his voice resonating slightly too loudly for the small space. "Being in the neighborhood, I ventured to save time by calling. Our friend, Mr. Deronda, mentioned to me an understanding that I was to have the honor of becoming acquainted with a young lady here—Miss Lapidoth."

Klesmer had discerned Mirah in the first moment of entering, but with subtle politeness, he looked round bowingly at the three sisters, feigning uncertainty as to which was the young lady in question.

"Those are my daughters: this is Miss Lapidoth," Mrs. Meyrick said, waving her hand toward Mirah.

"Ah," Klesmer said, in a tone of gratified expectation. He turned a radiant smile and a deep bow toward Mirah. Instead of being taken aback, she met his gaze with a calm pleasure. She liked the look of him, feeling a strange certainty that he would scold her, like a great musician and a kind man correcting a novice.

"You will not object to beginning our acquaintance by singing to me," he added, aware that they would all be relieved by getting rid of preliminaries.

"I shall be very glad. It is good of you to be willing to listen to me," Mirah said, moving to the piano. "Shall I accompany myself?"

"By all means," Klesmer replied, seating himself where Mrs. Meyrick indicated, ensuring he had a clear view of the singer. The acute little mother would not have acknowledged the weakness, but she whispered to herself, "He will like her singing better if he sees her."

All the feminine hearts except Mirah's beat fast with anxiety. Klesmer sat with a listening frown, terrifying in his intensity, and they dared only look at him furtively. If he said anything severe, it would be unbearable. They comforted themselves with the memory of Prince Camaralzaman, who had heard the finest things and still preferred Mirah's singing. She appeared to be doing her very best, radiating a confidence that belied her terror.

The song she chose was a fine setting of words from Leopardi's grand Ode to Italy. First came the recitative: *"O patria mia, vedo le mura e gli archi / E le colonne e i simulacri e l'erme torri / Degli avi nostri"*—seeing the walls, the arches, the columns, the empty towers of their ancestors. Then followed the mournful melody, a rhythmic plaint: *"Ma la gloria—non vedo"*—but glory, I do not see. After this came a climax of devout triumph, passing from the subdued adoration of a happy Andante in the words *"Beatissimi voi. Che offriste il petto alle nemiche lance / Per amor di costei che al sol vi diede"* to the joyous outburst of an exultant Allegro: *"Oh viva, oh viva: Beatissimi voi / Mentre nel mondo si favelli o scriva."*

When she ended, Klesmer said after a moment, "That is old Leo's music."

"Yes, he was my last master—at Vienna: so fierce and so good," Mirah said, with a melancholy smile. "He prophesied that my voice would not do for the stage. And he was right."

*"Continue, if you please,"* Klesmer commanded, putting out his lips and shaking his long fingers, while he went on with a smothered articulation quite unintelligible to the audience.

The three girls detested him unanimously for not saying one word of praise. Mrs. Meyrick was a little alarmed. Mirah, simply bent on doing what Klesmer desired and imagining that he would now like to hear her sing some German, went through Prince Radziwill's music to Gretchen's songs in the *Faust*, one after the other without any interrogatory pause. When she had finished, he rose and walked to the extremity of the small space at command, then walked back to the piano. Mirah had risen from her seat and stood looking toward him with her little hands crossed before her, meekly awaiting judgment.

Then, with a sudden unknitting of his brow and beaming eyes, he stretched out his hand and said abruptly, "Let us shake hands: you are a musician."

Mab felt herself beginning to cry, and all the three girls held Klesmer adorable. Mrs. Meyrick took a long breath.

But straightway the frown came again. The long hand, back uppermost, was stretched out in quite a different sense to touch with fingertip the back of Mirah's, and with protruded lip he said, "Not for great tasks. No high roofs. We are no skylarks. We must be modest." Klesmer paused here. And Mab ceased to think him adorable: "as if Mirah had shown the least sign of conceit!"

Mirah was silent, knowing that there was a specific opinion to be waited for. Klesmer presently went on—"I would not advise—I would not further your singing in any larger space than a private drawing-room. But you will do there. And here in London that is one of the best careers open. Lessons will follow. Will you come and sing at a private concert at my house on Wednesday?"

"Oh, I shall be grateful," said Mirah, putting her hands together devoutly. "I would rather get my bread in that way than by anything more public. I will try to improve. What should I work at most?"
