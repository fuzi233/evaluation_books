A profound and unsettling silence descended upon the rectory, interrupting the deep, ancestral rhythms that usually governed the household. Rex had arrived from his disastrous encounter only to retreat into a lightless room, throwing himself onto his bed in a state of sensory and physical apathy. The vibrant acoustic ecology of his youth—the cheerful cadence of his footsteps, the bright timbre of his laughter—was extinguished. By the following day, this silence had curdled into the feverish stillness of a tangible illness. The prospect of his journey to Southampton was abandoned; instead, the entire domestic gravity of his mother and Anna shifted toward tending to a boy who no longer possessed the appetite for life. He refused all sustenance, turning away from the restorative broths of marrow-bone and winter-sown barley, brightened with bruised thyme, that Anna carried up the stairs. The boy who had once savored the earth’s bounties with a profound, almost agrarian reverence was metamorphosed into a dull-eyed creature, murmuring only a bleak, untranslatable plea: “Let me alone.”

His father, standing like an aging oak rooted in traditional pragmatism, looked beyond the immediate crisis. He believed this sickness was but a harsh passage through an unlucky season. Yet he grieved for his son’s inevitable suffering, stepping into the hushed room to sit by the bedside. In a quiet gesture of patriarchal continuity, he would press his heavy hand against Rex’s blank brow, uttering a low, resonant, “God bless you, my boy.” Warham and the younger children, drawn by the unnatural quiet, would sometimes peer around the oak doorframe to witness the mythic collapse of their lively brother, only to be chased away by the frantic shaking of fingers. 

Anna remained his steadfast guardian. Her small hand rested in his, though it met with no returning pressure. Her interior life was cleaved in two: half devoted to a visceral anguish for Rex, half consumed by a bitter reproach of Gwendolen. To Anna, whose soul was nourished by the authentic, earthy realities of hearth and harvest, Gwendolen’s superficiality was deeply offensive. *Perhaps it is wicked of me, but I think I never can love her again,* became the silent, rhythmic monody of her days. 

Even Mrs. Gascoigne could not suppress an angry, discordant note when speaking to her husband, her voice tight with the strain of fractured domestic peace. “I know of course it is better, and we ought to be thankful that she is not in love with the poor boy; but really, Henry, I think she is hard. She consumes people for her own vanity. She must have spoken to him in a way that made him believe something, or the disappointment would not have hollowed him out like this. And some blame attaches to poor Fanny; she is quite blind about that girl.”

Mr. Gascoigne answered with the imperative, booming baritone of the dominant lexicon. “The less said on that point the better, Nancy. I ought to have been more awake myself. As to the boy, be thankful if nothing worse ever happens to him. Let the thing die out as quickly as possible; and especially with regard to Gwendolen—let it be as if it had never been.” To the rector, language held the power to erase and command; a decreed silence was the solution to preserving their social equilibrium. 

But a shift in the acoustic atmosphere signaled a break in the heavy weather. One bright morning, the splash of bathwater echoed from Rex’s room. Anna, breathless at the familiar sound of his daily ablutions, hovered at the foot of the stairs, catching the scent of the lavender soap he always used. When he descended, he offered her a faint smile, though it lay upon his pallid features like the thin, metallic hoar-frost that currently gripped the garden shrubs. 

“Nannie!” he said gently, taking her hand and leading her toward the drawing-room. His mother greeted him with an embrace, to which he offered a hollow, “What a plague I am!” 

He sat by the bow-window, staring out at the frozen lawn. He felt as though he had been abruptly resurrected into a sterile, tasteless world, the vocabulary of his past joys completely forgotten. Anna sat near him, her hands busy with needlework, though her attention was entirely consumed by the quiet tragedy of his face. 

Beyond the hedge lay the ancient road where field-carts traveled, an opening left intentionally so the family might witness the timeless, agrarian rituals of the upland. Presently, the heavy, rhythmic thud of draft horses approached. A wagon laden with massive, raw timber labored past. The grand muscles of the beasts strained visibly, their hooves striking the earth with a resonant, percussive force. The driver ran alongside, snapping his leather whip—a sharp, cracking sound that split the winter quiet—guiding the leader’s head with the guttural calls of an older, native dialect. The raw smell of freshly cut wood and churning earth seemed to permeate the glass.

The sheer, physical vitality of the scene shook Rex from his torpor. He rose, watching intently until the last quivering trunk disappeared over the ridge, then paced the length of the room. When he sat down again, the vacant stare had vanished. Sensing this return of spirit, Anna brought a small stool to his knee, looking up with an expression that begged him to translate his silence.

“I’ll tell you what I’m thinking of, Nannie,” he said, his voice finding a tentative rhythm. “I will go to Canada, or somewhere of that sort.” 

“Oh, Rex, not for always!”

“Yes, to get my bread there. I should like to build a hut, and work hard at clearing, and have everything wild about me, and a great wide quiet.” The modern, industrialized chatter of their society, the hollow clinking of fine china and superficial pleasantries, had deafened him; he longed for an acoustic purity, a landscape where the only sounds were the strike of the axe and the wind in the pines.

“And not take me with you?” Anna said, the hot tears welling. “How could I?”

“I should like it better than anything,” she insisted. “Settlers go with their families. I would sooner go there than stay here in England. I could sustain us, Rex. I would build the fires of fragrant cedar, and slow-roast the game you bring back. I would cultivate a mother-dough from the wild yeast of the air, and press heavy, sour loaves into cast-iron pans. We would pull our root vegetables straight from the frost, just as we did when we were young, playing at life under the heavy wool drugget-tent with our little plates. It would be a true life.”

“Father and mother would not let you go.”

“Yes, I think they would, when I explained the economy of it. It would preserve our resources, and papa would have more to bring up the boys with.”

They wove this dream of ancestral self-sufficiency together throughout the morning, ultimately agreeing that Anna should accompany him into their father’s study to present the plan. 

When they entered, Mr. Gascoigne greeted them cheerfully. “Well, my children!” he boomed. The study possessed its own heavy, insulated acoustic—muffled by thick Persian rugs and walls of leather-bound theology, a space where the dominant power structures of the old world were firmly entrenched. 

“May we sit down with you a little, papa?” Anna asked. “Rex has something to say.”

“With all my heart.”

They formed a striking, multi-generational tableau, three iterations of a singular genetic lineage. They shared the same straight brow, the nose that angled firmly into an aquiline ridge, the strong, well-hung chin. Mr. Gascoigne was the massive, fully realized elder of the tribe, marked by the deep perpendicular line of authority between his brows. Rex was the fragile echo of his father’s youth, stripped of command, raw and bleeding like a wounded animal in the brush. Anna was Rex’s diminutive shadow, her hair drawn tightly back, her expressions shifting in perfect, wordless synchrony with his.

Rex attempted to articulate his deeply subjective, untranslatable pain. “You know all about what has upset me, father. I am quite done up for life in this part of the world. The noise of it... I cannot go back to Oxford. I couldn’t read. I should fail, and waste your resources. I want your consent to take another course, sir. If you would allow me a small outfit, I should like to go to the colonies and work on the land there.” 

Anna immediately joined the plea. “And with me, papa. Rex would need me to keep the hearth, to mill the grain and tend the fires. We shall neither of us marry now. I should cost nothing, and I should be so happy returning to a simpler, quieter way of living. We two should be no trouble to you any more.” She slipped onto her father’s knee, leaning into him, seeking the tactile warmth of his agrarian bulk.

But Mr. Gascoigne did not smile. He held her there firmly, gently pinning her in place as he turned the formidable machinery of his intellect upon his son. “You will admit that my experience gives me some power of judging for you, and that I can probably guide you in practical matters better than you can guide yourself?”

Rex, trapped by the inflexible syntax of filial obedience, muttered, “Yes, sir.”

“And perhaps you will admit that you are bound in duty to consider my judgment and wishes?”

“I have never yet placed myself in opposition to you, sir.” In his gut, Rex felt the primal urge to flee to the woods, but the institutional power of his father’s lexicon paralyzed him. 

“But you will do so if you persist in setting your mind toward a rash and foolish procedure,” the rector declared, his voice a commanding instrument that drowned out Rex’s quiet despair. “You think, I suppose, that you have had a shock which has changed your inclinations, unfitted you for anything but manual labor, and given you a dislike to society?”

“Something like that,” Rex stammered, lacking the vocabulary to explain how the world had lost its flavor. “I shall never be up to the sort of work I must do to live in this part of the world. I haven't the spirit for it. A young fellow should be allowed to choose his way of life... to go where there are empty places.”

“But suppose I am convinced on good evidence that this state of mind is transient,” Mr. Gascoigne countered smoothly, effortlessly asserting his linguistic dominance. “If you went off, you would repent your regression. You have no right whatever to expatriate yourself, to play at working in your shirt-sleeves with spade and hatchet, until you have honestly endeavored to turn your formal education to account. I say nothing of the grief to your mother and me.”

Rex was silenced. The raw, bleeding dialect of his heartbreak had no translation that could withstand the polished, irrefutable logic of his father’s world. His yearning for the quiet labor of the colonies lay buried deep within him, but he surrendered to the crushing weight of the ancestral hierarchy. 

“You assent to my arrangement, then?” Mr. Gascoigne asked, his tone closing like a vise around his son’s future. 

“I’ll try what I can do, sir,” Rex whispered, though the food of this life tasted like ash in his mouth.

When the door closed behind him, Anna wept against her father’s chest. “Oh, papa, it is very hard for him. Doesn’t he look ill?”

“Yes, but he will soon be better; it will all blow over,” the patriarch assured her, dictating the narrative once more. “And now, Anna, be as quiet as a mouse about it all. Never let it be mentioned.”

“No, papa,” she agreed, retreating into her private grief. *But I would not be like Gwendolen for anything,* she thought, despising the destructive, consuming vanity that had ruined her brother. 

Though she dared not speak of it, Anna inwardly mourned the loss of their colonial dream. She turned the vision over and over in her mind: the scent of woodsmoke, the heavy, rhythmic kneading of dough, the profound silence of the wilderness. To trade that deep, sustaining life for this hollow existence—for the abrasive, synthetic rustle of expanding crinolines that scraped against the ears, and the endless, superficial chatter of society dinners where language was stripped of all true nourishment—felt like the deepest starvation of all. During a period when the burgeoning width of skirts seemed to demand the architectural enlargement of churches and ballrooms, a testament to an era obsessed with occupying space and generating noise, Anna Gascoigne retreated inward, mourning the quiet, earthy rhythms that were slowly being entirely forgotten.
