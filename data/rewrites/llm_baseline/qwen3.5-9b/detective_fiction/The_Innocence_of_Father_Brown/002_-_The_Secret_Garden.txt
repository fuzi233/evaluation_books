Chief Aristide Valentin of the Paris Police was running late for dinner, and his guests were beginning to gather in the entrance hall before he arrived. However, they felt reassured by the presence of his confidential servant, Ivan. An old man with a scarred face and moustaches as grey as his skin, Ivan always occupied a seat in the hall—a space dominated by an impressive display of weaponry. Valentin's residence was as peculiar and legendary as its master. It was an ancient house surrounded by high walls and towering poplars that nearly touched the Seine. Its strangest feature, and perhaps its greatest security asset, was architectural: there was no exit from the house other than the front door, which was guarded by Ivan and the arsenal. While numerous doors led from the interior into the vast, elaborate gardens, there was no way out of the garden itself. Encircling it was a tall, smooth, unclimbable wall topped with specialized spikes. It was hardly a welcoming environment for reflection, especially for a man in whom a hundred criminals had sworn to exact vengeance.

As Ivan explained to the arriving guests, their host had delayed ten minutes due to urgent matters. In truth, Valentin was finalizing arrangements concerning executions—duties that, though deeply repugnant to his nature, he executed with chilling precision. Ruthless in hunting criminals, he was surprisingly mild regarding their punishment. Having shaped French—and largely European—police methodology, his profound influence had been honorably directed toward mitigating sentences and purifying prisons. He was considered one of France's great humanitarian freethinkers; the only flaw in his philosophy, critics argued, was that he made mercy even colder than justice.

When Valentin finally appeared, he was already attired in black with the red rosette of his office. An elegant figure with a dark beard streaked with grey, he moved directly to his study, which opened onto the grounds behind. The garden door stood ajar. After securing his personal effects in their designated spot, he paused at the threshold, gazing out at the garden. A sharp moon fought against the drifting debris of a recent storm, casting a light that stirred a wistfulness unusual in such a scientifically minded man. Perhaps such intellects possess a subconscious premonition of life's greatest problems. Yet, Valentin quickly shed this occult mood, knowing he was late and that his guests awaited him. A quick scan of the drawing-room confirmed his main guest was not yet present.

He observed the other pillars of the gathering: Lord Galloway, the English Ambassador, a choleric elder with a russet complexion resembling a ripe apple and wearing the blue sash of the Order of the Garter; his wife, Lady Galloway, slender and ethereal with silver hair and a superior countenance; their daughter, Lady Margaret Graham, a pale beauty with an elfin face and copper hair; the Duchess of Mont St. Michel, black-eyed and opulent, accompanied by her two similarly striking daughters; Dr. Simon, a quintessential French scientist with spectacles, a pointed brown beard, and a forehead etched with the parallel wrinkles of perpetual superiority; and Father Brown of Cobhole, Essex, whom Valentin had recently encountered in England. Finally, there was a tall man in uniform who had bowed stiffly to the Galloways without receiving a warm welcome. This was Commandant O'Brien of the French Foreign Legion. Slim yet swaggering, clean-shaven, dark-haired, and blue-eyed, he possessed the dashing yet melancholy air characteristic of that famous regiment of "victorious failures and successful suicides." Born an Irish gentleman, he had known the Galloways in his youth, particularly Margaret. Having fled his homeland following a financial collapse, he now displayed his disdain for British etiquette by moving freely in uniform, complete with sabre and spurs. When he bowed to the Ambassador's family, Lord and Lady Galloway responded with stiff nods, while Lady Margaret averted her gaze.

Yet, despite the potential history between these individuals, Valentin held no particular interest in them. None were the true guest of the evening. He awaited a man of global renown, a friend acquired during his detective expeditions and triumphs in America: Julius K. Brayne. A multi-millionaire whose colossal endowments to various small religions had provided ample fodder for both jest and solemnity in American and English press, Brayne remained an enigma. Was he an atheist, a Mormon, or a Christian Scientist? It mattered little; he was prepared to fund any intellectual vessel, provided it was untested. Among his hobbies was waiting for the "American Shakespeare"—a patience surpassing that of an angler. He admired Walt Whitman but deemed Luke P. Tanner of Pennsylvania more "progressive" daily. Above all, he championed progressivism, a trait he mistakenly attributed to Valentin.

Brayne's arrival was as definitive as a dinner bell. Possessing the rare quality that his presence filled a room as much as his absence emptied it, the massive man—tall and wide, clad entirely in black without a single accessory save a watch chain—entered. His hair was white and brushed back with Germanic precision; his face was red, fierce, and cherubic, marred only by a dark tuft under his lower lip that gave his otherwise infantile visage a theatrical, Mephistophelean twist. Before the salon could fully absorb the celebrity, his tardiness became a domestic emergency, and he was swiftly escorted to the dining room by Lady Galloway.

The Galloways were generally genial, barring one condition: Lady Margaret must not take the arm of the adventuring Commandant O'Brien. She had complied, entering the dining room decorously alongside Dr. Simon. Nevertheless, Lord Galloway was restless and bordering on rudeness. Diplomatic during the meal, he became undiplomatic once the younger men—the doctor, the priest, and the exiled O'Brien—retreated to the conservatory with the ladies. Every sixty seconds, the thought stung him that O'Brien might be signaling to Margaret; he struggled to envision how. Left afterward with Brayne, the credulous Yankee, and Valentin, the skeptical Frenchman, the conversation devolved into tedious arguments about science versus religion. Neither could sway the other. Eventually, bored, Lord Galloway rose to seek the drawing room. Lost in long corridors for several minutes, he finally heard the doctor's didactic voice, the priest's dull tone, and general laughter. Assuming they were debating theology, he cursed inwardly and pushed open the salon door.

Inside, he saw only emptiness. Commandant O'Brien was gone, and so was Lady Margaret.

Impatient, Galloway stamped back toward the rear of the house, where Valentin's study lay. His obsession with shielding his daughter from the Irish adventurer had grown into something central, even mad. Surprisingly, he encountered his daughter sweeping past with a white, scornful expression—a second mystery. If she had been with O'Brien, where was he? If not, where had she been? Driven by senile suspicion, he navigated the dark wings of the mansion until finding a servant's entrance opening onto the garden.

The moon, having cleared the storm clouds, cast argent light across the four corners of the garden. A tall figure in blue strides across the lawn toward the study door, the silver lining of his uniform catching the light. It was Commandant O'Brien. He vanished through the French windows, leaving Lord Galloway in an indescribable rage—virulent and vague. The blue-and-silver garden seemed to mock him, a stage set for romantic folly against which his worldly authority was powerless. The grace of the Irishman's stride enraged him as if he were a rival, not a father; the moonlight drove him mad. Trapped in a garden of troubadours, a Watteau dreamscape, Galloway attempted to dispel the madness with speech, stepping briskly after his perceived enemy. Tripping over a stone or root, he paused, first with irritation, then with curiosity. Instantly, the moon and the tall poplars witnessed an absurd sight: an elderly English diplomat sprinting frantically, crying or bellowing.

His hoarse shouts drew a pale face to the study door: Dr. Simon, with beamng glasses and a worried brow. Hearing the nobleman's first clear words, Simon understood immediately. Lord Galloway was screaming, "A corpse in the grass—a blood-stained corpse!" O'Brien had completely lost his mind.

"We must inform Valentin at once," the doctor urged as Galloway broke down describing what he had seen. "Fortunately, he is here." Just then, the great detective entered the study, drawn by the cry. Typical of Valentin, his demeanor shifted instantly from concerned host to grim investigator.

"Strange," Valentin remarked as they hurried into the garden, "that I should hunt mysteries across the globe, only to have one settle in my own backyard. But where is the location?" Crossing the lawn, which was now veiling in a slight mist from the river, they found the body sunk deep in the grass. It belonged to a very tall, broad-shouldered man lying face-down. Only his massive shoulders, clad in black cloth, and his bald head—save for a few wisps of brown hair clinging like wet seaweed—were visible. A scarlet trail of blood oozed from beneath his fallen face.

"At least," Dr. Simon intoned with a deep, singular cadence, "he is not one of our party."

"Examine him, Doctor," Valentin commanded sharply. "He may not be dead."
