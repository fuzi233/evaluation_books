Once Bratti had merged into the throng of gossipers, the young stranger, resigned to his inability to discern the source of the city's feverish excitement and indifferent to the particulars that mattered only to native Florentines, grew weary of waiting for his escort. He decided instead to wander the piazza, scanning the stalls for a vendor of provisions who might possess less than the usual fervor for public news. Suddenly, as if prompted by a sudden realization, he thrust his hand into the pouch hanging at his waist, rummaging through it with a look of mounting frustration.

"Not a single coin, by Jupiter!" he muttered, in a tongue that was neither Tuscan nor Italian. "I believed I had one poor piece left. I shall have to seek my breakfast for love, then!"

He had not taken many steps before it appeared he had stumbled upon a quarter of the market where such currency would not be spurned. In a secluded corner, far from the clusters of talkers, two mules stood adorned with red tassels and collars. One bore wooden milk vessels; the other, panniers overflowing with herbs and salads. Leaning her elbow against the neck of the milk-laden beast was a young girl, seemingly no older than sixteen, her face framed by a red hood that concealed her hair entirely. Her prettiness was all the more striking in its innocence, hidden as it was. Perhaps exhausted by her morning labor in the twilight, preparing for the trek from a castle several miles away, she had fallen asleep in that half-standing, half-leaning posture. Yet, the stranger felt no hesitation in waking her; his approach was so gentle that to the sleeping damsel, it seemed merely as though a sprig of thyme had brushed her lips as she bent to gather herbs.

The dream shattered, however, as she opened wide, baby-blue eyes and sprang up in astonishment and confusion to find the stranger standing close before her. She heard his voice, strange yet soft, and though she might have dismissed it as unintelligible had she been more composed, her first instinct was to turn her head slightly and lift a corner of her green serge mantle as a shield. He repeated his words.

"Forgive me, fair one, for disturbing your rest. I am dying of hunger, and the scent of milk makes breakfast seem infinitely more desirable."

He had chosen the phrase *muoio di fame* because he knew it would be familiar to her ears; he spoke it playfully, with the intonation of a mendicant. This time, he was understood. The corner of the mantle dropped, and moments later, a large cup of fragrant milk was offered to him. He accepted it without further compliment, raising it to his lips. As he drank, the little maiden gathered the courage to look up at the long, dark curls of this stranger with the singular voice. He had asked for food in the tones of a beggar, yet, though his clothes were tattered, he bore no resemblance to any beggar she had ever known.

While this scrutiny took place, another current of feeling moved her hand into a bag hanging beside the mule. When the stranger set down his cup, he saw a large piece of bread held out toward him, catching a glimpse of those blue eyes that seemed to encourage him to accept this additional gift.

"But perhaps that is your own breakfast," he said. "No, I have had enough without payment. A thousand thanks, my gentle one."

There was no verbal response, yet the bread was pushed a fraction closer, as if impatient at his refusal. As the stranger's long dark eyes rested on the infantine face, it seemed the girl was gathering more courage to look up and meet his gaze.

"Ah, then, if I must take the bread," he said, laying his hand upon it, "I shall grow bolder still, and beg for another kiss to make the bread sweeter."

His speech was becoming wonderfully intelligible despite the strange timbre of his voice, which had initially seemed a barrier. She blushed deeply and lifted the corner of her mantle to her mouth again. Just as the presumptuous stranger leaned forward, his fingers brushing the arm that held up the screening mantle, he was startled by a harsh voice close to his ear.

"Who are _you_—with a murrain to you? No honest buyer, I'll warrant, but a hanger-on of the dicers—or something worse. Go! Dance off and find fitter company, or I'll give you a tune to a quicker time than you'll like."

The young stranger drew back, meeting the speaker's glare with a look provocatively free of alarm or deprecation. His slight expression of saucy amusement broke into a broad, beaming smile as he surveyed the figure of his threatener. She was a stout, brawny woman, wearing a man's jerkin over her green serge gown, with the peaked hood of some departed mantle fastened around her sunburnt face. Beneath all its coarseness and premature wrinkles, her face showed a half-sad, half-ludicrous maternal resemblance to the tender baby-face of her daughter—a resemblance that often serves as a more croaking, shudder-inducing prophecy than that of the death's-head.

There was something irresistibly propitiating in that bright young smile, but Monna Ghita was no woman to betray weakness. She continued speaking with heightened exasperation.

"Yes, yes, you can grin as well as other monkeys in cap and jerkin. You're a minstrel or a mountebank, I'll be sworn; you look for all the world as silly as a tumbler when he has been upside down and has scrambled back onto his heels. And what fool's tricks hast thou been after, Tessa?" she added, turning to her daughter, whose frightened face was more inviting to abuse. "Giving away milk and victuals, it seems? Ay, ay, thou'dst carry water in thy ears for any idle vagabond that didn't like to stoop for it, thou silly staring rabbit! Turn thy back and lift the herbs from the panniers, else I'll make thee say a few Aves without counting."

"Nay, Madonna," said the stranger with a pleading smile, "do not be angry with your pretty Tessa for taking pity on a hungry traveler who found himself unexpectedly without a quattrino. Your handsome face looks so well when it frowns that I long to see it illuminated by a smile."

"_Va via_! I know what paste you are made of. You may tickle me with that straw a good long while before I shall laugh, I can tell you. Get along with a bad Easter, else I'll make a beauty-spot or two on that face of yours that shall spoil your kissing before Advent."

As Monna Ghita lifted her formidable talons to enforce her commands, Bratti, who had arrived a moment prior, was saying to his companion, "What think you of this pretty parrot, Nello? Doesn't his tongue smack of Venice?"

"Nay, Bratti," said the barber in an undertone, "thy wisdom has much of the ass in it, as I told thee just now; especially regarding the ears. This stranger is a Greek, else I am not the barber who has had the sole and exclusive shaving of the excellent Demetrio, and drawn more than one sorry tooth from his learned jaw. And this youth might be taken to have come straight from Olympus—at least once he has had a touch of my razor."

"_Orsù_! Monna Ghita!" continued Nello, not sorry to see some sport. "What has happened to cause such a thunderstorm? Has this young stranger been misbehaving himself?"

"By San Giovanni!" said the cautious Bratti, who had not shaken off his original suspicions concerning the shabbily-clad possessor of jewels, "he did right to run away from _me_, if he meant to get into mischief. I can swear that I found him under the Loggia de' Cerchi, with a ring on his finger such as I've seen worn by Bernardo Rucellai himself. Not another rusty nail's worth do I know about him."

"The fact is," said Nello, eyeing the stranger good-humoredly, "this _bello giovane_ has been a little too presumptuous in admiring the charms of Monna Ghita, and has attempted to kiss her while her daughter's back is turned; for I observe that the pretty Tessa is too busy to look this way at present. Was it not so, Messer?" Nello concluded in a tone of courtesy.

"You have divined the offense like a soothsayer," said the stranger, laughing. "Only that I had not the good fortune to find Monna Ghita here at first. I begged a cup of milk from her daughter and had accepted this gift of bread, for which I was making a humble offering of gratitude, before I had the higher pleasure of being face to face with these riper charms which I was perhaps too bold in admiring."

"_Va, va_! Be off, every one of you, and stay in purgatory till I pay to get you out, will you?" said Monna Ghita, fiercely, elbowing Nello and leading her mule forward to compel the stranger to step aside. "Tessa, thou simpleton, bring forward thy mule a bit: the cart will be upon us."

As Tessa turned to take the mule's bridle, she cast one timid glance at the stranger, who was now moving with Nello out of the way of an approaching market-cart. The glance lasted just long enough to catch the beckoning movement of his hand, indicating that he had been watching for this opportunity of an adieu.

"_Ebbene_," said Bratti, raising his voice to speak across the cart, "I leave you with Nello, young man, for there's no pushing my bag and basket any farther, and I have business at home. But you'll remember our bargain, because if you found Tessa without me, it was not my fault. Nello will show you my shop in the Ferravecchi, and I'll not turn my back on you."

"A thousand thanks, friend!" said the stranger, laughing, and then turned away with Nello up the narrow street that led most directly to the Piazza del Duomo.
