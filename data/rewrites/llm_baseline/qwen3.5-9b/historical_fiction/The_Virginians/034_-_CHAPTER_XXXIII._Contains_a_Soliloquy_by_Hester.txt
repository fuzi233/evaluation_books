Colonel Lambert's initial reaction to discovering the secret hidden within his youngest daughter's heart was anger directed squarely at the young man responsible. "A plague upon all scapegraces, whether English or Indian!" he bellowed to his wife. "I wish this fellow had broken his nose against every doorpost in the house except our own!"

"Perhaps we are destined to cure him of being a scapegrace, my dear," Mrs. Lambert interposed gently. "And that fall at our doorstep holds something providential. You laughed at me, Mr. Lambert, when I suggested this before; yet if Heaven did not send this young gentleman to us, who did? It may well be for the blessing and happiness of us all that he arrived."

"It's hard, Molly!" the Colonel groaned. "We cherish and fondle them, rear them through sickness and health, toil and scheme, and hoard money in stockings while patching our own old coats. If they have a headache, we cannot sleep; if they have a whim, we work day and night to grant it. They are darling daddy and dearest pappy, and whose father is like ours? Then, on Tuesday morning, I am king of my household. But on Tuesday evening, Prince Whippersnapper appears, and my reign ends. A whole life is forgotten and forsaken for a pair of blue eyes, a pair of lean legs, and a head of yellow hair."

"'Tis written that we women must leave everything to follow our husbands," the matron said, placing her hand on her husband's arm. "I think our courtship was not very long, dear Martin!"

"'Tis human nature, and what can you expect of the jade?" the Colonel sighed.

"And I believe I fulfilled my duty to my husband, though I admit I left my father for him," Mrs. Lambert added softly.

"Excellent wench! Perdition catch my soul, but I do love thee, Molly!" the good Colonel declared. "But then, mind you, your father never loved me; and if I ever have sons-in-law—"

"Ever, indeed! Of course my girls are to have husbands, Mr. Lambert!" cried the mother.

"Well, when they come, I'll hate them, madam, just as your father hated me; and quite rightly, for taking his treasure away from him."

"Don't be irreligious and unnatural, Martin Lambert! I say you are unnatural, sir!" the matron continued.

"Nay, my dear, I have an old tooth in my left jaw here," the Colonel replied, tapping his chin. "'Tis natural for the tooth to come out. But when the tooth-drawer pulls it, 'tis natural that I should feel pain. Do you suppose, madam, that I do not love Hetty better than any tooth in my head?"

Yet no woman is ever truly averse to the idea of her daughter finding a husband, even if fathers revolt against the intrusion of the son-in-law. Mothers and grandmothers, in fact, are remarried anew in the marriages of their young ones; their souls don fresh laces and muslins reminiscent of forty years past, the postilion's white ribbons bloom once more, and they flutter into the post-chaise, driving away. What woman, regardless of age, has not stored away bridal favors and raiment, packed in lavender in the deepest corners of her heart?

"It will be a sad thing, parting with her," Mrs. Lambert continued, sighing.

"You have settled that point already, Molly," the Colonel laughed. "Had I not best go out and order raisins and currants for the wedding cake?"

"And then I shall have to leave the house in their charge when I go to her in Virginia. How many miles is it to Virginia, Martin? I should think it must be thousands."

"A hundred and seventy-three thousand three hundred and ninety-one and three-quarters, my dear, by the near way—that through Prester John's country. By the other route, through Persia—"

"Oh, give me the one with the least amount of sea, and your horrid ships, which I simply cannot bear!" cried the Colonel's spouse.

"I hope Rachel Esmond and I shall become better friends. She had a very high spirit when we were girls at school."

"Shall we not attend to the baby linen, Mrs. Martin Lambert?" her husband interjected, sensing a shift in mood.

Mrs. Lambert likely assumed there was no cause for wonderment, having recently admired some lovely lace caps and bibs in Mrs. Bobbinit's toy shop. That Sunday afternoon, when the discovery was made, while little Hetty lay upon her pillow with feverish cheeks, closed eyes, and a pitiful expression, her mother regarded the child with remarkable equanimity, seeming almost pleased by Hetty's distress.

The girl was not merely unhappy; she was enraged with herself for having revealed her secret. Perhaps she had not fully understood her own state until the sudden emotion clarified it. Now, the little maid behaved as if she had committed a grave wrongdoing and been caught red-handed. Indignant at her own weakness, she erupted in transports of self-loathing, vowing never to forgive herself for such humiliation. Like the wounded young stag chafing in rage against the hunter's arrow, she bit and snarled at her sister-cubs and the leopardess, her spotted mother. Little Hetty tore and gnawed and growled so ferociously that I would not wish to be her sibling, her mother, or her father.

"What business has any young woman," she cried aloud, "to indulge in such nonsense? Mamma, I ought to be whipped and sent to bed. I know perfectly well that Mr. Warrington does not care a fig about me. I daresay he prefers French actresses and the commonest little milliner-girl in the toy shop to me. And so he ought, and so they are better than me. Why, what a fool I am to burst into tears like a ninny about nothing, simply because Mr. Wolfe said Harry plays cards on Sundays! I know he is not clever like Papa. I believe he is stupid—I am certain he is stupid—but he is not so stupid as I am. Why, of course, I cannot marry him. How am I to go to America and leave you and Theo? Of course, he likes someone else, perhaps in America, or Tunbridge, or Jericho, or somewhere. He is a prince in his own country and cannot possibly think of marrying a poor half-pay officer's daughter with only twopence to her fortune. Did you not tell me, when I was a baby, that I cried wanting the moon? I am a baby now, a most absurd, silly little baby—do not talk to me, Mrs. Lambert, I am one. Only, there is this to be said: he knows nothing about it, and I would rather cut out my tongue than tell him."

Dire were the threats Hetty leveled against Theo, should her sister betray her. As for the infant Charley, whose mind was entirely fixed on cheese-cakes, he remained oblivious to Miss Hester's turmoil. Naturally, the parents and the kind sister promised to keep the little maid's secret safe.

"I begin to think it had been best for us to stay at home," sighed Mrs. Lambert to her husband.

"Nay, my dear," the other replied. "Human nature will always be human nature. Surely Hetty's mother told me herself that she had a slight liking for a certain young curate before falling head-over-heels in love with a certain young officer of Kingsley's. And as for me, my heart was wounded in a dozen places before Miss Molly Benson took entire possession of it. Our sons and daughters must follow in the footsteps of their parents, I suppose. Indeed, yesterday you were scolding me for grumbling at Miss Hetty's precocious fancies. To do the child justice, she disguises her feelings entirely, and I defy Mr. Warrington to guess from her behavior how she feels toward him."

"A daughter of mine and yours, Martin," the mother cried with great dignity, "is not going to fling herself at a gentleman's head!"

"Neither herself nor the teacup, my dear," answered the Colonel.

"Little Miss Het treats Mr. Warrington like a vixen. He never visits us without her boxing his ears in one fashion or another. I protest she is barely civil to him; but, knowing what is passing in the young hypocrite's mind, I am not going to be angry at her rudeness."

"She needs no reason to be rude, Martin; and our girl is good enough for any gentleman in England or America. Why, if their ages suit, shouldn't they marry after all, sir?"

"Why, if he wants her, shouldn't he ask her, my dear? I am sorry we came. I intend to put the horses into the carriage and turn their heads toward home immediately."

"But mamma fondly insisted, 'Depend on it, my dear, that these matters are wisely ordained for us. Depend upon it, Martin, it was not for nothing that Harry Warrington was brought to our gate in that manner; and that he and our children are thus brought together again. If that marriage has been decreed in Heaven, a marriage it will be.'"
