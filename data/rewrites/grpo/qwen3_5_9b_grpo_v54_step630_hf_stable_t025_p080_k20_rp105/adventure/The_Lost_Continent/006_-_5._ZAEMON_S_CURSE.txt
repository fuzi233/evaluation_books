The glittering cavalcade halted before the massive stone entrance of the royal pyramid, massing in a sea of gold and silk in the great paved square. The mammoth, a beast of immense stature, was tethered before the doorway. As the trumpets blared, signaling our descent from the golden half-castle draped in the canopy of snakes, I stepped onto the ground.

It was clear we were moving from the open sky into the labyrinthine depths of the pyramid. Instinctively, a reflex of custom and reverence that had long since become part of my nature compelled me to turn toward the towering rocks of the Sacred Mountain looming above the city. I bowed low, offering the silent, prescribed prayer to the old Gods. I performed this act unthinkingly, as a matter of rote duty. Yet, as I rose, a sharp, dissonant sound cut through the air—a titter of laughter emanating from the crowd of onlookers.

I frowned, scanning the faces of the scoffers, and immediately turned to Phorenice to demand justice for their insolence. But a strange stillness gripped me. I had expected to see her mirroring my gesture, kneeling in obeisance. Instead, she stood erect, her forehead never having touched the dust. She regarded me with a look of peculiar intensity that I could not decipher.

She made no move to address the crowd's mockery. Instead, she simply said, "Come," and turned toward the doorway, crying out the secret word appointed for the day. The ponderous stone blocks barring the porch swung back on their hinges. With stately tread, she passed from the hot sunshine into the cool, shadowed gloom beyond, followed decorously by the fan-girl. A heaviness settled in my chest. I followed her inside, and the stone doors slammed shut with a sullen thud, sealing us away from the world.

We did not travel far. Phorenice halted in the Hall of Waiting. I remembered this place vividly: the red walls painted with the likenesses of kings, and the burning fountain of earth-breath that blazed from a bronze jet in the center of the floor, casting its light upon the space. Twenty years prior, the old King had welcomed me here before sending me to my vice-royalty in Yucatan. But the air had changed. Once pure and sweet, it now hung heavy with a cloying scent, making the atmosphere languid and oppressive.

"My minister," the Empress said, her voice cool. "I acquit you of intentional insult. But I believe the colonial air has rendered you a very simple man. Such an obeisance to that mountain a moment ago has not been made since I was sent to reign over this kingdom."

"Your Majesty," I replied, standing tall. "I am a member of the Priests' Clan, raised in their tenets. Before entering any house, I am taught to thank the Gods, and especially our Lord the Sun, for the air they provide. It has been my fate more than once to be chased by streams of fire and stinking vapors amidst the mountains during their boils. Therefore, I recite the prescribed prayer from the heart."

"Circumstances have changed since you left Atlantis," Phorenice countered. "And when thanks are given now, they are not thrown at those old Gods."

The implication struck me like a physical blow. If this was the new order, I would have no part in it. Serving a monarch was one thing; to palter with sacrilege, to accept a swineherd's daughter as a deity worthy of prayer, revolted my very manhood. I invited a crisis.

"Phorenice," I said, my voice steady. "I have been a priest from childhood, revering the Gods and growing intimate with their mysteries. Until I find for myself that those old things are false, I must stand by that allegiance. If there is a cost for this faithfulness, I must pay it."

She looked at me with a slow, calculating smile. "You are a strong man, Deucalion."

I bowed.

"I have heard others as stubborn," she continued, shaking out the ruddy bunches of her hair so the light of the burning earth-breath illuminated the perfection of her face. "But they were converted. I have found it as easy to convert the stubborn as to burn them. Indeed, there has been little talk of burning; they have all rushed to conversion, whether I would or no. It seems my poor looks and tongue lack charm today."

"Phorenice is Empress," I said stolidly. "I am her servant. Tomorrow, if she grants leave, I will clear away the rabble clamoring outside the walls. I must begin to prove my utility."

"I am told you are a pretty fighter," she said. "I possess some skill in arms myself and consider myself a judge. Tomorrow, we shall taste battle together. But today, I must complete the honorable reception I have planned. The feast will be ready soon, and you will wish to prepare. Chambers have been selected for your use, stocked with necessities. Ylga will show you the way."

We waited until Phorenice had vanished into the shadows of the hall's far angle, then, guided by the fan-girl who carried a lamp, we threaded our way through the narrow, twisting mazes of the pyramid.

The air was thick with perfumes, and the passages doubled back upon themselves with such cunning complexity that a stranger might spend days searching for a single chamber. These forgotten builders had ensured that kings could not be slain by vulgar assassins entering from without. Legend held that the king of the time had killed everyone who had built the pyramid or even seen the layout of its inner stones.

Yet, the fan-girl led us with practiced ease. She pushed stones to reveal passages, pressed flagstones to uncover steep stairs, and navigated the inclines with the confidence of one born to these halls. Finally, we arrived at the chamber designated for me.

"There is raiment in these chests by the walls," the girl said, gesturing to the bronze coffer filled with jewels. "These are Phorenice's first presents, a small earnest of what is to come. My Lord Deucalion can drop his simplicity and dress in finery to suit the fashion."

"Girl," I said sharply, "be more decorous with your tongue and spare me such advice."

"If my Lord Deucalion thinks this a rudeness, he can give a word to Phorenice, and I shall be whipped. If he asks, I can be stripped and scourged before him. The Empress will do much for Deucalion just now."

"You are nearer to that whipping than you think," I warned.

"I have a name," she retorted, looking at me sullenly from under black brows. "They call me Ylga. You might have heard it as we rode on the mammoth, had you not been so wrapped up in Phorenice."

I studied her curiously. "You have never seen me before, and your first words are those that could bring trouble upon yourself. There is some object in all this."

She pushed the massive stone of the chamber door and drew me away from the airshaft into the far corner. "I am the daughter of Zaemon," she whispered. "Whom you knew."

"You bring me a message from him?"

"How could I? He lives in the priests' dwellings on the Mountain you bowed to. I have not seen him in two years. But when I saw you step out from that red pavilion at the harbor, I felt a pity for you, Deucalion. I remembered you were my father's friend, and I knew what Phorenice had in store. She has been plotting this for two months."

"I cannot hear words against the Empress."

"And yet..." She stamped her sandal on the stone floor. "You must be either very blind or very daring. But I shall not interfere further—not now. Still, I shall watch. If you ever seem to want a friend, I will try to serve you."

"I thank you for your friendship."

"You take it lightly. Even now, I do not believe you know my power, nor my motive. You may be the first man in this kingdom, but let me tell you, I rank as the second lady. Remember, women stand high in Atlantis now. Believe me, my friendship is a commodity sought with frequency and industry."

"As I say, I am grateful. You seem to think little of my gratitude, Ylga, but credit me: I have never bestowed it upon a woman before. Treasure it for its rarity."

"Well, my lord, there is an education before you." She left me then, showing me how to summon slaves. For a full minute, I stood wondering at the words I had spoken. Who was the daughter of Zaemon that she should induce me to change the habits of a lifetime?

Slaves arrived eager to deck me in robes and gauds, offering a dozen perfumes and unguents. Their manner irritated me. I was clean, shaved, and my robe was unsoiled. Considering their attentions an impertinence, I ordered them to beat one another, promising that if they failed to do so thoroughly, I would hand them to the brander for stripes that would endure. It is strange, but a common menial can often surpass a rebellious general in the power to ruffle one's temper.

What shocked me most that day, however, was Phorenice's method of summoning guests to her feast. I sat in my chamber awaiting the call when, gradually, a sound grew from nothingness into a clanging din that hurt the ears. Then, the entire massive fabric of the pyramid trembled and groaned, shaking as though it were a child's wooden toy brushed by a strong hand.

This was the portent served yearly by the chiefs of the Priests' Clan on the Sacred Mountain, a sacred reminder that man was born from roaring fire and earth-tremors, and would eventually be swallowed up by them along with his sins. Here, the Empress was prostituting these solemnities into a mere call to gluttony, a signal for ribald laughter and sensuous display.

How had she acquired the authority to do this? Who was she to tamper with the dimly understood powers dwelling within the liquid heart of the earth? Had there been treachery? Had a member of the Priests' Clan betrayed the sacred vows? Or had Phorenice discovered a key to these mysteries with her own agile brain?

If the latter, I could continue to serve her with a silent conscience. She was Empress, and obedience was my duty. But if she had suborned a weaker member of the Clan, that would be a different matter. Our constitution demanded that we preserve our secrets inviolate and pursue with undying hatred both the betrayer and the recipient of his confidence.

With very undecided feelings, I obeyed the summons and allowed the slaves to lead me to the great banqueting hall. The scene was dazzling. The majestic chamber, carved with marvels, was filled with a company decked in every conceivable gaud and color. They seemed to ignore the solemn portent that had summoned them, the death stalking the city wards, or the rebels leaguered beyond the walls. They were gluttonous for the passions of the moment, their fashion dictating that they look at nothing else.

Flaming jets of earth-breath lit the hall to the brightness of midday. When I stepped onto the pavement, trumpets blared to announce my coming, yet there was no roar of welcome. "Deucalion," they lisped with mincing voices, bowing ridiculously so their ornaments jangled. When Phorenice appeared, the artificiality remained. They meant well, but heartiness had been deemed barbarism by this new culture.

Chamberlains ushered me to the dais at the far end. I was to sit on Phorenice's divan and eat from her dish.

"There is no stint to the honor the Empress puts upon me," I said as I knelt.

She gave me one of her queer, sidelong looks. "Deucalion may have more, if he asks prettily. He may have what all the men in the known world have sighed for, and what none will ever get. But I have given enough of my own accord; he must ask me warmly for those further favors."

"I ask," I said, "first, that I may sweep the boundaries clear of this rabble clamoring against the city walls."

"Pah," she frowned. "Have you appetite only for the sterner pleasures of life? My good Deucalion, they must have been rustic folk in that colony of yours. Well, you shall give me news now of the toothsome nature of this feast."

Dishes and goblets were placed before us. I had little appetite for victuals so broken and highly spiced, but the other diners appreciated the finicking cookery and luscious wines to the full. They sat like a tangle of rainbows, going into raptures over every titillation of the palate as though it were a caress of the Gods.

Phorenice, with her quick, bright eyes, looked on, occasionally tossing a word to me or calling a favored creature up to receive a scrap of viand from the royal dish. The honored ones ate with extravagant gestures or, as happened twice, tucked the food into the folds of their clothes as treasures too dear to be profaned by human lips.

To me, this flattery appeared gross and disgusting, though Phorenice, through use, seemed to take it as merely her due. There was, one had to suppose, a weakness in her somewhere, though outwardly none was apparent. Her face was strong, subtle, and wondrously comely. All the courtiers raved about her beauty, and though I was not given to such appreciation, I could see they had a groundwork for their admiration; surely the Gods had never favored a mortal woman more highly. Yet, lovely though she was, I preferred to look upon Ylga, the girl, who sat on the divan behind us as immediate attendant. There was an honesty in Ylga's face which Phorenice lacked.

These feasters did not eat to nourish their bodies but to cloy themselves, strutting forth new usages with every platter. Some manners were closely touching on disrespect.
