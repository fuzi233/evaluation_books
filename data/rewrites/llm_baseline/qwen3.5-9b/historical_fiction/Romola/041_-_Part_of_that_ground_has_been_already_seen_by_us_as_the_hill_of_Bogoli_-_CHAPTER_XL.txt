When Romola sat down upon the rough stone beneath the cypress, the very air seemed to exhale a promise of liberty. The familiar confines of Florence's walls and its crowded thoroughfares lay behind her, receding into a widening distance. Her husband, Tito, was already riding toward Siena, moving away from her on one side of the horizon while she prepared to move further eastward on the other. The morning hung heavy and still, broken only by the vast depression of the roadside—a natural gulf separating her from the somber, sleeping calm of the mountains.

For the first time in her life, Romola felt truly alone in the presence of the earth and the sky. No human eye watched her; no social law dictated her movements. She breathed deeply, intoxicated by this sudden solitude.

Then, a voice cut through the silence, close to her ear.

"You are Romola de' Bardi, the wife of Tito Melema."

She knew that voice instantly. It had vibrated through her life more than once before. Because she recognized it, she did not turn around nor lift her eyes. Instead, she sat frozen, shaken by a profound awe that warred violently with an inner rebellion. It was one of the black-robed monks, daring to intrude upon her privacy, to interrupt her flight. That was all, she told herself. And yet, she felt as though Destiny—that capricious deity men often imagined—had suddenly descended, seizing her with tangible, flesh-and-blood fingers.

"You are fleeing from Florence in disguise," the voice continued, imperious and unyielding. "I hold a command from God to stop you. You are not permitted to flee."

Anger flared in Romola's chest at the intrusion, mounting with every syllable. She refused to turn, resenting the scrutiny of the examiner's gaze. Remaining perfectly motionless, she challenged him.

"What right have you to speak to me, or to hinder me?"

"The right of a messenger," the voice replied calmly. "You have donned a religious habit, yet you possess no religious purpose. You sought this garb merely as a disguise. But you were not allowed to pass undetected. It was revealed to me who you are: a woman attempting to escape the lot God has assigned you. You wish to hide your true name and your true station, choosing instead a new identity and a new existence ruled solely by your own will. I have been commanded to call you back. My daughter, you must return to your place."

With every word, Romola's spirit rose in sharper revolt. Determined not to display even a flicker of submission—fearing that any weakness would lead to total collapse—she spoke with increasing irritation.

"I will not return. I acknowledge no authority of priests or monks to dictate my actions. You have no power over me."

"I know," the voice conceded gently. "I know you have been raised in contempt for obedience. But listen closely: it is not the poor monk who claims dominion over you; it is Truth itself that commands you. And you cannot escape it. Either you submit, and it shall become your guide; or you resist, and it shall hang upon you like an iron chain, dragging you through eternity. But you *will* obey, my daughter. Your old servant returns with the mules; my companion has gone ahead to meet him. You will go back to Florence."

Suddenly, Romola sprang to her feet, her eyes blazing with defiance. She faced the speaker directly. It was Fra Girolamo. She knew him well enough now. They were nearly equal in height, their faces almost level. She had risen with a torrent of angry words ready to spill, but they died in her throat.

Fra Girolamo's gaze remained steady and calm. The impact of his look was entirely new to her, causing her anger to sink, ashamed and irrelevant. There was nothing transcendent or ethereal about Savonarola's face; it was not beautiful in the conventional sense. It was strong-featured, refined only by the rigors of mental discipline and bodily austerity. Yet, the effect of his glance upon Romola was immediate and overwhelming. It conveyed a deep, abiding interest in her, a care that existed apart from any personal affection.

It was the first time she had encountered a gaze where simple human fellowship manifested as a binding, strongly felt connection. Such a look constitutes half the vocation of a priest or spiritual guide, and Romola found herself unable to question his authority any longer. She stood silent, staring at him.

"You assert your freedom proudly, my daughter," he said softly. "But who is so base as the debtor who believes himself free?"

A stinging barb struck Romola, and her countenance altered as if a pale lightning had flashed across it.

"And you are fleeing from your debts: the debt of a Florentine woman; the debt of a wife. You are turning your back on the lot appointed for you, seeking to choose another. But can man or woman choose their duties? No more than they can choose their birthplace or their parents. My daughter, you are running from the presence of God into the wilderness."

As the heat of anger melted from her mind, it was replaced by a strange presentiment of the strength inherent in submission—if this man, whom she began to regard with a vague reverence, indeed possessed a valid law to offer her. Yet, it was impossible; he could not possibly understand what drove her. Nevertheless, she could not simply refuse guidance. Constrained by a newfound need for reverence amidst her resistance, the title she had never previously used slipped from her lips without premeditation.

"My father," she whispered, "you cannot know the reasons that compel me to go. None can know them but myself. None can judge for me. I have been driven by great sorrow. I am resolved to go."

"I know enough, my daughter," Savonarola replied, his tone illuminating with certainty. "My mind has been so far enlightened regarding you that I know enough. You are unhappy in your marriage; but I am not your confessor, and I seek to know nothing reserved for the seal of confession. I hold a divine warrant to stop you, which depends on no such private knowledge. You were warned by a message from Heaven, delivered in my presence—warned before your marriage, when you might still have lawfully chosen to remain free from the marital bond. But you chose the bond. And in willfully breaking it—I speak to you as a pagan, if the holy mystery of matrimony holds no sanctity for you—you are violating a pledge.

"Of what wrongs will you complain, my daughter, when you yourself commit one of the gravest sins a woman and a citizen can perpetrate: withdrawing in secret and disguise from a pledge sworn before God and your fellow-men? Of what grievances will you speak when you break the most fundamental law underpinning the trust between man and man—faithfulness to the spoken word? Is this the wisdom you have gained by scorning the mysteries of the Church? Not to perceive the bare duty of integrity, where the Church would teach you to see not merely integrity, but religion?"

Blood rushed to Romola's face, and she shrank back as if struck.

"I would not have put on a disguise," she began, but her voice faltered. She could not continue; the suggestion in the friar's words—that her conduct mirrored Tito's—shook her to her core.

"And to break that pledge, you flee from Florence," he pressed on. "Florence, where reside the only men and women in the world to whom you owe the debt of a fellow-citizen."

"I should never have left Florence," Romola murmured, her voice trembling, "as long as there was any hope of fulfilling a duty to my father there."

"Do you own no tie save that of a child to her father in the flesh?" Savonarola asked sharply. "Your life has been spent in blindness, my daughter. You have lived with those who sit aloof on a hill, looking down upon the lives of their neighbors with condescension. I know their vain discourses. They speak of the past, filling themselves with fancied wisdom while scorning God's work in the present. Doubtless you were taught that there were pagan women who understood how to live for the Republic, yet you never felt that you, a Florentine woman, should live *for* Florence. If your own people wear a yoke, will you slip from under it rather than struggle alongside them to lighten it? There is hunger and misery in our streets, yet you say, 'I care not; I have my own sorrows; I will depart, perhaps finding relief elsewhere.'

"While the servants of God strive for a law of justice, peace, and charity so that the hundred thousand citizens among whom you were born may be governed rightly, you regard this as insignificant—as if you were a bird spreading its wings to fly wherever it pleases in search of food. And yet you have scorned the teaching of the Church, my daughter. As if you, a wilful wanderer following your own blind choices, were inferior to the humblest Florentine woman who stretches out her hands with her own people, craving a blessing for them; who feels a close sisterhood with the neighbor kneeling beside her, regardless of bloodline; who contemplates the mighty purpose God has for Florence and waits, enduring, because the promised work is great and she feels herself small."

"I was not leaving for ease or self-indulgence!" Romola cried, lifting her head again, compelled to defend herself. "I was going to hardship. I expect no joy; it has departed from my life."
