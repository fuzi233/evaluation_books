Women who have baked bread through fevers, and watched children pale and swoon at the smell of blood or the crush of sorrow, do not scream at a faint as if a spirit had flown out of the window. Catherine, who had handled more than dough and dairy in her time, moved as briskly and surely as if she were laying a loaf to cool.

She caught Margaret as the girl went slack, lowered her at once to the floor, and set her flat—head low, as one sets a basin to drain—so the blood might find its road back. She tugged the laces loose at the throat and bodice, and threw the casement wide. Night-air slid in, damp with canal-mist and the faint sour-sweet of yeast from some baker’s yard beyond; with it came the street’s small soundscape: a distant watchman’s call, a hoof striking cobble, the soft clink of a tether chain, all of it clean and human, not the grinding din of carts at full market that makes the head ache and the heart skitter like a startled hen.

Only after she had done what hands must do did she step to the door and call, loud enough to carry over the house’s hush: “Come here, if you please.”

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht entered and found the young woman stretched quite flat, and Catherine briskly beating her hands—hard enough to stir life, gentle enough not to bruise. The elder lady’s face went up like a flame.

“Oh, my poor girl! What have you done to her?”

“Me?” Catherine’s voice came out sharp as a knife on a board. “What has happened, then?”

“Nothing, madam; nothing more than is natural in her situation.”

Margaret Van Eyck coloured with ire. “You do well to speak so coolly,” she said, “you that are the cause of her situation.”

“That I am not,” said Catherine bluntly; “nor any woman born.”

“What! was it not you and your husband that kept them apart? and now he has gone to Italy all alone. Situation indeed! You have broken her heart amongst you.”

“Why, madam? Who is it then? in Heaven’s name!” Catherine threw out her hands. “To hear you, one would think this was my Gerard’s lass. But that can’t be. This fur never cost less than five crowns the ell; besides, this young gentlewoman is a wife; or ought to be.”

“Of course she ought. And who is the cause she is none? Who came before them at the very altar?”

“God forgive them, whoever it was,” Catherine answered, and for once her briskness dropped into gravity like a stone into a pot. “Me it was not, nor my man.”

“Well,” said Margaret Van Eyck, a little softened by that plain earnestness, “now you have seen her, perhaps you will not be quite so bitter against her, madam. She is coming to, thank Heaven.”

“Me bitter against her?” Catherine’s eyes went to the girl’s face, where the pallor was already loosening its grip. “No, that is all over. Poor soul! trouble behind her and trouble afore her; and to think of my setting her, of all living women, to read Gerard’s letter to me. Ay, and that was what made her go off, I’ll be sworn.”

She knelt, put her rough, warm palm to Margaret’s cheek, and spoke as one coaxes a child to swallow broth. “She is coming to. What, sweetheart! be not afeard; none are here but friends.”

They raised the girl into an easy chair. Colour crept back into lip and eyelid in slow, reluctant tides. Her breath, which had been a thin thread, thickened; her lashes trembled as if she listened for some sound only she could hear.

Catherine drew Margaret Van Eyck a little aside, as a housewife draws a neighbour from the hearth when there is counsel to be given. “Is she staying with you, if you please?”

“No, madam.”

“I wouldn’t let her go back to Sevenbergen to-night, then.”

“That is as she pleases. She still refuses to bide the night.”

“Ay, but you are older than she is; you can make her.” Catherine glanced at the girl. “There—she is beginning to notice.”

Then Catherine bent and put her mouth close to Margaret Van Eyck’s ear. The whisper lasted no longer than it takes to pinch salt between finger and thumb; yet the old lady started as if a coal had dropped on her skin.

“It’s false!” she breathed back. “It is a calumny! it is monstrous! look at her face. It is blasphemy to accuse such a face.”

“Tut! tut! tut!” Catherine returned, impatient as a cook contradicted about her own kitchen. “You might as well say this is not my hand. I ought to know; and I tell ye it is so.”

And then—most strangely to Margaret Van Eyck, who had expected coldness and found a storm—Catherine went straight to the young woman, took her round the neck, and kissed her with a warmth that had in it both pardon and claim.

“I suffered for Gerard,” she said aloud, and her voice shook, not with age but with the sudden rush of feeling; “and you shed your blood for him I do hear; his own words show me that I have been to blame, the very words you have read to me. Ay, Gerard, my child, I have held aloof from her; but I’ll make it up to her once I begin. You are my daughter from this hour.”

Another close embrace sealed the hasty compact, and the woman of impulse was gone, as if she had flung her heart on the table and swept out to tend some unseen task.

Margaret lay back in her chair. A feeble smile stole over her mouth—thin as the first scent of bread before it shows itself—because Gerard’s mother had kissed her and called her daughter. But the next moment she saw her old friend’s face set and vexed, as if some harsh note had been struck in a familiar song.

“I wonder you let that woman kiss you.”

“His mother!” Margaret murmured, half reproachfully, as though the word itself should hush all discord.

“Mother, or no mother, you would not let her touch you if you knew what she whispered in my ear about you.”

“About me?” The faintness came back in a shadow; the words scarcely left her throat.

“Ay, about you, whom she never saw till to-night.”

Margaret Van Eyck began—slowly, with that careful choosing of terms by which the old try to keep mercy in their mouths even when anger is hot—to shape her indignation into speech, to make the younger woman share it and be defended by it. But before the first full sentence could be born, the room altered.

The girl slid from the chair to her knees, as if the very wood would not hold her up under the weight of what she carried. The boards creaked under her small fall; outside, the street’s noises seemed to draw back and listen. She clasped Margaret Van Eyck’s hands and began to pray piteously for pardon—so humbly, so brokenly, that any bystander would have thought she had committed some cruel wrong, some intolerable insult, against her venerable friend, and could find no language—no dialect, no set of words—large enough to translate the pain in her breast except by kneeling and begging it away.
