During the lean years—the years when my dinners were mostly hope and a crust bought with yesterday’s courage—I kept clear of old Hasluck. He was the sort of man who, if you admitted to being hungry, would clap you on the back hard enough to dislodge your backbone and insist on feeding you until your buttons resigned. He could not understand a misery that wanted privacy. His sympathy came in a roaring, practical key, like a butcher’s cleaver brought down with the friendliest intention.

Why I preferred half-starving under the brisk, cheerful dishonesty of Lott and Co. to earning honest bread from Hasluck, whose very name smelt of roast joints and respectable gravy, I can’t entirely say. It was not that Lott and Co. were more virtuous—if anything they were less so, only quieter about it. Nor, if I’m truthful, was it because he was Barbara’s father. In my mind her parents were no more connected with her than a kitchen range is connected with the sunrise. Barbara belonged to a different pantry altogether: the one where gods keep their offerings and mortals are not allowed to touch, only to kneel and inhale.

There are instincts in us that refuse to be translated into reason’s flat, public language. I have always been one of those people who will walk three miles out of his way guided by a map, rather than ask a polite official who is paid to tell him he has taken the wrong turning. Possibly some ancient ancestor of mine was a sour, solitary cave-man who disliked the tribe’s dancing and insisted on making his own stone spoon, just to prove he could starve independently.

But independence is a luxury one can only afford after one has eaten. When at last I had got my own footing—my own small, decent shelf in the larder of life—I felt able to hold out my hand without fearing it would be mistaken for begging. I wrote to old Hasluck.

By the next post came a note so hearty it might have been written with a carving-knife. Barbara, he told me, had just returned from abroad. He added, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, that she would be delighted to see me; and, as I knew he would, he flung his doors wide, with the exuberance of a man proud of his table and pleased at the thought of another mouth to praise it.

Of my boyish passion for Barbara I had never spoken—not to my mother, not to my dearest friend, not to my own pillow. I do not think anyone guessed it, save Barbara herself. My mother liked her, certainly, but to my mother Barbara was still a girl with charms and faults, and she would talk of the faults freely, in the kindly, domestic dialect of mothers. It hurt me as a theological discussion hurts a new convert: not because it is cruel, but because it refuses to treat one’s idol as sacred.

Often, at dusk or later, I would go up to Stamford Hill and wander round and round that great red-brick house, set in its old garden like a respectable pudding in its basin. Then I would descend again into the clamorous streets—their carts, their cries, their metal-on-stone racket—feeling as a man feels who has left a chapel and re-entered a market. Even the sound changed. Up there the wind made a mild, leafy music, and the birds spoke in their own careful grammar. Down below, everything shouted in one vulgar tongue: Commerce.

During Barbara’s four years’ absence my adoration did not fade; it fermented. Like a sauce left to simmer too long, it grew rich, potent, and rather removed from its original ingredients. The living girl I had known—the child I had played with, talked to, touched—receded. In her place stood a figure compounded of memory, desire, and that strange human habit of building temples out of what ought to remain a simple room. I looked forward to seeing her again, but without impatience. The anticipation was its own feast.

Indeed, the thought of having her too near—of sitting opposite her while she ate, watching the ordinary mechanics of a mortal appetite, seeing her hair in curl-papers, hearing her complain of a tooth or a chilblain—filled me with a peculiar horror. It would have been like hearing a sacred hymn sung by a street-organ whose handle squeaks. I did not want to drag her down into the everyday; and I was oddly grateful there was no danger of my doing so. She would be older now—a woman. I felt, with equal certainty, that I was not yet a man. She would marry; and the thought gave me no jealousy. My feeling for her was devoid of hunger. It was worship, not appetite. No one but myself could close the temple I had built; no jealous priest could deny me entry. Since then I have learned that what I loved in her was not a living woman walking beside me through life, but that impalpable religion of sex by which we turn human clay into idols—then, if the idol insists on being human, we become angry with it for not fulfilling a dream we forged with our own hands.

A week passed before I went. It was late afternoon in early summer, when the day’s heat has been eaten up and the garden begins to smell more intensely of itself: warm earth, bruised leaf, the sweet, faintly peppery breath of flowers closing for evening. Hasluck had not yet returned from the City. Mrs. Hasluck was out visiting. Miss Hasluck, said the supercilious footman, was in the garden.

I told him not to trouble. I would find her.

I knew the place. It had always been her favourite: a sunny corner bright with flowers, fenced in by a thick yew hedge clipped into quaint Dutch animals and birds—silent green shapes that seemed to listen. The hedge, trimmed into imitation life, made no sound at all; it stood there absorbing the garden’s small acoustic world—the bee’s drone, the leaf’s whisper, the faint click of a distant gate. Even then I noticed how a place can hold you by its noises, or by their absence.

She was walking there, as I had expected, a book in her hand.

And again, as I saw her, something in me leapt back into boyhood: that sensation of fairy tales abruptly made real. Only now, instead of Princess, she was Queen.

She was taller; and where once she had lacked dignity, she had it now as naturally as she wore her dress. She did not hear me. My steps made no sound on the short grass. For a little while I stood in the yew’s shadow, drinking her in as a thirsty man drinks in the smell of bread from a bakery door. Her clear-cut profile bent over the page; the long line of her neck; her white hand—so white it seemed to catch the light and give it back—resting against the lilac of her dress.

I did not speak. If I could have chosen, I would have remained there, an unseen worshipper, content with the sight.

But at the end of the path she turned, saw me, and came forward, holding out her hand.

I knelt on the path and raised it to my lips. I did it without thinking; the body sometimes performs its own old rituals before the mind has time to object. When I looked up, she was smiling—amusement with the faintest seasoning of contempt, like a dish garnished with something sharp. Yet she looked pleased; and if there was contempt, it did not wound me. One does not feel insulted by a goddess’s weather.

“So you are still in love with me?” she said. “I wondered if you would be.”

“Did you know,” I asked, “that I was in love with you?”

“I should have been blind if I had not.”

“But I was only a boy.”

“You were not the usual type of boy. You are not going to be the usual type of man.”

“You do not mind my loving you?”

“I cannot help it, can I? Nor can you.”

She sat on a stone bench facing the sun-dial, leaned back, clasped her hands behind her head, and laughed—a laugh as light as the clink of a spoon against thin china.

“I shall always love you,” I said, “but it is a curious sort of love. I do not understand it myself.”

“Tell me,” she commanded. “Describe it to me.”

I stood opposite her, resting my arm on the dial’s stone column. The sun was sinking, throwing long shadows on the velvet grass and gilding her upturned face as if the light itself were her servant.

“I wish,” I said, “that you were some great queen of old days, and I might always be near you—serving you, doing your bidding. Your love in return would spoil all. I shall never ask it, never desire it. Only that I might look upon you, touch at rare intervals your hand with my lips, kiss in secret the glove you had let fall, the shoe you had flung off, know that you knew of my love—know I was yours to do with, to live or die according to your wish. Or that you were priestess in some temple of forgotten gods, where I might steal at daybreak and dusk to gaze upon you; kneel and watch your sandalled feet coming and going on the altar steps; lie with my lips pressed to the stones your trailing robes had touched.”

She laughed—a light, mocking laugh. “I should prefer to be the queen. Priestess would not suit me. Temples are so cold.” A slight shiver ran through her, as if the very word had let in a draught. She beckoned, and I came nearer.

“That is how you shall love me, Paul,” she said. “Adoring me—worshipping me—blindly. I will be your queen and treat you as it chooses me. All I think, all I do, I will tell you, and you shall tell me it is right. The queen can do no wrong.”

She took my face between her hands and bent over me, staring into my eyes with a steadiness that was almost fierce.

“You understand, Paul—the queen can do no wrong. Never, never.”

“My queen can do no wrong,” I repeated.

She laughed again and let her hands drop into her lap.

“Now you may sit beside me. So much honour, Paul, shall you have to-day, but it must last you a long time. Tell me what you have been doing. It may amuse me. Afterward you shall hear what I have done, and you shall say it was right and good.”

So I sat and told her. I sketched it briefly—my struggle, my work, the shifts and humiliations—and I left nothing out, not even that transit of the Lady ’Ortensia, though the memory of it tasted sour in my mouth, like wine gone wrong.

At that she grew a little grave.

“You must do nothing again, Paul,” she commanded, “to make me ashamed of you, or I shall dismiss you from my presence for ever. I must be proud of you, or you shall not serve me. In dishonouring yourself you dishonour me. I am angry with you, Paul. Do not let me be angry with you again.”

And so that passed. Her love—her demand for worship, her insistence that I remain worthy of her—did not save me always from the chattering, simian voices that whisper to the animal in us. But it did help my life, as only love can: by making one ashamed to be small.

Later, the morning glory fades, the veil tears, and we see with cold eyes. My love was a woman. She lies dead. They mocked her white, sweet limbs with rags and tatters, but they cannot cheat love’s eyes. God knows I loved her in all purity. Only with false love do we love the false. Beneath the unclean clinging garments she sleeps fair.

When my tale was done, she said, “Now I will tell you mine. I am going to be married soon. I shall be a Countess, Paul—the Countess Huescar. I will teach you how to pronounce it. And I shall have a real castle in Spain.”

I must have looked as if someone had unexpectedly put pepper in my tea, for she laughed.

“You need not look so frightened. We shall not live there. It is half-ruined and gloomy, among the mountains, and he loves it even less than I do. Paris and London will be my courts—so you will see me often. You shall know the great world, Paul: the world I mean to conquer, where I mean to rule.”

“Is he very rich?” I asked.

“As poor,” she laughed, “as a Spanish nobleman. The money I shall have to provide—or rather, poor dear Dad will. He gives me title, position. Of course I do not love him, handsome though he is. Don’t look so solemn, Paul. We shall get on together well enough. Queens do not make love matches; they contract alliances. I have done well. Congratulate me. Do you hear? Say that I have acted rightly.”

“Does he love you?” I asked.

“He tells me so,” she answered, laughing. “How uncourtier-like you are, Paul! Do you suggest that any man could see me and not love me?”

She sprang to her feet, and there was a sudden crackle in her, as if the smooth surface had split and fire showed underneath.

“I do not want his love,” she cried. “It would bore me. Women hate love they cannot return. I don’t mean love like yours, devout little Paul,” she added, with a laugh that tried to make a jest of the bitterness. “That is sweet incense wafted round us, that we like to scent with our noses in the air. Give me that, Paul; I want it, I ask for it. But the love of a hand, the love of a husband one does not care for—it would be horrible!”

For a moment I felt myself grow older, as if I had been handed a burden I had not expected. My goddess looked suddenly like a child who needed help and did not know how to ask for it plainly.

“But have you thought—” I began.

“Yes, yes,” she cut in quickly. “I have thought and thought till I can think no more. There must be some sacrifice; it must be as little as need be, that is all. He does not love me. He is marrying me for my money—I know that, and I am glad of it.

“You do not know me, Paul. I must have rank, position. What am I? The daughter of rich old Hasluck, who began life as a butcher in the Mile End Road. As the Princess Huescar, society will forget; as Mrs.”—and there she checked herself, abruptly—“Jones or Brown it would remember, however rich I might be. I am vain, Paul—caring for power—ambition. I have my father’s blood in me. All his nights and days he has spent gaining wealth; he can do no more. We upstarts have our pride of race. He has done his share; I must do mine.”

“But you need not be mere Mrs. Anybody,” I argued. “Why not wait? You will meet someone who can give you position and whom at the same time you can love. Would that not be better?”

“He will never come,” she said. “Because, my little Paul, he has come already. Hush. The queen can do no wrong.”

“Who is he?” I asked. “May I not know?”

“Yes,” she answered. “You shall know. I want you to know—then you shall tell me I have acted rightly. Do you hear me? Quite rightly—that you still respect me and honour me.

“He could not help me. As his wife I should be less even than I am: a mere rich nobody giving long dinner-parties to other rich nobodies; living among City men and retired trades-people; envied only by their fat, vulgarly dressed wives; courted by seedy Bohemians for the sake of my cook; with perhaps an opera singer or an impecunious nobleman or two out of Dad’s City list for show-guests. Is that the court where you would have your queen reign?”

“Is he so commonplace a man,” I said, “the man you love? I cannot believe it.”

“He is not commonplace,” she answered. “It is I who am commonplace. The things I desire are beneath him. He will never trouble himself to secure them.”

“Not even for love of you?”

“I would not have him do so even were he willing. He is great—with a greatness I cannot even understand. He is not the man for these times. In old days I should have married him, knowing he would climb to greatness by sheer strength of manhood. But now men do not climb; they crawl to greatness. He could not do that. I have done right, Paul.”

“What does he say?” I asked.

“Shall I tell you?” She laughed, but there was a harshness in it now, like a knife scraping a plate. “I can give you his exact words: ‘You are half a woman and half a fool, so woman-like you will follow your folly. But let your folly see to it that your woman makes no fool of herself.’”

The words rang in my mind with a voice I knew—strong, contemptuous, honest in its brutality.

“Hal!” I cried. “It is he.”

“So you never guessed even that,” she said. “I thought at times it would be sweet to cry it aloud—that it could have made no difference—that everyone who knew me must have read it in my eyes.”

“But he never seemed to take much notice of you,” I said.

She laughed. “You needn’t be so unkind. What did I ever do for you much more than snub you? We boys and girls—there isn’t so much difference between us: we love our masters. Yet you must not think so poorly of me. I was only a child to him then. But we were locked up in Paris together during the entire siege. Have you not heard? He did take a little notice of me there, Paul, I assure you.”

Would it have been better, I sometimes wonder, had she followed the woman and not the fool? It sounds an easy question. Yet my mind loops back—always back—to a winter night years later at Tiefenkasten in the Julier Pass, as if life insists on repeating its refrains until we learn the tune.

I was travelling from St. Moritz to Chur. I was the sole passenger. The sledge ride had turned my face into something that felt carved from salted meat. At the inn I stood thawing before the stove in the common room, while the room’s noises—boots stamping, a spoon clinking in a mug, a log shifting—seemed louder for the thick hush outside, where snow muffled the world as thoroughly as a pillow over a bell.

A waiter handed me a pencilled note:

“Come up and see me. I am a prisoner in this damned hole till the weather breaks. Hal.”

I went up.

I hardly recognised him. He was only a ghost of the Hal I had known—a magnificent body reduced, by privation and savage exertion during the Paris siege, and then by the loose, wild living that followed, into a ruin that still somehow looked defiant about being ruined.

His room was a gloomy vaulted place that had once been a chapel. It was dimly lit by a cheap lamp that smelt of bad oil and worse economies, and it was heated to suffocation by one of those great green-tiled German ovens that seem built less to warm you than to persuade you to give up and melt.

He sat propped on pillows near one of the high windows. His eyes—those deep eyes—flared out of his drawn face like two caverns with fire at the bottom.

“I saw you from the window,” he said. “It is the only excitement I get: twice a day when the sledges come in. I broke down crossing the Pass a fortnight ago, on my way from Davos. We were stuck in a drift for eighteen hours; it nearly finished my last lung. And I haven’t even a book. By God, lad, I was glad to see your frosted face ten minutes ago in the lantern-light.”

He seized my hand with long bony fingers.

“Sit down, and let me hear my voice using again its mother tongue. For the last eight years I have hardly spoken it. You were always a good listener. Can you stand the room? The windows ought to be open, but what does it matter? I may as well get accustomed to the heat before I die.”

I drew a chair close. For a while, between his fits of coughing, he talked and talked—boisterously, wildly—laughing at my remonstrances until the laughter became a struggle for breath. The room had its own ugly accompaniment: the oven’s faint ticking, the lamp’s sickly hiss, the building’s old stones holding the silence like a taste you cannot rinse away. Outside, the wind made a long, lonely sound through the Pass—a clean, natural voice, far kinder than the commercial clatter of cities. It struck me then how much of our sanity depends upon what we hear all day. In towns we are bullied by noise. Here the world spoke softly and humanly, and Hal, in his feverish life, had somehow lost even that.

Suddenly he stopped.

“What is she doing?” he asked. “Do you ever see her?”

“She is playing in—” I named a comic opera then running in Paris. “No; I have not seen her for some time.”

He laid his wasted hand on mine. “What a pity you and I could not have rolled ourselves into one, Paul—you, the saint, and I, the satyr. Together we should have made her perfect lover.”

I remembered, absurdly and painfully, those long nights of my childhood when I had lain awake listening to my father and mother’s voices through the thin wall—anger seeping through plaster, love and cruelty sharing one cramped vocabulary. It seemed my fate to stand helpless between people I loved, watching them hurt one another against their will.

“Tell me,” I said. “I loved her, knowing her. I was not blind. Whose fault was it—yours or hers?”

He laughed.

“Whose fault? God made us.”

For an instant I hated him for the laugh. But then I looked into his eyes and saw the pain living there like a tenant who would never be evicted, and my hatred drained away into pity.

A smile twisted his ugly mouth.

“You’ve been on the stage, Paul,” he said. “You must have heard it: ‘Ah, well, the curtain must come down, however badly things are going.’ It is only a play. We do not choose our parts. I did not even know I was the villain till I heard the booing of the gallery. I even thought I was the hero—full of noble sentiment—sacrificing myself for the happiness of the heroine. She would have married me in the beginning, had I plagued her sufficiently.”

I tried to speak, but he waved me down and went on, his voice pushing forward as if it had to empty itself before it ran out.

“Ah yes, it might have been better. That is easy to say, not knowing. So too it might have been worse—in all probability much the same. All roads lead to the end. You know I was always a fatalist. We tried both ways.

“She loved me well enough, but she loved the world also. I thought she loved it better. So I kissed her on her brow, mumbled a prayer for her happiness, and made my exit to a choking sob. So ended the first act. Wasn’t I the hero throughout that, Paul? I thought so—slapped myself on the back, told myself what a fine fellow I had been.

“Then—you know what followed. She was finer clay than she fancied. Love is woman’s kingdom, not the world. Even then I thought more of her than of myself. I could have borne my share of the burden if I had not seen her fainting under hers—shamed, degraded. So we dared to think for ourselves, injuring nobody but ourselves; played the man and woman; lost the world for love. Wasn’t it brave? Were we not hero and heroine? They printed the playbill wrong, Paul, that was all. I was really the hero, but the printing devil made a slip—so instead of applauding you booed. How could you know? It was not your fault.”

“But that was not the end,” I said. “If the curtain had fallen then, I could have forgiven you.”

He grinned. “That fatal last act. Even yours don’t always come right, so the critics tell me.”

The grin went. He stared past me a moment, as if he saw the rest of his story written on the wall like a play he had to perform to the end.

“We may never see each other again, Paul. Don’t think too badly of me. I found I had made a second mistake—or thought I had. She was no happier with me, after a time, than she had been with him. If all our longings were one, life would be easy. But they are not. What is to be done but toss for it? If it comes down head, we wish it had been tail; and if tail, we think of what we have lost through its not coming down head.

“Love is no more the whole of a woman’s life than it is of a man’s. He did not apply for a divorce—that was smart of him. We were shunned, ignored. To some women it might not have mattered; but she had been used to being sought, courted, feted. She made no complaint—did worse: made desperate effort to appear cheerful, to pretend that our humdrum life was not boring her to death. I watched her growing more listless, more depressed; grew angry with her, angrier with myself.

“There was no bond between us except our passion. That was real enough—‘grand,’ I believe, is the approved literary adjective. It is good enough for what nature intended it: a summer season in a cave. It makes a poor marriage settlement in these more complicated days. We fell to mutual recriminations—vulgar scenes. Ah, most of us look better at a little distance.

“The sordid side of life became important. I was never rich; by contrast with all she had known, miserably poor. The mere sight of the food our twelve-pound-a-year cook put on the table would take away her appetite. Love does not change the palate, give you a taste for cheap claret when you’ve been accustomed to dry champagne. We have bodies to think of as well as souls; we forget that in moments of excitement.”

He coughed then—a deep tearing cough that made me want to strike the green oven for its heat and the lamp for its stench, as if either were to blame. When he spoke again his voice had lost its bravado and sounded like something scraped raw.

“She fell ill. And it seemed to me I had dragged her from the soil where she had grown only to watch her die.

“And then he came—precisely at the right moment. I cannot help admiring him. Most men take revenge clumsily, hurting themselves. He was neat. He had been patient. I am not even ashamed of falling into his trap; it was admirably baited.

“Maybe I had despised him for seeming to submit meekly to the blow. What cared he for me and my opinion? It was she he cared for. He knew her better than I did—knew that sooner or later she would tire, not of love but of the cottage; look back with longing eyes to all she had lost.

“Fool! Cuckold! What was it to him that the world would laugh at him, despise him? Love such as his makes fools of men. Would I not give her back to him?

“Return—”
