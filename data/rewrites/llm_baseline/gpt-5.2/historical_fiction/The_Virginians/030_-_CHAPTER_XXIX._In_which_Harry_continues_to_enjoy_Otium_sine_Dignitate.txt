So long as there were cards to be cut at her lodging and a chair ready at the assembly-rooms, Madame de Bernstein remained tolerably content at Tunbridge Wells. She scolded her niece with the same steady relish with which she played her rubber; and, if the talk ran high or low, it ran always, like a brook, around her—never through her.

At Harry Warrington’s age, nearly every place is pleasant, provided there is brisk air to fill the lungs, company to quicken the blood, and some honest sport to set the limbs going. Youth has a cookery of its own: it can take a plain joint and swear it is a feast; it can drink thin port and call it nectar—because appetite, that best of sauces, seasons it. A man older will remember his first ball as he remembers the first loaf he ever helped to knead: not for the flour, but for the warmth of hands and the smell of it rising. When we are young, we go to pleasure like a hungry boy to table, counting the days until the dish comes on; later, we look at the same board with a cooler eye, and sometimes sit—as mothers do—while the daughters dance and the music carries them where we can no longer go.

Harry, being of that age when idleness itself tastes sweet, had little mind to be as severe upon his own otium as Colonel Lambert of the Twentieth was apt to be. There is a kind of leisure that is not noble, but is delicious all the same: the slatternly enchantress who lets a man lie late in his dressing-gown, draw a book to him as one draws a coverlet, and call for a glass mixed cold and sharp—sherry beaten with sugar and bruised mint, the ice clinking like small bells—while a cigar sends its blue ribbon into the afternoon. People may cry shame on her, and name her the mother of mischief; yet she is excellent company, and her voice is soft.

At the Wells the fashionable great came and went. My Lord of March rode northward; my Lord Chesterfield, finding that the water did not mend his deafness, returned to his lonely quiet at Blackheath—seeking a kinder soundscape than these public rooms afford, where every foot and fan makes its own small storm. Yet plenty of gentlemen remained, and Mr. Warrington lacked no companions at his ordinary at the White Horse.

He learned quickly to command a French dinner as neatly as any man who had been bred within sight of St. James’s. He could give his orders to Monsieur Barbeau in Barbeau’s own tongue with a fluency uncommon among those who borrowed foreign words as they would borrow a lace ruffle—only to show it. Harry liked the speech as he liked the food: for the taste of it in the mouth, and the world it carried with it. He had a decided palate for wines too, and could tell, with a certainty that made older topers stare, the deep, furred richness of Clos Vougeot from the more delicate, scented grace of a Romande. The bottles came up from the cellar sweating as if they, too, had traveled; the cork drew with a sigh; and the first pour sent its fragrance rising—berries and earth and old wood—as if some buried season were being uncapped.

Thus the young Virginian, in that little kingdom of pump-room and bowling-green, grew into what people called the young King of the Wells. The common frequenters were easy men of the world, not much shocked by the rumor of gallantry and extravagance which had begun to buzz about him, and which had frightened Mr. Wolfe. Yet, though Harry swam among loose fish, he had a natural shrewdness and honesty that kept him clear of many hooks. He made few foolish bets, and those jolly idlers who lived by taking in the unwary found him hard to handle. He played games indoors and out because he had a quick eye and a straight hand; but he was scrupulous about his company—playing only with gentlemen he knew—and he would settle his own debts on the spot, as a man pays for bread before he leaves the baker, unwilling to owe even the smallest crumb.

He would have cut a sorry figure at a college examination, perhaps; but there are other examinations in the world—tests of temper, of courage, of that fidelity which keeps a man whole. Harry had to sit for some of those.

For instance, when that unhappy little Cattarina—she who had already brought him into enough trouble—carried her importunities beyond the mark at which he thought generosity should stop, he drew away from the Opera-house siren with a coolness and skill that left her no hold upon him. The mermaid’s mother then appeared, a woman who could weep as quickly as she could grin. She came with a tale ready salted: a cruel bailiff, she vowed, had seized all her daughter’s goods for debt; the venerable father was languishing in a London gaol; nothing would serve but that Mr. Warrington should pay past accounts at Tunbridge and stand bail in town.

Harry answered that, between himself and a bailiff, there could be no dealings; and that because he had had the misfortune—or good fortune, as she chose to call it—to be known to Mademoiselle Cattarina, and had gratified her caprices with trinkets and knick-knacks, he was not thereby bound to discharge the old debts of her family. He would not be surety for her papa; he would not pay her outstanding bills.

The mother’s face shifted like a bad cook’s fire—now flaring, now sinking. She called him monster and ingrate; and then, with a veteran smirk, asked why he did not take pay for the services he had rendered to the young person. At first Harry did not understand what payment she meant. When the meaning was made plain, the honest lad rose as if he had been struck by a foul smell.

He told her he came from a country where even the savages would recoil from such trafficking; and that a woman who bartered a child’s dishonour was beyond his comprehension—beyond his language, too, for there are infamies which no clean word will carry. He bowed her out with ceremony, as if the formality of good breeding might keep the stain from his threshold, and ordered Gumbo to mark her well and never admit her again.

No doubt she went away breathing vengeance against the “Iroquois,” as she chose to call him; declaring that no Turk or Persian would treat a lady so. She and her daughter, in fact, retreated to London as soon as their anxious landlord would let them.

If Harry had escaped the perils of gallantry, there remained those of gaming. A man who plays at bowls—so the phrase ran—must expect rubbers. One evening after dinner at the ordinary, he declined to play piquet any further with Captain Batts. The Captain roughly demanded his reason. Harry told him fairly that he played only with gentlemen who paid, like himself; and he spoke with such readiness to satisfy Mr. Batts, as soon as the outstanding little account was settled, that the Captain declared himself satisfied in advance—satisfied d’avance—and straightway left the Wells without paying Harry or any other creditor.

There were other occasions when Harry’s spirit showed itself without calculation. One evening, as old Miss Whiffler was going to the assembly, a chairman was rude to her. Harry, who could not abide insolence laid upon age—any more than he could endure a harsh, needless noise in a quiet place—fell upon the fellow and beat him soundly; and the thump of it, though ungentle, seemed to many bystanders a wholesome correction in the day’s clatter.

Then, too, the calumny about himself and that unlucky opera-dancer was repeated aloud by Mr. Hector Buckler, one of the fiercest frequenters of the Wells. Buckler, in the pump-room, was regaling a circle of water-drinkers with the story, and the room was full of its particular din: the splashing of the fountain, the scraping of chairs, the thin laughter, the coughs and whispers, and that perpetual hum which public places breed—an acoustic fog that sets nerves jangling and makes scandal travel faster, because it has so many mouths to lodge in.

Harry stepped up to Buckler before them all and publicly informed him that the story was a falsehood; and that henceforth he would hold any man accountable who uttered it. He did not declaim: he spoke plainly, like one stating a fact, and the sudden stillness that followed—broken only by the water’s trickle—showed that plain words, when they are true, can master a crowd’s noise better than any flourish.

So, though our friend, being at Rome, did as Rome did in matters of diversion, he proved himself a valorous and worthy Roman; and, howling with the wolves, was acknowledged by Mr. Wolfe himself to be as brave as the best of the pack. If that officer had carried to Colonel Lambert the tales which had caused the latter so much pain, we may be sure that, when Wolfe found the young man innocent of the worse charges, he took the first opportunity to withdraw them.

At Lambert’s house, the retraction fell like rain upon a thirsty field. There was joy among them at Harry’s acquittal—something of that pure pleasure which better souls than ours may feel at the recovery of a sinner, though here it was rather the recovery of an unjustly suspected man.

Never had the little family been so happy—no, not even when they had news of Brother Tom winning his scholarship—as when Colonel Wolfe rode over with the account of his conversation with Harry Warrington.

“Hadst thou brought me a regiment, James, I think I should not have been better pleased,” said Mr. Lambert.

Mrs. Lambert called to her daughters, who were in the garden, and kissed them both when they came in, and cried out the good news. Hetty jumped for joy; Theo sat to the harpsichord and, in the evening, performed such uncommonly brilliant operations upon it that the very house seemed to ring more kindly than usual, as if the notes had brushed the corners clean of the old suspicion.

When Dr. Boyle came in for his backgammon, he could not at first account for the illumination in all their faces, until the three ladies, in a happy chorus, told him how right he had been in his sermon and how dreadfully they had wronged that poor dear, good young Mr. Warrington.

“What shall we do, my dear?” says the Colonel to his wife. “The hay is in, the corn won’t be cut for a fortnight,—the horses have nothing to do. Suppose we—” And here he leaned over the table and whispered in her ear.

“My dearest Martin! The very thing!” cries Mrs. Lambert, taking her husband’s hand and pressing it.

“What’s the very thing, mother?” cries young Charley, home for his Bartlemytide holidays.

“The very thing is to go to supper. Come, Doctor! We will have a bottle of wine to-night, and drink repentance to all who think evil.”

“Amen,” says the Doctor, “with all my heart!”

And with this the worthy family went to their supper; and if the fare was simple, it was made splendid by their spirits. The clink of plates, the soft bustle from the kitchen, the cork drawn clean, the first warm scent of bread and meat upon the table—these were honest domestic sounds, not the jangling of the pump-room; and in them, for that night at least, the Lamberts found their peace.
