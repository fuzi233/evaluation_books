“Mist clogs the sunshine,
Smoky dwarf houses
Hem me round everywhere.”

The next afternoon, roughly twenty miles outside of Milton-Northern, their train pulled onto the narrow little branch line that ran straight to the seaside town of Heston. Heston itself was no more than one long, straggling street stretched parallel to the shore, with a character entirely its own: as different from the small genteel bathing spots of southern England as those were from the holiday towns of the continent. To borrow a well-loved Scots phrase, every last thing about the place felt “purpose-like.” Country carts had far more iron reinforcement and far less flimsy wood and leather on their horse tackle, even people out for a leisurely day at the beach carried the sharp, preoccupied edge of folks who never fully turn off their work mode. All the colours around them were muted gray, sturdy and long-wearing, none of the bright, frilly frippery Margaret was used to back home in Hampshire. No one wore smock frocks anymore, even the local farm labourers: the loose, billowing garments got caught in machinery too easily, and the habit had died out generations before. Down south, Margaret had often seen shop clerks with a spare minute lolling in their doorways, breathing fresh air and watching passersby. But here, if a shopkeeper had no customers to wait on, they invented tiny unnecessary tasks to keep busy—Margaret swore she spotted more than one unrolling and re-rolling the same spool of ribbons just to occupy their hands—while she and her mother set out the next morning to hunt for temporary lodgings.

Their first two nights at Heston’s inn had cost far more than Mr. Hale had budgeted for, so they jumped at the first clean, cheery set of rooms they found available. For the first time in days, Margaret felt fully at rest. There was a soft, dreamy haze to the quiet that made the rest feel almost luxurious: the low, steady lap of the distant sea against the sand, the high, clear calls of the donkey boys trotting the beach, new strange sights passing before her like half-remembered paintings that she was too lazy to press for full context before they slipped away. Even late in November, the sea air blowing off the shore was soft and warm enough to wander the waterfront at her leisure, the long misty horizon blurring the dark line of the ocean into the tender pale blue of the wintry sky, the white sail of a distant fishing boat glinting silver through a stray sliver of sun. For a little while, she was half convinced she could dream her days away in that soft pensiveness, refusing to look back on the life they’d left in Helstone, refusing to peer forward into the uncertain future waiting in Milton.

But the future would not wait to be avoided. One evening, the family agreed that Margaret and her father would travel to Milton-Northern the next day to hunt for their permanent new home. Mr. Hale had received several letters from his old university friend Mr. Bell, and two more from Mr. Thornton, the local mill-owner who was acting as their liaison on the ground, and he was desperate to meet Thornton face to face to sort out all the practical details of his new tutoring work. Margaret was loath to make the trip: she already had a deep, unspoken distaste for the very idea of a smoky manufacturing town, and was sure her mother was growing stronger by the day breathing Heston’s sea air. She would happily have delayed the expedition indefinitely.

Miles before their train even pulled into Milton’s station, a thick lead-coloured cloud hung low over the horizon in the direction of the town, far darker than the pale frosty blue of the wintry sky that had still held traces of Heston’s first light frost that morning. The closer they drew to the town limits, the more the air carried a faint taste and scent of smoke—more the absence of the sweet grass and hedgerow fragrance she was used to, than a sharp, acrid fume. They were whirled past miles of straight, identical, unrelenting streets lined with tiny uniform brick houses, dotted at regular intervals with great boxy factory blocks, hulking over the small terraces like hens over their chicks, belching great plumes of thick black “unparliamentary” smoke that fully explained the great far-off cloud Margaret had mistaken for a rain storm. As their carriage drove from the station to their hotel, they were forced to stop every few minutes: heavy, overloaded cotton lorries choked the narrow thoroughfares, leaving barely enough space for a horse and cab to squeeze past. Even her visits to the busy streets of London with her aunt Edith had never looked like this: every single van, waggon, and truck on the roads carried nothing but cotton, either raw and bulging in rough sacks, or woven into finished calico stacked in heavy bales. The footpaths were thronged with people wearing clothes of good sturdy material, but cut loose and slovenly, nothing like the shabby, threadbare smartness Margaret was familiar with among similar working and middle class crowds in London.

“This is New Street,” Mr. Hale said, leaning out the window. “The main thoroughfare in Milton. Bell talked of it constantly. When they widened it from a narrow lane into a proper public road thirty years ago, his property along here more than tripled in value overnight. Mr. Thornton’s mill can’t be far off—he’s Bell’s tenant, though I believe he runs most of his operations out of his main warehouse nearby.”

“Where is our hotel, papa?” Margaret asked.

“Just at the far end of this street, as I recall. Shall we have lunch first, or head straight out to see the list of houses we marked in the Milton Times?”

“Oh, let’s get the work done first,” Margaret said.

“Very well then. I’ll just check the desk for any note Mr. Thornton may have left for me—he said he’d send word of any good properties he heard of. We’ll keep the cab waiting, so we don’t lose our way wandering and miss our train back to Heston this afternoon.”

There were no notes waiting for them at the front desk, so they set off house-hunting. Thirty pounds a year was the absolute maximum they could afford in rent—money that would have bought them a roomy cottage with a pretty walled garden back in Hampshire. But in Milton, even the most basic accommodation of two sitting rooms and four bedrooms seemed completely out of reach. They went through every property on their list, rejecting every last one as they visited it, before staring at each other in quiet dismay.

“I think we have to go back to the second house we saw,” Margaret said at last. “The one in Crampton suburb, don’t you remember? It had three sitting rooms, we laughed at how many there were compared to only three bedrooms. But I’ve mapped it all out. The front downstairs room can double as your study and our dining room, poor papa, so mamma can have the sunniest back sitting room in the house just like we agreed. The upstairs front room, even with its horrid blue and pink rose wallpaper and the heavy overdone cornice, has a pretty view out over the plain, with that great bend in the river or canal down below. I can take the tiny little bedroom tucked over the kitchen at the top of the first flight of stairs, and you and mamma can have the room behind the drawing room. That little closet nook in the roof will make you a perfect dressing room.”

“But what about Dixon, and the young housemaid we’re planning to hire to help her?” her father asked.

“Wait, I’ve worked that out too,” Margaret said, grinning at her own sudden burst of domestic genius. “Dixon can have the back sitting room down stairs, so she doesn’t have to climb half a dozen flights of stairs all day. She complained nonstop about the stairs at Heston, you know. The young girl can have the sloped attic above yours and mamma’s bedroom. Won’t that work?”

“I’m sure it will,” Mr. Hale sighed. “But that wallpaper. What appalling taste. To smother such a small, plain house in bright patterns and such heavy trim!”

“Never mind papa,” Margaret said. “Surely you can charm the landlord into re-papering the drawing room and your bedroom at least, for mamma will spend most of her time in those two rooms. And your bookshelves will cover almost all of that gaudy print in the dining room, anyway.”

They agreed it was the best they could possibly do, so Mr. Hale walked Margaret back to the hotel, left her at the foot of the stairs, and headed off to find the landlord, Mr. Donkin, at his address on Canute Street. Margaret’s hand was just on the door of their hotel sitting room when a harried waiter came hurrying up behind her.

“Beg your pardon, ma’am. Your husband left so quickly I didn’t have a chance to flag him down. A Mr. Thornton called not five minutes after you two stepped in the door, then came back again just now, and I told him you said you’d be back in an hour, so he’s waiting in your sitting room for Mr. Hale.”

“Thank you. My father will be back very soon, just tell him that when he arrives,” Margaret said. She opened the door and stepped inside with the straight, unhurried, dignified poise that was habitual to her, no trace of awkwardness: she was far too used to formal social company to be flustered by a stranger waiting in her sitting room on business for her father.

Mr. Thornton was far more surprised and discomfited than she was. He had expected a quiet, mild middle-aged clergyman to walk through the door, and instead a young lady stepped forward with frank, unapologetic dignity—entirely different from the type of woman he was used to meeting in Milton’s industrial circles. Her dress was perfectly plain: a fine, well-shaped straw bonnet trimmed with nothing but white ribbon, a large soft Indian shawl draped in long heavy folds over her shoulders, which she wore with the easy grace of an empress wearing royal drapery. He fumbled for words, utterly taken aback: he had only ever heard Mr. Hale mention that he had a daughter, and had assumed the girl was still small.

“Mr. Thornton, I presume?” Margaret said, after a short quiet pause where he failed to find his voice. “Do sit down. My father dropped me at the door barely a minute ago, but no one had the chance to tell him you were here, so he stepped out on an errand. He will be back almost immediately. I am so sorry you’ve had the trouble of calling twice.”

Thornton was a man well used to being the one in charge, but he found himself sitting down unthinkingly at her bidding. He had been fuming a minute before, annoyed at wasting precious time on a busy market day waiting for a man who never showed up, but now he found himself calm, unhurried, in no rush to leave. “Do you know where Mr. Hale has gone? I might be able to go find him for you, to save him the extra walk.”

“He has gone to see a Mr. Donkin in Canute Street,” Margaret told him. “He is the landlord of the house we are hoping to rent in Crampton.”

Mr. Thornton knew that house well. He had seen the advertisement himself, and gone to inspect it on Mr. Bell’s direct request, to do what he could to help his old friend’s new protégé settle into Milton. He had also been curious about the clergyman who had given up a comfortable, respected country living to start a quiet new life in the industrial north. Back then he had thought the Crampton house was ideal for the Hales, but now, seeing Margaret move with that quiet, elegant poise of a woman raised on soft country gentility, he suddenly felt ashamed he had ever thought that gaudy, overdecorated house would be good enough for them.

Margaret was tired after a morning traipsing the hard pavement, and would have happily sat quiet and rested, but she knew she had a duty to play the gentlewoman and make polite small talk to their guest. But Mr. Thornton was distracted, his short curled upper lip, strong squared upturned chin, and the unyielding set of his shoulders making him look every inch the resolute, no-nonsense manufacturer he was. He misread her quiet, careful politeness as haughty coldness, even contempt for his rough, unpolished northern ways. He found himself caught in two conflicting feelings: he could not repress a sharp, unlooked-for admiration for her pale clear beauty, her soft dark eyes, the graceful line of her throat as she spoke, but he resented what he assumed was her supercilious disdain for a man he considered himself to be: a rough, plain working man with no fine southern graces. He was half ready to storm out in irritation when Mr. Hale finally stepped through the door, full of warm, gentlemanly apologies for missing him, and smoothed over the awkward tension between them at once.

The two men spent nearly an hour chatting about their mutual old friend Mr. Bell, while Margaret slipped over to the window to lose herself watching the crowds move past on New Street, barely hearing her father when he called her over to tell her that the landlord was refusing point blank to re-paper the Crampton house, insisting the pink and blue rose print was a very expensive, fashionable new design. Margaret sighed, mentally mapping out if she could hang her watercolour sketches to cover the worst of the gaudy patterns, before Mr. Hale warmly pressed Thornton to stay and share luncheon with them. Thornton would have accepted in a heartbeat if Margaret had so much as smiled to second her father’s invitation, but she was too lost in thought to notice, and said nothing. He left with a stiff, formal bow, and walked out onto New Street feeling more awkward and self-conscious in every limb than he ever had in his entire life.

As soon as he was gone, Margaret laughed softly. “I was so relieved you walked in when you did. He never once elaborated on any subject, just gave me little short, abrupt answers to every remark I made. I was all out of small talk.”

“But he was very much to the point, wasn’t he?” Mr. Hale said, defending his new friend. “He is a remarkably clear-headed fellow. He told me you were right about Crampton being the best place for us—it’s built on gravelly soil, far and away the healthiest suburb within miles of Milton.”

When they arrived back in Heston, they sat around the tea table recounting every last detail of the day to Mrs. Hale, who was full of endless eager questions. She asked Margaret to describe Mr. Thornton, and Margaret, still tired from the long day, roused herself to paint a quick portrait: tall, broad-shouldered, around thirty years old, face neither plain nor handsome, with an expression of such sharp unyielding resolution that no amount of rough edges could make him seem common or vulgar at all, exactly the sort of strong, sagacious man who was born to run a great manufacturing operation. She warned her mother to prepare herself for the hideous pink and blue wallpaper in their new drawing room, and the absurdly heavy overdone cornice running around the ceiling.

But when their moving van pulled up to the Crampton house a few days later, the obnoxious bright patterned paper was gone entirely, replaced with a soft, plain neutral print that suited their furniture perfectly. The landlord accepted their thanks very calmly, and let them believe he had relented out of kindness, choosing not to mention that he had only agreed to take on the extra work after one short, sharp remonstrance from Mr. Thornton, the wealthiest and most respected man in the whole town of Milton.
