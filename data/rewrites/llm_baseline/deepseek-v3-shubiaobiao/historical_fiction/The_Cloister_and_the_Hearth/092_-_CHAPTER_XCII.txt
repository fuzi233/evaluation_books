The hermit of Gouda was, in truth, the vicar of Gouda—though he knew it not, so absolute was his seclusion. When the storm of his passion had passed, remorse seized him like a serpent coiling about his heart. Only those who have climbed high in faith and then fallen can understand the terror that now gripped him. He stood as one who, lured by pride to a cliff’s edge, looks down into the abyss and trembles.

*"Ah, Jerome,"* he groaned, *"how much wiser you were than I! Your warnings were bitter, but they were medicine for my soul."*

Searching his own heart, he saw the truth plainly: Margaret still held him. *"I love her more than God,"* he whispered in despair. *"More than the Church. What fruit can such love bear but ruin?"* A shudder ran through him. *"Let the strong wrestle with temptation—the weak must flee. And who is weaker than I?"*

His faith, his penance—had they been anything more than a fragile structure of cards, swept away by a single breath of earthly longing? *"I must begin anew,"* he resolved, *"and build upon rock, not sand."*

He would leave Holland at once, seek some distant cloister, and spend years in solitude before returning. By then, his heart would be hardened against earthly affections. And Margaret—she would forget him, marry another. The very pain this thought brought him proved the necessity of his flight. *"Better she should hate me than damn her soul with mine."*

Yet before he left, he would ensure her worldly comfort. The thought that he could lift her from poverty was his one solace. *"Let her never lose another true love for want of gold,"* he murmured, though the words tore at him.

His first task was done—confronting Ghysbrecht, forcing him to restore what he had stolen. Then, as fate would have it, he came to the dying hermit of Gouda. The man had been a soldier once, wearing a steel corselet beneath his hair shirt, calling himself *"Christ’s soldier now, where once I was Satan’s."* Clement shrived him, prayed with him, and in turn sought counsel from one who faced death with such peace.

*"The world, the flesh, and the devil,"* whispered the hermit, *"I fought them all. But only in solitude did I find victory."*

Those words, and the hermit’s serene passing, struck deep into Clement’s soul. Here was a man who had ended his days in holy stillness, his battles won. And Clement knew much of hermits—had read their lives in the Vatican’s manuscripts, spoken with mendicant friars, heard Jerome’s tales of Camaldoli’s anchorites, where nobles and warriors had ended their days in caves, wrestling with their own weakness until they turned it to strength.

*"Heaven has led me here,"* he thought. *"This cell shall be my fortress."*

Yet he would not take its comforts without penance. He compared it to the brutal solitudes of the ancient saints—Jerome’s desert, Benedict’s thorn-choked crevice, the damp dungeons and stone coffins of others. *"This place is too kind,"* he muttered. *"I must make it a crucible."*

And so he vowed: *"For a year and a day, I will see no man’s face."*

He filled his hours with prayer, labor, and mortification. By night, he stole out to dig gardens for the poor, left baskets at humble doors, carved scripture into the rocks along the road—words of comfort for weary travelers, who had never heard the Gospels in their own tongue. *"Perhaps,"* he thought, *"a chance word may light some darkened heart."*

The villagers, awed by his unseen presence, his wise counsel, his refusal of all but bread and water, soon hailed him as a saint. Yet inside the cell, Clement knew himself no saint. *"The birds do not trust me,"* he lamented. *"The owl that lived here fled. They know a true hermit’s spirit is lacking."*

For a time, he numbed his grief with labor. But then came a darkness deeper than any before—a despair so vast it choked prayer itself. Voices whispered: *"Kill yourself. End it."* He begged God for any other trial—flames, scourges, demons in visible form—anything but this hollow, gnawing void.

*"What if solitude destroys more souls than it saves?"* he wondered wildly. *"What if the hermits who vanished were not martyrs but lost souls, devoured by their own thoughts?"*

He tried music, as David had for Saul, but his own hands could not lift his spirit. *"Saul had a holy minstrel,"* he wept. *"I am alone with the darkness."*

And so he lay, crushed beneath the weight of his own heart, while the ancient warning echoed in his ears:

---

(1) In an age before printed Scripture, the poor found solace in words carved on stone—gospels in their own tongue, fresh as dew on parched earth.

(2) Some ancient writers hinted that hermits lost to "serpents" were not martyrs but victims of despair—a thought Clement now knew too well.
