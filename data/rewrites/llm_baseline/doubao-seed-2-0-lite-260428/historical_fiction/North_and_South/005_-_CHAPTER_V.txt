“I ask Thee for a thoughtful love, Through constant watching wise, To meet the glad with joyful smiles, And to wipe the weeping eyes; And a heart at leisure from itself To soothe and sympathise.” — ANON.

Margaret sat and listened gently as her mother chattered through all her little plans to bring small, much-needed comforts to the poorest of Helstone’s parishioners. She could not do otherwise than pay attention, though each new project landed like a quiet stab to her heart. By the time the first frost settled sharp on the ground, they would be hundreds of miles away from the village. Old Simon’s rheumatism would gnaw at his joints, his eyesight would dim even further, and there would be no one to pop in and read him his favourite psalms, leave a steaming porringer of broth on his windowsill, or wrap his aching limbs in that soft red flannel he had long complained he could not afford. Even if some stranger stepped in to tend to him, he would watch in vain through the forest edge for Margaret’s familiar step. Mary Domville’s little crippled boy would drag himself to the cottage door day after day, waiting for her to bring the carved wooden animals she whittled for him, and she would never come. None of these dear, simple friends would ever understand why she had vanished — and there were dozens more besides.
“Your father has always spent every penny of his living’s income back on this parish,” Mrs. Hale was saying. “I may be dipping a little into the next quarter’s dues to cover these little extras, but the winter is shaping up to be a harsh one, and our poor must not go without.”
“Oh, mamma, let us give them all we possibly can,” Margaret burst out eagerly, not stopping to weigh the practical prudence of the cost, only clinging tight to the thought that every kindness they offered now was the last they would ever be able to give. “We may not be here much longer, after all.”
“Darling, are you unwell?” Mrs. Hale asked, her face softening with anxious concern as she misinterpreted Margaret’s quiet hint at their coming upheaval. “You look so pale and worn. It’s that soft, damp, unhealthy Helstone air, I’m sure of it.”
“No, no, mamma, it is not that,” Margaret said, smiling faintly as she kissed her mother’s cheek. “This air is delicious. It smells of wild pine and hawthorn, sweeter by far than all the soot and smoke of Harley Street. I am only tired. It must be close to bedtime, surely?”
“Half-past nine, nearly. Off you go to bed, dear, ask Dixon to bring you up a basin of warm gruel. I’ll come tuck you in the second you’re settled. I do fear you’ve caught a chill, or breathed in the foul fumes rising off those stagnant ponds at the edge of the common —”
Margaret slipped up the stairs, and meekly ate the gruel Dixon brought her to soothe her mother’s worries. She lay languid under the covers until she heard her mother’s bedroom door click shut and lock, then she sprang out of bed, threw on her wool dressing gown, and paced the length of her small room until a creak from the loose floorboard by the wardrobe reminded her she must not make a sound. She curled herself up on the deep, recessed window seat, and pressed her forehead to the cold glass. That same morning, she had stood in this very spot and laughed to see the sun glint bright off the square stone church tower, promising a day of clear, golden sunlight. Now, barely sixteen hours later, her heart was too heavy for tears, a cold, dull ache leaching all the youth and buoyancy out of her chest as if it would never return. Henry Lennox’s visit, his unexpected proposal, felt like a half-remembered dream, something entirely separate from her real life. The harsh, unyielding truth was that her father had let temptation fester into deep, unshakable doubt until he could no longer bear to serve as a priest in the Church of England — a schismatic, an outcast — and every single terrible change coming for them all bloomed out from that one blighting fact.

She stared out at the dark grey outline of the church tower, sharp and straight against the endless, deep blue of the night sky. She gazed and gazed, feeling as if she could fall forever into that transparent vastness, seeing further and further into the dark silence with no end in sight, but no sign of God anywhere. For a wild, despairing moment, it felt worse than if the whole earth was sealed under an iron dome — at least beyond that dome you might imagine the Almighty’s peace and glory waiting. This infinite, quiet stretch of space felt like a mockery: all the cries of the world’s suffering poor might soar up into it and vanish completely, lost forever before they ever reached His throne.
Lost in that dark spiral, she did not hear her father come in. The moonlight was bright enough to show him her huddled, bowed form by the window, and he touched her shoulder gently before she realised he was there.
“Margaret, I saw your light under the door. Come, pray with me — just the Lord’s Prayer. It will do us both good, I think.”
Mr. Hale and Margaret knelt side by side on the carpet before the window seat, he looking up toward the stars, her head bowed low in humbled shame. God was there, close around them, hearing her father’s soft, whispered words. Her father might have abandoned his clerical post, but only minutes before, Margaret had tangled herself in far more bitter, unthinking doubt than he ever had. She did not speak a word once the prayer was done, but slipped back to her bed like a small child ashamed of a fault, resolved that no matter how tangled and perplexing the world around her grew, she would trust to take only the one small step laid out in front of her that hour.

That night, the memory of Henry Lennox’s proposal, rudely pushed aside by the day’s upheaval, haunted all her fevered dreams. He was climbing a tree of impossible, mythic height to reach the branch where her bonnet had got stuck, he lost his footing and fell, she screamed and struggled to save him but an invisible, iron-strong hand held her back. He was dead. Then the scene shifted without warning, and she was back in the Harley Street drawing room, chatting with him as easily as she always had, all while the cold, unshakable certainty lingered in her bones that she had watched him plummet to his death.
It was a miserable, restless night, no rest at all for the day ahead. She woke with a start, unrefreshed, and the full weight of their reality pressing down on her heavier than any bad dream. It was not only the sorrow of leaving Helstone, but the terrible sharp discord woven through it: how far had her father wandered, led by doubts that had always felt to her like temptations of the Devil? She ached to ask him, and yet she would not have borne to hear the answer for all the world.

The bright, crisp morning put Mrs. Hale in an unusually light, happy mood at breakfast. She chattered on nonstop, laying out all her village kindness plans, paying no mind to her husband’s silence or Margaret’s short, monosyllabic replies. Before the table had even been cleared, Mr. Hale stood up, leaned one hand flat on the wood to steady himself, and did not look at either of them as he spoke.
“I will not be home till evening. I am going out to Bracy Common, and will ask Farmer Dobson to put me up for dinner. I shall be back for tea at seven.”
Margaret knew exactly what that meant. By seven o’clock, he planned to break the full truth of his resignation to Mrs. Hale. But she could not bear to let that heavy, impending weight hang over all of them for a whole long day. It would take every hour left to comfort her mother, once the news was out. While she stood by the window turning over how to start the conversation, and waiting for the servant to leave the parlour, Mrs. Hale had already slipped upstairs to pull on her bonnet and shawl, ready to head to the village school. She came back down brisker than usual, in unusually high spirits.
“Mamma, come take just one turn round the garden with me,” Margaret said, slipping her arm tight around her mother’s waist.
They stepped out the open French window. Mrs. Hale prattled on about something Margaret barely heard, her eyes fixed on a bumblebee rummaging deep in the bell of a foxglove. She would not speak until that bee flew out with its spoil, she decided — that would be her sign.
The bee buzzed free, and Margaret blurted the three hard, unvarnished facts out before she could second-guess herself.
“Mamma! Papa is going to leave Helstone. He’s going to leave the Church, and we are going to live in Milton-Northern.”
“What nonsense is this?” Mrs. Hale asked, her voice sharp with shocked disbelief. “Who has been filling your head with such ridiculous stories?”
“Papa told me himself,” Margaret said, reaching for the soft, comforting words that would not come. They were right by the old wooden garden bench, and Mrs. Hale sank down onto it and began to cry.
“I do not understand you,” she said. “Either you have made some terrible mistake, or I have missed everything.”
“No, mamma, I have made no mistake.” Margaret sat down beside her, and told her every last detail: the letter to the bishop, the consultation with Mr. Bell, Frederick’s godfather, the plan to resettle in the northern manufacturing town. Mrs. Hale never took her eyes off Margaret’s face as she spoke, and the shadow that fell over her features told Margaret she had accepted it was all true.
“It cannot be real,” Mrs. Hale said at last. “He would have told me long before it ever came to this.”
Margaret felt a sharp rush of anger on her mother’s behalf. Whatever her mother’s small faults of discontent and complaining, her father had been wrong to leave her to learn of his life-altering choice from their daughter. She pulled her mother’s unresisting head to rest on her breast, and stroked her hair softly.
“Dearest, darling mamma, we were both so afraid of causing you pain. Papa suffered so terribly, all those months he was wrestling with his doubts — you know your health is frail, and he did not want to put you through all that awful suspense.”
“When did he tell you, Margaret?” Mrs. Hale asked, a faint edge of wounded jealousy in her voice.
“Only yesterday,” Margaret said quickly, sensing that sting. “Poor papa — think of all the quiet pain he has carried alone all this time.”
“But what does he mean by ‘doubts’?” Mrs. Hale asked, half impatient. “Surely he cannot think he knows better than the whole Church? Cannot the bishop talk some sense into him, set him right?”
“I am afraid not,” Margaret said, her own eyes filling with tears as her mother prodded the raw nerve of regret she had been carrying all day. “I did not dare ask him for the details. It is all settled now, in any case. He plans to leave Helstone in a fortnight. I think he has already sent in his formal deed of resignation.”
“A fortnight?” Mrs. Hale cried out. “This is monstrous. It is so unfeeling of him! He has these doubts, gives up his post, and makes all these plans without ever once consulting me. I am sure if he had come to me the very first day he started questioning things, I could have nipped the whole silly idea right in the bud.”
Margaret could not bear to hear her father blamed, even when she thought he had acted foolishly. She knew his long, stubborn silence had come not from cruelty, but from a soft, cowardly tenderness for his wife’s delicate health.
“I almost hoped you would be glad to leave Helstone, mamma,” she said after a quiet pause. “You have never been well in this soft, relaxing air, you know.”
“You cannot possibly think the smoky, sooty air of a manufacturing town, all chimneys and factory grime, is better than this sweet pure air?” Mrs. Hale said, almost indignant. “Fancy living in the middle of factories, surrounded by coarse factory workers! And of course, once word gets out your father has left the Church, no decent society will receive us anywhere. It will be a terrible, terrible disgrace for our whole family! Poor dear Sir John! Thank God he is not alive to see what your father has come to! When I was a girl staying with your Aunt Shaw at Beresford Court, he would toast ‘Church and King, down with the Rump’ every single night at dinner without fail.”
Margaret was glad her mother’s thoughts had drifted away from the hurt of being kept in the dark. “We barely see any society here anyway, mamma. The Gormans, our nearest neighbours, who we never even visit, make their living as carriage-trade merchants — just as much in trade as any of the Milton manufacturers.”
“Yes, but the Gormans made carriages for half the county’s gentry, and mixed in proper society! These factory folk — who on earth wears cotton when they can afford linen, anyway?”
“Very well, mamma,” Margaret said, smiling faintly. “I will not defend the cotton spinners, or any other tradespeople, at all. We will hardly see much of them, in any case.”
“Why on earth did your father pick Milton-Northern of all places to settle?”
“Partly,” Margaret sighed, “because it is so very different from Helstone — partly because Mr. Bell says there is steady work for a private tutor there.”
“A private tutor in a factory town? Why can’t he go up to Oxford, and tutor gentlemen’s sons like he ought to?”
“You forget, mamma,” Margaret said gently. “He left the Church over his religious opinions. His doubts would make him a pariah at Oxford, no one would hire him.”
Mrs. Hale fell quiet for a long time, crying softly. At last she looked up, her face streaked with tears.
“And the furniture? How on earth are we supposed to manage a whole removal? I have never moved house in my whole life, and we only have a fortnight to sort it all out!”
Margaret felt an enormous, unnameable relief. Her mother’s distress had shifted away from the great, unanswerable spiritual and social grief, to a practical problem she could help her fix. She planned, promised, and steered her mother through listing every item that needed packing, sorting, or selling, until the woman was almost fully occupied with household logistics. All day long, Margaret never left her mother’s side, bending all her energy to soothe every shifting turn of her feelings. As evening drew near, she was desperate to make sure her father walked in to a soft, welcoming home after a day of draining, tearful goodbyes to every last parishioner.
When she heard his heavy, tired step in the hall, Margaret’s heart sank. She dared not go to meet him, to admit she had broken the news early, for fear of stoking her mother’s jealous, hurt feelings. She saw her mother’s lips twitch, her face flush and pale in turns, as she registered he was home too. Mr. Hale opened the parlour door, and stood in the threshold, uncertain if he should step in. His face was grey and drawn, his eyes wide with a timid, beaten look that made him look pitiful, far older than his years. The sight of that weary, defeated uncertainty tugged straight at his wife’s heart. She walked over to him, and flung herself into his arms, sobbing against his shoulder.
“Oh Richard, Richard, you should have told me sooner!”
Margaret did not wait to hear more. She slipped out of the room, ran up the stairs, threw herself face down on her bed, and buried her face in the pillows to muffle the heaving, hysterical sobs that burst loose after the whole day of rigid, careful self-control.
She had no idea how long she lay there. The housemaid Charlotte crept in to tidy the room, saw her sobbing, and fled on tiptoe to tell Dixon that Miss Hale was crying so hard she was sure to make herself deathly ill. A few moments later, Margaret felt a hand touch her shoulder, and jolted upright. Dixon stood a little ways back, holding a candle behind her so the light would not sting Margaret’s swollen, tear-blinded eyes.
“Oh Dixon, I did not hear you come in,” Margaret said, quickly forcing her trembling self back under control, lifting her feet off the floor as if she had only just woken from sleep. “Is it very late?”
“I can scarcely tell what time it is,” Dixon said, in a deeply aggrieved tone. “Ever since your mamma told me this terrible news when I dressed her for tea, I have lost all count of the hours. I do not know what is to become of all of us. I told Mrs. Hale as much, that poor Sir John would have raged worse than ever if he knew what Mr. Hale had done, leaving the Church at his time of life! I had a cousin once, miss, who became a Methodist preacher at fifty after working as a tailor his whole life — but then he could never even sew a pair of trousers that fit, so it was no surprise. But for Mr. Hale, respected by the whole parish —”
“Dixon.” Margaret’s voice was low and sharp, the kind of quiet tone that carried the weight of a distant, coming storm. She stood upright, tall and steady, and fixed the waiting-maid with a firm, unyielding stare. “Dixon, you forget to whom you are speaking. I am Mr. Hale’s daughter. That kind of talk will not be tolerated in this house. Go now. You have made a great mistake, and once you think on it, I know your own good heart will make you sorry for it.”
Dixon hovered irresolutely by the door for a minute or two. Margaret spoke again, her tone unwavering. “You may leave me, Dixon. I wish to go to bed now.”
Dixon did not know if she ought to take offense and storm off, or burst into tears. Later, she would mutter to Charlotte that Miss Margaret had the same fierce, proud streak in her that poor Master Frederick had always had, and no one else could have spoken to her like that and gotten away with it. She who would have resented such firm words from any other servant or acquaintance found herself subdued entirely, and mumbled in a half-humbled, half-injured tone, “Shall I unfasten your gown and do your hair for you, miss?”
