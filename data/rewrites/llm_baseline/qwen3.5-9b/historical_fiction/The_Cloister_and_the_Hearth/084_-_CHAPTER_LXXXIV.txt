The following day, a Sunday brimming with activity, saw Catherine's household in the Hoog Straet buzzing with purpose. The shop was finally prepared, and Cornelis and Sybrandt were set to open their doors the morrow; their names were already painted boldly above the entrance, accompanied by their distinctive sign: a white lamb suckling a gilded sheep. Eli had arrived, bringing fresh stock from his own stores to ensure they began with a solid foundation. The parents' hearts burned with pride at their enterprise, and the couple strolled together through the garden, united in their resolve. They confessed a weariness for their former existence, agreeing that earning wealth held a far greater charm than squandering it. With vows spoken as firm as cooling wax, they pledged to remain steadfast in commerce.

Catherine's sharp, ever-watchful ear caught this declaration drifting through an open window. She relayed the news to Eli: the evening supper would feature Margaret and her son, serving as a sort of inauguration feast. Here, seasoned wisdom regarding trade would flow from the elders, paired with generous libations to toast the successful transition of these converts from agriculture—a path often yielding wild oats—to the lucrative fields of commerce.

Margaret had joined her mother-in-law, eager to assist and perhaps shake off the deep lethargy that had lately weighed upon her spirit; indeed, both women's cheeks flushed with the heat of exertion and anticipation. Soon, Joan entered carrying a crisp salad harvested from Jorian's garden. "He cut it specifically for you, Margaret," Joan remarked warmly. "You are his constant topic; I fear I shall grow jealous. I did tell him you were hosting a feast today. But, alas, lass, what a sermon was delivered in the new church! Did you hear preaching? I never witnessed it until this very day."

"Would that I had been present," Margaret sighed, her voice tinged with longing. "For I feel utterly parched, lacking the dew of heaven."

"Why, he preaches again this afternoon," Joan noted. "Though perhaps you are needed here?"

"Not she," Catherine interjected firmly. "Go forth if you are inclined."

"Indeed," Margaret replied, hesitating. "Yet, methinks I would dampen the spirits less if I could attend, warmed by the inspiration of a fine sermon."

"Then you must move swiftly," Joan advised. "Observe how the congregation drifts that way. By my soul, there goes the holy friar!" The energetic woman bounded from the room, crossed the street, and intercepted the friar before returning promptly. "There, I was determined to catch him close at hand."

"What did he say to you?"

"He declared, 'My daughter, I shall visit him ere sunset, God willing,'" Joan recounted, her tone softening. "His voice was sweeter than honey. Yet, oh, my ladies, look at those thin cheeks on a young man, and those large eyes—so much like yours, Margaret."

"I have a strong desire to hear him," Margaret admitted. "But my cap is not quite clean, and everyone will be wearing their pristine white head coverings."

"There, take my handkerchief from the basket," Catherine instructed gently. "You cannot keep the child; I require him for my poor Kate. She is having one of her difficult days."

Margaret ascended the stairs, leaving the toddler behind. She found Kate lying in bed. "How dost thou, sweetheart? Nay, I need not inquire. Thou art in grievous pain, yet thou smilest so brightly."

She placed something in the girl's lap. "Here is one thou lovest."

"Two, by my count," Kate answered with an ethereal smile. Suddenly, a spasm seized her, a convulsion that might have caused onlookers to roar like bulls.

"In your lap?" Margaret asked, responding to the girl's gesture.

"Nay, he is too heavy, and thou art in such agony," Kate murmured.

"I love him too dearly to feel his weight," the patient replied softly.

Seizing the moment, Margaret attended to her own preparations. "I am bound for the church," she announced, "to listen to a magnificent preacher."

Kate sighed deeply. "A short while ago, I too was eager to depart. Such is my state for the past month; up and down, up and down, like the waves of the Zuyder Zee. I would rather stay beside thee; but say the word!"

"Nay," Kate insisted, her voice trembling slightly. "Pray, go. Bring me back every single word. Alas, that I cannot go myself." Tears welled in the sufferer's eyes.

This plea resolved Margaret. She kissed Kate tenderly, glanced beneath her eyelashes at the sleeping boy, and let out a small, resigned sigh. "I believe I dare not," she whispered. "I could never kiss him lightly; nor could my father bear to wake a child by day or night. If it is thy wish to rouse him, speak to Aunt Kate the two new words thou hast learned."

With a final, loving glance over her shoulder, she exited, closing the door without a sound.

"Joan, lend me a hand to peel these vegetables," Catherine called out.

"That I shall, mistress," Joan responded immediately.

Cooking resumed with silent, vigorous efficiency. "Now, Joan, remember this rule: those who aid me in cooking and serving the meat shall also share in eating it."

"There are harsher laws in Holland than that," Joan quipped cheerfully. "Thy will is my delight, mistress. Besides, my Luke has missed his supper due to bad luck; he is digging today, though fortune smiles upon him now."

Margaret descended the stairs. "Eh, woman, that is a grim trade," Catherine observed, snapping her fingers toward Rotterdam in admiration. "Look at her! She has merely washed her face and arranged her hair, and now who resembles her? Truly, Rotterdam is the place for you!"

"Give us a kiss, hussy!" Catherine commanded playfully. "Mind now, Eli will not wait for the Duke to finish his sermon. Do not linger once the service concludes."

Joan and Catherine followed Margaret to the doorway, standing there and watching her walk steadily down the street. Amongst the daily routines of homely housewives, a departure holds half the significance of an event itself.

"There, Joan," Catherine mused as they watched her go. "It feels almost as though I had just sent my own daughter to church and stood watching her leave, which I have done countless times. Joan, lass, she will not hear a word against our Gerard. And since he lives, he has treated her cruelly—that is why my heart aches for the poor wretch. I am older and wiser than she. I shall wed her to that simple Luke, and thus, an end to it. What is one grandchild more?"
