The three or four "To Let" boards had leaned against the low paling as long as anyone in the little triangular "Square" could remember, though if they had ever stood upright, it was an age ago. Now they overhung the fence at jagged, threatening angles, resembling a row of wooden choppers poised to strike a passerby, yet never quite severing the connection between the old house and the world outside. There was hardly any "world" to connect to; the street traffic flowed a furlong away, severed by a labyrinth of tenements and alleys that had grown up around the ancient structure like a malignant tumor. The house itself seemed merely tolerated, waiting for a lease or two to expire before the inevitable clearance of the entire neighborhood.

It was built of bloomy, sickly red brick, its walls scarred with the faded crests and clasped hands of insurance companies long since dissolved into dust. Children of the square had once swung on the low gate at the end of the entrance alley until only the solid top bar remained, a skeletal reminder of playfulness in a place of decay. The alley ran past basement windows boarded tight, where tramps had scrawled their cryptic warnings in chalk. Water spilled constantly from the eaves of the encroaching neighbor, washing the path into an uneven, muddy track that cats and dogs claimed as their own. The chances of a tenant were so slim that the "To Let" signs were left to rot, illegible and broken.

For six months, Oleron had passed this ruin twice daily on his way to his new workroom, ten minutes' walk away. For six months, no hatchet-like board had fallen across his path. He usually took the other side of the square, avoiding the broken gate and the rain-worn alley. But one morning, chance led him to the side that ran past the decay, and he paused before one of the inclined boards. It bore the agent's name and an announcement, written in fading ink from the time of Oleron's own youth, stating that the key was to be had at Number Six.

Oleron was already paying more than an author without private means could afford for his separate bedroom and workroom, plus a small rent for storing his grandmother's furniture. His life was a study in inconvenience: the book he needed in bed sat half a mile away at his office, while urgent letters often lay in the pocket of a coat hanging behind his bedroom door. A divided domicile was a curse. Suddenly halted by the notice-board, Oleron looked down through scanty privet bushes at the boarded basements, then up at the blank, grimy windows of the first and second floors, finally resting his gaze on the flat stone coping of the roof. He stood for a minute, thumbing his lean, shaven jaw, before walking slowly across the square to Number Six.

He knocked and waited, but though the door stood ajar, no answer came. A long-nosed man in shirt-sleeves eventually appeared.

"I was arsking a blessing on our food," he said, offering a severe explanation for his silence.

Oleron asked for the key to the old house. The man withdrew again.

Five minutes later, the man returned, chewing thoughtfully, to announce that the key was lost.

"But you won't want it," he added. "The entrance door isn't closed, and a push opens any of the others. I'm the agent, if you're thinking of taking it..."

Oleron crossed the square, descended the broken gate, and stepped into the old wide doorway. To the right, steps descended to roomy cellars; ahead, a staircase with a carved rail rose broad and handsome, though filthy. Oleron ascended, avoiding the rail and wall, and stopped at the first landing. A door facing him was boarded up, but he pushed the one to his right, and an insecure bolt yielded. He entered the empty first floor.

He spent a quarter of an hour exploring the silence, then descended and returned to Number Six.

"Can you tell me the rent?" he asked.

The man quoted a figure, low enough to be explained by the neighborhood's character and the place's abominable state of disrepair.

"Could I rent just a single floor?"

The long-nosed man hesitated. They might...

"Who are they?"

The man named a firm of lawyers in Lincoln's Inn.

"You might mention my name—Barrett," Oleron added.

Work prevented Oleron from visiting Lincoln's Inn that afternoon, but the next day he went and was instantly offered the entire house for purchase: fifty pounds down, the rest on mortgage. It took him half an hour to convince the lawyer that he only wished to rent a single floor. The lawyer hummed and hawed, uncertain of his power to accommodate such a request, but finally agreed that if the notice-boards remained up and the arrangement terminated automatically should the whole house let, something could be done. The risk of the house suddenly letting over his head seemed negligible to Oleron, and he promised a decision within a week. The next day, he toured the house from top to bottom before returning home to bathe.

He was captivated by the portion he intended to claim. Scoured clean, repainted, and furnished with his grandmother's effects, it would be entirely charming. He visited the storage warehouse to refresh his memory of his belongings and take measurements, then went to a decorator. He was busy with his regular work and wished the board had caught his eye sooner or later, but the quickest solution was to suspend work entirely until after his removal.

Two weeks later, his first floor was painted a tender, elder-flower white. The paint was dry, and Oleron was in the midst of his installation. Animated and delighted, he rubbed his hands as he arranged his grandmother's treasures: the tall lattice-paned china cupboard filled with Derby, Mason, and Spode; the large folding Sheraton table; the long, low bookshelves (two of which he had copied); the chairs, Sheffield candlesticks, and riveted rose-bowls. He set these against the newly painted white walls—wood panelled in happy proportions, moulded and coffered to the low window-recesses with a gaiety and rest that modern builders no longer understand. The ceilings were lofty, faintly painted with an old pattern of stars; even the tapering mouldings of his iron fireplace were delicately designed like jewellery. Oleron walked about, rubbing his hands, frequently stopping to enjoy the glimpses from white room to white room.

"Charming, charming!" he murmured. "I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this!"

He bought a bolt and a Yale lock for his door, shutting off his quarters from the rest of the house. Now, if he wanted to read in bed, his book was merely a step away. Throughout the process, he felt exceedingly lucky to have secured the place. He installed a hat-rack in the small square hall and hung his coats and caps. Late at night, passersby looking up over the little serried row of wooden "To Let" hatchets could see the light within Oleron's red blinds, or witness the sudden darkening of one blind and the illumination of another as Oleron, candlestick in hand, moved from room to room, making final adjustments to his furniture or preparing to resume the work his removal had interrupted.

The world had treated Paul Oleron with a cruelty that far exceeded the indifference he usually extended to it, yet at forty-four, he refused to calculate the deficit. To tally his losses against his ambitions would only deepen the gloom he already carried. He was a man who had chosen a path of noble, disinterested generosity, a road paved with ideals that demanded a life devoid of selfish calculation. Recently, a shadow had crept in—a vague suspicion that such a life might be unsustainable, that there existed a limit to his power beyond which decline was inevitable. But he pushed the thought away. He was committed to his craft, to *Romilly Bishop*, and he would not let the fear of obsolescence dictate his steps.

His new home, a house marked by insurance stamps embedded in its brickwork, offered a sanctuary for this struggle. Here, amidst the silence of the old walls, Oleron moved with the precise, spinster-like efficiency born of twenty years of transient living. He had arranged with Mrs. Barrett, a stout Welshwoman whose falsetto voice still carried the distinct accent of Merionethshire, to manage his breakfast and the Saturday cleaning. For the rest, he welcomed the domestic labor as a necessary respite from the strain of creation.

The kitchen, overlooking a narrow alley, contained a peculiar closet that puzzled him upon inspection. It was a former powder room, evidenced by a square sliding hatch high in the door, designed for the receipt of a pistol's discharge. Oleron smiled faintly at the absurdity of it. This space would serve a different master now; it would become his larder. While rummaging on an upper shelf hidden deep within the wall, he discovered two old wooden wig-stands, mushroom-shaped relics of the house's past. They were likely discarded by the painters, but they filled a void in his storage. With ingenuity, he fitted his household linens, boxes, and accumulated papers into the few available spaces, turning the strange architecture of the house to his advantage.

It was early spring when Oleron took possession, driven by the anxiety to publish *Romilly Bishop* before the autumn. He did not intend to force the muse; the novel had begun to speak of itself in his previous workroom, and it required its own time and length. Yet, the distraction of moving had stalled his momentum. On a March morning, he returned from the street carrying two great bunches of yellow daffodils, placing them on his mantelpiece and table to brighten the gloom. Before opening his manuscript, however, he consulted his cheque-book. His balance sat below fifty pounds, a stark reminder that his installation had cost more than anticipated.

"Hm! I'd forgotten rugs and chintz curtains mounted up so," he muttered, his monk-like face clouding with thought. "But it would have been a pity to spoil the place for the want of ten pounds... Well, *Romilly* simply *must* be out for the autumn."

He drew his papers forward, ready to work, but the effort failed immediately. The square outside possessed a chaotic vitality that invaded his peace. Hawkers cried their wares; at midday, children returned from school to swing on his gate; later, an itinerant musician strummed a mandolin beneath his window. Oleron tossed a penny to the musician and tried again, but the silence of his mind was broken by memories of his former rooms—buttercup satin settees, crystal chandeliers, tambour frames. He realized too late that he was merely staring at the emptiness of his new surroundings. He decided to walk, but exhaustion claimed him in his chair, and he slept until half-past four.

The next morning brought a deceptive promise of productivity. The sky was a brilliant white and blue, clouds rolling swiftly over the square, casting shifting patterns of light across his polished table. Oleron made a pattern of dots on the paper, then adjusted his daffodils to center them perfectly. He wrote a sentence that flowed for two lines before dissolving into frantic jottings. He paced the room, convinced he was working, until a sudden idea struck him: the room needed more color. It was too pale, too mild, lacking the robust warmth of life. He measured the window recesses, muttering that he could not afford the expense, until his finger brushed against the painted surface of a window seat.

Beneath the layers of fresh paint, he detected the ghost of old nailheads. Further investigation revealed a keyhole that had been puttied over. A thrill of genuine adventure, rare for Oleron, seized him. "Oho, oho!" he exclaimed. "This is an adventure!"

Armed with a penknife, a hammer, and a chisel, he approached the window seat with cautious reverence. The sound of the mallet striking the wedge echoed violently through the sweet, pale apartment, rattling the panelling like a sounding board. The entire house seemed to wake, a flock of echoes rising from the cellar to the garrets, irritating his nerves. He paused, muffling the tool with a duster, and lifted the lid. Inside, save for a layer of scurf, mold, and cobwebs, lay nothing.

"No treasure there," he said, amused by his own expectation. "*Romilly* will still have to be out by the autumn."

He moved to the second window, and the process repeated until late afternoon. The bedroom seat was empty, but the second sitting-room seat yielded a strange object: a large, irregular triangle of fabric, furred with an inch of dust. It was an ancient bag of frieze-like material, faded to a yellowish-brown on top but revealing shades of crimson where the folds had worn thin.

"Now whatever can that have been?" Oleron mused, standing over the unfolding mystery. "I give it up. Whatever it is, it's settled my work for today."

He folded the enigmatic bundle and shoved it into a corner of the kitchen. Taking pans, brushes, and an old knife, he returned to the sitting-room to scrape, wash, and line his newly discovered boxes with paper. Once finished, he stowed his boots, books, and papers inside, closing the lids with a sense of amusement mixed with a lingering anxiety for the day when he could finally settle down to write.

The sight of the room, which Oleron had found so hauntingly singular, elicited from Miss Bengough not a glance, but a swift dismissal. She turned away as if the place were merely a backdrop for her own entrance, indifferent to the graces of the setting. This was characteristic of her; she cared little for appearances, preferring the messy reality of biscuits from a paper bag to the rigid observance of dining conventions. At thirty-four, the unattached journalist was a striking figure—large, showy, and fair as butter, with the vibrant, almost artificial bloom of a florist's finest specimen. She moved with sudden, ample gestures, leaving behind small whirlwinds of air where her veils and scarves fluttered and spun.

Oleron heard the frantic rustle of her skirts on the staircase and the single, loud knock at his door, a sound that had become familiar since he had settled into this new abode a month prior. Her garments carried the chill of the outer air, and she flung a bundle of ladies' journals onto a chair with theatrical flair.

"Don't knock off for me," she commanded, her mouth full of large-headed hatpins as she removed her hat and veil. "I didn't know if you were straight yet, so I've brought sandwiches for lunch. You've got coffee, I suppose? No, don't get up—I'll find the kitchen."

"Oh, that's all right, I'll clear these things away. To tell the truth, I'm rather glad to be interrupted," Oleron replied, gathering his scattered work and stowing it away.

She was already in the kitchen; he heard the rush of water into the kettle. Ten minutes later, he followed her back to the sitting-room, carrying the coffee and sandwiches on a tray. They sat opposite one another, the tray resting on a small table between them.

"Well, what do you think of the new place?" Oleron asked as she poured the dark liquid.

"Hm!... Anybody'd think you were going to get married, Paul."

He laughed. "Oh no. But it's an improvement on some of them, isn't it?"

"Is it? I suppose it is; I don't know. I liked the last place, in spite of the black ceiling and no watertap. How's *Romilly*?"

Oleron thumbed his chin, a nervous habit. "Hm! I'm rather ashamed to tell you. The fact is, I've not got on very well with it. But it will be all right on the night, as you used to say."

"Stuck?"

"Rather stuck."

"Got any of it you care to read to me?"

Oleron had long been in the habit of reading portions of his work to Miss Bengough. Her comments were always quick and practical, sometimes directly useful, sometimes indirectly suggestive. In return for his confidence, she kept all mention of her own work sedulously from him. His, she insisted, was "real work"; hers merely filled space, not always even grammatically.

"I'm afraid there isn't," Oleron replied, still meditatively dry-shaving his chin. Then, with a burst of candour, he added, "The fact is, Elsie, I've not written—not actually written—very much more of it. Not *any* more of it, in fact. But, of course, that doesn't mean I haven't progressed. I've progressed, in one sense, rather alarmingly. I'm now thinking of reconstructing the whole thing."

Miss Bengough gave a sharp gasp. "Reconstructing!"

"Making *Romilly* herself a different type of woman. Somehow, I've begun to feel that I'm not getting the most out of her. As she stands, I've certainly lost interest in her to some extent."

"But—but—" Miss Bengough protested, her eyes wide. "You had her so real, so *living*, Paul!"

Oleron smiled faintly. He had been quite prepared for her disapproval. He wasn't surprised that she liked *Romilly* as she currently existed; she would. Whether she realized it or not, there was much of herself in his fictitious creation. Naturally, *Romilly* would seem "real," "living," to her.

"But are you really serious, Paul?" Miss Bengough asked presently, staring at him with round-eyed intensity.

"Quite serious."

"You're really going to scrap those fifteen chapters?"

"I didn't exactly say that."

"That fine, rich love-scene?"

"I should only do it reluctantly, and for the sake of something I thought better."

"And that beautiful, *beautiful* description of *Romilly* on the shore?"

"It wouldn't necessarily be wasted," he said a little uneasily.

But Miss Bengough made a large, windy gesture, and then let him have it.

"Really, you are *too* trying!" she broke out. "I do wish sometimes you'd remember you're human, and live in a world! You know I'd be the *last* to wish you to lower your standard one inch, but it wouldn't be lowering it to bring it within human comprehension. Oh, you're sometimes altogether too godlike!... Why, it would be a wicked, criminal waste of your powers to destroy those fifteen chapters! Look at it reasonably, now. You've been working for nearly twenty years; you've now got what you've been working for almost within your grasp; your affairs are at a most critical stage (oh, don't tell me; I know you're about at the end of your money); and here you are, deliberately proposing to withdraw a thing that will probably make your name, and to substitute for it something that ten to one nobody on earth will ever want to read—and small blame to them! Really, you try my patience!"

Oleron had shaken his head slowly as she had talked. It was an old story between them. The noisy, able, practical journalist was an admirable friend—up to a certain point; beyond that... well, each of us knows that point beyond which we stand alone. Elsie Bengough sometimes said that had she had one-tenth part of Oleron's genius, there were few things she could not have done—thus making that genius a quantitatively divisible thing, a sort of ingredient to be added to or subtracted from in the admixture of his work. That it was a qualitative thing, essential and indivisible, passed her comprehension. Their spirits parted company at that point. Oleron knew it. She did not appear to know it.

"Yes, yes, yes," he said a little wearily, by-and-by, "practically you're quite right, entirely right, and I haven't a word to say. If I could only turn *Romilly* over to you, you'd make an enormous success of her. But that can't be, and I, for my part, am seriously doubting whether she's worth my while. You know what that means."

"What does it mean?" she demanded bluntly.

"Well," he said, smiling wanly, "what *does* it mean when you're convinced a thing isn't worth doing? You simply don't do it."

Miss Bengough's eyes swept the ceiling for assistance against this impossible man.

"What utter rubbish!" she broke out at last. "Why, when I saw you last you were simply oozing *Romilly*; you were turning her off at the rate of four chapters a week; if you hadn't moved you'd have had her three-parts done by now. What on earth possessed you to move right in the middle of your most important work?"

Oleron tried to put her off with a recital of inconveniences, but she wouldn't have it. Perhaps in her heart she partly suspected the reason. He was simply mortally weary of the narrow circumstances of his life. He had had twenty years of it—twenty years of garrets and roof-chambers and dingy flats and shabby lodgings, and he was tired of dinginess and shabbiness. The reward was as far off as ever—or if it was not, he no longer cared as once he would have cared to put out his hand and take it. It is all very well to tell a man who is at the point of exhaustion that only another effort is required of him; if he cannot make it, he is as far off as ever.

"Anyway," Oleron summed up, "I'm happier here than I've been for a long time. That's some sort of a justification."

"And doing no work," said Miss Bengough pointedly.

At that, a trifling petulance that had been gathering in Oleron came to a head.

"And why should I do nothing but work?" he demanded. "How much happier am I for it? I don't say I don't love my work—when it's done; but I hate doing it. Sometimes it's an intolerable burden that I simply long to be rid of. Once in many weeks it has a moment, one moment, of glow and thrill for me; I remember the days when it was all glow and thrill; and now I'm forty-four, and it's becoming drudgery. Nobody wants it; I'm ceasing to want it myself; and if any ordinary sensible man were to ask me whether I didn't think I was a fool to go on, I think I should agree that I was."

Miss Bengough's comely pink face grew serious.

"But you knew all that, many, many years ago, Paul—and still you chose it," she said in a low voice.

"Well, and how should I have known?" he demanded. "I didn't know. I was told so. My heart, if you like, told me so, and I thought I knew. Youth always thinks it knows; then one day it discovers that it is nearly fifty—"

"Forty-four, Paul—"

"—forty-four, then—and it finds that the glamour isn't in front, but behind. Yes, I knew and chose, if *that's* knowing and choosing... but it's a costly choice we're called on to make when we're young!"

Miss Bengough's eyes were on the floor. Without moving them she said, "You're not regretting it, Paul?"

"Am I not?" he took her up. "Upon my word, I've lately thought I am! What *do* I get in return for it all?"

"You know what you get," she replied.

He might have known from her tone what else he could have had for the holding up of a finger—herself. She knew, but could not tell him, that he could have done no better thing for himself. Had he, any time these ten years, asked her to marry him, she would have replied quietly, "Very well; when?" He had never thought of it.

"Yours is the real work," she continued quietly. "Without you we jackals couldn't exist. You and a few like you hold everything upon your shoulders."

For a minute there was a silence. Then it occurred to Oleron that this was common vulgar grumbling. It was not his habit. Suddenly he rose and began to stack cups and plates on the tray.

"Sorry you catch me like this, Elsie," he said, with a little laugh. "... No, I'll take them out; then we'll go for a walk, if you like...."

He carried out the tray, and then began to show Miss Bengough round his flat. She made few comments. In the kitchen she asked what an old faded square of reddish frieze was, that Mrs. Barrett used as a cushion for her wooden chair.

"That? I should be glad if you could tell *me* what it is," Oleron replied as he unfolded the bag and related the story of its finding in the window-seat.

"I think I know what it is," said Miss Bengough. "It's been used to wrap up a harp before putting it into its case."

"By Jove, that's probably just what it was," said Oleron. "I could make neither head nor tail of it...."

They finished the tour of the flat, and returned to the sitting-room.

"And who lives in the rest of the house?" Miss Bengough asked.

"I dare say a tramp sleeps in the cellar occasionally. Nobody else."

"Hm!... Well, I'll tell you what I think about it, if you like."

"I should like."

"You'll never work here."

"Oh?" said Oleron quickly. "Why not?"

"You'll never finish *Romilly* here. Why, I don't know, but you won't. I know it. You'll have to leave before you get on with that book."

He mused for a moment, and then said:

"Isn't that a little—prejudiced, Elsie?"

"Perfectly ridiculous. As an argument it hasn't a leg to stand on. But there it is," she replied, her mouth once more full of the large-headed hat pins.

Oleron was reaching down his hat and coat. He laughed.

"I can only hope you're entirely wrong," he said, "for I shall be in a serious mess if *Romilly* isn't out in the autumn."

The fire crackled, casting long, dancing shadows against the walls of Oleron's study. He sat hunched in his chair, the warmth doing little to dispel the chill that Miss Bengough's prophecy had left in his bones. She had spoken of difficulties ahead for *Romilly*, of obstacles that would stall his progress. Now, staring into the flames, Oleron concluded with a bitter certainty that she had been right to keep her mouth shut. There is a peculiar alchemy in human speech where warning acts as a seed; plant the idea of failure, and the soil of the mind inevitably sprouts the very disaster one fears. By voicing her doubts, she had inadvertently conjured the influence hostile to the completion of his novel.

It was absurd, this illogical, dogmatic tendency women possessed to attach such antagonistic forces to specific places. *"You'll never finish *Romilly* here,"* she had declared. Why? Was this her definition of luxury—the kind that saps the springs of action and drags a man down into indolence? The house was charming, undeniably so, yet it was hardly a demoralizing trap. No, Elsie had simply missed the mark again.

Oleron shifted his chair, turning to survey the room that seemed to smile back at him in the firelight. He smiled too, a pitying expression reserved for a maligned apartment. Even the slight lack of robust color he had noted earlier vanished under the soft, golden glow. The drawn chintz curtains, patterned with flowers and trellises and holding baskets of oaten pipes, fell in long, quiet folds. The rows of old books in the cases drank the light richly. The last trace of sallowness had fled with the daylight, leaving behind a space that felt, if anything, more vibrant than before. It was Elsie herself who had seemed the outlier that afternoon, pale and out of step with the room's hidden vitality.

That thought struck him with sudden force, and he returned to it. Yes, the room had done Miss Bengough a disservice. It had subtly highlighted a contrast of qualities. If one were to accept the ridiculous notion that his room was characterized by a certain sparsity and lack of vigor, then so much the worse for Miss Bengough; she erred on the side of redundancy and "muchness." If abstract qualities had to be weighed, Oleron inclined toward the austere.

Here, in the firelight, Oleron made a distinct discovery he wished he had seen sooner. He pictured Miss Bengough as she had appeared that afternoon: large, showy, moistly pink, radiating the quality of a prize bloom exuding from her very skin. Instantly, she suffered in his thought. He recognized now that he had noticed something odd at the time, that his unconscious attitude had been one of critical detachment. The mechanism of her existence was obvious; her melting humidity was the result of analyzable processes. Behind her, lurking in the shadows of her presence, there had seemed to be a dim shape emblematic of mortality. He had never, during ten years of intimacy, dared to ask her to marry him; yet now, for the first time, he felt a profound thankfulness that he had not.

Then, suddenly, his face flamed with shame. What was he doing? Thinking thus of his friend! Elsie Bengough, the good chum, the loyal soul upon whose help he would have counted if the rest of the world failed him—Elsie, whose loyalty would not swerve as long as there was breath in her—to be dissected in his mind like a specimen! He was an ingrate, a cad. Had she been standing there in that moment, he would have abased himself before her.

For ten minutes he sat, gazing into the fire, watching the humiliating red fade from his cheeks. The only sound was a tiny, musical tinkling from the kitchen: the drip of water from an imperfectly turned-off tap into the vessel below. Mechanically, he began to beat his finger against his knee, syncing with the faintly heard falling drops. The tiny, regular movement seemed to hasten the shameful withdrawal from his face. He grew cool once more, and when he resumed his meditation, he was entirely unconscious that he had picked up exactly where he had left off.

It was not only her florid superfluity of build that he had approached with criticism; he was acutely aware of the wide divergence between her mind and his own. He felt no gratitude that their natures had ever run companionably side by side; now, he was filled with questions that extended far beyond that point. Their intellects did not match. Looking back, he doubted whether there had ever been any real coincidence. True, he had read his writings to her, and she had appeared to speak comprehendingly, to the point. But what can a man do when, having assumed another sees the world as he does, he is suddenly brought up short by something that falsifies and discredits everything that has gone before? He doubted all of it now. It occurred to him briefly that the man who demands of a friend more than can be given is in danger of losing that friend, but he pushed the thought aside.

Again, he ceased to think, and again his finger tapped out the rhythm of the distant tap.

And now, if these things were true of Elsie Bengough, they were also true of the creation of which she was the prototype—Romilly Bishop. Since he could say of Romilly what, for sheer shame, he could not say of Elsie, he gave his thoughts rein. He did so in that smiling, fire-lit room, accompanied by the faint, rhythmic plink of the tap.

There was no longer any doubt. He hated the central character of his novel. Even as he had described her physically, she overpowered the senses; she was coarse-fibred, over-colored, rank. It became true the moment he formulated the thought. Gulliver had described the Brobdingnagian maids-of-honor thus, and mentally and spiritually, Romilly corresponded—she was unsensitive, limited, common. The model, he realized with a jolt, stuck out through fifteen vulgar and blatant chapters to such a pitch that, without seeing the reason, he had been unable to begin the sixteenth. He marveled that it had only just dawned upon him.

And *this* was to have been his Beatrice, his vision! As Elsie, she was to have gone into the furnace of his art and emerged as the Woman all men desire. Her thoughts were to have been culled from his finest, her form from his dearest dreams, her setting chosen wherever he could find one fit for her worth. He had brooded long before making the attempt; then one day he had felt her stir within him as a mother feels a quickening, and he had begun to write. Chapter after chapter had followed...

And those fifteen sodden chapters were what he had produced!

Oleron sat up, the realization settling heavily in his chest. She must go. All fifteen chapters of her. That was settled. For what was to take her place, his mind was currently a blank, but one thing at a time. A man is not excused from taking the wrong course because the right one is not immediately revealed. Better would come if it was to come; in the meantime...

He rose, fetched the manuscript pages, and read them over before he should drop them into the fire.

But instead of putting them into the fire, he let them fall from his hand. He became conscious of the dripping of the tap again. It had a tinkling gamut of four or five notes, on which it rang irregular changes. It was foolishly sweet and dulcimer-like. In his mind, Oleron could see the gathering of each drop, its little tremble on the lip of the tap, and the tiny percussion of its fall. *Plink—plunk.* Minimized almost to inaudibility. Following the lowest note, there seemed to be a brief phrase, irregularly repeated. Presently, Oleron found himself waiting for the recurrence of this phrase. It was quite pretty.

But it did not conduce to wakefulness, and Oleron dozed over his fire.

When he awoke again, the fire had burned low, and the flames of the candles were licking the rims of the Sheffield sticks. Sluggishly, he rose, yawned, and went his nightly round of door-locks and window-fastenings before passing into his bedroom. Soon, he slept soundly.

But a curious little sequel followed on the morrow. Mrs. Barrett usually tapped, not at his door, but at the wooden wall beyond which lay Oleron's bed. Then Oleron rose, put on his dressing-gown, and admitted her. He was not conscious that as he did so that morning he hummed an air; but Mrs. Barrett lingered with her hand on the door-knob and her face a little averted and smiling.

"De-ar me!" her soft falsetto rose. "But that will be a very o-ald tune, Mr. Oleron! I will not have heard it this for-ty years!"

"What tune?" Oleron asked.

"The tune, indeed, that you was humming, sir."

Oleron had his thumb in the flap of a letter. It remained there.

"_I_ was humming?... Sing it, Mrs. Barrett."

Mrs. Barrett prut-prutted.

"I have no voice for singing, Mr. Oleron; it was Ann Pugh was the singer of our family; but the tune will be very o-ald, and it is called 'The Beckoning Fair One.'"

"Try to sing it," said Oleron, his thumb still in the envelope; and Mrs. Barrett, with much dimpling and confusion, hummed the air.

"They do say it was sung to a harp, Mr. Oleron, and it will be very o-ald," she concluded.

"And _I_ was singing that?"

"Indeed you wass. I would not be likely to tell you lies."

With a "Very well—let me have breakfast," Oleron opened his letter; but the trifling circumstance struck him as more odd than he would have admitted to himself. The phrase he had hummed had been that which he had associated with the falling from the tap on the evening before.

The mundane, rhythmic drip of the kitchen tap seemed to possess a sinister resonance, echoing with a terrifying precision against the reality of the air itself. This uncanny synchronization did more than unsettle Oleron; it acted as a key, unlocking a hyper-sensitive auditory apparatus within him. The old house, previously a backdrop of silence, suddenly erupted into a cacophony of life. It was not merely the scuttling of mice or the settling of plaster, but a symphony of the inanimate becoming animate. Beams groaned with the weariness of aging giants; joints cracked like dry bones; window sashes whispered their shifts in the frame. Oleron sat in his chair, paralyzed by the realization that the very timber and mortar of his home possessed a winsome, sentient personality. He amused himself with the absurd notion that he was slowly merging with the structure, a harmless author inadvertently laying the foundation for a future haunting.

Yet, while Oleron found solace in this strange communion, Elsie Bengough recoiled in horror. Her initial indifference curdled into a visceral aversion. "This place does not belong to today," she declared, her voice sharp with disapproval. "It is poison for you, Paul. You are drifting into apathy, slipping away from the solid world. You need concrete floors, a gas meter, the grind of real human contact. If I could secure you a job, even just a few days a week, it would save your soul."

Oleron felt a sting of resentment at her diagnosis. "Thank you, Miss Bengough," he replied, his tone cool. "But we must each live our own lives."

"Your own life!" she snapped, turning away and then snapping back. "How long has it been since you stepped outside? Not to mail a letter, but to breathe the open air?"

"Perhaps a little while," Oleron hedged, avoiding her gaze. "Romilly is progressing. I am laying the foundations."

Elsie frowned, the memory of their earlier struggle over the manuscript fresh in her mind. "Ah! So the ridiculous idea persists?"

"I have discarded the old Romilly," Oleron admitted slowly. "I am working on a new one."

The lack of warmth in his voice ignited her temper. "Where is the old manuscript?" she demanded. "I want to see it. I want to drag you back to your senses."

Oleron turned his back, then softened his stance. "It is useless, Elsie. I am responsible for my own path. The manuscript is in the window seat. I was on the verge of burning it, but I spared it."

Elsie moved to the window seat and lifted the lid. A sudden gasp escaped her, followed by a hand pressed firmly to her mouth. "Paul, you must hammer those nails in," she cried out, her voice trembling.

Oleron rushed to her side, panic rising in his chest. "What is it? What happened?"

"You left enough to scratch with," she whispered, revealing a deep, weeping gash running from her wrist to her knuckles.

"Good heavens!" Oleron exclaimed, seizing her hand. "Come to the bathroom. Quickly."

He hurried her through the house, turning on the tap to wash the wound. His hands shook as he cleansed the gash, muttering broken phrases of disbelief. "How did this happen? I checked every box a dozen times. There was no nail there."

"It shall be removed now," he muttered, grabbing a pair of pincers with steady resolve.

While he worked, Elsie sank into a nearby chair, her face pale. In her lap lay the manuscript of *Romilly*. She had not abandoned her obsession. Once the wound was dressed, she returned to her charge. "Oh, Paul, if you publish this, it will be the greatest mistake of your life!" she insisted.

Oleron hung his head, genuinely distressed. The image of the hidden nail haunted him, pushing *Romilly* to the back of his mind. "I can only hope the new version convinces you," he said, his voice barely audible. "If you still dislike her... I must follow my own light."

Silence stretched between them until Oleron sighed. "Come, Elsie. We have gotten along well. Let us not break now."

The words hung in the air, heavy with regret. Elsie was nursing her hand, her eyes closed, but her lips and eyelids quivered in unison. Her voice trembled when she finally spoke. "I cannot help saying it, Paul, but you are so greatly changed."

"Hush, Elsie," he soothed, though his heart hammered. "You have had a shock. How could I change?"

"I don't know," she whispered, her voice thick with emotion. "You are not yourself. This place has stopped your work, twisted you into a stranger, and filled me with terror for you. Oh, how my hand throbs!"

"Poor child," he murmured, offering to take her to a doctor.

"No," she refused, leaning back. "I will manage. Just take the other hand, Paul. Goodbye."

She rose and walked to the door, leaving him alone in the suffocating quiet of the room. He accompanied her to the bottom of the stairs, where she paused in the narrow alleyway. "It is a long walk if you are not here," she said, her voice hollow. "I will send a postcard next time."

At the gate, she turned one last time, her face etched with sorrow. "Leave here, Paul. Everything is wrong with this house."

And then she was gone.

Oleron retreated to his room, crossing directly to the window seat. He opened the lid, staring into the darkness of the box, searching for the phantom nail that had drawn blood from his friend. He found nothing but empty wood. With a shudder, he closed the lid and turned away.

"That is frightening," he muttered to the empty room. "It is impossible that I did not remove that nail."

Oleron understood perfectly what Elsie had intended when she mentioned the postcard preceding her next visit. He realized, with a crushing finality, that she had seen through him—that he knew, and yet he did not want her. This realization struck him with a miserable, pitiful pang when she returned within a week, knocking at his door without announcement. She spoke from the landing, declaring she did not intend to stay, yet he had to press her physically before she would deign to enter.

Her excuse was a professional one: she had heard of an inquiry for short stories and urged him to pursue it. He thanked her politely. Once her business was concluded, however, her anxiety to depart became palpable. Oleron saw through the pretext immediately; he did not seek to detain her, though he accompanied her down the stairs nonetheless.

But Elsie Bengough found no fortune in that house. A second accident befell her. Halfway down the staircase, there was a sharp, splintering crack, and she stifled a loud cry. Oleron knew the woodwork was ancient, yet he himself had ascended and descended these steps frequently without mishap.

Elsie had driven her foot through one of the treads.

He sprang to her side in alarm.

"Oh, I say! My poor girl!"

She erupted into hysterical laughter.

"It's my weight—I know I'm getting fat—"

"Keep still—let me clear these splinters away," he muttered through gritted teeth.

She continued to laugh and sob, insisting it was her weight, that she was becoming too heavy. He thrust downward at the broken boards. The extrication was no easy matter, and her torn boot revealed how badly the foot and ankle within must be abraded.

"Good God—good God!" he muttered over and over again.

"I shall be too heavy for anything soon," she sobbed, her voice trembling between grief and mockery.

But she refused to reascend to examine her injury properly.

"No, let me go quickly—let me go quickly," she repeated.

"But it's a frightful gash!"

"No—not so bad—let me get away quickly—I'm—I'm not wanted."

At those words, that she was not wanted, his head dropped as if she had delivered him a physical blow.

"Elsie!" he choked out, broken and shocked.

But she made a quick, violent gesture, as if discarding something unbearable.

"Oh, Paul, not _that_—not _you_—of course I do mean that too in a sense—oh, you know what I mean!... But if the other can't be, spare me this now! I—I wouldn't have come, but—but—oh, I did, I _did_ try to keep away!"

It was intolerable, heartbreaking; yet what could he do? What could he say? He did not love her...

"Let me go—I'm not wanted—let me take away what's left of me—"

"Dear Elsie—you are very dear to me—"

Again, she made the gesture, as of violently putting something aside.

"No, not that—not anything less—don't offer me anything less—leave me a little pride—"

"Let me get my hat and coat—let me take you to a doctor," he muttered desperately.

She refused. She refused even the support of his arm. She gave another unsteady, jagged laugh.

"I'm sorry I broke your stairs, Paul.... You will go and see about the short stories, won't you?"

He groaned.

"Then if you won't see a doctor, will you go across the square and let Mrs. Barrett look at you? Look, there's Barrett passing now—"

The long-nosed Barrett was looking curiously down the alley, but as Oleron opened his mouth to call him, the man made off without a word. Elsie seemed anxious for nothing so much as to be clear of the place. Finally, she promised to go straight to a doctor, but insisted on going alone.

"Good-bye," she said.

Oleron watched her until she passed the hatchet-like "To Let" boards, holding his breath as if he feared even they might fall upon her and maim her further.

That night, Oleron did not dine. He had far too much on his mind. He paced from room to room of his flat, haunted by Elsie Bengough's cry that still rang in his ears. "I'm not wanted—don't offer me anything less—let me take away what's left of me—"

Oh, if he could only have persuaded himself that he loved her!

He walked until twilight fell, then, without lighting candles, he stirred up the fire and flung himself into a chair.

Poor, poor Elsie!...

But even while his heart ached for her, it was out of the question. If only he had known! If only he had used common observation! But those walks, those sisterly takings of the arm—what a fool he had been!... Well, it was too late now. It was she, not he, who must now act—act by keeping away. He would help her all he could. He himself would not sit in her presence. If she came, he would hurry her out again as fast as he could.... Poor, poor Elsie!

His room grew dark; the fire burned dead; and he continued to sit, wincing from time to time as a fresh, tortured phrase rang again in his ears.

Then suddenly, he knew not why, he found himself anxious for her in a new sense—uneasy about her personal safety. A horrible fancy took him: that even then she might be looking over an embankment down into dark water, that she might even now be glancing up at the hook on the door. Women had been known to do those things.... Then there would be an inquest, and he himself would be called upon to identify her, and would be asked how she had come by an ill-healed wound on the hand and a bad abrasion of the ankle. Barrett would say that he had seen her leaving his house....

Then he recognized that his thoughts were morbid. By an effort of will he put them aside, and sat for a while listening to the faint creakings and tickings and rappings within his panelling.... If only he could have married her!... But he couldn't. Her face had risen before him again as he had seen it on the stairs, drawn with pain and ugly and swollen with tears. Ugly—yes, positively blubbered; if tears were women's weapons, as they were said to be, such tears were weapons turned against themselves... suicide again....

Then all at once he found himself attentively considering her two accidents.

Extraordinary they had been, both of them. He _could not_ have left that old nail standing in the wood; why, he had fetched tools specially from the kitchen; and he was convinced that that step that had broken beneath her weight had been as sound as the others. It was inexplicable. If these things could happen, anything could happen. There was not a beam nor a jamb in the place that might not fall without warning, not a plank that might not crash inwards, not a nail that might not become a dagger. The whole place was full of life even now; as he sat there in the dark he heard its crowds of noises as if the house had been one great microphone....

Only half conscious that he did so, he had been sitting for some time identifying these noises, attributing to each crack or creak or knock its material cause; but there was one noise which, again not fully conscious of the omission, he had not sought to account for. It had last come some minutes ago; it came again now—a sort of soft sweeping rustle that seemed to hold an almost inaudibly minute crackling. For half a minute or so it held Oleron's attention; then his heavy thoughts drifted back to Elsie Bengough.

He was nearer to loving her in that moment than he had ever been. He thought how to some men their loved ones were but the dearer for those poor mortal blemishes that tell us we are but sojourners on earth, with a common fate not far distant that makes it hardly worth while to do anything but love for the time remaining. Strangling sobs, blearing tears, bodies buffeted by sickness, hearts and minds callous and hard with the rubs of the world—how little love there would be were these things a barrier to love! In that sense he did love Elsie Bengough. What her happiness had never moved in him, her sorrow almost awoke....

Suddenly his meditation vanished. His ear had once more become conscious of that soft and repeated noise—the long sweep with the almost inaudible crackle in it. Again and again it came, with a curious insistence and urgency. It quickened a little as he became increasingly attentive... it seemed to Oleron that it grew louder....

All at once he started bolt upright in his chair, tense and listening. The silky rustle came again; he was trying to attach it to something....

The next moment he had leapt to his feet, unnerved and terrified. His chair hung poised for a moment, and then went over, setting the fire-irons clattering as it fell. There was only one noise in the world like that which had caused him to spring thus to his feet....

The next time it came, Oleron felt behind him at the empty air with his hand, and backed slowly until he found himself pressed against the wall.

"God in Heaven!" The ejaculation broke from Oleron's lips. The sound had ceased.

The next moment he had given a high cry.

"What is it? What's there? _Who's_ there?"

A sound of scuttling caused his knees to bend under him for a moment; but that, he knew, was a mouse. That was not something that his stomach turned sick and his mind reeled to entertain. That other sound, the like of which was not in the world, had now entirely ceased; and again he called....

He called and continued to call; and then another terror, a terror of the sound of his own voice, seized him. He did not dare to call again. His shaking hand went to his pocket for a match, but found none. He thought there might be matches on the mantelpiece—

He worked his way to the mantelpiece round a little recess, without for a moment leaving the wall. Then his hand encountered the mantelpiece, and groped along it. A box of matches fell to the hearth. He could just see them in the firelight, but his hand could not pick them up until he had cornered them inside the fender.

Then he rose and struck a light.

The room was as usual. He struck a second match. A candle stood on the table. He lighted it, and the flame sank for a moment and then burned up clear. Again he looked round.

There was nothing.

There was nothing; but there had been something, and might still be something. Formerly, Oleron had smiled at the fantastic thought that, by a merging and interplay of identities between himself and his beautiful room, he might be preparing a ghost for the future; it had not occurred to him _that there might have been a similar merging and coalescence in the past_. Yet with this staggering impossibility he was now face to face. Something did persist in the house; it had a tenant other than himself; and that tenant, whatsoever or whosoever, had appalled Oleron's soul by producing the sound of a woman brushing her hair.

Oleron found himself striding over the loose board Miss Bengough had hastily laid upon the broken step, descending the stairs into the gloom. Hatless and breathless, he did not realize until the cold air of the hallway bit at his neck that he had left the candle burning on the table, the door ajar just enough for his escape, and the latch clicking softly shut behind him. At the foot of the stairs, a frantic shape darted upward from the disused cellars and vanished through the open door. It was only a cat, yet Oleron let out a childish sob, the sound swallowed instantly by the heavy silence of the house.

He pushed through the gate and paused beneath the "To Let" boards, nervously picking at his lip while staring up at the faint, sickly glow filtering through one of the red blinds. Still glancing over his shoulder as if expecting pursuit, he stumbled up the square. A small public-house rounded the corner—a place he had never dared enter—yet now he threw open its door and slammed it shut behind him. He dropped a shilling onto the counter, missing the edge by mere inches.

"B--b--bran--brandy," he stammered, then bent to retrieve his coin.

The bar was empty save for him; the usual clientele of carters, laborers, and local tradesmen huddled in the far compartment beyond the reach of the white-haired landlady's domain. Oleron collapsed onto a hardwood settee with a perforated seat, drained half his brandy, and then, unable to bear the waste, finished the glass.

His mind, fogged by alcohol and terror, drifted to the practicalities of survival. Which of the men in the shadows across the room would agree to move his effects tomorrow?

He ordered another glass.

He could not return to that room where the candle burned alone. No, he could not face the entry, the treacherous staircase, or that pith-white, fascinating chamber that seemed to breathe with malevolent intent. For now, he would revert to his old arrangement: a workroom and separate sleeping quarters. He would visit his old landlady shortly, once he had consumed enough spirits to steady his nerves, and beg for a night's lodging.

His glass was empty. He signaled for a refill and sank back into the chair.

If anyone questioned his sudden departure, he had ample justification. Nails that retracted into the wood to gash a man's hand, steps that shattered underfoot, and women who invaded a man's sanctuary to brush their hair in the darkness—these were reasons enough! He felt querulous, deeply wounded. He had taken the flat for himself, not for invisible specters to perform their rituals. The lawyer in Lincoln's Inn would learn of this outrage within the hour; it was scandalous to bind a man to such a cursed agreement!

A cut-glass partition separated Oleron's corner from the landlady's workspace, though it ended mere inches above the counter. There was no barrier at the far bar. Raising his eyes, Oleron saw faces watching him through the gap, but they dissolved into the shadows the moment he focused on them. He shifted to a deeper corner, hidden from the other patrons, only to find himself directly in the line of sight of the white-haired woman.

She recognized him, perhaps having seen him pass a dozen times already, and soon remarked on the weather. Oleron offered a vague reply, which prompted her to note that the winter had been terrible for influenza, but spring seemed imminent. This trivial exchange with the mundane world steadied him; the idle, nascent wonder whether the landlady brushed her hair nightly and emitted those electric crackles was snuffed out with a snap. He felt better.

With his next glass, the urge to return to his flat became overwhelming. Return? Indeed, he would return! They would soon discover if he was to be evicted like a rat! He wondered why he was behaving so uncharacteristically—sitting hatless, drowning his sorrows in brandy. What if he confessed to the landlady? What if he told her of the caller who scratched her hand on a nail, the misfortune of stepping through a rotten stair, and his own flight from an old house filled with squeaks, creaks, and whispers? She would surely deem him mad. Pshaw! The truth was simpler: he had done too little work, filling his days with moonshine about a new *Romilly* (as if the old one wasn't sufficient), and now he was surprised that the devil would inhabit an empty head.

Yes, he would go back. First, a walk in the fresh air—he had neglected that lately—then he would take himself in hand, settle the mess of the sixteenth chapter of *Romilly* (fancy that he had considered destroying fifteen!), and finally remember his obligations to his fellow men. That was the matter in a nutshell.

He finished the brandy and stepped out into the night.

He walked for some time before the nature of his situation clarified itself. Initially, the fresh air amplified the brandy's heady effect, but soon his mind cleared, sharper than it had been since morning. As clarity returned, his boastful self-assurances wavered, replaced by a firm conviction that, once all explanations were exhausted, something remained utterly inexplicable. The hysteria of the hour faded, replaced by a growing calm, yet a deep, disquieting fear took root. It was a fear for Elsie.

Something in his flat was inimical to her safety. Her two accidents alone might not have convinced him, but she had spoken: "_I'm not wanted here_..." She had declared the place wrong, seeing it before he had. Clearly, if this was true, she must be kept away for reasons far more profound than the humiliation Oleron had suffered. Fortunately, she had expressed an intention to stay away; he must ensure she adhered to it.

Moreover, he realized his first impulse—to never set foot in the place again—was absurd. People did not simply abandon their homes to shadows. With Elsie secured, he could not, with any respect for himself, allow a shadow or a mere danger to expel him. He had to live somewhere, and he would live there. He had to return.

He mastered the faint chill of fear that accompanied this decision, turning abruptly in his walk. If the dread returned, he might seek one more glass of brandy...

But by the time he reached the short street leading to the square, it was too late for more spirits. The public-house was still lit but closed, with a few men standing on the curb. A sudden silence fell over them as Oleron passed. Lower down, the long-nosed Barrett did not return his greeting. Oleron turned into the broken gate, hesitated for an instant in the alley, and then climbed his stairs again.

Only an inch of wax remained in the Sheffield stick, and Oleron refused to light another candle. Deliberately, he forced himself to carry the dying flame and inspect all five rooms before retiring. It was as he returned from the kitchen across his small hall that he noticed a letter lying on the floor. He carried it into the sitting-room and glanced at the envelope before opening it.

It was unstamped, inserted by hand. The handwriting was clumsy, running from start to finish without a single comma or period. Oleron read the first line, checked the signature, and finished the letter.

It was from Barrett, informing Oleron that he would be obliged if Mr. Oleron made other arrangements for preparing breakfasts and cleaning the premises. The sting lay in the postscript, a text of Scripture containing an allusion that could only refer to Elsie Bengough.

A seldom-seen frown carved deeply into Oleron's brow. So! That was it! Very well; they would see about that tomorrow. For the rest, this seemed merely another reason why Elsie must remain away.

Then his suppressed rage erupted.

The foul-minded lot! The devil himself could not have leered more contemptuously at anything that had passed between Paul Oleron and Elsie Bengough, yet this nosing rascal must pry and talk!

Oleron crumpled the paper, held it in the candle flame until it curled and blackened, and then ground the ashes under his heel.

One useful purpose the letter had served: it had ignited a wrathful blaze in Oleron that effectively banished the pale shadows. Nevertheless, one other puzzling circumstance closed the day. As he undressed, he glanced at his bed. The coverlets bore an impress as if someone had lain upon them. Oleron could not recall lying down during the day—offhand, he would say certainly not—but he could not be positive. His indignation for Elsie, acting perhaps with the residue of the brandy, excluded all other considerations. He blew out the candle, lay down, and immediately sank into a deep, dreamless sleep that, in the absence of Mrs. Barrett's morning call, lasted almost a full twenty-four hours.

The twilight settled over Oleron's soul like a physical weight, bringing with it the absolute terror that usually demanded a fortress of convention to hold it at bay. But Oleron was dismantling those fortifications. He had begun the treasonous act of admitting the inexplicable and the horrible into his daily familiarity, treating them not as monsters to be fled, but as variables to be solved. The safeguards of usages and observances, once his bulwarks against the dark, now felt like impertinences when applied to Elsie Bengough's warnings. Two months ago, the label "haunted house" would have chilled his marrow; now, his senses had dulled to a point where he could ask, "Haunted by what?" and feel no chill at all. Horror, he realized with a chilling clarity, lost its power when it could be proven relative.

This descent was driven by a voracious, crafty inquisitiveness. He needed to know, and nothing short of absolute knowledge would suffice. The universe seemed to conspire to answer his questions the moment they were formed, granting him a joy in his own power he hadn't felt since his writing was still a daily delight. The path forward dictated itself: define the problem mathematically. If the old house was the denominator and he the numerator, the unknown co-tenant must be the missing variable. And the key to finding that variable lay in his half-finished novels, the *Romillys*.

Where once he would have deemed such a connection madness, Oleron now embraced the dizzying hypothesis without a tremor. He turned to the pages of the first *Romilly*, reviewing its history with a detached, analytical gaze. He recalled the instant he had found the heroine insufficient, the night he had nearly burned the manuscript, and the morning he had conceived the new version. It was then, humming a tune suggested by a dripping tap, that Mrs. Barrett had informed him he was singing "The Beckoning Fair One."

The title echoed in his mind, a ghostly refrain. The first *Romilly* had been discarded, only for the second to fasten itself upon him with a passion that bordered on hate. He felt a strange, almost credible thrill in the idea that his discarded creation had offended the new one, a malignancy that seemed poised to extend from fiction to the mortal prototype.

"A Good God!" he gasped, the abstraction shattering.

The intrusion of Elsie's real-world existence into his theoretical framework was fatal to his inquiry. He could not think of her, nor of Barrett's letter, without a rising flush and a tightening of the brow. The confrontation with Barrett on the doorstep remained a fresh wound. Barrett, a man of vague threats and muttered farragos about "nameless proceedings," had accused Oleron of having an unwholesome mind. Oleron had retorted with fury, warning the man to keep his tongue, only to return home to find Barrett's face peering through curtains, hunting for evidence. The square had turned against them; Oleron now shopped elsewhere, his domestic peace fractured by the shadow of scandal.

One midday, the weather broke into a raw, gusty storm. Oleron, tired and ill, watched from his window as Elsie Bengough crossed the square. The wind whipped her ample skirts and spun her veil, making her look like a vessel tossed on a rough sea. Panic seized him instantly. He grabbed his hat and sprinted down the stairs, his eyes drawn upward to the eaves as if something unseen pulled them there.

He intercepted her at the gate, his voice a frantic stream of lies. "Too bad, Elsie! I'm urgently called away! I'm afraid I must seem inhospitable." He spun a tale of a sudden trip to town to see Chambers, a man leaving for Brighton, fabricating a brilliant excuse to keep her at bay. He led her away from his house, toward the bus terminus, pretending the world was normal.

On the bus, the air grew heavy. Oleron, pale and shivering without his coat, tried to maintain the charade, but Elsie's calmness unnerved him. When he attempted to announce his departure for a change of air, she simply replied, "Oh?"

"You don't ask me where I'm going," he challenged, trying to rally her.

She stared straight ahead, past the driver. "I know."

"How do you know?" he stammered.

"You're not going anywhere," she said, her voice controlled and terrifyingly even. "You weren't going out this morning. You only came out because I appeared. Don't behave as if we were strangers, Paul."

A flush of shame burned his cheeks. She pressed on, analyzing his visible distress, noting how people turned to look at him. "You ought to go away," she said, her tone shifting from accusation to pity. "But you won't, and I know why."

Oleron shivered, the cold seeping into his bones. He tried to deflect, blaming the harsh life of independent women, the sacrifices they made, the way their innocent acts were twisted into leers and suspicion. He spoke of Barrett, hoping she would take the bait, but she saw through him with perfect ease.

As the bus halted, fresh passengers boarding, she spoke in a dull, final tone. "You'd better get down here and go back, Paul. I understand perfectly. It isn't Barrett. You'd be able to deal with Barrett. It's merely convenient for you to say it's Barrett. I know what it is."

She paused, her eyes earnest. "Before you go, let me tell you why I came up this morning. I came to force your hand. Things couldn't go on as they have been going, you know; and now that's all over."

"All over," he repeated, his mind blank.

"All over. I want you now to consider yourself, as far as I'm concerned, perfectly free. I make only one reservation."

He lacked the spirit to ask what it was.

"If *I* merely need *you*," she said softly, dropping her voice, "please don't give that a thought; that's nothing; I shan't come near for that. But, if *you're* in need of *me*, Paul—and I shall know if you are, and you will be—then I shall come at no matter what cost. You understand that?"

He could only groan.

"So that's understood," she concluded. "And I think that's all. Now go back. I should advise you to walk back, for you're shivering—good-bye."

She handed him a cold, lifeless grip. As the bus lurched forward, Oleron stood on the edge of the kerb. For the first time in all the years he had known her, she parted from him with no smile, no wave of her long arm, leaving him alone with the terrible certainty that the door to his sanctuary was closing, and the thing inside was waking up.

He stood on the curb, submerged in a misery so profound it felt like drowning, watching her until the last fragment of her form vanished from the street. The moment she disappeared, however, a strange, heavy lifting occurred within his spirit. She had granted him his liberty. Technically, he had never truly possessed it, yet now was not the season for semantic nitpicking. He was free to act, and the path forward was suddenly, terrifyingly clear. A swift buoyancy seized him, transforming into a jubilant celebration of his newfound autonomy. Before he had even reached the halfway point to his home, the decision for his next move had crystallized in his mind.

The vicar of the parish where he resided lived merely ten minutes from the square. Oleron turned his steps toward the rectory. He required every scrap of intelligence he could muster regarding the dilapidated house, the insurance markings, and the slanted "To Let" signs plastered against its facade. The vicar was the most logical source for such data. Once this preliminary hurdle was cleared—*aha!* Oleron chuckled internally—the stage was set for things to finally unfold.

Yet, the information yielded was scant. The house was old, the vicar confirmed, though Oleron knew that required no sermon to establish. It was rumored to be haunted, but few ancient dwellings escaped such whispers among the superstitious; the vicar's lament regarding the modern world's lack of faith seemed designed to soothe rather than inform. Oleron smiled, noting the priest's careful, evasive tone.

"You may leave your nerves out of the question," Oleron said smoothly. "How long has the place been empty?"

"A dozen years, I should say," the vicar replied.

"And the last tenant—did you know him, or her?" Oleron felt a distinct prickling along his nerves as he offered the vicar the binary choice of gender.

"Him," the vicar said. "A man. If memory serves, his name was Madley; an artist. He was a great recluse, rarely venturing beyond the premises—and..." The vicar hesitated, then broke into a sudden, candid rush. "Since you seem intent on acquiring this information, and since truth is preferable to garbled rumors, I shall speak plainly. This man Madley died there under somewhat unusual circumstances. The post-mortem revealed not a particle of food in his stomach, despite the fact that he was found to be in possession of money. His frame was utterly worn out. Suicide was suggested, but you must agree that deliberate starvation is, at the very least, an uncommon method of ending one's life. An open verdict was returned."

"Ah!" Oleron murmured. "Does this parish possess a comprehensive history?"

"No; only partial records. I have kept notes on the purely ecclesiastical history, the registers, and such matters, which I would be delighted to show you if you wish to see them. However, it is a vast parish, I have but one curate, and my leisure, as you can easily imagine..."

The discussion of the parish's size and the vicar's limited free time consumed the remainder of the interview. Oleron thanked the clergyman, took his leave, and walked slowly home.

He walked slowly for a calculated reason. Twice within a stone's throw of the gate, he turned away, forcing himself to detour for another twenty minutes or so. He faced a perilous task requiring absolute mental concentration: he needed to bring his mind into a state of unpreoccupied receptivity, allowing him to perceive the house exactly as he had upon that morning when, having completed his removal, he had sat down to write the sixteenth chapter of the first *Romilly*.

If he could recapture that initial impression, he hoped for far more than mere observation. Previously, he had carried a heavy burden of mental clutter. Before the influence of the place could penetrate his defenses, it had been forced to contend with the inertia of those dreary chapters. No results had manifested. The process had been one of slow saturation, a gradual charging until the vessel brimmed. But now he was light, unburdened, finally rid of both the *Romilly* and its prototype. Now, for the new unknown: coy, jealous, bewitching, the Beckoning Fair One!

At half-past two in the afternoon, he inserted his key into the Yale lock, entered, and closed the door behind him.

His fantastical attempt was instantly and astonishingly successful. He could have shouted with triumph as he stepped into the room; it felt as though he had *escaped* into it. Once more, as in the days when his writing possessed a daily freshness, wonder, and promise, he felt that new ease, mastery, exhilaration, and release. The air of the place seemed thick with oxygen; his specific gravity appeared to have shifted, his tread less ponderous. The flowers in the bowls, the fair proportions of the meadowsweet-colored panels and moldings, the polished floor, and the lofty, faintly starred ceiling seemed to laugh their welcome. Oleron actually laughed back, speaking aloud.

"Oh, you're pretty, pretty!" he flattered the room.

Then he lay down on his couch.

He spent that afternoon as a convalescent expecting a dear visitor might spend it—in a delicious vacancy, smiling occasionally as if in sleep, ever lifting drowsy, contented eyes to his alluring surroundings. He remained thus until darkness fell, and with it, the nocturnal noises of the old house.

But if he waited for a specific occurrence, he waited in vain.

He waited similarly in vain the following day, maintaining, though with less ease, that sensitized-plate-like condition of his mind. Nothing happened to register an impression. Whatever entity he so patiently courted seemed both shy and exacting.

Then, on the third day, he believed he understood. A look of gentle drollery and cunning settled into his eyes, and he chuckled.

"Oho, oho!... Well, if the wind sits in *that* quarter, we must see what else there is to be done. What is there, now?... No, I won't send for Elsie; we don't need a wheel to break the butterfly on; we won't go to those lengths, my butterfly...."

He stood musing, thumbing his lean jaw, looking askance. Suddenly, he crossed to his hall, took down his hat, and went out.

"My lady is coquettish, is she? Well, we'll see what a little neglect will do," he chuckled as he descended the stairs.

He sought a railway station, boarded a train, and spent the rest of the day in the countryside. Yes, Oleron believed *he* was the man to deal with Fair Ones who beckoned, invited, and then retreated into shyness and hesitation!

He did not return until after eleven that night.

"Now," he murmured as he walked along the alley, reaching into his pocket for his keys, "my Fair Beckoner!"

Inside his flat, he was perfectly composed, perfectly deliberate, exceedingly careful not to reveal himself. As if to signal his intention to retire immediately, he lit only a single candle. Setting out with it on his nightly round, he affected a yawn. He entered the kitchen first. A full moon hung overhead, casting a lozenge of moonlight, almost peacock-blue by contrast with the candle's frame, onto the floor. The window was uncurtained, allowing him to see the reflection of the candle and, faintly, his own face as he moved. The door of the powder-closet stood slightly ajar; he closed it before sitting down to remove his boots on the chair cushioned with the folded harp-bag. From the kitchen, he passed to the bathroom. There, another slant of blue moonlight cut the windowsill and lay across the pipes on the wall. He visited his seldom-used study, standing for a moment gazing at the silvered roofs across the square. Then, walking straight through his sitting-room, his stockinged feet making no sound, he entered his bedroom and placed the candle on the chest of drawers. His face throughout bore no expression save that of tiredness. He had never been wiler nor more alert.

His small bedroom fireplace stood opposite the chest of drawers where the mirror rested, while his bed and the window occupied the remaining sides of the room. Oleron drew down his blind, removed his coat, and then stooped to retrieve his slippers from beneath the bed.

He could offer no rational explanation, yet he never for an instant doubted that the manifestation withheld for two days was imminent. Nor, though he could not guess its shape, did he feel fear. Startling or surprising it might be; he was prepared for that. His scale of sensation had simply become depressed. His hand moved this way and that under the bed, searching for his slippers.

But despite his caution, method, and preparedness, his heart gave a leap and a pause that was almost horrid. His hand had found the slippers, yet he remained on his knees; save for this circumstance, he would have fallen. The bed was low; the groping for the slippers accounted for the turn of his head to one side; and he maintained the posture until he had partly recovered his composure. When he finally rose, there was a drop of blood on his lower lip where he had caught it with his teeth, and his watch had jerked from the pocket of his waistcoat, dangling at the end of its short leather guard.

Then, before the watch ceased its little oscillation, he was himself again.

In the center of his mantelpiece stood a picture, a portrait of his grandmother. He positioned himself before it so that he could see in the glass of the portrait the steady flame of the candle burning behind him on the chest of drawers. He could also see in the picture-glass the little glancings of light from the bevels and facets of the objects surrounding the mirror and candle. But he saw more. These twinklings and reflections and re-reflections did not change their positions; yet there was one gleam that moved. It was fainter than the rest, drifting up and down through the air. It was the reflection of the candle on Oleron's black vulcanite comb, and each downward movement was accompanied by a silky, crackling rustle.

Oleron, watching the drama unfold in the glass of his grandmother's portrait, continued to play his part. He felt for his dangling watch and began to wind it slowly. Then, for a moment ceasing to watch, he emptied his trouser pockets, placing the pennies and halfpennies methodically in a neat row on the mantelpiece. The sweeping, minutely electric noise filled the entire bedroom, and had Oleron altered his point of observation, he could have positioned the dim gleam of the moving comb so that it almost outlined his grandmother's head.

Any other head whose outline it might have been following was invisible.

Oleron finished emptying his pockets. Then, under the cover of another simulated yawn—not so much summoning his resolution as overmastered by an exorbitant curiosity—he swung suddenly around. That which was being combed was still unseen, but the comb did not stop. It had altered its angle slightly and moved a bit to the left. It was passing, in fairly regular sweeps, from a point more than five feet from the ground in a roughly vertical direction to another point a few inches below the level of the chest of drawers.

Oleron continued to act to perfection. He walked to his little washstand in the corner, poured out water, and began washing his hands. He removed his waistcoat and continued his preparations for bed. The combing did not cease, and he stood for a moment in thought. Again his eyes twinkled. The next move was very cunning.

"Hm!... I think I'll read for a quarter of an hour," he said aloud.

He passed out of the room.

He was away for a couple of minutes; when he returned, the room was suddenly quiet. He glanced at the chest of drawers; the comb lay still, resting between the collar he had removed and a pair of gloves. Without hesitation, Oleron extended his hand and picked it up. It was an ordinary eighteenpenny comb, taken from a card in a chemist's shop, of a substance with a definite specific gravity, no more capable of rebellion against the laws of existence than the worlds keeping their orbits through the void. Oleron placed it down again, then glanced at the bundle of papers in his hand. What he had fetched was the fifteen chapters of the original *Romilly*.

"Hm!" he muttered as he threw the manuscript into a chair. "As I thought.... She's just blindly, ragingly, murderously jealous."

***

On the night following that one, and on the subsequent night, and for many nights and days thereafter—so many that he began to lose count—Oleron, courting, cajoling, neglecting, threatening, beseeching, consumed by unappeased curiosity and indifferent to the fact that his life had become a consuming passion and desire, continued his search for the unknown co-numerator of his abode.

As the days bled into one another, the rhythm of Oleron's life slowed to a near halt. Few climbed his stairs save for the postman, and even his footsteps grew so sparse that the silence between visits stretched longer than a week. Letters piled up, unanswered, until the publishers' inquiries regarding his new manuscript dissolved into dust. He ignored the first, forgot the second, and never saw a third.

Outside, the world bloomed with a deceptive cheer. The privet bushes burst into flower, lining the kerbs with baskets of color, and the sun warmed the air with a brightness that felt almost hostile. Oleron bought flowers daily, filling his room with a clamoring demand for freshness, yet the act of leaving his sanctuary became a torture he could not endure. With every step taken to procure blooms, he returned with a profound, trembling relief. A subtle shift had occurred within him—a distortion of sensation that was not a return to normalcy, but an expansion of his capacity for terror. He had acquired a new name for his dread: agoraphobia. The open air, the vast spaces, the unguarded back exposed to the void—all of it now promised a horror that could strike without warning.

He engineered a solution: his food and flowers were delivered to his door. He rubbed his hands in triumph at this expedient. Now, he could choose his confinement. Soon, immurement became his pleasure.

Yet, he was not happy. If he was, his joy twisted into something grotesque. He fretted, he wept for a weakness that felt like a disease, yet he refused to trade his sad, static existence for the noisy mirth of the street. Noise itself became an acute physical pain, sharper than the fear that made him cling to railings when he dared venture out. He moved through his rooms in silence, closing doors with a gentleness that preserved the stillness, finding delight in the quiet. Sundays were intolerable. Under the fine weather, the square below his window became a stage for the sect of the long-nosed Barrett. Drums beat, brass instruments swelled, and voices rose in anguished prayer, struggling against their God. One Sunday, mid-rhapsody, Oleron lunged to his blind, pulling it down just as his own name was shouted in a fresh torrent of religious fervor.

Sometimes, in the suffocating quiet, he would stand and whisper. "Romilly!" he called, waiting for a phantom reply. More often, his whispers were nameless pleas into the dark.

There was a specific spot in his apartment he haunted with increasing obsession: the threshold of his bedroom door. By opening every internal door and positioning himself there, he could survey his entire domain—his sitting room, most of his bedroom, and glimpses of the kitchen, bathroom, and study—without moving. He stood there breathless, finger pressed to his lips, watching the empty rooms. On one occasion, the thought struck him with cold clarity: had the dead Madley ever realized the strategic power of this vantage point?

Light, once a comfort, now burned like a flame in his brain. Direct sunlight was unbearable; diffused light was a dull, numbing ache. He began the daily ritual of lowering his crimson blinds, transforming his rooms into the blood-red gloom of a photographer's darkroom. He made brief, daring excursions to adjust them, always ensuring his retreat remained open.

One afternoon, as he lowered the blind in his study, he broke into a soft, hysterical laugh. "That bilks Mr. Barrett!" he whispered, finding amusement in the baffling of the fanatic.

But the laughter was short-lived. Another day, a terror seized him. Standing in the embrasure of the window where he had found the harp-bag, he thought he saw the tail of a black-and-white checkered skirt vanish around the corner. Was it Elsie? He listened in agony for the sound of her tread on the stairs.

Silence. After a few minutes, he exhaled in relief. "By Jove, but that would have compromised me horribly!" he muttered, repeating the phrase as if it were a mantra. "No woman would stand that... oh, compromising in the extreme!"

Yet, beneath the relief, a deeper sorrow persisted. Fits of quiet weeping took him, coming and going like clouds over the square. Perhaps he was neither happy nor unhappy; perhaps he was simply waiting. Nothing had been withdrawn from him, for nothing had been granted. He waited in his flower-laden, crimson-dimmed apartment for the granting, for the arrival of the Fair One.

He paid no mind to his dwindling funds or his cessation of work. He had not stopped working; he was merely gestating a masterpiece. He was making a Mistress. Lovely creations were not born easily; they required the weeping, the vain hope, the relentless pursuit of the capricious, mocking spirit that eluded the hunter. Let him hunt a little longer, and she would be panting in his arms. Those who thought he had ceased his labor were fools.

If everything else was falling away, he let it go willingly. When the Fair One beckons, all else fades. He winked at the world, promising himself he would endure her neglect, turning her jealous wiles against her. In the end, all falls away. She beckons, and everything goes.

So Oleron kept his strategic post at the bedroom door, watching, waiting, smiling with his finger on his lips. It was his duteous service, his worship, his troth-plighting. Even when he found himself hating the dead Madley, wishing he had never lived, he felt that hatred, too, was an acceptable offering.

But as he moped, chafing that the Bride made no sign, he made a discovery he should have made weeks prior. It was through his jealousy of Madley that he remembered the fifteen discarded chapters of *Romilly*. He had forgotten them, thrown them back into the window seat, but his own envy of the dead man triggered a memory of Elsie's jealousy of her flesh-and-blood rival. Had he truly expected his Desire to manifest while the evidence of his divided allegiance still existed? What of her passion, fierce enough to attempt destruction twice?

Fool that he had been!

"If that was all the pledge and sacrifice she required," he thought, "she should have it—and quickly!"

He retrieved the manuscript from the window seat and carried it to the fire, which he kept burning constantly. The warmth released the last scent of the flowers that banked his room. Time had lost meaning; his clock had long since stopped. He knew it was late. Kneeling before the flames, he began to remove the fastening holding the sheets together.

Then, a start. He turned his head, listening intently. The sound was faint, barely a tap repeated twice or thrice, yet it filled him with alarm. His face darkened as it came again.

A voice drifted from the landing outside.

"Paul!... Paul!..."

It was Elsie.

"Paul!... I know you're in... I want to see you...."

He cursed her silently but remained perfectly still. He had no intention of admitting her.

"Paul!... You're in trouble.... I believe you're in danger... at least come to the door!..."

Oleron smothered a low laugh. It amused him that she, supposedly in danger, spoke of *his* peril. Well, if she was, serve her right. She knew, or claimed to know, all about it.

"Paul!... Paul!..."

"_Paul!... Paul!_..." He mimicked her under his breath.

"Oh, Paul, it's _horrible_!..."

Horrible, was it? Let her get away.

"I only want to help you, Paul.... I didn't promise not to come if you needed me...."

He was impervious to the pitiful sob that cut through her cry. The devil take the woman! Should he shout at her to leave? No. Let her call, knock, and sob. She had a gift for it; she must not think her tears would move him. They only irritated him, setting his teeth and shaking his fist in the dark. Let her sob.

"_Paul!... Paul!_..."

With his jaw clenched, he dropped the first page of *Romilly* into the fire. Then, sheet by sheet, he fed the manuscript to the flames.

For many minutes, the calling continued behind his door, a desperate plea for entry. Then, suddenly, it ceased. He heard the slow, heavy descent of feet down the stairs. He listened for a fall, a cry, the crash of the handrail, but there was none. She was spared. Apparently, her rival had allowed her to crawl away abject and beaten. Oleron heard her footsteps pass under his window before she vanished into the night.

He dropped the last page into the fire and rose with a low laugh. He looked fondly around his room.

"Lucky to get away like that," he remarked. "She wouldn't have got away if I'd given her as much as a word or a look! What devils these women are!... But no; I oughtn't to say that; one of 'em showed forbearance...."

Who showed forbearance? And what was forborne? Ah, Oleron knew. Contempt, no doubt, had driven her away, but that didn't matter. The pestering creature had been allowed to go unharmed. Yes, she was lucky; Oleron hoped she knew it.

Now, now, now for his reward!

Oleron crossed the room. All his doors stood open. His eyes shone as he placed himself within the frame of his bedroom door.

Fool that he had been, not to destroy the manuscript sooner!

In a houseful of shadows, how should he know his own Shadow? How, amidst a cacophony of noises, distinguish the summons that was at hand? Trust him! He would know! The room was a jugglery of dim lights: the struggle of a street lamp through the blind at his elbow, the greenish-blue moonlight filtering through the distant kitchen door, the sulky glow of the dying fire beneath the black ashes of the burnt manuscript, the glimmering of tulips and moon-daisies in their bowls. None of these tricks bewildered him. It was he, not she, who had delayed the shadowy Bridal. He hung his head in mute acknowledgment, then bent his eyes back to the deceiving, puzzling gloom. He would have called her name had he known it—but now he would not ask her to share even a name with the other.

His own face, framed by the door, glimmered white as the narcissi in the darkness.

A shadow, light as fleece, seemed to take shape in the kitchen—a cloud passing over an unseen moon. The low illumination on the blind dimmed, as if a lamplighter had turned down the flame. The fire settled, letting down the charred papers; a flower fell from a bowl, lying indistinct on the floor. All was still. Then, a stray draft moved through the old house, passing before Oleron's face.

Suddenly, inclining his head, he withdrew slightly from the doorjamb. The wandering draft caused the door to shift on its hinges. Oleron trembled violently, stood for a moment longer, and then, reaching out, softly drew the door shut. He sat down on the nearest chair and waited, as a man might await the calling of his name to summon him to some weighty, high, and privy audience.

The soul possesses a specific susceptibility to anemia. When the pitch of life drops, and the spirit is inverted so thoroughly that the once-sweet becomes horrific, human connection evaporates. The sane mind recoils in terror, not merely for itself, but for the integrity of its own sanity. We are not gods; we cannot exorcise demons. Our only duty is selfish vigilance: ensuring the devil does not enter our own flesh.

We must maintain this vigilance even when Love floods us, making us feel half-divine. Words of honor and duty become hollow echoes. A letter slipped beneath a dark door remains unread, or is glanced at between two abyssal lapses before being forgotten. Telegrams go undelivered. The whistling messenger, wiser than he knows, walks away unaware that a blind has been nudged aside by a trembling finger and then fearfully restored. Let the madman wrestle his own shadows; let him clip and enfold his succubus within a house where no breath of heaven enters and no sunlight pierces the filthy twilight. The lost must remain lost. Humanity has other business.

For Oleron, the handwriting of two letters found on his kitchen floor held no meaning, resembling the script of a distant dream. When the telegraph boy pounded near his bed, Oleron gnashed his teeth and stuffed his ears. He visualized the lad standing just beyond the partition, surrounded by packets of provisions and bundles of dead, dying flowers. His outer landing was littered with this rot. Oleron feared opening his door to accept them. After a week, the errand boys reported a mistake and ceased deliveries. Inside, in the red twilight, the old blooms turned brown, fell, and decayed where they lay.

Gradually, his power drained away. The Abomination fastened onto Oleron's vitality. The steady sapping left him for hours of prostration, gazing vacantly at his red-tinged ceiling, allowing idle fancies to take root. Even his strongest memories held only a precarious grip. Sometimes a fleeting fragment—a novel he must write—tantalized him before vanishing. Other times, whole, perfect novels rose magically before his eyes. Sometimes the memories were absurdly trivial: garrets inhabited, lodgings sheltered. Oleron knew much of such things in his time, but all was past. He had finally found a place he did not intend to leave until they fetched him out—a place some might deem green-sick, others too redolent of morbid things for a living man to inhabit, yet irresistible, authoritative, and filled with delights once a man ceased struggling against its will! A novel? Someone ought to write about a place like that! There must be plenty to write about if one could get to the bottom of it! It had probably already been painted by a man named Madley who lived there... but Oleron did not know this Madley. He had a strong feeling he wouldn't have liked him; he hated the fellow. (Aha! That was a joke!). He seriously doubted whether Madley led the life he ought; Oleron was torn between telling the long-nosed guardian of public morals across the way, but probably the man knew and had made his praying hullabaloos for him. That was his line. Why, Oleron himself had had a dust-up with him about something... some girl... Elsie Bengough.

Oleron felt deep unease regarding Elsie Bengough. Or rather, he was restless about the things she did. Chief among them was her persistence in thrusting herself into his thoughts. Whenever he was quick enough, he sent her packing the moment she appeared. The truth was she was not merely a bore; she had always been that. Now, her presence in his fancy was inimical to the enjoyment of certain experiences. She had no tact; she ought to know people are not at home to everyone's thoughts all the time; she ought, in mere politeness, to allow him seasons quite to himself. She was monstrously ignorant if she did not know there were special hours when a man's veins ran with fire and daring, in which he had a reasonable right to treat folk as he had treated the prying Barrett—to shut them out completely. But no: up she popped, the thought of her, ruining everything. Bright towering fabrics, by the side of which even his perfect, magical novels were dull and grey, vanished utterly at her intrusion. It was as if a fog quenched a fair-beaming star, as if a pit gaped at the threshold of a golden portal, as if a bat-like shadow turned growing dawn to mire. Therefore, Oleron strove to stifle even the nascent thought of her.

Nevertheless, there came an occasion when this woman Bengough absolutely refused suppression. Oleron could not tell exactly when; he only knew by the glimmer of the street lamp on his blind that it was some time during the night, and that for some time she had not presented herself.

He had no warning. She just came—was there. Strive as he would, he could not shake off the thought of her nor the image of her face. She haunted him.

But for her to come at that moment of all moments!... Really, it was past belief! How she could endure it, Oleron could not conceive! Actually, to look on, as it were, at the triumph of a Rival.... Good God! It was monstrous! Tact—reticence—he had never credited her with an overwhelming amount of either: but he had never attributed mere... oh, there was no word for it! Monstrous! Did she intend thenceforward.... Good God! To look on!...

Oleron felt the blood rush up to the roots of his hair with anger against her.

"Damnation take her!" he choked.

But the next moment his heat and resentment changed to a cold sweat of cowering fear. Panic-stricken, he strove to comprehend what he had done. For though he knew not what, he knew he had done something fatal, irreparable, blasting. Anger he had felt, but not *this* blaze of ire that suddenly flooded the twilight of his consciousness with a white infernal light. *That* appalling flash was not his—not his *that* open rift of bright and searing Hell—not his, not his! His had been the hand of a child, preparing a puny blow; but what was *this other* horrific hand drawn back to strike in the same place? Had *he* set that in motion? Had *he* provided the spark that touched off the whole accumulated power of that formidable and relentless place? He did not know. He only knew that that poor igniting particle in himself was blown out, that—Oh, impossible!--a clinging kiss (how else to express it?) had changed on his very lips to a gnashing and a removal, and that for very pity of the awful odds he must cry out to her against whom he had lately raged to guard herself... guard herself....

"Look out!" he shrieked aloud.

* * * * *

The revulsion was instant. As if a cold, slow billow had broken over him, he came to find that he was lying in his bed, that the mist and horror that had enwrapped him had departed, that he was Paul Oleron, and that he was sick, naked, helpless, and unutterably abandoned and alone. His faculties, though weak, answered at last to his calls upon them; and he knew it must have been a hideous nightmare that left him sweating and shaking.

Yes, he was himself, Paul Oleron, a tired novelist, already past the summit of his best work, slipping downhill again empty-handed. He had struck short in his life's aim. He had tried too much, over-estimated his strength, and was a failure, a failure....

It all came to him in the single word, enwrapped and complete; it needed no sequential thought: he was a failure. He had missed....

And he had missed not one happiness, but two. He had missed the ease of this world, which men love, and he had missed also that other shining prize for which men forgo ease, the snatching and holding and triumphant bearing aloft of which is the only justification of the mad adventurer who hazards the enterprise. And there was no second attempt. Fate has no morrow. Oleron's morrow must be to sit down to profitless, ill-done, unrequired work again, and so on the morrow after that, and the morrow after that, and as many morrows as there might be....

He lay there, weakly yet sanely considering it....

And since the whole attempt had failed, it was hardly worth while to consider whether a little might be saved from the general wreck. No good would ever come of that half-finished novel. He had intended it to appear in the autumn; was under contract that it should appear; no matter; it was better to pay forfeit to his publishers than to waste what days were left. He was spent; age was not far off; and paths of wisdom and sadness were the properest for the remainder of the journey....

If only he had chosen the wife, the child, the faithful friend at the fireside, and let them follow an *ignis fatuus* that list!...

In the meantime, it began to puzzle him exceedingly what he should be so weak, that his room smelled so overpoweringly of decaying vegetable matter, and that his hand, chancing to stray to his face in the darkness, should encounter a beard.

"Most extraordinary!" he began to mutter to himself. "Have I been ill? Am I ill now? And if so, why have they left me alone?... Extraordinary!..."

He thought he heard a sound from the kitchen or bathroom. He rose a little on his pillow and listened.... Ah! He was not alone, then! It certainly would have been extraordinary if they had left him ill and alone—Alone? Oh no. He would be looked after. He wouldn't be left, ill, to shift for himself. If everybody else had forsaken him, he could trust Elsie Bengough, the dearest chum he had, for that... bless her faithful heart!

But suddenly a short, stifled, spluttering cry rang sharply out:

"Paul!"

It came from the kitchen.

And in the same moment it flashed upon Oleron, he knew not how, that two, three, five, he knew not how many minutes before, another sound, unmarked at the time but suddenly transfixing his attention now, had striven to reach his intelligence. This sound had been the slight touch of metal on metal—just such a sound as Oleron made when he put his key into the lock.

"Hallo!... Who's that?" he called sharply from his bed.

He had no answer.

He called again. "Hallo!... Who's there?... Who is it?"

This time he was sure he heard noises, soft and heavy, in the kitchen.

"This is a queer thing altogether," he muttered. "By Jove, I'm as weak as a kitten too.... Hallo, there! Somebody called, didn't they?... Elsie! Is that you?..."

Then he began to knock with his hand on the wall at the side of his bed.

"Elsie!... Elsie!... You called, didn't you?... Please come here, whoever it is!..."

There was a sound as of a closing door, and then silence. Oleron began to get rather alarmed.

"It may be a nurse," he muttered; "Elsie'd have to get me a nurse, of course. She'd sit with me as long as she could spare the time, brave lass, and she'd get a nurse for the rest.... But it was awfully like her voice.... Elsie, or whoever it is!... I can't make this out at all. I must go and see what's the matter...."

He put one leg out of bed. Feeling its feebleness, he reached with his hand for the additional support of the wall....

* * * * *

But before putting out the other leg he stopped and considered, picking at his new-found beard. He was suddenly wondering whether he *dared* go into the kitchen. It was such a frightfully long way; no man knew what horror might not leap and huddle on his shoulders if he went so far; when a man has an overmastering impulse to get back into bed he ought to take heed of the warning and obey it. Besides, why should he go? What was there to go for? If it was that Bengough creature again, let her look after herself; Oleron was not going to have things cramp themselves on his defenceless back for the sake of such a spoilsport as *she*!... If she was in, let her let herself out again, and the sooner the better for her! Oleron simply couldn't be bothered. He had his work to do. On the morrow, he must set about the writing of a novel with a heroine so winsome, capricious, adorable, jealous, wicked, beautiful, inflaming, and altogether evil, that men should stand amazed. She was coming over him now; he knew by the alteration of the very air of the room when she was near him; and that soft thrill of bliss that had begun to stir in him never came unless she was beckoning, beckoning....

He let go the wall and fell back into bed again as—oh, unthinkable!--the other half of that kiss that a gnash had interrupted was placed (how else convey it?) on his lips, robbing him of very breath....

The June sun beat down upon the square, turning the pavement into a blistering white sheet, while a dense, churning crowd pressed against the narrow entrance-alley. Their eyes were fixed upward, transfixed by the windows of the old house—a grim structure of red brick scarred with antique insurance marks, its facade dominated by agents' notice-boards that hung like wooden choppers over the paling. At the broken gate, two constables formed a rigid barrier, holding back the surging tide of humanity. Women huddled at the periphery of the throng, shifting restlessly as if compelled to view the drawn red blinds of the house from every possible angle, their lips moving in hushed, frantic whispers. The children had been driven indoors, sealed behind closed doors.

Near the front of the crowd, a long-nosed man captivated a small circle, repeating a tale with theatrical fervor. Beside him, a little, fat man with wide, terrified eyes attempted to steal the audience, weaving a narrative centered on a key.

"...and it was revealed to me that there'd been something that very afternoon," the long-nosed man declared, his voice rising above the murmur. "I was standing there, where Constable Saunders is—or rather, passing about my business—when they came out. There was no deceiving me, oh, no deceiving *me!* I saw her face...."

"What was it like, Mr. Barrett?" someone demanded.

"It was like hers whom our Lord said to, 'Woman, doth any man accuse thee?'—white as paper, and no mistake! Don't tell *me!*... And so I walks straight across to Mrs. Barrett, and 'Jane,' I says, 'this must stop, and stop at once; we are commanded to avoid evil,' I says, 'and it must come to an end now; let him get help elsewhere.'"

"And she says to me, 'John,' she says, 'it's four-and-sixpence a week'"—those were her exact words.

"'Jane,' I says, 'if it was forty-six thousand pounds it should stop'... and from that day to this she hasn't set foot inside that gate."

A brief silence fell over the group, broken only by a vague inquiry: "Did Mrs. Barrett ever... *see* anythink, like?"

Barrett turned on the speaker with austere severity. "What Mrs. Barrett saw and Mrs. Barrett didn't see shall not pass these lips; even as it is written, keep thy tongue from speaking evil," he intoned.

Another voice cut in. "He was pretty near canned up in the *Waggon and Horses* that night, weren't he, Jim?"

"Yes, 'e 'adn't 'alf copped it...."

"Not standing treat much, neither; he was in the bar, all on his own...."

"So 'e was; we talked about it...."

The fat, scared-eyed man made another desperate bid for attention. "She got the key off of me—she 'ad the number of it—she come into my shop of a Tuesday evening...."

The crowd ignored him entirely.

"Shut your heads," a heavy laborer grunted, his voice rough with impatience. "She hasn't been found yet. 'Ere's the inspectors; we shall know more in a bit."

Two inspectors had emerged and were conferring with the constables guarding the gate. Seizing the moment, the little fat man rushed forward, breathless. "She bought the key of me! I remember the number, because of it's being three one's and three three's—111333!" he cried excitedly.

An inspector shoved him aside gently but firmly. "Nobody's been in?" he asked one of the constables.

"No, sir."

"Then you, Brackley, come with us; you, Smith, keep the gate. There's a squad on its way."

The two inspectors and the constable descended the alley and entered the house. They ascended the wide, carved staircase.

"This don't look as if he'd been out much lately," one inspector muttered, kicking aside a litter of dead leaves and paper that lay outside Oleron's door. "I don't think we need knock—break a pane, Brackley."

The door featured two glazed panels. With a sharp crack of shattering glass, Brackley forced his elbow through the hole, reaching back to draw the latch.

"Faugh!" choked one inspector as they crossed the threshold. "Let some light and air in, quick. It stinks like a hearse—"

Outside, the assembly watched as the red blinds rose and the windows of the old house were thrown wide open to the blinding sun.

"That's better," said the inspector, sticking his head out of a window to draw a deep, ragged breath. "That seems to be the bedroom in there; will you go in, Simms, while I go over the rest?..."

They had raised the bedroom blind as well, and the waxy-white, emaciated man on the bed had fashioned a blinker of his hand against the torturing flood of brightness. He could scarcely believe his hearing was not playing tricks upon him; there were two policemen in his room, bending over him, demanding to know where "she" was. He shook his head, his eyes vacant.

"This woman Bengough... goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough... d'ye hear? Where is she?... No good, Brackley; get him up; be careful with him; I'll just shove *my* head out of the window, I think...."

Meanwhile, the other inspector had ransacked Oleron's study, finding nothing, and was now in the kitchen. He kicked aside an ankle-deep mass of vegetable refuse that cumbered the floor. The kitchen window lacked a blind and was overshadowed by the blank end of the house across the alley. The room appeared empty.

Yet, as the inspector swept aside the dead flowers, he noticed a shuffling track, not of his making, leading to a cupboard in the corner. In the upper part of the cupboard door was a square panel that looked as if it slid on runners. The door itself was closed.

The inspector advanced, reached for the little knob, and slid the hatch along its groove.

Then he took an involuntary step backward.

Framed in the aperture, falling forward slightly before jamming again in its frame, was something that resembled a large, lumpy pudding, wrapped in a pudding-bag of faded, brownish-red frieze.

"Ah!" said the inspector.

To close the hatch again, he would have had to thrust that pudding back with his hand; somehow, he did not quite like the idea of touching it. Instead, he turned the handle of the cupboard itself. There was immense weight behind it—so much weight that, after opening the door three or four inches and peering inside, he had to put his shoulder to it to force it shut. In closing it, he left sticking out, a few inches from the floor, a triangle of black-and-white check skirt.

He stepped into the small hall.

"All right!" he called.

They had dressed Oleron. He still used his hands as blinkers, his brain a chaotic tangle. A number of things were happening that he could not comprehend: the extraordinary mess of dead flowers scattered everywhere; the presence of police officers in his room; the reason one officer was summoning a four-wheeler and a stretcher; and the fact that they seemed to be moving a heavy article about in his kitchen.

"What's the matter?" he muttered sleepily.

Then he heard a murmur in the square and the stopping of a four-wheeler outside. A police officer stood at his elbow, and when Oleron whispered something to him, the man ran off a string of words—something about "used in evidence against you." They lifted him to his feet, assisting him toward the door.

No, Oleron couldn't understand it at all.

They carried him down the stairs and along the alley. Oleron was aware of confused, angry shoutings; he gathered that a number of people wanted to lynch somebody or other. His attention then fixed on the little fat, frightened-eyed man, who appeared to be making a statement that an officer was taking down in a notebook.

"I'd seen her with him... they was often together... she came into my shop and said it was for him... I thought it was all right... 111333 the number was," the man was saying.

The people seemed furious; many police were keeping them back. But one of the inspectors had a voice that Oleron thought quite kind and friendly. He was telling someone to get someone else into the cab before something or other was brought out. Oleron noticed that a four-wheeler was drawn up at the gate. It appeared that it was himself who was to be put into it. As they lifted him up, he saw that the inspector tried to stand between him and something that stood behind the cab, but was not quick enough to prevent Oleron seeing that this thing was a hooded stretcher. The angry voices sounded like a roaring sea; something hard, like a stone, struck the back of the cab; and the inspector followed Oleron in, standing with his back to the window, nearer the side where the people were. The door they had put Oleron in remained open, apparently until the other inspector should arrive. Through the opening, Oleron caught a glimpse of the hatchet-like "To Let" boards among the privet-trees. One of them noted that the key was at Number Six....

Suddenly, the raging of voices was hushed. Along the entrance-alley, shuffling steps were heard, and the other inspector appeared at the cab door.

"Right away," he said to the driver.

He entered, fastened the door after him, and blocked up the second window with his back. Between the two inspectors, Oleron slept peacefully. The cab moved down the square, the other vehicle went up the hill. The mortuary lay that way.
