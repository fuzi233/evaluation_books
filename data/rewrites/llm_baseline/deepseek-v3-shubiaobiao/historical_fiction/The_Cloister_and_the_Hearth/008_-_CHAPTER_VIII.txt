While the burgomaster was dragging Gerard through the streets of Tergou, Margaret faced her own trial in Sevenbergen—a small sorrow, perhaps, but sharp as a needle to a housewife’s heart. She stood before Martin Wittenhaagen, the grizzled archer, her eyes brimming with tears.

“Martin,” she said, “there’s nothing in the house, and Gerard will come soon. He forgets to eat when he works—he’ll be faint with hunger, and I’ve nothing to give him.”

Martin scratched his grizzled head. “What can I do?”

“It’s Thursday—your day to shoot. I thought… I hoped…”

Martin shook his head. “Not when the Duke’s hunting. See for yourself.” He pulled a worn parchment from his pouch, its grand seal still intact. A royal license from Philip of Burgundy granted Martin—once an archer in his wars—the right to loose three arrows each Thursday, so long as no nobleman was in the forest.

Margaret sighed.

“Ah, don’t fret,” Martin grumbled. “For you, I’ll risk it. I’ve done as much for worse folk.” He strung his great yew bow, taller than a man, its string humming like a harp when drawn. “I’ll keep to the edge of the wood. They’ll never see me.”

Margaret wrung her hands. “Promise me you’ll not go far. Better Gerard goes hungry than harm comes to you.”

With a nod, Martin slipped into the trees. The distant call of horns told him the hunt was on, the game afoot. He took his stand behind an oak, arrow nocked, eyes sharp.

A hare bounded into the glade, ears pricked. Martin loosed his shaft—too quick. The arrow hissed beneath her belly, and she fled.

“Clumsy fool,” he muttered.

A wood-pigeon alighted above him. This time, he took care, drew smoothly—but the arrow only grazed the bird. It fluttered away, dying somewhere unseen.

“Luck’s against me,” he growled.

Then—movement. A great stag burst into the clearing, and behind it, low to the ground, came the Duke’s leopard, sleek and deadly. Martin ducked behind the oak. The beast sprang upon the buck, tearing at its throat.

A reckless thought struck him. He drew his last arrow, aimed true, and buried it in the stag’s heart. The noble beast crashed down. The leopard, undisturbed, feasted on the kill.

Martin waited, then stepped forward, gripping the stag’s leg. The leopard’s growl froze his blood. He met its gaze, willing it to cower—but the brute knew no fear. With a snarl, it leaped at him.

Martin caught it by the throat. Claws raked his shoulder, pain searing like fire, but he clenched his jaw and throttled the beast, his grip iron. The leopard writhed, tail lashing—then went limp. He flung it aside, breath ragged.

Now came terror. *I’ve killed the Duke’s leopard.*

He covered the body with leaves, shouldered the stag, and staggered home, blood trailing behind him.

Margaret gasped when he burst in. “No questions,” he rasped. “Cook it quick.”

She saw only the deer’s blood, not his wounds, and set to work. Martin bound his shoulder in silence. Soon, the scent of roasting venison filled the cottage. Gerard arrived, and with a flask of Schiedam between them, they feasted.

Martin told his tale. They laughed—until Margaret’s face went pale.

She pointed. At the window, two green eyes burned in the dusk. The leopard.

A single deep bay echoed through the wood. Then another.

Martin’s blood ran cold. “Hounds. They’ve caught the scent.” He snatched Gerard’s bow. “I’ll kill her, throw her in the forest—maybe they’ll think she died fighting the stag.”

But Margaret grabbed the arrow. It snapped in her hands.

“Woman, you’ve doomed me!”

“No.” Her voice was steel. “Stand back.” She seized his knife and ran outside.

The hounds were closing in, baying, men shouting. The leopard’s eyes gleamed, unblinking.

Then—Margaret stepped forward, alone, the knife in her hand.
