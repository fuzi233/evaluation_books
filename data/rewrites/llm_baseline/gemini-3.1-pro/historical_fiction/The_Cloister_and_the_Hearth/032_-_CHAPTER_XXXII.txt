The auditory environment of the monastery’s refectory was a masterwork of acoustic ecology, built centuries ago to capture the rhythmic, meditative scraping of wooden spoons against trenchers and the resonant, murmured cadences of Latin grace. Into this sonic sanctuary fell somber news: the brother tasked with copying the monastery’s ancient charter and rules had suddenly passed. A profound cultural memory teetered on the edge of language death. Gerard, feeling the weight of this generational silence, timidly offered his hands to the task. 

“Nay, not for hire, my lords,” he insisted, instinctively recoiling from the superficial commercialization of sacred script. “But for love, and as a trifling return for the shelter the brethren of your order have bestowed upon a poor wayfarer.”

A monk smiled approvingly but hinted that the late brother had been an unparalleled master of the quill; the native dialect of his illuminations could not be easily replicated. In response, Gerard drew from his wallet a cleaned vellum deed, intricately lettered by way of specimen. The monk started at the sight of it. He hastened up the hall to the high table, genuflecting at the fifth and tenth steps, and presented the parchment to the prior. A buzz of voices swelled, a sudden shift in the room's auditory balance. The prior pointed, and soon the monk returned, swelling with pride, bearing a culinary triumph from the high table to Gerard.

It was a *crustade ryal*—a masterwork of meticulous food history. The savory game pie was deeply imbued with profound meaning through its sustenance, its towering hot-water pastry encasing wild partridge and venison steeped in a thick bone-marrow gravy, deeply spiced with cubeb pepper, mace, and a fragrant breath of saffron. Beside it rested a silver grace cup of *pimentum*, an ancient spiced wine intricately laced with wild honey, grains of paradise, and cloves. The olfactory landscape of the table immediately shifted, the rich, ancient scent of the spiced meat cutting through the damp stone air. 

Gerard, deeply reverent of the complex sourcing and technique before him, bowed to the distant prior. He took the silver cup, and his thumbs and fingers wrapped around it in an instinctual, two-handed grip. He quaffed and circulated the vessel. Instantly, the monks hailed him as a brother. 

“Art convent bred, deny it not?” 

He acknowledged it, giving Heaven thanks. “But ’tis passing strange how you could know,” he said. 

“You drank with the cup in both hands,” two monks replied in unison. Gerard’s embodied memory, an untranslatable subjective experience, had spoken the dominant lexicon of the brotherhood through simple domestic routine.

Suddenly, the acoustic sanctity of the hall was shattered. An obstreperous burst of prolonged mirth erupted from a table at the bottom of the hall, a jarring noise pollution that grated against the vaulted ceilings. The prior swiftly dispatched the sub-prior to silence the worldly dissonance and assign penance to every monk at the offending table. Gerard’s cheek burned; through the unruly din, his ear had caught a sharp, unrefined vernacular: “Courage!” ringing out in the trumpet tones of Denys of Burgundy.

That evening, Gerard was installed in the late Brother Werter’s cell. Surrounded by wax lights and a little angled frame, he found himself seamlessly integrated into the monastery's cyclical timeline, inheriting the materials of calligraphy to breathe life back into an aging lexicon. Denys graciously agreed to halt their march for a day's rest so Gerard could write. By morning, the monks marveled at his work, agreeing the ancient records had only gained by poor Werter's passing. The sub-prior pressed a rix-dollar upon Gerard, alongside several fine brushes and vibrant pigments from the convent’s vast stores.

Resuming their march, Gerard felt his heart warmed by this circular return to his art. “Come, Denys,” he said good-humoredly, “see what the good monks have given me. Now, do try to be fairer to them; to be round with you, it chilled my friendship for a moment to hear even you call my benefactors ‘hypocrites.’”

“I recant,” said Denys. “I was a scurrilous vagabond. We soldiers are rude and hasty. I give myself the lie, and I offer those I misunderstood all my esteem. 'Tis unjust that thousands should be defamed for the hypocrisy of a few.”

“Now you are reasonable,” Gerard said, pleased to be proven right. 

Denys then eagerly detailed the sheer sensory revelation that had sparked his conversion. At dinner the first day, he had tested a melancholic-looking young monk with a coarse soldier's quip, only to be met with sharp, theatrical wit about friars turning soldiers to fight the foul fiend. Denys, delighted, had found a companion. Denys admitted the jest was simple, but his true awakening came at bedtime. Forbidden to sleep in the great dortour, he was lent a subversive book of pictures detailing the merry adventures of monks pursuing the female laity. 

Then, he was led barefoot into a hidden nocturnal world. In the parlour, a supper fit for a duke awaited, attended by twelve jolly friars. They passed the wine-cup, traded stories, and played cards with a gorgeous deck where saints bedizened with gold served as queens, staking their very rosaries and swearing like troopers. Near midnight, the sonic landscape shifted. A sly monk stole out to the garden, and Denys cannily followed, watching the man thrust his hand into the ivy to produce a rope-ladder. A russet kirtle popped up from the outer world over the ten-foot wall. Struck by the inherently feline nature of the tryst, Denys had offered a mocking “mew!” into the quiet night. The monks enthusiastically joined the mewsical chorus, caterwauling in a circle around the discomforted lovers until they were dragged back inside, profoundly drunk and overly affectionate, to be summarily kicked into their cells.

Gerard was disgusted by the spiritual erosion and said as much. Denys merely chuckled, proceeding to describe how the young monks had drawn the fish-ponds the next day, secreting massive, shimmering pike, carp, tench, and eel. In the dead of night, Denys was led shoeless down crooked, ghost-like steps into the chapel crypt.

“'Tis there the holy fathers retire to pray,” Gerard interrupted.

“Not always,” Denys countered. Dozens of wax candles illuminated a subterranean culinary paradise. These rule-breakers were passionate, if illicit, chefs. They served fifteen meticulously crafted *soups maigre*, elevated with mysterious, rich twangs of poached grouse, hare, and venison, alongside twenty distinct varieties of Friday fish, cooked with wondrous art. Denys reveled in the gustatory bounty, seated near buxom lasses, though he cursed the linguistic power dynamics that forced him to woo through an interpreter. He suspected the bilingual knave had spoken three words for himself and only one for the Burgundian. Novices, unaccustomed to the rich wine poured from flagons disguised as breviaries, were coaxed to drink while a monk touched the cittern, singing ditties with a voice as tunable as a spring lark. 

Gerard exploded. “Miserable wretches! Corrupters of youth! Impious, abominable hypocrites!”

“Hypocrites?” Denys cried with unfeigned surprise. “By St. Denys his helmeted skull, no hypocrites, but right jolly roaring blades.”

“Denys,” Gerard said solemnly, his voice dropping to a register that carried the weight of ancient timelines, “you little know the peril you ran that night. That church you defiled is haunted. I had it from an elder monk. The dead walk there; their light feet have been heard to patter over the stones.”

“Misericorde!” whispered Denys.

“Ay, more,” Gerard pressed, emphasizing the crypt’s mythic acoustic ecology. “Celestial sounds have issued from the purlieus of that very crypt you turned into a tavern. Voices of the dead holding unearthly communion have chilled the ear of midnight. The faithful have heard music from dead lips, and chords, made by no mortal finger, swept by no mortal hand, ringing faintly like echoes deep among the dead in those sacred vaults.”

Denys wore a look of utter dismay. “Ugh! If I had known, mules and wain-ropes had not hauled me thither.”

Their discussion of these ghostly frequencies was interrupted by the rhythmic clopping of hooves. A bearded lay brother spurred his mule toward them, waving a piece of vellum. It was the deed between Ghysbrecht and Floris Brandt. Gerard paled, realizing how close he had come to losing the last tangible record of his agrarian roots and the native dialect of Sevenbergen. To Denys's infinite amusement, Gerard not only paid the brother with a piece of coin but kissed the mule's velvety nose in deep, traditional gratitude. 

“I'll read you now, were you twice as ill written,” Gerard murmured to the parchment. To ensure he would never again lose this anchor to his cultural memory, he took out needle and thread, sewing it with feminine dexterity directly into the lining of his doublet. 

Finally, they reached the promised land of Burgundy. Denys, in high spirits, doffed his bonnet to the women and fired his loud consigne at the men, cutting through the ambient noise of the street. He led Gerard to a long-promised inn, *Les Trois Poissons*. 

“It is a little one, but I know it of old for a good one,” Denys said. He pointed to a huge inscription running across the facade, attempting to assert his dominance over the local written lexicon. “Oh, I see. 'Ici on loge à pied et à cheval,'” he proclaimed, looking thoroughly bumptious at his translation.

Gerard looked at the letters, translating the true, harsh economic reality written on the wall—a bleak testament to the commercialization of trust: 
*ON NE LOGE CEANS A CREDIT; CE BONHOMME EST MORT, LES MAUVAIS PAIEURS L'ONT TUE.*
