Years later, among the old family papers at home, I discovered a packet labeled in my mother’s precise hand: *"From London, April, 1760. My son’s dreadful letter."* When it fell into my possession, I burned it. Some wounds are not meant to be preserved for posterity. Nor did I keep her reply—a missive of cold dignity, each word measured, every line a reproach wrapped in scripture.

At the time, my heart was too raw for temperance. Maddened by the forced separation from my Theo, I had written to Virginia in a fury, declaring my mother the architect of my misery. I accused her of destroying not just my happiness, but that of an innocent girl whose health and spirit had been broken by her interference. I told her plainly that the bond between us was severed, that I could no longer be her dutiful son.

She answered as a woman certain of heaven’s approval, invoking divine judgment as her shield. How often do we call upon God to justify our own will? In our quarrels, our ambitions, our stubborn refusals—do we not demand the skies themselves ratify our choices? My mother did so with the zeal of a prophetess, while I, in my rebellion, clung to my own righteousness.

Yet love, once rooted, is not so easily torn up. Theo and I had tried to part, but the separation was unbearable. Her father, kind despite his disapproval, saw the truth: we were Pyramus and Thisbe, whispering through the wall. The more he sought to divide us, the tighter our hands clasped through the cracks.

And so, in defiance of all authority, we found our way back to one another. I took to riding beside her carriage during her convalescent drives. Hetty, ever the vigilant chaperone, developed a sudden fascination with the scenery outside the window. Letters flew between us, carried by the indefatigable Gumbo and Theo’s maid, Molly. By autumn, the roses had returned to Theo’s cheeks—and hope to my heart.

Meanwhile, the world beyond our little drama turned. King George II died; a new reign began. The change meant little to us then, though it would alter our fortunes in ways we could not foresee. For now, we were content in our stolen moments, weaving dreams of a shared future—a cottage, a humble living, a life built despite the storms of disapproval.

Then came the thunderclap: General Lambert was appointed Governor of Jamaica. A frigate waited to carry him and his family across the sea. The news struck like a blade. Theo, pale as death, trembled at the thought of parting. Even Hetty, usually so composed, wept. Only Aunt Lambert, practical as ever, murmured something about *"orders for the ladies’ outfits"*—and a certain dressmaker who remembered a young Miss Esmond of Virginia.

That evening, I walked home in a daze. Gumbo, usually so cheerful, was inconsolable. His sweetheart Molly would be sailing away too. We were a pair of broken-hearted fools, he and I.

But love, once kindled, is not so easily quenched. If walls divide us, we will whisper through them still. If oceans part us, we will find a way across. For Theo and I, the story was not yet over.
