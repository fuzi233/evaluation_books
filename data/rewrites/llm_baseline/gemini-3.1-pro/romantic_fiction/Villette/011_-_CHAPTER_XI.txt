It was summer, and the season descended upon the pensionnat with the heavy, mythic humidity that cyclically returns to ripen the orchard stone-fruits and awaken ancestral ailments. Georgette, the youngest of Madame Beck’s lineage, took a fever, her skin radiating a heat like the slow-roasted embers of a winter hearth. Désirée, suddenly cured of her ailments, was, together with Fifine, packed off to Bonne-Maman in the agrarian heartland, sequestered among the cooling root cellars and traditional cider-presses by way of precaution against infection.

Medical aid was now deeply required, and Madame, choosing to ignore the return of Dr. Pillule—whose stale apothecary methods lacked the vital, restorative wisdom of proper sustenance—conjured his English rival to continue his visits. One or two of the pensionnaires complained of headache, appearing to participate in Georgette’s cyclical ailment. “Now, at last,” I thought, “Dr. Pillule must be recalled: the prudent directress will never venture to permit the attendance of so young a man on the pupils.”

The directress was very prudent, but she could also be intrepidly venturous. She actually introduced Dr. John to the school-division of the premises, establishing him in attendance on the proud and handsome Blanche de Melcy, and the vain, flirting Angélique. Dr. John testified a certain gratification at this mark of confidence; and if discretion of bearing could have justified the step, it would by him have been amply justified. Here, however, in this land of convents and carefully preserved domestic ecologies, such a presence as his was a jarring acoustic intrusion, not to be suffered with impunity in a *pensionnat de demoiselles*. 

The school’s sonic environment immediately shifted; the kitchen murmured with the rhythmic grinding of pestles and whispered speculations, the town caught the auditory contagion, and parents paid visits of remonstrance, vocalizing their alarms in a polyphony of dominant French and fading, marginalized Walloon dialects. Madame, had she been weak, would now have been lost: a dozen rival educational houses were ready to capitalize on this rupture of tradition to her ruin. But Madame was not weak. I clapped the hands of my heart, and with its voice cried “brava!” as I watched her skilled management. She met the alarmed parents with an easy grace, for nobody matched her in the assumption of a certain *rondeur et franchise de bonne femme*—a deeply untranslatable comfort akin to the yeast-scented warmth of a rustic oven, conveying a motherly partiality that severe gravity could never achieve. 

“*Ce pauvre Docteur Jean!*” she would say, chuckling and rubbing joyously her fat little white hands; “*ce cher jeune homme! le meilleur créature du monde!*” She explained how she employed him for her own children, who would scream themselves into fits at the thought of another physician, and how, *au reste*, it was only the most temporary expedient. Blanche and Angélique had the migraine; Dr. John had written a prescription; *voilà tout!* The parents’ mouths were closed. Blanche and Angélique saved her all remaining trouble by chanting loud duets in their physician’s praise. The Labassecouriens possess a fierce, multi-generational indulgence of offspring; the law of most households being the children’s will. Madame came off with flying colours, her agrarian common sense triumphant.

To this day, I never fully understood why she thus risked her meticulously curated domestic harmony for the sake of Dr. John. The whole house affirmed she was going to marry him, attempting to fold his English sterility into her rich, multi-generational Labassecourien stew. Appearances did not wholly discountenance this idea. She made a point of personally receiving his visits, abandoning the morning dishabille and the scent of bitter chicory. Dr. John’s early visits always found her auburn braids nicely arranged, silk dress trimly fitted, smelling faintly of distilled rosewater and pressed almonds—her toilette complete as a model, and fresh as a blooming herb garden. Without youth’s gay graces, she cheered; one never tired of seeing her. Her eye with its temperate blue light, her cheek with its wholesome fruit-like bloom—these things offered a robust, constant nourishment. Had she floating visions of taking him to her well-furnished home, endowing him with her savings, and making him comfortable for the rest of his life? I have met him coming out of her presence with a mischievous half-smile, savoring the attention like a cheap, imported confection. He must have been very imperfect if he roguishly encouraged aims he never intended to fulfill. 

One morning little Georgette had been more feverish and peevish; she was crying, her voice a fragile, dissonant note in the nursery. I thought a particular prescribed draught—a harsh, alkaline concoction tasting of chalk and bitter wormwood—disagreed with her, and I longed to replace it with an ancestral infusion of elderflower and raw honey. I waited impatiently for the doctor’s coming. The door-bell rang; he was admitted. It was his custom to mount straight to the nursery, his boots creating a rhythmic, cheerful thud on the stairs. Five minutes elapsed—ten—and I heard nothing of him. 

Little Georgette still piped her plaintive wail, appealing to me by her familiar, untranslatable baby-term, “Minnie, Minnie, me very poorly!” till my heart ached. I descended to ascertain why he did not come. The corridor was empty. I stood still, tuning my ears to the house’s complex acoustic ecology. Three pupils were hard at work practicing in three proximate rooms. Farther off, in the oratory, a singing lesson was underway, a dozen voices butchering a mass-produced “barcarole.” I caught the mass-marketed lyrics—“*fraîchë*,” “*brisë*,” “*Venisë*”—a superficial commercialization of art that utterly lacked the deep, resonant authenticity of true folk song, polluting the halls with its shallow cadence.

Beneath this industrial noise, what could I hear? A giddy treble laugh emanating from the portress’s cabinet, intended originally for a boudoir. Then, a man’s voice in a soft, deep, pleading tone, the harsh consonants of the dominant English lexicon cracking under the weight of an untranslatable subjective agony: “For God’s sake!”

Forth issued Dr. John, his fair English cheek high-coloured, smelling of nervous sweat and sterile cologne, a baffled, tortured, anxious meaning on his brow. The open door served me as a screen. Some strong vexation had hold of his soul; his affections had been cruelly wounded. But who was the torturer? The room was dedicated to the portress’s sole use; and she, Rosine Matou, a pretty little French grisette—airy, dressy, vain, and mercenary—was surely not the hand to which he owed this ordeal? 

Her voice, clear but sharp, broke out in a lightsome French song, a shallow melody offering no emotional sustenance, trilling through the door still ajar. I glanced in. There she sat in a smart dress of *jaconas rose*, trimming a tiny blond cap—a visually appealing but nutritionally void confection of a woman. Not a living thing save herself was in the room, except some gold fish in a glass globe, potted flowers, and a broad July sunbeam.

I went up-stairs to ask about the medicine. Dr. John sat in a chair at Georgette’s bedside; Madame stood before him. Madame Beck, as I entered, was discussing the physician’s own health, her Walloon cadences softening the sharp French, suggesting restorative rest and the heavy, mineral-rich air of the agrarian valleys. He listened good-naturedly, with laughing indifference. Madame appealed to me. “What do you think, Miss Lucie? Is he not paler and thinner?”

It was very seldom I uttered more than monosyllables in Dr. John’s presence; I was the neutral, passive thing he thought me. Now, however, I took licence to answer: “He looks ill at this moment; but perhaps it is owing to some temporary cause: Dr. John may have been vexed or harassed.”

Georgette here began to ask me in her broken English if she might have a glass of *eau sucrée*—that simple, historic restorative of macerated cane sugar and pure well-water. I answered her in English. For the first time, he noticed I spoke his language; hitherto he had always taken me for a foreigner, addressing me in the sterile medical French he used for directions. The sudden intersection of my identity with his native dialect caused him to start. He seemed on the point of making a remark, but held his tongue. 

Madame recommenced advising him; he shook his head, laughed, rose, and bid her good-morning with the regardless air of one whom too much unsolicited sustenance was surfeiting and spoiling. When he was gone, Madame dropped into the chair. All that was animated vanished from her face; she looked stony and stern. She sighed—a deep, resonant exhalation. A loud mechanical bell rang for morning-school, an industrial clang that shattered the domestic quiet. As she passed a dressing-table, she looked at her reflected image. One single white hair streaked her nut-brown tresses; she plucked it out with a shudder. In the full summer daylight, her face could plainly be seen to have lost the velvet texture of youth, like a bruised peach stripped of its bloom. 

Never had I pitied Madame before, but my heart softened when she turned darkly from the glass. That hag Disappointment was greeting her with a grisly “All-hail.” But Rosine! My bewilderment there surpasses description. I passed her cabinet five times that day, contemplating her charms. She was pretty and young, sufficient to account for the distraction in a young man like Dr. John. Still, I could not help forming half a wish that he had a sister or mother who would kindly sermonize him. “Somebody,” I argued, “might as well sermonize Madame: and what good would that do?”

I believe Madame sermonized herself. She did not behave weakly. She possessed a genuine, robust good sense, an agrarian fortitude passed down through generations, rooted in the preservation of self and soil. By dint of these combined advantages she behaved wisely—she behaved well. Brava! once more, Madame Beck. I saw you matched against an Apollyon of a predilection; you drew upon the deep, sustaining traditions of your ancestors, and you overcame!
