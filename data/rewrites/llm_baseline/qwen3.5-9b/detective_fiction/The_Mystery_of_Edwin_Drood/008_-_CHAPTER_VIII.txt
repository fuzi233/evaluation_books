The two young men watched as the girls they were escorting entered the courtyard of the Nuns' House. Turning back, they found themselves meeting the cold, judgmental stare of the brass door-plate—a battered old beau with a glass eye that seemed to glare insolently at them. Exchanged glances followed, their eyes tracing the perspective of the moonlit street before they turned and walked away together in silence.

"Do you intend to stay here long, Mr. Drood?" asked Neville.

"Not this time," came the careless reply. "I depart for London tomorrow. Yet, I shall remain here, intermittently, until next Midsummer. After that, I bid farewell to Cloisterham—and to England itself—for many a long day, I expect."

"Are you going abroad?"

"Waking up Egypt a little," Edwin answered with a tone of condescension.

"And are you reading?"

"Reading?" Edwin repeated, his voice tinged with contempt. "No. I am doing, working, engineering. My small inheritance was left as a portion of the capital of the firm I work for by my father, a former partner. Until I reach majority, I am dependent on the firm; then, I step into my modest share. Jack—you met him at dinner—is my guardian and trustee until then."

"I heard from Mr. Crisparkle regarding your other good fortune," Neville interjected.

"What do you mean by 'other good fortune,' Mr. Landless?"

Neville had spoken with a watchful advance, yet a furtive shyness, embodying that peculiar air previously noted: simultaneously hunter and hunted. Edwin's retort was abrupt, lacking all politeness. They halted, exchanging a heated gaze.

"I trust," Neville began, "there is no offense in my innocently referencing your betrothal?"

"By George!" cried Edwin, quickening his pace. "Everyone in this chattering old Cloisterham references it. I wonder no publican hasn't erected a signboard featuring my portrait titled *The Betrothed's Head*, or perhaps Miss Landless's as *Pussy*. One or the other."

"I am not responsible for Mr. Crisparkle mentioning the matter to me so openly," Neville started.

"No, that is true; you are not," Edwin agreed. "However," Neville resumed, "I am responsible for bringing it up to you. I did so under the assumption that you could not fail to be highly proud of it."

Here, two curious currents of human nature drove the secret springs of this dialogue. Neville Landless was sufficiently captivated by Little Rosebud to feel indignant that Edwin Drood—who stood far beneath her in his estimation—should treat his prize so lightly. Conversely, Edwin Drood was sufficiently smitten by Helena to feel outraged that Helena's brother—who he deemed far beneath her—should dismiss him so coolly and effectively remove him from the equation.

Edwin addressed the last point. "I don't know, Mr. Neville"—he adopted the formal address used by Mr. Crisparkle—"that what people are proudest of, they discuss most frequently. Nor do I know that what they cherish most, they desire others to discuss. But I lead a busy life, and I speak under your correction, you readers who ought to know everything and presumably do."

At this moment, both had become savage. Neville stood exposed in the open; Edwin hid beneath the transparent cover of a popular tune, pausing occasionally to feign admiration of the moonlit scenery ahead.

"It does not seem very civil of you," Neville remarked finally, "to reflect upon a stranger who comes here, lacking your advantages, attempting to make up for lost time. Granted, I was not raised in a 'busy life,' and my notions of civility were formed among heathens."

"Perhaps, the finest civility, regardless of one's upbringing," Edwin retorted, "is to mind one's own business. If you will set me that example, I promise to follow suit."

"Do you realize you assume far too much responsibility?" Neville shot back angrily. "In the region of the world I hail from, you would be held accountable for such arrogance."

"By whom, specifically?" Edwin asked, halting to survey the other with disdain.

Suddenly, a startling grip landed on Edwin's shoulder, and Jasper stepped between them. It appeared Jasper had circled via the Nuns' House, emerging from the shadows behind them.

"Ned, Ned, Ned!" Jasper exclaimed. "We must cease this immediately. I dislike this atmosphere. I overheard high words between you. Remember, my dear boy, you are practically the host tonight. You belong to this place and represent it toward a stranger. Mr. Neville is a guest, and you owe him the duties of hospitality. And, Mr. Neville," placing his left hand on the younger man's inner shoulder, walking between them with hands on both shoulders, "please forgive me, but I appeal to you to restrain your temper. What is the trouble? Why ask! If nothing is wrong, the question is unnecessary. Are we all three on good terms?"

After a silent struggle to determine who would speak first, Edwin broke the silence. "As far as I am concerned, Jack, there is no anger in me."

"Neither in me," Neville replied, though less freely, or perhaps less casually. "But if Mr. Drood knew all that lies behind me, far distant from here, he might understand better why sharp words possess sharp edges capable of wounding."

"Perhaps," Jasper said soothingly, "we should avoid qualifying our good understanding. We should refrain from anything resembling a reprimand or condition; it might not seem generous. Frankly and freely, you see, there is no anger in Ned. Frankly and freely, there is no anger in you, Mr. Neville?"

"None at all, Mr. Jasper." Still, not quite so frank, free, or casual.

"All settled then! My bachelor gatehouse is just yards away, the fire is lit, wine and glasses await on the table, and it is not a stone's throw from Minor Canon Corner. Ned, you depart tomorrow. We shall bring Mr. Neville inside for a stirrup-cup."

"With all my heart, Jack."

"And with all mine, Mr. Jasper."

Neville felt unable to refuse, though he wished desperately not to go. He sensed he had lost control of his temper; Edwin's coolness, rather than calming him, had ignited him into a red-hot rage.

Jasper, still walking centrally with hands on both shoulders, beautifully refrained a drinking song, and the trio ascended to his rooms. Upon entering, the first thing visible, once Jasper added the lamp's glow to the firelight, was the portrait hanging above the mantelpiece. It was hardly an image conducive to improving relations between the two young men, awkwardly resurrecting the source of their discord. Both glanced at it consciously but remained silent.

Jasper, however—who seemed to have grasped only an imperfect clue to the cause of their shouting—immediately drew attention to it.

"You recognize that picture, Mr. Neville?" he shaded the lamp to illuminate it.

"I recognize it, but it is far from flattering the original," Neville replied.

"Oh, you are harsh on it!" Edwin interrupted. "It was painted by Ned, who gave it to me as a gift."

"I apologize for that, Mr. Drood," Neville said, genuinely contrite. "Had I known I was in the artist's presence..."

"Oh, a joke, sir, a mere jest," Edwin cut in with a provoking yawn. "Just a bit of humor for Pussy's sake! Someday, if she behaves, I shall paint her gravely."

The air of leisurely patronage and indifference with which this was delivered—as the speaker threw himself back in his chair, clasping his hands behind his head as a resting spot—was deeply exasperating to the excitable Neville. Jasper looked from one to the other, smiled faintly, and turned his back to mix a jug of mulled wine by the fire. It required considerable mixing and compounding.

"I suppose, Mr. Neville," Edwin said, quick to resent the indignation visible in Neville's face—which rivaled the portrait, the fire, or the lamp itself—"that if you were to paint the portrait of your beloved..."

"I cannot paint," Neville hastened to interrupt.

"That is your misfortune, not your fault. You would, if you could. But if you could, I suppose you would depict her (regardless of her actual nature) as Juno, Minerva, Diana, and Venus all combined. Would you not?"

"I have no lady love, and I cannot say."

"If I were to attempt my hand," Edwin declared, a boyish boastfulness rising within him, "on a portrait of Miss Landless—in earnest, mind you; truly—I should show you what I could do!"

"My sister's consent to sit for it being obtained first, I presume? Since that will never happen, I fear I shall never witness your skill. I must accept the loss."

Jasper turned from the fire, filled a large goblet for Neville, then one for Edwin, handing each his own. He filled his own as well, declaring, "Come, Mr. Neville, we toast my nephew, Ned. Since it is his foot in the stirrup—metaphorically speaking—our stirrup-cup is dedicated to him. Ned, my dearest fellow, my love!"
