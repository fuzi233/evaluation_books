My wife and Franz, startled at first by Fritz’s empty place, were still glad enough to see our little party come safely home. They listened close, as if every word were a footstep brought back to the hearth. When I spoke of the lion and his mate, my wife’s breath caught in her throat; she turned pale, and her fingers tightened on the bowl she had been holding as though it might steady her. And Jack—though he would have laughed at any one else’s fear—looked so wan and shaken that she searched his face again and again, unable to believe he carried no wound.

When the tale came to Juno, Franz’s eyes brimmed over. He asked, softly and with the dignity of one who has a historian’s tenderness for the faithful dead, whether we had brought her back to be laid near the home she had guarded so long. The next day he watched her burial as carefully as if the very soil must be set in order; and Ernest, at his request, composed a little epitaph, which we cut upon a slab and set above her:

JUNO

A servant true lies here:
A faithful friend,
A Dog,
To all most dear;
Who met her end
Fighting right bravely in her master's cause.

Then came the more ordinary consolations—the kind that can be tasted. The wild boar’s flesh, still sweet with the clean, foresty smell of the hunt, and the truffles, dark as damp earth and pungent as thunder-rain, were given over to my wife. She received them with the bright, practical joy of a woman who could turn peril into supper. Already she was speaking of stews thickened slowly, of slices smoked and dried for journeys, of sauces coaxed into richness with patience and heat. She would gladly have had the boar’s head as well; but my promise stood. Ernest’s museum was to claim it, not the oven—though my own mouth watered at the thought of it baked whole, in the fashion we had once learned, sealed with clay until the scent of fat and spice would have crept under every door like a warm hand.

The boar’s head and the lions’ skins we carried to the tannery on Whale Island, where salt, scraping, and long, rhythmic labor would turn raw spoil into something that could last—an island craftwork accompanied always by its own music: the rasp of tools, the slap of wet hide, the hush and suck of water along the shore.

Five days went by, each day with its familiar sounds—the morning birds, the insects’ thin fiddling in the heat, the evening fire’s crackle—yet none of it soothed the wrongness left by Fritz’s absence. The island, when one listened for it, seemed to hold its breath where his step should have been. At last I could no longer hide my uneasiness. I resolved to follow him.

The decision lifted everyone’s spirits as if a cloud had moved off the sun. Even my wife, once she knew we would sail in the pinnace and not attempt any reckless crossing in a smaller craft, agreed to come with us. It was settled, provisions stowed, water secured, guns examined, and the dogs called aboard, their nails clicking sharply on the planks.

On a bright morning, with a fair breeze, the five of us pushed off for Cape Minster.

The little yacht bounded over the water as if delighted with its own swiftness. The sea-breeze, salt and cool, made our cheeks glow; the sun painted the wave-crests into quick, silver sparks. I set our course for the archway’s entrance ahead, that familiar landmark like a gate into another chamber of the island’s world.

Then, straight before us, I saw a dark, greenish shadow just under the surface—a mass that looked like a sunken rock. It startled me, not for its presence alone but for the way it seemed not to belong to any map in my mind. I threw down the helm at once, but we were already upon it.

A slight shock ran through the hull—wood and water speaking together in a sudden, blunt syllable—and then, as quickly, the resistance vanished. We had surged over it. The danger, for that instant, was past.

I looked astern to mark the spot—and the “rock” was gone. Where the great shadow had been, the water lay smooth and innocent, as though it had never held anything at all.

Before our amazement had time to settle, Jack shouted, pointing hard to starboard.

“There is another!”

Sure enough, another greenish bulk seemed to lie just beneath the surface.

“The rock is moving!” cried Franz, and his words had barely left him when the sea itself heaved and a great black body rose, sleek and terrible, from the depths. From its upper extremity burst a column of water with a roar like a cannon’s breath, flung high into the air, then falling in cold spray around us—rain made violent, and loud enough to drown even our thoughts.

The mystery was plain now: a cachalot—an immense sperm whale, its length and massive head leaving no doubt.

The monster, angered by the scrape of our keel, slid back as if to measure us, then turned with dreadful intent. I remembered grim seamen’s tales—boats smashed like toys, ships stove in by a furious rush—and for a moment the bright day narrowed to the sound of our own breathing and the slap of water against the hull.

In desperation I sprang to one of the guns; Jack, quick as a spark, leaped to the other. We fired almost together. The shots struck home; the whale lashed the sea into foam, the sound of its tail like a great wet whip cracking again and again, and then it plunged down and vanished.

We did not relax. I would not lose such a prize, nor risk a second assault unready. We reloaded and stood toward shore, where the whale seemed to be making. Soon we sighted it again in shallower water, thrashing fearfully, the sea darkening around it with blood.

We approached as close as I dared, and fired again.

For a few moments its struggles grew even more frantic—then the vast body shuddered from head to tail, and the sea, which had been roaring with violence, fell into an uncanny quiet around it. The cachalot lay motionless: dead.

The boys’ shout of triumph rose—then died on their lips. Behind a rock a canoe shot into view, paddled by a tall, muscular savage. He stood up in his narrow skiff to study us, watchful as a heron. When he saw we were turning toward him, he snatched up his paddle and darted behind the rock again.

An awful thought struck me with a force colder than the whale’s spray: there might be a tribe of blacks hidden on these shores—and Fritz might have fallen into their hands.

We were not to be taken easily. Our guns were loaded and run out. The bay’s sounds sharpened—water ticking against stone, the wind’s steady hiss in the rigging, and, somewhere far in the forest, a deep, indistinct calling that might have been bird or beast or human voice. In such a place one learned that sound could mislead; and yet it was also the first warning, the first comfort, the first sign that the world still lived beyond one’s own fear.

A dusky face appeared, peering from a lofty rock, then vanished. Another peeped from lower down. Then the skiff put out again as if to reconnoitre. Even Jack looked uneasy and waited for my word.

“Hoist a white flag,” said I, “and give me the speaking-trumpet.”

I raised the trumpet and called out as peaceably as I could in Malay, searching my memory for words learned long ago. But neither the white cloth nor my careful phrases seemed to calm him; he began to turn back.

Jack, impatient as ever, snatched the trumpet.

“Come here, you black son of a gun,” he shouted. “Come on board and make friends, or we’ll blow you and your—”

“Stop! Stop!” I cried. “You foolish boy! You’ll only frighten him with such mad words and gestures.”

But Jack was already staring.

“No! Look—he’s paddling towards us!”

The canoe was indeed coming fast.

Then Franz gave a shriek that cut straight through the breeze.

“Look! Look! The villain is in Fritz’s cajack! I can see the walrus’ head!”

Only Ernest stayed calm. He took the speaking-trumpet with the quiet certainty that sometimes made him seem older than us all.

“Fritz, ahoy!” he called. “Welcome, old fellow!”

The words had scarcely flown when I recognized the familiar face beneath the darkened disguise. In another minute the canoe was alongside, and Fritz sprang aboard. We kissed and embraced him in spite of his blackened skin, and joy rushed through us like a sudden fire.

Questions broke over him from every side: where had he been, why so long, what danger, what news—and why in the world had he turned blackamoor?

“The last question,” he said, smiling, “is the only one I will answer just now. The rest must wait until I can tell everything properly. When I heard guns, I thought at once of Malay pirates. I never dreamed you could be here in the yacht. So I disguised myself and came out to reconnoitre. And when you spoke Malay, father, you only frightened me more—I was sure you were pirates.”

We told him, in turn, about the cachalot; and when I asked where we could safely anchor, he answered at once, with a glance that meant more than his words.

“Certainly. I can lead you to an island with splendid anchorage—and it is worth seeing too, for it holds all kinds of strange things.”

He rubbed away the stains from his skin as best he could, then returned to his canoe and piloted us toward a picturesque little island in the bay.

Now that Fritz’s success could no longer be doubted, I felt there was no reason to keep my wife in suspense. I told her, as gently as I could, what his project had been, and tried to prepare her for the surprise waiting on shore. The thought that after so many years we were to see another human being—another woman—shook her deeply. For a moment she could not speak.

“But why,” she asked at last, “did you not tell me sooner? Why keep such joyful news until the very last moment?”

“I would not raise hopes I might be forced to disappoint,” I said. “But now—thank Heaven—he has succeeded.”

The boys understood nothing of the half-silence, the pressed-down excitement that my wife, Fritz, and I could not wholly conceal. They stared at the island as if it were a riddle. When the sails were furled and the anchor dropped, they leaped ashore at once, full of restless curiosity. We followed Fritz in a body, and at his sign we kept perfect silence.

We moved through a thicket—leaves brushing sleeves and cheeks, birds scolding overhead, the soft thud of our boots on damp ground—until we came suddenly upon a hut of sheltering boughs. A cheerful fire burned at its entrance, and its smoke climbed straight up in the still air, thin and blue, smelling of green wood and some resinous leaf that gave it a sharp sweetness. In that scent was something startlingly homelike: the ordinary miracle of a meal prepared, a place kept.

Fritz dived into the leafy bower, leaving his brothers outside, struck dumb. In a moment he emerged, leading by the hand a slight, handsome youth, dressed like a young English naval officer. They came toward us, and Fritz, radiant, introduced him as Edward Montrose.

“And,” he added, looking from my wife to me, “will you not welcome him as a friend—and a brother—to our family circle?”

“That will we indeed,” I said, taking the stranger’s hands. “Our wild life may have roughened us, but I trust it has not hardened our hearts.”

My wife embraced the seeming youth with a warmth that trembled. The boys and even the dogs pressed close—boys delighted with the thought of a new companion; the dogs won by a voice that was sweet and gentle, carrying a music unlike our own island speech.

From Fritz’s careful phrases, I understood that the young person wished her sex to remain concealed from the others until my wife could provide a dress more suited to her true condition. The secret hung between us like a thread of another language: plain to those who knew how to hear it, yet easily missed by those who did not. It reminded me—strangely—how power can sit inside mere words and names, how a title can shelter a life, and how much of any person cannot truly be translated at all.

The young men ran down to the yacht to fetch what was needed for supper and to arrange a camp for the night. When they returned, my wife set herself to work at once, as if the act of feeding us all could steady the world.

She handled the fire as she always did—listening to it, coaxing it, knowing by sound when the heat was ready. A pot was set to simmer; the bubbling began low and steady, a comforting pulse. She laid out what we had brought, and what the island offered, making abundance from oddness with that sure household alchemy. The air filled with mingled scents—smoke, warm fat, herbs bruised between fingers, and the clean, faint sourness of our mead.

The boys, eager to make the new acquaintance feel at home, did their best to amuse “Edward.” And she—after the first stiffness wore away—answered them brightly, laughed when they laughed, and spoke with a lively ease that made the hut feel suddenly larger, as if it had room for new stories. She admired the dishes, tasted our fragrant mead, and without once dwelling on her former life kept conversation moving like a well-stirred stew, never sticking, never burning.

The mere fact of meeting any human being after so long an isolation would have been enough to set the boys’ minds on fire. But that this newcomer was so handsome, so gay, so entirely charming—this made them almost foolish with excitement. When at last I gave the sign to break up the feast, and our guest was led to the night-quarters prepared on board the yacht, Jack insisted the health of Edward Montrose be proposed. We drank it in sweet-smelling mead, with cheers and loud acclamations that rang over the bay and came back faintly from the rocks—an echo like the island itself joining in.

When she had gone and quiet returned, Jack flung more wood on the fire. The flames leaped, and their crackle filled the pauses between us like a living thing. He threw himself down in his careless way and fixed Fritz with an impatient grin.

“Now then, Fritz—out with it. Where did you find this jolly fellow? Was that your mysterious voyage for him, or did you stumble on him by chance? Tell us everything while we’re warm and comfortable.”

Fritz hesitated a moment, as if choosing the right doorway into his tale. Then he began.

“Perhaps you remember,” he said, “when I came back in the cajack the other day, I struck down an albatross. None but father knew what became of the wounded bird, or thought more of it. Yet it was that albatross which brought me notice of the shipwrecked stranger—and I determined it should carry back a message in return, to cheer whoever had sent the signal.

“I first prepared my cajack, as you know, to carry two persons. Then, with Pounce seated before me, I left you and the yacht and made for the open sea. For hours I paddled steadily. As the wind freshened, I kept nearer shore, thinking that if a storm rose I might find shelter in some bay.

“It was well I did. I had barely reached a quiet cove when the sea turned to foam. Great waves lifted and broke; even in that sheltered place I felt the danger. I spent the night in my cajack. Next morning I ate a frugal meal of pemmican and drank from my flask, then pushed out again. The wind had fallen; the sea was tolerably smooth. I watched in every direction for smoke, for anything that might prove a human being lived beyond the emptiness.

“About noon the coast changed. The shore grew sandy; inland, dense forests rose dark and thick. From their depths came a dreadful chorus: the roar of beasts of prey, the yelling of apes, the hyaena’s fiendish laugh, and now and then the despairing death-cry of a deer. The sounds made the solitude sharper. In that frail canoe, with those cries all around, I felt as if the world had been given over to wild throats and I alone had no true voice in it. I rested my paddle and let the cajack drift while I listened.”

He paused, and for a moment we heard only our own fire and the night insects, the island’s small percussion.

“As I neared the shore,” he went on, “I saw strange birds—fluttering round me, then darting back to the forest edge to feed on what looked like the pepper-plant. They seized the berries in great heavy beaks, tossed them into the air, and caught them again. Their beaks were monstrous, as if their heads must ache from carrying them; yet their colors were magnificent—beaks bright as painted wood, plumage like spilled dyes. I ought to have brought a specimen, but at the time I only watched their antics. I decided they were toucans. Was I right, Ernest?”

Ernest, unwilling to interrupt, merely nodded in his grave, professorial way, and Fritz continued.

“For hours I paddled on, passing river mouths and streams. Had I been merely exploring, I might have gone up one of those waterways into the forest; but I had a goal, and I felt I must reach it. When night came I found another sheltered cove, moored the cajack, and stepped ashore. It was a blessing to stretch my legs after sitting cramped so long. But it would not do to sleep on land. I ate, then returned to the canoe and slept there.

“Next morning Pounce and I landed for breakfast. I lit a fire, and hung up a plump young parrot to roast. As I turned it, listening to the fat begin to hiss and the skin tighten in the heat, I heard a rustle in the long grass behind me. I looked round—and saw an immense tiger. His eyes were fixed on me; his great tail swayed.

“In another moment he would have sprung. My gun lay near, but if I had stooped to seize it he would have been upon me.

“Pounce saw. He understood. The heroic bird darted at the tiger, beating his wings into the brute’s face, striking with his beak so fiercely that the spring was checked. I snatched my gun and fired. The tiger, pierced to the heart, leaped once—and rolled over at my feet.”

Fritz’s voice shook.

“The enemy was dead,” he said, and swallowed, “but beside him—poor Pounce—crushed and lifeless. One blow of that paw had struck him down for ever.”

We were silent. Even Jack did not speak. It is one thing to lose a dog in battle, another to lose a brave bird that had been more companion than tool; and in our island life, where every creature’s place in the household carried its own history, such a death felt like the tearing of a page from a book none of us could rewrite.

“With a sad heart,” Fritz went on quickly, “I buried him where he fell. Then I left at once, unable to bear the place, and paddled away, leaving the tiger as it lay.

“My thoughts were dark. Without my companion I felt I could not continue. The albatross might have flown hundreds of miles; the stranger might be on entirely different shores. Every stroke might be carrying me farther from the signal. Discouragement seized me.

“But it did not last. A sight appeared that banished doubt like sunlight breaking through cloud.

“A high point of land lay ahead. I rounded it and found a calm bay. A reef ran out from the wooded shore, and from the far end of that reef rose a column of smoke—steady, clear, curling upward in the still air. I stopped as if dreaming. Then I seized my paddle and strained every nerve to reach it.

“In a few strokes I was across, secured my canoe, and leaped onto the rock where the beacon burned. No one was there. The fire had been recently piled; I knew the person could not be far. I was about to shout when I saw a slight figure moving along the chain of rocks toward me.

“I advanced a few paces and said in English: ‘Welcome, fair stranger! God, in His mercy, has heard your call, and has sent me to your aid!’

“Miss Montrose came quickly forward—”

“Who? What?” cried the boys at once, and the circle around the fire broke into startled noise. Ernest rose, calm amid the commotion, and spoke with quiet certainty.

“I did not wish to interrupt you until you revealed it yourself, Fritz, but there is no need to pretend any longer. We all see through the disguise of Edward Montrose.”

Fritz colored, disconcerted for a moment; but his good humor returned as quickly as it had ever come. He endured his brothers’ jokes with equanimity, then joined in three loud cheers for their new sister. When the laughter died down, he resumed his story.

“Miss Montrose grasped my hands,” he said, “and guessed—by my accent, I suppose—that I did not speak English every day. She answered in French: ‘Long, long have I waited since the bird returned with your message. Thank God—you have come at last!’

“Then, with tears of joy, she led me to her shelter. She had built a hut and a safe sleeping-place—like a smaller Falconhurst—among the branches of a tree. I was astonished at her skill. Around the walls hung bows and arrows, lances and snares. On her work-table were boxes and cases carved neatly with a knife, holding fish-hooks of mother-of-pearl, needles made of fishbone, bodkins from birds’ beaks, fishing-lines of many kinds, knives and tools.

“These tools, she told me, and a chest of clothing were almost the only things washed ashore after the wreck, when three years ago she was cast alone upon that coast. I could hardly believe what she had accomplished. Obstacles that would have overwhelmed most people she had met with patience and invention.

“The hut itself was wonderfully made: stout posts driven into the ground, bamboo crosspieces, walls woven of reeds, a roof thatched with palm leaves, the whole plastered smooth with clay, and an opening left in the roof so smoke could escape like a chimney.

“As we entered, a cormorant flew out from under the table with an angry cry and darted at me. Miss Montrose called it off. She told me she had captured and tamed it soon after landing, and trained it to help her. It was not only her companion, but her provider—bringing fish from the water, striking birds from the air, even seizing rabbits and small animals on land, and laying everything at her feet.

“Before darkness fell she showed me all her contrivances—the kitchen-stove, cooking utensils, skin bottles, shell plates and spoons, a fishing raft, and many other things. Then we sat down to a most appetizing meal, and she gave me the short history of her life.

“Jenny Montrose—” he added, and glanced toward Ernest as if to confirm the name, “—was the daughter of a British officer who served long in India, where she was born. She lost her mother at three years old. After that, her father’s care was wholly hers. He had her taught the accomplishments suitable to her, yet he also gave her his own love of field sports, so that by seventeen she was as much at home on horseback in the open as in a drawing-room.

“Then Colonel Montrose received orders to return home with his regiment. For reasons of his own he did not wish her to sail with the troops, so he found her passage on a vessel leaving at the same time. The parting was painful, but unavoidable. She sailed in the Dorcas for England.

“A week out of Calcutta a storm drove the ship far off course. More bad weather followed; leaks sprang in all directions, and the crew took to the boats. Jenny gained a place in one of the largest. After many days of peril, land was sighted, but the other boats were gone. They attempted to land; the boat capsized, and she alone reached the shore. For a long time she lay on the sand like one dead. At last she revived enough to move, found shellfish, and little by little regained her strength…”
