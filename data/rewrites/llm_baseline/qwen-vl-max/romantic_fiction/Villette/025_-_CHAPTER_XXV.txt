The evening at La Terrasse was quiet, even subdued, despite the warmth of the fire and the cheerfulness of my godmother’s nature. She made an effort to entertain us, but true joy did not stir until the distant sound of hooves broke through the howling wind outside—signals that someone had returned from the storm. How often, I thought, do women sit safely by the hearth while their hearts wander into the cold dark, braving blizzards and icy roads, waiting for a loved one to come home. That night, both father and son arrived—the Count de Bassompierre riding alongside Dr. Bretton.

We could not tell who first heard the horses; the weather was so fierce we all rushed down to meet them. But as they entered, both were covered in snow—like two figures carved from winter itself. Mrs. Bretton immediately ordered them to the kitchen, forbidding them to step on her carpeted stairs until they had shed their frosty cloaks. Into the kitchen we followed, drawn by the scene: it was a large, old Dutch-style room, warm and inviting with its beams and hearth. The little Countess darted around her equally snowy father, clapping her hands. “Papa, papa!” she cried. “You look like a giant Polar bear!”

He shook himself, sending a shower of snow flying, and she fled back laughing, only to return moments later eager to help him remove his arctic disguise. When he finally freed himself from his coat, he playfully threatened to bury her under it like an avalanche.

“Then come,” she said, bending forward as if to invite the fall. And when he raised the coat above her head, she leapt aside like a young chamois, quick and light.

Her movements were soft, supple, graceful—like a kitten. Her laughter rang clear and bright, like silver bells. As she took her father’s cold hands in hers and rubbed them gently, standing on tiptoe to kiss his lips, there seemed to glow about her a halo of pure affection. The dignified seigneur looked down upon her with the kind of love men reserve for what they hold most dear.

“Mrs. Bretton,” he said, “what am I to do with this daughter—or daughterling—of mine? She doesn’t grow in wisdom or stature. Doesn’t she seem just as much a child as she was ten years ago?”

“She cannot be more of a child than your own son here,” replied Mrs. Bretton, who was arguing with Graham over his clothes—she insisted on a change he resisted. He leaned against the dresser, laughing, keeping her at arm’s length.

“Come now, mamma,” he said, “let’s compromise. Let’s have a Christmas wassail cup, and toast Old England right here by the fire.”

So while the Count warmed himself by the flames and Paulina danced freely across the spacious kitchen—happy in her freedom—Mrs. Bretton directed Martha to prepare the wassail bowl. She poured the steaming drink into a Bretton flagon, serving it round with a small silver cup I recognized as Graham’s christening cup.

“Here’s to Auld Lang Syne!” the Count declared, raising his glass high. Then, turning to Mrs. Bretton:

“We twa ha’ paidlet i’ the burn
Fra morning sun till dine,
But seas between us braid ha’ roared
Sin’ auld lang syne.

“And surely ye’ll be your pint-stoup,
And surely I’ll be mine;
And we’ll taste a cup o’ kindness yet
For auld lang syne.”

“Scotch! Scotch!” Paulina called out. “Papa is speaking Scotch—and he is partly Scotch. We are Home and de Bassompierre, Caledonian and Gallic.”

“And is that a Scotch reel you’re dancing, you Highland fairy?” her father asked. “Mrs. Bretton, there will soon be a green ring growing in the middle of your kitchen. I wouldn’t answer for her being quite cannie—she’s a strange little mortal.”

“Tell Lucy to dance with me, papa,” she said. “There’s Lucy Snowe.”

Mr. Home—still very much the plain Mr. Home beneath the title of Count—held out his hand to me kindly. “I remember you well,” he said. “Even if my memory weren’t trustworthy, your name has been on my daughter’s lips so often, and I’ve heard so many stories about you, that you feel like an old friend.”

Everyone had tasted the wassail except Paulina, whose dancing had not once been interrupted. But she wasn’t to be left out.

“Let me taste,” she said to Graham, reaching toward the cup he was placing beyond her reach on the dresser.

Mrs. Bretton and Mr. Home were deep in conversation now. Dr. John had watched Paulina’s dance closely—he liked it. Not only were her movements soft and beautiful, pleasing to his eye, but her ease in her father’s house pleased him too—it made him feel at ease. Once again, she seemed a child to him—almost his playmate. I wondered how he would speak to her. His first words revealed that the memories of “little Polly” had returned, stirred by her joyful spirit.

“Your ladyship wishes for the tankard?”

“I think I said so. I think I intimated as much.”

“Couldn’t consent to a step of the kind on any account. Sorry for it, but couldn’t do it.”

“Why? I’m quite well now. It won’t break my collarbone again or dislocate my shoulder. Is it wine?”

“No. Nor dew.”

“I don’t want dew—I don’t like dew. What is it?”

“Ale—strong ale—old October. Brewed, perhaps, when I was born.”

“It must be curious. Is it good?”

“Excessively good.”

He took it down, drank a second dose of this potent brew, smiled mischievously, then placed it carefully back on the shelf.

“I should like a little,” Paulina said, looking up. “I’ve never had ‘old October.’ Is it sweet?”

“Perilously sweet,” Graham answered.

She gazed at him with the expression of a child longing for something forbidden. At last, he relented, took the cup down, and let her sip from his hand. His eyes—always alive with emotion—shone with quiet delight. He held the cup just so, allowing only a single drop at a time to touch her rosy lips.

“A little more—a little more,” she said, tapping his wrist lightly with her finger. “It smells of spice and sugar, but I can’t taste it. Your wrist is so stiff, and you’re so stingy.”

He gave in, whispering seriously, “Don’t tell my mother or Lucy. They wouldn’t approve.”

“Nor do I,” she said, suddenly changing tone—just as if the drink had lifted a spell. “It’s anything but sweet. It’s bitter and hot, and takes away my breath. Your old October was only desirable because it was forbidden. Thank you, no more.”

With a slight, graceful bow—careless yet elegant—she glided away and rejoined her father.

I believe she spoke the truth: the girl of seventeen still carried within her the heart of a seven-year-old.

Graham watched her go, puzzled, uncertain. He kept his eyes on her throughout the evening, but she seemed unaware of his gaze.

As we moved upstairs to the drawing-room for tea, she took her father’s arm—her natural place was beside him. Her attention was wholly his. He and Mrs. Bretton led the conversation, and Paulina was their most attentive listener, urging them to repeat tales and adventures.

“And where were you then, papa? What did you say? Tell Mrs. Bretton what happened that time.” So she drew him out.

She no longer danced or laughed wildly—the infantine sparkle had faded. She was calm, thoughtful, obedient. It was lovely to see her bid goodnight. Her manner toward Graham was touched with dignity. In her quiet smile and gentle bow, the Countess showed herself. Graham responded with solemnity. I saw he struggled to reconcile the dancing sprite with the refined young woman.

The next morning, gathered round the breakfast table, shivering after our cold ablutions, Mrs. Bretton declared no one should leave the house unless absolutely necessary.

Indeed, leaving seemed impossible. Snow drifted against the lower windows, and outside, sky and air were chaotic, wind and snow locked in furious battle. No new snow fell, but the old was torn up, whirled into spirals, cast into strange shapes.

Paulina supported her mother’s decree.

“Papa shall not go out,” she said, seating herself beside his chair. “I’ll take care of him. You won’t go into town, will you, papa?”

“Ay, and No,” he replied. “If you and Mrs. Bretton are *very* good to me, Polly—kind and attentive—if you pet me nicely and make much of me, I might wait an hour after breakfast to see if this razor-edged wind calms. But you give me nothing—no breakfast—you let me starve.”

“Quick, Mrs. Bretton,” Paulina urged, “pour the coffee—while I attend to the Count de Bassompierre in other ways. Since he became a count, he’s needed so much care.”

She cut and prepared a roll.

“There, papa, are your ‘pistolets’ charged,” she said. “And here’s marmalade—just like the kind we had at Bretton, which you said was as good as if it had been made in Scotland—”

“And which your little ladyship used to beg for my boy,” Mrs. Bretton interjected. “Do you remember? How you’d come to my elbow, whisper, ‘Please, ma’am, something good for Graham—a little marmalade, or honey?’”

“No, mamma,” Dr. John broke in, laughing but blushing. “It surely wasn’t so. I couldn’t have cared for such things.”

“Did he or didn’t he, Paulina?”

“He liked them,” she insisted.

“Never blush for it, John,” Mr. Home said warmly. “I like them myself. And Polly showed good sense in caring for a friend’s comfort. I taught her that—though I never let her forget it. Polly, offer me a slice of tongue.”

“There, papa,” she said. “But remember—you’re only waited on with such care on condition that you’re persuadable and stay at La Terrasse today.”

“Mrs. Bretton,” the Count said, “I want to send my daughter to school. Do you know of any good one?”

“There’s Miss Snowe’s—Madame Beck’s.”

“Miss Snowe teaches?”

“I am a teacher,” I said, glad of the chance to clarify. For some time I’d felt awkward—Mrs. Bretton and Graham knew my situation, but the Count and Paulina did not. They might alter their kindness once they learned my station. I spoke plainly, though a wave of unexpected thoughts rose in me, making me sigh without meaning to.

Mr. Home didn’t raise his eyes for nearly two minutes. Perhaps he hadn’t heard. Or perhaps, as a Scotsman—pride being proverbial—he felt silence was proper after such a confession. Yet though he was simple in appearance and habits, I had always sensed a depth in him. Was his pride real or false? I leave that question open. But for me, he was always a gentleman—true and kind.

By nature, he was reflective and sensitive. A quiet melancholy shadowed his thoughts and feelings—not merely a tint, but a deeper cloud shaped by sorrow and loss. He didn’t know much about me, nor fully understand what I was. Often, his assumptions amused me. But he saw my life lay on the shaded side of the hill. He believed I tried to walk straight, honestly. If he could have helped, he would have. Unable to, he still wished me well. When he looked at me, his eyes were kind. When he spoke, his voice was gentle.

“Yours,” he said, “is a difficult calling. I wish you health and strength to succeed in it.”

His daughter reacted differently. Her eyes widened—astonished, almost alarmed.

“Are you a teacher?” she exclaimed. Then, pausing, “Well, I never knew what you were. I never thought to ask. To me, you’ve always been Lucy Snowe.”

“And what am I now?” I asked, unable to resist.

“Yourself, of course. But do you really teach here, in Villette?”

“I do.”

“And do you like it?”

“Not always.”

“And why do you keep doing it?”

Her father glanced at her, perhaps ready to stop her—but only said, “Proceed, Polly, proceed with your catechism. Prove yourself the little wiseacre you are. If Miss Snowe blushed, I’d have to silence you. But she only smiles—so press her harder. Well, Miss Snowe, why do you continue?”

“Chiefly,” I said, “for the money.”

“Not then from pure philanthropy? Polly and I were clinging to that idea as the kindest explanation for your oddness.”

“No, sir. Rather for the roof over my head, and the peace of mind knowing I work for myself—so I don’t burden anyone.”

“Papa,” Paulina said softly, “I pity Lucy.”

“Take that pity,” Mr. Home replied, “and put it back where it came from—into the heart that sent it. If my Polly ever learns by experience how fragile this world’s comforts are, I hope she’ll act as Lucy does: work for herself, so she burdens neither friend nor family.”

“Yes, papa,” she said quietly. “But poor Lucy—I thought she was rich, with wealthy friends.”

“You thought like a child,” he said. “I never did. When I studied Lucy’s manner, I saw she guarded herself, acted, rather than was served. This path, I believe, has given her wisdom. If she lives long enough, she may bless Providence for it. Now—about this school: would Madame Beck accept my Polly, do you think, Miss Lucy?”

I said it would be easy to try. Madame Beck liked English pupils. “If you, sir,” I added, “take Miss de Bassompierre in your carriage this afternoon, I’m sure Rosine will answer your ring quickly, and Madame will put on her best gloves to receive you.”

“In that case,” Mr. Home said, “there’s no need to delay. Mrs. Hurst can send her things later. Polly can settle into her horn-book by nightfall. And you, Miss Lucy, I trust you’ll keep an eye on her and let me know how she fares. Do you approve, Countess?”

The Countess hesitated. “I thought… I thought I had finished my education…”

“That only proves we can be wrong. I believe otherwise—especially after hearing your insights this morning. Ah, my little girl, you have much to learn. Papa should have taught you more. Come—try Madame Beck. The weather seems to calm, and I’ve finished my breakfast.”

“But, papa!”

“Well?”

“I see an obstacle.”

“I don’t.”

“It’s enormous—impossible to overcome. As big as you in your greatcoat and the snowdrift atop it.”

“And, like that drift, capable of melting?”

“No! It’s too solid—too real. It’s you, papa. Miss Lucy, warn Madame Beck not to accept me—because in the end, she’d have to take papa too. He’s so teasing, I’ll tell tales about him. Listen all: Five years ago, when I was twelve, he decided I was spoiled, unfit for the world. I cried—but M. de Bassompierre was firm. I went to school. What happened? He came too—every other day. Madame Aigredoux complained, but it did no good. Eventually, both of us were expelled. Tell Madame Beck this—fair warning.”

Mrs. Bretton turned to Mr. Home. “What do you say to that?”

He offered no defense. Judgment was given—Paulina triumphed.

Yet she had other moods. After breakfast, when the elders withdrew—likely to discuss business—Paulina, Dr. Bretton, and I were left alone. The child vanished. With us, her peers, she rose to the level of a young lady. Her face changed—its dimpled openness softened into something more serious, her features sharper, less mobile.

Graham noticed it too. He stood near the window, watching the snow. Then he approached the hearth and began to talk—not with his usual ease. He chose words carefully, hesitantly, poorly. He spoke vaguely of Villette—its people, buildings, sights.

She responded with maturity—intelligent, poised. Though traces of little Polly lingered—quick glances, animated gestures—there was a polish, a grace that smoothed them into elegance. A man less observant than Graham might not have seen the remnants of childhood.

Yet Graham observed everything. Every flicker, every hesitation, every lisp—he caught it. Sometimes, when speaking fast, she still lisped. But she flushed and corrected herself carefully, with a charm that was almost as amusing as the error.

Each time, Graham smiled.

Gradually, the tension eased. Their conversation grew warmer. Paulina’s cheeks and lips softened into a familiar, dimpling smile. She lisped once—and forgot to correct herself.

And Dr. John changed. Not gayer—no jests, no levity—but his presence seemed more comfortable, his speech smoother, more confident. Ten years had passed since they last met, but the bond remained. Some natures deepen with time, not diminish. For them, silence bred closeness, closeness led to unity.

But Graham must go. His profession demanded it. He left the room—but returned before leaving the house. I know he came back—not for the paper or card in his desk, but to confirm, one last time, that the image of Paulina he carried was true. That he hadn’t mistaken her under artificial light.

No—he found the impression stronger. He took away a final glance—shy, tender, innocent—as beautiful as a fawn lifting its head from ferns, or a lamb rising from its bed.

Left alone, Paulina and I sat in silence. We each took up our work. Her old wooden box was replaced by one of mosaic, adorned with golden tools. Her fingers, once tiny and trembling, were now swift and skilled. Yet the same delicate mannerisms remained—the quick turn to fix a strand of hair, the brush of silk to remove imaginary dust.

That morning, I craved silence. The storm’s fury had left me hushed, awed. The white, bloodless passion of January still raged—though spent, it was not spent. Had Ginevra Fanshawe been there, she would not have let me rest. She’d have questioned me endlessly, filled the room with noise and speculation.

But Paulina only glanced at me—quiet, piercing. Her lips parted, as if to speak—but she saw my silence and respected it.
