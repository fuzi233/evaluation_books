The hush of Harley Street, while Edith lay confined and slowly gathered strength again, was a kind of narcotic mercy to Margaret. It let her sleep; it let her stop trembling at every remembered footstep in a Milton passage; it let her discover, in the calm light of days that asked nothing of her, how utterly her life had altered within eight short weeks.

She was lodged now among thick carpets that drank sound, among curtains that fell in disciplined folds and muffled even the passing wheels outside, among chairs that invited languor as if it were a duty. In this house the bare notion that care might exist elsewhere seemed never to have travelled. The daily order moved like a well-fed kitchen—machinery oiled, service timed, everything smoothed before one could feel the edge of want. Mrs. Shaw and Edith could scarcely do enough in their petting welcome; they persisted in calling it Margaret’s “home,” and said it with the soft tyranny of those who think affection may rename a place and make it true.

Yet the word “home,” like a flavour that will not take, refused to settle on Margaret’s tongue. Home, to her secret mind, was Helstone—nay, even the little house in Milton with its scant economies, the anxious father, the ailing mother, the small tiresome cares that had once filled the hours with a sort of honest purpose. It was almost ungrateful, she thought, to feel this; and still she did. Edith, impatient to be well, spoke eagerly of refitting Margaret’s bedchamber with every pliant comfort—sofas that yielded, footstools, screens, the pretty, useless ornaments that caught light and proclaimed leisure. Mrs. Shaw and the maid, as if nothing could be kinder than to bury a woman beneath satin, found perpetual employment in restoring Margaret’s wardrobe to “elegant variety,” as though the cut of a sleeve could mend a wound.

Captain Lennox moved through it all with that easy, gentlemanly kindness which requires no imagination. He sat beside Edith in her dressing-room a dutiful hour or two each day; he played with Sholto for another; he spent the rest where men like him spent it—in his club, or at dinner—gliding from one warm room to another, never compelled to hear the rawer noises of life. And just as Margaret’s need for quiet began to ebb—just as she might have started to feel the days too long, too smooth, too empty—Edith came downstairs again and resumed her part as queen of the household.

Then Margaret slid, by old habit, into watching, admiring, and ministering. She was grateful, almost, for occupation that was occupation only in name: she took charge of the semblances of duty, answered Edith’s notes, reminded her of engagements, sat by her when there was no gaiety in prospect and Edith, by a kind of luxurious caprice, fancied herself ill. Margaret would smooth the pillows, fetch the shawls, ring for the little restoratives that were brought up with grave faces as if they were remedies and not indulgences. Edith repaid her with caresses and praises—“your eloquence du billet, Maggie!”—as though a knack for phrases could stand in place of a life.

But the rest of the family lived in the full hurry of the Season. The house often emptied, leaving Margaret alone in rooms too finished, too silent. In those hours her thoughts ran backward to Milton with an ache sharpened by contrast: the sharp clang of the streets there, the breath of the mills that seemed to thicken the very air, the human voices strained high to cross the din. She found herself surfeited with ease that required no effort, no endurance, no striving. She began to fear that such softness would make her forget; that she might be lulled into a sleepy deadness, unable to feel beyond the circle of luxury that lapped her round.

There were toilers somewhere in London—she knew it as one knows a fact in a book—yet she never saw them. Even the servants belonged to an underground world: they rose soundlessly, performed, vanished; their lives, their hopes and fears, were as unknown to her as if they had no language Margaret could hear. The house above was a theatre kept sweet by invisible hands, and the sweetness itself—polished silver, warmed plates, the faint perfume of hot water and soap and flowers—began to oppress her like a scent too constant.

Once, when she hinted something of this to Edith, her cousin—wearied with dancing the night before—stroked Margaret’s cheek languidly as she sat on her old footstool by the sofa.

“Poor child!” said Edith, with a soft, complacent pity. “It is a little sad for you to be left night after night, just at this time when all the world is so gay. But we shall be having our dinner-parties soon—as soon as Henry comes back from circuit—and then there will be a little pleasant variety for you. No wonder it is moped, poor darling!”

Dinner-parties, Margaret thought, could not cure a hunger like hers; yet she did not say so. Edith prided herself on these entertainments—“so different,” she declared, from the old dowager dinners under mamma’s régime. Mrs. Shaw took as much pleasure in the new arrangements and newer circle as she had once taken in the heavier formality of her own, as if one might change a menu and imagine the appetite changed with it.

Captain Lennox, for his part, was always kindly brotherly to Margaret. She was truly fond of him—except in those moments when his attention fixed anxiously on Edith’s dress and appearance, as if her beauty were a dish to be served at precisely the right temperature to impress the world. Then something fierce and proud woke in Margaret. She could feel it like heat rising; she could hardly keep from speaking. It was not that she despised adornment; it was that she hated the way admiration was demanded and parceled out, as if a woman’s value depended on the proper arrangement of lace.

Her days settled into a dull rotation. There would be a quiet hour or two before a late breakfast; then a languid, unpunctual meal, eaten by half-awake people who drew it out as if time itself were their property. She must sit through it all, because afterwards came the discussion of plans—calls, drives, purchases, parties—plans that did not concern her and yet required her sympathy, her nods, her dutiful murmurs. Then an endless number of notes to write—Edith always leaving them with kisses and compliments; a little play with Sholto fresh from his morning walk; the care of the children during the servants’ dinner; a drive or callers; then some engagement for aunt and cousins that left Margaret “free,” though freedom like this was merely empty space, and the day’s inactivity pressed upon her, heavy as indigestible food.

She looked forward, with a longing she did not name aloud, to the homely event of Dixon’s return from Milton, where the old servant had been winding up all the Hales’ affairs. The cessation of news felt like famine. Dixon wrote business-like letters: Mr. Thornton’s opinion about what should be done with furniture; how to deal with the landlord at Crampton Terrace. Names came in sparsely, like crumbs when one has remembered bread. Mr. Thornton’s was there now and then; Milton names, seldom; and for Margaret, who had lived so intensely among those people, it was like being made suddenly deaf.

One evening she sat alone in the Lennox drawing-room. Dixon’s letters lay in her hand, but she was not reading—only thinking over them, tasting each phrase as if it might carry some hidden savour of the life she had been torn from. Her mind moved among memories: the hot, close streets, the unceasing mill-sound, the way voices in Milton had to fight to be heard; and she pictured that busy whirl going on as if she and her father had never existed there, as if their absence made no hollow at all. Did no one miss her in all that crowd? (Not Higgins; it was not of him she was thinking.) The thought stung her with a shame she could not account for.

The door opened. Mr. Bell was announced.

Margaret started as if caught in theft, and hurried the letters into her work-basket. She rose, colour rushing into her face, ashamed even of her own secrecy.

“Oh, Mr. Bell! I never thought of seeing you!”

“But you give me a welcome, I hope, as well as that very pretty start of surprise.”

“Have you dined? How did you come? Let me order you some dinner.”

“If you’re going to have any. Otherwise, you know, there is no one who cares less for eating than I do. But where are the others? Gone out to dinner? Left you alone?”

“Oh yes—and it is such a rest. I was just thinking—But will you run the risk of dinner? I don’t know if there is anything in the house.”

“Why, to tell you the truth, I dined at my club. Only they don’t cook as well as they did, so I thought, if you were going to dine, I might try and make out my dinner. But never mind, never mind! There aren’t ten cooks in England to be trusted at impromptu dinners. If their skill and their fires will stand it, their tempers won’t. You shall make me some tea, Margaret. And now—what were you thinking of? you were going to tell me. Whose letters were those, god-daughter, that you hid away so speedily?”

“Only Dixon’s,” Margaret answered; and felt, absurdly, her cheeks grow hotter.

“Whew! is that all? Who do you think came up in the train with me?”

“I don’t know,” said Margaret, and set her mouth as if she would not be coaxed into guessing.

“Your what d’ye call him? What’s the right name for a cousin-in-law’s brother?”

“Mr. Henry Lennox?” she asked.

“Yes,” replied Mr. Bell. “You knew him formerly, didn’t you? What sort of a person is he, Margaret?”

“I liked him long ago,” she said, her eyes dropping for the briefest moment. Then, as if she refused to let the past take advantage of her, she looked up and continued in her natural tone: “You know we have been corresponding about Frederick since; but I have not seen him for nearly three years, and he may be changed. What did you think of him?”

“I don’t know. He was so busy trying to find out who I was, in the first instance, and what I was in the second, that he never let out what he was; unless indeed that veiled curiosity of his as to what manner of man he had to talk to was not a good piece, and a fair indication of his character. Do you call him good-looking, Margaret?”

“No! certainly not. Do you?”

“Not I. But I thought perhaps you might. Is he a great deal here?”

“I fancy he is when he is in town. He has been on circuit now since I came. But—Mr. Bell—have you come from Oxford or from Milton?”

“From Milton. Don’t you see I’m smoke-dried?”

“Certainly. But I thought that it might be the effect of the antiquities of Oxford.”

“Come now, be a sensible woman! In Oxford, I could have managed all the landlords in the place, and had my own way, with half the trouble your Milton landlord has given me, and defeated me after all. He won’t take the house off our hands till next June twelve-month. Luckily, Mr. Thornton found a tenant for it. Why don’t you ask after Mr. Thornton, Margaret? He has proved himself a very active friend of yours, I can tell you. Taken more than half the trouble off my hands.”

“And how is he? How is Mrs. Thornton?” Margaret asked quickly, her voice dipping as if she feared the sound might carry.

“I suppose they’re well. I’ve been staying at their house till I was driven out of it by the perpetual clack about that Thornton girl’s marriage. It was too much for Thornton himself, though she was his sister. He used to go and sit in his own room perpetually. He’s getting past the age for caring for such things, either as principal or accessory. I was surprised to find the old lady falling into the current, and carried away by her daughter’s enthusiasm for orange-blossoms and lace. I thought Mrs. Thornton had been made of sterner stuff.”

“She would put on any assumption of feeling to veil her daughter’s weakness,” Margaret said, low.

“Perhaps so. You’ve studied her, have you? She doesn’t seem over fond of you, Margaret.”

“I know it.”

“Oh, here is tea, at last!” Margaret exclaimed, with a relief that was almost too evident.

And with tea came Mr. Henry Lennox.

He had walked up after a late dinner, expecting to find his brother and sister-in-law at home. Margaret, seeing him, felt the old Helstone terrace rise as plainly before her as if the gravel were underfoot and the evening air full of that south-country scent of leaf and earth. She suspected that he, too, was grateful for the presence of a third person; their first meeting since the day of his offer and her refusal would have been hard enough with Edith and the Captain in the room, and was doubly awkward now, when she was the only lady there and must, by mere force of custom, bear the weight of most of his speech.

At first she could scarcely find words. She was thankful for tea-table occupations—the little clink of china, the measured pour, the faint steam rising—because they gave her a decent reason for silence, and him time to gather his composure. For he had rather forced himself there that evening, with the stern prudence of a man determined to step over an awkwardness by standing on it; and now, finding her alone, he had no refuge but talk.

Margaret recovered first. She began with what lay uppermost in her mind, and perhaps in her heart’s need of duty.

“Mr. Lennox, I have been so much obliged to you for all you have done about Frederick.”

“I am only sorry it has been so unsuccessful,” he replied; and his quick glance slid towards Mr. Bell, as if to measure how far he might speak.

Margaret, reading that glance, turned to Mr. Bell, both drawing him into the conversation and implying, by the very act, that he was already fully trusted.

“That Horrocks—that very last witness of all—has proved as unavailing as the others. Mr. Lennox discovered that he sailed for Australia only last August; only two months before Frederick was in England, and gave us the names of—”

“Frederick in England! you never told me that!” Mr. Bell broke out, astonished.

“I thought you knew. I never doubted you had been told. Of course, it was a great secret, and perhaps I should not have named it now,” Margaret said, dismayed, and feeling again that hot shame as if she had betrayed something sacred.

“I have never named it to either my brother or your cousin,” said Mr. Lennox, with the dry restraint of a professional man who rebukes by precision.

“Never mind, Margaret. I am not living in a talking, babbling world, nor yet among people who are trying to worm facts out of me; you needn’t look so frightened because you have let the cat out of the bag to a faithful old hermit like me. I shall never name his having been in England; I shall be out of temptation, for no one will ask me. Stay!” He interrupted himself abruptly. “Was it at your mother’s funeral?”

“He was with mamma when she died,” Margaret answered softly; and the room seemed, for a moment, to hold its breath.

“To be sure! To be sure! Why, some one asked me if he had not been over then, and I denied it stoutly—not many weeks ago—who could it have been? Oh! I recollect!”

He did not speak the name. Margaret’s mind leapt—she would have given much to know if her suspicion was right, if it had been Mr. Thornton who had asked. But she could not ask. There are questions that, once spoken, change the air.

A pause followed—one of those little pauses in which the very soundscape of a room asserts itself: the faint crackle of coal, the distant roll of a carriage, the soft footfall of a servant beyond the door. Then Mr. Lennox said, addressing Margaret:

“I suppose, as Mr. Bell is now acquainted with all the circumstances attending your brother’s unfortunate dilemma, I cannot do better than inform him exactly how the research into the evidence we once hoped to produce in his favour stands at present. So, if he will do me the honour to breakfast with me to-morrow, we will go over the names of these missing gentry.”

“I should like to hear all the particulars, if I may. Cannot you come here? I dare not ask you both to breakfast, though I am sure you would be welcome. But let me know all you can about Frederick, even though there may be no hope at present.”

“I have an engagement at half-past eleven. But I will certainly come if you wish it,” Mr. Lennox replied; and the afterthought of eagerness in his tone made Margaret shrink inwardly, almost wishing she had not asked so naturally.

Mr. Bell rose and began to look about for his hat, which had been moved to make room at the tea-table.

“Well!” said he. “I don’t know what Mr. Lennox is inclined to do, but I’m disposed to be moving off homewards. I’ve been a journey to-day, and journeys begin to tell upon my sixty and odd years.”

“I believe I shall stay and see my brother and sister,” said Mr. Lennox, making no movement.

A shy dread seized Margaret—quick, unreasonable, irresistible. To be left alone with him! Helstone seemed to press upon her again: the terrace, the soft southern air, the words spoken there that could not be unsaid. She could hardly help believing the scene was as present to him as to her.

“Don’t go yet, please, Mr. Bell,” she said hastily. “I want you to see Edith; and I want Edith to know you. Please!” And she laid a light, determined hand upon his arm.

He looked at her and saw the confusion stirred in her face. He sat down again, as if the touch had authority no argument could resist.

“You see how she overpowers me, Mr. Lennox,” he said, forcing a jest to give her time. “And I hope you noticed the happy choice of her expressions; she wants me to ‘see’ this cousin Edith, who, I am told, is a great beauty; but she has the honesty to change her word when she comes to me—Mrs. Lennox is to ‘know’ me. I suppose I am not much to ‘see,’ eh, Margaret?”

Margaret caught at the tone and returned it, grateful for any bridge over the awkwardness.

Mr. Lennox, watching her in the quiet black dress that made her paleness more distinct, wondered how his brother could have reported her as having lost her good looks. She was a contrast to Edith, who entered not long after—dancing, as it were, into the room in her white crape mourning, golden hair floating, all softness and glitter, the sort of brightness that seems made for candlelight and admiration.

Edith dimpled and blushed becomingly when Mr. Bell was introduced, conscious of her reputation and unwilling that even an old Oxford Fellow, from a college “nobody had ever heard of,” should refuse the proper homage. Mrs. Shaw and Captain Lennox, each in their own manner, welcomed him kindly and sincerely, winning him to like them almost in spite of himself, especially when he saw how naturally Margaret slipped into her place as if she had always belonged to this bright, easy circle—sister, daughter, helper, yet not mistress of herself.

“What a shame that we were not at home to receive you,” said Edith. “You, too, Henry! though I don’t know that we should have stayed at home for you. And for Mr. Bell! for Margaret’s Mr. Bell—”

“There is no knowing what sacrifices you would not have made,” said her brother-in-law. “Even a dinner-party! and the delight of wearing this very becoming dress.”

Edith did not know whether to frown or smile. Mr. Lennox, seeing he had driven her too near to displeasure, went on smoothly.

“Will you show your readiness to make sacrifices to-morrow morning—first by asking me to breakfast, to meet Mr. Bell, and secondly by being so kind as to order it at half-past nine, instead of at ten o’clock? I have some letters and papers that I want to show to Miss Hale and Mr. Bell.”

“I hope Mr. Bell will make our house his own during his stay in London,” said Captain Lennox. “I am only so sorry we cannot offer him a bedroom.”

“Thank you. I am much obliged to you. You would only think me a churl if you had, for I should decline it, I believe, in spite of all the temptations of such agreeable company,” Mr. Bell replied, bowing all round, and secretly congratulating himself on the neat polish of his phrase. Plain truth, he knew, would have been: he could not bear the restraint of so proper a household. Good manners, he thought with amusement, were a kind of seasoning; and he had just begun to catch the trick of them.

His self-satisfaction lasted him until he was fairly out in the street, walking side by side with Henry Lennox. Here the open air, with its harder noises—the clatter of hooves, the grind of wheels, the cries that rose and fell like brief, ragged melodies—shook other thoughts loose. He remembered Margaret’s quick look of entreaty as she begged him to stay; remembered, too, hints given long ago by an acquaintance of Lennox’s about the man’s admiration of her. A new suspicion took shape, and with it a certain protectiveness that pricked him on.

“You have known Miss Hale for a long time, I believe. How do you think her looking? She strikes me as pale and ill.”

“I thought her looking remarkably well. Perhaps not when I first came—now I think of it. But certainly, when she grew animated, she looked as well as ever I saw her do.”

“She has had a great deal to go through,” said Mr. Bell.

“Yes! I have been sorry to hear of all she has had to bear; not merely the common and universal sorrow arising from death, but all the annoyance which her father’s conduct must have caused her, and then—”

“Her father’s conduct!” Mr. Bell exclaimed, startled into sharpness. “You must have heard some wrong statement. He behaved in the most conscientious manner. He showed more resolute strength than I should ever have given him credit for formerly.”

“Perhaps I have been wrongly informed. But I have been told—by his successor in the living, a clever sensible man, and a thoroughly active clergyman—that there was no call upon Mr. Hale to do what he did; relinquish the living, and throw himself and his family on the tender mercies of private teaching in a manufacturing town. The bishop had offered him another living, it is true, but if he had come to entertain certain doubts, he could have remained where he was, and so had no occasion to resign. The truth is, these country clergymen live such isolated lives—isolated, I mean, from all intercourse with men of equal cultivation with themselves, by whose minds they might regulate their own, and discover when they were going either too fast or too slow—that they are very apt to disturb themselves with imaginary doubts as to the articles of faith, and throw up certain opportunities of doing good for very uncertain fancies of their own.”

“I differ from you. I do not think they are very apt to do as my poor friend Hale did.”

Mr. Bell felt himself chafe inwardly. The words “imaginary doubts” grated like grit between teeth.

“Perhaps I used too general an expression in saying ‘very apt.’ But certainly their lives are such as very often to produce either inordinate self-sufficiency, or a morbid state of conscience,” Mr. Lennox returned, with perfect coolness.

“You don’t meet with any self-sufficiency among the lawyers, for instance?” Mr. Bell asked, his tone hardening. “And seldom, I imagine, any cases of morbid conscience.”

His vexation made him forget his newly-practised urbanity. Mr. Lennox saw that he had annoyed his companion; and since he had spoken chiefly to fill the time while their road lay together, he was indifferent as to the side he defended. He came round quietly.

“To be sure, there is something fine in a man of Mr. Hale’s age leaving his home of twenty years, and giving up all settled habits, for an idea which was probably erroneous—but that does not matter—an untangible thought. One cannot help admiring him, with a mixture of pity in one’s admiration, something like what one feels for Don Quixote. Such a gentleman as he was, too! I shall never forget the refined and simple hospitality he showed to me that last day at Helstone.”

Mr. Bell was only half mollified. Yet he felt, in order to lull certain uneasy qualms of his own, a temptation to accept even the faintest hint of Quixotism in Hale’s conduct—anything that would make it less a stern, accusing integrity. He growled out:

“Aye! And you don’t know Milton. Such a change from Helstone! It is years since I have been at Helstone—but I’ll answer for it, it is standing there yet—every stick and every stone as it has done for the last century; while Milton! I go there every four or five years—and I was born there—yet I do assure you, I often lose my way—aye, among the very piles of warehouses that are built upon my father’s orchard.”

The London street around them rang and rasped, a restless iron music, and Mr. Bell thought of Milton’s deeper roar—how a town’s sound could swallow up a man’s memories until he needed landmarks for his own childhood.

“Do we part here? Well, good-night, sir; I suppose we shall meet in Harley Street to-morrow morning.”
