"And I cannot touch her face,
And I cannot touch her hair,
And I kneel to empty shadows—
Just memories of her grace;
And her voice sings in the winds,
And in the sobs of dawn,
And among the flowers at night,
And from the brooks at sunrise,
And from the sea at sunset,
And I answer with vain callings..."

It was the joy of the sunset that brought us to speech. I had wandered far from my home, walking alone, pausing often to watch the battlements of evening rise like ancient fortresses against the sky, and to feel the quiet hush of dusk settle over the world.

The last time I stopped, I was lost in a deep reverence for the coming night—so much so that I let out a soft laugh, standing there in solitude beneath the dimming light. And then, from the trees lining the road to my right, came an answer: *"And thou also!"*—a voice full of understanding, as if some hidden spirit had mirrored my own joy. I laughed again, half-doubting it was real, wondering if it were only a dream or a phantom tuned to my mood.

But then she spoke—clearly, directly—calling me by name. When I stepped to the side of the road to see her, I recognized her instantly: Lady Mirdath the Beautiful, famed across Kent for her grace and elegance, and neighbor to me, since her guardian’s lands adjoined mine. Yet until that moment, we had never met. I had been away often, absorbed in study and training, and though rumors spoke of her, I had known little more than her name—and even that, only in passing.

Now, I stood with my hat in hand, bowing as best I could, while peering through the twilight at this radiant figure. Rumor had not done justice to her beauty. She stood before me, laughing lightly, claiming kinship as cousin—a truth now revealed, once I remembered our shared bloodline. She called me by my boyhood name, and welcomed me to call her simply Mirdath, no title, no formality—just as she was.

She invited me through a gap in the hedge, a secret path she used when dressed as a village maid for country revels. I passed through, and found myself beside her. She seemed tall, but I towered over her—by a head and more. She asked me to walk with her to the Hall, to meet her guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles, and to apologize for my long neglect in visiting. Her eyes sparkled with mischief as she teased me gently for my absence.

But then she grew still. She raised a finger, signaling silence—something moved in the woods. I heard it too: rustling leaves, then the sharp crack of a twig snapping in the quiet.

Three men burst from the trees, rushing toward us. I shouted a warning, stepping between them and Mirdath, shielding her with one arm while gripping my oak staff with the other. They made no sound, just charged forward. I saw the glint of knives. Without hesitation, I struck—swift and sure. My staff drove into the first man’s chest; he fell like a stone. I swung hard at the second’s head, cracking his skull. The third I met with a fist—no need for a second blow. He crumpled to the ground.

Before the fight had truly begun, it was over. I smiled faintly, proud and unshaken, knowing the look of astonishment on Mirdath’s face as she watched me in the fading light.

Then—three great boar-hounds burst from the woods, summoned by a silver whistle she blew. She struggled to hold them back, and I turned to drive them off the fallen men, lest they tear them apart. Soon after, shouts echoed, lanterns flared, and footmen arrived with cudgels and torches. They hesitated—unsure whether to attack or defend me. But when they saw the men down and recognized my name, they held their distance. Still, Mirdath showed no fear—only a deeper warmth, a new closeness.

The servants asked what should be done with the thieves. I left it to them, giving them silver. Justice was swift—I heard their cries long after we left.

At the Hall, Mirdath led me to her guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles—an old man of noble bearing whom I knew slightly. She praised me warmly, teasingly, and he thanked me with honor and grace. From that moment, I was welcomed as a friend.

That evening, I dined with them, then walked again with Mirdath through the gardens. She was more open than any woman I’d ever known—gentle, familiar, as though we had always belonged together. I felt the same—like we shared a silent language, a hidden history. We discovered common dreams, shared fancies, and found delight in each other’s thoughts.

One thing especially captivated her: my strength. After the fight, she asked plainly if I were truly strong. I laughed, proud and young. Then she took my arm, testing it—her fingers tightening, her breath catching as she felt the power beneath my skin. She released me suddenly, gasping in surprise. From then on, she walked beside me quietly, thoughtful, never straying far.

And I, in turn, marveled at her beauty—especially under candlelight, where every feature shone with quiet fire.

In the days that followed, we found deeper joys. She loved the mystery of twilight, the magic of night, the tender glow of dawn. One evening, as we strolled through the park, she murmured—half to herself—that it was an *elves-night*. She stopped herself, as if afraid I wouldn’t understand. But I did. I replied calmly, “The Towers of Sleep will rise tonight. I feel it in my bones—the night for the Giant’s Tomb, or the Tree with the Painted Head…” I paused. She gripped my arm—her hand trembling. “Say on,” she whispered, breathless. “Say on.”

I told her I meant the Moon Garden—an old dream of mine. At that, she gasped, pulled me to a halt, and questioned me fiercely. I answered just as earnestly. For the first time, I sensed something profound: she knew these places too. She confessed she believed she was the only one who dreamed of them. Now, to find someone else who had journeyed through those same dreamlands—this was wonder beyond words.

She admitted she had felt drawn to me that night, seeing me pause on the road. Though she’d learned of our kinship before—having seen me ride past often—she had grown annoyed that I paid no heed to *Lady Mirdath the Beautiful*. But I had been lost in thought, unaware of her presence. If only I had met her sooner…

Still, I was stirred—not by pride, but by awe. Our dreams overlapped, yet not entirely. Some things I knew she didn’t; others she knew I couldn’t grasp. Yet again and again, one would speak of a place or feeling, and the other would finish the tale—filling in the gaps with perfect understanding. In that way, hour by hour, we aged together in friendship, growing closer with every word.

Time slipped away unnoticed—until sudden shouts and barking dogs broke the silence. Lanterns flickered in the dark. I realized we had wandered so long, so deeply in conversation, that her guardian, uneasy after the attack, had sent men to search. We laughed—she with a soft, strange amusement—then turned homeward.

The dogs found us first, leaping around me, friendly now. The men returned to tell Sir Jarles all was well.

That was how we met. That was how our bond began—and how my love for Mirdath the Beautiful was born.

From then on, every evening I walked the quiet road from my estate to hers. Always, I entered through the hedge-gap. Often, I found Mirdath wandering in the woods—always with her boar-hounds, as I had begged for her safety. She seemed to enjoy pleasing me, yet often defied me, testing how far she could go before I turned away.

Once, approaching the gap, I saw two country maids emerge from the woods. I barely glanced at them—until one curtseyed with such unnatural grace. I paused. The taller one… could it be her?

I stepped forward, curious. When I asked her name, she simpered and bowed again. I wasn’t sure—but I followed.

They moved quickly, as if wary of me. They reached the village green, where a torchlit dance was underway, music drifting on the air, ale flowing freely. The two joined the dance, dancing only with each other, avoiding the flames.

Now I was certain: it was Mirdath, disguised. I waited until they danced near me, then stepped forward boldly. “A dance?” I asked.

She smiled coyly. “Promised.” And immediately, she took the hand of a large, clumsy farmer and twirled him about. Poor girl—she nearly tripped under his heavy steps. I watched, amused and irritated.

When the dance ended, I approached. “Mirdath,” I whispered, naming her.

She turned away, stamped her foot, and returned to the farmer. He escorted her partway. His companion followed. But as they left the torchlight, the rough lads grabbed the women’s waists, uncaring of who they touched.

Mirdath could endure no more. She screamed in terror, striking the man who held her—hard enough to make him recoil, swearing. But he came back, grabbing her again, forcing a kiss. She fought wildly, beating his face with her hands. Then—she cried out my name.

I lunged forward, seized the man, and struck him once—hard enough to leave a memory, but not harm. I threw him aside. The second fled, hearing my name. Word of my strength had spread.

I caught Mirdath by the shoulders, shook her sharply in anger. Then I sent her maid onward—she had no orders to stay. We walked in silence to the gap. She walked close, almost as if she enjoyed being near me.

I led her through, to the Hall. She bid me goodnight at a side door, holding the key. Her voice was soft, quiet—almost reluctant to leave.

But the next day, she mocked me playfully. So, when dusk came, I confronted her alone. “Why do you always defy me?” I asked. “I ache to be near you. Instead, you deny me.”

Her tone softened. She understood. She brought out her harp and played old songs—melodies from our childhood. That night, she walked me to the gap, her dogs trailing behind. I followed her silently, watching until she was safely inside. One dog ran back to me, nosing me affectionately. I sent it away—she never knew I was there.

And as she walked home, singing softly, I wondered: did she love me? Or was it only kindness?

The next evening, I arrived early. There stood Mirdath—talking to a man in fine court clothes. He blocked the gap, staring at me with insolent eyes. I reached out, lifted him aside.

Mirdath turned on me—bitter, cutting. She called me crude, uncouth, brutal—before this stranger. I was stunned. A true lover would never shame her true cousin so.

There was truth in her words—but the man could have shown more respect. And she had no right to humiliate me, her friend, before a stranger. I bowed low to her, then offered a small apology to the man. I had been better served showing more courtesy.

I turned and walked away.

Twenty miles I traveled before reaching home. That night, I was consumed—deadly in love, heartbroken. For weeks, I walked elsewhere. But at the end of the week, I returned to the old path—hoping only to glimpse her.

And I did.

She stood near the edge of the wood, arm linked with the courtier. I saw it clearly: lovers. She had no brothers, no suitors—only him.

When she saw me, she pulled away, flushed, bowed stiffly. I bowed in return—still young, still respectful—and walked on. My heart was dead.

As I passed, I saw her lover return to her, arm around her again. They watched me go—stiff, desperate. I did not look back.

For a month, I stayed away. Love burned in me, pride wounded. No justice had been done. But slowly, pain shaped me. Love and sorrow carved a gentler man from the one I had been.

At the end of that time, I began walking past the gap again—though I never saw her. Once, one of her dogs came out of the woods, nosed at me, then vanished. I waited. But she did not come.

Two weeks passed—lonely, aching. I decided: I would go through the gap, reach the gardens, and perhaps see her.

That evening, I went. Through the gap. Into the gardens. And there—lanterns, torches, laughter. A feast. People danced in colorful dress. I feared it was her wedding. But no—she had turned twenty-one. This was her coming-of-age celebration.

The scene was bright and lively—yet I felt only loneliness. The lawn was lit with lamps, arbors woven from leaves, tables laden with food and silver. The dance never ceased.

Then—Mirdath stepped from the crowd, lovely in her gown, yet pale beneath the lights. She sat. Instantly, a dozen young noblemen gathered around her, jesting, vying for her attention. She was radiant—but distant. Her gaze drifted beyond them. I understood: her lover was not there. Perhaps recalled to court.

I watched the men—burning with jealousy. I could have torn her from them, dragged her into the woods as we once did. But it would mean nothing. For I saw plainly: her heart belonged to the small man of the Court.

And so I stood in the shadows, a ghost among the light, watching the woman I loved—knowing she was lost to me.

Since Mirdath, my beloved, passed from this world and left me alone, I have known a sorrow so deep, so endless, that no words can hold it. I who once had all life in her love—whose laughter lit the days, whose presence made the world whole—now wander through a silence that chokes the soul. The pain of absence has become a constant shadow, a weight upon my chest, a fire beneath my bones.

Yet I write again—not for fame, nor for record, but because something within me stirs. A strange hope has risen, born not in waking thought, but in dreams that were not dreams at all. For in sleep, I have awakened into the future—into a time long after the sun has died, when the earth is cloaked in eternal night. And there, I have seen wonders beyond imagining: landscapes of fire and shadow, ancient watchers that stand like gods, and places where time itself seems to fold back upon itself.

In those visions, I have glimpsed her again—Mirdath—not as memory, but as if she lived still, somewhere across the dark ages. We meet in dreams, only to part; then reunite after centuries, each reunion more terrible than the last, yet each one filled with a joy that burns brighter than any star. And so I must tell what I have seen, even if the tale breaks my heart to speak it. Perhaps, by writing it down, I may ease my own burden—and perhaps, in some distant way, offer comfort to another who walks alone in grief.

Some will call this madness. Some will say it is fantasy. But let them read. Let them see what I have seen. For in these words lies not just a story—but a doorway into eternity.

I do not dream when I enter that future. I *wake*. I wake into a world without light, where the sky is black as ink, and the stars are gone. The sun is dead. Time has ended. And yet, in that darkness, I am not lost. I know everything. Not as knowledge learned, but as truth felt in the marrow of my being.

When I first opened my eyes in that far future, I stood on a high ledge, gazing out over a land of endless night. Around me was a blur of grey—like mist or smoke—until it lifted, revealing the vastness below. And then I saw: a world lit not by suns, but by fires of strange color—red, blue, silver—burning in pits and valleys, glowing from unseen sources. There were shapes in the dark: towering monstrosities, silent roads winding through emptiness, and great beings that moved without sound.

But I knew this place. Not as a traveler might know a city, but as a man knows his home. This was the Last Redoubt—the final refuge of humanity, a pyramid of cold metal rising seven miles into the night sky. And I was not merely an observer. I was a boy of seventeen, standing in one of its high embrasures, looking out through a glass that showed the world in sharp, terrifying detail.

And though I was young, I carried the memories of a lifetime—of living in this place, of studying in its halls, of watching the horrors of the Night Land from its walls. Yet beneath that memory, deeper still, was a ghost—a half-remembered past where the sun shone, where trees grew green, and where a woman named Mirdath sang to me under a sky full of light. That life felt like a dream now—yet it was real. And I, in this future, remembered it with a longing so fierce it brought tears to my eyes.

To the north-west, I saw the Red Pit, blazing with fire that rose against the chin of the North-West Watcher—a colossal shape, immovable and ancient, said to have watched since time began. Its name echoed in my mind: *"That which hath watched from the beginning, until the opening of the Gateway of Eternity."* The words belonged to Aesworpth, a poet from that age, though he lived far beyond our own. And as I looked, I realized—I could remember the sunlight of my present life. I could feel the warmth of a summer day, hear the birdsong, see the golden glow of dawn. And in that moment, I understood: I was both men. The youth in the Redoubt *was* me. And I, here now, was him. Two lives, one soul, bridged across time.

This knowledge stunned me. For in that future, I had no memory of this world—until the moment I woke. Then, like a flood, the past rushed in: the love, the loss, the beauty of a world now vanished. And with it came hunger—not for food, but for her. I ached for Mirdath, not as a memory, but as a need. And so, even in that future, I listened. I waited. I searched the dark for any sign of her return.

Around me stretched the Night Land—a realm of horror and mystery. To the right of the Red Pit lay the Vale of Red Fire, a long river of flame cutting through the black. Beyond it, the Plain of Blue Fire cast a cold, eerie glow. Farther still, on the edge of the unknown, stood a range of low volcanoes, their peaks glowing faintly, lighting up the Black Hills where seven lights burned steady—neither flickering nor fading. No one had ever reached them. No explorer returned from that direction. They were called the Seven Lights, and they were said to be older than the Earth itself.

Below, in the Great Library of the Redoubt, countless books recorded the journeys of those who dared leave the safety of the Pyramid. Most never came back. Those who did spoke of things too terrible to describe—beings that walked without sound, lands where the air hummed with unseen power, and creatures that fed not on flesh, but on fear.

And among the most feared of all was the House of Silence—perched on a hill far to the north. It had lights, yes, but no sound. Never a whisper, never a breath. And around it wound the Road Where the Silent Ones Walk—a path that emerged from the Unknown Lands, passing near the Place of the Ab-humans, where a green mist always hovered. No one knew where it led. But it was believed to be the only road built by human hands in that age—constructed long before the fall, when men still worked together, not in terror, but in purpose.

I stepped away from the embrasure and walked along the central roadway of the One Thousandth Plateau—six miles above the Night Land, wide enough for a hundred men to walk side by side. At the southern wall, I looked toward the Three Silver-fire Holes, glowing before the Thing That Nods, a monstrous form in the south-east. Near it loomed the South-East Watcher—an immense, squat creature that seemed carved from stone and shadow. Torches flared beside it, casting light on its lumbering head, yet it never stirred.

To the east, I heard a sound—low, deep, like thunder echoing through caverns. A laugh. Not human. Not animal. Something ancient and terrible. We called it “The Country Whence Comes The Great Laughter,” though none had ever seen it. The sound sent shivers through my spine, reminding me how small we were—how fragile our survival.

Then I turned my gaze to the Giants’ Pit, south of the Kilns. The giants—huge, slow-moving figures—climbed from the depths, their forms blurred by dancing shadows. And beyond them, a black headland jutted between the Valley of the Hounds and the giants. From its edge, strange things peered out—shapes that darted forward into the light, then vanished back into darkness. We called it The Headland From Which Strange Things Peer. And it had been that way for ages.

I followed the Road Where the Silent Ones Walk. And there, alone in the vast grey expanse, I saw one—a cloaked figure, moving slowly, silently, eyes fixed ahead. It did not turn. It did not look. It simply walked. And I knew, as I had been told, that these beings were harmless—if you stayed far away. But no one approached them. Their silence was not peaceful. It was profound. Like the quiet before a storm.

The road curved southward, passing the Dark Palace, then bending west around the mountain bulk of the Watcher of the South—the largest monster in sight, a living hill of darkness brooding over the Glowing Dome. It had come from the South Unknown Lands a million years ago, growing slowly, relentlessly, until it reached the Dome—a pale, radiant sphere that rose from the ground before it. And there, it stopped. As if something held it back. Some said it was magic. Others, that it was fate. But I believe there are forces in the Night Land not of evil, but of balance—powers that oppose the monsters, though they act in ways we cannot understand.

There were men in that age called Monstruwacans—watchers, scholars, keepers of records. Their duty was to observe the beasts, measure their movements, record every shift in the dark. And they had done so for millions of years. They tracked the Watcher’s approach, noting its slow creep toward the Redoubt. It had moved barely an inch per century—yet over millennia, it had crossed continents.

And so the Redoubt stood—not just as a fortress, but as a beacon. A last island of life in a sea of death. It had been built when the sun was fading, when the world was turning cold. The builders—millions strong—had fled into its towers, sealing themselves behind walls of iron and steel. It had a thousand three hundred and twenty floors, each thickened to withstand the horrors outside. Above, a tower housed the watchmen. Below, the lower half-mile was sealed—no one could enter or exit.

For a time, peace reigned. The electric circle, powered by the Earth-Current, surrounded the pyramid, repelling the creatures. The outer lands teemed with fallen races—half-human, half-beast—some of whom attacked, only to be slaughtered in droves. But eventually, the war ceased. The Redoubt became a city of silence, of study, of waiting.

Yet the darkness grew stronger. New monsters emerged—not from the earth, but from dimensions beyond. Drawn to the last spark of humanity, they gathered like sharks around a dying ship. And the Night Land became not just a place, but a living nightmare.

All of this—the history, the horrors, the loneliness—I carry in my soul. And I write it not to impress, but to bear witness. For in that future, I was not just a watcher. I was a man who loved. Who mourned. Who dreamed of reunion. And in those dreams, I found a glimmer of hope—not that the world would end, but that love, even across time, may endure.

And so I continue. Not knowing if anyone will read. Not knowing if this will matter. But I must tell it. Because in the silence of the Night Land, even the smallest voice can echo through eternity.

For many years, I had heard whispers—stories carried on the breath of memory and passed from mouth to mouth in the great cities that climbed the thousandth floor of the Pyramid. Across the one thousand, three hundred and twenty tiers of our Last Redoubt, there was a tale that would not die: somewhere beyond the endless night, far out in the desolation of the Night Lands, another refuge existed—a second stronghold where the last remnants of humanity had gathered, fighting against oblivion.

I heard this story everywhere. From the time I turned seventeen, I journeyed through each city for one day, as was the custom of every youth’s training. Three years and two hundred twenty-five days I spent in that pilgrimage—each city a brief pause in a life too short to hold more than fleeting moments of connection. I met many souls along the way—some whose names I still carry like sacred embers in my heart—but none I could keep. Life is measured not by years but by duty, and each of us must return to the work of preserving the Redoubt. Still, even now, I remember their faces, their laughter, the warmth of their hands—though they are gone.

And always, wherever I went, the same legend echoed: *There is another place*. In the libraries of those cities, I found countless writings—ancient records, fragmented histories, fevered speculations—all speaking of a second Redoubt. Once, long ago, these texts were accepted without doubt. But time has worn faith thin. Now, only scholars read them, their eyes skeptical even as they turn the pages. Such is the fate of truth when it lives too long in silence.

Yet I never doubted. Not after the first time I heard the story from Master Monstruwacan himself, who stood atop the Tower of Observation, his mind a beacon among the stars of knowledge. He and I had always been bound by something deeper than mere rank or age. Though he was full-grown and I but a boy, our bond was unbroken. When I reached twenty-one, he granted me a post within the Tower—the highest honor in all the Redoubt. For here, amid the instruments and the silent hum of the aether, we strained to listen to the world beyond the dark, seeking answers no machine could give.

And there was reason for my appointment. I possessed a gift rare among millions: Night-Hearing. It was not a name born of science, but of myth—yet it described what I could do. I could perceive the invisible vibrations of the aether, the faintest echoes of thought sent across the void. Without instruments, I could hear messages that danced through the eternal night—messages clearer, purer, than any device could catch.

And so, over the years, I listened—not just for signals, but for *her*. A voice from before time, a melody woven into the fabric of my soul. Mirdath—beautiful, lost, remembered. She slept within me, whispering in dreams that felt more real than waking. Her voice came not from memory alone, but from longing, from love that had survived the death of the sun and the fall of worlds.

Then, one evening at the fifteenth hour—when Sleep-Time began—I stood alone in the observation chamber, lost in thought. I pondered her, that woman who had lived in an age before light, whose face I could not see but whose voice rang in my bones. And as I dreamed, I fancied she spoke to me—softly, gently, as if from the edge of eternity.

And then—*it came*.

A whisper, trembling through the darkness, brushing against my inner hearing like a breath upon skin. It was not sound, but sensation—an echo from a world buried beneath aeons of silence. I knew it instantly. The voice, the rhythm, the sorrow behind it—it was hers.

But I did not speak. Not yet. I was trained in wisdom, and I knew the danger of false calls. The Evil Forces, the monstrous things that dwelled beyond the walls of the Redoubt, sometimes mimicked voices. Or worse—the House of Silence, that unseen power said to twist minds and steal souls. Only one thing could prove it was truly her: the Master-Word.

So I sent it—not with lips, but with my mind, with every fiber of my being, driving it into the night.

And then—silence.

Then, a throb. A slow, steady pulse vibrating through the aether, echoing around me like a heartbeat in the void. The Master-Word. Answered.

I trembled. My spirit soared. With all the joy I could summon, I called again, not with words, but with thought: *"Mirdath! Mirdath! Mirdath!"*

At that moment, the door opened. Master Monstruwacan stepped into the chamber, his face pale, his eyes wide. He had not heard the call—he lacked Night-Hearing—but he had sensed the disturbance. He had come from the receiving instrument, thinking he’d caught a flicker of the Master-Word—too faint to register, but strong enough to make him search for me.

He saw my face—my eyes alight, my body rigid with awe—and he said nothing. Instead, he waited, listening, as I told him everything: the dream, the memory, the awakening, the call.

He listened. And though he could not hear the aether, he understood. In that age, men spoke of things now deemed madness. They believed in spirits, in dreams that shaped reality, in voices that crossed time. Our ancestors had known such things. We, today, have forgotten. But he—wise, gentle, open—believed.

As I spoke, I kept listening. And then—faint, sweet, almost playful—a laugh. Like wind through flowers. Then nothing.

No more that day.

Yet the memory stayed. And I found myself arguing with scholars, disputing their doubts about the old stories—the Sun, the Days of Light, the world before the Night. They scoffed, calling my tales fairy fables. But I remembered. I *knew*. And I told them anyway.

Master Monstruwacan always listened. Even when I spoke for hours, drawing from the deep well of my Memory-Dreams, he sat in silence, absorbed. Sometimes, when I returned to the present, I found the entire Tower gathered around me—Monstruwacans, assistants, observers—all captivated, forgetting their duties. He would snap back into awareness, chide me softly, and send them away—only to find them later, still whispering, still wondering.

Even beyond the Tower, my words spread. Downward, through the thousand cities, until I reached the Underground Fields—hundred miles beneath the surface, where the ploughboys worked the soil under artificial light. There, I once found a group of them gathered, talking about "the man who remembers the sun."

And indeed, those Fields were a marvel. A pyramid of land, stacked one beneath the other—three hundred and six levels, each smaller than the one below, until the topmost field, four miles square, lay beneath the lowest floor of the Redoubt. Each level was encased in the grey metal of the fortress, pillared, floored, protected. No monster could dig in.

The Earth-Current powered it all—light, heat, life. It ran through the soil, feeding the crops, making the poppies bloom, the corn rustle in the breeze. It had taken a million years to build. The waste from its creation had been dumped into the Crack—the source of the Current—where it vanished into depths no probe could reach.

And in that underground world, air flowed, winds stirred, and people walked, loved, laughed—just as they might in any garden of the old days. I had seen it. And sometimes, in the quiet corners, I thought I heard her voice again—faint, distant, like a sigh on the edge of sleep.

But there was a law: no man could venture into the Night Lands before twenty-two. No woman ever. Those who wished to go must endure three lectures on the horrors of the dark—on the mutilations, the monstrosities, the fates of those who had tried. Only if they remained sane and resolute could they be allowed.

And to each, a capsule was implanted beneath the skin of the left forearm. If trapped, they were to bite it open—so that their spirit might escape in death, preserved by the sanctity of their soul.

This was the price of courage.

And I? I thought of it often. For always, I listened. Twice, I sent the Master-Word into the night—solemnly, carefully. But no more. The Word must not be wasted. Yet I called her name—"Mirdath!"—with my mind, sending it into the dark. Sometimes, I thought I felt a reply—weak, fragile, as if from a dying star.

Then, one afternoon at the thirteenth hour, I stood beside the instruments. Suddenly, the aether thrummed. Not a single note—but a vibration, a ripple, spreading through the void. I made the Sign for Silence. All movement ceased. The men bowed over their breathing-bells, still as stone.

Then—again. A soft thrill. And then, clear and low, a voice in my mind:

My name. The old-earth name. Not the name of this age.

It struck me like lightning. Memories flooded—faces, scenes, a sunlit sky, a hand holding mine. I sent the Master-Word again.

And then—silence.

Then, far off, a beat. Faint, but unmistakable. And then—another throb. Stronger. Closer.

Only I could hear it.

And then—full clarity. A voice, young, clear, trembling:

I turned to the instruments. "Mirdath," I said aloud.

And from the darkness, a reply—swift, tender, perfect:

Not Mirdath. Naani.

And then came the truth. She had read a book. An ancient record. Of a man named Alec, who loved a girl named Mirdath. When she first called, she heard a voice cry *"Mirdath!"*—and it reminded her of the story. So she answered, as if she were the one from the tale.

The romance—my lifelong dream—shattered.

Yet, even in sorrow, I felt a spark. She said something strange: *"Your voice... it should be deeper."*

I froze. In my time, my voice had been deep. But now—after centuries of change—was it different? Could it be that the man in the book was not me? Or was it possible... that I was the man? That someone else had written of me?

I asked her. She hesitated. Said no. But I pressed. And in her confusion, I saw something else: *She didn’t know.* She didn’t know the truth. She only knew the story.

And in that moment, I realized: perhaps the story wasn’t fiction. Perhaps it was memory. Perhaps the past was not dead.

And then, she told me of her world.

Far out in the Night Lands, a second Redoubt stood—a small, three-sided pyramid, barely a mile high. Built long ago, on the shore of a sea that no longer existed. Its founders had fled the chaos, the monsters, the half-human tribes. One man—once exiled from the Great Redoubt for rebellion—had designed it. He had seen our tower, once, and now built a smaller version, aided by four million weary wanderers.

They found the Earth-Current in a valley leading to the sea. They dug a shaft—ten years of labor—and tapped into it. Enough to sustain them.

They built. They guarded. They created instruments. And for ages, they spoke with us—daily, through the aether.

But then—the Current failed. Slowly, steadily. The instruments died. The messages stopped.

And for a million years—maybe more—there was silence.

The people dwindled. Generations passed. The Current weakened. The world grew cold.

Until—recently—the Current surged again. Youth lasted longer. Children were born anew. Hope returned.

And then—Naani, daughter of their Master Monstruwacan, became Sensitive. She heard vibrations. She spoke of them.

They rebuilt the instruments. And now—she had called.

And now—she was speaking to me.

And though she was not Mirdath, and though I was not the man of the book—something deeper had happened.

The silence had broken.

And the Night Land had begun to speak.

("Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night—
Treading, as moon-flakes step across a dark—
Kissing the very dew to holier light ...
Thy Voice a song past mountains, which to hark
Frightens my soul with an utter lost delight.")

One night, near the end of the sixteenth hour, as I prepared for sleep, the aether stirred around me—its familiar hum now charged with something deeper, more urgent. And within that thrumming silence, I heard her voice: Naani’s, clear and close, echoing not through instruments but deep in my mind.

Yet I did not answer at once. Instead, I sent the Master-Word into the darkness—a signal only we who knew the ancient codes could recognize. Instantly, it returned, pulsing like a heartbeat across the void. Then I questioned her: why speak now, when all slept? The Monstruwacans in the Lesser Redoubt were meant to rest from the eleventh hour onward. It was already five hours past their bedtime. She should have been asleep—safe within the pyramid, beside her father. I assumed she used the Instrument, her voice resonating so vividly in my thoughts. But when I asked, she gave no reply. Instead, she placed words directly into my spirit:

"Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night—"

And in that moment, I trembled. For those words—those exact words—I had once spoken to Mirdath, the Beautiful, long before this Age, when she died and left me alone beneath the endless sky. A memory surged, sharp and unbearable: the grief, the love, the ache of losing her forever. My heart wrenched. I called out to Naani, demanding an explanation—why these words? Why now?

Again, no direct answer. Only the same line repeated, drifting through the dark between us. And then it struck me: it was not Naani speaking. Not entirely. It was Mirdath—the ghost of her voice rising from the depths of time, carried by Naani’s spirit. I cried out, “Mirdath! Mirdath!” into the night, pouring my soul into the call. And lo! the voice came again—faint, distant, yet unmistakable. The same words. The same tone. The same sorrow and beauty.

It was Mirdath. Yet it was also Naani. And I knew, without doubt, that this was no illusion. That somewhere across the vastness of eternity, the spirit of Mirdath had been reborn—not in flesh, but in soul—within the body of Naani. That I, who had loved her across ages, had been given one last chance to meet her again, not as memory, but as living breath.

I called to Naani again and again, pleading, questioning—but there was no response. No sign she had heard. Hours passed. I grew exhausted. My mind reeled. I fell into sleep.

When I woke, the dream remained fresh—the memory of those words, impossible to forget. No one alive could have known them. Only Mirdath, or someone who had lived them. And yet here they were, whispered through the dark, carried on the wind of a forgotten love.

I reached out to Naani immediately. Once. Twice. And soon, the Master-Word pulsed back, steady and strong. I sent it in return, and then—her voice came, weak but clear, delivered not by machine, but by the raw power of her mind. I asked her about the words, the dreams, the strange connection.

She denied knowing anything. She claimed to have slept deeply, dreamlessly. But then, she confessed to a dream—strange, vivid, haunting. In it, she saw a tall man, broad and sorrowful, dressed in clothes unlike any she had seen. He sat in a small room, writing—trying to ease his pain through words. Though the language was unknown to her waking self, she read it in her dream. And she remembered only one line—written above all others: *Mirdath*.

I asked her to repeat what she recalled of the writing. Her voice trembled as it came back to me:

"Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night—"

No more. But it was enough. With a wild, trembling certainty, I completed the line in my mind, sending it forth into the darkness. And as I did, I felt it strike her spirit like a blow—awakening memory, flooding her with shock and wonder. She stumbled, silent for a moment, then wept in her mind. Tears of recognition, of awe, of terror and joy mingled together.

And suddenly, her voice changed. It was no longer just Naani’s. It was Mirdath’s—echoing through time, through death, through rebirth. She asked me, trembling, whether she might truly be Mirdath.

I could not speak. My blood pounded. My soul shivered. The truth was too great, too beautiful, too terrifying. And yet, in that moment, our spirits met—not through machines or magic, but through love, through memory, through the unbroken thread of soul across time.

Imagine, if you will, Naani standing in that far future, her consciousness reaching back through the veil of existence, seeing not just the life we shared in this age, but others—older, dimmer, half-forgotten. She saw echoes of our union in past lives, glimpses of loves that had never been named. And though I could not grasp all of it, I understood enough: this was no coincidence. This was destiny. This was reunion.

And now, I burned with the need to be with her—to see her, to touch her, to know if she truly was the one I had loved across all eternity.

But how?

The Lesser Redoubt lay somewhere beyond the horizon, hidden in the Night Land. None among the scholars of the Mighty Pyramid knew its location. Records offered only guesses: somewhere between North-West and North-East. But no distance, no bearing. Only speculation.

And even if we knew where it was, the journey would be suicide. The Outer Lands—known as the Unknown Lands—were filled with horrors, with creatures born of darkness and decay. The Night Land was not a place for men. It was a tomb.

Still, I spoke with Naani, probing, asking. Her father, the Master Monstruwacan, admitted they had no knowledge of our position either. Only that the Builder of the Lesser Redoubt had come from the Southward World, long ago, as the records said.

They had tried to use the ancient compass. But it spun wildly, drawn by the Earth-Current flowing from the "Crack" beneath our pyramid. The needle danced between North and South, always off-center. It was useless. Or perhaps… not entirely. They speculated that our current might pull the needle toward us. So if we were south, they were north—and vice versa. But it was guesswork. A hope built on sand.

We brought the compass from the Great Museum. We tested it. It spun. It refused to settle. The Earth-Current made it impossible to trust. To us, it seemed absurd—how could such a tool ever have worked? Yet in earlier times, when the Sun still rose, they had used it with the stars, naming directions by sight. Now, we kept the names—North, South, East, West—by tradition alone. We used them because our ancestors did, even though the world was always night.

Naani, sensing my desperation, begged me to stay. She pleaded with me not to risk everything. “Better to live in spirit,” she said, “than die in flesh.” But I could not listen. If only I knew the direction—if only I could measure the distance—then I might go.

And then, something extraordinary happened.

When I sent my thoughts into the night, using my brain-elements, I found myself turning instinctively toward the North. Not because I saw anything, but because my body *knew*. I turned, again and again, even when blindfolded, even when surrounded by screens. The Master Monstruwacan noted it. He experimented. He tested me. And each time, I faced North—drawn by some unseen force.

He wrote a study of it. It was published in the Hour-Slips, copied throughout the cities. People began to talk. Some praised me. Others mocked. But all wondered: what was this power?

And still, Naani had no such sense. She could not feel it. Only I could. And I began to believe—perhaps not in reason, but in feeling—that her spirit pulled me northward. That she was out there, somewhere in the dark, waiting.

Then, disaster.

At the seventeenth hour, during the sleep-time, I was in the Tower of Observation with the Master Monstruwacan. Suddenly, the aether thrummed. Naani’s voice came to me—weak, fading. I sent the Master-Word. It returned. I called to her.

Her voice was thin, distant. She told me the Earth-Current had failed. The Lesser Redoubt was dying. Their power was gone. The instruments were dead. They feared this was the end—the final flicker before darkness consumed them.

As we spoke, her voice grew weaker. Each word was a wound. I felt my heart break. We talked as if we were saying goodbye—lovers facing eternity apart.

When dawn came (though there was no dawn), the news spread. Millions grieved. The cities mourned.

For weeks, Naani’s voice grew fainter. Even my Night-Hearing could barely catch her words. Each syllable became a treasure. I stopped eating. I could not rest. The Master Monstruwacan scolded me. “If you fall ill,” he said, “who will hear them?”

I obeyed him. I ate. I rested. But still, I felt the dread grow. For soon, I learned worse: the monsters were coming. They attacked the Lesser Redoubt. There was a battle. Madness broke out. Some opened the gate and fled into the dark. Their bodies were taken by the beasts. Their souls? Who knows?

All this, they blamed on the failure of the Earth-Current. Without it, they were powerless. Lifeless. Fearful. And yet, strangely, they did not hunger. Did not thirst. Only fear remained—fear of death.

And so, the people of the Great Redoubt began to question their own fate. Was the Crack beneath our pyramid failing too? Were we next? The Hour-Slips filled with warnings, with poems, with plans—none of which could be carried out. Still, I knew one thing: I must go.

Even if I died, I would go. Better to die trying than to live knowing I had not tried.

That night, in the eighteenth hour, the aether erupted. I was awakened by the Master Monstruwacan. “Listen,” he said. “The Master-Word. Is it coming?”

I sat up. And there it was—beating in the night, strong and clear. And then, a cry:
“We are coming! We are coming!”

My heart leapt. Then sank. The message seemed to come from near the Great Pyramid. As if someone—or something—was approaching.

I sent the Master-Word back. No answer. Then, a faint pulse—distant, weak. I recognized it: Naani. I asked her if she was safe, if she was still in the Lesser Redoubt.

A whisper came—so faint it was almost gone. She was there. But she had heard the call too. She had woken in terror. She thought we were coming to rescue them.

I told her not to hope. “Do not build your heart on dreams,” I said. “I cannot save you. But I will try.”

I bid her sleep. Then I turned to the Master Monstruwacan.

“I heard it,” I said. “The Master-Word. But it did not come from the Lesser Redoubt. It came from here. From near us. Sent not by instruments—but by many minds, calling together.”

He listened, pale.

We went to the Tower of Observation. Through the great spy-glass, we saw it: hundreds of figures crossing the Electric Circle. Not toward the pyramid. Away from it. Into the blackness.

We stared at each other. We knew.

People had left the Mighty Pyramid during the sleep-time.

The Master Watchman was summoned. He rushed to the Great Gate. He found the Sleep-Time Warders bound, gagged. Five hundred young men—strong, fierce, from the Upper Cities—had slipped through the Eye-Gate at the top of the Gate and vanished into the night.

The Watchmen had tried to call the central dome—but the instruments were dead. Tampered with.

Rules were rewritten. Tests were imposed. But the damage was done.

And I knew—somewhere in that darkness, among those fleeing men, was the seed of my journey. The beginning of the end.

Or perhaps, the beginning of the beginning.

After the destruction of the Ten-thousand, and with fresh terror seeping into our bones from the depths of the Night Land, it became clear that no rescue could be made. The Youths who now walked the Road Where The Silent Ones Walk were beyond reach—beyond help.

Yet one might wonder: why did we not signal them? Why did we not call out with the Home-Call—the great voice that rose from the Machine atop the sealed base of the Mighty Pyramid? We could not. To send that sound was to announce to the Monsters of the world that life still stirred beyond the walls of the Redoubt. And though we hoped the Evil Forces knew nothing of the Youths’ journey, we could never be sure. For such powers, if they held knowledge, or ignorance, neither could be trusted.

Still, we sensed something strange in the air—an Influence, quiet but undeniable. The Instruments registered it faintly, like a whisper on the edge of hearing. As I have said before, we believed this force came from the House of Silence, far off in the Night Land, perched upon a low hill north of the Great Road. Many among the Monstruwacans feared it reached for the Youths—but we had no proof. So we watched. We waited.

Then, as the hours passed, the Youths drew near to the bend in the Road where it turned sharply northward—closer now to the grim House. And suddenly, the Influence grew stronger. I felt it in my soul, certain it originated from that place. But certainty was not yet ours. Still, I spoke of it to the Master Monstruwacan. He trusted me, and he too sensed a hidden power emanating from the House.

There was talk—brief, desperate talk—of sending the Home-Call across the night, warning the Youths, begging them to return. But the Master refused. Such a cry would risk their lives. It was not right to summon them back unless we were certain they would perish without intervention. For the Home-Call was not merely a voice—it was a thunderclap, echoing over the world, loud enough to tell every Monster in existence that men still walked outside the Redoubt. In ancient times, when flying-ships sailed the skies, the Call had been a beacon of hope. Now, it was a death-knell.

A day and a night passed. Throughout, multitudes pressed to the embrasures, staring into the dark, watching the Youths. They knew of the Influence. They felt the dread gathering. There was much talk—some wise, some foolish, all full of fear. Yet no one dared go forth. No one stepped beyond the wall. And truthfully, this is not surprising. I have written it before, and thought it often.

The land itself seemed awake—restless, watchful. Low roars echoed across the wastes, sounds that sent chills through the spine. If I had not mentioned this earlier, it is my failing. But the task of recording all I have seen is immense. Bear with me, reader, for I strive to speak plainly, even if my words falter.

During those twenty-four hours, we learned the Youths had not slept. They had eaten only once. Those who watched through the Great Spy-Glass confirmed it. They moved forward with relentless speed, driven by some unseen force, racing toward the North along the Dismal Road. Soon, they would collapse—or kill themselves in their desperation.

This confirmed our worst fears: they were under a spell. A curse from the House of Silence. And then, a Monstruwacan arrived at the Master’s side, breathless, reporting a sudden surge in the Influence. At the same moment, I looked through the Great Spy-Glass—and saw them break from the road, running faster than ever, drawn directly toward the House.

The Master hesitated not. He sent the Home-Call into the night—a roar that shook the very foundations of the Pyramid. Then, through the natural eye, he sent a message: *They are being led to destruction by a force from within the House of Silence. Fight for your souls. If you cannot resist, end your lives quickly. Do not enter that place.*

Silence fell over the Pyramid. The bellowing of the Home-Call brought stillness, for all knew what it meant. Millions heard it. Millions prayed. From the heart of the Mighty Pyramid, a counter-force surged—not physical, but spiritual—a wave of will, of love, of sorrow, rising from the prayers of countless souls.

I felt it in my spirit. A vast, trembling energy flooded the aether, a storm of supplication so powerful it stunned me. The night itself seemed to hum with a sacred noise. And through the Great Spy-Glass, I saw the Youths stop. They gathered, confused, as if waking from a dream, realizing they had wandered into a nightmare.

Then came a roar from the embrasures—five hundred thousand eyes watching, and more on the View-Tables. The people shouted, a tide of triumph rising. But it was premature. The counter-force broke. The Evil Force pulled again. The Youths turned. They ran once more.

And in that moment, something new appeared before them—waves of mist, like cold white fire, glowing with an icy light that cast no warmth, no illumination. It formed a barrier between them and the House.

We knew then: a Power of Goodness had intervened. One of the sweet spirits who guard us from the darkness. All saw it—some clearly, others dimly. But none had the Night-Hearing, save a few. I was one.

Soon, a Monstruwacan reported the Influence had vanished from the Instruments. The force from the House was severed. We knew then—something mighty fought for the Youths’ salvation.

The Pyramid held its breath. A hush, thick with awe and dread. Hope flickered.

But the Youths wavered. Then—once more—the Master sent the Home-Call. A final plea: *For the sake of your souls, for the love of your mothers, come home while you can. This power protects you. Return to sanity.*

I thought I saw some turn toward the Pyramid. Perhaps they heard. But then—Aschoff, the leader, turned. A man of strength from the Nine-Hundredth-City. He leapt into the cold fire.

The mist vanished. The barrier dissolved. Aschoff ran forward. The others followed.

They reached the low hill. They climbed swiftly. Two hundred and fifty young men—pure of heart, innocent except for the waywardness of youth.

They came to the great open doorway—*“hath been open since the Beginning.”* Through it poured the cold, unchanging light and the silence so deep it could be *felt*, a silence that spread across the land like a shroud. The windows gave no view, only emptiness.

Aschoff entered. The others followed.

They were never seen again.

Their parents watched from the walls. Their faces reflected the horror of the night. The people remained silent. Some whispered they would return. But all knew—deep in their hearts—that these youths had entered the mouth of destruction.

For me—whose spirit holds the Night-Hearing—there came a terrible dread. Not sound, but a whisper of agony, a scream from the void. But no voice came. Nothing. Only silence—so complete, so absolute, that it drowned all other horrors.

For the first time, I understood how dreadful true silence could be. Once, I would have welcomed the distant thunder of the Great Laughter, or the whine from the Silver-fire Holes, or the bay of the Hounds. But now, that silence—*that* silence—was worse than any roar. It was the silence of annihilation.

And so it was clear: no aid could reach the People of the Lesser Redoubt. We did not know where it stood. We could not go.

And thus, those poor thousands must face their fate alone. A thought that pierced the soul.

But I—my heart burned with another grief. For I had long held a secret hope: that one day, I might walk into the Night Land, find the Lesser Pyramid, and stand before Naani—my lost love—my soul’s twin—and say: *I am That One.* And she, who had been mine in a past eternity, would know me instantly. She would run to me, and we would be together again, as we had been before time began.

Now I knew that dream was dead. And the thought that she might suffer—perhaps even now, in the grip of some monstrous thing—was unbearable. I nearly seized the Diskos and ran into the dark, not to save her, but to die trying.

I called to her often. With my mind, I sent the Master-Word—our secret signal—so she would know it was truly me, not a trick of evil. I warned her: *Never leave the Redoubt. Wait for the Master-Word. No friend would lure you into the night.*

I spoke to her across the darkness, sending thoughts through the aether. But always, there was no reply. Only silence. Or sometimes—a faint tremor, a weak pulse of the Master-Word—just enough to make my heart leap, then sink.

I listened constantly. My body weakened. My spirit strained. I cursed myself for lacking control. But I could not stop.

Day after day, my heart grew heavier. Life seemed trivial against the loss I carried.

And then—voices. Voices from the night, claiming to be Naani. I tested them with the Master-Word. None answered correctly. I did not mock them. I let them fade. But I kept my distance. For to speak with such voices is to invite madness.

And so, the Monsters of the Night toyed with me—luring, tormenting. Perhaps they sought to destroy me. Or perhaps they simply wished to suffer me.

My love consumed me. I grew thin. The Master Monstruwacan—like a father to me—spoke gently, tried to reason with me. I loved him more for it. But my health did not return. My heart was dying, torn apart by love denied.

It was strange that my own parents did not speak to me. But I had no parents. Not for many years. I should have said this sooner. My fault.

One night, I woke from a troubled sleep. And I heard it—Naani’s voice. Calling my old name. Crying out in anguish. I sat up, heart pounding. I sent the Master-Word into the night.

And then—around me—the aether thrummed. The Master-Word beat back—faint, fading, almost gone. I called again. *Naani!* I begged her to tell me what wrong was done to her.

But no voice came. Only silence. And then, a single, weak pulse.

I was broken. I stood, hands raised, and vowed to the blackness: *I will not stay here. While she suffers, I will not live in safety. I will find her. I will die trying.*

I sent the vow through my mind. *Be strong, Naani. I will come.*

But the night answered only with silence.

I dressed quickly. I went to the Tower of Observation. I needed to speak with the Master. My heart burned with purpose.

I told him everything. That I would no longer wait. That I would go into the Night Land—to find Naani, or die.

He listened. His face fell. He pleaded. He said no one could survive such a journey. That I would be lost before the week ended.

But I said nothing. Only that this was my duty. My promise. My fate.

At last, he saw I would not change. He warned me: I was too thin, too weak. I should wait, regain my strength.

But I said: *I am ready. I am lean, yes—but purer. The dross has burned away. Fear does not touch me. This is love. And love makes the soul fearless.*

And so I entered the Room of Preparation. The door closed behind me.

For three days and nights, I underwent the ritual—the cleansing of the spirit. The truths of the Night Land were revealed: horrors no child should hear, mutilations, soul-crushing abasements. These were locked away, guarded by the Master of Preparation.

When I heard them, I wondered how anyone could ever leave the Redoubt. How any student would dare step into the dark.

But I knew the answer: because youth craves adventure. Because the spirit rebels against safety. And though I grieve for violence between men, I do not mourn this hunger. It is part of us. It is eternal.

On the fourth day, my armor was brought. Grey, heavy, forged for the cold. Beneath it, a close-fitting suit to shield me from the night’s chill. I took the scrip—food and drink, specially prepared, stitched with the Mark of Honour. Women had sewn it. Love had shaped it.

And so I stood—ready. Lean, pure, untouched by fear.

For I had prepared not just my body, but my soul. And in that preparation, I found strength.

And now—my journey begins.

I moved forward, my eyes fixed ahead, refusing to look back. My heart was steady, though the weight of what lay before me pressed like a stone—either salvation for the Maid in the dark, or death itself, if fate so willed. I had no choice but to go on.

And so I walked, every step echoing with the memory of parting from the great Pyramid, my home, the last refuge of mankind. The warmth of that place—the care, the wisdom beneath its rigid laws—lingered in my soul. And I carried with me the pride of knowing the Master Monstruwacan had come down in full armor to stand beside the Full Watch, to see me off, to strengthen me in spirit. That honor meant more than words could say.

As I walked, the night air clung to me—cold, sharp, and thick as though it bore more substance than the breath of the plateau where I’d lived. It stung my lips, filled my lungs with a strange clarity. I knew now why those who lived lower down breathed easier; the air here, deep in the Fields, was different—thicker, more alive. Each city had its own atmosphere, shaped by height and distance from the dying sun. Some peoples migrated seeking health, guided by science and decree. But not all were healthy. Some thrived in what others called poison.

And yet, even this small difference—the taste of the air—spoke of a world far greater than we ever dreamed. Beneath the silence of the Night Land, there was life, movement, hidden currents in the ether. I felt them—like whispers carried on the wind—echoes of the millions I left behind, their thoughts and hopes swirling around me like unseen hands.

For a time, I let myself be lost in memory. The vastness of the Pyramid, the watchful towers, the glassed embrasures through which we once peered at the monstrous shapes beyond—those images flooded back. As a child, we’d take turns watching, hoping to be the first to spot a Beast-Monster leaning over the glowing Circle, its face half-lost in shadow. We’d cheer when one appeared, then shiver as it vanished into the darkness, repelled by the light.

Now, standing just beyond the Circle, I saw it again—smaller than I expected. A thin tube of light, barely two inches wide, yet blazing bright enough to seem enormous from afar. It was the barrier between us and the outer world. And now I stepped across it.

I turned back once, only once, to look upon the Pyramid. And what I saw took my breath away.

It rose like a mountain forged from steel and fire, stretching up into the endless night. Its sides were covered in rows upon rows of embrasures—three hundred thousand on the nearest slope, twelve hundred thousand in total. Lights flickered from each, climbing upward in a spiral of brilliance until they blurred into a single, shimmering peak that pierced the heavens. From below, the people looked like insects clustered against the glow. I knew they watched me—through their spy-glasses, their hearts beating with fear and hope.

And then I saw it—the tiny star at the very top, the Observation Tower. That was where the Master Monstrudes had stood, where I had spent my days scanning the void. He was there now, looking down. I raised my Diskos—a salute, a farewell—though I could not see him.

A murmur rose from the lower tiers. A wave of voices, waves of hands. They were sending me off. Calling for me to return. Or perhaps, only wishing me well.

But I could not linger. I lowered the Diskos, reversed it—symbol of respect from a young man to the millions—and stepped fully into the Night Land.

The air resisted me, thick and heavy, like swimming through cold syrup. I pushed forward, not looking back. To gaze upon the Pyramid again would break my resolve.

Yet even as I walked, I felt the presence of those I’d left behind—not just in memory, but in the ether. Their thoughts clung to me, prayers, wishes, silent vigilance. It was comforting, almost like companionship. But then came the fear: such a disturbance might draw attention. Might alert something ancient and terrible that lurked in the dark.

Could I stop it? No. Even if I had warned them, they could not have ceased thinking. The mind of a million is a storm—one that cannot be silenced.

At first, I walked blindly, driven only by the need to put distance between myself and the Pyramid. But soon, I slowed. I began to think.

I could not follow the path of the Youths—they were watched, hunted. So I changed course. Instead of heading due north, I veered northwest, planning to circle around the North-West Watcher, then cut toward the Plain of Blue Fire. From there, I would aim true north, avoiding the House of Silence—the place that chilled me more than any beast.

It was longer, yes. But better to walk slowly and live, than rush toward death.

Why did I choose north? Not just instinct. I had studied. In the Great Library, I found an old metal book, forgotten for millennia. Its pages spoke of a time long before the Sun dimmed—a cataclysmic earthquake that split the world open, swallowing oceans, creating a chasm so deep no eye could see its bottom. Steam burst upward, shaking the continents. Rain fell for days. The sky turned red.

This was not myth. Ancient records confirmed it. But this book—this one—was different. It didn’t speak in legends. It quoted witnesses. It described the aftermath: a valley formed by the rift, stretching a thousand miles, with walls a hundred miles high. At its base, seas and forests, mountains and fires, beasts both ancient and new.

And in that valley, a people emerged—hardened, fierce, born of necessity. They called themselves the Road Makers. They climbed down the cliffs, generation after generation, building paths, cities, forging a way through the darkness. They fought monsters, mastered the heat, the steam, the poisons. They built roads from city to city, winding through the Valley of Shadow, where the Sun never reached.

But the Valley bred horror. Evil forces stirred. The Road Makers fought, won, and retreated to the western end, where the sun still glowed faintly. There, they built cities, grew wise—but also reckless. They experimented with forbidden powers, opened doors they should not have touched. And the world paid the price.

Then came the twilight. The Sun faded. Madness spread. Cities fell. Beasts from the west attacked. Chaos reigned.

And from that chaos rose a leader. He rallied the survivors. He declared: *Build a refuge.*

They built a Great House. But it was not enough. So they wandered, finally reaching the Bight—the curve where the valley turned north. There, they built the Pyramid.

That was the story. And I had read it recently, discussed it with the Master Monstruwacan. We both believed it true.

And if it was true—if the Valley existed—then the Lesser Redoubt must lie within it. Perhaps near the western edge, where the Ab-humans dwelled. Or farther north. But I had no map, no guide. Only a feeling—that it was north.

So I would search northward first. If I found nothing, then I would turn west. But I would not wander blindly. I would follow the logic of the book.

And now, as I walked, I understood something deeper: the cliffs that surrounded us were not just rock. They were the edges of a world-long wound. The Pyramid sat in a crater, not a sea bed. The land rose sharply around us, hidden in darkness. We had always thought we lived in a basin. But the truth was worse—we lived in a tomb.

Most of the people didn’t know. The schools taught fragments, theories, myths. Some laughed. Others believed wildly. Children loved the stories. The wise remained cautious. But none truly grasped the scale of what lay beyond the light.

They accepted the night, the monsters, the fires. They lived in comfort, content with what they saw.

But I was no longer content.

I walked on, into the blackness, carrying the weight of a world’s past, and the hope of a future that might never be.

I moved steadily north and west, my path cautious and deliberate, as I navigated the vast, unrelenting darkness of the Night Land. My mind was fixed on survival—on every decision that could mean life or death in this desolate realm. The first thing I considered was pace. I knew I couldn’t rush; the journey ahead was immense, and no one could predict its end. A measured, steady rhythm would preserve my strength for when it was truly needed.

So I set a strict routine: eat and drink every six hours, sleep from the eighteenth to the twenty-fourth hour. That gave me three meals and six hours of rest each day. It wasn’t much, but it was enough to keep my body functioning under the crushing weight of endless night. This became my law, my anchor in a world without time or mercy.

But even rules are broken in desperation. For over twenty-one hours, I walked without pause—hiding, creeping, avoiding any trace of movement that might draw the attention of the Great Watcher of the North-West. Hunger gnawed at me, but the thought of food turned sour in my throat. I pushed forward, driven by instinct more than reason.

When exhaustion finally seized me, I collapsed near a small fire-hole—a flickering beacon of warmth in the cold void. It was just a few paces wide, filled with dull, smoldering embers that hissed faintly and released a thin, acrid smoke. I sat beside it, placing the Diskos carefully on the rock beside me. For a long moment, I did nothing but stare into the distance, my eyes drawn back to the distant silhouette of the Mighty Pyramid.

It loomed impossibly close, though I had traveled far. Its size made it seem to press upon me, both comforting and terrifying. I felt the presence of millions watching from within—silent guardians of the Redoubt, their thoughts brushing against the aether like whispers across still water. I didn’t wave, didn’t speak. There was no farewell to be made. Only departure. And yet, knowing the Master Monstruwacan might see me through the Great Spy-Glass, I felt a quiet joy. Perhaps he saw me now, sitting beneath the stars of the dead sky.

I forced myself to eat. Three tablets from my scrip—compact, nutrient-rich blocks that sustained life in minimal bulk. They were tasteless, unsatisfying, but they fed me. Then came the water: I shook a fine dust into a cup. As air touched it, the powder fizzed and boiled, rising into a clear liquid—pure, simple water born of chemistry and necessity. It was strange to witness, but familiar now, like breathing in this cursed land.

Afterward, I checked my gear. Besides the Diskos and food, I carried a pouch with various tools. Among them was a compass—crafted by the Master Monstruwacan himself, a relic from an ancient museum, reworked with skill and care. He had told me it might still respond to Earth-Currents, even if it no longer pointed north. If I wandered too far, if the Pyramid vanished behind the dark, perhaps this device would still guide me home.

I placed it on the ground. But it spun wildly, wavering without direction. I understood why—the Underground Fields beneath the earth interfered with its workings. I was still near the “Crack,” though high above it. Still, I wondered if the Master watched. The fire-hole’s glow was bright enough, and the Spy-Glass powerful. But I knew better than to trust sight alone. Light played tricks here—flickering, shifting, revealing nothing or everything in sudden bursts. Smoke rose unpredictably from fissures in the earth, blurring vision, distorting reality.

Eventually, I tucked the compass away and prepared to sleep. I wrapped myself in my cloak, lay down beside the fire-hole, and placed the Diskos beside me, nestled in the fabric. It was more than a weapon—it was a companion, a silent ally in the silence. As drowsiness took hold, I sensed a subtle vibration in the air, a hum beneath the surface of things. I believed, then, that the millions within the Redoubt were aware of me—watching, feeling, sharing my moment of surrender to sleep. Their collective empathy stirred the aether around me, a warm current in the cold night.

And so I slept—deeply, heavily, worn out by days of travel and fear. My last conscious thought was of her—the woman I sought, the one who had become a dream, a hope, a reason to endure.

Then—**a sound.**

Not a whisper, not a sigh, but a roar that tore through the silence. The Home-Call. The voice of the Redoubt, echoing across the Night Land, summoning all to awareness. I snapped awake, cloak slipping off, Diskos gripped tight in my hand.

I looked toward the Pyramid, searching for a signal. Something terrible had happened. The call wasn’t used lightly. Only dire threats warranted such a wake-up. A predator was near. A monster.

As I stared, I couldn’t keep my gaze fixed on the tower. Fear pulled my eyes outward—scanning the blackness. Nothing. But then, high above, flashes of green fire erupted in the sky—swift, sharp, coded messages in the Set-Speech.

They spelled it clearly: *A Grey Man has found you. It is crawling through the moss beyond the fire-hole. Hide to your left. Take advantage.*

Before the warning faded, I was already moving—darting into a clump of moss-bushes, heart pounding, breath ragged. I crouched, trembling, but focused. The air smelled of sulfur and damp stone.

Then—movement.

Something emerged from the shadows beyond the fire-hole. Tall, broad, entirely grey. It crept low, head pressed to the ground, scanning with slow, brutal precision. The fire disturbed it. It hesitated, sniffing, peering along the earth as if reading invisible signs. Then it retreated—only to reappear again, circling the fire-hole, testing the perimeter.

Three times it came from the left. Three times from the right. Each time, it lowered its head, hunched its shoulders, thrust its jaw forward in a grotesque display of predatory focus. I knew what it meant—each pass was a probe, a test. And I feared it had seen me.

But then—within my mind, a voice spoke.

I froze. Not a memory. Not imagination. A message. From the Master Monstruwacan. Sent through brain-elements, reaching me via the Night-Hearing I possessed. And with it came the deep, resonant throb of the Master-Word—a seal of truth, a confirmation.

I leapt—not to flee, but to attack.

I burst from my hiding place and struck. The Diskos swung in a single, perfect arc. The Grey Man fell sideways, legs twitching, head trapped in the bushes where I’d been. It died quickly. The Diskos spun in my hand, glowing with inner fire, as if alive, as if proud.

I stepped back, watching it die. Then I raised the Diskos high, spinning it, sending sparks into the night. A signal. Proof I had survived. Proof I had killed.

The Master did not reply. No need. Such contact was dangerous. Powers in the Night Land listened. To speak again might bring more danger.

But I felt the aether stir—warm, alive. The millions within the Redoubt rejoiced. Their hearts beat in sympathy. I was not alone. I was watched. I was loved.

I sat then, catching my breath, checking the time. Ten hours had passed. I scolded myself. I had broken my own rule. Sleep had overtaken me, uncontrolled. I vowed to obey discipline henceforth—eat, rest, move—by the clock of reason.

I gathered my things, stood, and looked once more at the Pyramid. I did not salute. I did not linger. I turned away.

And I walked into the night—swift, cautious, the Diskos held close, humming with power. It felt alive. It felt *mine*. Not just a tool, but a partner. A bond formed not by blood, but by shared purpose, by fire and sacrifice.

I knew others wouldn’t understand. But those who carried swords in ancient times—those who trusted steel as a friend—might grasp it. Yet the Diskos was more. It pulsed with Earth-Current, with the same energy that flowed through the Redoubt, through the planet itself. It lived.

And there was a law: no one touched another’s Diskos. It rejected strangers. It would falter, fail, betray. Because the Diskos grew tied to its wielder—body, soul, spirit. When a man died, his Diskos was laid upon The Last Road, returned to the Earth-Current, so its power could flow again. Not just custom. Wisdom. Respect. Love.

Now I walked deeper into the Night Land, eyes darting, ears straining. Every shadow held a threat. Every rustle could be death. But I carried pride too—pride in having slain the Grey Man. Though I knew the truth: I would have died in my sleep if not for the watchful minds of the Redoubt.

I stopped after a while, weak from battle. I ate three tablets, drank water from the dust-tube. I sat, listening, watching the Pyramid rise into the black sky. Silence. Then I rose again.

Six hours north and west. I veered slightly westward to avoid the Watcher—but then, foolishly, I changed course, drawing closer to the beast. Why? I don’t know. Fear? Curiosity? Some unseen pull?

It was reckless. If the Watcher saw me, it would signal. And then—destruction.

But the heart is fickle. It trembles, then leaps. It fears, then dares. And so I walked onward, unwisely, into the unknown.

For days, I kept my rhythm—eat, rest, move. I crawled through moss, bled my hands, then donned the heavy gloves of my grey armor. I crossed stony ground, breathed sulfur-laden air, always alert.

Always watching.

And always, in the distance, the towering form of the Great Redoubt—my home, my refuge, my only light in the eternal night.

I descended slowly, quietly, into the endless dark. Each step was cautious, deliberate—every movement a battle against the crushing weight of night that pressed in on me like a living thing. This darkness was not mere absence of light; it was thick, suffocating, as if it had seeped into my bones and clouded my mind. I felt unmoored, adrift in a world without form or sense. At times, I walked with no awareness of ground beneath me, as though I were drifting into nothingness, stepping beyond the edge of reality.

But reason would snap me back. A sudden kick against an unseen rock. A stumble over a boulder hidden in shadow. These shocks reminded me—I was still on solid earth. I was real. The world was real. Yet the illusion clung to me like frost: that I might walk forever through this void, never touching land again.

And so I kept descending. That was my only guide—the slope itself, pulling me downward. But progress was agonizingly slow. In the utter blackness, I could barely manage a mile per hour, sometimes less. My frustration grew with each passing moment, each failed stride. I longed to move freely, to run, to leap—but here, even walking was an act of endurance.

Then came an idea. I would use the Diskos. Not often, not recklessly—but when I needed it. I spun the disc, its strange, pale glow cutting through the abyss for a few yards ahead. I studied the terrain by its faint radiance, memorizing contours, rocks, gaps. Then I extinguished it, letting the dark reclaim me, trusting memory to carry me forward. When doubt crept in, or my feet tripped once more, I’d summon the light again—just enough to reorient myself, then shut it away.

The glow seemed impossibly bright, not because it was strong, but because the surrounding dark was so absolute. It was like holding a candle in the heart of a tomb. And yet, I used it sparingly. I knew the power of the Diskos was finite. To waste it would be madness.

For six long hours, I repeated this ritual—step, pause, glimpse, remember, advance. I had not slept since the previous day, and exhaustion gnawed at me. By the twentieth hour since last rest, I sat upon the slope, numb and weary. I ate two tablets, mixed water, and drank slowly, tasting only the cold metal of the cup and the grit of dust on my lips. I wrapped my cloak around me, placed my pack and pouch beneath my head, and held the Diskos close. Sleep came swiftly, but not without dreams—fragments of Naani’s face, her voice echoing from some distant past.

I woke suddenly, jolted upright in the dark. Something had touched me—or nearly. I gripped the Diskos instinctively, listening. Silence. No sound. No breath. Nothing. After a while, I calmed. I sat up, reached for my supplies, and ate again, fumbling in the dark. When done, I gathered my gear and resumed my crawl.

All that day, I carried a quiet dread. My spirit whispered that something followed me—close, silent, always just behind. But my ears heard nothing. Only the wind, the shifting stones, the rhythm of my own breath. Still, I stopped often to listen. And every time, I moved forward again, deeper into the night.

Near the seventh hour, I misstepped. My foot caught in a hollow, and I fell hard against a jagged stone. Pain shot through my side. I lay still, stunned. The armor saved me from worse injury, but the fall left me shaken. When I regained strength, I made a decision: I would no longer walk. I would crawl.

On hands and knees, I could feel the ground beneath me. I could sense changes in elevation, avoid sudden drops. The Diskos became less necessary. I relied more on touch than sight. For the next eighteen hours, I crept forward, inch by inch. I ate three times, rested briefly, and continued. When I finally stopped, I searched the dark for a level spot, clearing small rocks until I found one suitable. I ate, drank, and prepared to sleep. But the unease lingered—this feeling of being watched. Twice during the night, I woke abruptly, gripping the Diskos, straining to hear. Nothing. Still, I did not sleep deeply. Even in slumber, I listened.

Six hours later, I woke again—startled, alert. Same sensation. Something near. But again, silence. No threat. No proof. Only fear.

The next day brought new danger. Near the eighth hour, I nearly plunged into a vast pit. My chest struck the edge, halting my fall. I pulled back, trembling, and crawled around the chasm in the dark. I emerged on the other side, shaken, more afraid than before. How many such traps lay ahead? Were they natural? Or made by something else?

That night, I devised a plan. I needed a line—a way to probe ahead. But I had no rope. Then I remembered: the straps of my pouch. One was long, thin, strong. I fastened a stone to one end, tied it securely, and began casting it forward as I crawled. Holding the other end, I could feel the tension—if the line slackened, there was a drop. If it snapped taut, there was a ledge. It was clumsy, slow—but it worked. For the first time since beginning my descent, I moved with greater confidence.

At the eighteenth hour, I slept. But I woke early—again, abruptly—as if stirred by a presence. The pattern repeated. I could not explain it. But no harm came. Still, I could not shake the belief that something was near—something unseen, unspoken, but ever-present.

Three days passed in this manner. I crawled down the slope, using the line, the Diskos only when needed. Six full days now I had spent descending into the eternal night. I had no idea where I was going—only that I must go further. The air had changed. It was warmer, heavier. It pressed on my chest, thick and dense. I realized then: the atmosphere was different here. Lower, richer. Unlike the thin, biting air of the Night Land above.

This led me to reflect on the people of the Pyramid. They had broad chests, stronger lungs. Was it because they lived in higher altitudes, where air was thinner? Or was it simply adaptation? I thought of the Upper Cities—those who breathed rarefied air all their lives. Their bodies had grown to match their environment. And perhaps, too, the Road-Makers of ancient times had flown—until the air grew too weak to sustain flight. Maybe they forgot how. Or feared falling into the deep below.

I wondered why they hadn’t flown down into the valley. Why build a road instead? Perhaps because they believed they would never rise again. Or because the lower world was filled with monsters—creatures unknown, alien, born from the chaos after the world split. The great rift had been cataclysmic—oceans rushing in, fire roaring, storms tearing the sky. It looked like the end. But it wasn’t. It was the beginning. From death came life. From destruction, renewal.

And so, too, had humanity rebuilt. First with roads, then with cities, then with airships. But eventually, even those were lost. Progress is not linear. It circles. We rise, we fall, we rise again.

Now, after six days, I stood at the edge of something new. The air was thick, rich. When I mixed my water, it fizzed violently, boiling over the cup, splashing onto my hand. I knew then: I had entered a deeper layer of the world. The pressure, the density—it was unlike anything above.

And then—light.

Far off, in the blackness, a flicker. Weak, uncertain. I froze. Could it be real? I stood up, staring. Yes—there it was. A glimmer, growing fainter, then stronger. Hope surged through me—then collapsed. Was it illusion? A trick of the mind? But then it returned, clear and undeniable.

I ran. Not carefully. Not cautiously. I charged down the slope, heart pounding. I stumbled, nearly broke my ankle, but held on. Teeth clenched, breath ragged, I forced myself forward.

When I stopped, I crawled again. Slowly. Carefully. The light grew brighter, but still distant. I walked for hours—six, then three more—before I came near. And still, it seemed far.

Then I paused. I stood, looking. And I heard it—a sound. A piping. Strange, mournful, rising from the depths. I dropped to my hands and knees, hiding among the rocks. The sound grew louder. Not mechanical. Not human. It was wild, elemental—like the earth itself singing.

And then I saw it.

I had reached the mouth of a massive gorge. To my left, a wall of stone stretched endlessly. To my right, a mountain rose into the night, its peak lost in darkness. Between them, the gorge opened wide.

And in the distance—fires. Faint, shimmering, scattered across the valley floor.

But what truly amazed me was the source of the light.

From the earth, a great blue flame erupted—towering, roaring, dancing wildly. It surged upward, a thousand feet high, then dipped low, swirling like a storm in reverse. It lit the canyon walls, revealed the jagged cliffs, cast shadows that danced like spirits.

It was not magic. Not supernatural. It was gas—natural gas—igniting in the fissures of the land. But still, it was breathtaking. A wonder. A miracle of nature, raw and untamed.

And I knew then: I had reached the bottom of the Mighty Slope.

Not the end. But the threshold.

Beyond lay the unknown.

And the fire burned on.

Two hours after descending from the Gorge, I stood at last upon the open land beyond. Though my mind was still reeling—unsettled by the suddenness of it all—I felt a deep surge of joy in the overwhelming brilliance of this new world. The air shimmered with light, and the horizon burned with strange colors, as if the very earth had been set ablaze.

Before I’d even reached the bottom of the Gorge, I’d paused at its mouth, high above the valley, to gaze across the vast landscape. From there, I counted twenty-seven towering volcanoes—great mountains of fire that pulsed and raged like living things. That number did not include two distant ranges of fiery hills burning on the horizon to my right, nor the countless smaller vents scattered across the land like embers tossed into the wind.

This was a realm shaped by fire and water. A small volcanic hill rose from the sea, no more than a mile away from where I stood, and behind it, a broad stretch of water spread out in every direction. I saw three smaller seas and one immense ocean that stretched endlessly into the crimson glow of the distant fire-mountains, vanishing into the dark sky without end.

Islands dotted the waters, each crowned with smoking peaks. In other places, the fire-hills erupted directly from the sea floor, rising like jagged teeth from the waves. Over the nearer waters, thick clouds of steam hung low, as though the sea itself boiled in fits and starts.

And beneath it all—a constant, deep rumble, like thunder trapped in stone—shook the air. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a steady vibration that seemed to emanate from the heart of the land. I knew then that these were not mere eruptions, but voices—the groans of molten worlds speaking through their own flesh.

It was utterly alien to me. Yet here, too, I sensed something familiar: the World-Noise, the faint hum of life-energy that once filled our ancient age. It was stronger here, clearer, as though time had sharpened its presence rather than dimmed it.

Now, let me describe what I saw with greater detail.

First, to the left of the Gorge’s mouth, loomed a massive, blackened mountain that pierced the night sky—perhaps fifteen or twenty miles high. On its far side, a great volcanic peak jutted upward, five miles into the darkness, and above it, another, even higher, rose ten miles into the void. And atop the left ridge of this mountain, two colossal fire-hills blazed with such intensity they looked like dying suns suspended in the night.

Below them, vast mounds of ash and scorched debris lay piled like monuments to destruction—evidence of eruptions that had poured down for eternity, building silent tombs to time’s fury.

To my right, the sea stretched endlessly, lit by the red glare of the fire-hills. To my left, dense forests rose up, broken only by the occasional monstrous volcano erupting from beyond the trees. This was the first impression seared into my mind: a world alive with heat, motion, and mystery.

After descending, I hesitated. Which way should I go? I searched for signs, then climbed back into the Gorge, cursing myself for not mapping my path earlier.

But when I returned to the rim, I realized there was only one route forward. To the right, only sea. To the left, a narrow strip of land along the shore—possibly leading farther ahead. So I descended again, stepping into the realm I would come to call the Land of Seas and Fire.

By now, twenty-four hours had passed since my last sleep. I needed rest. I found a sheltered hollow beneath three large trees, ringed by dry, warm rock. After eating three nutrient tablets and drinking some water—my stomach still yearning for real food—I made a bed in the basin and lay down. My thoughts drifted to Naani, but sleep overtook me before I could finish the thought.

Water flowed in from a narrow fissure on the opposite side, bubbling and gurgling as if fed from a deep, boiling well below. I was glad it didn’t erupt in flames.

I quickly stripped off my armor, having already removed my gear from the pool. I laid my clothes on a heated rock to dry, then stepped into the warm water. It was soothing, invigorating. I felt safe—surely the horrors of the Night Lands were behind me. Still, I kept the Diskos within reach. I couldn。

I believe the Dark Pyramid drew such forces, much as sharks gather around a ship carrying livestock. But how these powers entered our world… I cannot say. That day, I moved swiftly, passing wonders—boiling springs, fire-hills, strange creatures. I saw four enormous beasts pass by, but hid in time.

Why was the Pyramid completely dark? If it truly was the Lesser Refuge, why did it show no sign of life? A cold dread crept into my mind: perhaps it was not refuge at all, but a fortress of evil—a trap, a deception woven by some malevolent force. Yet, when I looked toward the distant fires flickering across the land, the Pyramid stood clearly defined against the glow. I could not deny the truth: it *was* the Lesser Refuge.

At first, when I saw it, I had been consumed by blind instinct—run!—after months of searching. Then, I had considered calling to Naani through my brain-elements, sending the Master-Word, my voice echoing across the void to tell her I was near. But now, reason returned. I must proceed with care. What if the darkness meant something terrible?

So I descended again, cautiously at first, then with growing urgency, the fire of hope rekindled in my chest after the shock of my fall.

I had climbed to the summit of the great volcano in roughly thirteen hours. Now, descending, I reached the plain in ten—faster, yes, but shaken, unsteady, still weakened by the fall.

By the tenth hour, I stood once more on the vast plain beneath the stars. The Pyramid was no longer visible—it was hidden behind the hill’s shadow. But I could see the massive bulk of the hill itself, silhouetted between me and the distant fires.

I walked four hours across the land, passing through scattered fire-holes that cast dim red glows into the night. Though the darkness was deep, it was never total—the fires kept the shadows from being absolute.

After four hours, the distant lights vanished entirely, blocked by the hill’s base. That was when I realized I was close. But even then, it took another hour to reach its foot. During those five hours since leaving the volcano, I had heard strange sounds—creatures running through the dark, the thunderous roar of a giant, and once, a shriek so twisted and unnatural it sent chills through my bones.

Now I began the climb. At first, my heart pounded with excitement. I almost shouted Naani’s name into the night, longing for her voice to answer, even if only in spirit. But as I ascended, the thrill faded. Caution returned. A chill settled over me—not just fear, but a deeper, quieter horror. My spirit sensed something beyond sight, something ancient and hostile.

Near the top, I finally saw the Pyramid in full—its jagged form piercing the black sky, silent and abandoned. And then, the truth struck me like lightning: *No one lived there.* My spirit knew it with absolute certainty. No human breath stirred within those walls. Instead, monstrous things dwelled there—beasts of nightmare, waiting to devour any who approached.

I turned away. I walked back down the hill, quietly, quickly, until I was far from that cursed place.

It took four long hours to escape the hill’s shadow. When I finally stood clear, I felt no safety anywhere. The entire world seemed poisoned. I wandered aimlessly at first, lost in grief and exhaustion.

Then, without warning, I found myself on the shore of an ancient sea—dry, cracked, and dead. How I had gotten there, I did not know. Perhaps the seabed curved around this region, or maybe there were multiple ancient seas buried beneath the night.

I collapsed onto the ground, weak and broken. My heart felt dead. I knew now that the Pyramid held evil—perhaps the entire Lesser Refuge had fallen. The people were gone. The Maid was likely dead. And if that was true, then I had come too late.

Yet, in that moment of utter desolation, a thought came: *If she is gone, let something terrible find me. Let me die swiftly. There is nothing left to live for.*

And so, I sat in silence, numb with sorrow.

Then, suddenly, I remembered—there was still one thing I could do. I could send the Master-Word. Not knowing if she lived, not expecting anything, but needing to know.

I rose. I looked out into the endless dark. And with every ounce of will, I projected the Master-Word through the aether. Then, three times, I called Naani—using my brain-elements, my voice, my very soul.

And then—*it came*.

From the depths of the night, low and solemn, the Master-Word echoed back. A single, distant voice—tiny, trembling, yet unmistakable—spoke within my mind. It was Naani. It was Mirdath. She called me by my old love-name.

I nearly choked. Joy flooded my body, fierce and terrifying. My despair vanished as if it had never existed. She was alive. She was calling me. After all this time—after the loneliness, the suffering, the endless journey—I had found her.

But the voice sounded so far away. As if she were at the edge of the world. And in that same moment, my spirit told me: *She is not here. She is somewhere else. And danger may be near.*

Then, instantly, I sensed something else—someone nearby. Hidden in the bushes beside a fire-hole. My spirit knew it before my eyes did.

I said nothing to her. I stepped silently into the thicket, moving fast, and crouched low.

Through the branches, I saw her. A small figure kneeling by the fire, sobbing into the dirt. A slender girl, thin and pale, wrapped in tattered clothes. She was listening—desperately listening—to something only she could hear. To a cry from the spirit-world, one she believed came from the Mighty Pyramid.

And in that instant, I *knew*.

She was Naani. Mirdath. My own.

She didn’t recognize me yet. She trembled, weeping, huddled in the cold. I drew in a breath, clenched my teeth, and spoke aloud—plain human words:

She froze. Looked around wildly. Fear flashed in her eyes, then a fragile hope.

I pushed through the bush and stepped into the open. I stood before her in my grey armor, tall and strange in the firelight.

For a heartbeat, she screamed. She tried to flee, stumbling backward into the undergrowth. But then she saw—human, not monster. And I said the Master-Word aloud, so she would know peace.

“I am That One,” I said. “I am the One you called.”

Her eyes widened. Recognition flooded her face. She gasped, then threw herself forward, grabbing my hands with both of hers. She broke into fresh sobs, collapsing against me, shaking uncontrollably.

I held her gently, not daring to touch her too much—my gloves were off, and her skin was cold. I couldn’t speak. I simply held her, letting her cry.

She leaned against me, frail as a child. Her breath came in ragged bursts. I noticed her hands—so thin, so wasted. I lifted her carefully and placed her on a smooth rock, propping her back with a stone. Then I removed my cloak and wrapped it around her. Her clothes were torn, barely covering her. She shivered—not just from cold, but from weakness, from hunger, from grief.

I opened my pack and pulled out a tablet. I broke it into my cup, mixed it with water, and heated it on a hot rock near the fire. Then I fed her the broth—her hands shook so badly she would have spilled it all.

She drank. Slowly, her breathing steadied. The tears slowed. I held her hands in mine, and gradually, the trembling ceased. The broth helped.

After a while, she stirred. Her fingers curled slightly in my grip. I loosened my hold. She grasped mine—weakly, gently—then lowered her gaze. She was gathering strength.

I was afraid. Afraid of what might come. Now that she was safe, I could not bear the thought of losing her. I listened constantly, scanning the dark, alert for movement.

Then, suddenly, she tried to rise. I stepped back to help. She grabbed my hand, sank to her knees, and kissed it. Then she wept again.

I was stunned. I pulled away. This could not be. So I knelt before her, took her hands, and kissed them—once, humbly. I wanted her to know everything: my love, my sorrow, my devotion.

She sobbed harder. Not because of shame, but because I had come. Because I had crossed the night of the world for her.

I released her hands, but she left them in mine. She bowed her head, still weeping, yet somehow, in that gesture, she gave me her whole self—her spirit, her trust.

Then I pulled her into my arms. Gently. Without passion. Just holding her. I stroked her hair, whispered her names—Naani, Mirdath—said things I don’t remember now. But she understood.

She was quiet in my arms. Content. Still crying, but softly now. She had endured so much—loneliness, terror, grief, starvation.

Eventually, she grew still. I tried to move her to rest, but she clung to me, nestling close, her warmth seeping into my heart. She murmured words—soft, broken, half-formed.

I made more broth. She drank it, using her own hands. I ate tablets and drank water—my own hunger had long been forgotten.

As the warmth returned to her, she began to speak. Her voice was weak, but steady. She told me of the disaster at the Lesser Redoubt.

An Evil Force had attacked. Some of the people, weakened by failing Earth-Currents, had opened the Great Door and fled. Then, monsters surged in—giant, brutish creatures—slaughtering many. Some escaped into the night.

Among them was Naani. Her father—the Master Monstruwacan—had been killed by a shaggy, monstrous man. Three girls had fled with her. But two were taken by creatures in the night. The third ran off. Naani ran alone.

She had been lost ever since. All this time—while I journeyed across the wasteland—she had wandered, starving, hunted.

She had called to me—often. But her spirit was too weak. The Word did not travel far. And her calls, though desperate, had drawn predators. Beasts had followed her scent. She learned to stay silent, to hide.

Her clothes were torn away by rocks and brambles. She wore little. She ate moss, bitter berries, drank sulfurous spring water—sometimes sickening, sometimes saving her.

She had seen horrors: the death of a young girl nearby, the pounding feet of giants, the cries of fleeing humans who ignored her pleas. She had once found survivors hiding—but they ran from her, terrified.

The cold drove her to seek fire-holes. But the fires attracted the beasts. So she stayed far away—shivering in the dark.

And once, twice, thrice—she had nearly died in her sleep, hunted by creatures that crept close while she rested.

Now, as she lay by the fire, exhausted, dying, she had seen them—spider-crabs, squatting all around her, waiting. She feared to sleep. She believed death was near.

Then—out of the void—the Master-Word came. Strong. Thunderous. But she thought it came from the Mighty Pyramid. So it brought no hope—only deeper despair.

Then—her name. Spoken aloud. A different name than the one in the Word.

And I appeared.

She recoiled—thinking a monster had found her. Then she saw me. Grey armor. Familiar face. The One from her dreams. The One who had spoken to her across the world.

And I had come.

She was safe.

But shattered.

And as she spoke of the spider-crabs, I looked around.

They were still there.

A ring of silent, watchful horrors, surrounding us.

Anger flared in me. Disgust. I rose, stepped to the edge of the firelight, and kicked at them—one after another. Dozens. They scuttled away, but did not fight. They were cowardly. Foolish. Yet their presence was obscene.

And that was how I knew: the world was not just empty. It was *alive*—with nightmares.

But tonight, I had found her.

And that was enough.

The night air felt lighter now, after the suffocating darkness that had clung to the depths of the ancient sea-bed. I stepped onto a stretch of land clearly to the right of where I’d first entered the submerged plain—my path reversed, my journey homeward begun. Around me, fire-holes burned in abundance, their crimson glows casting flickering warmth across the stone. My heart stirred at the sight, but caution held me back. These fires were not merely sources of heat; they were gathering places for life—some of it terrible, some of it cruel.

I looked down at Naani, and she rose to meet my gaze. She came closer, her face pale, her breath shallow. The long walk had drained her, and I felt a pang of guilt—had I pushed her too hard? I was strong, forged by hardship into something almost unyielding, while she remained delicate, tender, human. But she refused my remorse, standing beside me with quiet resolve, her eyes holding mine with a depth of trust that made my chest tighten.

I reached out and pulled her close. We kissed, brief and fierce, a moment of solace before the world demanded again. Then I turned my attention to the horizon.

Before me stretched the blue glow I’d seen from the mouth of the Upward Gorge—a distant shimmer in the black sky, like a wound in the night. It had been my compass across the sea-bed, a beacon guiding me toward the far shore. Though vague and broad, it had kept me from circling blindly in the dark.

Now, studying the terrain, I began to place the Gorge. It lay somewhere to my left, but not too far—too near, and I risked the red fire-pit of the Giants. And too close to the blue shine itself… that I distrusted deeply. Something about that light felt wrong, unnatural. It wasn’t just illumination—it was a presence.

I took Naani’s hand, and pointed westward. “The Gorge is there,” I said, “but we cannot go straight.” I explained our route: around the gas-laden wastes, beneath the old sea-bed, then up again, avoiding both the giants’ pit and the blue glow.

She listened, then asked, “How did you find your way before?”

I told her how I’d walked along the edge of the gas field, unknowingly, guided by instinct or fate. When I finished, she smiled faintly and suggested a new plan: descend into the sea-bed once more, walk south-west beneath the surface until we passed the gas region, then climb back up and make for the Gorge, always watching for the giants.

It was sound. In fact, it was exactly what I would have devised. But it was *her* mind that shaped it—sharp, practical, quick. And hearing her speak, seeing her think, planning beside me, filled me with a quiet joy. Her voice, her presence, the way she saw the world—these things had become part of me. To share thought with her was to be whole.

We agreed on the course. But the hours had taken their toll. Seventeen since our last rest. Naani trembled, her breath ragged. I insisted we stop soon, and she nodded without protest.

We chose a fire-hole nearby, to the north, one glowing fiercely from within a rocky hollow. As we approached, I signaled silence. Drawing the Diskos from my hip, I moved ahead, dropping to hands and knees, beckoning her to follow.

At the edge of the pit, we peered down. A great fire roared in the earth, but no monstrous shapes lurked near it. That eased my fear—slightly. Still, I remembered: all life gathered around fire. It was safer to stay away.

But Naani shivered violently. “We must warm ourselves,” she whispered. “Just for a little.”

And I, too, felt the cold gnawing at my bones. So I relented. We descended into the hollow, drawn by the heat.

As we warmed, I searched the rocks. Three snakes slithered in crevices. Two scorpion-like creatures watched from holes, neither fleeing nor attacking. Their stillness unsettled me. They liked the warmth. And if they liked warmth, they might come for us in sleep.

I didn’t tell Naani. Let her rest. Let her forget the dangers for a moment. I would warn her later.

She ate, slowly, quietly. Her movements were subdued. I knew she remembered the whipping. And yet—she was mine. Utterly.

After dinner, she noticed my tension. She glanced over her shoulder—and saw a snake coil among the stones. Her breath caught. “We must leave,” she said.

We searched for shelter. And found it: a small cave in a rock wall, perhaps a hundred feet from the fire. Dry. Safe. No signs of creeping things.

Inside, we huddled together, wrapped in the cloak. The firelight filtered through the entrance, painting shadows on the walls. For a moment, it felt like refuge.

But the Land never rests.

Twenty hours since sleep. We drifted into exhaustion, our minds alert, ears tuned to the dark.

Then—screams.

Not animal cries. Human. Women. Children. Lost, terrified, pleading.

I sat bolt upright. Naani woke instantly.

More sounds followed—roars, deep and guttural, echoing across the land. Giant voices. Men as big as houses, shouting, chasing.

Naani trembled. I pulled her deeper into the shadow.

Then—a scream cut through the night. A young woman, dying. Brutal. Sudden. My stomach twisted. I wanted to run, to fight, to *do something*. But I couldn’t leave her.

The roarings grew louder. Then—thudding footsteps. Heavy, pounding. Coming closer.

I crept to the mouth of the cave. Outside, figures ran past—the desperate, ragged remnants of a hunted people. Men, women, children, naked or torn, faces streaked with dirt and blood. They fled like animals.

Then—four giants emerged from the dark. Three were massive, thick-furred, brutish. The fourth—pale, livid, its skin blotched with sickness. A monster. A thing beyond nature.

They vanished into the night, leaving behind only echoes and the fading cries of the dying.

When the thunder of their feet faded, I heard them bellow again—far off—then another scream. Then silence.

A wave of fury surged through me. Why didn’t they fight? They could have turned, charged, died together. But they ran. Because they were broken. Starved. Weak. Not by choice—but by generations of suffering.

The Lesser Redoubt had bred weakness into its people. But Naani was different. She was strong. Even now.

Then—movement in the cave.

I turned. Naani was sobbing softly in the back corner. I started toward her—but froze.

A naked woman ran across the rim of the hollow, looking back as she fled. She slipped down, crawled under a ledge—exhausted, terrified. And then, a squat, hairy giant appeared from the shadows, silent as death. He saw her. He moved.

I didn’t hesitate.

I leaped from the cave—twenty feet down—landing hard, but unharmed. I was upon the beast before he could act.

He lunged at the maid. She screamed once—then was gone.

Rage exploded in me. I swung the Diskos. It shrieked as it spun, alive with fury. The giant turned, roaring, but I struck high—too high. He dropped to his hands, catching my legs. I slashed with the Diskos—its single remaining talon bit deep. He threw me across the hollow, metal clanging, the Diskos ringing like a bell.

I rose, unharmed. He bounded at me—silent, frothing at the mouth, teeth like daggers. I leaped, struck—*and severed head and shoulder in one blow.*

The body fell, knocking me back. I staggered, bruised, bleeding, but alive.

I knelt beside the dead woman. Took her body. Threw it into the fire.

Then I turned to the cave.

Naani stood there. Pale. Silent. In her hand—my belt-knife.

She had come to help.

She stepped forward, her eyes locked on me. No words. Just that look—filled with awe, with respect, with something deeper than love.

I didn’t need her to say it. I *knew*. She saw me—not just as a warrior, but as someone worthy of her trust, her courage, her devotion.

And when she finally allowed herself to fall into my arms, trembling, I held her tight. We had both seen horror. And we were both changed.

Later, we ate. Two tablets each. Water. Then, after an hour of listening, we climbed out of the hollow, gear in hand.

We walked slowly, cautiously, toward the sea-bed. The fear of the giants lingered. But no harm came.

Two hours passed. We reached the edge of the old sea-bed.

And the journey home continued.

For an hour I carried the Maid down through the depths of the Gorge, as I had said. By then, my strength was nearly spent—my legs trembled with fatigue, and each step felt like a battle against gravity itself. My feet no longer obeyed me with certainty; they dragged, unsteady, as if the very will to walk had begun to slip from me. I knew I could not go much farther without collapsing—either myself or both of us would fall, and that would be the end.

I forced myself to focus on the cliffs around us, though for much of that hour I had walked in a kind of trance. Only now, as I regained awareness, did I truly see where we were. The Gorge seemed new to me, as though I had awakened mid-step into a dream. And yet—memory stirred. I recalled the journey outward, every twist and turn, every landmark etched into my mind. And then I saw it: ahead, glowing faintly red, was the fire-hole—a familiar sight. Nearby, on the cliff face, I remembered seeing a cluster of small caves when I passed this way before. At the time, I had longed for such a place—to bring her here, safe and sheltered, away from the horrors that pursued us.

Now, that longing was about to be fulfilled. It was almost too good to be true. But I did not let joy blind me. There was still danger. Even here, deep within the Gorge, some monstrous thing might descend from above, drawn by our presence, and strike while we slept. I wished we had gone deeper still, but my body refused to carry on. The topmost cave—seven in all, carved into the left wall—would have to do. If we reached it, few creatures could climb up without warning. And even if they did, they would have to face the Diskos—and that, I knew, would be no easy task.

I gently roused the Maid. She lay in my arms, breathing softly, and when I kissed her forehead, she answered with a sleepy press of her lips. Her eyes fluttered open, half-lidded, dazed. I loved her so completely that my heart ached with it. I kissed her again, shook her lightly, and whispered that we had found a place to rest. She stood slowly, blinking, her limbs stiff with sleep, and scolded herself quietly for falling asleep while I bore her weight.

But I only smiled and kissed her once more. She looked at me, still half-asleep, her face soft and radiant, and said, “I love you—forever,” in a voice barely above a whisper. It was pure, unguarded truth.

Then I climbed to the highest cave, calling to her to walk a little while to wake fully. I wanted her alert, strong, ready to climb. When I reached the opening, I gasped. The cave was dry, warm, and sweet-smelling. A low heat seeped from somewhere deep in the rock, and the flickering light from the fire-hole below cast dancing shadows across the walls. It was quiet, secluded—perfect for rest.

I returned to her, calling out that the cave was safe. She followed, clinging to me as we ascended. We made it inside, breathless but whole, and for a moment, the world felt right. We were protected.

Before sleep, I freed the scrip and pouch, took the straps, and descended again into the Gorge. I found a heavy boulder—just manageable—and secured it to my back. With great care, I carried it back up and placed it at the mouth of the cave, balanced just so that a slight touch would send it tumbling down. Any creature attempting to enter would likely trigger it, and the noise would wake us. That was the purpose—not to kill, but to warn.

We settled in. She spread the cloak over the stone floor, a soft bed for both of us. I didn’t need it—my armor was too thick to feel comfort—but she insisted. It wasn’t for warmth, but for closeness. She arranged it with gentle hands, careful not to lose her modesty, yet unable to resist being near me. It was natural, tender, and deeply intimate. I loved her more than words could say.

She knelt beside me, kissed my lips softly, then lay down beside me. Within moments, we both slipped into sleep.

Twelve hours later, I woke to the hiss of water. The Maid was already awake, preparing our meal. She laughed when she saw me stirring—bright, sweet, full of affection. She came to me, kissed me, and knelt beside me, her gaze full of quiet devotion. I knew, without speaking, that she had watched me while I slept—like a mother guarding her child. How I knew, I couldn’t say. Perhaps it was in her eyes, or in the way her thoughts brushed mine.

I needed that love. All of us do. I reached for her, and she came into my arms, snuggling close with a joy so pure it made my chest ache. The armor between us was hard, unyielding—but she didn’t seem to mind. Or perhaps, in her own way, she found comfort in its strength. Still, I feared hurting her, and so held her gently.

After a while, she pulled away, and I let her go. I always honored her freedom, never forcing closeness. Yet I guarded it carefully—not out of fear, but because she deserved nothing less than reverence.

She went to the water and tablets, bringing them to me. She wouldn’t let me rise. Instead, she cradled my head in her lap, kissed my lips once, and fed me a tablet—then touched another to my lips, saving one for herself. We ate slowly, contentedly, like children sharing a secret. Then we drank.

When we finished, she asked me to stand. I hesitated—then rose, and nearly groaned. Every muscle screamed. I was stiff, bruised, worse than before. I hadn’t realized how badly I’d been hurt by the Four-Armed Man. But she had known. She had prepared the ointment, waiting.

She helped me remove the armor, then my clothes. The bruises were deep, visible beneath my skin. She looked at them with such tenderness that I felt a warmth spread through me. She laid me down, wrapped me in the cloak, and began to rub the ointment into my flesh with skilled, gentle hands. For an hour, she worked, soothing my pain until I felt renewed.

As she cared for me, I listened to the distant mutter of the fire-pits below—the sound of a world alive, yet peaceful here. I felt like a child cradled in safety, loved and guided. When she finished, she handed me my spare vest, but paused—her eyes wide with admiration.

She studied my arms, the muscles hard and defined from years of training. I had been raised in the traditions of the Mighty Pyramid, trained in strength and discipline. My body was shaped by both nature and nurture—by the woman who bore me, and by the life I lived. And now, she looked at me as if I were something beyond ordinary.

Her gaze softened. She dropped the vest and reached for me. I took her into my arms, trembling with emotion. She rested there quietly, then pressed her face to my chest—kissing the ridges of my muscles with a sweetness that made my heart swell. Then, suddenly, she stepped back and helped me dress.

When I stood again, she looked at me—half shy, half playful. She reached up, touched my shoulders, lowered her lashes, and stole glances upward. Then, without warning, she fell to her knees before me and wept.

I knelt beside her instantly.

I didn’t ask why. I understood. She was overwhelmed—by love, by awe, by the depth of what she felt. She saw me not just as her lover, but as a hero. And in that moment, I felt both proud and humbled. I was worthy of her love, yes—but also unworthy. I resolved not to pretend otherwise, nor to diminish myself in her eyes. I would earn her respect, not demand it. And I would accept her worship—not as arrogance, but as a gift.

I know I am not perfect. I have faults, as any man does. But I also know that love can make us greater. Think back to your own youth—how your beloved made you feel strong, noble, capable. That is what she did for me. And it is human. It is real.

Eventually, she calmed. She wiped her eyes, nestled close, and whispered her needs—quiet, trusting, utterly herself. I held her, feeling both father and lover in one. I slipped off her shoes, something I should have done earlier. I chided myself—then stopped. She was hurt by my self-reproach. I ceased, but the guilt lingered.

After tending to her feet, we gathered our gear and descended from the cave, careful, cautious. We moved down the Gorge, slower now, mindful of the path ahead. In six hours, we stopped to eat. I bathed her feet in warm water from a rock basin, rubbed them thoroughly with ointment, and eased her pain.

We continued onward, but more deliberately. I recalled the journey outward—now I knew we were nearing the Slugs’ territory. That stretch would take twelve hours or more, with no rest. We needed strength. We needed sleep.

So we searched for a place to rest. And there it was—the same cave where I had slept after escaping the Slugs. The spring, the fire-hole, everything was unchanged. We both exclaimed in wonder. She was thrilled to stand where I had once lain. It felt meaningful—almost sacred.

I checked the cave first, ensuring no creeping things lurked. Then we went to the hot spring. I inspected the waters—no snakes, no rat-things. She sat on the edge, and I removed her shoes, bathed her feet, and rubbed them with care, watching the Gorge all the while.

When done, we returned to the cave and gathered boulders. Together, we built a rough wall across the entrance, high enough to block most threats. I sealed the lower gaps tightly, so no small creature could sneak through. Then she set the scrip and pouch beside me as a pillow—her torn garments folded neatly beside her.

She placed the Diskos within reach, her fingers trembling slightly. The weapon terrified her—but it also comforted her. Then she covered me with the cloak and kissed my lips once, solemnly. She curled beside me, happy, secure.

She fell asleep quickly. I stayed awake for a while, watching her breathe. Then, slowly, she turned toward me, nestling close like a child. I longed to hold her, to kiss her—but I did not move. I respected her trust, even in sleep.

Eventually, I drifted into slumber. Eight hours passed before I woke to the hissing of water—she had risen early, as planned, preparing our food.

When I stirred, she smiled and rushed to me, wrapping her arms around my neck. She kissed me three times—soft, loving, lingering. In that moment, I remembered—I had dreamed of her kissing me. But now I knew: she had taken advantage of my sleep, kissing me while I was unconscious. And she said nothing. Neither did I. But I decided—next time, I would wake just in time to catch her in the act.

It sounds foolish. But it was true.

I rose, dismantled part of the wall, and looked out. The Gorge was still. Only one rat-thing lay asleep near the fire-hole, bloated and motionless.

We were safe—for now.

We emerged from the oppressive, shadowed passage of the Upward Gorge—a place that had clung to us like a suffocating shroud—and stepped into the open expanse beyond the mountains. The air was different here: broader, lighter, filled with a quiet breath of freedom. Naani turned her face toward the sky, her eyes wide and bright with wonder, her chest rising and falling quickly as if she could not believe she was still alive in such a world.

She glanced back once, toward the dark maw of the gorge, where silence seemed to coil like smoke. Fear flickered across her features—just for a moment—but then she turned away, stepping forward into the light, drawn by the vastness of the sea and the endless stretch of land before us. Her laughter rose, almost swallowed by the wind, but it carried joy nonetheless—raw, unfiltered, and trembling with awe.

For the first time in her life, she stood beneath an open sky. She spun slowly, taking in every direction, each breath deeper than the last. No more narrow tunnels, no more creeping dread. Only the sea on one side, the mountains behind us, and the horizon stretching endlessly ahead.

We no longer needed to speak in whispers. The silence of the gorge was gone, replaced by the soft murmur of wind and distant waves. Naani let out a sudden cry—pure, childlike—and listened as it echoed off the cliffs. Then, just as abruptly, we heard something return—an echo, or perhaps something else entirely? A voice from the dark? We froze, turning sharply toward the gorge’s mouth. But there was nothing. Only the whispering wind. Still, we moved farther down, until the shadows receded completely, until we were certain the darkness had released us.

Then we found a flat rock, high above the shore, broad enough for both of us to rest upon. We climbed up and sat together, sharing our food and water. The meal was simple, but it tasted richer than any feast I had ever known—because it was shared with her.

As we ate, Naani stared out over the sea, her gaze drifting far beyond the visible edge. Her expression shifted—between wonder and sorrow, between memory and dream. She blinked rapidly, as though trying to hold back tears. And then she did—wept, quietly at first, then openly. I pulled her close, cradling her against me until she calmed, until her breathing slowed and her hands relaxed in mine. When she looked up again, her eyes sparkled with a new kind of light—one born not of fear, but of recognition.

She spoke little after that, but when she did, it was in fragments—phrases that sounded ancient, half-remembered. Words that slipped through time like moonlight over water. And then, just as suddenly, she would be present again, fully aware, her attention fixed on me, her heart beating in rhythm with mine.

The sea called to her—not with sound, but with memory. It stirred something deep within her, a resonance older than language. And I, too, felt it—the pull of forgotten things, the ghost of a world long buried under night. Our spirits trembled together, caught between dreams and reality, between what was and what might have been.

Far off, beyond the foot of the mountains, a thick mist curled along the shoreline. It was the same steam I had passed through on my way outward—the breath of sleeping volcanoes, spewing fire and fury into the sea. Naani asked about it, and I told her what I knew: that we would soon cross it, that it was dangerous, but not impossible.

She gazed at the distant peaks, their forms jagged and immense, rising like broken teeth into the black sky. They dwarfed everything around them, towering into the upper darkness where even stars dared not go. I had seen them before, yes—but never so clearly, never with someone beside me who saw them with equal wonder.

And then, without warning, she kissed me—once, twice, thrice—each kiss deepening, growing warmer, more urgent. Her small hands gripped my shoulders, pulling me closer, as if to demand a response faster than words could give. I answered her, but slowly, deliberately—each kiss followed by a word, a promise, a whisper of love.

She teased me—slipping her hand between our lips, forcing me to kiss her palm instead. Then, quick as thought, she opened her fingers and kissed me through them, sealing my mouth shut with her own. I laughed, caught between anger and delight, and she only smiled wider.

I made her stand on the rock, then loosened her hair so it fell freely over her shoulders. I removed her boots, revealing her bare feet—small, delicate, perfect. At first she hesitated, shy, but then she allowed me, letting herself be shaped by my touch, by my desire.

When I stepped back, she turned slowly, like a figure posed for admiration. Then, with a mischievous glint in her eye, she stretched out her foot and placed her pink toes gently against my lips. I froze, stunned—until she pulled them away, laughing, and leapt into my arms.

I held her tightly, laughing too, because I loved her so completely, so utterly. I told her I wished I had a pocket large enough to carry her always—close to my heart, safe from all harm. She replied with a grin, saying she’d tickle me if I tried to keep her there, and pinch me too. I said nothing. Instead, I shook her gently, and she kissed me hard, right in the middle of it.

Later, she insisted on sitting on the rock again, this time reaching behind me to retrieve the comb from my pack. She brushed her hair slowly, carefully, while I watched, talking lightly, joking, feeling lighter than I had in years. Though I feared the Humpt Men and the beasts that roamed these shores, I no longer felt the crushing weight of terror. Here, the world felt natural—even the horrors seemed part of a greater order, not evil, but simply… real.

When she finished, she tied her hair back, but I asked her to leave it loose. “It looks better,” I said. She smiled and obeyed.

We packed up, and began our descent into the coastal lands. We walked steadily, not too fast, not too slow. My plan was to rest before reaching the steaming zone near the shore. We traveled along the base of the mountains for six hours, still miles from the mist. I kept my pace measured, mindful of her strength. On my journey outward, I had moved with desperate speed—but now, with her, I walked with care.

At dusk, we found a tall, flat-topped rock, hard to climb but secure. We ascended, ate, drank, and lay down beneath the stars. The air was warm, so we left our cloaks behind. Sleep came easily, deep and peaceful.

Seven hours later, we woke, blinking in the morning light. We looked at each other—newly awake, newly alive. She crawled into my arms, kissed me fiercely, and refused to let go. We both hungered for each other, not just physically, but in spirit. And yet, somehow, we wanted more.

Breakfast was simple—water that fizzed strangely, tablets of preserved food—but it was delicious. As we ate, she studied the trees rising on our right, great ancient beings stretching toward the sky. She touched their bark, smelled their leaves, marveling at their size, their age. And again, memories stirred—faint, fragmented dreams of a world she had never truly lived in, yet somehow remembered.

By midday, we paused near a warm pool. She asked me to turn away, but stay close—her protector, as always. She bathed, washing herself clean, her movements graceful, tender. When she was done, she took my place, helping me undress, drying me, tending to me as if I were her child. And I, in turn, felt deeply cherished—not just loved, but *seen*.

We continued, walking seven hours more. By then, we neared the burning fire-hill offshore—the same one that had lit my path during my sleep in the tree. I remembered it well, and I knew she would want to see it again.

So I led her to the tree, climbed with her to the branch where I had rested, and showed her the marks left by my armor on the bark. She studied them closely, then knelt, kissed the spot where I had lain, and cut a piece of bark with her knife. She tucked it into her breast, a keepsake of our journey.

Afterward, I told her about the beast that had charged past—how its body had scraped the earth, how its footprints were torn and massive. She pointed out the damage, her eyes wide with awe. “It was huge,” she whispered. “But still small compared to the Slug.”

I nodded. Seventeen days had passed since I had left this place. It felt like a lifetime, yet also like yesterday. Time had warped under stress, under danger. And yet, I knew—those days had been longer than they seemed, each hour stretching into two or three.

We pressed on. After twelve hours of walking, I lifted her into my arms. She protested, insisting she could walk another six hours. But I knew better. She was weakening—brought low by months of isolation, by fear, by flight. I would not risk her health. I carried her as I had always done, and she settled into my embrace, content.

She marveled at my strength—my body, my will. I was strong, yes, forged by hardship. But so was she—strong in spirit, in courage, in love. And she admired that. I smiled, proud, not of myself, but of her admiration.

She nestled close, speaking softly, her head resting against my chest. Once, she slipped her face beneath my chin and kissed me there, just once, sweet and secret. Then she returned to her usual place in my arms, silent, trusting.

But then I noticed—her shoulders were bruised. From lying against the hardness of my armor. I felt a surge of anger—first at myself, for not noticing sooner, then at her, for not telling me.

I set her down instantly. She bared her shoulders, and I saw the welts, red and raw. I nearly shook her, furious that she had suffered this pain, that she had let it happen. But then she looked up—lips parted, eyes sparkling—and kissed me, teasing me, daring me to stay angry.

I almost slapped her. But she stopped, turned her back, and let me button her shirt—like a child needing help. When I finished, she came around, closed her hand into a tiny fist, and slipped it into mine.

I didn’t squeeze it. I didn’t pull away. I let it rest there, small and warm in my palm. She moved it gently, wanting me to notice, to respond. But I remained stern. I was angry. Yet, beneath the anger, something stirred—something softer, deeper.

She withdrew her hand. For a moment, she was cold. Then I felt it—my anger flaring again. I said she deserved to be whipped, that she had earned a proper punishment.

She looked at me, saucy, defiant. “What do you mean?” she asked.

“I mean you’ve earned a flogging,” I said, meaning every word.

And then—she turned, rushed into my arms, wrapped both her fists around my hand, and clung to me. I couldn’t stay angry. Not with her. I put my arm around her, holding her close, feeling the need to shield her, to protect her from everything—especially from the world.

And in that moment, I understood: love is not just tenderness. It is strength. It is rage. It is forgiveness. It is everything.

I came to myself slowly, weak and disoriented, pain thrumming through my body like a distant echo. I tried to rise, but something held me down—perhaps the weight of my wounds, perhaps the exhaustion that had dragged me under. My head throbbed, and the world swam in shadows.

I lay on my back, breath shallow, when a soft sound reached me—a faint, ragged panting. Turning my head with great effort, I saw her. The Maid. She was near, still bare, her form trembling as she pushed the raft forward with a long pole—the very one I’d cut during the attack of the Humpt Men. In that moment, memory flooded back: the violence, the darkness, the suddenness of it all. I was on the raft. She had saved me.

I made a small sound, a whisper of life, but she didn’t hear. Her gaze was fixed on the shore, eyes sharp with fear. Behind her, from the trees, came the howling of the Humpt Men—familiar, terrible voices. I understood then: she had pulled me from the wreckage, carried me to safety while I was unconscious. How? I never knew. Neither did she. Only love gave her the strength to act, to risk everything for me.

Later, she told me what had happened. When she regained consciousness, she found me lying across her chest, motionless. My head was heavy, my blood staining the earth around us. She thought I was dead. Heartbreaking, she lifted me gently, cradled me, and wept. But then—life. A faint pulse. Hope surged within her. She ran to the raft, filled my headpiece with river water, and splashed it over my face. Nothing. Still no response.

Then, a sudden instinct—a warning from the depths of her spirit. Danger was coming. She would not lose me. She struggled, dragged me across the rocky ground, brought me to the raft, climbed aboard, and pushed us into the water. As she grabbed the pole, she saw her torn clothes still clutched in the hand of a fallen Humpt Man. Without hesitation, she snatched them and ran. She leaped onto the raft just as two survivors burst from the woods, chasing her silently, hungrily. But they stopped at the edge of the water. They could not swim—or feared the unknown terrors beneath. They stood, stunned, then let out their howls of rage and frustration.

That was the sound I heard when I woke—her desperate efforts pulling us away from death, the echoes of our enemies fading into the distance. I wanted to help, to rise, to fight beside her. But weakness overwhelmed me again, and I slipped back into unconsciousness. And she—my beloved, my naked guardian—guided us safely to the little island, her only thought: to save me.

When I finally awoke again, clarity returned slowly. Pain lingered, but so did warmth. I was laid upon something soft, wrapped in cloth. Peace settled around me like a gentle fog. And there she was—kneeling beside me, her eyes full of love and relief. She leaned down and kissed me, tears falling softly onto my face. I kissed her back, every movement a promise, a return to life.

She cradled my head, fed me a broth made from tablets and water, measured by my timepiece. Then she kissed me once more—so tenderly, it felt like a breeze brushing my lips. I drifted into sleep, safe in her care.

This scene repeated three times. On the third awakening, I moved my hand slightly. She noticed, took it in hers, and held it tightly. I slept again, content, watching her with half-lidded eyes.

On the fourth morning, I whispered, “I love you.” She wept, clutching my hand to her chest, as if holding me could keep me alive forever.

By the fifth day, I was healing. My body was bandaged—her own torn garments stitched together with threads from her clothes, needles fashioned from thorns. She had worked tirelessly, mending, cleaning, caring. I watched her, and she blushed, kissing me to hide her shyness. I wished I were strong enough to kneel before her, to honor her sacrifice. But even in weakness, I felt whole because of her.

Each day, she brought me broth, washed me, changed the bandages, and dried them carefully. We were nearly out of supplies, but she made do. On the sixth day, I spoke freely. I told her how much I loved her—not just with words, but with every breath. She said she was well, though I saw the toll it had taken—she was thinner, her eyes shadowed, yet radiant with joy.

I asked her to bring me the tablets. She did. I kissed them, then ate. I asked for broth, and she prepared it. Then I requested the Diskos be placed near me. I called her to my side, and she lay beside me, her head resting in my arm. I told her to sleep. She hesitated—afraid she might weigh too heavily—but I held her gently, showing her I was stronger now. She nestled close, and slept deeply for twelve hours, her breathing calm, her face peaceful.

I watched her, entranced. She was beautiful—delicate, pure. Her presence filled me with quiet awe. I drank broth at intervals, my right arm free to reach for the Diskos. I touched it often, as if it were an old friend. It felt alive, connected to me. Perhaps it was. Or perhaps it was simply my heart, uplifted by love, that made it feel so.

At noon, she woke suddenly, startled. She looked at me—then smiled, relieved. I laughed quietly, and she scolded herself for losing track of time. I teased her, forbidding her to speak another word about it. She playfully punched my nose, and I laughed so hard she feared I’d reopen my wounds. But I was fine. Just happy.

I asked her if she had brothers—why she played so freely. Instantly, I realized how foolish the question was. I took her hand, silent, and she nodded, then kissed my palm. She stepped away, and I knew she was hiding tears. I called to her softly. She returned, smiling, but I saw the dampness in her eyes. Still, she made new broth, and I insisted she come to me first.

We ate together, laughing, talking. I fell asleep, but kept her near. That night, I dreamed of her—of us—safe, together.

The seventh day dawned bright and sweet. I woke to find her sleeping beside me, her face pressed against my shoulder. She stirred at once—watchful, ever vigilant. We ate, bathed, and joked. I was allowed full tablets now, and I devoured them, feeling strength return. Each tablet helped rebuild my blood, restore my vitality. I needed this power—to protect her, to reach the Mighty Pyramid.

We shared food, kissed each other’s tablets, drank from the same cup. We were children, lovers, partners. She changed my bandages, washed me, and kept me low-lying. I didn’t mind. I was too weak, and she was too precious to resist.

Afterward, she went to bathe in a pool beyond the bushes. I asked her to hurry. She promised, and returned soon—her hair flowing like a cloud, her feet bare and glistening. She teased me for being impatient, but I knew she stayed longer because she enjoyed my gaze.

I pulled her to me, scolding her for not caring for her feet. I made her place them toward me, and then—slyly—I tied her toes together with a strand of her hair. She gasped, then laughed. I had captured her. She tried to pull away, but kept the hair intact, sitting bound beside me, pretending not to notice.

Then she styled her hair, and I untied it, teasing her. She kissed me and asked how she’d ever manage if I kept doing this. I plucked another lock of her hair, and she cut one from mine. We braided them together—our hair joined—and she tucked it into her bosom. I wanted to do the same, but was too weak. So she cut another pair, and we kissed each other’s locks, then braided them again. I placed mine beneath the bandage over my heart. She pouted—because the second braid had been kissed, the first hadn’t. I refused to swap. We quarreled, then kissed, forgiving each other.

She made me lie still, threatened to punish me if I touched her hair again. I pretended to be humble. She held my hands with one small fist, barely able to grasp me. Then, with her free hand, she covered my eyes. While I was helpless, she kissed me—daring, sweet—then released me, demure.

We sat in silence. Then I reached out, my hand trembling, pale from lack of blood. She knew what I meant. She clenched both fists and placed them in my palm. They were small, fragile. But I felt whole. I was happy.

I teased her again, saying her hands were too tiny to do anything. She wrapped her arms around my neck, kissed me fiercely, then stepped back, afraid of unbalancing me.

I asked her to sit beside me. I told her a story—an old tale from the past. Of a man who met the One Maid Upon All The Earth. They married, she died, and he was consumed by grief. Then, in a dream, he awoke into a future age—only to learn she lived there too. He searched for her, found her, and though she was changed, she remained lovely. He revered her—his wife from a forgotten time—love so deep it became a constant ache of sweetness and sorrow.

I couldn’t finish. She began to weep, dropped to her knees, and pressed my hand to her breast. She placed her hand over my lips, and whispered through her tears. Her face glowed with motherhood, her eyes lit with memory—something ancient, painful, yet sacred.

And then I remembered. The babe. The child who lived on after she died. I told her. There was silence. Then she leaned into me, and I drew her into my arms. She tried to speak of the vision, but words failed her. I stayed silent too. Some truths are beyond speech.

She kissed me, and returned to her tasks.

It was a perfect day. I had strength enough to talk, to laugh, to be truly present. She was rested, her heart at ease. We joked, teased, and shared memories. I asked her an old riddle—about a horse. She answered instantly, as if remembering from beyond time. "He was a little hoarse," she said.

We both paused. We had never seen a horse. Yet we knew. Not with our eyes, but with our souls. We looked at each other, wondering what such a creature was—yet feeling its shape in the dim corners of our minds.

We wandered through ages, dreams, and echoes of a world long gone. Then, I pulled us back. From the abyss, into the present. And she smiled again.

I told her everything I knew of the Mighty Pyramid—its towers, its secrets, its purpose. She listened, spellbound. It was like hearing tales from another star. She asked questions, endlessly curious. And I answered, my voice steady, my heart full.

For hours, we talked. She believed. She hoped. She dreamed.

And in that moment, on the quiet island, surrounded by silence and love, I knew—no matter what lay ahead, we would face it together.

It was in the tenth hour of that day that we launched the raft. The paddles moved with ease, balanced by the rests I had rigged, and the craft glided forward with little effort. I took the rear paddle, the Maid the front, and together we pushed steadily, the raft gaining a rhythm just shy of walking pace across the rough terrain of the Land.

By the twelfth hour, we paused to eat and drink, then resumed our journey. Once underway, the motion became almost automatic—no more than a gentle rocking on our feet. Time slipped away in quiet conversation, punctuated by the Maid’s soft glances back at me, her lips parting as if to tempt me closer. But each time I reached for my paddle, she shook her head—tenderly, firmly—refusing to let me leave my post.

At the eighteenth hour, we pulled in the paddles. The Maid laid out the cloak, fashioning it into a bed. We ate, drank, and fell asleep almost instantly—deep, untroubled rest, as though the world had cradled us in silence. Eight hours later, we woke together, blinking at one another, disoriented for a moment, then smiling as recognition returned. We were safe. Nothing had disturbed us. We laughed, kissed, washed our faces in the sea, and set off again.

That day passed in peace. We drifted along the coast, unhurried, content. Our voyage lasted four full days—each twenty-four hours long. We made no haste, moving only as fast as my strength allowed. Nothing notable occurred, save once: a great beast emerged from the sea, lumbering onto the shore to feed upon the sparse growth. It seemed natural, not monstrous. Its presence stirred no fear—only relief that we were distant from it. And so it was with all creatures of this land: they bore no taint of horror, but rather the quiet dignity of ancient life. Perhaps, I mused, the early world had been like this—simple, wild, yet untouched by unnatural dread. It struck me then that extremes do meet: the deep world had birthed beings not unlike those of the beginning. Not proof, merely thought—shaped by circumstance, yes, but perhaps also by something deeper, something beyond reason’s grasp. Still, I held no objection. For even if all form is shaped by environment, I believed there must be an inner force—a spirit peculiar to each living thing. That spirit, I thought, remained constant beneath change. Whether it was the cause or effect of life, its purity defined humanity. Where it was untainted, man stood. Man, I believed, had always been man—though his body might have changed, his spirit evolved slowly, shaped by need. Yet no development could make him cease to be man. His nature had limits, boundaries of being. And so, even if science proved man once walked the seas, I would still see him as man—not fish, but man modified. I could accept such truths without losing faith in his essence. Though I would never claim certainty, neither would I close my mind.

I did not speak here of what comes after death—for who can say? But I held a hope: that beyond this life, we may reach a world of beauty, uplifted, perfected. A vision glimpsed when we stood in holiness beside the Beloved. That hope sustained me.

But now, back to the journey.

Another wonder marked our passage: a fire-hill rising from the sea. The waters boiled around it, not everywhere, but in places where jets of flame shot skyward, roaring across the distance. Strange grunts rose from the depths near its base—gases escaping, I thought. The sight told us of immense power stirring beneath the earth. Long after we passed, we turned to watch it, awestruck.

Beyond that, nothing stirred—save the narrowing of the sea into smaller channels, linked by narrow waterways that carried us onward.

I showed the Maid the two places where I had slept when first arriving in this land. She listened closely, eager to know every detail, every memory.

On the fourth day, at the tenth hour, we reached the shore—a flat stretch leading up to the mouth of the First Gorge. We had begun our voyage at the same hour. The journey had been peaceful, blessed. If only the road ahead held such calm. But I knew better. Danger lay ahead. Yet our hearts were steady. We would go forward, conquer, and return to the Mighty Home. There, we would live in love, forever. That was worth every trial.

We beached the raft as best we could, wondering if anyone would ever see it again. The Maid cut a small piece of wood—a token. Then we gathered our gear. She helped me don my armor. I strapped the scrip and pouch to my back, gripped the Diskos. She wore her bundle, now smaller, and her knife at her side.

Then she knelt and kissed the raft. Memories flooded her—of beginnings, of survival, of rebirth. Her heart stirred with sorrow. I knelt beside her, gently lifting her to her feet. I took her hand, and together we stepped away from the water, toward the dark mouth of the Great Gorge.

To our right, miles away, loomed the mountain—huge, grim—where four fire-hills burned high above the night. Below them, ash-covered hills stretched upward, built over eternity. The Maid stared, transfixed. None of us could ever forget such a sight.

We climbed into the gorge’s high mouth, stepping into shadow. After a short while, we reached a bend—a sharp turn left into utter darkness. We paused. Turned back.

And looked one last time upon the Deep Land—the sea, the fire-mountains, the whispering life below, hidden in eternal night.

In that moment, I wondered: would the Humpt Men ever awaken to the fullness of human spirit? A strange thought, yes—but not unnatural. The land before us felt like the old world renewed. We saw ancient things through new eyes.

We stood silent, listening to the distant mutter of fire and gas, the faint pulse of life across the void. Only a few paces separated us from the silence of the gorge—our gateway to the Night Land. The Maid clung to my arm. Tears traced her cheeks—not only grief, but joy, longing, the ache of leaving behind the island where she had nursed me back to life. She would tell stories to her children—of this place, unseen by them, a wonder forever.

Then I turned. She slipped her hand into mine. We walked past the mighty corner of the gorge, stumbling into the gloom.

For sixteen hours we moved steadily, the darkness our only companion. Twenty-six hours since our last sleep. Foolish, I knew—I needed strength. The Maid warned me. But wisdom came soon enough. We found a safe spot, ate, drank, and counted our progress. From then on, we limited our journey to sixteen hours a day, sleeping eight. We kept this rhythm until we emerged from the gorge—five days in total.

When we entered the lighter parts of the gorge, we felt a lift in spirits. But often, thick gases rose from fissures, choking the air. The smell was rank. Silence reigned—except when a lone gas-fire moaned or whistled, its sound echoing across the waste, making loneliness a weight in the chest. The Maid felt it too.

She remembered how I had come alone, searching for her in the unknown. And I, knowing this, felt a quiet pride—natural, true. When a woman remembers your sacrifice, loves you more for it, the heart swells. Think of your own love-days—do not such thoughts echo with familiar pain?

On the fifth day, near the seventh hour, I began to hear odd sounds—whispers from the rocks, as if the stone itself spoke. I drew the Maid close, Diskos in hand, and we advanced cautiously.

Three times we passed gas-fires, their flames dancing, moaning softly, sometimes whistling. Other sounds came from the rocks—strange, unexpected, yet oddly familiar.

Then, suddenly, I realized: these were echoes. Distant echoes, caught by nearby cliffs, bouncing back to us. A faraway sound, distorted, made to seem near. And then I knew—it was the monstrous piping of the Great Gas Fountain. I told the Maid. She grew eager. This was both wondrous and a sign—we were nearing the end.

We looked ahead. Fires leapt everywhere, blurring the view. The fountain’s dance was still too far to see clearly, dwarfed by nearer flames.

But soon, as we advanced, the gorge flickered—light and dark shifting in the distance, like shudders across the night. That was it—the distant dance of the Great Flame. We watched as the light trembled, growing stronger, merging into a slow rise and fall of blue fire.

The sound grew louder—steady, then swelling into a monstrous piping, rich and changing in tone.

We passed the last small fires. Now we stood in the fireless stretch, where only the Great Fountain lit the way. It loomed huge, casting a trembling glow across the gorge.

We came near, stunned by the roar. Standing at the mouth of the gorge, we gazed at the flame, voiceless. My arm wrapped around the Maid. We stood close, silent. How could we speak? The noise was overwhelming.

After a long pause, we turned to face each8 other. We kissed—solemn, tender—in the light of the monster flame.

Then we looked again. The light spread wide, blue and strange, illuminating the far side of the gorge. There, under the flickering glow, lay a desolate world of mountains—lost, forgotten.

And now, we saw the path ahead. The bottom of the Mighty Slope—the final leg of our journey—appeared only when the flame leapt high, revealing jagged rocks and steep inclines.

We walked on a mile, seeking shelter from the noise. It was past the seventeenth hour. We ate, drank, rested among boulders.

When we woke, we ate again. We sat in silence, watching the flame dance—lonesome, majestic, surrounded by silent giants of rock. Then we gathered our gear and began the climb toward the utter dark of the Mighty Slope.

The ascent began. For sixteen hours, we stumbled upward, blind, hands and knees dragging us forward. I led, Diskos ready. I tied two straps from the scrip and pouch, connecting my belt to hers—so I could feel her close behind.

We traveled sixteen-hour days. We ate and drank at the sixth and twelfth hours, again before sleep, and upon waking. Sleep was always eight hours. I guarded our strength carefully. The Night Land awaited.

Often, I grew sickened—exhausted, lost in endless darkness. Each step was a fumble, each climb a battle against invisible walls. It felt as if we wandered beyond life, trapped in a blackness that never lessened.

Then I would stop. Whisper to the Maid. Call her close. Pull her into my arms, out of the dark. And in that embrace, we found comfort.

And so we climbed—sixteen hours a day. Then, for eight days, we ascended through the most dreadful night. After the first day, we crawled on hands and knees. I led, the Diskos at my hip. The strap between us—my anchor.

We moved in rhythm, eating, drinking, sleeping. Our bodies grew numb. The darkness affected us—making us feel unreal, dreamlike.

Yet still, I stopped often. Called her near. Held her. And in those moments, we found strength—not just in will, but in love.

And so we climbed—upward, forever—into the heart of the Night Land.

I drew near to the Mighty Pyramid, and my great Home rose into the endless night like a mountain of life and safety—towering, immense, and awe-inspiring. Yet despair had hollowed my heart too deeply for wonder. All that mattered was getting Mine Own Maid into the shelter of the vast refuge. Still, it remained far beyond reach.

I pressed forward across the desolate land with grim determination. As I passed a hollow where a fire-hole burned, something emerged from the shadows. A massive, hirsute man crawled up, then rose to his feet. He stared at me, then stepped closer, hands outstretched, eager and wild. In the flickering light, I saw them clearly—monstrous hands, thick with cruel claws, capable of tearing flesh as easily as a beast.

I dropped Mine Own Maid to the ground. I no longer cared for life or survival. This creature would delay me, and I could not endure any obstacle. With a surge of cold fury, I lunged at him, swinging the Diskos. He sidestepped in an instant, avoiding the blow. Then, from the half-darkness cast by the dancing flames, he thrust his arm forward and seized my headpiece, wrenching it from my head with brutal force. The impact threw me back nearly a dozen feet, slamming me onto the earth. I was shaken, bruised—but not broken. I rose immediately, charged again, and brought the Diskos down hard. It struck true, cleaving through his midsection. The weapon drank deep, slicing clean through him. His upper body collapsed; the lower half stood upright, blood gushing upward like a fountain into the black sky.

I did not hesitate. I leapt to Mine Own Maid, cradled her in my arms, and ran past the fallen giant, who toppled only then with a terrible thud. The night itself seemed to stir—millions of spirits awakened, their cries rising around me, filled with awe and love. They had seen what I had done. They rejoiced. Their energy surged through me, a wave of shared purpose.

Not long after, two indistinct shapes emerged from behind jagged rocks. I struck them both with the Diskos and moved on. What they were, I never knew.

From then on, it felt as though I fought endlessly. Creatures crawled from bushes and crevices—beasts of every kind, twisted and monstrous. The land teemed with life, foul and deadly. I swung the Diskos in a daze, driven by a fierce, unrelenting desperation. Our time was running out. I could not save her. Not if I failed now.

The air groaned with roars and low, guttural sounds—deadly and unnatural. Once, I heard the thunderous tread of giants. The night was evil. And yet—I survived. How? Perhaps because I had been forged in fire, hardened by relentless trial. My body had become immune to weakness, my spirit armored against fear.

Then came the baying of the Night-Hounds—deep, dreadful, and drawing nearer from the southeast. I knew now: no strength of mine could protect her.

And from the Last Light high above, a strange blue flash lanced downward into the land. Again, and again—dozens of times. A crackling sound followed, sharper than thunder but more intense than any noise I’d ever known. I understood instantly—the Humans were fighting for me. They were using the Earth-Current to save us.

The land erupted into chaos. The night trembled. Monsters howled, stirred by some unseen force. From the hidden east, that terrible laughter echoed once more—ancient, mocking, and full of dread.

Now the Hounds bayed constantly, their pack unmistakable. They were less than a mile away. I was alone, save for the dying woman in my arms. I looked desperately for the Hundred Thousand—those who had prepared to aid me. But there was nothing. Only shadows, shifting lights, and the movement of monstrous life. The Hounds closed in with each passing second. Death was near.

I kept moving. Then I began to run. The Pyramid loomed ahead, its circle of light visible despite occasional veils of darkness. Hope, fragile and desperate, flared: perhaps we would make it.

The baying grew louder. The Hounds were half a mile away—perhaps less. They had scent of us. I called out, voice raw with anguish, but no one answered. No help came.

And then—through the spiritual link, I felt it: the Millions mourned with me. Their sorrow washed over me like a tide. They saw my despair, my frantic glances, my cries. In response, a gentle, powerful presence surrounded me—a warmth born of their empathy, their love, their understanding. They knew I was at the edge of hope. They knew the Hounds were upon me.

At that moment, the Earth-Current pulsed beneath my feet. I knew the Humans had unleashed their final defense.

Then I saw them—a vast pack of Hounds, charging from the left. Their heads were low, their bodies the size of horses. They came in waves, shadow and form merging in the dim light.

I realized: we would die within minutes unless they acted.

I stopped running. There was no point. I turned, staring first at the Hounds, then at the Pyramid. Again, I looked at the Hounds. Then at the Pyramid. Two hundred fathoms away. Hundreds of beasts. No escape.

And just as I gazed upon the Pyramid one last time—there it was.

A monstrous flame burst from the sealed base of the structure. It shot downward, engulfing the land where the Hounds ran. The night vanished in blinding light. I could see nothing—no Pyramid, no sky, only fire and radiant horror. The blast hit the air, scorching me even from afar. The heat withered my skin. I understood: the Humans had activated the Earth-Current. They had unleashed it upon the Hounds.

Thunder shook the world. The force tore through the air, split the earth. Rocks flew like cannonballs. The roar of monsters faded beneath the storm. I saw no Hounds—only fire, destruction, and chaos. I was thankful I hadn’t been crushed under a falling boulder.

Then, abruptly, the Current ceased. The Humans regained control.

Silence returned—absolute, crushing. Darkness swallowed everything, except the distant glow and smoke where the current had struck.

I shook off the daze and ran again. Now, I might survive.

My eyes adjusted. I scanned the surroundings, wary of new threats. For a time, there was nothing—only the distant, horrid laughter from the dead East.

As I ran, I glanced upward at the Pyramid. Its light dimmed. At first, I thought it was due to the glare of the explosion. But soon I realized: the light was truly fading. The power of the Earth-Current had been drained. The mighty fortress was weakening.

This chilled me. If the Current faltered too much, all the Humans—millions of lives—would be in danger. The Masters knew this. They could not use the Current again until it recovered. To do so would risk destroying all life on Earth.

I understood this fully as I ran—and it made me more desperate than ever.

Still, I expected the Hundred Thousand to come. They did not.

Around me, the land stirred again. New sounds arose—creatures awakening, forces stirring. I saw living things crawling between me and the Circle’s glow. I would have to fight again.

I raised the Diskos and pressed on.

Suddenly, my spirit sensed danger. I looked up, hoping the Master Monstruwacan would warn me via the Set Speech. But there was no signal—only silence and dimming light.

Later, I learned the Master *had* tried. But the instruments in the Tower of Observation had failed. The machinery of the Pyramid had shut down—lifts stalled, air pumps silenced. Everything had gone dark for nearly an hour, until the Current stabilized.

That meant death had nearly claimed the entire civilization.

Yet my spirit still felt the warning—not from machines, but from the pain of the Millions, from the faint telepathic call of the Master himself. I walked more cautiously, scanning every direction.

Then—above us—a pale, quiet circle hovered in the night. Steady. Unmoving. I recognized it: one of the Holy Powers, a guardian of souls. A shield against the darkness that threatened to consume us. I felt no fear. I trusted the Force of Holiness. I ran on.

I reached within four hundred paces of the Circle. I thought I had won. But the light of the Circle burned dim. Was it still protective? Could it guard us if the Current weakened?

I ran swiftly, warily, consumed by anxiety.

Then—three beast-men rose from the earth in a shadowed patch. They growled, charging. The first was too close for the Diskos. I struck him with the haft, smashing his skull. I leapt aside, spun, and swung. Fury gripped me—cold, absolute. The Maid was a child in my arm. I met the other two head-on, cutting with precision, swift and merciless. I killed them as if they were mice. None touched me.

In that moment, a shout erupted from within the Circle—joyful, welcoming. I looked, then ran faster. Men in grey armor stood inside, but none came to help.

Then I understood why.

Along the outer edge of the Circle, monstrous Black Mounds swayed and pulsed with unnatural life. Horrors churned beneath their surfaces. If any Human stepped beyond the boundary, they would be destroyed in spirit—lost forever. No one dared. Even sacrifice would be useless. Dead men could not save us.

But the Hundred Thousand shouted urgently: “Haste! Haste!”

I obeyed. I ran with all my strength.

The glowing Circle loomed ahead. The Holy Light above us held steady. We were saved.

I was less than a hundred paces away when the attack came.

An herd of squat, brutish men erupted from the shadows. They grabbed at me, at the Maid, trying to tear her from my arms.

They nearly succeeded. I couldnARED to free myself, defend her, and wield the Diskos at once.

Then—kicks from my metal boots. Twisting. Struggling. I broke free and stumbled back. The herd pursued.

Now I had room. I turned, charged, and cut through them. The Diskos spun, roaring, casting eerie light on their pig-like tusks. I raged through them, striking from right and left, circling fast. They struck me with stones—thousands of blows. My armor rang, cracked. I was battered, wounded, near collapse. But they never reached the Maid. She remained safe above their reach.

The brutes seemed endless. But I advanced, toward the Circle. The night echoed with the shouts of the Hundred Thousand. Many had tried to rush forward—but others held them back. Sacrifice would mean nothing.

Fifty paces. I was nearly spent. Dazed, bleeding, exhausted. I had not slept. I had carried her through days and nights. Fought countless battles.

Then—before the Circle, the Hundred Thousand stood ready. Each man raised his Diskos. They hurled them into the swarm.

It worked. The herd thinned. I gathered my strength and charged, smiting without pause. Bodies fell all around. I broke through, clutching Mine Own Maid, and stepped over the Circle.

Its resistance was weak—almost gone.

A thousand hands reached out—but none touched me. They recoiled. Something about me repelled them. I was changed. Strange.

I stood in silence. The Diskos dripped blood to the haft. I swayed. Hands reached again—then pulled back. Fear, respect, awe.

I looked at them. They looked back. I gasped, struggling to speak. I needed doctors. My Maid was dying.

Then—outside, the sound of giants running.

Voices cried out: “Help him!” “Beware the giants!” “Bring the doctors!”

Others shouted: “The Holy Light is gone!” “The Black Mounds are vanishing!”

The land roared. The noise overwhelmed me. My mind, already shattered by exhaustion, began to fail.

But another sound rose—soft, constant, coming from above and near. The voice of millions—shouting upward, a murmuring chorus from the heights, echoing through the miles.

I found my voice. “Is there a doctor here?”

A Master of the Diskos stepped forward—commander of this age. He saluted with his weapon, offered to take the Maid. I asked again: “Is there a Doctor?”

He gave an order. Thousands shifted, forming a path to the Great Gateway.

The Master signaled. Men stood around me—supporting, but not touching. I was not to be touched. I feared I had come too late. And they seemed to sense my strangeness.

Orders flew. Soon, two men from the Upward Cities arrived, carrying a small figure on a sling. A Master Doctor. He helped me lay the Maid gently on the ground.

The Master of the Diskos gestured. The guards turned away. The Doctor knelt.

Silence fell. The Hundred Thousand stood still. The Pyramid was quiet. Everyone knew: the fate of the maid rested on this moment.

Then the Doctor looked up—calm, sorrowful.

I knew.

Mine Own Maid was dead.

He saw my knowing. He covered her face. Rose quickly. Whispered orders. Some men approached to support me. Others lifted her body, bearing her toward the Gateway.

He studied me closely. I fought to breathe. Then I raised my hands—signaling them not to touch me, nor her.

The Doctor understood. I was strong—until the end.

I bent slowly, lifted Mine Own Maid into my arms once more—for the final journey.

I walked down the silent lane of the Hundred Thousand, clad in grey armor. Each man reversed his Diskos in salute, silently honoring me.

I barely registered anything. The world was empty. My mission had failed.

Yet—had it truly?

I had saved her from the terror of the Second Night Land. She had not died alone, mad and lost. She had died in my arms. And she had known, in her spirit, the depth of my love.

Memories flooded me—her soft touches, her quiet smiles, her whispered words. Then—like a knife—recollection: I had never woken to find her kissing me in sleep, as I had meant to.

A wave of agony tore through me. I blinked, vision blurred. I staggered. But the Master Doctor steadied me briefly—then let go. I walked on, alone.

As I neared the Great Gateway, the Pyramid’s lights flared again. The lifts whirred. The air pumps hummed. The Earth-Current had recovered. The gate opened—mechanized, grand.

Masters emerged. The dear Master Monstruwacan led them, rushing forward with the urgency of a father.

He had heard rumors—fear for the maid.

One near the gateway told him: she was dead.

He paused. All the Masters stood silent. Each reversed his Diskos.

This was the highest honor.

And I walked through—bearing the weight of grief, of love, of loss.

But also—of salvation.

When I returned to life, I found myself ascending in the Lift, lying upon that same bed where I had once believed I would never need rest again—nor ever rise from the depths of the Country of Silence. My awareness was faint, drifting like smoke on a still night. From the endless dark beneath the world, I heard the deep thunder of the Underground Organs—a distant, mournful music that seemed to echo beyond death itself. A rolling chant rose and fell, as though countless voices sang across far mountains; sometimes it was the whisper of wind through caverns, other times it swelled into the ancient melody of the Song of Honour. I sensed, half-dreaming, that the millions below were rejoicing, celebrating this miracle of joy that had come to us.

But all was dim and veiled. My eyes could not open fully. I floated on currents of unreality, caught between waking and dreaming. Yet there were scents—sweet and real—that drifted to me: the perfume of blossoms from the great Fields, blooming endlessly along the paths of the Lifts. The machine carried me upward through miles of silence, yet life stirred around me.

I may have moved slightly. Then came the soft voice of the Master Doctor, gentle as moonlight: “Rest. All is well with the Maid.”

And so I slipped into a haze, drifting through days that blurred between sleep and wakefulness. I no longer knew if I lived or died. Then came a time when I lay still, thoughtless, while the Master Doctor bent over me often, his gaze sharp and searching.

At last, another figure leaned close—Mine Own. Her face, dear and radiant, looked down at me. Her eyes spoke love directly into my soul, calm and quiet, yet full of strength. And then, slowly, I began to live again. I fumbled weakly with my hands—she took them in hers. Life flowed from her to me. She said nothing, only held me, tender and still. Contentment grew within me, and soon I sank into a peaceful slumber.

A day came when I was allowed to sit up. Those who cared for me carried me to one of the Quiet Gardens of the Pyramid and left me there, alone.

Then, around a bush, she appeared—shy, yet glowing. It was Mine Own Maid. But I had never seen Naani dressed so beautifully, so delicately. She looked more exquisite than I had ever imagined. I tried to rise toward her—but she darted forward, stopping me with a laugh, gentle and loving. She sat beside me, cradling my head against her breast, offering her lips without hesitation. In that moment, she was both maiden and mother, fierce and tender.

Afterward, she insisted I remain still. We sat together in perfect silence, wrapped in a happiness too deep for words, until the attendants returned. Among them was the Master of the Doctors, and I saw satisfaction in his face.

From that day on, I saw her every day. I recovered swiftly—not from medicine, but from Love. Soon, I was permitted to descend into the Fields, though by hidden paths, for the Multitudes might have followed me, drawn by curiosity and reverence. I needed peace.

She was with me always. The Master Monstruwacan and the Master of the Doctors had agreed—so an Officer of Marriage wed us. Our wedding was simple, quiet. I was still too weak for the grand Public Marriage, which would come later. But when it did, the Millions formed a Guard of Honour eight miles high, stretching from the summit to the base of the Mighty Pyramid—a ceremonial tribute, for they could not deny me their honor.

Yet even then, she remained by my side. She was now my wife, my strength, my healing. My health returned completely, and we entered the Love Days—the most beautiful time of all, when Love is true.

We wandered freely through the vast Fields, walking the Love Paths near the scattered villages. I kept our name hidden, lest others seek us out in kindness or curiosity. We needed only each other, and quiet.

We chose our resting places where flowers bloomed in wild splendor, carrying food with us, but also eating in the villages—small towns spread across lands as large as nations. She fulfilled her promise a hundredfold, preparing hearty meals and teasing me playfully as I ate, kissing me before I could defend myself. She was everything my heart and spirit longed for—companion, lover, soul.

Once, we descended into the Country of Silence, but stayed only briefly, for my memories were returning. Later, we returned often—walking through that sacred land with memory, holiness, and love holding us together.

As we left, I told her how, when she had been suspended in death by the Horrid Force of the House, I had suffered a terrible pain—the knowledge that I had slept through her final kiss. She blushed deeply, lovely and ashamed, for she had never known I was aware of her secret tenderness. She confessed that she had felt only agony during her stillness, unaware of the world until the Earth-Force revived her.

The Doctors explained that her spirit had been stunned, frozen—her life suspended. Only the immense life-force of the Earth-Current had awakened her, restoring her body, her blood, her breath. They had searched ancient records and found one similar case—yet none like this in ages.

As we walked and rested in the Fields, I shared my thoughts with her. I learned she had studied many things while I was unconscious—though not much, for she too had been near death. When I lay still, she had risen from her own bed, driven by the Master Doctor’s command—he feared I might die unless my spirit was stirred. And she had held my hands, brave and gentle, though she could barely stand.

I whispered a silent prayer of gratitude for her courage.

And so I come to the end of my tale—save for one final thing.

Some time after our second marriage—the Public Ceremony—one day, my Wife, Mine Own, led me with sweet cunning to the Hall of Honour.

When I entered, I saw the People gathered in silence, standing still as if waiting for something greater than words. She guided me to the center—and then I understood why.

In the Place of Honour stood a statue: a man in shattered armor, holding a maid forever in his arms.

I stood dumbfounded.

You who live in this age may not know what such an honour means. It is given only to the Great Dead—those whose deeds echo through eternity. I was young, unknown, far from greatness. Yet here I stood, honoured not for power or conquest, but for love—for having loved with every part of my being.

And you will understand, as I did, that Love makes the heart brave, the soul sweet. I felt wonder, humility, and a quiet pride that no title could match. That anyone should think to honour me in such a way—was a gift beyond measure.

Mine Own wept beside me—joy and pride shining in her eyes. The entire hall was hushed, filled with a profound, loving silence. No one spoke. No one interrupted. They simply let us go, together, in peace.

And I walked with her—loving, thoughtful, whole. She was close, always near.

I had gained honour. But I had learned that honour is but ash without love.

I had love.

And to have love is to have all.

For true love gives birth to honour and faithfulness—and together, the three build the House of Joy.
