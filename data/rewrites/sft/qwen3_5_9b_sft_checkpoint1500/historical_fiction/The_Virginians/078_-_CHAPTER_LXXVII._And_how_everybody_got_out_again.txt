Captain Miles Warrington, you possess a rare distinction: the favor of the Duchess of Devonshire, the attentions of Mrs. Crew, the gaze of the Queen of Prussia, and the admiration of countless others. Yet, if you secure a lady's heart, do you invariably march into the mess to recount your triumphs?"

"Not a fool, Squire!" retorted the Captain, scrutinizing his sideburns in the mirror.

"Have you, Miss Theo, recounted every syllable spoken to Mr. Joe Blake, Jr., in the shrubbery this morn?"

"Joe Blake, indeed!" cried Theo Junior.

"And you, Mademoiselle? Did you reveal the contents of that scented billet, franked by Sir Thomas? Observe her blush! It rivals the crimson curtain, I swear! Nay, Mademoiselle, we all harbor secrets," the Squire declared, executing a bow of his finest French persuasion. "No, Theo, there was naught in the shrubbery save nuts, my child! Nor do we divulge everything, not even to the most benevolent of fathers. And were I to recount my journey in a landau along Hampstead Road on the twenty-fifth of May, 1760, may Chevalier Ruspini pluck every tooth from my skull!"

"Do tell, Papa!" urged Mama. "Or, since Jobson drives us, perchance you will summon him from the stables! I demand the tale!"

"What is this mystery?" asked Mademoiselle, her charming French accent softening the query, directed at my wife.

"Eh, ma fille!" whispered the lady. "Should I answer what I said? I said 'Yes!'—see, that is all I said." Thus, it was my wife who had betrayed us, not I. Our exchange, as the carriage raced toward Hampstead and thundered back, amounted to this: Theo had not sworn to defy her honored parents. We, however, resolved to wed no other soul—not if we lived to Methuselah's age, nor would she accept the Prince of Wales himself. She had surrendered her heart with her father's consent, nay, his command; it was not hers to reclaim. Yet, a father of such kindness must eventually relent. If George kept his promise—whether today, in twenty years, or in another world—she knew she would never break hers.

Hetty's face lit with pure delight when I concluded my brief audience, seeing Theo's countenance wear a tranquil sweetness. "All the physician's medicine has done her less good," the devoted sister murmured. After their act of defiance, the girls returned home. I relinquished my post beside my cherished invalid. Hetty bounded back to her seat, and Charley scrambled onto his box. He later confessed that the sermon he had heard was dreadfully dull and obtuse; the little fellow was too orthodox to appreciate dissenting preachers.

Hetty was not the solitary observer of her sister's transformed visage and elevated spirits. Upon the girls' return, their mother embraced both, particularly the invalid, with an ardour surpassing the norm. "Nothing compares to a country ride," Aunt Lambert observed. "It has done her dear Theo wonders. She traversed the road to Hampstead, did she not? She requires another ride tomorrow. Blessed be heaven, Lord Wrotham's horses stand at our disposal thrice weekly, and the sweet child shall enjoy their advantage!" As for the notion that Mr. Warrington might have encountered the children during their drive, Aunt Lambert never entertained it, or at least, never voiced it. I leave it to those intrigued by the matter to conjecture whether Mrs. Lambert could possibly have imagined her daughter and her sweetheart reuniting. Do women aid one another in matters of love? Do they scheme, intrigue, weave small lies, fashion romantic opportunities, dangle the rope-ladder, coax and wheedle guardians, and divert attention while Strephon and Chloe whisper in the twilight or flee to Gretna Green? My dear young ones, some folk are thus constituted; and some kind souls, having loved tenderly and truly in their own time, remain kindly and tenderly disposed toward their young successors as they commence their own playful games.

Miss Prim differs. If she hears of two youths attached, she snaps at them as fools or imagines every conceivable malice. Possessed of a humpback, she bites at everyone else's. I believe if she spotted a pair of turtles cooing in a wood, she would cast down her gaze or hurl a stone to startle them. Yet, I speak of your grandmother, Aunt Lambert, who was a veritable syllabub of human kindness. Furthermore, concerning the affair currently under discussion, how am I to discern whether she knew anything of it or not?

Thus, all she imparted to Theo upon her return was, "My child, the country air has wrought miracles for you, and I hope you shall venture out again tomorrow, and the day after, and so on."

"Pray, do you not deem the ride has effected a wondrous transformation in the child, and ought she not be compelled to breathe the fresh air?" Aunt Lambert asked the General as he arrived for supper.

"Yes, assuredly! If a coach-and-six benefits little Theo, she shall have it," Lambert proclaimed. "Or he shall drag the landau up Hampstead Hill himself if horses are lacking!" And so, the generous man would have squandered his guineas, his breath, or his blood to grant his child pleasure. He was enchanted by his girl's altered countenance; she devoured a morsel of chicken with appetite; she sipped a negus he prepared, which seemed superior to the kind doctor's best remedies, which, God witting, had yielded little benefit. Mama was gracious and joyful. Hetty shone with radiance. It resembled an evening at home at Oakhurst. Not for months past, not since that fatal, cruel day unspoken of, had they shared such a delightful night.

Yet, if the other women sought to coax and cajole the simple father, Theo herself was too honest to sustain that sweet, fond delusion. When, for the third or fourth time, he returned to the delightful topic of his daughter's restored health, asking, "What hath wrought this? Is it the country air? Is it the Jesuit's bark? Is it the new medicine?"

"Cannot you divine, dear, what it is?" she said, placing a hand upon her father's with a tremble in her voice, though her eyes remained open and bright.

"And what is it, my child?" asked the General.

"It is because I have seen him again, Papa!" she declared.

The other two women turned pale, and Theo's heart began to pound, her cheeks flushing white as she continued to gaze into her father's terrified face.

"It was not wrong to behold him," she pressed, more urgently; "it would have been wrong not to inform you."

"Great God!" groaned the father, recoiling his hand, his countenance etched with dreadful grief, until Hetty ran to her nearly swooning sister, clutching her to her bosom and crying out, "Theo knew nothing of it, sir! It was my doing—it was all my doing!"

Theo lay upon her sister's neck, kissing it twenty, fifty times.

"Women, women! Are ye playing with my honor?" cried the father, bursting forth with a fierce exclamation.

"Aunt Lambert sobbed wildly, 'Martin! Martin! Speak not a word to her!'" Hetty shrieked again, collapsing backward, staggering toward the wall, for Theo had fainted upon her shoulder.

I took my breakfast the following morn, with whatever appetite I possessed, when my door opened and my faithful black announced, "General Lambert." Instantly, by the General's visage, I perceived that the prior day's transaction was known to him. "Your accomplices did not confess," the General stated as soon as my servant departed, "but sided with you against their father—a testament to how desirable clandestine meetings are. It was from Theo herself I learned she had beheld you."

"Accomplices, sir!" I replied (perhaps not unwilling to deflect the conversation from the crux of the issue). "You know how fondly and dutifully your young ones regard their father. If they oppose you herein, it must be because justice stands against you. A man like you shall not erect *sic volo, sic jubeo* as the sole law in his household!"

"Pshaw, George!" cried the General. "Though we are parted, God forbid I should wish our love to cease. I had promised you you would not seek to see her."

"Nor did I go to her, sir," I said, turning a deep shade of red, for though this was truth, I owned it was untrue.

"You mean she was brought to you?" asked Theo's father, in great agitation. "Shall you shelter yourself behind Hester's petticoat? What a fine defense for a gentleman!"

"Well, I will not screen myself behind the poor child," I rejoined. "To speak as I did was an attempt at evasion, and I am ill-accustomed to dissimulation. I did not infringe the letter of my agreement, but I acted against its spirit. From this moment I annul it altogether."

"You break your word given to me!" cried Mr. Lambert.
