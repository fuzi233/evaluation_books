My journey across the vast continent of Atlantis was long, fraught with peril, and marked by solitude—so much so that only one pause in my travels deserves mention, and even that was not by choice. One day, I brought down a stag after a grueling chase across an open savannah. After ensuring the area was clear of danger, I began to cut the meat into portions, preparing a bundle for transport.

But beneath the grass, hidden from sight, coiled a monstrous green worm—great-legged and venomous. As I shifted my weight, my heel brushed its body, and in a flash it lunged, fangs sinking deep into my lower thigh. I reacted swiftly, slashing through its neck with my knife. It writhed violently, its hundred legs twitching in grotesque spasms. Then, cleaning my blade on the earth, I drove it repeatedly around the wound, forcing blood to flow freely—hoping to flush out the poison. The blood trickled down my leg, warm and steady, and I shouldered my meat and pressed onward, grateful to have escaped what might have become a fatal encounter.

Yet within hours, a tightening pain seized my leg, growing stronger with each passing mile. Soon, swelling followed, stretching my skin taut as parchment. A fever took hold, shaking me with violent chills. Had any predator come upon me then, I would have been helpless. As night fell, I dragged myself into a tree, perching in the crook of a branch, enduring agony through the long darkness.

At dawn, I descended, leaning heavily on my spear. The moss on the north side of trees had always guided me, but now I noticed it encircled the trunks—everywhere. My head swam. Still, I pressed forward, driven by duty: to fulfill the High Council’s command and reclaim Nais, as promised.

My final memory was of a figure emerging from the black forest—someone who helped me. After that, consciousness slipped away.

When I awoke, I found myself on a bed of leaves in a narrow cleft between two rocks, crudely furnished and guarded with stakes against predators. My wound was bound with a poultice of herbs. At the far end of the cave, a woman sat roasting wood-grubs and honey over a fire.

“How did I get here?” I asked.

“I carried you,” she replied.

“And who are you?”

“A nymph,” she said. “I gather herbs, heal the sick, read fortunes, predict futures. In return, people give what they can. If they don’t, I curse them—let them wither. Not much living these days, when war and plague have left so few.”

“Do you travel to Atlantis?”

“No. Phorenice would boil me alive if she could. She tolerates no magic but her own. They say she plans to pull the Priests off their mountain.”

“So you hear news of the city?”

“Of course. That’s how I earn my trade. And I knew you weren’t just another seeker of spells. Your speech marked you as noble. No need to fear speaking plainly.”

“Then tell me—does Phorenice still rule?”

“Most cruelly.”

“As a maiden?”

“She is mother to twin sons. Tatho is her husband now—has been three years.”

“Tatho? The viceroy of Yucatan?”

“There is no Yucatan. A horde of small, hairy men came from the west—thousands strong. They wielded stone-tipped clubs, nothing more. But numbers were their weapon. They didn’t seek gold or slaves. Only flesh—Atlantean flesh. They devoured entire cities, leaving ruins behind, then moved on. When they reached the coast, Tatho and a handful escaped by sea. Even his wife was eaten. These creatures are relentless. May the Gods keep them from crossing the seas—otherwise, they’d be worse than Phorenice herself.”

I had heard whispers of such beings—half-human, half-beast, creatures held in check by divine decree. Sometimes, when justice demanded it, they were unleashed as instruments of destruction. But I said nothing to this woman—she was merely a folk healer, perhaps foolish, like most who dabble in magic for profit. Yet I grieved deeply for Yucatan, where I had once labored so hard.

Still, I kept my identity hidden. “What of Egypt?” I asked.

“In darkness, they say. Who cares about Egypt now? Everyone only thinks of themselves. Once, villagers brought offerings here out of piety. Now, they come only for gain. I live on grubs and honey. A poor life, a nymph’s life in these times. No one believes in mysteries anymore.”

“What of the pestilence?”

“I’ve seen none. Thank the Gods it hasn’t reached here. But it’s said to have grown from the unburied dead—those Phorenice has slain. She kills constantly, and the bodies pile up faster than beasts can consume them. Why did the Gods allow her to rise? I wish she and her children were boiled in brine before they ruined a honest nymph’s livelihood.”

“They say she’s placed her image in every temple. Mandated prayers, sacrifices twice daily. Declared all other gods inferior. Those who refuse to worship her properly have their hamstrings cut—so they’ll kneel in devotion forever.”

“Will you eat my grubs and honey? There’s nothing else. You were bleeding from your back when I found you—your pack gone. Either taste this, or go hungry.”

I stayed six days, recovering under her care. When healed, I hunted and killed a cloven-hoofed horse for her, then continued my journey.

The land beyond had once thrived—villages dotted the forests, homes built high in trees or low on the ground from massive logs, interlocked and fortified. Such dwellings offered safety from cave-bears and tigers. But now, scarcely one in ten remained. Houses stood silent, hearths choked with weeds, gardens reclaimed by jungle. Villages with thick walls lay abandoned—gates fallen, logs scattered, only middens marking where humans once lived. Phorenice’s reign seemed to have spread like a blight across a once-fertile realm.

So far, I had met little resistance. Some fled at my approach; others stood their ground but did not attack. A few offered shelter. But near the city, paths grew muddy and crowded. Watchmen atop towers would cry: “A masterless man!” and villagers would rush out to seize my weapons, then assign me to labor.

I had no desire to kill them—pity filled me at their downfall. Words often sufficed to let me pass. When they failed, I struck lightly, aiming only to move forward without unnecessary bloodshed. Eventually, I began avoiding villages altogether, taking forest paths to skirt their stench and danger.

As I neared Atlantis, the Sacred Mountain loomed beside the city, crowned by a ring of eternal fires. Caution became paramount. The countryside was drained of soldiers. Women prayed for the end of the siege—either the fall of the Priests or the death of Phorenice. Peace was all they desired.

An army surrounded the mountain, guarding its single access tightly. After so many miles, I feared I’d reach the city only to be stopped short. Force was useless—my fight would be brief, glorious, and futile. One man against an army. My mission would end there.

No. This called for cunning. I found cover in the woods, lying there, thinking desperately of a way in.

Before me stretched the city’s grim walls—temples, palaces, pyramids rising beyond. The royal pyramid caught my eye. Its steps had been plated with gold, Phorenice’s vanity on display. But it was not the gold that stirred me. I remembered the square before it—the granite throne carved with a serpent and an outstretched hand. Beneath it, in a hollow, slept Nais—my love—drugged for eight years, yet alive, waiting only for me to reach her.

In that hiding place, I raged for a day and night, tormented by failure. But at dawn, a man ran toward me.

“You needn’t threaten me,” he shouted. “I mean no harm. You’re Deucalion—though I wouldn’t have recognized you in those rags and that beard. I know you’ll give me your word. I’ve wasted no time.”

He was Ro, a priest I knew—greedy, unreliable. “Where do you come from?” I asked.

“Zaemon sent me. He appeared at my house—how, I don’t know. The army blocks all roads to the mountain. I told him I wanted no part of his schemes. I’m a peaceful man, Deucalion. I have a wife to feed. I still serve in the temple, though we’ve replaced the old gods with Phorenice’s image. People are pious now—offering more than ever. So why risk losing my post, or my head?”

“I believe you,” I said.

“But Zaemon wasn’t listening. He flew into a rage. I felt death coming. You know his powers?”

“I’ve seen some.”

“He has power—no denying it. But I serve the god who feeds me. Yet Zaemon’s gods give him strength. Look at my right hand—it’s paralyzed, dead from the wrist. He gave it to me—‘to focus my attention.’ Then he accused me of apostasy. I couldn’t defend myself. Finally, he said I could either obey him fully—or suffer the punishment of my betrayal. ‘I won’t kill you,’ he said, ‘but I’ll paralyze every limb. When you die in a gutter, you’ll welcome it.’”

“If Zaemon said that, he meant it. So you agreed?”

“With a wife depending on me, what choice did I have? I asked his orders. Find you when you arrive—and deliver his message.”

“Then tell me the meeting place.”

“There is none. You must watch and search. But when he comes, you’ll be led to him.” He paused. “I think I was led. I don’t know how. But I’ve found you. If there’s gratitude, please speak to Zaemon about my hand. It’s unbearable—no fault of mine. He could cure it with a word.”

“You still trust the old priests’ powers,” I said. “But you haven’t delivered your message.”

“It’s simple. You’re to make a great blaze—burn the grasses before this wood. Then, on the following night, go to the flank of the mountain opposite the city, where the cliffs drop sheer. A rope will be lowered. They’ll haul you up.”

“It seems straightforward. Thank you. I’ll ask Zaemon to restore your hand.”

“You shall have my prayers. And look—this is small, but it might slip your mind later. Do it now, when you arrive. Phorenice will try to pull you down once she knows you’re there. She never fails. She’s becoming a goddess—already she’s mastered the ancient knowledge of our priests. She’s discovered the Secret of Life…”

“She’s found it?” I gasped. “How? Only the Three know that. Even the Seven aren’t told.”

“I know nothing of her methods. But she has it. She claims to be immortal now. Equal to the gods.”

I stared at him. “You’re risking much by being gone.”

“I must return. Please remember me. And—” He glanced at my ragged clothes. “If you value my service…?”

“You see me poor,” I said. “Nearly destitute.”

“A coin? Or a bronze link? I need food for my wife. If you have nothing—then farewell.”

He left. I didn’t regret it. I never liked Ro. But I wasted no time. Before he was out of sight, I lit the grass. Flames leapt high.

That night, under cover of darkness, I made for the cliffside. No rope hung—then, slowly, one descended, thin and sinuous. I looped it over my leg, pulled hard. Above, hands began hauling. I rose, unseen, back to the Sacred Mountain after years of exile.

The men pulling me were old, breathing heavily. I wondered why younger priests weren’t chosen—until I realized: in the Priest Clan, obedience is assumed. Orders are given for reason. Questions are not asked.

They brought me straight to Zaemon.

He greeted me with the royal sign. “All hail, Deucalion—King of Atlantis, duly called by the High Council.”

“Is Phorenice dead?” I asked.

“It remains for you to slay her. To claim your kingdom—if anything remains to rule. The sentence has been passed. She must die, even if we must unleash the darkest powers from the Ark of Mysteries and destroy this continent. We’ve endured her crimes for years—by divine command. But now she has crossed the line. The Gods have spoken.”

“You are one of the Three. I am only of the Seven. You know the cost.”

“There can be no counting the cost now, brother, king. It is an order.”

“It is an order,” I repeated. “I obey.”

“If it were not impious, I could justify it. She usurped the throne—yet was forgiven. She corrupted our religion—forgiven. She waged senseless wars, destroyed trade—forgiven. She defiled temples, set up her own image—forgiven. But now—she has discovered the Secret of Life and Death. That is beyond tolerance.”

“I confronted her in the banqueting hall. I read the edict. She laughed. She said she would live forever, share the secret with whom she chose. Tatho would be immortal too. She would shout it aloud—let all know. Death from age would be a myth.”

“She cared nothing for nature’s laws. She is Phorenice—her own highest law. She defied me. Defied the Gods behind my voice. ‘My magic equals yours, you pompous fool,’ she screamed. ‘Soon, we shall stand side by side on trial.’”

From that moment, she gathered her army. We prepared ours. Our arts revealed you lived. You were elected King. How you were summoned—only you know. But now you are here. As King, you must lead.”

“Then as King, I speak without threat: I must have my Queen first. Without her, I cannot rule.”

“She shall be brought.”

“Good. Then I shall be General. I will inspect the defenses, the men.”

I stepped outside. Zaemon followed. Night still reigned—but on the mountain crest, no darkness existed. A ring of fires burned eternally, fed by the breath of the earth, lighting the summit as brightly as daylight. These flames were not mere light—they were defense. Legend says the first men came to the mountain because of this natural fortress.

And now, I stood among them again—ready to reclaim what was mine.
