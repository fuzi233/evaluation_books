John Durrance stepped off the steamer from Egypt one sun-dappled June morning, and by that same afternoon he’d laced up his scuffed campaign boots and taken the first unhurried walk of his exile back home, wandering straight into Hyde Park. He sank onto a bench beneath an old oak, staring half-mesmerized at the easy grace of the women drifting past in their crisp pastel summer gowns. He was a solitary, striking figure: skin baked dark as worn leather from three years under desert sun, the quiet, watchful tightness around his eyes that marks every man who has spent years posted to the far, unforgiving edges of the Empire, cut off from all soft, familiar comforts of home.

Of all the strollers that sauntered past, only one smiled at him. When he stood to stretch his legs, Mrs. Adair glided to his side, and ran a quick, almost furtive glance over him from head to toe, as if checking a fragile treasure for cracks. She was comparing the weathered soldier in front of her to the Durrance she’d known three years prior, searching for every small shift time had carved into him, her gaze sharp with quiet unspoken anxiety. All Durrance noticed, though, was that she wore unrelieved black. She read the unasked question on his face before he could speak.
“My husband died eighteen months ago,” she said evenly. “Thrown from his horse mid-run with the Pytchley. Killed on the spot.”
“I never heard the news,” Durrance said, awkward and ill at ease. “I’m terribly sorry.”
Mrs. Adair sat down on the empty chair next to him and said no more. She was a woman of confounding silences, her pale, classically placid face giving no clue at all to the thoughts churning behind her eyes. Durrance fidgeted, out of his depth. He remembered Adair as a jolly, mild man whose only defining trait had been his unreserved devotion to his wife, but he’d never once stopped to wonder how the woman beside him had felt about that shadow of a husband. He couldn’t dredge up enough genuine grief to fill the silence, so he pivoted quickly.
“Are Harry Feversham and his wife in town yet?”
Mrs. Adair paused for a long beat. “Not yet,” she said, then corrected herself sharply, voice hurrying as if she’d blurted a half-lie by accident. “No— the marriage never took place.”

Durrance was not a man given to gasps or exclamations, even when shocked. “I don’t understand. Why was it called off?”
Mrs. Adair snapped a sharp glance at him, as if she was searching for some hidden trick behind his steady, unflappable tone. “I don’t know. Ethne can keep a secret tighter than a locked safe when she chooses. It broke the night of the dance at Lennon House.”
Durrance turned to face her fully. “The night right before I sailed for the Soudan three years back?”
“Yes.”
“Then a lot of odd pieces fell into place that very night I stood on Dover pier,” Durrance said slowly. “I didn’t know the engagement was broken, but I saw a man on that pier as the Channel ferry cast off, face haggard and wild with despair. That was Harry, wasn’t it? He left the country that same night, no one’s seen him since?”
Mrs. Adair shrugged. “Not a soul I’ve spoken to. He’s vanished completely.”
Durrance thought that over for a long minute, then asked, a faint, uncharacteristic tremor in his voice, “And Miss Eustace? She’s married now, I suppose?”
Mrs. Adair shook her head. “No.”
“Still living at Lennon House?”
“Lennon House burned to the ground a year ago,” she said, and Durrance’s eyes went wide. She told him the whole ridiculous, catastrophic story of Constable Bastable, the toddy recipe that required a single simmered oyster to be perfect, the two men getting roaring drunk at two a.m. and knocking over the spirit lamp under the saucepan. Half the house burned to ash before they could call the fire brigade, the rest was gutted and ruined by thousands of gallons of hose water. Dermod Eustace, already drowning under a mountain of gambling debts, went completely bankrupt the second the news spread. He caught a brutal chill from the freezing hoses that wrecked his once robust health for good, and Ethne dragged him off to a tiny, isolated mountain village in Donegal, all their land and old home lost forever.
“The village is called Glenalla,” she added, not looking at him, her voice flat as if she was forcing herself to say the words against some unspoken reservation. “A track leads up to it off the main road halfway between Rathmullen and Ramelton.” She stood, held out her hand. “Will I see you around town?”
“For a while, yes,” Durrance said.
Mrs. Adair raised an eyebrow, confused. She couldn’t fathom why the clearly smitten soldier wasn’t catching the first train north to Donegal the second she gave him the directions. She saw him loitering in the inner courtyard of his Service Club for weeks after that, camped out under a great spreading oak, cornering every single old friend of Harry Feversham’s that wandered through, pestering them for scraps of news about the vanished man. None of them knew a thing. He’d even trundled out to Surrey to call on Harry’s father, General Feversham, but the second he so much as breathed his missing son’s name, the old veteran’s face set hard as plaster, blank and unyielding as a mask, and refused to utter another word on the subject. Durrance headed back to London no closer to answers than he’d left.

Toward the end of June, a limping retired naval lieutenant named Sutch hobbled into the courtyard, spotted Durrance, hesitated, and immediately turned to scuttle away at top speed. Durrance shot to his feet.
“Mr. Sutch,” he called. “Sure you haven’t forgotten an old acquaintance?”
Sutch froze, turned around, flustered. “Colonel Durrance! Of course I remember you. Old man’s memory’s like a leaky whaler, though— half my cargo of recollections swamped at port.”
Durrance didn’t buy the act for a second. “We met at Broad Place. I’ve been waiting here a full month for you. I need two answers: why was Harry’s engagement broken off, and where on earth is he now?”
Sutch’s eyes gleamed for a split second with relief. He’d always been terrified someone would spill Harry’s secret to Durrance, and clearly the decorated soldier still hadn’t heard a whisper of his friend’s disgrace. “I can answer both,” he admitted, scratching at his beard, “but I won’t. I swore a solemn oath to Harry before he vanished that I’d hold my tongue.”
“Harry’s my friend,” Durrance said, quiet and steady. “I’m not asking out of idle curiosity. I won’t press you for your secrets. But I have a different question. A much harder one for me to say out loud.” His sunburnt cheeks flushed dark. “Would it be disloyalty to Harry, if I courted Miss Eustace? If I asked her to marry me?”
The question hit Sutch like a fist to the jaw. He stared at Durrance, suddenly reeling. He’d nursed a quiet, fierce, motherly kind of protectiveness over Harry for years, daydreaming of the day the disgraced boy would come back from wherever he was hiding, restored to honor, win Ethne back, take his old place in the world like a king retaking his throne. Now here was Durrance, war hero, steady, brave, the kind of man women fell for hard, ready to step in and claim the life Harry had thrown away.
He was tempted to lie, more tempted than he’d ever been in his life. All he had to do was say yes, it would be disloyal, and Durrance would head straight back to his posting in the Soudan, leave Harry’s path completely clear. He pictured Harry thousands of miles away, toiling under the blistering desert sun, suffering for his honor, then looked back at the comfortable, decorated soldier in front of him. For a second he almost broke his oath entirely, spilled every last secret, told Durrance the full story of the four feathers.
“Well?” Durrance prompted, quiet.
Sutch sighed, heavy with regret. Harry had never wanted Ethne to wait for him forever, after all. “No,” he said. “There would be no disloyalty.”
That very same evening, Durrance boarded the overnight train bound for Holyhead, heading straight for Donegal.
