A month after that Carnival, on a morning near the end of March, Tito came down the marble steps of the Old Palace with the measured ease of a man whose errand was heavy but whose face must never confess it. The air had the thin, rinsed cleanness of early spring; night’s damp still clung in the joints of the stones, and the city, waking, was a choir of small noises—the slap of sandals on flagging, the creak of shutters, the cough of a mule in some courtyard, and, above all, the bells, whose bronze throats kept calling and calling, as if sound alone could hold Florence together.

He was bound for San Marco; yet he chose not the straight way, but a circuit by Santa Croce, where the people would be streaming out after the early sermon. It was a roundabout road, but it offered what he wanted: a crowd, already warmed by argument, and the thick hum of many voices that could be steered, as one steers a pot by turning the flame and letting the simmer do the work.

Savonarola’s voice had been silenced now—even in San Marco. The Signoria, hostile and harried, had obeyed a new papal letter, threatening Florence with an interdict if that “wretched worm” and “monstrous idol” were not forbidden to preach and sent to Rome to ask pardon. And when a man cannot be heard, the next best thing, for those who hunger for heat, is to hear him assaulted. In Santa Croce the Franciscan appointed for the Quaresimal sermons had offered to clinch his arguments by walking through fire with Fra Girolamo. Had not the Dominican prophesied that his doctrine would be proved by miracle at the fitting time? Here, then—said Santa Croce—was the fitting time. Let him walk into flame; if he came out unhurt, Heaven had set its seal; if he burned, his falsity would smoke in every nostril.

Savonarola, according to his habit, had taken no notice of these pulpit blows. But the challenger was Fra Francesco di Puglia—the very Franciscan who, at Prato the year before, had flung a similar gauntlet at Fra Domenico, the Prophet’s ardent follower, only to be called away while the heat was still only in words. Honest Fra Domenico, preaching Lenten sermons to the women in the Via del Cocomero, heard of this and, as a man leaps to snatch a knife from a child, took up the challenge himself. The people, who loved a short road to certainty, began to thicken with interest. But Savonarola, keenly alive to the dangers in the discussion itself, commanded Fra Domenico to withdraw and secede. The Franciscan professed himself content: he had not challenged any subaltern; he had challenged Fra Girolamo.

Since then the daily frenzy had slackened—until this morning.

When Tito entered the Piazza di Santa Croce, he found, as he had expected, a broad pour of people from the great church doors. The piazza received them like a basin takes a spill: they did not scatter at once, but eddied toward a particular spot near the entrance of the Franciscan monastery. There, fastened up by order of the Signoria, hung a large placard, its official hand bold enough to be read at a distance. Tito drifted with the current, threading the press with a careless leisure that deceived anyone who did not know the kind of patience a schemer cultivates: the patience of a cook watching a sauce that must not be hurried, though the whole feast depends on it.

Curiosity, however, was balked. The manuscript was chiefly in Latin—black letters with the air of authority, yet dumb to most of the mouths around it. Men who could shout a phrase of Scripture caught from a sermon, and who could spell their own names in a jumble of consonants, stood now with the irritating sensation that their neighbour might know more than they did. In the crowd the soundscape shifted: less laughter, more snappish questioning, the scrape of boots as men jostled for a better angle, the quick, impatient sucking of breath through teeth. Ignorance has its own noise.

“It’s the Frate’s doctrines that he’s to prove by being burned,” declared Goro, that large public character, wheezing his way to the forefront as if he were born for it. “The Signoria has taken it in hand, and the writing is to let us know. It’s what the Padre has been telling us about in his sermon.”

“Nay, Goro,” said a sleek shopkeeper, pitying him as one pities a man who salts his stew twice, “thou hast got thy legs into twisted hose there. The Frate has to prove his doctrines by not being burned. He is to walk through the fire, and come out on the other side sound and whole.”

“Yes, yes,” put in a young sculptor, white-streaked cap and tunic worn jauntily like a badge of work and youth. “But Fra Girolamo objects to walking through the fire. Being sound and whole already, he sees no reason to walk through the fire to come out in just the same condition. He leaves such odds and ends to Fra Domenico.”

“Then I say he flinches like a coward,” cried Goro. “Suffocation! that was what he did at the Carnival. He had us all in the Piazza to see the lightning strike him, and nothing came of it.”

“Stop that bleating,” said a tall shoemaker who had stepped in to catch part of the sermon, bunches of slippers slung over his shoulders like garlands of leather. Even his voice seemed tanned, toughened by work. “You are about as wise as a calf with water on its brain. The Frate will flinch from nothing. He’ll say nothing beforehand, perhaps, but when the moment comes he’ll walk through the fire without asking any grey-frock to keep him company. But I would give a shoestring to know what this Latin all is.”

“There’s so much of it,” muttered the shopkeeper, peering up as if the letters might yield by force. “Else I’m pretty good at guessing. Is there no scholar to be seen?”

Heads turned. In that turning, eyes caught on Tito approaching from behind.

“Here is one,” said the young sculptor, smiling and lifting his cap.

“It is the secretary of the Ten,” said the shopkeeper, and he too doffed—an uncommon respect from Florentines, who do not bend easily except to the very highest. But Tito’s splendour and grace exacted it as naturally as fine cloth asks to be touched. His black mantle, fastened with a gold fibula, lay on him like a regal garment; his ordinary black velvet cap seemed by some trick of bearing an exceptional head-dress. The hardening about his cheeks and mouth—the chief change since his coming to Florence—read, to a superficial glance, as added manliness.

He raised his cap at once.

“Thanks, my friend,” he said. “I merely wished, as you did, to see what is at the foot of this placard—ah, it is as I expected. I had been informed that the government permits any one who will to subscribe his name as a candidate to enter the fire—which is a liberality worthy of the magnificent Signoria—reserving, of course, the right to make a selection.”

He let the pause fall, tasting it, then went on with that cool, liquid mildness that compelled attention.

“And doubtless many believers will be eager to subscribe. For what is it to enter the fire to one whose faith is firm? A man is afraid of fire because he believes it will burn him; but if he believes the contrary—” He lifted his shoulders and left the sentence to complete itself in their minds. “For which reason I have never been one to disbelieve the Frate when he has said that he would enter the fire to prove his doctrine. For in his place, if you believed the fire would not burn you, which of you, my friends, would not enter it as readily as you would walk along the dry bed of the Mugnone?”

As he looked from face to face, something changed in several men—an eagerness that became suddenly cautious, like a dog invited to sniff something pungent and not sure whether it will please or sting. Once burning becomes practical, theories acquire a smell.

“Then, Messer Segretario,” said the young sculptor, “it seems to me Fra Francesco is the greater hero, for he offers to enter the fire for the truth, though he is sure the fire will burn him.”

“I do not deny it,” Tito answered, blandly. “But if it turns out that Fra Francesco is mistaken, he will have been burned for the wrong side, and the Church has never reckoned such victims martyrs. We must suspend our judgment until the trial has really taken place.”

“It is true,” said the shopkeeper, with subdued impatience. “But will you favour us by interpreting the Latin?”

“Assuredly.” Tito smiled, as if the request were a compliment he could not refuse. “It does but express the conclusions—the doctrines—which the Frate specially teaches, and which the trial by fire is to prove true or false. They are doubtless familiar to you. First, that Florence—”

“Let us have the Latin bit by bit, and then tell us what it means,” said the shoemaker. “Then we’ll know if you give the right meaning.”

“Yes, yes; that’s fair,” wheezed Goro.

“Willingly,” said Tito, as if he were beginning a recipe in a kitchen where all had opinions and few could read. “You will then judge if I give the right meaning. _Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione_; that is, the Church of God needs purifying, or regenerating.”

“It is true,” came several voices at once, as if the words, though foreign, were seasoned with a familiar bitterness.

“That means, the priests ought to lead better lives,” said the shoemaker. “There needs no miracle to prove that. That’s what the Frate has always been saying.”

“_Flagellabitur_,” Tito went on, his finger moving as if he were slicing the text into portions. “That is, it will be scourged. _Renovabitur_: it will be purified. _Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur_: Florence also, after the scourging, shall be purified and shall prosper.”

“That means we are to get Pisa again,” said the shopkeeper, swiftly translating prophecy into commerce.

“And get the wool from England as we used to do, I should hope,” said an elderly man in an old-fashioned berretta, who had been silent until now. “There’s been scourging enough with the sinking of the trade.”

At that moment a tall personage with a red feather issued from the convent door and threw Tito an indifferent glance. The crowd, with more timidity than respect, shrank to make a lane: Dolfo Spini. Tito tossed his becchetto carelessly over his left shoulder and turned back to his reading, as if a captain of the Compagnacci were no more than a gust of wind.

“_Infideles convertentur ad Christum_,” he continued. “That is, the infidels shall be converted to Christ.”

“Those are the Turks and the Moors,” said the shopkeeper, dispassionately. “Well, I’ve nothing to say against that.”

“_Haec autem omnia erunt temporibus nostris_: and all these things shall happen in our times.”

“Why, what use would they be else?” said Goro, pleased to find himself able to clap at something.

“_Excommunicato nuper lata contra Reverendum Patrem nostrum Fratrem Hieronymum nulla est_: the excommunication lately pronounced against our reverend father, Fra Girolamo, is null. _Non observantes eam non peccant_: those who disregard it are not committing a sin.”

“I shall know better what to say to that when we have had the Trial by Fire,” the shopkeeper answered, with the air of a man who will not buy a cheese until he has cut it.

“Which doubtless will clear up everything,” said Tito, and in his voice the phrase could be taken as reverence or ridicule, as the hearer pleased. “That is all the Latin—all the conclusions that are to be proved true or false by the trial. The rest you can perceive is simply a proclamation of the Signoria in good Tuscan, calling on such as are eager to walk through the fire, to come to the Palazzo and subscribe their names. Can I serve you further? If not—”

He raised his cap and bent slightly. The movement was so easy it looked not like strategy but like nature. Then he slipped away, quickening his pace once the piazza noises fell behind him. The bells of Santa Croce were still pulsing, but now there were quieter sounds in the side streets: a woman shaking a cloth from an upper window, the soft clink of a pail chain, the whisper of broom on stone—domestic rhythms that made public miracles seem, for an instant, like theatre set against the daily cooking of lives.

After two or three turnings he paused before a door in a quiet street and gave a light, peculiar knock. It was opened by a young woman. He chucked her under the chin as he asked if the Padrone was within, and then, without further ceremony, passed through another door standing ajar.

He entered a handsome but untidy room where Dolfo Spini sat playing with a fine stag-hound. The creature alternately snuffed at a basket of pups and licked Spini’s hands with that affectionate disregard of her master’s morals which some men find among the most agreeable attributes of her sex. Spini merely looked up as Tito came in, but continued his play, out of that obstinate persistence in an irrelevant action by which slow-witted sensual people thwart their own purposes.

Tito waited, patient as a man who knows that time, like dough, can be made to rise by warmth and left alone.

“A handsome _bracca_ that,” he said quietly, standing with his thumbs in his belt. Presently he added, in that mild tone that nevertheless drew the ear as surely as a knife draws the eye, “When you have finished such caresses as cannot possibly be deferred, my Dolfo, we will talk of business, if you please. My time—which I could wish to be eternity at your service—is not entirely my own this morning.”

“Down, Mischief, down!” Spini snarled suddenly, shoving the dog aside. “Malediction!” He rose and came close, laying a heavy hand on Tito’s shoulder. “I hope your sharp wits see all the ins and outs of this business, my fine necromancer, for it seems to me no clearer than the bottom of a sack.”

“What is your difficulty, my cavalier?”

“These accursed Frati Minori at Santa Croce. They are drawing back now. Fra Francesco himself seems afraid of sticking to his challenge. He talks of the Prophet using magic to get up a false miracle—thinks he himself might be dragged into the fire and burned, and the Prophet might come out whole by magic, and the Church be none the better. And then, after all our talking, there’s not so much as a blessed lay brother who will offer himself to pair with that pious sheep Fra Domenico.”

“It is the peculiar stupidity of the tonsured skull,” said Tito, “that prevents them from seeing how little consequence it is whether they are burned or not. Have you sworn well to them that they shall be in no danger of entering the fire?”

“No,” said Spini, blinking, “because one of them will be obliged to go in with Fra Domenico, who thinks it a thousand years till the fagots are ready.”

“Not at all.” Tito’s voice was gentle, but it laid out the steps as if he were setting a board and placing each dish exactly where it must stand. “Fra Domenico himself is not likely to go in. I have told you before—only your powerful mind is not to be impressed without more repetition than suffices for the vulgar—I have told you that now you have got the Signoria to take up this affair and prevent it from being hushed up by Fra Girolamo, nothing is necessary but that on a given day the fuel should be prepared in the Piazza, and the people got together with the expectation of seeing something prodigious. If, after that, the Prophet quits the Piazza without any appearance of a miracle on his side, he is ruined with the people. They will be ready to pelt him out of the city. The Signoria will find it easy to banish him from the territory, and his Holiness may do as he likes with him. Therefore, my Alcibiades, swear to the Franciscans that their grey-frocks shall not come within singeing distance of the fire.”

Spini rubbed the back of his head with one hand and tapped his sword against his leg with the other, as if the clang might drive sense through bone.

“But,” he said at last, “unless we fall on him in the Piazza when the people are in a rage, and make an end of him and his lies then and there, Valori and the Salviati and the Albizzi will take up arms and raise a fight for him. I know that was talked of when there was the hubbub on Ascension Sunday. And the people may turn round again: there may be a story raised of the French king coming again, or some other cursed chance in the hypocrite’s favour. The city will never be safe till he’s out of it.”

“He will be out of it before long,” said Tito, “without your giving yourself any further trouble than this little comedy of the Trial by Fire. The wine and the sun will make vinegar without any shouting to help them, as your Florentine sages would say. You will have the satisfaction of delivering your city from an incubus by an able stratagem, instead of risking blunders with sword-thrusts.”

“But suppose he did get magic and the devil to help him, and walk through the fire after all?” Spini’s mouth twisted into a grimace meant to disguise a certain shyness, as if he were reluctant to let superstition be heard in daylight. “How do you know there’s nothing in those things? Plenty of scholars believe in them, and this Frate is bad enough for anything.”

“Oh, of course there are such things,” said Tito, with a shrug, as if granting the point cost him nothing. “But I have particular reasons for knowing that the Frate is not on such terms with the devil as can give him any confidence in this affair. The only magic he relies on is his own ability.”

“Ability!” Spini scoffed. “Do you call it ability to be setting Florence at loggerheads with the Pope and all the powers of Italy—all to keep beckoning at the French king who never comes? You may call him able, but I call him a hypocrite who wants to be master of everybody and get himself made Pope.”

“You judge with your usual penetration, my captain,” said Tito, and the compliment slid in like oil, making the hard thought shine. “But our opinions do not clash. The Frate, wanting to be master, and to carry out his projects against the Pope, requires the lever of a foreign power, and requires Florence as a fulcrum. I used to think him a narrow-minded bigot, but now I think him a shrewd ambitious man who knows what he is aiming at, and directs his aim as skilfully as you direct a ball when you are playing at _maglio_.”

“Yes, yes,” said Spini, mollified, even pleased. “I can aim a ball.”

“It is true,” Tito returned, with bland gravity. “And I should not have troubled you with my trivial remark on the Frate’s ability, but that you may see how this will heighten the credit of your success against him at Rome and at Milan, which is sure to serve you in good stead when the city comes to change its policy.”

“Well, thou art a good little demon, and shalt have good pay,” Spini said, patronisingly; and it seemed only natural to him that Tito should smile with gratification as he began—

“Of course, any advantage to me depends entirely on your—”

“We shall have our supper at my palace to-night,” Spini broke in, with a significant nod and an affectionate pat on Tito’s shoulder, “and I shall expound the new scheme to them all.”

“Pardon, my magnificent patron,” said Tito. “The scheme has been the same from the first—it has never varied except in your memory. Are you sure you have fast hold of it now?”

Spini rehearsed it, slowly, with the air of a man repeating a charm.

“One thing more,” he said, as Tito turned to go. “There is that sharp-nosed notary, Ser Ceccone. He has been handy of late. Tell me—you who can see a man wink when you’re behind him—do you think I may go on making use of him?”

Tito dared not say No. He knew Spini too well to trust him with advice when Spini’s vanity and self-interest were not already engaged in concealing the adviser.

“Doubtless,” he answered promptly. “I have nothing to say against Ceccone.”

Yet the mention pricked him—an abrupt, passing twinge, like a fishbone caught in a mouthful that was meant to be smoothly enjoyed. Tito’s nature was keenly alive to circumstances that might be turned against him; his memory kept a pantry of such possibilities, always stocked, always ready for sudden hunger. He did not forget that October morning more than a year ago when Romola had appeared at the door of Nello’s shop and compelled him, under her direct gaze, to declare his certainty that Fra Girolamo was not going outside the gates. Ser Ceccone had witnessed that scene. And Tito had long felt, without knowing why, that the notary disliked him.

Lately, since a turn in affairs, that dislike had become more dangerous. After being implicated in Medicean plots and finding it advisable to retire into the country, Ser Ceccone had reappeared in the city, attached himself to the Arrabbiati, and cultivated Dolfo Spini’s patronage. Spini, among intimates, loved confidential narratives of his own doings; and if the notary’s power of combination were sharpened by enmity, he might gather knowledge that could be used against Tito with unpleasant effect.

It would be pitiable—to be balked in well-conducted schemes by an insignificant notary; to be lamed by the sting of an insect offended unawares. “But,” Tito told himself, as he went his way again into the streets where the day’s noises had risen—hammer on metal, vendor’s cry, the shuffle of many feet—“the man’s dislike can be nothing deeper than the ill-humour of a dinnerless dog. I shall conquer it if I can make him prosperous.” And he had been glad of the opportunity that had presented itself to provide the notary with a temporary post as an extra _cancelliere_ under the Ten, believing that with this sop and the expectation of more, the waspish cur must be cured of biting.

But perfect scheming demands omniscience. The envy that fed Ser Ceccone’s spite had been sharpened into hatred by causes Tito did not know.

That evening when Tito, returning from his critical audience with the Special Council, had brushed past Ser Ceccone on the stairs, the notary—newly returned from Pistoja and just learning of the conspirators’ arrest—had been on an errand that bore a humble resemblance to Tito’s. He too, without giving up a show of popular zeal, had put himself into the Medicean lottery. He too had been privy to the unexecuted plot, willing to tell what he knew—only he knew much less to tell. He too would have gone on treacherous errands, but a more eligible agent had forestalled him.

His propositions were received coldly. The council, he was told, already possessed what was needed; and since he had been thus busy in sedition, it would be well for him to retire out of the way of mischief, otherwise the government might be obliged to take note of him. Ser Ceccone wanted no evidence to attribute his failure to Tito; his spite was bitterer because the nature of the case compelled him to silence.

Nor was that the whole of his grudge. On issuing from his hiding-place and attaching himself to the Arrabbiati, he had earned some pay as a spy reporting Florentine affairs to the Milanese court. But the pay had been small, despite his pains to write full letters; and lately he had been told that his news was seldom more than a late and imperfect edition of what was known already. Ser Ceccone had no positive knowledge that Tito had an underhand connection with the Arrabbiati and the Court of Milan—but he had a suspicion, and he chewed it with as strong a savour as if it were certainty, rolling it in his mind the way a man rolls a harsh herb on his tongue, not for pleasure but because the bitterness itself feels like power.
