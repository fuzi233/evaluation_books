"Pack clouds away."

"For joy or grief, for hope or fear,
For all hereafter, as for here,
In peace or strife, in storm or shine."

The next morning, Edith moved through the house on tiptoe, silencing Sholto whenever he raised his voice, terrified that a sudden clamor would shatter the intense quietude of the drawing room. By two o'clock, the doors remained shut. Then, the sharp rhythm of a man's footsteps echoed down the staircase. Edith peeked out from behind the curtains.

"Well, Henry?" she asked, her gaze questioning.

"Well!" he replied, briefly.

"Come in to lunch!"

"No, thank you. I've squandered far too much time here already."

"Then it isn't resolved?" Edith asked, her spirits sinking.

"Not at all. Never will it be, if 'it' refers to what I suspect you mean. That path is impossible, Edith. Give up thinking about it."

"But it would be so wonderful for everyone," Edith pleaded. "I'd rest easy knowing the children were safe with Margaret nearby. As it stands, I'm constantly afraid she'll drift off to Cadiz."

"When I wed, I shall seek a young lady who understands child-rearing. That is all I can offer. Miss Hale refuses me, and I shall not press her."

"And what exactly have you discussed?"

"A thousand matters you wouldn't grasp: investments, leases, the valuation of land."

"Oh, leave if that's all. If you've spent this long debating such weary subjects, you and she will be unbearably dull company."

"As you wish. I return tomorrow, bringing Mr. Thornton, to discuss further with Miss Hale."

"Mr. Thornton? Why does he matter?"

"He is Miss Hale's tenant," Mr. Lennox said, turning aside. "And he wishes to surrender his lease."

"Oh! Very well. I cannot fathom the details, so please spare me."

"The only detail that counts is this: ensure the back drawing room remains undisturbed as it was today. The children and servants come and go incessantly, making satisfactory business explanation impossible. Tomorrow's arrangements are of critical importance."

No one understood why Mr. Lennox failed to appear the following day. Yet Mr. Thornton arrived punctually. After waiting nearly an hour, Margaret entered, her complexion pale, her features etched with anxiety.

"I am deeply sorry Mr. Lennox is absent," she began hurriedly. "He could manage this far better than I. He serves as my adviser in this..."

"I am sorry to have intruded if I cause you trouble. Should I proceed to Mr. Lennox's chambers to locate him?"

"No, thank you. I wished to express my sorrow at losing you as a tenant. However, Mr. Lennox assures me circumstances will inevitably brighten—"

"Mr. Lennox knows little of the truth," Mr. Thornton interrupted, his voice low and steady. "A man blessed in every regard he cherishes fails to comprehend the weight of finding oneself no longer youthful, yet thrust back to the starting line demanding the hopeful vigor of youth alone. To feel half a life vanished, with nothing accomplished save the bitter memory of wasted opportunities. Miss Hale, I prefer not to hear Mr. Lennox's counsel regarding my affairs. Those who are themselves happy and prosperous often trivialize the misfortunes of others."

"You are unfair," Margaret said softly. "Mr. Lennox merely spoke of the high probability he sees in your redemption—not just of what you lost, but surpassing it. Please, do not speak until I finish." Gathering herself, she quickly flipped through legal documents and account statements, her hands trembling. "Ah, here it is! He drafted a proposal... showing that if you accept eighteen hundred and fifty-seven pounds of mine, currently idle in the bank yielding a mere two and a half percent, you could offer me superior interest and continue operating Marlborough Mills." Her voice had steadied. Mr. Thornton remained silent as she searched for the document outlining security proposals, desperate to view this solely as a commercial transaction where the advantage lay squarely with her.

As she hunted for the paper, her pulse seized at the timbre of Mr. Thornton's voice. It was rough, quivering with a tenderness that transcended business.

"Margaret!"

For a fleeting second, she lifted her gaze, then veiled her luminous eyes by bowing her forehead onto her hands. Stepping closer, he beseeched her again, his voice trembling with urgent eagerness.

"Margaret!"

Her head sank lower, her face concealed, resting almost upon the table. He approached, kneeling beside her to align his face with her ear, whispering, panting out the words:

"Take care.—If you remain silent, I shall claim you as my own in some strange, presumptuous fashion.—Send me away immediately if I must depart;—Margaret!—"

At that third summons, she turned her face, still masked by her small, white-clad hands, toward him, laying it upon his shoulder, hiding within its embrace. It was too exquisite a sensation to feel her soft cheek against his for him to desire seeing deep blushes or loving eyes. He drew her close. They held their silence. Finally, she murmured in a fractured voice:

"Oh, Mr. Thornton, I am not worthy enough!"

"Not worthy! Do not mock my own profound sense of unworthiness."

After a brief pause, he gently freed her hands from her face, arranging them as he had once positioned them to shield him from the rioters.

"Do you recall, love?" he murmured. "And how I repaid you with insolence the following day?"

"I remember how wrongly I spoke to you—that is all."

"Look here! Raise your head. I have something to show you!" She slowly faced him, burning with a beautiful, radiant shame.

"Do you recognize these roses?" He produced his pocket-book, revealing dried flowers carefully preserved within.

"No!" she replied, with innocent wonder. "Did you receive them from me?"

"No! Vanity prevented you. Perhaps you wore similar blossoms."

She studied them, puzzled for a moment, then smiled faintly.

"They are from Helstone, are they not? I know the deep indentations around the leaves. Oh! Have you visited there? When?"

"I sought to visit the place where Margaret grew into what she is, even during the darkest hours, when I harbored no hope of ever calling her mine. I went upon my return from Havre."

"You must give them to me," she said, attempting to pluck them from his hand with gentle firmness.

"As you wish. Provided you pay me for them!"

"How shall I inform Aunt Shaw?" she whispered after a moment of delicious, heavy silence.

"Let me address her."

"Oh, no! I am indebted to her—but what will she say?"

"I can surmise. Her first cry will be, 'That man!'"

"Hush!" said Margaret, "or I shall attempt to emulate your mother's indignant tones as she cries, 'That woman!'"
