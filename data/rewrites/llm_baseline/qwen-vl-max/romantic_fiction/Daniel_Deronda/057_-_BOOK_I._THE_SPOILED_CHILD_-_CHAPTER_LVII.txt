CHAPTER LVII. “The unripe grape, the ripe, and the dried. All things are changes, not into nothing, but into that which is not at present.”—MARCUS AURELIUS.

Deeds are the pulse of time, its living rhythm—righteous or unrighteous, once done, they echo through the ages, vibrating in the silence until time itself grows still, and the world breathes no more upon a mirror.

That evening, she called for him again. It was nearly the hour when she had been brought in from the sea the night before, and the light was soft, dimmed by drawn blinds and open windows. She sat gazing fixedly at the ocean, her cheek resting on her hand, quieter now than when he had last left her, yet shadowed with a deep sorrow that shifted into anxious hesitation as he approached. She did not reach out to him, but said softly, “How long ago it seems.”

Then, almost pleading: “Will you sit near me just a little while?”

He took his place beside her, just as he had before. He saw the look in her eyes—the quiet urgency, the unspoken words trembling on her lips—and waited. But she turned again toward the window, silent, then back to him, the same longing expression returning, yet no words came. There was something holding her back. To ease her fear, Deronda gently turned his face away.

After a moment, she whispered, broken and desperate: “You won’t tell anyone else? You’ll never let another know?”

“Never,” he answered firmly. “There is no action to be taken. No wrong can be undone this way. No justice can be served by revealing what happened. It would only deepen the wound.”

She was so still, so still, that it seemed she held her breath before speaking again. “But if I hadn’t hesitated—if I hadn’t let that cruel thought take hold—if I’d thrown the rope at once… might it have saved him?”

“No,” Deronda said slowly. “I don’t think so. If he could swim, he must have suffered cramp. Even with your swiftest effort, it would have been impossible. That fleeting wish—to see him die—could not have changed fate. Its true consequence lies within you. In our hearts, evil desires matter deeply. They may twist us into harm, or awaken shame that drives us toward better things. And that shame, Gwendolen—your horror of yourself—it may be the beginning of healing.”

“I’ve spared others,” she murmured. “Others will have everything they need. They’ll have what they deserve. I knew that before I left town. You don’t think I wanted anything for myself?”

Her voice faltered. “I didn’t even consider that,” Deronda replied. “I was thinking only of what you’ve endured.”

“Perhaps you don’t know where it all began,” she said slowly, as though forcing herself forward. “There was someone else—he should have married her. I knew it. I told her I wouldn’t stand in the way. And I left—that was when you first saw me. But then we lost everything. Poverty came suddenly, and I was overwhelmed. I felt tempted. I thought, *I’ll do as I please—I’ll make it right.* I convinced myself. But it wasn’t right. It was terrible. Then came hatred, dark thoughts. That’s how it started. I told you I feared myself. And I tried to turn that fear into protection. I imagined what would happen if I gave in—how I’d dread the morning, wish it were always night, yet see death in every shadow. If you hadn’t known how much I suffered, you might have judged me differently—but now it’s too late. It’s all been for nothing. All I care about now is protecting them—my mother, who has never known joy.”

Silence fell. Then, choked by sobs: “You can’t bear to look at me anymore. You think I’m beyond saving. You don’t believe I can become worthy—of anything—of love—of being good. I’ll always be too wicked…”

Her voice broke, helpless.

Deronda’s heart ached. He looked at her face—so frail, so full of anguish—and said, “I believe you can become more worthy than you’ve ever been—worthy to live a life that brings peace to others. No sin condemns us forever unless we cling to it, desire it, refuse to break free. You *have* fought it. You will keep fighting.”

“But you were the one who made me want to change,” she whispered, pressing her hands together on the armrest, her eyes fixed on him. Her face bore the traces of a life shattered in twenty-four hours—a new existence born from a deed that fulfilled a forbidden desire. “I’ll endure any penance. I’ll live any life you choose. But don’t leave me. Stay close. If you had been here—if I could have told you everything—I might have been different. Please, don’t abandon me. Will you stay?”

“It would never occur to me to leave you,” Deronda said quickly, his voice steady, warm. His tone—like his eyes—had an unintended depth, making his kindness seem more personal than it truly was. In that moment, he felt a flicker of unease. His compassion for her pain stirred images of future burdens, of promises that might one day feel unkept. He met her gaze, but beneath it, he sensed the weight of what he was saying—an indefinite vow tied to uncertain hope.

Anxieties crowded his mind—both near and far. After a pause, he said, “Sir Hugo Mallinger should arrive by tomorrow night. And I hope Mrs. Davilow will follow soon. Her presence will mean the most to you. It will give you reason to spare her unnecessary grief.”

“Yes,” she breathed. “Yes, I’ll try. And you won’t go?”

“Not until after Sir Hugo arrives.”

“And then—we’ll all return to England?”

“As soon as possible,” Deronda replied, avoiding further details.

She turned again toward the window, her expression changing—as if awakening from a daze. The twilight deepened, but Deronda noticed the subtle shift in her eyes, in her hands, like the slow return of awareness after shock.

“You’ll always be with Sir Hugo now?” she asked, looking at him. “You’ll live at the Abbey—or Diplow?”

“I don’t know where I’ll live,” he said, his cheeks flushing slightly.

She saw the change in him and fell silent, realizing she had spoken too boldly.

After a while, she began again, her gaze drifting away. “I can’t imagine how my life will go on. Now I think it would be better to be poor, to work.”

“New paths will open as days pass,” Deronda said gently. “When you’re among your friends again, you’ll find new duties. For now, make it your task to recover—to grow calm, to regain yourself—before—”

He hesitated.

“Before my mother comes,” she finished.

“Oh,” she added, turning to him, “I haven’t looked at myself. Would you have recognized me—if you’d seen me now? Would you have known me, the woman you met in Leubronn?”

“Yes,” he said quietly. “I would have known you. The outward change isn’t great. I would have seen at once that it was you—and that you had suffered terribly.”

“Don’t wish you’d never seen me,” she begged, tears welling. “Don’t wish that.”

“I couldn’t despise myself for wishing it,” he said. “How could I know what I wished? We must accept our duties in what happens—not in what might have been. If I allowed myself such foolish dreams, I wouldn’t wish I’d never seen you. I’d wish I’d been able to save you from this.”

“You’ve saved me from worse,” she whispered, sobbing. “I’d have been worse without you. If you hadn’t been kind, I’d have been more wicked than I am.”

“I should go now,” Deronda said, weary from the emotional strain. “Remember what we spoke of—your task. Be strong, be calm. Prepare yourself before others arrive.”

He rose. She offered her hand, obedient, subdued.

But once he was gone, she collapsed to her knees, consumed by uncontrollable weeping. The distance between them was too vast. She was a soul cast out—gazing at a life she had forfeited through sin. Found there, crushed on the floor, her grief seemed natural enough for a woman whose husband had drowned before her eyes.
