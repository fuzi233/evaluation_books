Colonel James Wolfe, Harry's new commander, returned from America shortly after our Virginian had joined his regiment. Having previously served as Lieutenant-Colonel of Kingsley's forces, Wolfe had been granted command of a second battalion in recognition of his distinguished gallantry at Cape Breton. Harry approached his new leader with genuine respect and cordiality, mindful that the entire nation now held its breath in anticipation of Wolfe becoming a great general. Recent events in France had seen several officers of high reputation fail miserably under pressure; the Duke of Marlborough proved unworthy of his illustrious lineage, and even Lord George Sackville's genius was questioned before his conduct at Minden sealed his fate. Eager for military glory, the public and the Ministry alike sought a commander capable of satisfying their fervent desires. While Wolfe and Lambert were frequently occupied with business in London, their friendship flourished anew, much to Lambert's delight at witnessing his younger friend's rising fortune.

Away from duty, Harry never grew weary of listening to Wolfe recount the military campaigns of the preceding year. Wolfe possessed a heroic simplicity reminiscent of Nelson, speaking frankly of his actions without reserve. Though some refined gentlemen at St. James's might have scoffed at such candor, within our intimate circle, he found devoted admirers. The young General retained the romantic spirit of a boy, delighting deeply in music and poetry. On the eve of his death, he confessed he would have preferred composing Gray's *Elegy* to winning a battle. Given these literary inclinations, it is certain that George and Wolfe became close companions. Both were in love, both accepted lovers, and both desperate for happiness; surely they shared countless sentimental conversations that would have been fascinating to record had we access to accurate accounts. In a later letter, Warrington wrote:

> "I had the honor of knowing the famous General Wolfe and spending much time with him during his final stay in London. Our conversations were invariably engaging, and I admired his simplicity, frankness, and a glorious bravery that defined him. He was deeply in love and craved heaps of laurels to present to his mistress. 'If it be a sin to covet honour,' he used to say with Harry the Fifth—who was passionately fond of plays and poetry—'I am the most offending soul alive.' Surely, on his last day, he feasted sufficiently to satisfy the greediest appetite for glory. He hungered for it. To me, he seemed less like a soldier merely fulfilling his duty than a knight seeking dragons and giants. My own country has recently produced a chief of a vastly different order, possessing a contrary genius. I scarce know which to admire most: the Briton's chivalrous ardour or the Roman constancy of our great Virginian."

While Lambert's official duties kept him in London, his family remained content there. Mr. Warrington, seemingly satisfied with the rural tranquility of Southampton Row and the beauty of Bedford Gardens, showed little inclination to leave London permanently. He did visit Castlewood, staying briefly in the chamber his grandfather had praised and which Colonel Esmond had occupied as a boy, receiving a warm welcome from the family members present. Yet, undoubtedly, he preferred the company of a young woman in London who offered him pleasures far surpassing anything available in Lord Castlewood's circle. Although all the ladies were civil—and Lady Maria particularly gracious, enchanted by the tragedy George and Parson Sampson recited—the Chaplain's enthusiasm ultimately secured Warrington's play for the stage. While Mr. Johnson had urged Garrick at Drury Lane to produce it, Garrick had already committed to a tragedy by the author of *Douglas*. Consequently, *Carpezan* was taken up by Mr. Rich at Covent Garden.

On the night of the premiere, Mr. Warrington hosted an elegant gathering at the Bedford Head in Covent Garden, from which his friends proceeded en masse to the theatre. Only one or two remained behind with the young author at the coffee-house, where messengers periodically arrived to relay updates on the performance. The role of Carpezan was played by Barry; Shuter portrayed the old nobleman; Reddish excelled as Ulric; and the King of Bohemia was enacted by a young Dublin actor named Mr. Geoghegan, known on stage as Hagan, who performed with admirable grace and presence. Mrs. Woffington appeared slightly aged in the first act as the heroine, yet her murder in the fourth act terrified and delighted the audience. Miss Wayn sang the ballad intended for the king's page at the moment of the unfortunate wife's execution, earning universal acclaim. All agreed that Barry was terrifying and pathos-filled as Carpezan, particularly during the execution scene, while the youth and elegance of Hagan won general applause. Mr. Rich staged the piece with great elegance, though some doubted the propriety of introducing a favorite elephant—a staple of pantomime—to represent the Janissaries' march, causing one of Mr. Warrington's black servants to enter in Turkish attire while another sat in the footman's gallery, weeping and applauding uproariously at appropriate moments.

The execution of Sybilla marked the turning point of the drama. Once her head fell, George's friends exhaled in relief, and messenger after messenger rushed to the coffee-house to declare the tragedy a complete triumph. Amidst thunderous applause, Mr. Barry announced the play for repetition, revealing it as the debut dramatic effort of a young gentleman from Virginia.

How we wished to occupy the box where our friends sat, watching Theo's fluttering anxiety as the play's success hung in the balance, and observing the blushes and sparkling eyes that lit up when victory was assured. During the turmoil of the fourth act, Harry turned deadly pale—paler, Mrs. Lambert noted, than Barry with all his chalk. Yet, if Briareus could have clapped his hands, he could not have created more noise than Harry did at the conclusion. Mr. Wolfe and General Lambert cheered enthusiastically; Mrs. Lambert wept. Though Hetty remarked, "Why do you cry, mamma? You don't want any of them alive again; you know it serves them all right," the girl was genuinely as delighted as anyone present, including little Charley from the Chartreux, who had special permission from Dr. Crusius to attend, and Miss Lucy, brought specifically from boarding school for the occasion. Lord Castlewood and his sister, Lady Maria, were also there; the latter rose to compliment the actors on stage. Parson Sampson proved invaluable in the pit, leading the applause, presumably guided by Gumbo in the gallery to ensure he followed instructions.

A truly jovial supper ensued among Mr. Warrington's friends that night—far more spirited than Mr. Garrick's, whose *Agis* and dreary choruses met with poor success, forcing him to regret rejecting our young author's work in favor of Mr. Home's tragedy. Many celebrations occurred: Mr. Gumbo entertained several "shoulder-knot" gentlemen who supported his master's masterpiece; Mr. Henry Warrington hosted ten officers of his new regiment at the Star and Garter in Pall Mall, who had traveled expressly to back *Carpezan*; and finally, Mr. Warrington treated the three principal actors, our family party from the side box, Mr. Johnson, his clever friend Mr. Reynolds the painter, Lord Castlewood, Lady Maria, and others. Lady Maria sat beside the young actor who played the King. Mr. Warrington somehow managed to seat Miss Theo nearby, ensuring a pleasant evening for both. Great animation and cordiality prevailed. When toasts were proposed, Lady Maria cheerfully offered "The King of Hungary." That gentleman, full of eloquence, fire, and excellent manners both on and off stage, declared he had already died once that evening, hoping to die a hundred times more on the same field, yet vowed eternally to serve his humble mistress. Oh, if he possessed a real crown instead of his pasteboard diadem, what joy he would show laying it at her ladyship's feet! Neither Lord nor Mr. Esmond were overly pleased with the gentleman's excessive gallantry, which they rightly attributed to the liberal consumption of wine and punch. Theo and her sister, inexperienced in the world, were initially startled by Mr. Hagan's intense manner, but Lady Maria, more seasoned, took it all in good part. Late coaches were summoned to escort the ladies, after which some gentlemen returned to the supper room. The result was that *Carpezan* had to be carried out in a chair, the King of Hungary suffered a severe headache, and the Poet, despite delivering numerous speeches, was astonished when half a dozen guests appeared at his house the following morning, having been invited the night prior to dine again.

As he escorted Mrs. Lambert and her daughters into their carriage the previous night, all the ladies were flustered, delighted, and excited. Naturally, our gentleman accompanied them the next day to discuss the play, the audience, the actors, and the beauties of the piece repeatedly. Mrs. Lambert had heard warnings that theatrical women were dangerous company for young men and feared George might frequent the greenroom too much.

George smiled, assuring her he possessed a preventive against all greenroom temptations, fearing none. As he spoke, he looked into Theo's face as if those eyes held the amulet preserving him from peril.

"Why should he be afraid, mamma?" the maiden asked simply, unaware of danger or guile.

"No, my darling, I don't think he needs fear," the mother replied, kissing her.
