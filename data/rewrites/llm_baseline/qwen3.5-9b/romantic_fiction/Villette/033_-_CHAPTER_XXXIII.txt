On the first of May, a notice was issued to the entire household—the twenty boarders and the four teachers—to rise at five o'clock, be dressed by six, and assemble under the command of M. le Professeur Emanuel. Today was the day he intended to fulfill his promise of leading us on an excursion to breakfast in the country. I must confess, as the reader may recall, I had not originally been invited to this project; quite the opposite. However, when I recently raised the matter and asked how it was to be arranged, I received a sharp tug behind the ear. I did not dare challenge the repetition of this gesture, and M. Emanuel merely declared imperiously, "Je vous conseille de vous faire prier" (*I advise you to beg*). One Napoleonic compliment was sufficient persuasion, so I resolved to join the party.

The morning broke calm and summer-like, filled with the song of birds in the garden and a light dew-mist promising warmth. Everyone agreed it would be hot, and we all felt a genuine pleasure in discarding heavy garments for attire suitable to the sunny season. The uniform was a clean, fresh print dress and a light straw bonnet, crafted and trimmed by French workwomen alone capable of uniting the utterly unpretending with the perfectly becoming. No one flaunted faded silks; no one wore a second-hand best article. At six, the bell rang merrily, and we poured down the stairs, through the *carré*, along the corridor, and into the vestibule.

There stood our Professor. He had discarded his savage-looking *paletôt* and severe Greek-style bonnet for a youthful belted blouse and a cheerful straw hat. He greeted us all with the kindest good morning, and most of us returned a smile of thanksgiving. We were marshaled in order and soon departed. The streets were still quiet; the boulevards were fresh and peaceful as fields. I believe we were very happy as we walked along. Our leader possessed a secret ability to impart a certain impetus to happiness when he chose; conversely, in a different mood, he could instill a thrill of fear. He neither led nor followed us strictly but walked along the line, offering a word to everyone, chatting extensively with his favorites while not entirely neglecting those he disliked.

It was my personal desire, for reasons I need not detail, to remain slightly aloof from notice. Paired with Ginevra Fanshawe, I bore the dear pressure of that angel's not unsubstantial arm upon mine. She remained in excellent condition, and I can assure the reader it was no trifling task to carry the burden of her loveliness; many times throughout that warm day, I wished with all my heart that there had been less of this charming commodity. Nevertheless, having her, as I stated, I attempted to make her useful by interposing her always between myself and M. Paul, shifting my position depending on whether I heard him approaching from the right or the left.

My private motive for this maneuver stemmed from the fact that my new print dress was pink. Under our current escort, this color made me feel somewhat akin to the sensation I experienced once, clad in a shawl with a red border, forced to traverse a meadow where a bull grazed. For a while, this shifting strategy, combined with some adjustments to a black silk scarf, served my purpose. However, eventually, he discovered that regardless of which side he approached from, Miss Fanshawe remained his neighbor. The acquaintance between Ginevra and him had never been so smooth that his temper did not undergo a certain crisping process whenever he heard her English accent. Their dispositions simply did not fit; they jarred upon contact. He found her empty and affected; she deemed him bearish, meddling, and repellent.

At last, after changing his position for about the sixth time with no improvement to the situation, he thrust his head forward, fixed his eyes on mine, and demanded with impatience, "Qu'est-ce que c'est? Vous me jouez des tours?" (*What is this? Are you playing tricks on me?*) The words were hardly out of his mouth before, with his customary quickness, he grasped the root of the procedure. In vain did I shake out the long fringe and spread the broad end of my scarf.

"A-h-h! c'est la robe rose!" (*Ah! it's the pink dress!*), he exclaimed, affecting me very much like the sudden, irate lowing of some lord of the meadow.

"It is only cotton," I alleged hurriedly; "it is cheaper and washes better than any other color."

"And Mademoiselle Lucy is as coquettish as ten Parisians," he answered. "Have you ever seen an Englishwoman like this? Look at her hat, her gloves, her boots!"

These articles of dress were exactly like what my companions wore; certainly not one whit smarter—perhaps rather plainer than most—but Monsieur had now seized upon his text, and I began to chafe under the expected sermon. Yet, it went off as mildly as the threat of a storm sometimes passes on a summer day. I received but one flash of sheet lightning in the form of a single bantering smile from his eyes. Then he said, "Courage! To tell the truth, I am not angry; perhaps I am even glad you have dressed so beautifully for my little festival."

"But my dress is not beautiful, Monsieur—it is merely neat."

"I like neatness," he replied.

In short, he was not to be dissatisfied. The sun of good humor was destined to triumph on this auspicious morning, consuming scudding clouds before they could soil its disk.

Now we were in the country, amidst what they called "les bois et les petits sentiers" (*woods and little paths*). These woods and lanes, a month later, would offer but a dusty and doubtful seclusion; now, however, in their May greenness and morning repose, they looked very pleasant. We reached a certain well, planted around in the style of Labassecour, with an orderly circle of lime-trees. Here a halt was called. On the green swell of ground surrounding the well, we were ordered to sit, Monsieur taking his place in our midst and allowing us to gather in knots around him. Those who liked him more than they feared came close, and these were chiefly the little ones; those who feared more than they liked kept somewhat aloof; and those in whom much affection had given, even to what remained of fear, a pleasurable zest, observed the greatest distance.

He began to tell us a story. Well could he narrate: in a diction that children love and learned men emulate; simple in its strength, and strong in its simplicity. There were beautiful touches in that little tale; sweet glimpses of feeling and hues of description that, while I listened, sank into my mind and have never faded. He tinted a twilight scene—I hold it in memory still—such a picture I have never beheld from an artist's pencil.

I have said that, for myself, I possess no impromptu faculty; and perhaps that very deficiency made me marvel the more at one who possessed it in perfection. M. Emanuel was not a man to write books; yet I have heard him lavish, with careless, unconscious prodigality, such mental wealth as books seldom boast. His mind was indeed my library, and whenever it was opened to me, I entered bliss. Intellectually imperfect as I was, I could read little; there were few bound and printed volumes that did not weary me—whose perusal did not fag and blind—but his tomes of thought were collyrium to the spirit's eyes; over their contents, inward sight grew clear and strong. I used to think what a delight it would be for one who loved him better than he loved himself to gather and store up those handfuls of gold-dust, so recklessly flung to heaven's reckless winds.

When his story was done, he approached the little knoll where I and Ginevra sat apart. In his usual mode of demanding an opinion (he has no reticence to wait until it is voluntarily offered), he asked, "Were you interested?"

According to my wonted undemonstrative fashion, I simply answered—"Yes."

"Was it good?"

"Very good."

"Yet I could not write that down," he said.

"Why not, Monsieur?"

"I hate the mechanical labor; I hate to stoop and sit still. I could dictate it, though, with pleasure, to an amanuensis who suited me. Would Mademoiselle Lucy write for me if I asked her?"

"Monsieur would be too quick; he would urge me, and be angry if my pen did not keep pace with his lips."

"Try some day; let us see the monster I can make of myself under the circumstances. But just now, there is no question of dictation; I mean to make you useful in another office. Do you see yonder farmhouse?"

"Surrounded with trees? Yes."

"There we are to breakfast; and while the good *fermière* makes the café au lait in a caldron, you and five others, whom I shall select, will spread with butter half a hundred rolls."

Having formed his troop into line once more, he marched us straight to the farm, which, on seeing our force, surrendered without capitulation.
