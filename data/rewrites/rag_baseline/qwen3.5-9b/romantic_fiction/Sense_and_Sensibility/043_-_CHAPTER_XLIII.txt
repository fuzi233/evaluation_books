The morning sun rose on a scene of deceptive tranquility. Marianne rose at her accustomed hour, offering a brittle smile to every inquiry regarding her health, attempting to mask her frailty with the pretense of normalcy. Yet, the evidence contradicted her efforts: a day spent shivering beside the fire, clutching a book she could neither focus upon nor comprehend, or collapsing onto a sofa in a state of profound lethargy, spoke volumes against her claimed improvement. When she finally retreated to bed, increasingly indisposed, Colonel Brandon found himself astonished solely by Elinor's composure. Though Elinor had nursed her relentlessly throughout the day and administered harsh remedies against Marianne's will, she shared her sister's fatalistic reliance on sleep, dismissing genuine alarm with a stoicism that bordered on negligence.

However, the night that followed shattered this fragile equilibrium. A feverish, restless darkness dispelled all expectations of recovery. When Marianne, refusing to yield, eventually confessed her inability to sit upright and collapsed back into bed, Elinor seized upon Mrs. Jennings' counsel to summon the Palmers' physician. Dr. Harris arrived promptly, examining the patient with clinical detachment. While he encouraged Miss Dashwood with the assurance that mere days would restore her sister to health, his diagnosis carried a darker weight. By identifying a "putrid tendency" and casually invoking the term "infection," he triggered an instantaneous panic in Mrs. Palmer, who feared contagion for her infant son. Mrs. Jennings, who had harbored suspicions of the illness's gravity since the onset, grew grave upon hearing Dr. Harris's report. Validating Charlotte's fears, she insisted on the immediate evacuation of the household with the baby. Mr. Palmer, though initially dismissive of their hysteria, found his wife's anxiety and relentless pleading insurmountable. Consequently, her departure was finalized; within an hour of the doctor's arrival, she departed for a relative's estate several miles beyond Bath, taking her child and nurse with her. At her desperate request, Mr. Palmer pledged to follow shortly, and she implored her mother to accompany her. Yet, Mrs. Jennings, driven by a kindness that earned Elinor's deepest affection, declared her resolve to remain at Cleveland until Marianne's condition stabilized, vowing to fill the void of the absent mother through her own vigilant care. Elinor found herself inundated with her support, eager to share the burden and utilizing her superior nursing experience to mitigate the crisis.

Poor Marianne, drained by the virulence of her malady and acutely aware of her universal decline, could no longer cling to the illusion of imminent recovery. The knowledge that tomorrow—the day they were scheduled to depart for home, accompanied by Mrs. Jennings' servant to surprise their mother—would instead mark a catastrophic delay, intensified every ache. Her sparse utterances were laments of this inevitable postponement, though Elinor strove to lift her spirits, convincing her that the delay would be fleeting, a belief Elinor held firmly in that moment.

The subsequent day yielded no significant change. Marianne showed neither improvement nor deterioration, remaining static in her suffering. The household had diminished further; Mr. Palmer, reluctant to flee due to his inherent benevolence and a desire not to appear cowardly before his wife, was ultimately persuaded by Colonel Brandon to fulfill his promise. As he prepared to leave, Colonel Brandon himself voiced intentions to depart as well, exerting himself significantly to broach the subject. Here, Mrs. Jennings intervened with tactful wisdom. To remove the Colonel while his heart was torn apart by concern for his beloved sister would be to strip them of their sole solace. She explained that his presence was indispensable to her, proposing games of piquet in the evenings while Elinor tended to Marianne upstairs. Her plea, warmly echoed by Mr. Palmer—who found relief in leaving someone capable of guiding Elinor in emergencies—proved irresistible. Colonel Brandon, gratified by the opportunity to remain, could not sustain even a feigned objection.

Marianne remained ignorant of these shifting tides. Unaware that she had inadvertently precipitated the departure of the Cleveland household's masters within a week of their arrival, she felt no surprise at the absence of Mrs. Palmer, nor did she harbor concern, omitting the name entirely from her discourse.

Two days elapsed since Mr. Palmer's exit, and Marianne's condition remained stubbornly unchanged. Dr. Harris, attending daily, maintained his bold prognosis of swift recovery, and Elinor matched his sanguinity. However, the outlook for the others remained bleak. Mrs. Jennings had concluded early on that Marianne would never survive, and Colonel Brandon, whose primary utility lay in absorbing Mrs. Jennings' dire predictions, lacked the mental fortitude to resist their influence. He attempted to logically dismantle fears that the apothecary's contradictory judgments rendered absurd, yet the solitary hours stretching across each day fostered the intrusion of every melancholic thought. He could not banish the conviction that he would never see Marianne alive again.

Then, on the morning of the third day, the gloomy forecasts nearly evaporated. Upon Dr. Harris's arrival, he declared the patient materially improved. Her pulse strengthened considerably, and symptoms appeared markedly more favorable than the previous visit. Elinor, bolstered by renewed hope, radiated cheerfulness, rejoicing that her correspondence to her mother had reflected her own judgment rather than her friend's pessimism, minimizing the indisposition that had stalled their journey. She began to fixate on the date of Marianne's potential travel.

Yet, the day closed without the auspicious conclusion it had promised. Toward evening, Marianne relapsed, becoming heavier, more restless, and increasingly uncomfortable. Elinor, still optimistic, attributed the shift merely to the exhaustion of sitting up for her bed to be made. Administering the prescribed cordials, she watched with satisfaction as Marianne sank into a slumber, anticipating beneficial results. Although the sleep was not as tranquil as desired, it lasted a considerable duration. Determined to witness the outcome firsthand, Elinor resolved to remain by her side throughout. Mrs. Jennings, unaware of the sudden turn, retired unusually early; her maid, one of the primary caregivers, sought recreation in the housekeeper's quarters, leaving Elinor alone with Marianne.

The patient's repose deteriorated progressively. Elinor, watching with unceasing vigilance as her sister shifted positions constantly and noting the frequent, inarticulate murmurs of distress, found herself almost wishing to wake her from such agonizing dreams. Suddenly, jolted awake by an incidental noise within the house, Marianne sprang up with feverish intensity, crying out, "Is Mama coming?"

"Not yet," Elinor replied, masking her own terror while helping Marianne settle back down. "But I hope she arrives soon. It is a great distance from here to Barton."

"But she must not go via London!" Marianne shrieked, her voice hurried and frantic. "If she takes the road through London, I shall never see her!"

Elinor sensed with alarm that Marianne was losing her faculties. As she attempted to soothe her, she urgently checked the pulse. It was lower and quicker than ever before. Marianne's continued ramblings about her mother caused Elinor's alarm to escalate rapidly, compelling her to dispatch an immediate summons to Dr. Harris and send a messenger to Barton for their mother. The thought of consulting Colonel Brandon on how to facilitate the mother's arrival followed instantly. Having rung for the maid to replace her at the bedside, Elinor hastened to the drawing-room, where she knew he typically resided at a later hour.

There was no time for deliberation. Her fears and logistical challenges confronted him immediately. Regarding her fears, he possessed no courage or confidence to alleviate them; he listened in silent despair. Yet, her difficulties were instantly resolved. With a readiness that suggested a pre-meditated plan, he volunteered to serve as the messenger to fetch Mrs. Dashwood. Elinor offered no resistance that proved formidable. Thanking him with brief, fervent gratitude, she watched as he hurried to dispatch his servant with instructions to Dr. Harris and to arrange for post-horses directly. Meanwhile, she penned a few lines to her mother.

The comfort of such a steadfast ally in that critical moment—Colonel Brandon, or rather, the companionship he represented for her mother—was felt with profound gratitude. A companion whose judgment would steer, whose attentions would relieve, and whose friendship would soothe. In mitigating the shock of such a summons, his presence, his demeanor, and his assistance served as a buffer.

Meanwhile, regardless of his internal turmoil, Colonel Brandon acted with the firmness of a collected mind. He orchestrated every necessary arrangement with utmost dispatch, calculating with precision the timeframe for his return. No moment was wasted. The horses arrived ahead of schedule. Pressing Elinor's hand with a look of solemn gravity and speaking words too low for her to hear, he hurried into the carriage. It was approximately twelve o'clock. Elinor returned to Marianne's chamber to await the apothecary and tend to her sister through the remainder of the night. It was a period of nearly equal torment for both: hour after hour of sleepless agony and delirium for Marianne, countered by the most excruciating anxiety for Elinor, before Dr. Harris finally appeared. Once her apprehensions were ignited, they exacted a toll disproportionate to her prior sense of security. The servant who stayed with her, refusing to disturb Mrs. Jennings, only exacerbated her torment by hinting at the mistress's longstanding doubts.
