To the discerning eye, the truth regarding Harry Warrington and the unfortunate Cattarina was clear; yet, to the gossips of Tunbridge Wells, the Virginian appeared a dissipated nobleman of the highest caliber. Madame de Bernstein, in particular, remained steadfast in her disbelief of his innocence. To the old Baroness, a woman of Roman lineage, it was preferable that her nephew emulate the licentiousness of Rome itself. She eagerly accepted every scandal concerning Mr. Warrington's alleged "Lovelace" adventures. Having witnessed him host tea and music at the Wells, and noting his gallantry toward ladies of superior station and character compared to Cattarina, she had grown complacent. The suspicion that once plagued her regarding the affair begun at Castlewood had faded, replaced by a careless vigilance over Lady Maria. Perhaps she believed the danger had passed, or perhaps she had simply ceased to care, lulled by Maria's artful cajoling. At Maria's age, maidens learn to be cunning; at Madame Bernstein's age, dragons lose their ferocity. Their scales soften, their claws weaken to a feeble pat, and their venom becomes merely disagreeable rather than deadly. They trail their languid tails back to their caverns, their maleficence diminished by the softening influence of years.

If the virtuous folk of Oakhurst could not withstand the testimony of Harry's ill-doings, why should Madame Bernstein, with her vast experience of evil, be more credulous? Every woman in her circle believed the stories of his dissipation. When the little dancer returned to London, it was because heartless Harry had deserted her for another—whose name, or rather whose half-dozen whispered aliases, the society at Tunbridge Wells delighted to circulate. In an age where ancestors of all stripes met at Epsom, Bath, and Tunbridge to dance, game, and drink, Harry's bad reputation served to comfort his aunt, easing her mind regarding his passion for Lady Maria. So secure was she that when the chaplain arrived to escort her niece home, she refused to part, preferring to keep Maria under her watchful eye while whispering tales of her cousin's wild extravagances and promising to secure him a suitable wife of age and fortune.

During the last century, a certain French Duke and Marquis distinguished himself across Europe and America, leaving behind a volume of memoirs that posterity is wisely warned against consulting. Having played the part of Don Juan everywhere, he cataloged the court-beauties who fell to his fascination. It is curious to contrast the elegant refinements of European society, where a siege was all it took to vanquish a woman, with the simple, modest lives of the colonial folks. This European enslaver of hearts, it appears, made not a single conquest in America. While travelers loudly criticized the rudeness of transatlantic manners, the truth remains that American gentlemen conversed modestly and the lives of their women were pure. Harry Warrington brought this colonial modesty to England. Though surrounded by men of pleasure, he remained silent, never indulging in double entendres, boasting no victories over the sex, and appearing shy and awkward when others recounted such tales.

Mr. Sampson had noted this youthfulness at Castlewood, where Harry grew uneasy while his cousin Will told choice stories. My Lord had curtly rebuked his brother, advising him to keep his jokes for the usher's table at Kensington. Thus, the chaplain addressed his neighbor with the exclamation, "Reverentia pueris." Mr. Sampson, lacking the strength to do right himself, possessed at least the grace not to offend innocent young gentlemen with his cynicism.

The chaplain was touched by Harry's gift of the horse and felt a genuine friendliness toward the lad. "You see, sir," he said, "I am of the world and must do as the rest do. I have led a rough life and cannot afford to be more particular than my neighbors. *Video meliora, deteriora sequor*, as we said at college. I have a little sister at boarding school, and since I keep a decent tongue in my head when talking with her, I expect others to do the same, and surely I may try to do as much by you."

The chaplain praised Harry loudly to the old Baroness, who delighted in hearing him commended. She was fond of his good looks, manly bearing, ready blushes, bright eyes, and deep youthful voice. His shrewdness and simplicity constantly amused her. "We must find a good wife for him, Chaplain," she told Mr. Sampson. "I have one or two in my eye who will suit him. We must set him up here; he will never bear returning to his savages or living with his little Methodist mother."

On this point, Mr. Sampson was equally anxious and had a wife in his eye for Harry. He hoped that conversations with the Earl of Castlewood regarding a provision for the chaplain might facilitate a match for the anxious lady. If his good offices could help, Sampson was ready to employ them, waiting to see how effectively he could bring his influence to bear.

Sampson's society was agreeable, and within hours, he and his young friend became intimate. The parson rejoiced in high spirits, good appetite, and good humor. He pretended to no squeamishness and indulged in no sanctimonious hypocrisy, yet he took care not to shock his young friend with needless outbreaks of levity. He initiated his pupil only into the minor mysteries of the trade, withholding the secrets with which the unlucky adept himself was only too familiar. With Harry, Sampson was a brisk, lively companion, ready for drinking bouts, cock-fights, shooting matches, card games, or gallops across the common. Yet his conversation remained decent, aiming more to amuse than to lead astray. The chaplain was successful; possessing immense animal spirits, natural wit, and experience in the business of the "toad-eater"—a calling that had been his livelihood since entering college as a servitor—he navigated the social landscape with ease.

"What a reputation, sir, they have made for you in this place!" cried Mr. Sampson, returning from the coffee-house to his patron. "Monsieur de Richelieu was nothing to you!"

"How do you mean, Monsieur de Richelieu? Never was at Minorca in my life," said straightforward Harry, unaware of the French duke's famous victories at home.

Mr. Sampson explained. The pretty widow Patcham, who had just arrived, was desperate about Mr. Warrington; her behavior at the rooms the night before proved it. As for Mrs. Hooper, that was a known case, and the Alderman had fetched his wife back to London for no other reason. It was the talk of the whole Wells.

"Who says so?" cried Harry, indignantly. "I should like to meet the man who dares say so, and confound the villain!"

"I should not like to show him to you," said Mr. Sampson, laughing. "It might be the worse for him."

"It's a shame to speak with such levity about the character of ladies or gentlemen," continued Mr. Warrington, pacing the room in a fume.

"So I told them," said the chaplain, wagging his head and looking grave, though the truth was he had never considered being angry at such charges.

"It's a shame, I say, to talk away the reputation of any man or woman as people do here. Do you know, in our country, a fellow's ears would not be safe; a little before I left home, three brothers shot down a man for having spoken ill of their sister."

"Serve the villain right!" cried Sampson.

"Already they have had that calumny about me set a-going here, Sampson—about me and the poor little French dancing-girl."

"I have heard," said Mr. Sampson, shaking powder from his wig.

"Wicked, wasn't it?"

"Abominable."

"They said the very same thing about my Lord March. Isn't it shameful?"

"Indeed it is," said Mr. Sampson, preserving a face of wonderful gravity.

"I don't know what I should do if these stories came to my mother's ears. It would break her heart. Why, only a few days before you came, a military friend of mine, Mr. Wolfe, told me how the most horrible lies were circulated about me. Good heavens! What do they think a gentleman of my name and country can be capable of—I a seducer of women? They might as well say I was a horse-stealer or a housebreaker. I vow if I hear any man say so, I'll have his ears!"

"I have read, sir, that the Grand Seignior of Turkey has bushels of ears sometimes sent in to him," said Mr. Sampson, laughing. "If you took all those that had heard scandal against you or others, what basketsful you would fill!"

"And so I would, Sampson, as soon as look at 'em: any fellow who said a word against a lady or a gentleman of honor!" cried the Virginian.

"If you'll go down to the Well, you'll find a harvest of 'em. I just came from there. It was the high tide of Scandal. Detraction was at its height. And you may see the *nymphas discentes* and the *aures satyrorum acutas*," cried the chaplain, shrugging his shoulders.

"That may be as you say, Sampson," replied Mr. Warrington, "but if ever I hear any man speak against my character, I'll punish him. Mark that."

"I shall be very sorry for his sake, sir; for you'll mark him in a way he won't like, and I know you are a man of your word."

"You may be sure of that, Sampson. And now shall we go to dinner, and afterwards to my Lady Trumpington's tea?"

"You know, sir, I can't resist a card or a bottle," said Mr. Sampson. "Let us have the last first and then the first shall come last." And with this, the two gentlemen departed for their accustomed place of refectory.

That was an age in which wine-bibbing was more common than in our politer times. Since the arrival of General Braddock's army in his native country, the young Virginian had acquired a liking for filling bumpers and calling toasts, having heard it was a point of honor among officers never to decline a toast or a challenge. So Harry and his chaplain drank their claret in peace and plenty, naming some favorite lady with each glass.

The chaplain had reasons of his own for desiring to know the extent of the affair between Harry and Lady Maria. He and his young friend were warm enough with the claret to speak with that great eloquence and candor that good wine, taken in rather injudicious quantity, inspires. O kindly harvests of the Aquitanian grape! O sunny banks of Garonne! O friendly caves of Gledstane and Morol! May we not say a word of thanks for all the pleasure we owe you? Are the Temperance men to be allowed to shout in public places? Are the Vegetarians to bellow "Cabbage for ever?" and may we modest Enophilists not sing the praises of our favorite plant? After drinking good Bordeaux, there is a point at which men arrive when all the generous faculties of the soul are awakened; when wit brightens in sudden flashes, intellects are keenest, and pent-up thoughts rush abroad. The kindliest affection shakes hands with mankind, and the timid Truth jumps up naked to proclaim himself to all the world. How, by the kind influence of the wine-cup, we succor the poor and humble! How bravely we rush to the rescue of the oppressed! I say, in the face of all the pumps which ever spouted, that there is a moment in a bout of good wine at which, if a man could but remain, wit, wisdom, courage, generosity, eloquence, and happiness were his; but the moment passes, and that other glass somehow spoils the state of beatitude. There is a headache in the morning; we are not going into Parliament for our native town; we are not going to shoot those French officers who have been speaking disrespectfully of our country; and poor Jeremy Diddler calls about eleven o'clock for another half-sovereign, and we are unwell in bed, sending him away empty-handed.

Well, then, as they sat over their generous cups, the company having departed and the bottle of claret brought in by Monsieur Barbeau, the chaplain found himself in an eloquent state, with a strong desire to inculcate sublime moral precepts while Harry was moved by an extreme longing to explain his private history and impart all his present feelings to his new friend.

"I am glad to hear what your conduct has really been with regard to the Cattarina, Mr. Warrington; I am glad from my soul," said the impetuous chaplain. "The wine is with you. You have shown that you can bear down calumny and resist temptation. Ah! my dear sir, men are not all so fortunate. What famous good wine this is!" and he sucked up a glass with, "A toast from you, my dear sir, if you please?"

"I give you 'Miss Fanny Mountain, of Virginia,'" said Mr. Warrington, filling a bumper as his thoughts flew straightway, thousands of miles, to home.

"One of your American conquests, I suppose?" said the chaplain.

"Nay, she is but ten years old, and I have never made any conquests at all in Virginia, Mr. Sampson," said the young gentleman.

"You are like a true gentleman, and don't kiss and tell, sir."

"I neither kiss nor tell. It isn't the custom of our country, Sampson, to ruin girls, or frequent the society of low women. We Virginian gentlemen honor women; we don't wish to bring them to shame," cried the young toper, looking very proud and handsome. "The young lady whose name I mentioned hath lived in our family since her infancy, and I would shoot the man who did her a wrong;—by Heaven, I would!"
