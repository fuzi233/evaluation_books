On the appointed day, Major Pendennis and Arthur Pendennis arrived at Sir Francis Clavering's townhouse with the enthusiasm of two men who had absolutely nothing else to do. They found the drawing-room occupied only by Sir Francis, his wife, and Captain Strong. Arthur beamed with delight at the sight of Strong, a man he clearly admired, while his uncle, the Major, glared at him with the sour expression of a man forced to dine with a house steward. "Clavering's d—— house-steward," the Major muttered, though he quickly softened his features when Mr. Welbore Welbore, a dull but wealthy country gentleman, breezed in. The Major felt instantly safe in the presence of such solid, ancient respectability.

The company swelled with the Dowager Lady Rockminster, Lady Agnes Foker, and her son Harry, alongside the inevitable Miss Blanche Amory. Blanche arrived in a white silk dress that seemed to glow with its own internal light, displaying shoulders that Arthur deemed "pearly" and "killing." She was, in short, a stunner. She greeted Arthur with an affectionate simplicity, asking after his mother and Laura, and expressing a longing for the country that was so genuine it bordered on artlessness.

Harry Foker, usually a bit shy around ladies, found his tongue loosened by Blanche's charm. He spoke with a liveliness that surprised his own mother, who watched him from afar with wide eyes. As the party moved downstairs, Harry hoped to escort Blanche, but fate—or social protocol—assigned him to Mrs. Welbore Welbore, leaving Blanche to accompany the Major. However, Harry's ambition was not crushed; he maneuvered himself to sit directly beside her at the dinner table, flattered that he had secured such a prime spot, though it may have been arranged by someone else entirely.

At the table, Blanche found herself sandwiched between the two young men, each vying for her attention. Harry, eager to impress, launched into a monologue about Taglioni and *La Sylphide*, humming the famous air and asking if she had seen the dancer. "Miss Amory is a sylph herself," the Major interjected smoothly. Blanche, charmed, praised Harry's tenor voice and expressed a desire to sing with him. The Major, remembering similar advances from years past, felt a twinge of jealousy but masked it with a tender compliment, claiming he preferred her voice to Laura's contralto. He declared himself tired of loud professionals and London's blase atmosphere, insisting he only cared for the songs of his youth.

"I like English music best," Blanche agreed, turning to Harry to order a saddle of mutton. "Sing me one of the old songs after dinner," the Major pleaded. Blanche, however, turned her gaze to Harry, promising to sing if he would come up to tea immediately. Harry, a simple soul, accepted with a grin, explaining his modest drinking habits and ordering jelly with cherries inside, baffling the Major with his question on how the cherries got inside. Blanche listened to Harry's chatter with inexhaustible patience, dropping her gloves near him and her handkerchief near the Major, treating both with a kindness that made them both feel special.

When the ladies retired to the drawing-room, Blanche left a final, bright glance that swept over Captain Strong's white waistcoat and landed squarely on Harry. The door closed, leaving Harry sighing and swallowing a bumper of claret.

The dinner concluded early, allowing the ladies to retreat to the piano while the gentlemen lingered over wine. Through the open windows, the street scene unfolded: children peering at the fruit on the table, carriages rattling past, and the policeman patrolling the pavement. Among the passersby was a man gorgeously attired in chains, shiny boots, and a wig that defied sobriety. He was Captain Strong, laughing loudly through the window, recounting a joke that made a policeman pause in confusion.

Suddenly, the man outside began to squabble with the policeman, shouting that he knew everyone in the room, including Sir Francis and "that d—— Altamont." Inside, Sir Francis recognized the voice and the intruder. It was Colonel Altamont, a deserter from the Horse Artillery currently serving the Nawab of Lucknow. Strong rushed out to manage the situation, dragging the intoxicated Colonel into the house.

Altamont, furious at the indignity of being stopped at the door, demanded wine. Sir Francis, pale and agitated, managed to usher him into the dining room. The Colonel, reeling with triumph, scanned the room, glaring at the footman Jeames before sinking into a chair. He began drinking voraciously, making encomiastic remarks about the vintage.

"Very singular man," Strong noted gravely. "Indian habits."
"Very," the Major replied dryly.

Harry Foker whispered to the Major that this was the man who had sung the Malay song at the Back Kitchen. The Colonel, however, was in no mood for subtlety. Spotting the Major, he roared, "I know you, too, young fellow! Baymouth ball! Wanted to fight the Frenchman!" He laughed wildly, recalling the incident with drunken fervor.

"Mr. Pendennis, you remember Colonel Altamont?" Strong prompted.
The Major bowed stiffly. "He had the pleasure of remembering that circumstance perfectly."
"What's his name?" the Colonel bellowed.
"Pendennis!" the Major corrected sharply. "My name is Pendennis, and I will be obliged to you not to curse it too loudly."

The Colonel paused, his eyes narrowing as he stared at the Major. For a moment, the drunken haze seemed to lift, replaced by a fierce, sober intensity. Then, as if shaken by a sudden realization, he buttoned his coat, stumbled toward the door, and vanished, shouting, "Captain Beak! Captain Beak, by jingo!"

Within twenty minutes, the Colonel had arrived and departed. The remaining guests were bewildered. Sir Francis looked terrified, and the Major, realizing the Colonel was a deserter in foreign service, breathed a sigh of relief.

The evening continued with music. Blanche played the piano, enrapturing Harry, while the Major sat silently, watching her with a newfound appreciation. He later complimented her singing and pronunciation effusively, a stark contrast to his earlier disdain. After the party dispersed, the Major walked Harry Foker home, urging him to visit Grosvenor Place the next day. As they walked down the Strand, Harry couldn't stop talking about Blanche and her music, leaving the Major to wonder at the boy's single-minded devotion.
