The shock of what followed was so sudden, so unearthly in its speed, that the man who had loosed the crossbow stood frozen, stupefied, for a full heartbeat. Then he roared for his comrades to rally to him and help seize their quarry, and started scrambling up the grassy slope himself. But before he had covered half the distance, a broad, grim figure stepped out from behind the gnarled trunk of an ancient oak: Martin Wittenhaagen, his yew bow bent to full draw, a sharp, broad-headed arrow aimed dead straight down the path.

Eric Wouverman spotted him in that stance and darted behind the thickest tree he could reach, pressing himself flat to the bark as if he meant to melt right into it. Martin’s legendary skill with the bow was known across every village for ten miles around, and the dead bloodhound they had left lying at the edge of the wood was a keen, fresh reminder of what that skill could do. Wouverman poked one cautious eye around the edge of the bark, and there it was: the glint of the arrow’s point, still locked straight on where he huddled. He did not dare shift an inch, not even to brush the stinging nettles off his bare ankle.

He huddled there peeking out for minutes that stretched like hours, until the rest of his band came trampling up the hill in a noisy, grumbling crowd, clubs and crossbows raised, four deep. At that, Martin let out a short, scornful laugh that echoed off the treetops, and vanished into the underbrush. A moment later, they heard the clatter of hooves, and he was riding off at a gallop on the burgomaster’s own mule.

All the hunters pelted up to the hill crest together, and the high ground gave them an unobstructed view down a narrow, seemingly endless glade that stretched for miles ahead. Far, far below, they spotted Gerard and Margaret running for all they were worth, so tiny against the green grass they looked no bigger than dancing gnats, and Martin galloping after them, heels dug into the mule’s flanks, riding as if the hounds of hell were nipping at his heels. The hunters had been outwitted before they even realized they had fallen for a trick, and they would soon be outrun too.

A few simple words explain exactly how Martin’s gambit came together. We are all apt to chase clear causes behind coincidences, but coincidences are often deceptive. Everyone has seen a hare stumble over a bramble the very moment a musket goes off, raising a hunter’s hopes that he has struck his mark, only to dash them as the animal leaps up and streaks away unharmed. That is exactly what played out here: the burgomaster’s stubborn mule put her hoof straight down a rabbit burrow the very same instant the crossbow bolt whizzed harmlessly over the top of her head. She stumbled, reared, and fell hard, throwing both her riders clear. Gerard caught Margaret mid-fall to break her impact, but her weight and momentum dragged him right down beside her, and suddenly the whole party was sprawling winded in the dirt.

The docile mule was back on her feet in a trice, trembling all over. Martin staggered upright next, and glanced back to see only one lone hunter had crested the path behind them, far out of bowshot. That was when he sent the young lovers sprinting ahead on foot, while he hung back to check their pursuers exactly as we saw.

He galloped after them now, and when he finally caught up to the breathless pair after what felt like an endless chase, he boosted both Gerard and Margaret up onto the mule’s back at once, then loped along beside them until his own lungs burned too hard to keep the pace. Then he took his turn riding, letting the two of them run alongside to catch their breath. They rotated like that, so whoever was on their feet stayed fresh, and long before they even thought to slacken their speed, every sound of Dierich and his men, every trace of their hunt, had vanished hopelessly behind them. The crestfallen band of hunters trundled back through the woods eventually, to tend to their injured chief and mourn their prized dead bloodhound, their entire manhunt gone up in smoke.
