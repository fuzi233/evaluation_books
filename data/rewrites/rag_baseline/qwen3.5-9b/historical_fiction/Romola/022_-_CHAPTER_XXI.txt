November 17, 1494. Eighteen months had passed since Tito and Romola were wedded in the spring, a union marked by the ancient Greek custom of raining comfits from the sky to signify celestial blessing upon their dual existence. Yet, the landscape of Florence had shifted violently since that Easter. Just as a tree's bounty depends entirely on the flow of its sap, the private fortunes of the couple were now tethered to the grand, turbulent currents of Italian politics.

Only a week prior, the spirit of antiquity had surged back into the Florentine populace. The great bell of the palace tower had shattered the afternoon silence with a hammer-stroke of alarm, summoning the citizenry to arm themselves with rusted weapons, farming tools, and improvised clubs. They drove out the Medici. The gates of San Gallo slammed shut on Piero de' Medici, fleeing toward Bologna with his mercenaries in panic, and barred the path of his younger brother, the Cardinal, who escaped disguised as a Franciscan friar. A price was placed on their heads. Following the expulsion, the customary sackings occurred; the effigies of former conspirators were scrubbed from public walls, and the exiled enemies of the Medici were welcomed home. The fledgling tyrants were cast from their nests in the Via Larga, and the Republic regained its autonomy.

Now, however, the great palace in the Via Larga was being transformed for a different occupant. Drapery draped the streets in unfamiliar hues; banners spilled from windows; carpets and tapestries covered every step where exceptional feet might tread. This was undeniable evidence of civic joy, born of anticipation for a new guest. The stream of color flowed from the palace, winding past the Cathedral, sweeping the Piazza della Signoria, crossing the Ponte Vecchio, and reaching the Porta San Frediano, the gateway facing Pisa. Near this gate, a platform and canopy had been erected for the Signoria. Messer Luca Corsini, a doctor of law, felt his heart race with the weight of a Latin oration awaiting delivery. Every chief elder prepared himself, smoothing their chins and adjusting their silk *luccos* for the procession. Well-born youths inspected their new tunics, modeled after the French style, designed to impress the stranger with a peculiar grace. Meanwhile, a vast assembly of the clergy—from the Archbishop in his radiance to the trains of black, white, and grey monks—conferred early in the morning on the marshaling of relics, sacred banners, and consecrated jewels, ensuring their movements aligned perfectly with the anticipated arrival of the illustrious visitor at three in the afternoon.

This was an unprecedented guest. He had traversed the Alpine passes with an army Italy had never witnessed: thousands of formidable Swiss mercenaries, hardened to fight for love, hate, or coin; a host of gallant cavaliers proud of their lineage; and an infantry unit where one in every hundred carried an arquebus. Worse still, he brought bronze cannons that fired iron balls instead of stone, drawn by horses rather than oxen, capable of firing a second shot before a city could repair the breach caused by the first. Some compared this newcomer to Charlemagne, the legendary restorer of Florence and conqueror of degenerate kings; others likened him to Cyrus, the liberator of the chosen people and restorer of the Temple. He arrived with glorious designs: to march through Italy amid the jubilation of a grateful populace, to settle conflicting disputes at Rome, to seize the Kingdom of Naples by hereditary right and minimal bloodshed, and from that strategic foothold, to launch a crusade against the Turks, intending to slaughter or convert them to the Christian faith.

Such a scheme seemed fitting for the Most Christian King, head of a nation strengthened by the cunning of Louis XI, who had died in terror of his own prospects a decade earlier. This antitype of Cyrus and Charlemagne was none other than the son of that subtle king—the young Charles VIII of France.

On a general level, nothing could appear more grandiose or more capable of stirring memories of divine dispensations that reshaped human history. A widespread conviction held that the arrival of the French king and his army was an event beyond the ordinary order of Providence—one where marble statues might sweat, phantom warriors fought in the air, and beasts birthed monsters. This belief rested less on the sheer magnitude of a foreign invasion than on the moral emotions of the age, crystallized into presentiments. These sentiments found their most remarkable expression in the voice of a single man.

That man was Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of the Dominican convent of San Marco in Florence. On a September morning, as news of the French army's entry rang in men's ears, he preached in the Cathedral from the text, "Behold I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth." He believed this was supreme guidance, marking the exact point in his exposition of Genesis reached during the previous Lent. He interpreted the "flood"—a symbol of both avenging wrath and purifying mercy—as the divinely indicated manifestation of the French army. His audience, comprising some of the finest spirits of the age, the most cultivated intellects in the most cultured Italian city, accepted this interpretation with shuddering awe. Savonarola possessed a rare power to imprint his beliefs upon others and sway varied minds. Four years prior, he had proclaimed from Florence's chief pulpit that a scourge was descending upon Italy to purge the Church. Savonarola appeared to believe, and his followers waveringly agreed, that he held a mission akin to the Hebrew prophets, positioning the Florentines as a second chosen people.

While prophetic gifts were not unknown in that era—seers and heralds of the future existed within and without the cloisters—Savonarola stood out as a grand exception. In others, prophecy was like a farthing candle illuminating small corners of destiny with gossip; in Savonarola, it was a mighty beacon casting a warning light far ahead for humanity. To the soberest minds, the supernatural quality of his foresight gained credence from the peculiar conditions of the age.

By the close of 1492, the year Lorenzo de' Medici died and Tito Melema wandered into Florence, Italy enjoyed a peace and prosperity unthreatened by immediate danger. There was no fear of famine, for the harvests of corn, wine, and oil had been abundant. New palaces rose in fair cities, and villas dotted pleasant slopes. Those who possessed more than their share feared neither poverty nor the larger masses who possessed less. Citizens' armor grew rusty, and populations seemed tamed, licking the hands of masters who purchased ready-made armies as easily as they bought goods from Smyrna. Even the fear of the Turk had faded; the Pope found it more profitable to accept bribes for potential poisoning than to plan conquest or conversion.

This world, with its partitioned empire and spacious universal Church, appeared to be a handsome establishment for the fortunate and the wise—a realm where lust, obscenity, lying, treachery, oppression, and murder were pleasant, useful, and manageable. Adorning this substantial delight of tyranny and avarice was the patronage of polite learning and the fine arts, allowing flattery to be expressed in the choicest Latin and sublime artists to paint the holy and the unclean with equal skill. The Church, though reportedly disgraced in its leadership and lacking signs of renewal in its lower ranks, was more prosperous than in past eras. The heavens smiled above, and the earth remained still.

Yet, amidst this splendid masquerade, certain forces worked to disturb the general festivity. Ludovico Sforza, the Duke of Milan, a patron of Leonardo da Vinci, coveted the ducal crown for himself rather than letting it rest on a feeble nephew susceptible to poison. He feared the Spanish-born King Ferdinand of Naples and his Crown Prince Alfonso, who despised the cruelty required to eliminate a near relative for a Lombard usurper. The Neapolitan royals, in turn, feared their suzerain, Pope Alexander Borgia. All three watched Florence anxiously, fearing its central position might tip the balance through underhanded support. Furthermore, all states, including Venice—the cautious, stable, and powerful republic seeking dominion across the Adriatic and onto the western coast—feared one another.

It was thought that Lorenzo de' Medici prevented the fatal outbreak of these jealousies by maintaining alliances with Naples and the Pope while persuading Milan of their mutual benefit. However, the rash vanity of young Piero de' Medici quickly nullified his father's wary policy. Ludovico Sforza, suspecting a league against him, devised a counter-move: inviting the French king to march into Italy and claim Naples as heir to the House of Anjou. Ambassadors, termed "orators" in those harangue-filled times, traveled constantly. A recalcitrant cardinal, refusing to acknowledge a Pope elected by bribery and viewing him as a personal enemy, also journeyed back and forth, seconding the invitation with fiery rhetoric. The young king lent a willing ear. By 1493, rumors spread that Charles VIII of France was preparing to cross the Alps with a mighty army. The Italian populations, accustomed since the fall of the Roman Empire to seeking arbiters from afar, began to view his coming as a means of avenging wrongs and redressing grievances.

Within these rumors, Savonarola heard confirmation that his prophecy was being verified. What filled the ears of ancient prophets but the distant tread of foreign armies arriving to execute justice? He no longer gazed vaguely at the horizon for a storm; he pointed directly to the rising cloud. The French army was the new deluge destined to purify the earth from iniquity
