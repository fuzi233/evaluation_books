"If some mortal, born too soon,
Were laid away in some great trance—the ages
Coming and going all the while—till dawned
His true time's advent; and could then record
The words they spoke who kept watch by his bed,
Then I might tell more of the breath so light
Upon my eyelids, and the fingers warm
Among my hair. Youth is confused; yet never
So dull was I but, when that spirit passed,
I turned to him, scarce consciously, as turns
A water-snake when fairies cross his sleep."
—BROWNING: *Paracelsus*.

These lines framed the letter Sir Hugo pressed into Deronda's hand, a document that, despite its plain, almost colorless phrasing, promised a revelation of profound weight.

My good friend and yours, Sir Hugo Mallinger, will have told you that
I wish to see you. My health is shaken, and I desire there should be
no time lost before I deliver to you what I have long withheld. Let
nothing hinder you from being at the *Albergo dell' Italia* in
Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Wait for me there. I am
uncertain when I shall be able to make the journey from Spezia, where
I shall be staying. That will depend on several things. Wait for
me—the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Bring with you the diamond ring that
Sir Hugo gave you. I shall like to see it again.—Your unknown mother,

To Deronda, this epistle offered no clues as to the specific content of the withheld secret, yet it rendered resistance impossible. He accepted Sir Hugo's reticence, sensing an implicit pledge not to preface the mother's disclosures, and found his lifelong conjectures regarding his parentage abruptly stilled. Though his imagination soared quickly over a landscape of possibilities, he rigorously refused to privilege any single scenario. To do so would be to nurse a desire or repugnance that would cloud his judgment; instead, he resolved to prepare himself with a brave neutrality to face whatever truth awaited.

In this guarded state of mind, Deronda could not articulate the reason for his sudden absence to those around him, particularly Mordecai, whom the news would strike with a power akin to his own internal turmoil, albeit differently. If he confessed, "I am going to learn the truth about my birth," Mordecai's hope would ignite a dangerous, painful excitement. With careful exclusion of suppositions, Deronda framed his journey as undertaken solely by Sir Hugo's wish, masking his uncertainty with feigned indifference. He suggested the trip might be brief, leaving the duration vague.

"I will ask to have the child Jacob to stay with me," Mordecai comforted himself, offering a small measure of relief after the initial, mournful glances exchanged between them.

"I will drive round and ask Mrs. Cohen to let him come," Mirah offered gently.

"The grandmother will deny you nothing," Deronda replied, a smile softening his features. "I'm glad you were a little wrong as well as I," he added, looking kindly at Mordecai. "You thought that old Mrs. Cohen would not bear to see Mirah."

"I undervalued her heart," Mordecai admitted, his tone respectful. "She is capable of rejoicing that another's plant blooms though her own be withered."

"Oh, they are dear good people; I feel as if we all belonged to each other," Mirah said, a tinge of merriment coloring her smile.

"What should you have felt if that Ezra had been your brother?" Deronda asked, his voice tinged with mischief, though a flicker of provocation remained—he chafed slightly at how readily she had embraced people who stood to cause him significant annoyance.

Mirah regarded him with a momentary surprise before speaking softly. "He is not a bad man—I think he would never forsake any one." As the words left her lips, she blushed deeply, casting a timid glance at Mordecai before turning away to some quiet occupation. Her father lingered in her mind, a presence that cast a shadow over her conversation with her brother. To Mirah, the thought of him arriving to find them made the daylight streets seem as shadowy as a haunted forest, where every corner concealed an imaginary apparition.

Deronda perceived her involuntary allusion and understood the blush. How could he be slow to recognize feelings that now seemed closer to his own than ever? For the words of his mother's letter implied that his filial bond was not to be severed from painful conditions; indeed, singularly, the letter that had made his mother a living reality for the first time had paradoxically thrown her into greater remoteness for his affections. The tender yearning for a being whose life might have been marred by a lack of his care, the image of a mother who had received neither full reverence nor compassion, had long hovered secretly in his observations of the women he met. Yet, now, it appeared that picturing his mother according to this ideal fit the facts no better than his former conceptions about Sir Hugo. He wondered how, when his mother's very handwriting arrived bearing words of her actual feeling, his affections had instantly contracted into a state of comparative neutrality. A veiled figure with enigmatic speech had pushed aside the image he had, in spite of uncertainty, gradually molded and claimed as the possessor of his tenderness and dutiful longing.

As he prepared to depart for Genoa, the interest truly uppermost in his mind related less to his mother than to Mordecai and Mirah.

"God bless you, Dan!" Sir Hugo had said, shaking his hand. "Whatever else changes for you, it can't change my being the oldest friend you have known, and the one who has all along felt the most for you. I couldn't have loved you better if you'd been my own—only I should have been better pleased with thinking of you always as the future master of the Abbey instead of my fine nephew; and then you would have seen it necessary for you to take a political line. However—things must be as they may." It was a defensive movement on the baronet's part, mingling purposeless remarks with the expression of serious feeling.

When Deronda finally arrived at the *Italia* in Genoa, no Princess Halm-Eberstein was present. On the second day, however, a letter reached him stating that her arrival might occur within a week or be deferred by a fortnight and more. She was under circumstances rendering it impossible to fix her journey precisely, and she entreated him to wait as patiently as he could.

Faced with this indefinite prospect of suspense concerning matters of supreme moment, Deronda undertook the difficult task of seeking amusement on philosophic grounds, hoping to quiet his excited feelings and grant patience a lift over a weary road. His previous visit to this superb city had been cursory, leaving much to explore beyond the prescribed rounds of sight-seeing. He spent the cooler hours wandering observantly through the streets, the quay, and the environs, often taking a boat to enjoy the magnificent view of the city and harbor from the sea. Every sight, every subject—even the anticipated meeting with his mother—found a central union in Mordecai and Mirah, and the ideas immediately associated with them.

Amidst the thoughts that most filled his mind while his boat drifted within view of the grand harbor, rose the vision of the multitudinous Spanish Jews centuries ago, driven destitute from their homes, allowed land from crowded ships only for a brief rest on this grand quay of Genoa. They overspread it with a pall of famine and plague—dying mothers clutching dying children at their breasts, fathers and sons gazing at each other's haggardness, like groups from a hundred Hunger-towers turned out beneath the midday sun. Inevitably, dreamy constructions of a possible ancestry for himself wove themselves with these historic memories, which had begun to hold a new interest since his discovery of Mirah, and now, under Mordecai's influence, had become irresistibly dominant. He would have sealed his mind against such constructions if it had been possible, and he had never fully admitted to himself that he wished the facts to verify Mordecai's conviction. He inwardly repeated that he had no choice in the matter, and that wishing was folly; nay, on the question of parentage, wishing seemed part of that meanness which disowns kinship: it was a disowning by anticipation. What he had to do was simply to accept the fact; and he had really no strong presumption to go upon, now that he was assured of his mistake about Sir Hugo. There had been a resolved concealment which made all inference untrustworthy, and the very name he bore might be a false one. If Mordecai was wrong—if he, the so-called Daniel Deronda, were held by ties entirely aloof from any such course as his friend's pathetic hope had marked out?—he would not say "I wish"; but he could not help feeling on which side the sacrifice lay.

Across these two importunate thoughts, which he resisted as much as one can resist anything in that unstrung condition which belongs to suspense, there came continually an anxiety which he made no effort to banish—dwelling on it rather with a mournfulness, which often seems to us the best atonement we can make to one whose need we have been unable to meet.
