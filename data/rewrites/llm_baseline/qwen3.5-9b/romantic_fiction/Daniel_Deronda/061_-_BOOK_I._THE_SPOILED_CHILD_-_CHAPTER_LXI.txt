"Within the gentle heart Love shelters him, As birds within the green shade of the grove. Before the gentle heart, in Nature's scheme, Love was not, nor the gentle heart ere Love." —GUIDO GUINICELLI (*Rossetti's Translation*).

There existed another household beside the white house at Pennicote, another bosom besides Rex Gascoigne's, where the tidings of Grandcourt's demise stirred a tumult of profound agitation followed by a desperate effort to suppress it. It was the custom of Hans Meyrick to dispatch or carry the *Times* for his mother's perusal. She was a voracious consumer of current affairs, ranging from sweeping political upheavals to the latest lists of nuptials; the latter, she remarked, afforded her the delightful illusion of completing fashionable romances without the labor of reading them, allowing her to witness the fates of heroes and heroines resolved happily, unaware of the tragic realities awaiting the fictional characters.

On a typical Wednesday, Hans invariably delivered the paper at a precise hour, coinciding with the near-completion of Mab's weekly lesson, claiming he arrived solely to hear Mirah sing. Yet, on this specific Wednesday, after slipping into the parlor with his customary quiet tread and latch-key, he emerged shaking the newspaper aloft with a sharp, crackling noise that brutally interrupted Mab's attempt to render *Lascia ch'io pianga*, mimicking her teacher with distant fidelity. The piano fell silent instantly; Mirah, accompanying the melody, jumped involuntarily, startled by the sound, which, amidst the usual domestic noises, had registered in her ears as thunderous.

"Oh-o-o, Hans!" exclaimed Mab. "Why do you bring a noise more terrible than my singing?"

"What on earth is this wonderful news?" asked Mrs. Meyrick, the only other occupant of the room. "Any report from Italy? Have the Austrians surrendered Venice?"

"Not from Italy, but concerning someone from Italy," Hans replied, his tone and manner bearing a peculiar inflection that prompted immediate interpretation from his mother. Consider how some of us behave when an event, seemingly benign, appears to validate our private suspicions. We adopt a pregnant tone, asking, "What do you think?" of an innocent bystander who hasn't invested their wisdom in the same vessel as ourselves, only to find their response flat and unhelpful.

"No bad news?" Mrs. Meyrick asked anxiously, her thoughts instantly leaping to Deronda; Mirah's heart had already tightened with the same apprehension.

"Not bad for anyone we truly care about," Hans said quickly. "Rather remarkably fortunate, I'd say. I've never known anyone die so conveniently. Given how precious a gazelle I am, I'm constantly surprised to find myself still breathing."

"Oh, me, Hans!" Mab interjected impatiently. "If you must chatter about yourself, do it behind your back. What actually happened?"

"Duke Alfonso is drowned, and the Duchess lives—that's the gist," Hans stated, placing the paper before his mother and pointing to a specific paragraph. "But the most significant detail is this: Deronda was staying at the same hotel in Genoa with them. He witnessed her being carried ashore by the fishermen who rescued her from the water in time to prevent further harm. Apparently, they saw her leap in after her husband—a decision far less prudent than I would have expected from the Duchess. Fortunately, Deronda was there to ensure she received proper care."

Mirah sank back onto the music stool, lowering her eyelids and clutching her hands tightly. Mrs. Meyrick handed the paper to Mab and murmured, "Poor thing! She must have loved her husband dearly to jump in after him."

"It was mere inadvertence—a lapse of attention," Hans corrected, scrunching his face in a rogue's grin and throwing himself into a chair near Mirah. "Who could possibly harbor affection for a jealous baritone, casting freezing glances and singing perpetual asides? That was undoubtedly the husband's role. Nothing could be neater than his drowning. The Duchess is now free to wed a man with a magnificent head of hair and glances capable of melting her heart rather than freezing it. And I shall certainly be invited to the wedding."

At this, Mirah sprang upright from her seated posture. Fixing her gaze upon Hans with an angry gleam, she spoke in a voice deeply shaken by indignation. "Mr. Hans, you ought not to speak thus. Mr. Deronda would not wish you to address matters in this manner. Why attribute luck to him? Why employ such phrasing regarding life and death when what constitutes life for one may be death for another? How can you claim it was fortunate if he loved Mrs. Grandcourt? It could prove a great evil to him. She would tear him away from my brother—I know she would. Mr. Deronda would not consider it 'lucky' to pierce my brother's heart."

All three were struck by the sudden transformation in the atmosphere. Mirah's face, bearing an expression of anger reminiscent of Ithuriel, was pale, even to the lips usually flushed with color. She bore a striking resemblance to Hans, who sat transfixed, blushing furiously as if he were a girl, while stammering, "I am a fool and a brute, and I retract every word. I'll go and hang myself like Judas—if mentioning him is permissible." Even in his moments of sorrow, Hans's improvised words retained an inherent drollery. Yet, Mirah's anger remained undimmed; how could it be appeased? She had erupted in indignant speech much like creatures in acute pain biting their own flesh to make their agony bearable. She said no more, but seated herself at the piano, pressing the sheet music before her as if intending to resume playing.

It was Mab who broke the tension, while Mrs. Meyrick's countenance mirrored some of Hans's discomfort. "Mirah is entirely right to scold you, Hans. You are constantly taking Mr. Deronda's name in vain. And it is horrifying to joke in such a manner about his potential marriage to Mrs. Grandcourt. Men's minds must be exceedingly dark, I think," concluded Mab with considerable scorn.

"Quite true, my dear," Hans replied softly, rising and turning on his heel to walk toward the rear window. "We had best proceed, Mab; you haven't devoted your full time to the lesson," Mirah added, her tone unusually elevated. "Shall I sing this again for you, or would you prefer to sing it?"

"Oh, please sing it to me," Mab requested, eager to ignore the preceding turmoil.

Mirah immediately began *Lascia ch'io pianga*, pouring forth its melodious sobs and cries with renewed fullness and energy. Hans paused mid-stride, leaning against the mantelpiece, deliberately keeping his eyes averted from his mother's. Once Mirah finished her final note and struck the concluding chord, she rose and announced, "I must return home now. Ezra awaits me." She offered her hand silently to Mrs. Meyrick and hesitated slightly, unable to summon the courage to kiss her as was her custom. Instead, the small mother drew Mirah's face down to hers and whispered soothingly, "God bless you, my dear."

Mirah sensed she had committed an offense against Mrs. Meyrick by angrily rebuking Hans, mingled with her suffering was a pang of guilt, feeling she had displayed a kind of proud ingratitude, an unworthy assertion of superiority. Her friend, however, had intuitively grasped this compunction. Meanwhile, Hans had grabbed his wake-up cap and prepared to open the door.

"Now, Hans," Mab said, masking a sister's tenderness with cunning, "you aren't walking home with Mirah. I'm certain she would prefer otherwise. You are dreadfully disagreeable today."

"I shall accompany her if she does not forbid it," Hans replied, opening the door.

Mirah remained silent. When he held the outer door open for her and stepped inside to close it behind him, he walked by her side without invitation. She lacked the courage to initiate conversation again, conscious that her words to him may have been excessively severe, yet finding only harsher sentiments lurking beneath them in her own heart. Furthermore, she was besieged by a swarm of thoughts rushing forward to interpret that consciousness which remained unchanged within herself.

Hans, too, harbored a mind equally preoccupied. Mirah's anger had awakened in him a fresh realization, accompanied by the unpleasant sensation that he had been a fool not to perceive it sooner. He pondered whether Mirah's heart was entirely consumed by Deronda in a capacity other than that of her brother's benefactor. This supposition bred anxieties in Hans's mind that, to his credit, were not wholly selfish. He possessed a strong conviction, unshakable except by direct counter-evidence, that a serious attachment existed between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt. Piecing together numerous fragments of observation and gathering knowledge corroborated by what his sisters had heard from Anna Gascoigne, he was convinced not only that Mrs. Grandcourt harbored a passion for Deronda but also that, despite his friend's austere self-restraint, Deronda's susceptibility toward her signaled concealed love.

Some men, holding such a belief, would avoid allusions capable of stirring that susceptibility; but Hans's natural inclination drifted toward mischief, enjoying a form of playful experimentation on living subjects consisting of irritating his friends. These experiments had ultimately satisfied him that his suspicions were correct.
