The world of Westward Ho! was not merely a stage for adventure; it was a tapestry woven from the threads of destiny, where the path of honor often led away from the comfort of home. It began with John Oxenham, a man whose life would serve as the tragic counterpoint to the chivalry he sought to emulate. He saw the "White Bird"—a symbol of purity and high calling—not as a fable, but as a reality that demanded sacrifice. To him, the call of the sea and the sword was a summons to a higher law than mere self-preservation. Yet, his vision, though noble, was clouded by a fatal flaw: an inability to distinguish between the ideal and the practical, leading him down a road where the white bird became a ghost, seen only in the distance, never caught.

Amidst the shadow of Oxenham's tragedy stood Amyas Leigh, a young gentleman of Wales who returned from the wilds of the New World with eyes wide open to both beauty and brutality. His first return was not a triumphant parade, but a quiet, weary step back into the familiar lanes of Devonshire. He carried stories of distant shores, of forests where the deer ran alongside hounds, and of men who had faced the unknown without flinching. Amyas did not seek glory; he sought understanding. He wanted to know if the virtues he had witnessed abroad could take root in the soil of England, or if they were destined to remain exotic flowers, beautiful but untouchable.

There were two brothers in spirit, though separated by circumstance, who embodied the duality of the hunter and the hunted. They rode with the pack, their hearts set on the chase, yet when the moment came, they found themselves running *with* the prey rather than against it. This was not cowardice, but a profound empathy born of long journeys through foreign lands. They learned that the line between predator and protector is thin, and that true strength lies not in domination, but in the ability to coexist with the forces one seeks to master. Their lives became a lesson in balance, teaching them that to hunt was to understand the nature of the thing hunted, and to respect the wildness within oneself.

Love, too, had its two paths, just as there were two ways to be crossed in fortune. For some, love was a gentle guide, leading them steadily toward union and peace. For others, like many who would find themselves entangled in the dramas of Clowley Court, love was a storm that swept them off course, leaving wreckage in its wake. One way led to the heart, the other to the head; one brought warmth, the other burned like fire. Those who walked the second path often found themselves trapped in webs of their own making, unable to see clearly until the smoke cleared. In the olden time, at Clowley Court, these choices defined the fate of families, turning estates into battlegrounds where affection turned to enmity.

Clowley Court stood then as a fortress of tradition, a place where the past held sway over the present. Its halls echoed with the laughter of generations, but also with the whispers of secrets kept in silence. Here, the nobility of the Rose Brotherhood took shape, forged in the fires of loyalty and tested by the trials of war. The court was more than stone and mortar; it was a living entity, shaped by the hands of those who built it and the hearts of those who dwelt within. It was a microcosm of the wider world, where alliances were made and broken, and where the stakes were always high.

Beyond the borders of England lay the Combes of the Far West, a land of mystery and wonder. There, the rules of the civilized world gave way to the raw instincts of survival. It was here that the true test of character awaited, where men were stripped of their titles and reduced to their essential selves. The Combes offered no easy answers, only challenges that demanded courage, wit, and an unyielding spirit. Those who ventured there returned changed, carrying with them the weight of experiences that could not be forgotten.

And so, the tale of John Oxenham unfolded, a story of ambition and misjudgment. He had dreamed of greatness, of becoming a figurehead for the cause of exploration and discovery. But his dreams were outpaced by reality, which proved far more cruel than any fiction. His journey ended in sorrow, a testament to the dangers of ignoring the wisdom of experience. His story served as a warning to all who followed, reminding them that the road to glory is paved with pitfalls, and that even the most virtuous intentions can lead to disaster if not guided by sound judgment.

From the ashes of failure rose the Noble Brotherhood of the Rose, a group united by a shared vision of justice and honor. They were formed in the shadow of Oxenham's downfall, determined to forge a different kind of legacy. Their bond was strong, forged in the crucible of adversity and tempered by the fires of conflict. Together, they would face whatever challenges lay ahead, bound by a code of conduct that placed duty above personal gain. The Rose became their symbol, representing the resilience of the human spirit and the enduring power of friendship.

Amyas Leigh, however, found solace in the traditions of home. On Christmas Day, he kept the spirit alive, gathering friends and family around the hearth to share stories of the year past. It was a day of reflection, a chance to look back on the adventures and struggles that had shaped him. In the warmth of the season, he found a sense of belonging, a reminder that despite the hardships of the road, there were still moments of joy and connection to be cherished.

But not everyone was content with the status quo. The Mayor of Bideford, a man of sharp tongue and sharper mind, decided to challenge the norms of society. He baited his hook with his own flesh, daring others to follow suit. His actions sparked a wave of change, forcing people to confront their own fears and prejudices. In doing so, he revealed the depths of human potential, showing that even the most unlikely individuals could rise to meet the challenges of the times.

Meanwhile, Eustace Leigh, another brother of the house, embarked on a journey that would take him to the highest circles of power. He met the Pope's Legate, a man of great influence and authority. Their meeting was a pivotal moment, shaping the future of the Leigh family and the broader community. Through this encounter, Eustace gained insights into the workings of the Church and the complexities of international relations. His experiences broadened his horizons, preparing him for the challenges that lay ahead.

Back in Bideford, the bridge itself seemed to come alive, hosting a feast at Annery House. It was a celebration of unity, bringing together people from all walks of life to enjoy good food and lively conversation. The atmosphere was festive, filled with music and laughter. For a brief moment, the divisions of class and status faded, replaced by a sense of camaraderie and shared purpose.

And then, the Golden Hind returned, a ship laden with treasures and tales of the sea. Her arrival marked the beginning of a new era, one where the possibilities of exploration knew no bounds. The crew, battered but victorious, stepped onto the shore, ready to share their stories with the world. Their return inspired hope and excitement, fueling the imaginations of those who heard their accounts.

In the midst of this upheaval, Salvation Yeo emerged as a hero, slaying the King of the Gubbings in a fierce battle. His feat was legendary, earning him the admiration of all who knew him. It was a testament to the power of courage and determination, proving that even the smallest among us could achieve great things when called upon.

John Brimblecombe, a man of integrity, reflected deeply on the meaning of an oath. He understood that words carried weight, and that breaking a promise was akin to betraying one's soul. His insights guided others, helping them navigate the moral dilemmas that arose in the chaos of the times.

The Good Ship Rose embarked on a voyage that would go down in history as the most chivalrous adventure ever undertaken. Her crew displayed bravery and compassion, saving lives and upholding the ideals of knighthood. Their journey became a beacon of hope, inspiring others to strive for excellence and justice.

Their next destination was Barbados, a place of natural beauty but devoid of human presence.

Any traveler who has wandered through the lush, undulating landscapes of North Devon knows the quiet, white-walled town of Bideford. It climbs gently from the broad river, paved with yellow sands, past a bridge where salmon wait for the autumn rains, rising toward the western uplands. Above the town, hills wrapped in deep oak forests rise sharply, interrupted here and there by crags of fern-fringed slate. Below, the land softens into rolling knolls and fertile squares of red and green, eventually sinking into the vast, hazy flats of salt marsh and sand dune where the Torridge meets the Taw. Together, these waters flow quietly toward the Atlantic swell, joining the endless, thundering rhythm of the ocean.

There, pleasantly situated beneath a sky reminiscent of Italy, the town breathes in the constant, cooling breeze from the sea—a climate that spares it both the biting winter frosts and the fierce heat of the interior. This haven has stood for nearly eight hundred years, since the first Grenville, a cousin of the Conqueror, returned from conquering South Wales. He gathered around him faithful Saxon serfs, free Norse rovers with their golden hair, and dark Silurian Britons from the shores of Swansea. Their mingled blood still flows through the seaward people of the region, granting them a strength, an intellect, and a peculiar beauty of countenance that remains distinct even in these leveling times.

Yet, at the time of this writing, Bideford was more than merely a pleasant country town. Its quay was not haunted by a few local craft; it was one of England's chief ports. It had furnished seven ships to fight the Armada, and chroniclers note that even a century later, "it sent more vessels to the northern trade than any port in England, saving London and Topsham." It was the center of a vibrant local civilization, however humble compared to the industrial might of today. One need not despise the day of small things if it proves to be the dawn of mighty ones. England owes the foundation of her naval and commercial glory to the sea-life and labor of towns like Bideford, Dartmouth, Topsham, and Plymouth—then a mere settlement—and to the men of Devon: the Drakes, Hawkins, Gilberts, Raleighs, Grenvilles, and Oxenhams, alongside a host of forgotten worthies. Had they not first crippled the ill-gotten resources of the Spaniard through West Indian raids, and then crushed his final, massive effort in Britain's Salamis in 1588, what would we be today but a papal appendage of a world-tyranny as cruel as heathen Rome, if not far more devilish?

I write this book in memory of these men—their voyages, their battles, their faith, their valor, and their heroic, untimely deaths. If I occasionally warm into a style that seems too stilted or pompous, let me be excused for the sake of my subject. It is a matter fit to be sung, not spoken, and proclaimed to all true English hearts not as a novel, but as an epic. Like the songs of Troy, the Persian Wars, or the trophies of Marathon and Salamis, it speaks to the heart of every true Greek—or Englishman—from ages past.

One bright summer afternoon in the year of grace 1575, a tall and fair boy lingered along the Bideford quay. Clad in a scholar's gown, carrying a satchel and slate, he watched wistfully the shipping and the sailors until he reached the bottom of the High Street, directly opposite one of the many taverns overlooking the river. Inside the open bay window sat merchants and gentlemen discussing their afternoon draught of sack. Outside the door, a group of sailors gathered, listening intently to a speaker standing amidst them.

The boy, alive with the news of the sea, stepped forward to join the sailor-lads peeping and whispering beneath the men's elbows. He caught the following speech, delivered in a loud, bold voice with a strong Devonshire accent and a fair sprinkling of oaths.

"If you don't believe me, go and see, or stay here and grow all over blue mould. I tell you, as I am a gentleman, I saw it with these eyes, and so did Salvation Yeo there, through a window in the lower room. We measured the heap, as I am a christened man, seventy foot long, ten foot broad, and twelve foot high, of silver bars, each weighing between thirty and forty pounds. And says Captain Drake: 'There, my lads of Devon, I've brought you to the mouth of the world's treasure-house, and it's your own fault now if you don't sweep it out as empty as a stockfish.'"

"Why didn't you bring some of them home, then, Mr. Oxenham?"

"Why weren't you there to help carry them? We would have brought them away safely, and Young Drake and I had broken the door abroad already. But Captain Drake goes off in a dead faint. When we came to look, he had a wound in his leg you might have laid three fingers into, and his boots were full of blood, having been so for an hour or more. But the point of it was, he never knew it till he dropped. Then his brother and I got him away to the boats, him kicking and struggling, bidding us let him go on with the fight, though every step he took in the sand sank him deeper in a pool of blood. And tell me, ye sons of shotten herrings, wasn't it worth more to save him than the dirty silver? Silver we can get again, brave boys. There's more fish in the sea than ever came out of it, and more silver in Nombre de Dios than would pave all the streets in the west country. But of such captains as Franky Drake, Heaven never makes but one at a time. And if we lose him, good-bye to England's luck."

"He who delivered this harangue" was a tall, sturdy personage with a florid, black-bearded face and bold, restless dark eyes. He leaned against the wall with crossed legs and arms akimbo, appearing to the schoolboy a very *magnifico*, some prince or duke at least. He was dressed contrary to all sumptuary laws of the time in a suit of crimson velvet, slightly the worse for wear. By his side hung a long Spanish rapier and a brace of gaudy daggers. His fingers sparkled with rings, and several gold chains adorned his neck. Large earrings hung from his ears, behind one of which a red rose was stuck jauntily among glossy black curls. Upon his head sat a broad velvet Spanish hat. Instead of a feather, a great gold clasp fastened a whole Quetzal bird, whose gorgeous plumage of fretted golden-green shone like an entire precious stone.

As he finished his speech, he removed the hat and looked at the bird.

"Look ye, my lads, did you ever see such a fowl as that before? That's the bird which the old Indian kings of Mexico let no one wear but their own selves. And therefore I wear it—I, John Oxenham of South Tawton—as a sign to all brave lads of Devon, that as the Spaniards are the masters of the Indians, we're the masters of the Spaniards." He replaced his hat.

A murmur of applause followed, though one voice hinted that the Spaniards were "too many for them."

"Too many? How many men did we take Nombre de Dios with? Seventy-three were we, and no more when we sailed out of Plymouth Sound. Before we saw the Spanish Main, half were *gastados*—used up, as the Dons say—with scurvy. In Port Pheasant, Captain Rawse of Cowes fell in with us, adding some thirty hands. With that handful, only fifty-three in all, we picked the lock of the New World! And whom did we lose but our trumpeter, who stood braying like an ass in the middle of the square, instead of taking care of his neck like a Christian? I tell you, those Spaniards are rank cowards, as all bullies are. They pray to a woman, the idolatrous rascals! No wonder they fight like women."

"You're right, captain!" sang out a tall, gaunt fellow standing close by. "One west-country-man can fight two east-landers, and an east-lander can beat three Dons any day. Eh! my lads of Devon?"

Five years have slipped away like shadows on a wall. It is nine o'clock on a crisp, luminous November morning, yet the bells of Bideford Church are tolling the daily service two hours ahead of schedule. Instead of a somber, dutiful peal, they burst forth in intervals of jocular ecstasy, tumbling head-over-heels in a frenzy of joy. The streets of Bideford have transformed into a riotous garden of color, swarming with seamen and burghers, husbands and daughters clad in festive attire. Garlands span the thoroughfares, and tapestries adorn every window. The ships anchored in the pool flutter every flag, unleashing a cacophony of ordnance from every caliber. Every stable groans under the weight of horses, while Sir Richard Grenville's house resembles a bustling tavern, filled with the clamor of eating, drinking, unsaddling, and the frantic movement of grooms and serving-men.

Alongside the churchyard, densely packed with women, flows the gentle blood of North Devon—tall, stately men and fair ladies, embodying the spirit of a time when the gentry led the people not merely by intellect, but by personal prowess and beauty. First comes the Countess of Bath, escorted by Sir Richard Grenville with his cap in hand, for her Earl husband, Bourchier, remains in London attending to the Queen. Following her are the Bassetts of Umberleigh, the Carys of Clovelly, the Fortescues of Wear and Buckland, the Coles of Slade, the Stukelys of Affton, the St. Legers of Annery, the Coffins of Portledge, and the Coplestones journeying from Eggesford, thirty miles away. Last, but certainly not least, comes Sir John Chichester of Ralegh, trailed in single file by his eight daughters and three of his five celebrated sons—one currently fighting a valiant campaign in Ireland to avenge his murdered brother, destined to rule wisely as Lord Deputy and Baron of Belfast.

At the gate, Sir John meets his cousin of Arlington, followed by a retinue of four daughters and nineteen sons; the youngest has not yet passed the town hall, while the eldest stands at the Lychgate, laughing as the younger makes way for the senior branch of that prolific tree. Inside the church, the congregation arranges itself by degree, though not without some sour glances, shoves, and whispers between the high-born matrons. The churchwardens and sidesmen, overwhelmed by such a distinguished assembly, bustle in a state of frantic heat and redness, eventually imploring Sir Richard himself to identify everyone, distinguish the elder from the younger branches, and determine who bears eight quarterings versus four, lest half the ladies of North Devon descend upon one another in deadly feud. The elderly patriots are safely seated in the corporation pews, while the youth crowd the benches nearest the door, eager to gaze upon the ladies.

Finally, a hush falls. Eyes turn toward the door, and distant music—flutes, hautboys, drums, and trumpets—brays, screams, and thunders merrily up to the threshold before ceasing abruptly. The churchwardens rush down with rods in hand amidst a general whisper and rustle, accompanied by glad tears and blessings from many a woman and some men, as the wonder of the day arrives. The rector does not commence the morning service but rather offers the ancient Thanksgiving for a naval victory.

What, pray, has sent old Bideford wild with this "goodly joy and pious mirth"? Why do all eyes, with greedy admiration, fix upon the four weather-beaten mariners adorned with knots and ribbons by loving hands? And why, with even greater intensity, do they stare at the gigantic figure walking before them—a beardless boy, yet possessing the frame and stature of Hercules, towering like Saul of old, a head and shoulders above the entire congregation, his golden locks flowing over his shoulders? As the five men instinctively ascend to the altar and kneel before the rails, why do all gazes turn to the pew where Mrs. Leigh of Burrough hides her face between her hands, her hood rustling and shaking with joyful sobs?

Because there existed a profound fellowship in merry England, both in county and town. These are Devon men, men of Bideford: Amyas Leigh of Burrough, John Staveley, Michael Heard, Jonas Marshall of Bideford, and Thomas Braund of Clovelly. They are the first of all English mariners to sail round the world with Sir Francis Drake, having returned here to give thanks unto God.

'Tis a long tale. To explain how it came to pass, we must journey back a page or two, almost to the point from whence we started in the previous chapter.

Some time past twelve months after Mr. Oxenham's departure, young Amyas continued quietly enough, adhering to his promises, save for certain occasional outbursts of fierceness common to all young males, particularly boys of strong character. His scholarship advanced no faster than before, but his home education flourished healthily. He was rapidly becoming, in his youth, a proficient archer, rider, and swordsman in the old school of buckler practice. Then, his father, traveling on business to the Exeter Assizes, contracted the gaol-fever from the prisoners. He sickened swiftly within the court and died within a week.

Thus, Mrs. Leigh was left to God and her own soul, tasked with taming and training this young lion-cub for both this life and the life to come. She had loved her husband fervently and holily. He had often been peevish and melancholy; a disappointed man whose estate was impoverished by his father's folly and his own youthful ambition. That ambition had drawn him to Court, causing him to waste his heart and purse chasing a vain shadow. He possessed almost every gift save the ability to wield them effectively. Though a scholar, courtier, and soldier, he found himself, past forty, without settled employment or aim, hampered by a certain shyness, pride, or delicate honor that prevented him from playing a winning game in the world after whose prizes he had so long hungered. Consequently, he continually grumbled.

At last, by sheer good fortune, he met a fair young Miss Foljambe of Derbyshire, then at Queen Elizabeth's Court. She, too, was weary of the world's sins, though she had witnessed fewer. The two contrived to please one another so completely that, despite the Queen's mild grumbles regarding the lady's marriage and the gentleman's adoration of anyone other than her royal self, they obtained leave to vanish from the little Babylon of Whitehall and settle in peace at Burrough. In her, he found a treasure, knowing exactly what he had discovered.

Mrs. Leigh, from her youth, had been one of those noble old English churchwomen, devoid of superstition yet lacking in severity, representing among the fairest features of that heroic era. Yet, a melancholy clung to her, rooted in childhood memories when it was an awful thing to be a Protestant. She recalled twenty-five years prior the burning of poor blind Joan Waste at Derby and Mistress Joyce Lewis, a lady born, like herself. Sometimes, even in her nightly dreams, she heard her mother's bitter cries to God, begging either to spare her that fiery torment or to grant her the strength to endure it, as her beloved predecessor had done. Her mother, of good Yorkshire stock, had been a bedchamber woman to Queen Catherine and a bosom friend and disciple of Anne Askew. She had sat in Smithfield, her blood curdled by horror, watching the hapless Court beauty, a month removed from the paragon of Henry VIII's court, carried in a chair—crippled by the rack—to her fiery doom at the stake beside her fellow-courtier, Mr. Lascelles. As the shuddering mob gazed, the heavens seemed to speak their wrath and grief in solemn thunder and heavy, hissing rains falling upon the crackling pile.

Thus, a sadness hung upon her throughout her life, deepening during the reign of Queen Mary. As a notorious Protestant and heretic, she had been forced to hide for her life among the hills and caverns of the Peak. She survived only by the love her husband's tenants bore her and by his bold declaration: that, though a good Catholic, he would run through the body of any constable, justice, priest, bishop, or cardinal who dared to execute the Queen's warrant upon his wife.

She escaped, but that sadness remained her constant companion. Its dark mantle fell upon the young girl who had shared her wanderings and hidings among the lonely hills. After marriage, she gave herself utterly up to God. Yet, in giving herself to God, she gave herself to her husband, her children, and the poor of Northam Town. She remained welcome to the Grenvilles, Fortescues, Chichesters, and all the gentle families surrounding them, who honored her husband's talents and enjoyed his wit. She accustomed herself to austerities, often prompting kindly rebukes from her husband, yet she undertook them without a superstitious thought of appeasing a fancied wrath of God or seeking to please Him through self-inflicted pain. Amyas slept that night a wearied and troubled slumber. To his mother and to Frank, who leaned over his pillow, his countenance betrayed a mind busy with visions. And truly, why should it not be? Beyond the sheer excitement of the day, the memory of John Oxenham had seized hold of his spirit with a strangeness that refused to loosen its grip. Throughout the evening, sitting in the bay-windowed room where he had last beheld the lost adventurer, Amyas had recalled every glance and gesture of the fugitive, puzzling at himself for such fixation until sleep claimed him, only to renew the fantasy in dreams.

In his dream, he found himself sailing westward, chasing the wake of the setting sun, pursuing a tiny sail that was John Oxenham's. A heavy dread weighed upon him: if he did not catch her in time, something terrible would unfold. Yet his ship would not move. Around him drifted the Sargasso Sea, its long, snake-like coils of weeds choking his bow. Still, he strained at the oars, imagining motion where there was none, until the sun sank and darkness fell absolute. Then the moon rose, and suddenly John Oxenham's vessel drew close. Her sails were rent and flapping; tar streamed from her sides; her bulwarks rotted away. What hung along the mainyard, a line of dark shapes? Men hanged! And overhead, from the yard-arm, John Oxenham's corpse gazed down with grave, light-filled eyes, beckoning and pointing as if to guide his path, striving to speak but silent, pointing not forward but backward along their course. When Amyas turned back, lo! behind him stood the snow-range of the Andes, glittering in the moonlight. He realized he was in the South Seas again, with all of America separating him from home. The corpse continued to point back, eyes burning with agony, lips longing to whisper some awful secret, until Amyas sprang awake with a cry of terror. He found himself lying in the little coved chamber at Burrough, the gray light of autumn morning already seeping in.

Fevershot and agitated, he failed to return to sleep. An hour later, tossing and turning, he rose, dressed, and made his way to bathe on his beloved pebble ridge. Passing his mother's door, he could not resist a glance within. The dim morning light revealed the bed; its pillow had not been disturbed since last night. His mother, clad in a long white nightdress, knelt at the far end of the chamber at her prie-dieu, lost in devotion. Gently, he slipped in without a word and knelt beside her. She turned, smiling, passing an arm around him, continuing her silent prayers. Why not? They were for him, and he knew it, praying also—for her, for the lost John Oxenham, and for all his vanished crew.

At last she rose, standing above him, parting the golden locks from his brow, gazing long and tenderly into his face. There was nothing to speak, for there was nothing to conceal between these two souls as transparent as glass. Each knew the depths of the other's meaning; each knew that their thoughts were fully shared. Finally, the mutual gaze ended; she stooped to kiss him on the brow, and as she moved to turn away, a tear fell onto his forehead. Her small, bare feet peeked out from beneath her dress. He walked down to the pebble ridge, where the ocean's fury had been defeated over ages, rolling up a rampart of gray boulders, two miles long, cunningly curved, smoothed, and fitted as if wrought by human hands. This barrier protected a fertile sheet of smooth, alluvial turf from the high tides of spring and autumn. Sniffing the keen salt air like a young sea-dog, he stripped and plunged into the breakers, diving, rolling, and tossing about the foam with stalwart arms. Then he heard himself hailed from the shore and, looking up, saw standing atop the rampart the tall figure of his cousin, Eustace.

Amyas was half-disappointed at his arrival; love-lorn as he was, he had been dreaming all the way there of Rose Salterne, and harbored no desire for a companion who might interrupt his reveries on the return journey. Nevertheless, having not seen Eustace for three years, it was merely polite to scramble out and dress, while his cousin paced up and down on the inner turf.

Eustace Leigh was the son of a younger brother of Leigh of Burrough, who had effectively severed ties with his family and countrymen by remaining a Papist. Though born a Catholic, he had not always been one; like many gentry, he became a Protestant under Edward VI, then reverted to Catholicism under Mary. But honor be to him, he stopped there, possessing too much honesty to turn Protestant a second time when Elizabeth ascended the throne. Thus, a Papist he remained, living apart from the world in a great, rambling, dark house still called "Chapel," situated on the Atlantic cliffs of Moorwinstow parish, not far from Sir Richard Grenville's estate at Stow. The penal laws never troubled him. First, they never troubled anyone who did not make conspiracy and rebellion an integral doctrine of his faith; second, they seldom troubled even such individuals unless, fired by the glory of martyrdom, they bullied the long-suffering of Elizabeth and her council into granting them their due, insisting on being hanged regardless of Burleigh's wishes. Moreover, in such a no-man's-land, an end-to-all-the-earth, that ancient house at Moorwinstow stood, that a dozen conspiracies might have been hatched there unheard of. Jesuits and seminary priests skulked in and out all year, unquestioned though unblest, finding a sort of piquant pleasure, like naughty boys in a store-closet, in inhabiting mysterious little dens in a lonely turret, ascending a trapdoor to celebrate Mass in a secret chamber in the roof, where they were permitted by the authorities to play as much as they chose at persecuted saints and preach about hiding in dens and caves of the earth. On one occasion, when the zealous parson of Moorwinstow, having discovered (what everyone already knew) the existence of "mass priests and their idolatry" at Chapel House, made formal complaint to Sir Richard, calling upon him, as the nearest justice of the peace, to enforce the Act of the Fourteenth of Elizabeth, that worthy knight soundly rated him for fantastical Puritanism and bade him mind his own business if he wished not to make the place too hot for them. Whereupon (for the temporal authorities, happily for the peace of England, kept a somewhat tight hand upon the spiritual ones in those days) the worthy parson subsided—for, after all, Mr. Thomas Leigh paid his tithes regularly enough—and was content, as he expressed it, to bow his head in the house of Rimmon like Naaman of old, by eating Mr. Leigh's dinners as often as invited, ignoring the vocation of old Father Francis, who sat opposite, dressed as a layman, calling himself the young gentleman's pedagogue.

She sat in the little farmhouse beside the mill, nestled in the green depths of the Combe valley, halfway between Stow and Chapel. Here, cut off from the grand festivities of Bideford, she spent a Martinmas Lent in this far western glen, sulking as much as her sweet nature would permit. So utterly lonely was she that, despite regarding Eustace Leigh with a mixture of aversion and Protestant suspicion, she could not bear to avoid chatting with him whenever he descended to the farm and its mill. He contrived to find some "would-be errand" to bring him down almost daily. Her uncle and aunt initially appeared stiff at these visits, with the aunt carefully inserting herself as a third party in every conversation. Yet, Mr. Leigh was a gentleman's son, a neighboring squire and a valued customer; it would not do to be rude to him. Conversely, Rose was the rich man's daughter, and it would not do to quarrel with her. Moreover, the pretty maid, aided by both wilfulness and sweet winning tricks, generally contrived to get her own way wherever she went. Rose herself had wisely begged her aunt never to leave them alone, for she "could not a-bear the sight of Mr. Eustace, only she must have some one to talk with down here." Her aunt, considering herself but a simple country-woman compared to townsfolk, decided that people knew their own business best and let matters slide. Meanwhile, Eustace, acutely aware that the difference in creed between them was likely the hardest obstacle to his love, kept his private opinions strictly in the background. Instead of attempting conversion, he daily purchased milk or flour, giving it away to the old women of Moorwinstow, who agreed that, for a Papist, he was a godly young man enough. Though the two Jesuits found it expedient, for the holy cause in which they were embarked, to reconcile themselves outwardly to the powers that be, they remained busy in private plotting their overthrow. Ever since April, they had played at hide-and-seek through the length and breadth of England, now lying quiet awaiting news from Ireland that would give them their cue. A great "rising of the West" was expected to sweep from her throne that stiff-necked, persecuting, excommunicate, reprobate, illegitimate, and profligate usurper who falsely called herself the Queen of England. For they had as stoutly persuaded themselves in those days—as they have in these, with a real Baconian contempt for the results of sensible experience—that the heart of England was truly with them, and that the British nation was on the point of returning to the bosom of the Catholic Church, giving up Elizabeth to be led in chains to the feet of the rightful Lord of Creation, the Old Man of the Seven Hills. This fair hope, which had skipped just in front of them for centuries, always a step farther off like the place where the rainbow touches the ground, they announced at times in language that terrified old Mr. Leigh.

One day, indeed, as Eustace entered his father's private room after his usual visit to the mill, he could hear voices raised in dispute. Now it falls to my lot to chronicle the founding of that most chivalrous brotherhood of the Rose. Though its fame would shortly spread far beyond Devon, becoming formidable in Ireland, the Netherlands, and across the Spanish Main, I am aware that this narrative may seem to modern ears Quixotic and fantastical. And where was Amyas on this same Christmas afternoon?

Amyas sat bare-headed in the stern of a boat in Smerwick Bay, the spray whistling through his curly hair. He shouted cheerfully to his men, "Pull, and with a will, my merry men all, and never mind shipping a sea! Cannonballs are a cargo that doesn't spoil by taking salt-water."

His mother's premonition had proven true. Christmas Eve had marked the end of the still, dark, steaming nights of early winter. For the last twelve hours, the western gale had been roaring along the Irish coast. The short winter light faded rapidly. Behind him lay a leaping line of billows, lashed into mist by the tempest. Beside him, green foam-fringed columns rushed up the black rocks and crashed down in a thousand cataracts of snow.

Before him stretched the deep, sheltered bay, though visibility was limited. Some four miles out at sea, a sloping roof of thick gray cloud began, stretching overhead and inland, cutting off the Kerry mountains at mid-height and darkening the distant firths into the blackness of night. Underneath this awful roof, the storm howled inward, sweeping before it great foam-sponges and gray salt spray, rendering all the land hazy, dim, and dun.

Let it howl! For there was more mist than ever salt spray produced, and more thunder than ever a sea-surge echoed among the cliffs of Smerwick Bay. Along the sand-hills, red sparks flashed in the evening gloom—sparks that never came from heaven. These were from Fort Del Oro, now christened by the invaders, where the hated golden flag of Spain flew proudly. Within its walls held San Josepho and eight hundred foes. Just three nights prior, Amyas and Yeo, alongside the finest hands of Winter's crew, had hauled four culverins from the Admiral's main deck, floated them ashore, and dragged them up to the battery among the sand-hills. Now, it would be seen whether Spanish and Italian condottieri could hold their ground against the men of Devon.

Amyas was likely thinking not of his lonely mother at Burrough Court, but of the quick, bright flashes on the sand-hill and the fort where Salvation Yeo was hurling eighteen-pound shots with deadly aim. They ran their boat onto the shore through the surf, scrambling over the sand-hills with each man carrying a brace of shot.

Every traveler who knows Bideford must know Bideford Bridge; it is the very center, the focal point, and the soul around which the town, like a living organism, has organized itself. Just as Edinburgh defines itself by its castle, Rome by its Capitol, and Egypt by its pyramids, so Bideford exists as Bideford solely by virtue of this bridge. Yet, few understand the occult powers that have animated this wondrous structure for nearly five centuries, elevating it, according to Prince and Fuller, to the status of a chief wonder of Devonshire. It was first conceived as an inspired bridge, a soul-saving conduit, an alms-giving haven, an educational pillar, a sentient entity, and finally, though not least, a dinner-providing monument. Few know how, when construction began half a mile upstream, invisible hands carried the stones downstream each night to their final resting place. Nor do they recall how Sir Richard Gurney, parish priest, one night plagued by the fear of an evil spirit haunting his sheepfold, beheld a vision of an angel directing him to build precisely where those materials had been miraculously transported—the only spot offering sure foundation amidst the shifting sands. Even fewer know how Bishop Grandison of Exeter proclaimed indulgences and benedictions throughout his diocese to all who promoted this dangerous ford, urging men to "make the best of both worlds" by securing salvation and crossing safely.

Few, perhaps not even the narrator, know that "though the foundation of the bridge is laid upon wool, yet it shakes at the slightest step of a horse;" or that despite possessing twenty-three arches, a man named William Alford (another Milo) once carried four bushels of saltwater measure on his back for a wager across its entire length. The bridge is a veritable esquire, bearing its own coat of arms—a ship and a bridge on a plain field—and owning lands and tenements in numerous parishes. From these holdings, the miraculous bridge has founded charities, built schools, waged legal suits, and, most pertinently for our story, hosted annual dinners, maintaining for that purpose the finest wine cellar in all Devon.

To one of these banquets, in the year 1583, were invited all the notables of Bideford, alongside Mr. St. Leger of Annery Close, brother of the Marshal of Munster and of Lady Grenville. He was a worthy and hospitable gentleman, one who found riches a snare and parted with them so freely to his neighbors that he effectively shielded his descendants from the temptations thereof. Between Mr. St. Leger and one of the bridge trustees, a debate arose regarding whether a salmon caught below the bridge was superior to one caught above. Since this weighty question required practical experiment, Mr. St. Leger vowed that, having been graciously entertained by the bridge, he would reciprocate. Offering a bet of five pounds, he promised to procure a salmon from the pool below Annery as firm-flaked as the one they had just consumed from Appledore. In the fulness of his heart, he then extended an invitation to the entire company to dine with him at Annery three days hence, requesting that each bring a wife or daughter; and, as fortune would have it, Don Guzman was likewise invited.

Thus, a magnificent feast ensued in the great hall at Annery, scarcely rivaled since Judge Hankford feasted King Edward IV there. While guests ate their fill and drank their fill, Rose Salterne and Don Guzman engaged in a silent pantomime, watching one another with intensity. Rose, however, exercised extreme caution with her glances; not only was her father present at the table, but seated directly opposite her were Messrs. William Cary and Arthur St. Leger, lieutenants in Her Majesty's Irish Army returning on furlough.

Though Rose and the Spaniard had not exchanged a word in six months—having met frequently before—they had maintained a strict distance in her father's house. The Spaniard, however, ensured he appeared oblivious to her presence save for the stateliest salutations at entry and exit. Yet, whenever he was in her company, he laid himself open to the utmost advantage, striving to be wittier, more eloquent, more romantic, and more filled with marvelous tales than ever before. The cunning Don realized his initial tactics had failed; having been foiled by Rose's refusal of his love, he now employed a far more formidable strategy. First, Rose deserved severe punishment for daring to reject a Spanish nobleman. What greater penalty could he impose than withdrawing the honor of his attentions and the sunshine of his smiles? Though conceit fueled this notion, it was underpinned by cunning. The Spaniard knew better than anyone that women, like the world, tend to value a man—at least if there is genuine worth in him—at his own self-assessed price. The higher the price he demanded, the more they would pay.

Now, he intended to set a high price on himself and prick her pride, for she was too accustomed to worship to be won by flattery. He could have achieved this by attending to someone else, but he was too wise to resort to such a crude method, which might spark indignation, disgust, or despair in Rose's heart without ever bringing her to his knees. Instead, he quietly and unobtrusively signaled that he could do without her. The poor fool, as fate intended, immediately began to ask herself why? What hidden treasure, what reserve force rendered him independent of her, while she could not claim similar independence? Did he possess a secret? How delightful it would be to know! Some grand ambition? How sweet to share! Some mysterious knowledge? How delicious to learn! Some capacity for love beyond the common? How exquisite to possess exclusively! He must be greater, wiser, richer-hearted, and better-born than she. Ah, if only his wealth could supply her poverty! Step by step, she was being led to sue *in forma pauperis* to the very man she had spurned when he sued her in like form. The temptation of possessing some mysterious private treasure, of serving as the priestess of a hidden sanctuary, and of thanking Heaven for not being like other women, was becoming too heavy for Rose to bear, as it is for most. For few understood better than the Spaniard how deeply women, by the very laws of their sex, cherish worshipping more than being worshipped, and obeying more than being obeyed; how their coyness and scorn serve as masks to hide a consciousness of weakness, masks they themselves will often grow weary of first.

Rose was utterly exhausted by that same mask as she sat at the Annery table. Don Guzman perceived it in her uneasy, downcast gaze. Thinking himself a conceited coxcomb, he concluded she must be sufficiently punished. Stealing glances at her, he was unashamed when he saw her eyes drop upon contact, noticing how her silence and abstraction increased, and how a blush crept into her cheeks. So he feigned equal downcast abstraction, continuing his glances until he once found her looking at him to verify if he was looking back. Then he knew his prey was secure. He asked her, with his eyes, "Do you forgive me?" Seeing her halt abruptly in conversation with her neighbor, falter, lower her eyes, and lift them again after a minute in search of his, he repeated the pleasant inquiry. What choice did she have but to answer, with every feature and bend of her pretty neck, "And do you forgive me in turn?"

Whereupon Don Guzman erupted like a nightingale on a bough, bursting forth with stories, jests, and repartees. He instantly became the soul of the company and the most charming cavalier of all. Poor Rose knew she was the catalyst for his sudden mood shift, blaming herself for her actions, shuddering and blushing at her own delight, longing for the feast to end so she might hasten home and hide away with sweet fantasies about a love whose reality she felt she dared not face.

Beautiful was the scene on the great terrace at Annery that afternoon. Smart dames in gaudy dresses paraded up and down in twos and threes before the stately house, or looked down upon the park, where old oaks, deer, and a broad land-locked river spread like a lake lay bathed in the glare of midsummer. It was not safe, I regret to say, for the honor of our nation, to travel from Plymouth northward into Devonshire in those days. Unless one wished to make a wide detour, one had to pass through the dominions of a foreign and hostile potentate who had repeatedly ravaged the realm and defeated Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth's forces. Behind his back, at least, he was known as the King of the Gubbings. "So now I dare call them," wrote Fuller, "secure by distance, which one of more valor durst not do to their face, for fear their fury fall upon him." While scholars have offered various etymologies—from fish shavings to inhabitants of crooked hills—the truth remains that they are a peculiar breed, a Scythia within England, living in ignorance of law, luxury, and God. They dwell in cottages that are merely holes, multiplying without marriage, their language the dregs of the vulgar tongue, and their wealth consisting solely of stolen goods. During the Civil Wars, no soldiers were quartered upon them, lest they be quartered amongst them. Yet, rumors circulated that they were beginning to be civilized, offering their children to baptism and returning to the fold.

With this quip, Fuller ended his story, leaving us to picture Amyas riding out of Plymouth on a spirited horse, fully armored with lance, sword, and two great horse-pistols. From afar, wisps of blue smoke drifted from the dens of the Gubbings, and more than once the party halted, mistaking distant furze bushes and ponies for patrols of an advancing army.

Sir Richard was absent in Bideford, yet the old steward, Anthony, seized the initiative to summon the keepers. Down marched the serving-men to the mill, trailed by all the idle lads of the parish, viewing the maiden-hunt as excellent sport. They approached the case with eyes wide open, casting their judgment in favor of Rose.

They hurled vile curses upon the miller and his wife, labeling them hard-hearted old heathens, convinced that the poor maid had been driven to cast herself from a cliff or drown in the sea. Conversely, the women of Stow held a unanimous belief that the "hussy" had fled with some scoundrel, predicting inevitably that pride would bring a fall.

The facts, however, told a different tale. All of Rose's trinkets remained behind, proving she had departed honestly. Nothing appeared missing save for some of her linen, which Old Anthony hinted might be discovered in another's box. The sole evidence was a faint footprint beneath her bedroom window. Upon this track, the bloodhound was placed (though leashed). After a premonitory whimper, he raised his mighty voice and started bell-mouthed through the garden gate, up the lane, towing behind him the panting keeper until they reached the downs above, heading straight for Marslandmouth. There, the entire posse comitatus halted, breathless, before the door of Lucy Passmore.

Lucy, as I had perhaps noted previously, was now a widow and found her solitude not entirely contrary to her interests. Her augury regarding her old man had been fulfilled; he had never returned since the night he put to sea with Eustace and the Jesuits.

"Some natural tears she shed, but dried them soon"—these, at least, were those not required for business purposes. Determined to forestall suspicion by a bold move, she departed for Stow and recounted a most pathetic tale to Lady Grenville. She claimed her husband had gone out pollock fishing and never returned, yet she had heard horsemen galloping past her window in the dead of night. She was certain they were Jesuits who had carried off her old man by main force, likely killing and salting him down for provisions on their voyage back to the Pope in Rome. She concluded by entreating protection against those "Popish skulkers up to Chapel," who were sworn to do her mischief, and appealing to Lady Grenville's sense of justice: ought not the Queen grant her a pension, seeing her heart's love turned into a martyred saint by the hands of idolatrous traitors?

Lady Grenville, who held a high opinion of Lucy's medical skills and always summoned her when a child suffered a "housty" (sore throat), went forth and pleaded the case before Sir Richard with such effect that Lucy emerged better off than ever for the next two or three years. Yet now, what did she have to do with Rose's disappearance? And where was she herself? Her door was fastened, around which her flock of goats stood, crying in vain for her to milk them. From the down above, her donkeys, wandering at their sweet will, answered the bloodhound's bay with a burst of harmony.

"They're laughing at us, Keeper, they neddies; surely we've lost our labor here."

But the bloodhound, after circling the door for a while, turned down the glen and never ceased until he reached the sea's edge.

"They've taken water. Let us return and raid the old witch's house."

"'Tis just like that old Lucy to lock a poor maid into shame."

Returning, they assaulted the cottage, and by a general plebiscite, ransacked the humble dwelling, partly in indignation and partly, if truth be told, in the hope of plunder. Yet there was no plunder to be found. Lucy had decamped with all her movable wealth, save for the huge black cat among the embers, who, at the sight of the bloodhound, vanished up the chimney (some claiming with a strong scent of brimstone). Viewed outside, the beast was chased into the woods, where, I doubt not, it lived many happy years as a scourge to all the rabbits of the glen.

The goats and donkeys were driven off to Stow, and the mob returned, a little ashamed of themselves when their brief wrath had passed, and a little afraid of what Sir Richard might say.

When he returned, Sir Richard sold the donkeys and goats, giving the proceeds to the poor, promising to refund them if Lucy returned and surrendered to justice. But Lucy did not return. Her cottage, from which the neighbors shrank as from a haunted place, remained as she had left it, crumbling slowly into four fern-covered walls. Through them, the little stream murmured on from pool to pool—the only voice breaking the silence of that lonely glen for many a year to come.

A few days later, Sir Richard, traveling from Bideford to Stow, called in at Clovelly Court and mentioned, with a casual "by the by," news that caused Will Cary to leap from his seat almost to the ceiling. What it was, we already know.

"And there is no clue?" asked Old Cary, for his son was speechless.

"Only this," replied Sir Richard. "I hear that some fellow prowling about the cliffs that night saw a pinnace running for Lundy."

Will rose and hurried out of the room. Within half an hour, he and three or four armed servants were on board a trawling skiff, heading for Lundy. He did not return for three days, bringing news that an elderly man, seemingly a foreigner, had lodged for several months in a section of the ruined Moresco Castle tenanted by one John Braund. A few weeks prior, a younger man, also a foreigner, had joined him from a ship—a Flushinger or Easterling vessel of some sort. The ship came and went more than once, carrying the young man. A few days ago, a lady and her maid, a stout woman, had come with him to the castle, conferring secretly with the elder man for a long time. They stayed all night, and in the morning, all three sailed away. Fishermen on the beach had heard the young man call the other "father." He was a very still man, much like a mass-priest might be. More they did not know, nor did they wish to know.

Thereupon, Old Cary and Sir Richard sent Will on a second trip with the parish constable of Hartland (in which vast parish, for its sins, sits the Isle of Lundy, ten miles out at sea). He returned with the body of the hapless John Braund, farmer, fisherman, smuggler, and so forth. This worthy, after much fruitless examination (during which the examinee was afflicted with extreme deafness and loss of memory), departed for Exeter Gaol on charges of "harboring priests, Jesuits, gypsies, and other suspect and traitorous persons."

Poor John Braund, whose motive for entertaining such unsavory guests was probably not treason but a wife, seven children, and arrears of rent, did not thrive under the change from the pure air of Lundy to the pestilential atmosphere of Exeter Gaol, made infamous two years later (if I recall correctly) by a "black assizes," nearly as fatal as the more notorious one at Oxford. For in it, "whether by the stench of the prisoners, or by a stream of foul air," judge, jury, counsel, and bystanders—numbering many members of Devon's best families—sickened in court and died miserably within a few days.

John Braund caught the gaol-fever in a week and died raving in that noisome den. His secret, if he possessed one, perished with him, leaving nothing but vague suspicion regarding Rose Salterne's fate. Few doubted she had fled with the Spaniard, but whither, and in what capacity? On that last subject, be assured, no mercy was shown to her by many a Bideford dame, who had hated the poor girl simply for her beauty; nor by many a country lady, who had "always expected that the girl would be brought to ruin by the absurd notice, beyond what her station had a right to, which was taken of her," while every young maiden aspired to fill the throne Rose had abdicated. Thus, on the whole, Bideford considered itself to be proceeding as well without poor Rose as it had with her, or even better. And though she lingered in some hearts as a fair dream, the business and bustle of daily life soon swept that dream away, and her place knew her no more.

We took boat at Whitehall-stairs, following Amyas's lead, slipping silently past the shadow of Old London Bridge until we reached Deptford Creek. There, preserved like a relic in amber, lay the *Pelican*, the vessel that had carried Drake around the globe. She sat high and dry on the grassy bank of the Thames, resembling an ancient warrior resting after a lifetime of toil. Epigrams and verses honoring her and her captain were nailed to her mainmast; three of these, penned by the scholar Camden, survive in history. Elizabeth herself had consecrated the ship, feasted upon her decks, and there knighthooded Drake. "At which time a bridge of planks," the legend ran, "broke under the press of people, falling with a hundred men upon it, who, notwithstanding, had none of them any harm. So as that ship may seem to have been built under a lucky planet."

She had remained a show, and a dining-hall for jolly gatherings from the city, for some time. Today, however, the air was thick with the scent of savory steams issuing from windows that were once portholes, and the sounds of revelry mingled with the rush of white-aproned waiters from the neighboring Pelican Inn. Flags bedizened the masts. A great feast was in progress: the Lord Mayor entertained certain gentlemen of the Leicester house party, men deeply interested in foreign discoveries. What place so fit for such a feast as the *Pelican* itself?

Look at the men surrounding the table; a nobler company you will seldom see. Especially, if you be American, look at their faces and reverence them, for to them and their wisdom you owe the existence of your mighty fatherland.

At the head of the table sat the Lord Mayor, Sir Edward Osborne, clothworker, and ancestor of the dukes of Leeds. His romance now-a-days is in everyone's hands. He was aged, yet unchanged since he leaped from the window upon London Bridge into the roaring tide below to rescue the infant who was now his wife. The chivalry and promptitude of the prentice boy had hardened into the thoughtful daring of the wealthy merchant adventurer. There he sat, a right kingly man, with the Earl of Cumberland on his right hand and Walter Raleigh on his left. The three talked together in a low voice of the chances of vast and rich countries remaining undiscovered between Florida and the River of Canada. Raleigh's half-scientific declamation, laced with quotations from Doctor Dee the conjuror, had less effect on Osborne than on Cumberland—who had tried many adventures to foreign parts and failed in all of them, apparently for the simple reason that, instead of going himself, he sent other people. Raleigh was fain to call to his help the quiet student sitting on his left, Richard Hakluyt, of Oxford. Yet he was deep in talk with a reverend elder, whose long white beard flowed almost to his waist and whose face was furrowed by a thousand storms. This was Anthony Jenkinson, the great Asiatic traveler, discoursing to the Christ-church virtuoso of reindeer sledges, Siberian steppes, and the fossil ivory—plain proof of Noah's flood—which the Tungusoos dug from the ice-cliffs of the Arctic sea. Next to him sat Christopher Carlile, Walsingham's son-in-law (as Sidney also was now), a valiant captain who would afterward serve as general of the soldiery in Drake's triumphant West Indian raid of 1585. He was busy talking with Alderman Hart the grocer, Sheriff Spencer the clothworker, Charles Leigh (Amyas's merchant-cousin), Aldworth the mayor of Bristol, William Salterne, alderman thereof, and cousin of our friend at Bideford. For Carlile and Secretary Walsingham had been helping them heart and soul for the last two years to collect money for Humphrey and Adrian Gilbert's great adventures to the North-West. On one of these expeditions Carlile was indeed to have sailed himself, but did not go after all; I never could discover for what reason.

On the opposite side of the table sat a group scarcely less interesting. Martin Frobisher and John Davis, the pioneers of the North-West passage, talked with Alderman Sanderson, the great geographer and "setter forth of globes," alongside Mr. Towerson, Sir Gilbert Peckham, our old acquaintance Captain John Winter, and last, but not least, Philip Sidney himself. Sidney, with his accustomed courtesy, had given up his rightful place toward the head of the table that he might have a knot of virtuosi all to himself, and had brought with him his two especial intimates, Mr. Edward Dyer and Mr. Francis Leigh. They too were talking of the North-West passage, and Sidney lamented that he was tied to diplomacy and courts, expressing his envy of old Martin Frobisher in all sorts of pretty compliments. To which the other replied, "It's all very fine to talk of here, a sailing on dry land with a good glass of wine before you; but you'd find it another guess sort of business, knocking about among the icebergs with your beard frozen fast to your ruff, Sir Philip, specially if you were a bit squeamish about the stomach."

"That were a slight matter to endure, my dear sir, if by it I could win the honor which her majesty bestowed on you, when her own ivory hand waved a farewell kerchief to your ship from the windows of Greenwich Palace."

"Well, sir, folks say you have no reason to complain of lack of favors, as you have no reason to deserve lack; and if you can get them by staying ashore, don't you go to sea to look for more, say I. Eh, Master Towerson?"

Towerson's gray beard, which had stood many a foreign voyage, both fair and foul, wagged grim assent. But at this moment a waiter entered.

"Please my lord mayor's worship, there is a tall gentleman outside, would speak with the Right Honorable Sir Walter Raleigh."

Amyas entered and stood hesitating in the doorway.

"Captain Leigh!" cried half a dozen voices.

"Why did you not walk in, sir?" said Osborne. "You should know your way well enough between these decks."

"Well enough, my lords and gentlemen. But, Sir Walter—you will excuse me"—and he gave Raleigh a look which was enough for his quick wit. Turning pale as death, he rose and followed Amyas into an adjoining cabin. They were five minutes together, and then Amyas came out alone.

In few words he told the company the sad story which we already know. Ere it was ended, noble tears were glistening on some of those stern faces.

"The old Egyptians," said Sir Edward Osborne, "when they banqueted, set a corpse among their guests, for a memorial of human vanity. Have we forgotten God and our own weakness in this our feast, that He Himself has sent us thus a message from the dead?"

"Nay, my lord mayor," said Sidney, "not from the dead, but from the realm of everlasting life."

"Amen!" answered Osborne. "But, gentlemen, our feast is at an end. There are those here who would drink on merrily, as brave men should, in spite of the private losses of which they have just had news; but none here who can drink with the loss of so great a man still ringing in his ears."

It was true. Though many of the guests had suffered severely by the failure of the expedition, they had utterly forgotten that fact in the awful news of Sir Humphrey's death. The feast broke up sadly and hurriedly, while each man asked his neighbor, "What will the queen say?"

Raleigh re-entered in a few minutes, but was silent. Pressing many an honest hand as he passed, he went out to call a wherry, beckoning Amyas to follow him. Sidney, Cumberland, and Frank went with them in another boat, leaving the two brothers to talk over the sad details.

They disembarked at Whitehall-stairs; Raleigh, Sidney, and Cumberland went to the palace, and the two brothers to their mother's lodgings.

Amyas had prepared his speech to Frank about Rose Salterne, but now that it was come to the point, he had not the courage to begin, and longed that Frank would open the matter. Frank, too, shrank from what he knew must come, and all the more because he was ignorant that Amyas had been to Bideford, or knew aught of the *Rose*'s disappearance.

So they went upstairs; and it was a relief to both of them to find that their mother was at the Abbey, for it was for her sake that both dreaded what was coming. They stood in the bay-window which looked out upon the river and talked of things indifferent, looking earnestly at each other's faces by the fading light, for it was now three years since they had met.

"Land!" The cry tore through the air, raw and desperate. "There, far to the south and west, beside the dying red of the sun!" It lay there, a long, azure ribbon stretching between the blood-red sea and the burning gold of the sky. After months of fever-dreams and salt-stink, flesh had touched soil again. Fresh streams promised to cool the parched throats; cooling fruits offered relief to tongues numb with scurvy; free space awaited limbs cramped by weeks of swaying motion. And perhaps, hidden within this green mystery, lay gold and jewels, the boundless wealth of the Indies. Who could say? The old world of facts and grim prose lay a thousand miles behind them, swallowed by the past. Before them spread a realm of wonder and fable, a landscape of boundless hope.

Sick men dragged themselves from their stifling hammocks, gasping for air; strong men fell to their knees, pressing foreheads to the warm deck, giving thanks to the heavens. Every eye strained toward the far blue haze, watching it fade as the sun dipped lower, yet growing broader, brighter as the ship surged forward, caught in the grip of the rich trade-wind. It whispered softly around brow and canvas, a loyal companion to the daring. *"Blow steadily, blow harder still, O good trade-wind, of whom it is written that He maketh the winds His angels, ministering breath to the heirs of His salvation. His cheeks were hollow, wasted by illness, and the feverish flush on either cheek burned crimson in the sunset light. Minutes passed, and the western rainbows dissolved; emeralds, topazes, amethysts, rubies—all faded into a silver-gray twilight. "Barbados?" Amyas replied, looking around at the stunned faces. "I've never heard of it."

"I remember now," Amyas mused, his brow furrowed. "Some words I exchanged with poor Sir Humphrey regarding an island hereabouts. Oh, that he had gone thither instead of to Newfoundland!"

"He would have waited as gladly as he departed, if service to his Queen allowed it," Amyas admitted, though a shadow of regret crossed his face. "But what say you, my masters? Is there not wisdom in spending a few days here, letting the sick recover their strength, before we press on to the Main and begin our labors?"

The council voiced approval, a murmur of agreement rippling through the gathered men. Yet Frank remained silent, a statue amidst the moving crowd.

"Come, fellow-adventurer," Cary called out, nudging him. "We need your voice, too."

"To my impatience, Will," Frank whispered back, his eyes distant. "There is but one place on earth for me, and I burn with a desire to fly there. Still, the counsel is sound. I approve."

So the decision stood, met with a hearty cheer from the crew. Long before dawn, the vessel had skirted the southern shore of the island, threading carefully into the bay where Bridgetown now stands. Every eye locked onto the low, wooded hills sleeping under the moonlight, dotted with millions of dancing fireflies like fallen stars. Every nostril drank greedily the fragrant air sweeping from the land, heavy with the perfume of a thousand blooming flowers. Every ear welcomed the chorus of insects, the snores of tree-toads, and the plaintive cries of shore-fowl, a noisy, vibrant life that replaced the monotonous whisper of the open water.

Then, slowly, the ship shuddered to a halt. The cable rattled through the hawsehole. Then, disregarding the danger of lurking Spaniards or fierce Caribs, an instinctive roar erupted from every throat. Poor souls! Amyas had much ado to stop them from leaping straight onto the dark sand, reminding them that daylight was but two hours away.

"Never did two hours feel so long," complained a young lad, pacing anxiously up and down.

"Gunner!" a voice cried out. "Is there gold on that island?"

"Never heard of any," Yeo answered sharply. "And so much the better for us."

Up flushed the rose of morning; up rushed the sun, casting level rays that glittered on the smooth trunks of palm trees, throwing rainbows across the white foam of coral reefs, and gilding lonely uplands far away where, in centuries to come, stately estates and bustling factories would rise. Long lines of pelicans clanged their wings outward to the sea; the insect hum quieted, then a thousand birds burst into jubilant song. A thin blue mist crept upward toward the inner downs and vanished, leaving the hills to quiver in the burning glare. The land-breeze, which had breathed fresh from the ocean all night, died away into glassy calm, and the tropic day began.

The sick were lifted over the side, landing boat-load after boat-load onto the beach to stretch themselves in the shade of the palms. Within half an hour, the whole crew was scattered across the shore, save for a dozen brave men who had volunteered to keep watch and ward on board until noon.

Night had concealed their passage from the southern point of Grenada, and now they drifted into that enchanted circle of islands where nature concentrated her loveliest hues and humanity her darkest sins. If Barbados held a strange, distant glory in the eyes of these newcomers, how much vaster was the spell of these waters, which smiled in a perpetual, glassy calm, untouched by the hurricanes that roared in the northern latitudes. Sky, sea, and archipelago formed one vast, trembling rainbow. To the sturdy, practical sailors whose hearts beat only for Spanish gold and pearls, it was merely a stretch of exotic coast; to Amyas, weary of the tropics, it was a theater for war, where he speculated silently on the feasibility of extirpating the Spaniards and annexing the West Indies to Queen Elizabeth's dominions. Yet, even their unpoetic eyes could not gaze upon these lands—so famous, so new, the focal point of the age's wonder, pity, and greed—without a mixture of awe and terror.

It was a dreadful thought, yet invigorating: they were entering regions barely known to Englishmen, where failure meant not mere death, but the tortures of the Inquisition. Perhaps fewer than five times had English ships ventured these mysterious seas. Yet, aboard the *Pelican* and the *Minion*, there were men who knew them intimately—the survivors of Captain John Hawkins's previous voyages. Having been interdicted from necessities by Spanish jealousy, they had, in true English fashion, won their markets at the point of the sword and thereafter traded honestly. Throughout the day, these old mariners regaled the crew with the names of every isle, cape, fish, and bird, while Frank stood sentinel, his face grave and silent, drinking in the glory surrounding him. A profound awe possessed his soul, yet it was not sorrowful; he murmured to himself at intervals, "This is the gate of heaven," watching the forward plunge of the ship reveal fresh wonders. Islands and capes hung suspended in the air above their mirrored inverses; long sand-hills rolled in the heat haze; and the yellow flower-beds, alongside giant cacti resembling twisted candelabras, draped the glaring slopes. Under the influence of the mirage, everything seemed a blazing phantom-world, unstable and fantastic, even the sun, distorted into strange ovals and pears by beds of crimson mist as it sank.

While Frank marveled, Yeo rejoiced. South of the setting sun, a cluster of tall peaks rose from the sea. Unless his reckoning failed, these were the mountains of Macanao, marking the western end of Margarita, the Isle of Pearls. It was then renowned across the Mediterranean cities and the great German fairs, second only in richness to the Pearl Island in the Gulf of Panama—a treasure that fifteen years prior had cost John Oxenham his life.

By morning, they ran along the northern flank of the island, having passed undetected the fortress the Spaniards had erected at the eastern tip to guard the fisheries. Finally, they opened a deep, tranquil bight, forested to the water's edge. Lying in the roadstead was a single caravel with three small boats moored nearby. Instantly, every man sprang to the deck, voices rising in debate over strategy. Some argued for sailing directly into the bay, aided by a fresh breeze; others, noting the breaking waves at the entrance and fearing hidden reefs, advocated for a quiet approach via the pinnace and a launch. Yeo proposed hoisting Spanish colors to avoid alarm, but Amyas firmly refused, deeming it a baseness to lie except in dire peril or for matters of high state.

Holding their course until the next point blocked further view, they moved ahead. Cary led the larger boat with twenty men; Amyas commanded the smaller vessel with fifteen, including John Brimblecombe. He wore his cassock and bands, clutching an old sword belonging to his uncle, which he cherished deeply.

Approaching the mouth of the bight, they encountered the coral reefs they had anticipated—an obstacle forcing them to hug the edge of the shoals for a long distance. Those unfamiliar with the Indies gazed upward through the clear water, marveling at the submerged gardens of Nereus and Amphitrite. Suddenly, beneath them appeared what Yeo termed "a school of sharks"—some nearly as long as the boat themselves, regarding them with wistful, wicked eyes.

"Jack," whispered Amyas, sitting beside him, "look how that large fellow eyes thee. He has surely taken a fancy to that plump hide of thine and thinks thou wouldst eat as tender as any sucking porker."

Jack turned pale but said nothing. As fate would have it, the very moment the largest shark noticed a parrot-fish emerging from a coral cleft, it lunged upward, joined by two or three companions. The frightened fish leaped clear into the air, almost aboard the boat. Simultaneously, where it had emerged, three or four great, brown-shagreened snouts clashed within two yards of Jack, displaying terrible rows of saw-teeth before sinking sulkily back into the depths to hunt anew. At this spectacle, Jack softly muttered, "In manus tuas, Domine!" and turned his gaze away from the sea, losing all desire to look again.

Having navigated the reef, they entered the bay with a fair breeze. The caravel was now within musket range. Cary maneuvered her alongside before the Spaniards could man their guns. Standing in the stern sheets, he shouted for them to yield. The captain challenged him boldly for his authority. "In the name of common sense, ye dogs!" cried Will. "Do you not see that you are but fifty to our twenty?" Immediately, the captain scrambled up the side, firing a pistol at him. Cary knocked him down, sparing unnecessary blood. On this, the entire crew surrendered, some kneeling, others jumping overboard. The prize was secured.

Meanwhile, Amyas had circled under the caravel's stern and boarded the second boat, which lay nearest and thus offered a sure capture. What enabled Sir Richard Grenville's *Revenge*, in her last fearful fight off the Azores, to endure for twelve hours the attack of eight Spanish squadrons, two of which sank at her side? What enabled young Richard Hawkins's *Dainty*, though half her guns useless, to maintain a running fight with two Spaniards of equal size, double the weight of metal, and ten times the number of men? There were more causes than one. First, the build of the English ships: they were much shorter than the Spanish, giving them an ease in maneuvering which utterly confounded their foes. The English ships charged with marvelous agility, discharged their broadsides, and flew forth presently into the deep, leveling their shot directly at the heavy, unwieldy Spanish ships. Moreover, the Spanish fashion in the West Indies was to build their men-of-war flush-decked, leaving those on deck exposed and open; while the English fashion was to heighten the ship at stem and stern, both by the sweep of her lines and by stockades ("close fights and cage-works") on the poop and forecastle, giving the men shelter further increased by strong bulkheads across the main-deck, dividing the ship into separate forts fitted with swivels and loopholed for musketry and arrows.

But the great source of superiority lay, after all, in the men themselves. The English sailor was an amphibious and all-cunning animal, capable of turning his hand to everything from needlework to gunnery. He was a product of a nation where every citizen was not merely permitted but compelled by law to practice the use of the bow from childhood, considering sword-play and quarter-staff as necessary parts of education and pastime. They were the "fiercest nation upon earth," and the freest; each man fought for himself with the self-help and self-respect of a Yankee ranger, and once bidden to do his work, was trusted to carry it out by his own wit.

"She has confessed!" Eustace gasped, the words tearing from his throat. "Saints preserve us! She—she..."

The sound that followed shattered him. It was a wail of such depth and despair that it rang in his ears, his brain, and his very soul. It was the sound of a spirit breaking under the weight of false accusation. Eustace lunged for the door, his hand grasping at the wood, desperate to tear it open and stop the horror. But the superior grabbed his arm, dragging him back with brute force.

"They must finish the work," the superior said coldly. "Do not interfere."

Another wail. And another. Like the cry of an avenging angel turned madman, the sound pursued Eustace as he fled down the corridor and burst out into the night.

Outside, the air was thick with the scent of jasmine and the warmth of the tropics. Above, the moon poured a golden, silvery light over a garden that looked, in its stillness, like a fragment of Eden. But as Eustace stumbled into the cool air, the screams followed him, echoing through his mind, turning paradise into hell.

"Oh, misery! Misery, misery!" he groaned, clutching his head, his teeth ground together in silent anguish. "I have brought her to this! I have had to bring her to it! What else could I do? Who dares blame me? And yet, what devilish sin have I committed that requires punishment of this magnitude? Was there no one else? No one? And yet... it may save her soul. It may bring her to repentance!"

"It may, indeed," the superior's voice followed him, calm and detached. "For she is delicate, and cannot endure much. You ought to know, Señor, the merciful disposition of the Holy Office."

"I know it," Eustace stammered, his voice trembling now with a different kind of fear. "All in love—all in love. A paternal chastisement."

"And consider the proof of heresy," the superior continued, stepping closer. "Patent, undeniable. Alongside the strong suspicion of enchantment, and the notorious reputation of the Elder Sorceress. You yourself admitted, did you not, that she was a witch in England before the Señora brought her here as an attendant?"

"Yes," Eustace choked out, tears streaming silently down his face. "Of course she was. Of course. There was no other course open. And though the flesh may be weak in my case, Sir, let it be known that the spirit remains willing!"

With those final, hollow words, Eustace walked away. The garden, the moon, the beauty of the Indies—it all faded into a blur. By the time the sun dipped below the horizon, he had gone to the principal of the Jesuits. He had laid bare his soul—or whatever remained of it—and begged to be allowed to complete his novitiate, with the promise of immediate return to Europe. "Otherwise," he had pleaded, his voice breaking, "I shall go mad, even if I am not already."

The Jesuit, a man of gentle countenance, had interceded. He spoke of Eustace's past valor, framing his actions as zealous service rather than moral failure. The Inquisitors, satisfied, released him. That same night, Eustace Leigh left Cartagena. He sailed the following week, bound for a destination unknown, a ship carrying a cargo of a man who had died twice.

In the pages of this history, Eustace Leigh vanished. He might have risen to become a General of the Order, a conqueror of souls in the tropics, reducing both body and spirit to dust. Or he might have returned to England, only to hang alongside Ballard three years later for his part in treason. Perhaps he lived out his days in some forgotten forest, a slave to the will of the Society.

But it mattered not. Eustace was no longer a man. He was a tool, a mechanism of the Church, a vessel that carried out orders without question, indifferent to the good or evil they wrought. By an act of moral suicide, he had traded his soul for the illusion of salvation, shedding his conscience, his will, and his humanity to serve "The Society." As he himself had declared, he was dead. And as the old proverb went, let the dead bury their dead. There was no story left to tell about Eustace Leigh, only the shadow he cast on the future of those who followed.

Nearly three years had elapsed since that devoted handful knelt at vespers beneath the towering tree of Guayra—a span of time seemingly void, traceable only by fragmented notes and misspelled names. Across an expanse of eight hundred miles by four hundred, through uncharted hills and dense forests, they had pursued the Golden City in vain. They had traversed the timbered shores of the Orinoco, crossed the foaming chaos of Maypures, and ascended the upper reaches of the mighty Amazon. They had climbed into Peru itself, trampling the cinchona groves of Loxa, unaware as the world remained of their healing powers. They had gazed upon the virgin snows of Chimborazo, piercing the thunderclouds, and watched the volcanic fury of Cotopaxi spew molten streams before turning eastward. From the Alpine heights of the Andes, they had plunged into "the green and misty ocean of the Montana." Slowly, painfully, they had worked their way northward along the eastern foothills of the inland Cordillera, now bivouacked upon a feeder of the Meta, descending from the Suma Paz into the forest-clad plains. Here sat Amyas and Cary, Brimblecombe, Yeo, and the loyal Indian lad who had shadowed them through all their wanderings—alive and well, yet as distant from Manoa, with its fairy lakes and golden palaces, as ever. Repeated rumors of its existence had sparked fresh expeditions, only to yield fresh disappointments, a hope deferred that gnaws the heart.

Here they sat at last—forty-four men of the original eighty-four who departed Guayra: where were the others?

Drew, the master, lay upon the banks of the Rio Negro, accompanied by five valiant companions slain in combat by poisoned Indian arrows during a futile attempt to breach the Parima mountain gorges. Two more perished in the Andean valleys, frozen by hailstones driven from the condor's eyrie; four succumbed to the rapids of the Orinoco; five or six others, wounded, were abandoned among friendly tribes to be retrieved if fortune should smile—a probability fading with time. Fever, serpents, jaguars, alligators, cannibal fish, and electric eels had whittled their ranks month by month. Of their march through the primordial wilderness, no trail remained save these solitary graves.

Beside the silent stream, beneath the tropic moon, the survivors sat. Sun-burnt and gaunt, yet robust and bold, the quiet fire of English courage burned undimmed in every eye, and genial mirth lingered on every lip. They mocked danger and treated toil as sport, as cheerfully as when sailing the bar of Bideford in a past life. Beards grew down upon their chests; long hair, knotted like women's tresses, shielded them from the blazing sun. Leggings of the delicate Guazu-puti deer, shirts patched with Indian cotton, and trophies of jaguar, puma, and ape adorned their shoulders. Ammunition was exhausted, muskets ruined by the perpetual steam of the woods and left behind near an Orinoco cataract. Yet their swords shone bright and terrible, bows bent to a strength no Indian arm could match, tipped with remnants of armor. Many carried the *pocuna* or blowgun of the natives—far deadlier, and far quieter, than the firearms they had discarded. Thus they wandered, lords of the forest and its beasts, terrifying to hostile Indians, yet kind, just, and generous to those dealing faithfully. Countless Caribs, Atures, Solimos, and Guahibas recounted with wonder the righteousness of these bearded heroes, who declared themselves foes of the faithless Spaniard and messengers of a great and good queen beyond the seas, promised to deliver and avenge the oppressed.

Sleeping among the trees, some on the ground, some in hammocks strung between trunks, all lay silent save for the heavy plunge of a tapir tearing up weeds for his night meal. Occasionally, a jaguar climbing from bough to bough awakened the clustered monkeys, which in turn roused the birds. For ten minutes, unearthly roars, howls, shrieks, and cacklings rang the forest as if pandemonium had broken loose; yet this quickly subsided. Even amidst the din, it served to disturb neither the sleepers nor the council of war proceeding beside the watch-fire between the three adventurers and the faithful Yeo. A hundred times they had convened thus, in vain; this meeting, too, might prove fruitless. "Then I shall forbid you tobacco, Master Parson," Amyas replied. "We must rise and depart tomorrow. We have idled here three mortal days, accomplishing nothing."

"Shall we ever achieve anything?" Cary asked. "I fear the gold of Manoa resembles the gold where the rainbow touches the earth—a field perpetually beyond reach."

Amyas fell silent, as did the others. Denial was impossible; their hopes were nearly extinguished. Within the immense circle they had traced, they had encountered nothing but disillusionment.

"There is but one final chance," Amyas finally declared. "The mountains east of the Orinoco, where we failed initially. Perhaps the Incas moved on to them upon escaping."

"Why not?" Cary countered. "They would interpose all the forests, the Llanos, and half a dozen great rivers between themselves and those dogs of Spaniards."

"What care we?" Amyas retorted. "No forests can be denser than those we have already pierced. Alexander von Humboldt, in his travels, once observed a wretched band of Indians sitting listlessly around their fires, smeared with grease and paint, subsisting on ants and clay. He naively remarked that were it not for the teachings of science—which posit that this was the primitive state from which humanity evolved—he might have been tempted to view these unfortunate souls as the final, degraded remnants of a dying race. One wishes Humboldt had succumbed to that temptation, allowing his common sense to override the dogmas of modern anthropology, which lack factual basis for such wild notions. His instinctive judgment aligned with the Biblical doctrine that man, in a state of nature, is a fallen being doomed to death—a somber truth, perhaps, but far more honorable to humanity than the theory that we descended from apes. It is more comforting to believe these indigenous peoples were imperfect versions of what they ought to be, rather than unintelligent creatures dragging on for millennia without discovering better sustenance.

Yet, our voyagers, like their contemporaries, dismissed such philosophical inquiries. Accepting the Bible narrative as absolute truth, they sided with Humboldt's personal intuition rather than his scientific theories—or, more accurately, with Humboldt's conscience rather than the shallow anthropological fashions of the age. Consequently, they viewed their new hosts not as biological ancestors, but as immortal souls "captivated by the devil at his will," lost in the pathless forests and destined for spiritual ruin. Indeed, facts seemed to validate their antiquated views, though these Indians had not sunk quite as low as the Guahibas encountered on the lower reaches of the river.

Thus Ayacanora took up her residence in Lucy's cabin, officially accepted as a member of the crew. Yet she proved a troublesome addition, for now commenced in her that perilous transition common to savages encountering white men—a crisis that, within a few years, would accelerate the downfall of the North American tribes. Had not Amyas displayed admirable sense and constancy, Ayacanora might have met a fate far worse than that of Pocahontas, who, despite royal honors at the court of James the First, ultimately died in wretched poverty. For when the savage mind confronts the superior skill, wealth, and wisdom of Europeans, it often loses its self-respect; meanwhile, the body, indulged with luxuries, forfeits its capacity for self-reliance. With these twin losses, the few fragile virtues of the savage dissolve, paving the easy path toward begging, theft, and sloth.

It seemed, at first, that Ayacanora walked swiftly down this road. The warrior-prophetess of the Omaguas appeared transformed into a naughty child; the Diana of the Meta, after satisfying her curiosity with explorations of the ship's decks and cupboards, developed a penchant for stealing and hiding trifles that caught her fancy—too proud or shy to ask. When Amyas forbade taking anything without permission, she threatened to drown herself and sulks all day. Nevertheless, she obeyed, save regarding sweets. Whether by natural craving for forest fruits or otherwise, the Bishop's stores diminished rapidly, as did the sweet Spanish wine Amyas had reserved for sick Lucy. Another severe lecture ensued, wherein Amyas reproached her for robbing the ailing girl. Ayacanora, as usual, threatened suicide, running onto the deck until Amyas caught her and forgave her. This provoked a violent weeping session, accompanied by penitence and promises. A week later, Amyas discovered she had deceived Satan and her conscience by tormenting the Portuguese steward into providing alternative wine. Fortunately, Amyas's warnings about madness proving true, she performed several foolish acts one evening, suffering a bad headache the next morning. Thus the "murder" of the steward was exposed, leading to his sound flogging; yet Ayacanora, honorably, begged him off, offering to be whipped in his stead, confessing that the steward had acted only in fear for his life.

Her own headache and Amyas's cold demeanor served as sufficient lessons. After another suicidal attempt, the wilful beauty settled down temporarily—and remarkably, she never touched fermented liquor again. Meanwhile, Amyas faced numerous challenges in taming a lady who retreated into suicide threats at the slightest provocation. Punishment seemed impossible, and setting her ashore felt sacrilegious, for he sensed divine intent behind her strange affection for the English, believing God entrusted her soul to his charge. Desperate, Amyas prayed repeatedly for guidance, deciding eventually to entrust her education to Parson Jack, intending baptism upon reaching England.

Yet fresh troubles arose: Ayacanora refused to learn from anyone but Amyas himself, who declined the role of schoolmaster. For weeks, she remained untaught aside from the English tongue, which she acquired with remarkable speed. Soon, however, hostilities erupted—not merely toward John Brimblecombe, whose mannerisms she openly mocked, but also against Will Cary, whom she barred from approaching. Perhaps jealousy fueled her animosity toward Cary's intimacy with Amyas, or perhaps she intuitively recognized him as the sole other man capable of courting her. Though Will remained guiltless of any romantic intent, when admonished, Ayacanora declared Cary a "cacique" equal to Amyas, insisting there should not be two rulers. Negroes likewise suffered her disdain, initially evading them, then hurling objects until the terrified Quashies complained to Amyas and secured temporary respite. Among the sailors, she ruled like a princess, summoning them for errands and toys, occasionally enforcing obedience with sharp slaps. The crew, including old Yeo, treated her as a beloved pet, much like a tame leopard whose claws might strike at any moment. While amusing and anxiety-inducing, Ayacanora's presence occupied Amyas's mind, dispelling many somber forebodings about confronting his mother and the tragic news awaiting him.

Eventually, matters reached a climax. Having traversed the West Indies safely, the ship ventured into the Sargasso Sea, where discipline relaxed slightly. Amidst looted treasures—gold, silver, jewels—the crew found two chests containing Mexican featherwork and Spanish/East Indian finery destined for a Caracas noblewoman. Voted to Ayacanora as compensation for her Amazonian prowess and service, she greedily claimed them, carrying them to Lucy's cabin where she knelt gazing upon them. Despising the Mexican garments as "savage," she adored the Spanish attire, appearing on deck dressed in Seville mantillas, Madrid hats, and Indian brocade farthingales for days, basking in sunlight before Amyas's eyes. The crew chuckled; Amyas felt tears rising. Nothing is more poignant than childish vanity, especially when met with ridicule rather than admiration. Amyas hesitated to intervene, but the episode's inherent folly corrected itself. After three days, Ayacanora, unable to suppress her pride, asked Amyas, "Am I now quite an English girl?" Hearing titters behind her, she realized the truth, fled down the companion-ladder, and vanished.

Expecting her to jump overboard, Amyas hurried after her. Locked inside Lucy's cabin, Ayacanora sobbed violently, while Lucy, frightened and hungry, pleaded for answers. A significant source of friction for Mrs. Leigh was Ayacanora's treatment of the local ladies. As the months passed, the storms and sunshines of Ayacanora's adjustment gradually smoothed into a perpetual, genial calm. Slowly, however, fragments of her identity emerged. Familiarity with houses led her to remark that she had seen such structures before; the "red cattle" seemed known to her, while the sheep puzzled her until she admitted they were too small. Yeo, seizing upon every clue, suggested she had grown accustomed to llamas in Peru. Her greatest delight, however, was a horse. The ability to ride was a miracle to her, surpassing all other tamed creatures. After frustrating Amyas—who feared breaking her neck—she confided in Yeo about stealing one, failed in that scheme, and eventually negotiated with the vicar to trade her finery for his broken-kneed pony. The vicar, too honest to accept such a bargain, declined, leading Amyas to purchase her a jennet, which she mastered in a fortnight, riding with the skill of a Gaucho.

His baptism remains recorded in the crooked letters of the Bideford registers for 1587–3, listed twice: first as "Raleigh, a Winganditoian," and later, a year and a month later, simply as "Rawly," no longer marked among the baptisms. The free forest wanderer, having pined in vain for his deer-hunts and lagoons, departed for "happier hunting-grounds," leaving behind a grave in a narrow town churchyard, surrounded by dingy houses whose inhabitants would never mourn the Indian's passing. A somber emblem of the tragic fate awaiting the Red race, who arrived too late for salvation but too early for extinction.

While Amyas idled at home, England did not. That spring witnessed the dispatch of another, larger colony to Virginia under John White. Raleigh had repeatedly urged Amyas to take command, a decision that might have accelerated the founding of the United States by twenty years. Consequently, he sent five hundred pounds to fund the venture—a sum presumably still in Virginia today, as it never returned.

Imagine the spectators who last summer gazed with justifiable pride upon the noble port of Plymouth—the vast breakwater spanning the Sound, the arsenals and docks, the twin estuaries teeming with gallant vessels—watching the great screw-driven liners maneuver within their own lengths by invisible forces or weaving through crowded fleets with the agility of a skiff. Now imagine, by some magical twist, that the nineteenth century, with all the magnificence of its wealth and science, had suddenly vanished—as it may vanish hereafter—and they found themselves hurled back three centuries to the pleasant summer days of 1588.

Mount Edgecombe remains, beautiful as ever. Yet where are the docks, and where is Devonport? No vast dry-dock roofs rise at the water's edge. Drake's Island carries but a paltry battery, freshly erected by the man who lends it his name; Mount Wise stands alone, a gentleman's house amidst the fields; the citadel is a mere "pop-gun" fortification, something a third-class steamer would shell into rubble for an afternoon's diversion. And the shipping? The floating castles of the Hamoaze have shrunk to a handful of crawling lime-hoys; the Catwater is packed not with merchant craft as today, but with the ships that will, within a day, engage in the greatest sea-fight the world has ever witnessed.

To the modern eye, this appears a paltry squadron indeed; the largest vessel is not equal in size to a thirty-six-gun frigate, carrying less weight of metal than one of our newest gunboats, and capable of employing even that limited power at no more than a quarter of our modern range. Would our modern spectators, fresh from a brief excursion by rail to witness the cavalry embark and returning in time for dinner, have looked down upon this petty port and fleet with a contemptuous smile, launching some flippant remark about the progress of intellect and the triumphs of science, dismissing their benighted ancestors? Doubtlessly they would have done so if they belonged to the multitude who gaze at such achievements merely as a raree-show to feed their silly wonder, using and enjoying them without thankfulness or understanding, like an ox eating clover thrust into his rack without knowing or caring how it grew.

But if any of them were of the class that has *achieved* those very triumphs—the thinkers and workers who, instead of idly entering into other men's labors as the mob does, labor themselves; who know by hard experience the struggles, self-restraints, disappointments, and staggering steps by which the discoverer reaches his prize—then their smile would not be one of pity, but of filial love. For they would perceive in those outwardly modest armaments the potential germ of the mightier force that now loads the Black Sea waves. They would be acutely aware that producing it, with the materials and knowledge available then, demanded an intellect, an energy, and a spirit of progress and invention equal, if not superior, to those of which we now so loudly boast.

Yet, if one were a student of men rather than of machinery, he would find few nobler companies upon whom to exercise his discernment than those gathered on the little terrace bowling-green behind the Pelican Inn on the afternoon of July nineteenth. Chatting in groups or lounging over the low wall commanding a view of the Sound and the distant shipping, sat nearly every notable man of the Plymouth fleet, the entire *posse comitatus* of "England's forgotten worthies." The Armada had been scattered by a storm; Lord Howard had ventured out to seek it as far as the Spanish coast, but the wind had shifted south. Fearful that the Dons might slip past him unseen, he had returned to Plymouth, uncertain whether the Armada would actually arrive. Slip on, for a moment, like Prince Hal, the drawer's apron; step through the rose-clad door opening from the tavern, bearing a tray of long-necked Dutch glasses and a silver tankard of wine, and look around at the gallant captains awaiting the Spanish Armada, like lions in their lair waiting for the passing herd of deer.

Observe those five speaking earnestly in the center of a ring that longs to overhear yet respects boundaries too closely to approach. Those soft, long eyes and pointed chin are familiar; they belong to Walter Raleigh. Beside him, with an arm round Raleigh's neck, stands the fair youth in the flame-colored doublet, Lord Sheffield. Opposite them, standing beside Sir Richard Grenville, is a man as stately as Grenville himself, Lord Sheffield's uncle, Lord Charles Howard of Effingham, Lord High Admiral of England. Next to him is his son-in-law, Sir Robert Southwell, captain of the *Elizabeth Jonas*. But who is that short, sturdy, plainly dressed man, standing with legs slightly apart and hands behind his back, gazing upward with keen gray eyes into the face of each speaker? A coarse, plebeian stamp of a man: yet the entire figure and attitude radiate boundless determination, self-possession, and energy. When he finally speaks a few blunt words, all eyes turn respectfully upon him—for his name is Francis Drake.

A burly, grizzled elder, clad in greasy, sea-stained garments that contrast oddly with the massive gold chain about his neck, waddles forward as if born and living solely within a gale at sea. The upper half of his sharp, dogged visage resembles brick-red leather, the lower half badger's fur. As he claps Drake on the back and, with a broad Devon accent, shouts, "Be you a coming to drink your wine, Francis Drake, or be you not?—saving your presence, my lord?" the Lord High Admiral laughs and bids Drake go and drink his wine. For John Hawkins, Admiral of the Port, is the patriarch of Plymouth seamen; if Drake is their hero, Hawkins rules the realm, saying and doing pretty much as he pleases in any company on earth. Not to mention that today's prospect of an Armageddon fight has shaken him utterly out of his usual crabbed reserve, overflowing him with loquacious good humor, even toward his rival Drake.

Thus they push through the crowd, wherein lies many another man whom one would gladly have spoken with face-to-face on earth. The man pledging them better luck next time is George Fenner, known to "the Seven Portugals," Leicester's favorite, and captain of the galleon purchased by Queen Elizabeth. That short, prim man in the huge yellow ruff, with a sharp chin, minute imperial, and a self-satisfied smile, is Richard Hawkins, the "Complete Seaman"—Admiral John's hereafter-famous and hapless son. The elder speaking with him is his good uncle William, whose monument still stands, or should stand, in Deptford Church. For Admiral John set it up there but one year after this time; inscribed upon it reads how he was "A worshipper of the true religion, an especial benefactor of poor sailors, a most just arbiter in most difficult causes, and of a singular faith, piety, and prudence." That, and the fact that he passed some sharp work at Porto Rico credibly, is all I know of William Hawkins: but if you or I, reader, can have as much or half as much said of us when we must follow him, we shall have no cause to complain.

There is John Drake, Sir Francis's brother, ancestor of the present stock of Drakes; and there is George, his nephew, a man not overly wise, who has circled the globe with Amyas; and there is Amyas himself, conversing with one who answers him with fierce, curt sentences, Captain Barker of Bristol, brother of the unfortunate Andrew Barker who found John Oxenham's guns and, owing to a mutiny among his men, perished by the Spaniards in Honduras twelve years ago. Now commenced that epoch-making naval contest, destined to decide whether tyranny and idolatry or liberty and the Gospel should govern half of Europe and the future continent of America. It was a twelve-day epic, a narrative too grand for ordinary prose, demanding the thunder of Homeric verse; yet, compelled to recount it, I shall strive to employ the words of contemporary chroniclers wherever possible.

"The Lord High Admiral of England, sending a pinnace ahead named the *Defiance*, declared war by discharging her cannon; and immediately, drawing within musket-range, unleashed a tremendous barrage from his flagship, the *Ark Royal* (also known as the *Triumph*), striking at what he presumed to be the Spanish Admiral's vessel—which turned out to be the ship of Alfonso de Leon. Shortly thereafter, Drake, Hawkins, and Frobisher engaged stoutly against the rear squadron, commanded by Recalde." The Spaniards quickly recognized the superior speed and maneuverability of the English ships. Realizing they were giving more than they took, despite Recalde's efforts, the rear squadron hurried forward to join the main body. Admiral Medina, observing his fleet scattering rapidly, gathered them into a crescent formation. The Armada attempted to advance with solemn dignity, resembling a stately herd of buffalo marching across a prairie, disdainful of the wolves snarling at their heels. Yet, these were no mere wolves, but cunning hunters, swift-footed and keenly armed, determined to "shamefully shuffle" (to quote Drake's own phrase) that vast herd from the Lizard to Portland, and from Portland to Calais Roads. Even in this brief two-hour engagement, countless Spaniards began to question the boasted invincibility of their Armada.

One of the four great galliasses was already riddled with shot, causing severe disruption to her "pulpits, chapels," and the friars within. The fleet was forced to form a circle around her, lest Drake and Hawkins sink her. During this maneuver, the "principal galleon of Seville," carrying Pedro de Valdez and a host of blue-blooded nobility, ran aground upon her neighbor, tearing away her foremast. Despite Spanish chivalry, she was left to her fate. This did not appear like victory, certainly. But courage! Though Valdez remained behind, "Our Lady," the saints, and the bull *Caena Domini*—dictated by one I dare not name here—remained with them, and it would be blasphemous to doubt their presence. Meanwhile, however, if they had fared so poorly against merely a third of the Plymouth fleet, what would become of them when the forty belated ships, already whitening the blue horizon between them and the Mewstone, entered the fray?

Thus ended the first day; not a single English ship, hardly a man, was injured. It had forever destroyed, in the minds of the English, the prestige of boastful Spain. It had fully vindicated the policy advocated by the virtuous Lord Howard, following the counsel of Raleigh and Drake, to maintain a running fight rather than "clapping ships together without consideration." As Raleigh noted, had he adhered to the latter strategy, "he had been lost, if he had not been better advised than a great many malignant fools who found fault with his demeanor."

Be that as it may, the first day concluded with Amyas and the other Bideford ships having been exceedingly busy for two hours, punching holes in a massive galleon bearing on her poop a maiden with a wheel and named *Sta. The fleet did not find Lord Howard until nightfall; he and Lord Sheffield had held station steadfastly all night after the Spanish lanterns with only two ships. When the final blessing was spoken and the parson stepped back, the crowd lingered, crowding around the fresh mound of earth. Amyas stood motionless at the head of the grave, his figure silhouetted against the light. The villagers watched him with an instinctive dread, sensing that something profound was about to happen. Suddenly, Amyas straightened his spine to its full, towering height. He raised his hand, not in command, but in a gesture of majestic farewell, as if addressing the spirits of the sea.

"Good people, and ye who were my comrades in the fight!" he called out, his voice echoing strangely in the quiet square. Unchanging in purpose, swift in action, he lived a life of sober triumph and terrible endurance. He humbled the pride of Spain at Rio de la Hacha and Nombre, and first dared sail those southern seas that God intends to be free for English ships. Purged by strange torments and the cruelty of the Papists, he learned to fear only God. Now, he rests, exalted like Elijah to a chariot of fire. May his mantle fall upon you, leaving these shores never without brave and pious sailors, who count their lives as cheap for the sake of their Country, their Bible, and their Queen."

With that, he turned. Feeling for Cary and Brimblecombe, he stumbled slightly before regaining his balance, and they supported him as they walked slowly away from the churchyard, down the village street, and up the lane to Burrough Gate. The crowd parted before him, moving in a silent, reverent wave, as if he were a king returning from battle, though his kingdom was now locked inside his own chest.

Amyas knew exactly when they reached the gate. With a deftness that belied his blindness, he located the lock, his fingers finding the mechanism with practiced ease. He opened it and stepped forward onto the gravel path, his boots crunching softly. Cary and Brimblecombe followed, their steps hesitant, their hearts fluttering like trapped birds. They expected a storm of emotion, a collapse of the mighty man, but Amyas moved with a strange, stoic calm. He reached the front door, felt for the handle, and pushed it open.

"Mother!" he called, his voice quiet but clear.

Instantly, Mrs. Leigh was there, rushing to him. She sobbed silently, her eyes dry with a grief too deep for tears, as Amyas wrapped his great arms around her, holding her as if she were the only solid thing in a shifting world.

"Mother," he said after a long pause, his tone gentle and self-deprecating. "I am home, because I must. Will you take me in? Will you look after this useless carcase? I promise not to be much trouble." He looked down at her, a faint, sad smile touching his lips, and pressed a kiss to her brow.

She said nothing, merely passing her arm gently around his waist and leading him inside. "Be careful, dear child," she warned softly, guiding him past the threshold. "The doors are low."

Inside, Amyas paused, then turned to call for his companions. "Will! Jack!"

But they were gone, having slipped out the back door with surprising speed. Outside, Cary leaned against a tree, wiping sweat from his brow.

"I am glad we are away," Cary muttered. "Another minute, and I would have been a fool, staring at that angel of a woman. Look at her face working, trying to keep composure!"

"And there goes a brave servant of the Queen," Jack sighed, looking toward the house. "Cut down before his work was done. Heigho! I must go home to my old father. And then..."

"And then home with me," Cary insisted, shaking his head. "We have pulled in the same boat too long, Jack. You cannot go out spending your prize money on riotous living. I must see after you ashore, or you will treat half the town to a week of tavern brawling."

"Oh, Mr. Cary!" Jack protested, scandalized.

"Come home with me," Cary declared firmly. "We'll poison the parson, and your old father can give you the rectory."

"Oh, Mr. Cary!" Jack groaned.

Together, the two friends departed for Clovelly, leaving Amyas alone in the hall.

His mother had stepped out briefly to settle the seamen who had brought his luggage, giving them bread and ale. Amyas remained seated in the old bay-window where he had spent countless hours as a boy, reading tales of King Arthur and the cruelties of the Spaniards. He reached out, his hand brushing the leather bindings of the books lying beside each other, just as they had twenty years prior. The window stood open, and the cool autumn air drifted in, carrying the sweet scents of roses, rosemary, and gilliflowers. On the table before him sat a dish of apples. He recognized them instantly by their familiar, earthy perfume—the same fruit he had climbed trees to gather as a lad.

He extended his hand, taking an apple, rolling it between his palms. He played with it, tracing its skin, as if the intervening decades had never existed. In that moment, the floodgates of memory opened. As the text speaks of a drowning man seeing his entire life in a single flash, so Amyas beheld every place he had known, every word ever spoken to him. The visions swirled rapidly until he arrived at the fairy island of Meta, hearing the thunderous roar of the cataract once more. He saw the green tops of the palm trees sleeping in the sunlight above the spray, stepping across smooth trunks to leap down to the gravel beach beside the pool. And rising from the fern-covered rocks, beautiful and radiant, appeared the vision of Ayacanora.
