Tito walked lightly, the immediate dread passed, his usual cheerfulness returning as he made his way to see Romola. Yet her life—so full of quiet devotion, patient endurance, and gentle self-sacrifice—was a mirror of everything he had once avoided. He had not turned from goodness entirely; nor was he yet ready to embrace vice. Still young, with a heart open to beauty and joy, he remained untouched by corruption—for now. He had sold himself to evil, but it had not yet changed the shape of his days. Life still felt familiar, unaltered in its rhythm. He meant for things to continue as before: within and without. He would earn praise through diligence, learning, and charm. He would not do anything to disturb the harmony he cherished. And above all, he cared deeply for Romola. He longed to make her his wife—beautiful, loving, irreplaceable. Though wealthier matches might one day be possible, no woman in Florence could compare to her. When she looked at him with those honest hazel eyes, he felt a force like music—a deep, rhythmic sway that pulled him inward, making him want only to return to that moment again and again.

Even before reaching the door, standing on the stone steps with only Maso near, he felt that pull already stirring in his chest.

“Welcome, Tito mio,” came the old man’s voice, warm and alert, before Tito had spoken. There was new strength in Bardo’s tone, new brightness in his blind face since their first meeting over two months past. “You’ve brought fresh manuscript, I suppose? But since we spoke last night, I’ve had new thoughts—we must go back, widen our scope.”

Tito bowed to Romola as he stepped forward, then moved to Bardo’s chair as custom dictated, placing his hand into the one offered, settling onto the leather seat beside the old scholar.

“Yes,” he said softly, “I’ve brought the manuscript—but it can wait your pleasure. My legs are young; I’ll climb the hill again without trouble.”

He didn’t glance at Romola as he spoke, but he knew her eyes were upon him, filled with quiet delight.

“That is well said, my son,” Bardo replied, using the term more often lately. “And I am glad to see you do not shrink from labor, without which, as the poet wisely said, life has given nothing to mortals. Too often, ambition seeks the *palma sine pulvere*—glory without the dust of struggle. But what says the Greek? ‘In the morning of life, work; in the mid-day, give counsel; in the evening, pray.’ Though I may seem to have reached that twilight hour, I am not helpless so long as I have words left unspoken. My mind, as I’ve told you, was once dammed up—waters dark and still. But you, my Tito, have opened a channel, and now they surge forward with a force even I cannot fully command. Now, I wish us to revisit our earlier work, broaden our commentary and illustration. Otherwise, I may lose chances I now see clearly—chances that may never come again. You understand me, Tito?”

He bent slightly to retrieve his manuscript, which had slipped down, and Bardo’s sharp hearing caught the faint rustle.

Tito might have winced at the thought of more labor, but he was not naturally impatient. He had been trained in the rigorous scholarship of the age—minute, thorough, vast—and with Romola near, every effort felt lightened by the warmth of her presence.

“Certainly,” he answered. “You wish to expand our notes on certain passages we’ve cited.”

“Not only that. I wish to insert occasional *excursus*, where we’ve mentioned authors I’ve studied closely. For I may die too soon to complete any separate work. And now is no time to let learning lie idle—especially when we face not just ignorance, but men like Calderino, who, as Poliziano showed, resort to false citations to feed vanity and glorify their own errors. Therefore, Tito, I believe we should not miss this opportunity. Let us return to the point where we quoted Thucydides. First, I want you to go through all my notes on Lorenzo Valla’s Latin translation—the one Pope Nicholas the Fifth paid five hundred gold scudi for, a sum not unjustly granted. Though Valla’s reputation is otherwise questionable, he is praised for his strict scholarship, and the epigrammatist joked that since he descended to the shades, Pluto himself dares not speak in ancient tongues. Thus, his name must not become a seal for falsehood. So I will include an *excursus* on Thucydides, where my corrections of Valla’s text may properly belong. My Romola, bring the necessary volumes—you know them—they are on the fifth shelf of the cabinet.”

Tito rose at once, saying, “Let me fetch them if you’ll show me,” and followed Romola into the small adjoining room, whose walls were lined with books arranged in perfect order.

“There,” she said, pointing upward. “Every book is exactly where Father left it.”

Tito stood beside her, not hurrying to reach the volumes. They had never been alone in this room before.

“I hope,” she said, turning her gaze fully upon him, her expression calm and sincere, “he won’t weary you. This work brings him such joy.”

“And me too,” Tito murmured, “if you’ll allow me to say—I love you—if you’ll think me worth loving even a little.”

His voice was barely a whisper, and his face—dark, beautiful, closer to hers than ever before—looked at her with tender pleading.

“I do love you,” Romola whispered back. Her eyes held his with the same quiet dignity as always, but her voice had never dropped so low. It seemed to both of them that they stared at each other for a long time before her lips moved again—though it was only a breath before she said, “I know _now_ what it means to be happy.”

Their faces nearly touched; dark curls mingled with golden waves for a fleeting instant. Then, swift as lightning, Tito stepped onto a ledge of the shelves and retrieved the volumes. They returned to silence, content to be apart—because that first shared moment, that fragile awareness of one another, had been all the more powerful for being free of immediate sensation.

It had passed as quickly as water merging into water—so fast that even the watchful Bardo did not grow restless.

“You have the books, Romola?” the old man asked as they returned. “Now, prepare your pen. As Tito marks the scholia we decide to extract, you shall copy them immediately—number them carefully, so they match the numbers in the text he writes.”

Romola always had a role in their joint work. Tito stood at the leggio, writing and reading, while she sat at a table directly before him, ready to hand Bardo anything he needed or take a volume from his lap. They had occupied these positions since the beginning, but today it felt different—new. It was different to sit opposite one another, different for Tito to take a book from her hands as she lifted it from her father’s knee. Yet there was no calculated gesture, no attempt to prolong a glance or touch. Each person shapes the tokens of love in their own image, and Romola’s deep, tranquil joy wrapped Tito like the soft, rich light of dusk—calming, steady, dissolving all unease.

They had worked for two hours when the fading light prompted them to pause. Then the door opened, and a figure entered—strangely out of place amid the quiet study and solemn thoughts. It was Monna Brigida, a short, stout woman of fifty, with black eyes and a black velvet berretta embroidered with pearls. Beneath it, thick braids of hair framed a broad forehead, falling in heavy curves over her ears. A startling crimson blush colored her fat cheeks, contrasting with her pale skin. Three rows of pearls and a gold necklace rested on her thick neck. Her trailing black velvet gown and rose-colored damask sleeves, though slightly faded, bore the mark of skilled craftsmanship—six months’ labor, perhaps. The rose petticoat beneath, edged with dimmed white fringe and seed-pearl patterns, was displayed deliberately to signal its value. A coral rosary hung from one side of her belt, secured by a silver clasp engraved in niello; on the other side, a crimson velvet purse stitched with pearls dangled from her waist. In her plump right hand—pale, swollen as if baked—she carried a devotional book, also adorned with velvet, pearls, and silver.

She was familiar enough to Tito not to startle him. Monna Brigida often visited Bardo, spared from the banishment of trivial women due to her kinship with his late wife and her care for Romola in childhood. Now Romola smiled warmly and rose to move the leather seat away from her father’s chair, so the coming flood of talk wouldn’t disturb his ear.

“La cugina?” Bardo asked, sensing her steps and drapery.

“Yes, it’s your cousin,” Monna Brigida replied sharply, raising her fingers toward Tito in greeting, then lifting her face for Romola to kiss. “Always the troublesome cousin interrupting your wisdom,” she continued, seating herself and fanning herself with a white veil. “Well, well—if I didn’t bring you news now and then, I’d swear you’d forget there was anything alive but these dusty ancients, who need holy water to cleanse them, if all I hear is true. Not that the world isn’t rotten enough now—scandals popping up everywhere. I don’t want to see or hear such things, but you can’t walk with your head in a bag. Just yesterday—well, well, don’t burst out at me, Bardo, I’m not going to tell anything. I may not be as wise as the three kings, but I know how many legs go into one boot. Still, Florence is a wicked city—isn’t it, Messer Tito? You go out into the world. Not that we shouldn’t sin a little—Messer Domeneddio expects it, else what are the sacraments for? What I say is, we must honor the saints, not pretend we’re like them, or life becomes unbearable. Especially if things keep going like this. For what do you think? I went to a wedding today—Dianora Acciajoli’s with the young Albizzi everyone’s been talking about—and everyone wondered why it was today instead of yesterday. But, _cieli_! Such a wedding it was—it might have waited until next Lent for a penance. The bride looked like a white nun—not a pearl on her—and the groom as solemn as Saint Joseph. True! Half the guests were _Piagnoni_—that’s what they call them now, funeral mourners, these new saints of Fra Girolamo’s making. To think two families like the Albizzi and Acciajoli would adopt such ideas when they could afford the finest clothes! Well, they invited me—had to, since my husband was Luca Antonio’s uncle by the mother’s side—and I had a dreadful time waiting under the canopy before they let us in. I couldn’t stand in my clothes without offending someone. Monna Berta, who’s had worse secrets than I ever could, glared at me under her hood like a _pinzochera_, and told me to read the Frate’s book on widows—she found great comfort in it. Holy Madonna! It seems widows are supposed to buy their coffins now and count the years till they enter them, instead of enjoying themselves when they finally have freedom. And what music did we have to liven dinner? A long sermon from Fra Domenico of San Marco on the doctrines of their blessed Fra Girolamo—the three doctrines we’re all to memorize. He marked them off on his fingers until I shivered: first, Florence—or the Church, I’m not sure which—shall be scourged. If he means plague, the Signory should stop such preaching—it’s enough to make the lymph swell in fear. Then, Florence will be regenerated—but what good will that do if we’re all dead? And third, and most often repeated: it will happen in our lifetime. He marked that on his thumb until I trembled like the jelly before me. They served jellies, indeed—with the arms of the Albizzi and Acciajoli raised in color—so the world hasn’t turned completely upside down yet. But all they talk about is returning to old ways. Francesco Valori, whom I danced with in Via Larga when he was a bachelor and as loyal to the Medici as anyone, gave a speech about the old times—before Florentines stopped shouting ‘Popolo’ and started shouting ‘Palle’—as if that had anything to do with a wedding! He said we should follow the rules the Signory set centuries ago—what we could and couldn’t wear, eat—how our manners were corrupted, and we read bad books. Though he can’t accuse _me_—”

“Stop, cousin!” Bardo interrupted sharply, needing to speak. Monna Brigida jumped, pursed her lips, and stared at him wide-eyed.

“Francesco Valori is not entirely wrong,” Bardo continued. “Bernardo thinks poorly of him, believing he calls private grudges public zeal. Though I admit Bernardo is too slow to believe in pure patriotism, so clear among the ancients. Still, Tito, our manners have declined somewhat from the noble frugality that, as seen in our citizens’ public deeds, gives birth to true magnificence. Men now spend on a Giostra sums that could fund a library, a lasting gift to mankind. Yet I still believe Florentines hold more of that generous sobriety—hating petty extravagance so they may be grandly generous on grand occasions—than any other Italian city. I’ve heard Neapolitan and Milanese courtiers laugh at our lack of plate, scorn our noble families for borrowing tableware from one another. But in their foolish laughter, wisdom hears half its applause.”

“Laughter, indeed!” Monna Brigida exploded the moment Bardo paused. “If anyone wanted laughter at the wedding, they were disappointed. When young Niccolò Macchiavelli tried a joke, telling stories from Franco Sacchetti’s book—how the Signoria’s laws mean nothing to us women, because we’re cleverer than all painters, architects, and logicians, since we can make black look white, yellow pink, crooked straight, and rename anything forbidden—Holy Virgin! The Piagnoni grew even gloomier, and someone called Sacchetti’s book wicked. Well, I don’t read it—they can’t accuse _me_. Save me from weddings if that’s the fashion! All of us who weren’t Piagnoni felt like wet chickens. I’ve never been trapped worse than once—when I went to hear their precious Frate last Lent in San Lorenzo. Perhaps I never told you, Messer Tito? It nearly freezes my blood to recall it. How he railed at poor women—and men, too, though I didn’t mind that. He called us cows, flesh, wantons, mischief-makers—I could endure that, for others were fleshy and spiteful. But then he came to false hair—O misericordia! He painted a picture—I see it now—of a young woman lying pale and dead, and us light-hearted widows—of course he meant me—rushing to the corpse like bare-headed vultures, cutting off her hair to adorn our aging heads. Oh, the dreams I had after that! Then he cried, wrung his hands, and I wept too. On the way home, I took off my jewels—this very clasp—and packed them away. I almost sent them to the Good Men of Saint Martin for the poor, but by heaven’s mercy, I remembered my confessor, Fra Cristoforo, at Santa Croce. He told me it was all the devil’s work—Fra Girolamo’s preaching and prophecy, the Dominicans trying to turn the world upside down. I was never to listen again, or I’d have to do penance. The great preachers Fra Mariano and Fra Menico had shown Fra Girolamo preached lies—and I’d heard them both in the Duomo. And how the Pope’s dream of Saint Francis propping up the Church was still being fulfilled, while the Dominicans began to tear it down. Well and good—I left _con Dio_, and found peace. I won’t be frightened by a Frate Predicatore again. And all I say is, I wish it hadn’t been the Dominicans that poor Dino joined years ago. Then I’d have rejoiced when I heard he’d returned—”

“Silenzio!” Bardo shouted, his voice agitated. Romola half-started from her chair, clasping her hands and turning to Tito as if seeking help. Monna Brigida gasped, biting her lip.

“Donna!” Bardo said again, sternly. “Hear my will. Bring no talk of that name here. And thou, Romola, I forbid thee to ask. My son is dead.”

The old man trembled, his whole body vibrating with emotion. No one dared speak. Monna Brigida shrugged silently, rose quietly, nodded repeatedly to Tito and Romola—warning them not to move—and crept from the room like a guilty spaniel who had barked at the wrong time.

But Tito’s mind raced. Bardo’s son was not truly dead—he was a monk. He had “come back.” And Fra Luca—yes! That face, so like Bardo and Romola, had seemed vaguely familiar. If only he were dead now, at Fiesole! That selfish, desperate wish surged forward. True, Bardo’s rigid will would protect Romola from any contact with her brother—but not from the danger of Tito’s knowledge leaking, especially when Romola’s name was linked with that of Tito Melema, whose description he wore around his neck. Only Fra Luca’s death could end the threat. And death was likely. After the shock, Tito calmed, letting himself rest in the certainty of that hope.

They sat in silence, the room growing darker by the minute, until Romola ventured:

“Shall I light the lamp, father? Shall we continue?”

“No, my Romola,” Bardo said. “We will work no more tonight. Tito, come sit by me.”

Tito moved from the desk and sat beside Bardo, close to his left elbow.

“Come nearer, figliuola mia,” Bardo said after a pause. Romola sat on a low stool, resting her arm on his knee so he could stroke her hair, as he often did.

“Tito,” Bardo began, forgetting his earlier restraint, “I never told you I once had a son. But he left me—he is dead to me. I have disowned him forever. He was a gifted scholar, like you, but fiercer, quicker to rage, yet sometimes lost in himself, like a flame fed by uncertain winds. From the start, he turned from reason and philosophy, bowing to a dim mysticism that defied duty and argument. And so it ended. We will speak no more of him. He is dead to me. I wish his face could vanish from the world of memory, where the distant grows clearer and the near fades.”

Bardo paused. Neither Romola nor Tito dared speak—his voice shook, his composure uncertain. But then he placed his hand gently on Tito’s shoulder and continued, striving for calm.
