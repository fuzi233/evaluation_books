*"And I cannot touch her face
And I cannot touch her hair,
And I kneel to empty shadows—
Just memories of her grace;
And her voice sings in the winds
And in the sobs of dawn
And among the flowers at night
And from the brooks at sunrise
And from the sea at sunset,
And I answer with vain callings..."*

It was the Joy of the Sunset that first drew us into speech. I had wandered far from my home, pausing often to watch the evening sky rise like battlements above the world, feeling the hush of dusk settle over the land.

The last time I stopped, I stood lost in quiet reverence for the coming night—until laughter escaped me, soft and unbidden. Then, as if in answer, a voice called from the trees beside the road: *"And thou as well!"* as though sharing some secret understanding. I laughed again, half-doubting, wondering if some spirit of the gloaming mocked me.

But it was no phantom. She spoke my name, and when I stepped closer, I saw her clearly—Lady Mirdath the Beautiful, famed throughout Kent for her loveliness, and my neighbor, though we had never met.

She stood tall, though I towered over her, her eyes alight with mischief. She chided me for neglecting to call upon her guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles, and I—hat in hand—answered as best I could, struck by her radiance even in the fading light.

Then came a rustling from the wood. Three men burst forth, knives glinting. I shoved Mirdath behind me, gripping my oak staff. The first attacker fell to a sharp thrust; the second crumpled under a blow to the head; the third met my fist and dropped like a stone.

Mirdath’s whistle summoned her hounds, and soon servants arrived with lanterns. The footpads were dealt with, and I escorted her home, where Sir Jarles welcomed me warmly. That night, over dinner, I learned she was more than beautiful—she was sharp-witted, bold, and strangely attuned to the mysteries of dusk and dawn that I, too, cherished.

In the days that followed, I returned often. We wandered the estate, speaking of dreams and shared fancies—discovering, to our astonishment, that we had walked the same unseen paths in sleep. She knew lands I had only imagined; I described places she had longed to find. Time melted away in her company.

Yet she was willful, too. Once, disguised as a village maid, she dragged me to a country dance, where I rescued her from an overeager suitor. Another evening, I found her with a courtier, his arm around her waist. My heart twisted, but I bowed and left them—only to return weeks later, drawn by longing.

At her birthday celebration, I watched from the shadows as she moved among admirers, lovely but distant, her gaze searching for someone absent. My jealousy burned, but so did understanding—she loved another.

And so began my love for Mirdath the Beautiful—a love as fierce and untamed as the twilight we both adored.
