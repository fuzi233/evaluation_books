As the newspapers carried the announcement of Mrs. Palmer's safe delivery of a son and heir, the town buzzed with excitement. The news was particularly thrilling for Mrs. Jennings, whose happiness was directly tied to the well-being of her dear friend Charlotte. In celebration, she began spending her mornings at the Dashwood residence, indulging in lively conversation and warm camaraderie with her beloved friends. Meanwhile, the Miss Dashwoods found themselves relegated to the Middletons' estate in Conduit Street, where they spent their days tolerating the company of Lady Middleton and her daughters, the Miss Steeles.

While the Dashwoods would have preferred to linger over tea with Mrs. Jennings, they understood the social niceties involved in deferring to their hosts. However, they couldn't help but feel like unwanted guests, relegated to the periphery of conversations that revolved around the latest gossip and scandals. Lady Middleton, though polite on the surface, harbored a deep-seated disdain for the Dashwoods, viewing them as unsuitable companions due to their love of reading and their failure to flatter her children. Her younger daughter, Lucy, was equally wary, fearing that the Dashwoods would dismiss her attempts at flattery and mock her for them.

Only Miss Steele seemed relatively unaffected by the tension, content to bask in the attention of the older women. If she could have extracted a detailed account of Marianne's tumultuous relationship with Mr. Willoughby from the Dashwoods, she would have been eternally grateful, willing to surrender even the coveted spot by the fireplace after dinner. Alas, such a boon was not forthcoming, and she was left to console herself with snide remarks and feeble attempts at humor, which only earned her disinterest from Elinor or disgust from Marianne.

In contrast, a gentle tease about the eccentric Doctor would have gone a long way in winning Miss Steele over, but the Dashwoods remained resolutely uncooperative, leaving her to suffer through the meal in silence. She regaled them with stories of Charlotte's progress, taking great pains to recount every detail, from the baby's first cries to its first smiles, as if it were a personal triumph.
