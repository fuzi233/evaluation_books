When Romola reached the threshold of San Marco, a friar awaited her, standing like a sentinel in the twilight. Monna Brigida slipped silently into the adjacent church, and Romola was ushered through the cloisters to the chamber where the invalid lay confined—a precinct barred to women save for those immediate attendants.

As the heavy door swung open, the dim light filtering from outside merged with the flicker of two tapers set behind a wooden frame beside the bed. There lay Fra Luca, his face wasted to a skeletal map of bone, crowned with the shorn gold of a tonsure. His deep-set hazel eyes were fixed upon a small crucifix held close to his chest. Propped up with desperate effort, he seemed suspended between earth and air. Beside the bed stood another monk, his black cowl pulled low, who retreated toward the shadows as Romola stepped inside. Behind them, high on the frescoed wall, loomed the pale, agonized visage of Christ, while beneath it, ghostly faces of sorrow gazed out from the painted depths.

Then, her eyes met Fra Luca's as they lifted from the cross. A sharp, crushing pang of recognition seized her, binding this emaciated, holy stranger to the memory of her fair young brother.

"Dino!" she cried, her voice a raw whisper of pain. Yet she did not approach; she stood rigid, holding her distance at two paces. An invincible repulsion coiled within her at the sight of him robed and hooded. To Romola, this monastic guise seemed the brand of treacherous abandonment—the mark of a superstition that could twist filial duty into groveling piety. Her father, a man of stark, honest integrity who had raised her in the shadow of the Church's influence, had instilled in her a quiet defiance of ecclesiastical claims. To her mind, the Church belonged to the noisy, chaotic multitude from which they had always separated themselves. How, then, could she reconcile her brother's path with anything but indignant, cold contempt? Still, a thread of old love clung to her heart, and though her stance remained immovable, a yearning search burned in her eyes, seeking a spark of the boy she had lost.

Yet Fra Luca's countenance offered no mirror to her turmoil. He regarded the returned sister, now a woman in full bloom, with the detached, distant gaze of a spirit visiting its former home.

"My sister!" he murmured, his speech broken yet distinct. "It is well thou hast not delayed further. I have a message for thee, and my time is brief."

Romola took a tentative step forward. She expected words of penitence, a confession that would soothe her father's loneliness. Surely, the guilt of these long years of desertion would bring tears, understanding, and forgiveness. Dino would speak of his father's blindness, of the dark voids he had endured, of the quiet life now in the house where he had been born. The end would be tender, regretful.

"Romola," Fra Luca began, "I have seen a vision concerning thee. Three times in these last two months it has appeared to me, growing clearer each time. Thus, I traveled from Fiesole, convinced this was a mandate from heaven. I perceive a promise of mercy in this, that my breath remains to—"

A ragged gasp cut him off.

Chill swept through Romola's heart. This was a vision, then—a relic of those bitter tales her father had often cited. Indignation surged in her throat.

"Dino! Did you think to send some word to my father? You deserted him when his sight failed; you made his existence a wilderness. Does your conscience feel nothing? No repentance? What sorcery is this religion of yours, that places visions above natural obligations?"

Her hazel eyes turned slowly toward her, resting with a terrible, silent gravity for several moments, as if weighing the cost of an answer.

"No," he said finally, his voice low, passionless, sounding less like human speech and more like a spirit speaking through a vessel. "No, I have never repented for fleeing the suffocating, poisonous air of sin that choked me, threatening to drown my senses like strong wine. My father could not hear the Voice calling me night and day; he knew not of the demons that dragged me back from the path I chose. He lived amidst human sin and misery without seeing them. He was like one gathering shining stones in a mine, unaware that the whole world above was perishing with plague. I spoke, and he listened with scorn. I told him that the studies he cherished were either trivial toys or required to be heated by the fires of worldly ambition and carnal lust, for those were the very substances of the poetry and history he demanded I consume."

"Did my father not lead a pure and noble life, then?" Romola burst out, the words tearing from her before she could check them. "He sought no honors; he was truthful; he denied himself every luxury; he lived as the ancients sages did. He never wished you to serve worldly ambitions or fleshly lusts. He raised you to live by the purest maxims of philosophy, the very truths he breathed into you."

She spoke the lines of her upbringing, perhaps by rote, but with a fierce, burning conviction. Her cheeks flushed pink, her body trembling with the force of her protest. Again, Fra Luca was slow to answer, regarding her impassioned face with eyes devoid of warmth.

"What were those maxims of philosophy to me?" he asked softly. "They bade me be strong when I felt weak; they told me, like Saint Benedict, to roll in thorns and welcome smarting wounds as a deliverance from temptation. The Divine Love had sought me, penetrating me until a vast need arose within me, like a seed demanding room to expand. Though I had been raised in careless indifference to the true Faith, and though I had not studied the dogmas of our religion, it seized me like a rising flood. I sensed a life of perfect love and purity, free from hungry longing for pleasure, free from tormenting doubts, free from the fear of suffering. Before I knew the histories of the saints, I foresaw their ecstasy. For the same Truth permeated even pagan philosophy: that it is a bliss accessible to man to die to mortal needs and live in the life of God as the Unseen Perfection. To reach it, I must forsake the world. I must sever all affections and hopes tied to what passes away. I must relate to my fellow-men only as souls connected to the Eternal."

"That need drove me constantly," he continued, pausing as his breathing grew labored. "It visited me in visions when my mind grew weary of the empty words recording the passions of the dead. It came to me after I had yielded to sin and turned away in loathing from the scent of the hollow cup. In those visions, I saw the meaning of the Crucifix."

He stopped, his chest heaving violently for a minute or two. Romola said nothing; her mind recoiled, finding no purchase on the unearthly nature of this brother. As useless as trying to grasp a shadow, any contact with his logic seemed impossible. When he spoke again, the violent heaving of his chest had subsided.

"I felt whom I must follow. Yet, I saw that even among the servants of the Cross who claimed to have renounced the world, my soul would be stifled by the fumes of hypocrisy, lust, and pride. God did not choose me, as He chose Saint Dominic and Saint Francis, to wrestle with evil within the Church and the world. He commanded me to flee. I took the sacred vows and I fled—to lands where danger, scorn, and want bore me like angels to repose upon the bosom of God. I lived as a hermit, I tended to pilgrims, but my task was short. The veil separating me from my eternal rest has grown exceedingly thin. I returned to Florence that—"

"Dino!" Romola interrupted, the image of her father's suffering life striking anew with a desperate desire for reunion and absolution. "You *did* wish to know if my father lives?"

"—Yes. That before I depart, I might urge my brothers to study the Eastern tongues, as I never did, and pursue ends greater than I achieved. I find them already devoted to this labor. And since I have returned, Romola, I have felt I was sent partly to thee—not to restore bonds of earthly affection, but to deliver the heavenly warning conveyed in a vision. For I have seen it thrice. Through all the years since the Divine Voice first called me, while I still walked the world, I have been taught and guided by visions. For in the painful weaving of our waking thoughts, we can never be certain we have not mingled our own errors with the light we pray for; but in visions and dreams, we are passive, and our souls become instruments in the Hand of God. Therefore, listen, and speak not again—for time is fleeting."

Romola's mind flinched violently from the prospect of listening. Her indignation had faded, not because it was resolved, but because the chasm between her brother and herself seemed to widen into an abyss. Just as Fra Luca spoke, the figure of another monk crossed the chamber and stood on the opposite side of the bed, his cowl drawn low.
