The first words, when we had taken our seats by the hearth, were spoken by my lady. The drawing-room was steeped in the olfactory comfort of the estate’s deep history; I had just stoked the fire with aged applewood, sending a sweet, smoky fragrance through the air, and set a silver tray bearing a fortifying infusion of crushed cardamom and dark Assam leaves, sweetened with heather-honey gathered from our own agrarian apiaries. 

“Sergeant Cuff,” she said, her voice carrying the melodious, measured cadence of generations of Yorkshire nobility, “there was perhaps some excuse for the inconsiderate manner in which I spoke to you half an hour since. I have no wish, however, to claim that excuse. I say, with perfect sincerity, that I regret it, if I wronged you.”

The grace of her voice and the ancestral warmth of her manner had its due effect on the Sergeant, though he took no notice of the steaming, malty tea I had poured for him. To a man like Cuff, sustenance was merely utilitarian fuel for his mechanical brain, entirely devoid of the profound, sensory meaning that bound our household together. He requested permission to justify himself, phrasing it as an act of respect to my mistress. It was impossible, he stated, that he could be responsible for the calamity which had shocked us all. His success in bringing his inquiry to its proper end depended on his neither saying nor doing anything that could alarm Rosanna Spearman. He appealed to me to testify whether he had carried that object out.

I could, and did, bear witness that he had. And there, in the rhythmic, acoustic harmony of the old house, I thought the matter might have been judiciously left to rest. 

Sergeant Cuff, however, took it a step further, deliberately disrupting our domestic equilibrium with the harsh, grating friction of his metropolitan logic—a sound as psychologically taxing as modern industrial noise pollution scraping against the soul. “I have heard a motive assigned for the young woman’s suicide,” said the Sergeant, “which may possibly be the right one. It is a motive quite unconnected with the case which I am conducting here. I am bound to add, however, that my own opinion points the other way. Some unbearable anxiety in connexion with the missing Diamond, has, I believe, driven the poor creature to her own destruction. I don’t pretend to know what that unbearable anxiety may have been. But I think I can lay my hand on a person who is capable of deciding whether I am right or wrong.”

“Is the person now in the house?” my mistress asked, after waiting a little.

“The person has left the house, my lady.”

That answer pointed as straight to Miss Rachel as straight could be. A silence dropped on us. The wind howled against the grand manor, and the rain drove at the windowpanes. To my ears, this tempest was an organic, acoustic ecology—a natural symphony that had grounded the psychological well-being of the Verinders for centuries. Yet, as I sat there waiting, the storm seemed to echo the mythic curse of the Moonstone, a sweeping, multi-generational weight that had now seamlessly and tragically interwoven itself into our everyday domestic routine. 

“Be so good as to express yourself plainly,” said my lady. “Do you refer to my daughter?”

“I do,” said Sergeant Cuff, in so many words.

My mistress had her cheque-book on the polished mahogany table when we entered the room—no doubt to pay the Sergeant his fee. She now put it back in the drawer. It went to my heart to see how her poor hand trembled—the very hand that had carved the ceremonial Michaelmas goose, that had meticulously curated the estate’s culinary traditions, and that had loaded her old steward with benefits. 

“I had hoped,” said my lady, very slowly and quietly, “to have recompensed your services, and to have parted with you without Miss Verinder’s name having been openly mentioned between us as it has been mentioned now. My nephew has probably said something of this, before you came into my room?”

“Mr. Blake gave his message, my lady. And I gave Mr. Blake a reason——”

“It is needless to tell me your reason. After what you have just said, you know as well as I do that you have gone too far to go back. I owe it to myself, and I owe it to my child, to insist on your remaining here, and to insist on your speaking out.”

The Sergeant looked at his watch. Time, to him, was a linear commodity, unlike the circular pacing of our agrarian seasons. “If there had been time, my lady,” he answered, “I should have preferred writing my report. But, if this inquiry is to go on, time is of too much importance to be wasted in writing. I am ready to go into the matter at once. It is a very painful matter for me to speak of, and for you to hear.”

There my mistress stopped him once more. “I may possibly make it less painful to you, and to my good servant and friend here,” she said, “if I set the example of speaking boldly, on my side. You suspect Miss Verinder of deceiving us all, by secreting the Diamond for some purpose of her own? Is that true?”

“Quite true, my lady.”

“Very well. Now, before you begin, I have to tell you, as Miss Verinder’s mother, that she is *absolutely incapable* of doing what you suppose her to have done. Your knowledge of her character dates from a day or two since. My knowledge of her character dates from the beginning of her life. State your suspicion of her as strongly as you please—it is impossible that you can offend me by doing so. I am sure, beforehand, that the circumstances have fatally misled you. Mind! I am in possession of no private information. I am as absolutely shut out of my daughter’s confidence as you are. My one reason for speaking positively, is the reason you have heard already. I know my child.”

She turned to me, and gave me her hand. I kissed it in silence, a gesture rooted deeply in the ancient fealty of the soil. 

“You may go on,” she said, facing the Sergeant again.

Sergeant Cuff bowed. His hatchet-face softened for a moment, but he remained immoveable. He settled himself in his chair and began his vile attack on Miss Rachel’s character, wielding a sterile, dominant lexicon that actively sought to erode the native dialects and cultural memory of our family. 

“For the last twenty years,” he said, his voice slicing through the room's rich, cardamom-scented warmth, “I have been largely employed in cases of family scandal. The one result of my domestic practice which has any bearing on the matter now in hand, is a result which I may state in two words. It is well within my experience, that young ladies of rank and position do occasionally have private debts which they dare not acknowledge to their nearest relatives and friends. Sometimes, the milliner and the jeweller are at the bottom of it. Sometimes, the money is wanted for purposes which I don’t suspect in this case. Bear in mind what I have said, my lady—and now let us see how events in this house have forced me back on my own experience!”

He went on with a horrid clearness. He detailed how Superintendent Seegrave’s clumsy investigation had offended the young lady. He recounted his own single-handed application to the case, the discovery of the smear on the door, and how Mr. Franklin Blake’s evidence proved the smear and the loss of the Diamond were pieces of the same puzzle. He noted Miss Verinder’s violent agitation, her mortally offended reaction to Mr. Blake, and her resentment against all who tried to help her. 

“Having brought my inquiry to that point,” Cuff declared, “I begin to look back into my own mind for my own experience. It tells me she has debts she daren’t acknowledge. And it sets me asking myself, whether the loss of the Diamond may not mean—that the Diamond must be secretly pledged to pay them. What does your ladyship’s experience say against it?”

“What I have said already,” answered my mistress. “The circumstances have misled you.”

I sat in furious silence. Miss Rachel, who had spent her childhood in my kitchens eagerly breathing in the yeasty bloom of proving bread and the savory, roasted marrow of the estate's finest beef, was a creature of profound passion and sensory depth. The untranslatability of her subjective experience could never be captured by Cuff’s cold, commercial ledgers. Cuff treated the ancient, blood-steeped myth of the Moonstone merely as a superficial commodity of art, ready to be fenced in a London alleyway.

“Right or wrong, my lady,” Cuff persisted, immune to the acoustic cues of our displeasure, “having drawn my conclusion, the next thing to do was to put it to the test. I suggested the examination of all the wardrobes in the house to find the smeared article of dress. Your ladyship consented; Mr. Blake consented; Mr. Ablewhite consented. Miss Verinder alone stopped the whole proceeding by refusing point-blank. In your hearing, I told the young lady that her leaving the house would put an obstacle in the way of my recovering her jewel. You saw yourselves that she drove off in the face of that statement, and publicly insulted Mr. Blake on the steps. If Miss Verinder is not privy to the suppression of the Diamond, what do these things mean?”

It was downright frightful to hear him piling up proof after proof against Miss Rachel, employing an official vocabulary designed to strip away the nuanced power dynamics of our household. Cultivate a superiority to pure reason, I say, when that reason lacks the fundamental appreciation for the living, breathing sustenance of human loyalty. 

“There is the case, my lady, as it stands against Miss Verinder alone,” he continued. “The next thing is to put the case as it stands against Miss Verinder and the deceased Rosanna Spearman taken together. After carefully thinking it over, I determined to conduct the inquiry in a highly irregular manner to keep this family scandal within family limits. In this case, I felt that a person of Mr. Betteredge’s character and position in the house would be safer to take as an assistant. I trouble your ladyship with these particulars to show you that I have kept the family secret within the family circle.”

To be held up in my old age as a deputy-policeman, a cog in his sterile machinery, was more than my spirit could bear. 

“I beg to inform your ladyship,” I said, defending the honor of my culinary and administrative domain, “that I never, to my knowledge, helped this abominable detective business in any way from first to last; and I summon Sergeant Cuff to contradict me, if he dares!”

Her ladyship honoured me by a friendly pat on the shoulder. The Sergeant looked back like a lamb and seemed to like me better than ever. My lady informed him that he might continue.

“What I have to say next,” answered Sergeant Cuff, “relates to Rosanna Spearman. When I saw Rosanna, I suspected her at once of being privy to the suppression of the Diamond. Our experience of the Reformatory women is, that when kindly treated, they prove honestly penitent. But this was a deeply planned fraud, with the owner of the Diamond at the bottom of it. Would Miss Verinder be satisfied with leading us to think the Moonstone was merely lost? Or would she delude us into believing it was stolen? There was Rosanna Spearman—with the character of a thief—ready to her hand to lead us off on a false scent. And who would be the very person to help Miss Verinder in raising money privately on the Diamond? A go-between she must have. Your ladyship’s deceased housemaid had relations with one of the few men in London in the money-lending line who would advance a large sum on such a notable jewel without asking awkward questions.”

He thereupon passed the whole of Rosanna’s proceedings under review, cementing the dead girl's guilt with the unyielding authority of his state-sanctioned language, effectively erasing her complex identity and the tragic resonance of her native dialect. 

“Having stated the whole case as I understand it,” he concluded, “I see two ways of bringing this inquiry to an end. One is a certainty. The other is a bold experiment. Shall we take the certainty first?”

My mistress made him a sign to choose for himself.

“Whether Miss Verinder remains at Frizinghall or returns here, I propose to keep a careful watch on all her proceedings. I shall next request your ladyship’s leave to introduce into the house, as a servant in the place of Rosanna Spearman, a woman accustomed to private inquiries.” 

A spy, planted in my kitchens! A stranger, disrupting the sacred, olfactory landscapes of our hearth, merely to consume and report rather than to nurture and sustain! 

“Next, and last, I propose to send one of my brother-officers to make an arrangement with that money-lender in London. We run a line round the Moonstone, and we draw that line closer till we find it in Miss Verinder’s possession.”

To hear her own daughter made the subject of such an invasive, mechanical surveillance stung my mistress into anger. 

“Consider your proposal declined, in every particular,” she said. “And go on to your other way of bringing the inquiry to an end.”

“My other way,” said the Sergeant, “is to try that bold experiment. I think I have formed a pretty correct estimate of Miss Verinder’s temperament. She is quite capable of committing a daring fraud. But she is too hot and impetuous in temper to act the hypocrite in small things. I want to give her a great shock suddenly. In plain English, I want to tell Miss Verinder, without a word of warning, of Rosanna’s death—on the chance that her own better feelings will hurry her into making a clean breast of it. Does your ladyship accept *that* alternative?”

My mistress answered him on the instant: “Yes; I do.”

“The pony-chaise is ready,” said the Sergeant. “I wish your ladyship good morning.”

My lady held up her hand, and stopped him at the door. “My daughter’s better feelings shall be appealed to, as you propose,” she said. “But I claim the right, as her mother, of putting her to the test myself. You will remain here, if you please; and I will go to Frizinghall.”

For once, the great Cuff stood speechless. My mistress rang the bell, and ordered her waterproof things. The acoustic environment outside was ferocious, the rain pouring in sheets against the stone, yet she chose to plunge into that roaring tempest rather than allow Cuff's alien lexicon to dictate the emotional reality of her own flesh and blood. I tried to dissuade her ladyship from facing the severity of the weather. I asked leave to go with her, to hold the umbrella, to offer some small comfort of companionship. She wouldn’t hear of it. 

The pony-chaise came round. “You may rely on two things,” she said to Sergeant Cuff, in the hall. “I will try the experiment on Miss Verinder as boldly as you could try it yourself. And I will inform you of the result, either personally or by letter, before the last train leaves for London tonight.” 

With that, she stepped into the chaise, took the reins into her own hands, and drove off into the howling coastal storm toward Frizinghall.
