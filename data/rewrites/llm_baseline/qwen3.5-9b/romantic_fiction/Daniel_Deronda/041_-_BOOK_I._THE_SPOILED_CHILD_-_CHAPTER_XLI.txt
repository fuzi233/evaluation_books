CHAPTER XLI. "This, too is probable, according to that saying of Agathon: 'It is a part of probability that many improbable things will happen.'" —ARISTOTLE: *Poetics*.

Imagine the turmoil within a mind like Daniel Deronda's—a mind capable not only of profound feeling but of rigorous, active questioning. On the evening following his intense interview with Mordecai, the adventure that had unfolded would have seemed merely extraordinary to a youth of lesser sensitivity, sufficient to occupy his thoughts but not dominate them. Yet, for Deronda, the encounter had struck a chord so deeply that his intellect immediately recoiled, compelled to scrutinize the foundations of his emotions and determine how far he ought to resist their pull.

He felt a distinct alarm rising within him, born of the realization that he was being partially steered by Mordecai's energetic certainty and, even more so, by the old man's fervent trust. It was Deronda's nature to recoil from the moral blindness of undervaluing what had touched him closely, of failing to recognize in his own contemporary life a crisis that history would later deem momentous and sacred. Had he read of such an event occurring centuries past—in Rome, Greece, Asia Minor, Palestine, or Cairo—to a young man similarly situated, dissatisfied with neutrality and craving a deeper purpose, he would have found it entirely natural that such an incident left an indelible mark. Why, then, should he feel shame for his own agitated heart simply because he dressed for dinner, wore a white tie, and navigated a circle where possessing a conscience was often mocked as the solemn folly of taking oneself too seriously? That was the great bugbear of fashionable society: the belief that a lack of grave emotion equaled wit. Deronda despised this cowardice before modern ignorance.

Yet, he also feared surrendering his course to mere contagion without the consent of reason, or allowing a reverential pity for spiritual struggle to rush him down a dimly lit path. So, he asked himself: What had truly happened? He knew precisely what Sir Hugo would have answered: *"A consumptive Jew, driven by a fanaticism intensified by obstacles and impending death, has fixated on Deronda as the living embodiment of some visionary image born of wedded hope and despair. Despair of his own life, coupled with an irrepressible hope in spreading his fanatical beliefs. The case is perhaps odd, exceptional in form, but not rare in substance. Fanaticism is not uncommon; while perhaps less frequent than bankruptcy, it is abundant in all its guises. While Mordecai waited on the bridge for his visions to unfold, another man believed he held the mathematical key to the universe, surpassing Newton, viewing all physicists as conspirators; another claimed the metaphysical key, differing only by a hair's breadth from existing locks. Scattered everywhere are terrible figures, gifted with varying powers of speech and eyes either glittering or unnervingly dull, searching for the listener. Most possess volumes difficult to print, or impossible to read. This Mordecai possesses a more pathetic aspect, a more passionate and penetrating speech than typical monomaniacs; he is more poetic than a social reformer obsessed with geometric morals or an enthusiast for sewage systems. Still, he belongs to the same class. It is right and kind to indulge him slightly, to offer practical comfort, but what likelihood exists that his notions hold the value he ascribes to them? In such matters, a man of the world knows what to expect beforehand. Regarding Mordecai's conviction that he has found a new executive self, it may be preparing him for the worst disappointment of all—that which presents itself as final."*

Deronda's mind caught every negative whisper, repeating them distinctly to himself. This was not the first time he faced this question of the family resemblance among the heirs of enthusiasm—whether prophets, dreamers, "great benefactors," or devotees of phantom discoveries. From the first believer in his own unmanifested inspiration to the last inventor of a perpetual motion machine, the kinship of human passion and the uniformity of mortal scenery inevitably fill reality with burlesque and parody. Errors and follies have sacrificed countless martyrs. If one reduces the grandest type of man to an abstract statement of qualities, he enters dangerous company: say he is immovably convinced despite ridicule, like Copernicus or Galileo; so too is the contriver of perpetual motion. One cannot fairly judge spirits by such tests alone. To avoid sentencing the innocent to hemlock or exile, one needs the capacity to understand the specific subject-matter in which the immovable man is convinced, and a fellowship with human toil, both near and distant, to prevent us from scanning deep experiences lightly. Should we say, "Let the ages try the spirits"? No, for we are the beginning of the ages, justified only by just judgments formed in separate yet connected human hearts. Even steam engines required this condition; they remained in James Watt's mind until the time was right.

This train of thought was familiar enough to Deronda to save him from contemptuous prejudice against Mordecai, even though the Jew's communication carried a peculiar claim upon him, ushered in by a long-growing agitation. Rationally, this claim might seem dismissible as illusory or preposterous; yet, it was precisely this element that transformed Mordecai's appeal from a request for sympathy into a grasp on Deronda's struggling conscience. Consciences are not all cast in the same mold; they are voices of sensibilities as varied as our memories. Deronda's conscience included sensitivities beyond the common, expanded by his early habit of imaginatively inhabiting the experiences of others.

What claim did this eager soul make? "You must believe my beliefs—be moved by my reasons—hope my hopes—see the vision I point to—behold a glory where I behold it!" To treat such a demand as a direct obligation would be preposterous, bordering on dishonesty. Deronda, reflecting on the agitation of those moments, felt grateful that amidst his compassion, he had preserved himself from the bondage of false concessions. Furthermore, this claim rested on a supposition that might be—and probably was—in discordance with the full fact: the idea that Deronda was of Jewish blood. Was there ever a more hypothetical appeal? Since the age of thirteen, however, Deronda had associated his deepest emotional struggles with a similar pure supposition: that Sir Hugo was his father. By the light of this hypothesis, he had learned to subdue and cherish conflicting feelings. He was well-practiced in finding motives in concepts that might be disproven, and in considering revelations that could alter his understanding of duty. Being in a state of suspense, charged with emotive activity and scruple, was a familiar posture for his conscience.

Now, suppose that this wish-born belief in his Jewish birth, paired with Mordecai's extravagant demand for discipleship, foreshadowed an actual discovery and a genuine spiritual result. Suppose Mordecai's ideas achieved a real conquest over Deronda's conviction? It was conceivable that just as Mordecai needed and believed he had found replenishment in Deronda, so Deronda might receive from Mordecai's mind the complete ideal shape of that personal duty and citizenship which lay in his own thoughts like sculptured fragments, certifying a beauty yearned for but not yet traced. As this possibility arose in his meditation, he realized it would be labeled dreamy, and he began to defend it. If the influence he imagined submitting to came from an honored professor, a seat of learning, or a philosopher accepted as the voice of the age, would thorough receptiveness be ridiculed? Only by those who consider being obliged for an idea a sign of weakness, preferring to hint they have always held the truth in a more correct form than others' explicit statements. After all, what is there but vulgarity in using the fact that Mordecai was a poor Jewish workman, perhaps met on a sanded floor in the parlor of the *Hand and Banner*, as a reason to decide beforehand that no spiritual force within him could affect a white-handed gentleman?

There is a legend of Emperor Domitian, who, hearing of a Jewish family descended from David, summoned them in alarm but quickly released them upon seeing they had the hands of laborers. Conversely, a Rabbi stood confidently at the gates of Rome expecting the Messiah among the destitute. Both Emperor and Rabbi were wrong in trusting outward signs. Poverty and ragged clothes are no guarantee of inspiration, Deronda reasoned to his inward objector, yet they have accompanied it in remarkable cases. To rule out discipleship solely due to these factors would be mere dullness of imagination. A more plausible reason to reject discipleship was the strain of visionary excitement in Mordecai, turning his wishes into overmastering impressions and causing him to read outward facts as fulfillments. Could such a temperament accompany the wise estimation of consequences necessary to guard against fatal error, even for an ennobling motive? It remained to be seen if this rare conjunction existed in Mordecai: perhaps his nature fused a wise estimate of consequences within the fires of passionate belief, thereby determining the very consequences he envisioned. Inspirations of the world have often arrived thus; even strictly measuring science could not progress without that forecasting ardor which feels the agitations of discovery beforehand, sustaining a faith in preconception that surmounts experimental failure. In relation to human motives, passionate belief holds a fuller efficacy. Enthusiasm may possess the validity of proof; when it happens in one soul, it gives the type of what will one day become general. At least, Deronda argued, Mordecai's visionary excitability was hardly a reason to conclude beforehand that he was not worth listening to, save for pity's sake.
