A deeper, more enduring trouble had settled upon the rectory. Rex returned there only to collapse onto his bed, draped in an armor of apparent apathy that held firm until the following day, when it began to crack under the weight of positive sickness. The conversation regarding Southampton vanished entirely. Instead, the minds of his mother and Anna were consumed by a singular, desperate question: How does one tend a patient who refuses to heal? The boy who had once been the household's brightest, most grateful light, had metamorphosed into a dull-eyed, unresponsive creature. Affectionate advances were met only with a weary murmur, "Let me alone."

His father, Mr. Gascoigne, looked past the immediate crisis, viewing it as the shortest path to extricating himself from an unfortunate entanglement. Yet, despite his pragmatic outlook, he felt a pang for the inevitable suffering. He would occasionally sit by Rex's side in silence, pressing a gentle hand upon his son's blank brow before departing with a soft, "God bless you, my boy." Warham and the younger siblings often peeked around the doorframe, transfixed by the sight of their lively brother laid low, only to be shooed away by stern fingers. The constant guardian in this shadow was Anna. Her small hand rested within her brother's, though he offered no welcoming squeeze. Her soul was torn in twain: half in anguish for Rex, the other half burning with reproach for Gwendolen.

"Perhaps it is wicked of me, but I think I never *can* love her again," whispered Anna, the recurring burden of her inner lament. Even Mrs. Gascoigne harbored an angry sentiment toward her niece, which she expressed to her husband with an apology that did little to soften the blow.

"I know, of course, it is better, and we ought to be thankful she is not in love with the poor boy," Mrs. Gascoigne confessed. "But really, Henry, I think she is hard. She has the heart of a coquette. I cannot help thinking she must have led him on with false promises; otherwise, the disappointment would not have struck him with such devastating force. And some blame must attach to poor Fanny; she is utterly blind concerning that girl."

Mr. Gascoigne answered imperatively. "The less said on that point the better, Nancy. I ought to have been more awake myself. As for the boy, be thankful if nothing worse ever happens to him. Let the matter die out as quickly as possible. Especially regarding Gwendolen—let it be as if it had never existed."

The rector's dominant emotion was that of a man who had escaped disaster. If Gwendolen had loved Rex in return, it would have created a problem far harder to solve, one potentially beyond his control. However, he faced further difficulties.

On a fine morning, Rex requested his bath and prepared for the day as he usually did. Anna, filled with electric hope at this sign of change, listened intently for his descent. Finally, hearing his steps, she ran to the foot of the stairs to meet him. For the first time since his illness, he gave her a faint smile. It was so melancholy upon his pale face that she could barely suppress the urge to cry.

"Nannie!" he said gently, taking her hand and leading her slowly down to the drawing-room. His mother was present, and when she kissed him, he exclaimed, "What a plague I am!"

He then sat still, gazing out of the bow-window at the lawn and shrubs dusted with hoarfrost, across which the sun sent faint, occasional gleams—a reflection, Anna thought, of that sad smile on Rex's face. He felt as though he had been resurrected into a new world, leaving the old interests far behind, unsure of how to inhabit this strange space. Anna sat near him, pretending to work while secretly watching him with yearning eyes. Beyond the garden hedge, a road stretched where wagons and carts sometimes traveled during field-work. A railed opening cut through the hedge, revealing an upland pasture bordered by woods and a cluster of ash trees against the sky.

Soon, a wagon laden with lumber appeared. The horses strained their magnificent muscles, and the driver, cracking his whip, ran alongside to guide the lead horse's head, fearful of a sudden swerve. Rex seemed shaken from his lethargy by the motion. He rose and watched until the last quivering trunk of timber disappeared, then paced the floor once or twice. Mrs. Gascoigne had retired, and when Rex sat down again, Anna, sensing a return of speech in his eyes, could not resist bringing a small stool. She seated herself against his knee, looking up at him with an expression that silently pleaded, *"Do speak to me."* And he spoke.

"I'll tell you what I'm thinking of, Nannie," Rex said. "I will go to Canada, or somewhere of that sort." (Rex had not yet studied the complex character of colonial possessions).

"Oh, Rex, not for always!"

"Yes, to earn my bread there. I should like to build a hut, work hard at clearing the land, have everything wild around me, and enjoy a great, wide quiet."

"And not take me with you?" Anna asked, large tears rushing to her eyes.

"How could I?"

"I should like it better than anything. Settlers go with their families. I would sooner go there than stay here in England. I could make the fires, mend the clothes, cook the food; I could learn how to bake the bread before we depart. It would be nicer than anything—it would be like playing at life over again, as we used to do when we made our tent with the drugget and played with our little plates and dishes."

"Father and mother would not let you go."

"Yes, I think they would, if I explained everything properly. It would save money, and Papa would have more to support the boys."

Further conversations of this practical nature followed at intervals, ending with Rex consenting that Anna accompany him, pending his discussion with his father.

Of course, this plan required the rector's attention when he was alone in his study. Their mother, who would eventually reconcile to whatever decision he made, would have found the initial question distressing to hear.

"Well, my children!" said Mr. Gascoigne, cheerfully as they entered. It was a comfort to see Rex moving about again.

"May we sit down with you a little, papa?" said Anna. "Rex has something to say."

"With all my heart."

These three formed a striking group, each possessing a face of identical structural type: the straight brow, the nose abruptly straightened from an intention to be aquiline, the short upper lip, the short but strong and well-hung chin. There was also the same tone of complexion and the same set of the eye. The gray-haired father was both massive and keen-looking; a perpendicular line in his brow deepened whenever he spoke with force or interest, and the habit of ruling bestowed upon him an air of reserved authority. Rex would have seemed a vision of his father's youth, if one could imagine Mr. Gascoigne stripped of his distinct plans and command, smitten with heart-sorrow, and devoid of any notion of concealment other than that of a sick animal. Anna, meanwhile, was a tiny copy of Rex, her hair drawn back and knotted, her face mirroring every shift in his expression, as if they shared a single soul.

"You know all about what has upset me, father," Rex began, and Mr. Gascoigne nodded.

"I am quite done up for life in this part of the world. I am sure it will be useless for me to return to Oxford. I could not possibly do any reading. I should fail, and cause you expense for nothing. I want your consent to pursue another course, sir."

Mr. Gascoigne nodded more slowly; the vertical line on his brow deepened, and Anna's trembling grew.

"If you would allow me a small outfit, I should like to go to the colonies and work on the land there." Rex thought the vagueness of the phrase prudent; "the colonies" necessarily embraced broader advantages and were less susceptible to refutation on a single specific ground than any particular settlement.

"Oh, and with me, papa," said Anna, unable to bear even temporary exclusion from the proposal. "Rex needs someone to take care of him, you know—someone to keep house. And we shall never, either of us, be married. And I should cost nothing, and I should be so happy. I know it would be hard to leave you and Mamma, but there are all the others to raise, and the two of us would be no trouble to you anymore."

Anna had risen from her seat, employing the feminine argument of proximity as she spoke, drawing closer to her father. He did not smile, but he drew her onto his knee and held her there, as if to gently remove her from the equation while he addressed Rex.

"You will admit that my experience gives me some power of judging for you, and that I can probably guide you in practical matters better than you can guide yourself?"

Rex was obliged to say, "Yes, sir."

"And perhaps you will admit—though I do not wish to press that point—that you are bound in duty to consider my judgment and wishes?"

"I have never yet placed myself in opposition to you, sir."
