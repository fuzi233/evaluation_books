As Fanny gazed upon the pair of ladies and the elder's troubled visage, she instinctively recognized the widow Pendennis, her features eerily mirroring those of her son, Arthur, who lay abed with fever. A faint glimmer of recognition sparked within Fanny, yet the faces of Mrs. Pendennis and her companion, Laura, remained as impassive as stone, their countenances etched with hardness and foreboding. The Major, standing behind them, cast a furtive glance towards Arthur's bedside, his eyes veiled beneath half-lowered lids.

"I—wrote to you yesterday, ma'am," Fanny stammered, her limbs trembling as she spoke, her complexion as wan as Laura's.

"Did you, madam?" Mrs. Pendennis replied, her tone devoid of warmth. "I suppose I may now relieve you from nursing my son. I am his mother, you understand."

"Yes, ma'am. This is the path to his—Ah, wait," Fanny exclaimed, her words faltering. "I must prepare you for his—"

The widow's face, previously set in a mask of cruelty, betrayed a fleeting moment of distress as she recoiled with a stifled gasp and cry. "He's been so since yesterday," Fanny continued, her voice quivering.

A cacophony of laughter erupted from Arthur's room, where the door stood ajar, and the young man's delirium reached a crescendo. He began to warble a college drinking song, shouting and thumping his fist against the wainscot as if in the midst of a revelry. Fanny's words tumbled forth in a rush: "He does not know me, ma'am."

"Indeed," Mrs. Pendennis said, her voice cold and detached. "Perhaps he will know his mother; let me pass, if you please, and go in to him." With a swift motion, she pushed past Fanny and into the dark passage leading to Arthur's sitting room, followed by Laura and the Major. Fanny sank onto a nearby bench, overcome with emotion, praying silently as she wept. The fine ladies had offered her no word of gratitude or kindness; their disdain cut her like a knife.
