Mrs. John Dashwood, being as devoted to her husband’s opinion as she was to her own consequence, waited the very next day upon Mrs. Jennings and her daughter. The experiment rewarded her exactly as she had hoped: Mrs. Jennings—though the very woman who sheltered those poor Dashwood girls—proved, upon nearer inspection, not wholly beneath notice; and Lady Middleton, she declared, was one of the most charming women in the world.

Lady Middleton, for her part, was equally taken with Mrs. John Dashwood. There was, on both sides, a cool self-regard—polished, well-bred, and empty as a covered dish that arrives grandly at table and yields nothing but steam—that drew them together at once. They met, they approved, they smiled with the same measured propriety; and each found in the other a gratifying absence of troublesome understanding.

Yet the very manners which made Mrs. John Dashwood so agreeable to Lady Middleton were ill-seasoned to Mrs. Jennings’s taste. To her, Fanny seemed only a small proud-looking woman with an address too stiff to be cordial, who sat down among her husband’s sisters as if affection were an inconvenience and conversation an expense. Of the quarter of an hour she bestowed on Berkeley Street, she passed at least half of it in silence—like a kitchen clock that will not strike though everyone is waiting to hear it.

Elinor, all the while, had one question beating time beneath every civil word and every practiced smile. Was Edward in town? She wished it exceedingly to know, but would not ask. Fanny would not have spoken his name for any persuasion; not until she could announce, with a triumph she had long been airing in imagination, that his marriage with Miss Morton was resolved on, or until John’s expectations from Colonel Brandon were answered. She believed, with that busy certainty which flatters itself into prophecy, that Edward and Elinor were still so attached, they must be divided—divided in word, divided in deed—on every occasion.

But what Fanny withheld, another tongue supplied with eager industry. Lucy Steele came, very shortly, to claim Elinor’s compassion—poor Lucy, who could not see Edward, though he had arrived in town with Mr. and Mrs. Dashwood. He dared not come to Bartlett’s Buildings for fear of detection; and though their mutual impatience was too tender and too guilty to be proclaimed, they could do nothing at present but write.

Edward himself, in his quiet, painful honesty, confirmed his being in town in a way that pierced Elinor more surely than any spoken intelligence. Twice he called in Berkeley Street. Twice his card lay upon the table when they returned from their morning engagements—small, stiff, and mute as a folded napkin, set down where it ought not to be and yet cannot be ignored. Elinor was pleased that he had called; and still more pleased that she had missed him.

The Dashwoods, prodigiously delighted with the Middletons, determined—though not much in the habit of giving anything—to give them a dinner. They had taken, for three months, a very good house in Harley Street; and thither they invited Lady Middleton and her husband, Mrs. Jennings, and their sisters. John Dashwood, careful as a man can be when he means to profit by his civility, secured Colonel Brandon also. The Colonel, always glad to be where the Miss Dashwoods were, received this eager attention with some surprise and more pleasure.

They were to meet Mrs. Ferrars. Elinor could not learn whether her sons were to be of the party; but the expectation of seeing Edward’s mother was enough to fix her mind upon the engagement. Once, such an introduction would have filled her with dread; now, though the old curiosity still pricked her—what was Mrs. Ferrars like?—she could anticipate meeting her with an indifference as to her opinion of herself, which felt almost like a strange new language upon Elinor’s tongue.

That interest, however, was soon increased—more strongly than pleasantly—by hearing that the Miss Steeles were also to be of the party. They had recommended themselves to Lady Middleton with that patient assiduity which clings like perfume: not always delicate, not always natural, but persistent. Lucy was certainly not elegant, and her sister not even genteel; yet Lady Middleton, as ready as Sir John to be surrounded by those who would admire her arrangements, asked them to spend a week or two in Conduit Street. It happened to be particularly convenient to the Miss Steeles—particularly convenient, indeed—that their visit should begin a few days before the party took place.

Their claims to Mrs. John Dashwood’s notice, as nieces of the gentleman who for many years had had the care of her brother, might not have carried them far toward her table; but as Lady Middleton’s guests they must be received. And Lucy, who had long wanted to be personally known to Edward’s family—to have a nearer view of their tempers, their habits, and her own difficulties, and to find the tender places where she might press—had seldom been happier than on receiving Mrs. John Dashwood’s card. She took it almost as one might take a place at a great table: not merely a seat, but an opening.

On Elinor the effect was very different. She began immediately to determine that Edward, who lived with his mother, must be asked as his mother was, to a party given by his sister; and to see him for the first time, after all that had passed, in the company of Lucy—Lucy beside him, Lucy smiling, Lucy certain—she hardly knew how she could bear it.

These apprehensions were not founded entirely on reason, and certainly not at all on truth. They were relieved, however, not by Elinor’s own recollection, but by Lucy’s good will; for Lucy, believing herself to be inflicting a severe disappointment, told her that Edward certainly would not be in Harley Street on Tuesday. She even hoped to sharpen the wound by persuading her that he was kept away by the extreme affection for herself, which he could not conceal when they were together—an insinuation delivered with that careful sweetness which is meant to be tasted and remembered.

The important Tuesday came.

“Pity me, dear Miss Dashwood!” said Lucy, as they walked up the stairs together; for the Middletons arrived so directly after Mrs. Jennings that they all followed the servant at the same time, their footsteps and the rustle of their clothes making a kind of domestic overture in the narrow passage. “There is nobody here but you that can feel for me. I declare I can hardly stand. Good gracious! In a moment I shall see the person that all my happiness depends on—that is to be my mother!”

Elinor could have given her immediate relief by suggesting the possibility of its being Miss Morton’s mother, rather than her own, whom they were about to behold; but instead of doing that, she assured her—and with great sincerity—that she did pity her. Lucy, though really uncomfortable herself, had at least hoped to be an object of irrepressible envy; and Elinor’s pity astonished her more than any coldness could have done.

Mrs. Ferrars was a little, thin woman, upright even to formality, in her figure, and serious even to sourness, in her aspect. Her complexion was sallow; her features small, without beauty, and naturally without expression; but a lucky contraction of the brow rescued her countenance from the disgrace of insipidity by giving it the strong characters of pride and ill nature. She was not a woman of many words; unlike people in general, she proportioned her speech to the number of her ideas. Of the few syllables that escaped her, not one fell to the share of Miss Dashwood, whom she eyed with the spirited determination of disliking her at all events—as if dislike were an heirloom, handed down with the silver and the name, and requiring only a proper object to keep it bright.

Elinor could not now be made unhappy by such behaviour. A few months ago it would have hurt her exceedingly; but it was not in Mrs. Ferrars’s power to distress her by it now. The difference of her manners to the Miss Steeles—a difference which seemed purposely made to humble Elinor more—only amused her. She could not but smile to see the graciousness of both mother and daughter toward the very person—Lucy was particularly distinguished—whom, of all others, had they known as much as Elinor did, they would have been most anxious to mortify; while she herself, who had comparatively no power to wound them, sat pointedly slighted by both.

But while she smiled at a graciousness so misapplied, she could not reflect on the mean-spirited folly from which it sprang, nor observe the studied attentions with which the Miss Steeles courted its continuance, without thoroughly despising them all four. Lucy was all exultation on being so honorably noticed; and Miss Steele wanted only to be teased about Dr. Davies to be perfectly happy.

Dinner was a grand one. The servants were numerous; the plate shone; every course arrived with a practiced punctuality; and everything bespoke the mistress’s inclination for show and the master’s ability to support it. In spite of the improvements and additions making to the Norland estate—and in spite of its owner having once been within some thousand pounds of being obliged to sell out at a loss—nothing in Harley Street gave the least symptom of that indigence which John Dashwood had tried to infer from it. No poverty appeared—except of conversation; and there the deficiency was considerable. John Dashwood had not much to say for himself that was worth hearing, and his wife still less; but there was no peculiar disgrace in this, for it was much the case with the chief of their visitors, who almost all laboured under one or other disqualification for being agreeable: want of sense, either natural or improved; want of elegance; want of spirits; or want of temper.

When the ladies withdrew to the drawing-room after dinner, this poverty was particularly evident; for the gentlemen had supplied the discourse with some variety—the variety of politics, inclosing land, and breaking horses—but then it was all over. One subject only engaged the ladies till coffee came in: the comparative heights of Harry Dashwood and Lady Middleton’s second son William, who were nearly of the same age.

Had both the children been there, the question might have been determined too easily by measuring them at once; but as Harry only was present, all was conjectural assertion on both sides. Everyone had a right to be equally positive, and to repeat that positiveness as often as it pleased them—until the very air of the room seemed filled with the thin, tapping sound of opinions being set down and set down again, like spoons on china.

The parties stood thus: the two mothers, though each really convinced her own son the tallest, politely decided in favour of the other. The two grandmothers, with not less partiality but more sincerity, were equally earnest for their own descendant. Lucy, hardly less anxious to please one parent than the other, thought both boys remarkably tall for their age, and could not conceive that there could be the smallest difference in the world between them. Miss Steele, with yet greater address, gave her opinion as fast as she could in favour of each.

Elinor, having once delivered hers on William’s side—by which she offended Mrs. Ferrars and Fanny still more—did not see the necessity of enforcing it by any farther assertion. Marianne, when called upon, offended them all by declaring she had no opinion to give, as she had never thought about it.

Before removing from Norland, Elinor had painted a very pretty pair of screens for her sister-in-law. They were now just mounted and brought home, and ornamented the present drawing-room. These screens caught John Dashwood’s eye as he followed the other gentlemen in, and with the officiousness of a man eager to display his connexions, he handed them to Colonel Brandon for admiration.

“These are done by my eldest sister,” said he. “And you, as a man of taste, will, I dare say, be pleased with them. I do not know whether you have ever happened to see any of her performances before, but she is in general reckoned to draw extremely well.”

The Colonel, disclaiming all pretensions to connoisseurship, warmly admired the screens—as he would have admired anything painted by Miss Dashwood; and as curiosity was of course excited, they were handed round.

Mrs. Ferrars, not aware of their being Elinor’s work, particularly requested to look at them; and after they had received gratifying testimony of Lady Middleton’s approbation, Fanny presented them to her mother, considerately informing her at the same time that they were done by Miss Dashwood.

“Hum,” said Mrs. Ferrars. “Very pretty.” And without regarding them at all, returned them to her daughter.

Perhaps Fanny thought for a moment that her mother had been quite rude enough—for colouring a little, she immediately said, “They are very pretty, ma’am—an’t they?” But then, the dread of having been too civil—too encouraging—came over her, for she presently added, “Do you not think they are something in Miss Morton’s style of painting, ma’am? She does paint most delightfully! How beautifully her last landscape is done!”

“Beautifully indeed! But she does every thing well.”

Marianne could not bear this. She was already greatly displeased with Mrs. Ferrars; and such ill-timed praise of another at Elinor’s expense—though she had not any notion of what was principally meant by it—provoked her immediately to say, with warmth, “This is admiration of a very particular kind! What is Miss Morton to us? Who knows, or who cares, for her? It is Elinor of whom we think and speak.” And so saying, she took the screens out of her sister-in-law’s hands, to admire them herself as they ought to be admired.

Mrs. Ferrars looked exceedingly angry; and drawing herself up more stiffly than ever, pronounced, in retort, this bitter philippic: “Miss Morton is Lord Morton’s daughter.”

Fanny looked very angry too; her husband was all in a fright at his sister’s audacity. Elinor was much more hurt by Marianne’s warmth than she had been by what produced it; but Colonel Brandon’s eyes, fixed on Marianne, declared that he noticed only what was amiable in it—the affectionate heart which could not bear to see a sister slighted in the smallest point.

Marianne’s feelings did not stop there. The cold insolence of Mrs. Ferrars’s general behaviour to Elinor seemed, to her, to foretell such difficulties and distresses as her own wounded heart taught her to think of with horror. Urged by a strong impulse of affectionate sensibility, she moved, after a moment, to Elinor’s chair; and putting one arm round her neck and one cheek close to hers, said in a low but eager voice, “Dear, dear Elinor, don’t mind them. Don’t let them make you unhappy.”

She could say no more. Her spirits were quite overcome; and hiding her face on Elinor’s shoulder, she burst into tears.

Everybody’s attention was called; almost everybody was concerned. Colonel Brandon rose and went to them without knowing what he did. Mrs. Jennings, with a very intelligent “Ah! poor dear,” immediately gave her her salts. Sir John felt so desperately enraged against the author of this nervous distress that he instantly changed his seat to one close by Lucy Steele, and gave her in a whisper a brief account of the whole shocking affair—as if whispering could soften its harshness, or as if the sound of indignation, kept low, were less injurious to the room.

In a few minutes Marianne was recovered enough to put an end to the bustle and sit down among the rest; though her spirits retained the impression of what had passed the whole evening, as a room retains the smell of spilt wine long after the cloth is changed.

“Poor Marianne!” said her brother to Colonel Brandon, in a low voice, as soon as he could secure his attention. “She has not such good health as her sister,—she is very nervous,—she has not Elinor’s constitution;—and one must allow that there is something very trying to a young woman who has been a beauty in the loss of her personal attractions. You would not think it perhaps, but Marianne was remarkably handsome a few months ago; quite as handsome as Elinor. Now you see it is all gone.”
