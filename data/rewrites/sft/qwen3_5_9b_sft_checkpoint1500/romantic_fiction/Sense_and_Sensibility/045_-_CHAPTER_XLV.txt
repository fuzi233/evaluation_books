For a considerable time after Willoughby's departure, and long after the rumble of his carriage had faded into silence, Elinor lay under a heavy burden of conflicting thoughts. While these ideas varied wildly in nature, they converged upon a single, prevailing emotion: sorrow. Though her sister, Marianne, had awakened refreshed from a deep slumber, Elinor's heart swelled with an agitation that kept the weariness of the ordeal at bay. Her mind raced with the past, the precarious present, and a future fraught with uncertainty regarding Willoughby's fate, Marianne's health, and her mother's imminent return. She feared revealing her true feelings to Marianne, lest her sister be shaken by the news of the visitor who had just left.

However, scarcely had half an hour elapsed since Willoughby's exit when the sound of another carriage wheels announced a second arrival. Driven by a desperate need to spare her mother further torment, Elinor rushed into the hall and reached the outer door just as it opened. She stepped forward to catch and steady Mrs. Dashwood before she could collapse.

Mrs. Dashwood, whose terror as they approached the house had nearly convinced her that Marianne was lost, possessed no voice to inquire for her child or for Elinor. Instead, the moment she crossed the threshold, Elinor, awaiting no greeting, burst forth with the joyful relief. This news struck her mother like a balm to a wound; the woman who had moments before been consumed by dread was instantly overwhelmed by a flood of happiness. Supported between her daughter and Colonel Brandon, she was ushered into the drawing-room, where she wept tears of deliverance. Though speech failed her, her embrace of Elinor was fervent, and she pressed Colonel Brandon's hand with a look that spoke volumes of gratitude and a belief that he shared in her profound bliss. He, however, offered a response of profound silence, his composure perhaps even deeper than her own.

Once Mrs. Dashwood regained her composure, her singular desire was to see Marianne. In two minutes, she stood beside her beloved child, rendered dearer to her heart by the trials of absence, unhappiness, and danger. Elinor watched the reunion with delight, though she held back from expressing it openly, fearing it might disturb Marianne's fragile rest. Yet, when the crisis of life hung in the balance, Mrs. Dashwood could summon a prudence usually absent in her nature. Marianne, content to know her mother was near, submitted willingly to the enforced silence around her, too weak for conversation. Mrs. Dashwood resolved to stay by her bedside all night; Elinor, yielding to her mother's entreaty, retired to her chamber.

But the necessary rest, born of a night spent without sleep and hours of agonizing anxiety, eluded her. "Poor Willoughby" haunted her thoughts. For the first time, she entertained a notion of his defense, oscillating between blaming herself for her harsh initial judgment and acquitting him. Yet, the promise she had made to relate the truth to her sister remained a source of pain. She dreaded the revelation, fearing it might shatter Marianne's newfound peace, doubting whether her sister could ever find happiness with anyone else, and momentarily wishing Willoughby were a widower. Then, the thought of Colonel Brandon anchored her; she reproached herself, recognizing that the debt of her sister's restoration was owed to Brandon's suffering and constancy, not to his rival's. She prayed above all for Mrs. Willoughby's survival.

The shock of Colonel Brandon's mission at Barton had been significantly softened for Mrs. Dashwood by her own prior alarm. So intense had been her uneasiness regarding Marianne that she had already decided to depart for Cleveland on that day, settling her journey before Brandon's arrival, with the Careys expecting to take Margaret away at any moment due to fears of infection.

Marianne improved daily, and the radiant cheerfulness of Mrs. Dashwood's countenance declared her, as she repeatedly proclaimed, to be one of the happiest women alive. Elinor listened to these declarations, witnessing the proof, with a lingering sense of wonder whether her mother truly remembered Edward Ferrars. Trusting in the temperate account Elinor had sent, Mrs. Dashwood was carried away by the exuberance of her joy, focusing solely on the blessings surrounding her. Marianne had been snatched from a perilous danger, one in which Mrs. Dashwood now felt her own misguided judgment in fostering the attachment to Willoughby had played a role. Yet, in this recovery, she discovered another source of joy, unknown to Elinor, which was revealed the moment privacy was secured.

"At last we are alone," Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed, turning to her daughter. "My Elinor, you do not yet know the full measure of my happiness. Colonel Brandon loves Marianne. He has told me so himself."

Elinor, feeling a complex mixture of pleasure and pain, surprise and lack thereof, fell silent, offering only attentive stillness.

"You are never like me, dear Elinor, or I should marvel at your composure now. If I had sat down to wish for any possible good for my family, I would have fixed upon Colonel Brandon marrying one of you as the most desirable outcome. And I believe Marianne will be the happier of the two."

Elinor was half-inclined to question the basis of her mother's assertion, sensing that no argument grounded in an impartial consideration of their ages, characters, or feelings could possibly hold water. However, knowing how easily her mother's imagination was swept away by any subject of interest, she chose to pass the matter over with a gentle smile rather than an inquiry.

"He opened his whole heart to me yesterday as we traveled," Mrs. Dashwood continued, her voice filled with emotion. "It came out quite unawares, quite undesignedly. I talked of nothing but my child; he could not conceal his distress. I saw that it equaled my own. Perhaps, thinking that mere friendship, as the world goes, would not justify such warm sympathy—or simply giving way to irresistible feelings—he made me acquainted with his earnest, tender, constant affection for Marianne. He has loved her, my Elinor, ever since the first moment he saw her."

Here, Elinor perceived clearly that she was hearing not the raw language or professions of Colonel Brandon, but the natural embellishments of her mother's active fancy, fashioning every detail into something delightful as it chose.

"His regard for her," Mrs. Dashwood raged on, "infinitely surpasses anything Willoughby ever felt or feigned. It is as much warmer as it is more sincere, or more constant—which ever we are to call it—it has subsisted through all knowledge of dear Marianne's unhappy prepossession for that worthless young man! Without selfishness! Without encouraging a hope! Could he have seen her happy with another? Such a noble mind! Such openness, such sincerity! No one can be deceived in him."

"Colonel Brandon's character," said Elinor, gently, "as an excellent man, is well established."

"I know it is," replied her mother seriously, "or after such a warning, I should be the last to encourage such affection, or even to be pleased by it. But his coming for me as he did, with such active, such ready friendship, is enough to prove him one of the worthiest of men."

"His character, however," answered Elinor, "does not rest on *one* act of kindness, to which his affection for Marianne, were humanity out of the case, would have prompted him. To Mrs. Jennings, to the Middletons, he has been long and intimately known; they equally love and respect him. Even my own knowledge of him, though lately acquired, is very considerable. And so highly do I value and esteem him that, if Marianne can be happy with him, I shall be as ready as yourself to think our connection the greatest blessing to us in the world. What answer did you give him? Did you allow him to hope?"

"Oh! my love, I could not then talk of hope to him or to myself," Mrs. Dashwood explained, her eyes shining. "Marianne might at that moment be dying. But he did not ask for hope or encouragement. His was an involuntary confidence, an irrepressible effusion to a soothing friend, not an application to a parent. Yet, after a time, I *did* say—for at first I was quite overcome—that if she lived, as I trusted she might, my greatest happiness would lie in promoting their marriage. And since our arrival, since our delightful security, I have repeated it to him more fully, giving him every encouragement in my power. Time, a very little time, I tell him, will do everything; Marianne's heart is not to be wasted forever on such a man as Willoughby. His own merits must soon secure it."

"To judge from the Colonel's spirits, however, you have not yet made him equally sanguine," Elinor observed.

"No," admitted her mother. "He thinks Marianne's affection is too deeply rooted for any change, even under a great length of time.
