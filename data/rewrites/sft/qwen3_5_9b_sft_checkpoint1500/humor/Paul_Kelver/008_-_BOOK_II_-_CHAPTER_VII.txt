A proverb whispered by a giant, "Better a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith," hung over our table like a damp, unsmiling banner. For weeks, the feast had been a mechanical ritual: joints arrived hot and heavy, puddings accumulated in a monotonous rhythm, and the joy-giving luxuries of life had evaporated, replaced by the tedious inventory of uninteresting needs. We ate without stint, freed from the tyranny of saving for tomorrow's hash, yet the silence at our board was absolute. A silent, unseen guest sat with us, a shadow that had swallowed the laughter over sausages and the teasing over herrings, leaving us to converse of empty things that lay outside us.

It would have been easy to move to Guilford Street. My parents occasionally discussed it in spirits tones, but every conversation collapsed under the weight of a loosened thought, stirring up anger and sending the aching months drifting forward until the cloud grew heavier.

Then, the news arrived that old Teidelmann had died suddenly in his counting-house.

"You are going to her?" my mother asked.

"I have been sent for," said my father. "I must—it may mean business."

My mother laughed bitterly—a sound I could not fathom—and my father flung out of the house. While he was away, my mother locked herself in her room. Stealing to the door, I was certain I heard her weeping. To grieve so deeply for old Teidelmann baffled me, though the atmosphere in the house shifted irrevocably.

Afterward, she visited us more often. Her mourning seemed to soften the hardness of her eyes, lending her a fragile beauty. She was sweetness incarnate, offering trifling gifts and schemes for childish treats, intuitively knowing what delighted me—books and pleasures my mother, in her stern vision of "Must" and "Must not," had denied me. Where my mother chilled me with the vista of a narrow, viewless road, Mrs. Teidelmann painted life as sunny fields and winding paths of Will. Though a fear lurked beneath my thoughts, something within me, uncontrollable and drawn by her subtle sympathy, reached out to her.

One morning, looking at me with a whimsical screw of her mouth, she asked my father, "Has he ever seen a pantomime?"

My heart leaped. My father raised his eyebrows. "What would your mother say, do you think?" he asked. My heart sank.

"She thinks," I replied, "that theatres are very wicked places." It was the first time I had ever doubted the correctness of my mother's judgments.

Mrs. Teidelmann's smile fortified the doubt. "Dear me," she said, "I am afraid I must be very wicked. I have always regarded a pantomime as quite a moral entertainment. All the bad people go down so very straight to—the fit and proper place for them. And we could promise to leave before the Clown stole the sausages, couldn't we, Paul?"

My mother appeared, looking insignificant in her pale face and plain dark frock beside the shining lady in her rustling clothes. Standing silently, clasping and unclasping her hands in a childish trick of distress, she trembled.

"You will let him come, Mrs. Kelver," Mrs. Teidelmann pleaded in soft, caressing tones. "It's *Dick Whittington*, you know—an excellent moral."

"I am very sorry," said my mother, her voice sounding harsh and grating. "It is very kind of you. But I would rather he did not go."

"Just this once!" persisted Mrs. Teidelmann. "It is holiday time."

A ray of sunlight fell upon her coaxing face, making where my mother stood seem like shadow.

"When he is older others must judge for him," my mother insisted, "but for the present he must be guided by me—alone."

"It really doesn't seem to me there could be any harm," urged my father. "Things have changed since we were young."

"That may be," answered my mother, "but it is long ago since then."

"We will say no more about it," said Mrs. Teidelmann sweetly. "I merely thought it would give him pleasure; and he has worked so hard this last term."

She laid her hand caressingly on my shoulder, drawing me closer, and it remained there. "It was very kind of you," said my mother, tears welling. "I would do anything to give him pleasure... He understands." Her hand sought mine, but I was angry and turned away. Without another word, she left the room.

That afternoon, she took me to a hall in the neighborhood where we saw a magic lantern and a conjurer. She wore a prettier frock, brighter and gayer than she had in many days, laughing and talking merrily. But I, nursing my wrongs, remained moody and sulky. The wonders of the great theatre I had dreamed of from posters floated vaguely before me. Neither the open-mouthed sleeper swallowing chains of rats nor the live rabbit in the stout gentleman's hat could divert my mind from the joy I had glimpsed only to lose.

Walking home through muddy, darkening streets, I woke—or rather half-waking—as children do, seeing a figure in white crouching at the foot of my bed. Frightened, I cried out, and my mother rose from her knees.

"It was a silly dream," she explained with a curious, broken laugh. "I must have been thinking of the conjurer. I dreamt a wicked Magician spirited you away from me. I could not find you and was all alone in the world."

She put her arms around me, tight as almost to hurt, and we stayed thus until sleep claimed me again.

Towards the close of the holidays, my mother and I visited Mrs. Teidelmann at her great stone-built house in Clapton. A note had announced terrible headaches and impending departure from England. Inside, the house was a cavern of treasures, a chaotic heap of pictures, statues, and curiosities collected by the late Mr. Teidelmann, contrasting sharply with the shrivelled, uninteresting corpse in the coffin.

Mrs. Teidelmann lay asleep on a sofa, dressed in a loose lace robe revealing thin, snow-white arms. As we entered, the room fell silent, save for the muffled ticking of a great French clock. After a few restless movements, she began to murmur, whispering my father's name clearly in the quiet: "Ah no, we must not. Luke, my darling."

My mother rose swiftly, speaking in matter-of-fact tones. "Go, Paul," she said, pushing me gently out and closing the door.

Half an hour later, she returned, walking as one in a dream, eyes seeing nothing. At the gate, she snapped back to life. "You must say nothing, Paul," she commanded. "Delirious people use strange words. Never breathe a word."

I promised, and from that day, her manner changed entirely. Not another angry word escaped her lips, no angry flash lit her eyes. Mrs. Teidelmann remained away for three months, during which time my father managed her affairs, though my mother also wrote long letters in the twilight, a secret vigil near the window. When she returned, they were instantly reconciled, appearing as the closest of friends. Peace returned to the house; my father became tender again, and my mother thoughtful and soft-spoken. Yet, despite this restoration, every day my mother grew feebler, losing the little ways that had once been hers.

Summer came, with its panting heat and stifling nights, and my mother moved about the house ever more listlessly. Dr. Hal suggested the air was the problem, and eventually, we packed for a holiday to the village where old Jacob's ruined tower stood on the headland.

There, the village folk had aged or transformed. Mrs. Fursey, once bent on suppressing me, now boasted of her triumphs with me as a "natural gift." Anna, a buxom wife, kissed me with uncontrollable fervor, requiring constant vigilance to escape. Old Chumbley cobbled shoes, his bench a stage for the comical complaints of his boots, while Tom Pinfold scratched his head over a rejected fish that seemed to hang eternally from his hand.

One afternoon, nearing the end of our stay, my mother and I climbed to the top platform of the tower. Leaning on the parapet, she smiled dreamily. "Oh, only of things that happened over there," she said, nodding toward the distant hills. "When I was a girl."

"You lived there, long ago, didn't you?" I asked.

"You're very rude," she replied, a tone of her old self ringing briefly. "It was a very little while ago."

Suddenly, she raised her head, listening. A faint, long-drawn "Coo-ee" drifted from the woods. We ran to the side of the tower overlooking the path, and moments later, my father emerged into the sunlight. His gallant bearing, the spring of his step, and the light in his eyes signaled that trouble had come upon him. He always rose to meet it with that look.

Breathing a little hard, he laughed as he stood beside my mother, gazing at the distant hills where their memories seemed to meet.

"Do you remember," said my father, "we climbed up here—the first walk we took together after coming here? We discussed our plans for the future."
