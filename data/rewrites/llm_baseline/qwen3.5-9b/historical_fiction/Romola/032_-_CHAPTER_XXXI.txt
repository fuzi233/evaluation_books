"My dear Romola," Tito said on the second morning following his impassioned speech in the Piazza del Duomo, "I shall be receiving distinguished guests today. The Count from Milan returns, accompanied by the Seneschal de Beaucaire, the favorite of the King of France. I know you have no inclination to endure the smiling formalities required by such rustling magnates, figures we are unlikely to encounter again. Since they intend to inspect our antiquities and library, perhaps you should set aside your work for the day and accompany your cousin Brigida."

Romola perceived the underlying wish in this suggestion and immediately agreed. Yet, moments later, returning to the room wrapped in her hood and mantle, she remarked, "Oh, what a long breath Florence will draw when the gates swing wide and the last French soldier walks out! Even you seem weary, Tito, despite your patience; confess it. Ah, your brow feels warm."

Tito was leaning over his desk, his pen moving steadily across the parchment. She had reached out to lay her hand upon his head, intending a final caress. This posture was familiar to him; usually, when he felt her touch, he would lift his head, lean back slightly, and gaze upward at her. But now, he found himself as incapable of raising his head as if her hand were a heavy, leaden cowl resting upon him. Instead, speaking in a light, casual tone while his pen continued its journey, he replied, "The French are as eager to depart from Florence as wasps leaving a ripe pear once they have finished feeding."

Sensing sharply the absence of his customary emotional resonance, Romola withdrew her hand and declared, "I am going, Tito."

"Farewell, my sweet one. I must remain here. Take Maso with you."

Still, Tito did not look up. Romola departed without further word. In the quiet rhythm of marriage, even the slightest shifts mark epochs. That morning, for the first time, she acknowledged within herself not merely that Tito had changed, but that he had altered specifically toward her. Could the fault lie with her? She might have suspected so had it not been for the tangible evidence of the armor and the painting—facts suggesting an external catastrophe entirely mysterious to her.

The moment Romola stepped outside, however, Tito lowered his pen and lifted his gaze, finding a delightful security in facing only parchment and broken marble. A profound disgust rose within him at his failure to look up and treat her as he always had. If he could choose, he would strive to be kinder than ever; yet he could not suddenly master an involuntary shrinking from her presence. This retreat stemmed paradoxically from the very traits that drove him to desire constant displays of affection.

He stood poised to take a step certain to provoke her deepest indignation—a path fraught with unpleasantness that he would have to traverse to earn her forgiveness. Tito possessed a nature utterly devoid of defiance or impudence; every instinct leaned toward preserving her tenderness. He was not plagued by sentimental scruples, which he had quickly dismissed as irrelevant to practical utility. Yet, his lack of scruples did not shield him from the dread of discomfort. Unscrupulousness removes many burdens, but not toothaches, wounded vanity, or the crushing sense of loneliness. Against these, the world offers no defense save a sound jaw and a just, loving soul. At this precise moment, Tito felt acutely that no strategy could spare him the pain of the inevitable collision with Romola; no smooth persuasion could soften the blow driving him forward like a timid beast urged toward a desperate leap by the terror of teeth and claws closing in.

A secret conviction had long held that his stubborn adherence to Bardo's wishes regarding the library had devolved into sentimental folly, robbing both himself and Romola of vital advantages. Perhaps this realization would never have crystallized into action without the events of the past week, which introduced a pressing new motive alongside a rare opportunity. Indeed, it was only after his dread intensified upon seeing Baldassarre regain his composure—and only after he began to fear he might be forced to flee Florence—that he resolved to exercise his legal right to sell the library before the chance presented by the French and Milanese bidders vanished. For if exile became necessary, he refused to depart as a destitute wanderer. Accustomed to an agreeable existence, he sought to retain the means for maintaining it. Among his desires was to carry Romola with him, and, if possible, to carry no infamy. Success had awakened in him an appetite for the pleasures afforded by advantageous social standing. To decamp under dishonor, even laden with diamonds, appeared to him not as a temptation, but as a hideous alternative, inviting the life of an adventurer.

He could not achieve independence even among those Florentines who treated him with mere respect, let alone with Romola. She was the wife of his first love; he still loved her. She belonged to the essential furniture of his life, from which he shrank at the thought of separation. He winced under her judgment, unsure how far her revulsion might extend. All the power a husband typically wields over a wife—the power that drives men to betrayals a lover would never dare—proved insufficient to quell Tito's unease. This was the leaden weight that had overwhelmed his will, preventing him from lifting his head to meet her eyes. Her pure light illuminated too clearly the prospect of an approaching struggle.

Yet, there was no help for it. If they were to leave Florence, they needed money. Indeed, Tito could not arrange his life to his satisfaction without a considerable sum. This obsession with securing financial stability had been the root of all his transgressions. He would have been capable of any sacrifice that did not involve personal discomfort.

The distinguished visitors arrived and departed; the bargains were struck. When Romola returned home that evening, nothing grave was spoken. Tito was merely gay and chatty, pouring out stories and descriptions of the French visit with a liveliness Romola had not seen before. She sensed an effort in his cheerfulness, interpreting it as a sign of contrition born of knowing he had wounded her earlier. Internally, she ached at this visible widening of the distance between them—that there existed an offense neither dared to name.

The following day, Tito stayed away until late at night. It proved to be a significant day for Romola, for Piero di Cosimo, spurred by guilt over potentially causing her pain in the previous week, had dispatched her father's portrait. She had propped it against the back of his old chair and gazed at it for some time when the door opened behind her, revealing Bernardo del Nero.

"It is you, godfather! How I wish you had come sooner! It is already growing dusk," Romola said, moving toward him.

"I stopped by simply to share good news, knowing Tito had not yet returned," Bernardo replied. "The French King departs tomorrow; it is finally high time. There was another skirmish this morning between our citizens and his soldiers, but now there is hope the city will restore order and trade resume."

"That is joyful," Romola responded. "But it seems sudden. Tito seemed to believe yesterday there was little prospect of the King leaving soon."

"He has been well barked at, that is the reason," Bernardo smiled. "His own generals opened their throats quite effectively, and eventually, our Signoria unleashed the city's mastiff, Fra Girolamo. The Cristianissimo was frightened by that thunderclap and issued orders to move. I fear there will be little unity among us once he is gone, but at least all parties are relieved not to have Florence stifled by soldiery anymore. The Friar barked successfully this time."

"Ah, what is this?" Bernardo asked, noticing Romola leading him before the portrait, her arm around his. "Let us see."

He began uncoiling his long scarf as she arranged a seat for him. "Do you not want your spectacles, godfather?" Romola asked anxiously, wishing he might see exactly what she saw.

"No, child, no," Bernardo replied, uncovering his grey head as he sat with firm erectness. "For viewing at this distance, my old eyes are perhaps superior to yours. An old man's eyes are like an old man's memory; they are strongest for things far away."

"It is better than having no portrait at all," Romola said apologetically after a brief silence. "It resembles him less than the image I hold in my mind, though that image may fade with the years."

She rested her arm on the old man's shoulder, drawn to him by their shared reverence for the dead. "I do not know," Bernardo mused. "Almost, I think I see Bardo as he was in his youth more clearly than this picture shows him in his old age. Your father possessed great fire in his eyes when young. It was something I could never fully grasp—that he, with such an impatient spirit, could dedicate himself to books and dwell among shadows his whole life. Nevertheless, he poured his heart into it."

Bernardo gave a slight shrug at the end, but Romola detected in his voice a sentiment resonating with her own. "And he was disappointed to the very end," she said involuntarily. Immediately fearing her words might sound like an accusation against Tito, she hastened to add, "If only we could witness his longest, dearest wish fulfilled precisely according to his mind!"
