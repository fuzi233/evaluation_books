“Well, Miss Dashwood,” cried Mrs. Jennings the moment the gentleman’s step had died upon the stair, and the door had settled back into its frame, “I don’t ask what the Colonel has been saying, for though I protest I tried to keep my ears on my own side of the room, words will travel as they please; and I heard enough to know his meaning. And I declare I never was better pleased in my life. I wish you joy of it with all my heart.”

“Thank you, ma’am,” said Elinor. Her voice was steady, but there was in her composure the strain of a string drawn too tight. “It is, indeed, a matter of joy to me; and I feel Colonel Brandon’s goodness most sensibly. There are not many men who would act as he has done—few with so compassionate a heart. I never was more astonished.”

“Lord, my dear, modest as ever! Astonished? I am not astonished a bit. I have been thinking this good while there was nothing more likely.”

“You judged from his general benevolence,” Elinor returned; “but you could not foresee that the opportunity would occur so soon.”

“Opportunity!” repeated Mrs. Jennings, as if the word were a spice she did not mean to be cheated of. “When a man has once made up his mind, somehow or other he will find the opportunity, as sure as cook finds a hot corner in a cold hearth. Well—joy to you again and again; and if ever there was to be a happy couple, I shall soon know where to look.”

“You mean to go to Delaford after them, I suppose,” said Elinor, forcing a faint smile.

“Aye, that I do indeed. And as to the house being bad, I don’t know what the Colonel can be at. It’s as good a one as ever I saw.”

“He spoke of its being out of repair.”

“Well, and whose fault is that? Why doesn’t he repair it? Who should do it but himself?”

At that instant the servant entered to announce the carriage at the door. Mrs. Jennings, already gathering her gloves as if they were part of her very argument, began to bustle out with a final gust of words.

“Well, my dear, I must be gone before I have had half my talk out; but we shall be quite alone this evening, and then I’ll have it all. I don’t ask you to go with me; I dare say your mind’s too full for company—and besides, you must be longing to tell your sister.”

Marianne had slipped away before the talk began; her departure had been as quiet as a kettle removed from the hob before it sings.

“Certainly, ma’am, I shall tell Marianne of it,” Elinor said; “but I shall not mention it at present to anybody else.”

“Oh!—very well,” returned Mrs. Jennings, visibly balked, like a hostess told she must not set out the sweetmeats. “Then you would not have me tell it to Lucy? For I think of going as far as Holborn to-day.”

“No, ma’am—not even Lucy, if you please. One day’s delay will not be very material; and till I have written to Mr. Ferrars, I think it ought not to be mentioned to any body else. I shall do that directly. It is of importance that no time should be lost with him, for he will of course have much to do relative to his ordination.”

That last word—ordination—fell upon Mrs. Jennings’s understanding like a dropped ladle: it made a sound, but she did not immediately know what it meant for the whole dinner. She stared, reflected, and then brightened with the sudden triumph of a solution.

“Oh, ho! I understand you. Mr. Ferrars is to be the man. Well, so much the better for him. Ay, to be sure, he must be ordained in readiness; and I am very glad to find things are so forward between you. But, my dear, is not this rather out of character? Should not the Colonel write himself?—sure he is the proper person.”

Elinor, not comprehending the beginning and refusing—by long habit—to chase every flying notion of Mrs. Jennings’s, answered only the close.

“Colonel Brandon is so delicate a man, that he rather wished any one to announce his intentions to Mr. Ferrars than himself.”

“And so you are forced to do it. Well, that is an odd kind of delicacy! However, I won’t disturb you.” She saw Elinor draw paper to her and take up the pen as if it were a knife that must make one clean cut and no more. “You know your own concerns best. So good-bye, my dear. I have not heard of any thing to please me so well since Charlotte was brought to bed.”

She went; but returned again almost directly, her mind unable to quit the domestic train of thought that always ran alongside her grandest conclusions.

“I have just been thinking of Betty’s sister, my dear. I should be very glad to get her so good a mistress. But whether she would do for a lady’s maid, I can’t tell. She’s an excellent housemaid, and works very well at her needle. However, you will think of all that at your leisure.”

“Certainly, ma’am,” Elinor answered—though she heard little. She wanted solitude, not servants.

When the door closed upon Mrs. Jennings at last, the house seemed to change its tone. London was never truly silent; there was always the far-off grind of wheels, the clipped cry of a vendor, a hammer somewhere that would not be reasoned with, and, nearer at hand, the low shifting of a coal in the grate. Yet for Elinor the room had the hush of a pantry at dawn, when all is laid out and the day’s labour is waiting to begin.

How should she begin? How say enough, and not too much? The very plainness of what she had to do made it difficult; certain duties are like simple dishes that demand more care than feasts, because there is no abundance of seasoning to hide a mistake. She sat with her pen poised, as if the ink might betray her heart in a blot, deliberating over a blank sheet until she was broken in upon by the entrance of Edward himself.

He had met Mrs. Jennings at the door, as she hurried to her carriage; and she, apologising for not returning his farewell card, had nevertheless almost pushed him within, declaring that Miss Dashwood was above, and wanted to speak to him on very particular business.

Elinor had been congratulating herself that, however hard it was to shape such news into a letter, it was still better than saying it aloud; and now, by the mere opening of a door, she found herself compelled to the very exertion she most dreaded.

Her astonishment and confusion were great. Since his engagement had become public she had not seen him—therefore not since he knew her to be acquainted with it; and the consciousness of what she had been thinking, and what she must tell him, made her feel for a moment as if the air itself had thickened.

Edward was distressed too. They sat down in a silence so awkward that even the small sounds of the room—the faint snap of the fire, the soft fall of a cinder—seemed to speak for them, as if the house would not consent to be entirely mute.

Whether he had asked pardon for his intrusion at first, he could not recollect; and, determined to be on the safe side, he made his apology in form as soon as he could compel his tongue to work.

“Mrs. Jennings told me,” said he, “that you wished to speak with me—at least I understood her so—or I certainly should not have intruded in such a manner; though at the same time I should have been extremely sorry to leave London without seeing you and your sister—especially as it will most likely be some time. It is not probable I should soon have the pleasure of meeting you again. I go to Oxford tomorrow.”

“You would not have gone, however,” Elinor said, recovering herself with the resolution of a person who will finish a task before she allows herself to feel it, “without receiving our good wishes, even if we had not been able to give them in person. Mrs. Jennings was quite right in what she said. I have something of consequence to inform you, which I was on the point of communicating by paper. I am charged with a most agreeable office.”

She found herself breathing faster, but pushed on.

“Colonel Brandon, who was here only ten minutes ago, has desired me to say, that, understanding you mean to take orders, he has great pleasure in offering you the living of Delaford, now just vacant, and only wishes it were more valuable. Allow me to congratulate you on having so respectable and well-judging a friend; and to join in his wish that the living—it is about two hundred a-year—were much more considerable, and such as might better enable you to—as might be more than a temporary accommodation to yourself—such, in short, as might establish all your views of happiness.”

What Edward felt—what the sudden lifting of such a weight did to him—no other mouth could properly tell. His face said what words would not manage: astonishment, gratitude, the bewildered disbelief of a man who has lived on scant fare and is unexpectedly offered a plentiful table. He uttered only,

“Colonel Brandon!”

“Yes,” Elinor continued, gathering more firmness now that the worst had been spoken, “Colonel Brandon means it as a testimony of his concern for what has lately passed—for the cruel situation in which the unjustifiable conduct of your family has placed you—a concern which I am sure Marianne, myself, and all your friends must share; and likewise as a proof of his high esteem for your general character, and his particular approbation of your behaviour on the present occasion.”

“Colonel Brandon give me a living!—Can it be possible?”

“The unkindness of your own relations has made you astonished to find friendship any where.”

“No,” he replied, with sudden consciousness, “not to find it in you; for I cannot be ignorant that to you—to your goodness—I owe it all. I feel it—I would express it if I could—but as you well know, I am no orator.”

“You are very much mistaken. I do assure you you owe it entirely—at least almost entirely—to your own merit, and Colonel Brandon’s discernment of it. I have had no hand in it. I did not even know, till I understood his design, that the living was vacant; nor had it ever occurred to me that he might have such a living in his gift. As a friend of mine—of my family—he may perhaps—indeed I know he has—still greater pleasure in bestowing it; but, upon my word, you owe nothing to my solicitation.”

Truth required her to admit some small share; yet she did it with such reluctance, as if she feared to season his gratitude with the least suspicion of obligation. That hesitation seemed only to settle something in Edward’s mind. He sat for a short time in deep thought—silent, fixed, as if turning the whole matter over like a hard crust he must break in order to eat.

At last, as though speaking were an effort, he said, “Colonel Brandon seems a man of great worth and respectability. I have always heard him spoken of as such; and your brother I know esteems him highly. He is undoubtedly a sensible man, and in his manners perfectly the gentleman.”

“Indeed,” replied Elinor, “I believe you will find him, on farther acquaintance, all that you have heard him to be; and as you will be such very near neighbours—for I understand the parsonage is almost close to the mansion-house—it is particularly important that he should be all this.”

Edward made no answer; but when she turned away her head, he looked at her—serious, earnest, uncheerful—so plainly expressing, without speech, that he might hereafter wish the distance between parsonage and mansion-house much greater, that Elinor felt the glance like the sudden change of air when a door is opened upon a cold passage.

“Colonel Brandon, I think, lodges in St. James Street,” said he soon afterwards, rising.

Elinor told him the number.

“I must hurry away then, to give him those thanks which you will not allow me to give you—to assure him that he has made me a very—an exceedingly happy man.”

Elinor did not attempt to detain him. They parted with her earnest assurance of unceasing good wishes for his happiness, in every change of situation that might befall him; and on his side, rather an endeavour to return the same good will than the ability to clothe it in proper words.

When the door shut him out, Elinor remained standing a moment, listening—absurdly, as she would have called it—to the sound of his steps retreating, as if each footfall carried away some part of her composure. London’s noise flowed back in at once: a cart-rattle, a call in the street, the distant throb of life that never paused. She sank into her chair.

“When I see him again,” she said to herself, “I shall see him the husband of Lucy.”

And with that pleasing anticipation—pleasing, because it promised an end to hopes she had no right to nourish—she sat down to reconsider the past: to recall his words, to measure his looks, to attempt comprehension of his feelings; and, as naturally, to reflect on her own with discontent.

When Mrs. Jennings returned—though she came from visiting people she had never seen before, and therefore had, in common course, a great deal to say—her mind was far more occupied by the important secret she believed herself to carry. She reverted to it as soon as Elinor appeared, as a cook returns to the principal dish, however many small plates have been tasted.

“Well, my dear!” she cried. “I sent you up the young man. Did not I do right? And I suppose you had no great difficulty—You did not find him very unwilling to accept your proposal?”

“No, ma’am; that was not very likely,” said Elinor.

“Well—and how soon will he be ready? For it seems all to depend upon that.”

“Really,” replied Elinor, “I know so little of these kind of forms, that I can hardly even conjecture as to the time, or the preparation necessary; but I suppose two or three months will complete his ordination.”

“Two or three months!” Mrs. Jennings cried. “Lord! my dear, how calmly you talk of it! And can the Colonel wait two or three months! Bless me! it would put me quite out of patience! And though one would be very glad to do a kindness by poor Mr. Ferrars, I do think it is not worth while to wait two or three months for him. Sure somebody else might be found that would do as well; somebody that is in orders already.”

“My dear ma’am,” said Elinor, “what can you be thinking of? Colonel Brandon’s only object is to be of use to Mr. Ferrars.”

“Bless you, my dear! Sure you do not mean to persuade me that the Colonel only marries you for the sake of giving ten guineas to Mr. Ferrars!”

There was no maintaining the deception after that. Elinor explained at once; and in the clearing up of meanings—like flour shaken from a cloth and leaving the pattern plain—both found momentary amusement, with no real loss of happiness to either. Mrs. Jennings merely exchanged one form of delight for another, and did not even relinquish her expectation of the first, but carried it forward, as she did all her pleasures, in spite of contradiction.

“Aye, aye, the parsonage is but a small one,” said she, when her first surprise and satisfaction had bubbled over and subsided, “and very likely may be out of repair; but to hear a man apologising—as I thought—for a house that, to my knowledge, has five sitting-rooms on the ground floor, and I think the housekeeper told me could make up fifteen beds!—and to you too, that had been used to live in Barton Cottage! It seems quite ridiculous. But, my dear, we must touch up the Colonel to do something to the parsonage, and make it comfortable for them, before Lucy goes to it.”

“But Colonel Brandon does not seem to have any idea of the living’s being enough to allow them to marry,” Elinor observed.

“The Colonel is a ninny, my dear; because he has two thousand a-year himself, he thinks nobody else can marry on less. Take my word for it—if I am alive, I shall be paying a visit at Delaford Parsonage before Michaelmas; and I am sure I shan’t go if Lucy an’t there.”

Elinor was quite of her opinion, as to the probability of their not waiting for any thing more.
