Betteredge, having disclosed the name of Mr. Candy’s assistant, seemed to consider the matter trivial enough to dismiss. He returned to Rosanna Spearman’s letter, while I remained by the window, waiting.

Gradually, the peculiar impression left by Ezra Jennings—odd, given the turmoil of my own mind—faded. My thoughts returned, grim and relentless, to the impossible position I found myself in. Once more, I forced myself to confront it. My course was decided: return to London at once, lay the case before Mr. Bruff, and, above all, secure an audience with Rachel, no matter the cost.

The train would not depart for another hour. There was still the slim chance Betteredge might uncover something useful in the remainder of Rosanna’s letter. So I waited.

Her words concluded thus:

*"You need not resent my fleeting triumph, Mr. Franklin, knowing I held your fate in my hands. But fear soon overtook me. Sergeant Cuff’s suspicions meant every inch of the house would be searched. Where could I hide the nightgown where even he would not find it? And how, without wasting a moment? The solution, when it came, might make you laugh. I put it on. After all, you had worn it—and I took a small, bitter pleasure in that."*

The next development proved her haste justified. Cuff demanded the washing-book.

*"I brought it to him in the sitting room. We had crossed paths before—I knew he might recognize me, and I dreaded what that might mean. Better to face it at once. But he treated me as a stranger, polite and detached. A bad sign. I was certain he was biding his time. Then came word of your return from the railway. I went to the shrubbery, hoping for one last chance to speak. You never came. Worse, Betteredge and Cuff passed by—and the Sergeant saw me."*

She had no choice but to retreat.

*"As I moved to leave, you appeared—walking straight toward me, then turning sharply away as if I carried contagion. (A misunderstanding, sir. You never noticed her. Your change of path was mere coincidence—a thought of your aunt diverting you indoors.)*

*"Back in the laundry, alone, my thoughts spiraled. The Shivering Sand whispered to me again. Which would be harder to endure: your indifference, or the quick end the quicksand promised?"*

She confessed her inability to explain her own actions—why she hadn’t stopped me, why she hadn’t spoken plainly.

*"I had the power to compel you, had I chosen. I even knew a man in London who would have given you money for the Diamond, no questions asked. But fear silenced me—fear of your anger, your contempt. Behind your back, I loved you desperately. To your face, I was a coward."*

Penelope interrupted then, bringing grim news: Rachel, furious at the police presence, meant to leave for her aunt’s. And where Rachel went, Franklin would not stay.

*"A flicker of hope—if you and Rachel were truly at odds. But Penelope dashed it: ‘He’s too fond of her to quarrel.’ Then came the summons. Cuff was questioning the staff."*

Rosanna’s turn came after the upper housemaids.

*"His questions were cunning. He knew of the nightgown, but not the truth of it. He suspected me—yet not as the prime culprit. Someone else, he thought, had directed me. Who? I couldn’t guess. But one thing was clear: as long as the nightgown remained hidden, you were safe."*

Desperation drove her next move. She could no longer risk wearing the garment.

*"Destroy it? I couldn’t. It was my only proof—my only leverage if you ever doubted me. So I resolved to hide it in the one place I knew best: the Shivering Sand."*

Under pretext of fresh air, she went to the Yollands’ cottage to write this letter and remove the nightgown unseen.

*"When this is done, I will make one last attempt to speak to you. If I fail—if you leave, if you spurn me again—then I have nothing left. Forgive me, sir, if it ends that way. But if you ever learn what I’ve done for you, spare me a kind word. Perhaps my ghost will hear."*

She sealed the letter, resigned yet clinging to frail hope.

Betteredge finished reading in heavy silence. At my question, he shook his head.

I tucked it away, remembering the two occasions I had unwittingly turned Rosanna aside—once at the billiard table, once in the shrubbery. Each time, my attempts to shield her had only driven her deeper into despair.

Now, the past was laid bare. The tragedy of Rosanna Spearman was done. It remained only to look forward—to the living, and the slow, arduous path from shadow into light.
