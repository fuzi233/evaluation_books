For a long, soft stretch of days, I wanted nothing more than to spend the rest of my existence curled in the grass, laughing and chasing fireflies with the Little Ones. But a slow, restless thrum began to build under the surface of that quiet bliss: first the vague, gnawing certainty that I was not meant to laze and be fattened like the sows the brutish local boors kept penned in their styes. Then came the sharper realization that I was standing in a world stitched together by laws no outsider had ever mapped, and it was my duty to learn its shape. If I was ever to repay the gentle kindness the small beings had shown me, I could not stay willfully ignorant of the secrets they were too unworldly to explain.

It could not be natural, I reasoned, for their growth to be arrested forever. No universe could be built so discordantly that loveliness, truth, and purity could only survive if you throttled every natural path of development. Law and life could not be at such bitter odds that perfection required you to break what a creature was born to become. But the Little Ones *were* frozen, stunted by some invisible force I could not name. Lona, their de facto matriarch, looked no older than fifteen, yet had ruled this horde of childlike beings for longer than any of them could recall, all of whom called her mother, obeyed her without question, and loved her more than the sun itself. They had no concept of time, no memory of their own ages—even Lona swore she could not remember ever beginning to exist. Brimming with unnameable, intuitive wisdom and empty of every scrap of book-learned knowledge, she was both their Love and their Law. And the only fear she ever harbored was that her Little Ones would one day wake up and start to grow, and turn into the cruel, stupid “bad giants” that haunted the surrounding valleys.

Their “good giant” could not leave them to fend for themselves, I told myself, but neither could he help them without more understanding of their history, their nature, the thing that was holding them captive in eternal childhood. My presence was already drawing unwanted attention: I was not their protector, they were mine, and my lingering had only put them at greater risk of discovery by their violent neighbors. Knowledge, I knew, made wicked people far worse, but it could only make gentle people better. I ached to teach them to trace numbers in the dirt, to write down the dainty, wordless melodies they hummed at dusk and forgot by dawn. So I resolved to pack my wits and set off wandering, in search of whatever clue could unravel the riddle of their strange, stunted fortunes.

I might have dawdled for weeks more before acting on that plan, if fate had not intervened. One afternoon, as we all worked picking wild berries, I told them I would have left the bad giants’ lands long ago, if not for how deeply I loved the Little Ones. Before I could finish the sentence, they swarmed me as one: scrambling over each other, leaping from the lower boughs of the great oak we sat beneath, dropping onto my head and shoulders until I was half smothered. Three tiny ones clung to my arms, one perched on each shoulder digging her little fingers into my neck, a third stood straight up on the crown of my head, four or five wrapped their arms tight around my calves, and still more climbed and tumbled over the pile of bodies already clinging to me. I was as helpless as a man buried under a slow avalanche of soft, warm lava.

None of them noticed the brute creeping through the treeline until he was almost within arm’s reach. One sharp cry of *“Take care, good giant!”* ripped through the air, and in an instant they were gone: vanishing under the undergrowth like skittish mice, dropping off me like ripe hedgehogs, scurrying up the oak’s trunk like squirrels. The same second, the bad giant rounded the tree, swung a thick gnarled cudgel, and cracked it against the side of my head so hard the world went black.

I woke at midnight. A handful of pale stars hung low above me, waiting for the moon to rise. My head throbbed so badly I could barely lift it, my tongue swollen and heavy with thirst. When I rolled wearily onto my side, my ear pressed to the loam, and I heard the gurgling, gushing sound of a hidden spring buried just below the grass. Before I could move, the whole horde of Little Ones was gathered around me, silent as ghosts, pressing cool, juicy wild berries to my lips one after another until my thirst ebbed away. Only then did I notice the soft, tiny sobs rippling through the air, a sound I had never heard from their carefree throats before. When I tried to sit up, a heap of small warm bodies piled instantly at my back to prop me upright.

“You must go away, good giant,” Lona whispered, kneeling at my side. “The bad giant who hit you has told all his clan you are a wizard, and that we are your familiars. At dawn they will come to hunt you, and trample you to nothing.”

One of the older boys piped up that he had pressed his ear to the brute’s cottage door, and heard him boast that he had found me idling talking to a horde of moles and squirrels, and that the creatures had tried to kill him after he struck me down. They would all come for me before the sun was high, he said, to put an end to my magic.

“I will go at once,” I told them, as the Little Ones hauled me gently to my feet, their small hands surprisingly strong for their size. “And I will come back as soon as I find what it takes to make you bigger, and stronger, so the bad giants can never hurt you again.”

They looked at me, their little faces grave as polished stone. “We don’t want to be bigger. We won’t grow bad giants. We are strong now—you have no idea how strong.”

There was no use pushing the point. I bade them lead the way, and we trundled slowly up the slope of the valley, the small procession winding along the old riverbed. They fed me slices of sweet root and handfuls of fruit the whole way, chattering that I had spilled far too much of my red juice, and I needed to eat enough to make it back. When we reached the lip of the valley, the moon lifted her silver forehead over the horizon, soft and glowing.

“She has come to watch over you,” Lona said. “She will show you the road.”

I asked them what lay ahead. They told me of a far-off great city ruled by a giant-girl queen, though they could not say exactly where it was, how far it stretched, or what her name was—only that she hated the Little Ones, hunted for them across every valley, and longed to kill them all, if only she could track their hidden glen down. When I said I would go to her and ask why she wished them harm, they shrieked in terror, clinging to the hem of my tunic. “No no no! She is an awful bad-giant witch, she will tear you apart!”

They bade me head past the baby forest, toward the smooth green plain where the moon rose, no rocks, no sharp thorns, soft under every step. They stopped walking when the moon hung high overhead, at a fork in the stream, and told me they had never wandered this far from their oak trees before.

“Mind you memorize the path, deep inside your eyes,” Lona said, “so you can find your way back to us.”

“Beware the Cat-Woman that lives in the desert past the plain,” one of the older girls called over her shoulder as the rest turned to leave. “She is horridly ugly, and she scratches anyone who comes near her.”

The smaller children were already running, gamboling back down the path. Lona was the last to stay, holding her swaddled baby up for me to kiss, gazing deep into my eyes, before she whispered, “The Cat-Woman will not hurt *you*,” and turned to vanish into the dark after the rest.

I stood there a long time in the silver moonlight, watching their faint bobbing lanterns recede. Soon their laughter drifted back to me, like the ring of a thousand tiny sheep bells, rippling through the air and echoing off the rocks. The smaller ones chased each other and tumbled as they went, no care in their sweet souls, while Lona walked slowly apart, cradling her baby against her chest.

As I turned to start my solitary journey, my mind turned over and over their strange, gentle ways. Once I had suggested they leave the valley of the bad giants entirely, and come with me to find a safer home, and they had told me that leaving would mean they stopped being themselves. They had no ambition, no greed, no fear of discomfort, no desire for change of any kind. Their only terror was the unknown that waited if they ever started to grow. Could I really call myself their friend, if I forced my own world’s ideas of improvement on them? Might I not warp their soft, perfect innocence, distort and weaken them, in my eagerness to “help”? The role of self-appointed philanthropist was a dangerous one, I realized. If a man truly wanted to do his neighbor good, he must first learn how to do him no harm at all—and he must pull the great wooden beam out of his own eye, before he ever tried to pluck the tiny splinter out of another’s.
