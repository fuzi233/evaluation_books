The cry rose loud, the horses neighed, and the beacons blazed high—a frantic signal to save their great treasure, for time was short. Though the men of the *Golden Hind* would have lingered around Margarita and Cubagua to hunt for pearls, Amyas Preston, having "fleshed his dogs" as he put it, refused to linger after the alarm sounded. They pushed southwest across the mouth of the great bay, stretching from the Peninsula of Paria to Cape Codera. To their right lay the jagged rocks of Tortuga; to their left, the meadow-islands of the Piritoos, mere green ribbons barely kissing the tideless sea.

Yeo and Drew knew every inch of this coast; they had been the first Englishmen to trade here with Sir Francis Hawkins. Ahead, rising sheer from the water to piercing heights, stood the mighty range of the Caracas Mountains. Beside them, the hills most sailors had ever seen looked like insignificant mounds. Even Frank, who knew the Alps, and Amyas, who had faced the Andes, felt dwarfed by this grandeur. Poor Cary, however, whose knowledge of height ended with M'Gillicuddy's Reeks, spent a whole day clinging to the rigging, chin raised higher than his nose, staring upward until his neck grew stiff, all to Cary's infinite amusement.

As they neared the cape, the sea grew choppy despite a gentle breeze. They battled the strong eastward current that baffled winter travelers, struggling through towering waves with the massive wall of Cape Codera looming a thousand feet above them to the left. Beyond it, bank upon bank of mountain rose in the yellow moonlight.

By morning, a large ship appeared, having passed them during the night heading east. Yeo immediately proposed turning back to capture her. Amyas and Frank, however, declined.

"Why, Yeo?" Frank asked. "You said one more day would bring us to La Guayra."

"All the more reason," Yeo replied gravely, "to do the Lord's work thoroughly now that He has brought us safely this far."

"She can pass easily, and there is no loss involved," Amyas countered.

"Aye, sirs, but she is delivered into your hands. You must give an account of her."

"My good Yeo," Frank interjected, "we shall give ample account of many a tall Spaniard before we return. But you know La Guayra, and the salvation of the lady we believe resides there, was our first objective."

Yeo shook his head sorrowfully. "Ah, sirs, a lady brought Captain Oxenham to ruin."

"You dare not compare her with this one?" Frank and Cary exclaimed in unison.

"God forbid!" Yeo protested. "Yet no adventure prospers without a single eye fixed on the Lord's work: crippling the Spaniard and exaltin' Her Majesty the Queen. I believed nothing was dearer to Captain Leigh's heart than that."

Amyas hesitated. Duty to the Queen demanded he pursue the Spanish vessel; duty to his vow demanded he press on to La Guayra. It was a practical dilemma indeed. Ultimately, Frank's counsel prevailed, and they steered eastward toward La Guayra. Had they followed the Spanish ship, the story would have ended quite differently.

Around midday, a canoe staggered toward them under a huge triangular sail, carrying two Indians.

"Metal floats in these seas," Cary observed. "A fresh marvel for you, Frank."

"Explain," Frank commanded, always eager for new wonders.

"How else could those bronze statues dare to navigate in such a cockleshell?" Cary retorted playfully. "Master Courtier, have I given you the 'dor' now?"

"I am past 'dors,' Will," Frank smiled. "But what noble creatures! How fearlessly they approach! Do they know we are English, the avengers of their people?"

"They likely take us for Spaniards wishing to buy coconuts. See, the canoe is laden with vegetables."

"Hail them, Yeo!" Amyas ordered. "You speak the best Spanish; I need to speak with one of them."

Yeo approached. The canoe pulled alongside, lowering its sail as a splendid Indian scrambled aboard with the agility of a cat. Standing nearly six feet tall, he possessed the bold, graceful bearing of Frank or Amyas himself. Initially smiling broadly to reveal white teeth, his expression shifted instantly. Springing to the side, he shouted to his companion in Spanish, "Treachery! No Spaniard!" attempting to leap overboard, but a dozen strong men held him fast.

It took effort to subdue him; his naked limbs were slippery and his strength considerable. Amyas alternated between pleading with the crew not to harm the native and urging the Indian to remain calm. After five minutes of confusion, the stranger submitted sulkily.

"Do not bind him," Amyas instructed. "Loosen him and form a ring around him. Here is a dollar for you, my man."

The Indian's eyes gleamed as he accepted the coin.

"All I require," Amyas said, "is to know what ships are in La Guayra, and then to guide me to the Governor's house and the Custom House."

The Indian placed the coin on the deck, crossed himself, and stared Amyas in the face. "No, Senor! I am a freeman, a cavalier, a Christian Guayqueria. Our forefathers swore fealty to the King of Spain before any other Indians, and he still calls us his most faithful and noble subjects. God forbid I should aid his enemies, who are my enemies too."

A murmur of approval rose from the men who understood him, with several hinting that a rope around the head or a lit match would quickly yield the desired information.

"God forbid!" Amyas declared warmly. "He is a brave and loyal man, and I shall treat him as such. Tell me, my brave fellow, how do you know we are His Catholic Majesty's enemies?"

With a shrewd smile, the Indian pointed to various objects on the ship, declaring each "Not Spanish."

"Well, and what of that?" Amyas asked.

"None but Spaniards and free Guayquerias have the right to sail these seas."

Amyas laughed. "You are a valiant piece of copper. Take your dollar and go in peace. Clear the way, men; we can learn what we need without your assistance."

The Indian paused, astonished. "Overboard with you!" Amyas commanded gently. "Do you not know when you are well off?"

"Most illustrious Senor," the Indian began in his slow, sententious tone, "I have been deceived. I heard you heretics roast and eat true Catholics—and that all your priests possess tails."

"Plague on you, sirrah!" squeaked Jack Brimblecombe. "Do I have a tail? Look here!"

"Quien sabe? Who knows?" the Indian sniffed through his nose.

"How do you know we are heretics?" Amyas pressed.

"Hmph! But in repayment for your kindness, I warn you, Illustrious Senor, not to proceed to La Guayra. Ships of war await you there. Moreover, Governor Don Guzman sailed eastward only yesterday specifically to search for you. I wonder greatly that you did not encounter him."

"To search for us! On the lookout for us!" Cary cried. "Impossible! Lies! Amyas, this is some trick to frighten us away."

"Don Guzman came out yesterday to look for us? Are you sure?" Amyas asked sharply.

"As I live, Senor, he and another ship—for which I took yours."

Amyas stamped his foot. "That was the ship we passed! Fool that I was to stay close to my enemy and let my opportunity slip! If I had fulfilled my duty, all would have gone right!"

But it was too late to regret. Besides, the Indian's tale was likely false.

"Off with you!" Amyas ordered. The Indian bounded back into his canoe, leaving the crew marveling at the stateliness and courtesy of this bold sea-cavalier.

Thus, Westward-Ho they ran beneath the mighty northern wall—the highest cliff on Earth, seven thousand feet of rock separated from the sea by a narrow strip of verdant lowland. Patches of sugar cane and clusters of coconut trees near the water's edge confirmed their tropical location, while above, everything was savage, rough, and bare as an Alpine precipice. Deep clefts allowed the southern sun to pour blazing light onto the sea margin, revealing glimpses of strange, stately trees lining the glens and a veil of perpetual mist shrouding the inner summits. White, fleecy clouds hung motionless in the burning air, enhancing the sense of vastness and solemn rest that was already overwhelming.

"Within those mountains, three thousand feet above our heads," said Drew, the Master, "lies Saint Yago de Leon, the great city founded by the Spaniards fifteen years ago."

"Is it a rich place?" Cary asked.

"Very, they say."

"And a strong place?" Amyas inquired.

"No forts, they claim. The Spaniards boast that Heaven has built such excellent walls that man needs build none."

"I doubt that," Amyas muttered. "Lads, could you climb those hills?"

"Higher than Harty Point, sir," Drew replied cautiously. "But it depends entirely on what lies behind them."
