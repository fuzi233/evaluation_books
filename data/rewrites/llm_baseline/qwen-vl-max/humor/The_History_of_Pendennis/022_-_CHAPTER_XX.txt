CHAPTER XX. Rake’s Progress (Rewritten for Humor)

Sometime before Mr. Foker packed his bags and fled Oxbridge like a man escaping from a bad dinner party, a certain gentleman arrived at Boniface College—though not without a bit of scandal attached to his past. This was Horace Bloundell, a name that sounded suspiciously like a family crest someone had scribbled on a napkin after too many claret cups. He hailed from the ancient Suffolk line of Bloundell-Bloundell, of Bloundell-Bloundell Hall, in Bloundell-Bloundellshire—a place so rich in hyphens it could have been written by a grammarian with a grudge against simplicity. No one quite knew why Boniface took him in, especially since St. George’s and several other colleges had politely declined him, but perhaps it was because Dr. Donne, the Master, was also from Suffolk and may have shared a distant cousin or two with the Bloundells—or maybe he just liked the sound of “Bloundell-Bloundell” when spoken with a flourish.

Mr. Bloundell had once been a dragoon—crimson pantaloons, sable shakos, the whole military kit—and had served bravely until his third brother, who was supposed to inherit the family living, fell ill and died. At which point, Mr. Bloundell decided to swap his sword for a surplice, his sabre for a sermon, and become an English divine. A noble ambition, you might say—except for the fact that he’d already left Camford under a cloud of suspicion involving tutors, a stolen quill pen, and possibly a pigeon. But undeterred, he came to Oxbridge with the same fierce determination as a man trying to win a game of poker with only one card.

During Pen’s second year, Major Pendennis made a brief visit to see his nephew and met the young men who hung around his rooms. There was Lord Plinlimmon, gentle as a lamb and polite as a teacup; Magnus Charters, gallant and open-hearted (though prone to quoting Rousseau while wearing a cravat); Harland, sly and witty, whose Greek quotations were more confusing than enlightening; Ringwood, known as Rupert in the Union Debating Club because of his daring opinions and even bolder blunders; Broadbent, nicknamed Barebones Broadbent for his republican views and his habit of wearing boots that looked like they’d seen a revolution; and finally, Mr. Bloundell-Bloundell, who had somehow managed to insert himself into the inner circle of university society within days.

Major Pendennis, though he didn’t understand half of what Harland said (and frankly, didn’t care), enjoyed the company—except for one particular young man who seemed to believe he was a character in a novel rather than a student in a college. This man, of course, was Mr. Bloundell, who walked about with the air of a man who had been to Paris, seen everything, and was now merely waiting for the world to bow down.

At breakfast the next morning, the Major gave his verdict on the lot. He praised Pen’s dinner (“Merveille! Though a tad too fine, my boy”), complimented Lord Plinlimmon’s resemblance to his mother (Lady Aquila Brownbill, whom he’d once danced with at a ball), and said Lord Magnus would grow out of his republicanism—“though I wouldn’t want to sit next to him during a coronation.” He admired Broadbent’s eloquence and Foker’s charm, but then turned sharply to Pen.

“And your friend Bloundell?” he asked, peeling an egg with the precision of a surgeon. “He struck me as the most ineligible young man I’ve ever met.”

“Bless my soul, sir!” Pen laughed. “Bloundell-Bloundell? Why, he’s the most popular man in the university! We elected him to the Barmecides the very first week—he’s of an excellent family, descended from Richard’s Blondel, bear a harp in chief—and motto O Mong Roy!”

“A man may have a coat-of-arms,” said the Major, “but still be a tiger. And this fellow is a tiger, mark my words. Low. I’ll lay odds he left his regiment in disgrace—though I admit, it was a good one. My friend Lord Martingale never sat in a saddle without looking like a king. But this Bloundell? He wears too many rings. Too much jewellery. That’s the sign of a scamp. He haunts low gambling-houses, billiard-hells, third-rate clubs—I know it. I can tell a man’s character by his style. And I’ve never been wrong yet. Did you notice how he carried himself? Like a man who’s used to winning, but not used to paying.”

Pen, however, was not convinced. “But sir, he’s charming! Everyone likes him. Even the proctors seem to nod at him.”

“Then let them nod,” said the Major. “But don’t you follow him. He’s a wolf in velvet. Mark my words.”

Poor Pen. The worthy uncle had no idea how often those dinners actually happened. In truth, Pen was hosting them every other night, like a modern-day Bacchus with a budget. There’s nothing boys love more than pretending to know about wine and cuisine—it gives them an air of sophistication, like they’re part of some secret society of epicures. They wink at claret as if it were a lover, discuss salmi with solemnity, and imagine themselves dining with kings. But alas, as they grow older, they realize they know nothing—except that a leg of mutton and a quiet heart are far better than a table full of truffles and regret.

And Pen, in his role as the Admirable Crichton of Oxford, was determined to be the greatest host in all the land. His staircase became a veritable circus: waiters, dish-covers, skips with iced champagne, and a constant stream of piteous-faced servants begging for tips or complaining about broken silverware. It was chaos—and Pen loved every minute of it.

The Major’s advice? Forgotten. Pen continued to hang around Bloundell, who, despite his dubious reputation, had one thing going for him: he *knew* life. Or at least, he claimed to. A man who speaks of Paris, garrison conquests, and the Palais Royal with confidence is always welcome among students who’ve never been farther than the library. Pen, in particular, adored stories of adventure—especially if they involved money, women, and dice.

Bloundell, of course, was an expert at all three.

One night, after a supper-party where vingt-et-un had been played (a genteel alternative to singing songs, which always attracted proctors like flies to honey), Bloundell did something remarkable. He picked up a green wine-glass meant for iced punch, slipped in a pair of dice, and began tossing them with the grace of a man who’d spent years perfecting the art. “Sevens the main!” he declared, and with a flourish, sent the ivory cubes flying across the table. Then he sang the chorus from *Robert the Devil*, and soon everyone was playing hazard, the glass passing from hand to hand until Pen accidentally shattered it—after rolling six mains, naturally.

From that moment, Pen was hooked. Dice became his new passion. They played mornings, afternoons, evenings—any time the bones could rattle. Bloundell even invented a felt-lined box so the noise wouldn’t betray them to the tutors. Once, they nearly got caught by Mr. Buck, who heard “Two to one on the caster” through Pen’s window. But when he entered, he found three Homers spread across the table and Pen asking gravely, “What is the current state of the River Scamander, sir? Is it navigable?”

Mr. Buck, baffled, replied, “I believe it flows, Mr. Pendennis.”

Pen nodded sagely. “Ah, yes. Navigable, then.”

And thus, the dice game continued.

Pen didn’t win much money—though he learned the odds of hazard better than any book could teach. But Bloundell had other talents. He told stories—fabulous, improbable tales of nights in Paris, of winning twelve thousand francs under his pillow, of finding a hundred and forty-nine Napoleons in his boot. Pen listened wide-eyed, believing every word. He respected the man who could win such sums—what a coup, indeed!

When Pen confessed his love for Miss Fotheringay, Bloundell laughed. “Marry her?” he sneered. “You might as well marry the actress at the Theatre Royal. She hasn’t a shred of character. Twenty men are openly courting her. I know them all—here’s the list.”

And so he named them, along with the amounts each had spent. Pen was stunned. His pure flame, his noble passion—was it all a lie? He didn’t question Bloundell. After all, Bloundell never spoke well of women. So why should he doubt him?

Later, during Easter vacation, Pen went to London with Bloundell. They stayed at a hotel in Covent Garden, where Bloundell had a “tick”—meaning he owed the landlord a fortune but was still allowed to stay. They dined at military clubs, drank pale ale, and beat the town like two young lions loose in a zoo. One night, Pen saw Miss Fotheringay perform—at the Museum Theatre, no less. He was seated in the boxes, separated from Major Pendennis by only a plank of wood. The Major was in Lord Steyne’s box, watching the show with a look of mild disapproval.

Fotheringay was in full glory. She had drawn good houses, starred in the provinces, and returned to London with slightly dimmed luster—but still enough to fill the theatre. Pen watched her. He remembered the day they parted in Chatteris. He remembered how he used to watch for her entrance, how her voice used to thrill him.

But now? The light was gone from her eyes. Her voice was loud, but mechanical. The brogue beneath was coarse. He realized, with horror, that he’d once mistaken imitation for genius. He felt ashamed. Lonely. As if he’d lived a dream and woken up to find it was all a hoax.

After the play, they had a wild supper. Pen woke the next morning with a headache and an empty purse. He returned to Oxbridge, broke, humiliated, and utterly disillusioned.

As for his finances? Well, Pen never kept track. He spent money like water, and trusted everyone. Even Finch, the money-lender Bloundell introduced him to, treated him kindly—never charging more than a hundred per cent interest. The old college cook, who adored him, sent dinners up to the very end and never pressed for payment. Pen was honest, generous, and utterly naive. He’d share a guinea with a poor friend, pay off debts with reckless cheerfulness, and leave parties to sit with sick comrades.

In short, he was the kind of man who could charm a thief into giving him back his wallet.

So while he may have been ruined by Bloundell’s influence, he remained beloved by most. For all his follies, there was something about Arthur Pendennis that made people forgive him—even when he deserved it least. And perhaps, in the end, that was his greatest fault—and his greatest strength.
