The night hung thick as pitch: not a star pricked through the cloud curtain, not a sliver of moon broke the murk. By daylight those clouds would have been soft, rain-heavy gray, but now they draped the world in sable, swallowing all light save for the tremulous, lurid orange glow leaking from the Stilbro’ ironworks furnaces far to the east, painting the horizon like a smoldering wound.

Malone, the broad-shouldered Irish curate, never spared much thought for nature’s small shifts. He could tramp miles across a capricious April day and never notice the soft, fleeting kiss of sunbeams on hilltops, turning the grass to glistening green, nor see a summer shower drape the peaks in cloud tresses like loose, disheveled hair. He did not pause to contrast that black, streaming, rain-soaked sky above him with the crisp, frosty, unbroken dark of a clear winter night. He did not wonder where the constellations had vanished, nor mourn the deep, velvet black-blue of the open sky, now smothered beneath a low, rolling sea of cold rainwater. He just doggedly put one foot in front of the other, leaning forward against the wind, his hat pushed back off his forehead in the easy, careless Irish way. Tramp, tramp, he went along the flagged causeway where the road was well-kept; splash, splash, he sloshed through mud-caked cart ruts where the paving ran out entirely. He only looked for two landmarks: the sharp spire of Briarfield Church, and farther on, the warm lantern glow of the Redhouse inn.

When he reached that roadside tavern, the golden spill of firelight through a half-curtained window, the glint of glass tumblers on a scrubbed round table, and the low roar of revellers huddled on an oaken settle nearly lured him off his path entirely. He ached for a tumbler of hot whisky and water. In any strange town he would have darted through the door before he had time to think, but the men drinking in that kitchen were all Mr. Helstone’s parishioners — every last one of them knew their curate by sight. He heaved a loud, disappointed sigh, and trundled on past.

Soon he left the main highroad entirely, taking a short cut across flat, monotonous fields that would cut his remaining walk to Hollow’s Mill nearly in half. He loped straight across the grass, vaulting low hedges and loose stone walls without breaking stride. Only one large, irregular building stood between him and the valley below: a sprawling, half-hidden pile with a high gable, a long weathered front, another lower gable, and a thick, towering stack of chimneys, ringed by shadowy old oak trees. No candle glowed in any window. The whole place was dead still, save for rain dripping off the eaves, and a thin, wild little whistle of wind curling around the chimneys and through bare branches. It was Fieldhead, the old estate half-lost to the night, holding its secrets close — secrets not even Malone, who knew every lane and byway in the parish, had guessed at yet.

Once he passed that dark house, the flat fields sloped sharply downward into a vale, and he could hear the gurgle of a swollen mill stream rushing far below. One tiny pinprick of light glimmered in the hollow, and he steered straight for it.

It led him to a little white cottage — bright enough that even through the thick, rain-choked dark he could make out the whitewash on its walls — and he rapped hard on the door. A fresh-faced young servant named Sarah opened it, holding a guttering tallow candle that lit up a narrow passage ending in an equally narrow stair. Two doors draped in soft crimson baize, and a strip of matching crimson carpet running down the steps, popped vivid against pale plaster walls and white scrubbed floor, making the whole tiny house feel warm and clean.

“Mr. Moore at home, I suppose?” Malone asked, shaking rain off his coat sleeves.

“Ah, sir, he’s near, but not in this part of the house,” the girl replied. “He’s down at the mill, in his counting-house.”

Just then one of the crimson baize doors swung open. “Have the wagons come yet, Sarah?” a woman’s voice called, and a head poked out, two little screws of curl paper sticking out on either side of her temples, making it very clear she was half way through preparing for bed. It was no Gorgon’s head, not by a long shot, but big as he was, Malone flinched back bashfully into the rain anyway, as if he’d caught a woman mid-dressing, and stammered “I’ll go find him,” before hurrying at a near-run down a short muddy lane, across a shadowy yard, straight for the huge, black bulk of the mill.

Work hours were long over. All the hands had gone home for the night, the great water-powered machinery was still and silent, and the main mill doors were locked tight. Malone tramped all the way around the soot-stained outer wall until he spotted another thin sliver of light peeking through a crack in a side door, and banged on the wood hard with the thick end of his shillelagh, beating out a loud, rousing tattoo. A key turned in the lock, and the door creaked open.

“Is that you, Joe Scott? What news of the wagons, Joe?” the voice that spoke was sharp, tight with tired expectation.

“Nah, it’s me. Mr. Helstone sent me, to check you’d got in safe,” Malone rumbled, stepping over the threshold, rain dripping off his cuffs onto the dirty floor.

“Oh! Mr. Malone.” The voice held the faintest, flicker of disappointment, so subtle you might have missed it if you weren’t listening close. After a short pause, it continued, polite but a little formally distant: “Do come in, Mr. Malone. I’m very sorry Mr. Helstone thought he needed to drag you all this way on such a night. I told him there was no need for it at all — but do walk forwards.”

Malone followed the speaker through a dark, unlit store room where he could make out nothing but stacked bales of cloth, and into a bright, warm counting house beyond. After an hour of picking his way through fog and pitch dark, the room felt almost dazzling to his eyes: its only light came from a crackling, roaring fire on the hearth, and a sleek, bright lamp burning on the centre of the plain wooden table. The floor was bare, uncarpeted boards. Three or four stiff-backed, green-painted chairs looked like they had been salvaged from an old farm kitchen, and a heavy, solid oak desk, plus a few framed sheets on the stone-coloured walls holding building plans, garden layouts, and new machinery designs, made up the rest of the furniture.

Plain as it was, Malone could not have loved it more. He hung up his soaking wet overcoat and dripping hat by the door, dragged one of those stiff old chairs right up to the hearth, and held his knees so close to the red-hot grate they nearly touched the bars.

“Comfortable little quarters you’ve got here, Mr. Moore, all snug and private,” he said happily.

“They do well enough. My sister would be glad to have you in the cottage, if you’d rather sit somewhere warmer,” Moore offered.

“Not at all! Ladies are far better left to themselves. I was never much of a ladies’ man, you know. You don’t mistake me for my friend Sweeting, do you?”

“Sweeting? Which one is that? The gentleman in the chocolate overcoat, or the tiny little one?”

“The little one, curate of Nunnely! The man who’s half in love with all six of the Misses Sykes, ha ha!”

“Better be half in love with all six than head over heels for one of them, I’d have thought, in that family,” Moore said, a faint, dry smile tugging at his mouth.

“But he is head over heels for one of them, on top of all that. When me and Donne teased him to pick a favourite out of the whole lot, he named — guess who?”

“Dora, of course,” Moore said, still smiling quiet, “or Harriet.”

“Ha! You’ve got an excellent guess! What made you hit on those two?”

“They’re the tallest, the handsomest, and Dora at least is the sturdiest. Your friend Mr. Sweeting is such a slight little fellow, I just figured he’d go for the complete contrast, as men so often do.”

“You’re dead right, it’s Dora. But he hasn’t got a chance, has he, Moore?”

“What has Mr. Sweeting, besides his tiny curacy, to offer her?”

The question tickled Malone so much he laughed for three full minutes before he could catch his breath enough to answer. “What has Sweeting? Why, David had his harp or his flute, same sort of thing. He’s got a pinchbeck watch, a pinchbeck ring, a pinchbeck eyeglass. That’s all he’s got, the daft lad.”

“How does he plan to keep Miss Sykes in new gowns, then?”

“Ha ha! That’s excellent. I’ll roast him for that presumption next time I see him. But he must be expecting old Christopher Sykes to settle something handsome, right? The man’s rolling in it, isn’t he? They live in that great big grand house.”

“Sykes runs an enormous cloth manufacturing concern, that’s true.”

“So he must be wealthy, eh?”

“So he’s got every penny of his capital tied up in keeping that concern running. These days, he’s about as likely to pull money out of his business to give dowries to six daughters as I am to tear down that little cottage outside the mill, and build a house twice the size of Fieldhead on its ruins.”

“Wait, you know what I heard people saying about you the other day?” Malone leaned forward, grinning.

“Nothing good, I’ll wager. Your Briarfield gossips are capable of saying far sillier things than that.”

“They say you’re going to take Fieldhead on a long lease — I swear it looked like the loneliest, dismalest place on earth when I passed it tonight — and that you’re going to install one of the Misses Sykes there as mistress, that you’re getting married, ha ha! It’s Dora, isn’t it? You said she was the prettiest.”

Moore huffed a quiet, exasperated little laugh, staring into the fire. “I wonder how many times the good people of this parish have decided I’m getting married in the two years I’ve been here. They’ve assigned me every single unmarried woman within ten miles. First it was the two Miss Wynns, dark one then the fair one, then the red-haired Miss Armitage, then the middle-aged Ann Pearson. Now I’m supposed to marry the whole Sykes tribe. God knows where all this nonsense comes from. I visit no one. I avoid female company just as much as you do, Mr. Malone. If I ever drive over to Whinbury, it’s only to knock on Sykes or Pearson’s counting house door, and all we talk about is cloth we can’t sell, workers we can’t pay, mills we can’t run, not courtships, settlements, dowries. Those things take up all our minds right now, pushing out all that silly romantic nonsense entirely.”

“Couldn’t agree with you more, Moore. If there’s one thing I hate it’s that whole silly idea of marriage being just sentiment — two daft beggars agreeing to tie their penniless lives together over some stupid feeling. Humbug! But a good, solid advantageous match, built on shared interests and sensible stability? That’s not half bad, now is it?”

Moore only nodded absently, as if he was barely listening. The whole topic held no interest for him at all. He sat staring at the flames for a long minute, his forehead furrowed, before he suddenly tilted his head.

“Hark!” he said. “Did you hear wheels?”

He stood up, crossed to the window, and threw the sash open, listening hard to the dark, rain-soaked yard outside. A second later he closed it again, shaking his head. “Only the wind picking up, and the mill stream swelling, rushing down the gully. I expected those wagons at six o’clock. It’s nearly nine now.”

“Seriously, do you think bringing this new machinery in is going to land you in danger?” Malone asked. “Helstone seems convinced the frame breakers will come after you.”

“I only wish the new frames were here safe, locked inside these mill walls. Once I get them installed, I defy every last frame breaker in the shire to try and touch them. My mill is my castle. Let them come, and they’ll get what’s coming to them.”

“Ugh, those low scoundrels aren’t worth wasting fear on,” Malone said, pensive. “I almost wish a whole party of them would turn up tonight! But the road was dead quiet when I walked in. I didn’t see a single soul stirring.”

“You came along the Redhouse road, didn’t you?” Moore asked.

“Aye.”

“Then you wouldn’t have seen a thing. The risk all lies on the Stilbro’ side of the valley.”

“So you do think there’s real danger, then?”

“They’ve done the exact same thing to every other manufacturer in the district. The only difference is, most of them turned into rabbits the second they got attacked. Sykes, for example, when his dressing shed got burned to the ground, when his cloth got torn off the tenters and left in rags out in the fields? He never even tried to find who did it, never punished a single one of the miscreants. He gave up meeker than a rabbit in a ferret’s jaws. I’m not like that. If I know myself, I’ll stand by my trade, my mill, and my machinery, come what may.”

“Helstone says those three things are your gods,” Malone chuckled. “That the Orders in Council are just another name for the seven deadly sins to you, that Castlereagh is your Antichrist, and the whole war party is his army of devils.”

“And he’s not wrong. I abhor all of it, because every last bit of it is ruining me. It stands in my way. I can’t move forward with my plans, I get blocked at every single turn.”

“But you’re rich and thriving, aren’t you, Moore?”

“I’m rich in bales of cloth I can’t sell. Come look in my warehouse sometime — it’s piled all the way up to the rafters. Roakes and Pearson are in the exact same mess. America used to be our main market, and the Orders in Council cut it right off, no warning.”

Malone didn’t have much patience for talk of trade laws and blocked markets. He started tapping the heels of his boots together on the hearth stones, and yawned so wide his jaw ached.

“Just think,” Moore went on, too wrapped up in his own worries to notice his guest’s boredom, “all those silly gossips in Whinbury and Briarfield keep badgering me about getting married! As if there’s nothing else to do with a whole life but hang around paying ‘attention’ to some young lady, march her down the church aisle, go off on a bridal tour, do the rounds of all the neighbourhood calls, and then settle down to have a house full of brats. To hell with it all!” He caught himself mid-outburst, and softened his tone. “I do believe women think of nothing else all day long, so naturally they assume men’s heads are full of the same nonsense.”

“Right, right, exactly,” Malone nodded, fidgeting in his chair, clearly wanting something the conversation wasn’t giving him. This time Moore noticed his restlessness, and understood at once.

“Mr. Malone, you must be starving after that long wet walk. I’ve been such a bad host I entirely forgot hospitality.”

“Not at all,” Malone protested — though his face lit up like a lantern, making it very clear he had been waiting for exactly that. Moore stood up and opened a large oak cupboard tucked against the wall.

“I make a point of keeping everything I need stocked right here,” he explained. “I don’t like running to the cottage for every bite to eat or sip to drink. Half the time I spend the whole evening and sleep right here in the mill with Joe Scott, no need to disturb anyone. Sometimes I work as my own night watchman. I don’t need much sleep, and on fine nights I love wandering around the hollow with my musket for an hour or two. Mr. Malone, do you know how to cook a mutton chop?”

“Try me. I did hundreds of them back at college, no bother.”

“Then there’s a plate full of chops right there, and a gridiron. Turn them fast, you know the trick to keep all the juice in, right?”

“Never you fear. Hand me a knife and fork, will you?”

Malone rolled up his coat cuffs, and threw himself into cooking the chops with enormous vigour. Moore set plates, a loaf of crusty bread, a black glass bottle and two tumblers on the table, then pulled out a small copper kettle, filled it with water from a big stone jar in the corner, set it on the hearth beside the hissing gridiron, and fetched out lemons, sugar, and a tiny china punch bowl. He was just stirring the sugar into the hot water when a knock rapped on the outer door.

“Sarah?” he called.

“Yes, sir. Will you be coming in to supper tonight, sir?” the servant called back.

“No, I won’t be in the cottage at all. I’m sleeping in the mill tonight. Lock all the doors, and tell your mistress to go straight to bed.”

He came back to the counting house, just as Malone flipped the last chop, his face ruddy from the fire, a brown, juicy sheen on the meat.

“You run a tight ship, no mistake about it,” Malone said approvingly. “You’re not under petticoat rule like poor Sweeting. That lad’s going to spend his whole life being bossed around by women. Now us two, Moore — that’s a perfect chop, look at all that gravy — us two, we’ll never let a woman run our households if we ever do get around to marrying.”

“I don’t know, I never think about it. If the woman in question was handsome, and easy to get along with, what would be the harm?”

“The chops are done. Is the punch brewed yet?”
