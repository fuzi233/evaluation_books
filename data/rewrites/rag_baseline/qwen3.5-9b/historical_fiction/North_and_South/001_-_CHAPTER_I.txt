"Haste to the wedding!" the chatter rang out, a frantic drumbeat against the quietude of the drawing-room. "Wooed and married and a'."

"Edith!" Margaret called, her voice cutting through the haze of anticipation. "Edith!"

But Edith had already surrendered to the spell of the evening. She lay curled upon the crimson damask sofa in the back drawing-room of Harley Street, a vision of white muslin and blue ribbons so ethereal she might have been a terrestrial Titania, dreaming amidst the dust motes of a London parlor. Margaret watched her cousin with a sudden, sharpened intensity. For years, Edith's beauty had been a public fact, remarked upon by everyone save Margaret herself; yet now, facing the imminent loss of her constant companion, every sweet quality of Edith's presence seemed amplified, glowing with a fragile, precious light. They had spoken of wedding gowns, of Captain Lennox's descriptions of life at Corfu, of the formidable challenge of tuning a piano in a climate far from home, and of the Scottish gowns required for the visit that would inevitably follow the nuptials. But the whispers had dulled into a drowsy murmur. Now, despite the lively hum from the adjacent room, Edith had rolled herself into a soft sphere of fabric and ribbon, her silken curls fanned out like a halo, drifting into a peaceful, post-prandial slumber.

Margaret had been poised to share her own visions of the future—the quiet life awaiting her in the country parsonage where her parents resided, a place of bright holidays abandoned a decade ago for the social orbit of her aunt Shaw's house. Deprived of a listener, she fell silent, brooding over the impending separation from her gentle aunt and beloved cousin. It was a happy brooding, nonetheless, tinged only with a regret for the indefinite distance separating them. As she contemplated the weight and significance of becoming the sole daughter in Helstone, fragments of the conversation from the next room drifted to her ears.

Mrs. Shaw was engaged in animated discourse with the five or six ladies who had dined there, their husbands lingering in the dining-room. These were the familiar acquaintances of the house, neighbors whom Mrs. Shaw termed "friends" simply because she dined with them more frequently than anyone else, and because mutual convenience dictated calls before luncheon. Invited as guests of honor for Edith's farewell, these women and their husbands were celebrating the departure of the heiress. Edith had initially resisted the arrangement, knowing Captain Lennox was due by a late train that very evening; yet, being a creature of carelessness and idleness rather than strong will, she yielded when her mother insisted on the extra delicacies of the season, deemed essential to counteract the immoderate grief of farewells. Edith leaned back, playing absently with the food on her plate, her expression grave and distant, while those around her savored the witty remarks of Mr. Grey, the perpetual guest of honor at Mrs. Shaw's tables who had requested music from Edith in the drawing-room. Mr. Grey was particularly obliging tonight, ensuring the gentlemen remained downstairs longer than usual—a courtesy that proved wise, judging by the snippets Margaret overheard.

"I suffered too much myself," Mrs. Shaw confided, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper that Margaret could catch clearly. "Not that I was not extremely happy with the poor dear General, but disparity of age is a drawback—one I was resolved Edith should not encounter. Without any maternal partiality, I foresaw the dear child was likely to marry early; indeed, I often said I was sure she would be wed before nineteen. I had quite a prophetic feeling regarding Captain Lennox"—here the voice trailed off, leaving Margaret to fill the silence. The course of true love in Edith's case had run remarkably smooth. Mrs. Shaw had succumbed to this presentiment, urging the marriage forward, even though it fell below the expectations many held for a young, pretty heiress. "My only child should marry for love," Mrs. Shaw declared, sighing emphatically as if love had not been her primary motive in uniting herself with the General. She seemed to enjoy the romance of the current engagement more than her daughter did. Though Edith was thoroughly and properly in love, she would undoubtedly have preferred a grand house in Belgravia to the picturesque, makeshift existence Captain Lennox described at Corfu. The very aspects that made Margaret glow with excitement caused Edith to pretend to shiver; partly because she delighted in being coaxed out of her dislike by her fond lover, and partly because anything resembling a gypsy or transient life was inherently distasteful to her. Yet, had someone offered a magnificent house, a vast estate, and a noble title, Edith would have clung to Captain Lennox while the temptation lasted. Once the allure faded, she might well harbor regrets that he could not embody every desirable trait simultaneously. In this, she was merely her mother's child: a woman who, after deliberately marrying General Shaw out of respect for his character and establishment alone, spent her days quietly lamenting her hard lot in loving neither her husband nor her station.

"I have spared no expense in her trousseau," Mrs. Shaw continued. "She possesses all the beautiful Indian shawls and scarves the General gave to me, which I shall never wear again."

"She is a lucky girl," replied another voice, identified by Margaret as Mrs. Gibson, a lady taking a keen interest since one of her daughters had recently wed. "Helen had set her heart upon an Indian shawl, but when I discovered the extravagant price, I was forced to refuse. She will be quite envious to hear of Edith's fortune. What kind are they? Delhi? With the lovely little borders?"

Margaret heard her aunt rise from her half-recumbent posture, her voice carrying into the dimmer back drawing-room. "Edith! Edith!" she cried, then sank back as if weary by the exertion. Margaret stepped forward.

"Edith is asleep, Aunt Shaw. Is there anything I can do?"

"Poor child!" the ladies chorused, their voices filled with performative pity. The small lapdog in Mrs. Shaw's arms let out a sharp bark, seemingly agitated by the sudden outpouring of sentiment.

"Hush, Tiny! You naughty little girl! You will wake your mistress. We only wished to ask Edith if she would tell Newton to bring down her shawls. Perhaps you would go, Margaret dear?"

Margaret ascended to the old nursery at the very top of the house, where Newton was busy preparing laces required for the wedding. As Newton departed—not without muttering complaints—to retrieve the shawls, which had already been displayed four or five times that day, Margaret surveyed the room. It was the first space in this house she had known intimately nine years prior, when she arrived, untamed from the forest, to share the home, play, and lessons of her cousin Edith. She recalled the dark, somber atmosphere of the London nursery, presided over by an austere, ceremonious nurse obsessed with clean hands and unblemished frocks. She remembered the first tea there, consumed separately from her father and aunt, who dined far below in an infinite descent of stairs; unless she was in the sky, the children reasoned, her parents must be deep in the bowels of the earth. Back home, before coming to Harley Street, her mother's dressing-room had served as her nursery. In the country parsonage, where they kept early hours, Margaret had always eaten with her father and mother. How vividly the tall, stately girl of eighteen remembered the wild, passionate tears of the little girl of nine, hiding her face beneath the bedclothes on that first night, bidding the nurse not to cry lest it disturb Miss Edith. She had sobbed quietly until her newly arrived, grand, and pretty aunt descended with Mr. Hale to show him his sleeping daughter. Then, little Margaret had hushed her sobs, feigning sleep, terrified of making her father unhappy by expressing a grief she dared not voice before her aunt, and convinced it was wrong to feel sorrow after the months of hope, planning, and contrivance undertaken at home to prepare her for these grander circumstances, before her father could leave his parish to visit London, even briefly.

Now, she loved the old nursery, though it was a dismantled shell, and she looked around with a cat-like regret at the thought of leaving it forever in three days.

"Oh, Newton!" she said. "I think we shall all be sorry to leave this dear old room."

"Indeed, miss, I shan't for one. My eyes are not so good as they were, and the light here is so bad I cannot see to mend laces except just at the window, where there's always a shocking draught—enough to give one one's death of cold."

"Well, I dare say you will have both good light and plenty of warmth at Naples. You must keep as much of your darning as you can till then. Thank you, Newton, I can take them down—you're busy."

Thus, Margaret descended, laden with shawls, inhaling their spicy, exotic fragrance. Her aunt asked her to serve as a living mannequin to display the fabrics, as Edith remained asleep. No one noticed, but Margaret's tall, finely proportioned figure, clad in a black silk dress worn as mourning for a distant relative of her father's, framed the long, beautiful folds of the gorgeous shawls that would have nearly smothered Edith.
