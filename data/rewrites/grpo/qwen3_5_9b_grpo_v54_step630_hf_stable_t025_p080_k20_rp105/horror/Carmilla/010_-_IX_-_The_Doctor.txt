IX. The Doctor

My father, refusing to allow Carmilla to remain unattended, stationed a servant outside her chamber door. This precaution was intended to intercept her should she attempt another nocturnal flight, ensuring she could not slip away unnoticed. That night passed in silence, undisturbed by her usual wanderings.

Early the following morning, before I had even risen, the doctor arrived. My father had summoned him in strict confidence, revealing nothing of his purpose to me. Madame escorted me to the library, where the physician awaited. He was a grave figure, distinguished by white hair and spectacles, a man I had noted previously.

I recounted my tale, and as the words left my lips, his expression darkened, growing heavier with each sentence. We stood in the deep recess of a window, facing one another. When I finished, he leaned back against the stone wall, his gaze fixed upon me with an intensity that bordered on horror. After a moment of silent reflection, he requested to see my father.

My father was summoned and entered with a smile, remarking, "I dare say, doctor, you are going to tell me that I am an old fool for having brought you here; I hope I am." But his smile vanished instantly as the doctor beckoned him forward, his face a mask of solemnity.

For some time, the two men conversed in the window recess where I had just spoken. They appeared engaged in a heated, earnest debate. From the far end of the vast room, Madame and I watched, burning with curiosity yet silenced by distance. The thick walls and deep alcove obscured the doctor entirely, leaving only my father's foot, arm, and shoulder visible. Their voices were hushed, rendered unintelligible by the acoustic isolation of the space.

Eventually, my father's face emerged into the light, pale and thoughtful, tinged with agitation. "Laura, dear, come here for a moment," he called softly. "Madame, we shall not trouble you; the doctor requires my presence."

I approached, feeling a sudden tremor of alarm. Though I felt weak, I did not feel ill; strength, one assumes, is a resource available on demand. My father extended his hand as I drew near, yet his eyes remained locked on the physician. "It certainly is very odd; I do not fully comprehend it," he murmured. "Laura, listen to Doctor Spielsberg now, and compose yourself."

"You mentioned a sensation akin to two needles piercing your skin, somewhere near your neck, during your first terrible dream," the doctor asked, his voice low. "Is there still any soreness?"

"None at all," I replied.

"Can you indicate with your finger the precise point where this occurred?"

"Very little below my throat—here," I answered, pointing to the spot covered by my morning dress.

"Now you can verify it yourself," the doctor said. "You will not mind your father lowering your dress slightly. It is necessary to detect the symptom underlying your condition."

I acquiesced. The fabric fell only an inch or two below my collar.

"God bless me!—so it is!" my father exclaimed, his complexion draining of color.

"You see it now with your own eyes," the doctor stated, his triumph grim and absolute.

"What is it?" I cried, fear seizing my heart.

"Nothing, my dear young lady, but a small blue spot, no larger than the tip of your little finger," he replied, turning to my father. "Now, the question is: what course of action is best?"

"Is there any danger?" I urged, trembling.

"I trust not, my dear," the doctor answered calmly. "I see no reason why you should not recover. Indeed, I see no reason why you should not begin to improve immediately. That is the point where the sensation of strangulation begins?"

"Yes," I whispered.

"And—recall as clearly as you can—that same point served as the center of that thrill you described, like the current of a cold stream rushing against you?"

"It may have been; I think it was."

"Ay, you see?" he added, addressing my father. "Shall I speak to Madame?"

"Certainly," my father agreed.

He summoned Madame and instructed her, "I find my young friend here in a precarious state. While I hope it will not prove serious, immediate steps must be taken, which I shall explain later. For now, Madame, you must ensure Miss Laura is never alone for a single moment. This is indispensable."

"We may rely upon your kindness, Madame, I know," my father added.

Madame responded with eager assurance. "And you, dear Laura, I know you will heed the doctor's instructions."

"The doctor has another patient whose symptoms slightly resemble those of my daughter," my father interjected smoothly. "They are milder in degree, but undoubtedly of the same nature. She is a young lady, our guest. Since you are passing this way again this evening, you cannot do better than take your supper here and examine her. She does not descend until the afternoon."

"I thank you," the doctor said. "I shall be with you at approximately seven this evening."

With these final directives issued to both of us, my father departed. I watched him walk out with the doctor, pacing together along the grassy platform between the road and the moat, deeply absorbed in their serious discourse. The doctor did not return. I saw him mount his horse, bid farewell, and ride eastward into the forest.

Almost simultaneously, a man arrived from Dranfield bearing letters. He dismounted and handed the bag to my father.

Meanwhile, Madame and I were lost in conjecture regarding the singular and urgent nature of the doctor's orders. As she later confessed to me, she feared the physician anticipated a sudden seizure, believing that without prompt assistance, I might lose my life in a fit or suffer grave injury. Such an interpretation did not occur to me. Perhaps, fortunately for my nerves, I assumed the arrangement was merely a precautionary measure to prevent me from overexerting myself, consuming unripe fruit, or indulging in the myriad foolish habits attributed to youth.

About half an hour later, my father returned, holding a letter. "This correspondence was delayed; it is from General Spielsdorf. He might have arrived yesterday, or he may not come until tomorrow, though he could be here today."

He placed the open letter in my hand, yet his countenance lacked the usual pleasure he reserved for the arrival of a beloved guest. On the contrary, he seemed to wish the General lost in the depths of the Red Sea. There was clearly something troubling his mind that he refused to disclose.

"Papa, darling, will you tell me this?" I asked suddenly, placing my hand on his arm and gazing imploringly into his face.

"Perhaps," he answered, smoothing my hair caressingly over my eyes. "Does the doctor think me very ill?"

"No, dear," he replied, his tone somewhat dry. "He believes that if proper measures are taken, you will be quite well again, or at least on the high road to complete recovery within a day or two. I wish our good friend, the General, had chosen another time; that is, I wish you had been perfectly well to receive him."

"But do tell me, papa," I insisted, "what does he think is the matter with me?"

"Nothing; you must not plague me with questions," he snapped, displaying more irritation than I had ever witnessed before. Seeing my wounded expression, he kissed me and added, "You shall know all about it in a day or two; that is, all that I know. In the meantime, do not trouble your head about it."

He turned to leave but returned before I could dwell further on the oddity of the situation. He announced his intention to travel to Karnstein, ordering the carriage ready at noon. He insisted that I and Madame accompany him to consult a priest residing near the picturesque grounds on business. Since Carmilla had never visited the site, she could follow when she descended, accompanied by Mademoiselle, who would prepare provisions for what you might call a picnic, to be laid out in the ruined castle.

At noon, I was prepared. Shortly thereafter, my father, Madame, and I set out on our projected drive. Crossing the drawbridge, we turned right, following the road over the steep Gothic bridge westward toward the deserted village and the ruined castle of Karnstein.

No sylvan drive could be imagined more picturesque. The terrain broke into gentle hills and hollows, all draped in beautiful woodland, utterly devoid of the formal rigidity imposed by artificial planting and cultivation. The irregularities of the land frequently diverted the road from its straight course, causing it to wind beautifully around the sides of broken hollows and the steeper slopes of the hills, traversing varieties of ground almost inexhaustible.

Turning one of these bends, we suddenly encountered our old friend, the General, riding toward us, attended by a mounted servant. His portmanteaus followed in a hired wagon. The General dismounted as we pulled up. After the customary greetings, he was easily persuaded to accept the vacant seat in the carriage, sending his horse and servant ahead to the castle.
