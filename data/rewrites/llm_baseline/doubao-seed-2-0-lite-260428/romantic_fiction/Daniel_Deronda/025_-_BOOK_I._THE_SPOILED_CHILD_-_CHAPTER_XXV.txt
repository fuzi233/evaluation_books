How do you untangle the messy motives of a mind reduced to the barrenness of fastidious egoism? A mind where every sharp, direct desire has dulled into a vague, vacillating expectation of wanting something eventually, a patch of weeds where a single unruly impulse might sprout tall and conspicuous out of nowhere? That is the exact fate that befalls a life lived entirely unshackled by obligation, untethered to any duty that might shape its drift. The old adage holds true: total freedom is a terrible compass.

Potentates state their intentions and shift entire markets with only a handful of words. So when Henleigh Grandcourt, fresh off learning that Gwendolen Harleth had left Leubronn, casually dismissed the glittering resort as a disgusting dump far worse than Baden, Mr. Lush knew exactly what that meant: his patron intended to head straight back to his Diplow estate.

Grandcourt dragged his feet the next full day, deliberately avoiding giving any clear order to depart, just to amuse himself by making Lush wait. He lingered for hours over his toilet, descending at last with that perfectly faded, effortless air of high refinement that made every rosy-cheeked, blooded newcomer around him look crude and vulgar by comparison. He loitered on the terrace, in the gambling rooms, in the quiet reading nook, devoting all his energy to being utterly indifferent to every person and every trivial amusement around him. The only exception was when he ran into Lady Mallinger: he tipped his hat, paused, and let his languid seriousness play off her innocent chatter about the supposed health benefits of the resort springs, dryly noting “I heard someone say it’s awfully convenient that gambling towns always happen to have mineral springs.” Lady Mallinger, missing the joke entirely, trotted off to tell Sir Hugo, who laughed and said “Don’t you be fooled, my dear. That man is no fool. He plays his cards as well as any of us.” Privately, Lady Mallinger still felt a prickle of discomfort around Grandcourt—he was nothing less to her than a living reminder of what she saw as her own failure as a wife: her inability to give Sir Hugo a son of their own to inherit his title.

Daniel Deronda received a brief, deliberate nod from Grandcourt that afternoon, and he did not feel grateful for it, though he returned the greeting with perfect civility. No logic about the arbitrary rules of inheritance could erase the deep, old sting of the knowledge that but for the circumstances of his birth, all of Grandcourt’s fortune and status could have been his. Proud as he was, he refused to ever let a man like Grandcourt see even a flicker of bitterness, even the faintest hint of petty envy. He would not sink into that ugly pit of unreasoning rancor, clinging to the kind, generous parts of his nature that he valued far more than any estate. But there was no guarding against Grandcourt’s assumptions: he was fully convinced the “poor devil” of his cousin secretly ached at their unequal positions, and that made Deronda’s company far more pleasant than it would otherwise have been. The fantasy that others envied his good fortune was the favorite shadow that followed his egoism everywhere; he liked to feel he had power over people enough to make them jealous, not just his prized hunting hounds. So he even stopped to chat with Deronda on the terrace about the upcoming hunting season around Diplow, and casually invited him over to join in a few runs when the season opened.

Lush, for his part, was perfectly happy with the delay in their departure. He gossiped easily with Sir Hugo, answering all the baronet’s questions about Grandcourt’s tangled financial and personal affairs, no longer holding back on sunny autumn afternoons when there was no finer pleasure on the promenade than to speak freely of his tyrannical patron behind his back. Sir Hugo loved this kind of light, juicy gossip he called *traits de mœurs*, and never passed snippets on to anyone who might take them too seriously. He never breathed a word of Grandcourt’s secrets to Deronda, who wrote the man off as a pale, listless mortal and had no desire to hear the sordid details of how he’d ended up the way he was.

“Let me know the moment things shift,” Sir Hugo told Lush. “If that marriage looks like it might go through after all, or if any trouble pops up that makes him need cash fast. My plan for him to sell off his partial interest in Diplow will be far better for him than burdening his Ryelands estate with endless debt.” Lush agreed, but added that you could never push Grandcourt directly on what was good for him—especially if he suspected it was also good for someone else. “I’ve stuck by him for fifteen years now,” Lush added, with a put-upon loyal air. “He’d never find anyone else to put up with his peculiar ways. He could be the most charming man alive if he chose to, but he’s grown far more stubborn these past years. I do my best to steer him right.” Sir Hugo did not bother to offer sympathy or agreement; as a member of Parliament, he was well used to the convenient little stories hanger-ons wove to justify their comfortable positions.

But Lush’s stretch of easy procrastination was cut short the very next morning, when Grandcourt greeted him with a cold, sharp question: “Have you made all the arrangements for us to take the Paris train today?” Lush, not as surprised as he let on, said he had not realized they were leaving so soon. “You should have known,” Grandcourt murmured, low and disgusted as he stared at the smoldering end of his cigar. “See to everything at once. Make sure no stranger or strange brute gets into the same carriage as us, and leave my calling card for the Mallingers to say goodbye.”

They reached Paris the next day, but Grandcourt immediately sent Lush straight on ahead to Diplow to make sure the house and stables were perfectly prepared, lingering in Paris himself with his valet for three more days. When Lush finally got the telegram ordering the Diplow carriage to meet Grandcourt at Wanchester station, he had spent every spare minute digging up every scrap of gossip from the local townsfolk about Gwendolen Harleth and her family. He knew her family had been ruined utterly by a rascally bank failure, left penniless, forced to move out of Offendene into a tiny cottage little better than a laborer’s hovel. Lush could not predict how Grandcourt’s flighty obstinacy would react to the news. On one hand, Gwendolen’s poverty meant she would almost certainly accept a proposal from him now, no more risk of the humiliating rejection that had been holding Grandcourt back for months. On the other, the certainty that she would say yes was exactly the kind of boring, guaranteed outcome that might make Grandcourt lose interest entirely, drifting away like a moth that had already gotten too close to the flame.

Lush did not want this marriage to happen at all. He would have been perfectly happy if Grandcourt married a wealthy heiress, or even married his long-time mistress Mrs. Glasher: either outcome would keep Lush in his comfortable, privileged position as the man the grand household relied on. But Gwendolen? She already openly despised him. She would boot him out of the estate before the wedding bells stopped ringing, and he would lose every last bit of the easy, indulgent life he had earned after fifteen years of pandering to Grandcourt’s whims. He was determined to do everything in his power to stop the match, even if that meant throwing a few well-placed spanners in the works.

When Grandcourt arrived at Diplow, he spent the entire first day fussing over his stables, and even ordered a side-saddle strapped to his finest hunter Criterion to test the horse’s paces—a clear, unmistakeable sign of his intentions. Lush spent the whole night turning the situation over in his head, nearly resolved that he would reveal Gwendolen’s ruined circumstances the moment Grandcourt was in a talkative mood, to see how he reacted. But Grandcourt was dead silent all the next morning, answering no small talk, giving only short, sharp orders for the household. Lush got up to leave the room when Grandcourt dismissed him, only for the man to call after him, quiet and languid: “Oh, shut the door, will you? I can’t shout into the corridor.”

Lush shut the door, came back, and sat down. After a long drag on his cigar, Grandcourt said flatly, “Is Miss Harleth at Offendene?”

Lush tried to play it casual. “Well, I hardly know. Her whole family is done for. They and the Gascoignes lost every last penny in that bank fraud. The poor mother hasn’t a sou to her name. They’re being kicked out of Offendene any day now, moving into that tiny cottage on the edge of the village.”

Grandcourt’s voice dropped so low it was almost a purr of menace. “Don’t lie to me, if you please. It’s not amusing, and it serves no purpose.”

Lush was stung out of his usual careful calm. “That’s no lie. Bazley, Brackenshaw’s man, told me himself they’re already searching for a new tenant for Offendene.”

“I’m not talking about the house. Is Miss Harleth there, or not?” Grandcourt repeated.

“Upon my soul, I can’t tell,” Lush said, sullen. “She might have left yesterday. I heard she’d taken a post as a governess, for all I know she’s already gone to it. Though I suppose her mother would send for her back if you wanted to see her.”

Grandcourt did not rise to the sneer. He only said, “Send Hutchins to their house to ask if she will be there tomorrow.”

Lush did not move. All the arguments he had rehearsed for weeks came tumbling out, sharp with irritation. “You might want to think this through. This isn’t the casual half-hearted flirting you were doing before, where nothing has to mean anything. You have to decide right now if you want to be accepted—and more than that, how you’ll take it if she turns you down. You can’t hang around philandering after her for six more weeks.”

Grandcourt said nothing, only flattened the newspaper on his knees and lit a new cigar. Lush took that as a sign he was listening, and pushed harder. “Marrying her will mean you have to provide for her entire penniless family. It’ll pin you down tighter than you’ve ever been pinned before, and you don’t have that much extra cash to spare to go around supporting three generations of Harleths. Why burden your estate over a whim you’ll regret in a year?”

Grandcourt plucked the cigar from his mouth, adjusted the moist leaf between his delicate fingertips, and said, “I knew before that you objected to my marrying Miss Harleth. But I never considered that a reason not to.”

“It wasn’t my objection I was pointing out,” Lush said, dry now. “It’s the absurdity of it. You couldn’t make up your mind before, so it’s impossible you can care that deeply about her now. And you know how headstrong she is—she ran away from Leubronn for a reason, remember? She’s not the kind of girl you can predict.” Then he dropped the bomb he had been hoarding for months. “She saw Lydia Glasher that day in Cardell Chase. She heard every last word of the whole story.”

Grandcourt did not flinch. He did not move, did not change his expression, only kept smoking. Lush waited, pacing to the window, expecting him to blow up, to call the whole thing off, to realize how impossible it would be to court a girl who already knew his darkest secret. But at last Grandcourt looked up at him, narrow eyes steady, and said, perfectly cool: “What follows?”

Lush realized he had been outplayed. There was no more argument to make. He shrugged one shoulder, ready to storm out, when Grandcourt nodded at the desk. “Oblige me by pushing that pen and paper over here, will you?” The quiet, unshakable authority he carried was impossible to resist. Lush passed the stationery over, and Grandcourt scrawled a short, brief note in seconds, addressed it to Gwendolen Harleth, Offendene. “Send Hutchins off with this right away,” he said, pushing the letter across the desk.

Lush left the room seething, when his anger cooled he knew two things for certain. There was a permanent mark against him now, and Grandcourt would make him pay for that little reveal sooner or later. And worst of all? The secret he had spilled to scare Grandcourt off had not put him off at all. It had only hardened his resolve, making him more determined than ever to claim Gwendolen Harleth as his own.
