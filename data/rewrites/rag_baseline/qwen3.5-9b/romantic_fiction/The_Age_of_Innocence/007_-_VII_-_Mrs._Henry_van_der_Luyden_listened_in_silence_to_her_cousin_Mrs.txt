Mrs. Henry van der Luyden listened. The silence was not empty; it was a vessel filled with the weight of expectation. She had prepared herself for this moment, aligning her internal compass with the rigid geography of her own character: silent by nature, kind by training, yet perpetually distant. Yet, even this preparation offered no shield against the cold that settled in the high-ceilinged, white-walled drawing room of Madison Avenue. The air was still, preserving the pale brocaded armchairs, untouched and waiting, like artifacts in a museum. Gauze veiled the ormolu ornaments; the frame of Gainsborough's "Lady Angelica du Lac" stood behind the glass, a ghost of a past century.

Beneath the portrait by Huntington, Mrs. van der Luyden sat. In black velvet and Venetian point, she mirrored the fair ancestress above her. Twenty years had passed, yet the resemblance was uncanny, a mathematical precision of preservation. Her hair, faded to a silver-white without ever truly greying, lay in flat, overlapping points across her forehead. The straight nose dividing her pale blue eyes retained the same slight pinch at the nostrils. To Newland Archer, she appeared less like a living woman and more like a specimen caught in a glacier—a body suspended in an airless atmosphere of irreproachable existence, holding onto a rosy life-in-death.

He admired her, as his family did, but found her gentle sweetness impenetrable, colder perhaps than the fierce, principled "No"s of his mother's aunties. Mrs. van der Luyden offered neither affirmation nor denial. Instead, her thin lips wavered into the shadow of a smile, delivering the inevitable refrain: "I shall first have to talk this over with my husband."

She and Mr. van der Luyden were two halves of a single equation, merged after forty years of conjugality. How they managed to separate themselves enough to deliberate on matters as controversial as the current crisis remained a mystery to Archer. Neither could reach a conclusion without the preliminary conclave. Thus, Mrs. Archer and her son waited, resigned, for the familiar phrase.

Instead, Mrs. van der Luyden reached her long hand toward the bell-rope. "I think," she said, "I should like Henry to hear what you have told me."

A footman appeared. With grave precision, she instructed him: "If Mr. van der Luyden has finished reading the newspaper, please ask him to be kind enough to come." She spoke of the newspaper with the detached dignity of a minister addressing a cabinet meeting—not from arrogance, but because a lifetime of conditioning had elevated his least gestures to a sacerdotal level of importance. Her promptness suggested urgency, yet she softened the blow with a sweet, calculated look: "Henry always enjoys seeing you, dear Adeline; and he will wish to congratulate Newland."

The double doors opened. Between them stepped Mr. Henry van der Luyden. Tall, spare, and frock-coated, he possessed faded fair hair, a straight nose identical to his wife's, and the same expression of frozen gentleness in eyes that were merely pale grey. He greeted Mrs. Archer with cousinly affability, offered Newland congratulations couched in the same sterile language, and seated himself in a brocade armchair with the simplicity of a reigning sovereign.

"I had just finished reading the Times," he announced, laying his long fingertips together. "In town, my mornings are so much occupied that I find it more convenient to read the newspapers after luncheon."

"Ah, there's a great deal to be said for that plan," Mrs. Archer responded, seizing the opening. "Indeed, I think my uncle Egmont used to say he found it less agitating not to read the morning papers till after dinner."

"Yes," Mr. van der Luyden agreed in measured tones, surveying the large, shrouded room—an image of his own owners, complete and static. "My good father abhorred hurry. But now we live in a constant rush."

"But I hope you *had* finished your reading, Henry?" his wife interposed.

"Quite—quite," he reassured her. "Then I should like Adeline to tell you—"

"Oh, it's really Newland's story," his mother smiled, proceeding to rehearse the monstrous tale of the affront inflicted on Mrs. Lovell Mingott. "Of course," she concluded, "Augusta Welland and Mary Mingott both felt that, especially in view of Newland's engagement, you and Henry *ought to know*."

"Ah—" Mr. van der Luyden drew a deep breath.

Silence stretched, thick and heavy. The tick of the monumental ormolu clock on the white marble mantelpiece grew louder, rising to the volume of a minute-gun. Archer watched with awe the two slender, faded figures seated side by side in a viceregal rigidity, mouthpieces of some remote ancestral authority they would far rather have discarded to dig invisible weeds in the lawns of Skuytercliff.

Mr. van der Luyden broke the spell. "You really think this is due to some... some intentional interference of Lawrence Lefferts's?" he enquired, turning to Archer.

"I'm certain of it, sir," Archer replied. "Larry has been going it rather harder than usual lately—if cousin Louisa won't mind my mentioning it—having rather a stiff affair with the postmaster's wife in their village, or someone of that sort. And whenever poor Gertrude Lefferts begins to suspect anything, and he's afraid of trouble, he gets up a fuss of this kind, to show how awfully moral he is, and talks at the top of his voice about the impertinence of inviting his wife to meet people he doesn't wish her to know. He's simply using Madame Olenska as a lightning-rod; I've seen him try the same thing often before."

"The Leffertses!—" Mrs. van der Luyden gasped.

"The Leffertses!—" echoed Mrs. Archer. "What would uncle Egmont have said of Lawrence Lefferts's pronouncing on anybody's social position? It shows what Society has come to."

"We'll hope it has not quite come to that," Mr. van der Luyden said firmly.

"Ah, if only you and Louisa went out more!" sighed Mrs. Archer. Instantly, she realized her error. The van der Luydens were morbidly sensitive to any criticism of their secluded existence. They were the arbiters of fashion, the Court of Last Appeal, and they knew it, bowing to their fate. Yet, being shy and retiring, with no natural inclination for their part, they lived as much as possible in the sylvan solitude of Skuytercliff, declining all invitations on the plea of Mrs. van der Luyden's health.

Newland Archer came to his mother's rescue. "Everybody in New York knows what you and cousin Louisa represent. That's why Mrs. Mingott felt she ought not to allow this slight on Countess Olenska to pass without consulting you."

Mrs. van der Luyden glanced at her husband, who glanced back. "It is the principle that I dislike," said Mr. van der Luyden. "As long as a member of a well-known family is backed up by that family it should be considered—final."

"It seems so to me," his wife said, as if producing a new thought.

"I had no idea," Mr. van der Luyden continued, "that things had come to such a pass." He paused, looking at his wife again. "It occurs to me, my dear, that the Countess Olenska is already a sort of relation—through Medora Manson's first husband. At any rate, she will be when Newland marries."

He turned toward the young man. "Have you read this morning's Times, Newland?"

"Why, yes, sir," said Archer, who usually tossed off half a dozen papers with his morning coffee.

Husband and wife looked at each other again. Their pale eyes clung together in prolonged, serious consultation; then a faint smile fluttered over Mrs. van der Luyden's face. She had evidently guessed and approved.

Mr. van der Luyden turned to Mrs. Archer. "If Louisa's health allowed her to dine out—I wish you would say to Mrs. Lovell Mingott—she and I would have been happy to—er—fill the places of the Lawrence Leffertses at her dinner." He paused to let the irony of this sink in. "As you know, this is impossible."

Mrs. Archer sounded a sympathetic assent. "But Newland tells me he has read this morning's Times; therefore he has probably seen that Louisa's relative, the Duke of St. Austrey, arrives next week on the Russia. He is coming to enter his new sloop, the Guinevere, in next summer's International Cup Race; and also to have a little canvasback shooting at Trevenna."

Mr. van der Luyden paused again, continuing with increasing benevolence: "Before taking him down to Maryland we are inviting a few friends to meet him here—only a little dinner—with a reception afterward. I am sure Louisa will be as glad as I am if Countess Olenska will let us include her among our guests."
