Tito's meticulous scheming had been rendered obsolete by a trifling accident, a variable too insignificant to factor into the calculus of a mastermind. Yet, fate conspired to place him walking beside Romola on this very evening, rendering her presence an insurmountable obstacle. Life remained a labyrinthine game where the sharpest strategies were routinely dismantled by the chaotic drift of circumstance, unpredictable as falling dandelion seeds. Tito cared little for the collapse of Spini's plot; rather, he faced a tactical dilemma: how to deliver warnings to both Savonarola and Spini without triggering suspicion. Alerting the friar immediately, following the delivery of the forged letters, would invite unfavorable conjecture regarding his motives. Conversely, approaching Spini instantly required confessing the truth—that Savonarola had altered his course—which Spini's sharp intellect would interpret solely as Tito's sudden reversal and sabotage of the operation. Delaying the warning to the friar until morning carried the high probability of losing the chance to stop Spini entirely, leaving the Compagnacci faction to return in a fury of thwarted ambition.

This was the risk Tito elected to accept, banking on his ability to soothe Spini by attributing the failure to the Friar's mere caution. Annoyed, though finding it difficult to suppress such emotion given his usual composure, Tito resolved to avoid further contact with Romola and declined to return home that night.

Inside the house, Romola sat vigil through the darkness, refusing to remove her garments. She listened as the rain intensified, a rhythmic drumming that she found strangely comforting; the tempestuous sky seemed a divine shield against human machinations, forcing men into paralysis. Yet, her own mind was besieged by a dual torment: profound uncertainty regarding her husband's actions and a gnawing doubt concerning her own complicity. What falsehoods might he have woven? What designs remained hidden from her view? Trusting Tito was perilous; to convince herself otherwise was futile. Was she not acting out of selfish pride by shrinking from the duty to warn others against him? *"If her husband was a criminal,"* she reasoned, *"her proper station was within the prison walls beside him."* That sentiment held weight, yet it did not absolve her. Could a wife permit her spouse to commit acts that would brand him a malefactor when she possessed the agency to intervene? Prayer felt impossible; her agitated thoughts precluded the necessary state of expectant passivity. As the night wore on, her imagination spun frantic scenarios of how Tito might evade her threats. Just as dawn approached, the violence of the storm subsided, the wind dispersing the clouds to reveal a clear morning that only exacerbated her unease.

Wrapping her mantle tighter, Romola rushed to the loggia, scanning the vast landscape as if some external detail might dictate her next move, though she suspected only rooftops hid the path toward the Palazzo Vecchio where Savonarola might walk toward treachery. Should she seek her godfather? Might he act preemptively to block Fra Girolamo from crossing the gates without explicit proof? But that might be too late. With fresh distress, Romola realized she had gleaned no concrete intelligence from Tito, and the hour had long passed seven. There was no alternative.

She descended the stairs, ignoring the sick members of her household, and stepped out onto the Via de' Bardi, heading swiftly toward the Ponte Vecchio. She intended to traverse the city's heart via the most direct route; furthermore, the great Piazza offered a slim chance of intercepting her husband. By some lingering hope, he might appear there, clarify the Friar's status, and render a trip to San Marco unnecessary. Upon reaching the courtyard of the Palazzo Vecchio, she peered eagerly into the colonnade, then swept her gaze across the Piazza. The familiar silhouette, once etched by youthful affection and now scarred by suffering, was absent. Undeterred, she pressed onward to the Piazza del Duomo.

The square teemed with activity: worshippers ascending and descending the marble steps, men pausing for conversation, vendors laden with goods. Amidst this throng, Romola spotted her husband. Having exited San Marco, he had diverted into Nello's shop and was now leaning casually against the doorpost. As she drew nearer, she observed him conversing with effortless nonchalance, holding his cap in one hand and tossing back his freshly combed hair. The jarring contrast between this display of ease and the bitter anxieties he had instilled convulsed her with indignation; the renewed vision of his hardness intensified her dread. Standing nearby were Cronaca and two other regulars of San Marco. A sudden resolve struck her: *"I will force him to speak before these witnesses."*

Her light step closed the distance before Tito could retreat, coinciding with Cronaca's announcement, "Here comes Madonna Romola." A subtle shock rippled through Tito's frame as he confronted his wife. She appeared gaunt from sleepless vigilance, yet a flash of intensity beyond mere worry ignited in her eyes.

"Has the Frate crossed the gates?" she demanded.

"No," Tito replied, feeling utterly powerless before her scrutiny. He summoned every ounce of self-command to maintain a facade of simple surprise.

"And are you certain he is not departing?" she pressed.

"I am certain he is not going."

"That suffices," Romola declared, turning up the steps to seek refuge within the Duomo, needing solitude to quell her agitation.

Tito harbored no emotion closer to hatred than the one tracking her ascent. Present among the crowd were not only devoted followers of the Friar but also Ser Ceccone, the notary. At that moment, like Tito, Ceccone operated covertly as an agent of the Medici. Ser Francesco di Ser Barone, universally despised as Ser Ceccone, lacked learning, good looks, success, and generosity. He was a traitor devoid of charisma, and consequently, he held no affection for Tito Melema.
