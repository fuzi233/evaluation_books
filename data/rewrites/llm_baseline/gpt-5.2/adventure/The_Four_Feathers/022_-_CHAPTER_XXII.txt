Captain Willoughby had the sort of voice that could fill a room the way steam fills a scullery: everywhere at once, dampening talk, softening resistance, leaving only the slow drip of endurance. At his club he was famous—famous in the specific, weary way a bad smell becomes famous in a kitchen. He told stories about people nobody knew, threaded with maxims nobody wanted, and he delivered each stale morsel as if it had just come sizzling from a pan.

Men who had learned the sound of him—his approaching tread, the clearing of his throat, the first confident, meaningless “I remember a fellow—”—would lift their papers and flee. There was a small choreography to it, as practiced as a dining-room drill: chairs scraping, murmured excuses, a sudden need to see a man about a horse. Willoughby always chose the thickest knot of listeners, like a wasp choosing the sweetest fruit, and within half an hour the most popular corner would be empty but for him.

On an early October afternoon he sat in possession of his usual kingdom of deserted leather chairs and stale cigar-smoke. The club’s air had that familiar mixture—polished wood, old wool, extinguished matches, and tobacco that had seeped into the curtains over decades like a sauce reduced too far and clung to the bottom of the pan. Outside, London’s modern racket—iron wheels, shouted news, the ceaseless grind of traffic—pressed at the windows. Inside, the room’s quiet had a gentler soundscape: pages turned, a glass set down, the soft consonants of men trying not to be overheard.

A waiter crossed to him and offered a visiting card.

Willoughby took it with greedy relief. Company was a kind of sustenance to him; he could not taste his own talk unless someone sat opposite to swallow it. But the name on the card drained his eagerness as if someone had sluiced cold water through a warm cup.

“Colonel Durrance!” he said, scratching his head.

Durrance had never, in all the years of acquaintance, sought him out for friendliness. If Durrance came now, he came for a reason; and Willoughby’s mind, slow as it was, could still sniff danger the way a cook sniffs milk on the turn.

“Does Colonel Durrance know I am in the club?” he asked.

“Yes, sir.”

“Very well. Show him in.”

He told himself, with a stubborn little pride, that he had no intention of meddling further in Miss Ethne Eustace’s affairs. Feversham and Durrance could fight their own campaign. Willoughby had already gone as far as he meant—too far, perhaps. He did not doubt his own diplomacy; he only disliked spending it in this particular currency.

Durrance entered with the quiet assurance of a man who has learned to measure rooms by sound rather than sight. He moved as if he were listening to the air’s contours—the hum of the place, the soft footfalls, the angle of a voice—and Willoughby, watching him, felt that uncomfortable sensation of being mapped without being seen.

Willoughby rose and guided him to the empty corner.

“Will you smoke?” he began, then stopped, remembering, and flushed. “I beg your pardon.”

“Oh, I’ll smoke,” Durrance answered easily. “It’s not quite true that a man can’t enjoy his tobacco without seeing the smoke of it. If I let my cigar go out I should know at once. But you will see—I shall not let it out.”

He took his time striking a match, shielding the flame, bringing it to the cigar as carefully as if he were searing the surface of meat to seal in juices. The first draw was measured; the ember caught; a faint crackle like dry leaves sounded at the tip. He leaned back, face angled upward, as if he could read the ceiling’s patterns by memory.

“I am lucky to find you, Willoughby,” he said. “I am only in town for to-day. I come up from Devonshire now and then to see my oculist, and I was anxious to meet you if I could. On my last visit Mather told me you were away in the country. You remember Mather? He was with us in Suakin.”

Willoughby’s relief was immediate. Mather was safe ground: the talk of campaigns and men and posted orders, none of it pointing like a bayonet at Kingsbridge.

“Of course I remember him,” he said, and meant it. He clung to the name as to a familiar flavour.

“We are both curious,” Durrance continued, “and you can clear up the point we are curious about. Did you ever come across an Arab called Abou Fatma?”

Willoughby repeated the name slowly, as though rolling a seed on his tongue to decide whether it would crack.

“Abou Fatma… one of the Hadendoas?”

“No. A man of the Kabbabish tribe.”

“Abou Fatma,” Willoughby said again. “No, I never came across him.” He stopped there—too abruptly.

The silence that followed had its own sound: the faint hiss of a lamp, the scrape of a chair far away, the soft pressure of London outside. Durrance, trained by necessity to hear what men did not say, noted that unnatural stopping-place as clearly as if it were written on paper.

“That makes me still more curious,” Durrance went on. “Mather and I were together on the last reconnaissance in ’84, and we found Abou Fatma hiding in the bushes by the Sinkat fort. He told us about the Gordon letters which he had hidden in Berber. Ah—you remember his name now.”

“I was merely getting my pipe out of my pocket,” Willoughby said.

“But you do remember the name now that I mention the letters?”

“Yes. I remember it,” Willoughby admitted, and felt, too late, that the admission was a door left ajar.

“They were brought to you in Suakin fifteen months or so back,” Durrance said. “Mather showed me the paragraph in the Evening Standard. I am curious whether Abou Fatma returned to Berber and recovered them. But since you have never come across him, it follows he was not the man.”

A small nausea of regret moved in Willoughby. He had been too quick to deny—too eager to be clean of the name. He tried to patch the hole.

“No. It was not Abou Fatma,” he said, awkwardly, hearing in his own voice that particular strain men use when they are lifting a pot they know is too heavy and praying not to drop it.

Durrance did not pounce. He let the matter sit, as if he were letting flavours settle.

“I wondered,” he said slowly. “I thought Abou Fatma would hardly return to Berber. For whoever undertook the job undertook it at the risk of his life—and since Gordon was dead, for no very obvious reason.”

“Quite so,” Willoughby said quickly, relieved at the retreat.

“For no obvious reason, I think I said,” Durrance remarked, mild as milk, but with a thin edge that could curdle it.

Willoughby glanced at him with suspicion. He wondered how much Durrance knew. But Durrance lay back, smoking, the cigar’s ember brightening and dimming with each measured breath. He seemed to have lost all interest in the Gordon letters now that he had touched them—like a man who, having tested the crust of a loaf, is satisfied there is warmth within and turns to other matters.

Yet Durrance’s mind, unseen, was busy. The past years had taught him to weigh pauses, to listen for caution in a word, to hear the tension in a throat. Ethne Eustace’s voice had once had that same careful accent, that same guardedness, and it stung him now to hear it echoed. He had laid small traps—the phrase about “no obvious reason” among them—and he had felt Willoughby’s tiny starts as surely as a fisherman feels a tremor on the line.

He spoke again, and his voice changed, becoming more deliberate, more intimate.

“I want to tell you why I was anxious to meet you. It was because of Harry Feversham.”

Willoughby jumped in his chair as if a coal had dropped on his knee. Durrance did not acknowledge the movement.

“Something happened to Feversham,” he said. “More than five years ago now. He did something, I suppose—or left something undone. The secret has been closely kept. He dropped out, and his place knew him no more.” He turned his head slightly, listening to Willoughby’s breathing. “Now you are going back to the Soudan?”

“Yes,” Willoughby answered. “In a week’s time.”

“Well, Harry Feversham is in the Soudan,” Durrance said, leaning forward, the chair leather sighing under him.

“You know that?” Willoughby blurted.

“I do. I came across him this spring at Wadi Halfa,” Durrance replied, and he told of the meeting outside the café at Tewfikieh—of the ruin and squalor, the man who had once stood straight and clean among friends now fallen low. He spoke with the flat honesty of someone who has no use for pity as ornament. “It’s strange, isn’t it? A man you knew well going under like that in a second—disappearing before your eyes, dropping plumb out of sight as though down an oubliette in an old French castle.”

Outside, as Durrance spoke, a cart rumbled by, iron-shod wheels biting the stones. London’s noise, constant as a factory, had an indifference that made private grief feel smaller, squeezed thin between clanging days.

“I want you to look out for him, Willoughby,” Durrance said. “Do what you can to set him on his legs again. Let me know if you chance on him. Feversham was a friend of mine—one of my few real friends.”

“All right,” Willoughby said, and his cheerfulness came too easily, like a ladling of sauce meant to hide a burned taste. “I will look out for him. I remember he was a great friend of yours.”

He reached for the matches. The phosphorus scraped; the match flared. He put it to his pipe, sucking at the stem. The briar was well-seasoned but badly in need of cleaning; it bubbled wetly as it drew. The smell of scorched tobacco rose—sharp, sour, like a spice used without care.

“Yes,” Durrance said. “A great friend. You and I dined with him in his flat above St. James’s Park just before we left England.”

At that casual sentence the pipe stopped bubbling, abruptly, as if the breath behind it had been held. There was a moment of stillness. Then Willoughby swore—an ugly, violent oath—and the next instant he stamped on the carpet.

Durrance’s imagination, quickened by sound, painted the scene with cruel precision: Willoughby staring at the blind man’s face, searching it for knowledge; the match burning down unnoticed to the fingers; the curse, the dropped flame, the stamp to kill it. The smallest domestic accident had betrayed a whole hidden panic.

Durrance had not thought of that dinner in years. Now it unfolded in his mind like a cloth pulled from a covered dish, releasing a smell long trapped.

“There were you and I and Feversham present,” he went on, as if he were merely recounting an old menu. “Feversham asked us there to tell us of his engagement to Miss Eustace. He had just come back from Dublin. That was almost the last we saw of him.” He took a pull at the cigar, then added, lightly, “By the way, there was a third man present.”

“Was there?” Willoughby asked too quickly. “It’s so long ago.”

“Yes—Trench.”

“To be sure,” Willoughby said. “Trench was present. It will be a long time, I am afraid, before we dine at the same table with poor old Trench again.”

His carelessness sounded manufactured, like tinned food arranged on a platter. He leaned forward, struck another match, and tried to light his pipe again with elaborate calm.

Durrance laid his cigar along the table edge. The club’s table had the faint tackiness of long-spilled spirits wiped too late.

“And we shall never dine with Castleton again,” Durrance said slowly.

“Castleton wasn’t there,” Willoughby exclaimed, quick enough to show that the dinner was not distant at all in his mind, not softened by time’s cooking.

“No. But he was expected,” Durrance said.

“No—not even expected,” Willoughby corrected. “He was dining elsewhere. He sent the telegram, you remember.”

“Ah, yes,” said Durrance. “A telegram came.”

That small correction, offered so eagerly, landed in Durrance’s mind with the weight of a stone dropped into a well. The water at the bottom was dark, but the splash told him where it was.

Castleton. Trench. Willoughby. The three names circled around Feversham’s disgrace like birds around carrion. Durrance tried to recover the evening in detail: Feversham’s bright room high above the park, the cool darkness outside, the tattoo from Wellington Barracks tapping the air like a heartbeat. He remembered himself leaning by the window, listening, while at the table Feversham read a telegram—read it again—and then, without a word, crumpled it and threw it into the fire.

Now, today, Willoughby had named the sender.

“Castleton was dining with a big man from the War Office that night,” Durrance said, and heard a small movement beside him—Willoughby shifting, heated by the searchlight suddenly turned in his direction.

Durrance, however, did not press harder. He talked on for a little while about the prospects of the Soudan—of routes and garrisons and the old, dry taste of desert wind—until the moment felt safe enough to end. Then he rose.

“Well,” he said, “I can rely on you, Willoughby, to help Feversham if ever you find him. Draw on me for money.”

“I will do my best,” Willoughby answered, and the words came with a swift eagerness, as though generosity might blot out suspicion. “You are going? I could have won a bet off you this afternoon.”

“How?” Durrance asked.

“You said you did not let your cigars go out. This one’s stone cold.”

Durrance’s hand went to the cigar on the table. He had not noticed; or rather, he had noticed only other flames.

“I forgot about it,” he said quietly. “I was thinking of Feversham. Good-bye.”

He took a cab from the club door and drove away.

Willoughby, left behind, exhaled like a man who has finally set down a tray of glasses without dropping one. He congratulated himself on his diplomacy. Poor old Durrance—blind, sentimental, easy to mislead. It would have been strange indeed if Willoughby had not been able to hoodwink him. He returned to the smoking-room and ordered a whiskey and potass, letting the sharp medicinal tang sting his nose and throat as if it could cleanse the air of anxiety.

But Durrance had not been hoodwinked.

As the cab rolled over London’s stones, the city’s noise rose and fell around him—hoofbeats, wheels, distant shouts—an industrial tide that drowned subtle things. Yet within that roar, Durrance’s mind held to the small, decisive sounds of the afternoon: the pipe’s bubbling cut off, the oath, the stamp, the too-quick protest about Castleton. Those were the true words, spoken by accident.

He saw the sequence now complete—not the secret thoughts, not the inexplicable moment of panic, but the chain of facts, linked cleanly one to the next. At the dinner no one had mentioned the sender of the telegram. Feversham had read it in silence, and in silence had fed it to the flames. To-day Willoughby had named Castleton. Castleton had been dining with a high official of the War Office. And Durrance knew, with the cold certainty of a man aligning evidence, that Feversham had not resigned when that telegram came.

The message could have carried only one kind of news: that Feversham’s regiment was ordered on active service.

Almost the next day Feversham had told Durrance in the Row that he had resigned his commission.

The story, at last, stood clear: the act of cowardice, the three white feathers at Ramelton, the disappearance. And beyond it, equally clear now, another truth—harder, simpler, and, to Durrance, of real importance: Harry Feversham was atoning for that one act with a full and overflowing measure of atonement.

Why he had been seized with panic at that particular moment—why, just after his engagement to Ethne Eustace, when every manly quality ought to have been strongest—remained a question that would never be answered for Durrance. It belonged to that region of life he had learned, painfully, to set aside: the private, untranslatable core where motives live beyond any dominant lexicon’s reach, beyond any neat vocabulary of honour or shame. The world might name the act. It could not name the sensation inside it.

“I shall astonish old Sutch,” Durrance thought, and the thought carried a brief, rough chuckle.

That evening he took the night mail down into Devonshire, back toward quieter country sound—hedgerows in wind, wheels softened by earth, the old rural hush that let a man hear himself think. Before midday he reached his home.
