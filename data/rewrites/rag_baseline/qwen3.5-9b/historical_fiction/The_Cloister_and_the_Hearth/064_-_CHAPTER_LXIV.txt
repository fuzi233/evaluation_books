In a chamber draped in the opulent textures of wealth, where the floor was paved with the striped and spotted hides of beasts, a woman sat poised in a tableau of torment. Her arms stretched rigidly forward, fingers curled into claws of tension, mirroring the violent oscillation of her countenance. One moment her pallor suggested death, the next a flush of crimson burned across her cheeks, while her foot tapped a frantic, restless rhythm against the ground. Suddenly, the heavy curtain parted, drawn by a silent servant. The woman's brow flared with heat. The maid, trembling in awe, whispered, "Altezza, the man is here."

With a sharp command to admit him, the lady seized a small black mask and pressed it over her face. Instantly, the color drained from her visage, leaving a stark, spectral contrast between the jet-black fabric and the marble-white skin beneath—a sight both strange and terrifying. A man entered, bowing and scraping with the mechanical precision of a crowd-made figure: short hair, a roundish head, dressed plainly yet decently, possessing features neither handsome nor forbidding. There was nothing remarkable about him save for a pair of eyes that darted with singular, unsettling restlessness.

After a profusion of deferential bows, he halted opposite the lady, awaiting her pleasure. "They have told you for what you are wanted?" she asked.
"Yes, Signora."
"Did those who spoke to you agree as to what you are to receive?"
"Yes, Signora. 'Tis the full price; and purchases the greater vendetta: unless of your benevolence you choose to content yourself with the lesser."

"I understand you not," the lady replied, her voice cool despite the fever in her veins.
"Ah; this is the Signora's first lesson," the man corrected smoothly. "The lesser vendetta claims the death of the body only. We watch our man emerge from a church, or seize him in an innocent hour, and so deal with him. In the greater vendetta, however, we watch him, catching him hot from some unrepented sin, and thus slay his soul as well as his body. But this vendetta is not so run upon now as it was a few years ago."

"Man, silence me his tongue, and let his treasonable heart beat no more. But his soul I have no feud with."
"So be it, Signora. He who spoke to me knew not the man, nor his name, nor his abode. From whom shall I learn these?"
"From myself."

At this, the man displayed the first flicker of anxiety he had shown since entering. He entreated her to be cautious, particular, in this delicate part of the business.
"Fear me not," she said, her tone dropping to a dangerous whisper. "Listen. It is a young man, tall of stature, with auburn hair and dark blue eyes, and an honest face that would deceive a saint. He lives in the Via Claudia, at the corner house—the glover's. In that house lodge but three males: he; a painter short of stature and dark-visaged; and a young, slim boy. He that hath betrayed me is a stranger, fair, and taller than thou art."

The bravo listened with every sense alert, his restless eyes narrowing.
"It is enough," he murmured. "Stay, Signora; haunteth he any secret place where I may deal with him?"
"My spy doth report me he hath of late frequented the banks of the Tiber after dusk," she continued, her voice rising with venomous delight. "Doubtless to meet his light o' love, who calls me her rival; even there slay him! And let my rival come and find him; the smooth, heartless, insolent traitor."
"Be calm, Signora. He will betray no more ladies."
"I know not that. He weareth a sword, and can use it. He is young and resolute."
"Neither will avail him."
"Are ye so sure of your hand? What are your weapons?"

The bravo produced a steel gauntlet. "We strike with such force we need must guard our hand. This is our mallet." He then undid his doublet, revealing a coat of mail beneath, and finally laid his glittering stiletto on the table with a flourish. The lady shuddered at first, but presently she took it up in her white hand and tested its point against her finger.
"Beware, madam," warned the bravo.
"What? Is it poisoned?"
"Saints forbid! We steal no lives. We take them with steel point, not drugs. But 'tis newly ground, and I feared for the Signora's white skin."
"His skin is as white as mine," she said, a sudden, fleeting gleam of pity crossing her features. It vanished as quickly as it arrived. "But his heart is black as soot. Say, do I not well to remove a traitor that slanders me?"
"The Signora will settle that with her confessor. I am but a tool in noble hands; like my stiletto."

The princess appeared not to hear the speaker. Lost in the labyrinth of her own emotions, she muttered, "Oh, how I could have loved him; to the death; as now I hate him. Fool! He will learn to trifle with princes; to spurn them and fawn on them, and prefer the scum of the town to them, and make them a by-word." She looked up, her eyes blazing. "Why loiter'st thou here? Haste thee, revenge me."
"It is customary to pay half the price beforehand, Signora."
"Ah! I forgot; thy revenge is bought. Here is more than half," she said, pushing a heavy bag across the table to him. "When the blow is struck, come for the rest."
"You will soon see me again, Signora."

And he retired, bowing and scraping until he disappeared. The princess, burning with jealousy, mortified pride, and a profound dread of exposure—for until she knew Gerard, no public stain had fallen on her—sat where he left her. Masked, with her arms straight out before her and the nails of her clenched hand nipping the hard surface of the table, she resembled the fabled Sphinx. Or rather, a tigress coiled in ambush.

Yet, as the assassin faded from view, a chilling realization crept upon her. Moody misgivings heaved within her breast, precursors of vain remorse. *Gerard and Margaret were before their age,* she thought, the names echoing in the hollow room. *This was your true mediaeval.* Proud, amorous, vindictive, generous, foolish, cunning, impulsive, unprincipled—and ignorant as dirt. Power was the curse of such a creature. Forced to do her own crimes, the weakness of her nerves might have balanced the violence of her passions, making her bark worse than her bite. But power gave a feeble, furious woman male instruments. And the effect was as terrible as the combination was unnatural. In this instance, it had whetted an assassin's dagger for a poor, forlorn wretch just meditating suicide.
