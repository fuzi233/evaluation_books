Rocked gently by the soft swell of the Mediterranean all night long, Romola drifted out of fevered dreams of the Bargello’s flickering tapers into a deep, untroubled slumber, then back again to half-waking visions where dark, anguished scenes dissolved in an ever-brightening glow. When her eyes fluttered open at last, that glow was no taper’s flame: it was the full golden light of early morning.

Her small boat rested motionless in a sheltered, hidden creek. To her right stretched the sea, a flawless sapphire so bright it seemed to hum under the rising sun. To her left lay one of those timeless, gentle coastal landscapes that loop along the Italian shore like a half-remembered lullaby: a deep curve of fertile green land cupped between soft, tree-draped mountain slopes that rose slow and easy toward craggy, sunlit peaks. Winding pathways glinted through the oak and olive boughs, leading up to a huddle of stone buildings that had clambered haphazardly up the mountainside, its tall, slim belfry raised high to be visible to every cottage and farmstead scattered across the valley below. Only the far, western edge of this crescent nook lay bathed in direct sun; every other corner was wrapped in dewy, cool shadow. No sound stirred across the stillness. Even the waves lapped so softly at the shingle it seemed the sea itself had curved into this cove just to rest.

The warm sun fell across Romola’s face and limbs like a tender caress, and for a long, perfect moment she lay motionless, not thinking, not watching, simply steeped in that quiet peace. Youth brings such moments sometimes, in the first blur of waking: passive existence feels like its own healing, a bliss untethered to memory, unburdened by desire. The agonies of the past months, the despair that had driven her to push off from Florence’s shore the evening before, all slipped away like the dark Bargello dream, and for a breath it felt as if the soft, idle daydreams of her girlhood had risen unbroken to wrap around her again. She did not even dare to hope she could stay here forever — she only felt, at last, that she was resting.

Then memory seeped back. This was the same small boat that had carried her across the dark waters all night, the boat she had half-expected would be her bier. Instead, it had lulled her into a new life. The despair of the previous evening melted: she was glad to see the sun rise again, glad to be alive in familiar light rather than lost in the cold unknown of death. Could she stay here, hidden? No echo of Florence’s feuds, no trace of the betrayals that had broken her, would ever reach this quiet cove. For a little while, at least, she could stop fighting, stop resolving, just be. She would go ashore, find bread and milk from the village, and nestle into the green quiet until the jagged edges of her grief softened.

She had turned back to gaze at the sun-dappled valley, to hold fast to that soothing peace, when a piercing, unceasing cry cut through the stillness. It was the wail of a small child, in pain, left untended. Romola started to her feet, one foot already over the boat’s gunwale, before she paused to listen, half-expecting a mother to appear and hush the sound. But the cry did not stop. It grew louder, more piteous, the sharper for the utter silence that had reigned only moments before, and it drew her irresistibly ashore. She scrambled over a low broken stone fence, pushing through weedy unharvested crops and patches of overripe, neglected corn, toward the crumpled half-ruined hovel she had spotted a dozen yards inland. The air grew thick and oppressive the further she got from the sea breeze, the green luxuriance around her suddenly feeling cloying, almost dangerous.

The moment she stepped across the hovel’s shadowed threshold, she froze. On the straw-strewn floor lay three dead bodies: a tall broad-shouldered man, a thin little girl no more than eight years old, and a young woman whose long black hair was being tugged at, over and over, by a tiny living toddler — the source of the desperate crying.

Years of tending the sick through Florence’s repeated plague outbreaks made Romola’s hands and mind move fast. She lifted the wailing little boy into her arms, pressing him to her chest to soothe his sobs, even as her eyes took in their distinct Sephardic features, their unfamiliar woolen garb. She guessed at once: they were Spanish or Portuguese Jews, refugees fleeing the Inquisition, set ashore by greedy sailors who had stolen all their possessions and left them to die. It was a cruelty that had grown far too common on these shores: greed chased them from the sea, and superstitious fear chased them back again. She reached to feel if the young mother still had a pulse, lifting the edge of the yellow wool drape across her bosom — and spotted the dark purple plague spots blistering her skin.

Romola’s blood ran cold. If the villagers knew a plague party had washed ashore here, they would slam their doors on her and the child rather than risk contact. But she had coin to offer, and so she set off at once toward the clustered cottages higher up the valley, trying to hush the little one by feeding him small bits of wild fig she had plucked from a wayside tree. The further she walked, the eerier the scene grew: fields of corn left rotting uncut, figs and apples browning on the ground, no cows, no goats, no villagers out tending to their work at this late morning hour. The first door she peered through stood open to utter empty darkness. The second held a man dead on his earthen floor, his head fallen across the handle of his spade, an earthen pitcher still clutched in his hand as if he had dropped mid-step, overtaken by the fever in an instant. The whole village was a place of the unburied dead.

The child wailed again, just as she spotted a figure crawling slowly out of a nearby doorway, collapsing against the outer wall. It was a young woman, burning with fever, a pitcher loose in her grip. “Water,” she moaned, barely noticing Romola standing over her. “Get me water.” Romola took the pitcher from her limp hand, asked her to point the way to the well, and set off at a run, the child now quiet against her shoulder as he sucked on the sweet fig pulp.

As she neared the well, her eyes drifted to the grassy patch high on the slope below the village church, where she had spotted a cow and two goats grazing. She was so busy planning her path to that patch to beg milk for the child, that she did not notice a fifteen-year-old altar boy, coming the other way to draw water, who froze at the sight of her. Draped in her long, plain grey pilgrim’s gown, her golden hair streaked with sunlight, the dark-eyed olive child tucked in her arm, the pitcher in her hand to tend the sick, the boy was convinced he was looking at no mortal woman. He was certain the Holy Mother had appeared, come down to save their plague-stricken village. Terrified that she would be angered at the villagers for hoarding water while the sick suffered, he dropped his own clay vessel and fled as fast as his legs could carry him up the slope.

Romola, confused but not stopping to wonder at his flight, drew the water quickly and hurried back to give the fevered woman a drink. When the woman had drunk her fill, she told Romola the whole grim story: weeks earlier, three families of Jewish refugees, twenty souls in all, had been set ashore sick with the plague. The villagers had shut them out in that distant hovel, and when the first of them died, they had tried to throw the bodies into the sea — but a storm washed them right back. After they buried the corpses, the plague had spread through every cottage. Most of the villagers had fled over the mountain, driving all their livestock ahead of them. Only she, the parish priest, and the young altar boy Jacopo were left behind.

Romola wasted no time. She promised the woman she would come back to tend her, then set off up the slope to find the priest. The old man had been hiding behind the olive boughs for ten minutes already, his bucket in hand, half-convinced Jacopo’s tale of a Madonna with a glowing halo tending the sick was true, half-terrified that the plague would take him if he dared step down into the village. When Romola called out to him gently, asking for milk for the hungry child, he crept out from behind the trees, too fearful to disobey. He poured her a brimming cup of warm goat’s milk, and as he watched Romola feed the little boy and then drink deeply herself, the last of his superstitious awe melted into a quiet, humble certainty that God had sent a woman over the sea to save them.

That morning, the three of them walked back down into the valley together: the priest, young Jacopo, and Romola. They found the handful of survivors still clinging to life, fed them, changed their linens, eased their fevers, and carried all the unburied dead to consecrated ground to lay them to rest.

Days turned into weeks, then months. Slowly, the remaining villagers who had fled the plague straggled back. The men took up their spades to dig and sow the fields again, the women carried their vases to the well and smiled as they passed Romola on the lane, and the little Jewish toddler, named Benedetto, was baptized in the small mountain church, his tiny feet already tottering unsteadily across the straw floor of the cottage Romola had made her home. When the last of the sick had recovered, the long months of unbroken labor caught up with her, and she grew weak and faint, happy to rest through the long daylight hours tended by a widow who had lost her whole family to the plague.

Every day, the priest, Jacopo, and all the surviving villagers climbed the small hill to her cottage, bearing offerings of their finest honey, warm polenta, fresh eggs, and honey cakes. For the rest of their lives, they would tell their children of the sainted lady who had come to them over the sea, her face soft and fair, her brown eyes holding a blessing for everyone she met, who had lifted them out of despair when all hope was gone. For generations after, the valley held legends of the blessed woman from the sea — not a myth, but the memory of the quiet, fierce kindness of one woman who chose love over despair, and saved a whole forgotten village from ruin.
