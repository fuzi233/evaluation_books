The third night after our confrontation with the Kukuana king found us camped at the base of the Three Witches—a trio of jagged peaks that loomed like sentinels over Solomon’s forgotten road. Our party was small: Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, myself, and Foulata, who attended us with quiet devotion. Infadoos, the grizzled warrior, accompanied us, as did the venomous crone Gagool, carried in a litter, her mutterings a constant undercurrent of malice.

As dawn broke, the Three Witches revealed themselves in full splendor. Their summits, wreathed in eternal snow, pierced the heavens, while their lower slopes blushed purple with heather. Before us stretched the Great Road, a pale ribbon leading to the central peak—and its abrupt end. This was the threshold of the legendary mines, the same that had lured the doomed Portuguese explorer and, we feared, Sir Henry’s lost brother.

Gagool’s cackle slithered through the air as we marched. "Why hurry, fools?" she sneered. "Death waits ahead!" Her words sent a chill through me, but we pressed on until the earth itself seemed to yawn open—a vast pit, half a mile wide, its depths veiled in shadow.

"Diamond diggings," I murmured. "Solomon’s work." The telltale blue clay and worn stone washing tables confirmed it. The road forked around the abyss, its edges reinforced with ancient masonry, and as we followed its curve, three colossal figures emerged from the haze.

The Silent Ones.

Carved from dark stone, they sat upon their thrones, two male and one female, their faces weathered but their presence undiminished. The female bore crescent horns—Astarte, perhaps, or some forgotten goddess. The males were draped in stone, one serene, the other a nightmare of snarling malice. Infadoos saluted them with raised spear, but Gagool only grinned.

"Ready to see the treasure, *white men*?" she taunted.

We were.

A narrow arch in the cliff face swallowed us, Gagool’s gourd-lamp casting flickering shadows. The passage opened into a cavern so vast it dwarfed cathedrals. Stalactites hung like frozen waterfalls, their bases swelling into pillars that glittered as if carved from diamond. Water dripped—slow, eternal—building new spires over centuries. One column bore a crude etching: a mummy and an Egyptian god, left by some long-dead laborer. The stone had grown barely eight feet in three millennia.

But Gagool cared nothing for nature’s artistry. She herded us deeper, to a square-cut door.

"The Place of Death," she hissed.

Inside, the air thickened. A stone table dominated the chamber, flanked by figures—*white* figures. No, not statues. *Corpses*.

Petrified kings.

Their bodies, sheathed in glassy spar, sat frozen in time. At the table’s head loomed Death himself—a colossal skeleton, fifteen feet tall, carved from a single stalactite. Its hollow eyes seemed to watch us, its spear poised to strike.

And on the table, naked and glistening with mineral drip, sat Twala.

His severed head perched on his knees, his neck vertebrae protruding like broken spikes. The water’s ceaseless fall had begun encasing him in stone, turning flesh into relic.

Gagool’s laughter clawed at our nerves. "Meet your handiwork, Incubu!"

Sir Henry recoiled. Good swore. Foulata buried her face in his shoulder.

Twenty-seven kings surrounded us, preserved in silent judgment. Death presided over them all.

The White Dead. And their master, the White Death.
