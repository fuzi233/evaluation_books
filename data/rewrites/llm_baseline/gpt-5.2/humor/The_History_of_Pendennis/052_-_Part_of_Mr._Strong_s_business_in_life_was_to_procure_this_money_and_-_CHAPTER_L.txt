Fashion, like a spoiled housecat, had long ago turned its back on the Temple Garden, found something noisier to sit on, and pretended not to hear when you called it. The place was still green enough to soothe a respectable eye; still cut into neat beds and walks, still offering a view of the Thames that could make even a lawyer momentarily ashamed of his own existence. But the famous roses—those historical, war-starting roses that York and Lancaster once plucked with such fatal leisure—had given up the attempt. The air was too smoky, the soot too confident. A rose, like a language, needs room to breathe; and London, in those parts, preferred to speak in coal.

Most of the present tenants of the surrounding buildings neither knew nor cared whether a bud ever dared show itself. Their Sundays were for digestion and debts. Attorneys’ clerks did not rush through the gate with posies under their arms (their bags were already heavy enough with briefs and other desolations), and the few barristers who walked for “constitution” marched as if their feet were cross-examining the gravel. Only antiquarians and literary idlers came to look with any tenderness—imagining Addison’s thin face passing, or Goldsmith in the summer-house, counting the day’s disappointments against tomorrow’s suit from Mr. Filby the tailor. If you listened with a proper ear—an ear that cared about the world’s breathing—you could almost hear those old footsteps: the genteel rasp of shoe-leather, the careful tap of a cane, the soft hush of a page turned. Then, over it all, the modern sound: the impatient city, forever grinding its own teeth.

Arthur Pendennis, being much more devoted to such phantoms than to the law, chose the Temple Gardens that evening for exercise and meditation, as one chooses a quiet pantry to think in—away from the clang of pots, away from eyes. It was vacation-time. The chambers were mostly empty; the great men were dining at distances measured in claret; the lesser men were dining at distances measured in half-pints; and the Temple itself held its breath in that Sunday calm when even the river seems to run more politely.

Pen had dined alone at a tavern nearby—one of those meals that fills a stomach without ever speaking to it. No attentive butter, no grateful herb; nothing that could make a man feel rooted to the earth by taste. Perhaps that was why, as he returned toward his chambers, he drifted through the old gate and into the gardens as if seeking a better kind of sustenance.

The evening was bright and still. Across the water the warehouses looked as rosy and clean as if, for once, they had washed for the holiday. Steamers hurried by, packed with holiday passengers whose laughter came in gusts and then vanished, like a tune played past an open window. From the city, church bells rang for evening prayers—a whole multitude of metallic throats calling in different dialects of devotion. The sound lay over the garden like a cloth, smoothing the mind. It carried Pen back, not by argument but by rhythm, to the terrace at home: his mother beside him, the old familiar village noises—elm leaves whispering, the church tower holding its steady note against the sunset.

Was it that memory, or only the sun sinking behind some imagined Clavering elms, that made him colour? He sat in one of the summer-houses and drummed time on the bench to the bells outside. Then—startled at himself, as people are when they catch their own heart in the act—he brushed dust from his shining boots, rose abruptly, stamped once as if to crush a thought, and said aloud, “No, by Jove, I’ll go home.”

He stepped out—and nearly collided with two very small persons trotting along the gravel, their long blue shadows slanting eastward like little banners.

One child cried “Oh!” from sheer surprise; the other, who possessed a more worldly philosophy, began to laugh and said, with a knowing infant chuckle, “Missa Pendennis!”

Arthur looked down—and there they were again: Mesdemoiselles Ameliar-Ann and Betsy-Jane, his acquaintances of the previous day, as recognisable as if they had been served again on the same plate. He blushed—more than a grown man has any right to blush at two children—and, seizing the one he had almost upset, he lifted her into the air and kissed her with the suddenness of a man who has been thinking too long and needs an interruption.

Ameliar-Ann, being unprepared for aerial embraces, burst into frightened tears.

That cry brought up their attendants at once, as efficiently as the clatter of a dropped ladle summons a cook: Mrs. Bolton in a rich scarlet Caledonian cashmere shawl over a black silk dress, and Miss F. Bolton with a yellow scarf, a sweet sprigged muslin, and a parasol held like a badge of gentility. Fanny said not a single word; but her eyes spoke with an inconvenient brilliance, flashing welcome as brightly as any window in Paper Buildings.

Mrs. Bolton, after admonishing Betsy-Jane with a maternal “Now then,” exclaimed, “Lor, sir—how very odd that we should meet you ’ere! I ope you ave your ealth well, sir.—Ain’t it odd, Fanny, that we should meet Mr. Pendennis?”

Odd? People snigger at such “coincidences” when they occur below stairs, and call them “romance” when they happen in shrubberies. But why should accident be restricted to country-houses? When a young Croesus strolls at a Hall, have you never found yourself, by singular chance, walking your own Fanny along the same path? When a band plays at Brighton, do Captain Padmore and young De Boots not also, by miracle, clink down the pier at precisely the moment you are listening? Providence has a wide service, and does not confine its little ironies to the wealthy.

In truth, Pen had only said the previous day—artlessly enough—that though some Temple courts were gloomy, parts were cheerful, especially the chambers by the river and around the gardens, and that the gardens made a pleasant Sunday walk and were frequented by many people. Here, by the merest chance, were these people.

Arthur, as if to defend himself with starch and linen, was unusually smart. White duck trousers and a white hat; a many-coloured neckcloth; a light waistcoat; gold chains and shirt-studs—he looked, if not exactly like a prince, then certainly like a prince’s tailor’s daydream. The splendour suited him; and worse, his blushes suited him too.

The children had been so struck with him yesterday that, after he left, they had played at being him. Ameliar-Ann, sticking her chubby fingers into the arm-holes of her pinafore in imitation of Pen’s favourite waistcoat-attitude, had declared, “Now, Bessy-Jane, I’ll be Missa Pendennis.” Fanny had laughed till she cried and smothered her sister with kisses for the performance. And now, seeing Arthur embrace the child, she looked as if she had been given a feast after a famine.

Yet, close as she stood, Fanny herself seemed worn and pale—her face having the look of someone who has lived all night by lamplight and longing.

Arthur remarked it, and asked why she looked so fatigued.

“I was awake all night,” said Fanny, and began to blush a little, as if the night had been a secret she could taste again.

“I put out her candle,” Mrs. Bolton broke in, proud of discipline, “and hordered her to go to sleep and leave off readin.”

“You were reading? And what was it that interested you so?” Arthur asked, amused.

“Oh, it’s so beautiful!” Fanny breathed.

“What?”

“‘Walter Lorraine,’” she sighed out, as though the title itself were perfume. “How I do hate that Neaera—Neaera—I don’t know the pronunciation. And I love Leonora, and Walter—oh, how dear he is!”

Now, how had she come by ‘Walter Lorraine’? And how had she discovered that the author was Arthur Pendennis? The little person had stored every word he spoke the night before, as carefully as a thrifty cook saves drippings. He wrote in books and newspapers, he had said. What books? Her curiosity had been so hungry that it nearly drove her to be civil to old Mr. Bows again—Mr. Bows, who was currently suffering under her displeasure—but she had decided first to question Captain Costigan.

She began by coaxing the Captain and smiling upon him with her most winning air, while she set his humble apartment in order and fussed over his dinner. She announced, with the authority of a domestic monarch, that his linen wanted mending—an opinion in which the Captain’s linen-closet, containing specimens of flax and cotton that looked as if they had survived a battle, meekly concurred. She would mend his shirts—all his shirts. What horrid holes, what funny holes! She put her little face through one of them and laughed at the old warrior, as if turning ruin into a game.

Then she removed his dinner things daintily, tripping about the room as she had seen dancers do at the play—light-footed enough to make the very floorboards sound less shabby. She danced to the cupboard, produced the whisky-bottle, mixed him a tumbler, insisted on tasting a drop herself—a little drop—and the Captain must sing her one of his songs, his dear songs, and teach it to her. So the room filled with the quavering richness of an Irish melody, Costigan believing himself the enchanter, while Fanny, in truth, listened only until her appetite could be served.

And then, at the proper moment—like a cook slipping in the crucial question while the guest’s mouth is busy—she asked about Mr. Pendennis and his novel. Having got her answer, she cared for nothing else. She left the Captain at the piano, about to sing again; left the dinner-tray in the passage; left the shirts on the chair; and ran downstairs, her pace quickening with triumph.

Costigan, as he would have said, was not a litherary cyarkter and had not yet found time to peruse his young friend’s ellygant perfaurumance, though he intended to teak an early opporchunitee of purchasing a cawpee. But he knew the name because Messrs. Finucane, Bludyer, and the other frequenters of the Back Kitchen spoke of Mr. Pendennis—often unkindly, Bludyer calling him a confounded coxcomb, and Hoolan wondering why Doolan didn’t kick him—by the sobriquet of Walter Lorraine.

“And she went and ast for it at the libery,” Mrs. Bolton explained now, “—several liberies—and some ad it and it was hout, and some adn’t it. And one of the liberies as ad it wouldn’t let ’er ave it without a sovering: and she adn’t one, and she came back a-cryin to me—didn’t you, Fanny?—and I gave her a sovering.”

“And, oh,” Fanny added, cheeks and eyes glowing as if she had swallowed a bit of sunlight, “I was in such a fright lest any one should have come to the libery and took it while I was away. And, oh, I do like it so!”

Arthur, vain as he was, could not help being touched by sympathy so naïve and so fiercely felt. It flattered him—no review had ever tasted so sweet. For a moment he wanted to feed it: to bring her the book, to give her more words, more pages, to see her light up again. His heart made a foolish little leap—like a boy reaching for jam.

“Do you like it?” he said. “If you will come up to my chambers I will—No, I will bring you one—no, I will send you one. Good night. Thank you, Fanny. God bless you. I mustn’t stay with you. Good-bye, good-bye.”

And pressing her hand once—one brief pressure, like a hand on a hot stove—he nodded to Mrs. Bolton and the children and strode out of the gardens.

He quickened his pace as he went, as if the sound of the bells behind him were chasing him forward. He ran out at the gate talking to himself, passers-by turning to look at the smart young man with the hunted face.

“Dear, dear little thing,” he said. “Darling little Fanny! You are worth them all. I wish to heaven Shandon was back. I’d go home to my mother. I mustn’t see her. I won’t. I won’t, so help me—”

As he was speaking thus, and running, he ran against a little old man.

Mr. Bows.

“Your very umble servant, sir,” said Mr. Bows, making a sarcastic bow and lifting his old hat.

“I wish you a good day,” Arthur answered, sulkily. “Don’t let me detain you, or give you the trouble to follow me again. I am in a hurry, sir. Good evening.”

Bows’s face sharpened with suspicion. “Where are they?” he cried. “You know whom I mean. They’re not in your rooms, sir, are they? They told Bolton they were going to church at the Temple, they weren’t there. They are in your chambers: they mustn’t stay in your chambers, Mr. Pendennis.”

“Damn it, sir!” Arthur cried, stopping short with fury. “Come and see if they are in my chambers: here’s the court and the door—come in and see.”

And Bows, taking off his hat and bowing first—as if politeness were armour—followed him up.

The chambers, as we know, contained no women and no children. They contained only the poor masculine disorder of a man trying to look prosperous on credit: Warrington’s old bookcase and battered library; Pen’s writing-table littered with papers; the air of a room that had never known the smell of bread baking, or onions softening in a pan, or any of the homely sounds that make four walls into a life. Outside, the city’s murmur crept in; inside, the quiet felt almost accusatory.

“Will you like to look in the bedrooms, Mr. Bows,” Arthur said bitterly, “and see if my victims are there? Or whether I have made away with the little girls, and hid them in the coal-hole?”

“Your word is sufficient, Mr. Pendennis,” Bows answered in his sad tone. “You say they are not here, and I know they are not. And I hope they never have been here, and never will come.”

“Upon my word, sir,” Arthur said haughtily, “you are very good, to choose my acquaintances for me; and to suppose that anybody would be the worse for my society. I remember you, and owe you kindness from old times, Mr. Bows; or I should speak more angrily than I do, about a very intolerable sort of persecution to which you seem inclined to subject me. You followed me out of your Inn yesterday, as if you wanted to watch that I shouldn’t steal something.”

He stammered and turned red the instant the words were out. He had given the other man his opening, and Bows took it like a lawyer with a grievance.

“I do think you came to steal something, as you say the words, sir,” Bows replied. “Do you mean to say that you came to pay a visit to poor old Bows, the fiddler; or to Mrs. Bolton, at the porter’s lodge? O fie! Such a fine gentleman as Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, doesn’t condescend to walk up to my garret, or to sit in a laundress’s kitchen, but for reasons of his own. And my belief is that you came to steal a pretty girl’s heart away, and to ruin it, and to spurn it afterwards, Mr. Arthur Pendennis.”

He went on—heated, indignant, full of those large denunciations that come easily to the wronged and do not always hit the right target. He spoke of “you young dandies,” of “aristocrats” trampling upon the people, of sport and shame, of toys flung into the street when the rich grow bored. His voice rang in Pen’s bare rooms, and the words sounded doubly loud because there was so little else in the place: no crackle of fire, no clink of spoon, no domestic music to soften anger. Even outside, the bells were fading; the city’s own harsher noises waited to take their place.

Bows was speaking beside the immediate point, and Pen—being no saint, and very glad to dodge—took advantage of it.

Arthur broke out with a laugh, and then, because he was not altogether hard, begged pardon for laughing.

“Yes, I am an aristocrat,” he said. “In a palace up three pair of stairs, with a carpet nearly as handsome as yours, Mr. Bows. My life is passed in grinding the people, is it? In ruining virgins and robbing the poor? My good sir, this is very well in a comedy, where Job Thornberry slaps his breast and asks my Lord how dare he trample on an honest man and poke out an Englishman’s fireside; but in real life—to a man who has to work for his bread as much as you do—how can you talk about aristocrats tyrannising over the people? Have I ever done you a wrong? Or assumed airs of superiority over you? Did you not have an early regard for me—in days when we were both romantic young fellows, Mr. Bows? Come, don’t be angry with me now, and let us be as good friends as we were before.”

“Those days were very different,” Bows answered. “And Mr. Arthur Pendennis was an honest, impetuous young fellow then; rather selfish and conceited, perhaps, but honest. He liked you then, because you were ready to ruin yourself for a woman.”

“And now, sir?” Arthur asked, the question coming out sharper than he intended.

“And now times are changed, and you want a woman to ruin herself for you,” Bows said.

Then the old man’s anger shifted into something more personal and more grievous. He spoke of Fanny as a child with a heated brain—warmed and warped by novels until love and lovers were her whole diet, and she scarcely noticed that she trod on a kitchen floor. He admitted her talents, her winning ways. He confessed his own fondness: a lonely old man among boon companions who left him melancholy; a life he did not like; this girl the only creature he cared for. And as he spoke, his voice broke.

“Have pity upon me,” he said, “and don’t take her away from me, Mr. Pendennis—don’t take her away.”

That crack in the voice did more than all his threats. Pen felt it like a hand laid suddenly on a sore place.

“Indeed,” Arthur said kindly, “you do me a wrong if you fancy I intend one to poor little Fanny. I never saw her till Friday night. It was the merest chance that our friend Costigan threw her into my way. I have no intentions regarding her—that is—”

“That is, you know very well that she is a foolish girl, and her mother a foolish woman,—that is, you meet her in the Temple Gardens, and of course without previous concert,—that is, that when I found her yesterday reading the book you’ve wrote, she scorned me,” Bows said, bitterness returning like an aftertaste. “What am I good for but to be laughed at? A deformed old fellow like me; an old fiddler, that wears a threadbare coat, and gets his bread by playing tunes at an ale-house? You are a fine gentleman, you are. You wear scent in your handkerchief, and a ring on your finger. You go to dine with great people. Who ever gives a crust to old Bows?”

He plunged then into his own history—ambition once, plays, poems, music, no hearing, women laughing at him, all hopes failing, and now old age alone. The words tumbled out with the desperation of someone trying to keep hold of the last scraps of himself before the world sweeps them away. Identity, like a dialect, can be rubbed down until it seems fit only for jokes; and Bows had lived too long in a world that translated him into “fiddler” and “oddity” and forgot the rest.

“Don’t take this girl from me,” he said again. “Leave her with me a little longer. She was like a child to me till yesterday. Why did you step in, and made her to mock my deformity and old age?”

“I am guiltless of that, at least,” Arthur said, with a sigh that surprised him by being sincere. “Upon my word of honour, I wish I had never seen the girl. My calling is not seduction, Mr. Bows. I did not imagine that I had made an impression on poor Fanny, until—until to-night. And then, sir, I was sorry, and was flying from my temptation, as you came upon me.”

He paused. The room had gone dim; the day’s last light was slipping away, and with it that easy confidence which daylight lends to people who live by appearances. When he spoke again, his voice shook.

“And I do not mind telling you, sir,” he said, “that on this Sabbath evening, as the church bells were ringing, I thought of my own home, and of women angelically pure and good, who dwell there; and I was running hither as I met you, that I might avoid the danger which beset me, and ask strength of God Almighty to do my duty.”

After that, there was silence. The city’s noises seemed louder by contrast: a distant cart, a shout, some harsh metallic scrape—modern life insisting, always, on its own accent. When Bows spoke again, his tone was gentler, as if Pen’s words had translated him back into “old friend” rather than “enemy.”

He stayed only a little longer. On taking leave, he asked permission to shake hands. There was warmth and affection in the grasp on both sides, and Bows, with a humility that cost him something, apologised for having mistaken Arthur. He even paid Pen compliments that made the young man squeeze his hand again, hard, as if clinging to reconciliation.

At the door, Arthur said, “I have given a promise, and I hope and trust that you may rely on it?”

“Amen to that prayer,” said Mr. Bows; and he went slowly down the stairs, his steps fading one by one into the Temple’s listening hush.
