More than three and a half centuries ago, in the soft mid-spring of 1492, the dawn angel on his slow, broad flight would have looked down on a world far more familiar to us than we might guess. Travelling from the sun-warmed shores of the Levant to the Pillars of Hercules, and across the snow-capped peaks of the Caucasus over the snowy Alpine ridges all the way to the bare, dark coasts of the western isles, he would have traced nearly the same outlines of firm land and restless sea we know today: the same great mountain shadows sloping over the same valleys, olive-cloaked hills, dark pine forests, broad plains lush with young corn and rain-freshened grass, and the domes and spires of cities huddled beside rivers or rising above the sedge-thick masts of curved coastal harbors, sitting in the exact spots they still occupy now. As his faint first light seeped through the cracks of cottage windows and palace doors alike, it fell just as it does now: on the warm, pink faces of sleeping children curled in their nests of blankets, on the haggard wake of the sick and grieving, on the calloused hands of laborers rising before the sun, and on the pale, bleary eyes of night-students hunched over manuscripts, questioning the stars, the ancient sages, and the depths of their own souls for the hidden truth that would break the narrow limits of a human lifespan and turn what seemed a dark, aimless path into one small arc of an immeasurable circle of light and glory.

The great rivers that have shaped human lives have barely shifted their courses in all that time, and the deeper currents of the human heart ebb and flow in the same rhythm, answering to the same hungers, the same loves, the same terrors. Following in the wake of that slow dawn, we cannot escape the overwhelming truth that the core of the human lot never changes: hunger and labor, seedtime and harvest, love and loss.

If our imagination pauses to linger over one world-famous city that has barely altered its outline since Columbus set sail, we find a living symbol standing against all the chaos of human change, reminding us that we share far more with the people of the past than separates us. The same principles of engineering that raised its domes and towers created a likeness in human building far deeper and more lasting than any fashion or technological shift. We can easily imagine that the shade of a Florentine citizen, who drew his last breath in 1492 while Columbus was still haggling for his three tiny, leaky ships in Palos harbor, could return to walk the golden morning light once more, and find fellowship and understanding among the inheritors of his city.

Let us imagine that very shade, permitted to revisit the waking world, standing now on the hill of San Miniato, looking south over Florence. He wears the clothes he lived in: the long, unbroken folds of his heavy black silk lucco falling straight from his neck to his ankles, his plain cloth cap draped with a long soft becchetto strip he can wrap around his neck as a scarf if the wind turns cold. His face is sharp and firm, not classically handsome, but with a mouth cut clear and determined, softened to warm humanity by the clean-shaven curve of his lip and chin. It is a face lined with a lifetime of sharp, varied memories made on the banks of the gleaming Arno, and as he stares out at the view, the sense of familiarity overwhelms any small changes he spots. He is convinced he could climb down the hill back into the city streets and pick up his busy, full life exactly where he left off.

He does not just recognize the dark mass of Mount Morello rising opposite him, the long Arno valley stretching its grey green luxuriance all the way to the distant marble ridges of Carrara, and the steep hill of Fiesole topped with monastic walls and dark cypresses, or all the scattered green slopes dotted with villas he can name without thinking. He spots far closer, more intimate landmarks of his daily walks. He misses the seventy or more tall, fortified medieval towers that once ringed the city walls like a king’s jeweled diadem, but he does not linger on that blank space for long. His eyes are drawn irresistibly to the famous tower of the Palazzo Vecchio, rising like a tall flower stem leaning toward the sun, from the great square turreted mass of the old seat of government at the very heart of Florence, looking none the worse for the four hundred years that have passed since he walked beneath it as a young man. There, too, is Brunelleschi’s great dome, the largest in the world, which was only a wild, daring dream in the mind of that small, quick-eyed architect when our shade was a small boy, its great curved mass rising high enough to eclipse the surrounding hills. Giotto’s bell tower, with its distant glint of rich colored marble, the graceful spire of the Badia, and all the other familiar belltowers he first looked at balanced on his nurse’s shoulder, are all still there.

“Surely,” he thinks, “Florence can still ring her bells with that deep, solemn hammer clang that used to beat on the hearts of every citizen and strike fire into their souls. There on the right stands the long, dark bulk of Santa Croce, where we laid our greatest dead to rest, pressing laurel to their cold brows and fanning their graves with the banners of their victories. But Santa Croce never had a spire when I lived: we Florentines were so full of grand building plans we could never finish all the facades and spires we dreamed of. We had frescoes to paint and shrines to pay for, not to mention greedy mercenary captains, bribed kings, and territories we had to buy at exorbitant prices, so that spire must have waited all these centuries. What architect could the Franciscan friars have hired to build it? If it had been raised in my day, Brunelleschi or Michelozzo would have designed something far finer, something worthy to crown the great old church Arnolfo laid out.”

He sighs, and lets his gaze drift over the city walls, staring in confused wonder at the changes of this strange new era. Why have five of the eleven convenient old gates been bricked up? Worse than that, why are all the tall defensive towers he once took such pride in levelled flat? Has the world grown so peaceful, then, that Florentines live in such perfect harmony there are no more conspiracies to smuggle ambitious exiles back into the city at the head of armed bands? These are hard questions to untangle, and it is far easier, far pleasanter, to linger on the familiar sights than to puzzle out the new ones.

And there is the Arno, all its bridges standing exactly where he remembers them: the odd, one-of-a-kind Ponte Vecchio, crammed full of the same tiny, quaint little shops where he used to linger for a minute on his walk, either to check on the construction of the massive new palace Messer Luca Pitti was building with stone quarried out of the hill behind his house, or to transact small business with the cloth dyers across the river in Oltrarno. The oversize, sprawling roofline of the Pitti Palace is hidden from this vantage point on San Miniato, but the old Florentine has no wish to see that overly ambitious private palace. He only aches to climb down into those narrow, winding streets, the humming, crowded piazzas, to step back into the eager, communal life he inherited from his fathers.

He wonders if the old system of voting with black and white beans still goes on down there. Who are the current Priori, eating their strictly regulated official dinners in the Palazzo Vecchio, with courses of tripe and boiled partridge, enlivened by practical jokes played on the most awkward, unlucky man among the ruling signors? Do they still hang the bright, heavy banners from every window, and parade them with proper pomp beneath Orcagna’s Loggia every two months?

Life had a sharp, sweet zest for him when he trod those marble steps and held those public honors himself. His trade stretched from Syria to Britain, so his politics spanned the whole of the known world, but they had the fierce, intimate intensity only possible in a small, tight-knit community: a city ringed by six miles of walls and surrounded by hills, where every man knew every other by sight as they passed in the street, where every glance fell on the memorials of the commonwealth every day, and every citizen knew not just that he had the right to vote, but the chance of being voted into the highest offices. He loved his honors, his profits, the work of his counting house, his guild, and the public council chamber. He loved his enmities too, and he used to finger the white bean that would keep a hated rival’s name out of the election bag with far more satisfaction than if it had been a golden florin. He took pride in arranging good, advantageous marriages for his sons and daughters, and walked home under his favorite cool loggia in the evenings with triumphant light in his eyes to toast the match. He loved to play chess there with his friends, to trade sharp jests and even rough, coarse jokes, for he knew a man eligible for the highest magistracy did not need to be above that kind of simple pleasure.

He knew every corner of domestic and foreign affairs: he had served on the Council of Ten that ran the war department, the Council of Eight that managed domestic law and order, and the body of Priori that formed the Republic’s executive government. He had even risen to the supreme office of Gonfaloniere, led embassies to the Pope and the Venetian Republic, and served as commissary to Florence’s hired mercenary army, directing the inglorious, almost bloodless skirmishes where no man died of a brave, clean breast wound, only from accidental falls and being trampled by panicking horses. In that long life, he learned to distrust men without bitterness, seeing most of life as a fair game of skill, but never losing his respect for old tales of heroism and clean, uncorrupted honor. The human soul is hospitable, after all, and can hold conflicting sentiments and contradictory opinions side by side with perfect impartiality.

He was proud, too, to have soaked up all the learning of his age, to judge the world not by the crude opinions of the vulgar, but in harmony with the ancient Greek and Roman authors. In the prime of his life he had chased after the most perfectly corrected classical manuscripts, paid dozens of florins for antique vases and unearthed busts of the ancient immortals, even if some of them were truncis naribus, missing their noses, he still considered them perfectly authentic. In his old age, he had rushed to buy one of the very first copies of the fine printed edition of Homer that became one of the early glories of the Florentine press.

But for all that, he had never forgotten to hang a wax votive effigy of himself beneath the protection of the Madonna Annunziata, or give large gifts to the shrines of saints whose lives had nothing to do with the classics. He had even made generous bequests to build new chapels for the Franciscan friars, the same friars he had mocked with a hundred sharp jokes over dinner. The Unseen Powers were far too mighty to trifle with. Who could be absolutely sure there was no angry force to appease, no intercessory pity to win, hiding behind any one of the thousands of names men called on for help? Weren’t healing gems known to work their magic even if they only pressed against the tip of your finger? Weren’t all things in the world steeped in hidden occult virtues? Lucretius the pagan philosopher might be right, the great poet who argued there were no gods meddling in human affairs. Luigi Pulci, the Florentine poet suspected of believing nothing at all beyond the roof of his own house, had sounded very right over the supper table when the wine and jests flowed fast, even if he only wrote in the crude vulgar tongue. Even learned scholars argued that Aristotle, the wisest of all men (unless Plato was wiser, of course), was a thorough irreligious thinker, and a liberal educated man had to entertain every possible speculation. But all these negations might still turn out to be wrong. They felt very wrong, as the years rolled past and took on graver and graver faces. The world was Christian, after all. He had been baptized in the great baptistery of San Giovanni, where the dome was covered in terrible frescoes of the last judgment, and the altar bore a crucified Christ that forever destroyed any chance he might have felt perfectly complacent about his own sins and the state of the world.

He was no pagan philosopher, no mocking pagan poet. He was a man of the fifteenth century, heir to that strange tangled web of belief and unbelief: of Epicurean lightheartedness and superstitious dread, of pedantic, impossible ethical maxims he recited by rote and crude, unruly passions he acted on with childlike impulsiveness, of a pull toward self-indulgent pagan ease and an unavoidable submission to the human conscience that, in the ferment of that new age, filled the air with strange prophecies and dark presentiments. He had smiled and shaken his head dubiously when he heard simple folk talk of the coming “Angelic Pope,” who would purify the Church of simony and clear the lives of the clergy of all their scandal, a state of affairs so far from the corruption of Pope Innocent VIII’s court that no shrewd merchant and politician like him could ever have counted on it coming to pass. But he had felt the weight of all the evils of his age anyway, for he was a man of public spirit, and public spirit can never be wholly immoral, for its very essence is care for the common good. That very Lent of 1492, the last before he died still hale and upright in his old age, he had sat in the church of San Lorenzo and listened, half reluctant, half moved, to the preaching of the fierce Dominican friar Girolamo Savonarola. The friar denounced the worldliness and vice of the higher clergy with unmatched boldness, and insisted that Christian men had no right to live only for their own ease when wrongdoing ran rampant in high places, no right to spend their wealth on grand pomp and decoration even in churches while their fellow citizens starved and sickened in the streets. The frate pushed his doctrine far too far for the taste of elderly, settled men, but it was unforgettable to see him move his audience so deeply that even the noblewomen took off all their gold and jewels and handed them over to be sold for the benefit of the poor.

“He was a remarkable man, that Prior of San Marco,” our shade thinks. “Arrogant and extreme, perhaps, especially in his fiery denunciations of swift, certain divine vengeance. Ah, Iddio non paga il Sabato—God does not pay his wages on a Saturday. The wages of sin are often delayed for years, I saw decades of prosperous, unpunished wickedness in my own time. But a preaching friar who wanted to move the whole people to action could never be moderate. He might have been a little less defiant and curt to Lorenzo de’ Medici, after all, Lorenzo’s family had funded and built most of San Marco monastery. Did the two of them ever make up their quarrel? And Lorenzo himself, with his weak outward eyes and his sharp, subtle inward vision, did he ever recover from that last illness at his villa in Careggi? He left the world with a sad, uneasy face, and everyone suspected his handsome, hot-tempered son Piero would turn out to be a tyrant like Rehoboam of old. How did it all turn out? Which party is currently in danger of being exiled, their houses sacked by the mob? Is there any successor to the incomparable Lorenzo now, to whom the Great Turk will send gifts of rare wild animals, holy relics, priceless manuscripts, and fugitive political enemies, all perfectly suited to the tastes of a Christian Magnifico who is both learned and devout—and just a little bit vindictive? Which famous scholar writes the Republic’s official Latin letters these days? What fiery philosopher is lecturing on Dante in the Duomo, then going home to write bitter, scathing rants against the parents of any fool who dares criticize his classical spelling? Are the wise heads in Florence leaning toward an alliance with the Pope and the Kingdom of Naples, or are they listening instead to the ambassadors from France and Milan?”

All that news is waiting down in the streets, on the familiar broad marble paving stones in front of the churches, under the sheltered loggias where Florentines still gather to gossip and debate, to trade bitter jests and merry jokes just as they did in his day. All the old buildings are still standing, after all. The changes can’t be that big. He will go down and hear, he will step onto the familiar pavement, and hear the old speech of Florentines once more.

Do not go down, good shade. The changes are greater than you dream, and the speech of modern Florentines would sound like a meaningless riddle to your ears. If you must go, do not stop to chat with politicians loitering on the marble pavements, do not ask questions about the old cloth trade in the Calimara district, do not puzzle over the new currents of scholarship, secular or monastic. Only look at the sunlight and shadows falling on the grand, solid old walls that were built to last, and have endured in all their majesty. Look at the faces of the little children playing in the squares, making their own bright sunlight amid the shadows of age. Look into the churches if you will, hear the same ancient chants, see the same images you remember: the images of willing suffering endured for a great good, of boundless benevolent love, of souls ascending to glory. See the upturned faces of the living, their lips moving in the same old prayers for help. These things have never changed. Sunlight and shadow wake the same old beauty and stir the same old chords in the human heart at dawn, noon, and dusk. Little children remain the eternal symbol of the unbreakable marriage between love and duty. And all across the centuries, men still yearn for a reign of peace and righteousness, still know that the highest life of all is the one lived in conscious, willing sacrifice for others. The Angelic Pope, that old promise of a world made new, has still not come. That age-old yearning burns as bright in the human heart today as it did when you drew your last breath in that spring of 1492.
