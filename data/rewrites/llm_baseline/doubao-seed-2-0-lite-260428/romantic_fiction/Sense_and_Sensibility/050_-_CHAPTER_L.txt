After putting up exactly the right amount of dramatic, steady resistance—just enough to spare herself the dreaded, unthinkable reputation of being too kind and amiable—Mrs. Ferrars finally deigned to admit Edward back into her household, and formally pronounced him her son once more. Her family had been in a most chaotic state of flux of late: for decades she had been blessed with two sons, but Edward’s scandalous near-elopement had “annihilated” him from her affections a few weeks prior, and then Robert’s runaway marriage to Lucy had left her with no sons at all for a full, miserable fortnight. Now, with Edward’s return to favour, she was back down to one child to coddle and chide.

Even granted permission to live under her roof again, Edward did not dare feel secure until he had confessed his new engagement to Elinor Dashwood, half-convinced the shock would send his mother into such a fury she would cast him off all over again. He broke the news with trepidation, only to be met with unexpected calm. Mrs. Ferrars went through her expected motions first, laying out every argument she could muster to dissuade him: Miss Morton, the nobleman’s daughter with thirty thousand pounds to her name, was far higher rank and fortune than Elinor, the private gentleman’s daughter with barely three thousand. But when she found Edward fully acknowledged the truth of her words, and would not be swayed an inch, she took the hard-earned lesson of Robert’s elopement to heart and submitted. She dragged her feet for a few ungracious, chilly days to preserve her dignity, leaving no room for anyone to suspect she had caved out of genuine good will, before issuing her formal consent to the marriage of Edward and Elinor.

Next came the matter of their household income. It quickly became clear that while Edward was now her only remaining favourite son, he was still by no means treated as her eldest: Robert was guaranteed an allowance of a thousand pounds a year for life, while Edward was permitted nothing beyond the Delaford parsonage living that paid at most two hundred and fifty pounds, plus the ten thousand pounds she had gifted Fanny on her marriage to John Dashwood, with no extra promises of support for the present or the future. It was far more than Edward and Elinor had ever dared hope for, and Mrs. Ferrars herself, fumbling through flimsy excuses, was the only person surprised she had not given them even less.

With an income more than enough to meet their simple needs, all they had left to wait for was the completion of the parsonage renovations Colonel Brandon had rushed forward with, desperate to make the home as perfect as possible for Elinor. As always, however, workmen proved impossibly dilatory, throwing up one delay and disappointment after another, until Elinor finally broke her own strict resolution to wait until every last room was finished before marrying. Their quiet ceremony was held in Barton Church in the soft golden light of early autumn.

They spent the first month of their marriage at Delaford Mansion as the Colonel’s guests, popping over to the parsonage daily to oversee the final touches: pick wallpaper, plan their rose shrubberies, map out a curving new path down to the village. Mrs. Jennings’ jumbled prophecies came almost entirely true: by Michaelmas she was trundling over to visit their new home, and declared Elinor and Edward the happiest couple she had ever laid eyes on. Their only small unmet wishes were for Marianne to be happily wed to Colonel Brandon, and slightly lusher pasture grass for their small herd of dairy cows.

Nearly all their relations came to call in their first months of settling in. Mrs. Ferrars made a trip to inspect the domestic bliss she had almost forbidden, even John and Fanny Dashwood making the long journey from Sussex to pay their respects. “I won’t say I’m disappointed, my dear sister,” John said to Elinor as they walked the grounds of Delaford House one morning, “that would be going too far, for you are one of the most fortunate young women I know. But I confess, it would give me the greatest pleasure to call Colonel Brandon brother. His estate, his house, his land—everything is in such excellent, respectable order! I’ve never seen timber as fine as the oaks standing in Delaford Hanger anywhere in Dorsetshire. Marianne may not seem the obvious person to catch his eye, but I do think it would be very wise of you to have them stay with you often. The Colonel spends so much time at home, you never know what might happen when two people are thrown together constantly, with no other company to distract them. You can show her off to her best advantage, set the scene perfectly—give her a chance, you understand.”

For all that Mrs. Ferrars came to call on them, and always treated them to a thin, performative show of affection, neither Edward nor Elinor ever earned her real, warm favour. That honour was reserved for Robert’s foolishness and Lucy’s relentless cunning, and the pair earned it within a matter of months. Lucy’s endless, fawning humility, her constant assiduous attentions, her unceasing flattery, won Mrs. Ferrars over the second she gave the girl even a tiny opening, erasing all anger over the elopement and putting Robert back at the very centre of his mother’s affections. Lucy’s whole story became a perfect example of how unyielding, single-minded devotion to one’s own self-interest, no matter what obstacles stand in the way, can secure every advantage of fortune with no greater sacrifice than a little time and a clear conscience.

When Robert first went to visit Lucy at her rooms in Bartlett’s Buildings, he had only intended to talk her out of her engagement to Edward, certain one or two interviews would wrap the whole mess up. He was wrong about only that: Lucy always gave him hope he might convince her in time, but always claimed to have lingering doubts that could only be settled by one more half-hour of conversation with him. Before long, they stopped talking about Edward entirely, and only talked of Robert. He was proud of his conquest, proud of tricking his brother, proud of eloping without his mother’s permission. They spent months at Dawlish cutting off all Lucy’s old undesirable acquaintances and drawing up grand, ridiculous plans for fancy cottages, before returning to London to beg Mrs. Ferrars’ forgiveness on Lucy’s urging. The forgiveness at first only extended to Robert, and Lucy remained uninvited in her eyes for weeks, but her constant grovelling, her self-condemnation for Robert’s “offence,” her endless gratitude for every small slight Mrs. Ferrars threw her way, eventually won her over completely. Lucy soon became as beloved to Mrs. Ferrars as Fanny or Robert: Edward was never fully forgiven for ever having planned to marry her, Elinor was always dismissed as an unwelcome intruder despite her superior birth, but Lucy was openly, unapologetically treated like a favourite child. The whole family settled in London, living in apparent harmony—save for the constant petty jealousies between Fanny and Lucy, their husbands taking sides in every squabble, and the frequent domestic fights between Robert and Lucy themselves.

No one could explain why Edward, the eldest son, had been so thoroughly disinherited, or why the vain, frivolous Robert had been granted every luxury. But the arrangement suited everyone just fine: Robert spent his thousand a year happily, never once regretting that his brother got so little, and Edward threw himself fully into his parish duties, growing more attached to Elinor, his home, and his quiet peaceful life every day, never once wishing he could trade places with his brother.

Elinor’s marriage did not separate her much from her family at all: her mother and Marianne and Margaret spent more than half their time staying at the Delaford parsonage or the big house. Mrs. Dashwood’s frequent visits were born of equal parts affection and quiet, gentle planning: she was as determined as John had been, but far more generous of spirit, to see Marianne and Colonel Brandon wed. It was her dearest wish now, seconded wholeheartedly by Edward and Elinor, who knew all the Colonel’s long years of grief over his lost first love, and all the kindness he had shown their family. Marianne, with every single person around her quietly rooting for the match, with a deep, long-proven knowledge of Colonel Brandon’s goodness, and the slow, inevitable realization that his quiet, steady adoration for her was far more real than any reckless passion she had ever known, finally could not resist.

Marianne Dashwood was born to a most unexpected fate. She was born to learn the falseness of all the passionate, rash maxims she had lived by as a girl. She was born to overcome the first great love of her life at seventeen, and voluntarily give her hand in marriage to a man she loved not with wild, burning fire, but with deep, abiding esteem and warm, lively affection—this man, who had suffered as much heartbreak as she had in his youth, who she had once declared too old to ever marry at all, who still wore a flannel waistcoat to ward off the autumn chills. At nineteen, instead of pining away for a lost great passion as she had once dreamed, instead of spending the rest of her life in quiet retirement and study with her mother as she had once resolved, she became a wife, the mistress of Delaford Mansion, the beloved patroness of the village, entering a new life far happier than any of her girlish fantasies.

Colonel Brandon was as happy as everyone who loved him had always hoped he would be: Marianne was the perfect salve for every old wound, her company restoring his old cheerfulness and lightness of spirit. Marianne, in turn, poured her whole heart into making him happy, and in time loved him completely, as fully and unreservedly as she had once loved Willoughby.

When Willoughby heard of Marianne’s marriage, the news cut him to the quick, and his punishment was complete shortly after, when Mrs. Smith finally forgave him for his misconduct, and let him know that if he had acted honourably towards Marianne, he would have gotten both the fortune he craved and the woman he loved. His regret was sincere, he envied Colonel Brandon for years, and thought of Marianne often with a sharp, lingering sadness. But he did not waste away of a broken heart, did not flee from society, did not fall into permanent gloom: he lived a full, busy life, enjoying his hunting, his stables of fine horses and hounds, finding a quiet, imperfect kind of domestic peace with his wife. For the rest of his life, every new beautiful woman he met was measured against the memory of Marianne, and none of them ever quite measured up.

Mrs. Dashwood stayed prudently at Barton Cottage, never making any move to relocate to Delaford, which worked out perfectly for Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, for Margaret was now old enough to dance at all their parties, and more than charming enough to attract a string of young suitors to keep them entertained for years to come. Between Barton Cottage, the Delaford Parsonage, and Delaford Mansion, the two sisters were almost within sight of each other, visiting back and forth every day, with never a petty disagreement between them, and never a hint of cool awkwardness between their two husbands. For Elinor and Marianne, the greatest happiness of all was that they had each found exactly the kind of love their hearts had been made for: sense wedded to quiet, steady devotion, sensibility wedded to a deep, lasting peace.
