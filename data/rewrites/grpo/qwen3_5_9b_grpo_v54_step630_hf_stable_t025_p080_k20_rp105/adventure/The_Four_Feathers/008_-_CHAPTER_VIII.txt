The sun hung heavy over London in early June, baking the city into a haze of dust and heat. Captain Durrance stepped off the train, the air thick with the scent of coal smoke and unwashed crowds. He sought refuge in the cool shade of Hyde Park, sitting beneath the sprawling canopy of an ancient oak. Here, amidst the fluttering silk and lace of the countrywomen strolling past, he felt the sharp contrast of his own exile. His skin was darkened by the desert sun, his face etched with the weary lines of a man who had walked too far from home. He watched the world spin around him, a solitary figure marked by the eyes of those who had seen the edge of the map.

Among the passersby, a shadow detached itself and settled beside him. It was Mrs. Adair. Her gaze swept over him with a quick, almost furtive intensity, searching for the ghost of the man she had known three years prior. She was comparing the living man to the memory of the boy, hunting for the subtle scars of time. Durrance, however, merely noted the stark black of her mourning dress.

"My husband died eighteen months ago," she said, her voice quiet and steady. "He was thrown from his horse during a hunt with the Pytchleys. Killed instantly."

"I had not heard," Durrance replied, his tone awkward. "I am very sorry."

Mrs. Adair sat without moving, a woman of perplexing silences. Her pale, placid face offered no window into the turmoil of her thoughts. Durrance felt the weight of her presence, recalling Mr. Adair as a kindly man whose love for his wife was evident, yet he struggled to imagine how that wife had ever viewed him. The memory of Mr. Adair was faint, a shadow in a small household, leaving Durrance unable to summon the full depth of his regret.

"Are Harry Feversham and his wife in town?" Durrance asked, breaking the silence.

Mrs. Adair paused, her eyes distant. "Not yet," she said slowly. Then, correcting herself with a hurried sharpness, "I mean—the marriage never took place."

Durrance was not easily startled. Even when surprised, his reaction was contained. "I don't think I understand. Why did it never happen?"

Mrs. Adair looked at him sharply, as if probing the reason for his deliberate calm. "I don't know why. Ethne can keep a secret if she wishes." Durrance nodded. "The engagement was broken off on the night of a dance at Lennon House."

"Just before I left England three years ago?" Durrance pressed.

"Yes. So you knew?"

"No. You have explained to me something that happened on the very night I left Dover. What has become of Harry?"

Mrs. Adair shrugged. "I do not know. I have met no one who does. I suspect he has left England."

Durrance pondered this. The man with the troubled face he had seen on the pier at the Channel boat's departure was, after all, his friend. "And Miss Eustace?" he asked, his voice tinged with a strange timidity. "Has she married since?"

"No," Mrs. Adair replied after a long pause.

"Then she is still at Ramelton?"

Mrs. Adair shook her head. "There was a fire at Lennon House a year ago. Did you ever hear of a constable named Bastable?"

"I did. He introduced me to Miss Eustace and her father. I was traveling from Londonderry to Letterkenny. Mr. Eustace, whom I didn't know, invited me to stay the night. I declined, and Bastable arrested me on a magistrate's warrant the moment I landed."

"That is the man," Mrs. Adair confirmed. She recounted the history of the fire. It seemed Bastable's claim to Dermod Eustace's friendship rested on a peculiar brew of toddy, requiring a single oyster simmered in the saucepan to achieve perfection. At two o'clock on a June morning, the spirit lamp tipped over. Both men, drunk and careless, were unaware until the house was half-burnt and the rest ruined by water.

"There were consequences worse than the destruction," she continued, her voice devoid of emotion, as if speaking a script she had memorized. "The fire was a beacon to Dermod's creditors. Already burdened with debt, he fell into complete ruin. He was drenched by the hoses, caught a chill that nearly killed him, and has never fully recovered. You will find him a broken man. The estates are leased out, and Ethne lives now with her father in a small mountain village in Donegal."

Mrs. Adair did not look at Durrance while she spoke, her eyes fixed straight ahead. She spoke not from feeling, but from necessity.

"So she has lost everything?" Durrance asked.

"She still has a home in Donegal," Mrs. Adair returned.

"And that means a great deal to her," Durrance said slowly. "Yes, I think you are right."

"It means," Mrs. Adair added, "that Ethne, with all her ill-luck, has reason to be envied by many other women."

Durrance did not answer directly. He watched the carriages roll by, listened to the chatter and laughter of the crowd, and let his eyes rest on the women in their light-colored frocks. Yet, his mind worked steadily toward a conclusion. Finally, Mrs. Adair turned to him with a hint of impatience.

"Of what are you thinking?" she asked.

"That women suffer much more than men when the world goes wrong," he answered, his words sounding more like a question than a statement. "I know very little, of course. I can only guess. But I think women gather up their sorrows into themselves much more than we do. To them, the past becomes a real part of them, as integral as a limb. To us, it is always external—either a rung on a ladder or a weight on the heel. Don't you think so? I phrase it poorly. But put it this way: women look backward; we look forward. So misfortune hits them harder, doesn't it?"

Mrs. Adair answered in her own way. She did not explicitly agree, but a note of humility entered her voice. "The mountain village where Ethne lives is called Glenalla. A track leads up to it from the road halfway between Rathmullen and Ramelton." She rose, holding out her hand. "Shall I see you?"

"You are still in Hill Street?" Durrance asked. "I shall be in London for some time."

Mrs. Adair raised her eyebrows. By nature, she sought intricate motives, often baffled by simple reasons. She was puzzled now by Durrance's resolve to stay in the city. Why not travel immediately to Donegal, she wondered, since his thoughts clearly preceded him? She heard of his constant presence at his Service Club and could not fathom it. She had no suspicion of his true motive when he later informed her he had traveled to Surrey to spend a day with General Feversham.

It had been an ineffectual day for Durrance. The General kept him firmly anchored to the history of the campaign from which he had just returned. Only once did Durrance manage to touch upon Harry Feversham's disappearance. At the mere mention of his son's name, the old General's face set like plaster, becoming void of expression and inattentive as a mask.

"We will talk of something else, if you please," the General said. And so, Durrance returned to London not an inch closer to Donegal.

Thereafter, he sat under the great tree in the inner courtyard of his club, talking to this man and that, yet remaining unsatisfied. Throughout June, every afternoon and evening found him at his post. No friend of Feversham passed by without Durrance offering a word, which invariably led to a question. But the answers were always the same: a shrug of the shoulders and a muttered, "Hanged if I know!" Harry Feversham's place knew him no longer; he had dropped even from the speculations of his friends.

Toward the end of June, an old retired naval officer limped into the courtyard. Upon seeing Durrance, he hesitated and then moved away with remarkable alacrity.

Durrance sprang up from his seat. "Mr. Sutch," he said. "You have forgotten me?"

"Colonel Durrance, to be sure," the embarrassed lieutenant replied. "It is some while since we met, but I remember you very well now. I think we met—let me see—where was it? An old man's memory, Colonel Durrance, is like a leaky ship. It comes to harbor with its cargo of recollections swamped."

Neither Sutch's current embarrassment nor his previous hesitation escaped Durrance's notice.

"We met at Broad Place," Durrance said. "I wish you to give me news of my friend Feversham. Why was his engagement with Miss Eustace broken off? Where is he now?"

Sutch's eyes gleamed for a moment with satisfaction. He had always been doubtful whether Durrance knew of Harry's fall into disgrace. Durrance plainly did not.

"There is only one person in the world, I believe," Sutch said, "who can answer both your questions."

Durrance was not disconcerted. "Yes. I have waited here a month for you," he replied.

Sutch pushed his fingers through his beard and stared down at his companion. "Well, it is true," he admitted. "I can answer your questions, but I will not."

"Harry Feversham is my friend."

"General Feversham is his father, yet he knows only half the truth. Miss Eustace was betrothed to him, and she knows no more. I pledged my word to Harry that I would keep silence."

"It is not curiosity which makes me ask."

"I am sure that, on the contrary, it is friendship," Sutch said cordially.

"Nor is that entirely. There is another aspect of the matter. I will not ask you to answer my questions, but I will put a third one to you. It is one harder for me to ask than for you to answer. Would a friend of Harry Feversham be at all disloyal to that friendship, if"—and Durrance flushed beneath his sunburn—"if he tried his luck with Miss Eustace?"

The question startled Lieutenant Sutch.

"You?" he exclaimed, standing and considering Durrance. He remembered the rapidity of Durrance's promotion, speculating on his likelihood to win a woman's heart. Here was an aspect of the case he had not considered, and he was no less troubled than startled. For within him had grown a jealousy on behalf of Harry Feversham as strong as a mother's for a favorite second son. He had nurtured with pleasurable anticipation the hope that, in the end, Harry would return to all he once owned, like a rethroned king. He stared at Durrance and saw that hope struck down. Durrance looked the man of courage his record proved him to be, and Lieutenant Sutch applied his theory of women: "Brute courage—they make a god of it."

"Well?" Durrance asked.

Sutch knew he must answer. He was sorely tempted to lie. He knew enough of the man questioning him to be certain that a lie would have its effect. Durrance would go back to the Sudan, and leave his suit unpressed.

"Well?"

Sutch looked up at the sky and down upon the flags. Harry had foreseen that this complication was likely to occur; he had not wished that Ethne should wait. Sutch imagined him at this very moment, lost somewhere under the burning sun, and compared that picture with the one before his eyes—the successful soldier taking his ease at his club. He felt inclined to break his promise, to tell the whole truth, to answer both the questions Durrance had first asked. And again, the pitiless monosyllable demanded his reply.

"Well?"

"No," said Sutch, regretfully. "There would be no disloyalty."

And on that evening, Durrance took the train for Holyhead.
