In a quiet guest chamber within the walls of a Dominican convent, a solitary figure lay spent, his body wracked by violent waves of nausea that had finally ceased, leaving him as frail and weakened as Margaret had been on that fateful night in Holland. A great fire crackled in the hearth, casting flickering shadows across the stone floor, while his clothes hung near the bed, still damp with the chill of the river. Beside him sat a massive friar, his voice resonant and steady as he read aloud from the breviary—pious prayers meant to draw the soul closer to God.

The man in the bed opened his eyes now and then, watching the monk with a dazed gaze. At other times, he closed them tightly, murmuring in pain or exhaustion. The friar knelt suddenly, his forehead touching the cold stone, and prayed long and fervently for the stranger’s recovery. Then, rising slowly, he turned to bid farewell.

“Day is breaking,” he said. “I must prepare for matins.”

“Good Father Jerome,” the sick man whispered, “before you go—how did I come here?”

“By the hand of Heaven,” the friar replied. “You cast aside God’s gift. He gave it back to you. Consider this: you have tasted the world, and found only bitterness. Now turn to the Church. The Church is peace. *Pax vobiscum.*”

And with that, he was gone.

Gerard lay back, lost in thought, his mind drifting between confusion and sorrow. He drifted into a shallow doze, only to awaken hours later to find a new presence beside him—a man dressed in plain garments, his face sharp and restless, his eyes darting about like those of a bird of prey. This was no monk, but a layman, and yet there was something familiar in his look.

“How do you feel?” the man asked, leaning forward with concern.

“Very weak,” Gerard murmured. “And… I’ve seen you before. Where?”

“None the worse for my gauntlet?” the man asked, his voice tinged with anxiety. “I had to strike you with it—or both of us would have drowned in the Tiber.”

Gerard stared. “You saved me? But how?”

“Aye,” the man said, lowering his voice. “I was near the banks of the Tiber on business—none of your concern. You came to me, begging for a dagger thrust. But even as you spoke, I recognized you. You were the nobleman who saved my wife and child at sea.”

“It’s Teresa’s husband,” Gerard breathed. “And an assassin?”

“At your service,” the man replied with a grim smile. “Well, Ser Gerard, when you flung yourself into the river and told me to stay away—I might have obeyed. Men are not my friends. Let them hang or drown, for all I care. But when you, sinking beneath the water, cried out ‘Margaret!’—my heart answered, ‘Teresa!’ How could I return home and meet her eyes if I let you die? So I plunged in—and thank God, I swim like a duck.”

He paused, then continued. “You tried to grab me, desperate to take us both down. But I swam around you and—forgive me—struck you hard on the neck with my steel glove. You passed out. I pulled you ashore against the current, barely alive, full of water. Then I carried you on my back toward my home. ‘Teresa will nurse him,’ I thought. ‘And be proud of me.’ But just beyond the monastery, a giant of a friar stopped us. He looked at you and said, ‘I know this face. It’s Gerard, the young man from Holland.’ I confirmed it. Then he said, ‘He has friends among our brothers. Leave him with us. Charity is our duty.’”

“And so,” the man added, “I agreed to visit once a day. And here you are.”

He gazed at Gerard with an odd mixture of affection and curiosity. But Gerard felt no warmth. Instead, he recoiled inwardly—as if a serpent had slithered into the room.

“Ah, you wish to sleep,” the man said quickly. “I shall leave.” And he slipped away silently, promising to return each day.

Gerard kept his eyes shut, though he was not asleep. His mind churned with thoughts. Saved from death—by a killer? Was this not the hand of Heaven itself? The same Heaven he had mocked, cursed, defied?

He shuddered at the memory of his blasphemies. He tried to pray. He could speak the words—but not mean them. “I am doomed,” he moaned. “Doomed forever.”

Then, through the wall, the organ of the convent church burst forth—rich, solemn, divine. The choir rose in song, chanting the morning liturgy. Among the voices, one stood apart—a boy’s voice, pure and sweet, soaring like an angel’s hymn. It stirred something deep within Gerard. Memories flooded back—the innocence of his youth, the peace of the cloister, untouched by sin or passion. No rage, no lust, no corruption—only stillness, clarity, grace.

“Oh,” he sighed, tears welling in his eyes. “The Church is peace. Before I left her, I knew neither sorrow nor sin.”

And he wept.

Just then, a gentle light seemed to fall upon him. He opened his eyes—and there, standing beside the bed, was Father Anselm.

The old priest had arrived at the convent only the night before. Yet Gerard recognized him instantly. “Father Anselm!” he gasped. “You healed my body in Juliers. Now heal my soul in Rome! Alas—I fear you cannot.”

Anselm sat beside him, placed a calming hand upon his brow, and spoke softly, soothingly. When he learned how Gerard had come to be there, he addressed him with kindness, but also with solemnity. He made him see the depth of his sins, urged him to repent, and reminded him of the mercy that had spared his life.

“Come, my son,” Anselm said gently. “First, cleanse your heart.”

“Ah, father,” Gerard whispered, “in Juliers I could confess. Then I was innocent. Now—now I am a monster, impious and corrupt. I dare not speak to you.”

“Why not?” Anselm asked. “Do you think I have never sinned? Oh, how deeply I have erred! Come, poor burdened soul. Pour out your grief. Hide nothing. Do not carry hidden guilt like a stone in your chest.”

And so, kneeling before the priest, Gerard confessed everything—every deed, every thought, every betrayal—sighing, groaning, broken with remorse.

“Your sins are great,” Anselm said. “But your temptation was greater still. I must consult our prior.”

He kissed Gerard’s forehead and left, to seek counsel on the proper penance.

And then—miracle of miracles—Gerard could pray. Not with forced words, but with a heart laid bare, trembling, yet open. He poured out his soul in prayer, his spirit humbled, his will surrendered.

This transformation—this profound inner shift—can be named in one word: *penitence*. He turned from the world with horror, longing only to remain within the cloister’s shelter. To him, raised in its quiet ways, it was like a wounded bird returning to its nest. He began his novitiate in silence, in prayer, in fasting, in reading sacred texts and meditating on divine truth.

Meanwhile, far from Rome, Princess Claelia received news from her spy: Gerard was dead—though the manner remained unknown. She froze, stunned. When she finally spoke, it was with bitter lamentation.

“So soon,” she murmured. “How swiftly these creatures act. They come to us in our passion, offer their daggers for gold, then carry out deeds we never imagined—until they plant the seed.”

Within hours, her grief for Gerard had curdled into hatred for the man who had killed him. Her anger burned like fever.

“Poor soul,” she whispered. “I cannot bring you back. But he—*he*—shall not live.”

She sent armed men into hiding, stationed them throughout the palace, ready to ambush Lodovico when he came for his payment. “Strike at his head,” she ordered. “He wears a secret coat of mail. If he escapes, you shall answer with your own heads.”

And so she sat, weeping for the man she had loved, while pulling the strings of vengeance—plotting the death of another, not for justice, but because he had been her tool in destroying the first.
