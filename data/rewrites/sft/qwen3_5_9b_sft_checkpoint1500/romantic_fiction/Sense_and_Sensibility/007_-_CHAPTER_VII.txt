Barton Park stood roughly half a mile from the cottage, hidden from the sisters' view by the sweeping curve of a nearby hill. Inside, the house was a testament to grandeur, inhabited by the Middletons, who lived with a blend of generous hospitality and polished elegance. Yet, beneath this veneer lay a profound emptiness. Sir John derived his purpose from hunting and shooting, while Lady Middleton found her sole fulfillment in doting on her children. Their lives, devoid of genuine talent or intellectual curiosity, revolved entirely around the endless cycle of hosting guests. For them, social engagement was not merely a pastime but a necessity, filling the void where substance and sensibility should have resided.

Lady Middleton took immense pride in the perfection of her table settings and the flawless arrangement of her home, deriving her greatest joy from the admiration of her domestic displays. Conversely, Sir John possessed a more authentic, if boisterous, enthusiasm for society. He delighted in crowding his halls with young people, finding the noise and chaos of their gatherings a form of entertainment. In the summer, he organized outdoor feasts of cold ham and chicken; in the winter, he hosted lively private balls, eager to entertain anyone young enough to possess an "unsatiable appetite" for diversion.

To Sir John, the arrival of the Dashwood sisters was a cause for genuine celebration. He found them young, beautiful, and unpretentious—a combination he deemed irresistible. To a man like Sir John, where being "unaffected" was the highest virtue, the sisters' lack of artificiality secured his immediate affection. Furthermore, he took a peculiar pleasure in accommodating them, viewing their modest circumstances as a challenge to his generosity. By housing them, he satisfied his benevolent instincts while simultaneously enjoying the practical convenience of keeping a group of women close at hand, sparing himself the trouble of entertaining them elsewhere.

Upon their arrival, Sir John greeted them at the door with a warmth that felt almost sincere, leading them directly into the drawing room. There, he regaled them with tales of his recent struggles to find suitable company, lamenting the scarcity of "smart young men" in the area. He introduced them to the single gentleman present, a particular friend of his who was neither notably youthful nor particularly vivacious, hoping they would overlook the meagerness of the party. Fortunately, he noted, Lady Middleton's mother, Mrs. Jennings, had arrived just hours prior. Described as a cheerful and agreeable soul, she promised to enliven the gathering, ensuring the young ladies would not find the evening tedious. Indeed, the sisters, relieved to have two new acquaintances, expressed little desire for further additions to the circle.

Mrs. Jennings proved to be exactly as described: a jolly, plump, elderly woman whose conversations flowed rapidly between jokes and observations. While seemingly happy-go-lucky, her wit lacked refinement, bordering on the vulgar. Before dinner concluded, she had already launched into animated discussions regarding love and matrimony, teasing the guests about leaving their hearts behind in Sussex and pretending to detect blushing cheeks where none existed. This relentless prying caused Marianne significant distress, especially considering her sister's delicate feelings. Turning anxiously to Elinor, Marianne watched with deep concern to see how her calm companion would endure such intrusive banter, a sight that weighed heavily on her heart far more than any ordinary teasing could.

Seated among them was Colonel Brandon, Sir John's companion. A strikingly different figure from his host, Colonel Brandon appeared ill-suited to be the friend of a man like Sir John, just as Lady Middleton seemed ill-matched to him. He was silent, grave, and unsmiling. To Marianne and Margaret, he looked like an "absolute old bachelor," yet he was actually closer to fifty than forty. Though his features were not conventionally handsome, his countenance radiated a quiet sense, and his manners were impeccably gentlemanly. Despite the initial impression of the other guests, there was something about his demeanor that commanded a certain, if restrained, respect.

As the evening progressed, the company offered little that the Dashwoods could truly admire. However, the pervasive coldness and insipidity of Lady Middleton rendered the gravity of Colonel Brandon, and even the raucous hilarity of Sir John and his mother, somewhat bearable by comparison. The atmosphere shifted dramatically only after dinner, when Lady Middleton's four noisy children burst into the room, pulling at their mother's dress, tearing at her gown, and reducing all conversation to frantic inquiries about their own needs.

Later, as twilight settled, it became apparent that Marianne possessed a musical talent. An invitation was immediately extended, and the piano was unlocked with anticipation. Every eye turned to her, expecting to be enchanted. At their request, Marianne sang several of the popular songs that Lady Middleton had recently introduced to the household. It was a curious irony that these melodies, once performed with great skill by Lady Middleton's mother, had gathered dust on the instrument since her daughter married, having been abandoned in favor of a life dedicated solely to raising children.

Marianne's performance was met with thunderous applause. Sir John was the loudest in his praise, cheering enthusiastically at the conclusion of every aria and engaging in equally loud chatter with the others throughout the song. Lady Middleton, however, grew increasingly agitated, calling him to order repeatedly. She marveled at how anyone could possibly divert their attention from music for even a moment and implored Marianne to repeat a specific song she had just heard, displaying a complete inability to appreciate nuance or silence.

Amidst this cacophony, Colonel Brandon remained the solitary observer. He listened without joining the frenzy, offering only the polite compliment of attentive hearing. As the others lost themselves in shallow adoration, Marianne felt a sudden, sharp respect for the Colonel. The shamelessness of the crowd's lack of discernment stung her deeply. Her pleasure in his appreciation, though it fell short of the ecstatic fervor required to fully mirror her own inner world, was estimable precisely because it existed in stark contrast to the "horrible insensibility" surrounding them. And yet, as the notes faded, Marianne exercised a measure of reason. She acknowledged that a man of fifty-three might well have outgrown the acute sensitivity and exquisite capacity for enjoyment that fueled her own soul. With a heavy heart, she decided to grant him the allowance of humanity due to his age, forgiving the coldness that time had undoubtedly carved into his spirit.
