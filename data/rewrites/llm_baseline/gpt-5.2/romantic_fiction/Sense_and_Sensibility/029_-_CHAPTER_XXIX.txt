Before the maid had stirred the grate, before even the thin January sun could make a bargain with the fog and win an ounce of warmth from it, Marianne was already awake—if that tossing, tearful fever of mind could be called waking. Half dressed, her hair loose upon her shoulders, she had drawn herself to the window-seat, as though the faintest gray light might serve her for inkstand and courage both. The room was cold enough that Elinor, starting from sleep at the sound of suppressed sobbing, felt it in her lungs; yet Marianne, kneeling, wrote with a desperate speed, pausing only when tears blotted her words and she had to lift her hand to her mouth as if to stifle a cry.

Elinor watched in silence a few moments, her own heart tightening with each shaking breath Marianne drew, then spoke in a voice softened by dread.

“Marianne, may I ask—?”

“No, Elinor,” she said, without turning; and the steadiness of it was more alarming than any wail. “Ask nothing; you will soon know all.”

The calm broke as soon as it was uttered. Grief returned like a wave, and the pen halted again and again. Elinor, moving as gently as if any louder sound might fracture her sister, offered every quiet service—reaching for a ribbon, setting out what clothes she could, standing ready if summoned; but Marianne, in the quick irritability of anguish, begged her—almost with fury—not to speak to her for the world.

So Elinor withdrew, because remaining would only make them both raw with pain, and because Marianne’s mind seemed to require at once solitude and motion, as a sick body craves both heat and air. Marianne dressed at last and began to wander through the house like a spirit that cannot rest—down the passage and back again, to the landing, to the parlour door, away again—avoiding every face.

At breakfast she sat, but did not eat. The table smoked with a comfortable domestic abundance—Mrs. Jennings’s favourite hour, her tea fragrant and dark, toast crisping at the edge of the dish, butter softening into gloss in its little saucer—yet Marianne’s plate remained untouched, the scent of it all seeming only to mock her. Elinor’s whole attention, therefore, turned upon a different sort of management: she tried, by the look of her eyes and the turn of her answers, to keep Mrs. Jennings engaged with herself alone, to draw away notice from Marianne’s white face and fixed silence. Mrs. Jennings lingered, as she liked to do, taking her time with her meal and her cheerful commentary, as steady as the ticking of a clock.

They were scarcely settled afterwards about the common working-table, Mrs. Jennings already measuring out lengths of worsted with the solemnity of an old ritual, when a letter was brought in and placed in Marianne’s hand. She seized it as if it were bread to a starving creature—then the colour fled her cheeks so suddenly that it was like watching a candle go out. Without a word she fled the room.

Elinor needed no direction on the envelope to know whose hand had written. A sickness rose in her, and her limbs trembled so that she feared Mrs. Jennings must see it. But Mrs. Jennings saw only what pleased her notion of romance and humour.

“Upon my word,” she cried, laughing, “I never saw a young woman so desperately in love in my life! My girls were nothing to her, and yet they used to be foolish enough; but as for Miss Marianne, she is quite an altered creature. I hope, from the bottom of my heart, he won’t keep her waiting much longer, for it is quite grievous to see her look so ill and forlorn. Pray, when are they to be married?”

Elinor forced her lips into something like a smile, though it felt stiff as ice.

“And have you really, ma’am,” she said, “talked yourself into a persuasion of my sister’s being engaged to Mr. Willoughby? I thought it had been only a joke, but so serious a question seems to imply more; and I must beg, therefore, that you will not deceive yourself any longer. I do assure you that nothing would surprise me more than to hear of their being going to be married.”

“For shame, for shame, Miss Dashwood! how can you talk so?” Mrs. Jennings cried, not at all checked. “Don’t we all know that it must be a match, that they were over head and ears in love with each other from the first moment they met? Did not I see them together in Devonshire every day, and all day long; and did not I know that your sister came to town with me on purpose to buy wedding clothes? Come, come, this won’t do. Because you are so sly about it yourself, you think nobody else has any senses; but it is no such thing, I can tell you, for it has been known all over town this ever so long. I tell every body of it and so does Charlotte.”

“Indeed, ma’am,” Elinor said, her seriousness cutting through any pretence, “you are mistaken. Indeed, you are doing a very unkind thing in spreading the report, and you will find that you have—though you will not believe me now.”

Mrs. Jennings only laughed again, her hands busy with the worsted, the soft scrape and pull of it through her fingers a homely sound in a morning suddenly made dreadful.

Elinor had no strength for more. She hurried away.

In their room Marianne lay stretched upon the bed as if the bones beneath her skin had gone slack. Her breath came in choking bursts. In one hand she clutched a letter; two or three others lay scattered near her, as though they had fallen from her grasp in the violence of her despair.

Elinor crossed the room and sat beside her without speaking. She took Marianne’s hand and kissed it—once, and again—until her own restraint gave way and tears poured down, answering Marianne’s grief with a grief of her own. For a time they could do nothing but weep, the sound of it filling the cold chamber more completely than any fire could have filled it with heat.

At length Marianne, still unable to form words, gathered the letters with shaking hands and thrust them into Elinor’s lap. Then she covered her face with her handkerchief and cried out, not like a girl disappointed, but like a creature wounded in the vital part.

Elinor waited, because such suffering must spend itself; and when Marianne’s sobs lessened by the smallest degree, Elinor opened the letter that had come that morning.

It was as follows:

“Bond Street, January.

“MY DEAR MADAM,

“I have just had the honour of receiving your letter, for which I beg to return my sincere acknowledgments. I am much concerned to find there was anything in my behaviour last night that did not meet your approbation; and though I am quite at a loss to discover in what point I could be so unfortunate as to offend you, I entreat your forgiveness of what I can assure you to have been perfectly unintentional. I shall never reflect on my former acquaintance with your family in Devonshire without the most grateful pleasure, and flatter myself it will not be broken by any mistake or misapprehension of my actions. My esteem for your whole family is very sincere; but if I have been so unfortunate as to give rise to a belief of more than I felt, or meant to express, I shall reproach myself for not having been more guarded in my professions of that esteem. That I should ever have meant more you will allow to be impossible, when you understand that my affections have been long engaged elsewhere, and it will not be many weeks, I believe, before this engagement is fulfilled. It is with great regret that I obey your commands in returning the letters with which I have been honoured from you, and the lock of hair, which you so obligingly bestowed on me.

“I am, dear Madam,

“Your most obedient humble servant,

“JOHN WILLOUGHBY.”

Elinor’s indignation was so sharp it was almost clarifying, like the sting of spirits on a raw cut. She had expected confession; she had even braced herself for coldness. But this—this careful cruelty, this pretence of distance and decorum, this turning of intimacy into a formal insult—was beyond what she had believed possible in him.

She read it again, and again; and with each perusal her abhorrence grew. Yet she did not dare speak what she felt, because to call it a deliverance—true as it might be—would have been to trample on Marianne’s heart while it still bled.

In the midst of that bitter contemplation, the rumble of wheels came from below—unreasonably early. Elinor went to the window, astonished to see Mrs. Jennings’s chariot at the door though it had not been ordered till one. The street’s winter noise—hooves on hard ground, a coachman’s voice, the brief clank of harness—rose and faded again, as if the world persisted in its daily music without caring what tragedy lay upstairs.

Determined not to quit Marianne, Elinor went down only long enough to excuse herself from attending Mrs. Jennings, pleading her sister’s indisposition.

Mrs. Jennings, in a thoroughly good-humoured concern, admitted the excuse with readiness. Elinor saw her safe off, then returned quickly.

She arrived in time to catch Marianne as she attempted to rise and swayed, faint and giddy, toward the floor. Long want of rest and food had thinned her strength to nothing; for days she had scarcely eaten, and nights had brought her no real sleep. Now that suspense was gone, the body, no longer held up by feverish hope, sank into aching head, weakened stomach, and a general trembling faintness.

Elinor procured a glass of wine at once and persuaded her to take it. The warmth of it—sharp, living—seemed to call her back a little. Marianne’s eyes, swollen and wild, softened into a moment of human sense.

“Poor Elinor,” she whispered, “how unhappy I make you!”

“I only wish,” Elinor answered, “there were any thing I could do, which might be of comfort to you.”

It was too much kindness to bear.

“Oh! Elinor, I am miserable, indeed,” Marianne cried, and her voice broke again into sobbing.

Elinor could not watch it in silence any longer.

“Exert yourself, dear Marianne,” she cried, “if you would not kill yourself and all who love you. Think of your mother; think of her misery while you suffer: for her sake you must exert yourself.”

“I cannot, I cannot,” Marianne sobbed, turning away as if Elinor’s words were a bright light to her sore eyes. “Leave me, leave me, if I distress you; leave me, hate me, forget me! but do not torture me so. Oh! how easy for those, who have no sorrow of their own to talk of exertion! Happy, happy Elinor, you cannot have an idea of what I suffer.”

“Do you call me happy, Marianne? Ah! if you knew!—And can you believe me to be so, while I see you so wretched!”

“Forgive me, forgive me,” Marianne said, flinging her arms around her sister’s neck, clinging as if Elinor were the one steady thing left in a world that had shifted. “I know you feel for me; I know what a heart you have; but yet you are—you must be happy; Edward loves you—what, oh what, can do away such happiness as that?”

“Many, many circumstances,” Elinor said, very gravely.

“No, no, no!” Marianne cried wildly. “He loves you, and only you. You can have no grief.”

“I can have no pleasure while I see you in this state.”

“And you will never see me otherwise. Mine is a misery which nothing can do away.”

“You must not talk so, Marianne. Have you no comforts? no friends? Is your loss such as leaves no opening for consolation? Much as you suffer now, think of what you would have suffered if the discovery of his character had been delayed to a later period—if your engagement had been carried on for months and months, as it might have been, before he chose to put an end to it. Every additional day of unhappy confidence, on your side, would have made the blow more dreadful.”

“Engagement!” Marianne cried, and even in her despair there was a sudden defiance. “There has been no engagement.”

“No engagement!”

“No—he is not so unworthy as you believe him. He has broken no faith with me.”

“But he told you that he loved you.”

“Yes—no—never absolutely. It was every day implied, but never professedly declared. Sometimes I thought it had been, but it never was.”

“Yet you wrote to him?”

“Yes: could that be wrong after all that had passed? But I cannot talk.”

Elinor said no more then. The other letters—three of them now—burned with a stronger curiosity than before. She unfolded them and read quickly, while Marianne lay turned away, exhausted by her own sobbing.

The first, written on their arrival in town, ran thus:

“Berkeley Street, January.

“How surprised you will be, Willoughby, on receiving this; and I think you will feel something more than surprise, when you know that I am in town. An opportunity of coming hither, though with Mrs. Jennings, was a temptation we could not resist. I wish you may receive this in time to come here to-night, but I will not depend on it. At any rate I shall expect you to-morrow. For the present, adieu.

“M.D.”

The second, written the morning after the dance at the Middletons’, said:

“I cannot express my disappointment in having missed you the day before yesterday, nor my astonishment at not having received any answer to a note which I sent you above a week ago. I have been expecting to hear from you, and still more to see you, every hour of the day. Pray call again as soon as possible, and explain the reason of my having expected this in vain. You had better come earlier another time, because we are generally out by one. We were last night at Lady Middleton’s, where there was a dance. I have been told that you were asked to be of the party. But could it be so? You must be very much altered indeed since we parted, if that could be the case, and you not there. But I will not suppose this possible, and I hope very soon to receive your personal assurance of its being otherwise.

“M.D.”

The last, written after the affront of the previous evening, was this:

“What am I to imagine, Willoughby, by your behaviour last night? Again I demand an explanation of it. I was prepared to meet you with the pleasure which our separation naturally produced, with the familiarity which our intimacy at Barton appeared to me to justify. I was repulsed indeed! I have passed a wretched night in endeavouring to excuse a conduct which can scarcely be called less than insulting; but though I have not yet been able to form any reasonable apology for your behaviour, I am perfectly ready to hear your justification of it. You have perhaps been misinformed, or purposely deceived, in something concerning me, which may have lowered me in your opinion. Tell me what it is, explain the grounds on which you acted, and I shall be satisfied, in being able to satisfy you. It would grieve me indeed to be obliged to think ill of you; but if I am to do it, if I am to learn that you are not what we have hitherto believed you, that your regard for us all was insincere, that your behaviour to me was intended only to deceive, let it be told as soon as possible. My feelings are at present in a state of dreadful indecision; I wish to acquit you, but certainty on either side will be ease to what I now suffer. If your sentiments are no longer what they were, you will return my notes, and the lock of my hair which is in your possession.

“M.D.”

Elinor, though furious at Willoughby’s reply, could not read these without a different pain—one made of shame on Marianne’s behalf, of fear at what such unguarded tenderness had risked. The words were so open, so confident, written as if affection itself were a language everyone honoured and interpreted fairly; yet the world did not always speak that tongue, and it could be twisted—coldly, precisely—into an accusation.

Marianne, sensing Elinor had finished, turned her head slightly and said, with a bitterness that tried to disguise itself as certainty, that the letters contained nothing but what any one would have written in the same situation.

“I felt myself,” she added, “to be as solemnly engaged to him, as if the strictest legal covenant had bound us to each other.”

“I can believe it,” Elinor said; “but unfortunately he did not feel the same.”

“He did feel the same, Elinor—for weeks and weeks he felt it. I know he did. Whatever may have changed him now, (and nothing but the blackest art employed against me can have done it), I was once as dear to him as my own soul could wish. This lock of hair, which now he can so readily give up, was begged of me with the most earnest supplication. Had you seen his look, his manner, had you heard his voice at that moment! Have you forgot the last evening of our being together at Barton? The morning that we parted too! When he told me that it might be many weeks before we met again—his distress—can I ever forget his distress?”

Her breath failed her. Then, with a sudden firmness—as if she would rather be wounded again than surrender her belief—she said, “Elinor, I have been cruelly used; but not by Willoughby.”

“Dearest Marianne, who but himself? By whom can he have been instigated?”

“By all the world, rather than by his own heart. I could rather believe every creature of my acquaintance leagued together to ruin me in his opinion, than believe his nature capable of such cruelty. This woman of whom he writes—whoever she be—or any one, in short, but your own dear self, mama, and Edward, may have been so barbarous to bely me. Beyond you three, is there a creature in the world whom I would not rather suspect of evil than Willoughby, whose heart I know so well?”

Elinor would not contend. She only tried to offer the one consolation she could find.

“Whoever may have been so detestably your enemy,” she said, “let them be cheated of their malignant triumph, my dear sister, by seeing how nobly the consciousness of your own innocence and good intentions supports your spirits. It is a reasonable and laudable pride which resists such malevolence.”

“No, no,” Marianne cried. “Misery such as mine has no pride. I care not who knows that I am wretched. The triumph of seeing me so may be open to all the world. Elinor, Elinor, they who suffer little may be proud and independent as they like—may resist insult, or return mortification—but I cannot. I must feel—I must be wretched—and they are welcome to enjoy the consciousness of it that can.”

“But for my mother’s sake and mine—”

“I would do more than for my own. But to appear happy when I am so miserable—Oh! who can require it?”

Silence followed—heavy, restless. Elinor walked from the fire to the window and back again without truly feeling the heat or seeing the day. The household sounds—the distant opening of a door, a servant’s step on the stairs, the faint murmur of London beyond the glass—seemed all at once intrusive, as if the world’s ordinary noise were an offence against private sorrow.

Marianne, seated now at the foot of the bed with her head against one of its posts, took up Willoughby’s letter again. She shuddered over each sentence as if it carried chill on it, and then broke out:

“It is too much! Oh, Willoughby, Willoughby, could this be yours! Cruel, cruel—nothing can acquit you. Elinor, nothing can. Whatever he might have heard against me—ought he not to have suspended his belief? ought he not to have told me of it, to have given me the power of clearing myself? ‘The lock of hair,’” she repeated from the letter, “‘which you so obligingly bestowed on me’—That is unpardonable. Willoughby, where was your heart when you wrote those words? Oh, barbarously insolent!—Elinor, can he be justified?”

“No, Marianne,” Elinor answered, “in no possible way.”

“And yet this woman—who knows what her art may have been?—how long it may have been premeditated, and how deeply contrived by her!—Who is she?—Who can she be?—Whom did I ever hear him talk of as young and attractive among his female acquaintance?—Oh! no one, no one—he talked to me only of myself.”

Another pause, and then Marianne, as if fleeing the very air of London, exclaimed:

“Elinor, I must go home. I must go and comfort mama. Can not we be gone to-morrow?”

“To-morrow, Marianne!”

“Yes—why should I stay here? I came only for Willoughby’s sake—and now who cares for me? Who regards me?”

“It would be impossible to go to-morrow. We owe Mrs. Jennings much more than civility; and civility of the commonest kind must prevent such a hasty removal as that.”

“Well then, another day or two, perhaps; but I cannot stay here long, I cannot stay to endure the questions and remarks of all these people. The Middletons and Palmers—how am I to bear their pity? The pity of such a woman as Lady Middleton! Oh, what would he say to that!”

Elinor urged her to lie down again. Marianne obeyed for a moment, but no posture could keep her still. She shifted, restless in body as in mind, and her agitation grew so violent—so near hysteria—that Elinor feared she might be forced to call for help. At last Marianne allowed herself to take some lavender drops. The sharp, clean scent—like bruised stems, like a garden remembered in winter—seemed to steady her breathing. From then until Mrs. Jennings returned, she lay quiet and motionless on the bed, while Elinor sat near, keeping watch over her as one watches a beloved flame in a draughty room, afraid it will go out.
