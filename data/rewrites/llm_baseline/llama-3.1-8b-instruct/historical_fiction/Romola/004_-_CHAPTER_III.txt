As they extricated themselves from the tangled web of vehicles and mules, the young stranger turned to Nello and confessed, "To be frank, I'm not sorry to be entrusted to you instead of that patron of mine, who spoke with a barbarous accent and a most enigmatic air. Is it common among your fellow Florentines for an itinerant peddler of second-hand goods to boast of a shop selling lutes and swords?"

Nello, whose loquacious nature flowed like an overflowing fountain, replied, "Not common, I assure you. Bratti is no ordinary man. He harbors a theory, and lives by it, which is more than I can say for any philosopher I've had the privilege of shaving. Bratti aims to extract the maximum amount of pleasure – or, rather, the greatest bargains – from life. He intends to leave the world with as little as possible, having already made arrangements to bequeath his earnings to the Church upon his passing. His will, drawn up on the cheapest terms possible, ensures a smooth passage through the afterlife. However, I've often cautioned him, 'Bratti, your bargain is one-sided, and you're at a disadvantage. Don't you find it melancholy to gaze upon the images of the Paradiso? You'll never be able to haggle for scraps and rusty nails there; the saints and angels require neither pins nor tinder, and I see no chance of trading in second-hand clothing, save perhaps with San Bartolommeo, who, as you know, carries his skin about in an awkward manner.'"

As Nello's words trailed off, he crossed himself and changed his tone, "Forgive me, dear stranger, for speaking so lightly on a morning when Lorenzo's passing weighs heavily on our minds, and the Muses are wringing their hands in sorrow – a painful thought for a barber such as myself. And, I daresay, you too must be troubled, for a man of your stature and bearing would not normally take up residence in such humble lodgings unless beset by misfortune."
