The silence of the Harley Street house was not peace; it was a heavy, suffocating blanket woven from the threads of Edith's recovery and the sheer absence of struggle. For Margaret, this extreme quietude was a double-edged sword. While it granted her the natural rest her delicate constitution craved, it also stripped away the familiar friction of life, leaving her adrift in a sea of luxurious stagnation. Within the span of two months, the world had shifted beneath her feet. She was no longer the vicar's daughter of Helstone, nor the humble resident of the Milton cottage where anxiety and poverty were constant companions. Here, the machinery of domesticity ran on silent, oiled gears. Troubles were unknown quantities; cares were non-existent variables.

Mrs. Shaw and Edith treated Margaret with a cloying affection that bordered on infantilization, persistently referring to this grand estate as *her* home. Yet, a bitter seed took root in Margaret's chest. Was it truly ungrateful to harbor a secret longing for the chaotic warmth of Milton? There, amidst her father's worried brow and her mother's frailty, life had a texture, a grit that defined the soul. Now, Edith, eager to reclaim her health, rushed to transform Margaret's bedroom into a mirror of her own vanity, flooding the space with soft comforts and pretty knick-knacks. Mrs. Shaw and her maid labored tirelessly to restore Margaret's wardrobe to a state of elegant variety, while Captain Lennox moved through the days with a gentle, detached competence. He spent hours in his wife's dressing room, playing briefly with his son, and then retreating to his club or dining out, leaving the atmosphere of the house to fester in his wake.

Just as Margaret began to feel the crushing weight of this eventless ease, Edith descended the stairs, resuming her role as the center of the social universe. Margaret, ever dutiful, slipped back into the shadow of service, managing the semblance of duty for her cousin. She answered notes, reminded Edith of engagements, and tended to her when the gaiety faded, all while cultivating a faint illness that allowed her to remain close without demanding energy. But when the rest of the family plunged into the frenetic business of the London Season, Margaret was left alone in the vast rooms. Her thoughts drifted inevitably to Milton, haunted by a strange, hollow vacuum in her heart. She feared she was becoming sleepily deadened, forgetting everything beyond the lapping luxury of her surroundings. The servants, she observed, lived in an underground realm of their own, appearing only to serve a whim or a want, their hopes and fears invisible to her.

One evening, sitting alone in the Lennox drawing room, Margaret held Dixon's letters in her hand but refused to open them. The cessation of news from Milton felt like a sudden famine. Occasionally, Dixon's business-like correspondence quoted Mr. Thornton regarding furniture or landlords, but the name rarely surfaced, and the connection to her old life remained severed. She wondered if anyone in that whirlwind missed her, if Higgins remembered her, when suddenly the door opened and Mr. Bell was announced. Margaret hurried the letters into her workbasket, rising with a blush that betrayed her guilt.

"Oh, Mr. Bell! I never thought of seeing you!"

"You give me a welcome, I hope, as well as that very pretty start of surprise," Mr. Bell replied, his eyes twinkling.

"Have you dined? How did you come? Let me order you some dinner."

"If you're going to have any. Otherwise, there is no one who cares less for eating than I do. But where are the others? Gone out to dinner? Left you alone?"

"Oh yes! and it is such a rest. I was just thinking—"

"But will you run the risk of dinner? I don't know if there is anything in the house."

"Why, to tell you the truth, I dined at my club. Only they don't cook as well as they did, so I thought, if you were going to dine, I might try and make out my dinner. But never mind! There aren't ten cooks in England to be trusted at impromptu dinners. If their skill and their fires will stand it, their tempers won't. You shall make me some tea, Margaret. And now, what were you thinking of? Whose letters were those, god-daughter, that you hid away so speedily?"

"Only Dixon's," Margaret replied, her face flushing deeper.

"Whew! Is that all? Who do you think came up in the train with me?"

"I don't know," Margaret said, resolving against guessing.

"Your what d'ye call him? What's the right name for a cousin-in-law's brother?"

"Mr. Henry Lennox?"

"Yes," Mr. Bell confirmed. "You knew him formerly, didn't you? What sort of a person is he, Margaret?"

"I liked him long ago," Margaret admitted, glancing down. Then she looked up, her expression hardening slightly. "You know we have been corresponding about Frederick since; but I have not seen him for nearly three years, and he may be changed. What did you think of him?"

"I don't know. He was so busy trying to find out who I was, in the first instance, and what I was in the second, that he never let out what he was; unless indeed that veiled curiosity of his as to what manner of man he had to talk to was not a good piece, and a fair indication of his character. Do you call him good-looking, Margaret?"

"No! certainly not. Do you?"

"Not I. But I thought perhaps you might. Is he a great deal here?"

"I fancy he is when he is in town. He has been on circuit now since I came. But—Mr. Bell—have you come from Oxford or from Milton?"

"From Milton. Don't you see I'm smoke-dried?"

"Certainly. But I thought that it might be the effect of the antiquities of Oxford."

"Come now, be a sensible woman! In Oxford, I could have managed all the landlords in the place, and had my own way, with half the trouble your Milton landlord has given me, and defeated me after all. He won't take the house off our hands till next June twelve-month. Luckily, Mr. Thornton found a tenant for it. Why don't you ask after Mr. Thornton, Margaret? He has proved himself a very active friend of yours, I can tell you. Taken more than half the trouble off my hands."

"And how is he? How is Mrs. Thornton?" Margaret asked hurriedly, her voice dropping below her normal pitch despite her effort to sound casual.

"I suppose they're well. I've been staying at their house till I was driven out of it by the perpetual clack about that Thornton girl's marriage. It was too much for Thornton himself, though she was his sister. He used to go and sit in his own room perpetually. He's getting past the age for caring for such things, either as principal or accessory. I was surprised to find the old lady falling into the current, and carried away by her daughter's enthusiasm for orange-blossoms and lace. I thought Mrs. Thornton had been made of sterner stuff."

"She would put on any assumption of feeling to veil her daughter's weakness," Margaret whispered.

"Perhaps so. You've studied her, have you? She doesn't seem over fond of you, Margaret."

"I know it," Margaret said. "Oh, here is tea, at last!" she exclaimed, as if relieved by the interruption.

With the arrival of tea came Mr. Henry Lennox. He had walked up to Harley Street after a late dinner, expecting to find his brother and sister-in-law at home. Margaret suspected him of being as thankful as she was for the presence of a third party, especially considering their first meeting since the memorable day of his offer and her refusal at Helstone. She could hardly find words, grateful for the trivial occupations of the tea table that provided an excuse for silence and allowed him to recover his composure. Indeed, he had forced himself to Harley Street tonight, hoping to navigate an awkward encounter that was already difficult in the presence of Captain Lennox and Edith, and now doubly so with her standing alone as the primary conversational partner.

She was the first to regain her self-possession.

"Mr. Lennox, I have been so much obliged to you for all you have done about Frederick."

"I am only sorry it has been so unsuccessful," he replied, casting a quick, assessing glance toward Mr. Bell as if gauging how far he could push the conversation before hitting a wall. Margaret, sensing his hesitation, immediately pivoted to address Mr. Bell, effectively including him in the dialogue and implying his awareness of the efforts made to clear Frederick's name.

"That Horrocks—that very last witness of all—has proved as unavailing as all the others. Mr. Lennox has discovered that he sailed for Australia only last August; only two months before Frederick was in England, and gave us the names of—"

"Frederick in England! You never told me that!" Mr. Bell exclaimed in genuine surprise.

"I thought you knew. I never doubted you had been told. Of course, it was a great secret, and perhaps I should not have named it now," Margaret said, a flicker of dismay crossing her features.

"I have never named it to either my brother or your cousin," Mr. Lennox stated, his tone carrying a professional dryness that felt like an implied reproach.
