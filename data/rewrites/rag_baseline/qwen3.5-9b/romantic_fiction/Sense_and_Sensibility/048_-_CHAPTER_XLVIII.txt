Elinor now understood the crushing weight of certainty compared to the mere anticipation of disaster. While her mind had long accepted the inevitability of Edward's marriage to Lucy, a stubborn, irrational hope had persisted until the final moment—that some twist of fate, a sudden shift in his resolve, or a new opportunity for the lady would intervene to save them all. But the wedding was done. The news struck her with a force that condemned her own heart for the lingering flattery that had amplified the agony of the truth.

She was startled initially by the speed of the union; he was married before he could possibly be ordained, before he held the living that defined his future. Yet, logic quickly clarified the chaos. Lucy, driven by a frantic self-providence and a desperate haste to secure him, had undoubtedly overlooked every precaution except the risk of delay. They were wed in town and now racing toward her uncle's estate. Elinor tried to visualize the scene: Edward, within four miles of Barton, spotting her mother's servant, receiving Lucy's message. Soon they would be settled at Delaford—a place woven so tightly into her own interests that she simultaneously craved acquaintance with it and recoiled from its proximity.

In her mind's eye, she saw the parsonage house. She saw Lucy as the active, scheming manager, balancing a desire for smart appearances with extreme frugality, terrified of suspicion regarding her economy. She pursued her own agenda in every thought, courting the favor of Colonel Brandon, Mrs. Jennings, and every wealthy patron. As for Edward, she saw nothing distinct, nothing she wished to see. Whether happy or wretched, nothing pleased her; she turned her head from every mental sketch of him.

Elinor consoled herself with the belief that a connection in London would soon write to announce the event and provide details. Day after day passed, bringing no letter, no tidings. Though unsure who was at fault, she blamed every absent friend for being thoughtless or indolent. "When do you write to Colonel Brandon, ma'am?" the question sprang from her mind's impatience to find *something* happening. "I wrote to him, my love, last week, and rather expect to see, than to hear from him again. I earnestly pressed his coming to us, and should not be surprised to see him walk in today or tomorrow, or any day." This was progress, something to anchor her gaze upon. Colonel Brandon *must* possess information.

Scarcely had she solidified this determination when the silhouette of a rider on horseback arrested her attention at the window. He stopped at their gate. A gentleman. Colonel Brandon himself. Now she could hear the truth; now she trembled in expectation. But it was not Brandon. Neither his bearing nor his stature matched the memory. Could it be Edward? She looked again. He had just dismounted; she could not be mistaken—it *was* Edward.

She moved away and sat down. "He comes from Mr. Pratt's purposely to see us. I *will* be calm; I *will* be mistress of myself." Instantly, she realized the others shared her confusion. She saw her mother and Marianne flush, glance at her, and whisper urgently. She would have given anything to speak, to assure them that no coolness, no slight, would mar their behavior toward him—but she possessed no utterance and was forced to surrender the matter entirely to their discretion. No syllable passed aloud. They all waited in silence for the arrival of the visitor.

His footsteps echoed along the gravel path; moments later, he appeared in the passage, and shortly after, stood before them. His countenance, as he entered, was not even remotely happy for Elinor. His complexion was pale with agitation, his expression suggesting a fear of his reception and a consciousness that he deserved none. Mrs. Dashwood, trusting implicitly in the judgment of that daughter whom she currently intended to guide her in all things, met him with a look of forced complacency, offered her hand, and wished him joy. He colored, stammering an unintelligible reply. Elinor's lips had moved in unison with her mother's, and when the ritual concluded, she wished desperately that she had shaken his hand too. But it was too late. With a countenance striving to remain open, she sat down again and discussed the weather.

Marianne had retreated as far as possible from view to hide her distress; Margaret, grasping only fragments of the case, deemed it incumbent upon her to maintain dignity, so she seated herself as distant from him as possible and kept a strict silence. When Elinor finally ceased feigning rejoicing in the dryness of the season, an awful, suffocating pause descended upon the room. Mrs. Dashwood broke it, feeling obliged to hope he had left Mrs. Ferrars well. In a hurried manner, he affirmed it. Another silence stretched taut.

Resolving to exert herself, though dreading the sound of her own voice, Elinor asked, "Is Mrs. Ferrars at Longstaple?"

"At Longstaple!" he replied, with an air of genuine surprise.

"No," Elinor corrected, picking up some work from the table, "I meant to inquire for Mrs. *Edward* Ferrars." She dared not look up, but her mother and Marianne both turned their eyes sharply onto him. He flushed, seemed perplexed, looked doubtful, and after a hesitation, said, "Perhaps you mean—my brother—you mean Mrs.—Mrs. *Robert* Ferrars."

"Mrs. Robert Ferrars!" Marianne and her mother repeated in accents of utmost amazement. Though Elinor could not speak, her eyes were fixed on him with the same impatient wonder. He rose from his seat, walked to the window as if unsure of his next move, picked up a pair of scissors lying nearby, and began slicing the sheath to pieces as he spoke in a hurried voice.

"Perhaps you do not know: you may not have heard that my brother is lately married to—to the youngest—to Miss Lucy Steele."

His words were echoed with unspeakable astonishment by everyone present except Elinor. She sat with her head bowed over her work, in a state of such violent agitation that she barely recognized her surroundings.

"Yes," he continued, "they were married last week, and are now at Dawlish."

Elinor could endure it no longer. She almost ran from the room. As soon as the door closed behind her, she burst into tears of joy, weeping so violently at first that she feared she would never stop. Edward, who had been looking everywhere else rather than at her, saw her hurry away. Perhaps he saw—or even heard—her emotion; for immediately afterward, he fell into a reverie that no remark, no inquiry, no affectionate address from Mrs. Dashwood could penetrate. Finally, without saying a word, he quit the room and walked out toward the village, leaving the others in the greatest astonishment and perplexity regarding a change in his situation so wonderful and so sudden. They had no means of lessening this confusion but through their own wild conjectures.
