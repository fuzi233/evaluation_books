"Open," Bardo commanded, his voice cutting through the quiet of the study.

Maso stepped aside, and the two figures entered. First came Nello, the barber, who performed a deep, theatrical reverence toward Romola before gently pushing Tito forward. The younger man advanced with a measured grace, positioning himself directly before the blind scholar.

"Messer Bardo," Nello announced, his tone shifting from his usual boisterous cadence to one of calculated respect, "I present to you a Greek scholar. He has sought your counsel with urgency, driven not only by the high reputation of your learning and your priceless collections—which I have detailed to him—but by the desperate necessity of finding patronage amidst the ruin brought upon him by shipwreck. His name is Tito Melema, at your service."

Romola's astonishment was absolute, bordering on disbelief. Had the stranger appeared clad in a panther-skin and wielding a thyrsus, her reaction could not have been sharper. Nello had omitted all mention of the youth's age or visage. In the company of her father's intellectual guests, she had rarely encountered anyone but middle-aged men, their faces etched with the gray of decades. Yet here stood a masculine face that was simultaneously youthful and radiant, an image that struck her mind with the force of a physical blow. It reminded her of her brother, long gone, whose fair face and sunny hair had once been hers to cherish.

However, the habitual armor of her mind—a proud self-reliance forged in the fires of her father's complaints against a cruel world—stood as a snowy embankment, halting the rushing tide of her admiration. She maintained her composure, returning Tito's initial bow with the same pale, reserved expression she wore for all strangers. As he drew nearer, though, the snow began to melt. When he glanced toward her again during Nello's introduction, a flush of color swept across her cheeks, vanishing instantly as if summoned back by her imperious will.

Tito's gaze, conversely, held a gentle, beseeching quality that served as the ultimate appeasement to a proud, shy woman. It possessed the finished fascination of a sleek, dark-eyed animal that delights not by fleeing, but by resting its chin upon one's palm, looking upward with a silent desire to be stroken—as if it loved the hand that touched it. There was no demand in his air, only an offering of presence.

"Messer, I give you welcome," Bardo said, his voice tinged with a condescending warmth. "Misfortune coupled with learning, particularly Greek learning, serves as a letter of credit that commands the attention of every educated Florentine. As you surely know, since the era when Manuelo Crisolora first diffused the light of his teachings across Italy nearly a century ago, no man earns the title of scholar unless he possesses the original sources. Those who rely solely on transplanted Latin derivatives are rightly stigmatized as *opici* or barbarians, echoing the Roman phrase regarding those who drink from secondary springs rather than the source. I perceive, as Nello has warned you, that I am totally blind. A calamity to which we Florentines are uniquely susceptible, whether due to the biting winds from the Apennine passes or the jarring transition from our dim interiors to the blinding glare of the summer sun—a condition said to have plagued the ancients. Or perhaps it stems from some occult cause beyond our grasp. Be seated, Messer. Nello, join us."

Bardo waited until the visitors settled, ensuring Romola took her customary place at his right hand, before continuing.

"From which region of Greece do you hail, Messer? I feared your homeland had been stripped bare of those capable of cherishing its former glory, though the barbarous Sultans recently demonstrated a willingness to graft their precious vines onto their own trampled stock. Where are you from?"

"I sailed last from Nauplia," Tito replied smoothly. "I have also resided in Constantinople and Thessalonica, traveling through regions largely untouched by Western Christians since the triumph of Turkish arms. However, Messer, I was not born in Greece, but in Bari. I spent my first sixteen years in Southern Italy and Sicily."

As Tito spoke, a subtle emotion rippled across Bardo's delicate features. He leaned forward, extending his right hand toward Romola, turning his head as if to address her, but then corrected himself, withdrawing slightly to speak in a subdued tone.

"Excuse me—is it not true... you are young?"

"I am twenty-three," Tito answered.

"Ah," Bardo murmured, his voice thick with suppressed excitement, "then you undoubtedly had a father devoted to your early education—one who was himself a scholar?"

A brief pause stretched between them. For Bardo, it was a moment of inquiry; for Romola and Nello, it triggered a sharp shock, causing Tito's lip to quiver momentarily, betraying the resurgence of a profoundly painful memory.

"Yes," Tito replied, his voice steadying. "At least a father by adoption. He was Neapolitan, a man of accomplished scholarship in both Latin and Greek. But," he added after another hesitation, "he is lost to me. Lost on a voyage he rashly undertook to Delos."

Bardo sank back in his chair, too refined to press further into a sorrow he sensed was raw and recent. Romola, acutely attuned to the fibers of her father's grief, realized with startling clarity that this new acquaintance had breached the barrier separating them from the alien world outside. Seizing the lull in conversation, Nello, relieved of the burden of silence, interjected.

"In truth, it is as transparent as Venetian glass that this fine young man received the finest training. The two Cennini have already set him to work on their Greek texts. These are not men to cut before feeling the edge of their tools; they tested him thoroughly beforehand. If there are two things impossible to conceal—love and a cough—I posit a third: ignorance. Once a man must act beyond mere nodding, the *tonsor inequalis* betrays himself. Is it not so, Messer Bardo? I speak as a barber, yet as Luigi Pulci declares—"

"Nay, my good Nello," Bardo interrupted with a severity masked by friendliness, "you are not entirely illiterate and could have achieved greater respectability had you avoided the *cicalata* and street-corner gossip to which our Florentines are so prone. Even more, you should have spared your memory for frivolous productions like those of Luigi Pulci—a compendium of extravagances and incongruities far removed from the purity of earlier ages, resembling the *grylli* or conceits of a time when mystic meaning justified monstrous forms. The difference lies in the fact that while the monstrosity remains, the mystic meaning is absent. This stands in contemptible contrast to the great poem of Virgil, whom I long upheld with Filelfo, before Landino dared to echo similar opinions, as embodying the deepest philosophical lessons within a graceful, coherent fable. I cannot help but view the proliferation of these babbling, lawless works—even those countenanced by the patronage of Lorenzo himself, otherwise a friend of true learning—as a sign that the glorious hopes of this century will be extinguished in gloom. Nay, they serve as a delusive prologue to an age worse than Iron: the age of tinsel and gossamer, where no thought possesses the substance to be molded into anything consistent or enduring."

"Once more, pardon," Nello pleaded, spreading his palms and shrugging his shoulders. "I find myself knowing so much in good Tuscan before I can summon the Latin equivalent; Messer Luigi's rhymes slip off my customers' lips—that is what corrupts me. Indeed, speaking of customers, I have left my shop and my reputation too long in the custody of my slow Sandro, who deserves neither the title of *tonsor inequalis* nor the label of a bungler in the vernacular. With your permission, Messer Bardo, I shall take my leave, understanding that I remain at your service whenever Maso visits. It feels like a thousand years since I dressed and perfumed the damigella's hair, which deserves to shine in the heavens as a constellation, though 'twould be a pity for it ever to stray so far from reach."

Three voices wove a fugue of friendly farewells as Nello retreated, bowing to Romola and gesturing to Tito. The sharp-eyed barber recognized that the pretty youth, who had captured his affection through some potent magic, was firmly established in Bardo's favorable regard. Satisfied that his introduction had succeeded thus far, he withdrew.

The brief outburst of anger provoked by Nello's unfortunate quotation had diverted Bardo's mind from the emotions that had previously constrained his speech. He now addressed Tito with his usual calm demeanor.

"Ah! Young man, you are fortunate to have united the advantages of travel with those of study. You will be welcomed among us as a bearer of fresh tidings from a land that has become strangely distant to us, accessible only through the limited channels of commerce and the hurried accounts of pilgrims. Those days lie far in the past when men like Aurispa and Guarino journeyed to Greece as to a storehouse, returning laden with manuscripts that scholars coveted and often failed to return.
