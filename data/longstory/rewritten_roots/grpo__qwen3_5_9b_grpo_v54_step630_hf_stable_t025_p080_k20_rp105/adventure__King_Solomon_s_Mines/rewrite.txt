The year was 1898, and the world outside my study was changing faster than the maps I kept pinned to the wall. It was a time when the old ways of exploration were being swept away by the steam and steel of empire, yet there remained a certain romantic hunger for the unknown, a longing for the "wild adventure" that still thrilled the blood of those who dared to seek it.

It was in this spirit that I first encountered Sir Henry Curtis. He was a man of striking presence, with eyes that held the sharp, calculating gleam of a gambler and the steady resolve of a soldier. We met under circumstances that would soon bind our fates together, drawn by a shared fascination with a legend that had haunted the British imagination for centuries: the lost mines of King Solomon.

The story, as it was told to me, was one of impossible wealth and ancient magic. Deep within the heart of Africa, hidden behind a veil of impenetrable jungle and guarded by tribesmen said to be immune to all but the most potent sorcery, lay the treasures of the biblical king. Gold, diamonds, and rubies of such magnitude that they could buy kingdoms. But the price of entry was steep. The entrance was marked by a stone pillar inscribed with a warning that had silenced countless expeditions before them. To find the mines was to face not just the elements, but the very wrath of the earth itself.

Our journey required more than just courage; it demanded local knowledge. Thus, we secured the services of Umbopa, a Zulu tracker of immense skill and quiet dignity. He was a man who understood the land better than any European, able to read the wind and the movement of insects to predict the path of a beast or the location of a spring. His inclusion in our party was the first step in a chain of events that would lead us far from civilization.

Before we could march into the desert, we needed to prove our worth and secure supplies. An elephant hunt became our trial by fire. It was a brutal affair, testing our marksmanship and our nerve against the might of the largest land animal on Earth. In the chaos of the chase, bonds of trust were forged in blood and sweat, and we learned the harsh realities of survival in the bush.

With our party assembled, we turned our backs on the greenery of the coast and headed north, into the scorching expanse of the Kalahari Desert. The heat was a physical weight, pressing down upon us, turning the sand into a shimmering mirage that played tricks on the mind. Every step was a battle against thirst and exhaustion, a test of endurance that separated the dreamers from the survivors.

The cry of "Water!" echoed through the ranks like a death knell. As the days stretched into weeks without relief, desperation began to set in. The men grew weak, their spirits fraying under the relentless sun. We were driven by a primal need, pushing forward until our feet bled and our throats burned, hoping against hope that the legends of hidden springs were true.

Finally, we found it. Not a spring, but a road—a faint, dusty track that seemed to appear out of nowhere, leading directly into the heart of the unknown. It was the Road of Solomon, the path that had eluded explorers for generations. Following it felt like walking into a dream, a place where the laws of nature seemed suspended, waiting for us to make our final choice.

Beyond the road lay Kukuanaland, a land shrouded in mystery and fear. The air changed here, growing heavier, charged with an unseen energy. The landscape shifted from open scrub to dense, alien vegetation. We knew we were entering territory where the rules of the civilized world no longer applied, and where the inhabitants were said to possess powers that defied explanation.

We encountered Twala, the King of the Kukuanas. He was a figure of terrifying majesty, a warrior-king who commanded the loyalty of his people through strength and sorcery. His presence was overwhelming, a mix of regal authority and primal ferocity. He saw us not as allies, but as intruders, and the tension between our two worlds was palpable, hanging in the air like a storm about to break.

Conflict erupted quickly. What began as a misunderstanding spiraled into a full-scale witch-hunt, fueled by superstition and the fear of the unknown. Our men were accused of bringing bad luck, of being agents of evil. The line between friend and foe blurred in the chaos, and we were forced to fight for our lives against a people who believed they were defending their sacred land from destruction.

In the midst of the confusion, we made a desperate attempt to communicate, to show that we came in peace. We gave a sign, a gesture of goodwill that we hoped would bridge the gap between our cultures. But in the heat of battle, signs are often misinterpreted, and trust is easily broken.

The calm before the storm was short-lived. As the dust settled, the reality of the situation became clear. We were outnumbered and outmatched, facing a foe that was both physically superior and spiritually formidable. The men looked to their leaders, seeking guidance in the face of impending doom.

Then, the attack came. It was swift and devastating, a whirlwind of spears and arrows that swept through our lines. The battle was fierce, a clash of steel and sorcery that tested every ounce of our courage. We fought with everything we had, knowing that retreat was not an option.

Among our ranks were the Grey Boys, a group of seasoned veterans who had seen more battles than most men could count. They formed a last stand, holding the line against the overwhelming odds. Their sacrifice was heroic, a testament to the code of honor that bound them together, even as the tide of battle turned against them.

Amidst the carnage, Good fell ill. The stress of the battle, combined with the harsh conditions, took its toll on his already fragile health. He lay dying, a poignant reminder of the cost of our quest. His passing weighed heavily on our hearts, a shadow that followed us as we continued our fight.

We sought a place of rest, a sanctuary where we could mourn our fallen and regroup. But the land offered no comfort, only the cold indifference of nature. The place of death was a somber reminder of the fragility of life in the face of such vast and unforgiving wilderness.

Despite our losses, we pressed on, driven by the promise of the treasure. Finally, we reached the chamber of Solomon. It was a sight to behold, a hall of gold and jewels that sparkled in the torchlight. The wealth was beyond imagination, a testament to the power and glory of the ancient king.

Yet, as we stood amidst the riches, a strange feeling of emptiness washed over us. The treasure, so coveted, offered no solace for the lives we had lost. We abandoned hope, realizing that some things were not meant to be taken, and that the true cost of our adventure was far greater than the gold we had found.

Ignosi, a key figure in our journey, bid us farewell. His words were heavy with meaning, a final lesson on the nature of fate and the inevitability of loss. He reminded us that some doors, once opened, cannot be closed, and that the path we had chosen would forever change us.

And so, we found ourselves back in the world of the living, changed by the experience. The mines of Solomon were real, the legends true, but the story we carried in our hearts was far more complex than any map could show. We had found what we sought, but in doing so, we had also lost something precious. And yet, as I write this now, looking back on those days, I know that the adventure was worth every sacrifice, every moment of terror, and every tear shed along the way.

As this volume presses against the world, a heavy weight settles upon my shoulders—a profound sense of its inadequacies, both in style and substance. Regarding the content, let it be known that this is not a comprehensive chronicle of every step taken or sight witnessed during our expedition into the heart of Kukuanaland. There are threads of our journey I would have woven at length, yet here they remain barely touched. Among these omissions are the haunting legends of the chain armor that shielded us in the great battle of Loo, and the terrifying mysteries of the "Silent Ones," those colossal guardians guarding the mouth of the stalactite cave. Had I yielded to my own impulses, I might have dissected the subtle, suggestive divergences between the Zulu and Kukuana dialects, or dedicated pages to the indigenous flora and fauna of this strange land.

Yet, the most fascinating subject of all—the magnificent military organization of the Kukuanas, which surpasses even Chaka's reforms in Zululand by allowing rapid mobilization without the scourge of enforced celibacy—has been mentioned only incidentally. Nor have I detailed their domestic customs, which are exceedingly quaint, nor their mastery over the smelting and welding of metals. Their "tollas," those heavy throwing knives forged with such skill that hammered iron backs meet beautifully tempered steel edges, stand as testament to a science they have perfected.

In truth, alongside Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good, I concluded that the wisest path was to recount our tale in a plain, straightforward manner, leaving such specialized inquiries for future study. For now, I offer this account as it stands, ready to share any further details with those who seek them.

Finally, I must apologize for the bluntness of my prose. I confess I am far more accustomed to handling a rifle than a pen. I cannot pretend to the grand literary flourishes found in novels, though I admit I enjoy reading them. Perhaps those embellishments are necessary, and I regret my inability to provide them; yet, I cannot shake the belief that simple truths are often the most striking. Books become clearer when written in plain language. As the Kukuana saying goes, "A sharp spear needs no polish." By that same logic, I venture to hope that a true story, however extraordinary, does not require decoration with fine words.

It is a peculiar irony that at fifty-five, a man who has spent his life in the mud and blood of the bush should sit here in Durban, attempting to chronicle his existence. I wonder what sort of history this will be when I am done, if I ever finish. My life has been a long one, largely because I began working so young. While other boys were learning their ABCs, I was already a trader in the old Colony. Since then, I have traded, hunted, fought, and mined. Yet, it was only eight months ago that I finally made my fortune—a pile so large I still cannot fully grasp its magnitude. And yet, I would not willingly endure the last year and a half of that life again, not even knowing I would emerge safe with my gold. I am a timid soul by nature, averse to violence, and frankly, I am weary of adventure. Which brings me to the question: why write this book? It is not my trade. I am no literary man, though I hold the Old Testament and the *Ingoldsby Legends* in high regard.

Let me list my reasons, simply to see if they hold water.

First, Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good asked me to.

Second, I am currently bedridden here in Durban, plagued by a pain in my left leg. That cursed lion, which I thought I had bested, has left me with a lingering malady. There seems to be a poison in a lion's teeth that reawakens the wound annually, usually around the time of the original mauling. It is a cruel twist of fate to have shot sixty-five lions in my day only for the sixty-sixth to chew my leg like a piece of tobacco. It disrupts the rhythm of my life, and an orderly man dislikes disorder.

Third, I wish to amuse my boy, Harry, who is studying medicine in London. Hospital work can become tedious, even dissecting the dead fails to sustain interest forever. This tale, whatever else it may be, will surely provide a spark of life for him during his studies.

Fourth, and finally, because I intend to recount the strangest story I have ever known. It is a strange admission, considering there is no woman in it—save for Foulata. Stop! There is Gagaoola, if she counts as a woman, though she is more fiend than female. She was a hundred years old, so marriageability is not an issue. Regardless, I can safely state there is not a single petticoat in this entire history.

Enough preamble. The yoke is heavy, and I feel bogged down to the axle. But *sutjes, sutjes*, as the Boers say—softly, softly does it. A strong team will pull through eventually, provided they are not too poor. Now, let us begin.

I, Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal, Gentleman, make oath... No, that is how I began my deposition regarding the tragic deaths of Khiva and Ventvögel, but it lacks the gravitas of a novel's opening. Besides, am I truly a gentleman? The definition eludes me. I have certainly dealt with natives who fit the description, and I have encountered wealthy whites from home who do not. Perhaps I was born one, though my life as a wandering trader and hunter suggests otherwise. Heaven knows I have tried to maintain the dignity of the title. I have taken many lives, but never wantonly, and never in innocent blood. The Almighty gave us life, I assume, for us to defend it. I have acted on that principle, and I pray it will not be my undoing when my clock strikes. This world is cruel and wicked, and for a timid man, I have been embroiled in considerable fighting. I cannot justify every action, but I have never stolen, though I must confess to cheating a Kafir out of a herd of cattle once. He had done me a dirty turn, and the guilt has haunted me ever since.

It was roughly eighteen months ago that I first encountered Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good. I had returned from an ill-fated elephant hunt beyond Bamangwato, suffering from fever. Once recovered, I trekked to the Diamond Fields, liquidated my ivory, wagon, and oxen, and took the post-cart to the Cape. After a week in Cape Town—where I found the hotels overpriced and the Houses of Parliament unlikely to achieve much—I decided to return to Natal aboard the *Dunkeld*, waiting for the *Edinburgh Castle*.

Among the passengers from the *Edinburgh Castle* were two men who immediately captured my attention. One was a gentleman of about thirty, perhaps the most imposing figure I had ever seen. He possessed a chest of immense breadth, long arms, yellow hair, a thick yellow beard, and clear-cut features with large grey eyes set deep in his head. He reminded me vividly of an ancient Dane. I recall a picture of such gentry, drinking from large horns with long hair cascading down their backs. If Sir Henry Curtis, as it later turned out he was of Danish blood, had grown his hair slightly longer, donned a chain shirt, and gripped a battle-axe and a horn mug, he would have been a perfect model for that painting.

The other man, conversing with Sir Henry, was stout, dark, and distinctly different. I recognized him instantly as a naval officer; one rarely mistakes a man of the sea. They are, in my experience, the finest fellows, washed clean by the wide oceans and the breath of God's winds. I learned his name was Captain John Good. He was broad, dark, and neatly groomed, wearing an eyeglass permanently fitted into his right eye. It seemed to grow there, lacking a string, removed only to be wiped. He also possessed two sets of false teeth, beautiful ones that have tempted me to break the tenth commandment more than once.

As evening fell, bringing with it a foul, aggravated Scotch mist, we retreated below deck. The *Dunkeld*, a flat-bottomed punt, rolled heavily in the keen breeze. Standing near the warm engines, I watched a pendulum swing lazily.

"That pendulum is wrong; it is not properly weighted," a testy voice remarked beside me.

Turning, I saw Captain Good. "Indeed? What makes you think so?"

"I don't think at all," he replied sharply. "If the ship had rolled to the degree indicated, it would never have righted itself. These merchant skippers are confoundedly careless."

The dinner bell rang, a relief, for listening to a Royal Navy officer on ship stability is a dreadful experience. Captain Good and I dined together, falling into conversation about shooting. He was inquisitive, asking endless questions until he turned to elephants.

"Ah, sir," someone nearby interjected, "you've reached the right man. Hunter Quatermain knows elephants."

Sir Henry Curtis, who had been silent, leaned forward, his deep voice resonating across the table. "Excuse me, sir, but is your name Allan Quatermain?"

"Yes," I confirmed.

He muttered "fortunate" into his beard. Later, as we left the saloon, Sir Henry invited me to his cabin. It was a spacious room, formerly two cabins knocked open by Sir Garnet Wolseley or similar grandees. With a bottle of whisky and pipes lit, the three of us settled in.

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry began, "about this time last year, you were at Bamangwato, north of the Transvaal."

"I was," I admitted, surprised by his knowledge of my travels.

"You were trading there?" Captain Good added quickly.

"Yes. I camped outside the settlement until my goods were sold."

Sir Henry fixed his large grey eyes upon me, a look of intense anxiety in them. "Did you meet a man named Neville there?"

"Oh, yes. He outspanned alongside me for a fortnight to rest his oxen. I received a letter from a lawyer recently asking if I knew his whereabouts."

"Yes," Sir Henry continued. "Your letter was forwarded to me. You stated that Neville left Bamangwato in early May with a wagon, a driver, and a Kafir hunter named Jim, intending to trek as far as Inyati, the trading post in Matabeleland. You mentioned he sold his wagon there six months later to a Portuguese trader, who claimed the buyer was a white man with a native servant heading into the interior on a shooting trip."

"Yes."

A silence stretched between us.

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said suddenly, "do you know, or can you guess, the purpose of Mr. Neville's journey north?"

"I heard something," I replied, halting. The subject was not one I wished to discuss openly.

Sir Henry and Captain Good exchanged glances.

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry pressed, "I am going to tell you a story and ask for your advice and assistance. The agent who forwarded your letter assured me of your implicit reliability and your reputation for discretion."

I bowed, taking a drink of whisky and water to steady my nerves. I am a modest man, after all.

"Mr. Neville was my brother," Sir Henry revealed.

"Oh," I started. Now I recalled the resemblance. His brother was smaller, with a dark beard, but the same shade of grey eyes and the same keen intensity.

"He was my only and younger brother," Sir Henry explained, his voice heavy. "Until five years ago, we were inseparable. Then a misfortune struck. We quarreled bitterly, and in my anger, I behaved unjustly toward him."

Captain Good nodded vigorously, the ship rolling beneath us.

"As you may know," Sir Henry continued, "if a man dies intestate with only land, it descends to the eldest son. Our father died intestate just as our quarrel reached its peak. My brother, untrained for any profession, was left penniless. It was my duty to provide, but the bitterness was so great I did not offer help. I waited for him to make advances, but he made none. I am ashamed to admit this, Captain."

"Quite so," Captain Good agreed. "Mr. Quatermain will keep this history to himself."

"Of course," I assured him, proud of my discretion.

"My brother had a few hundred pounds," Sir Henry went on. "Without telling me, he withdrew the sum, adopted the name Neville, and sailed for South Africa in a wild hope of fortune. Three years passed with no news, despite my letters. Time wore on, and my anxiety grew. I realized, Mr. Quatermain, that blood is thicker than water. I would give half my fortune to know my brother George was safe and well."

"But you never did, Curtis," Captain Good interrupted, glancing sharply at the big man.

"Well," Sir Henry sighed, "as time went on, my desire to find him and bring him home became overwhelming. I set enquiries in motion, and your letter was a crucial result. It confirmed George was alive until recently, but it did not go far enough. So, I resolved to come out myself and search for him. Captain Good was kind enough to accompany me."

"Yes," the captain said. "Turned out by the Lords of the Admiralty to starve on half-pay. And now, perhaps, you will tell us what you know of Mr. Neville."

"What news did you gather regarding my brother's expedition at Bamangwato?" Sir Henry asked, pausing as I stopped to pack my pipe.

"I heard this," I replied, "and kept it strictly to myself until today. I was told he was heading for Solomon's Mines."

"Solomon's Mines?" both Sir Henry and Captain Good exclaimed simultaneously. "Where exactly are they located?"

"I do not know their precise location," I admitted. "I only know where they are said to be. Once, I caught sight of the peaks bordering them, but there lay a hundred and thirty miles of barren desert between me and the mountains. To my knowledge, no white man has ever crossed that wasteland except for one solitary individual. However, the best course of action may be to recount the legend of Solomon's Mines as I understand it, provided you promise not to disclose anything I share without my explicit permission. Do you agree? I have my reasons for requesting this secrecy."

Sir Henry nodded solemnly, and Captain Good assented with equal gravity.

"Well," I began, "as you might surmise, elephant hunters are generally a rugged breed of men, concerned little with more than the necessities of survival and the customs of the local tribes. Yet, occasionally, one encounters a man who takes the trouble to collect oral traditions from the natives, striving to piece together fragments of this dark continent's history. It was such a man who first imparted the legend of Solomon's Mines to me, nearly thirty years ago. This occurred during my inaugural elephant hunt in the Matabele lands. His name was Evans, a poor soul who was killed the following year by a wounded buffalo and now lies buried near the Zambesi Falls.

"One evening, I recall, I recounted to Evans some remarkable mining operations I had discovered while hunting koodoo and eland in what is now the Lydenburg district of the Transvaal. I observe that prospectors have recently rediscovered these workings in search of gold, though I had known of them years prior. There exists a vast, wide wagon road carved directly from solid rock, leading to the entrance of a gallery. Inside this mouth, stacks of gold quartz were piled high, ready for roasting, indicating that the workers, whoever they were, fled in haste. Furthermore, approximately twenty paces inward, the gallery was abruptly sealed off by exquisite masonry.

"'Ay,' Evans remarked, 'but I shall weave you a far stranger tale.' And so he did. He described finding a ruined city deep in the interior, which he believed to be the biblical Ophir—a notion shared by other scholars long before Evans's time. I listened with rapt attention, for I was young then, and the story of an ancient civilization and its treasures, extracted by old Jewish or Phoenician adventurers from a land long since descended into barbarism, seized my imagination completely. Suddenly, he asked, 'Lad, have you ever heard of the Suliman Mountains to the northwest of the Mushakulumbwe country?' I replied that I had not. 'Ah, well,' he said, 'that is where Solomon truly held his mines—his diamond mines, I mean.'

"'How can you know that?' I queried.

"'Know it!' he retorted. 'Why, what is "Suliman" but a corruption of Solomon? Besides, an old Isanusi witch-doctoress up in the Manica country told me everything. She claimed the people living beyond those mountains were a branch of the Zulus, speaking a dialect of Zulu, yet finer and larger men. Among them dwelt great wizards who had learned their arts from white men when the world was still dark, possessing the secret of a wondrous mine of "bright stones."'

"At the time, I laughed at this tale, though it intrigued me, for the Diamond Fields had not yet been discovered. Poor Evans departed and was soon killed, and for twenty years I gave the matter no further thought. However, just twenty years later—that is a considerable span, gentlemen; an elephant hunter rarely survives two decades in his trade—I received more concrete information regarding Suliman's Mountains and the territory beyond. I was stationed beyond the Manica country at a place called Sitanda's Kraal, a miserable outpost where sustenance was scarce and game was thin. I suffered from a severe fever and was in a wretched state when a Portuguese arrived with a single companion, a half-breed.

"I am well acquainted with the low-class Delagoa Portuguese, notorious devils who thrive on human suffering and flesh in the form of slaves. Yet, this man was entirely different. In appearance, he resembled the noble *doms* I had read about: tall, thin, with large dark eyes and curling grey mustaches. We conversed for a while; he spoke broken English, and I understood a little Portuguese. He introduced himself as José Silvestre, stating he owned a property near Delagoa Bay. The following day, accompanied by his half-breed servant, he bid farewell, removing his hat in the old-fashioned manner.

"'Good-bye, señor,' he said. 'If our paths cross again, I shall be the richest man in the world, and I shall remember you.' I chuckled slightly, though my weakness prevented much laughter, watching him stride toward the great western desert. I wondered if he was mad or what he hoped to find there.

"A week passed, and I recovered from my fever. One evening, sitting on the ground before my tent, chewing the last leg of a miserable fowl purchased from a native for a piece of cloth worth twenty birds, I stared at the hot red sun sinking over the desert. Suddenly, I spotted a figure, seemingly European due to its coat, crawling along the slope of the rising ground opposite me, about three hundred yards away. The figure moved on hands and knees, then attempted to stand, staggered a few yards, fell, and crawled again. Recognizing someone in distress, I dispatched one of my hunters. He returned shortly, revealing the truth.

"It was José Silvestre—or rather, his skeleton and a fragment of skin. His face was a sickly yellow from bilious fever, his large dark eyes bulging from their sockets as all flesh had vanished. Nothing remained but yellow, parchment-like skin, white hair, and gaunt bones protruding beneath.

"'Water! For the sake of Christ, water!' he moaned. His lips were cracked, and his tongue, protruding between them, was swollen and blackish.

"I gave him water mixed with a little milk, which he gulped down in great quantities, perhaps two quarts, without pause. I refused him any more. Then the fever returned, and he collapsed, beginning to rave about Suliman's Mountains, diamonds, and the desert. I carried him into the tent and did what little I could, knowing the end was near. Around eleven o'clock, he grew quieter. I lay down for a brief rest and fell asleep. At dawn, I awoke to see Silvestre sitting upright, a strange, gaunt figure, gazing out toward the desert. Soon, the first ray of sunlight swept across the vast plain, reaching the distant crest of one of the tallest Suliman Mountains, over a hundred miles away.

"'There it is!' cried the dying man in Portuguese, pointing with his long, thin arm. 'But I shall never reach it, never. No one will ever reach it!'

"Suddenly, he paused, appearing to resolve himself. 'Friend,' he said, turning to me, 'are you there? My eyes grow dark.'

"'Yes,' I replied. 'Lie down now and rest.'

"'Ay,' he answered. 'I shall rest soon; I have eternity to rest. Listen, I am dying! You have been kind to me. I will give you the writing. Perhaps you will reach it if you can survive the desert, which has claimed my poor servant and me.'

"He groped in his shirt and produced what I thought was a Boer tobacco pouch made of the skin of the Swart-vet-pens or sable antelope, fastened with a strip of hide known as a *rimpi*. He struggled to loosen it but failed, handing it to me instead. 'Untie it,' he instructed. I complied and extracted a piece of torn yellow linen bearing rusty letters. Inside the rag lay a paper.

"Then he grew weaker. 'The paper contains all that is on the linen. It took me years to decipher. Listen: my ancestor, a political refugee from Lisbon and one of the first Portuguese to land on these shores, wrote this when dying on those mountains no white foot has ever trodden before or since. His name was José da Silvestra, and he lived three hundred years ago. His slave, waiting on this side of the mountains, found him dead and brought the writing home to Delagoa. It has remained in the family ever since, but none cared to read it until I did. I have lost my life over it, but another may succeed and become the richest man in the world—the richest man in the world. Give it to no one, señor; go yourself!'

"He then wandered off, and within an hour, he was gone.

"God rest him! He died quietly, and I buried him deeply, placing large boulders upon his breast, so I doubt the jackals disturbed his grave. Afterward, I departed."

"But the document?" Sir Henry asked, his voice thick with interest.

"Yes, the document; what did it contain?" the captain added.

"Well, gentlemen, if you wish, I shall tell you. I have never shown it to anyone except a drunken old Portuguese trader who translated it for me and forgot it by the next morning. The original rag resides at my home in Durban, along with poor Dom José's translation, but I possess the English rendering in my pocket-book and a facsimile of the map, if one can be called that. Here it is."

*"I, José da Silvestra, who am now dying of hunger in the little cave where no snow falls on the north side of the nipple of the southernmost of the two mountains I have named Sheba's Breasts, write this in the year 1590 with a cleft bone upon a remnant of my raiment, my blood being the ink. If my slave should find it when he comes, and should bring it to Delagoa, let my friend (name illegible) bring the matter to the knowledge of the king, that he may send an army which, if they survive the desert and the mountains, and can overcome the brave Kukuanes and their devilish arts, to which end many priests should be brought, will make him the richest king since Solomon. With my own eyes I have seen the countless diamonds stored in Solomon's treasure chamber behind the white Death; but through the treachery of Gagool the witch-finder I might bring nought away, scarcely my life. Let him who comes follow the map, and climb the snow of Sheba's left breast till he reaches the nipple, on the north side of which is the great road Solomon made, from whence three days' journey to the King's Palace. Let him kill Gagool. Pray for my soul. Farewell.*

An illustration of a sketch map of the route to King Solomon's Mines followed the text.

Upon finishing the reading and displaying the copy of the map, drawn by the dying hand of the old Dom with blood for ink, a profound silence of astonishment fell over us.

"Well," said Captain Good, "I have circumnavigated the globe twice and visited most ports, but may I be hanged for a mutineer if I have ever heard a yarn like this from a storybook, or in one, for that matter."

"It is a peculiar tale, Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry observed. "I suppose you are not hoaxing us? It is sometimes considered permissible to deceive a greenhorn."

"If you think so, Sir Henry," I said, feeling deeply offended, and pocketing my paper—for I dislike being counted among those foolish individuals who deem it witty to lie, and who boast to newcomers of extraordinary hunting adventures that never occurred—"if you think so, then the matter ends here," and I rose to leave.

Sir Henry placed his large hand upon my shoulder. "Sit down, Mr. Quatermain," he said. "I beg your pardon; I see clearly that you do not wish to deceive us, but the story sounded so strange that I could hardly believe it."

"You shall see the original map and writing when we reach Durban," I answered, somewhat mollified, for upon reflection, it was scarcely surprising that he doubted my good faith.

"But," I continued, "I have not yet told you about your brother. I knew the man Jim who accompanied him. He was a Bechuana by birth, an excellent hunter, and for a native, a very clever man. On the morning Mr. Neville departed, I saw Jim standing by my wagon, cutting tobacco on the disselboom.

"'Jim,' I asked, 'where are you off to on this trip? Elephants?'

"'No, Baas,' he answered, 'we are after something worth much more than ivory.'

"'And what might that be?' I asked, curious. 'Is it gold?'

"'No, Baas, something worth more than gold,' and he grinned.

"I asked no further questions, unwilling to lower my dignity by appearing inquisitive, but I was puzzled. Presently, Jim finished cutting his tobacco.

"'Baas,' he said.

"I ignored him.

"'Baas,' he said again.

"'Eh, boy, what is it?' I asked.

"'Baas, we are going after diamonds.'

"'Diamonds! Why, then, you are heading in the wrong direction; you should aim for the Fields.'

"'Baas, have you ever heard of Suliman's Berg?'—that is, Solomon's Mountains, Sir Henry.

"'Ay!'

"'Have you ever heard of the diamonds there?'

"'I have heard a foolish story, Jim.'

The voyage from the Cape to Durban usually consumes four or five days, dictated by the vessel's speed and the whims of the weather. Occasionally, if the landing at East London proves treacherous—where they claim to be sinking fortunes into a harbor that remains little more than a dream—a ship may be stranded for a day while cargo boats struggle to navigate the breakers. But on this occasion, the Bar lay clear. The tugs surged forward immediately, trailing long lines of flat-bottomed barges that devoured the cargo with a chaotic crash. It mattered not whether the crates held delicate porcelain or heavy wool; they were tossed aboard with equal indifference. I watched a case containing four dozen bottles of champagne shatter instantly, the liquid fizzing and boiling in the filth of the barge. It was a wasteful spectacle, and the Kafirs on board seemed to share the sentiment. They scavenged two unbroken bottles, smashed the necks, and drank deeply. They had failed to account for the carbonation expanding within them; soon, they were rolling in the bilge, shouting that the "good liquor" was *tagati*—bewitched. I shouted down to them from the deck, warning that the white man's strongest medicine would kill them, but the terror in their eyes was sufficient punishment. They fled to the shore, and I suspect they will never touch champagne again.

While we steamed northward toward Natal, my mind was consumed by Sir Henry Curtis's proposal. We avoided the subject for a day or two, filling the silence with tales of the hunt, stories that required no embellishment for anyone who knows the wild.

Finally, on a brilliant January evening, our hottest month, we passed the Natal coast, intending to drop anchor at Durban Point before sunset. The shoreline was a tapestry of red sandhills and vivid green sweeps, dotted with Kafir kraals and laced with a ribbon of white surf crashing against the rocks. Yet, as we neared Durban, the landscape grew richer. Sheer kloofs carved by centuries of rain cut through the hills, feeding sparkling rivers, while the bush displayed its deepest greens alongside the cultivated patches of maize and sugar. Here and there, a white house smiled upon the placid sea, adding a touch of civilization to the scene. To me, a view is incomplete without the presence of man; perhaps it is because I have dwelt so long in the wilderness that I cherish the mark of society, even if it drives the game away. The Garden of Eden may have been fair before humanity, but I believe it was fairer when Eve adorned it.

We had miscalculated slightly; the sun dipped below the horizon before we anchored off the Point, hearing the signal gun that announced the arrival of the English Mail. Too late to cross the Bar that night, we retired to dinner, watching the mailbags secured in the lifeboat.

When we returned to the deck, the moon hung low, casting a silver pallor that nearly outshone the lighthouse beams. Sweet, spicy scents drifted from the shore, evoking hymns and missionaries, while a hundred lights twinkled in the windows of the Berea. Nearby, a brig groaned as sailors hauled up the anchor, their music mingling with the sea. It was a perfect night, one that draped a garment of peace over everyone, even the great bulldog of a sporting passenger. The beast, usually eager to confront the baboon in the forecastle cage, now snored contentedly at the cabin door, dreaming of victory.

Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, and I took our station by the wheel, falling into a quiet contemplation.

"Well, Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said eventually, "have you given thought to my proposals?"

"Ay," Captain Good echoed. "What is your decision, Mr. Quatermain? Will you join us for Solomon's Mines, or wherever this Mr. Neville has vanished?"

I rose, tapped out my pipe, and let the tobacco fall into the sea. I had needed that brief moment to decide.

"Yes, gentlemen," I said, resuming my seat. "I will go. But I must state my terms clearly.

"First, you shall bear all expenses. Any ivory or valuables we acquire shall be divided between Captain Good and myself.

"Second, you shall pay me £500 upfront for my services. I pledge my faithfulness until you abandon the enterprise, until we succeed, or until disaster claims us.

"Third, before we depart, you must execute a deed. In the event of my death or permanent disablement, you agree to provide my son, Harry—who studies medicine at Guy's Hospital in London—with £200 annually for five years. By then, he should be capable of supporting himself. That is my condition."

"No," Sir Henry replied promptly. "I accept them gladly. I am resolved on this project and would pay more for your unique knowledge."

"Pity I did not ask for more, but I shall not retract my word. Now, for my reasons. I have observed you both closely, and I find I like you; I believe we will work well together. That is a vital asset for such a journey.

"As for the journey itself, I must speak plainly: I do not believe we shall survive if we attempt to cross the Suliman Mountains. Consider the fate of Dom da Silvestra three centuries ago, his descendant twenty years later, and your own brother. Their ends were grim, and I fear ours will be similar."

I paused to gauge their reaction. Captain Good looked uneasy, but Sir Henry's expression remained stoic. "We must take our chance," he said.

"You may wonder," I continued, "why a man of my timidity undertakes a path I foresee as fatal. There are two reasons. First, I am a fatalist. I believe my time is appointed regardless of my will. If I am destined to die in the Suliman Mountains, I shall go and I shall die. God knows my fate, and I need not worry. Second, I am a poor man. For forty years I have hunted and traded, yet I have barely scraped a living. The average elephant hunter lives only four or five years. I have survived seven generations of my class; my time is near. If I were to die in the ordinary course of business, my debts would consume everything meant for my son Harry. But now, he is secured for five years. That is the whole matter."

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said, his gaze intense, "your motives for undertaking an enterprise you believe leads only to disaster reflect great credit upon you. Whether you are right or wrong, time will show. But I tell you now: I will see this through to the end, sweet or bitter. If we are to be knocked on the head, I hope we get a little shooting first, eh, Good?"

"Yes, yes," the captain agreed. "We are accustomed to holding our lives in our hands. There is no turning back now. Let us go below for a drink to toast our luck."

We descended through the bottom of a tumbler, and the next day we went ashore. I lodged Sir Henry and Captain Good in the small shanty I call home on the Berea—a three-room structure of green brick with a galvanized iron roof, surrounded by a garden of loquat and mango trees tended by Jack, an old Griqua hunter whose leg was shattered by a buffalo. Sir Henry and Good slept in a tent pitched in my grove of orange trees, enjoying the scent of bloom and the sight of fruit in all stages of ripeness.

With my decision made, preparations began. I secured the deed from Sir Henry, a process complicated by his status as a stranger and the overseas nature of the property, but eventually finalized with a lawyer's fee of £20. I pocketed my £500 cheque. On Sir Henry's behalf, I purchased a twenty-two-foot wagon with iron axles and seasoned stink wood, a "half-tented" design allowing space for necessities in the front and sleeping quarters in the rear. I bought a team of twenty Zulu oxen, smaller but hardier than Africander breeds, selected for their resistance to red water and inoculated against lung sickness—a cruel but necessary procedure involving a slit in the tail and a piece of diseased lung tissue.

Provisioning and medicine followed. Captain Good, possessing a surprising aptitude for surgery despite lacking formal qualifications, assembled a splendid traveling chest. He demonstrated his skills by amputating a Kafir's big toe with such precision that the patient, watching stolidly, requested a replacement toe, noting that a "white one" would suffice in a pinch.

Our armament was chosen with meticulous care from Sir Henry's English stores and my own collection. We selected three heavy breech-loading double-eight elephant guns, three double-500 Expresses for medium game and men, a double No. 12 central-fire shotgun for potting, three Winchester repeating rifles, and three Colt revolvers. Every weapon was of compatible caliber, ensuring interchangeable cartridges—a vital detail for survival.

For our crew, we limited ourselves to five: a driver, a leader, and three servants. We found two Zulus, Goza and Tom, easily. Recruiting servants, however, proved difficult. We required men of absolute trust and courage. Eventually, we secured Ventvögel, a Hottentot and a master tracker tough as whipcord, though prone to drink—a weakness irrelevant as we traveled beyond the reach of grog shops. We also hired Khiva, a small Zulu who spoke perfect English.

On the eve of our departure, Khiva informed me that a Kafir awaited me. A tall, handsome man of about thirty, lighter-skinned than most Zulus, entered and squatted silently, lifting his knob-stick in salute. He wore the black ring of a *Keshla*, a sign of dignity, and his face was strangely familiar.

"What is your name?" I asked.

"Umbopa," he replied in a deep, slow voice.

"I have seen your face before."

"Yes; the Inkoosi, my father, saw me at the Little Hand on the day before the battle."

The memory struck me. I had been a guide for Lord Chelmsford during the Zulu War, leaving the camp in charge of wagons the day before the disaster. I had spoken with this man, a commander of native auxiliaries, who had warned me of the camp's peril. I had dismissed his words then, but they had lingered in my mind.

"I remember," I said. "What do you want?"

"It is this, 'Macumazahn,'" he said. "I hear you embark on a great expedition far into the North with white chiefs. Is it true?"

"It is."

"I hear you go even to the Lukanga River, a moon's journey beyond Manica. Is this so?"

"Why do you ask? What is it to you?" I asked suspiciously, for our destination was a secret.

"It is this, O white men, that if you travel so far, I would travel with you."

There was an unusual dignity in his speech, particularly his use of "O white men" rather than "O Inkosis."

"You forget yourself," I said gently. "What is your name, and where is your kraal?"

"My name is Umbopa. I am of the Zulu people, yet not of them. My tribe's house lies in the far North, abandoned when the Zulus migrated south a thousand years ago, before Chaka reigned. I have no kraal; I have wandered for many years. I came from the North as a child to Zululand. I served in Cetewayo's Nkomabakosi Regiment under the great Captain, Umslopogaasi of the Axe, who taught my hands to fight. Later, I fled Zululand to see the white man's ways. I fought against Cetewayo in the war. Since then, I have worked in Natal. Now I am tired. This is not my place. I want no money, but I am a brave man, and I am worth my place and meat. I have spoken."

I was puzzled by his demeanor and his offer to serve without pay. Something about him felt distinct from the ordinary Zulu. Translating his words to Sir Henry and Captain Good, I sought their counsel.

Sir Henry instructed me to ask him to stand. Umbopa complied, shedding his long military greatcoat to reveal himself naked save for a *moocha* around his waist and a necklace of lion's claws. He was magnificent, standing over six feet three inches tall, broad and shapely, his skin appearing almost dark save for deep black scars marking old assegai wounds. Sir Henry approached him, studying his proud face.

"They make a good pair, don't they?" Captain Good remarked. "One as big as the other."

"I like your looks, Mr. Umbopa," Sir Henry said in English. "I will take you as my servant."

Umbopa understood, answering in Zulu, "It is well." He glanced at Sir Henry's great stature and breadth, adding, "We are men, thou and I."

I shall not chronicle in exhaustive detail the arduous trek from Durban to Sitanda's Kraal, a journey spanning over a thousand miles. The final three hundred were undertaken on foot, a necessity imposed by the dreaded tsetse fly, whose bite proves fatal to all creatures save donkeys and men. We departed Durban in late January, camping near the kraal only in the second week of May. While our travels were fraught with peril, the incidents were of a nature common to every African hunter, save for one exception I shall detail later. To avoid rendering this history tedious, I omit the mundane tribulations of the road.

At Inyati, the outpost in the Matabele lands ruled by the cruel King Lobengula, we reluctantly abandoned our comfortable wagon. Of the twenty fine oxen purchased in Durban, only twelve remained. One succumbed to a cobra's venom; three perished from thirst and starvation; another wandered lost; and three more died after consuming the poisonous "tulip" herb. Five others fell ill from the same cause, yet we saved them with a potent infusion of boiled tulip leaves, a timely antidote.

Leaving the wagon and the remaining oxen in the capable hands of Goza and Tom, our trusted drivers, alongside a local missionary, we set out on foot. Our party consisted of myself, Sir Henry, Captain John Good, Umbopa, Khiva, Ventvögel, and six hired bearers. A profound silence hung over us as we departed, each wondering if we would ever see our wagon again; I confess I never expected to. We tramped in silence until Umbopa, marching ahead, broke the quiet with a Zulu chant. He spoke of brave souls fleeing the tameness of civilization into a vast wilderness to find new things or die, only to discover that the wilderness was not a void, but a paradise of wives, cattle, game, and enemies. His words broke the spell, and we laughed, taking it as a fortunate omen. Umbopa, a cheerful savage with a knack for lifting spirits, quickly became a beloved companion.

Our first true adventure occurred about a fortnight's march from Inyati. We entered a breathtaking expanse of woodland, where kloofs were choked with dense "idoro" bush and "wacht-een-beche," while the lovely "machabell" trees bore heavy clusters of yellow fruit. These trees were the elephants' favorite food, and the land bore the scars of their destructive feasting: broken branches, uprooted trunks, and tracks everywhere.

One evening, after a long day's march, we found a spot of exceptional beauty. At the foot of a bush-clad hill lay a dry riverbed dotted with pools of crystal water, trodden smooth by game. Facing this hill was a park-like plain of flat-topped mimosa and glossy machabells, surrounded by a sea of silent, pathless bush.

Emerging onto the riverbed, we startled a troop of tall giraffes. They sailed away in their strange, swaying gait, tails curled over their backs, hooves rattling like castanets. Though three hundred yards away and beyond effective range, Good, walking ahead with an express loaded with solid ball, could not resist. He fired at the last young cow. By extraordinary chance, the ball struck it squarely on the neck, shattering the spinal column. The giraffe collapsed head over heels, a sight so bizarre it defied description.

"Curse it!" Good exclaimed, his nautical habit of strong language surfacing. "I've killed him."

"_Ou_, Bougwan!" the Kafirs cried, calling him "Glass Eye" for his spectacles.

"Oh, 'Bougwan!'" echoed Sir Henry and I. From that day, Good's reputation as a marvel of marksmanship was cemented among the Kafirs, despite his actual inaccuracy. We tasked some of the boys with cutting the giraffe's meat and then constructed a "scherm"—a circular hedge of thorn bushes—near a pool. Inside, we smoothed the ground, laid a bed of dry grass, and lit a fire.

As the moon rose, we dined on giraffe steaks and roasted marrow-bones, a luxury I know of none greater, save perhaps the elephant's heart, which we secured the next day. We ate by moonlight, pausing to thank Good for his lucky shot, before settling around the fire to smoke and talk. We made a striking tableau: I, short, dark, and thin with grizzled hair; Sir Henry, tall, broad, and fair with long yellow locks; and Captain Good, sitting upon a leather bag, immaculate in brown tweed, perfectly shaved, sporting a white gutta-percha collar, and carrying his false teeth and eyeglass in his pocket.

"They weigh so little," he explained innocently regarding his attire. "I always like to turn out like a gentleman." Alas, he could not foresee the raiment prepared for him.

While we yarned, the Kafirs smoked their intoxicating "daccha" from eland-horn pipes before rolling into their blankets. Umbopa, however, sat apart, deep in thought. Suddenly, a loud "woof, woof" echoed from the bush—a lion. Before we could react, the strident trumpeting of an elephant answered from the pool. "_Unkungunklovo! Indlovu!_" the Kafirs whispered. Shadowy giants emerged from the darkness.

Good leaped up, eager for slaughter, but I held him back. "It's no good," I whispered. "Let them go."

Sir Henry, however, argued for staying. "We are in a paradise of game. I vote we stop here a day or two and have a go at them." Surprised, for Henry usually favored haste, I realized his hunter instincts had taken hold. Good agreed, and so did I, for my conscience rebelled at letting such a herd escape.

"All right, my hearties," I said. "We need recreation. Let's turn in; we must be off by dawn."

Good meticulously removed his clothes, folded his glasses and false teeth, and placed them under his mackintosh to stay dry. Sir Henry and I made do with rougher arrangements and soon slept.

*Going, going, go.* What was that?

Violent scuffling erupted from the water, followed by awful roars. We scrambled up to see a confused mass of yellow and black staggering toward us. Seizing our rifles and slipping on our veldtschoons, we ran out of the scherm. The mass had fallen and was rolling on the ground.

We approached to find a sable antelope bull, the most beautiful of African antelopes, dead. Transfixing its great curved horns was a magnificent black-maned lion, also dead. The sable had come to drink, springing upon the waiting lion only to be impaled. Maddened by pain, the antelope had charged blindly until it dropped.

After examining the beasts, we called the Kafirs to drag the carcasses to the scherm and retired to sleep until dawn.

At first light, we prepared for the hunt, armed with three eight-bore rifles, ample ammunition, and water-bottles filled with weak cold tea, the best stimulant for shooting. Umbopa, Khiva, and Ventvögel accompanied us, leaving the others to skin the lion and antelope.

We easily found the broad elephant trail, which Ventvögel estimated was made by twenty to thirty full-grown bulls. The herd had moved during the night, but by nine o'clock, amidst broken trees and smoking droppings, we knew they were near.

We spotted the herd in a hollow, finishing their morning meal and flapping great ears. They were only two hundred yards away. Checking the wind, I confirmed it blew from the elephants toward us. Creeping stealthily through the cover, we closed to within forty yards. Three splendid bulls stood broadside before us, one with enormous tusks.

"Now," I whispered.

*Boom! Boom! Boom!* The heavy rifles thundered. Sir Henry's elephant fell instantly, shot through the heart. Mine hit its knees; I thought it dead, but it rose and charged straight past me. I fired the second barrel into its ribs, bringing it down, then finished it with a shot to the brain. Turning to Good, I found him in a frenzy. The bull he targeted had turned, charging blindly past him toward our camp, while the herd crashed off in the opposite direction.

We debated pursuing the wounded bull or the fleeing herd, eventually choosing the latter. We followed their trail, crushed like a carriage road through the thick bush, but catching up proved difficult. Under the broiling sun, we struggled for over two hours before finding them. Except for one bull, the herd stood together, alert and testing the air. That solitary bull, fifty yards from the herd and sixty from us, acted as sentry. Fearing he would wind us and start the flight, we aimed at him.

The three shots took effect, and he fell. The herd started again, but fate intervened. About a hundred yards further lay a nullah, a dried water track with steep banks, reminiscent of where the Prince Imperial fell in Zululand. The elephants plunged into it. Reaching the edge, we found them struggling in wild confusion to climb the other bank, screaming and trumpeting in panic, pushing one another aside like frightened humans.

Seizing the opportunity, we fired rapidly, killing five of the beasts. We might have taken the whole herd had they not suddenly abandoned their climb and rushed headlong down the nullah. Too tired and weary of slaughter, we rested. The Kafirs cut out the hearts of two dead elephants for supper, and we decided to send the bearers the next day to chop the tusks.

Shortly after passing the spot where Good had wounded the patriarchal bull, we encountered a herd of eland. Good, eager to see them close, handed his rifle to Umbopa and, followed by Khiva, strolled toward a bush patch where they had stopped. We waited, enjoying the sunset.

Suddenly, an elephant screamed. We saw its huge form silhouetted against the setting sun, rushing forward. Next, we saw Good and Khiva tearing back toward us, the wounded bull charging after them. For a moment, we hesitated to fire, fearing to hit either man or beast. Then, disaster struck. Good, bound by his obsession with civilized dress, wore trousers and gaiters that hindered his desperate flight. About sixty yards from us, his polished boot slipped on dry grass. He fell face-down directly in the elephant's path.

We gasped, knowing he must die, and ran toward him. In three seconds, it was over, but not as we feared. Khiva, seeing his master fall, bravely turned and threw his assegai straight into the elephant's face, lodging it in the trunk.

In a scream of agony, the brute seized the poor Zulu, hurled him to the earth, placed a huge foot on his body, and twisted its trunk around his upper part, tearing him in two.

We rushed forward, mad with horror, firing again and again until the elephant fell upon Khiva's mangled remains. Good rose, wringing his hands over the brave man who had died saving him. Even I, an old hand, felt a lump rise in my throat. Umbopa stood contemplating the dead giant and the tragic remains of his friend.

"Ah, well," he said softly. "He is dead, but he died like a man!"

The slaughter of the elephants was a grim business that consumed two full days. We hacked out nine magnificent tusks, averaging between forty and fifty pounds each—a finer lot of ivory I have never encountered. The pair from the great bull that felled poor Khiva weighed nearly one hundred and seventy pounds. Khiva himself, however, received a different burial; we interred his remains in an ant-bear hole, placing an assegai beside him as a weapon for his journey to the afterlife. By the third day, we resumed our march, carrying the heavy hope that we might one day return to reclaim our buried treasure. After a long, wearisome tramp punctuated by adventures I lack the space to detail, we finally reached Sitanda's Kraal, situated near the Lukanga River—the true starting point of our quest.

I remember our arrival vividly. To our right lay a scattered native settlement with stone cattle kraals and patches of cultivated grain near the water, beyond which stretched vast tracts of waving veld teeming with small game. To our left, however, the landscape abruptly terminated in the vast, unforgiving desert. This spot appeared to be the outpost of the fertile country, a stark boundary where the soil's character shifted with unnatural suddenness. Just below our encampment flowed a small stream, beyond which rose a stony slope. It was the same slope down which, twenty years prior, I had witnessed poor Silvestre creeping back after his failed attempt to reach Solomon's Mines. Beyond that slope began the waterless desert, carpeted with a hardy karoo shrub.

As evening fell, we pitched our camp. The great sun sank into the desert, casting glorious, multi-colored rays across its expanse. I left Good to organize the camp and took Sir Henry to the top of the opposite slope to gaze across the dunes. The air was crystal clear, and far in the distance, I could distinguish the faint blue outlines of the Suliman Berg, capped here and there with snow.

"There," I said, pointing, "there is the wall surrounding Solomon's Mines. But God knows if we shall ever climb it."

"My brother should be there," Sir Henry replied, his voice steady with that quiet confidence that defined him. "And if he is, I shall reach him somehow."

"I hope so," I answered, turning back toward camp, only to find we were not alone. Standing behind us, gazing earnestly at the distant mountains, was the great Kafir, Umbopa.

The Zulu spoke as he noticed my attention, addressing Sir Henry, whom the locals called Incubu. "Is it to that land thou wouldst journey, Incubu?" he asked, pointing with his broad assegai toward the mountains.

I demanded sharply why he addressed his master so familiarly. It is proper for natives to have names among themselves, but it is indecent to call a white man by their heathenish appellations in his face. Umbopa laughed a quiet, irritating laugh.

"How dost thou know that I am not the equal of the Inkosi whom I serve?" he countered. "He is of a royal house, no doubt; one sees it in his size and mien. So, mayhap, am I. At least, I am as great a man. Be my mouth, O Macumazahn, and say my words to the Inkoos Incubu, my master, for I would speak to him and to thee."

Anger flared within me; I was not accustomed to being spoken to thus by Kafirs. Yet, he impressed me, and curiosity held me back. I translated his words, noting his impudence and outrageous swagger.

"Yes, Umbopa," Sir Henry answered calmly, "I would journey there."

"The desert is wide and holds no water," Umbopa warned. "The mountains are high and covered with snow, and man cannot say what lies beyond them behind the place where the sun sets. How shalt thou come thither, Incubu, and wherefore dost thou go?"

I translated again.

"Tell him," Sir Henry said, "that I go because I believe a man of my blood, my brother, has gone there before me, and I journey to seek him."

"That is so, Incubu," Umbopa replied. "A Hottentot I met on the road told me that a white man went out into the desert two years ago towards those mountains with one servant, a hunter. They never came back."

"How do you know it was my brother?" Sir Henry asked.

"Nay, I know not. But the Hottentot, when I asked what the white man was like, said he had thine eyes and a black beard. He said, too, that the name of the hunter with him was Jim; that he was a Bechuana hunter and wore clothes."

"There is no doubt about it," I said. "I knew Jim well."

Sir Henry nodded. "I was sure of it," he said. "If George set his mind upon a thing he generally did it. It was always so from his boyhood. If he meant to cross the Suliman Berg, he has crossed it, unless some accident overtook him, and we must look for him on the other side."

Umbopa understood English, though he rarely spoke it. "It is a far journey, Incubu," he interjected.

"Yes," answered Sir Henry. "It is far. But there is no journey upon this earth that a man may not make if he sets his heart to it. There is nothing, Umbopa, that he cannot do, there are no mountains he may not climb, there are no deserts he cannot cross, save a mountain and a desert of which you are spared the knowledge, if love leads him and he holds his life in his hands counting it as nothing, ready to keep it or lose it as Heaven above may order."

I translated.

"Great words, my father," answered the Zulu—I always called him a Zulu, though he was not truly one—"great swelling words fit to fill the mouth of a man. Thou art right, my father Incubu. Listen! What is life? It is a feather, it is the seed of the grass, blown hither and thither, sometimes multiplying itself and dying in the act, sometimes carried away into the heavens. But if that seed be good and heavy it may perchance travel a little way on the road it wills. It is well to try and journey one's road and to fight with the air. Man must die. At the worst he can but die a little sooner. I will go with thee across the desert and over the mountains, unless perchance I fall to the ground on the way, my father."

He paused, then continued with one of those strange bursts of rhetorical eloquence that Zulus sometimes indulge in, which, full though they are of vain repetitions, show that the race is by no means devoid of poetic instinct or intellectual power.

"What is life? Tell me, O white men, who are wise, who know the secrets of the world, and of the world of stars, and the world that lies above and around the stars; who flash your words from afar without a voice; tell me, white men, the secret of our life—whither it goes and whence it comes!

"You cannot answer me; you know not. Listen, I will answer. Out of the dark we came, into the dark we go. Like a storm-driven bird at night we fly out of the Nowhere; for a moment our wings are seen in the light of the fire, and, lo! we are gone again into the Nowhere. Life is nothing. Life is all. It is the Hand with which we hold off Death. It is the glow-worm that shines in the night-time and is black in the morning; it is the white breath of the oxen in winter; it is the little shadow that runs across the grass and loses itself at sunset."

"You are a strange man," Sir Henry said when he had ceased.

Umbopa laughed. "It seems to me that we are much alike, Incubu. Perhaps _I_ seek a brother over the mountains."

I looked at him suspiciously. "What dost thou mean?" I asked. "What dost thou know of those mountains?"

"A little; a very little. There is a strange land yonder, a land of witchcraft and beautiful things; a land of brave people, and of trees, and streams, and snowy peaks, and of a great white road. I have heard of it. But what is the good of talking? It grows dark. Those who live to see will see."

Again I looked at him doubtfully. The man knew too much.

"You need not fear me, Macumazahn," he said, interpreting my look. "I dig no holes for you to fall in. I make no plots. If ever we cross those mountains behind the sun I will tell what I know. But Death sits upon them. Be wise and turn back. Go and hunt elephants, my masters. I have spoken."

Without another word, he lifted his spear in salutation and returned toward the camp, where we shortly found him cleaning a gun like any other Kafir.

"That is an odd man," Sir Henry observed.

"Yes," I answered. "Too odd by half. I don't like his little ways. He knows something, and will not speak out. But I suppose it is no use quarrelling with him. We are in for a curious trip, and a mysterious Zulu won't make much difference one way or another."

The following day we made our arrangements for departure. It was impossible to drag our heavy elephant rifles and other kit across the desert, so we dismissed our bearers and made an agreement with an old native who had a kraal nearby to keep them until our return. It went to my heart to leave such precious tools to the tender mercies of an old thief of a savage whose greedy eyes I could see gloating over them. But I took precautions.

First, I loaded all the rifles, placing them at full cock, and informed him that if he touched them they would go off. He tried the experiment instantly with my eight-bore, and it did go off, blowing a hole right through one of his oxen—which were just then being driven up to the kraal—and knocking him head over heels with the recoil. He rose considerably startled, not at all pleased at the loss of the ox, which he impudently asked me to pay for, and nothing induced him to touch the guns again.

"Put the live devils out of the way up there in the thatch," he said, "or they will murder us all."

Then I told him that when we returned, if one of those things was missing, I would kill him and his people by witchcraft; and if we died and he tried to steal the rifles, I would come and haunt him, turning his cattle mad and his milk sour until life was a weariness, and make the devils in the guns come out to talk to him in a way he did not like, generally giving him a good idea of judgment to come. After that, he promised to look after them as though they were his father's spirit. He was a very superstitious old Kafir and a great villain.

Thus disposed of our superfluous gear, we arranged the kit the five of us—Sir Henry, Good, myself, Umbopa, and the Hottentot Ventvögel—were to take. It was small, but no matter what we did, we could not reduce its weight to under about forty pounds a man. This was our total equipment:

The three express rifles and two hundred rounds of ammunition.
The two Winchester repeating rifles (for Umbopa and Ventvögel), with two hundred rounds of cartridge.
Five Cochrane's water-bottles, each holding four pints.
Five blankets.
Twenty-five pounds' weight of biltong—sun-dried game flesh.
Ten pounds' weight of best mixed beads for gifts.
A selection of medicine, including an ounce of quinine, and one or two small surgical instruments.
Our knives, a few sundries such as a compass, matches, a pocket filter, tobacco, a trowel, a bottle of brandy, and the clothes we stood in.

This was our total equipment, a small one indeed for such a venture, but we dared not attempt to carry more. That load was a heavy one per man with which to travel across the burning desert, for in such places every additional ounce tells. But we could not see our way to reducing the weight further. There was nothing taken but what was absolutely necessary.

With great difficulty, and by the promise of a hunting-knife each, I persuaded three wretched natives from the village to accompany us for the first stage, twenty miles, to carry a large gourd holding a gallon of water apiece. My object was to enable us to refill our water-bottles after the first night's march, for we determined to start in the cool of the evening. I told these natives we were going to shoot ostriches, with which the desert abounded. They jabbered and shrugged their shoulders, saying we were mad and should perish of thirst, which I must say seemed probable; but being desirous of obtaining the knives, which were almost unknown treasures up there, they consented to come, having probably reflected that our subsequent extinction would be no affair of theirs.

All next day we rested and slept, and at sunset ate a hearty meal of fresh beef washed down with tea, the last, as Good remarked sadly, we were likely to drink for many a long day. Then, having made our final preparations, we lay down and waited for the moon to rise. At last, about nine o'clock, she came up in all her glory, flooding the wild country with light and throwing a silver sheen on the expanse of rolling desert before us, which looked as solemn, quiet, and alien to man as the star-studded firmament above. We rose, and in a few minutes were ready, yet we hesitated a little, as human nature is prone to hesitate on the threshold of an irrevocable step. We three white men stood by ourselves. Umbopa, assegai in hand and a rifle across his shoulders, looked out fixedly across the desert a few paces ahead of us; while the hired natives, with the gourds of water, and Ventvögel, were gathered in a little knot behind.

The crushing weight of exhaustion lifted only to be immediately replaced by a sharper, more insidious torment: thirst. Two hours after collapsing, as the sun climbed toward its zenith, I awoke not to relief, but to a screaming void in my throat. Sleep had been a delusion; I had dreamed of cool, rushing streams and verdant banks, only to jolt awake into the suffocating reality of this arid wasteland. Umbopa's warning echoed in my mind: *If we find no water today, we perish.* In this heat, survival without moisture was impossible.

I sat up, scrubbing my grimy face with hands that felt like dried leather. My lips and eyelids were fused together by the dust and dehydration. It was barely dawn, yet the air offered no promise of freshness; instead, it was thick with a heavy, oppressive murk. The others remained unconscious, lost in their own struggles against the elements.

Once fully awake, the gravity of our situation settled over us. There was not a drop of water left. We inverted the bottles, licked the rims, and found them as dry as bone. Good, the man entrusted with the flask of brandy, eyed it with longing, but Sir Henry swiftly confiscated it. To drink raw spirit now would only hasten our demise.

"If we do not find water, we shall die," Sir Henry stated, his voice grim.

"If we trust the old Dom's map, there must be some nearby," I countered, though I sensed the lack of conviction in his tone. The map was a fragile hope in such a barren landscape.

As the light intensified, Ventvögel, the Hottentot guide, rose and began pacing, his eyes scanning the ground. He stopped abruptly, emitting a guttural cry and pointing to the sand.

"What is it?" we asked in unison, rushing to his side.

"It is fresh Springbok spoor," I noted.

"Springbucks do not stray far from water," Ventvögel replied in Dutch.

"No," I agreed, a surge of relief washing over me. "Thank God for that."

In desperation, men cling to the slightest thread of hope. This small discovery injected new vitality into us. Meanwhile, Ventvögel lifted his snub nose, sniffing the hot air with the intensity of a ram scenting danger.

"I *smell* water," he declared.

Jubilance surged through us. We knew the wild instincts of these people were rarely wrong. Just then, the sun broke the horizon with glorious brilliance, revealing a sight so magnificent that for a fleeting moment, our thirst was forgotten.

Before us, glittering like silver in the morning rays, soared Sheba's Breasts. Stretching for hundreds of miles on either side lay the great Suliman Berg. Attempting to describe the sheer grandeur of the scene feels inadequate; language fails before such memory. Straight ahead rose two colossal mountains, perhaps unmatched anywhere in Africa, measuring at least fifteen thousand feet each. Standing only a dozen miles apart, they were linked by a precipitous cliff of rock, towering into the sky in awful, white solemnity. They formed a gateway, shaped distinctly like a woman's breasts. At times, the mists beneath them took the form of a recumbent woman, veiled in mysterious sleep. Their bases swelled gently from the plain, appearing perfectly round and smooth, while atop each sat a vast snow-covered hillock, corresponding exactly to a nipple. The connecting cliff was thousands of feet high and perfectly vertical, flanked by similar lines of rock broken only by flat, table-topped mountains.

The view was inexpressibly solemn and overpowering. These were undoubtedly extinct volcanoes, and their majesty awed us. Morning light danced upon the snow and the brown, swelling masses below, until strange vapors and clouds gathered, veiling the majestic sight. Soon, we could only trace their ghostly outlines through a fleecy envelope of mist. Indeed, we later learned they were usually shrouded in this gauze-like mist, explaining why we had not seen them clearly before.

Scarcely had Sheba's Breasts vanished into the cloud-clad privacy when our thirst, a burning question, reasserted itself with renewed fury.

Ventvögel claimed to smell water, yet our eyes saw nothing but arid, sweltering sand and karoo scrub. We circled the hillock, gazing anxiously in every direction, but found no pan, pool, or spring.

"You are a fool," I snapped at Ventvögel. "There is no water."

He lifted his ugly nose again, sniffing insistently. "I smell it, Baas," he answered. "It is somewhere in the air."

"Yes," I retorted, "no doubt it is in the clouds, and in two months it will fall to wash our bones."

Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard thoughtfully. "Perhaps it is on the top of the hill."

"Rot," scoffed Good. "Whoever heard of water being found at the top of a hill!"

"Let us go and look," I urged. Hopelessly, we scrambled up the sandy sides of the kopje, Umbopa leading. He stopped suddenly, as if petrified.

"_Nanzia manzie!_" he cried loudly, meaning, "Here is water!"

We rushed to him. Sure enough, in a deep cut or indentation on the very summit of the sand koppie, lay an undeniable pool of water. We did not pause to inquire how it existed in such a strange place, nor did we hesitate at its black, unpleasant appearance. It was water, or a convincing imitation, and that was enough. We gave a bound and a rush, diving onto our stomachs to suck up the uninviting fluid as though it were nectar for the gods. Heavens, how we drank! Once satisfied, we tore off our clothes and sat in the pool, absorbing moisture through our parched skins. You, Harry, who can summon hot and cold water from unseen cisterns with a turn of a tap, can hardly conceive the luxury of that muddy wallow in brackish, tepid water.

Refreshed, we rose and fell upon our "biltong," which we had barely touched for twenty-four hours, eating our fill. We smoked pipes and lay by the blessed pool, under the shadow of its bank, sleeping until noon.

All day we rested, grateful for the find, however bad it was, and offering thanks to the shade of the departed da Silvestra, whose map had pinpointed the location so accurately. The mystery of the pan's longevity suggested it was fed by a spring deep within the sand.

Filled our bottles to the brim, we resumed our march under the moon. That night we covered nearly twenty-five miles but found no more water, though we secured some shade behind ant-heaps the following day. When the sun rose, clearing the mysterious mists, Suliman's Berg and the two majestic Breasts, now only twenty miles away, towered above us, looking grander than ever. By evening, we marched again, and by the next morning, we found ourselves on the lowest slopes of Sheba's left breast. Our water was exhausted once more, and the prospect of relief seemed distant until we reached the snow line far above.

Driven by torture, we toiled painfully up the burning heat of the lava slopes. The mountain's base was composed entirely of lava beds belched forth in some ancient age. By eleven o'clock, we were utterly exhausted, in a terrible state. The lava clinker, while smoother than that on Ascension, was rough enough to make our feet sore, compounding our misery. A few hundred yards above, we spotted large lumps of lava and sought shade beneath them. To our surprise, on a nearby plateau, the clinker was covered in dense green growth. Decomposed lava had formed soil, hosting seeds deposited by birds. Yet, we had little interest in the grass; one cannot live on it like Nebuchadnezzar.

We sat under the rocks, groaning, wishing we had never embarked on this fool's errand. Suddenly, I saw Umbopa hobble toward the green patch. Moments later, the usually dignified man was dancing and shouting like a madman, waving something green. We scrambled toward him, hoping for water.

"What is it, Umbopa, son of a fool?" I shouted in Zulu.

"It is food and water, Macumazahn," he waved again.

I saw what he had found: a melon. We had stumbled upon a patch of wild melons, thousands of them, dead ripe.

"Melons!" I yelled to Good, who immediately fixed his false teeth into one.

We ate about six each before finishing. Though poor fruit, I doubt I ever tasted anything nicer. But melons lack nutrition. After satisfying our thirst with their pulpy substance and cooling the halves in the sun to evaporate, hunger returned with a vengeance. We had some biltong left, but our stomachs rejected it, and we could not afford to waste it.

Just then, fortune smiled upon us. Looking across the desert, I saw a flock of ten large birds flying straight toward us.

"_Skit, Baas, skit!_ Shoot, master, shoot!" whispered the Hottentot, throwing himself prone—an example we all followed.

They were pauw, or bustards, passing within fifty yards of my head. Grabbing a repeating Winchester, I waited until they were nearly overhead. Seeing me, they bunched together. I fired two shots straight into the thick of them, bringing down a fine fellow weighing about twenty pounds. Within an hour, we had a fire of dry melon stalks, and the bird was toasting. We made a feast we had not tasted in a week, eating the pauw until nothing remained but leg-bones and beak. We felt considerably better.

That night, we continued under the moon, carrying as many melons as we could. As we ascended, the air grew cooler, a great relief. At dawn, we were perhaps a dozen miles from the snow line. Here, we found more melons, alleviating our anxiety about water, for snow was imminent. However, the ascent became precipitous, slowing our progress to a mile an hour. That night, we consumed our last morsel of biltong. Except for the pauw, we had seen no living thing, nor a single spring, which struck us as odd given the snow above. We later learned that, inexplicably, all streams flowed down the north side of the mountains.

Anxiety about food now overshadowed our thirst. We had escaped death by thirst, only to face starvation. The next three miserable days are best recorded in my notebook:

*"21st May.—Started 11 a.m., atmosphere cold enough to travel by day, carrying water-melons. Struggled all day, found no more melons, having passed their district. Saw no game. Halted at sundown, having had no food for many hours. Suffered much during the night from cold.*

*"22nd.—Started at sunrise, feeling faint and weak. Made only five miles; found patches of snow, ate thereof, but nothing else. Camped under a great plateau. Cold bitter. Drank a little brandy each, huddled in blankets to stay alive. Suffering frightfully from starvation and weariness. Thought Ventvögel would have died during the night.*

*"23rd.—Struggled forward as soon as the sun thawed our limbs. In a dreadful plight; fear this is our last day's journey unless we find food. Little brandy left. Good, Sir Henry, and Umbopa bear up wonderfully, but Ventvögel is in a very bad way. Like most Hottentots, he cannot stand cold. Pangs of hunger not so bad, but a numb feeling in the stomach. Others say the same. We are on a level with the precipitous chain linking the two Breasts. The view is glorious. Behind us, the glowing desert rolls to the horizon; before us, mile upon mile of smooth, hard snow, swelling gently upwards, out of the center of which the mountain's nipple rises four thousand feet. Not a living thing is to be seen. God help us; I fear our time has come."*

I shook my head.

"Why, the old Dom, José da Silvestra, of course—who else?" Good suggested.

"And what is there to prevent him from lasting for three thousand years in this atmosphere?" Good countered, his voice rising with excitement. "If the temperature is sufficiently low, flesh and blood will remain fresh as New Zealand mutton forever, and Heaven knows it is cold enough here. He stooped, picking up a strangely shaped bone scraped to a sharp point. "Here is the 'cleft bone' that Silvestra used to draw the map."

We stared in astonishment, momentarily forgetting our own misery before this extraordinary, semi-miraculous sight.

There was no longer any doubt. The realization appalled me completely. There sat the dead man, whose directions, written ten generations prior, had led us to this desolate spot. In my own hand lay the crude pen with which he had written them, and about his neck hung the crucifix his dying lips had kissed. Gazing upon him, my imagination reconstructed the final drama: the traveler dying of cold and starvation, yet striving to convey the great secret he had discovered—the awful loneliness of his death. The evidence sat before us, stark and undeniable. It even seemed to me that I could trace a likeness in his strongly marked features to my poor friend Silvestre, his descendant, who had died twenty years before in my arms, though perhaps that was merely fancy. There he sat, a sad memento of the fate that overtakes those who penetrate the unknown, destined to crown himself with the dread majesty of death for centuries yet unborn.

"Let us go," Henry whispered, his voice low. "Stay, we will give him a companion." He lifted the dead body of the Hottentot Ventvögel and placed it near that of the old Dom. Then, stooping, he broke the rotten string of the crucifix, for his fingers were too cold to unfasten it. I believe he still possesses it. I took the bone pen, and it remains before me as I write; sometimes I use it to sign my name.

Leaving these two—the proud white man of a past age and the poor Hottentot—to keep their eternal vigil amidst the eternal snows, we crept out of the cave into the welcome sunshine and resumed our path, wondering how many hours it would be before we were as they were.

After walking about half a mile, we reached the edge of the plateau. The mountain's nipple did not rise from its exact center, though from the desert side it had seemed to do so. Below us, the landscape was wreathed in billows of morning fog. Soon, however, the higher layers of mist cleared, revealing at the end of a long snow slope a patch of green grass, some five hundred yards beneath us, through which a stream ran. Nor was this all. By the stream, basking in the bright sun, stood and lay a group of ten to fifteen large antelopes.

The sight filled us with an unreasoning joy. If only we could secure them, there was food in plenty. But the question was how. The beasts were fully six hundred yards off—a very long shot, and one not to be depended upon when our lives hung on the result.

We rapidly discussed stalking the game but dismissed it reluctantly. The wind was unfavorable, and further, we would certainly be perceived against the blinding background of snow we would be obliged to traverse.

"Well, we must have a try from where we are," Henry said. "Which shall it be, Quatermain, the repeating rifles or the expresses?"

Here was a dilemma. The Winchester repeaters, of which we had two—Umbopa carrying poor Ventvögel's as well as his own—were sighted up to a thousand yards, whereas the expresses were only sighted to three hundred and fifty. Beyond that distance, shooting with them was guess-work. On the other hand, if they did hit, the expanding express bullets were much more likely to bring the game down. It was a knotty point, but I decided we must risk it and use the expresses.

"Let each of us take the buck opposite to him," I instructed. "Aim well at the point of the shoulder and high up. And Umbopa, do you give the word so that we may all fire together."

Then came a pause, each of us aiming his level best, as indeed a man is likely to do when he knows that life itself depends upon the shot.

"Fire," Umbopa called out in Zulu. At almost the same instant, the three rifles rang out loudly; three clouds of smoke hung before us, and a hundred echoes flew over the silent snow. Presently, the smoke cleared, and revealed—oh, joy!—a great buck lying on its back, kicking furiously in its death agony. We gave a yell of triumph; we were saved; we should not starve. Weak as we were, we rushed down the intervening slope of snow. In ten minutes from the time of shooting, that animal's heart and liver lay before us.

But now a new difficulty arose: we had no fuel, and therefore could make no fire to cook them. We gazed at each other in dismay.

"Starving men should not be fanciful," Good said pragmatically. "We must eat raw meat."

There was no other way out of the dilemma, and our gnawing hunger made the proposition less distasteful than it would otherwise have been. We took the heart and liver, burying them for a few minutes in a patch of snow to cool them. Then we washed them in the ice-cold water of the stream and lastly ate them greedily. It sounds horrible enough, but honestly, I never tasted anything so good as that raw meat. In a quarter of an hour, we were changed men. Our life and vigor came back to us; our feeble pulses grew strong again, and the blood coursed through our veins. Mindful of the results of over-feeding on starved stomachs, we were careful not to eat too much, stopping while we were still hungry.

"Thank Heaven!" Henry exclaimed. "That brute has saved our lives. What is it, Quatermain?"

I rose to look at the antelope, for I was not certain. It was about the size of a donkey, with large curved horns. I had never seen one like it before; the species was new to me. It was brown in color, with faint red stripes, and grew a thick coat. I later discovered that the natives of that wonderful country call these bucks "_inco_." They are very rare, found only at a great altitude where no other game will live. This animal had been fairly hit high up in the shoulder, though whose bullet brought it down we could not discover. I believe Good, mindful of his marvelous shot at the giraffe, secretly set it down to his own prowess, and we did not contradict him.

We had been so busy satisfying our hunger that we had not found time to look about us. But now, having set Umbopa to cut off as much of the best meat as we were likely to carry, we began to inspect our surroundings. The mist had cleared away; it was eight o'clock, and the sun had sucked it up, allowing us to take in the country before us at a glance. I know not how to describe the glorious panorama which unfolded itself to our gaze. I have never seen anything like it before, nor shall I, I suppose, again.

Behind and over us towered Sheba's snowy Breasts, and below, some five thousand feet beneath where we stood, lay league on league of the most lovely champaign country. Here were dense patches of lofty forest, there a great river wound its silvery way. To the left stretched a vast expanse of rich, undulating veld or grassland, whereon we could just make out countless herds of game or cattle, at that distance we could not tell which. This expanse appeared to be ringed in by a wall of distant mountains. To the right, the country was more or less mountainous; solitary hills stood up from its level, with stretches of cultivated land between, amongst which we could see groups of dome-shaped huts. The landscape lay before us as a map, wherein rivers flashed like silver snakes, and Alp-like peaks crowned with wildly twisted snow wreaths rose in grandeur, whilst over all was the glad sunlight and the breath of Nature's happy life.

Two curious things struck us as we gazed. First, that the country before us must lie at least three thousand feet higher than the desert we had crossed, and secondly, that all the rivers flowed from south to north. As we had painful reason to know, there was no water upon the southern side of the vast range on which we stood, but on the northern face were many streams, most of which appeared to unite with the great river we could see winding away farther than our eyes could follow.

We sat down for a while and gazed in silence at this wonderful view. Presently Henry spoke.

"Isn't there something on the map about Solomon's Great Road?" he asked.

I nodded, still gazing out over the far country.

"Well, look; there it is!" he pointed a little to our right.

Good and I looked accordingly, and there, winding away towards the plain, was what appeared to be a wide turnpike road. We had not seen it at first because, on reaching the plain, it turned behind some broken country. We did not say anything, at least, not much; we were beginning to lose the sense of wonder. Somehow it did not seem particularly unnatural that we should find a sort of Roman road in this strange land. We accepted the fact, that was all.

"Well," Good said, "it must be quite near us if we cut off to the right. Hadn't we better be making a start?"

This was sound advice. Soon as we had washed our faces and hands in the stream, we acted on it. For a mile or more we made our way over boulders and across patches of snow, till suddenly, on reaching the top of a little rise, we found the road at our feet. It was a splendid road cut out of the solid rock, at least fifty feet wide, and apparently well kept; though the odd thing was that it seemed to begin there. We walked down and stood on it, but one single hundred paces behind us, in the direction of Sheba's Breasts, it vanished, the entire surface of the mountain being strewn with boulders interspersed with patches of snow.

"What do you make of this, Quatermain?" Henry asked.

I shook my head; I could make nothing of the thing.

"I have it!" Good exclaimed. "The road no doubt ran right over the range and across the desert on the other side, but the sand there has covered it up, and above us it has been obliterated by some volcanic eruption of molten lava."

This seemed a good suggestion; at any rate, we accepted it and proceeded down the mountain. It proved a very different business traveling along downhill on that magnificent pathway with full stomachs from what it was traveling uphill over the snow quite starved and almost frozen. Indeed, had it not been for melancholy recollections of poor Ventvögel's sad fate and of that grim cave where he kept company with the old Dom, we should have felt positively cheerful, notwithstanding the sense of unknown dangers before us. Every mile we walked, the atmosphere grew softer and balmier, and the country before us shone with a yet more luminous beauty. As for the road itself, I never saw such an engineering work, though Henry said that the great road over the St. Gothard in Switzerland is very similar. No difficulty had been too great for the Old World engineer who laid it out. At one place we came to a ravine three hundred feet broad and at least a hundred feet deep. This vast gulf was actually filled in with huge blocks of dressed stone, having arches pierced through them at the bottom for a waterway, over which the road went on sublimely. At another place it was cut in zigzags out of the side of a precipice five hundred feet deep, and in a third it tunneled through the base of an intervening ridge, a space of thirty yards or more.

"At that moment, Imotu was sick, lying in his kraal unable to move. Gagool led Twala, my half-brother, whom she had hidden among the caves since birth, before the people. Stripping the *moocha* from his loins, she revealed the mark of the sacred snake coiled around his middle—the sign of the eldest son—and cried, 'Behold your king whom I have saved!'

"The people, mad with hunger and bereft of reason, shouted, 'The king! The king!' I knew it was false; Imotu was the elder twin and the lawful ruler. Just as the tumult peaked, Imotu, though gravely ill, crawled from his hut, holding his wife's hand, followed by his young son, Ignosi—the Lightning.

"'What is this noise?' he asked. 'Why cry ye The king! The king!'

"Then Twala, his twin brother born of the same womb and hour, ran to him, seized him by the hair, and stabbed him through the heart with his knife. The people, fickle and eager to worship the rising sun, clapped their hands and cried, 'Twala is king! Now we know that Twala is king!'"

"And what became of Imotu's wife and her son, Ignosi? Did Twala kill them too?"

"Nay, my lord. When she saw her lord fall, the queen seized the child and fled. Two days later, starving and rejected by all men who now hated the unfortunate, she sought shelter. At nightfall, a little girl brought her corn, and she blessed the child before leading her boy toward the mountains before sunrise. There she must have perished; none have seen her or Ignosi since."

"Then if that child Ignosi had lived, he would be the true king?"

"That is so, my lord. The sacred snake marks him. If he lives, he is king; but alas, he is long dead."

Infadoos pointed to a vast collection of huts surrounded by a fence and encircled by a great ditch on the plain below. "That is the kraal where the wife of Imotu was last seen with the child Ignosi. It is there we shall sleep tonight, if, indeed, my lords sleep at all upon this earth."

"When we are among the Kukuanas, my good friend Infadoos, we do as the Kukuanas do," I declared majestically. I turned to address Good, who tramped sullenly behind, vainly trying to stop his flannel shirt from flapping in the evening breeze. To my surprise, I bumped into Umbopa, walking immediately behind me. His expression was peculiar, suggesting a man struggling to recall something long forgotten.

We pressed on toward the undulating plain. The mountains we had crossed now loomed high above us, and Sheba's Breasts were veiled in diaphanous wreaths of mist. The country grew increasingly lovely; the vegetation was luxuriant yet not tropical, the sun bright and warm, and a gracious breeze swept along the odorous slopes. This new land was little less than an earthly paradise, surpassing even the Transvaal in beauty, natural wealth, and climate.

As we started, Infadoos dispatched a runner to warn the people of the kraal, which fell under his military command, of our arrival. The man departed at extraordinary speed, a feat Infadoos noted was common among his people.

The result of this warning became apparent within two miles. Companies of men issued from the gates, marching toward us. Sir Henry laid his hand on my arm, remarking that the reception looked warm. Something in his tone caught Infadoos' attention.

"Let not my lords be afraid," he said hastily. "In my breast dwells no guile. This regiment is under my command and comes out by my orders to greet you."

I nodded easily, though unease lingered in my mind.

Half a mile from the gates, on a rising stretch of ground, the companies formed. It was a splendid sight: each company, about three hundred strong, charged swiftly up the rise with flashing spears and waving plumes to take their appointed places. By the time we reached the slope, twelve such companies—three thousand six hundred men in total—had passed out and arrayed themselves along the road.

We approached the first company in astonishment. They were the most magnificent warriors I had ever seen: men of mature age, mostly veterans of forty, none under six feet tall, many standing six feet three or four. They wore heavy black plumes of Sakaboola feathers on their heads, similar to those of our guides. Around their waists and beneath their right knees were bound circlets of white ox tails. In their left hands, they carried round shields about twenty inches across, constructed of thin iron plates stretched with milk-white ox-hide.

Their weapons were simple but deadly: a short, heavy two-edged spear with a wooden shaft, the blade six inches wide at its widest point. Not for throwing, but for close-quarters combat like the Zulu *bangwan*, the wound inflicted was terrible. Additionally, each man carried three large, heavy knives, or *tollas*, weighing about two pounds each. One was fixed in the ox-tail girdle, the other two attached to the back of the shield. The Kukuanas could cast these with great accuracy to fifty yards, hurling a volley as they charged to close quarters.

Each company stood still as bronze statues until we were opposite. At a signal from the commanding officer, distinguished by a leopard-skin cloak, every spear was raised, and from three hundred throats sprang forth the royal salute of "Koom." Once we passed, the company formed behind us, following toward the kraal until the entire regiment of the "Greys"—named for their white shields—the crack corps of the Kukuana people, marched in our rear with a tread that shook the ground.

Branching off from Solomon's Great Road, we reached the wide fosse surrounding the kraal, a mile in circumference, fenced with a strong palisade of tree trunks. At the gateway, a primitive drawbridge was lowered to admit us. The kraal was excellently laid out. A wide pathway ran through the center, intersected at right angles by others, cutting the huts into square blocks, each quartering a company. The huts were dome-shaped, built of wattle and beautifully thatched with grass, but unlike Zulu huts, they featured doorways wide enough for men to walk through. They were larger, surrounded by verandas about six feet wide, paved with powdered lime trodden hard.

Along the central pathway, hundreds of women stood ranged, drawn by curiosity to view us. For a native race, they were exceedingly handsome: tall, graceful, with wonderfully fine figures. Their hair was short and curly rather than woolly, their features often aquiline, and their lips not unpleasantly thick. What struck us most was their quiet, dignified air. They were as well-bred as the *habituées* of a fashionable drawing-room, differing sharply from Zulu women or the Masai beyond Zanzibar. Their curiosity brought them out, yet they allowed no rude expressions of astonishment or savage criticism to escape their lips as we trudged wearily before them. Even when old Infadoos surreptitiously pointed out the crowning wonder of Poor Good's "beautiful white legs," the women merely fixed their dark eyes upon this snowy loveliness. It was quite enough for Good, who was modest by nature.

Reaching the center of the kraal, Infadoos halted at the door of a large hut surrounded by a circle of smaller ones.

"Enter, Sons of the Stars," he said in a magniloquent voice. "Deign to rest awhile in our humble habitations. A little food shall be brought to you, so that ye may have no need to draw your belts tight from hunger; some honey, some milk, an ox or two, and a few sheep; not much, my lords, but still a little food."

"It is good," I said. "Infadoos, we are weary with travelling through realms of air; now let us rest."

We entered the hut, finding it amply prepared for our comfort. Couches of tanned skins awaited us, and water was placed for washing.

Soon, shouting erupted outside. Stepping to the door, we saw a line of damsels bearing milk, roasted mealies, and honey in a pot. Behind them, youths drove a fat young ox. We received the gifts, and one of the young men dexterously cut the ox's throat. In ten minutes, it was dead, skinned, and jointed. The best meat was cut for us, while the rest, in the name of our party, I presented to the warriors around us, who took it and distributed the "white lords' gift."

Umbopa began to boil our portion in a large earthenware pot over an outdoor fire, assisted by an extremely prepossessing young woman. When the meal was nearly ready, we sent a message to Infadoos and Scragga, the king's son, inviting them to join us.

They arrived shortly, sitting on small stools, for the Kukuanas generally do not squat like the Zulus. They helped us dine. The old gentleman was affable and polite, but I noted that the young one regarded us with doubt. Like the rest, he had been overawed by our white appearance and perceived magic; yet, discovering that we ate, drank, and slept like ordinary mortals, his awe was wearing off, replaced by a sullen suspicion that made me feel uncomfortable.

During the meal, Sir Henry suggested we inquire if our hosts knew anything of his brother's fate. However, I thought it wiser to remain silent; explaining a relative lost from "the Stars" was difficult.

After supper, we produced our pipes. The act filled Infadoos and Scragga with astonishment; the Kukuanas were unacquainted with the divine delights of tobacco smoke. Though the herb was grown extensively among them, they used it only as snuff, failing to identify it in its new form.

I asked Infadoos when we would depart. Delighted to learn that preparations were made for us to leave the following morning, I learned that messengers had already been sent to inform King Twala of our coming.

Twala was at his principal place, known as Loo, preparing for the great annual feast scheduled for the first week of June. At this gathering, all regiments, except certain garrison detachments, would parade before the king, followed by the great annual witch-hunt.

We were to start at dawn. Infadoos, who would accompany us, expected we would reach Loo on the night of the second day, barring accidents or swollen rivers.

With this information, our visitors bade us good-night. Having arranged to watch turn and turn about, three of us flung ourselves down to sleep the sweet sleep of the weary, while the fourth sat up on the lookout for possible treachery.

The journey to Loo required two full days of travel along Solomon's Great Road, a path cutting straight through the heart of Kukuanaland. As we progressed, the landscape grew undeniably richer; the kraals multiplied, their wide belts of cultivation stretching endlessly, all constructed on the same formidable principles as our first camp and guarded by ample garrisons. In this land, as among the Germans, Zulus, and Masai, every able-bodied man is a soldier, rendering the entire nation a perpetual army. Thousands of warriors hurried ahead of us toward Loo for the great annual review, presenting a spectacle of military splendor I had never witnessed.

At sunset on the second day, we halted on a height overlooking the plain where Loo lay. It was an enormous native town, perhaps five miles in circumference, with outlying kraals serving as cantonments for the regiments on grand occasions. To the north, a curious horseshoe-shaped hill awaited our closer acquaintance. A river bisected the kraal, bridged in several places, mirroring what we had seen from the slopes of Sheba's Breasts. Far in the distance, sixty or seventy miles away, three great snow-capped mountains stood at the points of a triangle. Unlike the smooth, rounded forms of Sheba's Breasts, these were sheer and precipitous.

Infadoos, observing our gaze, volunteered a remark. "The road ends there," he said, pointing to the mountains known as the "Three Witches."

"Why does it end?" I asked.

"Who knows?" he shrugged. "The mountains are full of caves, and there is a great pit between them. It is there that the wise men of old went to obtain whatever they sought for this country, and it is there now that our kings are buried in the Place of Death."

"What did they seek?" I asked eagerly.

"Nay, I know not. My lords who have dropped from the Stars should know," he replied with a quick, knowing look. He clearly withheld more than he chose to reveal.

"Yes," I pressed, "you are right; in the Stars we learn many things. I have heard the wise men came to find bright stones, pretty playthings, and yellow iron."

"My lord is wise," Infadoos answered coldly. "I am but a child and cannot discourse on such matters with my lord. My lord must speak with Gagool the old, at the king's place, who is wise even as you." With that, he departed.

Immediately after his departure, I turned to the others, indicating the mountains. "There are Solomon's diamond mines," I declared.

Umbopa, standing nearby and seemingly lost in one of his characteristic fits of abstraction, caught my words. "Yes, Macumazahn," he interjected in Zulu, "the diamonds are surely there, and you shall have them, since you white men are so fond of toys and money."

"How dost thou know that, Umbopa?" I asked sharply, wary of his mysterious demeanor.

He laughed. "I dreamed it in the night, white men;" then he too turned and walked away.

"Now what," Sir Henry asked, "is our black friend driving at? He knows more than he chooses to say. By the way, Quatermain, has he heard anything of my brother?"

"Nothing," I replied. "He has asked everyone he befriended, but they all declare no white man has ever been seen in this country before."

"Do you suppose he got here at all?" Good suggested. "We reached this place by a miracle; is it likely he could have arrived without the map?"

"I don't know," Sir Henry said gloomily, "but somehow I think I shall find him."

Slowly the sun sank, and suddenly darkness rushed down upon the land like a tangible substance. There was no breathing space between day and night, no soft transition, for in these latitudes twilight does not exist. The change from day to night was as quick and absolute as the change from life to death. The sun vanished, and the world was wreathed in shadows. But not for long; in the west a glow appeared, followed by rays of silver light, until the full and glorious moon illuminated the plain, shooting gleaming arrows far and wide, filling the earth with a faint refulgence.

We stood watching this lovely sight, while the stars grew pale before this chastened majesty, feeling our hearts lifted by a beauty I cannot describe. My life has been rough, yet there are few things I am thankful to have lived for, and one of them is to have seen that moon shine over Kukuanaland.

Our meditation was broken by Infadoos. "If my lords are rested, we will journey on to Loo, where a hut is prepared for you tonight. The moon is bright, so we shall not falter by the way."

We assented, and within an hour we reached the outskirts of the town. Its extent, mapped out by thousands of campfires, appeared absolutely endless. Good, ever fond of a jest, christened it "Unlimited Loo." Soon we came to a moat with a drawbridge, where we were met by the rattling of arms and the hoarse challenge of a sentry. Infadoos gave a password I could not catch, which was met with a salute, allowing us to pass through the central street of the great grass city. After nearly half an hour's tramp past endless lines of huts, Infadoos halted before a small group of huts surrounding a courtyard of powdered limestone, informing us these were our "poor" quarters.

We entered and found a hut assigned to each of us. These were superior to any we had seen, containing comfortable beds of tanned skins spread upon mattresses of aromatic grass. Food was ready; as soon as we washed with water from earthenware jars, young women of handsome appearance brought roasted meats and mealie cobs served on wooden platters, presenting them with deep obeisances.

We ate and drank. Upon our request, the beds were moved into one hut—a precaution at which the amiable young ladies smiled—and we flung ourselves down to sleep, thoroughly weary from our long journey.

When we awoke, the sun was high, and the female attendants, seemingly untroubled by false shame, stood inside the hut, ordered to assist us in making ready.

"Make ready, indeed," growled Good. "When one has only a flannel shirt and a pair of boots, that takes little time. I wish you would ask them for my trousers, Quatermain."

I asked accordingly, but was informed these sacred relics had already been taken to the king, who would see us in the forenoon.

To their astonishment, having requested the young ladies step outside, we proceeded to make the best toilet the circumstances allowed. Good even shaved the right side of his face again; the left, sporting a fair crop of whiskers, we instructed him not to touch. For ourselves, a good wash and combing sufficed. Sir Henry's yellow locks were now almost upon his shoulders, making him resemble an ancient Dane, while my grizzled scrub was fully an inch long, exceeding my usual maximum.

By the time we had eaten breakfast and smoked a pipe, a message arrived from Infadoos himself: Twala the king was ready to see us if we were pleased to come.

We replied that we preferred to wait until the sun was higher, citing our weariness. It is always prudent when dealing with uncivilized peoples not to be in too great a hurry; they are apt to mistake politeness for awe or servility. Though we were as anxious to see Twala as he was to see us, we sat and waited an hour, preparing presents from our slender stock: the Winchester rifle formerly used by poor Ventvögel and some beads. We intended to present the rifle and ammunition to his royal highness and the beads to his wives and courtiers. We had already given a few to Infadoos and Scragga, finding them delighted, having never seen such things before. Finally declaring ourselves ready, we started for the audience, Umbopa carrying the rifle and beads.

After walking a few hundred yards, we reached an enclosure roughly fifty times the size of our quarters, covering at least six or seven acres. Around the outer fence stood rows of huts, the habitations of the king's wives. Exactly opposite the gateway, on the further side of the open space, stood a very large hut built apart, where his majesty resided. The rest was open ground, filled by company after company of warriors mustered to the number of seven or eight thousand. These men stood still as statues as we advanced, presenting a grandeur impossible to adequately describe, with their waving plumes, glancing spears, and iron-backed ox-hide shields.

The space before the large hut was empty, save for several stools. On three of these, at Infadoos' signal, we seated ourselves, with Umbopa standing behind us. Infadoos took a position by the door of the hut. We waited ten minutes or more in dead silence, conscious of being the object of some eight thousand pairs of eyes. It was a trying ordeal, but we endured it as best we could.

At length, the door opened, and a gigantic figure, draped in a splendid tiger-skin karross, stepped out, followed by the boy Scragga and what appeared to be a withered monkey wrapped in a fur cloak. The figure seated itself on a stool, Scragga stood behind, and the monkey crept on all fours into the shade of the hut.

Silence reigned. Then the gigantic figure slipped off the karross and stood before us, a truly alarming spectacle. It was an enormous man with the most repulsive countenance we had ever beheld. His lips were thick as a Negro's, his nose flat, and he possessed but one gleaming black eye; the other was a hollow in the face. His expression was cruel and sensual to a degree. From his large head rose a magnificent plume of white ostrich feathers. His body was clad in a shirt of shining chain armor, with white ox-tail garnishes around his waist and right knee. In his right hand was a huge spear, a thick torque of gold around his neck, and a single, enormous uncut diamond bound on his forehead.

Still silence. Then the man, whom we rightly guessed to be the king, raised the great javelin. Instantly, eight thousand spears were lifted in answer, and from eight thousand throats rang out the royal salute of "_Koom_." Three times this was repeated, each time shaking the earth with a noise comparable to the deepest notes of thunder.

"Be humble, O people," piped a thin voice seeming to come from the monkey in the shade.

"_It is the king_," boomed the eight thousand throats in answer. "_Be humble, O people, it is the king._"

Then silence again—dead silence. Presently, it was broken. A soldier on our left dropped his shield, which clattered onto the limestone flooring.

Twala turned his one cold eye toward the noise. "Come hither, thou," he said in a cold voice.

A fine young man stepped out of the ranks and stood before him. "It was thy shield that fell, thou awkward dog. Wilt thou make me a reproach in the eyes of these strangers from the Stars? What hast thou to say for thyself?"

We saw the poor fellow turn pale under his dusky skin. "It was by chance, O Calf of the Black Cow," he murmured.

"Then it is a chance for which thou must pay. Thou hast made me foolish; prepare for death."

"I am the king's ox," was the low answer.

"Scragga," roared the king, "let me see how thou canst use thy spear. Kill me this blundering fool."

Scragga stepped forward with an ill-favored grin, lifting his spear. The poor victim covered his eyes and stood still. As for us, we were petrified with horror.

"Once, twice," he waved the spear, and then struck—right home! The spear protruded a foot behind the soldier's back. He flung up his hands and dropped dead. From the multitude about us rose a murmur that rolled and died away. The tragedy was finished; there lay the corpse, and we had not yet realized it had been enacted. Sir Henry sprang up and swore a great oath, then, overcome by the sense of silence, sat down again.

"The thrust was a good one," said the king. "Take him away."

Four men stepped out, lifting the body of the murdered man and carrying it away.

"Cover up the blood-stains, cover them up," piped the thin voice from the monkey-like figure. "The king's word is spoken, the king's doom is done!"

A girl came forward from behind the hut, bearing a jar of powdered lime, which she scattered over the red mark, blotting it from sight.

Sir Henry meanwhile boiled with rage, and it was with difficulty we kept him still. "Sit down, for heaven's sake," I whispered. "Our lives depend on it."

He yielded and remained quiet.

Twala sat silent until the traces of the tragedy were removed, then he addressed us. "White people, who come hither, whence I know not, and why I know not, greeting."

"Greeting, Twala, King of the Kukuanas," I answered.

"White people, whence come ye, and what seek ye?"

"We come from the Stars, ask us not how. We come to see this land."

"Ye journey from far to see a little thing. And that man with you," pointing to Umbopa, "does he also come from the Stars?"

"Even so; there are people of thy colour in the heavens above; but ask not of matters too high for thee, Twala the king."

"Ye speak with a loud voice, people of the Stars," Twala answered in a tone I scarcely liked. "Remember that the Stars are far off, and ye are here. How if I make you as him whom they bore away?"

I laughed aloud, though there was little laughter in my heart. "O king," I said, "be careful, walk warily over hot stones, lest thou shouldst burn thy feet; hold the spear by the handle, lest thou shouldst cut

Upon reaching our hut, I signaled for Infadoos to join us inside.

"Now, Infadoos," I said, "we wish to speak with you."

"Let my lords speak," he replied.

"It appears to us, Infadoos, that King Twala is a cruel man."

"It is true, my lords. Alas! the land groans under his tyranny. Tonight you shall witness it. It is the Great Witch-Hunt, and many will be hunted out as wizards and slain. No man's life is safe. If the king covets a man's cattle, or a wife, or fears a man might incite rebellion, then Gagool, whom you have seen, or one of the witch-finding women she has trained, will scent that man as a sorcerer, and he will be killed. Many must die before the moon fades tonight. It is always so. Perhaps I too shall be killed. Thus far, I have been spared because I am skilled in war and beloved by the soldiers, but I know not how long my life remains. The land suffers under Twala's cruelty; it is weary of him and his bloody ways."

"Then why do the people not cast him down?"

"Nay, my lords. He is the king. If he were killed, Scragga would reign in his place, and Scragga's heart is blacker than Twala's. Under Scragga, the yoke upon our necks would be heavier than Twala's. Had Imotu never been slain, or Ignosi his son lived, it might have been different; but both are dead."

"How do you know that Ignosi is dead?" a voice asked from behind us. We turned, astonished, to see Umbopa.

"What means this, boy?" Infadoos demanded. "Who gave you leave to speak?"

"Listen, Infadoos," Umbopa answered calmly. "I will tell you a story. Years ago, King Imotu was killed in this land, and his wife fled with the boy Ignosi. Is that not so?"

"It is so."

"It was said that the woman and her son perished on the mountains. Is that not so?"

"It is even so."

"Well, it came to pass that the mother and the boy Ignosi did not die. They crossed the mountains and were led by a tribe of wandering desert men across the sands beyond, until they reached water, grass, and trees once more."

"How do you know this?"

"Listen. They traveled on for many months, until they reached a land inhabited by a people called the Amazulu, who are also of the Kukuana stock and live by war. There they stayed for many years, until at length the mother died. Then the son Ignosi became a wanderer again, journeying into a land of wonders where white people live. For many more years he learned the wisdom of the white men."

"It is a pretty story," Infadoos said, his skepticism evident.

"For years he lived there working as a servant and a soldier, yet holding in his heart all that his mother had told him of his own place. He cast about in his mind, planning how he might journey back to see his people and his father's house before he died. For long years he lived and waited, and at last the time came, as it ever does to those who can wait, and he met some white men seeking this unknown land. He joined them. The white men started and traveled on, searching for one who was lost. They crossed the burning desert, they crossed the snow-clad mountains, and finally reached the land of the Kukuanas, where they found *you*, O Infadoos."

"Surely you are mad to talk thus," the astonished old soldier exclaimed.

"You think so? See, I will show you, O my uncle."

"I am Ignosi, rightful king of the Kukuanas!"

With a single movement, Umbopa slipped off his "moocha" or girdle and stood naked before us.

"Look," he said, pointing to a picture of a great snake tattooed in blue around his middle, its tail disappearing into its open mouth just above where the thighs are set into the body.

Infadoos stared, his eyes bulging nearly from his head. Then he fell upon his knees.

"Koom! Koom!" he ejaculated. "It is my brother's son; it is the king."

"Did I not tell you so, my uncle? Rise. I am not yet the king, but with your help, and with the help of these brave white men who are my friends, I shall be. Yet the old witch Gagool was right: the land shall run with blood first, and hers shall run with it, if she has any and can die, for she killed my father with her words and drove my mother forth. Now, Infadoos, choose. Will you put your hands between mine and be my man? Will you share the dangers that lie before me and help me to overthrow this tyrant and murderer, or will you not? Choose."

The old man placed his hand to his head in thought. Then he rose, advanced to where Umbopa—or rather Ignosi—stood, knelt before him, and took his hand.

"Ignosi, rightful king of the Kukuanas, I put my hand between yours, and am your man till death. When you were a babe I dandled you upon my knees; now shall my old arm strike for you and for freedom."

"It is well, Infadoos. If I conquer, you shall be the greatest man in the kingdom after its king. If I fail, you can only die, and death is not far from you. Rise, my uncle."

"And you, white men, will you help me? What have I to offer you! The white stones! If I conquer and can find them, you shall have as many as you can carry hence. Will that suffice you?"

I translated this remark.

"Tell him," answered Sir Henry, "that he mistakes an Englishman. Wealth is good, and if it comes in our way we will take it; but a gentleman does not sell himself for wealth. Still, speaking for myself, I say this. I have always liked Umbopa, and so far as lies in me, I will stand by him in this business. It will be very pleasant to me to try to square matters with that cruel devil Twala. What do you say, Good, and you, Quatermain?"

"Well," said Good, adopting the language of hyperbole in which all these people seem to indulge, "you can tell him that a row is surely good and warms the cockles of the heart, and that so far as I am concerned, I'm his boy. My only stipulation is that he allows me to wear trousers."

I translated the substance of these answers.

"It is well, my friends," said Ignosi, formerly Umbopa. "And what sayest thou, Macumazahn? Art thou also with me, old hunter, cleverer than a wounded buffalo?"

I thought awhile and scratched my head.

"Umbopa, or Ignosi," I said, "I do not like revolutions. I am a man of peace and a bit of a coward"—here Umbopa smiled—"but, on the other hand, I stick up for my friends, Ignosi. You have stuck to us and played the part of a man, and I will stick by you. But mind you, I am a trader and have to make my living, so I accept your offer about those diamonds in case we should ever be in a position to avail ourselves of it. Another thing: we came, as you know, to look for Incubu's (Sir Henry's) lost brother. You must help us to find him."

"That I will do," answered Ignosi. "Stay, Infadoos, by the sign of the snake about my middle, tell me the truth. Has any white man to thy knowledge set his foot within the land?"

"None, O Ignosi."

"If any white man had been seen or heard of, wouldst thou have known?"

"I should certainly have known."

"Thou hearest, Incubu," said Ignosi to Sir Henry, "he has not been here."

"Well, well," said Sir Henry, with a sigh, "there it is; I suppose that he never got so far. Poor fellow, poor fellow! So it has all been for nothing. God's will be done."

"Now for business," I interjected, anxious to escape from a painful subject. "It is very well to be a king by right divine, Ignosi, but how dost thou propose to become a king indeed?"

"Nay, I know not. Infadoos, hast thou a plan?"

"Ignosi, Son of the Lightning," answered his uncle, "tonight is the great dance and witch-hunt. Many shall be smelt out and perish, and in the hearts of many others there will be grief and anguish and fury against the king Twala. When the dance is over, then I will speak to some of the great chiefs, who in turn, if I can win them over, will speak to their regiments. I shall speak to the chiefs softly at first, bringing them to see that thou art indeed the king, and I think that by tomorrow's light thou shalt have twenty thousand spears at thy command. And now I must go and think, and hear, and make ready. After the dance is done, if I am yet alive, and we are all alive, I will meet thee here, and we can talk. At the best there must be war."

At this moment our conference was interrupted by the cry that messengers had come from the king. Advancing to the door of the hut, we ordered them admitted, and presently three men entered, each bearing a shining shirt of chain armor and a magnificent battle-axe.

"The gifts of my lord the king to the white men from the Stars!" cried a herald who came with them.

"We thank the king," I answered. "Withdraw."

The men went, and we examined the armor with great interest. It was the most wonderful chain work either of us had ever seen. A whole coat fell together so closely that it formed a mass of links scarcely too big to be covered with both hands.

"Do you make these things in this country, Infadoos?" I asked. "They are very beautiful."

"Nay, my lord, they came down to us from our forefathers. We know not who made them, and there are but few left. None but those of royal blood may be clad in them. They are magic coats through which no spear can pass, and those who wear them are well-nigh safe in battle. The king is well pleased or much afraid, or he would not have sent these garments of steel. Clothe yourselves in them tonight, my lords."

The remainder of that day we spent quietly, resting and talking over the situation, which was sufficiently exciting. At last the sun went down, the thousand watch fires glowed out, and through the darkness we heard the tramp of many feet and the clashing of hundreds of spears as the regiments passed to their appointed places to be ready for the great dance. Then the full moon shone out in splendor. As we stood watching her rays, Infadoos arrived, clad in his war dress, accompanied by a guard of twenty men to escort us to the dance. As he recommended, we had already donned the shirts of chain armor which the king had sent us, putting them on under our ordinary clothing. To our surprise, they were neither very heavy nor uncomfortable. These steel shirts, which evidently had been made for men of very large stature, hung somewhat loosely upon Good and myself, but Sir Henry's fitted his magnificent frame like a glove. Then strapping our revolvers round our waists and taking in our hands the battle-axes which the king had sent with the armor, we started.

On arriving at the great kraal, where we had that morning been received by the king, we found it closely packed with some twenty thousand men arranged round it in regiments. These regiments were in turn divided into companies, and between each company ran a little path to allow space for the witch-finders to pass up and down. Nothing more imposing than the sight presented by this vast and orderly concourse of armed men can be conceived. There they stood perfectly silent, and the moon poured her light upon the forest of their raised spears, upon their majestic forms, waving plumes, and the harmonious shading of their various-colored shields. Wherever we looked were line upon line of dim faces surmounted by range upon range of shimmering spears.

"Surely," I said to Infadoos, "the whole army is here?"

"Nay, Macumazahn," he answered. "But a third of it. One third is present at this dance each year, another third is mustered outside in case there should be trouble when the killing begins, ten thousand more garrison the outposts round Loo, and the rest watch at the kraals in the country. Thou seest it is a great people."

"They are very silent," said Good. Indeed, the intense stillness among such a vast concourse of living men was almost overpowering.

"What says Bougwan?" asked Infadoos.

I translated.

"Those over whom the shadow of Death is hovering are silent," he answered grimly.

"Will many be killed?"

"Very many."

"It seems," I said to the others, "that we are going to assist at a gladiatorial show arranged regardless of expense."

Sir Henry shivered, and Good said he wished that we could get out of it.

"Tell me," I asked Infadoos, "are we in danger?"

"I know not, my lords. I trust not; but do not seem afraid. If ye live through the night all may go well with you. The soldiers murmur against the king."

All this while we had been advancing steadily towards the center of the open space, in the midst of which were placed some stools. As we proceeded, we perceived another small party coming from the direction of the royal hut.

"It is the king Twala, Scragga his son, and Gagool the old; and see, with them are those who slay," said Infadoos, pointing to a little group of about a dozen gigantic and savage-looking men

For two long hours, the silence within the hut was heavy, broken only by the ragged breathing of men too shaken by the horrors of the night to speak. As dawn approached, the rhythmic tread of boots echoed against the dirt floor. A sentry's challenge rang out, answered not in voice but in movement, and Infadoos entered, flanked by six towering chiefs.

"My lords," Infadoos declared, gesturing to the imposing figures behind him, "I have kept my word. These are the commanders of the Kukuanas, each leading three thousand souls loyal to the rightful king. They have heard my tale and witnessed the sacred snake coiled upon Ignosi's skin. Let them now judge whether they will stand with him against Twala."

Ignosi stripped his girdle to reveal the tattooed serpent once more. Under the dim lamp-light, the chiefs inspected the mark in solemn silence before moving on. Ignosi then recounted the morning's history, detailing the massacre and the tyranny of the current ruler.

"Chiefs," Infadoos pressed, "what is your verdict? Will you aid this man in reclaiming his father's throne, or will you remain silent while the land bleeds? Two other leaders I sought out have already fallen to the hyenas. Soon you will join them unless you act. Choose!"

The eldest chief, a thick-set warrior with white hair, stepped forward. "Your words are true, Infadoos, but this is a perilous path. How can we trust a thief and a liar? Many still worship the sun that shines for Twala, not the one that has not yet risen. Furthermore, these white men possess great magic; Ignosi hides under their wing. If he is truly the king, let him give us a sign—a sign the people can see. Only then will we follow."

"The snake is the sign," I offered.

"It is insufficient," the elder countered. "A tattoo can be placed in childhood. We require a miracle. We will not move without it."

The others agreed unanimously. Turning to Sir Henry and Good, I explained the impasse.

"I think I have it," Good exclaimed, retrieving a notebook from his medical kit. "Ask them to wait."

He consulted the almanack tucked into the fly-leaf. "Tomorrow is the fourth of June. Here it is: 'Total eclipse of the moon commences at 8:15 Greenwich time, visible in South Africa.' We will darken the moon for them."

Sir Henry expressed concern regarding the accuracy of the prediction, fearing that a failed prophecy would destroy our credibility and Ignosi's claim. Good, however, was confident in his calculations, estimating the eclipse would begin around ten o'clock local time and last for ninety minutes.

"We must risk it," Sir Henry conceded.

Summoning the chiefs back, I addressed them with gravity. "We do not show our powers lightly, for it disturbs the natural order. Yet, angered by the slaughter and the cruelty of the *Isanusi* Gagool, we have decided to break our rule. Tomorrow night, two hours before midnight, we will cause the moon to vanish for an hour and a half. This darkness shall be the sign that Ignosi is king. If we succeed, will you be satisfied?"

"Yes," the old chief smiled, "if you do this, we will be satisfied."

Infadoos then outlined the plan: two hours after sunset, Twala would summon the chiefs to witness the "Dance of Girls." One hour into the dance, the fairest maiden would be sacrificed to the Silent Ones atop the distant peaks. If we could darken the moon during this ritual, saving the girl, the people would believe.

The plan was set. That evening, as we prepared for the dance, Ignosi asked if we truly could perform such a wonder. "If we survive," he promised, "you shall be repaid." Sir Henry added a condition: if Ignosi became king, he must abolish the witch-finders and end executions without trial. Ignosi agreed, acknowledging the difference between black and white ways but pledging to change his laws if given the chance.

Worn out, we slept until Ignosi roused us at eleven. After a hearty breakfast, we walked the kraal, observing the women and the unique architecture of the huts. Sir Henry voiced his hope that the eclipse would occur; I replied mournfully that failure meant certain death, as the chiefs would likely report our deception to Twala, inviting a far worse fate.

As twilight fell, a messenger arrived from Twala, inviting us to the annual dance. We donned the chain shirts the king had provided, loaded our rifles, and marched toward the great open space before the kraal. The scene had transformed; instead of grim warriors, rows of Kukuana girls danced, crowned with flowers and holding white arum lilies.

Twala sat centrally, flanked by Gagool, Infadoos, and his son, Scragga. He greeted us with feigned cordiality, his single eye fixing viciously on Umbopa. He offered us wives from his people, a prospect Good wisely declined, citing the inevitable troubles of foreign entanglements. I politely refused on behalf of all, stating we wed only our own kind.

Twala laughed, citing proverbs about women's eyes and love, before turning his attention to the dance. The girls moved in swirling patterns, mimicking warfare and grace. Finally, a beautiful young woman stepped forward, pirouetting with such vigor that she outshone the rest. When the dance concluded, Twala asked which girl was fairest.

Unthinkingly, I answered, "The first."

Regret struck me instantly. Infadoos had warned us: the fairest girl must die.

"Ay, must die!" Gagool shrieked, her eyes darting toward the terrified girl, Foulata, who stood trembling before the crowd.

"Why?" I demanded, fighting back indignation. "She has pleased us!"

"It is our custom," Twala sneered, pointing to the silent peaks. "To fail in this sacrifice brings ruin upon my house. My predecessor refused, and his lineage ended. I reign today because I obeyed. She must die!"

Guards seized Foulata as she screamed and tried to flee. Gagool mocked her, asking for her name. "Foulata of the house of Suko," the girl sobbed. "Why must I die? I have done no wrong!"

Gagool twisted her words, claiming death was better than toil, promising the girl would die by the hand of the king's son. Foulata wrung her hands in agony, lamenting the loss of her father, her mother, her future children, and the simple joys of gathering flowers. Her despair was so profound it seemed to touch the very heavens, yet it moved neither Gagool nor Twala.

Seeing Good's hand creep to his revolver, Foulata suddenly threw herself at his legs, clasping them in a desperate plea for protection. "Oh, white father from the Stars! Save me from these cruel men!"

"All right, my hearty," Good stammered in nervous Saxon, catching her hand.

"Now's your time," Sir Henry whispered urgently. "What are you waiting for?"

"I am waiting for the eclipse," I replied, watching the moon. "It looks healthy."

"Risk it now, or the girl dies," Sir Henry urged.

Twala, losing patience, signaled Scragga to advance with his spear. I stepped between the girl and the weapon, raising my voice with authority. "King! It shall not be! We will not endure this!"

Twala rose in wrath. "Back, white dog! Or die by the girl's hand!" Guards surged forward, but Sir Henry, Good, and Umbopa raised their rifles.

"Stop!" I shouted. "We, the white men from the Stars, say it shall not be. Come one pace nearer, and we will put out the moon like a wind-blown lamp, plunging this land into darkness. Dare to disobey, and taste our magic!"

The threat held. The men halted. Gagool jeered at the lie, demanding the proof or death.

I looked up at the moon, my heart pounding. To my relief, a faint rim of shadow was creeping across the orb. "Grow dark, O Moon!" I cried, lifting my hand solemnly. Sir Henry and Good joined me, reciting verses from legends, scripture, and Latin poetry. Good unleashed a torrent of classical curses in various tongues.

Slowly, the penumbra spread. Gasps of terror rose from the crowd. Some knelt in prayer; others froze in petrified dread. Twala sat pale, while Gagool insisted the shadow would pass.

"Wait," I urged, hopping with excitement. "Keep it up, Good!"

For ten minutes, Good swore in a dozen languages, while the dark ring consumed the moon. The air grew thick with crimson gloom. The great pale orb turned coppery, then ashen, its mountains glowing luridly through the encroaching shadow.

"The moon is dying—the white wizards have killed the moon!" Scragga yelled, driven mad by fear. Enraged, he lunged at Sir Henry's breast. He forgot the mail shirt beneath his tunic; the steel rebounded harmlessly. Before Scragga could strike again, Curtis snatched the spear and drove it through the prince's chest.

Scragga dropped dead.

Panic erupted. The girls scattered in wild confusion, screaming for the gates. Twala, Gagool, and his guards fled into the huts. Left alone on the scene were the would-be victim Foulata, Infadoos, the chiefs, and the dead body of Scragga.

"Chiefs," I called out, "we have given you the sign. The charm cannot be stopped; it will last an hour and a half. Let us cover ourselves in the darkness."

Infadoos nodded, turning to lead the way. As we stumbled through the gate, the moon vanished utterly, and the stars burst forth into the inky sky. Holding hands, we plunged into the profound darkness, leaving the kraal behind.

Fortune favored our escape; Infadoos and the chiefs knew the labyrinthine paths of the great town Loo by heart, guiding us sideways through the shadows until we passed unmolested. For an hour we trudged onward, the darkness pressing heavy upon us, until the celestial drama began to shift. The eclipse receded, and that severed edge of the moon, previously hidden, reappeared. Suddenly, a silver streak erupted from its surface, trailed by a wondrous, ruddy glow that hung against the black sky like a celestial lamp. It was a sight both wild and lovely. Within five minutes, the stars began to fade, casting enough illumination to reveal our surroundings. We had cleared the town and were now ascending a large, flat-topped hill, roughly two miles in circumference. Common to the South African landscape, this hill was not towering in height—its peak rising barely two hundred feet—but it was shaped like a horseshoe, with precipitous sides strewn with boulders. The grassy plateau at its summit offered ample ground, formerly utilized as a military cantonment of significant strength. While its usual garrison numbered one regiment of three thousand men, the returning moonlight revealed that several such regiments were now encamped there.

Upon reaching the table-land, we found crowds of men roused from their slumber, shivering with fear and huddled in utter consternation at the unnatural phenomenon above. Passing through the panic-stricken throngs without a word, we reached a central hut where we were astonished to find two men waiting, laden with our few abandoned goods.

"I sent for them," Infadoos explained, gesturing to the men, and then lifted up Good's long-lost trousers.

With an exclamation of rapturous delight, Good sprang at them, instantly donning the garment.

"Surely my lord will not hide his beautiful white legs!" Infadoos exclaimed with regret.

Good persisted, however. Once only did the Kukuana people catch a glimpse of his legs again. Good is a man of profound modesty. From this moment forth, their aesthetic desires were satisfied only by his single whisker, his transparent eye, and his movable teeth. Still gazing with fond remembrance at the trousers, Infadoos informed us that he had ordered the regiments to muster at dawn. His purpose was to explain the origins of the rebellion decided upon by the chiefs and to introduce to them the rightful heir to the throne, Ignosi.

When the sun rose, the troops—some twenty thousand men, the flower of the Kukuana army—were mustered on a vast open space. Drawn up in three sides of a dense square, they presented a magnificent spectacle. We took our station on the open side and were swiftly surrounded by the principal chiefs and officers. After silence was proclaimed, Infadoos addressed them. In vigorous, graceful language, befitting a born orator, he recounted the history of Ignosi's father and his base murder by King Twala, detailing how the father's wife and child were driven out to starve. He pointed out that the people groaned under Twala's cruel rule, citing the previous night's atrocities where many of the nobility were dragged forth and wickedly executed under false pretenses. Then he declared that the white lords from the Stars, perceiving their country's troubles, had determined to alleviate its lot. They had taken the real king, Ignosi, who languished in exile, by the hand and led him over the mountains. Witnessing Twala's wickedness, they had, as a sign to the wavering and to save the life of the girl Foulata, exercised their high magic to darken the moon and slay the young fiend Scragga. They were prepared to stand by the people and assist them in overthrowing Twala to set up Ignosi.

The discourse ended amidst a murmur of approbation. Then Ignosi stepped forward. Reiterating Infadoos's words, he concluded with a powerful speech: "O chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people, ye have heard my words. Now must ye make choice between me and him who sits upon my throne, the uncle who killed his brother and hunted his brother's child forth to die in the cold and the night. That I am indeed the king these"—pointing to the chiefs—"can tell you, for they have seen the snake about my middle. If I were not the king, would these white men be on my side with all their magic? Tremble, chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people! Is not the darkness they have brought upon the land to confound Twala and cover our flight, darkness even in the hour of the full moon, yet before your eyes?"

"It is," answered the soldiers.

"I am the king; I say to you, I am the king," Ignosi continued, drawing up his great stature and lifting his broad-bladed battle-axe above his head. "If there be any man among you who says that it is not so, let him stand forth and I will fight him now, and his blood shall be a red token that I tell you true. Let him stand forth, I say;" and he shook the great axe till it flashed in the sunlight.

As no one responded to this heroic invitation to death, Ignosi proceeded. "I am indeed the king, and should ye stand by my side in the battle, if I win the day ye shall go with me to victory and honour. I will give you oxen and wives, and ye shall take place of all the regiments; and if ye fall, I will fall with you. And behold, I give you this promise, that when I sit upon the seat of my fathers, bloodshed shall cease in the land. No longer shall ye cry for justice to find slaughter, no longer shall the witch-finder hunt you out so that ye may be slain without a cause. No man shall die save he who offends against the laws. The 'eating up' of your kraals shall cease; each one of you shall sleep secure in his own hut and fear naught, and justice shall walk blindfold throughout the land. Have ye chosen, chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people?"

"We have chosen, O king," came the answer.

"It is well. Turn your heads and see how Twala's messengers go forth from the great town, east and west, and north and south, to gather a mighty army to slay me and you. To-morrow, or perchance the next day, he will come against us with all who are faithful to him. Then I shall see the man who is indeed my man, the man who fears not to die for his cause; and I tell you that he shall not be forgotten in the time of spoil. I have spoken. Now go to your huts and make you ready for war."

There was a pause until one of the chiefs lifted his hand, and out rolled the royal salute, "Koom." It was the sign that the soldiers accepted Ignosi as their king. Then they marched off in battalions.

Half an hour later, we held a council of war. It was evident that before long we should be attacked in overwhelming force. From our vantage point on the hill, we could see troops mustering and runners going forth from Loo in every direction, doubtless summoning soldiers to Twala's aid. We had about twenty thousand men on our side, composed of seven of the best regiments in the country. Twala, according to calculation, had at least thirty to thirty-five thousand assembled in Loo, and they expected to gather another five thousand by midday the following morning. While it was possible some of his troops might desert, it was not a contingency to be counted on. Active preparations were already underway; strong bodies of armed men patrolled the foot of the hill, signaling a coming assault.

Infadoos and the chiefs, however, believed no attack would occur that day, which would instead be devoted to preparation and removing the moral effect of the supposed magical darkening of the moon. The onslaught would come the next day, and they proved right.

Meanwhile, we set to work to strengthen the position. Almost every man was turned out. The paths up the hill—which was more a sanatorium than a fortress, used generally for regiments recovering from service in unhealthy regions—were carefully blocked with masses of stones. Every approach was made as impregnable as time allowed. Piles of boulders were collected to be rolled down upon an advancing enemy, stations were appointed to the different regiments, and all preparations were made that our joint ingenuity could suggest.

Just before sundown, resting after our toil, we perceived a small company of men advancing from Loo, one bearing a palm leaf as a herald's sign. As he drew near, Ignosi, Infadoos, a few chiefs, and ourselves went down to the foot of the mountain to meet him. He was a gallant-looking fellow wearing the regulation leopard-skin cloak.

"Greeting!" he cried. "The king's greeting to those who make unholy war against the king; the lion's greeting to the jackals that snarl around his heels."

"Speak," I said.

"These are the king's words. Surrender to the king's mercy ere a worse thing befall you. Already the shoulder has been torn from the black bull, and the king drives him bleeding about the camp."

"What are Twala's terms?" I asked.

"His terms are merciful, worthy of a great king. These are the words of Twala, the one-eyed, the mighty, the husband of a thousand wives... Terror of the evil-doer... king from generation to generation! These are the words of Twala: 'I will have mercy and be satisfied with a little blood. One in every ten shall die, the rest shall go free; but the white man Incubu, who slew Scragga my son, and the black man his servant, who pretends to my throne, and Infadoos my brother, who brews rebellion against me, these shall die by torture as an offering to the Silent Ones.' Such are the merciful words of Twala."

After consulting briefly, I answered him in a loud voice so the soldiers might hear: "Go back, thou dog, to Twala, who sent thee, and say that we, Ignosi, veritable king of the Kukuanas, Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn, the wise ones from the Stars, who make dark the moon, Infadoos, of the royal house, and the chiefs, captains, and people here gathered, make answer and say, 'That we will not surrender; that before the sun has gone down twice, Twala's corpse shall stiffen at Twala's gate, and Ignosi, whose father Twala slew, shall reign in his stead.' Now go, ere we whip thee away, and beware how thou dost lift a hand against such as we are."

The herald laughed loudly. "Ye frighten not men with such swelling words," he cried. "Show yourselves as bold to-morrow, O ye who darken the moon. Be bold, fight, and be merry, before the crows pick your bones till they are whiter than your faces. Farewell; perhaps we may meet in the fight; fly not to the Stars, but wait for me, I pray, white men." With this shaft of sarcasm he retired, and almost immediately the sun sank.

That night was busy. Weary as we were, preparations for the morrow's fight continued by moonlight, and messengers constantly came and went. About an hour after midnight, everything that could be done was done, and the camp sank into silence save for the occasional challenge of a sentry. Sir Henry and I, accompanied by Ignosi and a chief, descended the hill to inspect the pickets. Suddenly, from unexpected places, spears gleamed out in the moonlight, only to vanish when we uttered the password. It was clear that none were sleeping at their posts. We returned, picking our way warily through thousands of sleeping warriors, many taking their last earthly rest.

The moonlight flickering along their spears played upon their features, making them ghastly; the chilly night wind tossed their tall, hearse-like plumes. There they lay in wild confusion, with arms outstretched and twisted limbs; their stern, stalwart forms looking weird and unhuman.

"How many of these do you suppose will be alive at this time to-morrow?" asked Sir Henry.

I shook my head and looked again at the sleeping men. To my tired yet excited imagination, it seemed as though Death had already touched them. My mind singled out those sealed to slaughter, and a great sense of the mystery of human life, and an overwhelming sorrow at its futility, rushed upon my heart. Tonight these thousands slept their healthy sleep; tomorrow they, and many others, ourselves perhaps among them, would be stiffening in the cold; their wives would be widows, their children fatherless, and their place would know them no more forever. Only the old moon would shine serenely, the night wind stir the grasses, and the wide earth take its rest, even as it did aeons before we were, and will do aeons after we are forgotten.

Yet man dies not whilst the world, at once his mother and his monument, remains. His name is lost, indeed, but the breath he breathed still stirs the pine-tops on the mountains; the sound of the words he spoke yet echoes through space; the thoughts his brain gave birth to we have inherited today; his passions are our cause of life; the joys and sorrows he knew are our familiar friends—the end from which he fled aghast will surely overtake us also!

Truly the universe is full of ghosts, not sheeted churchyard spectres, but the inextinguishable elements of individual life, which having once been, can never die, though they blend and change forever.

Various reflections of this nature passed through my mind while I stood staring at those grim yet fantastic lines of warriors, sleeping "upon their spears."

"Curtis," I said, "I am in a condition of pitiable fear."

Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard and laughed. "I have heard you make that sort of remark before, Quatermain."

"Well, I mean it now. Do you know, I very much doubt if one of us will be alive to-morrow night. We shall be attacked in overwhelming force, and it is quite a chance if we can hold this place."

The three columns advanced with a terrifying, deliberate slowness, devoid of panic or haste. As they drew within half a mile, the central division halted at the base of a grassy spur jutting into the plain. This pause was tactical; it allowed the flanking wings to complete their encirclement of our horseshoe-shaped position, ensuring a simultaneous, three-pronged assault on the town of Loo.

"Oh, for a Gatling gun!" Good groaned, staring at the dense phalanx of enemies below. "I'd clear that field in twenty minutes."

"We lack such a weapon," Sir Henry replied calmly. "But try a shot, Quatermain. Aim for the tall figure commanding them. Even odds you miss, but I'll pay you a sovereign if you drop the bullet within five yards."

The wager stung my pride. I loaded the express rifle with a solid ball, waiting until the officer stepped forward alone to survey our defenses. Lying prone on a rock, I aligned the sights. The rifle was calibrated only to three hundred and fifty yards, so I calculated the trajectory drop, aiming for the center of his chest. He stood motionless, offering a perfect target. Yet, amidst the tension and the distance, my aim faltered. The report echoed, the smoke cleared, and to my fury, the commander stood unharmed. However, his orderly, standing just three paces to the left, lay motionless on the ground.

"Bravo, Quatermain!" Good shouted, delighted. "You've scared him."

Anger surged through me. A man masters one art, and he guards his reputation fiercely. Humiliated by the miss, I acted rashly. As the officer turned to flee in alarm, I fired the second barrel. The man threw up his arms and collapsed instantly. I had hit him true. A cold, brutal satisfaction washed over me—a dark delight at the sight of an enemy leader felled.

The enemy regiments watched this display of "white magic" and cheered wildly, interpreting it as an omen of victory. Conversely, the unit the fallen officer commanded broke ranks and retreated in confusion. Seizing the moment, Sir Henry and Good opened fire, Good methodically suppressing the mass with his Winchester repeater. We managed to disable six or eight men before they vanished from range.

Then, the air erupted. An ominous roar thundered from our right, mirrored instantly on our left. The flanking divisions had engaged.

The enemy mass before us widened, charging up the grassy spur at a trot, singing a guttural war-song. We returned fire steadily; Ignosi joined in, claiming several kills, yet our volley was as ineffective as pebbles thrown against a crashing wave. They surged forward, spears clashing, driving in our pickets. The advance slowed as they climbed the slope, conserving breath. Our defense was layered: the first line halfway down the slope, the second fifty yards back, and the third on the plateau's edge.

They stormed up, screaming "Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!" ("Smite! Smite!"), while our men roared back, "Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!" Throwing knives flashed in the sunlight. With a deafening yell, the melee closed in.

Warriors swayed in a chaotic mass, men falling like autumn leaves. But the sheer weight of numbers soon told. Our first line was pressed back into the second, where the fight was fiercest, before finally collapsing into the third. Twenty minutes in, the attackers were exhausted and bleeding heavily. Breaching our third, impenetrable wall of spears proved impossible. The lines swayed in a desperate ebb and flow, the outcome uncertain.

Sir Henry watched the struggle with burning eyes, then without a word, he charged into the thickest part of the fray, followed closely by Good. I remained where I stood.

The soldiers spotted his tall form plunging into battle and cried out, "Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!" ("Here is the Elephant!"), followed by "Chiele! Chiele!"

From that moment, the tide turned. Fighting with splendid gallantry, the enemy was pushed back inch by inch until they retreated in confusion. Just then, a messenger confirmed the left flank had been repulsed. Relief washed over us, but our horror was short-lived. We saw our right flank crumbling, men driven across the plain toward us, pursued by swarms of the enemy who had succeeded there.

Ignosi assessed the situation instantly and issued a rapid command. The reserve regiment, the Greys, extended its line. Another order rang out, and to my intense disgust, I found myself swept into a furious counter-charge. Clinging to Ignosi's massive frame, I moved forward, resigned to death.

Within moments, we were plunging through fleeing comrades reforming behind us. Then, chaos erupted—a dreadful rolling noise of colliding shields, and suddenly, a huge, enraged figure appeared, eyes bulging, spear dripping blood, charging straight at me. Most men would have frozen. I did not. Realizing standing meant death, I dropped flat. The giant, unable to stop, crashed over my prostrate form. Before he could rise, I sprang up and ended him with my revolver.

Someone knocked me down shortly after, and darkness took me.

When I regained consciousness, I was back at the kopje. Good bent over me, pouring water from a gourd.

"How do you feel, old fellow?" he asked anxiously.

I sat up, shaking off the daze. "Pretty well, thank you."

"Thank Heaven! When I saw them carry you in, I thought you were done for."

"Not this time, my boy. Just a blow to the head. How has it ended?"

"For now, they are repulsed everywhere. But the cost is terrible. We have lost nearly two thousand, and they must have lost three times as many." He pointed to long lines of men advancing by fours. In the center of each group, borne aloft, was a hide tray. These carried the wounded. Medicine men examined them swiftly. If the wound was mortal, the doctor would swiftly open an artery with a sharp knife. The sufferer would expire painlessly in minutes. It was a ghastly mercy, common among the Kukuanas, as the broad spears often made recovery impossible. I shuddered to recall the sight, remembering only too well a similar scene with Swazi warriors burying their dying alive.

Moving to the other side of the kopje, we found Sir Henry, Ignosi, Infadoos, and the chiefs in deep consultation.

"Thank Heaven you're here, Quatermain," Sir Henry said. "I can't read Ignosi's mind. We beat the attack, but Twala is bringing reinforcements and intends to starve us out."

"That is awkward," I agreed.

"Yes," Sir Henry continued. "Especially since Infadoos reports the water supply has failed. The spring cannot feed this multitude. By night, we shall all be thirsty."

Infadoos confirmed this. "Macumazahn, you are wise. You have seen many wars. Tell us what to do. Twala replaces his fallen, yet he learned his lesson today; our beak pierced his breast. He fears striking again. He will wait for us to die, winding around us like a snake around a buck, forcing a fight of 'sit down.'"

"I hear thee," I said.

"So, Macumazahn," Ignosi addressed me, "we have no water, little food, and three choices: languish like a starving lion, break away north, or launch ourselves at Twala's throat. Incubu, the great warrior, says 'Charge.' But the Elephant is prone to charge. What says Macumazahn, the wily fox who bites from behind? The last word lies with Ignosi, the King, but let us hear your voice, and the voice of him of the transparent eye."

"What sayest thou, Ignosi?" I asked.

"Nay, my father," Ignosi replied, looking every inch a warrior king despite his former role as servant, "speak, and let me, a child in wisdom, hearken."

Adjured, I conferred briefly with Good and Sir Henry, then delivered my opinion. Trapped and thirsty, our best chance was an immediate attack on Twala's forces. I urged striking "before our wounds grew stiff" and before fear paralyzed our soldiers' hearts. Otherwise, captains might seek peace with Twala or betray us.

My words were received with unusual respect among the Kukuanas. Yet, the final decision rested with Ignosi, the rightful king. After a pause of deep thought, he spoke.

"Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, brave white men; Infadoos, my uncle; and chiefs: my heart is fixed. I will strike at Twala today, setting my fortunes, and my life, and yours, on the blow. Listen. See how the hill curves like a half-moon, and the plain runs like a green tongue within the curve?"

"We see," I answered.

"Good. It is midday; the men rest. When the sun turns toward the darkness, let your regiment, my uncle, advance with one other down the green tongue. When Twala sees it, he will hurl his force to crush you. But the spot is narrow; they can come only one at a time. Thus, they shall be destroyed one by one, and all of Twala's eyes fixed on a struggle unseen by living man. With you, my uncle, shall go Incubu, so that when Twala sees his battle-axe flashing in the Greys, his heart may fail. I will come with the second regiment, following you, so that if you are destroyed, a king remains to fight. And with me comes Macumazahn the wise."

"It is well, O king," Infadoos said, contemplating the likely annihilation of his regiment with perfect calm. Death held no terror for them when incurred in duty.

"While the multitude of Twala's soldiers watches this fight," Ignosi continued, "one-third of our survivors—about six thousand—shall creep along the right horn of the hill to fall on Twala's left flank, and one-third along the left horn to strike his right. When I see the horns ready to toss Twala, I, with the men remaining, will charge home into his face. If fortune favors us, the day is ours, and before night drives her black oxen from the mountains, we shall sit in peace at Loo."

He ordered us to eat and prepare. Infadoos was tasked with ensuring the plan's execution. Ignosi decreed that Bougwan, the white father, accompany the right horn to lend courage to the captains with his shining eye.

The Kukuana military system operated with breathtaking speed. Within an hour, rations were consumed, divisions formed, and the scheme explained. The entire force, eighteen thousand strong, stood ready, save for a guard left with the wounded.

Good approached Sir Henry and me. "Good-bye, you fellows," he said, heading off with the right wing as ordered. "I have come to shake hands, in case we do not meet again."

We shook hands in silence, displaying the emotion typical of Anglo-Saxons.

"It is a queer business," Sir Henry said, his voice trembling slightly. "I confess I never expect to see tomorrow's sun. The Greys, with whom I go, are to fight until wiped out, enabling the wings to slip round and outflank Twala. Well, so be it; at least it will be a man's death. Good-bye, old fellow. God bless you. I hope you pull through and live to collar the diamonds; but if you do, take my advice and don't have anything more to do with Pretenders!"

In a second, Good had wrung our hands and vanished. Infadoos then led Sir Henry to the forefront of the Greys. With heavy misgivings, I departed with Ignosi to my station in the second attacking regiment, ready for the final gamble.

The flanking regiments vanished into the silence of the rising ground, their movements masked from Twala's keen scouts. A half-hour of tense stillness followed, a deliberate pause before the horns of the army stirred. Then, the Greys emerged, supported by the Buffaloes, the chest of the formation destined to bear the brunt of the coming storm. Both regiments were fresh, full of strength, and untouched by the morning's skirmish.

Infadoos, a wary old general, addressed his men in poetic terms, elevating their impending sacrifice to an honor. He spoke of the "Great White Warrior" fighting alongside them and promised cattle and promotion to survivors. I looked down the long lines of waving black plumes and stern faces, my heart heavy. Within an hour, most of these magnificent veterans, none under forty, would lie dead or dying in the dust. It was a wise recklessness, a necessary slaughter to secure the survival of the rest, yet they knew their doom. They stood ready to engage regiment after regiment on the narrow strip of green below, knowing they were foredoomed, yet not a single warrior showed a tremor of fear. Their absolute devotion to duty was a spectacle I envied, a complete indifference to the bitter fruits of their service that I could never muster.

"Behold your king!" Infadoos concluded, pointing to Ignosi. "Go fight and fall for him... cursed be the name of him who shrinks from death for his king."

A moment of silence followed, broken by a murmur that swelled into a roar of rolling sound, echoing like thunder against the mountains. It was the sound of six thousand spears tapping against shields. Then, the royal salute crashed out. Ignosi, proud beyond measure, lifted his battle-axe. The Greys filed off in a triple-line formation, followed by the Buffaloes. I prayed with every fiber of my being to emerge from this slaughter with my skin intact.

By the time we reached the plateau's edge, the Greys were descending the slope onto the tongue of grassland that jutted into the bend of the mountain. Below, Twala's camp was alive with excitement; regiment after regiment trotted forward to reach the root of the tongue before the attackers emerged. This narrow corridor, four hundred yards deep and widening from ninety to six hundred and fifty paces, was the bottleneck. The Greys halted at the broadening, reforming their triple line. Behind them, the Buffaloes took a stand in reserve.

Twala's force, now numbering at least forty thousand, advanced swiftly but hesitated at the gorge's mouth. They realized only one regiment could pass through at a time, and there, standing unassailable save from the front, were the Greys—the pride of the Kukuana army, ready to hold the way like the three Romans against the bridge.

Finally, Twala himself appeared, a tall general in nodding ostrich plumes. He signaled the first regiment to charge. The Greys remained silent and still until the foe was within forty yards, then unleashed a volley of *tollas*, throwing-knives that rattled among the attackers. Suddenly, the Greys sprang forward with uplifted spears. The meeting of shields roared like thunder, and the plain flashed with the light of shimmering weapons. The surging mass of humanity fought to and fro, but the attacking lines grew thin. With a slow, long heave, the Greys passed over them like a great wave over a sunken ridge. That regiment was destroyed; the Greys had two lines left, a third of their number dead.

Sir Henry's yellow beard was visible amidst the chaos, arranging the ranks. He was alive. We moved to the ground littered with four thousand prostrate bodies, stained red with blood. Ignosi ordered that no enemy wounded be killed, a command scrupulously followed amidst the carnage.

A second regiment, distinguished by white plumes, charged the remaining two thousand Greys. The tragedy repeated itself. The young men fought with furious weight, driving the veterans back, the slaughter awful. Hundreds fell every minute, the air filled with the hissing of triumph as victors drove their assegais through their foes. Yet, perfect discipline and unchanging valor held. Just as defeat seemed certain, Sir Henry's voice rang out, his battle-axe waving high. The Greys ceased to give, standing still as a rock against the furious waves of spearmen. Then, they moved forward. Without smoke to obscure the view, we saw the onslaught fade.

"These are men, indeed!" Ignosi shouted, grinding his teeth in excitement. "It is done!"

Like puffs of smoke, the attacking regiment broke away in flying groups. Of the three thousand-strong line, only six hundred blood-spattered men remained, yet they cheered and waved their spears in triumph. Instead of retreating, they ran forward a hundred yards, seized a rising knoll, and formed a threefold ring around its base. There, atop the mound, I saw Sir Henry, apparently unharmed, and Infadoos beside him. Then Twala's regiments rolled down upon the doomed band, and the battle closed in once more.

I, a man of peace and blood-aversion, felt my bosom burn with martial ardour for the first time. Warlike fragments of legend sprang to my mind; my blood, half-frozen with horror, beat wildly. I glanced at the warriors behind us, their features instinct with the hungry lust of battle, and wondered if my face looked like theirs. Only Ignosi seemed calm, though he too ground his teeth.

"Are we to stand here till we put out roots?" I asked.

"Nay, Macumazahn," Ignosi replied. "Now is the ripe moment: let us pluck it."

As he spoke, a fresh regiment rushed past the ring on the mound and attacked from the hither side. Ignosi lifted his axe, screamed the wild Kukuana war-cry, and the Buffaloes charged home with a rush like the sea.

What followed was a blur of irregular yet ordered advance, a sudden change of front, an awful shock, and a continuous flashing of spears seen through a red mist of blood. When my mind cleared, I found myself near the top of the mound, behind Sir Henry. He had borne me up during the initial charge and dragged me into shelter.

Again and again the multitudes surged against our shrinking circle, and again and again we beat them back. It was a splendid sight to see those brave battalions come on over the barriers of their dead, lifting corpses to receive our thrusts only to leave their own to swell the rising piles. Infadoos, cool as on parade, shouted orders and jests to keep spirits high, stepping forward to the thickest fighting. More gallant was the vision of Sir Henry, his ostrich plumes shorn, his long yellow hair streaming behind him. The great Dane stood there, hands, axe, and armor all red with blood. None could live before his stroke. Time after time, I saw his axe sweep down, shouting "O-hoy! O-hoy!" like his Berserker forefathers, crashing through shield, spear, and skull. He was the "umtagati," the wizard who killed and failed not.

Suddenly, a cry of "Twala, y' Twala" rose from the press. Out sprang the gigantic one-eyed king himself, armed with axe and shield, clad in chain armor.

"Where art thou, Incubu, thou white man, who slewest Scragga my son—see if thou canst slay me!" Twala shouted, hurling a *tolla* straight at Sir Henry. The white man caught it on his shield, the knife wedging in the iron plate. Twala sprang forward, striking such a blow on the shield that the shock brought Sir Henry down to his knees.

But the matter ended there. A shout of dismay rose from the surrounding regiments. To the right and left, the plain was alive with charging warriors. The outflanking squadrons had come to our relief. All Twala's army had been fixated on the bloody struggle in the center, believing our forces hidden in reserve. Now, before they could form a defense, the outflanking *Impis* leapt like greyhounds on their flanks.

In five minutes, the fate of the battle was decided. Taken on both flanks and dismayed by the slaughter, Twala's regiments broke into flight. The plain between us and Loo was scattered with running soldiers. Our hosts melted away as though by magic, leaving us standing like a rock from which the sea has retreated. Around us, the dead and dying lay in heaped-up masses. Of the gallant Greys, only ninety-five men remained upon their feet. More than three thousand four hundred had fallen.

"Men," Infadoos said calmly, binding a wound on his arm, "ye have kept up the reputation of your regiment. This day's fighting will be well spoken of by your children's children." He turned to shake Sir Henry's hand. "Thou art a great captain, Incubu... I have never seen a man like unto thee."

The Buffaloes marched past on the road to Loo. Ignosi requested that Infadoos, Sir Henry, and I join him to press on to Loo and capture Twala. Before we went far, we discovered the figure of Good sitting on an ant-heap. Beside him lay the body of a Kukuana.

"He must be wounded," Sir Henry said anxiously.

As he spoke, the dead body sprang up, knocking Good head over heels and beginning to spear him. We rushed forward in terror. The brawny warrior made dig after dig at the prostrate Good, who jerked his limbs into the air at each prod. Seeing us, the Kukuana gave one final vicious dig, shouted "Take that, wizard!" and bolted away. Good did not move. We approached, astonished to find him pale and faint but with a serene smile and his eyeglass still fixed in his eye.

"Capital armour this," he murmured. "How sold that beggar must have been," and then he fainted. Examination revealed a serious wound in the leg from a *tolla*, but the chain armor had prevented the spear from doing more than bruising him. It was a merciful escape. He was placed on a wicker shield and carried along.

Arriving before the nearest gate of Loo, we found a regiment watching it. Other regiments guarded the exits. The officer informed Ignosi that Twala's army had taken refuge in the town, thoroughly demoralized. Ignosi, taking counsel with us, sent heralds to each gate, promising life and forgiveness to every soldier who laid down arms, threatening to burn the town if they did not comply before nightfall. The message worked. Half an hour later, amid shouts and cheers, the bridge was dropped across the fosse, and the gates were flung open.

Taking precautions against treachery, we marched into the town. Thousands of dejected warriors stood along the roadways, heads drooping, shields and spears at their feet, saluting Ignosi as king. We marched straight to Twala's kraal. When we reached the great space, formerly the site of the review and witch hunt, we found it deserted. Not quite deserted, for there, on the further side in front of his hut, sat Twala himself with but one attendant—Gagool.

It was a melancholy sight. Seated with his battle-axe and shield by his side, chin upon his mailed breast, accompanied only by an old crone, the fallen king looked pitiable. Not a soldier, not a courtier, not even a wife remained to share his fate. He was learning the lesson that Fate teaches all: the eyes of mankind are blind to the discredited, and the fallen find few friends.

Filing through the kraal gate, we advanced to where the ex-king sat. At fifty yards, the regiment halted, and we proceeded with a small guard. Gagool reviled us bitterly. As we drew near, Twala lifted his plumed head, fixing his one eye, flashing with suppressed fury, upon his rival.

"Hail, O king!" he said with bitter mockery. "Thou who hast eaten of my bread... What fate hast thou in store for me, O king?"

"The fate thou gavest to my father, whose throne thou hast sat on these many years!" Ignosi answered sternly.

"It is good. I will show thee how to die... I crave the boon of the Kukuana royal House to die fighting."

"It is granted. Choose—with whom wilt thou fight?"

Twala's eye ran up and down our ranks. I felt a new horror as it rested on myself. What if he chose me? Against a desperate savage six feet five and broad in proportion, I had no chance. I resolved to decline, even if hooted out of Kukuanaland. Better to be hooted than quartered with a battle-axe.

Presently Twala spoke. "Incubu, what sayest thou, shall we end what we began to-day, or shall I call thee coward, white—even to the liver?"

"Nay," Ignosi interposed hastily. "Thou shalt not fight with Incubu."

"Not if he is afraid," said Twala.

Sir Henry understood the remark, and blood flamed into his cheeks. "I will fight him," he said. "He shall see if I am afraid."

"For Heaven's sake," I entreated, "don't risk your life..."

"I will fight him," was the sullen answer. "No living man shall call me a coward. I am ready now!" He stepped forward and lifted his axe.

I wrung my hands over this absurd piece of Quixotism, but if he was determined, I could not stop him. Ignosi laid his hand affectionately on Sir Henry's arm, pleading, but the Dane insisted. "Fight not, my white brother... thou hast fought enough."

"I will fight, Ignosi," Sir Henry answered. "It is well, Incubu; thou art a brave man. Behold, Twala, the Elephant is ready for thee."

Once the slaughter ceased, Sir Henry and Good were borne into Twala's hut, where I followed, joining them in their exhaustion. We were a trio utterly drained by exertion and blood loss; my own state was scarcely better. Though wiry and accustomed to fatigue, the night claimed me completely. As often happens when pushed to the limit, the old scar from the lion's claw began to ache, joined by a violent headache from the blow that had knocked me senseless that morning. It was a miserable evening, yet we possessed a grim comfort: we were lucky enough to feel misery rather than lying dead upon the plain among the thousands of brave men who had perished that night.

With the aid of Foulata, who had become our devoted handmaiden since we spared her life, and particularly Good's, we managed to remove the chain shirts. While they had saved our lives by stopping the weapons, the flesh beneath was a mass of terrible contusions. Foulata brought pounded green leaves with an aromatic scent, which, applied as a plaster, offered some relief. However, the bruises paled in comparison to the wounds. Good had a gaping hole through the fleshy part of his "beautiful white leg," from which he had lost a tremendous amount of blood. Sir Henry, besides other injuries, bore a deep cut across his jaw inflicted by Twala's battle-axe. Fortunately, Good was a competent surgeon. Once his small box of medicines was produced, he thoroughly cleansed the wounds and stitched them up with surprising skill, despite the dim light of the primitive Kukuana lamp. He then generously applied an antiseptic ointment and covered the injuries with the remnants of a pocket-handkerchief.

Meanwhile, Foulata prepared a strong broth, for we were too weary to eat. We swallowed it eagerly and collapsed onto piles of magnificent *karrosses*, or fur rugs, scattered across the dead king's great hut. By a cruel irony of fate, it was upon Twala's own couch, wrapped in his own particular rug, that Sir Henry, the man who had slain him, slept that night.

I say slept, but after such a day, rest was elusive. The air was thick with the sounds of farewell and mourning. From every direction came the wailing of women whose husbands, sons, and brothers had perished. Over twelve thousand men—nearly a fifth of the Kukuana army—had been destroyed in that awful struggle. It was heart-rending to listen to their cries for those who would never return, a sound that revealed the full horror of war done in the name of ambition. Towards midnight, the ceaseless crying subsided, leaving only intervals of silence broken by a long, piercing howl from a hut behind us. This was Gagool, "keening" over the dead king Twala.

I managed a fitful sleep, waking repeatedly to relive the terror of the last twenty-four hours. I saw the warrior I had sent to his death charging on the mountain-top; I stood again in the glorious ring of Greys making their immortal stand; I saw Twala's plumed and gory head roll past my feet with gnashing teeth.

When dawn broke, my companions had slept no better. Good was in a high fever, soon becoming light-headed and spitting blood, undoubtedly the result of an internal injury sustained when a Kukuana warrior had driven his spear through his chain armor. Sir Henry seemed relatively fresh, though his facial wound made eating difficult and laughter impossible; he was sore and stiff, barely able to stir.

Around eight o'clock, Infadoos visited us. The tough old warrior appeared little the worse for his exertions, though he admitted to staying up all night. He was delighted to see us but deeply grieved at Good's condition, shaking our hands cordially. I noted that he addressed Sir Henry with a reverence bordering on awe. Indeed, the great Englishman was viewed throughout Kukuanaland as a supernatural being. No soldier believed anyone could have fought as he did or slain Twala, the strongest warrior in the country, who could shear a bull's neck in a single stroke. That stroke became proverbial, and any extraordinary feat of strength was thereafter known as "Incubu's blow."

Infadoos informed us that Twala's regiments had submitted to Ignosi, and similar surrenders were coming from outlying chiefs. Twala's death ended all chance of disturbance, for Scragga, his only legitimate son, was dead, leaving no rival claimant. I remarked that Ignosi had risen to power through blood. The old chief shrugged. "Yes," he answered, "but the Kukuana people can only be kept cool by letting their blood flow sometimes. Many are killed, but the women remain, and others must soon grow up to take the places of the fallen. After this, the land will be quiet for a while."

Later that morning, Ignosi visited, the royal diadem now bound upon his brow. As he advanced with kingly dignity, flanked by an obsequious guard, I recalled the tall Zulu who had sought service at Durban months prior, reflecting on the strange revolutions of fortune.

"Hail, O king!" I said, rising.

"Yes, Macumazahn. King at last, by the might of your three right hands," he replied readily.

All was going well, he said, hoping to arrange a great feast in two weeks to show himself to the people. When I asked what he intended to do with Gagool, he declared her the evil genius of the land, promising to kill her and all the witch doctors with her. She had lived so long that none remembered her youth, and she had trained the witch-hunters, making the land wicked in the sight of heaven.

"Yet she knows much," I replied. "It is easier to destroy knowledge, Ignosi, than to gather it."

"That is so," he said thoughtfully. "She, and she only, knows the secret of the 'Three Witches' yonder, where the great road runs, where the kings are buried, and the Silent Ones sit."

"Yes, and the diamonds are. Forget not thy promise, Ignosi; thou must lead us to the mines, even if thou hast to spare Gagool alive to show the way."

"I will not forget, Macumazahn, and I will think on what thou sayest."

After Ignosi's departure, I went to see Good. He was quite delirious. The fever from his wound had taken a firm hold, complicated by the internal injury. For four or five days, his condition was critical. I am convinced that without Foulata's indefatigable nursing, he would have died.

Women are women, regardless of color, yet it seemed curious to watch this dusky beauty tending the fevered man night and day, performing the merciful errands of a sick-room with the instinct of a trained nurse. For the first couple of nights, I and Sir Henry tried to help, but Foulata bore our interference with impatience. She insisted we leave him to her, claiming our movements made him restless, which I believe was true. Day and night she watched him, giving him a native cooling drink made of milk infused with tulip bulb juice, and keeping flies away. I can see the picture now: Good tossing in his bed, emaciated, eyes shining large and luminous, jabbering nonsense; and beside him, seated on the ground, the soft-eyed, shapely Kukuana beauty, her face weary yet animated by infinite compassion—or perhaps something more?

For two days we feared he would die, moving about with heavy hearts. Only Foulata refused to believe it.

"He will live," she said.

For over three hundred yards around Twala's chief hut, there was absolute silence. By the king's order, everyone living in the habitations behind it, except Sir Henry and myself, had been removed so no noise might disturb the sick man. On the fifth day of his illness, I went to check on him before retiring.

I entered the hut carefully. The lamp on the floor showed Good tossing no more, lying quite still.

So it had come at last! In the bitterness of my heart, I gave a sob.

"Hush—h—h!" came from the patch of dark shadow behind Good's head.

Creeping closer, I saw he was not dead, but sleeping soundly, with Foulata's taper fingers clasped tightly in his poor white hand. The crisis had passed. He slept like that for eighteen hours. I scarcely like to say it, for fear of disbelief, but during the entire period, this devoted girl sat by him, fearing that if she moved and drew away her hand, it would wake him. What she must have suffered from cramp and weariness, not to mention hunger, nobody will ever know. When he finally woke, she had to be carried away; her limbs were so stiff she could not move them.

Once the turn was taken, Good's recovery was rapid and complete. It was not until he was nearly well that Sir Henry told him of all he owed to Foulata. When Good heard the story of her sitting by his side for eighteen hours, fearing to move lest she wake him, the honest sailor's eyes filled with tears. He turned straight to the hut where Foulata was preparing the mid-day meal, taking me with him to interpret, though she understood him marvelously despite his limited vocabulary.

"Tell her," said Good, "that I owe her my life, and that I will never forget her kindness to my dying day."

I interpreted, and under her dark skin, she actually seemed to blush. Turning to him with one of those swift, graceful motions that always reminded me of a wild bird, Foulata answered softly, glancing at him with her large brown eyes.

"Nay, my lord; my lord forgets! Did he not save *my* life, and am I not my lord's handmaiden?"

It was evident she had entirely forgotten the share Sir Henry and I had taken in her preservation. That is the way of women! I remember my dear wife was just the same. Well, I retired from that little interview sad at heart. I did not like Miss Foulata's soft glances, knowing the fatal amorous propensities of sailors, and of Good in particular.

There are two things in the world that cannot be prevented: you cannot keep a Zulu from fighting, or a sailor from falling in love upon the slightest provocation!

A few days later, Ignosi held his great "indaba," or council, and was formally recognized as king by the "indunas," or head men of Kukuanaland. The spectacle was imposing, including a grand review of troops. On this day, the remaining fragments of the Greys were paraded and thanked for their splendid conduct. The king made a large present of cattle to each man, promoting them all to officer rank in the new corps of Greys being formed. An order was promulgated that, while we honored the country by our presence, we three were to be greeted with the royal salute and treated with the same ceremony accorded to the king. Furthermore, the power of life and death was publicly conferred upon us. Ignosi, in the presence of his people, reaffirmed his promises that no man's blood should be shed without trial and that witch-hunting should cease.

When the ceremony ended, we waited upon Ignosi to express our anxiety to investigate the mystery of the mines to which Solomon's Road ran, asking if he had discovered anything.

"My friends," he answered, "I have discovered this. It is there that the three great figures sit, called the 'Silent Ones,' to whom Twala would have offered the girl Foulata as a sacrifice. It is there, too, in a great cave deep in the mountain, that the kings of the land are buried; there ye shall find Twala's body, sitting with those who went before him. There, also, is a deep pit, which long-dead men may have dug for the stones ye speak of, such as I have heard men in Natal tell of at Kimberley. There, too, in the Place of Death, is a secret chamber known to none but the king and Gagool. But Twala, who knew it, is dead, and I know it not, nor do I know what is in it. Yet there is a legend that once, many generations ago, a white man crossed the mountains, was led by a woman to the secret chamber, and shown the wealth hidden within. But before he could take it, she betrayed him, and he was driven back by the king of that day. Since then, no man has entered the place."

"The story is surely true, Ignosi, for on the mountains we found the white man," I said.

"Yes, we found him. And now I have promised you that if ye can come to that chamber, and the stones are there—"

"The gem upon thy forehead proves that they are there," I interjected, pointing to the great diamond I had taken from Twala's dead brows.

Darkness had fallen on the third day since our last ordeal, and we made camp at the foot of the "Three Witches," the jagged triangle of mountains marking the end of Solomon's Great Road. Our company was small and grim: myself, Sir Henry, and Good, accompanied by Foulata, who attended to our needs with a quiet efficiency that belied her youth. With us was Infadoos, the traitor, and Gagool, the witch, carried in a litter. Inside that woven cage, her voice was a constant, low mutter of curses and prophecies, a soundtrack to our weary journey. The mountains themselves were a testament to violent geological upheaval, three distinct peaks rising in a perfect triangle, their bases facing us.

The following morning, the sun revealed the true majesty of the Three Witches. High above, twisted snow-wreaths pierced the azure sky, while beneath the snowline, the slopes were a deep, bruised purple with heather. Before us, the white ribbon of Solomon's Road stretched upward for five miles before abruptly terminating at the foot of the central peak. It was a road with no destination, a final approach to the unknown.

An electric tension crackled through the group as we began our ascent. We were nearing the legendary mines, the source of ruin for Dom Pedro three centuries prior, the tragedy of my friend, and the probable doom of George Curtis. Superstition, a shadow that had clung to us since the beginning, tightened its grip. As we marched up the heather-fringed path, driven by a mixture of terror and insatiable curiosity, the bearers of Gagool's litter struggled to keep pace with our frantic energy.

"Walk more slowly, white men," Gagool's voice cut through the morning air, shrill and venomous. She leaned forward, her hideous, shriveled face pressing against the grass curtains of the litter, her eyes gleaming with malevolent intent. "Why will ye run to meet the evil that shall befall you, ye seekers after treasure?" Her laugh, a sound that seemed to scrape the inside of one's skull, sent a fresh chill down my spine, momentarily dampening our fervor. Yet, we pressed on.

Soon, the landscape opened up to reveal a vast, circular abyss, three hundred feet deep and half a mile wide, cutting into the earth between us and the peak.

"Do you not recognize this?" I asked Sir Henry and Good, who stared into the pit with wide-eyed astonishment.

They shook their heads in confusion.

"Then you have never seen the diamond diggings of Kimberley," I replied, my voice steady despite the adrenaline coursing through me. "This is Solomon's Diamond Mine. Observe the strata of stiff blue clay clinging to the grass-covered slopes; the formation is identical. If we descended, we would find pipes of soapy brecciated rock. And look there," I pointed to a series of worn, flat stone slabs arranged along a gentle incline below a dry watercourse. "Those are the washing tables. If they are not, I am a Dutchman."

Indeed, this was the pit marked on the old Dom's map. The Great Road branched here, circumventing the chasm, its path reinforced with massive stone blocks to prevent the edges from collapsing into the void. Driven by an overwhelming need to see the source of the legends, we followed the road around the pit's perimeter. From this vantage point, three towering objects emerged from the mist on the far side. As we drew closer, their nature became clear: they were Colossi.

Before us sat the "Silent Ones," revered and feared by the Kukuana people. They were colossal seated figures, two male and one female, each roughly thirty feet tall, perched on dark rock pedestals adorned with crude emblems of phallic worship. The female form, nude and severe in her beauty, bore crescent-shaped horns rising from her brow, though centuries of weather had eroded her features. Flanking her were the two males; the one to our right possessed the visage of a devil, while the one to our left wore a serene, terrifying calm—the kind of impassive cruelty the ancients attributed to gods who watched human suffering without pity.

A profound sense of awe settled over us. Whose hands had shaped these giants? Who had carved the pit and laid the stones? Suddenly, a memory struck me. Familiar with the Old Testament, I recalled the strange gods after whom Solomon had wandered: Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom. "These figures," I suggested, "may represent these false divinities."

Sir Henry, a man of classical learning, nodded thoughtfully. "There is merit in that theory. Ashtoreth was known to the Phoenicians as Astarte, the Queen of Heaven, often depicted with crescent horns. Perhaps these Colossi were commissioned by a Phoenician overseer managing the mines for Solomon. Who can truly say?"

Before we could delve deeper into this mystery, Infadoos approached. He saluted the Silent Ones with his spear and asked if we were ready to enter the "Place of Death" immediately or if we should wait for midday. Gagool, eager to begin her ritual, declared her willingness to guide us instantly. It was barely eleven o'clock, and our curiosity burned brighter than our caution. I instructed Foulata to pack a reed basket with dried game-flesh and water, a precaution for the inevitable delays within the cave.

Gagool stepped out of her litter, casting a final, evil grin at us before hobbling toward the sheer rock face rising fifty paces ahead. We followed her to a narrow, solidly arched portal that resembled the entrance to a mine gallery. Here, she paused, her grin widening.

"Now, white men from the Stars," she piped, her voice echoing strangely in the stillness. "Great warriors, Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn the wise—are ye ready? Behold, I am here to serve my lord the King and show you the store of bright stones. Ha! ha! ha!"

"We are ready," I replied.

"Good! Make strong your hearts to bear what ye shall see. And you, Infadoos, the betrayer—do you come?"

Infadoos frowned, his expression hardening. "Nay, I come not. It is not for me to enter there. But curb thy tongue, Gagool, and beware how thou dealest with my lords. At thy hands will I require them. If a hair of them be hurt, thou shalt die fifty times over. Hearest thou?"

"I hear, Infadoos," she sneered, her voice dripping with mockery. "I know thee; thou didst ever love big words. When thou wast a babe, thou threatenedst thine own mother. But fear not, for I live only to serve the King. I have served many kings until, in the end, they served me. Ha! ha! I go to look upon their faces once more, and Twala's also! Come, here is the lamp." She produced a large gourd filled with oil and fitted with a rush wick.

"Art thou coming, Foulata?" Good asked, his voice adopting the rough, villainous accent he had been practicing under her tutelage.

"I fear, my lord," the girl answered timidly, shrinking back.

"Then give me the basket."

"Nay, my lord, whither thou goest there, I go also."

I felt a pang of dread. "That may be rather awkward if we ever get out of this," I thought to myself.

Without further delay, Gagool plunged into the dark passage, wide enough for two to walk abreast. We followed the sound of her voice, our fear trembling in our limbs, until a sudden rush of wings startled us.

"Hullo! What's that?" Good shouted. "Somebody hit me in the face!"

"Bats," I said calmly. "On you go."

After perhaps fifty paces, the darkness began to fade, replaced by a faint, ethereal glow. Another minute passed, and we entered the most wonderful place the eyes of living man had ever beheld.

Imagine the nave of the world's greatest cathedral, windowless yet dimly illuminated from above by shafts piercing the roof. Now imagine that cathedral scaled up to a magnitude that defies comprehension. This was the cavern we found ourselves in, a natural cathedral loftier and wider than any human hand could build. But its size was merely the prelude to its wonder. Running in orderly rows down its length were gigantic pillars of white spar, resembling frozen ice. Some were twenty feet in diameter at the base, soaring delicately toward the distant roof, while others were still in the process of formation. On the rock floor, broken columns lay like the ruins of a Grecian temple, while high above, the tips of massive icicles hung from the ceiling.

We could hear the slow, rhythmic ticking of nature's clock as drops of water fell from the distant icicles onto the columns below. On some, the interval was minutes; on others, hours. One particular column, only eight feet high despite being nearly three thousand years old, bore a crude carving of a mummy and an Egyptian god at its head. The inscription told us that the column grew at a rate of one foot per millennium, a testament to the patience required to create such beauty.

The stalagmites took on strange, organic forms. One massive shape, weighing perhaps a hundred tons, resembled a pulpit fretted with lace-like designs. Others mimicked strange beasts, while fanlike ivory tracings adorned the cave walls like frost on a pane of glass. Smaller chambers branched off the main aisle like chapels from a great cathedral, some tiny nooks no larger than a dollhouse yet containing their own miniature formations of dripping water and growing crystals.

Gagool, however, showed no interest in the geology. She moved with impatient purpose, indifferent to the splendor surrounding us. Annoyed by her haste, I resolved to study the cave's mysteries on our return, but for now, we followed our uncanny guide toward the top of the vast chamber.

There, we found another doorway, square-topped and reminiscent of Egyptian temples.

"Are ye prepared to enter the Place of Death, white men?" Gagool asked, her tone deliberately unsettling.

"Lead on, Macduff," Good said solemnly, forcing a bravado that did not reach his eyes. Only Foulata clutched Good's arm for protection.

"This is getting rather ghastly," Sir Henry murmured, peering into the darkness. "Come on, Quatermain—seniores priores. We must not keep the old lady waiting!" He politely stepped aside, allowing me to lead the way, a gesture I inwardly resented.

*Tap, tap,* went Gagool's stick as she trotted down the passage, chuckling hideously. Overcome by a presentiment of evil, I hesitated.

"Come, get on, old fellow," Good urged. "Or we shall lose our fair guide."

Reluctantly, I started down. After twenty paces, I found myself in a gloomy apartment, roughly forty feet long and thirty high, clearly hollowed out by hand labor in ages past. At first, in the dim light, I saw only a massive stone table running down the center, flanked by colossal white figures. Then, my eyes adjusted, and I saw the horror that awaited us: a brown figure seated at the head of the table, and life-sized white figures surrounding it. My blood ran cold, and I turned to flee, but Sir Henry grabbed me by the collar, holding me fast.

"It is a ghastly sight," I gasped, my voice trembling. "What can it be?"

"And what are those things?" Good stammered, pointing to the white company.

"And what on earth is that thing?" Sir Henry cried, gesturing to the brown creature.

*"Hee! hee! hee!"* laughed Gagool. "To those who enter the Hall of the Dead, evil comes. Ha! ha!"

She reached out, grabbing Curtis's coat, and led us toward the table. She stopped and pointed to the brown object seated there. Sir Henry recoiled with an exclamation. There, quite naked, sat the gaunt corpse of Twala, the last King of the Kukuana. His head, severed by Curtis's battle-axe, rested upon his knees, the vertebrae protruding an inch above the shrunken flesh of the neck, looking like a grotesque black double of a man named Hamilton Tighe.

A thin, glassy film covered the corpse's surface, making it appear even more appalling. Then, we noticed the source: from the roof, water fell steadily—*drip! drop! drip!*—onto the neck of the corpse, running down over its entire surface and escaping through a tiny hole in the table.

I realized the truth: *Twala's body was being transformed into a stalactite.*

A look at the white forms seated around the stone bench confirmed it. They were human bodies, once alive, now petrified. They were stalactites. This was the method the Kukuana people had used for millennia to preserve their royal dead. They placed them under the dripping water, allowing the siliceous fluid to encase them in ice-like spar, freezing their features in eternal silence.

Twenty-seven of them sat there, the last being Ignosi's father, wrapped in shrouds of crystal through which their features could be dimly discerned. Seated around that inhospitable board, with Death himself for a host, the scene was beyond awe-inspiring. The practice must be ancient; calculating an average reign of fifteen years for the twenty-seven kings suggested the tradition began four and a quarter centuries ago.

But the colossal Death figure at the head of the board was far older. Hewn from a single stalactite, it was a masterpiece of anatomical perfection, a skeleton fifteen feet tall holding a great white spear aloft as if to strike. Its grinning skull projected forward, hollow eye-sockets fixed upon us, jaws slightly agape as if about to speak.

Was it a freak of fancy by an old-world sculptor, or a deliberate warning to marauders? I could not say. All I could do was describe it as it stood: the White Death presiding

While we lay recovering from our terror, inspecting the grisly remains of the Place of Death, Gagool was busy elsewhere. With surprising agility, she scrambled onto the great table and approached Twala's frozen form beneath the dripping rock. Whether she was checking on his "preservation" or serving some darker purpose, she bent to kiss his icy lips with feigned affection before hobbling back. She paused intermittently to murmur unintelligible greetings to the shrouded figures, treating them as old acquaintances. Once this macabre ritual concluded, she squatted directly beneath the White Death and began to chant prayers to the arch-enemy of mankind. The sight of this wicked creature supplicating the devil was so unnerving that it spurred us to hasten our investigation.

"Now, Gagool," I whispered, for the air itself seemed to forbid loud speech, "lead us to the chamber."

The old witch descended promptly. "My lords are not afraid?" she leered.

"Lead on."

"Good, my lords," she hobbled around the rear of the great stone. "Here is the chamber. Light the lamp and enter." She set a gourd of oil on the floor and leaned against the cave wall. I struck a match from our dwindling supply, lighting a rush wick, but before us lay only solid rock. Gagool grinned. "The way is there, my lords. Ha! ha! ha!"

"Do not jest with us," I commanded sternly.

"I jest not. See!" she pointed.

As she raised the lamp, we watched in disbelief as a massive slab of stone rose slowly from the floor, sliding upward into a cavity in the ceiling. The monolith, roughly ten feet high and five feet thick, weighed perhaps thirty tons, operated by a simple counter-weight system akin to a modern window sash. We did not see the trigger mechanism, for Gagool avoided it, but I suspected a hidden lever required only slight pressure to tip the balance. Slowly, the stone vanished, revealing a dark maw leading to Solomon's treasure.

Our excitement was palpable. Was this a hoax, or did vast hoards await us? We would know soon enough.

"Enter, white men from the Stars," Gagool called, stepping into the darkness. "First, hear your servant. The bright stones you seek were dug from the pit of the Silent Ones and stored here long before my memory begins. This place has been entered only once since those who hid the stones fled in haste. A white man arrived from over the mountains, perhaps also 'from the Stars,' and was welcomed by the king. He and a local woman journeyed here; by chance, the woman learned the secret of the door—a secret you would search a thousand years for in vain. They entered, found the stones, and filled a small goat-skin carried by the woman. As he departed, he took one final, large stone in his hand."

She paused. "Well," I breathed, "what happened to Da Silvestra?"

The hag started. "How knowest thou the dead man's name?" she snapped, then continued. "None can tell. The white man was frightened; he dropped the goat-skin and fled with only the single stone, which the king took. It is that very stone you, Macumazahn, removed from Twala's brow."

"Have none entered since?" I asked, peering down the dark passage.

"None, my lords. Only the kings have opened it, though none have entered. There is a saying: those who enter die within a moon, just as the white man died in the mountain cave. Therefore, the kings do not enter. Ha! ha! Mine are true words."

Her gaze locked with mine, and I felt a chill. How did she know all this?

"Enter, my lords. If I speak truth, the goat-skin lies on the floor; if death awaits us, you will learn later. Ha! ha! ha!" She hobbled through, carrying the light, though I hesitated.

"Oh, confound it!" Good declared. "Here goes. I will not be frightened by that old devil." Followed by Foulata, who shivered violently despite her fear, he plunged into the passage. We quickly followed.

A few yards down, Gagool waited. "See, my lords," she illuminated, "those who stored the treasure fled in haste, lacking time to finish their work." She pointed to square blocks of stone laid across the passage, forming a partial wall. Alongside were more blocks, a heap of mortar, and trowels identical to those used by workmen today.

Foulata, overcome by fear and faintness, collapsed. We set her down on the unfinished wall beside her basket of provisions and left her to recover.

Pressing onward for fifteen paces, we encountered an elaborately painted wooden door, standing wide open. Across the threshold lay a goat-skin bag, heavy and jingling.

"Hee! hee!" Gagool sniggered as the light fell upon it. "What did I tell you? The white man fled in haste and dropped the woman's bag. Look within; you will find a water-gourd among the stones."

Good lifted the bag. "By Jove! Full of diamonds," he whispered in awe.

"Go on," Sir Henry urged impatiently, taking the lamp. He stepped through, followed by us, momentarily forgetting the diamonds, and found ourselves in the treasure chamber.

The lamp's faint glow revealed a room hewn from living rock, perhaps ten feet square. Stacked against the arch of the roof were hundreds of elephant tusks of the finest quality—enough ivory to make a man wealthy for life. On the opposite side stood eighteen wooden boxes, painted red, resembling ammunition crates.

"There are the diamonds," I cried. "Bring the light."

Sir Henry held the lamp to the top box. The lid, rotted by time, had been smashed, likely by Da Silvestra. Pushing my hand through the hole, I withdrew it full not of diamonds, but of gold pieces stamped with Hebrew characters.

"We shan't go back empty-handed," I said. "There must be thousands of pieces here. Probably payment for workmen and merchants."

"I don't see any diamonds," Good noted. "Unless the old Portuguese put them all in his bag."

"Look where it is darkest," Gagool interpreted our silence. "There is a nook with three stone chests."

"How do you know this if no one has entered since the white man?" I asked.

"Some live long, and some have eyes that see through rock," she mocked.

"Look in that corner, Curtis."

"Hullo!" Curtis cried. "Here's a recess. Great heavens!"

We hurried to the niche, shaped like a bow window. Against the wall sat three stone chests. Two were sealed; the third lay open.

"See!" Curtis hoarsely held the lamp over the open chest.

For a moment, a silvery sheen blinded us. When our eyes adjusted, we saw the chest was three-quarters full of uncut diamonds, many of considerable size. I picked one up; the unmistakable soapy feel confirmed it.

I gasped. "We are the richest men in the whole world. Monte Christo was a fool to us."

"We shall flood the market," Good said.

"Get them out first," Sir Henry suggested.

We stood staring at the gems, lantern between us, feeling like conspirators rather than fortunate men.

"Hee! hee! hee!" Gagool cackled behind us, flitting like a vampire bat. "Take them, run them through your fingers, eat them, drink them, ha! ha!"

The absurdity of eating diamonds broke the tension, and we erupted into laughter, a sound echoing strangely in the tomb. We shrieked over the gems meant for us, stored millennia ago by Solomon's overseer. Solomon never got them, nor Da Silvestra. We had them: millions of pounds in diamonds, gold, and ivory waiting to be taken.

Suddenly, the laughter ceased.

"Open the other chests," Gagool croaked. "Take your fill!"

We broke the seals on the remaining two. The second was full to the brim; the third, though only a quarter full, contained picked stones, many over twenty carats, some as large as pigeon eggs. Several were slightly yellow, "off-coloured" by Kimberley standards.

What we did not see was the look of fearful malevolence Gagool fixed upon us as she slithered out of the chamber and down the passage toward the rock door.

Then, cries rang up the vaulted path. Foulata's voice!

"Oh, Bougwan! Help! The stone falls!"

"Leave her go!"

"Help! She has stabbed me!"

We ran down the passage. The rock door was closing, mere feet from the floor. Near it, Foulata and Gagool struggled. Blood ran to Foulata's knee, but the brave girl held the witch, who fought like a wild cat. Suddenly, Foulata fell. Gagool threw herself on the ground, twisting like a snake through the crack of the closing stone. She was under—but too late! The stone nipped her, and she screamed in agony. Down, down it came, thirty tons of rock slowly crushing her old body against the bedrock. Shrieks followed by a sickening crunch, and the door slammed shut just as we hurled ourselves against it.

It was all done in four seconds.

We turned to Foulata. She was mortally wounded.

"Bougwan, I die!" she gasped. "She crept out... the door began to fall... she came back... she stabbed me... I die, Bougwan!"

"Poor girl!" Good cried, falling to kiss her.

"Bougwan," she whispered, "is Macumazahn there? It grows dark..."

"Here I am, Foulata."

"Say to my lord Bougwan... I love him... I am glad to die because the sun may not mate with the darkness... Say that if I live again, I may see him in the Stars... Oh, hold me closer..."

"She is dead!" Good muttered, rising in grief.

"You need not let that trouble you," Sir Henry said grimly.

"Eh? What do you mean?"

"I mean you will soon be in a position to join her. Man, don't you see we are buried alive?"

Only then did the full horror strike us. The rock door had closed, sealing us in. The only one who knew the secret, Gagool, was crushed beneath it. We were trapped on the wrong side of a door no army could breach.

"This will never do," Sir Henry said hoarsely. "The lamp will soon go out. Let us find the spring that works the rock."

We searched desperately along the door and walls, but found no knob or lever.

"It does not work from the inside," I realized. "If it did, Gagool wouldn't have risked crawling under it."

"Retribution was swift," Sir Henry laughed bitterly. "Her end was almost as awful as ours. We can do nothing with the door; let us go back to the treasure room."

We returned, bringing Foulata's corpse to lie beside the boxes of coin. We seated ourselves against the stone chests containing the priceless loot.

"Divide the food," Sir Henry ordered. "Make it last."

We rationed the biltong and two quarts of water, enough for perhaps two days.

"Now," Sir Henry said grimly, "let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die."

We ate small portions, our appetites gone. Then we examined the walls of our prison-house, sounding them carefully. There was no exit.

"The lamp burns dim," I noted. "Quatermain, what is the time?"

"It is six. We entered at eleven."

"Infadoos will miss us," I suggested. "Curtis, if we don't return tonight, he will search in the morning."

"He may search in vain. He doesn't know the secret, nor where it is. No living person knew it yesterday except Gagool. Today, no one knows it. Even the Kukuana army couldn't break five feet of living rock. My friends, we must bow to the will of the Almighty. The search for treasure brings many to a bad end; we shall go to swell their number."

The lamp flickered and finally expired.

In the darkness, the scene remained: the great mass of white tusks, the boxes of gold, Foulata's corpse, the goat-skin of treasure, the glimmer of diamonds, and the wild, wan faces of the three white men seated there, awaiting death by starvation.

I cannot adequately describe the horrors of the night that followed. Mercifully, sleep came to us in fragments, for even in such a wretched position, nature occasionally asserts its claim. But I, at least, found rest impossible. Setting aside the terrifying thought of our impending doom—a fate that might well make the bravest man on earth quail—I was paralyzed by the silence itself. You, reader, may have lain awake in a quiet room and found the stillness oppressive, but you can have no conception of what perfect stillness truly is. On the surface of the earth, there is always some sound, some motion, however imperceptible, that deadens the sharp edge of absolute silence. Here, there was none. We were buried in the bowels of a huge, snow-clad peak. Thousands of feet above us, fresh air rushed over the white snow, yet no sound reached us. We were separated by a long tunnel and five feet of solid rock from the awful chamber of the Dead; and the dead make no noise. Did we not know it who lay by poor Foulata's side? The crashing of all the artillery of earth and heaven could not have penetrated our living tomb. We were cut off from every echo of the world—we were as men already in the grave.

Then the cruel irony of our situation forced itself upon me. There around us lay treasures enough to pay off a moderate national debt or build a fleet of ironclads, and yet we would have bartered them all gladly for the faintest chance of escape. Soon, doubtless, we should be rejoiced to exchange them for a bit of food or a cup of water, and, after that, even for the privilege of a speedy close to our sufferings. Truly, wealth, which men spend their lives acquiring, is a valueless thing at the last.

And so the night wore on.

"Good," said Sir Henry's voice at last, sounding awful in the intense stillness, "how many matches have you in the box?"

"Eight, Curtis."

"Strike one and let us see the time."

He did so, and in contrast to the dense darkness, the flame nearly blinded us. It was five o'clock by my watch. The beautiful dawn was now blushing on the snow-wreaths far over our heads, and the breeze would soon be stirring the night mists in the hollows.

"We had better eat something and keep up our strength," I suggested.

"What is the good of eating?" answered Good; "the sooner we die and get it over the better."

"While there is life there is hope," said Sir Henry.

Accordingly, we ate and sipped some water, and another period of time elapsed. Then Sir Henry suggested that it might be well to get as near the door as possible and shout, on the faint chance of somebody catching a sound outside. Accordingly, Good, who, from long practice at sea, possesses a fine, piercing note, groped his way down the passage and set to work. I must say that he made a most diabolical noise. I never heard such yells; but it might have been a mosquito buzzing for all the effect they produced.

After a while, he gave it up and came back very thirsty, and had to drink. Then we stopped yelling, as it encroached on our precious supply of water.

So we sat down once more against the chests of useless diamonds in that dreadful inaction which was one of the hardest circumstances of our fate; and I am bound to say that, for my part, I gave way in despair. Laying my head against Sir Henry's broad shoulder, I burst into tears; and I think that I heard Good gulping away on the other side, swearing hoarsely at himself for doing so.

Ah, how good and brave that great man was! Had we been two frightened children and he our nurse, he could not have treated us more tenderly. Forgetting his own share of miseries, he did all he could to soothe our broken nerves, telling stories of men who had been in somewhat similar circumstances and miraculously escaped. When these failed to cheer us, he pointed out how, after all, it was only anticipating an end which must come to us all, that it would soon be over, and that death from exhaustion was a merciful one (which is not true). Then, in a diffident sort of way, as once before I had heard him do, he suggested that we should throw ourselves on the mercy of a higher Power, which for my part I did with great vigor.

His is a beautiful character, very quiet, but very strong.

And so somehow the day went as the night had gone, if, indeed, one can use these terms where all was densest night. When I lit a match to see the time, it was seven o'clock.

Once more we ate and drank, and as we did so, an idea occurred to me.

"How is it," said I, "that the air in this place keeps fresh? It is thick and heavy, but it is perfectly fresh."

"Great heavens!" said Good, starting up, "I never thought of that. It can't come through the stone door, for it's air-tight, if ever a door was. It must come from somewhere. If there were no current of air in the place, we should have been stifled or poisoned when we first came in. Let us have a look."

It was wonderful what a change this mere spark of hope wrought in us. In a moment, we were all three groping about on our hands and knees, feeling for the slightest indication of a draught. Presently, my ardour received a check. I put my hand on something cold. It was dead Foulata's face.

For an hour or more, we went on feeling about, till at last Sir Henry and I gave it up in despair, having been considerably hurt by constantly knocking our heads against tusks, chests, and the sides of the chamber. But Good still persevered, saying, with an approach to cheerfulness, that it was better than doing nothing.

"I say, you fellows," he said presently, in a constrained sort of voice, "come here."

Needless to say, we scrambled towards him quickly enough.

"Quatermain, put your hand here where mine is. Now, do you feel anything?"

"I _think_ I feel air coming up."

"Now listen." He rose and stamped upon the place, and a flame of hope shot up in our hearts. _It rang hollow._

With trembling hands, I lit a match. I had only three left, and we saw that we were in the angle of the far corner of the chamber, a fact that accounted for our not having noticed the hollow sound during our former exhaustive examination. As the match burned, we scrutinized the spot. There was a join in the solid rock floor, and, great heavens! there, let in level with the rock, was a stone ring. We said no word; we were too excited, and our hearts beat too wildly with hope to allow us to speak. Good had a knife, at the back of which was one of those hooks made to extract stones from horses' hoofs. He opened it and scratched round the ring. Finally, he worked it under and levered away gently for fear of breaking the hook. The ring began to move. Being of stone, it had not rusted fast in all the centuries it had lain there, as would have been the case had it been of iron. Presently, it was upright. Then he thrust his hands into it and tugged with all his force, but nothing budged.

"Let me try," I said impatiently, for the situation of the stone, right in the angle of the corner, was such that it was impossible for two to pull at once. I took hold and strained away, but no result.

Then Sir Henry tried and failed.

Taking the hook again, Good scratched all round the crack where we felt the air coming up.

"Now, Curtis," he said, "tackle on, and put your back into it; you are as strong as two. Stop," and he took off a stout black silk handkerchief, which, true to his habits of neatness, he still wore, and ran it through the ring. "Quatermain, get Curtis round the middle and pull for dear life when I give the word. _Now._"

Sir Henry put out all his enormous strength, and Good and I did the same, with such power as nature had given us.

"Heave! heave! it's giving," gasped Sir Henry; and I heard the muscles of his great back cracking. Suddenly there was a grating sound, then a rush of air, and we were all on our backs on the floor with a heavy flag-stone upon the top of us. Sir Henry's strength had done it, and never did muscular power stand a man in better stead.

"Light a match, Quatermain," he said, so soon as we had picked ourselves up and got our breath; "carefully, now."

I did so, and there before us, Heaven be praised! was the _first step of a stone stair._

"Now what is to be done?" asked Good.

"Follow the stair, of course, and trust to Providence."

"Stop!" said Sir Henry; "Quatermain, get the bit of biltong and the water that are left; we may want them."

I went, creeping back to our place by the chests for that purpose, and as I was coming away, an idea struck me. We had not thought much of the diamonds for the last twenty-four hours; indeed, the very idea of diamonds was nauseous, seeing what they had entailed upon us. But, I reflected, I may as well pocket some in case we ever should get out of this ghastly hole. So I just put my fist into the first chest and filled all the available pockets of my old shooting-coat and trousers, topping up—with a happy thought—with a few handfuls of big ones from the third chest. Also, by an afterthought, I stuffed Foulata's basket, which, except for one water-gourd and a little biltong, was empty now, with great quantities of the stones.

"I say, you fellows," I sang out, "won't you take some diamonds with you? I've filled my pockets and the basket."

"Oh, come on, Quatermain! and hang the diamonds!" said Sir Henry. "I hope that I may never see another."

As for Good, he made no answer. He was, I think, taking his last farewell of all that was left of the poor girl who had loved him so well. And curious as it may seem to you, my reader, sitting at home at ease and reflecting on the vast, indeed the immeasurable, wealth which we were thus abandoning, I can assure you that if you had passed some twenty-eight hours with next to nothing to eat and drink in that place, you would not have cared to cumber yourself with diamonds whilst plunging down into the unknown bowels of the earth, in the wild hope of escape from an agonizing death. If from the habits of a lifetime, it had not become a sort of second nature with me never to leave anything worth having behind if there was the slightest chance of my being able to carry it away, I am sure that I should not have bothered to fill my pockets and that basket.

"Come on, Quatermain," repeated Sir Henry, who was already standing on the first step of the stone stair. "Steady, I will go first."

"Mind where you put your feet, there may be some awful hole underneath," I answered.

"Much more likely to be another room," said Sir Henry, while he descended slowly, counting the steps as he went.

When he got to "fifteen" he stopped. "Here's the bottom," he said. "Thank goodness! I think it's a passage. Follow me down."

Good went next, and I came last, carrying the basket, and on reaching the bottom, lit one of the two remaining matches. By its light, we could just see that we were standing in a narrow tunnel, which ran right and left at right angles to the staircase we had descended. Before we could make out any more, the match burnt my fingers and went out. Then arose the delicate question of which way to go. Of course, it was impossible to know what the tunnel was, or where it led to, and yet to turn one way might lead us to safety, and the other to destruction. We were utterly perplexed, till suddenly it struck Good that when I had lit the match, the draught of the passage blew the flame to the left.

"Let us go against the draught," he said; "air draws inwards, not outwards."

We took this suggestion, and feeling along the wall with our hands, whilst trying the ground before us at every step, we departed from that accursed treasure chamber on our terrible quest for life. If ever it should be entered again by living man, which I do not think probable, he will find tokens of our visit in the open chests of jewels, the empty lamp, and the white bones of poor Foulata.

When we had groped our way for about a quarter of an hour along the passage, suddenly it took a sharp turn, or else was bisected by another, which we followed, only in course of time to be led into a third. And so it went on for some hours. We seemed to be in a stone labyrinth that led nowhere. What all these passages are, of course I cannot say, but we thought that they must be the ancient workings of a mine, of which the various shafts and adits travelled hither and thither as the ore led them. This is the only way in which we could account for such a multitude of galleries.

At length, we halted, thoroughly worn out with fatigue and with that hope deferred which maketh the heart sick, and ate up our poor remaining piece of biltong and drank our last sup of water, for our throats were like lime-kilns. It seemed to us that we had escaped Death in the darkness of the treasure chamber only to meet him in the darkness of the tunnels.

Ten days after that harrowing morning, we returned to our quarters at Loo. Miraculously, we emerged from our ordeal scarcely worse for the wear, save for the fact that my stubble had emerged from the treasure cave three shades grayer than it entered, and that Good had never fully recovered from Foulata's death, a loss that seemed to weigh heavily upon his spirit. Yet, viewing the matter through the lens of a seasoned traveler, I must admit that her passing was a fortunate relief; without it, the entanglement between Good and such a creature of stately beauty and refined mind would have become an insurmountable complication. As she herself had poignantly asked, "Can the sun mate with the darkness, or the white with the black?"

We made no further attempt to penetrate Solomon's treasure chamber. After forty-eight hours of recovery, we descended into the great pit seeking the exit we had used, but the rain had washed away our tracks, and the cavern walls were riddled with ant-bear burrows, making our escape route impossible to identify. We also revisited the stalactite cave and the Chamber of the Dead, standing once more beneath the spear of the White Death to gaze at the massive rock that had sealed our fate. We searched for the secret mechanism of the sliding door, but found nothing; the join was invisible, and the method of operation was lost forever. It was a marvel of inscrutable simplicity, a testament to an age that could create such massive, silent machinery. Yet, we gave up in disgust. Even if the rock had risen before our eyes, revealing the path to the diamonds, I doubt we would have found the courage to step over Gagool's mangled remains to claim them. Still, the thought of leaving behind the greatest accumulation of wealth in human history brought a sting of regret.

There was, however, no help for it. Only dynamite could breach five feet of solid rock. Perhaps in some distant future, a more fortunate explorer might discover the "Open Sesame," flooding the world with gems. But I harbored a grim suspicion that the millions of pounds' worth of jewels lying in the three stone coffers would never adorn the neck of an earthly beauty; they and Foulata's bones would keep cold company until the end of time.

Disappointed, we turned back. Ironically, our disappointment was unwarranted. I had taken the precaution of filling the pockets of my shooting coat and trousers with gems before we left our prison, along with Foulata's basket, which held even more, though the water bottle had claimed some space. Several large stones had tumbled out during our descent, but an enormous quantity remained. We possessed enough to make us exceedingly wealthy men, and enough stones each to create the finest sets of gems in Europe. We had not done so badly.

Upon arriving at Loo, we were greeted with immense cordiality by Ignosi, who was in good health and busy consolidating his power and reorganizing his regiments after the great struggle with Twala. He listened with intense fascination to our tale, but when we recounted Gagool's frightful end, his expression turned thoughtful. He summoned an ancient Induna, a councillor who had known the witch-doctor since childhood. The old man confirmed that Gagaoola had been as ugly and wicked in his youth as she was in her final days. "She is no more; she is dead," Ignosi declared. "An ancient curse is taken from the land." He dismissed the councillor with a gesture of grim satisfaction, noting that she would have likely killed us in the dark place and subsequently sought to slay him to install Twala in his place.

Once I had finished narrating our escape, I addressed Ignosi regarding our departure from Kukuanaland. "Ignosi," I said, "the time has come to bid thee farewell and return to our own lands. Thou camest with us a servant, and now we leave thee a mighty king. If thou art grateful, remember to rule justly, respect the law, and put none to death without cause. So shalt thou prosper. Tomorrow, at break of day, thou wilt give us an escort to lead us across the mountains. Is it not so, O king?"

Ignosi covered his face with his hands for a moment before speaking. "My heart is sore," he said finally. "Your words split my heart in twain. What have I done to you, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, that ye should leave me desolate? Ye who stood by me in rebellion and in battle, will ye leave me in the day of peace and victory? Wives? Choose from among the maidens! A place to live? Behold, the land is yours as far as ye can see. The white man's houses? Ye shall teach my people how to build them. Cattle? Every married man shall bring you an ox or a cow. Wild game? Does not the elephant walk through my forests? Would ye make war? My Impis wait your word. If there is anything more I can give, that will I give you."

"Nay, Ignosi," I answered gently, "we seek our own place."

"Now do I learn," Ignosi said bitterly, his eyes flashing, "that ye love the bright stones more than me, your friend. Ye have the stones; now ye would go to Natal and across the moving black water to sell them and be rich, as it is the desire of a white man's heart. Cursed for your sake be the white stones, and cursed he who seeks them. Death shall it be to him who sets foot in the place of Death to find them. I have spoken. White men, ye can go."

I laid my hand upon his arm. "Ignosi," I said, "tell us, when thou didst wander in Zululand and among the white people of Natal, did not thine heart turn to the land thy mother told thee of, thy native place, where thou didst see the light and play when thou wast little?"

"It was even so, Macumazahn."

"In like manner, Ignosi, do our hearts turn to our land and to our own place."

A silence fell between us. When Ignosi broke it, his voice had changed. "I do perceive that now as ever thy words are wise and full of reason, Macumazahn; that which flies in the air loves not to run along the ground; the white man loves not to live on the level of the black or to house among his kraals. Well, ye must go, and leave my heart sore, because ye will be as dead to me, since from where ye are no tidings can come to me."

"But listen, and let all your brothers know my words. No other white man shall cross the mountains, even if any man live to come so far. I will see no traders with their guns and gin. My people shall fight with the spear, and drink water, like their forefathers before them. I will have no praying-men to put a fear of death into men's hearts, to stir them up against the law of the king, and make a path for the white folk who follow to run on. If a white man comes to my gates I will send him back; if a hundred come I will push them back; if armies come, I will make war on them with all my strength, and they shall not prevail against me. None shall ever seek for the shining stones: no, not an army, for if they come I will send a regiment and fill up the pit, and break down the white columns in the caves and choke them with rocks, so that none can reach even to that door of which ye speak, and whereof the way to move it is lost."

"But for you three, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, the path is always open; for, behold, ye are dearer to me than aught that breathes."

"And ye would go. Infadoos, my uncle, and my Induna, shall take you by the hand and guide you with a regiment. There is, as I have learned, another way across the mountains that he shall show you. Farewell, my brothers, brave white men. See me no more, for I have no heart to bear it. Behold! I make a decree, and it shall be published from the mountains to the mountains; your names, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, shall be '_hlonipa_' even as the names of dead kings, and he who speaks them shall die. So shall your memory be preserved in the land for ever."

"Go now, ere my eyes rain tears like a woman's. At times as ye look back down the path of life, or when ye are old and gather yourselves to crouch before the fire, because for you the sun has no more heat, ye will think of how we stood shoulder to shoulder, in that great battle which thy wise words planned, Macumazahn; of how thou wast the point of the horn that galled Twala's flank, Bougwan; whilst thou stood in the ring of the Greys, Incubu, and men went down before thine axe like corn before a sickle; ay, and of how thou didst break that wild bull Twala's strength, and bring his pride to dust. Fare ye well for ever, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, my lords and my friends."

Ignosi rose and looked earnestly at us for a few seconds. Then he threw the corner of his kaross over his head, covering his face from us. We went in silence.

Next day at dawn we left Loo, escorted by our old friend Infadoos, who was heart-broken at our departure, and by the regiment of Buffaloes. Early as was the hour, all the main street of the town was lined with multitudes of people who gave us the royal salute as we passed at the head of the regiment, while the women blessed us for having rid the land of Twala, throwing flowers before us as we went. It was truly affecting, a rare display of affection from natives.

One ludicrous incident occurred, however, which I rather welcomed, as it gave us something to laugh at. Just before we reached the confines of the town, a pretty young girl, holding lovely lilies, ran forward and presented them to Good. Some how, all seemed to like Good; perhaps his eyeglass and solitary whisker gave him a fictitious value. She then said she had a boon to ask.

"Speak on," he answered.

"Let my lord show his servant his beautiful white legs, that his servant may look upon them, and remember them all her days, and tell of them to her children; his servant has travelled four days' journey to see them, for the fame of them has gone throughout the land."

"I'll be hanged if I do!" exclaimed Good excitedly.

"Come, come, my dear fellow," said Sir Henry, "you can't refuse to oblige a lady."

"I won't," replied Good obstinately, "it is positively indecent."

However, in the end, he consented to draw up his trousers to the knee, amidst notes of rapturous admiration from all the women present, especially the gratified young lady. In this guise, he had to walk until we were clear of the town. Good's legs, I fear, will never be so greatly admired again. Of his melting teeth and even of his "transparent eye," the Kukuanas woreied more or less, but of his legs never.

As we traveled, Infadoos told us of another pass over the mountains to the north of the one followed by Solomon's Great Road. Rather more than two years previously, a party of Kukuana hunters had descended this path into the desert in search of ostriches, whose plumes are much prized for war head-dresses. In the course of their hunt, they had been led far from the mountains and troubled by thirst. Seeing trees on the horizon, they walked towards them and discovered a large and fertile oasis, plentifully watered. It was by way of this oasis that Infadoos suggested we should return, and the idea seemed excellent, as it would allow us to escape the rigors of the mountain pass. Some of the hunters were in attendance to guide us to the oasis, from which they stated they could perceive other fertile spots far away in the desert.

Travelling easily, on the night of the fourth day's journey, we found ourselves once more on the crest of the mountains that separate Kukuanaland from the desert, which rolled away in sandy billows at our feet, about twenty-five miles to the north of Sheba's Breasts.

At dawn on the following day, we were led to the edge of a very precipitous chasm, by which we were to descend the precipice and gain the plain two thousand feet below. Here we bade farewell to that true friend and sturdy old warrior, Infadoos, who solemnly wished us all good and nearly wept with grief. "Never, my lords," he said, "shall mine old eyes see the like of you again. Ah! the way that Incubu cut his men down in the battle! Ah! for the sight of that stroke with which he swept off my brother Twala's head! It was beautiful—beautiful! I may never hope to see such another, except perchance in happy dreams."

We were very sorry to part from him; indeed, Good was so moved that he gave him as a souvenir—an eyeglass. Afterwards, we discovered it was a spare one. Infadoos was delighted, foreseeing that the possession of such an article would increase his prestige enormously, and after several vain attempts, he actually succeeded in screwing it into his own eye. Anything more incongruous than the old warrior looked with an eyeglass I never saw. Eyeglasses do not go well with leopard-skin cloaks and black ostrich plumes.

Then, after seeing that our guides were well laden with water and provisions, and having received a thundering farewell salute from the Buffaloes, we wrung Infadoos by the hand and began our downward climb. A very arduous business it proved to be, but somehow that evening we found ourselves at the bottom without accident.

The strangest twist of fate in our entire odyssey occurred not through grand confrontation, but through the quiet discovery of a humble shelter. I had pressed ahead of the others, trudging along the dry bed of the stream that fed the oasis, watching it vanish into the thirsty sands. Suddenly, I halted, shielding my eyes against the glare. There, nestled beneath the canopy of a fig tree and facing the water, stood a modest hut. Constructed of grass and reeds in the local style, yet featuring a proper door rather than a mere hole, it seemed an impossibility in such a desolate place.

"What on earth could a dwelling be doing here?" I muttered to myself.

Before I could process the absurdity, the door swung open. Stepping out was a white man, limping heavily, clad in animal skins and sporting a massive black beard. The sight was so incongruous that I momentarily feared the sun had played tricks on my mind. Yet, there he was. No hunter would venture this far, let alone settle here. My companion paused, staring in disbelief, just as Sir Henry and Good approached.

"Look here, you fellows," I shouted, "is that a white man, or have I finally gone mad?"

Sir Henry and Good exchanged glances, their eyes widening. Then, the lame stranger let out a piercing cry and began hobbling toward us. Before he reached us, he collapsed, overcome by emotion. With a bound, Sir Henry was at his side.

"Great Powers!" he gasped. "It is my brother, George!"

The commotion drew another figure from the hut—a man armed with a gun. Upon spotting me, he cried out, "Macumazahn! Don't you recognize me, Baas? I am Jim, the hunter. I lost the note you entrusted to me for the Baas, and we have been trapped here for nearly two years." He fell at my feet, rolling in tears of joy.

"You careless scoundrel!" I chided, though my heart swelled with relief. "You deserve a severe hiding!"

Meanwhile, the bearded man had recovered, rising to embrace Sir Henry. Whatever bitter history they shared—likely involving a woman, though I never pressed the matter—was instantly washed away by the miracle of reunion.

"My dear old fellow," Sir Henry exclaimed, his voice thick with emotion, "I thought you dead. I traversed Solomon's Mountains searching for you, having long abandoned all hope. Now I find you perched in the desert like a vulture."

"I attempted to cross those mountains nearly two years ago," George replied, his voice trembling from disuse. "But a boulder fell upon my leg, crushing it. I cannot move forward or backward."

I stepped forward. "How do you do, Mr. Neville? Do you remember me?"

"Why," he stammered, clutching his head, "isn't it Hunter Quatermain, and Good? Hold on, I feel dizzy. It is all so strange, yet, for a man who has lost hope, so incredibly happy!"

That evening, by the campfire, George recounted his ordeal. Less than two years prior, he had left Sitanda's Kraal aiming for Suliman's Berg. The note I had sent via Jim had been lost, leaving him unaware of our progress. Acting on native intelligence, he had taken a different route down the mountain's ladder-like descent, a path superior to Dom Silvestra's old map. Hardships plagued him and Jim in the desert, but they eventually reached this oasis. Disaster struck on their arrival: while Jim harvested honey from a bee nest atop a bank, he dislodged a massive boulder that crushed George's right leg. Trapped between the certainty of death in the desert and the risk of starvation, they chose to remain. They survived by hunting game attracted to the water at night and trapping it in pits, using the meat for food and the hides for clothing.

"We lived like Robinson Crusoe and Friday," George concluded, his voice weary but grateful. "We hoped for rescue from passing natives, but none came. Last night, Jim decided to leave me to seek aid at Sitanda's Kraal. He was to depart tomorrow, and I had little hope of seeing him return. Then, you appeared—of all people, you whom I thought had forgotten me entirely, living comfortably in England. It is the most wonderful and merciful thing that has ever happened to me."

Sir Henry spent the night recounting our adventures. When I presented some of the diamonds, George remarked, "Well, at least you have gained something for your pains, besides my worthless self."

Sir Henry laughed. "They belong to Quatermain and Good. That was the agreement regarding any spoils."

This sparked a reflection in me. After consulting Good, I proposed to Sir Henry that he accept a third share of the diamonds, or alternatively, that his portion be given to his brother, who had suffered the most. We persuaded him to agree, though George remained unaware of this arrangement until later.

At this juncture, I believe my history should pause. The return journey to Sitanda's Kraal was grueling, particularly supporting George, whose leg frequently shattered under the strain. Yet, we succeeded. Six months later, having retrieved our gear at Sitanda's—where the old keeper was furious to see us alive—we returned safely to my estate on the Berea near Durban. Here, I write this account.

As I finished the final word, a Kafir entered my avenue of orange trees, bearing a letter in a cleft stick from the post. It was from Sir Henry.
