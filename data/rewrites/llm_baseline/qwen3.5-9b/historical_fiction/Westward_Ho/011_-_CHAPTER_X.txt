It happened thus to Chaucer's knight in prison; it happened thus to Don Guzman; and it happened on this wise.

He settled down quietly enough at Bideford on his parole, occupying quarters far superior to those he had known for many a day. Taking things as they came, he behaved like a seasoned soldier of fortune until, after scarcely a month with Sir Richard Grenville, Old Salterne, the Mayor, arrived for supper.

Though Don Guzman was initially perplexed by our strange English custom of inviting burghers and low-born folk to dine "above the salt" alongside nobility, he possessed sufficient gentility to recognize that Sir Richard Grenville acted only with correctness and propriety. Shrugging off his initial surprise, the Spaniard submitted to eating and drinking at the same table as a tradesman who sat behind a ledger, managed apprentices, and conducted business. Hearing the man speak with Grenville wisely and without vulgarity, Don Guzman quickly condescended to converse with him. He found Salterne to be a prudent and courteous individual, fully aware of the Spaniard's superior rank, yet holding his own ground as a wise elder—a manner the Spaniard had previously observed only among the merchant princes of Genoa and Venice.

At the conclusion of supper, Salterne invited Grenville to return the following evening, then turned to the Don with frankness. He acknowledged that it would be a great condescension for a nobleman of Spain to sit at the board of a simple merchant, but promised that if the Spaniard deigned to accept, the fare would prove fitting for any rank. Glad for any diversion, Don Guzman graciously accepted the invitation, gaining an excellent supper and, had he chosen to drink, much good wine.

Mr. Salterne, a wise merchant eager for adventure abroad—as later proven by his efforts in settling Virginia—was equally eager to extract knowledge from guests suspected of knowing strange lands. First, he attempted to loosen the guest's tongue with generous sack; second, he posed prudent questions regarding the Spanish Main, Peru, the Moluccas, China, and the Indies. The first scheme failed, for the Spaniard was as abstemious as a monk, drinking little but water. The second met with limited success, for the Spaniard was as cunning as a fox, answering little but wind.

Into this verbal stalemate stepped the Rose of Torridge, looking as beautiful as ever. Hearing their topic, she artlessly added her own questions. Don Guzman could not refuse her, and without revealing vital commercial secrets, he provided his host and his host's daughter with a delightful evening.

Little Eros, though sometimes called the eldest of the gods, is reported by Burton in *The Anatomy of Melancholy* to be merely the child of idleness and full bellies. This slander colored Don Guzman's thoughts; being idle as a captive and well-fed thanks to Sir Richard's generous table, he sought amusement in love. Lady Grenville, being nearest, was considered first, but the Spaniard, a man of honor and mindful of his host, suppressed the idea with considerable self-praise. Regarding the sinfulness of such a pursuit, he cared little, as Southern Europeans of his era—and indeed many Englishmen later in the Stuart days—did. Having discarded Lady Grenville, he allowed Rose Salterne to enter his thoughts. Not with intent to wrong her, but partly for amusement and partly because he could not help it, he found an innocent freshness in her, fond as she was of admiration, utterly new and attractive to him.

"The train of the peacock," he mused, "yet the heart of the dove." Such a combination was so charming that, if he could persuade her to love no one else, he might foolishly follow suit. Panic-stricken by this thought, he resolved to stay away, yet the days dragged on. When Lady Grenville was at home, she spoke only of her husband; when she departed for Stow, leaving the Don alone in the great house, what choice did he have but to lounge in the butt-gardens, display his fine black cloak and feather, shoot, play rackets with the youth, bowl with the elders, and eventually receive invitations to supper from Mr. Salterne? There, naturally, he ruled the roast, commanding respect not only by rank but by possessing knowledge worth hearing.

Those were the dawn days of English commerce. Every merchant in Bideford and England burned with imagination regarding discoveries, companies, patents, and settlements. They rivaled the brave exploits of Sir Edward Osborne and his London Company of Turkey Merchants; celebrated the privileges granted by Sultan Murad Khan; honored the Levant voyages of Roger Bodenham, John Fox, Lawrence Aldersey, and John Rule; and dreamed of Mediterranean trade opened by the crushing of Venetian power at Famagusta and the alliance between Elizabeth and the Grand Turk.

Every word the Spaniard uttered about the Mediterranean took root in fertile soil. Furthermore, Master Edmund Hogan had successfully embassied to the Emperor of Morocco; John Hawkins and George Fenner had traded in Guinea for musk, civet, gold, and grain; and just two months prior, Captain Hare sailed from London in the *Minion* for Brazil, carrying Sheffield hardware and Devonshire cloth, aided by John Whithall, who had married a Spanish heiress and owned an ingenio and slaves in Santos. Meanwhile, Drake had proven the viability of plundering American coasts and establishing an East Indian trade; Frobisher and Davis were boring through northern ice seeking a passage to China; and Anthony Jenkinson had penetrated Russia, Persia, Armenia, and the Arctic wastes. Everywhere, under Queen Elizabeth's wise rule, English commerce spread its roots.

As Don Guzman conversed with his new friends, he realized they belonged to a race that must be extinguished if Spain were to remain the mistress of the world. It was insufficient for Spain to claim the New World, crush the Hollanders, degrade the Venetians, or absorb Portuguese trade; these fierce islanders remained to assert, with cunning policy and scripture—or failing that, with sharp shot and cold steel—the principle of free seas and free trade for all nations. He saw it, and his countrymen saw it too; therefore, the Spanish Armada came.

Don Guzman also knew, from hard experience, that these islanders were not mere merchants. Though they preferred profit to fighting, they could fight with terrifying doggedness when necessary. Their merchant ships were armed to the teeth, manned by crews trained from childhood to wield weapons without mercy against anyone daring to stop their trade. One evening, after politely hinting that Englishmen venturing where they had no right might be sent back, he was met with a volley of retorts: *"We'll see that, sir."* *"Depends on who says 'no right.'"* *"You found might makes right when you claimed the Indian seas; we may find right makes might when we try."*

*"Try them, then, gentlemen,"* Don Guzman replied, rising. *"Find the sacred flag of Spain as invincible as ever was the Roman eagle."*

*"You are insolent burghers!"* cried Don Guzman, storming out, calling himself an ass for demeaning himself to such company, forgetting he had provoked it.

Salterne, prompted by Mammon, approached him the next day, begging forgiveness and promising that no one involved in the previous rudeness would be invited again, provided the Don returned. Appeased, the Don visited the next evening, sneering at himself throughout the journey. *"Fool that I am! That girl has bewitched me,"* he thought. *"I must go and eat my share of dirt for her sake."*

There, he cunningly hinted to Salterne that he had developed a liking for the merchant and felt bound by his hospitality to reveal truths he would not share elsewhere. He explained that Spaniards were not jealous of single traders but of general attempts to strip them of wealth, noting that opportunities existed for individuals to enrich themselves. Shrewd as Salterne was, he had a weakness, and the Spaniard struck it. Delighted to learn the mysteries of the Spanish monopoly, the old merchant often encouraged Rose to draw the Don out, blinded by greed and convinced that religious difference and family pride would keep them apart forever. He assumed that love without marriage was absurd: impossible for Rose due to her virtue, and impossible for the Don due to his natural fondness for preserving his own life.

Thus, for weeks and months, the merchant's house became the Don's favorite haunt. He saw Rose daily, and she listened to him. She had never seen such a man. He possessed the high-bred grace of Frank Leigh but in a manlier mold; he had just enough proud self-assertion to make women bow before him, tempered with enough tact to avoid the boastfulness often attributed to Spaniards. He recounted marvels of flood and field, surpassing even Amyas in number and delivered with an eloquence Amyas lacked. Being present while the Leighs were not, and surrounded by a countryside stripped of young gallants by the Netherlands and Irish wars, Rose and others found themselves amused only by him.
