The moment Lady Castlewood, her son Will, and daughter Fanny stepped through one saloon door to make their exit, the Earl of Castlewood glided out by the other, leaving only Harry Warrington and Lady Maria alone in the room. The demure eyes that had been fixed so diligently on the tambour frame, pretending to trace every last petal of the innocent violets and jonquils stitched there, lifted at once to find Harry. He stood beneath an ancestral portrait hung over the great stone fireplace, his honest countenance flooded with a blush so deep it spread across his cheeks, his forehead, and even the tips of his youthful ears, as though he had gathered every last bloom of that rare, late-blossoming flower that almost never survives the spring of polite society, and piled them all across his face.

“Why did you refuse to go with our aunt, cousin?” Maria asked.

“Because your ladyship bade me stay,” the lad answered simply.

“La, child! What a fool you are to take idle fun for earnest!” she laughed. “Are all Virginian gentlemen so obsequious that every light word a lady speaks counts as a royal command? Our sex must be worshipped like queens in your Virginia, if that is the case!”

“You said it—two nights ago, when we walked the terrace, O heaven!” Harry’s voice trembled so thick with emotion he could barely get the words out.

“Ah, that sweet night, cousin!” she sighed, feigning soft dreaminess.

“Whe—whe—when you plucked this rose from your own bodice and gave it to me!” Harry burst out, yanking a crumpled, half-dead faded bloom from his waistcoat pocket. “I will never part with it, never, by all that is holy, not as long as this heart beats! You told me then: ‘Harry, if your aunt asks you to go away, you will go, and if you go, you will forget me.’ Did you not say that?”

“All men forget, sooner or later,” the spinster murmured, heaving a melodramatic little sigh.

“Maybe in this cold, selfish old country—but not in ours!” Harry cried, glowing with all the fire of his colonial upbringing. “I almost hated turning that kind old lady down, it went right to my heart to say no to her, when she has been nothing but goodness to me, and it was she who first brought me to meet—to meet—oh heaven!” He gave the spaniel dozing before the fire a sharp kick that sent it yelping, and strode rapidly closer to her tambour frame. “Look here, cousin! If you bade me jump out that yonder window, I’d do it. If you bade me strike down a man, I’d do it.”

“La, there is no need to squeeze my hand so hard, you silly child!” Maria protested, though she did not pull her fingers away.

“I can’t help it! That is how we are in the south. When my heart speaks, my mouth cannot hold its peace—and you know exactly where my heart is! Ever since that night, I have hardly slept a wink. I want to do something great, something to distinguish myself. I wish there were giants like in the storybooks I read as a boy, so I could go and fight them for you. I wish you were in distress, so I could rescue you. I wish you needed my blood, so I could pour every last drop of it out for you. And when you told me not to go away with Madame Bernstein—”

“I never told you that, child!”

“I thought you did! You said I preferred my aunt to my cousin, and I told you then what I say now: incomparable Maria, I prefer you to every woman alive on this earth and every angel in Paradise! I would march straight to the dungeons if you gave the word. And do you think I would ever leave this place when all you wish for is that I stay near you?” He paused, breathless.

“Men always talk that way, or so I have heard,” she corrected herself, tossing her head a little. “A country-bred woman like me has no business knowing the ways of you creatures, after all. They say you are all raptures and flames and grand promises when you are at our sides—but the moment you step over the threshold, you forget every last word.”

“But I never want to go away, not for as long as I live!” Harry groaned. “I have grown tired of all the silly things that used to amuse me as a boy: games, sports, even the soldiering I once ached for so badly I tore my hair in rage when my brother went off on that fatal expedition instead of me. Now there is only one thing I care for in all the world, and you know what that is.”

“You silly child! Do you not realise I am almost old enough to be—”

“I know! I know what they say!” he cut her off. “Some of your family showed me the great family Bible, where every one of your names and all your birth dates are written down.”

“The cowards! Who dared do that?” Lady Maria shrieked, flying into a sudden fury. “Was it that grasping, odious, brazen harpy my stepmother? Do you know how she trapped my father into marrying her when he was deep in his cups, the horrid hussey? The shameless—”

“It was not Lady Castlewood, I promise you,” Harry interposed, bewildered.

“Then it was my aunt, that supposed moralist!” she raged on. “A bishop’s widow, forsooth! I dare say she’d have intrigued with the Pretender, the Hanoverian court, the Court of Rome, and the Sultan of Turkey too if she’d had the means! Do you know who her second husband was? That low creature who—”

“But our aunt never said a single ill word against you,” Harry broke in, more and more amazed at the ferocity of her temper.

She checked herself at once, reading the faint alarm on his innocent, inquisitive face. She did not want him to see her as a shrew. “Well, well, I am a fool,” she said, softening at once. “I only want you to think well of me, Harry.”

Her hand slipped into his of its own accord, and the rapturous youth seized it to press a dozen fervent kisses to it. “Angel!” he cried, gazing into her face with his bright, honest eyes. The old, experienced eyes he looked into glowed warmer than two fish pools lit by starlight, though Harry, for all his fire, never suspected that the calm, shining blue depths he thought held all of heaven were no more than the treacherous surface of a pond, where that silly old fable dog saw his own beef bone reflected, snapped at the water, and lost the very meat he was carrying in his jaws in the first place. What wreckage lies hidden under so many of those quiet, smiling surfaces! What dropped treasures, chased golden goblets, jewels of wasted love, piles of bones and bits of the very sweetest heart’s flesh have sunk there, never to rise again? Beware, ye foolish hounds, how you lap at that water! The mischievous siren nymphs never rest until they have dragged some poor young fellow down under the waves: they sing after him, dance after him, twist their glittering limbs around him, whisper soft secrets in his ear, kiss his feet, leer at him from the rushes, calling “Come, sweet youth! Hither, rosy Hylas!” And pop goes Hylas, pulled under forever. The siren no more remembers him than a Brighton fisherman remembers one individual herring out of a hundred thousand he hauls in his net.

Old Ulysses and his weathered crew would not have cared a fig even if a whole shoal of sirens were singing on the bank, combing their long false hair. But young Telemachus—our Harry Warrington—was ready to leap overboard at once. The song he hears is not stale to him, the voice that sings it is not cracked or out of tune. You and I, brother boatswain, who first heard Mozart’s *Cosi fan tutti* when we were young, may laugh now at how trite the whole old story is—but for the boy standing here, all is new, all is magic.

Any soul with half a penny’s worth of sense or personal experience would know exactly what comes next. The lad pulls out his crumpled dead rose, kisses it a dozen times, and treasures it as the most precious relic on earth. No need to trace every last tedious little step of how that rose came to his waistcoat: the way Maria happened to meet him on the garden path at dawn, the way they whispered behind the old curtain, the way she went to read to sick old Goody Jenkins in the village and let Harry carry her basket of calves’ foot jelly. Love stories are all the same, after all, as old as the lanes that generations of lovers have walked up and down before us. They will play out the same for Miss Fanny with her doll, for the governess, for the baby bawling for his bottle, as they did for Harry and Maria.

Maria had no mind to quarrel with her aunt or let Harry run off and forget her, after all. Harry was not yet the master of his Virginian estates, only the heir apparent, and who knew what might happen? His lordship her brother and she had never spoken a single word about their little scheme—but they understood each other perfectly, no words needed. The Earl knew his sister’s tricks, her faults, every twist of her character, after a lifetime of living under the same roof. He had been quietly nudging Harry to spend all his days riding the estate farms, visiting the sick tenants, sitting in his justice court, keeping him safely occupied and rooted at Castlewood, no chance to run off with the Baroness Bernstein.

Three days later, Harry sat down to write a letter back home:
> MY DEAR MOUNTAIN—At first I did not like Castlewood, nor my cousins here, very much. But now I am used to their ways, and we understand each other much better. Give my duty to my mother, and tell her I hope, given her ladyship’s great kindness to me, Madam Esmond will be reconciled to her half-sister the Baroness de Bernstein. The Baroness, you know, was my Grandmamma’s daughter by her first husband Lord Castlewood… She first married the Reverend Mr. Tusher, a learned and good man who was such a favourite of the King that he was made Bishop. When he died, the King kept his friendship for my aunt, who then married a Hanoverian nobleman at Court who left her very rich. My cousin the Earl of Castlewood told me all this, and I have found nothing but the greatest kindness and affection from them all.
> The Dowager Countess and my cousins Will and Lady Fanny are just as we described them in my last letter that went by the Falmouth packet on the 20th ult. Will and I are very good friends. We have ridden out together many a time, and had some famous cocking matches at Hampton and Winton. He is a sharp blade, but I think I showed him that we Virginians know a trick or two. Reverend Mr. Sampson, the family chaplain, is a most excellent preacher, with no bigotry at all.
> The kindness of my cousin the Earl grows every day, and I hope by next year’s ship my mother will send his lordship some of our best roll tobacco for his tenants, and some fine hams. He is most charitable to the poor, and his sister Lady Maria is every bit as good. She sits for hours reading good books to the sick, and is much beloved in the village. There is a very sick old woman there, Gammer Jenkins, who Madam Esmond would like very much, a most religious good soul. Lady Maria visits her constantly to read to her, which she says gives the old woman great comfort. Though her Ladyship has the sweetest voice, both when speaking and singing (she plays the church organ and sings there most beautifully), I do not think Gammer Jenkins can hear a word of it, for she is very deaf in her great old age. But her memory is perfectly sharp, and she remembers my honoured Grandmother Rachel, Lady Castlewood, living here. She says my grandmother was the best woman in all the world, gave her a cow on her wedding day, and cured her husband Gaffer Jenkins of the terrible winter colds he used to suffer from. I suppose that was with the pills and drops my mother packed in my boxes when I left Virginia. I have not been ill once since arriving, so I never used them, though Gumbo has made great use of them, eating and drinking far too much in the servants’ hall. Gammer Jenkins says the next best soul after my grandmother is Lady Maria, who remembers my grandfather and grandmother when they lived here as children. Their portrait hangs in the very rooms I am staying in, rooms Lord Castlewood says will one day be mine.
> Give my best love and duty to my honoured mother, my respects to Mr. Dempster, a kiss to little Fanny, and my kind remembrances to Old Gumbo, Nathan, Old and Young Dinah, the pointer dog and Slut, and all our friends.
> From your well-wisher,
> P.S. I have written to my Uncle Warrington in Norfolk to give him my respects, but have had no answer yet.

Harry handed the precious manuscript to Maria to read when he was done. “Nonsense!” she laughed, “Why do you flatter me so, cousin?”

“You are beloved in the village, and far outside it too,” Harry said, with a soft, knowing emphasis. “And I have flattered you a little more still, just a little further on.”

“I hope the spelling is all right, cousin?” he asked, when she had finished reading.
“’Tis good enough spelling for any person of fashion,” she answered, not caring to be questioned too closely on her own orthography.
“I know I spelled the word ‘angel’ wrong in my last letter to my mamma,” he said. “But I have learned how to spell it perfectly correctly now.”
“And how is that, sir?” she asked, smiling.
“By looking at you, cousin.” He bowed low before her, his face flooding with that familiar, warm blush, as if he laid a whole bouquet of the finest summer roses at her feet with the gesture.
