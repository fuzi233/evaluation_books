The chamber was opulent, its floor strewn with the striped and spotted pelts of exotic beasts. A woman sat rigid, her arms outstretched, fingers half-curled into fists. Her face was a tempest—flushed one moment, pallid the next—and her foot tapped incessantly against the rich furs beneath her.

A servant drew aside the heavy curtain, and the lady’s cheeks darkened with fresh color.

*"Altezza,"* the maid whispered, eyes downcast, *"the man is here."*

The lady snatched up a black mask and pressed it to her face. In an instant, the flush vanished, leaving only the stark contrast of the dark silk against her marble skin—a sight both striking and unsettling.

The man who entered was unremarkable in every way—neither handsome nor repulsive, his clothes plain but respectable. Only his eyes betrayed him, darting ceaselessly, like a creature accustomed to shadows. He bowed deeply, repeatedly, then stood waiting.

*"They have told you why you are summoned?"* the lady asked.

Her brow furrowed. *"Explain."*

*"The lesser vendetta is death alone. We take a man as he leaves a church, or in an unguarded hour. The greater—"* he paused, *"—we wait until he is fresh from sin, so his soul is damned as well as his body. Though, in truth, the fashion for such work has waned of late."*

The assassin exhaled, relieved. *"Then the deed is simple. Yet I must know the man—his name, his dwelling."*

For the first time, the killer showed unease. He urged caution, precision.

*"Do not fear,"* she replied coldly. *"Listen well. He is young, tall, with auburn hair and dark blue eyes—a face of honesty, though it lies. He lodges at the glover’s house on the Via Claudia, the corner dwelling. There are but three men within: himself, a dark and stunted painter, and a slender boy. The one who betrayed me is a stranger, fair, and taller than you."*

The bravo absorbed every word. *"It is enough."*

*"Wait,"* she commanded. *"He haunts the Tiber after dusk—no doubt to meet his whore, who dares name me her rival. Strike him there. Let her find him cold upon the bank."*

The assassin displayed a steel gauntlet. *"Our strikes require such force, we must guard our hands. This is our hammer."* He parted his doublet, revealing a glint of mail beneath, then laid a stiletto upon the table. Its edge gleamed.

The lady recoiled—then, with sudden fascination, took the blade in her pale fingers, testing its point against her skin.

*"Beware, madam,"* he warned.

*"His flesh is as fair as mine,"* she murmured, a flicker of pity crossing her face—gone as swiftly as it came. *"But his heart is black as pitch. Tell me—do I not act justly, removing a slanderer, a traitor?"*

She barely heard him. *"I could have loved him—to the death. As now I hate him. Fool! To scorn a princess, then grovel before the dregs of the city, making me a mockery!"* Her gaze snapped up. *"Why do you linger? Go. Be quick."*

*"Ah. I had forgotten."* She pushed a heavy purse across the table. *"Here is more than half. Return for the rest when the deed is done."*

*"You will see me soon, Signora."* He bowed himself out, leaving her alone.

The princess sat motionless, the mask still shielding her face, her nails digging into the wood. Jealousy, wounded pride, and fear of scandal burned within her—for until Gerard, no stain had touched her name. Now she was fury incarnate, a tigress poised to strike.

Yet as the assassin’s footsteps faded, a chill crept over her. A shadow of doubt. A whisper of regret.

Gerard and Margaret were souls out of time. But the princess was the very essence of her age—proud, passionate, vengeful, reckless. Power was her curse. Left to her own devices, her nerves might have tempered her fury, her crimes mere scratches. But power lent her the hands of men, and in those hands, her whims became deadly.

And so, as one lost soul contemplated ending his own life, another’s gold sharpened a dagger for him.
