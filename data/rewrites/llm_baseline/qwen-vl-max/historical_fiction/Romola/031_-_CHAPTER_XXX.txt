CHAPTER XXX. The Avenger’s Secret.

It was the first time since his escape that Baldassarre had set foot in the Piazza del Duomo. He longed to hear the remarkable monk preach again—his voice, once so powerful and resonant, now seemed like a distant echo of what it once was. But the memory of how he had been seen there before—the half-naked, disheveled man with rope around his neck, hair unkempt, face gaunt—made him hesitate. That same square where he had been called a madman by Tito’s lips still burned in his mind. Not merely because of the cruelty of those words, but because they had struck true: *“Some madman, surely.”* And at that moment, the truth had pierced him—not just the insult, but the fear that he might not be able to prove otherwise.

He was not mad. Yet within him lay the bitter knowledge of a mind broken, faculties frayed, thoughts slipping away like water through fingers. He felt the weight of his own weakness as keenly as the fire of vengeance that consumed him. The desire for retribution raged inside him, but alongside it came a trembling caution—a hesitation born not of cowardice, but of dread. It was as if Tito had been guided by some unseen force, whispering secrets only the deepest wounds could reveal. And the worst part? Baldassarre knew he could not defend himself against such accusations. His reason was intact enough to know its own ruin.

This self-awareness had shaped his every move since his return. When Piero di Cosimo offered kindness, he turned away. At the hospital, he gave only sparse answers: he had been taken prisoner by the French on his way from Genoa. His age, the dignity in his bearing, the quiet precision of his speech—these marked him apart from the common beggars and travelers who passed through. The monks, recognizing this, gave him more than mere alms: a coarse woolen tunic, peasant shoes, and a few *danari*, the smallest coins in Florence. He left early the next morning, heading toward Arezzo. But before reaching the city, he stopped at a small village and spent two of his coins to be shaved and have his hair cut short—back to the style he once wore.

The barber held a small hand-mirror made of polished steel. Baldassarre hesitated. It had been years since he had looked upon his own face. As he stared into the mirror, a sudden thought gripped him: *Could Tito no longer recognize me?* The idea struck like a blow. His hand trembled. He asked the barber to hold the mirror steady.

No. He was not changed beyond recognition. The wrinkles were deeper, yes—but they were the same ones he remembered from three years ago. The skin remained rough, pitted like ancient stone, the yellow pallor of illness now fixed beneath the surface. The beard—shaggy, white—would not fool anyone who had known him for sixteen years. Anyone who had searched for him would expect change, as one searches for a lost loved one among the wreckage of a flood. But something else had altered: his eyes. They held a new intensity, a sharpness that might make recognition all the more jarring—like hearing a familiar voice cry out in pain.

Yet the doubt was fleeting. He knew, deep down, that Tito had recognized him. He pushed the mirror aside. The currents of rage surged forward again, stronger than ever.

He retraced his steps toward Florence, but not yet ready to enter the city. He veered off the main road and sat beside a quiet pool shaded by alder bushes still clinging to their yellow leaves. It was a calm November day. The water was still, like glass. He saw in it an image—his own—and bent closer, drawn by a need to examine himself slowly, without interruption.

Was there madness in his face? Was there emptiness behind the eyes?

Not now. Not when he looked with purpose. Now, his gaze was focused, determined. But what of other times? When the past slipped away like smoke, when whispers faded before he could grasp them, when shapes approached and dissolved under his outstretched hand? Then, surely, confusion must show. A blankness, like a man suddenly blind.

And worse—when the fog lifted for a moment, and hope flared, only to vanish again, leaving him helpless? Then, perhaps, his face reflected the horror of sudden loss.

Could he prove anything? Could he speak coherently, trace a thought from beginning to end? Would anyone believe he had once possessed a mind rich with learning, fluent in tongues, alive with ideas? All that was gone—or was it?

He clung to a fragile hope: that the knowledge was not truly lost, but imprisoned. Like water trapped in a cracked vessel, waiting for the breach to widen. Since the day he first walked weakly from his straw bed, his mind had shifted—some changes slow, others sudden. His body had regained strength; his will had returned. Memories tied to emotion had come back vividly. But the intellectual world—those intricate patterns of thought—remained fragmented.

Yet once or twice, during moments of intense feeling, he had glimpsed his former self. For a flash, he saw Greek script and understood it. He felt his mind move freely, unburdened. Then darkness fell again—deeper, darker. But could that moment return? Last longer?

If it did—if it lasted long enough—he could devise a revenge worthy of his suffering. Not a quick death, but a torment—deliberate, cruel, lasting—such as only a mind could inflict.

He straightened up, arms crossed, forcing his thoughts into order. He needed patience. Knowledge. Opportunity. And food. He could not live on anger alone. He dreaded being seen as a helpless old man, a fool. No one must know half his memory was gone. The strength might return. If only for a little while—that might be enough.

He already knew how to begin gathering information about Tito. The name “Bratti Ferravecchi” echoed in his mind constantly. He had learned from a man in Genoa—whose finger bore Tito’s onyx ring—that the ring had been bought in Florence from a young Greek man, well-dressed, dark-haired, at the shop of a rag-merchant named Bratti Ferravecchi, in the street of the same name.

That revelation had shaken him. Until then, he had clung to the belief that Tito was searching for him. That he had not abandoned him willingly. He had told himself: *My letter never reached him. That is why I am still in Antioch.* He believed Tito was looking, that he would find him. When he was taken to Corinth, he convinced his captors that ransom would come, ensuring inquiries would be made. He hoped: *Here, finally, he will find me.*

But then came the sickness. When he recovered, he was broken—body and mind shattered. Useless to his owners. They let a Genoese merchant take him aboard a galley, moved by pity. During months at sea, he regained strength, but upon landing in Genoa, he felt only desolation. He almost wished he had died at Corinth.

Yet one thought held him back: Tito might still be alive—imprisoned, poor, forgotten. If so, there was still a chance. A faint, distant hope that he might see his son again—his child, his beloved boy—once more.

Then he met the stranger with the ring. Though he could not describe it beforehand, the sight of it stirred something deep within. He recognized it. And the realization crushed him: Tito, nearly a year after their separation, lived in prosperity in Florence—selling the very gem meant to be kept until the last resort.

Baldassarre refused to understand it. He preferred to be stunned, confused, rather than confront the truth. He allowed himself only joy: *I shall see Tito again.* Perhaps he thought his father dead. The mystery would be cleared.

Now, standing by the pool, he whispered: *I am not alone. I shall never be alone. My revenge is with me.*

He bent again to look at his reflection—not out of vanity, but as an instrument of vengeance. The face in the water was not his own, but the vessel of a wrath that had become his life. He saw in it not a man, but a weapon.

“I was a fool,” he said aloud, though no one heard. “I loved a woman once. I believed she could care for me. Then I took a helpless child and raised him. I watched him grow—wondering if he would care for me, even a little. If he would feel sorrow when I suffered. I would have torn open my chest to warm him with my blood, just to see him grieve.”

He clenched his fists. “Curses on him! Let me see him lie with those red lips turned white as ash. Let him look to me for mercy—and see only triumph in my eyes. This world is a lie. There is no goodness but in hate. Fool! You strained for love and found none. But hatred—hatred has depth. Hatred has power. I have memory for it. I have strength in my arm. In my will. And if I can do nothing but kill him—”

But the thought of mere killing repelled him. He wanted more. An eternal torment. To stand forever beside a soul that screamed in agony, knowing it was his doing. But the real desire was earthly—slow, visible, shared under the same sky where he had once fainted with despair.

Yet as soon as he tried to plan, the sense of weakness returned—like frost creeping into his bones. This body, meant to carry out a sublime revenge, must be fed, clothed, preserved. If he waited, he must work. Humble work. He had no skill. Could he try copying manuscripts? Might seeing letters stir his mind? Perhaps. But he dared not risk his hands or brain.

So he chose labor—simple, beast-like work. Porters were needed in Florence. He could carry burdens. His limbs had regained some of their old strength.

But then a new idea came. Around his neck hung a tiny parchment bag, blackened with age, tied by a cord. He had worn it since childhood—a charm given by his mother. He had never opened it. Piety forbade it. Once, he might have thought it contained only a scroll. But now, driven by a thirst fiercer than any hunger, he pulled it free.

With trembling fingers, he tore it open.

There, nestled in the brittle parchment, was a sapphire—small, but brilliant as the Mediterranean sea. Engraved. Worth gold.

He gasped. The ducats flashed before him—not just gold, but a dagger. Not to use yet—but to possess. To hold in his hand, to test its edge. A weapon that did not require strength or skill—only will. A tool for justice too feeble to fight alone.

A spark lit behind his dark brows. He carefully wrapped the sapphire back in the parchment and tied it tight.

Dusk fell. He rose and walked toward Florence. With his *danari*, he could buy bread. He could sleep outdoors—many did in Florence’s corners and alleys.

In the days that followed, he sold the sapphire. He added to his clothes. He bought a bright dagger—from Bratti’s shop. That evening, he visited the rag-merchant, pretending to bargain for the blade. He casually mentioned he came from Genoa, where an acquaintance had bought a valuable ring from Bratti. Did he have more?

Bratti, eager to impress, boasted of his connections, his wisdom, his rare dealings. He spoke of a young signor—handsome, once poor, now prosperous—who had first appeared sleeping under the Loggia de’ Cerchi. Through a few questions, Baldassarre learned Tito’s story: his sale of jewels, his quiet settlement in Florence, his marriage, his wealth.

But one thing remained unknown: what tale Tito had told about his father?

Baldassarre could not yet uncover that. But he began to learn Florence. Yet each new detail slipped from his grasp unless repeated endlessly. He feared listening to anything new—afraid it would erase what he had already fought to remember.

On the day he was seen by Tito in the Piazza del Duomo, that anguish gnawed at him. Tito’s smooth words sounded like mockery, like the taunts of a demon.

Walking home through the Via del Garbo, he paused before a bookseller’s stall. The open volumes beckoned. Could he stare long enough to grasp a thread of memory? Could he anchor himself in the written word?

He bought the cheapest Greek book he could find.

Back in his outhouse—straw piled high, light dim—he sat and studied the pages. But the letters remained meaningless. The darkness deepened. Still, his eyes lingered on the white sheets, as if hoping to see meaning where none existed.
