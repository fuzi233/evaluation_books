In Atlantis, when Phorenice spoke, her will moved faster than a wildfire through dry grass. The empress had long since decided the whole of her realm, every soul, every stone, every coin, existed solely to serve her whims, and no cost, no toil, no number of shattered lives was too great to see those whims fulfilled.

Her edict that Nais be buried alive beneath the stone throne Deucalion was to build went out at dawn, and before the sun cleared the palace pyramids, every mason, stonecutter, labourer, sculptor, engineer and architect in the city had dropped whatever work they held, summoned by heralds blaring the command through every ward. They seized every material they wanted, no payment, no negotiation, and tore down whole streets of buildings to haul the monstrous multi-ton stone blocks they’d selected to the open square before the royal pyramid, even as rebel war rams thudded unceasingly against the outer walls. The city’s craft guilds, boosted by Phorenice’s clever new lifting machines, could not heft the god-sized blocks of the ancient Sun Temple, but they handled weights of twenty men apiece as easily as a child moves a pebble.

The throne rose in the square through the night, lit by guttering torches. Seven tiers of stone formed the base, each knee-high, three steps cut into their front faces, and a hollow cavity carved into the topmost tier to hold Nais’s living body. Suspended high above that cavity, held steady by massive wooden lifting rams, hung the throne’s massive seat block, carved all around with the coiled shape of Atlantis’s royal cobra, its great hooded head looming over where Phorenice would sit. Relays of workers sweated through the dark, whips cracking over their backs, men fainting dead from exhaustion only to be dragged aside so the next labourer could take their place. No delay was tolerated when the empress had given her word. By sunrise, the last scaffolding and stone chippings were cleared away, along with the half-dozen workmen who’d died from falls or overexertion, and the full crowd of thousands had begun to pour into the square to gawk at the spectacle.

It was the cool of the morning when chamberlains summoned Deucalion out of the royal pyramid. Great nobles paraded in with their trumpeters and banner bearers, commoners wore their finest silk and coloured turbans, even starved rag-wrapped paupers had twisted scraps of bright cloth around their heads to mark the supposed “festivity” of the day. The trumpets blared his arrival, the crowd roared a welcome, and he walked stiff-backed to the scarlet silk awning set up for the guests, his face an impassive mask of iron.

Then Phorenice emerged, carried on an open litter of gold and ivory by brutish, tattooed savages imported from northern Europe, her sharp, glowing beauty made all the more striking by their ugly, feral faces. A collective gasp rippled through the crowd, every man there seething with envy that Deucalion, the warrior prince, had won her as his bride that very day. No one, save perhaps the hidden, veiled handmaiden Ylga, had the faintest clue the man beneath the mask was half-dead with grief. Deucalion had drilled his expression to total stillness: no shaking hands, no twitching cheek, no flicker of pain that would tip Phorenice off to their secret. He would carry his beloved Nais down into that dark hollow as calmly as a priest laying a stranger to rest, and she would play her part too, because for both of them, nothing came before the safety of Atlantis.

There was no cheap mockery in the ceremony, none of Phorenice’s usual frivolity. She’d ordered the full, rigid, sombre ritual of old Atlantis, and the crowd fell quiet as she was carried to the awning, Deucalion stepping forward to help her down to the cushions. She twined her fingers with his, the traditional pose for Atlantis’s rulers and their consorts, as a squad of soldiers marched forward to reveal Nais in their centre. A heavy iron collar locked around her neck, two guards holding the chains tight to make sure she could not throw herself on the spears of the escort to escape the slow, lingering death that had been sentenced to her. To Deucalion’s quiet, profound gratitude, she was still dressed in her fine linen robes – condemned prisoners normally had their clothes seized as plunder by their guards, and he knew without a doubt Ylga had paid them off to spare her that last humiliation.

She walked calmly to the edge of the awning’s shade, bowed low before Phorenice without being forced, her face pale but unflinching, no trace of fear or defiance in her steady eyes.

“Nais,” Phorenice purred, “you once ate from my platter, drank from my cup, I gave you every good thing you ever had. You should have known better than to steal what I have set aside for myself. I offered you pardon, once, on easy terms, and you rejected it. But today is my wedding day, and I feel generous. Kneel, tell me you are sorry, name any man in the city you wish to marry, and you shall go free, wed before we rise from these cushions.”

“I will not wed,” Nais said, her voice soft and unshaking.

“Think one last time of the alternative,” Phorenice said, her tone turning sharp. “You will be laid warm, alive, beautiful as you stand now, in that hollow beneath the throne. Deucalion, my new husband, will place you there as a symbol that all my enemies shall share your fate. Then he will release the rams, lower that granite block, and no eye in Atlantis will ever see your face again. Breathe the sweet air, look at the sun, girl. It does not have to end this way.”

“I will not wed,” Nais said again.

Phorenice nodded, pulling her fingers from Deucalion’s arm. “Show her I do not jest, Deucalion.”

“The Empress does not jest,” he called out loud for the crowd to hear. “This is the fate of all who defy her. Lead the prisoner to the throne.”

The guards turned, chains taut to drag Nais up the steps, but she walked willingly, her colour never shifting, and Deucalion followed behind her, ten thousand eyes fixed on his every movement. He reached the top step, ordered the guards to unclip her collar, then wrapped his arms around her roughly, as if he was restraining a struggling, violent captive. A surge of raw, overwhelming love and agony crashed through him so hard he nearly fainted, and it was only every scrap of the iron will he’d trained for battle that kept his grip firm, his face cold. He half-dragged her across the stone platform to the open hollow, and she did not fight him even once. Brass clamps had been set into the stone around the cavity to lock down over the opening, so no prisoner could wriggle free and escape by being crushed instantly under the falling block. He slammed the clamps home one by one, his other hand pressed hard to her throat hard enough to leave purple, obvious bruise marks on her pale skin, to sell the illusion of brutality. Sweat dripped off his chin onto the thirsty granite, and when he finally pulled his hand away, Nais never met his eye. She stared straight up at the massive stone block hanging above her, unblinking, and he turned and walked away before he could break.

He gave the signal to the engineers manning the rams. The heated metal in their mechanisms cooled, contracted, and the ton-weight throne block began to creep slowly, so slowly, down toward its bed. Deucalion stood there, face impassive, watching the work like any architect supervising his project, until the block thudded home, sealed tight, and the engineers dragged the rams away. He walked a circuit with the chief architect and his plumb line and level to confirm the stone sat perfectly in place, the useless, punctilious ritual of the masons’ guild, his chest hollow as if half his own flesh had been left trapped in the dark with Nais.

He walked down the steps to report to Phorenice that her order was fulfilled, following the ancient formal words of the ceremony.

“Then we are wed,” she said, grinning, rising to her feet. “We do not need priests, not for us. No profane hand will officiate for the Empress of Atlantis and her consort. We have the whole of this crowd for witnesses, and later we will cut our union deep into the face of this throne, letter it in gold, so it will shine for ten thousand years.”

Deucalion took her outstretched hand, helped her stand, and they stepped out into the bright sun, Phorenice leaning against his arm. The crowd roared, screaming that she was a living goddess, and no one spared a cheer for Deucalion, whose grim, set face scared and repelled them. They climbed the steps to the throne, Deucalion seating her under the shadow of the cobra’s hood, and she pulled him down to sit beside her. The crowd fell dead silent as she raised her jewelled hand.

“Hear me, my people, hear me the old High Gods of Atlantis!” she proclaimed. “I take Deucalion as my husband, to rule with me, to guard our great realm. May every last enemy of mine perish, as the one beneath us has perished!” She wrapped her arms around his neck, and kissed him hard on the mouth.

Deucalion spoke his own lines by rote, as the old rites demanded, and kissed her back, the first woman’s lips he had ever touched, when all the while his heart lay buried under the granite at his feet. Then came the blood rite: Phorenice pricked his forearm with her dagger, lapped the bead of blood that welled up, and Deucalion pricked the soft white skin of her shoulder and drank her blood in turn. The metallic taste clung to his dry, parched throat, and he thought he would choke. Phorenice laughed, glowing, drunk on the thrill of their union, gushing that she could feel his strength flowing into her, that they were bound now, no force in the world could tear them apart. Deucalion could not bring himself to speak soft, false lover’s words, and mumbled a hasty excuse that he was a warrior unused to pretty speeches, that his heart spoke too loud to put its feelings to words.

The gods spared him the need to pretend any longer. A wounded soldier stumbled screaming into the square, an arrow sticking out of his shoulder, and the roar of his cry rippled through the whole throng: “The rebels are in! They tunnelled under the walls! They killed the cave tiger guards and took the east post gate! They’re sweeping through the lower city!”

Panic exploded across the square. The distant siege that everyone had dismissed as a nuisance was suddenly at their throats. Phorenice’s soft, glowing look vanished in an instant, replaced by the sharp, bright glare of a warrior empress, and she grabbed for her sword. “I fight at your side,” she said. “We are newly wed, we do not split in battle.”

Deucalion stood, stretching his cramped chest, desperate for air, for action, anything to stop the screaming grief in his head. “Grant me a wedding gift, then,” he said.

“Anything you name, it is yours,” she answered at once.

“Give me five hundred picked troops, and order the west gate a mile from the rebel breach thrown open wide.”

“You can take two hundred of the guards here right now,” she said, nodding. “Pull the rest from the barracks along the south wall on your way. But what is your plan?”
