"Much ado there was, God wot;
He wold love and she wold not."
—Nicholas Breton.

Time, we know, is a deceptive ruler. The length of the sun's journey tells us nothing of life's advancement, nor does the acreage of a field reveal the storms brewing within its soil. A man may march south, stumble over a bone, and spend years reconstructing anatomy from that single fragment; or turn east, unlocking a new language that rewrites the history of races. He might lead an expedition that carves new paths across continents, only to return maimed and weary, finding his neighbors still grumbling about the same parish grievances, the same elderly gentleman shaking his head at the butcher's boy, staring into the same shop window. Even the swiftest thought, moving like a greyhound, cannot outrun the slow, limpet-like crawl of habit. Human experience swings wildly between the revolutionary rush of change and the quiet recurrence of the familiar, marked only by hunger and the turning of the heavens.

This contrast defined the year that had transformed the brilliant, self-assured Gwendolen Harleth of the Archery Meeting into a crushed penitent, desperate to confess her unworthiness where she once craved to be held worthy. Meanwhile, her family at Pennicote Rectory remained largely unchanged, save for the adjustment to a reduced income, fewer visitors, and fainter compliments. The rectory remained a pleasant home; the red and pink peonies bloomed as vibrantly as last year, and the rows of hollyhocks stood tall by the hedges. The rector maintained his cheerful confidence in the goodwill of his patrons, resolved to earn it through diligent duty, regardless of whether they noticed. He wrote two ecclesiastical articles annually, unsigned and attributed to anonymous scholars, a peaceful authorship that required no Dantesque leanness but rather fed on complacency, offering a suffusive sense of achievement far greater than composing a *Divina Commedia*.

There was, too, the father's recovered delight in his favorite son, a happiness that outweighed the loss of eighteen hundred pounds a year. Whatever hidden changes the disappointment of his first love had wrought in Rex, they seemed secondary to the evidence of serious ambition that followed the family's misfortune. Mr. Gascoigne viewed the previous year's anxiety as merely an evaporation of superfluous moisture, a final step in baking the human dough. Rex had recently returned for a summer visit, bringing Anna home. While he displayed the old liveliness with his siblings, he maintained the habits of the eager student, rising early and shutting himself away in the evenings to pursue a fixed course of study.

"You don't repent the choice of the law as a profession, Rex?" his father asked.

"There is no profession I would choose before it," Rex replied. "I should like to end my life as a first-rate judge, helping to draw up a code. I reverse the famous dictum. Give me something to do with making the laws, and let who will make the songs."

"You will have to stow in an immense amount of rubbish, I suppose—that's the worst of it," the rector noted.

"I don't see that law-rubbish is worse than any other sort," Rex countered. "It isn't as bad as the rubbishy literature that chokes the mind. It doesn't make one dull. Our wittiest men have often been lawyers. Any orderly way of looking at things as cases and evidence seems better than a perpetual wash of odds and ends bearing on nothing. From a higher point of view, the foundations and growth of law make the most interesting aspects of philosophy and history. Of course, there will be trouble, drudgery, exasperation. But the great prizes in life can't be won easily—I see that."

"My boy, the best augury of a man's success is that he thinks it the finest in the world," the rector said warmly. "But I fancy it so with most work when a man goes into it with a will. Brewitt, the blacksmith, told me the other day his apprentice had no mind for the trade. 'And yet, sir,' said Brewitt, 'what would a young fellow have if he doesn't like the blacksmithing?' "

The rector cherished a fatherly delight, allowing it to escape only in moderation. He had easily borne the parting with Warham, who had gone to India, but Rex was the romance of later life—a son he recognized as superior to himself, picturing a future eminence based on famous examples. He told his wife with decision, "Rex will be a distinguished man, Nancy, I am sure of it—as sure as Paley's father was about his son."

"Was Paley an old bachelor?" Mrs. Gascoigne asked.

"That is hardly to the point, my dear," the rector replied, forgetting the irrelevant detail. Mrs. Gascoigne felt she had spoken weakly.

This quiet trotting of time at the rectory was shared by the group who had exchanged the faded dignity of Offendene for the low white house known to villagers as "Jodson's." Mrs. Davilow's delicate face showed only a slight deepening of mild melancholy, her hair bearing a few more silver lines from the last year's trials. The four girls had bloomed slightly, freed from the shade, while the good Jocosa preserved her serviceable neutrality toward the world's pleasures and glories, viewing them as made for those not "in a situation."

In the low, narrow drawing-room, enlarged by two quaint projecting windows with lattices wide open to the scent of monthly roses and the murmur of the garden, Rex and Anna joined the usual group of six. Anna, always a favorite with her younger cousins, spoke eagerly of her new experiences in London and the acquaintances she had made. Questions flowed regarding Gwendolen's house in Grosvenor Square, what Gwendolen had said, and what others had whispered. Had Anna seen Gwendolen after the yacht news? No. This silence left speculation free regarding the mysterious vessel, beyond the fact that Gwendolen had written just before departing to say she and Mr. Grandcourt were yachting in the Mediterranean, and again from Marseilles to promise she would love the cabins and send no further letters until she had filled a diary with *dittos*. This movement of Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt had appeared in the newspapers, making this new phase of Gwendolen's exalted life a striking part of the sisters' romance, with the book-devouring Isabel adding a corsair or two to ensure an adventure that might end well.

But when Rex was present, the girls, following instructions, avoided this fascinating topic. Instead, they animatedly described the Meyricks and their extraordinary Jewish friends, causing astonished questioning from minds to which the idea of live Jews suggested a difference almost zoological, like a strange race in Pliny's *Natural History* sleeping under its own ears. Bertha could not imagine what Jews believed now, having a dim idea they rejected the Old Testament since it proved the New. Miss Merry thought Mirah and her brother could "never have been properly argued with," while the amiable Alice admitted she didn't mind their beliefs but was sure she "couldn't bear them." Mrs. Davilow corrected her, noting that great Jewish families in society were quite proper in London and Paris, though admitting commoner unconverted Jews were objectionable. Isabel asked if Mirah spoke differently, or if one might be with her without realizing she was a Jewess.

Rex, having no partisanship with the Israelites but having made a troublesome acquaintance with the minutiae of their ancient history through cramming, amused himself by playfully exaggerating each speaker's notion. Anna begged them to understand he was only joking, but their laughter was interrupted by the arrival of a letter for Mrs. Davilow. A messenger had run with it in great haste from the rectory. It enclosed a telegram, and as Mrs. Davilow read and re-read it in silence and agitation, all eyes turned to her with anxiety. No one dared to speak. Finally, looking up and seeing the young faces painted with fear, she remembered they might be imagining something worse than the truth, something akin to her own first dread that made understanding impossible. With a sob that was half relief, she said,

"My dears, Mr. Grandcourt—" She paused an instant, then began again, "Mr. Grandcourt is drowned."

Rex started up as if a missile had been thrown into the room. He could not help himself, and Anna's first look went to him. But then, gathering self-command while Mrs. Davilow read what the rector had written on the enclosing paper, he asked,

"Can I do anything, aunt? Can I carry any word to my father from you?"

"Yes, dear. Tell him I will be ready—he is very good. He says he will go with me to Genoa—he will be here at half-past six. Jocosa and Alice, help me to get ready. She is safe—Gwendolen is safe—but she must be ill. I am sure she must be very ill. Rex, dear—Rex and Anna—go and tell your father I will be quite ready. I would not for the world lose another night. And bless him for being ready so soon. I can travel night and day till we get there."

Rex and Anna hurried away through the sunshine, which suddenly felt solemn to them, without uttering a word to each other. She was chiefly possessed by solicitude about reopening his wound; he struggled with a tumultuary crowd of thoughts that were an offense against his better will. The tumult undiminished at the rectory gate, he said,

"Nannie, I will leave you to say everything to my father. If he wants me immediately, let me know. I shall stay in the shrubbery for ten minutes—only ten minutes."

Who has been quite free from egoistic escapes of the imagination, picturing desirable consequences on his own future in the presence of another's misfortune, sorrow, or death? The expected promotion or legacy is the common type of a temptation that makes speech and even prayer a severe avoidance of the most insistent thoughts, sometimes raising an inward shame worse than any other form of unpleasant companionship. In Rex's nature, the shame was immediate, overspreading like an ugly light all the hurrying images of what might come, thrusting themselves in with the idea that Gwendolen was again free. Perhaps these phantasms persisted more because every vision of hope was quickly nullified by a more substantial obstacle. Before the vision of "Gwendolen free" rose the impassable vision of "Gwendolen rich, exalted, courted." If in the former time, when both their lives were fresh, she had turned from his love with repugnance, what ground was there for supposing her heart would be more open to him in the future?

These thoughts, which he wanted to master and suspend, were like a tumultuary ringing of opposing chimes he could not escape by running. During the last year, he had brought himself to a state of calm resolve, and now it seemed three words had undone all that difficult work, casting him back into the wretched fluctuations of a longing he recognized as simply perturbing and hopeless. At this moment, the activity of such longing had an untimeliness that made it repulsive to his better self. Excuse poor Rex; it was not much more than eighteen months since he had been laid low by an archer who sometimes touches his arrow with a subtle, lingering poison. The disappointment of a youthful passion has effects as incalculable as smallpox, making one person plain and a genius, another less plain and more foolish, another plain without detriment, and leaving perhaps the majority without obvious change. Everything depends—not on the mere fact of disappointment, but on the nature affected and the force that stirs it. In Rex's well-endowed nature, brief as the hope had been, the passionate stirring had gone deep. The effect of disappointment was revolutionary, though fraught with a beneficent new order that retained most of the old virtues; in certain respects, he believed it had finally determined the bias and color of his life. Now, however, his inward peace seemed no better than that of republican Florence, and his heart no calmer than an alarm-bell that makes work slack and tumult busy.

Rex's love had been of that sudden, penetrating, clinging sort the ancients knew and sang, creating a fashion of talk for many moderns whose experience lacks a fiery, demonic character. To have the consciousness suddenly steeped with another's personality, to have the strongest inclinations possessed by an image that retains its dominance in spite of change and apart from worthiness—nay, to feel a passion which clings faster for the tragic pangs inflicted by a cruel, reorganized unworthiness—is a phase of love which in the feeble and common-minded has a repulsive likeness to blind animalism insensible to the higher sway of moral affinity or heaven-lit admiration. But when this attaching force is present in a nature not of brutish unmodifiableness, but of a human dignity that can risk itself safely, it may even result in a devotedness not unfit to be called divine in a higher sense than the ancient. Phlegmatic rationality stares and shakes its head at these unaccountable prepossessions, but they exist as undeniably as the winds and waves, determining here a wreck and there a triumphant voyage.

This sort of passion had nested in the sweet-natured, strong Rex, and he had made up his mind to its companionship, as if it had been an object supremely dear, stricken dumb and helpless, turning all the future of tenderness into a shadow of the past. But he had also made up his mind that his life was not to be pauperized because he had renounced one sort of joy. Rather, he had begun life again with a new counting-up of the treasures that remained, feeling a release of power such as may come from ceasing to be afraid of one's own neck.

And now, here he was pacing the shrubbery, angry with himself that the sense of irrevocableness in his lot, which ought in reason to have been as strong as ever, had been shaken by a change of circumstances that could make no change in relation to him. He told himself the truth quite roughly:

"She would never love me; and that is not the question—I could never approach her as a lover in her present position.
