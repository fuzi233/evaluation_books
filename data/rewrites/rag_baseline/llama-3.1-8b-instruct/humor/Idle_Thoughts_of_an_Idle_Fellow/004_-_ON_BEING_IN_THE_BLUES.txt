The mist-shrouded dawn crept over the sleepy town, casting an eerie gloom over the streets and homes, like a mourner's veil shrouding the faces of the sleeping townsfolk. In a small, dimly lit study, surrounded by towering shelves of leather-bound books and the faint scent of old paper, Lady Evelyn Fothergill lay abed, her mind racing with the weight of her own insignificance. The rain pattered against the panes, a relentless drumbeat that seemed to mirror the turmoil within her breast. 

She had always been prone to these episodes of wretchedness, these bouts of self-pity that left her feeling like a shipwrecked sailor clinging to a splintered spar. Her days blurred together in a haze of listlessness and discontent, each one a repetition of the last, a Sisyphean task of pushing against an unyielding tide. 

Her thoughts turned to the letters she had received from Mr. Lang, his words a gentle balm to her frazzled nerves, a reminder that even in the darkest moments, there were those who cared. But as she delved deeper into his missives, she found herself growing increasingly irritable, her mind recoiling from the very words that had once brought her solace. She tossed the papers aside, feeling like a child who had been forced to eat a bitter medicine, and rose from bed, determined to shake off the malaise that had taken hold of her.

She donned her wrapper and descended to the kitchen, where the cook, Mrs. Jenkins, was busily preparing breakfast. The aroma of toast and coffee wafted through the air, but even the prospect of food held little appeal for Lady Evelyn. She wandered over to the window, gazing out at the rain-soaked garden, her eyes tracing the droplets as they slid down the pane like tears.
