Life, which can sweep down marble staircases with plumes and jewels or huddle in back alleys with hot gin and a hoarse scream, came this night to the Meyricks like a thrifty matron with clean hands—quiet, nimble, and intent on making much of little.

Their house, narrow as a slice cut from London soot, held its peace. The front parlor faced the river, the back looked on gardens; so even with a lamp and two candles burning, a window could be left ajar, letting in air that tasted faintly of water and damp leaves instead of coal-smoke. Mrs. Meyrick sat in the small double room reading aloud in French, her voice clipping consonants neatly, bright as a sharp knife that makes daylight in whatever it divides. On a side table, two candles pooled steady light over Kate’s drawing—publisher’s work, exact and patient. The lamp on the center table served the reader and the two younger girls, Amy and Mab, who were stitching satin cushions intended for “the great world,” though no world could seem much greater than the one contained between their old prints and their faithful furniture.

The place had that rare cleanliness poverty can enforce when display is out of reach and vanity has nothing to spend itself upon. Everything stayed where it had always stayed. Those engravings—saved for by stinting in other matters—were not ornaments but ancestral voices; scenes and heads the children had learned like prayers, the way a family learns its own dialect long before it can explain it. The chairs and tables were old friends, their worn edges known to the fingers. No broker would have coveted much besides the prints and the piano, and yet there was room here for the finest things: music that could make the river outside seem to listen, poetry that could make the lamp-flame steady itself, painting that trained the eye to tenderness.

If there had been seasons when the scarcity was so great that even a servant to light fires and sweep floors was doubtful, still the Meyricks remained fastidious where it mattered to them. They could not quite believe the fashionable world was as coarse and slangy as “literary photographs” pretended. Their oddities—those unexpected recesses and crooked stairways in the mind—came from both parents; but all four women were bound by three cords twisted tight: family love, reverence for the best work and best action, and habitual industry.

Hans, their brother, had longed to spend his money in turning their modest life into something more luxurious. He had been resisted—by all of them, including himself, saved from that triumph of appetite for art which would, later, force him to give up his fellowship. They could laugh at his Gavarni caricatures and still let him be blameless in following a bent their independence did not hinder. A grand treat of opera-going—up in the gallery—when Hans came home: that was enough.

Tonight, as they made their compact little group, one could hardly wish them different. They were all small, suited to their miniature rooms—Mrs. Meyrick scarcely five feet, half French and half Scotch, rippling hair mostly gray under a quakerish net cap, brown brows and bright eyes, black dress buttoned like a priest’s cassock. The daughters matched her in scale and firmness: hair coiled tight and fastened back à la Chinoise, gray skirts worn in Puritan dissent from the then-fashion that demanded skirts to swell like architecture. Their faces looked as if the minds behind them had been shelled like horse-chestnuts—bright, ready, full of speech.

The only creature in the room who might be called large was Hafiz, the Persian cat, enthroned on the brown leather back of a chair, opening his wide eyes now and then to assure himself the lower animals were not plotting disorder.

Mrs. Meyrick had been reading Erckmann-Chatrian’s *Histoire d’un Conscrit*. When she closed the book, Mab—who had let her embroidery slip to the floor so she could lean toward the voice, hungry for it—cried out, “I think that is the finest story in the world.”

“Of course, Mab,” said Amy without lifting her needle. “It is the last you have heard. Whatever pleases you is always the best in its turn.”

“It’s hardly to be called a story,” Kate said, drawing back a little to judge her lines. “It’s history brought near with a strong telescope. We can see the soldiers’ faces—no, more than that—we can hear everything. We can almost hear their hearts beat.”

“I don’t care what you call it,” Mab said, flinging her thimble with a click. “Call it a chapter in Revelations. It makes me want to do something good—something grand. It makes me so sorry for everybody. It makes me like Schiller. I want to take the world in my arms and kiss it. I must kiss you instead, little mother!” And she threw herself about Mrs. Meyrick’s neck.

“Whenever you are in that mood, Mab, down goes your work,” Amy remarked. “It would be doing something good to finish your cushion without soiling it.”

Mab groaned theatrically as she stooped for her satin and thimble. “I wish I had three wounded conscripts to take care of.”

“You would spill their beef tea while you were talking,” said Amy.

“Poor Mab, don’t be hard on her,” Mrs. Meyrick returned. “Give me the embroidery now, child. You go on with your enthusiasm, and I will go on with the pink and white poppy.”

“Well, ma, I think you are more caustic than Amy,” Kate said, still at her drawing.

“Oh—oh—oh!” Mab cried again, standing and stretching her arms as if to push the ceiling higher. “I wish something wonderful would happen. I feel like the deluge—the waters of the great deep are broken up, and the windows of heaven are opened. I must sit down and play the scales.”

She was lifting the piano lid while the others laughed at her climax, when the hush of the house was cut by the soft, heavy stop of a cab and then a quick rap on the knocker—sharp in that quiet street, a small violence against their peace.

“Dear me!” Mrs. Meyrick started up. “It is after ten, and Phœbe is gone to bed.” She hurried out, leaving the parlor door ajar.

The girls heard her exclamation, half astonishment, half welcome: “Mr. Deronda!”

Mab clasped her hands and whispered loudly, as if afraid her own wish had called something down: “There now! something *is* going to happen.”

Kate and Amy set down pencil and needle, their surprise turning them suddenly still. Deronda’s reply was too low to catch; then Mrs. Meyrick closed the parlor door.

“I know I am trusting to your goodness in a most extraordinary way,” Deronda said, after his brief narrative, his voice held under as if the cab and the night were listening. “But you can imagine how helpless I feel with a young creature like this on my hands. I could not go with her among strangers; and in her nervous state I should dread taking her into a house full of servants. I have trusted to your mercy. I hope you will not think my act unwarrantable.”

“On the contrary,” Mrs. Meyrick answered at once—no hesitation, only that neat, practical compassion which is itself a kind of courage. “You have honored me by trusting me. I see your difficulty. Pray bring her in. I will go and prepare the girls.”

Deronda went back to the cab. Mrs. Meyrick returned to the parlor and spoke quickly, so the matter should be met as simply as one meets a sudden need at table.

“Here is somebody to take care of instead of your wounded conscripts, Mab: a poor girl who was going to drown herself in despair. Mr. Deronda found her only just in time to save her. He brought her along in his boat, and did not know what else it would be safe to do with her, so he has trusted us and brought her here. It seems she is a Jewess, but quite refined, he says—knowing Italian and music.”

The three girls came forward and stood close together, their confidence in one another showing itself in the very way their shoulders aligned. Mab looked almost afraid, as if the marvelous, asked for in jest, had answered in earnest and therefore must be approached with reverence.

At the cab door the pale face now gazing out had a roused, startled look, like a bird that has been handled and still expects the hand to close again. Deronda, keeping his voice gentle and distinct, said, “I have brought you to some of the kindest people in the world: there are daughters like you. It is a happy home. Will you let me take you to them?”

She stepped down obediently, slipping her hand into his as if the act had become her only remaining certainty, and forgetting her hat. When he led her into the full light of the parlor, where the four little women waited, she made a picture that would have touched dulled sensibilities: the darkness of her curls against the pallor of her face, the delicate exhaustion in her posture, the way her eyes struggled to focus as if the world kept shifting under her.

Deronda, inwardly relieved that the Meyricks were so small—the stranger’s head was the highest among them—put her hand at once into Mrs. Meyrick’s. There was no towering, no threatening space: only gentle faces near enough to be read.

“You must be weary, poor child,” Mrs. Meyrick said.

“We will take care of you—we will comfort you—we will love you,” cried Mab, unable to hold back, taking the small right hand between both her own with the eager softness of someone who has longed to give, not merely to feel.

Warmth, human and immediate, worked its way through the bewildered girl. She hung back a fraction—not in refusal but in the instinct to see clearly—letting her eyes move from one face to another. The good-will in them began to reflect in her, not as a smile, but as that subtle easing which tells you fear is loosening its grip. For an instant she looked up at Deronda, as if the mercy in this room must have its source in him; then she turned again to Mrs. Meyrick and said in a sweet tone now steadier than it had been on the river,

“I am a stranger. I am a Jewess. You might have thought I was wicked.”

“No, we are sure you are good,” Mab burst out, as if the very idea were an insult to the trembling hand in hers.

“We think no evil of you, poor child,” Mrs. Meyrick said. “You shall be safe with us. Come now and sit down. You must have some food, and then you must go to rest.”

Kate moved a chair forward with quiet decision; Amy ran to fetch tea—already thinking of water boiled to the right point, the leaf not scalded into bitterness, the small comforts that are more persuasive than speeches. The room filled with familiar domestic sounds: the clink of china, the whisper of fabric as someone shifted, the cat’s soft leap to the floor. Outside, the city’s distant rumble was kept at bay by their thick old walls and the river’s steady hush—an acoustic shelter that made the stranger’s breathing easier, as if the air itself were less harsh when not scored by shouting and wheels.

The girl yielded at last and sat with perfect grace, crossing her small feet, laying her hands one over the other in her lap, looking at her new friends with a kind of placid reverence, as if their faces were icons she dared not touch. Hafiz, having watched restlessly, came forward with tail erect and rubbed himself against her ankles, offering the silent treaty animals offer when they decide you belong.

Deronda felt the hour and the strain of the day gather on him; it was time to go before his presence made this refuge seem less purely theirs.

“Will you allow me to come again and inquire—perhaps at five to-morrow?” he asked Mrs. Meyrick.

“Yes, pray; we shall have had time to make acquaintance then.”

He turned to the girl. “Good-bye,” he said, and held out his hand.

She rose as she took it, and the motion brought back to both of them—sharp, immediate—the earlier moment when she had first grasped that offered hand out of darkness and water. Her eyes lifted to his with reverential fervor.

“The God of our fathers bless you and deliver you from all evil as you have delivered me,” she said. “I did not believe there was any man so good. None before have thought me worthy of the best. You found me poor and miserable, yet you have given me the best.”

Deronda could not answer. The words, the look, the small hand in his—everything pressed at the place in him where speech becomes inadequate. With silent adieux to the Meyricks, he hurried away, leaving behind him, in that small room by the river, the beginning of a sheltering bond.
