"What a pity it is, Elinor," Marianne sighed, her voice tinged with a melancholy that seemed to settle over the room, "that Edward should have no taste for drawing."

"No taste for drawing!" Elinor replied, her tone gentle but firm, seeking to steady her sister's enthusiasm. "Why should you think so? He does not draw himself, indeed, but he possesses a profound pleasure in witnessing the works of others. I assure you, he is by no means deficient in natural taste; he simply lacks the opportunity to cultivate it. Had he been fortunate enough to learn, I believe he would have drawn with great skill. His hesitation stems not from a lack of appreciation, but from a deep distrust of his own judgment in such matters. He is rarely willing to offer an opinion on a picture, fearing he may err. Yet, he possesses an innate propriety and a simplicity of taste that invariably guide him correctly."

Marianne, acutely aware of the danger of offending her sister, fell silent. However, the quiet approval Elinor attributed to Edward was worlds away from the rapturous ecstasy Marianne believed constituted true taste. Still, she smiled inwardly at the error, honoring her sister's blind devotion to Edward, which caused her to overlook such distinctions.

"I hope, Marianne," Elinor continued, "you do not consider him deficient in general taste. Indeed, I am certain you cannot, for your behavior toward him is perfectly cordial. If you truly held such an opinion, I am sure you could never be civil to him."

Marianne hesitated, torn between the desire not to wound her sister and the impossibility of voicing her true thoughts. Finally, she managed, "Do not be offended, Elinor, if my praise of him falls short of your perception of his merits. I have not had the same opportunities to discern the finer nuances of his mind, his inclinations, and his tastes as you have. Nevertheless, I hold the highest opinion of his goodness and sense. To me, he embodies everything worthy and amiable."

"Indeed," Elinor replied with a soft smile, "his dearest friends could not be dissatisfied with such commendation. I struggle to imagine how you could express yourself more warmly."

Relieved to see her sister so easily pleased, Marianne listened as Elinor elaborated. "Regarding his sense and goodness, no one could doubt them after engaging him in unreserved conversation. The brilliance of his understanding and the strength of his principles are often obscured only by his shyness, which keeps him silent. You know him well enough to appreciate his solid worth. As for his finer qualities, peculiar circumstances have kept you less informed than I. We have spent considerable time together, while you have been wholly absorbed in my mother's company. I have observed him closely, studied his sentiments, and listened to his opinions on literature and art. In sum, I venture to say his mind is well-informed; his enjoyment of books is great; his imagination is lively; his observations are just and correct; and his taste is delicate and pure. His abilities improve with every acquaintance, just as his manners and person do. At first glance, his address is not striking, and his appearance is hardly handsome until one perceives the uncommon goodness of his eyes and the general sweetness of his countenance. Now that I know him so well, I consider him truly handsome—or at least, almost so. What say you, Marianne?"

"I shall very soon think him handsome, Elinor, if I do not already," Marianne declared with fervor. "When you tell me to love him as a brother, I shall see no imperfection in his face, just as I see none in his heart."

Elinor started at the declaration, regretting the warmth she had inadvertently shown in speaking so highly of him. She knew Edward held a very high place in her own estimation. She believed the regard was mutual, yet she required greater certainty before she could accept Marianne's conviction of their attachment as anything more than a hopeful conjecture. She understood that for Marianne and her mother, wishing was hoping, and hoping was expecting. Elinor attempted to clarify the reality of their situation.

"I do not attempt to deny," she said gently, "that I think very highly of him—that I greatly esteem, and that I like him."

At these words, Marianne erupted in indignation. "Esteem him! Like him! Cold-hearted Elinor! Oh, worse than cold-hearted! Ashamed of being otherwise! Use those words again, and I will leave the room this very moment."

Elinor could not help but laugh softly. "Excuse me," she said, "and be assured that I meant no offense. I spoke quietly of my own feelings because I wished to be precise. Believe them to be stronger than I have declared; believe them to be such as his merit, and the suspicion—and perhaps the hope—of his affection for me may warrant, without imprudence or folly. But go no farther than this. I am by no means assured of his regard. There are moments when the extent of it seems doubtful. Until his sentiments are fully known, you cannot wonder that I wish to avoid encouraging my own partiality by believing or calling it more than it is. In my heart, I feel little—scarcely any—doubt of his preference. But there are other points to consider besides his inclination. He is far from independent. What his mother truly is, we cannot know entirely, but from Fanny's occasional remarks regarding her conduct and opinions, we have never found her amiable. I am very much mistaken if Edward is not himself aware that there would be many difficulties in his path if he were to wish to marry a woman who lacked either a great fortune or high rank."

Marianne was astonished to discover how much her mother's and her own imaginations had outstripped the truth.

"And you are not engaged to him!" she exclaimed. "Yet it certainly soon will happen. But this delay offers two advantages. First, I shall not lose you so soon. Second, Edward will have greater opportunity to improve that natural taste for your favorite pursuit, which must be indispensable to your future felicity. Oh, if he were so far stimulated by your genius as to learn to draw himself, how delightful it would be!"

Elinor had given her true opinion to her sister. She could not view Marianne's partiality for Edward in such a prosperous light as her sister believed. There were times when a certain lack of spirits about him suggested something almost unpromising, even if it did not denote indifference. A doubt of her regard, should he feel it, need not cause him more than inquietude; it would not likely produce the deep dejection that frequently attended him. A more reasonable cause lay in his dependent situation, which forbade the indulgence of his affection. She knew his mother neither behaved in a way to make his current home comfortable nor offered any assurance that he could form a home for himself without strictly adhering to her views for his aggrandizement. With such knowledge, it was impossible for Elinor to feel at ease. She was far from depending on the result of his preference for her, which her mother and sister still considered certain. Nay, the longer they remained together, the more doubtful the nature of his regard appeared; and sometimes, for a few painful minutes, she feared it was nothing more than friendship.

Yet, whatever its true limits might be, it was enough, once perceived by his sister, to make her uneasy and, all too commonly, to make her uncivil. Seizing the first opportunity, she affronted her mother-in-law, speaking expressively of her son's great expectations, of Mrs. Ferrars's resolution that both her sons should marry well, and of the peril attending any young woman who attempted to "draw him in." Mrs. Dashwood could neither pretend to be unconscious nor endeavor to remain calm. She gave an answer marked by contempt and instantly left the room, resolving that, regardless of the inconvenience or expense of such a sudden removal, her beloved Elinor should not be exposed another week to such insinuations.

In this state of spirits, a letter arrived from the post, offering a proposal particularly well timed. It was an offer of a small house on very easy terms, belonging to a relation of her own, a gentleman of consequence and property in Devonshire. The letter came from the gentleman himself, written in the true spirit of friendly accommodation. He understood her need for a dwelling; though the house he offered was merely a cottage, he assured her that everything necessary would be done to make it comfortable if the situation pleased her. He earnestly pressed her, along with his daughters, to visit Barton Park, his own residence, from where she could judge whether Barton Cottage, located in the same parish, could be altered to suit her needs. His letter was written in such a friendly style that it could not fail to please his cousin, especially at a moment when she suffered under the cold and unfeeling behavior of her nearer connections. She needed no time for deliberation or inquiry. Her resolution was formed as she read. The situation of Barton, in a county so distant from Sussex as Devonshire, which had previously been a sufficient objection to outweigh every advantage, became its first recommendation. To quit the neighborhood of Norland was no longer an evil; it was an object of desire, a blessing compared to the misery of remaining her daughter-in-law's guest. To remove forever from that beloved place would be less painful than to inhabit or visit it while such a woman remained its mistress. She instantly wrote Sir John Middleton acknowledging his kindness and accepting his proposal, then hastened to show both letters to her daughters, securing their approbation before sending her answer.

Elinor had always thought it more prudent for them to settle at some distance from Norland rather than immediately among their present acquaintances. On that point, therefore, she could not oppose her mother's intention of moving to Devonshire. The house, as described by Sir John, was on such a simple scale, and the rent so uncommonly moderate, that she had no right to object on either ground. Thus, though the plan brought no charm to her fancy and represented a removal from the vicinity of Norland beyond her wishes, she made no attempt to dissuade her mother from sending a letter of acquiescence.
