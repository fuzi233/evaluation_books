The biting chill of March 1886 settled over my cottage bluff, carrying with it the metallic, ash-laden taste of an encroaching industrial age. Below me, the noble Hudson churned like the grey spectre of a dead river, its waters choking beneath the relentless, mechanical groan of steamships and factories. This oppressive sonic pollution grated continually against my spirit, fraying the edges of my sanity and drowning out the natural acoustic harmony of the earth. For ten long years, I had sought to anchor my mythic past into an everyday domestic routine—brewing bitter black teas, kneading dough by the hearth, meticulously recording the culinary and sensory memories of a world millions of miles away. Yet, as I stood beneath the night sky, my palate longed not for the sterile sustenance of Earth, but for the deep, saffron-rich soils and the spiced, communal banquets of my beloved Mars. 

The cycle was turning once again. Just as it had on that fateful March night in 1866, when I lay in the spectral silence of an Arizona cave, the irresistible pull of the red star drew me into its mythic rhythm. I stretched my arms toward the lurid eye of the war god, praying for the return of that strange power. Suddenly, a wave of nausea swept over me. My senses swam, my knees yielded, and I pitched toward the dizzying precipice of the bluff. Instantly, the timeline folded back upon itself. My brain cleared, overwhelmed by the vivid, visceral memory of the dark Arizona cave. I made the same superhuman effort to break the bonds of my paralysis. A sharp click resonated in my mind—like the snapping of a taut wire across an acoustic chamber—and I stood naked and free beside the lifeless shell that had so recently pulsed with the warm, red blood of John Carter. With a final, fleeting glance at the commercialized, deafening world I was leaving behind, I turned my eyes to Mars and plunged into the unthinkable cold and silent void of interplanetary space.

I awakened to a feast for the senses, beneath the burning rays of a brazen sun that pierced the dome of a mighty, ancient forest. The olfactory landscape was immediately intoxicating—a rich bouquet of crushed juniper, sweet copper, and a heady, floral warmth that my native English tongue, a language already withered by the sterile constraints of modern thought, could never accurately translate. I lay upon a close-cropped sward of brilliant, red grass-like vegetation. All around me stood majestic trees whose massive stems, some fully a hundred feet in diameter, were polished to the gleam of fine porcelain. Their woods varied from the deepest ebony to pure azure, scarlet, and a vibrant yellow, crowned by thick, gorgeous blooms whose intricate layering and fragrant nectar would challenge the descriptive powers of Earth’s most meticulous botanists and food historians. 

The acoustic ecology of this forest was profoundly healing. It was filled with winged, brilliantly colored birds that were entirely voiceless. Their silent flights created a rustling canopy of pure, unblemished quietude, a sanctuary free from the abrasive din I had left behind. Furthermore, the land bore the unmistakable signature of careful, traditional agrarian hands. The sward was trimmed like an ancient English lawn, and the trees were pruned to a uniform height, turning the forest into a vast, high-ceilinged chamber. I recognized the reverence for the soil here, the careful curation of life that bespoke a civilized people who understood the profound meaning of their environment. 

As I attuned my Earthly sinews to the lesser gravity and rarefied atmosphere—laughing as my first attempt to rise sent me bounding several feet into the air—I walked down an imperceptible slope toward a shimmering blue sea. To my right, a broad, majestic river flowed between scarlet banks, originating from the base of mighty, perpendicular bluffs. But it was in the broad meadow before the river that the sheer strangeness of this world disrupted my sensory reverie.

A herd of perhaps a score of grotesque figures moved slowly across the meadow. They were massive, ten to twelve feet in height, with hairless, ghoulish blue bodies. Their faces bore a single, dead-white eye and a ragged, inflamed hole in the center that resembled a fresh bullet wound. From a culinary and biological perspective, their method of foraging was a horrifying travesty of agrarian grace. They possessed no true mouths on their faces. Instead, their oddly shaped, trunk-like arms moved in sinuous, snakelike undulations, ending in hands equipped with razor-like talons and a gaping orifice in each palm. I watched from behind an ebony tree as they cropped the tender red vegetation with their talons and sucked it up through their arm-like throats. It was a purely utilitarian consumption, devoid of any sensory appreciation—a mechanical fueling of their massive bodies that sickened the passionate epicurean within me. 

Most remarkable, however, was their connection to the sonic environment. The tops of their heads were covered in a tangled mass of jet-black, worm-like hairs, each writhing and crawling with independent life. These were not hairs, but a thousand sentient ears, perfectly evolved acoustic organs scanning the landscape. From their armpits dangled tiny, unopened buds and miniature replicas of themselves, feeding and growing in a bizarre, composite lifecycle.

I considered stepping forward, but my rash resolve was halted by a sudden, shrieking wail that tore through the pristine silence from the bluffs to my right. It was a harsh, dominant sound—a weaponized lexicon of pure aggression that violently disrupted the psychological equilibrium of the meadow. Instantly, every writhing ear upon the Plant Men stood stiffly perpendicular. The leader of the herd emitted a strange purring sound from the mouth in its palm, and the entire gruesome flock bounded toward the bluffs in great, kangaroo-like leaps of twenty to thirty feet.

Driven by the old martial rhythm that pulsed in my veins, I bounded after them, my athletic Earth-muscles propelling me in prodigious arcs. As I neared a cluster of huge boulders at the river’s source, the scene broke upon my horrified gaze. The herd had surrounded a small group of green Martians. There were two giant males and four females, their glossy green hides gleaming beneath the sun. I noted immediately the varied metal ornaments they wore, marking them as members of eternally warring hordes. To see them standing back-to-back against a common enemy spoke volumes of the desperate erosion of their cultural memory; their distinct tribal dialects and ancient animosities had been momentarily erased by the sheer necessity of survival. 

The slaughter commenced with terrifying efficiency. The Plant Men attacked using a method entirely foreign to the martial science of the green warriors. A blue monster leaped directly over the defenders, bringing down its massive, flat, six-foot tail in a sweeping arc that crushed a warrior’s skull like an eggshell. The shrill, screeching purr of their palm-mouths was designed to terrify and confuse, dominating the acoustic space. Two more bounded simultaneously, and two more green Martians fell to ignoble deaths. 

Only one warrior and two females remained. As two Plant Men charged, the surviving male swung his mighty long-sword, cleaving a beast from chin to groin. Yet, the other monster’s cruel tail lashed out, crushing both females into the scarlet sward. Seeing his kin obliterated and the entire herd converging upon him, the lone warrior embraced the old, circular dance of death. He hewed right and left, carving a path through the blue flesh, and sprinted toward the shelter of the forest. 

My heart swelled with admiration. Acting on the deep-seated impulse of my own warrior's palate, I sprang from my concealing rock, bounding toward the fallen green Martians. I snatched a discarded long-sword from the grass. The red mist of battle swam before my eyes, bringing with it the familiar, intoxicating scent of hot copper and ozone. I reached the fleeing warrior just as the hissing herd overtook him, pinning him against a boulder.

Their single white eyes were fixed so intently on their prey they did not hear my soundless approach. I was among them in an instant. Four of the beasts fell dead before they even registered my presence. As they recoiled, the great green warrior sprang to my side. We fought back-to-back, moving in perfect, interwoven arcs. My keen blade sliced through flesh and bone, releasing spurts of the creatures' lifeblood—a viscous, green syrup that coated us from head to foot. It was a sluggish, sticky fluid that smelled of crushed caterpillars and tasted like a ruined, deeply bitter reduction. 

The fight was intimate and desperate. Razor-like talons scored my limbs. At one point, the crushing weight of a monster bore down upon my back, its talons sinking into my flesh while its moist, utilitarian palm-lips attempted to suck the very lifeblood from my veins, threatening to reduce me to mere sustenance. I was struggling with a ferocious beast at my throat when the green warrior beside me swung his mighty blade, raking the horror from my back in a single, devastating sweep. 

Freed from the assault, we dispatched the remainder of our immediate attackers. But the acoustic assault was not over. High above us, on a natural balcony etched into the cliff face, stood a strange man. He was the author of the shrieking wail, acting as an orchestrator of this horrific harvest. He pointed his hand toward us, his voice a dominant, oppressive force cutting through the air. Following his gesture, I looked out across the meadow, the forest, and the flat land beyond the river. Converging upon us were hundreds of wildly leaping Plant Men, alongside strange, new monsters that ran erect and upon all fours.

The wheel had turned. The cycle of life and battle was reaching a beautiful, terrible crescendo.

"It will be a great death," I said to my companion, wiping the bitter green sap from my lip. 

He shot a quick glance at the approaching horde, and a smile broke across his tusked face. "We may at least die fighting and as great warriors should, John Carter," he replied.

I turned in profound, astonished wonderment. Hearing my own name spoken in the complex, native dialect of his people bridged the vast, untranslatable void between our subjective experiences. There, standing amidst the carnage on a world millions of miles from my domestic hearth, was the shrewd statesman, the passionate preserver of his people's history, and my great, good friend: Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark.
