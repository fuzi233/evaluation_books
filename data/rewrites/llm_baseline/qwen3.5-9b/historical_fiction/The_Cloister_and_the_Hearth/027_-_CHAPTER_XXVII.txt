The worthy physician returned home and declared to his housekeeper that he suffered agonizing pain from "a severe burn." These were his chosen words. In the realm of phlogiston, as in all else, we often cauterize our neighbor's digits while burning our own fingers. His housekeeper applied a soothing remedy, mild as milk. He submitted to her treatment like a lamb, his sole objective being recovery; meanwhile, he drafted his bill for the shattered phials and arranged immediately for the travelers' imprisonment. Before a magistrate, he swore an oath that these strangers, owing him money, had plotted instant flight from the township. Alas, it proved to be his unluckiest day. His sincere desire and honest endeavor to commit perjury were thwarted by a circumstance he had never foreseen, nor indeed deemed possible. He had spoken the truth. And IN AN AFFIDAVIT!

When the officers arrived at "The Silver Lion," they discovered the birds had flown. Descending to the river, they learned from local informants and launched a hot pursuit up the bank. This brief escape owed everything to Denys's good sense and keen observation. After a peal of laughter—so cordial it was a pleasure to hear—and after venting his watchword three times, Denys abruptly grew grave.

"Gerard Dusseldorf is no place for us," he stated firmly. "That old fellow went off unnaturally silent for such a chatterbox. We are strangers here, and the bailiff is his friend. Within five minutes, we shall lie in a dungeon for assaulting a Dusseldorf dignitary. Are you strong enough to hobble to the water's edge? It is nearby. Once there, you merely lie down in a boat instead of a bed; what is the odds?"

"The odds, Denys?" Gerard replied eagerly. "Untold, and entirely in favor of the boat. I pine for Rome; Rome is my road to Sevenbergen. Then we shall lie in the boat, but ON the Rhine—the famous Rhine! The cool, refreshing Rhine. I feel its breezes already; the mere sight will cure a little hop-o'-my-thumb fever like mine. Away! Away!"

Seeing his excitable friend in this mood, Denys hastily settled accounts with the landlord, and they hurried toward the river. Inquiry revealed to their dismay that the public boat had departed an hour prior, and no others would launch that afternoon. However, by asking numerous questions and gathering a crowd, they secured an offer of a private boat from an elderly man and his two sons. This arrangement was duly ridiculed by a bystander.

"The current is too strong for three oars," the critic noted.

"Then my comrade and I will help row," the invalid offered.

"No need," the old man replied. "Bless your silly heart, he owns the other boat."

A powerful breeze blew directly from astern. The boatmen set a broad sail and began rowing as well, propelling the vessel at a spanking pace.

"Are you feeling better, lad, for the river breeze?" someone asked.

"Much better. But indeed, the doctor did me good."

"The doctor? You wouldn't accept any of his cures."

"No, but I mean—you will say I am nothing—but knocking the old fool down somehow soothed me."

"Amiable dove! How thy little character opens more and more every day, like a rosebud. I read thee all wrong at first."

"Nay, Denys, mistake me not. I trust I bore with his idle threats, though in sooth his voice pierced my poor ears. Yet he was an infidel, or next door to one, and such I have been taught to abhor. Did he not as good as say we owed our inward parts to men with long Greek names, and not to Him, whose name is but a syllable, yet whose hand is over all the earth? Pagan!"

"So you knocked him down forthright—like a good Christian."

"Now, Denys, you will still be jesting. Do not take an ill man's part. Had it been a thunderbolt from Heaven, he would have met his due; yet he took but a sorry bolster from this weak arm."

"What weak arm?" Denys inquired, with twinkling eyes.

"I have lived among arms, and by Samson's hairy power, I never saw one more like a catapult. The bolster wrapped around his nose, and the two ends kissed behind his head. His forehead resounded, and had he been Goliath or Julius Caesar instead of an old quacksalver, down he would have gone. St. Denys, guard me from such feeble opposites as thou! And above all, from their weak arms—you diabolical young hypocrite."

The river wound in many turns, occasionally bringing the wind against their side rather than from astern. Then they all shifted to the weather side to prevent the boat from heeling dangerously. All except a child of about five years old, the grandson of the boatman and his darling. This urchin had slipped aboard at the moment of departure; being too light to affect the boat's trim, he existed above, or rather below, the laws of navigation.

They sailed merrily on, little conscious that they were pursued by a whole posse of constables armed with the bailiff's writ, and that their pursuers were closing in. For if the wind was strong, so was the current. Suddenly, Gerard remembered that this was a fine route to Rome, but not to Burgundy.

"Oh, Denys," he said, with an almost alarmed look, "this is not your road."

"I know it," Denys replied quietly. "But what can I do? I cannot leave thee until the fever leaves thee; and it remains with thee, for thou art both red and white by turns. I have watched thee. I must e'en go on to Cologne, I doubt, and then strike across."

"Thank Heaven," Gerard exclaimed joyfully. He added eagerly, with a touch of self-deception, "'Twere a sin to be so near Cologne and not see it. Oh, man, it is a vast and ancient city such as I have often dreamed of, but ne'er had the good luck to witness. Me miserable, by what hard fortune do I come to it now? Well then, Denys," he continued, less warmly, "it is old enough to have been founded by a Roman lady in the first century of grace, sacked by Attila the barbarian, and afterwards sore defaced by the Norman Lothaire. And it possesses a church for every week in the year, besides chapels and churches innumerable, convents, and nunneries. Above all, there is the stupendous Minster, yet unfinished, and therein, within their own chapel, lie the Three Kings who brought gifts to our Lord: Melchior with gold, Gaspar with frankincense, and Balthazar, the black king, who brought myrrh. Over their bones stands the shrine, the wonder of the world. It is of ever-shining brass, brighter than gold, studded with images fairly wrought and inlaid with exquisite devices, brave with colors. Two broad stripes run to and fro, of jewels so great and rare, each might adorn a crown or ransom its wearer at need. Upon it stand the three kings curiously counterfeited: two in solid silver, richly gilt; these are bareheaded. But he of Ethiopia, ebony-skinned, bears a golden crown. And in the midst, our Blessed Lady, in virgin silver, holds Christ in her arms. At the corners, in golden branches, four goodly waxen tapers burn night and day. Holy eyes have watched and renewed that light unceasingly for ages, and holy eyes shall watch them in saecula. I tell thee, Denys, the oldest song, the oldest Flemish or German legend, found them burning, and they shall light the earth to its grave. And there is St. Ursula's Church, a British saint's, where lie her bones and all the other virgins, her fellows. Eleven thousand were they who died for the faith, being put to the sword by barbarous Moors on the twenty-third day of October, two hundred and thirty-eight. Their bones are piled in the vaults, and many of their skulls are in the church. St. Ursula's is in a thin golden case and stands on the high altar, but shown to humble Christians only on solemn days."

"Eleven thousand virgins!" cried Denys. "What babies German men must have been in days of yore. Well, would all their bones might turn to flesh again, and their skulls to sweet faces, as we pass through the gates. 'Tis odds but some of them are wearied of their estate by this time."

"Tush, Denys!" said Gerard. "Why wilt thou, being good, still make thyself seem evil? If thy wishing-cap be on, pray that we may meet the meanest she of all those wise virgins in the next world, and to that end let us reverence their holy dust in this one. And then there is the Church of the Maccabees, and the cauldron in which they and their mother Solomona were boiled by a wicked king for refusing to eat swine's flesh."

"Oh, peremptory king! And pig-headed Maccabees! I had eaten bacon with my pork liver rather than change places at the fire with my meat."

"What scurvy words are these? It was their faith."
