Sally didn’t make it to Detroit until Friday. By Saturday morning, she was rolling up to the Hotel Statler in a taxi, her spirits somewhere around her ankles. She’d spent the last week playing nurse to poor, flu-ridden Mr. Faucitt, slept fitfully on the train, and now, to top it all off, Gerald had greeted her over the phone like she was a mildly inconvenient telegram.

Not exactly the romantic reunion she’d imagined.

Coffee helped. Men, she reminded herself, were practically comatose before noon. Surely a cold shower and a proper breakfast would resurrect the Gerald she knew—the one who might actually be pleased to see her.

She was halfway through her second cup when a familiar shape lumbered into the dining room, squinting around like a shortsighted bulldog.

Sally blinked. Her brother wasn’t supposed to be west of New York, let alone prowling a Detroit hotel lobby. Yet here he was, looking like a man who’d just realized he’d left the stove on.

“Fillmore?” she called.

He jerked like a startled rabbit, spotted her, and shuffled over with the air of someone approaching a dentist’s chair.

“Sally! What—uh—what are you doing here?”

“Seeing a play. Or I *was*, until the theatres closed. What’s *your* excuse?”

Fillmore coughed. “Well, you see, I’m—er—sort of involved with the production.”

“Involved?”

“Financially.”

Sally raised an eyebrow. “Since when do *you* finance Broadway plays?”

Fillmore puffed up slightly. “Cracknell—old college pal—invited me in. Big opportunity.”

“Ah. So you’re a theatrical impresario now.”

“Well, I wouldn’t say *impresario*—”

“I would. And while we’re on the subject, when were you planning to tell me you got engaged?”

Fillmore’s face turned the shade of a ripe tomato. “You *know*?”

“Mr. Faucitt spilled the beans. Who is she?”

“Gladys Winch. She’s in the play. Only a small part, but—” His voice took on the reverence of a man describing a religious experience. “Sally, she’s a *genius*. The way she says *‘Did you ring, madam?’*—it’s *art*.”

Sally fought a smile. “I can’t wait to meet her.”

Fillmore glanced at his watch. “I’ve got to find Bunbury. The producer. He’s probably upstairs.”

“Run along, then. Don’t let me keep you from your high-powered theatrical dealings.”

Fillmore retreated, and Sally turned her attention back to her coffee, feeling marginally better. At least Gerald’s gloom made sense now—no wonder he’d been terse.

When he finally appeared, he looked like a man who’d been handed a life sentence.

“Oh, Jerry,” she said sympathetically, “I just heard about the theatres closing. What rotten luck.”

Gerald stabbed at his grapefruit. “It’s a farce. They shut down theatres but let people cram into stores like sardines. And I don’t even *believe* in this Spanish flu nonsense.”

Sally bit back a retort. Mr. Faucitt had been genuinely ill, but Gerald wasn’t in the mood for perspective.

“The company’s falling apart,” he muttered. “Rehearsing for nothing, actors going stale. And that *Hobson woman*—”

“The star?”

“If you can call her that. She couldn’t act her way out of a paper bag. Elsa Doland would’ve been ten times better, but no, Cracknell insisted on Hobson. And now she’s throwing tantrums because she thinks someone might get an extra *line*.”

Sally sipped her coffee. “Sounds like a nightmare.”

“It *is*.” Gerald scowled. “And for God’s sake, don’t mention we’re engaged. I have to keep Hobson happy, and she’s the jealous type.”

Sally’s cup clinked sharply against the saucer. “If my being engaged to you is such a *handicap*—”

“Don’t be *ridiculous*,” Gerald groaned. “It’s just—everything’s a mess, and I can’t afford any more complications.”

She exhaled. Maybe she was just tired. Maybe *he* was just tired. Maybe the world was out of joint because she hadn’t had a bath yet.

She reached across the table and squeezed his hand. “I’m sorry. I *do* understand.”

Gerald gave her a look of wounded gratitude. “You’re the only thing keeping me sane.”

By the time Sally reached the theatre, the rehearsal was already in full swing—if *chaos* could be called “full swing.”

She slipped into a back row as Elsa Doland delivered a line to a man in a bowler hat, who responded with:

From the front row, a voice bellowed:

A walking stick sailed across the auditorium.

Sally blinked. This was *not* how she imagined rehearsals.

Enter Gladys Winch.

Sally leaned forward. *So this is Fillmore’s genius.*

Gladys was square-jawed, no-nonsense, and chewing gum with the focus of a philosopher contemplating the universe.

Mr. Bunbury, the orange-haired producer, erupted.

Gladys considered this. *“Bit o’ business. Maids chew gum.”*

*“Huh. Maybe you’re right.”* She brightened. *“Hey, how about Elsa scolds me, I snap back, we build a whole comic scene—”*

Before Bunbury could combust, a vision in blue velvet stormed onstage.

“*Listen here!*” Her voice could have shattered glass. “If *anybody’s* part gets ‘written up,’ I walk. *So quick you won’t see me go!*”

A nervous young man (presumably Cracknell) fluttered after her. *“Now, sweetie—”*

*“Oh, *can* it, Reggie!”*

Gladys shrugged. *“Just a thought. Working for the good of the show.”*

Bunbury massaged his temples. *“Can we *please* get back to rehearsal?”*

They tried.

It was a disaster.

Mabel Hobson was stunning—if the role had required standing still and looking expensive. But acting? No.

Then came the paper-knife incident.

*“How can I toy with it if it’s *not there*?”*

*“It’s *not*!”*

A furious pause. Then—

From the wings, like a man walking to his execution, shuffled Fillmore.

Sally’s jaw dropped.

Not a financier. Not an impresario. Just the guy who’d misplaced the paper-knife.

Fillmore stood under the spotlight, looking like he wished the stage would swallow him whole.

Sally’s heart twinged. *Oh, Fillmore.*

All his bluster at breakfast—*“big opportunity,” “Cracknell’s pal”*—had been a smokescreen. He was broke. Again.

And now he was getting yelled at in front of everyone.

She sighed. *Time for some first aid.*
