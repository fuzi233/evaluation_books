Nais herself commanded me to attend to my sterner duties. The clamor of the assault reached us within the house where I cared for her, and she demanded its meaning. With as much brevity as her condition allowed—she was far too frail for tedious explanations—I recounted the history of her nine-year slumber.

A flush rose to her cheeks. "My lord is the properest man in all the world to be King," she whispered.

"I refused to engage in trade until they delivered the Queen I desired, safe and alive, right here upon this Mountain."

"How we poor women are made the chattel of men!" she lamented. "But, for my part, I seem to enjoy the transaction well enough. You should not have let me obstruct Atlantis's progress, Deucalion. Still, it is sweet to know you were weak for once, and that I caused it. What is that bath over there? Ah! I recall; my wits are currently clouded."

"You have begun the journey. Your strength will return quickly, but it cannot be hurried. You must exercise patience."

"Sir, lend me your ear for a moment, and then I shall rest in peace. Have my features vanished entirely? You possess no mirror here. I dreamed I would awaken wrinkled and ancient."

"You remain exactly as you were that first night I beheld you—the most beautiful woman in all the world."

"Pleased you approve of me," she said, accepting the broth cup I offered. "My hair appears to have grown, yet it desperately needs combing. Once, I fancied you preferred ruddy locks to plain brown." Closing her eyes, she sank back among the cushions I had arranged, drifting into a sound, healthy sleep while smiles played upon her lips. I covered her with the blanket and kissed her forehead gently, holding back my beard lest it brush her cheek. Then I departed the chamber.

That beard had become increasingly repulsive to me in recent hours. I entered a room in the house, retrieved grooming tools, and shaved it down to my bare chin. I also found a fresh robe and donned it, discarding my squalid rags. If a man is truly a king, he owes these tokens to the dignity of his office.

However, judging by the din of the combat, my kingdom was shrinking. Hour by hour, the fighting grew fiercer and nearer. It became evident that several gates along the passage ascending the cliff's cleft—which all men had deemed impregnable—had succumbed to the ferocity of Phorenice's assault. Indeed, it was hardly surprising. With her genius fueled to madness by the destruction of such a magnificent portion of her city, the Empress would be a formidable adversary even for a fortress. Yet, the Sacred Mountain was no longer invincible.

Its defenses of stone, cunningly designed and massively constructed, still existed, but stone fights alone only if manned. Furthermore, it requires abundant manpower. In storms of this magnitude, desperate men spit defiance at death, grapple hand-to-hand, or allow slingers and archers to slip their shots. Throwing fire finds its mark, or—as here—newfangled contraptions like Phorenice's fire-tubes ensure that one in a thousand of their wavering darts strikes vital flesh. Thus, while the general attacker loses hundreds, the defenders suffer casualties as well.

The slaughter proved prodigious. As the attackers surged upward, the Priests guarding the lowest remaining gate rained great rocks upon them until the narrow stair-alley was paved with writhing corpses. Yet Phorenice stood on a rocky spur below, urging on successive charges with insane valor. Company after company marched up to hurl themselves hopelessly against the fortifications. Lacking machines to batter the massive gates, their assault was as pathetic as a child hammering a wall with an orange, while the terrible stones from above mowed them down remorselessly.

Troop after troop marched into this deadly trap, and not a single soul returned. This was not Phorenice's intent. Driven by a lust for final conquest, she intended to pour out troops until she had filled the passes with the slain, creating a bridge of bodies over which she might march to a level fight. Counting the cost was never part of her mood. She set her objective, and it was her policy that the people of Atlantis exist solely to achieve it for her.

In this dreadful manner, she had captured two gates, slaughtering the Priests behind them alongside those already shot down. Here, I descended from above to claim my share of the fray. There was no trumpet announcing my arrival, no herald proclaiming my status, yet the Priests, by sheer custom, picked up "Deucalion!" as a battle cry. Some shouted that with a King to lead, no further ground would be lost.

Clearly, the name carried weight across the divide. A company of doomed wretches hurrying up halted in their charge. The word "Deucalion!" was bandied back and forth down the line. I felt a grim satisfaction; here was proof I was not entirely forgotten in the land.

Shouts from behind urged them to advance, but they did not move. Presently, a glittering officer panted up, slashing right and left among them with his sword. Standing on the high rampart above the gate, I recognized him instantly.

"It matters not what they use for their battle-cry," he shouted. "You hold the orders of your divine Empress, and that is sufficient. You should be proud to die for her wish, you cowards. Disobey, and you will die later under the instruments of tormentors, very painfully. As for Deucalion, he has been dead for nine years."

"There it seems you lie, my Lord Tatho," I shouted down.

He started, looking up at me.

"So you are here in truth, then? Well, old comrade, I am sorry. But it is too late for reconciliation. You stand with these mangy Priests, and the Empress has issued an edict to root them out. I am her most obedient servant."

"You used to be skilled at fencing," I replied, noting the parity between us. "If you wish to halt this pitiful slaughter, make yourself the champion of your side. I will stand for mine, and we will settle this quarrel in a fair place, binding our parties to abide by the result."

"It would be a grand duel between us, old friend, and it pains me to deny you. But I cannot indulge you. I am general here under Phorenice, and she has given strict orders not to risk myself. Besides, though you are a great man, Deucalion, you are not chief. You are not even one of the Three."

"I am King."

Tatho laughed. "Few but yourself would dare say so, my lord."

"Few truly, but those who exist are powerful. I received the name for the first time yesterday, and as a first blow in the campaign, some mischief was wrought in the city. I was there myself and witnessed how you reacted."

"You were in Atlantis!"

"I went for Nais. She is on the mountain now, and tomorrow she will be my Queen. Tatho, as a priest to a priest, let me remind you solemnly of the infinite power you bite against on this Sacred Mountain. Your teachings warn you of the weapons stored in the Ark of the Mysteries. If you persist in this attack, at best you will merely lose; at worst, you will bring about a wreck over which even the High Gods will shudder as they order it."

"You cannot scare us back with words," Tatho said doggedly. "As for magic, it will be met by magic. Phorenice has discovered, through her own ingenuity, as many powers as were ever stored in the Ark of the Mysteries."

"Yet she looked on helplessly last night when her royal pyramid was reduced to a rubbish heap. Zaemon prophesied this outcome, and as a witness, I stood closer to her than we do now, observing it firsthand."

"I admit you surprised her somewhat there. I do not fully understand these matters; I was never more than one of the Seven in the old days. Now, correctly, Phorenice keeps the knowledge of her magic secret. But it seems time is required when one magic meets another."

"Well," I said, "I know little of the business either. I leave these matters to those higher in the priesthood. Indeed, having a liking for Nais, it seems I am barred from understanding the highest mysteries. So I content myself with being a soldier. When the appointed day comes, I shall fall and kiss my mother, the Earth, for the last time. You, however, are told to seek longer life."

He nodded. "Phorenice has found the Great Secret, and I am to be the first to share it with her. We shall be gods upon the earth, for Death will be powerless to touch us. And the twin sons she has borne me will be made immortal as well."

"Phorenice is headstrong. No need to shake your head and deny it. I have known her. But the order has been made, and her immortality will be snatched from her rudely. Mark my words solemnly: I, Deucalion, have been appointed King of Atlantis by the High Council of Priests, the mouthpieces of the Most High Gods. If I do not secure my reign, there will be no Atlantis left to carry either King or Empress. You know me, Tatho, as a man who never lies."

He nodded.
