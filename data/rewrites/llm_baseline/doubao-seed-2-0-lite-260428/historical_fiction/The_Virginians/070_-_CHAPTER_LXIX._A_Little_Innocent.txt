As George Warrington noted in the letter quoted just prior, for all the messy gambling disputes Lord Castlewood had once had with his younger brother Harry, the two kinsmen remained on easy, friendly terms, meeting with all the warmth of long acquainted relations. Should George need a frank to skip the exorbitant postage fees for a letter home, an introduction to gain admittance at St James’s, or a reserved spot in the House of Lords gallery to catch a fiery debate, his cousin was never anything but happy to oblige. He was a sharp, witty companion, and would bend over backwards to advance any of George’s interests—so long as his own were never put at risk.

At this point, he went so far as to promise he would pull every string he had with the current administration to secure George a steady, respectable government post. George had grown more averse by the day to sailing back to Virginia, and placing himself once more under the iron, unyielding rule of his mother Madam Esmond. It was no mere sentiment keeping him in England: London’s wide world of coffee house debates, theatre nights, and clever, congenial company suited him far better than any life he could carve out back on the plantation. A planter’s idle, quiet life might have suited him well enough, if he could have enjoyed true independence there. But in Virginia, he was nothing but the first, and as he saw it the worst treated, of his mother’s subjects. The very thought of returning home with his young bride Theo made his stomach turn: he could not bear the dull, rigid routine, the constant petty squabbles over household management, the endless jealousies and clashing opinions that would define their days the second they stepped off the ship. It was far better to scrape by on quiet freedom and a small income in England, surrounded by people who understood them, with the faint bright hope of future distinction, than to drag his soft, gentle wife into a life of unending subordination to Madam Esmond’s will.

So George accepted Castlewood’s promise of patronage with wholehearted eagerness. His lordship, to be fair, had never done much to provide even for his own younger brother, and for all his title of peer, his own position in society was far from secure. But we always believe what we wish to be true, and George chose to set great store by his kinsman’s offer of help. Unlike the rest of the Warrington family, Castlewood was unfailingly gracious when he learned of George’s engagement to Miss Theo Lambert. He called on her parents at their home in Soho, sang George’s praises to them, and praised Theo to George, and made himself so unfailingly charming that the kind, unworldly Lamberts forgot all the unsavoury rumours that clung to his name, and decided the gossips of the town must be the wicked, scandalous ones, not him. He swore he was a better man for spending time in their company—any man would be, he said. He left all his rakish worldliness and vice hanging up with his coat in the hall, and only put it back on when he climbed back into his coach to return to his usual circles. What weary worldling sailing through life’s storms has not found some quiet, calm harbour like that, to dock at for a little rest? It is very possible Castlewood was genuinely a better man for the hours he spent with the Lamberts, no hypocrite at all for that little stretch of time.

The Lambert elders certainly did not think any worse of his lordship when he openly confessed his quiet admiration for Theo. The fondness was perfectly genuine, and he never pretended it ran very deep.

“It won’t keep me up at night, and I am not going to break my heart that Miss Lambert prefers someone else,” he told Mrs Lambert, once. “I only wish that when I was a young man, I’d had the good fortune to meet someone as innocent and good as your daughter. I might have avoided so much harm. But innocent, good young women never crossed my path back then, or they would surely have made a better man of me.”

“Surely, my lord, it is not too late,” Mrs Lambert said softly.

Castlewood started back, misunderstanding her at first. “Not too late, madam?” he asked, confused.

She blushed. “Too late to court my dear daughter, my lord, yes—but never too late to repent. We read no man is turned away at the eleventh hour, so there is no reason you should give up hope.”

“Perhaps I know my own heart better than you,” he said, his tone quiet and plaintive. “I speak French and German fluently, you know why? I learned both in the nursery. A man who learns those languages later in life can never get the perfect accent on his tongue. It is the same with goodness. I cannot learn it at my age. I can only watch others live it, and admire them. When I find myself on the side opposite Lazarus, will Miss Theo toss me a drop of water? Do not frown! I know I am bound for there, Mrs Lambert. Some folks are fated that way, and I think a good number of my own family are among them. Some people are wishy-washy, you can never tell which way their moral scales will tip. But others are predestined angels, flying straight to heaven naturally, doing good without even trying.”

“Oh, my lord, why should you not be counted among the predestined?” the fond matron cried. “While there is still a day left, still an hour, there is hope!”

“I know what you are thinking, my dear madam—nay, I can see your prayers in your very face. But what good can they do?” Castlewood asked sadly. “You do not know the half of our worldly lives, how early they begin, how selfish nature, then necessity, then education have made us. It is Fate holding the reins of the chariot, and we cannot escape our doom. I know better. I see better people than I am, and I still go my own way. My way? No, not mine—Fate’s. And Fate is not entirely unkind to us, for it lets us stumble across good souls like yours every now and then.” He took her hand, bowed to her with melancholy grace, and looked her full in the face. Every word he said was true. There is no greater mistake than to assume weak, bad men are strangers to soft feelings, or lack all sensibility. The only catch is that the good feeling never lasts—those moments of sentiment are a kind of debauch for old libertines, as if the very softness and grief of good people adds a new, sharp flavour to their pleasures. Mrs Lambert, of course, knew nothing of the inner workings of his mind. To her, he was a deeply interesting, much maligned man, far better than his reputation made him out to be. Of course there was still hope for him.

Only time would tell which of the two of them had the right measure of his character. For now, even if the lady’s endless, fond prayers for the rake’s repentance never managed to reform him, they certainly did no harm. Kind-souled women—and what good woman is not a born physician of souls?—dote over a reprobate the way doctors hover over a dangerous, high-stakes case. The second their patient recovers their health, their interest drifts to the next sick man to tend to.

While his “malady” was still under treatment, Mrs Lambert could never get enough of her interesting new friend. A fast friendship bloomed between Castlewood and the entire Lambert household. It would be unfair to pretend that no small worldly motive crept in alongside her spiritual hopes for his soul, of course—even the purest Eden guarded by flaming angels finds some little crack for worldliness to slip through. Her own son Jack was soon to take holy orders, after all. Castlewood had already hinted that his current chaplain, the dissolute heterodox Mr Sampson, would likely soon leave his post, and the small, quiet, well-paid church living attached to the family estate would be vacant, perfect for a young clergyman of good principles and gentle manners, happy with a modest stipend and a small, friendly congregation.

So the two families grew close. The female members of the Castlewood household, ever on their best behaviour, came calling at the Lamberts’ drawing room more than once, curtsying politely to the parents and complimenting the two young ladies. Lady Castlewood’s weekly card assemblies were now open to Mrs Lambert and her girls. It was true everyone played cards there—everybody in London gambled these days: the King himself did, all the bishops, every peer and peeress in the land. No one was forced to play if they did not wish to, and there was no reason to feel guilty about the practice when such august, respected figures indulged in it every day. Mrs Lambert attended her ladyship’s routs more than once, and was grateful for the warm welcome she received, and delighted to see how much her daughters’ beauty was admired by all the guests.

As readers will recall from earlier letters, there was a family of Dutch-American extraction, neighbours of Madam Esmond’s in Virginia, who had come to England with a very strong, explicit letter of recommendation from her to her two sons. The match-making matron’s intentions were so clear that Mrs Lambert could not miss them at all. George was already engaged to Theo, and poor Harry, who had once been the target of the plan, was away on military service in Canada—something Hetty Lambert’s mother was secretly a little relieved by. She teased George openly, telling him he ought to obey his mother’s commands, break off his engagement to Theo, and court Miss Lydia Van den Bosch, who was ten times, no, a hundred times richer than her own poor daughter, and far more beautiful to boot.

“That much I will own,” George laughed. “She is prettier, richer, and very possibly even cleverer than my Theo. Mr Samuel Johnson is far cleverer than I am, too—Mr Hagan the actor is far taller and handsomer, Sir James Lowther far richer. But you do not suppose I am jealous of any of those three men, or that Theo would jilt me for any of them, do you? So why should I not admit Lydia is prettier, richer, livelier, better bred? Call her an angel if you like. My Theo is not afraid. Are you, child?”

“Never, George,” Theo said, her eyes so bright and honest they could shame any jealousy out of even the most sceptical man. And after that little exchange, if Mrs Lambert suddenly remembered she had left her scissors, or her thimble, or even her handkerchief in the parlour a few rooms away, and ducked out for two minutes, you would not catch me the least bit surprised to find George stood very close to Theo when she returned, his hand just slipping away from hers, her cheeks flushed bright pink. You would not catch me pretending I have any idea what they got up to in those two short minutes. Would you, dear reader? Do you not remember what you got up to when your own sweetheart came calling? If you were not there in that room with them, there is no sense crying shame and scandal. Shame on you, Mrs Grundy, you busybody.

With Harry off fighting in Canada, and George irrevocably engaged to Theo, Madam Esmond’s little scheme to marry one of her sons to the rich Lydia was dead in the water. So why should Mrs Lambert not have a new scheme of her own? Now that Lydia was free, surely her son Jack, home from Oxford, would make a perfect match for the beautiful, wealthy little heiress? That was the plan she chattered on about to General Lambert, who as usual laughed and called her a silly old goose for her pains. No harm in it, she protested. Madam Esmond had explicitly asked them to welcome the Van den Bosches to London, and it was not her fault Harry was off risking his life for the crown. Would he rather Harry abandon his regiment, throw away his reputation, sail back to England just to marry Lydia and break Hetty’s heart? We need not go into the rest of her very unconvincing arguments, nor be rude enough to point out that General Lambert was perfectly right to tease his fond, meddlesome wife.

Back then, there was a sharp divide between the lofty court set and the respectable merchant class of the City of London. When Mr Van den Bosch first arrived in London, he and his granddaughter barely mixed outside of City circles. He lodged near his merchant agent, and when Lydia came home from her boarding school for holidays, he took her on day trips out to Islington or Highgate, or an occasional walk in the Artillery Ground at Bunhill Fields. They attended the Baptist meeting house in Finsbury Fields, and snuck off a few times to see Mr Garrick perform, or the funny, roguish Mr Foote at the Little Theatre. An invitation to a Lord Mayor’s feast was the highest possible treat for the old merchant, and dancing with a young mercer’s son at the Hampstead assembly gave Lydia more joy than any grand ball could have. When George first called on his mother’s friends, he found their old acquaintance from the Temple, Mr Draper, hovering around Lydia, paying her endless attentions. The married lawyer warned George to watch his back, that the girl came with a very large fortune attached to her. A young Quaker gentleman named Mr Drabshaw, nephew of Madam Esmond’s Bristol agent Mr Trail, was also constantly at Lydia’s side, and flew into a jealous panic the second George made his first appearance.

Wishing to do honour to his mother’s neighbours, George invited the Van den Bosches to a dinner at his own apartments. Naturally, he invited his own friends the Lamberts to join them, and every last one of them had to admit Lydia was a little marvel of beauty. She had the tiny, light foot of a fairy, the arms and neck and flashing dark eyes of a little brown huntress of Diana, fresh from the woods. She carried a soft, plaintive Virginian lilt to her speech—an accent that all the rude Londoners loved to mock in bad Cockney impressions, but that I say, for my part, is utterly charming when it comes from a pretty girl’s lips. What right has anyone to sneer at Miss Whittington for dropping her h’s, or the bonnie Miss Nicol Jarvie for her Scottish burr, or the lovely Miss Molony for her Dublin lilt? All accents sound sweet on pretty lips, no matter if they come from a rose, a thistle, a shamrock, or the new star-spangled flag across the ocean. Lydia’s accent was nothing but pleasant the second she stepped on English soil, you may be sure of it—her schoolmistress in Kensington would never have done a poor job polishing her speech.

After six months at Kensington Academy, where according to her grandfather’s calculations she was supposed to learn every possible ladylike accomplishment, Lydia returned home happy, and ready to step out into society. At first her world was small, but she was a determined little thing, set on expanding her social circle, and her doting old grandfather followed wherever she led. He had been treated very harshly as a young man, he said, and it had done no one any good. He had been strict with his own son, and the boy had turned out badly. As for little Lyddy, he was determined to give her every bit of happiness he could. Did not George think he was right? It was rumoured in Virginia, he said, that the young Warrington boys at Castlewood would have been far happier if Madam Esmond had let them make a few more of their own choices as children. George could not disagree, nor bring himself to talk the kind old man out of spoiling his granddaughter. And the Lamberts, of course, could not help but welcome the gentle, generous old man—Madam Esmond’s old friend—with open arms.

The second Lydia came home from school, Grandfather Van den Bosch moved them out of their old lodgings in Monument Yard to an elegant, fashionable house in Bloomsbury. At first all their old City friends followed them: merchants from Virginia Walk, the worthy tradesmen the old man did business with, their wives and sons and daughters, all of them fawning over the beautiful Miss Lyddy. But one by one, the old familiar outings stopped. No more day trips to Belsize House, no more jaunts to Deputy Higgs’ villa in Highbury, no more country dances at honest Mr Lutestring’s house in Hackney. Even their Sunday routine changed: oh, the horror of it! Old Mr Van den Bosch left Bethesda Chapel in Bunhill Row, and took a new pew at Queen Square Church.

Queen Square Church, of course, was just a short walk from George’s own lodgings in Southampton Row. Everyone could see exactly who Lydia was setting her cap for. Mr Draper, who had sung the old merchant’s praises to the skies just a few months prior, now took George aside to warn him, and told him all kinds of ugly new rumours about the Van den Bosches: that for all his bragging about noble Dutch ancestry, the old man had been born a nobody in Albany, that he had made his fortune through shady land speculation, privateering that was barely distinguishable from piracy, and the Guinea slave trade. His son, he said, had run off with a servant girl in a union that barely counted as marriage, been disowned, and died young, lucky to die peacefully in his own bed.

“Mr Draper has been telling you bad tales about me, I expect,” the placid old man said to George, when they met for tea one afternoon. “Well, we are all sinners. There is a little bit of truth to the bad things people say about all of us, and far more that is completely made up. Did he tell you my son and I fell out? I already told you that myself. Did he spin wicked stories about my family? The rascal liked my family well enough that he wanted me to marry Lyddy off to his own brother. Heaven bless her, I have had dozens of offers for her hand. And you, young sir, are the one I would have chosen for her. I don’t even mind that you prefer someone else—though begging your pardon, I cannot for the life of me see what you find in your Miss Lambert next to my Lyddy.”

“There is no accounting for taste, my good sir,” George replied, with his usual easy, charming smile.
