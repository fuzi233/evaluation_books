Her demeanor invited pity rather than dread, to anyone whose eyes were not clouded by prejudice. Her chest heaved like a trapped bird's, the crimson and pallor of her cheeks shifting rapidly, while her hand clutched the rough stone wall—a reflex of timid creatures. She trembled violently. The gaze she cast upon the emaciated, glaring hermit was a marvel: a mixture of love, pious awe, pity, and tender memory. Those purple eyes fixed on the suffering recluse were a phenomenon in nature.

"Aha!" he cried. "Thou art come at last in flesh and blood; come to me as thou camest to holy Anthony. But I am wary of thee. I thought thy wiles were not exhausted. I am armed."

With this, he snatched up his small crucifix, holding it out at her, astonished, while clutching a candle in his other hand. Both objects shook violently. "Exorcizo te."

"Ah, no!" she cried piteously, holding up two pretty, deprecating palms. "Alas! work me no ill! It is Margaret."

"Liar!" shouted the hermit. "Margaret was fair, but not so supernaturally fair as thou. Thou didst shrink at that sacred name, thou subtle hypocrite. *In Nomine Dei* exorcizo vos."

"Oh, Jesus!" gasped Margaret, in extremity of terror. "Curse me not! I will go home. I thought I might come. Do not Latinize me for the sake of manhood! Oh, Gerard, is this how we meet, after all, after all?"

She cowered almost to her knees, sobbing with superstitious fear and wounded affection. Though steeped in *Satanophobia*, he might perhaps have still doubted whether this distressed creature, all woman and nature, was merely art and fiend. But her spontaneous appeal to that sacred name dissolved his delusion; he saw with his eyes and heard with his ears. He uttered a cry of self-reproach and tried to raise her, but fasting and the overwhelming emotion of a long solitude broken so abruptly rendered him unable.

"What," he gasped, shaking over her, "is it thou? And have I met thee with harsh words? Alas!"

They were both choked with emotion, unable to speak for a while.

"I heed it not much," said Margaret bravely, struggling with her tears. "You took me for another—for a devil! Oh! oh! oh! oh! oh!"

"Forget me, sweet soul!" As soon as he could speak more than a word at a time, he added, "I have been much beset by the Evil One since I came here."

Margaret looked around with a shudder. "Likely enough. Then, oh, take my hand and let me lead thee from this foul place."

He gazed at her in astonishment. "Desert my cell? Go back into the world again? Is that why thou hast come to me?" he said sadly and reproachfully.

"Yes, Gerard, I have come to take thee to thy pretty vicarage. Art vicar of Gouda, thanks to Heaven and thy good brother Giles; and mother and I have made it so neat for thee, Gerard. 'Tis well enough in winter, I promise thee. But wait until the hawthorn blooms, and anon thy walls will be clad in their kirtle of brave roses and sweet woodbine. Have we forgotten thee, and the foolish things thou lovest? And, dear Gerard, thy mother is waiting; 'tis late for her to be out of bed. Prithee, prithee, come! And the moment we are out of this foul hole, I'll show thee a treasure thou hast gained, and knowest naught of, or surely hadst never fled from us so. Alas, what have I ignorantly said to be regarded thus?"

For he had drawn himself up into a heap, staring at her with a strange gaze blending fear and suspicion.

"Unhappy girl," he said solemnly, yet deeply agitated, "would you have me risk my soul and yours for a miserable vicarage and the flowers that grow upon it? But this is not thy doing: the bowelless fiend sends thee, poor simple girl, to me with this bait. But oh, cunning fiend, I will unmask thee even to this thine instrument, and she shall see thee and abhor thee as I do. Margaret, my lost love, why am I here? Because I love thee."

"Oh! No, Gerard, you do not love me, or you would not have hidden from me; there was no need."

"Let there be no deceit between us two, who have loved so truly; and after this night, we shall meet no more on earth."

"Now God forbid!" she exclaimed. "I love thee, and thou hast not forgotten me, or thou hadst married ere this, and wouldst not be the one to find me, buried here from sight of man. I am a priest, a monk: what but folly or sin can come of us living neighbors, feeding a passion innocent once, but now (so Heaven wills it) impious and unholy? No, though my heart breaks, I must be firm. 'Tis I that am the man, 'tis I that am the priest. We must meet no more, till I am schooled by solitude, and thou art wedded to another."

"I consent to my doom but not to thine. I would ten times liefer die; yet I will marry, ay, wed misery itself, sooner than let thee lie in this foul, dismal place, with yon sweet manse awaiting for thee."

Clement groaned. With each word she spoke, two things stood clearer and clearer: his duty, and the agony it must cost.

"My beloved," he said, with a strange mixture of tenderness and dogged resolution, "I bless thee for granting me one more sight of thy sweet face. May God forgive thee, and bless thee, for destroying in a minute the holy peace it hath taken six months of solitude to build. No matter. A year of penance will, *Dei gratia*, restore me to my calm. My poor Margaret, I seem cruel: yet I am kind; 'tis best we part; ay, this moment."

"Part, Gerard? Never! We have seen what comes of parting. Part? Why, you have not heard half my story; no, nor the tithe. 'Tis not for thy mere comfort I take thee to the Gouda manse. Hear me!"

"I may not. Thy very voice is a temptation, with its music, memory's delight."

"But I say you shall hear me, Gerard, for forth this place I go not unheard."

"Then must we part by other means," said Clement sadly.

"Alack! what other means? Wouldst thou put me to thine own door, being the stronger?"

"Nay, Margaret, well thou knowest I would suffer many deaths rather than use force upon thee; thy sweet body is dearer to me than my own; but a million times dearer to me are our immortal souls, both thine and mine. I have withstood this direst temptation of all long enough. Now I must fly it: Farewell! Farewell!"

He moved toward the door, having actually opened it and stepped halfway out, when she darted after him and caught hold of his arm.

"Nay, then another must speak for me. I thought to reward thee for yielding to me; but unkind that thou art, I find I need his help. Turn then this way one moment."

"Nay, nay."

"But I say ay! And then turn thy back on us if thou canst."

She somewhat relaxed her grasp, thinking he would never deny her so small a favor. But at this, he saw his opportunity and seized it.

"Flee, Clement, flee!" he almost shrieked. His religious enthusiasm giving him for a moment his old strength, he burst wildly away from her. After a few steps, he bounded over the little stream and ran alongside it, but finding he was not followed, he stopped and looked back.

She lay on her face, hands spread out. Yes, without meaning it, he had thrown her down and injured her. When he saw that, he groaned and turned back a step; but suddenly, by another impulse, he flung himself into the icy water instead.

"There, kill my body!" he cried, "but save my soul!"

While he stood there, up to his throat in liquid ice, Margaret uttered one long, piteous moan and rose to her knees. He saw her as plainly as in midday—her pale face, her glistening eyes—and then, in the still night, he heard these words:

"Oh, God! Thou that knowest all, Thou seest how I am used. Forgive me then! For I will not live another day."

With this, she suddenly sprang to her feet and flew like some wild creature, wounded to death, close by his miserable hiding-place, shrieking:

What manifold anguish may burst from a human heart in a single syllable. There were wounded love, wounded pride, despair, and coming madness all in that piteous cry. Clement heard, and it froze his heart with terror and remorse, worse than the icy water chilled the marrow of his bones.
