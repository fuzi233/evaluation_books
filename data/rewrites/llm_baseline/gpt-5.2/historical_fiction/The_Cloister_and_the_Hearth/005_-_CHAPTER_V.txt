Ghysbrecht van Swieten had the sort of craft that did not announce itself with trumpets. It came softly, like a knife slid under a crust to test whether the loaf had taken the bake: no flourish, only certainty. He began, as a man begins who means to smell a secret before he tastes it.

“The town records,” said he, turning a stiff page as if it were a dried rind, “are crabbedly written, and the ink is rusty with age. They need a fair hand to make them readable again.”

Outside, the town’s day went on in its own key: wheels worrying the stones, a far hammer counting time on iron, a gull’s harsh cry over the river wind. Within, the room held its own quieter acoustics—paper whispering, a pen’s imagined scratch, and the burgomaster’s voice placed carefully, as one places salt.

He offered Gerard the honor of transcribing them.

Gerard, who had learned what steady work costs in hours and in the ache of the wrist, asked at once what he would be paid.

Ghysbrecht named a sum that would scarce have bought the tools: quills, ink, parchment—the bare victuals of the craft, leaving the worker to starve on gratitude.

“But, burgomaster,” said Gerard, “my labour? Here is a year’s work.”

“Your labour?” Ghysbrecht let the words fall as if they were chaff. “Call you marking parchment labour? Little sweat goes to that, I trow.”

“’Tis labour,” Gerard answered, and his voice held, like a well-made broth, both heat and restraint, “and skilled labour to boot; and that is better paid in all crafts than rude labour, sweat or no sweat. Besides, there’s my time.”

“Your time?” The burgomaster’s eyes narrowed, keen as a cook’s when a boy swears the oven was hot enough. “Why, what is time to you, at two-and-twenty?”

Then, fixing his gaze on Gerard, to watch what the words would draw out as surely as warm fat draws scent from herbs, he said, lightly and yet with a hook in it:

“Say, rather, you are idle grown. You are in love. Your body is with these chanting monks, but your heart is with Peter Brandt and his red-haired girl.”

The name, spoken into the room, changed its sound. It was like dropping a pebble into a cistern and waiting for the return. Even the street-noise seemed to pause, as if listening.

“I know no Peter Brandt,” said Gerard.

That denial, too neat, was a lid clapped on a pot too fast; it made Ghysbrecht surer there was something boiling underneath.

“Ye lie!” he shouted, and the word cracked in the air like a board snapped across a knee. “Did I not find you at her elbow on the road to Rotterdam?”

“Ah!”

“Ah!” Ghysbrecht pressed, savoring the small exclamation the way a man savors the first yielding of meat to the tooth. “And you were seen at Sevenbergen but t’other day.”

“Was I?”

“Ah—and at Peter’s house.”

“At Sevenbergen?” The question sprang from Gerard before he could bridle it, sharp and naked, betraying itself the way a tongue betrays a man’s birthplace on a single vowel.

“Ay, at Sevenbergen,” said Ghysbrecht, and the triumph in him was quiet, not unlike the satisfaction of a trap that has felt the brush of fur. It was a guess flung out boldly as fact—a draw, a baited line meant to make the fish show itself.

Yet the effect was not what the crafty man had expected.

Gerard started up as if a sudden sound had struck him from behind. His breath came quick; his fingers trembled, and in his eyes there was a bright, nervous sparkle—fear, yes, but not of the burgomaster’s anger. It was fear with direction, as if it heard a call only it could understand.

“Burgomaster,” he said, and his voice shook despite his effort to keep it steady, “I have not been at Sevenbergen these three years, and I know not the name of those you saw me with, nor where they dwelt; but, as my time is precious, though you value it not, give you good day.”

The words were plain, but they carried the stubborn weight of a man who knows his own measure, as a baker knows the weight of flour by the pull on his arms. And with that he darted out, leaving behind him only the hush after a door, and the street’s rougher music rushing in to fill the place.

Ghysbrecht sprang up in huge ire—his temper rising like scum in an ill-watched pot—yet he checked himself and sank back into his chair, thinking harder than he had spoken.

“He fears me not,” he muttered. “He knows something, if not all.”

The room’s quiet returned, but it was no longer restful. The sounds from outside—cartwheels, voices, the constant grind of the town—seemed suddenly like accomplices, able to hide a man’s passage in their common clamor.

He called hastily to his trusty servant and almost dragged him to the window.

“See you yon man?” he cried. “Haste! follow him! But let him not see you. He is young, but old in craft. Keep him in sight all day. Let me know whither he goes, and what he does.”

The servant went.

All day the town’s noises thickened and thinned: the midday bell, the chatter at stalls where salted fish and brown bread changed hands, the sharp rattle of shutters, the evening’s softer tread. Ghysbrecht waited through it as a man waits through a long cooking, smelling for change, listening for the first telltale bubble.

It was night when the servant returned.

“Well? well?” cried Van Swieten eagerly, the impatience in him like hunger.

“Master,” said the servant, and his breath still carried the day’s dust and the damp of roads, “the young man went from you to Sevenbergen.”

Ghysbrecht groaned, as if the sound had struck him in the belly. The name lay on his tongue with the weight of old stories, of dialects spoken low and differently beyond the town’s gates, where a man’s words could be as telling as his face.

“To the house,” the servant added, “of Peter the Magician.”

And Ghysbrecht, in the darkening room where the city’s din faded into a night’s thinner sound, felt the circle close.
