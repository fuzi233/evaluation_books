Madame Beck was a creature of iron consistency, a fortress of forbearance that knew no deviation, let alone tenderness. Even her own children, the blood of her veins, failed to soften the hard lines of her stoic calm. She was, undoubtedly, a vigilant guardian: solicitous for their safety, sharp-eyed regarding their futures, and endlessly practical. Yet, there was a profound, chilling void where affection usually resided. She never sought to draw them close, to press their cheeks against hers, or to wrap them in the gentle warmth of a maternal embrace. To her, love was a matter of administration, not feeling.

I once watched her from the garden bench, gazing at a cluster of bees laboring in the distance alongside Trinette, their *bonne*. Her expression was one of pure calculation, a mask of prudence masking deep anxiety for "leur avenir"—their future. But when the youngest, a fragile and delightful child named Fifine, managed to slip past Trinette and tumble down the path, breathless and giggling, seeking to cling to Madame's leg, the response was stark. With a single, unsmiling gesture, Madame raised a hand to gently block the collision. "Take care, mon enfant!" she intoned, her voice devoid of joy. She allowed the child to hover for a fleeting second, offering no kiss, no word of comfort, before rising with mechanical efficiency to return the toddler to Trinette. It was a lesson in boundaries, delivered without a shred of gentleness.

Her treatment of the eldest, Désirée, revealed another facet of her nature. This was a viper of a child, a "pest" and "poison" as the kitchen staff and teachers whispered. Désirée possessed a natural aptitude for chaos, delighting in tearing the hats and shawls of the servants, smashing porcelain in the dining hall, and raiding the pantry to empty jars of jam and bottles of wine, all while framing the blame upon the innocent cooks. When reports of these transgressions reached Madame, her reaction remained a model of serene detachment. "Désirée needs particularly close supervision," she would declare, her voice level as still water. And supervise she did. She rarely corrected Désirée's behavior directly, nor explained the harm of such habits; instead, she relied on constant surveillance, a strategy that ultimately failed. While the child was kept at arm's length from the household staff, she turned her mischief inward, stealing trinkets from her mother's dressing table and hiding them. When confronted, Désirée would lie with practiced ease, denying ownership of stolen rings or scissors. Madame, lacking the courage to truly confront her daughter's vices, would feign belief in the lies, only to later track the child down to a hidden corner or a crack in the wall to retrieve the loot. In this hollow game, mother and daughter played opposite roles: the thief and the enabler, united by a shared lack of integrity.

Then there was Fifine, the middle child, who bore a resemblance to her late father in spirit if not in face. Though she inherited her mother's sturdy frame, bright eyes, and rosy cheeks, her moral compass pointed northward. She was an honest, impulsive soul, a bundle of warmth and energy prone to stumbling into trouble. On one occasion, in a fit of curiosity, she decided to leap down a steep flight of stone stairs. Hearing the commotion, Madame emerged from the dining room to find the damage done. "This child has broken a bone," she announced quietly, assessing the injury with clinical precision.

Indeed, one of Fifine's plump arms hung limp and useless.

"Let 'Meess' [me] attend to her," Madame directed, "and immediately send for a carriage."

She moved with remarkable composure, arranging a *fiacre* with admirable self-possession. When the family surgeon, Dr. Pillule, proved unavailable, she located a substitute, bringing him back to the nursery. By then, I had already stripped the child and laid her in bed. The atmosphere in the small, stifling room was tense, filled with the anxious chatter of the *bonne*, the cook, and the porter. When the stranger entered, the focus shifted entirely to soothing Fifine, whose screams were piercing. The cries intensified as the doctor approached, until the little girl, clutching at the covers, shouted in broken English, "Leave me alone! I want Dr. Pillule!"

"And Dr. Pillule is my dearest friend," the newcomer replied in flawless English, "but he is occupied elsewhere. However, when we settle down, business shall begin, and that unfortunate arm shall soon be bound and restored."

To prove his point, he requested a glass of sweetened water. A gourmand, Fifine's heart melted instantly. Fed spoonfuls of syrup and promised treats, she calmed sufficiently for the procedure. Assistance was required, and the doctor looked to the cook, a woman of robust strength. Instantly, the cook, the porter, and the nurse fled the room, leaving me isolated with the trembling child. Reluctantly extending my hand to support the injured limb, I found myself anticipated. Madame Beck stepped forward, placing her own hand beneath the child's arm. Where my touch had been tentative and shaking, hers was steady, unwavering, and absolute.

"C'est mieux," the doctor observed, turning from me to her. "This is the kind of calm that matters."

His judgment was sound. My approach would have been a display of forced fortitude, an act of will. Hers was genuine, born of a nature that simply accepted duty as its own.

"Merci, Madame, excellent," the operator praised when finished. "Such coolness is worth a thousand misplaced outbursts of sentiment."

He was pleased by her resolve; she delighted in his compliment. As the lamp was lit, casting a soft glow over the scene, I began to examine him more closely. He was young, certainly, and his presence in that cramped room dominated the space. His profile was sharp and distinct, his features finely drawn. Perhaps his eyes darted a bit too vivaciously from face to face, and his smile carried an ambiguous quality—it charmed, yet simultaneously exposed one's vulnerabilities, inviting laughter. Nevertheless, Fifine, despite her pain, found him genial. As he prepared to leave, patting the child's hand kindly, I watched him descend the stairs alongside Madame. She talked incessantly, her spirits high, while he listened with an air of amused benevolence, his demeanor laced with a playful, unconscious roguishness.

As he passed me in the vestibule, pausing briefly to address Madame, I felt a sudden jolt of recognition. It was the gentleman from the bureau, the man who had assisted with my trunk and guided me through the rain-soaked park. His stride, firm and even, echoed the steps I had followed under the dripping trees days prior. The coincidence was undeniable.

It seemed logical that this young surgeon's first visit to the Rue Fossette would conclude there. With Dr. Pillule returning the following day, there was no apparent need for his temporary replacement to remain. Yet, fate, it seemed, had other designs. Dr. Pillule had been summoned to treat a wealthy, hypochondriacal elderly gentleman in the ancient university town of Bouquin-Moisi. Upon prescribing a change of air and travel, the patient insisted on accompanying him. Thus, the new doctor was compelled to extend his residence at the Rue Fossette.

Consequently, I saw him frequently. Madame Beck, distrusting Trinette, assigned me the duty of caring for the invalid, keeping me constantly in the nursery. I found him skilled; Fifine recovered swiftly under his guidance. Yet, even after her recovery, he remained, suggesting a bond formed between destiny and Madame Beck that defied logic. Both conspired to ensure he became intimately acquainted with the vestibule, the private staircases, and the upper chambers of our street.

The dynamic shifted dramatically when Désirée, displaying her innate talent for theatricality, declared herself ill. Captivated by the attention and indulgence afforded to the sick, she convinced herself that falling ill suited her tastes perfectly. She lay in bed, playing the part with convincing aplomb. To everyone else, it was a tragedy; to Madame Beck, it was merely a logistical problem. Although the deception was transparent to her—as clear as daylight—she maintained an air of grave concern and sincere worry.

What struck me most, however, was Dr. John's complicity. He readily adopted Madame's tactics, falling into step with her maneuvers. At times, I saw flickers of comic doubt cross his face, glances exchanged between the feigning child and the calculating mother, moments of silent consultation. Yet, he eventually surrendered to the role with graceful resignation, playing his part in the farce. Désirée ate voraciously, gamboling in her bed, pitching tents with sheets and blankets, lounging like a Sultan amidst pillows. She threw her shoes at the *bonne* and grimaced at her sisters, radiating unearned vitality and bad temper, save for those brief intervals when her mother and physician arrived for their daily rounds. Madame Beck, I knew, welcomed the distraction of having her daughter confined to bed, free from mischief. But I marveled at Dr. John's endurance; surely, he could not remain so entangled in the absurdity forever.
