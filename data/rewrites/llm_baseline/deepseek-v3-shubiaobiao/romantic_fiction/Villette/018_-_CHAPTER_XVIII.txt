The early days of my stay at the Terrace were marked by an unspoken tension. Whenever Graham grew quiet, whenever his pacing brought him near my corner of the room, I braced myself for the inevitable mention of *her* name. Ginevra Fanshawe—always Ginevra. I kept my composure like a soldier at readiness, my sympathy poised for deployment at the first sign of his need.

At last, after visible hesitation, he broached the subject—delicately, as though testing the waters.

"Your friend is traveling, I hear?"

*Friend?* I nearly scoffed. But I allowed him the fiction. Still, I couldn’t resist probing.

"Which friend do you mean?"

He took a seat beside my work-table, absently unraveling a spool of thread.

"Ginevra—Miss Fanshawe. She’s touring the south of France with the Cholmondeleys?"

"Yes."

"Do you correspond?"

"It may surprise you to learn I never sought the privilege."

His fingers stilled. "You’ve seen her letters, though?"

"Several, to her uncle."

"No doubt full of wit and charm. She has such natural sparkle, don’t you think?"

I suppressed a smile. "Her letters to M. de Bassompierre are certainly… direct." (Ginevra’s epistles were, in truth, mercenary—requests for funds, thinly veiled.)

"And her handwriting—light, elegant?"

It was. I said so.

"Everything she does is done well," he murmured, then, as if seeking validation, added, "You know her better than most. Can you name a single flaw?"

"She excels in many things." (*Flirtation chief among them*, I thought.)

His next question came swiftly. "When do you expect her back?"

I exhaled. "Dr. John, you overestimate my intimacy with Miss Fanshawe. I’m no confidante of hers. Her true friends move in loftier circles—the Cholmondeleys, for instance."

He mistook my tone for wounded pride. "Don’t resent her," he urged. "Fashion dazzles her, but she’ll see through those people soon enough. Hollow, every one of them. She’ll return to you with deeper affection."

"Kind of you to say," I replied flatly.

The words *I don’t want her affection* burned in my throat, but I swallowed them. Let him believe me the discarded, pining friend. It was a bitter role, but I played it.

"And yet," he continued, "I find no such comfort for myself. De Hamal is a fool, yet I fear she prefers him. What a wretched delusion!"

My patience shattered.

"Dr. Bretton, *you* are the deluded one. On every other subject, you are clear-eyed, rational—but here? You are a slave. I’ve lost all respect for you in this."

I stood abruptly and left, my pulse unsteady.

By evening, regret gnawed at me. Graham was not a man of common temperament; his nature, though robust, was finely wrought—more sensitive than most would guess. That sensitivity had been wounded, and I saw the shadow in his bearing. Yet when I handed him his tea, his voice was warm as ever.

"Thank you, Lucy."

The kindness undid me. I could not let the rift linger.

"Dr. Bretton," I began, my voice unsteady. "Forgive me. My words were harsh."

He looked up, his expression softening. "Perhaps I earned them. If I seem a fool, it’s because I am one, at least where Ginevra is concerned."

"You think too little of yourself and too much of her. That’s not a flaw—it’s an excess of heart."

"Can one think *too* much of Ginevra?"

"I believe so. You do not. Let us agree to disagree—but let me be forgiven."

His smile was immediate. "Do you think I’d hold a grudge over honesty? The pain is gone. More than forgive, I’m grateful."

And so peace was restored.

Yet from that day, something shifted between us. The ice of formality melted. He spoke more freely, trusting me with his thoughts—most of which, inevitably, circled back to *her*.

"Ginevra," he would muse, painting her in such radiant strokes that even my pragmatic mind began to see her through his lens—a glittering, impossible ideal.

Still, there were moments when his blindness maddened me.

"She *must* care for you," I insisted once. "Her acceptance of your gifts—"

"Means nothing. She takes them without a thought."

I nearly laughed. Ginevra, who calculated the value of every ribbon? "Then why keep them?"

"Kindness. Not wanting to offend."

"Dr. John," I said gently, "love is blind."

A flicker in his gaze—sharp, knowing—silenced me. For an instant, I wondered if he saw more than he let on. But the moment passed, and the conversation with it.

Perhaps some truths are too glaring to admit. Even for a man like Graham Bretton.
