In the alchemy of the human spirit, age is merely a variable, irrelevant to the temperature of the soul. At forty, some hearts calcify into the brittle amber of regret; others remain fluid, burning with the volatile heat of youth. Margaret Van Eyck belonged to the latter category. Her heart was an evergreen, defying the seasonal decay that claimed so many. She loved her young namesake, Margaret Brandt, with a fervor that mirrored the sun's direct gaze. Yet, this affection was no fleeting caprice born of loneliness. Margaret Van Eyck possessed a sharp, surgical intuition for character, and in the girl before her, she recognized a rare alloy: genuineness. But beyond this sterling virtue, Margaret Brandt held a more potent asset within the bosom of the elder artist—a debt incurred not by service rendered, but by kindness bestowed.

Human nature operates on a paradoxical current; our affections often warm most rapidly not toward those who give, but toward those who receive. The ancient Greeks noted this inversion, but it was the bard of Britain who etched it into the marrow of history:

The poet recalled shielding a friend from the storm, bearing wounds meant for another, sucking venom from an arrow's bite. These memories, like torrents, swept away the speaker's resolve to harm. Observe the mechanism: the assassin's hand is stayed not by gratitude for past favors, but by the sheer weight of benefits conferred.

Margaret Van Eyck had performed miracles for Margaret Brandt. She had shattered her own rigid habits to cross the threshold of the sickroom, nursing the fevered brow, soothing the delirium, and petting the fragile spirit until the illness fled. Consequently, her heart had opened wide to the recipient of her grace. She loved the girl now with a tenderness that eclipsed anything she had ever felt for Gerard, even though her initial visits had been motivated solely by concern for her son.

Thus, when Margaret Van Eyck beheld the crimson roses blooming on Margaret's cheek—the physical manifestation of the love letter—and read the parchment that commanded them, she surrendered her own strategic vision without a murmur.

"Sweetheart," she said, her voice softening like velvet over steel, "I desired he should linger in Italy for five or six years, returning rich and renowned as an artist. But your happiness precedes all else. I see you cannot breathe without him; therefore, we must summon him home as swiftly as possible."

"Oh, madam! You see the very thoughts dancing in my mind," the young woman confessed, hanging her head and flushing with a shame that was not hers. "But how to inform him? That surpasses my skill. He has departed for Italy, though I know not his destination. Wait—he listed the cities he must traverse. Florence... and Rome." A pause stretched, filled with the anxiety of uncertainty. Finally, the sensible girl realized that a letter addressed vaguely to "My Gerard—Italy" risked getting lost in the labyrinth of foreign streets. She turned imploring eyes to her mentor for counsel.

"You have arrived at the precise place and the exact hour," the old lady declared. "Behold, this Hans Memling sits with me today. He departs for Italy no later than next week, 'to refine his hand,' he claims. Not before necessity demanded it, I assure you."

"But how shall he locate my Gerard?"

"Why, he knows your Gerard, child. They have shared tables here more than once; they fit together like hand and glove. Since their trade is identical, their paths will inevitably converge. Soon, fate will drop them into one another's orbit. Therefore, compose a lengthy missive, copy this pardon into it, and I vouch for the messenger. Within six months at the utmost, Gerard shall possess it. Upon receiving it, he will press it to his bosom and fly home. What are you smiling at? And why do your cheeks burn? What are you stifling? I cannot guess. Yes! Happy days approach for my little pearl."

Meanwhile, in the shadowed warmth of the kitchen, Martin sat before the black-jack, while Reicht Heynes spun beside him. The atmosphere crackled with tension, and Reicht pumped Martin relentlessly that night, extracting secrets with the diligence of a interrogator.

Hans Memling, an old disciple of Jan Van Eyck and his sister, was indeed a painter, despite Margaret's earlier sneer, and a man of considerable integrity save for one fatal flaw. He possessed an insatiable hunger for the "nipperkin, canakin, and the brown bowl"—wine, ale, and beer—which kept him perpetually destitute. This singular vice prevented the accumulation of fortune, forcing him frequently to seek sustenance and coin from Margaret Van Eyck. Yet, this dependence forged a bond; she knew he would honor any commission she entrusted to him, for pride would not allow him to fail his patron.

The letter was penned and delivered to Margaret Van Eyck. The following week, Hans Memling returned from Flanders. With the document in hand and a piece of gold provided for his travels, he seemed driven by an urgent impulse to depart.

"All the better," the old artist remarked. "He will reach Italy the sooner."

Yet, as with horses that burn with a frenzied urge to start only to stall immediately after leaving the stable, Hans's haste cooled into inertia once he reached the principal inn of Tergou and spotted two of his boon companions seated in the bay window. He entered for a final drink, but when he attempted to settle the tab, they refused payment. "No," they insisted. "You are embarking on a long journey; we must treat you. Everyone must treat you, including the landlord."

The result was a loosening of the tongue akin to pouring oil over a dry reed. Confident in his inebriation, Hans boasted of showing the Italians how to paint, recounted his exploits in battle wielding a pike, and detailed his amorous conquests with women present to contradict his version of events. In short, *plenus rimarum erat*; he flowed with stories in every direction. Among the miscellaneous debris of his rambling, he blurted that he carried a letter for a townsman of theirs, a certain Gerard, a fine fellow. He added, "You are all good fellows," and to emphasize his praise, he slapped Sybrandt on the back with such forceful enthusiasm that he drove the air from the man's lungs.

Sybrandt circled the table to evade this muscular greeting, yet he absorbed every syllable, learning for the first time that Gerard had departed for Italy. Determined to verify the intelligence, he feigned skepticism.

"My brother Gerard is never in Italy."

"Ye lie, ye cur!" roared Hans, instantly shifting to an irascible mode, failing to recognize that the man sitting opposite him was the very individual he had just lauded and assaulted. "If he is ten times your brother, he is in Italy. What proof do you offer? There, read me that superscription!" He flung the letter across the table.

Sybrandt picked it up, examining it with grave deliberation, before laying it down with the excuse that he could not read. However, one of the company, blessed by some immense fortuity with literacy, seized the opportunity. Proud of this rare accomplishment, he took the paper and read aloud:

"To Gerard Eliassoen, of Tergou. These by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed."

"'Tis excellently well writ," observed the reader, scrutinizing every stroke.

"Ay!" Hans proclaimed bombastically. "And small wonder! 'Tis penned by a famous hand; by Margaret, sister of Jan Van Eyck. Blessed and honored be her memory! She is an old friend of mine, is Margaret Van Eyck."

Afterward, Miscellaneous Hans diverged into forty disparate topics.

Seizing the moment, Sybrandt slipped from the company and sought out Cornelis.

Together, they plotted over the news. Italy lay an immense distance away. Could they not detain him there?

"Detain him there? Nothing would hold him long from his Margaret."

"Curse her!" Sybrandt spat. "Why didn't she perish when she was about it?"

"She die? She would outlive the plague to vex us." Cornelis raged at her selfishness, unable to conceive of her death as a favor to them.

These two black sheep continued to confer, poisoning each other's minds until their corrupt hearts conceived a diabolical scheme: to keep Gerard in Italy forever, thereby securing his inheritance for themselves.

However, planning was easy; execution required talent, and iniquity stalled. Then, as if Satan had intervened, whispering simultaneously into the right ear of one and the left of the other, they both erupted in unison:

They approached Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, who received them immediately. The man writhing under the torture of suspense grasps eagerly at any fragment of knowledge. Certainty is often painful, but rarely, like suspense, is it intolerable.

"You have news of Gerard?" he asked eagerly.

They recounted the letter and Hans Memling. He listened with restless eyes. "Who wrote the letter?"

"Margaret Van Eyck," they replied, assuming the content matched the signature.
