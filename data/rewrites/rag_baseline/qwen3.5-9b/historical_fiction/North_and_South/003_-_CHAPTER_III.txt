The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Henry Lennox, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of London itself. He sat at the mahogany desk, the heavy scent of beeswax and damp wool clinging to the room, his gaze fixed upon the thick sheaf of letters that had arrived by the morning post. They were from Miss Hale, her script a testament to a life lived in the margins of propriety, detailing her domestic tribulations with a mixture of resignation and fierce endurance.

Lennox traced the edge of the parchment with a silver letter-opener, his expression tightening. Here was a woman whose existence was defined by the crushing weight of expectation, yet who managed to carve out moments of genuine connection amidst the suffocation of her station. He thought of the letter she had written regarding her father's living—a description so starkly honest it had momentarily unsettled him. *A village in a tale.* The phrase echoed in his mind, a cruel irony given the grandeur of his own profession, a monument to the very system that rendered places like Helstone invisible to the world.

He turned to the page where she had described the cottage dwellers, those squatters who had built their homes fifty years prior, now facing demolition as the old forester died. Lennox felt a surge of indignation, not just for the injustice done to the poor, but for the blindness of a society that allowed such erasure to happen without a second thought. These were not mere background details in a pastoral painting; they were the bruised backs upon which the comfort of the elite was built. He dismissed the notion of meritocracy with a bitter laugh; here, in this quiet parsonage, the hierarchy was not determined by talent or virtue, but by the color of one's skin and the solidity of one's inheritance.

The door opened, admitting a shaft of sunlight that cut through the gloom, illuminating dust motes dancing in the air. Margaret entered, her presence instantly transforming the atmosphere from one of somber reflection to vibrant life. She moved with a queenly grace that belied the shabby surroundings, her eyes bright with the warmth of the sun she carried within her.

"Mr. Henry Lennox," she said, her voice clear and steady. "Tell mamma, Sarah. Mamma and I want to ask you so many questions about Edith; I am so much obliged to you for coming."

Lennox looked up, surprised by the directness of her gratitude. "Did not I say that I should?" he replied, his tone dropping to a softer register, shedding the professional armor he wore in the city.

"But I heard of you so far away in the Highlands that I never thought Hampshire could come in."

"Oh!" he said, masking his amusement with a lightness that felt slightly performative. "Our young couple were playing such foolish pranks, running all sorts of risks, climbing mountains, sailing on lakes—that I really thought they needed a Mentor to take care of them. And indeed they did: they were quite beyond my uncle's management, keeping the old gentleman in a panic for sixteen hours out of the twenty-four. When I saw how unfit they were to be trusted alone, I thought it my duty not to leave them until I had seen them safely embarked at Plymouth."

Margaret's eyes widened. "Have you been at Plymouth? Oh! Edith never named that. To be sure, she has written in such a hurry lately. Did they really sail on Tuesday?"

"Really sailed, and relieved me from many responsibilities," Lennox admitted, a hint of weariness creeping into his voice. "Edith gave me all sorts of messages for you. I believe I have a little diminutive note somewhere; yes, here it is."

He handed her the folded paper. Margaret accepted it, her fingers brushing his briefly, before she feigned a need to inform her mother of his arrival, retreating into the adjacent room. As soon as the door closed, Lennox began to survey the space. The little drawing-room was bathed in the streaming light of the morning sun, yet the brilliance outside made the interior colors seem pale and faded. The carpet was worn, the chintz washed to fraying, the whole apartment smaller and shabbier than he had anticipated. Yet, standing there, Margaret appeared radiant, a queen presiding over a kingdom of modest means.

He picked up a book lying on the table: *The Paradiso* of Dante, bound in white vellum and gold. Beside it lay a dictionary and a sheet of paper covered in Margaret's handwriting—a dull list of words, meticulously copied. He studied them, finding a strange beauty in the discipline of her learning, a testament to her determination to rise above her circumstances. With a sigh, he set the book down.

"The living is evidently as small as she said," he muttered to himself. "It seems strange, for the Beresfords belong to a good family."

In the other room, Margaret found her mother. Mrs. Hale was having one of her fitful days, where every task felt like a monumental hardship. Mr. Lennox's visit added another layer of complexity to her already strained schedule. "It is most unfortunate! We are dining early today, and having nothing but cold meat, in order that the servants may get on with their ironing; and yet, of course, we must ask him to dinner—Edith's brother-in-law and all. And your papa is in such low spirits this morning about something—I don't know what. I went into the study just now, and he had his face on the table, covering it with his hands. I told him I was sure Helstone air did not agree with him any more than with me, and he suddenly lifted up his head, and begged me not to speak a word more against Helstone, he could not bear it; if there was one place he loved on earth it was Helstone. But I am sure, for all that, it is the damp and relaxing air."

Margaret felt a chill spread through her, a thin cold cloud obscuring the sun. She listened patiently, hoping to soothe her mother's distress, but knew it was time to redirect the focus to her guest.

"Papa likes Mr. Lennox; they got on together famously at the wedding breakfast. I daresay his coming will do papa good. And never mind the dinner, dear mamma. Cold meat will do capitally for a lunch, which is the light in which Mr. Lennox will most likely look upon a two o'clock dinner."

"But what are we to do with him till then? It is only half-past ten now."

"I'll ask him to go out sketching with me. I know he draws, and that will take him out of your way, mamma. Only do come in now; he will think it so strange if you don't."

Mrs. Hale removed her black silk apron, smoothing her face into a mask of polite composure. She greeted Mr. Lennox with the cordiality due to one who was almost a relation. He evidently expected to be asked to spend the day, accepting the invitation with a glad readiness that made Mrs. Hale wish she could conjure up some additional provisions. He was pleased with everything, delighted with Margaret's suggestion of a sketching expedition, and eager to avoid disturbing Mr. Hale, anticipating their reunion at dinner. Margaret produced her drawing materials, and after the paper and brushes were duly selected, the two departed in high spirits.

"Now, please, just stop here for a minute or two," Margaret instructed, stopping before a cluster of cottages that had haunted her imagination during a recent rainy fortnight. "These are the cottages that haunted me so during the rainy fortnight, reproaching me for not having sketched them."

"Before they tumbled down and were no more seen," Lennox observed, his eyes scanning the dilapidated structures. "Truly, if they are to be sketched—and they are very picturesque—we had better not put it off till next year. But where shall we sit?"

"Oh! You might have come straight from chambers in the Temple, instead of having been two months in the Highlands!" Margaret laughed, gesturing to a magnificent oak tree left exposed by the woodcutters. "Look at this beautiful trunk of a tree, which the woodcutters have left just in the right place for the light. I will put my plaid over it, and it will be a regular forest throne."

"With your feet in that puddle for a regal footstool!" Lennox countered, moving closer to inspect the scene. "Who lives in these cottages?"

"They were built by squatters fifty or sixty years ago. One is uninhabited; the foresters are going to take it down as soon as the other is dead, poor old fellow! Look—there he is—I must go and speak to him. He is so deaf you will hear all our secrets."

An elderly man stood bareheaded in the sun, leaning on his stick at the front of his cottage. His stiff features relaxed into a slow smile as Margaret approached. Lennox quickly incorporated the two figures into his sketch, adding a subtle reference to their interaction as Margaret perceived when they eventually broke apart to examine their work. She laughed and blushed; Lennox watched her countenance, noting the flush that betrayed her embarrassment.

"Now, I call that treacherous," she said, trying to recover her composure. "I little thought that you were making old Isaac and me into subjects, when you told me to ask him the history of these cottages."

"It was irresistible. You can't know how strong a temptation it was. I hardly dare tell you how much I shall like this sketch."
