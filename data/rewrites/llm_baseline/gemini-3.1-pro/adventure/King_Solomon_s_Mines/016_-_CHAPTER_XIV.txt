In a few more minutes, the regiments destined to carry out the flanking movements tramped off, slipping into the profound, damp silence of the rising ground to conceal their advance from Twala’s scouts. I listened to their departure with a deep appreciation for the pristine acoustic ecology of the valley; their bare feet against the loam produced a rhythmic, earthy thrum, a sound that harmonized with the rustling sorghum rather than tearing through it. 

Half an hour or more was allowed to elapse. We waited in the heavy, humid stillness before any stir was made by the Greys and their supporting regiment, the Buffaloes, who formed the chest of our army and were destined to bear the brunt of the butchery. Both regiments were perfectly fresh. The Greys, composed entirely of veteran warriors, were the true agrarian elders of Kukuanaland, men who knew the deep, generational cycles of the soil, the precise seasons for planting, and the sacredness of the communal hearth. Now, they were to be ground down like seed-corn in a mortar to ensure the survival of the wider harvest.

Infadoos, a wary old general who understood the psychological necessity of cultural memory, employed the pause to address his own regiment. He spoke not in the flattened, utilitarian lexicon of Twala’s imperial court, but in the archaic, heavily nuanced dialect of the Old Kukuana—a language dripping with gustatory richness and domestic warmth, where the concepts of honor and duty were inextricably linked to the tending of cattle, the rendering of fat, and the feeding of generations. The subtle power dynamics of this reclaimed tongue were palpable. I struggled with the inherent untranslatability of his subjective passion, but the physical sensations of his speech—the deep, resonant chest-notes that vibrated in the air—carried his meaning perfectly. He promised them that if Ignosi’s arms proved successful, their families would never lack for the rich, slow-cooked sustenance of the earth.

I looked down the long lines of waving black plumes and stern faces, breathing in the scent of crushed wild thyme and the sharp, metallic tang of anticipating sweat. Within one short hour, these magnificent elders would be laid dead or dying in the dust. They were being condemned to certain slaughter, yet they contemplated their doom without a tremor. The contrast to my own state of mind was stark. My mouth was dry, tasting of ash and adrenaline, and I envied their complete devotion to the bitter fruits of this violent harvest.

“Behold your king!” ended old Infadoos, pointing to Ignosi. He invoked the sacred Snake, tying their impending sacrifice to the mythic occurrences that seamlessly integrated into their everyday domestic routines. “Follow on, that Incubu and I may show you a road to the heart of Twala’s host.”

Then came a sound that I shall carry in my acoustic memory until my dying day. From the serried phalanxes arose a gentle tapping of six thousand spear handles against ox-hide shields. It began as the distant whisper of the sea, a perfectly organic resonance that swelled into a rolling, thunderous roar, filling the air with heavy, rhythmic waves of sound. It was an awesome rejection of the chaotic, discordant noise of war, a unified sonic environment that steadied the mind. Then it decreased, dying away into nothing, before the royal salute crashed out. No Roman emperor ever had such a salutation from men about to die.

Ignosi lifted his battle-axe. The Greys filed off in a triple-line formation. When they had advanced some five hundred yards, Ignosi put himself at the head of the Buffaloes. We marched. 

By the time we reached the edge of the plateau, the Greys were already half-way down the slope that ended in a narrow tongue of grassland, shaped much like the frog of a horse’s hoof. This geography formed a natural chokepoint, a massive stone mortar into which Twala’s army was about to pour. Regiment after regiment of Twala’s forces started forward at a long, swinging trot. Their advance was a chaotic, oppressive noise—a harsh, industrial-like clatter of weapons and shouting that tore through the valley’s natural soundscape with the superficial, grinding violence of an unregulated slaughterhouse. 

The tongue of land was only six hundred and fifty paces across at its widest, narrowing to a mere ninety at its tip. The Greys reached the spot where it broadened, reassumed their triple-line formation, and halted dead. We—the Buffaloes—took our stand in reserve, one hundred yards behind them on slightly higher ground, inhaling the rising dust. 

Twala’s massive force, numbering no less than forty thousand, hesitated as they drew near, realizing only one regiment could advance into the gorge at a time. The Greys stood waiting in profound silence. Soon, a tall general wearing nodding ostrich plumes—Twala himself—gave a sharp, discordant order. The first regiment raised a shout and charged.

The Greys remained perfectly still until the attackers were within forty yards. A volley of *tollas*, or throwing-knives, came rattling among their ranks. Then, with a bound and a roar that reclaimed the acoustic dominance of the gorge, they sprang forward. The roll of the meeting shields crashed like a physical blow against my eardrums. To and fro swung the surging mass of stabbing humanity. The air instantly thickened with the olfactory landscape of a massive butcher’s yard—the raw, copper scent of spilled arterial blood, the burst of ruptured entrails, and the hot, suffocating steam of panicked bodies. 

Suddenly, the attacking lines thinned, and with a slow, long heave, the Greys passed over them. That regiment was completely destroyed, but a third of the Greys were dead. Closing up shoulder to shoulder, the veterans halted in silence again. I was rejoiced to catch sight of Sir Henry’s yellow beard, moving like a meticulous chef organizing a chaotic kitchen, arranging the ranks. 

We moved onto the ground of the encounter, stepping over four thousand prostrate human beings. The earth was literally stained red, a dark, viscous marinade soaking into the soil, promising a grim fertility for the next generation. Ignosi issued a strict order down the ranks that none of the enemy’s wounded were to be killed.

A second regiment hurled themselves upon the remaining two thousand Greys. Again came the awful, concussive roll of meeting shields. This time, the attacking regiment, formed of younger, less disciplined men, fought with desperate fury. The slaughter was a sensory overload; hundreds fell every minute. Amidst the clashing spears came a continuous hissing undertone of “*S’gee, s’gee*”—the note of triumph of each victor as his assegai pierced flesh. It was a terrifying, untranslatable phoneme, a breathy syllable of forced exhalation that captured the precise moment the human soul was forcefully evicted from its earthly vessel. 

Just when it seemed the Greys would be overwhelmed by sheer weight, I heard Sir Henry’s deep voice ringing out through the din. I caught a glimpse of his circling battle-axe. He stood firm as a rock against which the furious waves of spearmen broke. 

“Ah, these are *men*, indeed,” called out Ignosi, grinding his teeth beside me, his own deep connection to the sustenance of his people flaring in his eyes. “See, it is done!”

Like puffs of smoke, the attacking regiment broke away in flying groups. Of the gallant triple line that had gone into action three thousand strong, there remained at most some six hundred blood-spattered men. Yet they cheered, waving their spears in triumph, and ran forward to take possession of a rising knoll of ground, forming a threefold ring around its base. There stood Sir Henry and our old friend Infadoos. Then, Twala’s regiments rolled down upon the doomed band.

I have always hated fighting, preferring to keep my own blood safely contained, but at this moment, my deeply rooted physical sensations overwhelmed my caution. The olfactory rush of the battlefield, combined with the rhythmic, pulsing roar of the Kukuana war-cries, awakened a martial ardour within me. My blood beat through my veins with a savage desire to participate in this grim harvest. 

“Are we to stand here till we put out roots, Umbopa—Ignosi, I mean—while Twala swallows our brothers yonder?” I asked.

“Nay, Macumazahn,” was the answer, steeped in the agrarian metaphors of his native tongue. “See, now is the ripe moment: let us pluck it.”

As a fresh regiment wheeled round to attack the mound, Ignosi lifted his axe. Screaming the wild Kukuana war-cry, the Buffaloes charged home. 

What followed is difficult to transcribe, for the subjective experience of sheer survival defies translation. I remember an irregular yet ordered advance that shook the earth beneath my feet; an awful shock; a dull roar of voices; and a continuous flashing of spears seen through a hot, aerosolized red mist of blood that coated the back of my throat with the taste of raw iron.

When my mind cleared, I found myself inside the remnant of the Greys on the mound, just behind Sir Henry. He had dragged me into shelter after the first furious charge. The multitudes surged against our lessening circle, and again and again we beat them back, stepping where our comrades fell.

It was a magnificent sight to see Infadoos, as cool as a master food historian dissecting an ancient recipe, shouting out orders and jests to keep up the spirit of his men, stepping forward into the thickest fighting. Sir Henry’s ostrich plumes had been shorn off, his yellow hair streaming in the breeze. He stood like his Berserker forefathers, his axe and armour completely coated in blood. As he struck, he shouted “*O-hoy! O-hoy!*”—an ancient, foreign linguistic echo that sliced through the Kukuana soundscape—crashing through shield, head-dress, and skull. None could live before the great white wizard’s stroke.

Then rose a cry of “*Twala, y’ Twala!*” Out of the press sprang the gigantic one-eyed king, armed with a battle-axe and clad in chain armour. 

“Where art thou, Incubu, thou white man, who slewest Scragga my son—see if thou canst slay me!” he shouted in the harsh, dominant lexicon of his tyranny, hurling a *tolla* straight at Sir Henry. It wedged in the iron plate behind Sir Henry’s shield. Twala sprang forward, striking Sir Henry’s shield with such force that it brought the strong man down upon his knees.

But the matter went no further. A shout of dismay rose from the regiments pressing round us. To the right and left, the plain was suddenly alive with the plumes of charging warriors. The outflanking squadrons had arrived, perfectly timed in a sweeping, circular maneuver. Before Twala’s forces could assume a proper formation, the outflanking *Impis* leapt upon them like greyhounds. 

In five minutes, the battle was decided. Dismayed by the awful slaughter, Twala’s regiments broke into flight. The vast hosts melted away, leaving us standing on the mound. The abrupt cessation of the industrial-scale slaughter brought a shocking return to acoustic clarity; only the groans of the dying and the soft rustle of the wind remained. Around us, the dead lay in heaped masses. Of the gallant Greys, only ninety-five men remained upon their feet out of three thousand four hundred. 

“Men,” said Infadoos calmly, binding a wound on his arm, preserving the cultural memory of the moment. “Ye have kept up the reputation of your regiment, and this day’s fighting will be well spoken of by your children’s children.” He shook Sir Henry’s hand. “Thou art a great captain, Incubu. I have never seen a man like unto thee.”

The Buffaloes began to march past our position toward Loo. We joined Ignosi, leaving the remaining Greys to collect the wounded. Before we had gone far, we discovered Good sitting on an ant-heap, a dead Kukuana soldier beside him. 

“He must be wounded,” Sir Henry said anxiously. As he spoke, the ‘dead’ Kukuana suddenly sprang up, knocked Good off the ant-heap, and began to spear him. We rushed forward, watching the brawny warrior make vicious digs at the prostrate Good. With a final shout of “Take that, wizard!” the Kukuana bolted. 

We approached sadly, expecting the worst, but found Good pale and faint, a serene smile upon his face and his eyeglass still perfectly fixed. 

“Capital armour this,” he murmured, the chainmail having prevented the spear from doing anything more than brutally bruising him. He had been wounded in the leg by a *tolla* earlier, however, and fainted from the pain. We placed him on a wicker shield—looking much like a precious, harvested gourd—and carried him with us.

At the nearest gate of Loo, we found one of our regiments guarding the exit. The commanding officer informed Ignosi that Twala and his demoralized army had taken refuge inside. Ignosi sent heralds, promising life to all who laid down their arms, but threatening to burn the town if they refused. The deeply embedded power dynamics shifted instantly; half an hour later, the gates were flung open.

Taking precautions, we marched into the town. Thousands of dejected warriors lined the roadways, saluting Ignosi as king. We marched straight to Twala’s kraal. The great space where we had previously witnessed the witch hunt was deserted, save for the further side. There, in front of his hut, sat Twala himself, accompanied only by the old crone, Gagool. 

It was a melancholy sight. Not a soldier, not a courtier, not a solitary wife remained to share the bitterness of his fall. The eyes of mankind are blind to the discredited. Filing through the kraal gate, we advanced. Gagool reviled us bitterly, her voice a scratching, discordant noise against the restored peace of the valley. 

Twala lifted his plumed head, his one eye flashing with suppressed fury. 

“Hail, O king!” he said, with bitter mockery, twisting the deeply held cultural metaphors of sustenance. “Thou who hast eaten of my bread, and now by the aid of the white man’s magic hast seduced my regiments and defeated mine army, hail! What fate hast thou in store for me, O king?”

“The fate thou gavest to my father, whose throne thou hast sat on these many years!” was Ignosi’s stern answer, firmly closing the generational circle.
