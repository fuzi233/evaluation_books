The imprisoned Captain Shandon, a man whose prospects were as tight as his waistcoat, announced with the crisp, emphatic cadence of a man selling ice cream in July that the hour had finally arrived for the gentlemen of England to unite. He spoke of defending their "glorious order" against a tempest of foreign revolutions, domestic radicals, and the treachery of mill-owners and cotton lords who, he claimed, gulled the masses.

"The ancient monarchy was insulted!" the Captain proclaimed, his voice rising in a crescendo that echoed off the damp prison walls. "By a ferocious republican rabble! The Church was deserted by envious dissenters and undermined by stealthy infidels!" He painted a dire picture where good institutions, once the pillars of glory, now sat defenseless against men who believed in nothing holy, knew no history, and followed no law save the signal to plunder given by their leaders.

As the Captain ranted, Mr. Shandon interjected with a flourish, citing the fall of Saint Louis's monarchy as proof that kings mistrust their gentlemen, while England's survival owed everything to the belief the people held in their class. "It was because we were headed by gentlemen," Shandon declaimed, "that the Eagles retreated before us from the Donro to the Garonne! It was a gentleman who broke the line at Trafalgar and swept the plains of Waterloo!"

At the mention of Waterloo, Bungay, the publisher, nodded with a knowing smirk and winked, clearly enjoying the spectacle. Warrington, however, couldn't hold back; he burst into laughter.

"You see how our venerable friend Bungay is affected?" Shandon whispered slyly, glancing down from his papers. "That's your true sort of test. I've used the Duke of Wellington and the Battle of Waterloo a hundred times, and I never knew the Duke to fail."

The Captain then confessed, with a candour that bordered on the absurd, that until now, the gentlemen of England had left their political interests to professionals, just as they left their estates to stewards. "That time," Shandon insisted, "is gone by. The gentlemen must be their own champions. The enemies of our order are brave, strong, numerous, and uncompromising. We must meet them in the field, not be belied by hireling advocates from Grub Street publishing gazettes from Whitehall."

"That's a dig at Bacon's people, Mr. Bungay," Shandon noted, turning to the publisher.

Bungay immediately clapped his stick on the floor with a sharp *clack*. "Hang him! Pitch into him, Captain!" he cried with exultation. Turning to Warrington, he wagged his dull head vehemently. "For a slashing article, sir, there's nobody like the Captain—not-o-body like him."

The prospectus writer continued, declaring that certain gentlemen, whose names were withheld for "obvious reasons," intended to launch a journal. These men, proud and loyal to their Sovereign and Church, sought to uphold the traditions of the "Gentlemen of England"—a phrase Bungay insisted be printed in large capitals.

"What's that?" asked Mr. Bungay, confused.

"An ancestor of mine sealed it with his sword-hilt," Pen declared with grave solemnity.

"It's the Habeas Corpus, Mr. Bungay," Warrington corrected. To which the publisher merely yawned, muttered "All right, I dare say," and urged the Captain to proceed.

Shandon rallied on, speaking of Runnymede and the readiness to defend liberty with sword and pen. "Bravo!" cried Warrington. Even the little child standing nearby seemed entranced, while the lady worked silently beside her, looking at the scene with fond admiration.

"Come here, little Mary," Warrington said, patting the child's fair curls with his large, rough hand. But the girl shrank back, preferring to seek refuge at Pen's knee to play with his fine watch-chain. Pen, a man of simple heart concealed beneath a shy and pompous exterior, was deeply pleased. She clambered onto his lap, allowing her father to finish reading the program.

"You were laughing," the Captain chided Warrington, "about 'the obvious reasons' I mentioned. Now, I'll show ye what they are, ye unbelieving heathen." He flourished his paper, explaining that they numbered influential friends in both Houses of Parliament and possessed sources of intelligence unavailable to any other journal. "The Pall Mall Gazette," he declared, "will be written by gentlemen for gentlemen. Its conductors speak to the classes in which they live and were born."

Shandon then descended with modesty upon the literary and fashionable departments of the proposed paper, conducted by men famous at the Universities and known at the Clubs. He pointed out delicately to potential advertisers that there would be no medium like the *Pall Mall Gazette* for promoting sales, nor any space for the "higher circles" to be ignored. He called upon the nobility, the baronetage, the clergy, and the matrons of England to rally around the "good old cause." At the conclusion of this grandiose speech, Bungay, waking from a second snooze, simply grunted, "It's all right."

Once the reading ended, the gentlemen discussed the political and literary management of the paper. Bungay sat listening, nodding as if understanding, though his opinions were remarkably simple: he believed the Captain could write the best "smashing article" in England. Whether the Captain wrote a letter of Junius or copied the Church Catechism, Bungay considered it a smash. He pocketed the manuscripts with satisfaction, paid for them, and even gave little Mary a penny as he departed.

Afterwards, Shandon, leading the conversation with a jaunty, fashionable air, complimented his two guests, presuming them to be of the *beau monde*. He spoke of the characters of the day and great personages with easy familiarity, telling anecdotes of private lives and entertainments as if he lived among them. Pen was amused to hear the shabby prisoner in a tattered dressing-gown talk so fluently of the great. Mrs. Shandon, delighted, believed every tale he spun, including one about an invitation to the Earl of X, which she treasured in her workbox.

Eventually, Mr. Bungay rose to take his leave. Warrington and Pen followed, though the publisher wished to linger. Shandon, with a rueful grin, assured Pen he was always at home and delighted to see him. "I'll see you to my park-gate, gentlemen," the Captain said, seizing his hat despite a deprecatory look from his wife.

Clad in shabby slippers, the Captain shuffled out before his guests, leading the way through the dismal passages of the prison. As he reached the wicket, his hand fiddled nervously with his waistcoat pocket, where Bungay's five-pound note lay. He took his leave of the three gentlemen, leaving Pen greatly relieved to be out of the horrid place and back on the flags of Farringdon Street.

Inside, Mrs. Shandon resumed her work by the window, watching the Captain run rapidly with two men toward the prison tavern. Her hopes of sharing a dinner of meat and salad with her husband and little Mary had vanished. Instead, he would spend the night playing cards or drinking in someone else's room, returning later with glazed eyes and a slight reel, requiring her nursing.

Despondent, Mrs. Shandon made herself some tea from the cupboard. In those moments of varied pain, the tea-pot played a significant role as a confidante, witnessing countless tears and secret sorrows. Finally, she and little Mary sat down to drink their tea together while the Captain went out to take his pleasure.

Just then, Mr. Jack Finucane, a townsman of Captain Shandon's, appeared. He found the wife and child, bringing a sweetmeat for the little girl. Jack, who thought Shandon the greatest of geniuses, was always ready to run errands, manage finances, or handle debts for the embarrassed Irish gentleman. He had never missed a day visiting his patron, acting as an aide-de-camp to a ragged staff of insolvent dependents who shared in the chief's rough fortunes.

Outside, as the group walked away, Bungay remarked, "He won't have that five-pound note very long, I bet a guinea." And the publisher proved right. When Mrs. Shandon emptied her husband's pockets later, she found only a couple of shillings and a few halfpence. Shandon had given a pound to one follower, sent a leg of mutton and potatoes to a poor acquaintance, paid a tavern bill, and lost several half-crowns at cards. The night had left him as poor as the morning.

Later, after quitting Shandon, the gentlemen conversed. Warrington reiterated to Bungay that Pen was a high fellow of great genius, well-connected with the peerage. Bungay agreed, expressing happiness to do business with Mr. Pendennis and hoping to dine with him soon. With mutual politeness, they parted.

Back in the lodging-house, Pen mused on the strange figure of Shandon—a man of multifarious accomplishments, undeniable talent, and humour, yet confined to a gaol for half his time and hanging on a bookseller's coat-tails when free.
