Thus Ayacanora took up her residence in Lucy's cabin, officially accepted as a member of the crew. Yet she proved a troublesome addition, for now commenced in her that perilous transition common to savages encountering white men—a crisis that, within a few years, would accelerate the downfall of the North American tribes. Had not Amyas displayed admirable sense and constancy, Ayacanora might have met a fate far worse than that of Pocahontas, who, despite royal honors at the court of James the First, ultimately died in wretched poverty. For when the savage mind confronts the superior skill, wealth, and wisdom of Europeans, it often loses its self-respect; meanwhile, the body, indulged with luxuries, forfeits its capacity for self-reliance. With these twin losses, the few fragile virtues of the savage dissolve, paving the easy path toward begging, theft, and sloth.

It seemed, at first, that Ayacanora walked swiftly down this road. The warrior-prophetess of the Omaguas appeared transformed into a naughty child; the Diana of the Meta, after satisfying her curiosity with explorations of the ship's decks and cupboards, developed a penchant for stealing and hiding trifles that caught her fancy—too proud or shy to ask. When Amyas forbade taking anything without permission, she threatened to drown herself and sulks all day. Nevertheless, she obeyed, save regarding sweets. Whether by natural craving for forest fruits or otherwise, the Bishop's stores diminished rapidly, as did the sweet Spanish wine Amyas had reserved for sick Lucy. Another severe lecture ensued, wherein Amyas reproached her for robbing the ailing girl. Ayacanora, as usual, threatened suicide, running onto the deck until Amyas caught her and forgave her. This provoked a violent weeping session, accompanied by penitence and promises. A week later, Amyas discovered she had deceived Satan and her conscience by tormenting the Portuguese steward into providing alternative wine. Fortunately, Amyas's warnings about madness proving true, she performed several foolish acts one evening, suffering a bad headache the next morning. Thus the "murder" of the steward was exposed, leading to his sound flogging; yet Ayacanora, honorably, begged him off, offering to be whipped in his stead, confessing that the steward had acted only in fear for his life.

Her own headache and Amyas's cold demeanor served as sufficient lessons. After another suicidal attempt, the wilful beauty settled down temporarily—and remarkably, she never touched fermented liquor again. Meanwhile, Amyas faced numerous challenges in taming a lady who retreated into suicide threats at the slightest provocation. Punishment seemed impossible, and setting her ashore felt sacrilegious, for he sensed divine intent behind her strange affection for the English, believing God entrusted her soul to his charge. Desperate, Amyas prayed repeatedly for guidance, deciding eventually to entrust her education to Parson Jack, intending baptism upon reaching England.

Yet fresh troubles arose: Ayacanora refused to learn from anyone but Amyas himself, who declined the role of schoolmaster. For weeks, she remained untaught aside from the English tongue, which she acquired with remarkable speed. Soon, however, hostilities erupted—not merely toward John Brimblecombe, whose mannerisms she openly mocked, but also against Will Cary, whom she barred from approaching. Perhaps jealousy fueled her animosity toward Cary's intimacy with Amyas, or perhaps she intuitively recognized him as the sole other man capable of courting her. Though Will remained guiltless of any romantic intent, when admonished, Ayacanora declared Cary a "cacique" equal to Amyas, insisting there should not be two rulers. One day, she even proposed killing Cary to claim exclusive ownership of the ship, sulking afterward when Amyas, amidst laughter, revealed her "precious advice" to its intended recipient.

Negroes likewise suffered her disdain, initially evading them, then hurling objects until the terrified Quashies complained to Amyas and secured temporary respite. Among the sailors, she ruled like a princess, summoning them for errands and toys, occasionally enforcing obedience with sharp slaps. The crew, including old Yeo, treated her as a beloved pet, much like a tame leopard whose claws might strike at any moment. While amusing and anxiety-inducing, Ayacanora's presence occupied Amyas's mind, dispelling many somber forebodings about confronting his mother and the tragic news awaiting him.

Eventually, matters reached a climax. Having traversed the West Indies safely, the ship ventured into the Sargasso Sea, where discipline relaxed slightly. Amidst looted treasures—gold, silver, jewels—the crew found two chests containing Mexican featherwork and Spanish/East Indian finery destined for a Caracas noblewoman. Voted to Ayacanora as compensation for her Amazonian prowess and service, she greedily claimed them, carrying them to Lucy's cabin where she knelt gazing upon them. Despising the Mexican garments as "savage," she adored the Spanish attire, appearing on deck dressed in Seville mantillas, Madrid hats, and Indian brocade farthingales for days, basking in sunlight before Amyas's eyes. The crew chuckled; Amyas felt tears rising. Nothing is more poignant than childish vanity, especially when met with ridicule rather than admiration. Amyas hesitated to intervene, but the episode's inherent folly corrected itself. After three days, Ayacanora, unable to suppress her pride, asked Amyas, "Am I now quite an English girl?" Hearing titters behind her, she realized the truth, fled down the companion-ladder, and vanished.

Expecting her to jump overboard, Amyas hurried after her. Locked inside Lucy's cabin, Ayacanora sobbed violently, while Lucy, frightened and hungry, pleaded for answers. Unable to enter, Amyas waited until evening, forcing entry to prevent starvation. Inside, Ayacanora sat amidst torn finery, weeping bitterly, while Lucy mourned alongside her. Amyas attempted comfort, promising future respect, but struggled to articulate further. "So what?" Ayacanora cried. "So *like* a wild girl, Ayacanora!" replied Amyas. Collapsing before him, she implored forgiveness. "Yes, wild girl... poor, bad wild girl..." she confessed. "But I will be an English girl now!" Amyas gently noted that fine clothes alone couldn't make her English. "Not English clothes—an English heart! Like yours!" she insisted, vowing goodness and requesting Sir John's instruction immediately. Amyas agreed, fetching Brimblecombe, who patiently instructed her for an hour that night. From then on, whenever Jack visited, Ayacanora stood attentively, kneeling and praying alongside him, striving to cultivate an English heart. Doubtless, her silent prayers went unheard by none.

Days passed peacefully until one morning, post-Sargasso Sea, chaos erupted below. Men worked on deck, chatting and repairing; Brimblecombe catechized Ayacanora in the cabin; Amyas and Cary smoked cigars, discussing adventures against Spain. Suddenly, commotion surged upward. Jack Brimblecombe burst onto deck, clutching his head, shouting, "Save me! Save me from that she-fiend! Possessed with a legion! Broken my nose, torn out half my hair!" He barricaded himself behind Amyas and Cary as Ayacanora whirled onto deck like a Maenad. Stopping abruptly upon seeing Amyas, she declared, "If you had defied Satan down there, I suspect he wouldn't break out so boldly, Master Jack." Cary laughed, remarking on Jack's innocence. Indignant, Jack protested, while Ayacanora stubbornly claimed, "He looked at me." Cary quipped, "A cat may look at a king." Ayacanora retorted, "But he sha'n't look at Ayacanora. Nobody shall but you, or I'll kill him!" Despite Jack's protests, the fantasy consumed her, preventing her return to lessons. Grumbling, Jack departed minus his hair and sporting a black eye for a week.

"At all events," Cary remarked, relighting his cigar, "it's a fault on the right side." "God grant me grace, lest it prove otherwise," Amyas sighed. As Cary draped his arm around Amyas's neck—disgusting Ayacanora, who retreated to fish line-play—the ship glided silently, save for Ayacanora's laughter chasing flying fish. Tired of idleness, she moved to the poop-rail, listening to John Squire, the armorer, tinkering a helmet and singing a bawdy ballad about Bideford ("mutato nomine"). His mate corrected him, noting Sunderland, not Bideford, as the proper subject. Squire defended his choice, citing harmonies, while Salvation Yeo interjected, criticizing Squire's carnal verses after witnessing divine providences. Urging sacred hymns instead, Yeo opened his psalm-book, chanting Psalm 107, describing ships descending into treacherous seas and beholding the Lord's wonders. Squire dismissed this as overly serious, preferring merry catches like "Rumbelow"—even during anchor-heaving. Yeo countered that praising the Lord suited immortal souls, contrasting "randy, dandy" tunes with solemn psalms.
