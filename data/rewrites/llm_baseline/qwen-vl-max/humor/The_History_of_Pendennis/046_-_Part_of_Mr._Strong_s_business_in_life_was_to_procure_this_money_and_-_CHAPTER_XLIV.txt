CHAPTER XLIV. In Which the Colonel Tells a Tale (Or Two) of His Adventures

The morning after the dinner at Grosvenor Place—where Colonel Altamont had made a grand entrance like a man who’d just won the lottery and lost his mind—he emerged from his bedroom at Shepherd’s Inn, looking as if he’d wrestled with a bear in his sleep and lost. He stomped into Captain Strong’s sitting room, where the Chevalier was already deep in the newspaper, puffing on a cigar like a man who believed smoking was an art form best practiced before breakfast.

Strong was the kind of fellow who could turn any lodging house into a palace. He’d already polished off a breakfast fit for a king—fried eggs, broiled rashers, and all the trimmings—prepared by Mr. Grady, whose culinary skills were so precise they might have been approved by a French chef. Good-natured, talkative, and utterly incapable of being alone, Strong would’ve welcomed even a badger into his home if it meant someone to chat with. Though he didn’t exactly *like* Altamont—indeed, he’d have cheered if the Colonel had suddenly vanished into thin air (a fate Sir Francis Clavering had long desired)—he kept up appearances. They were neighbors, after all, and neighbors don’t usually go around wishing each other dead unless there’s a particularly strong motive.

The night before, Strong had seen Altamont to bed with polite concern, taken away his candle to prevent fire hazards, and discovered that the spirit bottle he’d counted on for a midnight pick-me-up was empty. No matter. He drank a glass of water instead—because, really, what’s a little thirst compared to dignity?—and went to sleep like a man who’d never known stress. He woke refreshed, rosy-cheeked, and ready to face whatever the day threw at him: war, prison, or a particularly awkward conversation with a colonel who looked suspiciously like a man who’d dyed his whiskers and was hiding something under his wig.

Altamont’s first act upon entering the room was to bellow at Grady for a pint of pale ale. The servant delivered it promptly. The Colonel poured it into a pewter flagon, then into his mouth, drained it, set the tankard down, took a deep breath, wiped his mouth with his dressing gown (which, incidentally, contrasted sharply with his dyed whiskers—something Strong had noticed but wisely chosen not to comment on), and declared himself “immensely refreshed.”

“Nothing like that beer,” he announced, “when the coppers are hot.” He paused. “Many a day I’ve drunk a dozen of Bass at Calcutta, and—and——”

“And at Lucknow, I suppose?” Strong said with a grin. “I got the beer for you on purpose. Knew you’d need it after last night.”

Altamont launched into a tale of woe and whiskey.

“I cannot help myself,” he said, beating his head with a hand the size of a ham. “I’m a madman when I get the liquor on board me. Ain’t fit to be trusted with a spirit-bottle. Once I start, I can’t stop until it’s gone. And once it’s gone, Lord knows what I say or don’t say.”

He sighed. “I dined quietly here last night. Grady gave me two tumblers—just two—and I intended to spend the evening at the Black and Red as sober as a parson. But why did you leave that confounded sample-bottle of Hollands out, Strong? Grady had to go out, and left the kettle boiling for tea. It was no use. I couldn’t resist. Washed it all down, sir, by Jove. And I suspect I had more later at that infernal little thieves’ den.”

“What, were you there too?” Strong asked. “Before you came to Grosvenor Place? That was early.”

“Early hours to be drunk and cleared out before nine o’clock, eh? But so it was. Yes, like a great big fool, I must go there. Found the fellows dining—Blackland, young Moss, and half a dozen other rogues. If we’d gone to Rouge et Noir, I must have won. But we didn’t try the black and red. No, hang ’em—they knew I’d beat ’em. So they played hazard on the dining table instead. And I dropped all the money you gave me this morning. Be hanged to my luck. That’s what set me wild. I remember thinking I’d go get more from Clavering. Then… well, then I don’t remember much until I woke this morning and heard old Bows at No. 4 playing the piano.”

Strong lit his cigar with a coal and mused. “I should like to know how you always draw money from Clavering, Colonel.”

Altamont burst out laughing. “Ha, ha! He owes it to me.”

“I don’t know that that’s a reason with Frank,” Strong replied. “He owes plenty besides you.”

“Well, he gives it to me because he loves me like a brother,” the Colonel said, grinning like a man who’d just stolen a pie and was pretending it was a gift. “You know he does, Captain.—No?—He don’t?—Well, perhaps he don’t. And if you ask me no questions, perhaps I’ll tell you no lies, Captain Strong—put that in your pipe and smoke it, my boy.”

“But I’ll give up that confounded brandy-bottle,” Altamont continued, after a pause. “Must give it up, or it’ll be the ruin of me.”

“It makes you say queer things,” said Strong, eyeing him carefully. “Remember what you said last night at Clavering’s table.”

“Say? What did I say?” Altamont asked, suddenly alarmed. “Did I split anything? Dammy, Strong, did I split anything?”

“Ask me no questions, and I will tell you no lies,” Strong replied, quoting back the phrase with a smirk. He thought of the words Altamont had muttered—words that had sent him fleeing from Clavering’s house the moment he saw Major Pendennis. But Strong decided not to press the issue. “No,” he said finally, “you didn’t split, Colonel. It was only a trap of mine to see if I could make you speak. But you didn’t say a word anyone could understand—you were too far gone for that.”

So much the better, thought Altamont, and heaved a great sigh of relief. Strong noticed, but said nothing. The Colonel, now in full storytelling mode, pressed on.

“Yes, I own to my faults,” he said. “There are three things I can’t resist, no matter what: a bottle of brandy, a box of dice, and a beautiful woman. No man of pluck and spirit ever could, as I know of. There’s hardly a country in the world where those three haven’t gotten me into trouble.”

“Indeed?” said Strong.

“From the age of fifteen, when I ran away from home and became a cabin-boy on an Indiaman, till now, when I’m nearly fifty, women have always been my downfall. Why, one of them—black eyes, jewels, battens and ermine like a duchess—she swept off the best part of a thousand pounds I’d brought with me.”

He leaned forward. “Didn’t I ever tell you about it? Well, I don’t mind. At first I was cautious. Had a lot of money, kept it close, lived like a gentleman—Colonel Altamont, Meurice’s hotel, the whole shebang. Never played except at public tables, and won more than I lost. Then I met a chap at the hotel—a swell, white kid gloves, tuft on his chin—Bloundell-Bloundell, his name was. Asked me to dinner, took me to Madame the Countess de Foljambe’s soirées. Such a woman, Strong! Such an eye! Such a hand at the piano! Lor bless you, she’d sit down and sing, gaze at you, until she warbled your soul right out of your body.”

He paused. “She invited me to her parties every Tuesday. I took opera boxes, gave her dinners at the restaurateur’s—what else? But it wasn’t the dinners or the opera boxes that cost Clavering’s money. No, be hanged to it—it was swept off another way.”

One night at the Countess’s, several of them were at supper—Mr. Bloundell-Bloundell, the Honourable Deuceace, the Marky de la Tour de Force—all top-tier nobility, the height of fashion. Champagne flowed, then brandy. The Countess mixed punch for him. Cards followed. He played and drank until he didn’t know what he was doing. “I was like I was last night,” he said. “I was taken away and put to bed somehow. Woke the next day with a roaring headache, and my servant told me the Honourable Deuceace wanted to see me.”

“‘How are you, Colonel?’ he said, stepping into my bedroom. ‘How long did you stay after I left? The game was getting too high for me—I’d lost enough to you for one night.’”

“‘To me?’ I said. ‘How’s that, my dear feller?’—though he was an Earl’s son, we were as familiar as you and me. ‘How’s that?’ I said, and he told me he’d borrowed thirty louis at vingt-et-un, gave me an I.O.U., which I tucked into my pocket-book before he left.”

I pulled it out—Countess had worked it for me—and sure enough, there it was. He paid me thirty louis in gold right there at my bedside. I said he was a gentleman, offered him a drink—no, he doesn’t drink in the morning—and he left.

Then came another knock. This time it was Bloundell-Bloundell and the Marky.

“‘Bong jour, Marky,’ I said. ‘Good morning—no headache?’”

“‘No,’ he said. ‘But Deuceace has been here. We met him in the Palais Royal. Has he settled with you? Get it while you can—he’s slippery. And since he won three ponies from Bloundell, I recommend you collect your money while he still has some.’”

“‘He’s paid me,’ I said. ‘But I didn’t know he owed me anything. I don’t remember lending him thirty louis.’”

They exchanged glances. Bloundell said, “Colonel, you’re a queer feller. No man could’ve guessed you’d had more than tea all night. Yet you forget everything in the morning. Come, come—tell that to the marines.”

“‘En efet,’ said the Marky, twirling his little black mustache in the mirror. ‘Let us speak of affairs. Colonel, you understand that matters of honour are best settled at once. Perhaps it won’t inconvenience you to settle our little matters from last night.’”

“‘What little matters?’ I asked. ‘Do you owe me money, Marky?’”

“‘Bah!’ he said. ‘No more jesting. I have your note of hand for three hundred and forty louis. La voia!’ And he pulled it out.”

“‘And mine for two hundred and ten,’ said Bloundell, pulling out his own.”

I leapt out of bed, wrapped my dressing gown around me. “Are you here to make a fool of me? I don’t owe you two hundred, or two thousand, or two louis. I won’t pay a farthing. Do you think I’ll be caught with your notes of hand? I laugh at them—and at you. I believe you’re a couple——”

“‘A couple of what?’ asked Bloundell. ‘You’re aware, Colonel Altamont, that we are men of honour. We’re not here to trifle or listen to abuse. You will either pay us—or we will expose you as a cheat, and chastise you as a cheat, too.’”

“‘Oui, parbleu,’ said the Marky—but I didn’t care. I could’ve thrown him out the window. But Blound ell—big man, three stone heavier, six inches taller—he could’ve done for me.”

“‘Monsieur will pay, or Monsieur will give me the reason why. I believe you’re little better than a polisson, Colonel Altamont.’”

That was the phrase he used. Altamont grinned. “I got plenty more of that language. I was in the thick of it when another friend arrived—one I’d met at Boulogne, hadn’t played the night before, actually warned me against Bloundell and the others. I told him the story. He said: ‘I’m very sorry. You insisted on large stakes. The Countess begged you to stop. These gentlemen offered repeatedly to quit. It was you who pushed.’”

He charged me dead. When they left, he told me the Marky would shoot me for sure. “She hates these two men,” he said. “She’s warned you repeatedly against them.” And then—this is the kicker—he said: “It’s my belief that that woman is distractedly in love with you.”

“Do you think so?” I asked.

And he told me she’d actually knelt before him and said, “Save Colonel Altamont!”

As soon as I was dressed, I went to see her. She shrieked and nearly fainted. Called me Ferdinand. I’m blest if she didn’t.

“I thought your name was Jack,” Strong said, laughing.

“A man may have more names than one, mayn’t he?” Altamont asked, blushing behind his dyed whiskers. “When I’m with a lady, I like to take a good one. She called me by my Christian name. She cried fit to break your heart. I can’t stand seeing a woman cry—not when I’m fond of her. She said she couldn’t bear the thought of my losing so much money in her house. Wouldn’t I take her diamonds and necklaces and pay part?”

I swore I wouldn’t touch a farthing’s worth. Maybe I didn’t think they were worth much—but what can a woman do more than give you her all? That’s the sort I like. And I told her not to worry—the money was nothing. I wouldn’t pay a single farthing.

“Then they’ll shoot you,” she said. “They’ll kill my Ferdinand.”

“They’ll kill my Jack wouldn’t have sounded well in French,” Strong said, laughing.

“Never mind names,” said Altamont, sulkily. “A man of honour may take any name he chooses, I suppose.”

“Go on,” said Strong.

“She said they’d kill me.”

“No,” I said. “They won’t. I won’t let that scamp of a Marquis send me out of the world. If he lays a hand on me, I’ll brain him, Marquis as he is.”

At this, the Countess shrank back. “Do I understand Colonel Altamont aright?” she said. “That a British officer refuses to meet any person who provokes him to the field of honour?”

“Field of honour be hanged, Countess,” I said. “You wouldn’t have me be a target for that little scoundrel’s pistol practice.”

“Colonel Altamont,” she said, “I thought you were a man of honour—I thought, I—but no matter. Good-bye, sir.”

And she swept out, voice choking in her handkerchief.

“Countess!” I shouted, rushing after her, seizing her hand.

“Leave me, Monsieur le Colonel,” she said, shaking me off. “My father was a General of the Grand Army. A soldier should know how to pay all his debts of honour.”

What could I do? Everyone was against me. Caroline said I’d lost the money—though I didn’t remember a thing. I’d taken Deuceace’s money too—but he’d offered it, so that was different. Every one of these chaps was a man of fashion and honour. The Marky and the Countess were of the first families in France. And, by Jove, rather than offend her, I paid the money—five hundred and sixty gold Napoleons—plus three hundred I lost when I had my revenge.

And I can’t tell you at this minute whether I was done or not,” concluded Altamont, musing. “Sometimes I think I was. But then Caroline was so fond of me. That woman would never have seen me done. Never. I’m sure she wouldn’t. At least, if she would, I’m deceived in woman.”

Just as Altamont was about to dive into further revelations, a knock echoed through the chamber. Grady opened the door—and in walked none other than Sir Francis Clavering.

“The Governor, by Jove,” Strong said, surprised. “What’s brought you here?”

“Not good, I warrant,” growled Altamont, glaring at the Baronet under his heavy eyebrows. And indeed, good rarely brought Sir Francis Clavering anywhere.

Whenever he appeared at Shepherd’s Inn, it was money that lured him. There was usually a money-lender waiting in Strong’s chambers—or Campion’s below—with bills to negotiate or renew. Clavering was a man who’d never faced his debts honestly. As long as he could renew a bill, his conscience stayed clear. He’d sign almost anything for tomorrow, so long as today remained untouched. Fortune couldn’t save him permanently—he was built to be ruined, to cheat small tradesmen, to be duped by sharper rogues, to be niggardly and reckless, as dishonest as those who cheated him. He was a dupe because he was too mean to be a successful villain. He’d told more lies, endured more shameful schemes to avoid a debt, than enough to fund a fortune for a braver rogue. Even at his peak, he was abject and shuffling. Had he been a Crown Prince, he couldn’t have been weaker, more useless, more dissolute, or more ungrateful. He couldn’t move through life without leaning on someone else—and yet he distrusted every agent he hired, sabotaged plans meant to help him, and secretly undermined those he employed.

Strong knew Clavering. He judged him correctly. They weren’t friends. The Chevalier worked for his patron—as he might have marched through a siege or endured hardship in battle—because it was his duty, because he’d agreed to it. “What does he want?” the officers of the Shepherd’s Inn garrison wondered whenever the Baronet arrived.

Clavering’s face was pale, angry. “So, sir,” he said, addressing Altamont, “you’ve been at your old tricks.”

“Which ones?” asked Altamont, sneering.

“You’ve been at the Rouge et Noir. You were there last night,” Clavering screamed.

“How do you know?” Altamont asked. “Were you there? I was at the Club—but not on the colours. Ask the Captain—I’ve been telling him about it. It was with the bones. Hazard, Sir Francis, upon my word and honour it was.”

He looked at Clavering with mock humility so knowing it only made the Baronet angrier.

“What the deuce do I care, sir, how a man like you loses his money—whether it’s hazard or roulette?” Clavering yelled, spewing oaths. “What I will not have is that you use my name, or couple it with yours. Damn him, Strong, why don’t you keep him in better order? He’s drawn a bill on me, lost the money at the table—I can’t stand it! Flesh and blood won’t bear it. Do you know how much I’ve paid for you, sir?”

“This was only a little’un, Sir Francis—only fifteen pounds, Captain Strong. They wouldn’t stand another. And it oughtn’t to anger you, Governor. Why, it’s so trifling I didn’t even mention it to Strong—did I now, Captain? I protest it had quite slipped my memory, all on account of that confounded liquor I took.”

“Liquor or no liquor, sir, it’s no business of mine. I don’t care what you drink, or where you drink it—only it shan’t be in my house. And I will not have you breaking into my house of a night, a fellow like you intruding on my company. How dared you show yourself in Grosvenor Place last night, sir—and what do you suppose my friends must think of me when they see a man of your sort walking into my dining-room uninvited, drunk, calling for liquor as if you were the master of the house?”

“They’ll think you know some very queer sort of people, I dare say,” Altamont said, impenetrably cheerful. “Look here, Baronet, I apologise. On my honour I do. And ain’t an apology enough between two gentlemen? It was a strong measure, walking into your cuddy and calling for drink as if I was the Captain. But I’d had too much before—that’s why I wanted more. Nothing could be simpler. And it was because they wouldn’t give me more money on your name at the Black and Red that I thought I’d come down and speak to you about it. To refuse me was nothing. But to refuse a bill drawn on you—after all you’ve done for the shop, and being a baronet, member of parliament, gentleman and no mistake—Damme, it’s ungrateful.”

“By heavens, if ever you do it again—if ever you dare show yourself in my house, or give my name at a gambling-house or any other house, by Jove—or give any reference to me, or speak to me in the street, by God, or anywhere else until I speak to you—I disclaim you altogether. I won’t give you another shilling.”

“Governor, don’t be provoking,” Altamont said surlily. “Don’t talk to me about daring to do this or that. Or when my dander’s up, it’s the very thing to urge me on. I oughtn’t to have come last night—I know I oughtn’t. But I told you I was drunk. And that ought to be sufficient between gentleman and gentleman.”

“You a gentleman! Dammy, sir,” said the Baronet, “how dares a fellow like you call himself a gentleman?”
