The chamber was a cage of luxury, the floor strewn with the spotted and striped hides of beasts, a testament to wealth that felt less like comfort and more like a gilded trap. Within this room, a lady sat rigid, her arms stretched forward as if bracing against an invisible storm. Her face was a canvas of conflicting emotions, flushing pale and then crimson in rapid succession, her foot tapping a frantic, restless rhythm against the rug.

Suddenly, the heavy curtain parted, drawn back by a silent servant. The lady's brow flared with alarm.

"Altezza," the maid whispered, her voice trembling with awe-struck reverence, "the man is here."

With a sharp command, the lady signaled for entry. In a blur of motion, she snatched a small black mask from the table and pressed it over her face. Instantly, the flush drained from her cheeks, leaving her skin the color of marble. The stark contrast between the jet-black mask and her pallid features created a visage that was both strange and terrifying.

A man entered, bowing and scraping with the mechanical precision of a man made of the very crowds he served. He was unremarkable—short-haired, with a roundish head and plain, decent clothing. His features were neither handsome nor forbidding, save for one thing: a singularly restless eye that darted about the room.

He bowed profusely until he stood directly opposite the masked figure, waiting for her pleasure.

"They have told you for what you are wanted?" she asked, her voice cool despite the mask.

"Yes, Signora," he replied.

"Did those who spoke to you agree as to what you are to receive?"

"Yes, Signora. 'Tis the full price; and purchases the greater vendetta: unless of your benevolence you choose to content yourself with the lesser."

"I understand you not," she said, her tone sharpening.

"Ah; this is the Signora's first. The lesser vendetta, lady, is the death of the body only. We watch our man come out of a church; or take him in an innocent hour; and so deal with him. In the greater vendetta, we watch him, and catch him hot from some unrepented sin, and so slay his soul as well as his body. But this vendetta is not so run upon now as it was a few years ago."

"Man, silence his tongue, and let his treasonable heart beat no more. But his soul I have no feud with."

"So be it, signora. He who spoke to me knew not the man, nor his name, nor his abode. From whom shall I learn these?"

"From myself."

At this, the man, showing the first flicker of anxiety, entreated her to be cautious and particular.

"Fear me not," she said. "Listen. It is a young man, tall of stature, with auburn hair and dark blue eyes, and an honest face that would deceive a saint. He lives in the Via Claudia, at the corner house; the glover's. In that house there lodge but three males: he; and a painter short of stature and dark-visaged, and a young, slim boy. He that hath betrayed me is a stranger, fair, and taller than thou art."

The bravo listened with rapt attention. "It is enough," he said.

"Stay, Signora; haunteth he any secret place where I may deal with him?"

"My spy doth report me he hath of late frequented the banks of the Tiber after dusk; doubtless to meet his light o' love, who calls me her rival; even there slay him! and let my rival come and find him; the smooth, heartless, insolent traitor."

"Be calm, signora. He will betray no more ladies."

"I know not that. He weareth a sword, and can use it. He is young and resolute."

"Neither will avail him."

"Are ye so sure of your hand? What are your weapons?"

The bravo produced a steel gauntlet. "We strike with such force we need must guard our hand. This is our mallet." He then undid his doublet, revealing a coat of mail beneath, and finally laid his glittering stiletto on the table with a flourish.

The lady shuddered at first, but presently took it up in her white hand and tested its point against her finger.

"Beware, madam," warned the bravo.

"What? Is it poisoned?"

"Saints forbid! We steal no lives. We take them with steel point, not drugs. But 'tis newly ground, and I feared for the Signora's white skin."

"His skin is as white as mine," she said, a sudden, fleeting gleam of pity crossing her masked face. It vanished as quickly as it came. "But his heart is black as soot. Say, do I not well to remove a traitor that slanders me?"

"The signora will settle that with her confessor. I am but a tool in noble hands; like my stiletto."

The princess appeared not to hear the speaker. "Oh, how I could have loved him; to the death; as now I hate him. Fool! he will learn to trifle with princes; to spurn them and fawn on them, and prefer the scum of the town to them, and make them a by-word." She looked up, her eyes burning behind the mask. "Why loiter'st thou here? haste thee, revenge me."

"It is customary to pay half the price beforehand, Signora."

"Ah! I forgot; thy revenge is bought. Here is more than half," she said, pushing a heavy bag across the table to him. "When the blow is struck, come for the rest."

"You will soon see me again, signora."

And he retired, bowing and scraping once more.

The princess remained seated, burning with jealousy, mortified pride, and a deep dread of exposure—for until she knew Gerard, no public stain had fallen on her. She sat there, masked, with her arms straight out before her, the nails of her clenched hand digging into the wood of the table.

So sat the fabled Sphinx; so sits a tigress.

Yet, as the assassin faded from sight, a chill crept over her. Moody misgivings heaved within her chest, precursors of vain remorse. Gerard and Margaret were before their age. This was the true medieval world: proud, amorous, vindictive, generous, foolish, cunning, impulsive, unprincipled—and ignorant as dirt.

Power was the curse of such a creature. Forced to do her own crimes, the weakness of her nerves would have balanced the violence of her passions, and her bark would have been worse than her bite. But power gave a feeble, furious woman male instruments. And the effect was as terrible as the combination was unnatural.

In this instance, it had whetted an assassin's dagger for a poor, forlorn wretch just meditating suicide.
