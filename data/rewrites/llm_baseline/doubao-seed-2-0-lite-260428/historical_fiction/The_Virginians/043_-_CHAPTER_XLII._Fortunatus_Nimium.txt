Though Harry Warrington clung stubbornly to the dismal promise his cousin had wrung from him, no benevolent reader could mistake the engagement for anything but a heavy millstone hung about the young Virginian’s neck. He had no taste for the match, and longed heartily to be free of it. It was very possible he had schemed to bring that very freedom about when he picked a fight and thrashed poor Will Esmond: a proper boxing match would surely spark a family feud, he reasoned. Maria might take her brother’s side in the quarrel, Harry would refuse all apology, Will would be forced to call him out to a duel, and Harry, certain he was the better man with both fists and a blade, would soon be released from his vow by either victory or honourable wounds. The ruse went off without a hitch—until the very end. The explosion of the brawl left no lasting damage beyond Will Esmond’s swollen nose and a black eye that lingered for a full week. He never summoned the courage to send his cousin a challenge, so neither man killed the other, and neither was killed. Will was knocked down, and he simply got up again: a course of action many a sensible gentleman would choose, could he settle a petty score in private, no one the wiser about who came off worse.

Far from rallying to her brother’s side, Maria declared wholeheartedly for Harry, as did Lord Castlewood himself once he heard the full tale. The chaplain confirmed it was Will who struck the first blow, dead drunk at that—he’d quarrelled in his cups a dozen times before, Castlewood pointed out, and Harry had only acted like a gentleman in resenting the injury. It was Will, not Harry, who ought to beg pardon. Harry refused all reconciliation, though, until his stolen horse was returned and the disputed bet paid. The chronicler has never found a single surviving letter or account that clarifies how that wager was finally settled, but it is certain the cousins soon met again across drawing rooms at half a dozen different friends’ houses, no blows exchanged between them.

At first, Castlewood had been perfectly content to see his sister marry the young Virginian. Maria had remained unmarried for decades, the hem of her long life’s gown snagged on brambles, caked in mud, frayed and stained by years of small misadventures, and any bridegroom who would take her was a blessing. A gentleman from the American colonies was even better: she could retire to his plantation “wigwam” across the Atlantic and be comfortably disposed of, and young Harry would surely outlive his elderly bride by decades, free to console himself however he pleased after she was gone. That all changed, though, the moment Castlewood paid a call on Aunt Bernstein in London. He came away not only trying to dissuade Maria from the match, but professing loud pity for the young man doomed to a lifetime of misery over a silly promise made at one-and-twenty!

Misery, indeed! Maria could not fathom why Harry ought to be miserable. Pity, forsooth! Her brother had never spared a thought for pity before he visited Bernstein. She saw right through the game: their aunt had dangled cold hard cash before Castlewood to break the match off. Harry was a man of honour, she declared, and she could trust him to keep his word.

Off Castlewood stormed then, heading for White’s and the other haunts he frequented, his mind seething with bitter self-reproach. “And so,” he thought, “all my fine ‘virtue’ has done is hand this rich young lamb over to every other wolf on the hunt. There is no earthly use in my sparing him now. That old schoolmaster’s adage runs, *Quem deus vult perdere*—whom the gods would destroy, they first make mad. If I do not fleece him myself, someone else will, that is plain. My brother Will has already taken a petty slice of his fortune, my sister means to swallow the whole of him bodily. Here I have been playing the righteous guardian, refusing to play for stakes higher than a sixpence, protecting his youth and innocence, and what for? I am a fool fattening a fine goose just for other people to carve! I have done very few good deeds in my life, and the only one I thought I had to my credit benefits nothing but strangers. Talk of remorse! By all the fires and furies, the only remorse I feel is for the wicked things I did not do, when I had the chance! Why did I spare Lucretia years ago? She hated me ever after, and her husband went straight to the ruin he was always predestined for. Why did I let this lad off easy? So that March and the rest of the rakes, who do not even care half as much about him as I do, can pluck him bare? Everyone calls me the wicked lord, points me out in the street, warns their sons to keep clear of me, and I am not a penny worse than any of my neighbours, only far unluckier. My cursed softhearted weakness has been my greatest enemy all my life.”

Of course, a chronicler who sets down a gentleman’s unspoken thoughts risks overdraughting the patience of his readers, who are not obliged to believe every word. But did not Livy, Thucydides, and a score of other ancient historians compose entire speeches for their heroes that those men never spoke aloud? With as much right, we transcribe the inner thoughts of Lord Castlewood, whose character we know as intimately as we know the lines of our own palms. A whole pack of wolves are hunting this lamb to make their meal of him—shall one hungry old hunter stand aside and not claim even a single cutlet? Who has not admired that famous speech of Lord Clive’s, when men reproached him on his return from India for seizing too many jaghires, lakhs, gold mohurs, diamonds and pearls? “Upon my life,” the hero of Plassy said, “when I think of all my opportunities, I am surprised I took so little!”

It pains a feeling mind to tell unkind tales about a gentleman until necessity compels it, and so the chronicler has held back much of the unpleasant truth about Lord Castlewood, letting the candid reader see only that he was a nobleman no good man ought to like. It is true that Lord March and the rest of the rakes Castlewood envied would have thought no more of stripping Harry Warrington of his last shilling at the gaming table than they would think of picking clean a chicken’s bones. They would take every advantage their superior skill at cards, at dice, or at the Newmarket races gave them, including private information about the horses running—after all, that was the accepted way of gentlemen in that era. But at least they played fair, and if they lost, they paid their debts.

Madame Bernstein had been loath to tell her favourite Virginian nephew all she knew of his family’s discredit. She had even been touched by Castlewood’s apparent forbearance when Harry first arrived in Europe, pleased that he had complied with her wishes to treat the boy gently. But during their conversation about Maria’s designs on Harry, Castlewood had spoken with his usual brutal cynicism, calling himself a fool for sparing a lad whom no amount of mercy could save from ruin anyway. He’d pointed to Harry’s reckless late nights at faro and hazard, his mad cross-country rides to Newmarket, his spendthrift companions, and demanded to know why he alone ought to keep his hands off the young man. Madame Bernstein’s protest that Harry was not actually as rich as he seemed was waved off with a snort. Bah! Harry was heir to a Virginian estate vast enough to be worth a fortune that could restore their whole ruined family, Castlewood declared, and there were a dozen money-lenders in London who would advance him thousands on post-obit bonds any day. Castlewood spoke from bitter experience: he had applied to those very sharks in his father’s lifetime, and the cursed crew had eaten up two-thirds of his already meagre income.

His desperate ill humour made Madame Bernstein thoroughly alarmed for Harry, and she determined to caution him at the very first opportunity. That very evening she picked up her pen to write him a note, but she had always been slow and clumsy with writing, and hated the task. “I never knew any good come of writing more than a short bon jour or a note of business,” she used to say. “What is the use of writing poorly, when so many clever people do it well? And even they would be wiser to leave the pen alone.” So instead she sent one of her footmen to Harry’s lodgings, bidding him come drink a dish of tea with her the next day, so she might give him her warning. But the next morning she was taken ill, and could not receive him when he called. She kept her chamber for two days more, then there was a great formal engagement she could not skip, and by the time she was free Harry had set off on some jaunt of his own. In the constant whirl of London society, how rarely does a man see his neighbour, a brother his sister, an old schoolfellow his long-lost friend? Weeks passed before Mr. Warrington and his aunt finally got the confidential conversation she had so long planned.

She began by scolding him gently for his extravagance and madcap frolics, though in truth she was secretly charmed by his bold, high-spirited ways. Harry laughed it off, saying young men will be young men, and it was his constant attendance on her that had introduced him to the set of wild companions he spent most of his time with these days. Then she prefaced her warning about Lord Castlewood with much hesitation, and Harry burst into a bitter laugh, saying the good-natured gossips of London had told him every wicked story there was to tell about his cousin already. “To say of a man of his lordship’s rank, or of any gentleman, ‘Do not play with him,’ is more than I like to do,” she continued, “but—”

“Oh, say on, aunt!” Harry exclaimed, almost swearing under his breath.
“Then you have already played cards with your cousin?” the old worldly monitress asked.
“And lost and won, madam!” Harry replied, gallant as ever. “It does not become me to say which. When we have a bout with a neighbour back in Virginia, over a bottle, a pack of cards, or a quarrel, we do not run home to our mothers to tattle. No offence intended, aunt!” Blushing, the handsome young fellow stepped forward and kissed her wrinkled cheek. He looked every inch the hero, draped in a new suit of velvet stitched with gold thread, his fair face glowing, his fine hair powdered, lace ruffs spilling over his wrists. On taking his leave, he pressed a generous handful of coins into the palms of every one of her servants who crowded to see him out.

It was a cold rainy winter’s day, and to spare his fine silk stockings from the mud, Harry called for a sedan chair. “To White’s!” he called to the chairmen, and off they carried him to the club where he spent more and more of his days. His well-wishers might have hoped he would have been a less constant frequenter of that gambling den, but to Harry’s credit, once he sat down at the tables he fought his battles like a true gentleman. He was never flustered by a run of good luck, and perfectly calm when the odds turned against him. Fortune is proverbially fickle to gamblers, but more gamblers are fickle to Fortune: they run away frightened the moment she favours them, deserting her for no reason but cowardice, so that she leaves them for good.

“By George, Mr. Warrington!” Mr. Selwyn cried one night, roused to rare enthusiasm. “You deserve to win! You treat your luck as a gentleman should, and as long as she stays with you, you behave to her with the most perfect politeness. *Si celeres quatit pennas*—you know the rest? No? Well, you are none the worse off for it. You will bow to her ladyship as she steps into her coach, and wave her off politely. Look at Lord Castlewood yonder, passing the dice box. Did you ever hear a man curse and rave so over losing five or six measly pieces of gold? Fortune must be a cruel jade indeed if she ever grants her favours for long to a niggardly cur like that!”

“We do not consider members of our own family to be canaille, sir,” Harry said sharply, “and Lord Castlewood is one of my blood.”

“I forgot, I forgot! I beg your pardon,” his neighbour at the hazard table said. “And let me offer you my compliments on that relation of yours, and his brother Will Esmond, too! The box is mine now. Five’s the main! Deuce-ace, there’s my usual cursed luck. *Virtute mea me involvo!*” he sighed, sinking back into his chair.

We cannot say for certain if it was on this very night that Harry threw the fifteen mains in a row that Horace Walpole mentioned in one of his surviving letters, but it is undeniable that in his first weeks at White’s, Harry enjoyed a run of prodigious luck that seemed to make him the very personification of the Fortunate Youth. The five hundred pounds he had withdrawn from his patrimonial estate in Virginia multiplied into thousands. He bought the finest broadcloth and velvet suits, purchased blooded racehorses, gave grand dinner parties, lavished handsome presents on his friends, and lived as if he were as rich as Sir James Lowther or the Duke of Bedford—and yet his pile of gold never seemed to shrink. No wonder he gave so freely, when giving was so easy, no wonder he carried a Fortunatus’s purse that never emptied. Other men who stumble on such endless luck tie the purse strings tight, drink small beer, and burn cheap tallow candles to save pennies—but that was never Harry’s nature.

In the middle of this golden run, he took the trouble to find out the full sum of Lady Maria’s debts, and paid every last shilling of them off. He even lavished magnificent gifts on Maria’s cold stepmother and half-sister, who had never liked her. “Had you not better get yourself arrested for debt, Will?” my lord said sardonically to his brother. “Even though you bit him for that paltry sum on the horse bet, the ‘Mohock’ will certainly bail you out of pawn.” It was then that Will Esmond felt a true, if far from humble, repentance. “Confound it,” he groaned, “to think I let this fellow slip away for no more than forty pounds! He was good for at least a thousand.”

As for Maria, that generous soul accepted the unexpected fortune with a grateful heart, and stood ready to accept as much more as Harry cared to give her. No sooner had he paid off all her old debts to milliners, modistes, and purveyors than she began running up new ones. Her lady’s maid Betty went the rounds of every shop in town, informing tradespeople that her mistress was about to marry a young gentleman of immense colonial fortune, so they might extend her unlimited credit. The tradesfolk had heard that same story two or three times before, so they did not give it full credit—but Harry was paying the old bills promptly, so they obliged her readily. She even ordered fresh supplies from Mrs. Pincott, of Kensington, the shopkeeper she had once quarrelled with, and when she drove from the mercer to the toy shop to the jeweller in her coach, with her maid and Harry sitting beside her, all London shopkeepers agreed she was a fortunate woman to have secured the Fortunate Youth—though most wondered at the young man’s strange taste in choosing a bride so far past her first bloom.

Mr. Sparks, the jeweller of Tavistock Street in Covent Garden, even waited on Harry at his Bond Street lodgings one morning, carrying the pearl necklace and gold etwee Harry had picked out for Maria the day before, asking if he ought to leave them there or send them to her ladyship with his compliments. Harry added a fine ring from the stock Sparks had brought along with him, bade the jeweller send him the full bill, and dismissed him with a majestic wave of the hand. Sparks bowed so low he nearly scraped the floor, even nodding respectfully to Harry’s Black servant Gumbo as he left.

Harry’s bounties did not end there. A few days later he drove up to Sparks’s shop in his new phaeton, and bought two fine trinkets for the two young ladies, Hetty and Theo, who had been so kind to him in his earlier days in England. “Ah,” he thought, “how I wish I had my poor brother George’s wit and talent for poetry! I would send these presents with pretty verses for them. If goodwill and real affection could make a poet of me, I should have no trouble at all finding rhymes.” So he called in Parson Sampson, and the two of them concocted the little accompanying billet together.
