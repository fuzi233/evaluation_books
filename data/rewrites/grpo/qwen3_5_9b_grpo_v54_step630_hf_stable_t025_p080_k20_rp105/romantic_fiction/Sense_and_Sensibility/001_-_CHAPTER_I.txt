The Dashwoods were a family of substance, long rooted in the county of Sussex. Their home, Norland Park, sat proudly in the heart of their extensive lands, a residence that for generations had commanded the warm regard of their neighbors. The estate's late proprietor, a bachelor who lived to a ripe old age, had found his final years filled with the companionship of his sister, who served as his constant housekeeper until her death a decade prior. Grieving her loss, he opened his doors to his nephew, Mr. Henry Dashwood, the rightful heir, along with his wife and their three daughters. In the lively company of his nephew's family, the old gentleman's days became a tapestry of comfort. The unwavering kindness of Mr. and Mrs. Henry Dashwood, driven by genuine affection rather than mere duty, provided him with the solace age craves, while the boundless cheerfulness of the children seasoned his existence with delight.

Mr. Henry Dashwood had one son from a previous marriage, a steady and respectable youth who, upon reaching maturity, inherited a substantial fortune from his mother and further augmented it through his own marriage. Consequently, the succession to Norland held less weight for him than for his three sisters. Their mother possessed no dowry, and their father held only seven thousand pounds in his own name; the remainder of his first wife's fortune was legally secured to their brother, leaving him with only a life interest.

When the old gentleman passed, his will was read, bringing a mixture of relief and profound disappointment. He was neither unjust nor ungrateful in leaving the estate to his nephew, yet the conditions attached to the bequest stripped it of nearly half its value. Mr. Dashwood had coveted the property primarily for the sake of his wife and daughters, hoping to secure their future. However, the will stipulated that the estate pass to him and his son, and to the son's unborn child—a toddler of four years old. This arrangement left Mr. Dashwood powerless to provide for those he loved most, barring any charges against the estate or the sale of its valuable timberlands. The entire property was effectively locked away for the benefit of the child, who, during occasional visits, had charmed his uncle with the irresistible allure of early childhood: a stammering speech, a fierce desire for autonomy, cunning tricks, and endless noise. These youthful charms outweighed years of devoted care from his aunt and nieces. Though he meant no harm, he did leave each of the three girls a thousand pounds as a token of his affection.

Mr. Dashwood's initial despair was severe, yet his naturally cheerful and sanguine temperament offered hope. He reasoned that he could live many more years, managing the estate frugally to accumulate a significant sum. But fate intervened swiftly; the fortune that seemed so distant arrived only to vanish just as quickly. He survived his uncle by no more than a year. Ten thousand pounds, inclusive of the legacies, remained for his widow and daughters.

Upon learning of his father's critical condition, Mr. John Dashwood was summoned immediately. With the urgency of illness, his dying father implored him to ensure the comfort of his mother-in-law and sisters. Mr. John, lacking the deep emotional resonance of his family, was nonetheless moved by the gravity of the request and promised to use every means at his disposal to make them comfortable. This assurance eased his father's mind, granting Mr. John the quiet space to calculate the prudent limits of his generosity.

He was not a malicious soul, though perhaps somewhat cold-hearted and self-regarding; generally, he was well-respected for his propriety in daily affairs. Had he married a woman of greater warmth, he might have become more amiable himself, for he was young and fond of his wife. Yet, Mrs. John Dashwood was a sharp caricature of her husband—far narrower in her mind and more selfish in her nature.

In his private deliberations, Mr. John had initially envisioned bestowing a thousand pounds upon each of his sisters, a sum he genuinely believed he could afford. The prospect of adding four thousand pounds a year to his income, alongside the residual half of his mother's fortune, had warmed his heart and inspired a sense of capability. "Three thousand pounds," he had mused, "would be liberal and handsome! It would render them entirely secure. I can spare such a sum with little inconvenience." He had entertained this thought for days, finding no cause for regret.

However, the moment his father's funeral concluded, Mrs. John Dashwood arrived at Norland with her child and retinue, sending no prior notice to her mother-in-law. While no one could contest her right to enter the house, which now belonged to her husband, the indelicacy of her action was glaring. For a woman of Mrs. Dashwood's station, possessing only common feelings, such behavior would have been deeply offensive. Yet, in her own mind, Mrs. John Dashwood possessed a sense of honor so rigid and a generosity so romantic that any slight, whether given or received, provoked an immovable disgust. She had never been a favorite among her husband's family, but until this moment, she had lacked the opportunity to demonstrate how little regard she held for the comfort of others.

Mrs. Dashwood felt this ungracious conduct with acute pain, despising her daughter-in-law with such earnestness that she considered abandoning the house forever. Only the entreaties of her eldest daughter persuaded her to reconsider the propriety of staying, and her tender love for her three children ultimately anchored her decision to remain, avoiding a breach with her son for their sakes.

Elinor, the eldest daughter, whose counsel proved so effective, possessed a strength of understanding and coolness of judgment that qualified her, at nineteen, to guide her mother. She often countered the impulsiveness that might otherwise have led to imprudence, steering the family toward safety. Her heart was excellent; her disposition affectionate and her feelings strong, yet she knew how to govern them—a wisdom her mother had yet to acquire, and which one of her sisters had resolved never to learn.

Marianne's abilities rivaled Elinor's in many ways. She was sensible and clever, yet eager in everything; her sorrows and joys knew no moderation. Generous, amiable, and deeply interesting, she was everything but prudent. The resemblance between her and her mother was striking.

Elinor observed her sister's excessive sensibility with concern, but Mrs. Dashwood cherished and encouraged it. Together, they now indulged the violence of their grief, voluntarily renewing their sorrow, seeking out new causes for wretchedness, and resolving against ever accepting consolation. They surrendered wholly to their mourning, feeding their pain with every reflection that could deepen it. Elinor, too, was deeply afflicted, yet she retained the strength to struggle, to exert herself. She could consult with her brother, receive his wife with proper attention, and strive to rouse her mother to similar forbearance, encouraging her to endure with dignity.

Margaret, the youngest sister, was a good-humored and well-disposed girl. Having absorbed much of Marianne's romanticism without acquiring her sister's sense, she did not, at thirteen, show signs of equalling her sisters in later years.
