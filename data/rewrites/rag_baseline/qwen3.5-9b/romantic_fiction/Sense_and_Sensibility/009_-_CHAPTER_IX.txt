The Dashwoods had finally established themselves at Barton Cottage with a level of comfort that felt almost surreal compared to the austerity of Norland. The house, the garden, and the rolling landscape surrounding them had transformed from mere scenery into a living, breathing entity they now understood intimately. Where Norland had once offered charm only through the lens of lost potential, Barton provided genuine joy simply by existing. Sir John Middleton, a man rarely seen away from his own estate, visited daily during the first fortnight, his eyes widening in genuine astonishment at the ceaseless activity within the cottage. Yet, despite his earnest pleas for them to broaden their social circle and his constant offers of carriage rides, Mrs. Dashwood remained immovable. Her spirit possessed a fierce independence that rejected the performative nature of neighborhood society; she resolved to accept visits only from families within walking distance, a boundary that effectively excluded nearly everyone.

About a mile and a half from the cottage, nestled in the narrow, winding valley of Allenham, stood an ancient, dignified mansion. During one of their earliest excursions, Marianne and Margaret had stumbled upon it. Its resemblance to Norland sparked a vivid imagination, fueling a desire to know its inhabitants. However, inquiry revealed a sobering truth: the owner, an elderly woman of impeccable character, was too frail to venture outside, confined entirely to her home. With the main valley often choked by mud and gloom, the high downs became their primary sanctuary. These elevated ridges, visible from almost every window, offered crisp air and sweeping vistas, serving as a perfect refuge when the lower lands were impassable.

One memorable morning, driven by the lingering effects of two days of relentless rain, Marianne and Margaret ventured toward one of these hills. Though the weather offered only patchy sunlight against a backdrop of grey clouds, Marianne declared with unwavering conviction that the skies would clear completely. While Elinor and their mother remained indoors, engrossed in books and pencils, the two sisters ascended the downs with infectious enthusiasm. They reveled in the sudden glimpses of azure sky, mocking the caution that had kept their family grounded. When the biting, invigorating gusts of a southerly wind struck their faces, they felt a profound pity for anyone who dared remain cooped up.

"Is there any felicity in this world," Marianne proclaimed, her voice ringing against the wind, "superior to this?"

"Margaret," she continued, "we shall walk here for at least two hours."

Margaret agreed, and they pressed onward, laughing defiantly against the gale for twenty minutes longer. Suddenly, the heavens closed in. Clouds merged overhead, unleashing a driving rain that drenched them instantly. Chagrined yet resigned, they turned back, knowing no shelter awaited them until they returned to the cottage. Their only recourse was to sprint down the steep slope leading to the garden gate.

Marianne took the lead initially, but a misstep sent her crashing to the earth. Margaret, unable to halt her momentum to offer aid, was swept along by the gravity of the descent, reaching the bottom safely. Just as Marianne fell, a gentleman carrying a gun passed nearby, flanked by two pointers circling his heels. Witnessing the accident, he dropped his weapon and rushed to her side. Marianne had managed to raise herself, but her foot was twisted, leaving her barely capable of standing.

The gentleman offered his assistance. Recognizing that her modesty would likely refuse help under normal circumstances, he bypassed formalities entirely. Without hesitation, he lifted her into his arms and carried her down the hill. Passing through the open garden gate—which Margaret had left ajar—he bore her straight into the house, where she met Margaret just arriving. He held her firmly until he had seated her comfortably in a parlor chair.

Elinor and their mother rose in stunned silence, their gaze fixed on the stranger with a mixture of wonder and secret admiration. His appearance commanded immediate respect. He apologized for the intrusion, recounting the incident with a frankness and grace that seemed to enhance his already striking features. Even if he had been aged, plain, or coarse, Mrs. Dashwood's gratitude would have been secured by his concern for her daughter. Instead, the combination of his youth, beauty, and elegant demeanor struck a chord deep within her. She thanked him profusely and, with her characteristic sweetness, invited him to sit.

He declined, citing his wet and muddy state. Mrs. Dashwood then asked for his name.

"I am Willoughby," he replied smoothly. "My current residence is Allenham, where I hope to have the honor of calling upon you tomorrow to inquire after Miss Dashwood."

The invitation was granted willingly. As he departed into the pouring rain, his departure marked the beginning of a whirlwind of speculation. His manly beauty and exceptional grace became the subject of universal admiration. The laughter elicited by his gallant rescue of Marianne gained extra zest from his undeniable allure. Marianne herself had seen less of him than the others due to the crimson flush of embarrassment that covered her face when he lifted her, robbing her of the ability to observe him closely once inside. Nevertheless, she had witnessed enough to join the chorus of praise with energetic fervor. His bearing matched the heroes of her favorite novels; the speed and decisiveness with which he carried her into the house impressed her greatly. Every detail about him seemed significant: his noble name, his proximity to their beloved village, and the fact that a shooting jacket suited him perfectly. Pain from her sprained ankle faded into insignificance as her imagination spun pleasant fantasies.

Sir John called the following morning, eager to hear the full account of Marianne's mishap. When asked if he knew any gentleman named Willoughby at Allenham, his reaction was immediate.

"Willoughby!" Sir John exclaimed. "Is *he* in the country? That is excellent news. I shall ride over tomorrow and invite him to dine on Thursday."

"You know him, then?" Mrs. Dashwood asked.

"Know him! Naturally. He is down here every single year."

"And what sort of a young man is he?"

"As good a fellow as ever lived, I assure you. A very decent shot, and there is no bolder rider in England."

"And is that all you can say for him?" Marianne interjected indignantly. "What of his manners on closer acquaintance? His pursuits, his talents, his genius?"

Sir John appeared slightly perplexed. "Upon my soul," he admitted, "I know little of those finer points. But he is a pleasant, good-humored chap, and possesses the finest little black bitch of a pointer I have ever seen. Was she out with him today?"

Marianne could provide no more information about the dog's color than Sir John could about the depths of Willoughby's intellect.

"But who is he?" Elinor asked. "Where does he come from? Does he have a house at Allenham?"

On this point, Sir John was more informative. Mr. Willoughby owned no property locally; he resided at Allenham solely to visit the elderly lady at Allenham Court, to whom he was related and whose estate he was destined to inherit.

"Yes, yes, he is certainly worth pursuing," Sir John added, leaning in conspiratorially. "He owns a small estate in Somersetshire as well. If I were you, I would not give him up to my younger sister, regardless of this hill-climbing business. Miss Marianne must not expect to monopolize all the eligible men. Brandon will be jealous if she does not exercise some caution."

"I do not believe," Mrs. Dashwood smiled gently, "that Mr. Willoughby will be inconvenienced by the attentions of either of my daughters. It is not a pursuit they were raised for. Men are quite safe with us, whatever their wealth. I am pleased, however, to learn he is a respectable young man, making his acquaintance entirely acceptable."

"He is as good a sort of fellow as ever lived," Sir John reiterated. "Last Christmas, at a small gathering at the park, he danced from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon without once sitting down."

"Did he indeed?" Marianne cried, her eyes sparkling. "With elegance, with spirit?"

"Yes; and he was back on horseback at eight to hunt the covert."

"That is precisely what I admire," Marianne declared. "Whatever a young man's pursuits may be, his eagerness in them should know no moderation, nor should it leave him any sense of fatigue."

"Aye, aye, I see exactly how this will unfold," Sir John chuckled. "You will be setting your cap at him now, and never thinking of poor Brandon."

"That is an expression, Sir John," Marianne retorted warmly, "which I particularly detest. I abhor every cliché used to feign wit; 'setting one's cap at a man' or 'making a conquest' are among the most odious phrases. Their tendency is crude and illiberal, and even if their construction were clever, time has long since eroded their ingenuity."

Sir John struggled to grasp the severity of her rebuke but laughed heartily anyway, replying, "Well, you will make plenty of conquests, one way or another. Poor Brandon! He is already smitten, and he is very well worth your attention, despite all this tumbling and spraining of ankles."
