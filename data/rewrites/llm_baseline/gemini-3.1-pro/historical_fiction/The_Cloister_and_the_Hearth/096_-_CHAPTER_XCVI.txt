Margaret went back to Rotterdam long before Gerard awoke, leaving her boy behind in a world still echoing with the quiet, predawn rustle of the countryside. To ensure the physical and spiritual sustenance of the manse, she dispatched the faithful, sturdy Reicht directly to Gouda. Reicht carried not just a vicar’s grey frock and a large felt hat, but the inherited wisdom of generations of agrarian mothers, armed with minute instructions on how to govern her new master’s hearth and diet. 

Then she sought out Jorian Ketel; “he is the closest I ever met,” she told herself, finding in his taciturn nature a profound relief from the jarring, metallic clamor of Rotterdam’s burgeoning mercantile ports. In concert with him, she executed two deeply cunning maneuvers. To her dying day, she kept them secret, burying the deeds in the silent, untranslatable spaces of a woman’s internal life—and I, being a man, shall add no further word on the matter. 

She kept away from Gouda parsonage. The harsh acoustics of their prior arguments still reverberated in her mind. Offended that Gerard could think she might ever forget the lover within the priest, her pride allied with her modesty. She left little Gerard there to complete the conquest her maternal heart ascribed to him, knowing the child would anchor his father forever to the rich, sensory world of humanity.

But this generous stroke of policy starved her own heart. Having never been parted from the boy for an hour, the silence of her Rotterdam home became an intolerable, deafening void. After a single day, she crept at dark up to Gouda parsonage, navigating by the scent of woodsmoke and roasting root vegetables. Lurking like a thief, she tapped softly at the kitchen window when she saw Reicht alone among the hanging herbs and drying sausages. “Reicht, for pity’s sake, bring him out to me unbeknown.” In the overwhelming hunger of her maternal grief, the man she loved ceased to possess a name, reduced to a mere pronoun in the dominant lexicon of her sorrow. 

Reicht soon found an excuse to bring the child out. A scene of mutual rapture unfolded in the shadowed damp of the evening, followed by mutual tears when they parted again. 

They forged a new rhythm: Reicht would carry the boy halfway to Rotterdam every day at a set hour. At these stolen meetings, they shared rustic provisions—wedges of sharp, aged farm cheese, thick slices of dark, seeded rye bread that tasted of the soil, and apples stored in winter hay. As the boy sat at their feet, chewing contentedly on the crusts, the two women would slip into the thick, vanishing dialect of their foremothers to seriously discuss the fate of Luke Peterson. 

“Reicht,” Margaret said, the ancestral vowels softening her voice, “I as good as promised him to marry Luke Peterson. ‘Say you the word,’ quoth I, ‘and I’ll wed him.’”

“Poor Luke!” 

“Prithee, why poor Luke?” 

“To be bandied about so, atwixt yea and nay.”

Margaret eyed her. “Why, Reicht, you have not ever been so simple as to cast an eye of affection on the boy, that you take his part?” 

“Me?” Reicht tossed her head, her cheeks flushing the color of a simmered plum. 

“Oh, I ask your pardon. Well, then, you can do me a good turn.”

“Whisht! whisper! that little darling is listening to every word, and eyes like saucers.” 

And their heads would dip under one cap, plotting against the boy using the untranslatable nuances of their mother tongue. But after five days of these secret agrarian picnics, Margaret grew irritated and unhappy with the injustice of it all. 

She was weeping in her parlor when the clatter of a cart pierced the city’s industrial noise. There, clean as a new penny, his beard close-shaved, his hands white as flour, and a healthy color restored to his pale face, sat the Vicar of Gouda in the grey frock she had sent him. 

She ran upstairs, washed away her tears, and donned a theoretically cleaner cap before descending. He seized both her hands, kissing them until a tear fell against her skin. She turned her head to hide her own weeping. 

“My sweet Margaret,” he cried, his voice carrying the mellow, resonant acoustic of a man healed by quietudes. “Why hold you aloof from your own good deed? We have been waiting for you every day.” 

“You said things.” 

“What! When I was a hermit, and a donkey.” 

“Ay! No matter, you said things. And you had no reason.” 

“Forget all I said there. Who hearkens to the harsh ravings of a maniac? In a few months more, I should have been a gibbering idiot; no mortal could have persuaded me away but you. It is not here, amidst the mercantile noise of this city, that I can thank you. No, it is in the home you have given me, where I break bread with the flock you have made mine. Come to Gouda manse.”

“Humph! We will see about that.” 

“Why, Margaret, think you I had ever kept the dear child so long, but that I made sure you would be back to him? Confess now, thou hast had hard thoughts of me for this.” 

“Nay, nay. Ah! Thou art thyself again; wast ever thoughtful of others. I have half a mind to go to Gouda manse, for your saying that.” 

“Tell me first how you agree with your folk,” she deflected, though she was burning to go. 

“Already my poor have taken root in my heart,” Gerard said. “There are such good creatures among them; simple and rough, but wonderfully good. Two of them had been ill friends for four years. By the glory of God, I got on their bright side, and made them laugh at themselves for their folly.” 

“But are you in very deed their vicar?” 

“Certes; I have taken the oath, rung the church bell, and touched the altar and the holy cup. Nay, 'tis a real vicar inviting a true friend to Gouda manse.” 

“Tell me oceans more.” 

“Nearest to me of all my parish is a poor cripple my guardian angel hath placed beneath my roof. Sybrandt and I are that we never were till now: brothers. He is full of thy praises. Out of my house he ne’er shall go to any place but heaven.” 

“Tell me somewhat that happened thyself.” 

“At first I was much dazzled by the sun-light, but that is passed. And good Reicht Heynes—her voice goes through me like a knife, and all voices seem loud but thine, which is so mellow sweet. I spake yesterday with an old man who told me a strange thing. He said the devil came for the hermit of Gouda this week, leaving naught but a piece of skin and old iron. The strange thing is, the cave has verily fallen in.”

Margaret smiled at this mythic occurrence seamlessly blending into the village gossip. 

“Had I been so perverse as to resist thee,” Gerard continued, “it had assuredly buried me dead where I had buried myself alive. I see the finger of Providence condemning my late, approving my present, way of life.” 

“Nay, can I pierce the like mysteries? I am but a woman.” 

“This very tale proves thee my guardian angel. Come to Gouda manse.” 

“Well, go you on, I’ll follow.” 

“Nay, in the cart with me.” 

“Not so. All I know is I seem—to feel—to wish—to come alone.” 

When Margaret finally arrived at the manse, the air was thick with the scent of simmering bone broth and roasting garlic. She found the two Gerards together, the son performing his fluid caprices on the floor, the father slouching in a chair with pencil and paper, trying to sketch him. 

“In vain I strive to fix him,” Gerard laughed, tossing the paper away, which Margaret secretly spirited into her bosom. “Looking on thee, sir baby, I see what awkward, lopsided, ungainly toads I and my fellows painted missals with, calling them cherubs.” 

That night, as they sat around the glowing peat fire, the acoustics of the room were perfectly dampened by warmth and shared breath. Gerard marveled at the culinary and domestic artistry around him. The brass candlesticks gleamed like liquid gold, reflecting a table laden with sustenance. 

“Oh, the sheets I lie in here,” he said. “Dives was ne’er so flaxed as I. Let those who are not content pass six months in a hermit’s cell, devoid of the scent of baking bread and the sight of human faces. Then will they find how lovely and pleasant this wicked world is, and that men and women are God’s fairest creatures. Margaret was always fair, but never to my eye so bright as now. And my mother—I noted not her exceeding comeliness till now.” 

“A score years ago I might pass in a crowd,” Catherine smiled, stirring a wooden spoon through the thick, fragrant stew, “but not now.” 

“Each age has its beauty,” Gerard insisted. “See this mild grey eye that hath looked motherly love upon so many of us; all that love hath left its shadow, a beauty which defieth Time. See this delicate lip, these pure white teeth. Art beautiful in my eyes, mother dear.” 

Reicht and Catherine exchanged a fond look, silently agreeing they had an amiable maniac to superintend—one who finally understood the profound sanctity of the domestic sphere.

The next day was Sunday. The church was crammed with villagers. The acoustics of the stone vault carried Gerard’s voice—a divine, resonant music that bypassed the sterile, dominant Latin to strike the visceral chords of their native understanding. In a two-hour sermon that felt like twenty minutes, he uplifted the heart of the poor, leaving the multitude rapt. In the corner, Catherine wept into her apron. “Wherever gat he it all? By our Lady, not from me.” 

Margaret threw her arms around Catherine’s neck. “Mother, mother, I am not quite a happy woman, but oh, I am a proud one.” She vowed never to let her love come between this young saint and Heaven. 

Reader, did you ever stand by the seashore after a storm, when the wind has suddenly died? The waves do not cease at once; to the ear, they seem to lash the sounding shore even more fiercely in the quiet. Yet, the inevitable calm has begun, rocking the waters to sleep. So it was with these true, tempest-tossed lovers. The storm was over. 

United by deep affection and a living pledge of love, yet separated by Holy Church and their own consciences, they lived but a few miles apart. For years, Margaret brought the boy to Gouda every Sunday. The manse was always filled with the scents of feast days—roasted fowl, spiced wine, and sweet honey-cakes. Tergou had so little to offer compared to the rich, interwoven lives they now led, that eventually, Eli and Catherine left it behind to set up in the capital. 

And thus the years glided in a circular, seasonal rhythm, heavily steeped in agricultural time and domestic ritual. 

In the early spring of 1471, the Duchess of Burgundy raised forces for Edward the Fourth of England. Denys, enlisting once more, passed through Rotterdam. Finding Margaret alone, he offered to marry her to protect his comrade’s honor. Margaret smiled at the old soldier. “I choose rather to be still thy she-comrade,” she said. She equipped him not with sterile gold, but with a new sword of Antwerp make and a double handful of silver, sending him off with blessings to survive the harsh, metallic clashing of axes. 

By 1473, Sybrandt began to fail. His pitiable existence had been sweetened by Gerard’s inventive tenderness and Reicht’s meticulous, restorative broths, simmered long with marrow and healing herbs. When his end drew near, he sent for Margaret. He died blessing the two true lovers, a peaceful departure wrapped in the acoustic quiet of the manse. *Tantum religio scit suadere boni.*

In 1474, the kitchen plot reached its culmination: Luke Peterson married Reicht Heynes. For two years, Margaret had played the storm—using the harsh, peevish registers of the city's trade-tongue to berate him—while Reicht provided the sunshine, soothing him with the melodic, comforting vocabulary of their agrarian roots and plates of perfectly crisped pork. Luke’s affections transferred seamlessly. On the wedding day, Reicht embraced Margaret, confessing she had fancied him from the start. Margaret settled the newlyweds in her shop, giving them half the profits. 

The years 1476 and 1477 brought great trouble to Gerard. The Pope, siding with the Grey Friars, sought to erase a palpable distinction between the Virgin Mary and her Son, bribing the Christian world with pardons if they added a newly invented clause to the Ave Mary. Gerard, alongside many northern clergy, saw this not just as flat heresy, but as the imposition of a sterile, dominant lexicon meant to bulldoze the organic, lived faith of the people. He refused to utter it, and he refused to celebrate the Pope's new "feast of the miraculous conception." 

This drew the bitter enmity of the Franciscans, who subjected him to endless mortifications. In these emergencies, he consulted Margaret. With her marvelous tact, she navigated the treacherous linguistic and political power dynamics where his genius faltered. 

Yet where affection annihilated her judgment, he stepped in. Margaret and Catherine were thoroughly spoiling little Gerard, raising the nine-year-old with infinite unselfishness that bred only tyranny. Gerard, pained by this, intervened. In 1477, despite a storm of tears, little Gerard was sent to the renowned school kept by Haaghe at Deventer. The boy's remarkable progress soon reconciled Margaret, and with Reicht managing the business, Margaret spent weeks at Deventer, hovering over her scholar. 

And so these two guardians lived, an icy barrier of chastity between them, conversing with unrestrained tenderness about their boy, navigating a century that had not yet learned to mold sacred vows to the shape of human reason. Gerard was happy. He was the right man in the right place, occupied from dawn to dusk in good works, deeply rooted in the soil and souls of his parish. 

Margaret, however, was not. A sweet pensiveness masked her petulance; she hated the monks who had parted them, inoculating her boy with a contempt for them that would shape his entire life. Gerard bore her snapping like an angel, knowing her heart of gold.

He became the greatest peacemaker of his age, reconciling enemies by making them laugh at the very roots of their discord. He understood the profound connection between the human and natural worlds, taming squirrels, hares, and fawns. When a parishioner handed him a mule supposedly possessed by a devil, saying, “Tame this vagabone, parson,” Gerard integrated the beast into the manse's gentle rhythms. Within six months, the mule, Jack, would come running to his whistle like a dog. 

One day, taking shelter from a shower outside a public-house, Gerard heard a traveling toper boasting he could out-drink any man in Gouda. The jarring noise of the boast offended the village’s harmony. Gerard marched in. “I your parson do take him up. I’ll find thee a parishioner shall drink more at a draught than thou.” 

A bet was made. Gerard whistled. In clattered Jack the mule, hooves striking the floorboards with rhythmic composure, and thrust his nose into his master’s hand. 

“A pair of buckets!” shouted Gerard. “And let us see which of these two sons of asses can drink most at a draught.” 

On another occasion, two farmers fiercely debated whose hay was superior. They brought the dispute to their vicar, now the referee in every mortal thing. 

“How lucky you thought of me!” said Gerard. “I have one staying with me who is the best judge of hay in Holland. Bring me a double handful apiece.” 

When they returned with bundles that smelled richly of dried meadow-grass and sweet summer clover, Gerard placed each on a chair and whistled. In walked Jack. 

“Lord a mercy!” said one of the farmers. 

“Jack,” said the parson, in the gentle, everyday tone of agrarian communion, “just tell us which is the best hay of these two.”
