The salt-stung air of Devon carries tales as old as the cliffs themselves—tales of daring men and the sea’s cruel whims, of love lost and glory won. This is a chronicle of those who sailed into the unknown, driven by ambition, faith, or the sheer restlessness of youth. Among them stands Amyas Leigh, a son of England’s rugged coast, whose fate would be woven with the rise of an empire and the fall of Spanish galleons.

But before the cannons roared and the sails unfurled, there were smaller beginnings: a white bird glimpsed by Mr. Oxenham, an omen or a mere trick of the light; Amyas’s first return home, sunburnt and wide-eyed from distant shores; and the tangled loyalties of Welsh gentlemen who played both sides of a treacherous game. Love crossed, fortunes made and broken, the old halls of Clovelly echoing with laughter and whispered plots—these are the threads that bind our story.

And then, the darker strands: the doomed voyage of John Oxenham, whose name became both warning and legend; the founding of the Brotherhood of the Rose, sworn beneath flickering torchlight; and Christmas feasts that masked coming storms. From Bideford’s cobbled streets to the jungles of the New World, where bloodhounds hunted and mastiffs fought back, this is a saga of those who sought gold, God, or simply a chance to spit in the eye of fate.

So raise a glass to the *Golden Hind* and her ilk, to Yeo’s vengeance and Brimblecombe’s stubborn oaths, to the pearls of Margarita and the fires of the Inquisition. For every man who sailed west did so knowing the price—and still they went.

This is their story.

Those who have wandered through the lush valleys and rolling hills of North Devon will know the quiet charm of Bideford—a town of whitewashed houses climbing gently from the broad, sandy banks of the Torridge, its ancient bridge arching over waters where salmon lie in wait for autumn rains. Beyond the town, the land rises into wooded slopes, thick with oak and fern, while westward the hills soften into green pastures and russet fields, stretching down to the salt marshes where the Torridge meets the Taw, and both spill into the restless Atlantic.

Bideford has stood thus for centuries, bathed in soft sea air that spares it winter’s bite and summer’s swelter. But in the year 1575, it was no sleepy harbor. It was a port of daring men, a cradle of seafarers who had sent ships against the Armada and would yet shape the fate of England. For it was the men of Devon—the Drakes and Hawkinses, Raleighs and Grenvilles—who carved England’s path upon the waves, whose courage turned the tide against Spain and saved their land from becoming a mere outpost of a foreign tyranny.

It is for these men, their bold voyages and fiercer battles, their faith and their sacrifice, that I set down this tale. And if at times my words rise too proudly, forgive me—for theirs is a story fit for epic, not mere ink.

---

On a golden afternoon in that same year of 1575, a fair-haired boy in a scholar’s gown wandered along Bideford’s quay, his slate tucked under his arm, his eyes fixed on the ships rocking at anchor. The salt wind tugged at his curls, and the cries of sailors filled his ears as he drifted toward a knot of men gathered outside a tavern.

At their center stood a man who might have stepped from some old ballad—broad-shouldered, black-bearded, clad in crimson velvet, a great gold-clasped hat perched atop his head. From it flashed the iridescent plumage of a Quezal bird, a treasure torn from the New World.

“I tell ye true, lads,” the man boomed, his voice rich with Devonshire pride, “I saw it with these eyes—bars of silver, heaped like cordwood! Seventy foot long, ten foot wide, and near twelve foot high! And Captain Drake says to us, ‘There it is, my lads—the world’s treasure-house. Will ye let it lie?’”

A murmur ran through the crowd. One skeptic dared to voice his doubt.

“Too many Spaniards, Captain Oxenham.”

Oxenham laughed, a sound like rolling thunder. “Too many? We took Nombre de Dios with but fifty men! The Dons pray to painted saints—no wonder they fight like frightened girls!”

The boy, edging closer, drank in every word. His fingers itched to touch the great buffalo horn being passed among the men, etched with maps of distant lands, marked with promises of *Here is gold* and *Much silver*.

“Who’ll join us?” Oxenham called. “Who’ll sail for fortune and glory?”

The boy’s heart hammered. He stepped forward, clutching his lone tester coin. “I’ll buy that horn.”

Oxenham’s dark eyes gleamed. “That won’t buy the dirt from my boots, lad.”

“Then I’ll fight you for it.”

Laughter rippled through the crowd, but Oxenham only grinned. “Fifteen years, are you? You’ve the shoulders of a man. What’s your name?”

“Amyas Leigh. Of Burrough Court.”

“Leigh’s son? I know your father well. Tell him John Oxenham will sup with him tonight—and if you’ve a mind for the sea, lad, I’ll take you.”

One of the sailors, a gaunt fellow named Salvation Yeo, pressed the horn into Amyas’s hands. “No payment, young master. A gift—for the love of the sea.”

Amyas clutched it, his face alight. Then, with a sudden pang, he remembered his father, his duty. He had never kept secrets before.

---

Now, this Amyas Leigh was no scholar by modern measure. He knew little of books beyond his Bible and *Mort d’Arthur*, and believed firmly in pixies and the moon’s kinship to cheese. Yet he could track a hawk, break a colt, and thrash any bully who tormented the weak. He spoke broad Devon, thought the sun circled the earth, and held that a gentleman’s first duty was to shield those beneath him.

He was, in short, a boy of his time—untutored in science, but schooled in courage; ignorant of politics, but steeped in honor. And as he climbed the hill toward home, the horn burning in his hands, he knew one truth above all:

The sea was calling. And he would answer.

—Old Epigram on Drake

Five years had passed. The bells of Bideford church, though it was well past nine on a bright November morning, still rang joyously, breaking from their measured toll into jubilant peals as if they could not contain their delight. The streets were alive with color—seamen in their holiday best, burghers and their wives in fine gowns, garlands strung across the lanes, tapestries hanging from every window. The ships in the harbor flew their flags proudly, their cannon roaring in salute. Horses filled every stable, and Sir Richard Grenville’s house buzzed like a tavern, crowded with guests, servants rushing to and fro.

The churchyard overflowed with women, while the gentry of North Devon—tall, stately men and fair ladies—streamed in, a living testament to the days when England’s nobility led not by title alone, but by strength, beauty, and wisdom. The Countess of Bath arrived first, escorted by Sir Richard himself, hat in hand. Then came the Bassets of Umberleigh, the Carys of Clovelly, Fortescues from Wear and Buckland, Coles from Slade, Stukelys from Affton, St. Legers from Annery, and even the Coplestones of Eggesford, thirty miles distant. Last, but never least, strode Sir John Chichester of Raleigh, followed by his eight daughters and three of his five sons (one absent, fighting valiantly in Ireland). At the gate, he met his cousin of Arlington, leading a procession of four daughters and nineteen sons—so long a line that the youngest had not yet passed the town hall when the eldest reached the lychgate. Laughter and greetings filled the air as they arranged themselves in the church, though not without a few sharp glances and whispered rivalries among the high-born matrons. The overwhelmed churchwardens, unaccustomed to such illustrious company, flustered about until Sir Richard himself stepped in to mediate, lest the seating arrangements spark feuds among Devon’s finest.

Then—silence. All eyes turned toward the door. Distant music swelled: flutes, hautboys, drums, and trumpets, rising to a triumphant crescendo before ceasing at the church steps. The crowd rustled, some with tears in their eyes, as the cause of their celebration entered, and the rector began not the morning service, but a thanksgiving for victory at sea.

And who were these men who had stirred Bideford to such exultation? Four weathered mariners, decked in ribbons by loving hands—and before them, a beardless youth of Herculean frame, golden hair flowing over his shoulders, standing head and shoulders above the rest. As the five knelt at the altar rail, every gaze flickered to the pew where Mrs. Leigh of Burrough hid her face, trembling with joy. For these were Devon’s own: Amyas Leigh of Burrough, John Staveley, Michael Heard, and Jonas Marshall of Bideford, and Thomas Braund of Clovelly—the first Englishmen to sail around the world with Francis Drake, now returned to give thanks to God.

But how had Amyas come to this?

For more than a year after Mr. Oxenham’s departure, he had kept his promise, though not without the occasional outbursts befitting a boy of his fiery spirit. His studies progressed little, but he grew skilled in archery, horsemanship, and the old-school art of sword and buckler—until his father, attending the Exeter Assizes, caught gaol-fever from the prisoners and died within a week.

Now Mrs. Leigh was left alone to guide her lion-hearted son. She had loved her husband deeply, though his life had been one of quiet disappointment—his estate diminished by his father’s excesses and his own youthful ambition, which had led him to Court only to leave him adrift, too proud to play the sycophant. Yet in her, he had found solace: a woman of steadfast faith, shaped by the trials of her youth. She remembered the burnings of Protestants under Queen Mary, the terror of hiding in the wilds of the Peak District, saved only by her husband’s defiance of the Crown’s warrants. Those dark days had left a shadow on her spirit, but also a quiet strength, a holiness that shone in every word and deed.

At just past forty, she was a widow—lovely still, her beauty deepened by sorrow and grace. No wonder Sir Richard and Lady Grenville cherished her; no wonder her children adored her. And Amyas, once the storm of his grief had passed, resolved to be his mother’s protector. The very day after his father’s funeral, he marched to Sir Richard’s house and declared, *"You must be my father now."*

Sir Richard, struck by the boy’s determination, swore an oath to stand as both father and brother to the Leighs. Lady Grenville walked Amyas home, where the two women wept together—one for the loss endured, the other, perhaps, foreseeing a loss yet to come. For she knew her own husband’s fiery soul would not let him die in peace, but in battle.

Life at Burrough resumed. Amyas rode, shot, and roamed the quays with Sir Richard, for his mother was wise enough not to disrupt the training his father had begun. Her elder son, Frank, was already forging his own path—scholar, traveler, and now a rising figure at Court, praised even by the Queen herself. But Amyas?

Amyas had gone to sea for two reasons: an old schoolmaster and a young beauty.

Vindex Brimblecombe, master of Bideford’s grammar school, was a well-meaning but stern pedagogue, his heart hardened by years of wielding the rod. Believing it his duty to discipline the fatherless boy, he increased Amyas’s floggings to one a day—until the lad’s restless spirit rebelled.

For Amyas had never abandoned his dream of the sea. When he could not linger by the harbor or gaze at the ocean from Northam’s pebble ridge, he drew ships and maps on his slate, ignoring his Latin conjugations. One afternoon, deep in sketching an island with a castle, a dragon, and a ship bristling with lances (meant to represent himself and his schoolmates on a heroic quest), he failed to notice his classmates clustering around him—nor that Sir Vindex, after his customary post-dinner nap, had awoken.

What followed was inevitable. But it was only the beginning of the tale.

*“I know that Deformed; he has been a vile thief this seven years; he goes up and down like a gentleman: I remember his name.”*
—*Much Ado About Nothing*

Amyas Leigh slept that night in fitful unrest, his dreams a turbulent mirror of the day’s excitements. The memory of John Oxenham, the lost adventurer, had taken root in his mind, and as he lay in the bay-windowed chamber where he had last seen the man, every gesture, every word of Oxenham’s returned to him with strange persistence.

In his dreams, he found himself sailing westward, chasing a frail vessel that always seemed just beyond reach. The sea around him choked with sargasso weed, its serpentine coils clutching at his ship’s hull. The sun dipped below the horizon, plunging all into darkness—until the moon rose, ghastly and pale, illuminating Oxenham’s ship in full view.

The sight was a nightmare. The sails hung in tatters, the hull wept pitch, and along the mainyard swung a line of hanged men. Worst of all, Oxenham himself dangled from the yard-arm, his dead eyes fixed on Amyas, his lips moving soundlessly as if struggling to impart some dreadful secret. His skeletal finger pointed—not forward, but back, back toward the distant snow-capped Andes. And Amyas knew then, with a surge of horror, that he was trapped in the South Seas, an entire continent between him and home.

He awoke with a shout, drenched in sweat, to find himself safe in Burrough, the gray dawn seeping through his chamber window.

Restless, he rose and dressed, slipping out for an early morning bathe at the pebble ridge. As he passed his mother’s door, he paused. She knelt at her prie-dieu, absorbed in prayer, her pillow untouched. Quietly, he joined her, kneeling in wordless companionship. She turned, smiled, and cupped his face in her hands, her eyes full of silent love. No words were needed between them.

At last, she rose, pressing a kiss to his brow. A single tear fell upon his forehead, and he caught her bare foot as she turned away, pressing his lips to it in a sudden rush of tenderness.

She laughed, hiding them at once. *“Your father said the same thirty years ago.”*

*“And I say it still,”* he insisted. *“You were beautiful then—you are beautiful now.”*

She swatted him playfully. *“Silly boy! Save your gallantry for younger ladies, if you can find any worthy of you.”*

With that, he left her to her prayers and strode down to the shore, where the breakers crashed against the ancient rampart of stones. Stripping bare, he plunged into the cold surf, letting the salt sting his skin, the waves buffet his limbs—until a voice hailed him from the shore.

Eustace Leigh stood atop the ridge, watching.

Amyas clambered out, dressing quickly as his cousin paced the turf. Eustace was a tall, fair man, his face set in an expression of perpetual mildness that never quite reached his eyes. Three years abroad had left him stranger than ever, his smiles too deliberate, his gestures too precise.

*“From going to and fro in the earth, and from walking up and down in it,”* Eustace said airily when asked of his travels.

Amyas chuckled. *“Like the devil, eh?”*

They spoke of family, of home, but beneath the civility, tension hummed. Eustace had been shaped by forces Amyas neither understood nor trusted—Jesuit schools, foreign priests, whispered loyalties. And when Eustace ventured a remark about the Virgin’s intercession in Amyas’s safe return, the reply was swift.

*“Here’s Frank,”* Amyas said, deflecting. *“Let him answer.”*

Frank, ever the diplomat, turned the conversation with a toast to Queen Elizabeth—*“the spotless championess of truth, and right, and freedom.”*

Eustace’s smile tightened. *“I, at least, am certain that I speak the truth when I call my patroness a virgin undefiled.”*

The air chilled. Amyas, ever blunt, countered with the tale of a Frenchman whose slanders against the Queen had cost him his head.

*“So perish all her enemies,”* Frank said coolly.

*“I trust you do not number me among them?”* Eustace asked.

*“As you speak, I do,”* Frank replied.

Eustace excused himself then, claiming business in Northam—a lie, as Amyas soon discovered when they spotted him mounting a horse outside an Appledore inn with two unfamiliar companions. One was a gaunt, overdressed fellow, his sword absurdly large, his legs comically thin.

*“Young David in Saul’s weapons,”* Frank murmured.

Amyas grinned. *“Aye, and about as steady in them.”*

They watched from a shopfront as Eustace rode off, his secrets trailing behind him like shadows.

*“Well,”* Amyas said at last, *“whatever game he’s playing, I’ll not let him play it alone.”*

And with that, the hunt began.

What had become of the fair maiden who had so many hearts in her keeping, yet to whom we have scarcely been introduced?

She sat now in the little farmhouse beside the mill, nestled deep in the green valley of Combe, halfway between Stow and Chapel. Though her nature was sweet and patient, she could not help but sulk a little, shut away from the grand festivities of Bideford and forced to endure a dreary solitude in this quiet western glen. Lonely and restless, she found herself looking forward—despite her misgivings—to the visits of Eustace Leigh, who contrived some excuse or other to come down to the farm nearly every day.

At first, her uncle and aunt had been stiff and wary of these visits, her aunt always making sure to sit with them as a chaperone. But Eustace was, after all, a gentleman’s son, and it would not do to offend a neighbor of standing—especially one who was a good customer. And Rose herself, though only a poor relation, had a way of charming her way into getting what she pleased. She had, however, been careful to beg her aunt never to leave her alone with him, protesting that she “could not abide the sight of Mr. Eustace,” but needed someone to talk to in this dull place. Her aunt, simple countrywoman that she was, supposed that town folk had their own ways, and so let the matter be.

Eustace, shrewd enough to recognize that the difference in faith between himself and Rose was the greatest obstacle to his hopes, took care to keep his true opinions well hidden. Instead of preaching at the mill-folk, he bought their flour and milk only to give it away to the poor of Moorwinstow, who agreed that for a Papist, he was a decent enough young man. At last, after consulting with his fellow Jesuits, Campian and Parsons, he resolved—for the sake of appearances—to attend the parish church the following Sunday. There, the two priests, having studied the rubrics beforehand, behaved with perfect propriety, as did Eustace himself, much to the astonishment of the townsfolk.

But if the Jesuits outwardly conformed to the laws of the land, they were no less busy in secret, plotting its overthrow.

For months they had moved like shadows across England, waiting only for word from Ireland to ignite a great rising in the West—one that would sweep from the throne that stubborn, excommunicated usurper who dared to call herself Queen. They had convinced themselves, as men often do when blinded by zeal, that the heart of England was with them, that the nation was on the brink of returning to the true faith, and that Elizabeth would soon be dragged in chains to the feet of the Holy Father.

This grand delusion they announced one day in terms so bold that old Mr. Leigh grew pale with fear. Eustace, returning from one of his visits to the mill, overheard the heated debate within his father’s private chamber—Parsons blustering, Mr. Leigh protesting weakly, and Campian striving to soothe the storm.

“My dear sir,” pleaded Mr. Leigh, “does not my very hospitality prove my loyalty to the Catholic cause? Yet I cannot forget that I am an Englishman.”

“And what is England?” snapped Parsons. “A heretic Babylon, from which the faithful must flee lest they share her plagues! What is a country but lines drawn by princes who are dust and shall return to dust? Their power comes from the people—and the people’s will is subject to the Vicar of Christ. Strip away his sanction, and what is a king? The mob that made him can unmake him!”

“But I have sworn allegiance to Queen Elizabeth!”

“And that oath was void the moment His Holiness declared her a heretic and excommunicate! I tell you, rebellion is not merely permitted—it is a sacred duty!”

“For God’s sake, take care!” cried Mr. Leigh. “Such words must not be spoken beneath my roof! For the sake of my family, I beg you—”

“Brother Parsons,” interposed Campian gently, “consider our flock. Your opinion, though sound in doctrine, may not yet be safe here. The opposite view is at least prudent—and Mr. Leigh’s conscience may rest upon it. Are we not sent to relieve good Catholics of burdens too heavy for them to bear?”

Parsons scowled. “To let them bow in the house of Rimmon, then?”

“If need be, yes. The bull binds the queen and heretics—but not the faithful, so long as tyranny prevents its fulfillment.”

“Mark this, Mr. Leigh,” Parsons pressed. “Though the weak are excused, the strong are not. The seven thousand who did not bow to Baal were guiltless—but Elijah slew the priests, and his deed was blessed!”

“Heaven help me, I will hear no more of this!” Mr. Leigh exclaimed, rising. “Our queen is a good queen, if only she would repent! I have never been troubled for my faith—nor has any man I know in these parts.”

“You forget Father Mayne,” Eustace interjected softly, slipping into the room.

“And the blessed blood of Story,” added Campian.

Mr. Leigh turned on his son. “What, you too? And what brought Mayne to the gallows but such treason as you now utter in my house? Must you beggar us all?”

Campian raised a hand. “The Virgin forbid.”

“Then help her forbid it! We had no laws against Agnus Deis or blessed grains until that cursed bull made them treason!”

Parsons nearly roared. “You dare speak ill of the Holy Father’s decrees?”

“I—no! I meant only—”

“Mr. Leigh only meant,” Campian cut in smoothly, “that the bull, meant for good, has been twisted by heretics to harm the faithful.”

Eustace, eager to mend matters, added, “Thus my father blames not the Pope, but the wickedness of his foes.”

“And for that very reason,” said Campian, “we bring a merciful interpretation of the bull.”

Mr. Leigh, emboldened by their retreat, pressed on. “I say no declaration was needed! The law of ’71 was never enforced till hotheads like Mayne forced it. Why stir trouble now?”

“Your son, sir, if not yet vowed to God, has a vocation. He will not heed temptations of the flesh.”

“Will you teach him disobedience?”

“He is ours as well as yours. Strange words from one who owes the Church a debt—one we hoped he would pay in his son’s devotion.”

This struck home. Mr. Leigh, stung to fury, swore at Parsons. “A plague on you! You’ll hang yet! Where is the dispensation you promised for the Hartland lands? You’ve taken my money, my son, my conscience—must you have my head too?”

Eustace, shamed by his father’s distress, spoke firmly. “Courage, sir. I am still your son, not yet a Jesuit—and whether I become one depends on how these reverend gentlemen treat you.”

The priests stared, thunderstruck. At last Parsons found his voice.

“Do you know the curse on those who look back after putting hand to plough?”

Eustace, impulsive and wavering, tried to signal that he spoke only to calm his father—but Campian was too wary, Parsons too enraged.

“I read that such are unfit for God’s kingdom—a honor I now doubt I merit. I am, after all, a citizen of a free country.” And with that, he took his father’s arm and left.

The words struck hard. All their schemes, pardons, and indulgences were but cobwebs before English law.

Later, Campian contrived to hear Eustace’s confession—and soon uncovered the truth: the young man was in love.

Had he spoken frankly, Eustace might have trusted him. But suspicion was second nature to the Jesuit, and he probed with delicate, relentless questions, till at last Eustace broke.

“She is the heiress of a wealthy merchant—eighteen, and beautiful.”

Campian mused. “Time enough to reclaim her. Absolve you? You are absolved. But there is a task at hand—one of danger.”

“Test me,” said Eustace fiercely.

Campian showed him a cipher-letter: instructions for a secret meeting on the Clovelly shore.

“I will go,” said Eustace at once.

“Then prove your trust. Let me see this lady who has so ensnared you.”

And so they walked out together—and at the crest of the hill, who should pass them but Rose Salterne herself, in a scarlet hood, her dark eyes gleaming beneath it.

Eustace trembled. “There! Now judge if my fault is venial.”

Campian sighed. “Alas, that such beauty must feed worms.”

“A feast for gods!” Eustace cried—then, fearing rebuke, darted after her.

He caught her at the lane’s turn. She started, but he seized her hand, pouring out wild words of love.

She struggled, frightened. “Let me go! You are too rough!”

But he held fast, blind to her fear. “Tell me—may I love you?”

“Go!” she cried, tears starting. “I am alone—I will call for help!”

Eustace, mistaking her distress for coyness, pressed closer—until her shriek forced him to flee. Yet one question burned in him—one he would risk all to ask.

*"It was among the ways of good Queen Bess,
Who ruled as well as ever mortal can, sir,
When she was stogg'd, and the country in a mess,
She was wont to send for a Devon man, sir."*

—*West Country Song*

The next morning, Amyas Leigh was nowhere to be found. Not that despair had driven him to drown himself in the Torridge, nor even to brood in solitude upon its banks. Instead, he had ridden off—Frank soon discovered—to Sir Richard Grenville at Stow. His mother, shrewd as ever, guessed the truth: he meant to seek a commission in the Irish wars. She sent Frank after him at once, to bring him home and make him reconsider.

So Frank mounted his horse and rode ten miles or more. Finding no inns along the way (as was common then, and still was, for that matter), and facing another ten miles of rugged hills, he turned toward Clovelly Court, where hospitality awaited any traveler in those generous times.

When he strode unannounced into the long, dark-paneled hall, the first sight that met his eyes was the towering figure of Amyas, seated at the great table, devouring a pasty with the vigor of a man whose sorrows had only sharpened his appetite. Opposite him, young Will Cary leaned on his elbows, arguing fiercely in a hushed voice.

"Hullo, lad!" Amyas called. "Come rescue me from this firebrand before he slays me for refusing to let him slay someone else."

"Ah, Mr. Frank!" said Will, rising—for like all the young gentlemen of Devon, he held Frank in high esteem, seeing him as the very model of chivalry and wit. "You’re a welcome sight! I’ve been longing for your counsel on a dozen matters. Sit, eat! The ale’s good."

"None for me this early, thank you."

"Avoid strong ale before noon, does he?" Amyas mocked, raising his tankard. "Lest it heat the blood, thicken the spirits, and befuddle the brain with frenzied humors! Eh, young sage? Come, render judgment—though I’ll wager my tankard’s not half-drained, I see plainly enough that Will here shan’t fight."

"Shan’t I? Who says so? Mr. Frank, hear me out—"

"We sit in judgment," said Frank, helping himself to the pasty. "Proceed, plaintiff."

"Well, I was telling Amyas that Tom Coffin of Portledge has tried my patience too far."

"Then leave him be," said Amyas. "He stood well enough on his own feet when last I saw him."

"Plague take your jests! Has he any right to glare at me so when I pass?"

"That depends on the glare. A cat may look at a king, so long as she doesn’t mistake him for a mouse."

"I know his looks—and his meaning too. He’ll stop, or I’ll stop him. And the other day, when I spoke of Rose Salterne—"

Frank groaned. "The golden apple again!"

"—he burst out laughing in my face! Is that not quarrel enough? Worse, I know he sent her a sonnet by market-woman to Stow. What right has he to write verses when I cannot? It’s foul play, Mr. Frank—or I’m a Spaniard, a Papist, and a Jew!" Will slammed the table, making the plates jump.

Frank leaned back, grinning. "I have a solution, most chivalrous and fitting. Let us appoint a day and summon, by drumbeat, all gentlemen under thirty within fifteen miles of the fair Oriana’s abode."

"And the ’prentice-boys too!" Amyas added through a mouthful.

"Indeed, the bold lads shall fight first with quarterstaffs in Bideford Market, till heads are cracked like walnuts. Those who emerge unscathed shall pay for their cowardice with their backs. Then, the grand tournament—compared to which Tottenham’s famed fray is but a flea-bite—"

"Confound your long words!" Will snapped. "You’re mocking me."

"Patience, good knight. What follows is no mockery, but bloody earnest. The survivors shall adjourn to some boggy field—better than sand, for none may flee when knee-deep in mire—strip to their shirts, and duel with rapiers of equal length. Last man standing shall, in tragic fashion, lament the cruelty of our fair Oriana and the slaughter wrought by her basilisk eyes, then gracefully fall upon his sword. *Placetne Domini?* As they say in Oxford’s halls."

Will scowled. "This is too much."

"So is fighting Tom Coffin with anything sharper than a bodkin."

"Bodkins?" Amyas laughed. "Too short for such hot-blooded bravos. They’d be at fisticuffs after the first pass."

"Then let them duel with squirts across the marketplace. By heaven and the queen’s law, they’ll fight with nothing else!"

Frank’s tone softened suddenly. "Dear Will, do not think I mock your pain. I too have known love’s sting—and jealousy’s sharper bite. But is it not a sin to waste noble wrath on private quarrels, when heaven meant it for her whom all bad men fear and good men adore—our peerless queen? Who dares call sword or courage his own while she reigns? Are there no Spaniards to strike down, no Irish wrongs to right, that two Devon men must turn their blades on each other?"

Amyas thumped the table. "Frank speaks truth! Must Christian gentlemen brawl like bulls in a pasture?"

"And shall the heir of Clovelly," Frank added, "take the stag in his park for his example?"

Will sighed. "You’re a scholar, Mr. Frank, and speak well. But gentlemen must fight sometimes, or where’s their honor?"

"I speak not just as a scholar," said Frank, pride flashing in his eyes, "but as one who has fought—and killed his man, God have mercy on his soul. Yet I boast this: I have never drawn steel for myself. And I pray I never shall. To fight for those we love is blessed; to fight for pride is sin—unless honor or life itself be at stake. And even then..." He trailed off.

Amyas nodded. "I fear no ghosts, but when I struck down that French braggart, I thought: had he slandered me, not the queen, I’d see his head on my pillow nightly."

Will shuddered. "God forbid! But what then? Tomorrow I go to market, though it’s choked with Coffins—aye, and a ghost in every one!"

"Leave it to me," said Amyas. "I’ve a plan. If blades cross tomorrow, see if I don’t—"

And ocean was their grave."*

Adrian Gilbert leaned forward, his fingers tracing the edge of the silver ingot on the table. "You see, dear Mrs. Hawkins, this gossan—what the Cornish call the very marrow of the earth—holds more than mere lead and copper. With the right alchemy, the luna can be refined to silver, and what remains transformed into sol—gold. Imagine it! England need no longer grovel before Spain’s treasure fleets. We could rival Peru itself."

Mrs. Hawkins sighed, her worn hands clasped tightly in her lap. "And what good is gold if the soul is lost, Mr. Gilbert? If you could turn sinners to saints, now—that would be a miracle worth seeking."

Adrian chuckled, though his eyes remained distant. "Gold buys ships, madam. Ships win empires. And empires break the grip of Antichrist."

She shook her head. "God’s blessing does not rest on greed. My John—God keep him—has grown hard with it. Ever since he took to trafficking in those poor souls from Guinea..." Her voice faltered as her gaze lifted to the crest carved into the oak beam above—a bound Moor, grim reminder of her husband’s trade.

Adrian followed her glance and frowned. "Men must do what they must in these times."

"Must they?" she whispered. "The Lord sees, Mr. Gilbert. He sees the blood on our hands."

The fire crackled between them, casting long shadows over the Spanish silver on the sideboard, the charts of distant coasts, the portrait of old Captain Will Hawkins, whose honest face seemed to judge them from the wall.

A sudden knock shattered the silence.

Amyas Leigh stood in the doorway, salt-crusted and hollow-eyed.

Adrian surged to his feet. "Amyas! What news? Where is my brother?"

Amyas swallowed hard. "The *Golden Hind* has made port. But Sir Humphrey..."

Adrian’s face drained of color. "Speak, man!"

"He is with God."

The words fell like a stone. Adrian swayed, gripping the table. "How?"

Amyas steadied him, voice low. "The *Squirrel* went down off the Azores. He was reading when the wave took her. His last words were, *‘We are as near heaven by sea as by land.’*"

Mrs. Hawkins wept silently. Adrian stared into the fire, lips moving as if in prayer.

Amyas continued, recounting the storms, the desertions, the *Delight*’s wreck with all its scholars and dreams. Adrian barely heard. His brother—the scholar, the dreamer—was gone.

At last, Amyas fell silent. The clock ticked. The fire dimmed.

Adrian rose, voice cracked. "You will excuse me." He left without another word.

Mrs. Hawkins wiped her eyes. "Poor soul. And poor you, Captain Leigh—you’ve lost your venture."

Amyas nodded grimly. "Aye. But that’s not the worst of it." He hesitated. "There’s news from Bideford. Rose Salterne—the mayor’s daughter—has fled with that Spanish prisoner. The one I sent home."

Mrs. Hawkins gasped. "The Rose of Torridge? Gone? Oh, the wickedness!"

Amyas said nothing. His hands clenched, the knuckles white.

She pressed a cup into his grasp. "Drink, lad. You’re pale as death."

He drank mechanically, tasting nothing. The flames flickered, casting grotesque shapes on the wall—a lion’s gaping maw, a sinking ship, a girl’s face, vanishing into the dark.

At dawn, he rode for Bideford, the weight of failure heavy on his shoulders.

Behind him, the sea roared on, indifferent.

It grieves me to say that in those days, the road from Plymouth to the wilds of northern Devon was no safe passage. To reach their destination without a lengthy detour, travelers were forced to cross the domain of a rogue warlord, a man who had defied Queen Elizabeth’s authority time and again. Behind his back, men called him the King of the Gubbings.

*"Now I dare name them,"* wrote Fuller, *"safe by distance—a liberty the boldest among us would not take to their faces, for fear of their swift and savage vengeance. Yet none can say for certain whence their name is derived. We call fish-scraps ‘gubbings,’ worthless refuse, and they know well the word carries shame. Some say they dwell among the ‘gibbering’ hills, but let scholars smile—the truth is darker.*

*"They are a lawless breed, a heathen Scythia within England’s bounds. Near Brent Tor they fester, sprung from outcast women two centuries past, who fled to the moors to hide their shame. There, they multiplied without marriage, living in hovels like swine, sharing all, stealing sheep, and mocking both church and crown. No sheriff dares root them out, no constable can tame them. Fleet as deer, hardy as the moorland stones, they strike as one if one is wronged. Yet now, they say, some begin to turn Christian—baptizing their children, returning to the fold. Let us pray no civilized men among us grow barbarous, now that these barbarians learn civility."*

With that jab at the dissenters of his day, Fuller closed his account. And so, with this warning in mind, Amyas Leigh rode out of Plymouth armed for war—clad in full steel, lance in hand, sword at his side, and two heavy pistols at his belt. Behind him came Salvation Yeo and a handful of Devon men, veterans of Irish campaigns, each bristling with pike and blade, while Yeo bore his trusty arquebus. Two pack-ponies carried their gear, and the little troop made haste toward Tavistock, hoping to reach Lydford by nightfall.

But delays had kept them till noon, and dusk found them on the edge of the Gubbings’ territory—a bleak, wind-scoured heath, shadowed by the tors of Dartmoor to the east, sloping toward the great black cone of Brent Tor, its summit crowned by a tiny church, a merchant’s vow to the Virgin. Far below, threads of smoke curled from the dens of the outlaws. More than once, the men paused, eyeing distant shapes—was that a patrol, or only gorse and wandering ponies?

A lone inn squatted on the moor, a blot of lichen-spotted stone, its thatch half-torn, its windows patched with paper. Behind it sprawled a filthy yard where pigs and barefoot children wallowed together. At the door lounged the innkeeper—a hulking brute, hose sagging, hair a nest of greasy tangles, feathers clinging to him as though he’d tumbled straight from a stolen goose-bed.

*"Mirooi! Fushing pooale!"* he bellowed.

A stout maid, clad in a grimy bodice and kirtle, hauled out his fishing gear. The man grunted, squinting at his line.

*"Might be,"* said the girl. *"Old hen were pecking about."*

The host swore, swung a blow at her head—she dodged, smacked him back, and he shambled off, howling, *"Tell Patrico!"*

Mary ducked inside, scrubbed her face, slipped on stockings and a cleaner gown, and prepared to greet the guests. The men wrinkled their noses at the stench but chose to stay rather than camp in enemy land.

A scrawny hen (the supposed thief of the dun-fly) was soon roasting, and "black Dartmoor mutton" sizzled on the griddle, though its rich aroma betrayed it as venison. Amyas stabled his horse in a rickety shed—no lock, no key—and returned uneasy. The baggage was piled in a corner, and he stretched by the fire while Yeo, suspicious, kept watch. The sailors, drawn by the sight of Mary cooking, loitered near the hearth.

Then Yeo slipped in, tense.

*"Bid him join us,"* said Amyas.

Yeo vanished, returned, vanished again. When he came back the third time, his eyes burned.

Amyas scoffed, but Yeo was adamant. *"I knew him by his creeping step—like he feared to offend Heaven with a misstep. Saw him whisper to the girl, give her some Popish token. She kissed it, crossed herself! Then he sneaked to the stable. I barred him in!"*

Amyas sighed. *"Fool’s work, Yeo! What if he’s harmless?"*

Grumbling, Amyas freed the prisoner—and found himself face-to-face with his cousin, Eustace Leigh.

Eustace stood frozen, the very picture of a fox cornered by hounds. For a long moment, neither spoke.

*"Cousin sneak-thief,"* said Amyas at last, *"since when do you meddle with horse-stealing?"*

Eustace drew himself up. *"I entered an inn stable, nothing more."*

Amyas relented. *"Jest, coz. But why here? Prudence never was your strength."*

Yeo snorted. *"Aye, corrupting poor heathens!"*

Eustace flared. *"Heathens you Protestants abandon! While we risk our lives to save them, you scorn and persecute us! Turn me in if you will, disgrace your name—but I will not flee while one soul here remains unsaved!"*

Amyas laughed. *"Come, at least spare the horses conversion! Out with you—I’ll not betray you. Yeo holds his tongue if I order it."*

Yeo scowled but nodded.

Eustace, seeing no choice, followed Amyas inside. Supper passed warily at first, but old memories thawed the tension—until Eustace asked why Amyas rode for Bideford while Frank and his mother were in London.

Eustace went white. *"What of her?"*

Amyas told him. Eustace shrieked, clawing at the air.

Amyas stared. *"You loved her too?"*

Now Amyas started—how did Eustace know of the Brotherhood? But pity drowned suspicion. *"Then help me find her—"*

Eustace cut him off, suddenly icy. *"And what will your Brotherhood do? Hunt her down? Murder her lover, drag her back in chains?"*

Amyas stiffened. *"If she’s found peace, so be it. But I’ll know the truth."*

Eustace smirked. *"You forget—I am under your roof as your guest. Or does a Protestant’s honor mean so little?"*

Furious, Amyas flung open the door. *"To the devil with you, then!"*

Eustace vanished into the night.

Amyas turned to his men. *"Sleep here. We ride at dawn."*

But at midnight, chaos erupted—geese shrieking, steel clashing. Yeo, guarding the stable, was beset by a dozen ruffians. Amyas and his men burst into the yard, scattering the attackers—but not before Yeo ran two through. One lay dead; the other groaned in the straw.

As they bore the wounded man inside, shouts rang out again. Bursting into the kitchen, Amyas found Parsons—half-dressed, candle in hand—and Eustace, caught sneaking out the front.

*"A fine nest we’ve uncovered!"* Amyas laughed.

Parsons bristled. *"I tend my flock, sir. This wounded lamb needs shriving."*

The dying man moaned for the *"Patrico."*

*"See?"* said Parsons smugly. *"The sheep know their shepherd."*

Amyas shook his head. *"Then tend your sheep—but know this: I’ll have the truth of Rose Salterne, with or without your help."*

And with that, the night’s shadows deepened, and the moor held its secrets close.

Six weeks had passed since the duel on the sands, and the miller of Stow came to the great house in distress. Rose Salterne had vanished in the night, and none knew where she had gone.

Sir Richard was away in Bideford, but the old steward took charge, summoning the keepers with their bloodhounds. Soon, a crowd of villagers and serving-men swarmed down to the mill, eager for the hunt—some convinced the poor girl had been driven to despair, others whispering she had run off with some rogue.

Rose had left her trinkets behind, taking only a few linens—which old Anthony the steward darkly hinted might turn up in *someone’s* chest. The only trace was a faint footprint beneath her window. The hound caught the scent, leading them straight to the downs and then to Lucy Passmore’s cottage by Marslandmouth.

Lucy, now a widow, had spun a fine tale after her husband vanished with Eustace and the Jesuits. She had wept just enough to seem convincing, then marched to Stow, declaring her husband stolen by Papist villains—perhaps even *salted down* for their voyage back to Rome. Lady Grenville, who trusted Lucy’s remedies for the children’s ailments, persuaded Sir Richard to grant her a pension.

But now Lucy herself was gone. Her door was barred, her goats unmilked, her donkeys braying on the hillside. The bloodhound led them to the sea’s edge before losing the trail. Frustrated, the mob broke into her cottage, finding nothing but a black cat that fled up the chimney—some swore with a whiff of brimstone. The animals were driven off to Stow, and Lucy’s cottage was left to crumble, its silence broken only by the murmuring stream.

Days later, Sir Richard brought news to Clovelly Court that sent Will Cary leaping from his seat.

*"No trace?"* asked old Cary, while his son stood speechless.

Within hours, Will was aboard a fishing skiff with armed men, bound for the island. Three days later, he returned with scraps of rumor: An elderly foreigner had lodged in the ruins of Moresco Castle; a younger man joined him, arriving on a Flushing ship. Then, a lady and her maid came—spoke long with the elder man—and sailed away at dawn.

*"The young man called him *father*,"* the fishermen said.

John Braund, the smuggler who had housed them, was hauled to Exeter Gaol, where fever soon silenced him forever. Suspicion lingered, but certainty was lost.

Rose’s fate became gossip for Bideford’s tongues—some swore she had fled with the Spaniard, others that she was dead. The women who had envied her beauty now clucked over her ruin, while young maidens eyed her vacant throne.

Will Cary was half-mad with guilt, blaming himself for driving her away. St. Leger reasoned with him—*"She was lost to you the moment she loved him"*—but Will would not be comforted. He wrote to Frank Leigh, confessing all, and received a reply steeped in melancholy acceptance.

*"The stars ruled her choice,"* Frank wrote. *"Who are we to question heaven?"*

But Jack Brimblecombe, now curate of Hartland, saw things differently.

*"We swore an oath, Mr. Cary,"* he said, his voice steady. *"Oaths are oaths."*

*"If need be,"* said Jack. *"Better that than live wondering if she’s in hell or wed to a Papist."*

Cary tried to dissuade him, but Jack was resolved. He scraped together coin, walked to London, and returned with grim news.

*"Frank Leigh is dying,"* he said. *"Dying of love for her. But he says—wait."*

So Jack waited, though his heart was set.

Then, by chance, Cary met old Salterne on Bideford quay.

*"If I were you,"* the merchant said, *"I’d be in the Indies by now."*

The words struck like flint. Cary sought Amyas.

*"If you go, I go,"* he said. *"Westward-ho, come foul or fair."*

*"A wild chase,"* Amyas warned.

*"Then let it be wild,"* Cary replied. *"But we’ll find her—or make sure none else does."*

And so the plan was set. They would take Jack with them—for his conscience’ sake. And as for Cary’s father?

*"He has a new heir now,"* Will laughed. *"A lusty babe from his young wife. He’ll not miss me."*

Thus, with purpose hardened and oaths remembered, the path was clear.

Westward they would go. For honor. For vengeance. And for a Rose they could not forget.

—*Sir Andrew Barton*

Amyas Leigh stepped into the boat at Whitehall Stairs, gliding beneath the shadow of old London Bridge, his thoughts as turbulent as the Thames beneath him. The city’s clamor faded behind as the wherry carried him downriver, past the bustling docks, to where the *Pelican* lay at Deptford Creek—a relic of glory, high and dry upon the sedgy bank.

Drake’s famed ship, the vessel that had circled the world, stood like a weary warrior resting after battle. Her timbers, seasoned by salt and storm, bore verses nailed upon her mast—tributes to the captain who had defied the vast unknown. It was here that Elizabeth herself had feasted, where laughter and song once echoed across her decks, where she had knighted Drake upon those very planks.

Yet today, the *Pelican* was no silent monument. Flags fluttered from her masts; the scent of roasted meats and spiced wine drifted from her open ports. The Lord Mayor, Sir Edward Osborne, was hosting a feast—a gathering of men whose names would one day be etched into the annals of empire.

Amyas paused at the gangway, taking in the scene. At the head of the table sat Osborne, a man whose legend was already writ bold—the clothworker’s apprentice who had leaped into the churning Thames to save a child, now grown into a merchant prince. To his right, the Earl of Cumberland, ever eager for ventures beyond the horizon; to his left, Walter Raleigh, whose words wove dreams of undiscovered lands.

Nearby, Richard Hakluyt bent close to old Anthony Jenkinson, the traveler whose tales of Siberian steppes and ivory-laden ice cliffs held the room rapt. Across from them, Christopher Carlile spoke in low tones with Bristol’s aldermen—men whose gold financed the quest for a passage to Cathay.

And there, apart from the rest, a knot of scholars and adventurers clustered around Philip Sidney, whose courtly grace could not hide the fire in his eyes. Martin Frobisher and John Davis, veterans of frozen seas, traded jests with him, while Sidney lamented that politics kept him land-bound.

“A fine thing,” Frobisher grunted, “to talk of icebergs with a full glass in hand. Try it when your beard’s frozen to your ruff, eh?”

Sidney laughed, but his gaze was distant. “If her majesty but waved me onward, I’d brave worse than ice.”

At that moment, a servant approached Raleigh. “A gentleman to see you, sir.”

Amyas stepped forward, his face grim. A hush fell as Raleigh excused himself, and the two withdrew. When Amyas returned alone, the tale was written in his eyes before he spoke.

Sir Humphrey Gilbert was dead.

The revelry withered like frost upon glass. Noblemen who had wagered fortunes on the venture now forgot their losses in the shadow of that greater loss. Osborne rose, his voice solemn.

“The Egyptians, when they feasted, set a corpse among them—to remind their guests of mortality. Has God sent us such a messenger tonight?”

Sidney shook his head. “Not from death, my lord—from immortality.”

Yet the feast was over. The men dispersed, murmuring of what the queen would say. Raleigh, silent and pale, led Amyas away.

Days passed before Frank dared seek the queen’s leave. When at last he stood before her, Elizabeth’s wrath was sharp—yet so too was her mercy.

“You would abandon me for a dream?” she demanded.

Frank knelt. “If I dared dream at all, madam, it was first by your light.”

She relented, though not without a barb. “Go, then. But return to me alive—or not at all.”

As Frank left the palace, Spenser himself crossed his path, smiling. “Your tale is already half a ballad, my friend. The *Faerie Queene* will have a new knight ere long.”

Frank laughed, though his eyes were shadowed. “A knight errant, perhaps. But the queen’s frown is sharper than any dragon’s tooth.”

Amyas, waiting below, clapped him on the shoulder. “Then we’d best find our armor.”

The west wind whispered over the Thames, carrying with it the scent of salt and far horizons. Somewhere beyond the dying light, their fate awaited.

*“The sun’s rim dips; the stars rush out;
At one stride comes the dark.”*

Land! At last, after weeks of endless blue, a dark line shimmered on the horizon—a promise of solid earth beneath their feet. Barbados rose before them, bathed in the golden light of the setting sun, a silhouette between crimson waves and a sky aflame. The sight sent a thrill through the weary crew. Here was rest, fresh water, and perhaps—if fortune smiled—riches untold. The Old World lay far behind; before them stretched the unknown, a realm of dreams and possibility.

Men weak with scurvy dragged themselves from their hammocks. Strong hands clasped in prayer, voices murmuring thanks to God. Every eye strained toward that distant shore, now fading as dusk claimed the sky. Yet the trade wind, warm and steady, urged them forward, whispering like an old friend: *"I am the wind of daring men."*

Frank stood apart, his thin face alight with fevered hope. *"Blow, wind,"* he whispered, *"carry me not from death, but to her—to call her home before I die."*

As night fell, the colors of the sunset melted into silver and shadow. The moon rose, bright beside Venus, casting a path across the darkening sea.

*"That should be Barbados, sir,"* said Drew, the master. *"Unless my reckoning’s gone wild—and after such a voyage, God knows it shouldn’t."*

*"No Spaniards, no savages, nothing but peace,"* added Yeo. *"A garden set aside by the Lord for those who love Him. Drake spoke once of planting it, if given the chance."*

Amyas nodded. *"Aye, I recall some talk between him and poor Sir Humphrey. Would God he’d sailed here instead of to that frozen waste."*

*"Nay, sir,"* said Yeo gently. *"He’s in heaven now. Would you deny him that?"*

*"Not for the world. But he’d have stayed if it served the Queen."* Amyas turned to the crew. *"What say you, lads? A few days here to mend our sick before we push on?"*

A cheer answered him—all but Frank, who stood silent.

*"Speak, brother,"* urged Cary. *"Your voice counts as well."*

Frank sighed. *"My heart pulls me onward, Will. Yet reason agrees. We stay."*

By dawn, they had skirted the island’s southern shore, gliding into the calm bay where Bridgetown would one day rise. The men leaned over the rails, drinking in the scent of flowers, the hum of insects, the cries of night birds—sounds sweeter than the endless murmur of the waves.

At last, the anchor dropped. A roar of joy erupted from the crew.

*"Easy, lads!"* Amyas laughed. *"Dawn’s but two hours off. Save your shore-leave till then."*

*"Two hours?"* groaned a young sailor. *"An eternity!"*

*"Try two years in the Inquisition,"* Yeo muttered. *"Then you’d know patience."*

When morning came, the sick were carried ashore, laid beneath the palms. The rest scattered like boys unleashed—some gorging on wild grapes, others hacking at coconut trees with blunt axes. Yeo and a handful of wiser men ventured inland, returning laden with guavas, pineapples, and strange, tart fruits that set their mouths watering.

Frank wandered, awestruck, while Amyas—ever the practical guide—pointed out hummingbirds, crabs that waved defiant claws, and cranes standing sentinel in the shallows.

*"All new,"* Frank murmured. *"The very flies and flowers strange to me. Yet I remain unchanged."*

Amyas grinned, spitting out a junipa seed. *"If it’s good enough for God’s eyes, Frank, it’s good enough for ours."*

Frank smiled. *"A fair rebuke, brother. Who am I to scorn what the Lord made beautiful?"*

For four days, they rested—washing clothes, filling casks, and chasing rumors of gold. Yet Barbados gave no treasure but its own untouched splendor. At last, with laughter still ringing in their ears, they weighed anchor, leaving behind an island still wild, still waiting—unaware that they had been the first heralds of the change to come.

The night had carried them silently past the southern tip of Grenada, and now they sailed into the heart of an enchanted ring of islands—where nature had poured out her most dazzling beauty, and man his darkest sins. To those who had marveled at Barbados, these seas were a revelation: a world of unbroken calm, spared the fury of the northern hurricanes, where sky and water melted into a single shimmering dream. Yet the men aboard the ship had little eye for beauty; their thoughts were fixed on Spanish gold and pearls. Even Amyas, though no stranger to the tropics, was less moved by the scenery than by visions of conquest—of driving out the Spaniards and claiming these lands for the Crown of England.

Still, none could escape the thrill of these storied waters, so new to English sails, so heavy with the wonder and greed of the age. The stakes were high: failure meant not merely death, but the Inquisition’s torments. Few English ships had dared these passages before, but among the crew were men who knew them well—too well. Veterans of John Hawkins’ ill-fated voyage, they had learned these coasts at sword-point when Spanish hostility barred peaceful trade. Now, as the older sailors pointed out each isle and headland, Frank stood apart, his soul alight with awe.

*"This is the gate of heaven,"* he murmured, his gaze lost in the shifting horizon. The very air seemed to burn with mirages—mountains hanging inverted in the sky, sand-hills rolling like phantoms, the sun itself distorting into strange shapes as it sank through crimson haze. But while Frank marveled, Yeo watched the south with a sailor’s sharp eye. There, just where the sun dipped, rose the peaks of Macanao—the western spur of Margarita, the Isle of Pearls, famed from the Mediterranean to the German fairs.

By the next day, they had slipped unseen past the Spanish fort guarding the pearl fisheries and now approached a quiet bay, its waters sheltered by thick woods. Anchored within lay a caravel and three boats. At the sight, every man was on deck at once, voices clamoring over what to do. Some urged a bold strike into the bay, but caution won out—the reefs were treacherous. Instead, Amyas and Cary would take the pinnace and longboat in.

Yeo suggested flying Spanish colors, but Amyas refused. *"A lie is a mean thing, save for great need."*

So they held course until the bay’s mouth was out of sight, then launched their attack. Cary, with twenty men, swarmed the caravel before the Spaniards could man their guns. The captain fired a defiant pistol shot—and found himself sprawled on the deck as Cary’s return stroke sent him down. The crew surrendered at once, some begging mercy, others leaping into the sea.

Meanwhile, Amyas closed on the second boat. The Spaniards threw up their hands, crying *"Misericordia!"* while the enslaved Africans dove overboard, swimming for shore like seals. The third boat tried to flee—and here, Jack Brimblecombe, eager to prove himself, made his mark. Snatching a boat-hook, he latched onto the stern, bellowing, *"Yield, Papists!"*—only to be yanked headlong into the water.

What followed was pure chaos. Convinced sharks were upon him, Jack thrashed and howled like a man possessed, his cassock billowing behind him like some monstrous black fish. *"They’re biting my legs! Oh, that I were preaching in Hartland pulpit!"* The crew, torn between laughter and alarm, finally shot over the fugitives’ heads, driving them into the sea. Jack, gasping, hauled himself into the abandoned boat and brandished his sword at empty air before realizing the fight was done.

Only then did he notice his hose—drenched, but unharmed. His *"mortal wound"* was pure imagination. The men roared as he sat, panting and peeling off his sodden garments, while Amyas and Frank teased him mercilessly for his earlier panic.

*"A fine preacher you’ll be,"* laughed Amyas, *"if you cry havoc before the battle’s even begun!"*

Frank, ever the scholar, took a sharper tack. *"Tell me, Jack—does not a priest contain a man? Then if he acts less than a man, what is he?"*

Jack, red-faced, could only grumble about sharks and logic—until the jests grew too sharp, and he fled below, vowing to prove himself yet.

The next day, a Sunday, saw Jack shamefacedly leading prayers (though he refused to preach). Afterward, they landed on a quiet beach to wash clothes and refill water casks. Jack, still smarting from his ridicule, went armed with sword and arquebuse—*"for last night I dreamed of Spaniards, and dreams oft come true."*

And come true they did.

As the men worked, a volley of shots erupted from the trees. Fifty Spanish arquebusiers burst forth, led by a plumed officer on horseback. Amyas barely had time to shout *"Stand!"* before the attack was upon them.

But Jack, as if expecting it, calmly marked his Bible, set it aside, and charged. His first shot dropped the Spanish captain dead. Then, flinging aside his gun, he waded into the enemy with his sword, striking so fiercely that the Spaniards broke and ran before the others could even form ranks.

*"Come back, Jack! Are you mad?"* Amyas bellowed. But Jack, silent and relentless, chased them until a root sent him sprawling, knocking the wind from his lungs.

When the smoke cleared, the Spanish were gone, leaving their captain and several others dead or wounded. Cary, laughing, clapped Jack on the shoulder. *"Nineteen at least, and seven with one stroke! Will you mock the parson’s son now?"*

Jack, grinning through his exhaustion, could only wheeze, *"No more talk of sharks, eh?"*

That evening, Frank asked how he had found such courage so suddenly.

Jack shrugged. *"Fear makes a man foolish—but anger makes him brave."*

And with that, the day’s triumph was sealed—not just in pearls and prisoners, but in the proving of a man who had laughed at his own fears and risen above them.

The crew buzzed with restless energy, eager to scour the waters around Margarita and Cubagua for another fortune in pearls. But Amyas Leigh, with the fire of recent victory still burning in his veins, would not linger where Spanish ears had surely caught the alarm. They set their course southwest, skirting the vast bay that stretched from the Paria Peninsula to Cape Codera. To starboard lay Tortuga, its name a whisper of danger; to port, the low, green-fringed islands of the Piritoos, barely rising above the still, tideless sea.

Yeo and Drew knew these waters well—too well. They had sailed them once before with Hawkins, and the memory of Spanish treachery still festered. Now, as they pressed forward, the towering peaks of the Caracas mountains loomed ahead, their jagged summits scraping the heavens. For men accustomed to the rolling hills of Devon or the modest heights of Exmoor, the sight was staggering.

Brimblecombe, wide-eyed, clung to the rigging, craning his neck until it ached. "God's mercy, but they scrape the sky!" he muttered, earning a laugh from Cary.

The sea grew restless as they neared the cape, the current fighting against them even as the wind favored their course. All night they battled the waves, the black mass of Cape Codera looming above them, its cliffs lost in shadow, the moonlight painting the distant peaks in silver.

At dawn, a ship appeared on the horizon—a Spanish vessel, already ten miles eastward. Yeo's eyes gleamed. "We could take her, Captain. A soldier's wind both ways—easy enough to run her down."

Amyas hesitated. The thought of rich plunder tugged at him, but Frank's voice cut through the temptation. "La Guayra is our mark, Yeo. We've a greater purpose than prizes."

Yeo scowled. "Every Spaniard left afloat is a Spaniard left to strike at England. The Lord delivers her into our hands—shall we refuse Him?"

Amyas clenched his fists. Duty warred within him—duty to the queen, to his country, to the vow that had driven him across the ocean. Frank's steady gaze tipped the balance.

"We hold to our course," Amyas said.

The Spanish ship faded into the distance, and with it, perhaps, their best chance of striking a blow against Spain. But La Guayra lay ahead, and with it, the shadow of a name long unspoken—Rose Salterne.

***

A canoe approached at midday, its triangular sail straining against the wind. Two Indians stood aboard, bronzed and fearless. Cary grinned. "Look there, Frank—metal floats in these seas after all!"

Frank, ever the scholar, frowned. "Explain."

"Why else would those two statues dare sail in such a nutshell?" Cary teased.

The canoe drew alongside, and one of the Indians—tall, proud, his bearing that of a nobleman—leapt aboard with effortless grace. His eyes swept the deck, then darkened with recognition.

"Treachery!" he shouted in Spanish, lunging for the side. A dozen hands seized him before he could escape.

Amyas raised a hand. "No harm to him! Let him speak."

The Indian stood defiant. "I am a Guayqueria—a free man, loyal to the King of Spain. I will tell you nothing."

A murmur of anger rippled through the crew. Some muttered of ropes and hot irons, but Amyas silenced them.

"You are a brave man," he said. "But we are no enemies of yours. We seek only to know what ships lie in La Guayra."

The Indian hesitated, then shook his head. "I will not betray my king."

Amyas laughed. "Then go in peace."

As the Indian turned to leave, he paused. "A warning, señor. Do not go to La Guayra. Ships of war wait for you there. The governor, Don Guzman, sailed east yesterday—to hunt you."

Amyas's blood ran cold. The ship they had passed—had it been Guzman himself?

Too late now. They pressed on, the mountains rising ever higher, their slopes dense with jungle, their peaks lost in mist.

***

La Guayra at last. A meager strip of land clung between the sea and the mountains, its white houses huddled beneath the towering cliffs. The anchorage was open, exposed—no harbor, only a roadstead where five ships swayed at anchor.

Two merchant caravels lay outermost. Behind them, two galleys—low, sleek, deadly. And beyond them, a larger vessel, her gunports dark and waiting.

Yeo spat. "Galleys. And that’s no merchantman."

Amyas studied them. "Six guns on the main deck. Brass patararoes on the poop. We’re outmatched."

A puff of smoke from the eastern fort. A ball splashed into the sea nearby.

No warning. No parley.

"They know we’re coming," Drew muttered.

***

Night fell. The council of war was grim.

"We can’t land with those ships watching," Amyas said. "And we can’t take them head-on."

Frank’s eyes were distant, fixed on a light in the governor’s house—a single window glowing against the dark.

"We came for her," he said softly. "I will not leave without trying."

Amyas gripped his arm. "It’s death, Frank."

Frank smiled. "Is duty ever death? If God calls me to it, I will go."

John Brimblecombe stepped forward. "Then I go with you."

Cary and Amyas echoed him, but Frank shook his head. "No. Our mother needs you, Amyas. Cary, your lands await you. But John—"

"I have neither," Jack said stoutly. "Only my oath. I go."

They drew lots in the end, leaving it to God’s hand.

And as the boat pushed off into the darkness, Frank’s gaze never left that distant light—the beacon that might guide him to Rose, or to his end.

The dawn broke swift and golden over the Caribbean, the light flaring across the waves like a challenge. Amyas Leigh stood on the deck of the *Rose*, his clothes torn, his hair wild, his eyes burning with a grief too deep for tears. His heart was a tempest—rage, despair, and vengeance warring within him. Those who have lost a brother may fathom his torment; those who have not, let them thank God they never shall.

Impossible schemes stormed his mind—burning the villa at Guayra to ashes, sacking the port, demanding a life for every drop of his brother’s blood.

"We can do it, lads!" he roared. "If Drake took Nombre de Dios, we take La Guayra!" A thunderous shout answered him.

Will Cary, ever the steadfast voice, gripped his shoulder. "We’ll have it, Amyas. And Frank too, yet." But Amyas knew—somehow, he *knew*—no port in New Spain would ever return that beloved face to him.

His gaze snapped to the horizon. Three sails, no more than five miles to windward, cut the morning haze.

"There they are—the Spanish bloodhounds on our scent! The same ships from Guayra. Shall we welcome them, though they were a dozen?"

A roar of approval answered him. If any young heart quailed at the odds, the veterans drowned it with their cheers. Salvation Yeo’s voice boomed like judgment:

"Were there a score, the Lord is with us! Did He not say, *One shall chase a thousand?* Clear the decks, lads, and see His glory this day!"

"Amen!" Cary echoed.

Amyas felt the battle-lust rise, dulling pain and sorrow. Frank’s face faded behind the fire in his veins. His voice, steady once more, rang out:

"Now, my masters, prayers—then to breakfast, and after, clear for action!"

Jack Brimblecombe’s voice trembled only once—when he added to the Prayer for All Conditions of Men, "*Especially for our dear brother Mr. Francis Leigh, captive among idolaters.*"

Then, with grim mirth, Amyas said, "To breakfast. A Frenchman fights best fasting, a Dutchman drunk, an Englishman full, and a Spaniard when the devil’s in him—and he’s always in them."

"Good beef and a good cause are match enough for the devil," Cary retorted, forcing food into Amyas’s hands before vanishing below, his own cheeks wet.

Amyas took the helm, his face aged a decade in a night. A terrible calm gripped him. Woe to any who crossed him this day.

The enemy closed—a great galleon leading, two galleys flanking her.

"Let the ship wait," Amyas commanded. "We’ll take the galleys first."

Yeo grinned. "A Daniel for wisdom in a lad so young! Follow him, or be cursed like the men of Meroz!"

The *Rose* readied for battle. Amyas took the poop, Cary the forecastle, Yeo the guns, Drew the waist. Every man knew his place.

The Spanish galleon loomed, her golden standard proud. Trumpets blared defiance; the *Rose* answered. Then silence—before Amyas cried, "*Fortune my Foe!* God and the Queen with us!"

Musicians struck up the old war-song as Parson Jack sawed his fiddle like a man possessed.

The Spaniard, puzzled by their boldness, hauled up his mainsail, trying to keep the wind advantage.

Amyas grinned. "Let him try. Hold course. No firing till my word."

The Spaniard’s bow guns barked—shots splashed wide. Amyas waited.

"Ready about!" The *Rose* pivoted like a dancer, crossing the Spaniard’s stern at pistol-shot.

"*Fire!*"

A storm of iron and lead ripped into the galleon. Muskets and bows added their venom. The helmsman dropped; the mizzen mast toppled; stern windows shattered. The golden flag of Spain trailed in the water.

A deafening cheer. "She’s struck!" some cried.

"Not yet," Amyas growled. "Helm up—we’ve galleys to gut."

The galleys swept in, forty oars apiece, their long rams glistening. Soldiers packed the forecastles; cannon muzzles grinned from their bows.

Between the oar-banks, slaves writhed under the lash, their stench fouling the wind. The crack of whips, the screams of men—was there English blood among them?

"Must we fire on slaves?" a voice asked.

Amyas clenched his jaw. "Spare them if you can. But if they close—rake them, and God forgive us."

The galleys converged, aiming to crush the *Rose* between them.

Amyas waited.

At forty yards, he roared, "Hard to larboard!"

The *Rose* veered, her bow grinding through oars, hurling slaves like kindling. The starboard broadside roared, point-blank. A hellish shriek answered.

"*Cut the soldiers! Free the slaves!*"

Amyas led the boarding party, steel flashing. In minutes, the galley’s deck was theirs.

From the wreckage of oars, a Devon voice shrieked:

"Robert Drew! For Christ’s sake, get me out of hell!"

Drew, forgetting all discipline, leapt down with a hammer.

"William Prust! Captain Hawkins left us in Honduras—nine of us here!"

The two old comrades embraced amidst the carnage.

The *Rose* lived up to her name—blood-red, thorn-sharp, and blooming even in death’s shadow.

Amyas should have fallen to the yellow fever—would have, had he been any other man. But he had no time for sickness, not while his crew lay stricken around him. A soldier’s duty, as many a weary soul in the Crimea would later learn, is to stand until the battle is done—only then does exhaustion claim its due.

At dawn, he called his officers together—not for war now, but for survival. The men were mutinous with fear. Amyas faced them squarely.

“Kill me if you like,” he said, “though I’ll warn you, I won’t die easy. But ask yourselves—what good does it serve?”

Then he turned to his council. The surgeon babbled of humors and complexions. Jack Brimblecombe preached of divine wrath. Cary jested grimly. Yeo stood silent, quoting Scripture under his breath. The sailing master, Drew, shrugged. “My task is to sail, not to cure fevers.”

Amyas clenched his fists in his hair.

“Enough!” he roared. “Doctor, spare me your humors—can you change a man’s skin like a snake sheds its scales? Jack, this is no act of God. We do His work—why would He strike us down? No, this is the devil’s own mischief. And we’ll thwart him yet.”

He turned on them all, eyes blazing.

“The sickness is in the air. I felt it last night—rank as a London sewer. Why were my boat’s crew taken first? Because the mist hung low, and they breathed it while you above stayed clear. Those struck this morning? All slept on the main deck. All feared the fever. So I say this: Keep high, fear nothing but God, and we live.”

Cary frowned. “The fog reached our topsails at dawn.”

“Aye, but those on the half-deck had cleaner air. And mark me—heat draws the poison out, and cold quiets it. We must rise above it.”

Two centuries later, men would understand the laws of tropical fevers. But Amyas, though he knew not why, knew *how*.

Yeo stood, nodding. “The lad speaks truth. The Spaniards say these fevers cling to the lowlands, never climbing past a few hundred feet.”

“Then we climb,” said Amyas.

The men stared at him, then at each other.

“We cannot sail as we are. We cannot go home empty-handed. And we cannot rot here. We take what we can carry, leave the ship, and march inland—up to the mountains.”

“Inland?” voices cried.

“Aye. We fortify a camp, carry our sick, and when we’re strong again, we cross those peaks and take St. Jago de Leon by surprise.”

Cary swore, then laughed. “Madness!”

“Prudence,” said Yeo.

“I’ll follow,” squeaked Jack.

“Then so will I,” Cary declared.

Drew nodded. “Sir Francis would have done the same.”

The Pelicans and freed slaves joined eagerly. The rest resisted—until Amyas silenced them with a threat to burn the ship where she lay.

---

Ten days they toiled—up steep slopes, through thorn-choked ravines, under a sun that boiled the very air. Men fell, cursing. Others dragged them onward. At last, they reached a plateau a thousand feet above the sea, backed by cliffs no enemy could scale. A ceiba tree, vast as a cathedral, arched above them, its roots forming a natural fortress.

Amyas settled the sick beneath its shelter. The ceiba was a world unto itself—its trunk forty feet in girth, its branches a labyrinth of life. Orchids blazed among its boughs; hummingbirds darted like jewels. Parrots shrieked, lizards basked, and at night, tree-frogs croaked a chorus to the stars.

Yet Amyas could not rest. Each dawn, he climbed into the ceiba’s heights, scanning the horizon. La Guayra was beyond reach; Frank’s fate a gnawing torment. But eighty men could not be sacrificed for one.

At last, he made his choice.

“We cannot refit the ship without dying in the attempt,” he told Cary and Yeo. “And if we fight the Spaniards again, we’ll be outmatched. Eustace warned them—we know that much.”

“Then where?” Cary asked.

Amyas smiled. “Have you heard of Manoa? The golden city?”

Yeo’s eyes gleamed. “Aye. And so have the old hands.”

Cary listened, half-dazzled, as Amyas spun tales of El Dorado.

“You mean to play Cortez?”

“No,” said Amyas. “We’ll play neither Cortez nor Pizarro. But we’ll dare what they dared—and do it better.”

---

Then came the Spaniards.

A ship crept along the coast, then boats pushed through the mangroves. Amyas watched from the ceiba, heart pounding. At last, helmets glinted on the path below—single file, stumbling upward.

And at their head, sword in hand, strode Don Guzman.

Amyas stood atop the stockade, white flag raised.

“Don Guzman! The quarrel is between us, not our men. I challenge you—here and now.”

The Spaniard’s answer was a sneer. “Lutheran dog, I bring a noose, not a sword. You’ll share Oxenham’s fate.”

Amyas laughed. “Then the devil take you and the king of Spain together! Fire!”

The stockade erupted. The Spanish line crumpled under the storm of shot. Don Guzman, shouting orders, was swept away by fleeing men. Amyas gave chase, but the enemy vanished into the brush.

Among the dead, they found an Indian boy—wounded, terrified. He fought like a wildcat, convinced the English meant to eat him. Only when he saw them bury the Spanish dead did he believe otherwise.

That night, beneath the ceiba’s mighty boughs, the men knelt in prayer. The fever had not touched them here. Perhaps God had led them to this refuge.

Or perhaps the adventure had only just begun.

The tale I must now tell is a dark one—a fleeting glimpse into an abyss of human cruelty. Yet it must be told, for in its horror lies the truth of what English souls once faced in those merciless days.

Three weeks have passed. The scene is no longer the open sea or the wild coasts of the Indies but a dim, suffocating corridor in the heart of Cartagena. The air is thick with the scent of damp stone and despair. A door stands ajar, and within its confines, two cloaked figures linger—one familiar, one unknown.

Eustace Leigh is the first. The second, a silent attendant of the Holy Office, holds a lamp aloft. Its flickering light spills over a bed of straw and the figure of a sleeping man. The face is pale, the brow high and noble—Frank Leigh, spared from the savagery of the jungle only to face the calculated malice of men who call themselves holy.

He had endured the question that very afternoon. Now Eustace, the architect of his betrayal, has come to reason with him—or ensnare him? Even Eustace does not know. He tells himself he would give his life to save his cousin, yet what is his life now but a tool of higher wills? Duty has led him here, and duty demands he wake the sleeper, coax or threaten him into recantation while his spirit is still raw from the rack.

But Frank sleeps peacefully. Is it a trick of the lamplight, or does a faint smile linger on his lips? Eustace leans closer, the flame trembling in his grasp. Then he hears it—a whisper, soft as a breath: his mother’s name, and then another, holier still.

Eustace steps back. “Let him rest,” he murmurs. “I fear my words will do little good.”

The familiar’s voice is dry. “None, I think. He scoffed at their holinesses. A heretic to the bone.”

Eustace’s jaw tightens. “The devil’s power is great indeed.”

They move on, but Eustace hesitates as they pass another door. A woman’s voice rises within—sharp with pain, trembling with defiance.

“Witchcraft? Against Don Guzman? What need had I of witchcraft, in God’s name?”

A murmur follows, indistinct, then a sound that stops Eustace’s heart—a wail, raw and rending, tearing through the stone walls. He staggers, clutching at the corridor’s edge, but his companion pulls him away.

The cries follow him, even as he stumbles into the open air, into a garden bathed in moonlight, lush and perfumed as paradise. But there is no escape. The echoes of her agony cling to him, relentless.

*What have I done?* The question claws at him. *Was there no other way?*

His companion’s voice is smooth, almost amused. “The Holy Office is merciful. Pain purifies the soul.”

Eustace nods, his throat tight. “Yes—yes. A father’s chastisement.”

“And the evidence was clear. Her companion, that English crone—you yourself named her a witch.”

“I did. There was no choice.” He speaks as much to convince himself as the other. “The flesh is weak, but my spirit is willing.”

Yet that night, Eustace seeks the head of the Jesuits. He speaks in broken confessions, begging for release—for exile, for any escape from the torment of his own making. The Jesuit listens, then arranges his passage far from Cartagena. By dawn, Eustace is gone.

Where? It does not matter.

Eustace Leigh is no longer a man. He is a instrument, a shadow without will. He has surrendered his soul to the Society, believing it salvation. But in truth, he is dead—twice dead.

Let the dead bury their dead. Our story has no more room for Eustace Leigh.

*"My mariners, souls that have toiled, and wrought, and thought with me—Death closes all: but something ere the end, some work of noble note may yet be done, not unbecoming men that strove with gods!"*
—Tennyson’s *Ulysses*

Three years had slipped by since that band of Englishmen knelt beneath the towering tree of Guayra—three years of wandering, hardship, and fading hope. They had traversed vast, uncharted lands, eight hundred miles in length and half as wide, hunting the fabled Golden City of Manoa. Along the Orinoco’s tangled banks, beyond the thunderous cataracts of Maypures, and deep into the upper reaches of the Amazon they had pressed. They had climbed into the highlands of Peru, trodden the cinchona groves of Loxa, and gazed upon the snow-crowned peaks of Chimborazo and the smoldering wrath of Cotopaxi. Yet the city of gold remained as elusive as a dream.

Now, they camped upon the banks of a nameless tributary of the Meta, where the river coiled like a silver serpent beneath the shadows of ancient trees. The survivors—forty-four out of the eighty-four who had set forth—sat around their fires, lean and sun-scorched but unbroken. Their beards hung wild, their hair knotted against the sun’s fury, their clothes patched with native cotton and the skins of beasts. Their powder was spent, their muskets abandoned to the jungle’s rot, but their swords still gleamed, and they carried bows no Indian could draw.

Around them, the forest hummed with unseen life. The tapir splashed in the shallows; the jaguar’s growl sent monkeys shrieking through the canopy. Yet the men paid little heed. They had grown accustomed to nature’s grand indifference.

Amyas, Cary, Brimblecombe, and old Yeo huddled close, their faces lit by the flickering flames.

"We’ve scoured the Orinoco, the Amazon, the Andes," Cary muttered, chewing on a scrap of tobacco. "Every rumor of Manoa leads us in circles."

Jack Brimblecombe sighed. "Perhaps the Lord means it to stay hidden. There’s a providence in our failures."

Yeo nodded solemnly. "A voice tells me we’ll not see Manoa. Maybe it’s God’s mercy. Gold breeds greed, and greed breeds ruin."

Amyas clenched his fists. "Then what? Do we turn back?"

Cary grinned. "Westward still. There’s gold enough in New Granada—and Spaniards to take it from."

A murmur of agreement rippled through the men. They would cross the mountains to Bogotá, seize what they could, then carve a path to the northern sea. No longer chasing phantoms, they would grasp what fortune offered.

As the fire burned low, Amyas stared into the embers. The dream of El Dorado was dead. But the will to endure, to fight, to claim some prize from the vast unknown—that remained.

At dawn, they pushed onward. The river narrowed, the current quickened. Soon, the distant roar of rapids warned of fresh toil ahead.

"Another cursed portage," someone grumbled.

Amyas laughed. "Since when did Englishmen shy from sweat?"

The jungle closed around them, indifferent to their struggles. Yet still they pressed forward, not as conquerors of legend, but as men—weary, stubborn, and unyielding.

The air hung thick with the scent of damp earth and blooming orchids as Amyas Leigh and his men stepped ashore onto the riverbank. The village before them was a scattering of palm-thatched huts, nestled beneath the emerald canopy of the forest. The Omaguas, painted in vivid streaks of arnotto and indigo, stood in silent rows, their dark eyes watching.

Then came the noise—a cacophony of drums and hollow trumpets, a clamor fit to wake the dead.

*"They're calling on their devil,"* muttered Yeo, gripping his sword.

*"Let them,"* said Amyas, though his hand rested on the hilt of his cutlass. *"If their master shows his face, we’ll give him a taste of English steel."*

The Piache, the tribe’s sorcerer, slithered forward, draped in a jaguar’s pelt, his face a grotesque mask of feathers and teeth. He howled and contorted, working himself into a frenzy before collapsing in a feigned trance.

*"A fine performance,"* Cary remarked dryly. *"But I’d wager he’s no more a prophet than I am."*

Then—silence.

From the shadows of a nearby hut came a voice, low and sweet, rising in a melody that seemed to weave through the trees like mist. The Indians bowed their heads. The Englishmen stood transfixed.

It was her.

The same maiden they had glimpsed days before, now clad in robes of iridescent feathers, her bearing regal, her gaze unflinching. She stepped forward, and the crowd parted as if for royalty.

*"Who is she?"* Amyas asked the cacique.

The old man hesitated, then whispered, *"A child of the Sun."*

A descendant of the fallen Incas? A spirit? A demon in fair disguise? The answer mattered little to Amyas. There was power in her—power and mystery.

She approached him, her dark eyes searching his, then took his hand and pressed it to her brow. A murmur rippled through the tribe. The submission was clear.

*"Well,"* Cary chuckled, *"it seems you’ve made a conquest."*

But Amyas was not so sure.

---

In the days that followed, the maiden—Ayacanora, they learned—kept her distance, watching from the shadows. Yet curiosity soon drew her out. She joined Amyas on hunts, leading him through tangled thickets where sunlight barely pierced the leaves. She spoke little, but her eyes spoke volumes.

*"She’s no simple savage,"* Amyas told Cary one evening as they sat by the fire. *"There’s wit in her, and fire."*

*"And danger,"* Yeo grumbled. *"Mark my words, Captain. The devil’s in that one."*

Perhaps he was right.

For when the Piache, enraged by the Englishmen’s defiance of his authority, sought to turn the tribe against them, it was Ayacanora who stood between them. She silenced the sorcerer with a single fierce command, then turned to Amyas.

*"He would have you killed,"* she said. *"Shall I have him thrown to the alligators?"*

Amyas hesitated. *"No. Let him run. A coward’s tongue does little harm."*

She frowned, as if disappointed by his mercy.

---

Yet the Piache’s influence lingered. The tribe grew restless, whispering of curses and barren harvests. The sacred trumpet—hidden away in some dark cave—must be sounded, they claimed, or the palms would wither.

*"Superstition,"* Jack Brimblecombe scoffed. *"The Lord is our shield."*

But the Omaguas did not see it so.

One evening, as tensions reached their peak, Ayacanora’s voice rose again in song—a haunting, defiant melody that cut through the murmurs of the crowd. The Piache, clutching his charms, paled.

*"Enough!"* Amyas barked, stepping forward. *"We came as friends. If you’d rather we left, say so. But do not test our patience."*

The cacique wavered. The Piache spat a curse—and fled into the night.

Ayacanora watched him go, then turned to Amyas with a slow, knowing smile.

*"The weak fear the strong,"* she said. *"But the wise fear nothing."*

Amyas could not shake the feeling that she spoke not just of the Piache—but of herself.

And perhaps, of him.

*"God relents, forgiving debts long owed,
More pleased by mercy sought in prayer
Than rigid souls who choose the grave,
Too stern, too just—yet self-enslaved."*

—*Samson Agonistes*

---

A fortnight had passed in grueling toil, yet no more grueling than they had endured before. They had bid farewell to the vast green plains of the east, crossed the towering spine of the Cordillera, and glimpsed the distant city of Santa Fe, nestled among lush gardens on its high plateau. It was too large, too fortified—no target for their small band. But their time had not been wasted.

Their Indian guide had brought word of a gold-train descending from Santa Fe toward the Magdalena River. Now they waited beside the wretched excuse for a road, hidden in a forest of oaks that might have tricked them into thinking themselves back in Europe—were it not for the tree-ferns crowding the undergrowth, or the yawning gorges below, where the steaming jungle sprawled in riotous color beneath the cool mountain air.

They had made camp among the ferns, above a stretch where the path clung to a steep slope, a sheer drop of hundreds of feet beneath. Once, perhaps, this had been a proper road, part of the lost kingdom of Cundinamarca. Now, after Spanish rule, only broken steps remained, half-buried in mud. The gold-hungry conquerors had let all else decay—bridges, aqueducts, farms—save for their grand cathedrals, raised atop the ruins of Incan temples.

And now, as the jungle reclaimed its dominion, Amyas and his men might as well have been alone in the wilderness, though a Spanish settlement lay but a few miles distant.

---

Their search for sulfur and saltpeter had failed. They would have to rely on blade and bow—much to Yeo’s disgust. But Amyas reminded them: Drake had taken Nombre de Dios and its gold with no better weapons. They could do the same.

Blocking the road above with a felled tree, they crouched among the foliage, chewing coca to stave off hunger, listening to a strange, echoing roar that had followed them since leaving the Meta. No beast they knew made such a sound. Yet mysteries were common here—and the gold-train mattered far more.

Then, a crack—sharp, unmistakable—and a cry. Not the snap of a branch, nor the scream of a bird.

"A whip," Yeo muttered. "And a woman’s wail. They’re close."

"A woman?" Amyas frowned. "They force women into their gangs?"

"Why not? There they are—see their helmets glint?"

Amyas raised a hand. "Not a shot till I give the word. One volley, then swords. Pass it down."

The procession came into view, and every man’s pulse quickened.

First, twenty soldiers—half on foot, the rest carried like royalty in chairs upon the backs of Indian slaves. The footmen had handed off their armor and arquebuses to other slaves, each prodded along by pikes.

"Madness," Yeo spat. "Trusting their guns to slaves."

"An Indian prays to a musket not to shoot him," another scoffed. "They think their weapons safe enough."

Amyas counted. "Ten with firearms, ten with pikes. Will can handle them above."

Behind them came an officer in his litter, puffing on a cigar, hurling curses and prayers to every saint and demon as he urged the line onward.

Then—the horror.

A chain of Indians, Negroes, and Zambos, naked, scarred, staggering under the weight of baskets strapped to their foreheads. Women among them—young girls, mothers with children at their knees. A murmur of fury rippled through the Englishmen.

But the first forty baskets held something that made even pity fade—square bundles of hide, tightly corded.

Amyas knew them at once.

"What’s in those, Captain?"

"Gold."

The word sent a shiver through them all. A rustle of movement nearly betrayed them before Amyas hissed for silence.

The last of the Indians bore food—manioc, maize-bread—for the Spanish party. Behind them marched twenty more soldiers, then the officer, twirling his mustaches, oblivious to the arrows itching to find his heart.

The ambush was set. Only the moment remained.

Amyas hesitated. To strike unsuspecting men, even cruel ones, sat ill with him. But then—the spark.

The line halted. A tree blocked the path ahead. The officer bellowed oaths, storming forward—and there, in his way, an old man, collapsing under his burden.

The Spaniard drew his sword. Not to cut the chain—but the wrist it bound.

A shriek. A flash of crimson. The old man’s hand fell free.

Amyas nocked an arrow—but before he could loose it, the girl beside the old man moved.

Like a panther, she seized the officer and flung them both into the abyss.

A moment of stunned silence. Then—chaos.

"Haul her up! Hack her to pieces!"

The driver yanked the chain, dragging the girl toward the edge—until Amyas’s arrow took him through the throat.

Then, with a roar, the English sprang forth.

A storm of arrows. Swords flashing. The Spaniards, caught unready, fell like wheat before the sickle. In minutes, the path ran red, the survivors fleeing or tumbling into the gorge below.

"Cut the Indians loose!"

Armorer’s tools were found. The chains fell away.

Amyas spoke to them. "We are friends. Help us carry this gold to the Magdalena, and you go free."

Some kissed his feet. Most sat blank-eyed, broken. But the girl—the one who had leaped—clung to her father’s body, then, without a sound, followed him into the void.

A breath. A crash of leaves. Then—nothing.

"Brave lass," someone muttered.

"God forgive her," Yeo said. Then, grimly practical: "Now, their guns. Their clothes. We’ll need both if we mean to pass unseen."

They stripped the dead, hurled the corpses into the ravine, and prepared to move on—when a figure appeared on the road.

Ayacanora.

Feathered, armed, her garments torn from weeks of pursuit. At the sight of Amyas, she cried out, ran to him—then collapsed, sobbing.

Amyas groaned. "What do I do with *her* now?"

But there was no time. The gold awaited. And as vultures gathered above, the English turned their faces toward the Magdalena, their fortunes changed—if they could but keep them.

The sun dipped low over the bay of Santa Marta, painting the waters in molten gold. The air hummed with the song of cicadas, and fireflies winked like scattered embers in the gathering dusk. Two friars sat in deferential silence at the table’s end, their eyes downcast.

The bishop preened. "A poet’s soul feels deeply. Yet I am but a humble servant of the Church." He snapped his fingers at the Indian girl, Tita. "Fill their glasses. Tell me, Don Alverez, does more treasure come from the mines?"

The bishop chuckled. "Once, hoopoes wore crowns of gold—until men hunted them without mercy. They begged King Solomon to change their fate, and lo! Their golden crowns became feathers, and they lived in peace." He smirked. "Would that all gold turned so harmless."

Fray Gerundio, the quiet Dominican, muttered, "Then men would find new vanities to kill for."

The bishop shot him a withering glance. "Convert your Indians, friar, and leave statecraft to your betters."

The conversation turned to Drake’s recent raid.

"Perfidious dogs!" the bishop spat. "They drank my own wine—my finest Xeres!—then demanded ransom for the priory after sacking the town!"

Don Pedro grunted. "They outmaneuvered us. Who’d have thought they’d attack from the beach?"

"Devilry," the bishop declared. "No Christian fights so."

As the night wore on, wine loosened tongues. The intendant lamented Cartagena’s ruined defenses; the captain cursed the English pikes, longer than their own.

At last, the bishop yawned. "Enough. I am weary. Tita, my hammock."

The guests departed, and the bishop, muttering prayers, clutched a small casket of jewels. "Perhaps enough for a cardinal’s hat," he mused before sleep took him.

Ayacanora settled into Lucy’s cabin, a wild yet unwilling member of the crew. The transition from savage freedom to the rigid order of an English ship was no easy thing. The fierce prophetess of the Omaguas now seemed little more than a petulant child, her pride clashing daily with the discipline of the sea.

She had a knack for mischief—slipping into forbidden corners, pilfering trinkets that caught her eye, and when scolded, threatening darkly to fling herself into the waves. Amyas, torn between exasperation and pity, found himself at his wit’s end. He could not punish her, nor would he cast her ashore, for something in his heart whispered that Providence had placed her in his care.

The crew indulged her, treating her like some half-tamed creature, both cherished and feared. Old Yeo, especially, bore her caprices with gruff patience, though even his temper frayed when she took to hurling objects at the ship’s black sailors, her face twisted in inexplicable loathing.

Then came the matter of the Spanish finery.

Among the plunder of their prize were two chests—one filled with the gaudy feather-work of Mexico, the other with delicate silks and laces meant for some noblewoman of the Caracas. Her triumph lasted until she turned to him, eyes bright. *"Ayacanora quite English girl now, yes?"*

She seized his hands. *"No! English heart! Teach me, Amyas!"*

So Jack Brimblecombe was summoned to instruct her in Christian doctrine. But she would learn from no one but Amyas himself, and when Jack presumed to look at her too long, she flew at him in a fury, leaving the poor parson nursing a bruised nose and a handful of lost hair.

The men were at their tasks, John Squire humming an old Devon tune. Salvation Yeo, ever the stern saint, scolded him for his *"carnal vanities"* and struck up a psalm instead. But Ayacanora, perched near the rail, had caught the first melody. Her voice, clear and sweet, rang out—

*"A randy, dandy, dandy O!
A whet of ale and brandy O!
With a rumbelow and a Westward-ho!

Yeo went pale as death.

*"Who taught you that?"* he demanded, trembling.

She blinked, uncomprehending.

Amyas pressed her. *"Where did you hear such a song?"*

She shrugged. *"Don’t know. Always knew it."*

A flush spread over her face. She covered her eyes, trembling.

*"Do you remember?"* Amyas asked softly.

She did not answer. But for the first time, Salvation Yeo saw in her face the ghost of the child he had carried through the jungle, so many years ago.

The evening of February 15, 1587, found Mrs. Leigh standing upon the terrace at Burrough, her gaze fixed upon the distant sea. Three long years had passed—three years of silence, of unanswered prayers, of hollow eyes in Bideford and Appledore, watching for those who had vanished into the West. Once, hope had flared when news came of Drake’s return from the Spanish Main—but no word of the *Rose*, nor of the souls aboard her. And so, with the quiet resolve of one who has learned to endure, Mrs. Leigh bowed her head and whispered, *"The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord!"*

Her hair had silvered; her cheeks, once full of warmth, were hollowed by grief. Yet every evening, when the tide was high, she walked the terrace, scanning the horizon for a sail that never came.

Tonight, however, the air was alive with clamor. Bells pealed from Northam’s tower; bonfires blazed in the streets. The news had spread like wildfire: Mary, Queen of Scots, was dead, her head struck from her shoulders at Fotheringay. England breathed again, freed from the shadow of seventeen years of dread.

Mrs. Leigh neither cheered nor wept. She merely murmured a prayer—for even a queen’s soul might yet find mercy.

Then—a sound from the sea. A gunshot. Then another.

The crowd hushed, rushing to the churchyard that overlooked the river. A foreign ship, large and swift, was making for the harbor, her sails full beneath the fading light. No English vessel, this—yet she flew no flag of surrender, no veiled topsails in deference to the realm.

The mystery deepened as she passed the Hubbastone, music drifting from her decks—drums, fifes, trumpets. And then, as she rounded into full view of Burrough, a mighty cheer erupted from her crew, rolling like thunder across the water.

Mrs. Leigh did not wait. She turned to her maid.

Bideford’s quay was chaos—sailors, women, apprentices, all shouting, weeping, laughing. And then, towering above them, Amyas Leigh.

He fell to his knees before his mother. She clasped him to her, her fingers tightening in his hair. No words were needed. She knew, even before he whispered:

*"You need not tell me that,"* she answered.

A pause. Then, softer:

*"A martyr,"* Amyas said. *"In the Inquisition."*

A shudder passed through her, but she lifted her chin. *"Come home, Amyas."*

Then her eyes fell upon the girl standing behind him—dark-eyed, uncertain.

Mrs. Leigh extended a hand. *"Then she is my granddaughter. Come, child."*

That night, by the hearth, Amyas told his tale—of battles, of treasure, of Frank’s fate. Sir Richard Grenville arrived, breathless from haste, to clasp his godson and demand every detail.

*"Would God I had been with you!"* the old warrior sighed.

But Mrs. Leigh sat composed, her hands folded, her grief long since tempered into steel. When the story was done, she kissed Amyas’s brow.

Then she rose, leading Ayacanora away—to teach her, to comfort her, to weave her into the fabric of their lives.

For though one son was lost, another had returned. And in that, there was grace enough.

The next morning, Amyas sought out old Salterne. The merchant listened, stone-faced, to the tale of Rose’s fate. At the end, he merely nodded.

And when Amyas spoke of his resolve—to hunt Spaniards wherever they might be found—Salterne’s smile was grim.

For the past was dead. But the sea still called.

*"The daughter of debate, that discord still doth sow,

For twelve months after his return, Amyas Leigh remained at home in quietude, his restless spirit tempered by the peace of Burrough. Salvation Yeo, ever the faithful shadow, took up his post as steward, though his duties were more those of a guardian than a servant. His evenings were spent by the fire, Bible in hand, while his calloused fingers crafted trinkets—some useful, some whimsical—for the household. Yet none so often as for Ayacanora, to whom he offered childish baubles with humble reverence, only to be met with pouts and petulance. Still, she would accept them, stash them away, and forget them—for her mind was set on a grander ambition: to be an English lady.

She dogged Mrs. Leigh’s steps, demanding lessons in the mysteries of the kitchen, the still-room, and above all, the art of sewing. First, she would be a proper housewife; then, a new thought struck her—helping others. The boundless generosity of her savage kin had been instinctive, but the notion of giving without return was foreign. She watched Mrs. Leigh labor for the poor, visiting cottages with baskets of food and hand-sewn garments. She saw Amyas, after giving public thanks in church for his safe return, distribute alms with open hands—buying cottages to shelter aged sailors, pouring out his wealth as an offering to God.

To Ayacanora, this was baffling. The thank-offerings of her people had been blood and bone, sacrifices to the gods. But Amyas gave bread and roofs. When Mrs. Leigh explained that kindness to the poor was kindness to the Great Spirit, Ayacanora listened—and then acted. She stitched until her fingers ached, visited the sick, and when rewarded with a rare smile from Amyas, shone with joy. But let him ignore her, and she would sulk like a storm cloud.

Her vanity, however, remained unchecked. She swept into church each Sunday in a blaze of Spanish silks, drawing scandalized whispers until the vicar begged Mrs. Leigh to curb her finery. Ayacanora scoffed. "They need not look if they dislike it!" And when the vicar hinted at a donation, she nearly draped him in her discarded Mexican feather mantles—"Fit for a Piache, not me!"—before he retreated, clutching one as an altar cloth. Thus, for years after, Northam’s communion table blazed with the plundered splendor of an Aztec prince.

Her disdain extended to the local gentry. Ladies came to gawk at the "savage beauty," only to be met with cold shoulders. Even Lady Grenville was not spared. Mrs. Leigh scolded; Ayacanora wept. "They stare at me like a beast in a menagerie! I was a queen once—here, I am nothing!" Yet when asked if she wished to return to the wilds, she clung like a frightened child. "Better a kitchen slave here than free among those cruel people!"

Month by month, her tempests lessened under Mrs. Leigh’s gentle influence. Hints of her past emerged—she recognized houses, cattle (though sheep puzzled her—"too small"). But horses enchanted her. She begged Amyas for one, then tried bribing the vicar with her jewels for his broken-kneed nag. In the end, Amyas bought her a jennet, and within weeks she rode like a wild plains hunter.

Then came the dream. At Stow, sleeping beside Mrs. Leigh, she woke screaming. The "Old Ape of Panama" had dragged her from bed, forced her onto a horse, and ridden her through mountains into a dark wood—where she awoke a child once more. The vision felt real. Mrs. Leigh saw in it divine revelation: the girl had been abandoned in the Andes by a vengeful stepfather.

More fragments surfaced. She recalled nothing of her mother or faith—until the baptism of Sir Richard’s Indian servant, Raleigh. Ayacanora insisted on attending. As the rite began, she gasped, swayed—then collapsed. In her vision, the church had grown grander, the walls adorned with paintings, the altar alight with a jeweled Madonna. The scent of incense filled the air; Indians stood at the font. She was a child again, holding a lady’s hand.

Yeo recognized the scene at once. "She’s been christened, madam—in Quito’s cathedral, no less!"

So Ayacanora remained, while Raleigh the Indian faded fast. The forest-born pined for his homeland, and within a year, his name appeared again in the parish register—not for baptism, but burial.

Amyas, bound by promise to stay a year, chafed as others sailed without him. Raleigh begged him to lead a new Virginia colony, but he refused, sending money instead—lost, like so many dreams, to the New World.

Yet darker thoughts gnawed at him. His hatred for Don Guzman festered, poisoning his spirit. Yeo’s fanatic mutterings of Spanish sins fed the fire, until even gentle Mrs. Leigh grew sharp, hearing them debate whether slaughtering prisoners had been a holy duty.

But England was not idle. The year 1588 loomed—prophesied as the world’s climacteric. Spain, swollen with pride, prepared an armada to crush the heretics. The pope blessed their cannons; Jesuits whispered of divine vengeance. England would fall, Elizabeth would kneel in chains, and Rome would reign again.

Yet the people answered with one voice: *Enough.* Seventeen years of terror ended with a queen’s death warrant. The choice was made.

And so the storm gathered—while Amyas, restless, waited for his hour.

—*Cornish Ballad*

Imagine, if you will, the proud spectators of Plymouth’s mighty harbor in our own age—the vast breakwater, the arsenals bristling with might, the estuaries crowded with ships of war—suddenly cast back three hundred years to the summer of 1588. The green slopes of Mount Edgecombe still rise fair as ever, but where are the docks? Where is Devonport? The citadel is but a feeble fort, its walls fit to crumble before a single broadside. The ships that crowd the Catwater are not the merchant fleets of our time, but a humble squadron, the largest among them no match for a modern frigate.

Would men of our day scoff at such a sight? Would they sneer at these wooden vessels, these guns of feeble range? Only a fool would do so—a fool who takes the triumphs of our age for granted, never knowing the sweat and daring that built them. But a wiser man, one who has wrestled with invention and hardship, would look upon that fleet with reverence, knowing the genius and iron will that forged England’s first true navy.

And if such a man had an eye for character, he would have found no finer company than the gathering on the terrace behind the Pelican Inn that July afternoon. There, lounging against the low wall or clustered in eager knots, stood the very soul of England’s seafaring might—men whose names would echo through history. The Spanish Armada had been scattered by storms; Lord Howard had scoured the coast but found no trace. Now, with the wind shifting southward, the fleet waited, taut with anticipation.

Step unseen among them, and mark the faces of these sea-wolves at rest. There, in the center of a rapt circle, stands Walter Raleigh, his sharp chin lifted, his long eyes alight. Beside him, draped in a flame-colored doublet, is young Lord Sheffield, laughing at some jest. Towering over them both is Lord Howard of Effingham, Lord High Admiral of England, his bearing as stately as his office demands. And there—stocky, plain, with a face like weathered oak—is the man whose very name makes Spaniards tremble: Francis Drake.

A gruff voice breaks the air—Admiral John Hawkins, his sea-stained doublet at odds with the gold chain about his neck, claps Drake on the shoulder. "Be you coming to drink, Francis, or be you not?" No man dares take such a tone with Drake—save Hawkins, the patriarch of Plymouth’s seamen, whose crabbed humor has melted into rare good cheer at the prospect of battle.

And what a company surrounds them! Here sits Martin Frobisher, pipe in hand, exchanging tales with John Davis. There stands George Fenner, Leicester’s favorite, wishing better fortune to those who had failed where Drake had triumphed. And watch young Richard Hawkins, the very picture of a complete seaman, deep in talk with his uncle William—a man whose epitaph would one day praise his justice, piety, and care for poor sailors.

But one figure draws the eye—an ancient man propped against the tavern wall, his white beard flowing like sea-foam, his dim eyes searching the crowd. Little children cluster near him, for old Martin Cockrem is a living relic, a man who had seen Henry VIII crowned and had walked the shores of the New World before most men knew it existed.

"Where be young master?" the old man frets, until Hawkins himself, chuckling, waddles over to press a rare lump of sugar into his gnarled hand. "There, Martin—eat your sweet, you old rogue."

And as the ancient sailor’s tongue is loosened, he spins tales of days long past—of King Harry’s thunderous grief when the *Mary Rose* sank before his eyes; of the first Englishmen to brave the wild coasts of America; of golden cities and savage kings. He had seen the birth of England’s empire, and now, as the shadows lengthened, he would see its triumph.

"Look at them all!" he cackles, waving a trembling hand at the captains. "One’s been to the Brazils, another to the Indies, another round the world! Ah, we’ll sweep those Spanish dogs from the seas yet—mark my words!"

But Hawkins’ voice suddenly booms across the terrace, cutting through the murmur like a cannonade.

"Fail? *Fail?* What murrain-ridden cur dares croak of failure here?"

A hush falls. The offending captain flushes, hand on sword-hilt, but Hawkins only scoffs. "Fight a croaker? I’d as soon wrestle a frog! I fight men who *dare*, sir!"

Lord Howard steps in, ever the peacemaker. "Come, Admiral, we all tremble before such stakes—"

"Tremble?" Hawkins roars. "Let the Dons tremble! We’ll show them what English hearts are made of!"

And as the sun dips toward the horizon, the men of Plymouth laugh again, for the morrow will bring battle—and they do not mean to lose.

—Campbell, *Ye Mariners of England*

Now began the great sea-fight that would decide the fate of nations—whether Popery and tyranny, or liberty and the reformed faith, would rule the waves and the New World beyond. It was a battle worthy of Homer’s thunderous verse, but since I must tell it in plain prose, I will do my best, borrowing where I can the words of those who witnessed it.

The Lord High Admiral of England sent forth the *Defiance*, a swift pinnace, to fire the first shot in challenge. Then he bore down in the *Ark Royal* (or *Triumph*), his cannons roaring, and fell upon what he took to be the Spanish admiral’s ship—though it was, in truth, Alfonso de Leon’s. Drake, Hawkins, and Frobisher soon joined the fray, hammering Recalde’s rearguard with deadly fire. The Spaniards, though proud and stubborn, soon learned the hard lesson of English seamanship. Their ships were lumbering giants; ours were swift wolves. Recalde’s squadron, mauled and outmaneuvered, broke formation and fled to rejoin the main fleet.

Medina Sidonia, the Spanish admiral, struggled to hold his scattered ships together, marshaling them into a crescent. But it was no use. Like a herd of buffalo harried by wolves, the Armada lumbered forward, unable to shake off the English harriers. By the day’s end, not a single English ship was lost, and the myth of Spanish invincibility lay shattered.

One of the great galliasses, her decks cluttered with chapels and friars, was riddled with shot. The fleet had to rally around her lest Drake sink her outright. In the chaos, the galleon of Pedro de Valdez—a ship crowded with blue-blooded Dons—collided with another, lost her foremast, and was abandoned to her fate. A grim omen for Spain.

Night fell, but the English kept watch. At dawn, they found themselves off Torbay, where Drake sent word to Amyas Leigh:

*"Dear Lad,—I have been chasing shadows all night—five great hulks that turned to galleons in the dark, then to merchantmen by morning. But I’ve caught Valdez’s ship, and good plunder with it. The Dons left him like cowards. He stood on his honor at first, but when I told him my matches were lit, he came aboard quick enough, kissing my fist and spouting Spanish flattery. More news: your Don Guzman is aboard the *Sta. Catharina*, commanding her soldiers. She’s a tall ship, three tiers of guns. God keep you, and send you luck.—F. Drake."*

Amyas clenched his fists. The *Sta. Catharina*—the very ship they had battered yesterday!

The days that followed were a blur of smoke and thunder. The fleets clashed off Portland, then again near the Isle of Wight. The Spaniards, bound by rigid orders, fought like a bull tethered to a post, unable to match the English fleet’s speed and cunning. By the time they reached Calais Roads, their pride was bruised, their ships battered, but they were not yet broken.

Now came the test. The Sabbath dawned—St. James’s Day, Spain’s patron saint. Would he grant them victory? The galleys attacked, but the English met them with chain-shot that shattered oars and rigging. By noon, the wind rose, and the Spanish fleet closed ranks, rescuing their crippled ships.

All day the battle raged. Frobisher smashed the Spanish admiral’s mainmast; Howard and Drake wove through the chaos, each saving the other in turn. The great galliasses, their decks slick with blood, dared not engage again.

By nightfall, the Armada huddled in a defensive ring, drifting toward Calais. The English, though fewer in ships, had proved their mastery. Yet the danger was not past. Parma’s forces lurked in Flanders, and if the Spanish could join them, England might yet fall.

But Elizabeth and her captains had one last stratagem.

Amyas Leigh, meanwhile, burned with a single purpose: to find the *Sta. Catharina* and Don Guzman. He sharpened his sword like a man possessed, deaf to the prayers and cheers around him. When the Communion was offered, he refused.

“I cannot take it, Sir John,” he growled. “I hate as no Christian should.”

“There she is,” Amyas said, his voice low and terrible. “And here we are.”

He raised it high—not in triumph, but in something darker, something that tasted of ashes. Then, with a great cry, he hurled it into the sea.

The blade flashed once before the waves took it, sinking into the depths where the bones of his enemy would lie forever.

The first of October dawned bright and still, the sky softened by drifting autumn clouds, as though the heavens themselves rested after the fury of summer’s storms. The *Vengeance* slipped silently over the bar, her colors half-mast, for within her lay the body of Salvation Yeo.

A boat rowed ashore, bearing Amyas Leigh, led by Cary and Brimblecombe. The townsfolk of Appledore gathered, their voices hushed in pity as they made way for the blind captain.

"Spare me your kindness," Amyas murmured. "I come only to bury a better man than I."

Amyas stood at the grave’s head, his voice steady as he spoke of Yeo’s valor—of Rio de la Hacha, of the South Seas, of a life spent in service to England and God. The crowd listened, awed, as though hearing the voice of a prophet. Then, turning, Amyas walked away, his friends guiding him home.

"Mother," he said, "I’ve come back to be a burden."

She smiled, touching his face. "No burden, my son."

Alone in the bay window, Amyas let his fingers drift over familiar books—*King Arthur*, *Fox’s Martyrs*—the scent of roses and gilliflowers filling the air. An apple slipped from his grasp, and as he groped for it, frustration welled up, sharp and sudden. A tear fell, then another.

A rustle—the apple returned to his hand, and Ayacanora’s voice, trembling: "Do not weep. Let me serve you. Let me be yours."

He drew her to him, feeling the weight of years dissolve.

His mother’s step sounded at the door.

"Amyas," she said, soft as a prayer, "do not fear to take her. It is I who give her to you."

And so Ayacanora’s song returned, lifting like a lark’s flight, carrying with it the dreams of a blind man and the promise of a new world—Westward-Ho!
