The farmhouse stood a solitary sentinel a mile above the village, perched upon a wild moorland that seemed to reject the boundaries of civilization. Heather, invasive and untamed, crept aggressively over the edges of its garden, while the bridle-path abruptly terminated at its threshold, offering no soft transition from the road to the hearth. On three sides, an amphitheater of granite hills embraced the dwelling, shifting their hues with such rapidity according to the seasons that the landscape appeared to regenerate daily, donning fresh draperies of emerald, violet, and rust. These hills, devoid of timber save for patches of moss clinging to exposed rock, offered a sheltering embrace that felt almost conspiratorial. Durrance, riding up the track on an afternoon steeped in anticipation, recognized the house instantly. As he neared, the melody of a violin drifted from the windows—a sonic greeting that halted his hand upon the reins and suspended his breath, igniting a hope he had previously relegated to the shadows of his imagination.

He tethered his horse and crossed the gate. Outside, the air carried the scent of a barracks; inside, however, warmth reigned supreme. The room into which he was ushered, adorned with gleaming oak and polished brass, reflected the brilliance of the afternoon sun. Yet, Durrance's mind conjured a different tableau: the blinds drawn tight against a winter's chill, a fire roaring red upon the hearth, and the wind skirling violently against the glass, a stark contrast to the present tranquility.

Ethne received him without a flicker of surprise.

"I thought you would come," she said, a genuine smile illuminating her face.

Durrance laughed abruptly as their hands met, a sound that puzzled Ethne until her gaze shifted to the violin resting on the table beside her. The instrument was pale, bearing a visible scar near the bridge where worm-eaten wood had been painstakingly replaced.

"It is yours," she stated simply. "You were in Egypt. I could not bear to send it back to you there."

"I have hoped lately, since I learned of your situation," Durrance replied, "that you might accept it anyway."

"You see I have," Ethne affirmed, locking eyes with him. "I accepted it some time ago. There was a period when I required assurance that I possessed sure friends. Something tangible helped anchor me. I was very glad to possess it."

Durrance lifted the instrument from the table, handling it with the reverence due a sacred relic.

"Have you played upon it? Perhaps the Musoline Overture?" he asked.

"Do you recall that piece?" she countered with a laugh. "Yes, I have played it, though only recently. For a long time, I locked the violin away. It spoke to me too intimately of matters I desperately wished to forget," she added, her tone steady, her eyes unwavering.

The following day, Durrance transported his luggage from Rathmullen and remained at the farm for a week. Until the final hour of his visit, neither Ethne nor Durrance mentioned Harry Feversham, despite their constant proximity. Dermod, however, presented a harrowing picture of decline. Even worse than Mrs. Adair's descriptions had suggested, he was a man dismantled by grief. His speech had shrunk to monosyllables; his frame had wasted away, his clothes hanging loosely on limbs that seemed too thin to sustain them. His stature appeared diminished, and the fire that once burned in his eyes was extinguished. He had become a prisoner of his own home, spending most of the July days dozing by the fire, his longest excursion merely a trek to the small grey church standing naked on a nearby mound—a journey that taxed his remaining strength. He was an ancient figure fallen into decrepitude, nearly unrecognizable, his movements and rare vocalizations striking Durrance as the painful mimicry of a corpse. Even his collie dog seemed to age in his presence, creating a silent, sympathetic tableau of shared mortality.

Consequently, Durrance and Ethne were forced into frequent association. During the day, whether the weather was wet or fair, they trekked the hills. Ethne, her complexion flushed with vitality, her eyes burning with a mixture of jealousy and eagerness, showcased her homeland to him, demanding his admiration. In the evenings, she would retrieve her violin. Sitting as she had in the past, face averted, she commanded the strings to articulate the heights and depths of her sorrow. Durrance sat watching the sweep of her arm, the absorption etched into her features, silently calculating his prospects. He had not arrived at Glenalla carrying Lieutenant Sutch's confident expectations of success. The lingering shadow of Harry Feversham threatened to sever their paths. Furthermore, Durrance was acutely aware that poverty would weigh lighter upon Ethne than upon most women. He had witnessed proof of this resilience: though the Lennon House lay in ruins and her lands were forfeited, Ethne remained rooted among her own people. They awaited her visits with eager devotion; she remained the undisputed princess of that countryside. Conversely, she derived a frank pleasure from his company, guiding conversations toward his three years of service in the East. No detail was too trivial for her inquiries. As he spoke, her eyes scrutinized him relentlessly, her smile offering continuous, silent approval. Durrance failed to decipher her intent. Perhaps no one could have understood unless they knew the history between Harry Feversham and Ethne. Durrance wore the exterior of a man, yet she sought to ensure the spirit of a man animated that shell. He was a dark lantern to her; there might be a flame burning within, or merely vacancy and darkness. She was sliding back the cover, desperate to verify the contents.

On the final day of his visit, she steered the conversation toward Egypt. They sat upon the hillside, overlooking a stream that leaped from ledge to ledge down a miniature gorge of rock, cascading over deep pools in a torrent of clear, black water.

"I traveled once for four days amongst the mirages," he began, his voice rising with the memory. "Lagoons, still as mirrors and fringed with misty trees. One could almost walk a camel up to its knees in them before the lagoon receded and the sand blazed at you. One cannot imagine that glare. Every stone within sight dances and shakes like a heliograph; you can see—the heat flows breast-high across the desert, swift as this stream here, only pellucid. So until the sun sets ahead of you, level with your eyes! Imagine the nights that follow—nights of infinite silence, with a cool, friendly wind blowing from horizon to horizon—and your bedspread laid out for you under the great dome of stars. Oh," he cried, drawing a deep breath, "but that country grows on you. It is like the Southern Cross—four overrated stars when first seen, but in a week you begin to search for them, and you miss them when traveling north again." He raised himself on his elbow and turned suddenly toward her. "Do you know—I can only speak for myself—but I never feel alone in those empty spaces. On the contrary, I always feel very close to the things I care about, and to the few people I cherish."

Her eyes shone brilliantly upon him, her lips parted in a smile. He moved closer on the grass, sitting with his feet gathered beneath him on one side, leaning on his arm.

"I used to imagine you out there," he said. "You would have loved it—from the start before daybreak, in the dark, to the campfire at night. You would have been at home. I used to think so as I lay awake, wondering how the world went with my friends."

"And do you go back there?" she asked.

Durrance hesitated. The roar of the torrent pulsed around them. When he finally spoke, all enthusiasm had drained from his voice. He stared into the stream.

"To Wadi Halfa. For two years. I suppose so."

Ethne knelt on the grass at his side.

"I shall miss you," she said.

She knelt just behind him as he sat on the ground, and again, a profound silence descended between them.

"Of what are you thinking?" he asked.

"That you need not miss me," he replied, aware that she recoiled slightly and sank down upon her heels. "My appointment at Halfa... I might shorten its term. I might perhaps avoid it altogether. I still have half my furlough."

She offered no answer, nor did she alter her posture. She remained motionless, and Durrance felt alarm seize him; all his hopes sank. He knew that such stillness of attitude was, for her, as definitive an expression of distress as a cry of pain for another woman. He turned toward her. Her head was bowed, but she raised it as he turned, and though her lips smiled, there was a look of great turmoil in her eyes. Durrance was a man like any other. His first thought was whether some external obstacle prevented her compliance, even if she herself were willing.

"There is your father," he said.

"Yes," she answered, "there is my father too. I could not leave him."

"Nor need you," he said quickly. "That difficulty can be surmounted. To tell the truth, I was not thinking of your father at the moment."

"Nor was I," she replied.
