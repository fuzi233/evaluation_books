Ghysbrecht Van Swieten clutched the key to Gerard's prison within the folds of his tunic, waiting until the clock tolled ten before making his visit. "A touch of hunger serves to humble them," he muttered to himself, steeling his resolve. At the appointed hour, he ascended the creaking stairs, carrying a loaf and a pitcher, trailed closely by his loyal servant, who bore a weapon at his hip. Ghysbrecht pressed his ear to the heavy wooden door. Silence answered him—a profound, unnerving stillness. A grim satisfaction curled his lips. "By this time, he will be as despondent as Albert Koestein," he mused. With a slow turn of the lock, he pushed the door open.

But the cell was empty.

Ghysbrecht stood frozen, his breath hitching in his throat. Though his face remained obscured, his entire form seemed to drain of vitality, sinking into a stupor. Moments later, however, he collapsed to his knees, his legs giving way under him. His servant, seeing the tremors shake his master's frame, stepped forward and peered past the staggering old man. Upon beholding the vacant cot, the severed rope, and the missing iron bar, the servant gasped aloud, a cry of sheer disbelief. Yet, his astonishment deepened when he watched his master, ignoring everything save the hollow space, throw himself down before the empty chest. Ghysbrecht's hands, trembling violently, raked frantically over the wood, as though he dared not trust his own eyes to confirm the truth.

The servant stared, bewildered. "Mistress... Master, pray, what troubles you?"

Ghysbrecht's pale lips moved, forming words that escaped no sound. His hands dropped to his sides, and he fixed his gaze upon the void where the contents once lay. "Why stare into that emptiness?" the servant urged gently. "The boy is not there. Observe the cunning of the rogue; he removed the bar—"

"GONE!" Ghysbrecht screamed, the word tearing from his throat like a beast's roar. "GONE! GONE!"

"What is gone, holy saints? Is he stricken mad?"

"STOP THEEF!" shrieked Ghysbrecht, spinning around to seize his servant by the collar, shaking him with a fury born of sudden madness. "Do you stand idly by and watch your master robbed? Fly! Run! Offer a hundred crowns to anyone who brings back the box. Nay, it is futile! Oh, fool! Foolish me, to leave it unguarded in that same room. Yet, none before discovered the hidden spring. None could have, save him. It was meant to be. It *must* be. They are lost! Lost!" The weight of his age and failing health soon overpowered his brief frenzy, and he sank back against the chest, muttering brokenly, "Lost! Lost!"

"What precisely is lost, sir?" the servant asked, his voice kind.

"My house! My lands! My good name!" Ghysbrecht groaned, wringing his hands with feeble, frantic motions.

"WHA-AT?" the servant cried, his tone shifting instantly to eager curiosity.

That sharp emphasis, coupled with the master's look of desperate hope, reignited Ghysbrecht's natural cunning. "I have lost the town records," he stammered, glancing sideways at the man like a fox cornered near a henhouse.

"Oh, is that all?"

"Not enough! What shall the burghers say? What will the magistrates do?" Suddenly, Ghysbrecht erupted again. "A hundred crowns to whoever recovers them! Every single item that sat in this box. If even one piece is missing, I pay naught."

"'Tis a bargain, master," the servant replied quickly, patting his pouch. "See, those sheets of sheepskin you prize so highly are indeed there, right beside Gerard Eliassoen."

"That is true," Ghysbrecht whispered, relief washing over his features. "Good, faithful Dierich. All of it, mind you, every scrap."

"Sir," the servant said, straightening his stance, "I shall take the constables directly to Gerard's dwelling and seize him for theft."

"Theft? Ay, most certainly! Good! Very good. It is theft. I had forgotten that detail. Since he is now a thief, we shall cast him into the dungeons below, where the toads and rats dwell. Dierich, let this man never see the light of day again. It is his own doing; he was prying. Haste, haste! Before he finds time to speak, you understand how swiftly words can undo us."

Within the hour, Dierich Brower and four constables arrived at the hosier's home, demanding young Gerard from the terrified Catherine. "Alas, what mischief has he wrought now?" she cried, tears streaming down her cheeks. "That boy will break my heart."

"Nay, dame, 'twas merely youthful folly," Dierich smoothed, his voice dripping with false concern. "He has simply absconded with certain parchment skins. Doubtless a frolic, yet the Burgomaster is answerable to the citizens for their safekeeping, and thus he worries greatly. As for the youth, he shall likely receive a mere reprimand."

These smooth words lulled Catherine into a false sense of security, but her daughter, Kate, remained wary. Her suspicions grew sharper when Dierich learned that Gerard was nowhere to be found, nor had he returned that night. His disproportionate anger and disappointment masked his true intentions.

"Then depart," Dierich commanded roughly, turning on his heel. "Time is wasted. I shall locate him if he walks the earth."

"And what need did he have to steal?" Kate asked quietly as he prepared to leave.

Dierich paused, his brow furrowing. "What need?" he retorted bitterly.

"Gerard did not steal the trash," Kate insisted, her voice steady despite her fear. "He took it solely to spite the Burgomaster, who stole his freedom. Yet he shall answer to the Duke for his actions. And regarding these parchment skins you cherish so—look in the nearest stream or pigsty, and chances are you shall find them there."

"Think you so, mistress? Think you so?" Dierich's eyes flashed with suspicion. "Perhaps you know the truth?"

"I know this," Kate declared firmly, "that Gerard is too virtuous to steal, and too wise to burden himself with rubbish while traveling. Give you good day, then."

"And the sheepskin you scorn?" Dierich snapped, his composure cracking. "I value it more than the hide of any creature in Tergou."

With that, he hurried away, chasing a phantom trail.

Kate retreated to the inner chamber and called Giles aside. "Giles, my heart forebodes ill. Speak not a word of what I tell you to any soul. I told Dirk Brower that Gerard has fled Holland, but I strongly suspect he is not a league from Tergou."

"Why, where is he, then?" Giles asked, leaning closer.

"With whom he loves," Kate whispered urgently. "But if so, he must not linger. These men are deep, dark, and wicked, seeking him. Look into Dirk Brower's eye, Giles, and you see why I tremble. Oh, why cannot I flee to Sevenbergen myself and command him away? Why am I not strong and agile like other girls? God forgive me for fretting at His will, but I have never felt so helpless, so lame and weak and useless. But you are strong, dear Giles," she added, clutching his arm. "You are very strong."

"Yes, I am strong," Giles boomed, though his expression softened as he grasped her meaning. "But I detest walking on foot."

"But if you refuse to help..." Kate pleaded. "Dear Giles, do you not love Gerard?"

"Yes, I hold him in higher esteem than any other," Giles assured her. "I shall ride to Sevenbergen on Peter Buyskens' mule. Ask him, for he will surely not lend it to me otherwise."

Kate protested, warning that the whole town would follow, revealing Gerard's whereabouts and placing him in greater peril. Giles deflected her fears by vowing to ride out of town in the opposite direction, ensuring the mule did not turn toward Sevenbergen until they had successfully evaded the curious eyes of the crowd.

Kate finally relented, borrowing the mule. She entrusted Giles with a short, weighty message, drilling it into his memory until he could recite it word for word. As Giles mounted the animal, Kate withdrew, performing the final act within her power for her beloved brother: kneeling in prayer, pleading earnestly for his safety.
