OF THE GLORY AND GOODNESS AND THE EVIL THAT GO TO THE MAKING OF LOVE.

During my time of struggle, I’d avoided old Hasluck like the plague. Not because he was a bad man—far from it—but because he was the kind of fellow who’d cheerfully offer you a job, a meal, and a lecture on how to “pull yourself together,” all in one breath. He was a jolly sort, full of hearty laughter and advice that always came with a side of unsolicited wisdom. I could have taken his help, sure—I might’ve even lived better than I did with Lott and Co., half-starving on cold beans and dreams. But something in me rebelled at the idea of accepting kindness from someone I knew. It wasn’t about money or dignity, though I’ll admit I didn’t care much for honesty either—Lott and Co.’s business dealings were about as ethical as a fox in a chicken coop. No, it was deeper than that. It had nothing to do with Barbara’s father, or even Barbara herself, really. To me, she wasn’t part of any family tree, not even a real one. She was more like a myth, a legend whispered in the dark corners of my mind. If I’d been forced to name her parents, I’d have said they were some ancient gods who’d abandoned their temple long ago, leaving only the echo of their love behind.

I’ve always preferred maps to asking directions. Give me a complicated route, a foreign railway timetable, anything that lets me figure things out myself—even if I end up lost in a swamp. There’s a certain pride in getting lost by design, rather than being led straight into a trap by someone who claims to know the way. Maybe I’m descended from a grumpy cave-dweller who refused to dance in tribal rituals, preferring to make his own tools and mutter curses under his breath while everyone else sang songs of unity. That’s probably why I never felt right around people who were too nice. They made me nervous.

But now? Now I had a foothold. I wasn’t begging anymore. So I wrote to old Hasluck—not a letter of appeal, but of simple courtesy. And lo and behold, almost before the ink dried, he sent back a note so warm and cheerful it made me feel like I’d just won a lottery. He told me Barbara had returned from abroad—“just yesterday!”—and added, with the confidence of a man who knows exactly what’s going on in other people’s lives, that she’d be “delighted” to see me. Naturally, he invited me over. Of course he did. The door was wide open, metaphorically speaking, and probably literally too.

No one ever knew about my boyhood crush on Barbara—not even my mother, who adored her, though she’d talk about her faults like she was reviewing a new play. “She’s got a temper,” she’d say, “and she doesn’t wear her hair properly.” And I’d cringe inside, like someone watching a priest dissect a holy relic. I used to walk around Stamford Hill at night, circling that enormous red-brick house like a ghost haunting its own past. Each lap brought me closer to peace, yet farther from reality. I’d return to the noisy streets feeling purified, as if I’d just left a cathedral and stepped back into the world.

During those four years she was away, my feelings didn’t fade—they grew. She became less a person and more an ideal. A vision crafted entirely from memory and longing. I didn’t want to see her again; I wanted to *remember* her. To keep her perfect, untouched by time. In truth, I feared seeing her as a woman—someone who might sneeze, or complain about a sore toe, or leave her curlers in her hair after a bath. That would ruin everything. I wouldn’t have minded worshipping her from afar. In fact, I preferred it. To live with her, eat beside her, watch her yawn during dinner—that would be torture. I didn’t want to drag her down into the messy business of daily life. I was glad I couldn’t.

And now she was older. A woman. I still wasn’t quite a man. She’d marry. I didn’t mind. My feelings weren’t romantic—they were spiritual. She wasn’t a person to me; she was a symbol. A representation of love itself, stripped bare and placed on a pedestal. Like how we build statues of saints and then get angry when they don’t behave like saints. We forget: we made them. We gave them form. We’re the ones who expect miracles.

I waited a week after receiving Hasluck’s letter before visiting. It was late afternoon in early summer. Hasluck was still in the City. Mrs. Hasluck was visiting someone. Miss Hasluck—Barbara—was in the garden. I told the footman not to bother—he could go fetch tea or whatever. I’d find her myself. I knew exactly where she’d be. Her favorite spot: a sunny corner surrounded by yew hedges shaped like birds and animals, Dutch-style. She was there, reading, bent over her book. And when I saw her, the same shock hit me—the same moment I’d felt as a boy when she first appeared in my father’s office, silhouetted against the dusty books: *All the fairy tales had come true.* Only this time, she wasn’t a princess. She was a queen.

Taller. More dignified. More beautiful. I stood in the shadow of the hedges, watching her profile, the curve of her neck, the elegance of her hand resting on the lilac fabric of her dress. I didn’t speak. I didn’t move. I could’ve stayed there forever.

Then she turned. Saw me. Smiled faintly. Held out her hand.

I knelt. Took it. Kissed it. It wasn’t planned. It just happened. Like breathing.

Her smile widened slightly, but there was a hint of mockery beneath it. Yet she seemed pleased. Not offended. Not even surprised.

“So you’re still in love with me?” she asked. “I wondered.”

“Did you know?”

“I should’ve been blind not to.”

“But I was only a boy.”

“You weren’t the usual type of boy. You won’t be the usual type of man.”

“You don’t mind me loving you?”

“I can’t help it. Neither can you.”

She sat on a stone bench facing a sundial, hands behind her head, laughing softly.

“I’ll always love you,” I said. “But it’s a strange kind of love. I don’t understand it.”

“Tell me,” she said, still smiling. “Describe it.”

I leaned against the sundial column. The sun was setting, casting long shadows across the grass, lighting her face in golden warmth.

“I wish you were a queen,” I said. “An ancient queen. And I could serve you. Be near you. Do your bidding. Your love would ruin everything. I wouldn’t ask for it. I wouldn’t want it. Just to look at you. To touch your hand once in a while. To kiss the glove you dropped, the shoe you kicked off. To know you knew I loved you. That you owned me. That I lived or died by your whim.”

She laughed lightly. “I’d prefer to be the queen. The priestess role isn’t for me. Temples are too cold.”

A shiver passed through her. She gestured toward her feet. “That’s how you shall love me, Paul. Adoring me. Worshipping me—blindly. I’ll be your queen. And I’ll treat you—however I please. Everything I think, everything I do, you’ll tell me is right. The queen can do no wrong.”

She took my face in her hands, looked deeply into my eyes. “You understand, Paul? The queen can do no wrong—never, never.”

“I understand,” I whispered. “My queen can do no wrong.”

She laughed and let go.

“Now you may sit beside me. This honor will have to last you a long time. Tell me what you’ve done. Maybe it’ll amuse me. Then I’ll tell you what I’ve done—and you’ll say it was right.”

I told her everything—briefly, honestly. Even the Lady *Ortensia*, which I was ashamed of. She frowned slightly.

“You must never do anything again,” she said sharply, “that makes me feel ashamed of you. Or I’ll banish you forever. I must be proud of you. Dishonoring yourself dishonors me. I’m angry with you, Paul. Don’t make me angry again.”

And so it was settled. Though my love for her—exactly as she wanted it—didn’t always save me from the whispers of my darker self, it did give me strength. The desire to be worthy of her, to honor her, shaped my life. Love, in its purest form, does that.

The morning light fades. The magic veil tears. We see clearly. My love was a woman. She is dead. They dressed her corpse in rags, but love sees through lies. God knows I loved her purely. Only false love loves falsehood. Beneath the dirt, she sleeps fair.

When I finished, she said, “Now I’ll tell you mine.”

“I’m getting married soon,” she said. “I’ll be a Countess. Countess Huescar—I’ll teach you how to say it. I’ll have a real castle in Spain.”

“You’re not scared?” I asked.

“No,” she laughed. “We won’t live there. It’s a ruined mess, high in the mountains. He hates it even more than I do. Paris and London will be my courts. You’ll see me often. You’ll meet the great world, Paul. The world I mean to conquer.”

“Is he rich?”

“As poor as a Spanish nobleman,” she said. “The money’s mine—or Dad’s. He gives me title, position. I don’t love him. He’s handsome. Don’t look so solemn. Queens don’t make love matches. They make alliances. I’ve done well. Congratulate me. Say I’ve acted rightly.”

“Does he love you?”

“He says he does,” she laughed. “How uncourtier-like of you! Do you think any man could see me and not fall in love?”

She stood up abruptly. “I don’t want his love. It would bore me. Women hate love they can’t return. Not like yours, little Paul. That’s sweet incense. I want that. Give it to me. But the love of a husband? Horrible!”

For a moment, I saw her not as a goddess, but as a child needing protection.

“But have you thought—”

“Yes, yes,” she cut in. “I’ve thought until I can’t think anymore. There must be sacrifice. As little as possible. He doesn’t love me. He’s marrying me for my money. I know that. And I’m glad. You don’t know me, Paul. I need rank. Position. What am I? The daughter of a butcher who became rich? As Princess Huescar, society forgets. As Mrs. Jones or Brown, it remembers. I’m vain. Ambitious. I have my father’s blood. He spent his life building wealth. I must build something else.”

“But you don’t have to be Mrs. Nobody,” I said. “Why not wait? Someone might come along who can give you both status and love.”

“He won’t come,” she said. “Because he already has. Hush, Paul. The queen can do no wrong.”

“Who is he?”

“You’ll know,” she said. “I want you to know. Then you’ll say I’ve done right. That you still respect me. That you honor me.”

He couldn’t help me. As his wife, I’d be less than I am now—a rich nobody giving dinners to other rich nobodies, surrounded by retired tradesmen and opera singers. Is that the court you want your queen to reign in?

“Is he so ordinary?” I asked.

“He’s not ordinary,” she said. “It’s I who am ordinary. The things I want are beneath him. He’ll never care to get them.”

“Not even for love of you?”

“I wouldn’t have him do it, even if he would. He’s great. Great in a way I can’t understand. He’s not suited to these times. In old days, I’d have married him. I’d have known he’d rise by strength alone. But now men don’t climb. They crawl. He couldn’t crawl. I’ve done right, Paul.”

“What does he say?”

“Shall I tell you?” She laughed bitterly. “‘You’re half a woman and half a fool. You’ll follow your folly. But make sure your woman doesn’t make a fool of herself.’”

I recognized the voice instantly.

“Hal,” I said.

“So you never guessed,” she said. “I thought it would be sweet to cry it out. That it wouldn’t matter. That everyone who knew me could read it in my eyes.”

“But he never paid you much attention,” I said.

She laughed. “What did I ever do for you that was more than snubbing? We boys and girls—we’re not so different. We love our masters. But don’t think so poorly of me. I was just a child to him then. But we were locked together in Paris during the siege. Have you heard? He did take notice of me, Paul. I assure you.”

Years later, on a winter night in the Julier Pass, I received a pencilled note:

“Come up and see me. I’m a prisoner here till the weather breaks. Hal.”

I barely recognized him. A shadow of the boy I once knew. His body, once magnificent, was broken by war and excess. He sat propped up on pillows in a dim, vaulted room that used to be a chapel, lit by a cheap lamp, heated by a German oven that smelled like hell.

“I saw you from the window,” he said. “Twice a day, the sledges come in. That’s my only excitement. I broke down coming across the pass. Stuck in a drift for eighteen hours. Nearly killed my last lung. And no books. By God, lad, I was glad to see your frozen face.”

He gripped my hand. “Sit down. Let me hear my own language again. You were always a good listener. Eight years, I haven’t spoken English. Can you stand this room? The windows should be open, but what does it matter? Might as well get used to the heat before I die.”

I sat close. Between coughing fits, he talked—boisterously, wildly, laughing at my protests, choking on his own words.

Then: “What is she doing? Do you ever see her?”

“She’s playing in—” I named a comic opera. “No, I haven’t seen her lately.”

He laid his hand on mine. “What a pity you and I couldn’t have rolled into one. You, the saint. Me, the satyr. Together, we’d have made her perfect lover.”

I remembered nights listening to my parents argue through the wall. I was always caught between them, helpless.

“Tell me,” I said. “I loved her, knowing her. I wasn’t blind. Whose fault was it? Yours or hers?”

He laughed. “Whose fault, Paul? God made us.”

Thinking of her face, I hated his laugh. Then I saw the pain in his eyes. Pity replaced anger.

“You’ve been on the stage,” he said. “You’ve heard the saying: ‘Ah, well, the curtain must come down, however badly things are going.’ It’s only a play, Paul. We don’t choose our parts. I didn’t even know I was the villain until I heard the booing. I thought I was the hero—noble, sacrificing myself for her happiness. She would’ve married me if I’d bothered enough.”

I opened my mouth, but he went on.

“Ah, yes. It might have been better. Easy to say, not knowing. It might have been worse. Probably the same. All roads lead to the end. I was always a fatalist, Paul. We tried both ways. She loved me. But she loved the world too. I thought she loved it more. So I kissed her forehead, mumbled a prayer, and walked away. She sobbed. First act ended. Wasn’t I the hero? I thought so. Slapped myself on the back. Then—well, you know what happened. She was finer clay than she thought. Love is woman’s kingdom, not the world. Even then, I cared more for her than myself. I could’ve borne my share—had I not seen her suffering. Shamed. Degraded. So we dared to be ourselves. Lost the world for love. Wasn’t it brave? Were we not hero and heroine? They printed the playbill wrong. I was the hero. But the printer made a mistake. So instead of applause, you booed. How could you know?”

“But that wasn’t the end,” I said. “If the curtain had fallen then, I could’ve forgiven you.”

He grinned. “That fatal last act. Even yours don’t always come right, the critics say.”

The grin faded. “We may never see each other again, Paul. Don’t think too badly of me. I realized I’d made a second mistake. Or thought I had. She wasn’t happier with me than with him. Our longings aren’t one. Life’s hard. What can we do but toss a coin? And if it lands heads, we wish it was tails. If tails, we mourn the heads we lost. Love isn’t all a woman’s life. Nor a man’s. He didn’t file for divorce. Smart of him. We were shunned. Ignored. Some women wouldn’t care. But she was used to being courted. She pretended to be happy. Tried to laugh. I watched her grow listless. Angry with her. With myself. Our bond was passion. Real enough. ‘Grand,’ I suppose. Good for a summer in a cave. But not for marriage. We fought. Screamed. Became ugly. Most of us look better from a distance. The sordid side of life became important. I wasn’t rich. Compared to her past, I was poor. The sight of our twelve-pound-a-year cook’s food made her lose her appetite. Love doesn’t change taste. You can’t learn to like cheap claret after champagne.

“She fell ill. I felt I’d dragged her from fertile ground only to watch her die. Then he came. At the perfect moment. I admire him. Most men take revenge clumsily. He was neat. Patient. I’m not ashamed of falling into his trap. It was well baited. Maybe I despised him for seeming to accept the blow quietly. But what did he care for me? He cared only for her. He knew her better than I. Knew she’d tire—not of love, but of the cottage. Long for what she’d lost. Fool! Cuckold! What did it matter that the world would laugh? Love like his made fools of men. Would I not give her back to him?”

And so it ended. Not with glory, not with tragedy, but with quiet resignation. The queen had chosen her throne. The prince had chosen his exile. And I? I remained the loyal subject, forever kneeling in the shadow of the yews.
