Colonel Esmond’s grandson stood a full while at the gate of Castlewood, and made that honest clatter upon the knocker which in Virginia would have brought half a house to the door with smiles and questions and a hand already reaching for a saddle-bag. Here, the old stone seemed to swallow sound. The only answer was the distant hush of rooms shut up, and—more plainly than any welcome—the thin tick of leisure within, as if time itself were a servant who need not hurry.

At last the door opened no more than was necessary; and John the footman, who had been playing cards with Thomas in a window-seat, appeared with the air of a man obliged to interrupt a serious business for a trifling one. To be told that the visitor was of the family altered nothing in John’s face. The family were away. In their absence, he cared little for their kin, and much for the game waiting at the sill.

Harry, heat in his cheeks from travel and hope, begged—begged again—until permission was grudgingly given to step into my lady’s sitting-room and the picture-room. There, sure enough, the past was hung as neatly as a pantry of preserves: his grandfather in periwig and breastplate, the very counterpart of the canvas at home in Virginia; and his grandmother as Lady Castlewood, in an earlier fashion, her neck bare in Charles the Second’s time, her fair hair falling in ringlets—ringlets which in Harry’s living memory had been snowy white. He looked till the old varnish seemed to breathe, and the painted silks rustled in his fancy; but the housekeeper, brisk with preparations and sharp with importance, drove him onward as one drives a boy from a larder when dinner is to be served for guests of consequence.

“My lord and my lady are expected this evening,” she said. “There is my lady the Countess, and my lord and his brother, and the young ladies, and the Baroness, who is to have the state bedchamber.”

“The Baroness?” Harry asked, catching at any thread of relation as a hungry man snatches at the smell of bread.

“Baroness Bernstein—the young ladies’ aunt,” said the woman, and her eyes were already elsewhere, counting linens, weighing candles, measuring courtesies.

In the hall, with the stubborn politeness of a stranger who will not be made invisible, Harry wrote his name on a leaf torn from his own pocket-book and laid it where it must be seen.

Henry Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood, in Virginia, arrived in England yesterday—staying at the Three Castles in the village.

The lackeys, scenting a possible “wail,” rose from their cards only to profit by him; and Gumbo, faithful as a shadow, left the bench at the gate where he had been talking with old Lockwood the porter. Lockwood took Harry’s guinea with a bewildered gratitude, as though the yellow piece were a foreign coin whose value lay in the giver’s hand rather than in the King’s head upon it.

Harry walked away from that house of his fathers with a dryness in his mouth that no ale could mend. In all those faces—servant, housekeeper, porter—little Polly’s was the only countenance that had held any unselfish light; and he would not own, even to himself, how the chill of it had struck him. They ought to have known him. Had any ridden up to Castlewood in Virginia—master at home or abroad—the stranger would have had a chair drawn near the hearth, and a plate set as if he had been expected. Yet here, in sight of his ancestors’ hall, he must go and ask a country alehouse for a dish of bacon and eggs.

At the Three Castles they gave him what he asked, and more than he asked, for hunger makes men bold in ordering. The bacon came from a hog salted hard enough to keep its virtue through winter; the eggs were fresh, with yolks deep as marigolds, and the fat in the pan carried a clean sweet smoke that rose and clung to his mourning coat as if to comfort it. He watched the hostess turn the rashers with a fork worn bright at the tines, and for a moment the homely sizzle steadied him—sound as much as savour; a small domestic music against the great silence of Castlewood. He ate. He could taste pepper and salt, could feel the crust of fried edge and the soft give of white; and yet every mouthful seemed paid for in something more than coin.

After dinner he went to the bridge and sat upon it, looking towards the old house, behind which the sun was sinking. The rooks came cawing home to the elms, and their harsh, communal talk made a canopy of sound above the river; beneath it, the water moved with a low, constant tongue. Hard by, a blacksmith’s forge clinked and rang—iron on iron—so steady that it might have been a heart at work.

In that mingling of evening noises—rook-calls, forge-clinks, bird-cries from the hedges—his fancy peopled the ford with what his mother and grandsire had told him: knights and huntsmen crossing where the shallows glittered; cavaliers of King Charles’s day; my Lord Castlewood, his grandmother’s first husband, riding out with hawk and hound. The past came as naturally as the smell of smoke in a kitchen: not a story told, but something you breathe.

Then, as always when he allowed himself tenderness, the recollection of his dear dead brother rose and smote him. The pang was so sharp it made him bow his head as if struck. All his pleasures and griefs had been shared with that companion until of late; and now, with the rooks talking above him and the forge chiming beside, he felt again the cruel newness of being alone.

It was then that two young men on horseback dashed over the bridge, cutting across the calm as a knife cuts through cloth. One, with an oath, called him a fool and bade him keep out of the way; the other—who might well have jostled him clean over the parapet—spurred on the instant he had passed, shouting to his fellow to come on. They were up the hill towards Castlewood before Harry had recovered from surprise and wrath.

A moment later came two servants in livery on horseback, scowling at him with that peculiar British question which sounds like a curse: Who are you to be here at all? And after them, heavy as an age, a coach-and-six rumbled onto the bridge, so weighty the horses seemed to sweat at the very idea of drawing it. Within were three ladies, a couple of maids, and an armed man perched behind. Three handsome pale faces looked out at Harry as the carriage passed; not one returned the salute he gave, for he recognized the family arms and would not forget himself. The armed man glared haughtily, as if his stare were itself a weapon.

Harry remained on the bridge and felt the river’s cool talk below him and the rooks’ rough talk above, and in that moment both sounded like a language that did not include him. He thought—half sick with longing—of Captain Franks, of the Rachel and her small tossing cabin. Even that cramped place at sea seemed cheerful beside this ancestral ground where he stood unclaimed.

Back at the inn the folk did not know the name of Warrington; they knew only titles. They told him that was my lady in the coach, with her stepdaughter, my Lady Maria, and her daughter, my Lady Fanny; that the young gentleman in the grey frock was Mr. William, and he with powder on the chestnut was my lord. It was my lord who had sworn loudest and called him a fool; it was Mr. William in the grey frock who had nearly ridden him into the ditch.

The landlord had shown Harry a bedchamber, but Harry had refused to have his portmanteaux unpacked. Surely—surely—the great house would send for him. One hour passed, then two, then three; and no footman came with a civil message, no groom with a led horse, no note with a seal. At last he was forced to have his trunks opened like a man who admits defeat, and to call for slippers and gown.

Just before dark—two hours after the first carriage—another chariot with four horses crossed the bridge. A stout high-coloured lady with very dark eyes looked hard at him as she passed. That was the Baroness Bernstein, said the landlady—my lord’s aunt; and Harry remembered how the first Lady Castlewood had come of a German family. Earl and Countess and Baroness, postillions, gentlemen and horses, all vanished behind the gate; and he, the grandson of the house, lay down at last in the inn with a cruel sense of neglect pressing on his young heart.

Sleep would not come. And presently from below rose a prodigious noise—cursing, giggling, a woman’s scream, a man’s shout—the bar’s rude revel. The inn’s sounds were the opposite of Castlewood’s silence: not exclusion, but invasion; not coldness, but too much heat. The very boards seemed to shake with it.

Then Gumbo’s voice sounded without, steady and pleading. “You cannot go in, sar—my master asleep, sar!” But a shrill voice, thick with drink and oaths—a voice Harry recognized from the bridge—cursed Gumbo as a stupid negro woolly-pate. Gumbo was thrust aside, and a flood of profanity poured into the room, followed by a young gentleman reeling as if the floor were a deck at sea.

“Beg your pardon, Cousin Warrington,” cried the young blasphemer, “are you asleep? Beg your pardon for riding you over on the bridge. Didn’t know you—course shouldn’t have done it—thought it was a lawyer with a writ—dressed in black, you know. Gad! thought it was Nathan come to nab me.” And Mr. William laughed, not merrily but as liquor laughs through a man.

“You do me great honour to mistake me for a sheriff’s officer, cousin,” Harry said with great gravity, sitting up in his tall nightcap.

“Gad! I thought it was Nathan, and was going to send you souse into the river. But I ask your pardon. You see I had been drinking at the Bell at Hexton, and the punch is good at the Bell at Hexton. Hullo! you, Davis! a bowl of punch; d’you hear?”

The very word punch brought with it a phantom scent—hot spirit, lemon-peel bruised on the rim, sugar melting into sting; the sort of drink that makes talk louder than truth. Harry’s stomach turned from it, not from delicacy but from weariness.

“I have had my share for to-night, cousin, and I should think you have,” Harry answered, still in that dignified tone which is sometimes only anger in full dress.

“You want me to go, Cousin What’s-your-name, I see,” Mr. William said, suddenly solemn, swaying his judgment as he swayed his body. “You want me to go, and they want me to come, and I didn’t want to come. I said, I’d see him hanged first,—that’s what I said. Why should I trouble myself to come down all alone of an evening, and look after a fellow I don’t care a pin for? Zackly what I said. Zackly what Castlewood said. Why the devil should he go down? Castlewood says, and so said my lady, but the Baroness would have you. It’s all the Baroness’s doing, and if she says a thing, it must be done; so you must just get up and come.”

He poured it out in a stream, words running into one another like spilt liquor, and he tacked about the room as he spoke, his boots thudding, his breath sour with spirits and citrus. Harry’s wrath, which had been cooling into melancholy, flared up afresh.

“I tell you what, cousin,” he cried, “I won’t move for the Countess, or for the Baroness, or for all the cousins in Castlewood!”

The landlord entered with the bowl of punch Mr. William had ordered—a steaming, amber-coloured thing that smelled of peel and heat. Harry, furious at the insult of it in his chamber, called to the host to turn the sot out.

“Sot, you little tobacconist! Sot, you Cherokee!” screamed Mr. William. “Jump out of bed, and I’ll drive my sword through your body. Why didn’t I do it to-day when I took you for a bailiff—a confounded pettifogging bum-bailiff!”

He went on screeching oaths and incoherencies until the landlord, the drawer, the hostler, and half the kitchen folk crowded in to lead him away as men lead away a bull that has got into a garden. After which Harry shut himself again behind his anger as behind canvas—closed his tent round him, as soldiers say—and at last, no doubt from sheer exhaustion, slept.

In the morning the landlord was obsequious, having now learned precisely who his guest was. Messengers had come from the castle in the night, it appeared, to fetch both young gentlemen home; and poor Mr. William had returned in a wheelbarrow, being not altogether unaccustomed to that mode of conveyance. The landlord swore Mr. William never remembered a thing next day; he was of a real kind nature; the men got crowns and half-crowns from him by saying he beat them overnight when he was in liquor. “He’s the devil when he’s tipsy,” the landlord said, “but when he’s sober he is the very kindest of young gentlemen.”

Meanwhile, within Castlewood’s walls—where last night’s candles had burned down upon old plate and family portraits—Harry’s name upon the paper had occasioned a little council. My Lord Castlewood supposed that must have been the young gentleman they had seen on the bridge; as they had not drowned him they must invite him. Let a man go down with the proper messages. Let a servant carry a note. Lady Fanny thought it would be more civil if one of the brothers went, considering the greeting they had given. My Lord Castlewood had not the slightest objection to his brother William going—yes, William should go.

Upon which William said, with a stronger expression, that he would be hanged if he would. Lady Maria thought the young man on the bridge was pretty enough. Castlewood was dreadfully dull; neither of her brothers did anything to make it amusing. He might be vulgar—no doubt he was vulgar—but let them see the American. Such was Lady Maria’s charitable curiosity.

Lady Castlewood, however, was for neither receiving nor refusing him, but for delaying. “Wait till your aunt comes, children; perhaps the Baroness won’t like to see the young man; at least, let us consult her before we ask him.” Thus hospitality remained in abeyance like a dish kept back because one is uncertain who will sit at table.

At length the equipage of the Baroness Bernstein appeared; and if there was any doubt about the Virginian stranger, there was no doubt about this wealthy kinswoman. The state-chamber had been prepared. The cook had arrived the day before with instructions to make a supper such as her ladyship liked—sustenance here being not merely nourishment but a kind of language, the family’s truest dialect of deference.

The oak dining-room shone with old plate; the table was set as if to reflect every candle back into the faces that coveted favour. Around the walls looked down their dead: the late Viscount, his father, his mother, his sister—two lovely pictures; a predecessor by Vandyck and his Viscountess; and there Colonel Esmond too, in red coat and cuirass, black periwig vast as a cloud—about whose grandson the living Esmonds showed such a moderate sympathy.

The feast was a good one and the Baroness enjoyed it. The Countess pressed dish after dish upon her; the butler filled her glass with champagne the moment it was empty; the young folk and their mother kept the conversation not by speaking but by listening—by that careful, flattering silence which is itself a kind of music. The Baroness was full of spirits and humour. She knew everybody in Europe, and about those everybodies she told the wickedest stories. The Countess, ordinarily demure and severe and a stickler for propriety, smiled at the worst; the girls laughed when their mother signalled; the boys roared with especial delight at their sisters’ confusion. They drank freely too; and after supper came a bowl of smoking punch, hot and sweet and treacherous, set down like a centerpiece of temptation.

Many and many a night, the Baroness said, she had drunk at that table by her father’s side. “That was his place,” she said, pointing to the chair the Countess now occupied. She saw none of the old plate in those days. It had been melted to pay gambling debts. She hoped—young gentlemen—that they did not play.

“Never, on my word,” said Castlewood.

“Never, ’pon honour,” said Will, winking at his brother.

The Baroness was glad to hear they were such good boys. Her face grew redder with punch, and she became more voluble—might have been thought coarse, but times were different, and critics especially favourable to those who might leave them money.

She spoke of the boys’ father, their grandfather, other men and women of the house; and then, lifting that arm which was still beautifully round and white, she pointed toward the portrait of the military gentleman in the cuirass.

“The only man of the family was that,” she said.

“The Virginian? What is he good for? I always thought he was good for nothing but to cultivate tobacco and my grandmother,” said my lord, laughing.

The Baroness struck her hand upon the table with such energy the glasses danced. “I say he was the best of you all. There never was one of the male Esmonds that had more brains than a goose, except him. He was not fit for this wicked, selfish old world of ours, and he was right to go and live out of it. Where would your father have been, young people, but for him?”

“Was he particularly kind to our papa?” Lady Maria asked.

“Old stories, my dear Maria!” cried the Countess hastily. “I am sure my dear Earl was very kind to him in giving him that great estate in Virginia.”

“Since his brother’s death, the lad who has been here to-day is heir to that. Mr. Draper told me so! Peste! I don’t know why my father gave up such a property,” said my lord.

“Who has been here to-day?” asked the Baroness, the punch and something else making her eyes shine.

“Harry Esmond Warrington, of Virginia,” my lord answered; “a lad whom Will nearly pitched into the river, and whom I pressed my lady the Countess to invite to stay here.”

“You mean that one of the Virginian boys has been to Castlewood, and has not been asked to stay here?”

“There is but one of them, my dear creature,” the Earl interposed. “The other, you know, has just been—”

“For shame, for shame!”

“Oh! it ain’t pleasant, I confess, to be se—”

“Do you mean that a grandson of Henry Esmond, the master of this house, has been here, and none of you have offered him hospitality?”

“Since we didn’t know it, and he is staying at the Castles?” Will put in.

“That he is staying at the Inn, and you are sitting there!” cried the old lady. “This is too bad—call somebody to me. Get me my hood—I’ll go to the boy myself. Come with me this instant, my Lord Castlewood.”

The young man rose, plainly in wrath. “Madame the Baroness of Bernstein,” he said, “your ladyship is welcome to go; but as for me, I don’t choose to have such words as ‘shameful’ applied to my conduct. I won’t go and fetch the young gentleman from Virginia, and I propose to sit here and finish this bowl of punch. Eugene! Don’t Eugene me, madam. I know her ladyship has a great deal of money, which you are desirous should remain in our amiable family. You want it more than I do. Cringe for it—I won’t.” And he sank back again.

The Baroness looked from one lowered head to another; then at my lord—without dislike this time. She leaned toward him and spoke rapidly in German: “I had unright when I said the Colonel was the only man of the family. Thou canst, if thou willest, Eugene.” My lord only bowed.

“If you do not wish an old woman to go out at this hour of the night,” the Baroness said aloud, “let William, at least, go and fetch his cousin.”

“The very thing I proposed,” said my lord.

“And so did we—and so did we!” cried the daughters.

“I am sure I only wanted the dear Baroness’s consent,” said their mother, “and shall be charmed for my part to welcome our young relative.”

“Will! Put on thy pattens and get a lantern, and go fetch the Virginian,” my lord said.

“And we will have another bowl of punch when he comes,” Will answered—already too far gone. And so he went forth upon his embassy, as Harry had seen, and ordered more punch, and failed miserably at civility.

Lady Castlewood, catching sight of young Harry by the river-side, must have seen a handsome and interesting youth; and very likely had reasons of her own for not desiring his presence. All mothers are not eager to encourage the visits of a nineteen-year-old in a house where there are virgins of twenty. If Harry’s acres had lain in Norfolk or Devon instead of Virginia, no doubt her welcome would have been warmer. People of fashion, when selfish, are at least frank in their selfishness; when cold-hearted, they seldom waste hypocrisy of affection.

Why should her ladyship put herself out for a friendless stranger? Because he was alone? Only a simpleton imagines so romantic a motive in such circles. Great folk are friendly to those who have plenty of friends. A poor lad from a distant country, with moderate means and those not yet in his own power, likely uncouth manners and provincial habits—was a Countess called upon to trouble herself? Allons donc. He was quite as well at the alehouse as at the castle.

This, no doubt, was her ladyship’s opinion; and her kinswoman, the Baroness Bernstein, who knew her perfectly, understood it too. The Baroness herself was a woman of the world; she could be selfish enough on occasion. She understood the deference shown her by mother, daughters, sons; and being a woman of humour, played upon it—amused herself with their greediness and their humiliations, with their artless respect for her money-box and their clinging attachment to her purse. They were not very rich. Lady Castlewood’s own money was settled on her children. The two elder had inherited little but flaxen heads from their German mother and a pedigree of prodigious distinction. Those who had money and those who had none were alike eager for the Baroness’s; in this matter the rich are as greedy as the poor.

So if Madam Bernstein struck the table and made glasses—and hearts—tremble, it was partly because champagne and punch warmed her blood, and partly because a generous impulse does sometimes rise with generous wine. She thought of the poor lad outside his own door and felt indignant—not because she imagined her relatives different from what they were, but because she could not help seeing, with a sudden clarity, the shameful smallness of their silence.

She was restless and resolute, extraordinarily active for her years. Long before the languid Castlewood ladies—home from London routs and balls—had quitted their feather-beds, and before jolly Will had slept off his various potations, the Baroness was up and pacing the green terraces that sparkled with morning dew. The dew lay twinkling also upon trim parterres and the crisp dark walls of box, under which marble fauns and dryads cooled themselves. A thousand birds sang; fountains plashed and glittered in rosy sunshine; and rooks cawed from the great wood. The estate’s morning soundscape—water, wings, leaf, distant hoof—was the true music of Castlewood, older than any harpsichord brought in for show, unpurchased, unbranded, and therefore honest. One could not help thinking how cheap and harsh the bar’s racket at the inn had been by comparison, and how much of a man’s peace depends on the noises he must live inside.

After half an hour’s pacing, the person for whom she waited came to her: young Harry Warrington, to whom she had despatched an early billet by one of the Lockwoods. The note was signed B. Bernstein, and told Mr. Esmond Warrington that his relatives at Castlewood—and among them a dear friend of his grandfather—were most anxious he should come to “Colonel Esmond’s house in England.”

Now, accordingly, he appeared beneath the old Gothic doorway and came down the steps from terrace to terrace, hat in hand, his fair hair blowing off a flushed cheek. He was clad in mourning; his slim figure moved with the modesty of youth that has not yet learned to swagger in sorrow. The Baroness, watching, was pleased—as a woman may be pleased when the living carries something of the dead she once knew.

He bowed low, a bow that would have done credit to Versailles. She held out her little hand, and when his palm closed over it she laid her other hand softly upon his ruffle, as if to feel by touch the truth of him. She looked kindly into his honest blushing face.

“I knew your grandfather very well, Harry,” she said. “So you came yesterday to see his picture, and they turned you away—though you know the house was his of right?”

Harry’s blush deepened. “The servants did not know me. A young gentleman came to me last night,” he said, “when I was peevish, and he, I fear, was tipsy. I spoke rudely to my cousin, and would ask his pardon. Your ladyship knows that in Virginia our manners towards strangers are different. I own I had expected another kind of welcome. Was it you, madam, who sent my cousin to me last night?”

“I sent him,” she said; “but you will find your cousins most friendly to you to-day. You must stay here. Lord Castlewood would have been with you this morning, only I was so eager to see you. There will be breakfast in an hour; and meantime you must talk to me. We will send to the Three Castles for your servant and your baggage. Give me your arm. Stop—I dropped my cane when you came. You shall be my cane.”

“My grandfather used to call us his crutches,” Harry said.

“You are like him,” she answered, her dark eyes softening, “though you are fair.”
