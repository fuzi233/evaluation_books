When Buck secured sixteen hundred dollars for John Thornton in a single afternoon, he unlocked a future that promised both freedom and adventure. With those funds, Thornton settled his debts and joined his partners, Hans and Pete, on a perilous expedition eastward in search of a legendary lost mine. This mine was a subject of ancient lore, shrouded in tragedy and mystery, marking a site where dying men had once sworn oaths backed by gold of a unique, unrecognizable grade. While few had succeeded in finding it, many had vanished forever into its treacherous embrace. Now, Thornton, accompanied by Buck and a team of loyal dogs, ventured onto an unknown trail, hoping to claim what others had failed to achieve.

Their journey took them seventy miles upstream along the Yukon, then west into the Stewart River. They navigated past the Mayo and McQuestion rapids, following the river until it narrowed into a gentle streamlet winding through towering peaks that formed the continent's spine. Thornton was a man who lived lightly in relation to both humanity and nature. Unafraid of the wilderness, armed only with a rifle and a pouch of salt, he traveled with the unhurried rhythm of an Indian hunter. If the hunt yielded no meal, he pressed on, trusting that sustenance would eventually appear. Their sled loads consisted primarily of ammunition and tools, while their provisions varied from starvation to riotous feasting depending entirely on the luck of the hunt.

For Buck, this existence was pure delight. Weeks were spent traversing strange landscapes, camping in frozen muck, and panning for dirt. They rafted across alpine lakes and paddled slender canoes carved from standing timber down unknown rivers. The seasons shifted rapidly; they crossed mountain passes in summer blizzards, endured the midnight sun on exposed ridges, and foraged for strawberries in glacier-shaded valleys. By autumn, they entered a desolate, silent realm of weird lakes where wildfowl had once dwelt but now left no sign of life, save for the chilling winds and the melancholy ripple of icy waves.

Through another winter, they tracked the obliterated paths of previous explorers. At one point, they discovered an ancient blaze leading toward what seemed like the Lost Cabin, yet the trail emerged from nowhere and vanished into nothing, preserving its mystery alongside the man who made it. Later, amidst the rotting debris of an abandoned hunting lodge, Thornton unearthed a long-barreled flint-lock—a relic of the early Hudson Bay Company era, valued then as highly as a man's height in beaver skins. Yet, like the cabin, the gun offered no clue to its former owner.

Eventually, spring returned, and their wanderings concluded not with the mythical cabin, but with a shallow placer in a broad valley. Gold lay there like yellow butter at the bottom of the pan. They worked tirelessly, earning thousands daily, sacking the dust and nuggets into moose-hide bags and piling them beside their spruce-bough lodge. Now, the heavy labor ceased. Buck's duties reduced to occasional hauling of meat, leaving him ample time to muse by the fire. The vision of the "short-legged hairy man" from his past returned with renewed frequency. In those mental projections, fear was paramount. The hairy man slept restlessly, peering into the dark with terrified eyes, ready to flee at the slightest sign of danger. Whether walking a beach gathering shellfish or creeping through the forest, they moved with hyper-vigilance. The man could swing effortlessly through the canopy, a creature as at home in the trees as on the ground, often roosting high above the earth to spend nights in vigil.

Closely intertwined with these visions was the Call, a mysterious resonance emanating from the depths of the forest. It stirred a vague, sweet gladness within Buck, awakening wild yearnings for something he could not name. He began pursuing it, barking softly or defiantly, thrusting his nose into cool moss and rich soil, or crouching behind fallen logs, wide-eyed and attentive. Impulses seized him uncontrollably; he would dash from a lazy doze into the forest aisles, running for hours through the undergrowth and across open spaces where niggerheads clustered. He relished the twilight of summer midnights, listening to the forest's sleepy murmurs, deciphering signs and sounds as one reads a book, searching for the source of the summons.

One night, Buck jolted awake, his mane bristling, nostrils quivering. From the forest came the Call, distinct and undeniable—a long, drawn howl, neither exactly like nor unlike any noise a husky dog might make. He knew it, in the old familiar way, as a sound heard before. Springing silently through the sleeping camp, he dashed into the woods, slowing as he approached the cry until he reached an open glade. There, standing erect on haunches, nose pointed to the sky, was a long, lean timber wolf.

Buck stalked forward, body compact, tail stiff, his movements a mixture of threat and tentative friendship—a menacing truce typical of wild predators. The wolf fled, and Buck gave chase, driving him into a blind channel blocked by a timber jam. Cornered, the wolf spun on his hind legs, snarling and snapping his teeth. Buck did not attack immediately; instead, he circled, offering friendly advances. Suspicious and frightened, the smaller wolf darted away repeatedly, only to be caught again by Buck's persistence. Finally, seeing no malice, the wolf sniffed noses with Buck. Friendship ensued, a playful, nervous interaction between fierce beasts. Soon, the wolf signaled his departure, leading Buck across the bleak divide where the creek originated.

Down the opposite slope, they entered a vast expanse of forest and streams, running steadily as the sun climbed higher. Buck felt a wild joy, knowing he was answering the Call, running beside his "wood brother" toward the source. Memories of that primal world flooded back, and he stirred as he once had to reality. They paused to drink, and during the break, Buck thought of John Thornton. Sitting down, he watched the wolf start toward the source, then return to sniff at him, encouraging him. But Buck turned back, starting slowly down the path. For nearly an hour, the wild brother whined softly beside him. Then, sitting and pointing his nose upward, the wolf let out a mournful howl. As Buck walked on, the sound faded into the distance.

Back at camp, John Thornton was dining when Buck burst in, frenzied with affection. He overturned his master, scrambled over him, licking his face and biting his hand. Thornton, shaking him back and forth, cursed him lovingly, calling it "playing the general tom-fool." For two days and nights, Buck refused to leave Thornton's side, watching him eat and sleep. But gradually, the Call grew more imperious. Haunted by memories of the wild brother and the smiling lands beyond the divide, Buck began wandering again. The wolf did not return, nor did the mournful howl echo from the woods.

Buck started sleeping outdoors, venturing away from camp for days at a time. Crossing the divide, he descended into a land of timber and streams, wandering for a week seeking the wolf. He killed game as he traveled, moving with a long, easy lope that seemed tireless. He fished for salmon in a broad stream flowing toward the sea and, by that same stream, encountered a large black bear blinded by mosquitoes. Despite the bear's blindness and rage, it proved a hard fight, arousing the last remnants of Buck's ferocity. Two days later, returning to his kill, he found a dozen wolverines fighting over the spoil. He scattered them like chaff, leaving two behind to settle their dispute.

The blood-lust within him intensified. He became a killer, a predator living solely by virtue of his own strength and prowess, thriving in a hostile environment where only the strong survive. This new existence bred a profound pride, radiating through his muscles and posture. His coat seemed more glorious than ever, barring the stray brown on his muzzle and the white streak down his chest. Without these markings, he might have been mistaken for a giant wolf, larger than any of his kind. Possessed of wolf cunning, shepherd intelligence, and the wisdom of his St. Bernard lineage, combined with the hard school of experience, he was a formidable creature. Every muscle snapped with vitality, keying his nerves to exquisite sensitivity. He perceived, determined, and acted in a fraction of a second, responding to threats or opportunities with the speed of steel springs. Life streamed through him in a splendid flood, ecstatic and rampant.

"Never was there such a dog," John Thornton remarked one day as the partners watched Buck march out of camp. "When he was made, the mould was broke," agreed Pete. Hans nodded in affirmation.

They saw him emerge, but missed the instantaneous transformation that occurred the moment he stepped into the forest's secrecy. He no longer marched; he stole softly, moving like a shadow, crawling on his belly like a snake, striking with lethal precision. He could snatch a ptarmigan from its nest, kill a sleeping rabbit, or snap a fleeing chipmunk in mid-air. Fish in open pools and beavers mending dams were no match for him. He killed to eat, preferring his own kills, and a lurking humor guided his deeds. He delighted in stalking squirrels, allowing them to escape only when they were almost caught, sending them chattering in terror to the treetops.
