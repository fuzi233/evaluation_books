"Welcome bearer of the olive branch," the crowd murmured, a tide of eager faces pressing against the path. Tito accepted the accolade with the practiced indifference of a man who knows his value lies elsewhere. This initial triumph, however, was merely the prologue to a series of premeditated commissions. He halted before the Palazzo Vecchio, the weight of the city's governance pressing against the stone facade, and waited for the return of the Ten, the oligarchy managing external affairs and war. Their arrival was necessary to deposit the fruits of his clandestine mission to Pisa—a reconnaissance report designed to pave the way for the formal embassy led by Bernardo Rucellai, aimed at securing a peace with Emperor Maximilian and the League.

Tito's aptitude for diplomacy was undeniable. As he delivered his findings with surgical precision, detailing the intricacies of his interviews, Bernardo del Nero, one of the Ten, suppressed a visible flicker of admiration. Yet, this professional respect was poisoned by a deeper, personal animosity. Since the controversial sale of the library, Bernardo's disdain for Tito had calcified. Romola had remained silent regarding the transaction, interpreting it as a tacit command for Bernardo to avoid the subject, but the old man read her silence as a testament to a profound, blighting grief. His sharp eyes detected further signs of domestic unhappiness, a quiet unraveling in the marriage.

*Ah,* Bernardo thought, the cynicism of age settling over him. *That explains her turn toward Fra Girolamo and the Piagnoni.* In his view, unhappy women without offspring inevitably drift into folly or a fanatical religion that burdens them with the entirety of heaven's work. He viewed Romola's education in Latin and Greek as a cruel error, leaving her intellectually developed but emotionally stunted, akin to a girl who had spent her entire childhood pricking her fingers with needles. Her husband, a mere tool with a smooth handle, was destined to meet a bloody end at the hands of the Tornabuonis and similar factions. *Solco torto, sacco dritto,* he muttered internally. *Many a full sack comes from a crooked furrow; he who commands only honest men pays little in wages.*

Despite this internal monologue, Bernardo maintained a rigid neutrality. With Romola's privacy sacrosanct, he possessed no concrete grounds to oppose Tito, save for the fact that Tito was a Greek and personally disliked. Tito's duplicity—feigning loyalty to the popular government while harboring Medicean sympathies—was a trait shared by half the faction, though Tito executed it with superior skill. Consequently, Bernardo treated him with cold, distant respect, a dynamic Tito mirrored with scrupulous propriety. The prevailing sentiment in Florence held that any service rendered to Romola's husband was, by extension, a tribute to her godfather.

Once the official duties at the Old Palace concluded, Tito felt the need to shed the burden of his thoughts and the contents of his *scarsella*. Exhausted and parched from days of riding, he avoided returning home directly. Opportunities for action must be seized with such subtlety that they appeared accidental. He wandered aimlessly toward the Piazza del Duomo. The religious procession had ended, yet the bells continued to toll, and the populace moved restlessly, craving a tangible outlet for their jubilation. While the new discipline demanded Christian restraint, a latent, violent impulse stirred among the youths shouting "Viva Gesu!"—a desire to throw stones in thanks to the divine.

As he traversed the square, Tito could not evade recognition as the herald of peace. He dismissed the intrusive ovation, deflecting eager questions by claiming the true messenger from Leghorn, Meo di Sasso, was arriving at the Porta San Frediano. He spoke truths he did not possess, masking his movements with plausible deniability. Freeing himself from these importunities, he entered the Piazza del Duomo, casting a careless gaze that was actually a tactical scan for a specific opportunity. The termination of the procession meant an unusual density of loiterers near Nello's shop, precisely where he needed to be.

Near the north gates of the Baptistery, a group clustered against the rails. Tito feigned indifference until his eye caught the tallest figure, whom he greeted with a perfunctory nod and a toss of his *becchetto* over his shoulder. The man was Dolfo Spini, a broad-shouldered giant whose attire screamed excess. He wore a richly embroidered tunic and linen in the latest French style, a sword and poniard hanging prominently from his belt. His hat, adorned with a defiant red plume, mocked the somber austerity imposed by the Piagnoni. Youth and coarseness defined him; his eyes held a rude, desecrating stare that grated on the nerves of the refined.

Dolfo was the leader of the *Compagnacci*, the Evil Companions—an armed band of dissolute aristocrats, enemies of the Medici and the popular government, but most vitriolic foes of Savonarola. Organized as champions of indulgence against the reformist pietists who threatened to render the world sterile, Dolfo had recently proclaimed Florence doomed by the Frate's blindness. He argued that salvation lay solely in joining the League and expelling the friar to Rome. Thus, his current laughter at the news of foreign aid was suspect; it rang hollow, a mask for a heart burning with resentment.

Leaning against the doorpost amidst the group was Niccolò Machiavelli, a close-shaven, keen-eyed youth who had already dissected the petty egos of the room.

"Messer Dolfo's head is more of a pumpkin than I anticipated," Machiavelli remarked, measuring the man's stupidity by the delusions he trusted. "Your dullest creature is he who grins after having his shins kicked. If I were slightly less sharp, I would pretend to adore this Melema, who holds a secretaryship perfectly suited to my tastes—as if poorly paid Latin could love better-paid Latin! Melema, you are a pestiferous genius, very much in my camp. I regret hearing of your additional stroke of luck today."

"Questionable luck, Niccolò," Tito replied, placing a friendly hand on Machiavelli's shoulder. "I have gained nothing but being grabbed and suffocated by wool-combers, while I ride as battered as a letter-carrier from Bologna."

"Ah! Then you require a touch of my art, Messer Oratore," Nello interjected, stepping forward as the circle opened. "I see yesterday's stubble on your chin. Come, consign yourself to the priest of the Muses. Sandro, bring the lather!"

"In truth, Nello, that is my sole desire," Tito said, sitting down. "Hence my detour to your shop rather than heading home immediately after the Palazzo."

"It is unfitting to present yourself to Madonna Romola with a rusted chin and tangled hair," Nello chided gently. "Nothing coarse should approach the Florentine lily. Though I see her wandering like a sunbeam among the rags lining our corners—or perhaps a moonbeam now. Yesterday, she looked as pale and worn as the fainting Madonna of Fra Giovanni. You must attend to her, my learned friend; she observes too many fasts and vigils in your absence."

Tito sighed, a gesture of melancholy resignation. "Too true, Nello. She starves herself daily during this famine. But what can a husband do? His influence is powerless against the Frate's fire."

"As are all other influences, including that of the Holy Father," added Domenico Cennini, who had joined them. "Have you gleaned anything from Pisa regarding the winds blowing in Rome, Melema?"

"Secrets of the council chamber, Messer Domenico!" Tito smiled, spreading his palms in mock humility. "An envoy must remain as mute as a confessor."

"Certainly," Cennini agreed. "My belief is that if His Holiness drives Fra Girolamo to desperation, the Frate will mobilize heaven and earth to convene a General Council—and succeed. I would not be displeased, though I am no Piagnone."

"With deference to your wisdom, Messer Domenico," Machiavelli countered, his tone cool and analytical, "I must dissent. Not from your wish for a council to reform the Church, but from your assumption that the Frate will yield to the Pope. The Frate's strategy is impossible. Had he limited himself to preaching against Roman vices and prophesying vague Italian scourges, Pope Alexander would have tolerated him indefinitely. Spiritual blasts do not topple walls. But the Frate seeks to be more than a trumpet; he wishes to be a lever, and he *is* one. He aims to propagate Christ's doctrine by sustaining the popular government in Florence, whereas the Pope, according to reliable sources, holds opposing private views."

"Then Florence stands with the Frate," Cennini declared fervently. "Though I would prefer he cease his prophecies, if our right to govern ourselves is threatened, I shall vote to resist Pope Alexander the Sixth, just as our ancestors resisted Gregory the Eleventh."
