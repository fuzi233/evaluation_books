Newland Archer found himself in a peculiar and humiliating predicament. It was mortifying that the box commanding the exclusive attention of the male guests in New York was occupied by his fiancée, flanked by her mother and aunt. For a fleeting moment, he failed to recognize the lady in the empire-waisted gown, unable to fathom why her presence sparked such a ripple of curiosity among the cognoscenti. Then, clarity struck, followed by a sudden surge of indignant pride.

Certainly, no one would ever suspect the Mingotts of playing such a game! Yet, they had. The hushed whispers circulating behind him confirmed beyond doubt that the young woman was May Welland's cousin—the one invariably dismissed within the family as "poor Ellen Olenska." Newland knew she had arrived from Europe just days prior; he had even received a note from Miss Welland, devoid of reproach, confirming that she had visited Ellen, who was currently residing with the formidable old Mrs. Mingott. While Newland held family loyalty in the highest esteem—admiring the Mingotts' fierce defense of their few black sheep—he drew a sharp line between private charity and public spectacle. To welcome Countess Olenska into the domestic fold was one thing; to parade her before the entire opera house, specifically in the box of the girl whose union with Newland Archer was imminent, was quite another. In truth, he shared the sentiments of Old Sillerton Jackson: the Mingotts simply wouldn't dare attempt something so audacious.

Of course, Newland knew that within the bounds of Fifth Avenue, if anyone possessed the temerity to defy convention, it was Old Mrs. Manson Mingott, the matriarch of the lineage. He had long admired her towering stature, despite her origins as Catherine Spicer of Staten Island—a girl of mysterious parentage, lacking both fortune and standing sufficient to erase those shadows. Yet, she had allied herself with the head of the wealthy Mingott line, wedded two daughters to "foreigners"—an Italian marquis and an English banker—and cemented her legacy of audacity by constructing a vast mansion of pale cream-colored stone. In an era where brown sandstone was the unfailing standard for afternoon wear, her house stood isolated in an impenetrable wilderness near Central Park, a physical testament to her moral fortitude.

Throned within this sanctuary, amidst pre-Revolutionary furnishings and mementoes of the Tuileries under Louis Napoleon, she sat as calmly as if residing above Thirty-fourth Street or opening French windows like doors rather than sash windows were utterly normal. Every soul, including Mr. Sillerton Jackson, concurred that Old Catherine lacked conventional beauty—a divine gift that, in New York's eyes, legitimized every triumph and absolved numerous shortcomings. The uncharitable whispered that she resembled her imperial namesake, conquering success through iron will, a hardened heart, and a haughty impudence sanctified by the profound decency and dignity of her private life. Her husband, Mr. Manson Mingott, had perished at twenty-eight, leaving a fortune "tied up" with caution born of the Spicer family's notorious distrust. But his bold widow forged ahead fearlessly, mixing freely in international circles, marrying off her daughters to dubious aristocrats, socializing with dukes and ambassadors, associating intimately with papists, entertaining opera singers, and befriending Mme. Taglioni. And yet, as Sillerton Jackson was quick to declare, not a whisper tainted her reputation; the sole distinction lay in her unblemished conduct compared to the earlier Catherine.

Though Old Mrs. Mingott had successfully untied her husband's estate and lived in opulence for half a century, the ghosts of her early poverty haunted her. She practiced an excessive thriftiness, ensuring that every garment or piece of furniture purchased was of the utmost quality, yet refusing to lavish funds on the ephemeral pleasures of the table. Consequently, her fare mirrored that of the Archers—plain and unadorned—and her wines offered no redemption. Her relatives believed this culinary penury tarnished the Mingott name, traditionally synonymous with gastronomic excellence, prompting her son Lovell to hire the city's finest chef in an attempt to restore the family honor. To these protests, the old lady laughed lightly: "What's the use of employing two good cooks in one family when I've married the girls and can't enjoy sauces?"

As Newland Archer contemplated these matters, he cast his gaze back toward the Mingott box. He observed that Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law faced their semicircle of critics with the *aplomb* instilled in them by Old Catherine, save for May Welland, whose cheeks flushed slightly—perhaps because she sensed his scrutiny—and who clearly grasped the gravity of the moment. As for the source of the uproar, she sat gracefully in her corner, eyes fixed upon the stage, revealing a hint of shoulder and bosom that New York usually expected to remain concealed from view on ladies intent on passing unnoticed.

To Newland Archer, nothing seemed more abhorrent than an offense against "Taste," that distant deity for whom "Form" served merely as a visible vicar. Countess Olenska's pale, serious countenance seemed appropriate for her tragic circumstances, yet the manner in which her dress, devoid of a tucker, draped away from her slender shoulders disturbed and troubled him deeply. He loathed the idea of exposing May Welland to the influence of a young woman so indifferent to the dictates of Taste.

Then, he heard a younger voice behind him inquire, "After all, just *what* happened?"
"Well," replied a companion, "she left him. Nobody dares deny that."
"He's an awful brute, isn't he?" pressed a young man named Thorley, eager to champion the lady's cause.
"The very worst," interjected Lawrence Lefferts with authority. "I knew him at Nice. A half-paralyzed white sneerer, rather handsome in head, but with eyes full of lashes. When he wasn't surrounded by women, he was collecting china. He pays any price for either."
A murmur of laughter rippled through the crowd, and Thorley ventured, "Well, then..."
"Then she bolted with his secretary."
"Oh, I see." Thorley's face fell.
"It didn't endure long, however. I heard of her a few months later, living alone in Venice. I believe Lovell Mingott traveled abroad to find her; he claimed she was desolately wretched. That is acceptable—but parading her at the Opera is a different matter."
"Perhaps," suggested young Thorley tentatively, "she is too miserable to remain at home."
This elicited an irreverent chuckle, causing the youth to blush deeply as he attempted to feign innocence regarding what the astute termed a "double entendre."
"Well—it is strange to have brought Miss Welland, anyhow," someone murmured softly, casting a sidelong glance at Archer.
"Oh, that is merely part of the campaign: Granny's orders, surely," Lefferts laughed. "When the old lady undertakes a venture, she executes it thoroughly."

As the curtain descended and the box erupted in a general stir, Newland Archer felt an irresistible urge to take decisive action. The desire to be the first to step into Mrs. Mingott's box, to announce publicly his engagement to May Welland, and to stand united against whatever tribulations her cousin's unconventional existence might impose, suddenly silenced all his scruples and hesitations. Hurried through the crimson corridors to the farther side of the theater, he entered the box. His eyes met Miss Welland's, and he realized instantly that she comprehended his motive, though the rigid family dignity they both revered prevented her from voicing it. In their world, lives were lived in an atmosphere of faint implications and pale delicacies; the silent understanding between them, needing no words, bridged a distance deeper than any explanation could have achieved. Her eyes spoke, *"You see why Mother brought me,"* and his replied, *"I would not, for the world, have had you absent."*

"My niece, Countess Olenska?" Mrs. Welland asked as she extended her hand to her future son-in-law. Archer bowed respectfully, refraining from a handshake as per custom when introducing oneself to a lady. Ellen Olenska inclined her head slightly, her pale-gloved hands clasped over a massive fan of eagle feathers. After greeting Mrs. Lovell Mingott, a stately blonde in creaking satin, Archer sat beside his betrothed. In a low tone, he asked, "I trust you have informed Madame Olenska that we are engaged? I wish everyone to know—I want you to allow me to announce it tonight at the ball."

Miss Welland's face bloomed with the radiance of dawn, her eyes shining brightly. "If you can persuade Mother," she said, "but why alter what is already settled?" He offered no verbal response, trusting only the glint in his eyes, to which she smiled with renewed confidence. "Tell my cousin yourself," she urged. "I grant you permission. She remarked that she used to play with you when you were children."

She moved aside, pushing back her chair with deliberate promptness and a touch of ostentation, ensuring the entire house witnessed his gesture. Archer seated himself at Countess Olenska's side.

"We *did* play together, did we not?" she asked, turning grave eyes toward him. "You were a horrid boy, and you kissed me once behind a door; but it was your cousin Vandie Newland, who never so much as glanced at me, that I truly loved."
