Jorian Ketel pressed directly to Margaret's chamber, his heart hammering against his ribs with the frantic rhythm of a man who had run through the dark streets of a city holding his breath. He expected to find chaos, perhaps the man he sought fleeing or hiding in the shadows. Instead, he found a tableau of such stillness that the candle in his hand trembled, casting dancing, ghostly shadows against the walls.

There, in the center of the room, lay Gerard. He was pale as moonlight, motionless as a fallen statue, his head cradled in Margaret's lap. She knelt over him, rigid and silent, her eyes wide and dilated yet utterly glazed, seeing nothing of the world around her, hearing nothing of the man standing in the doorway. To her, the fire, the noise, the very air of the earth had ceased to exist; there was only the white, fragile face resting in her hands.

Jorian stood transfixed, awe-struck by the sheer weight of her despair. "Where was he, then, all this time?" he whispered, the question hanging heavy in the silence.

Margaret did not answer. She was lost in a trance of grief. Jorian stepped forward, his gaze falling on the empty chest that had once held the stolen goods. A cold realization settled over him, matching the chill in the room. He looked back at the girl, her body frozen in a posture of mute agony. "This is a sorry sight," he murmured, his voice thick with pity. "A black night's work, all for a few skins. She would have been safer with us. Poor wench, she is past answering me."

He hesitated, then moved to test the boy's condition. Taking down a small, round mirror from the wall, no larger than his own palm, he pressed it gently to Gerard's lips and nostrils, holding it steady. He watched the glass intently. When he withdrew it, the surface remained dull, clouded with condensation.

"There is life in him!" Jorian shouted to himself, the words bursting forth like a release of tension.

The sound struck Margaret like a physical blow. Though she could only mutter the words, the effect was instantaneous. As if a spell had broken upon a statue, she sprang to her feet, her form filling with sudden, fierce passion. She threw her arms around Jorian's neck, clutching him with an intensity that bordered on violence.

"Oh, bless the tongue that tells me so!" she cried, pressing her face against his rough coat, clinging to him as if he were the only anchor in a drowning sea.

"Easy now," Jorian said, trying to calm her trembling frame. "Let us lay him warm." In a moment, he lifted Gerard, placing him carefully upon the bedclothes. From his belt, he drew a flask and poured a generous measure of Schiedamze into his hand, flinging the pungent liquor sharply onto Gerard's face. The strong spirit seemed to wake the boy's dormant senses; he gave a faint, ragged sigh. It was a sound so full of relief that it brought fresh tears to Jorian's eyes. Margaret rushed forward, but stopped abruptly, quivering with fear that her touch might crush the fragile spark of life she had just witnessed. She had lost all confidence in her own strength.

"That is right—leave him alone," Jorian instructed gently. "Do not cuddle him as you did me, or you will drive his breath back. Let him rest; he is sure to come. He is not an old man."

Gerard sighed deeply, and a faint streak of color returned to his lips. Jorian turned toward the door, eager to escape the scene before suspicion could take root. Hardly had he reached the threshold when his legs were seized from behind.

It was Margaret. She curled around his knees like a desperate serpent, kissing his hand and fawning upon him with a mixture of gratitude and terror. "You won't tell? You have saved his life; you have not the heart to thrust him back into his grave, to undo your own good work?"

"No, no!" Jorian reassured her, his tone firm yet kind. "It is not the first time I have done you both a good turn; I told you in the church where we were to take him. Besides, what is Dierich Brower to me? I'll see him hanged ere I'll tell him. But I wish you'd tell me where the parchments are! There are a hundred crowns offered for them. That would be a good windfall for my Joan and the children, you know."

"They shall have those hundred crowns," Margaret promised, her voice trembling but resolute.

"What! Are the things in the house?" Jorian asked eagerly.

"No; but I know where they are. By God and St. Bavon, I swear you shall have them tomorrow. Come to me for them when you will, but come alone."

"I were made else," Jorian thought, his greed momentarily outweighing his caution. "What! Share the hundred crowns with Dirk Brower? And now may my bones rot in my skin if I let a soul know the poor boy is here."

He bolted from the room, terrified that any further delay might excite suspicion and draw the authorities back to the scene. Behind him, Margaret knelt, quivering from head to foot, praying beside Gerard and for Gerard.

Outside, the group regrouped. "What is to do?" Dierich Brower asked, his brow furrowed. "Why, we have scared the girl out of her wits. She was in a kind of fit."

"We had better all go and doctor her, then," someone suggested.

"Oh, yes! And frighten her into the churchyard," Jorian replied with his usual off-hand manner, which served to disarm all suspicion. "Her father is a doctor, and I have roused him to bring her round. Let us see the fire, will ye?"

Soon, the party agreed that the kitchen of the "Three Kings" was far warmer than Peter's house, and they departed, having first untied Martin. At the door, Dierich Brower chuckled. "Take note, mate, that I was right, and the burgomaster wrong. I said we should be too late to catch him, and we were too late."

Thus, Gerard, in one terrible night, had grazed the edge of the prison and the grave. How had he escaped? Not by cunningly contrived hiding places, nor by Margaret's ready wit, but by a good impulse in one of his captors, by the fragment of humanity left in a reckless fellow's heart, aided by a desire for gain. Human motives were so mixed and seemingly incongruous, and our shrewdest counsels so shortsighted.

Those whose moderate natures or gentle fates keep them from the fierce extremes of joy and anguish that human nature is capable of, are perhaps the best and certainly the happiest of mankind. But to such readers, I should try in vain to convey the unspeakable bliss that now settled upon these persecuted lovers. Even to those who have known great joy and suffered greatly, my feeble art can present but a pale reflection of Margaret's and Gerard's ecstasy.

To sit and watch a beloved face return from the grave to the world, to health and beauty, by swift gradations; to see the roses return to the loved cheek, love's glance to the loved eye, and his words to the loved mouth—this was Margaret's joy, a happiness to balance years of sorrow. For Gerard, it was to awake from a trance and find his head pillowed on Margaret's arm; to hear the woman he adored murmur new words of eloquent love, and to be showered with tears, tender kisses, and caresses. He never knew, until this sweet moment, how ardently, how tenderly she loved him. He thanked his enemies. They wreathed their arms sweetly around each other, and trouble and danger seemed a world, an age behind them. They called each other husband and wife. Were they not solemnly betrothed? Had they not stood before the altar together? Was not the blessing of Holy Church upon their union—and her curse on all who would part them?

Yet, as no woman's nerves can bear with impunity so terrible a strain, presently Margaret turned faint and sank on Gerard's shoulder, smiling feebly but quite unstrung. Gerard grew anxious and sought to seek assistance, but she held him with a gentle grasp, imploring him not to leave her for a moment.

"While I can lay my hand on you, I feel you are safe, not else," she whispered. "Foolish Gerard! Nothing ails me. I am weak, dearest, but happy, oh! so happy!"

Then it was Gerard's turn to support that dear head, with its great waves of hair flowing loose over him, to nurse her and soothe her, quivering on his bosom with soft encouraging words and murmurs of love. Sweetest of all her charms was a woman's weakness to a manly heart.

Poor things! They were happy. Tomorrow they must part, but that was nothing to them now. They had seen Death, and all other troubles seemed light as air. While there is life, there is hope; while there is hope, there is joy. Separation for a year or two, what was it to them, who were so young and had caught a glimpse of the grave? The future was bright, the present was Heaven; so passed the blissful hours.

Alas! Their innocence ran other risks besides the prison and the grave. They were in most danger from their own hearts and their inexperience, now that visible danger had vanished.
