Shirley's morning was unusually bright, a stark contrast to the dim, waning autumn that draped the English countryside. She descended the stairs in one of her best moods, her spirit radiating a light that seemed to defy the heavy, overcast sky outside.

"Who will take a walk with me?" she asked, her voice ringing with a rare, infectious joy after breakfast. "Isabella and Gertrude, will you?"

Such an invitation from Miss Keeldar was so uncommon that her cousins hesitated, unsure if they were being summoned for a genuine outing or merely tolerated for the sake of propriety. However, their mother's acquiescence sealed the deal. Fetching their bonnets, the trio stepped out into the damp air.

Usually, Miss Keeldar preferred the company of few ladies, finding true warmth only in the presence of Mrs. Pryor and Caroline Helstone. With her cousins, she was typically civil but distant, offering little more than polite conversation on ordinary themes. Yet, today, her spirit sparkled with an extraordinary vitality. She managed to entertain the Misses Sympson, infusing even the most mundane topics with a captivating interest.

What fueled this sudden effervescence? The cause lay entirely within herself. The day offered no solace; the woods were dull and brown, the atmosphere thick and oppressive. Yet, in Shirley's heart, the azure skies of Italy seemed to bloom, and the fervor of a southern sun warmed her gray English eyes.

As they approached Fieldhead on their return, a summons to the foreman, John, detained Shirley. Twenty minutes passed before she rejoined her cousins. During this interval, she had spoken briefly to John, then lingered at the gate. By the time lunch was announced, she had already retreated upstairs.

"Where is Shirley?" Isabella asked, noting her cousin's absence. "She said she was hungry."

An hour later, finding Shirley still in her chamber, one of the cousins ventured inside. She discovered Miss Keeldar sitting at the foot of the bed, her head resting in her hand, her complexion pale and her expression deeply thoughtful, bordering on sadness.

"Are you ill?" the cousin asked gently.

"A little sick," Shirley replied.

The transformation was startling. Just two hours prior, she had been vibrant; now, she seemed weighed down by an invisible burden. Yet, this change, born in a fleeting ten minutes, vanished as quickly as a summer cloud. At dinner, she chatted as usual, remaining with her friends throughout the evening. When questioned again about her health, she declared herself fully recovered, dismissing the episode as a mere passing faintness.

However, the shadow did not dissipate. The following day, and the week after, a peculiar quietude settled over Shirley. Her movements, her voice, and her gaze carried a new weight. The alteration was subtle enough to avoid constant inquiry, yet undeniable. Soon, it became clear that acknowledging this change annoyed her. She shrank from remarks, and if pressed, she repelled them with her characteristic hauteur.

"I am not ill," she would state decisively.

"Has something troubled your mind?"

She scoffed at the notion. "What do you mean by spirits? I have no spirits, black or white, to be affected."

"Something must be the matter; you are so altered."

"I have a right to alter at my ease," she retorted. "If I choose to grow plain, why should others fret?"

Peremptory requests to be left alone followed. When she could not maintain this facade, she would force a gaiety that seemed indignant in its artificiality. Alone, she lashed out at herself with harsh epithets—calling herself a fool, a coward, a poltroon. "Tremble in secret," she would command herself. "Hide your weakness."

To conceal her turmoil, she became resolutely lively in company. When exhaustion forced her to retreat, she sought not the solitude of her room, but the wilder freedom of the outdoors. Mounted on her mare, Zoë, she took long, solitary rides. Her uncle disapproved but dared not speak, for facing Shirley's anger was unpleasant even in her healthiest moments; now, with her face thin and her large eye hollow, her darkening countenance was both alarming and touching.

To strangers who commented on her appearance, she offered a single, firm reply: "I am perfectly well; I have not an ailment." Indeed, she possessed a strange resilience, enduring exposure to wind and rain on Stilbro' Moor, accompanied by Tartar, her wolf-like horse.

Gossips, ever-watchful, noted that instead of turning back at Rushedge, she rode all the way to the town. It was ascertained that she alighted at the door of Mr. Pearson Hall, a solicitor related to the vicar of Nunnely and the Keeldar family's agent for generations. Rumors swirled: some claimed she was involved in disastrous business speculations regarding Hollow's Mill and was forced to mortgage her land; others conjectured she was preparing for marriage.

In the schoolroom, Mr. Moore and Henry Sympson sat together. The tutor waited for a lesson, but Henry seemed distracted.

"Henry, make haste. The afternoon is getting on."

"Is it, sir?"

"Certainly. Are you nearly ready with that lesson?"

"No."

"Not nearly ready?"

"I have not construed a line."

Mr. Moore looked up, sensing a peculiar tone in the boy's voice. "The task presents no difficulties, Henry. Bring them to me. We will work together."

"Mr. Moore, I can do no work."

"My boy, you are ill."

"Sir, I am not worse in bodily health than usual, but my heart is full."

"Shut the book. Come hither, Harry. Come to the fireside."

Harry limped forward, his lips quivering, his eyes brimming with tears. He laid his crutch aside and wept.

"This distress is not occasioned by physical pain," Moore observed. "You have a grief; tell it to me."

"Sir, I have such a grief as I never had before. I wish it could be relieved."

"Who knows but that talking it over may help? What is the cause? Whom does it concern?"

"The cause, sir, is Shirley; it concerns Shirley."

"Does she seem changed?"

"All who know her think so," Henry replied. "You too, Mr. Moore."

"Not seriously," Moore said, seeing no grave alteration. "Besides, her own word must go for something: she says she is well."

"There it is, sir. As long as she maintained she was well, I believed her. When I was sad out of her sight, I soon recovered spirits in her presence. Now..."

"Well, Harry, now. Has she said anything to you?"

"She told me quite cheerfully that she has just made her will."

The tutor and pupil fell silent.

"She told you that?" Moore asked after a pause.

"She said I was the only person besides her solicitor, Mr. Hall, Mr. Helstone, and Mr. Yorke who knew anything about it. She wished specially to explain its provisions to me."

Henry recounted Shirley's words with trembling emotion. She spoke of him as her "darling," her "treasure," declaring that if she died before him, she intended to leave her vast fortune—not to him, for he was already rich, but to a "good man" who was gentle, brave, and merciful. She described this man as possessing the secret of religion pure and undefiled, one who cared for the fatherless and widows.

She had also bequeathed Fieldhead and its contents to Henry, refusing to take the old family place from her own blood. She left her sisters a modest sum, though she confessed she loved them less than one lock of Henry's fair hair.

"And now," Henry quoted, "in case anything should happen, you will know what to say to Malice when she comes whispering hard things in your ear... She said she did love me, Harry; that no sister could have loved you better."

Henry wept as he recalled her voice, her look, and her desperate plea that he lay his coffin by Shirley's side if she should pass him first.

Moore listened with a weighty calm that contrasted sharply with the boy's perturbed enthusiasm. "You are wrong, both of you—you harm each other. If youth once falls under the influence of a shadowy terror, it imagines there will never be full sunlight again."

Henry insisted that Shirley was wasting away, her hands and cheeks growing thin. He recalled how she had recently walked through a violent storm, telling him, "It would be too much good luck for me. I don't know, Harry, but the best thing that could happen to me would be to take a good cold and fever, and so pass off like other Christians."

"Reckless indeed!" Moore exclaimed. "Go and find out where she is. Request her to come here a minute."

Henry hesitated. "Do not deliver the message formally. Word it as an ordinary summons."

"Yes, sir."

Henry departed. Left alone, Mr. Moore reflected on his own feelings toward Shirley. He admitted to a strange, secret ecstasy that sometimes stole through his veins, a temptation to forget his role as a master and submit to her influence. Yet, he resolved to maintain his dignity, to remain the tutor and not the servant, even as his heart beat fast at the thought of her.

When Henry returned empty-handed, the door opened, and Miss Keeldar entered. She had not been riding; she wore her neat indoor dress and silk apron, her air devoid of the usual *crânerie*. Instead, a soft, youthful shyness mantled her cheek.

"Did you want me, sir?" she asked.

"I ventured to send for you," Moore replied, inviting her to sit. "The subject I would broach is one of some moment. It arises from a conversation with Henry. The boy is unhappy about your health; all your friends are unhappy on that subject. It is of your health I would speak."

"I am quite well," she said briefly.

"Yet changed."

"That matters to none but myself. We all change."

Moore pressed her, asking if she slept as she used to, if she had her appetite. She denied illness for each symptom. Then, he produced a small ring from his watch-chain, her mother's, which she had often worn. He asked her to try it on her finger.

She permitted the test. The ring slipped easily from her wasted hand.

"Not only have you lost sleep, appetite, and flesh," Moore proceeded, "but your spirits are always at ebb. Besides, there is a nervous alarm in your eye, a nervous disquiet in your manner."

"Mr. Moore, we will pause here. You have exactly hit it. I am nervous. Now, talk of something else."

"When it is exhausted; not till then. Nervous alarms should always be communicated, that they may be dissipated. I believe confession, in your case, would be half equivalent to cure."

"No," Shirley said abruptly. "I am afraid it is not."

She rested her head on her hand, serious and imploring. "I had better tell you than my aunt or cousins. They would make such a bustle, and it is that very bustle I dread. You can bear a little shock—eh?"

"A great one, if necessary."

"I could not come to you sooner. I seemed to have no title to trouble you. I wanted to keep it to myself."

She loosened a bracelet from her wrist, rolled up her sleeve, and revealed a mark on her arm—a deep, healed indentation, something between a burn and a cut.

"I would not show that to any one in Briarfield but you," she said. "On account of that little mark, I am obliged to look forward to a possibility that has its terrors."

Moore's pulse quickened. "Tell me the tale."

She recounted walking alone in the lane after her cousins had returned home. She had heard a panting sound and found Phœbe, one of Mr. Sam Wynne's pointers, running wildly, bruised and beaten. The dog had bitten her arm, drawing blood, before fleeing. Mr. Wynne's keeper had informed her that Phœbe was "raging mad" and ordered her to chain up her own horse and stay indoors.

Terrified, Shirley had fled to the laundry, taken a hot iron from the fire, and cauterized the wound herself before ascending to her room.

"And you told no one, sought no help, no cure?" Moore asked.

"I got as far as the schoolroom door; there my courage failed. I preferred to cushion the matter."

"Why? What can I demand better in this world than to be of use to you?"

"I had no claim."

"Monstrous! And you did nothing?"

"Yes. I walked straight into the laundry... I bored it well in. It cauterized the little wound."

Moore noted her composure despite her misery. "There was calm in your person. I remember listening the whole time we sat at luncheon to hear if you moved in the room above. All was quiet."

"I was sitting at the foot of the bed, wishing Phœbe had not bitten me."

"And alone. You like solitude."

"Pardon me."

"You disdain sympathy."

"Do I, Mr. Moore?"

"With your powerful mind you must feel independent of help, of advice, of society."

"So be it, since it pleases you."

She smiled, though a tear fell from her eye.

"If it is not so," Moore asked softly, "how is it, then?"

"I don't know."

"You do know, but you won't speak. All must be locked up in yourself."

"Because it is not worth sharing."

"Because nobody can give the high price you require for your confidence. Nobody is rich enough to purchase it. Nobody has the honour, the intellect, the power you demand in your adviser.
