The tale George Warrington had spun for Madame de Bernstein the night before was, in essence, the same story we have just heard. Yet, the reception differed vastly from the warm applause that greeted him in Hill Street. There, Hetty's wide-eyed wonder, Theo's sympathetic gaze, and their parents' gentle encouragement had fueled his modest eloquence. In contrast, the old lady at the Court End had nodded off repeatedly, only rousing herself to declare the narrative "most interesting" and demand its continuation. George, however, had stumbled through it—hemming, hawing, blushing, and speaking far less fluently than he did for his friendly audience. His aunt, the General, later remarked that his mother's performance during the telling was comparable to a professional tragedienne hired solely to weep in the front boxes, a sentiment that likely influenced our decision to stage George's first public performance at Lord Wrotham's house. We wished him to speak with dignity, free from the cynicism and sleepiness of that elderly listener.

"Very good and most interesting, I am sure, my dear sir," Madame Bernstein purred, lifting three lace-covered fingers to mask a convulsive twitch of her mouth. "And your mother must have been delighted to see you."

George offered a slight shrug and a low bow as his aunt raised her keen, aged eyes to meet his.

"She was delighted," she continued dryly, "and undoubtedly killed the fatted calf. Though I confess, nephew George, I struggle to understand why I say 'calf.' You were never the prodigal son. I might as well address my poor Harry: 'Thou hast been amongst the swine sure enough.' Evil companions have stripped you of both your money and your coat."

"He came to his family in England, madam," George retorted with rising heat, "and his friends were your ladyship's."

"They could not have chosen worse advisers," the old lady declared, tossing her head with theatrical flair. "Had my sister written to me through him, I would have informed her sooner. 'Hey! hey!' she cried last night to her maid while preparing for the rout. 'This young gentleman's mother is half sorry he has returned to life; I can see it in his face. She is half sorry, and I am perfectly furious! Why did he not lie still beneath the tree, and why did that young Florac carry him to the fort?'"

She paused, her mind drifting to the past. "I knew those Floracs in Paris, during the time of Monsieur le Regent. They were of the Floracs of Ivry—a house of no great consequence before Henri IV. His ancestor was the king's favorite. His ancestor—he! he!--his ancestress! Brett! *Entendez-vous?* Give me my card-purse. I dislike the grand airs of this Monsieur George, yet he bears a striking resemblance to his grandfather—the same look, and sometimes the same tones. You recall Colonel Esmond from my youth? This boy possesses his eyes. I suppose I favored the Colonel because he loved me."

Shortly thereafter, having concluded her card party—an amusement she pursued relentlessly regardless of the day of the week or the Sabbath—as long as she possessed the strength to hold trumps or sit upright, the old lady dismissed her two nephews. To the elder, George, she offered a couple of fingers and a stiff, stately curtsey. To Harry, she gave two hands and a kindly pat upon the cheek.

"My poor child, now that thou art disinherited, thou shalt see how differently the world treats thee!" she proclaimed. "In all of London, there is but one wicked, heartless old woman who will treat thee as before. Here is a pocket-book for you, child! Do not lose it at Ranelagh tonight. That suit of yours does not become your brother half so well as it did upon you! You shall present your brother to everyone and pace the room for at least two hours. Were I in your shoes, I would retreat to the Chocolate-House and play as if nothing had occurred. While you are there, your brother may return to dine with me. My Lady Flint serves wretched suppers, and I wish to discuss your mother's letter with him. *Au revoir*, gentlemen!" With that, she departed toward her toilette, her chairmen and flambeaux already waiting at the door.

The gentlemen proceeded to Ranelagh, where only a handful of Harry's acquaintances were present. They strolled the circular walk, encountered Mr. Tom Claypool with his country friends, listened to the music, and drank tea in a private box. Harry, acting as master of ceremonies, introduced his brother to the curiosities of the pleasure palace. George, however, proved even more excited than Harry had been on his initial visit. George possessed a profound love for music, having heard a full orchestra for the first time and witnessed a satisfactory performance of a work by Mr. Handel. It was a testament to Harry's humility and regard for his elder brother that he could respect George's passion for music at a time when fiddling was deemed effeminate and unmanly in England, and when patriotic prints urged Britons to sneer at the frivolous accomplishments of the "Squallinis" and "Monsieurs." Indeed, the spirit of the age had shifted; no longer was ignorance a source of pride, nor was there room for dullness or arrogance. Art had secured her naturalization, living here on terms of near-equality. Even if Mrs. Thrale chose to marry a music-master today, her friends would not shudder; with a good fortune and a competent cook, people would dine with her despite the misalliance, treating Mr. Piozzi with civility.

Following Ranelagh, and adhering to Madame Bernstein's counsel, George returned to her ladyship's house, while Harry presented himself at the club, where gentlemen assembled nightly to sup and then gamble. Naturally, no one alluded to Mr. Warrington's brief disappearance. Mr. Ruff, his former landlord, waited upon him with grave civility, as if no rift had ever existed between them. Mr. Warrington had indeed had his trunks and belongings removed from Bond Street that morning, and he and his brother were now established in separate apartments.

Once supper concluded and the gentlemen prepared to ascend to the macco-table, Harry announced he would not play further. He had already burned his fingers and could afford no further extravagance.

"Why," Mr. Morris remarked in a flippant tone, "you must have won more than you have lost, Mr. Warrington, after all is said and done."

"And of course I do not know my own business as well as you, Mr. Morris," Harry replied sternly, recalling the other's behavior upon hearing of his arrest. "But I have another reason. A few months or days ago, I was heir to a great estate and could afford to lose a little money. Now, thank God, I am heir to nothing." He glanced around, blushing slightly, at the knot of gentlemen lounging at the tables or gathered by the fire.

"How do you mean, Mr. Warrington?" cried Lord March. "Have you lost Virginia, too? Who has won it? I always fancied playing you myself for that stake."

"And grow an improved breed of slaves in the colony," added another.

"The rightful owner has won it. You have heard me speak of my twin elder brother?"

"Who was killed in the affair of Braddock two years ago! Yes. Gracious goodness, my dear sir, I hope in heaven he has not come back to life?"

"He arrived in London two days since. He was a prisoner in a French fort for eighteen months; he escaped only a few months ago and left our house in Virginia shortly after his release."

"You haven't had time to order mourning, I suppose, Mr. Warrington?" asked Mr. Selwyn, with a good-natured simplicity that Harry failed to grasp until his brother explained the jest.

"By Jove, I believe the fellow is absolutely glad of the reappearance of his confounded brother!" Lord March exclaimed as the conversation continued after the young Virginian took his leave.

"These savages practice the simple virtues of affection—they are barely civilized in America yet," yawned Selwyn.

"They love their kin and scalp their enemies," simpered Mr. Walpole. "It is not Christian, but natural. Would you not like to attend a scalping match, George, and watch a fellow skinned alive?"

"A man's elder brother is his natural enemy," Mr. Selwyn stated placidly, arranging his money and counters.

"Torture is like broiled bones and pepper. You would not relish simple hanging afterwards, George!" continued Horry.

"I'm hanged if there is any man in England who would like to see his elder brother alive," Lord March declared.

"No, nor his father either, my lord!" cried Jack Morris.

"First time I ever knew you had one, Jack. Give me counters for five hundred."

"I say, 'tis all mighty fine about dead brothers coming to life again," Jack continued. "Who is to know that this was not a scheme arranged between these two fellows? Here comes a young fellow calling himself the Fortunate Youth, claiming to be a Virginian Prince and the devil knows what else, who enters our society..."

A great laugh erupted at Jack's phrase "our society."

"Who is to know that this was not a cross?" Jack pressed on. "The younger one comes first. He marries an heiress, secures her fortune, and then the elder brother rises! When did this elder brother appear? Only when the younger's scheme was blown and all was lost for him! Who shall tell me that the fellow has not been living in Seven Dials or a cellar, dining on tripe and cow-heel until my younger gentleman was disposed of? Dammy, as gentlemen, I think we ought to take notice: that this Mr. Warrington has taken the most outrageous liberty with the entire club."

"Who put him up? It was March, I think, who put him up?" asked a bystander.

"Yes. But my lord thought he was putting up a very different person. Didn't you, March?"

"Hold your confounded tongue and mind your game!" the nobleman addressed snapped. Yet, Lord March's opinion found few supporters. Many agreed that it was indecorous for Mr. Harry Warrington to have ever believed in his brother's death; that there was something suspicious about the young man's first appearance and subsequent actions; and, in fine, that regarding such foreigners, adventurers, and the like, we ought to exercise extreme caution.

Though he was now out of prison and difficulty, though he held his aunt's liberal donation in his pocket, and though his dearest brother was restored to him—a return Harry never once thought to deplore, contrary to his friends at White's assumption; and though Maria had shown herself in such a favorable light during his misfortune—Harry, when alone, felt far from cheerful. He smoked his pipe of Virginia with a troubled mind. It was not that he was deposed from his principality; the loss of it never vexed him, for he knew his brother would share as he would have done. But after all those struggles and doubts within his own mind, to find himself poor, yet irrevocably bound to his elderly cousin! Yes, she was elderly, there was no doubt about it. When she had descended into that horrible den in Cursitor Street and the tears had washed her rouge away, she looked as old as his mother! Her face was wrinkled and yellow, and thinking of her brought on a qualm similar to the one he had felt when she fell ill in the coach on their road to Tunbridge. What would his mother say when he brought her home? Lord, what battles there would be between them! He would go to live on one of the plantations—the farther from home the better—and have a few negroes, farming as best he might and hunting often. But at Castlewood, or in the home he could make for her, what a life for poor Maria, who had been accustomed to court, cards, balls, and assemblies every night! If he could merely be the overseer of the estates—oh, he would be an honest factor, trying to make amends for his useless life and extravagance of recent days! Five thousand pounds, all his patrimony and the accumulations of his long minority, squandered in six months! He was a beggar, save for dear George's kindness, with nothing left to him but an old wife—a pretty beggar, dressed out in velvet and silver lace, forsooth! The poor lad was arrayed in his best clothes, a fine figure he had made in Europe, and yet what a wretched end he had come to! With all his fine friends at White's and Newmarket, with all his extravagance, had he been happy a single day since arriving in Europe? Yes, three days, four days, yesterday evening, when he had been with dear dear Mrs. Lambert, those affectionate kind girls, and that brave good Colonel. And the Colonel was right when he rebuked him for his spendthrift follies; he had been a brute to be angry as he had been, and God bless them all for their generous exertions in his behalf! Such were the thoughts Harry poured into his pipe, smoking them while he waited for his brother's return from Madame Bernstein.
