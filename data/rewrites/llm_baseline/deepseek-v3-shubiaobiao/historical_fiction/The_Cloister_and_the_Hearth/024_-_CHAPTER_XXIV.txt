Gerard trudged wearily along the muddy road, the weight of his journey pressing upon him. The afternoon sun slanted through the trees, casting long shadows that seemed to stretch endlessly before him. As he rounded a bend, he came upon an odd sight: two men in sober attire lounged lazily against either side of a horse, while its rider, clad in a gaudy green jerkin and hose, lay sprawled face-down in the dirt like a sunbathing lizard. His flaming yellow velvet cloak had been draped carefully over the horse’s back, as though the beast were the one in need of warmth.

Gerard paused. “Is something amiss?” he asked.

One of the servants glanced up, unperturbed. “Naught that I know of.”

“But your master—he lies like a dead man. Do you not think to lift him from the ground?”

The man chuckled. “The earth’s the best cure for his ailment. A hard bed sobers a man quicker than a soft one. Isn’t that so, Ulric?”

“Aye,” the other agreed, eyeing Gerard with sudden suspicion. “Who are you, stranger, that you don’t know the Count is ever drunk by this hour?”

Gerard flushed. “I am a traveler, and new to these parts.”

Ulric smirked. “Then pay us for the lesson—trinkgeld for our wisdom.”

Gerard hesitated but fished out a few coins, if only to escape further scrutiny. As he handed them over, he gestured to the cloak. “Why lay the man’s mantle on the horse?”

The servants, now emboldened by his coin, launched into competing theories—one claiming the Count would rage if his steed caught cold, the other insisting a horse, being a milksop water-drinker, needed warmth more than its ale-soaked master. Their debate grew heated, then violent, until cuffs were exchanged over the horse’s mane. The beast, startled, reared back—and planted a hoof squarely on the prone nobleman.

The Count awoke with a howl, sword flashing from its sheath. The servants fled, the horse bolted, and the nobleman staggered after them, cursing wildly, his blade glinting as he caromed off the hedgerows in drunken pursuit.

Gerard watched the chaos fade down the lane, then turned southward, his purse lighter but his spirits no brighter.

---

Dusk fell swiftly, and with it came rain—a deluge that soaked Gerard to the bone. The road ahead vanished into a wall of darkness, and soon he found himself groping through a dense forest, the trees looming like specters overhead. Wolves howled in the distance, their voices rising in a chorus that set his teeth on edge. He gripped his cudgel, heart pounding, until at last a flicker of light pierced the gloom—a candle in a window.

Relief flooded him. He stumbled toward the glow and pushed open the door of a low, crowded room, only to reel back as a wave of heat and stench struck him. The air was thick with the reek of wet wool, unwashed bodies, and the pungent tang of garlic. A great stove dominated the space, surrounded by travelers drying their sodden clothes, while in one corner, a brood of children added their own sour note to the mix.

Gerard hesitated, torn between the horrors within and the wolves without. Hunger decided him. He crept inside.

“Is this an inn?” he asked a man near the door.

The fellow snorted. “Where else? ‘The Star of the Forest,’ known to all.”

Gerard’s stomach growled. “Might I have supper?”

The landlady, plucking feathers from a heap beside her, barely glanced up. “Supper’s long past. Come by six next time.”

His heart sank. “Then might I at least sleep?”

“When the beds come.”

Baffled, Gerard leaned against the wall, watching as straw was strewn across the floor. Soon, the guests sprawled upon it like felled logs, their snores rising in discord. He claimed a corner, as far from the stove’s stifling heat as possible, and curled up, nose buried in straw to blunt the odors.

Sleep came fitfully, broken by the hind’s prodding as he delivered a foaming mug of sour beer. Gerard choked it down, too weary to protest.

Dawn brought relief. Most of the company had vanished, and Gerard paid his meager fee, eager to escape. The kind woman who had shared her pudding with him the night before stood at the door with her husband.

“Godspeed,” she said warmly as he passed.

He kissed her cheek in thanks, then stepped into the morning light, the road stretching before him once more.

---

By afternoon, he reached a grand monastery, its arched gateway welcoming. Within, he found charity—a clean bed, a meal, and the soaring voices of monks at vespers. Yet even their celestial harmonies could not ease the ache in his heart. *Margaret should be here,* he thought, and sighed.

The prior, noting his youth and purpose, gifted him a silver coin and sage advice: “Find company on the road. The way is perilous for one alone.”

Perilous? Gerard scoffed inwardly—until that evening, when he stood before another inn, its door barred, its windows dark. A face appeared at a hatch, eyed him dumbly, then pointed wordlessly to a side entrance.

Inside, the common room was a den of filth—mud-caked travelers scraping boots with knives, mothers comparing ailments, the reek of unwashed bodies thickening the air. The landlord, a grizzled tyrant, measured guests before feeding them, and the tablecloths were sacks fit only for scarecrows.

Gerard despaired.

A Burgundian soldier, laughing at his disgust, clapped him on the back. “Courage! The devil is dead!”

Gerard managed a weak smile. But as the room grew hotter, the company ranker, he wondered if the devil had merely moved—into Germany.

When straw was scattered for beds, he lay down, too exhausted to care. Tomorrow, he vowed, he would find better company. Or die trying.
