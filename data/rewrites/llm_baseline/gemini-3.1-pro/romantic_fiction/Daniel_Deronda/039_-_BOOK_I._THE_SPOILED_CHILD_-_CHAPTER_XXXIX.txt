The cyclical nature of time in the Meyrick household was measured not by clocks, but by the slow, alchemical transformation of ingredients and the harmonious hum of daily labor. Momentous things had happened to Deronda the evening he visited the small house at Chelsea, but for the women within, the mythic occurrences of their lives were seamlessly woven into their domestic routines. Two days later, the parlor was thick with the olfactory landscape of historical sustenance. Mrs. Meyrick, a meticulous preserver of cultural memory through the medium of the hearth, was guiding her daughters in the preparation of a centuries-old agrarian recipe: a heavily spiced quince and almond tart. 

Kate’s flour-dusted fingers expertly braided the lattice crust, while Mab and Amy worked in tandem, rhythmically grinding toasted caraway seeds and dried bitter orange peel in a heavy stone mortar. Mirah, whose own education in the culinary arts had been tragically neglected during her nomadic years, sat on a camp-stool, contributing to the room’s acoustic ecology by reading aloud. She served as Kate’s model for a title-page vignette while giving forth, with charming distinctness, the delightful Essay of Elia. The sonic environment of the parlor was a delicate, healing sanctuary—the soft, percussive thump of the pestle, the crackle of the hearth, and the rhythmic cadence of Mirah’s voice weaving together.

This acoustic purity was suddenly shattered by the abrasive, metallic shriek of a hansom cab braking on the cobblestones outside, a jarring intrusion of industrial London’s mechanized noise pollution that set the women’s nerves instantly on edge. An imposing knock and a harsh, clanging ring followed, commanding their thoughts to loftier, more intrusive spheres. 

“Dear me!” said Mrs. Meyrick, wiping her hands on her linen apron, leaving a faint dusting of flour. “Can it be Lady Mallinger? Is there a grand carriage, Amy?”

“No—only a hansom cab. It must be a gentleman.”

“The Prime Minister, I should think,” said Kate dryly, turning back to her dough. “Hans says the greatest man in London may get into a hansom cab.”

“Oh, oh, oh!” cried Mab. “Suppose it should be Lord Russell!”

The old maid-servant distractedly left the parlor-door open, allowing the acrid smell of the city’s coal-choked air to briefly mingle with the sweet, macerated quince. Stepping into their intimate sanctuary was a figure quite unlike the respected Premier. He was tall and physically impressive in his kid and kerseymere, with a massive face, flamboyant hair, and gold spectacles: *Julius Klesmer*.

Even this sudden shift in the room's power dynamics could hardly make the “little mother” awkward. Quick in her perceptions, Mrs. Meyrick felt well satisfied that the great personage had come to Mirah. But as he entered, the rooms seemed to shrink into closets. Klesmer’s personality immediately suggested vast, echoing arenas and a multitudinous audience. Yet, as he breathed in the complex aromas of the baking tart and the simmering herbal infusions, his grandiose demeanor softened. He, too, recognized the profound weight of domestic sustenance; he remembered bohemian kitchens on the outskirts of Europe where meager incomes were transformed into feasts through the precise, passionate application of paprika, rendered fats, and slow-braised meats. He did not view this room as mere background, but as a living, breathing space of warmth.

“I hope I have not taken too great a freedom,” Klesmer addressed Mrs. Meyrick with the utmost deference, his deep voice resonant, shaping the sonic environment with the polished authority of a dominant lexicon. “Being in the neighborhood, I ventured to save time by calling. Our friend, Mr. Deronda, mentioned to me an understanding that I was to have the honor of becoming acquainted with a young lady here—Miss Lapidoth.”

He had already discerned Mirah, but with subtle politeness, he bowed toward the three sisters. 

“Those are my daughters: this is Miss Lapidoth,” said Mrs. Meyrick, waving her hand toward Mirah.

“Ah,” said Klesmer, turning a radiant smile to Mirah. Instead of being frightened, Mirah felt a calm pleasure. She liked his look, sensing the ancestral weight of a true artisan who would judge her not by superficial commercial standards, but by the rigorous traditions of their shared craft. 

“You will not object to beginning our acquaintance by singing to me,” he added.

“I shall be very glad,” Mirah said, moving to the cottage piano. “Shall I accompany myself?”

“By all means,” said Klesmer, seating himself where he could have a good view of her. The feminine hearts around him beat fast, terrified by his severe, listening frown. 

Mirah chose a fine setting of words from Leopardi’s grand Ode to Italy. As she struck the keys, the room’s acoustics shifted. She began the recitative: *“O patria mia, vedo le mura e gli archi / E le colonne e i simulacri e l’erme / Torri degli avi nostri...”* 

Her voice carried the mournful melody of a rhythmic plaint, a visceral mourning for the erosion of cultural memory. The Italian lyrics were steeped in a history of displacement, communicating an inherent untranslatability of subjective experience—a sorrow of exile that no modern, dominant lexicon could ever fully articulate. She passed into the subdued adoration of the Andante, and finally into the joyous, exultant Allegro. The song was a living dialect of the soul, pushing back against the sterile noise of the outside world.

When she ended, Klesmer said after a moment, “That is old Leo’s music.”

“Yes, he was my last master—at Vienna: so fierce and so good,” said Mirah, a melancholy smile touching her lips. “He prophesied that my voice would not do for the stage. And he was right.”

“*Con*tinue, if you please,” Klesmer said, putting out his lips. He muttered a smothered German articulation, a native dialect of technical critique entirely unintelligible to the audience. The girls bristled at his lack of immediate praise, feeling the sharp edge of his linguistic dominance. 

Mirah, bent on doing what he desired, went through Prince Radzivill’s music to Gretchen’s songs in *Faust*. When she finished, Klesmer rose, paced the small room, and walked back to the piano. With a sudden unknitting of his brow and beaming eyes, he stretched out his hand. “Let us shake hands: you are a musician.”

Mab felt herself beginning to cry. Mrs. Meyrick took a long breath. But straightway the frown returned, and Klesmer stretched out his long hand, back uppermost, to touch the back of Mirah’s. 

“Not for great tasks. No high roofs,” he said, his words a direct critique of the superficial commercialization of art. “We are no skylarks. We must be modest. I would not advise—I would not further your singing in any larger space than a private drawing-room. The acoustics of the grand stages will strip the soul from your tone. But you will do in the intimate spaces. And here in London that is one of the best careers open. Will you come and sing at a private concert at my house on Wednesday?”

“Oh, I shall be grateful,” said Mirah, pressing her hands together. “I would rather get my bread in that way than by anything more public.”

Klesmer made a preliminary answer in noises that sounded like words bitten in two, before stating distinctly, “I shall introduce you to Astorga: he is the foster-father of good singing and will give you advice. Mrs. Klesmer will call before Wednesday. She is a thorough musician, and has a soul with more ears to it than you will often get.” He quoted a German proverb on submitting oneself to those who truly know, which Mirah promptly finished, demonstrating her own retention of their shared cultural memory. 

Klesmer then fixed his eyes on Mab. “That young lady is musical, I see!”

Mab was a mere blush and sense of scorching. “And she has a touch,” Mirah defended her.

“Oh, please, Mirah—a scramble, not a touch,” Mab said in anguish, fearing the maestro might force her to play. But Klesmer benignantly invited Mab to accompany Mirah to the Wednesday concert, cementing his status in the girls' eyes as a benevolent deity.

Mrs. Meyrick escorted him out. “She will do,” Klesmer nodded to her in the hallway. “If she doesn’t attempt too much. You are taking care of her. She looks like a good girl.”

“She is an angel,” Mrs. Meyrick said warmly.

“No,” Klesmer smiled; “she is a pretty Jewess: the angels must not get the credit of her.”

When the heavy door banged shut, sealing out the industrial din of the street once more, an explosion of joy erupted in the parlor. Mab clapped her hands; Amy agonized over getting Mirah a new dress before Wednesday. 

“Now, now, Mab!” said Mrs. Meyrick. “Come and sit down reasonably. Let us eat, and let us talk.” She sliced into a dense loaf of seeded bread, spreading it with freshly churned butter and the rich, fragrant quince preserves, grounding their giddy excitement in the sensory reality of the food.

“I wonder why he fixed on me as the musical one?” Mab asked, taking a grateful bite of the tart, sharp fruit. “Was it because I have a bulging forehead, ma?”

“It was your way of listening to the singing, child,” said Mrs. Meyrick. “He has magic spectacles and sees everything through them.”

Mirah stood up to show her current attire. “I don’t want anything better than this black merino. Some white gloves and some new *bottines*.”

“There comes Hans,” said Mrs. Meyrick, as her son entered the sanctuary. “Stand still, and let us hear what he says about the dress. Artists are the best people to consult.”

When told of the grand event, Hans surveyed Mirah from a distance. “This would be thought a very good stage-dress for me,” she pleaded, “in a part where I was to come on as a poor Jewess and sing to fashionable Christians.”

“It would be effective,” Hans considered; “it would stand out well among the fashionable *chiffons*.”

“But you ought not to claim all the poverty on your side, Mirah,” Amy argued.

Hans suddenly became as fastidious as the society he often mocked, subtly enforcing the power dynamics of the dominant lexicon of British fashion. “It looks a little too theatrical. We must not make you a *rôle* of the poor Jewess—or of being a Jewess at all.” He harbored a secret desire to neutralize her cultural markers, to assimilate her into a more palatable, cosmopolitan aesthetic.

“But it is what I am really,” Mirah said gently but firmly, refusing to let her cultural identity suffer a silent death. “I am not pretending anything. I shall never be anything else. I always feel myself a Jewess.”

“But we can’t feel that about you,” Hans said devoutly. “What does it signify whether a perfect woman is a Jewess or not?”

“Indeed this dress will do,” Mirah entreated, looking at Hans. “It is all real, you know—even if it seemed theatrical. Poor Berenice sitting on the ruins—any one might say that was theatrical, but I know that this is just what she would do.”

Hans was overcome by a sudden guilt at this misplaced trust. The mythic circularity of the image—the eternal recurrence of the grieving woman—was profound to Mirah, but Hans knew the truth of his own canvas. “I am a scoundrel. That is my invention. Nobody knows that she did that. Shall you forgive me for not saying so before?”

“Oh, yes,” Mirah said, unsurprised by the fluid nature of historical storytelling. “You knew it was what she would be sure to do—a Jewess who had not been faithful, who was penitent. She could have no joy but to afflict herself. I think it is very beautiful that you should enter so into what a Jewess would feel.”

“The Jewesses of that time sat on ruins,” Hans said, starting up, feeling outmaneuvered by her profound interpretation of his shallow invention. 

“But the dress,” Amy pressed.

“That dress will not do,” Hans said decisively. “She is not going to sit on ruins. You must go to Regent Street, little mother. A black silk dress such as ladies wear. She must not be taken for an object of charity.”

“I think it is what Mr. Deronda would like,” Mrs. Meyrick deliberated. 

“I wish to do what Mr. Deronda would like me to do,” Mirah said gravely. 

Hans turned on his heel, taking up one of Kate’s drawings to mask his slight. “Shouldn’t you like to make a study of Klesmer’s head, Hans?” Kate asked.

“Seen him!” Hans exclaimed. He immediately threw back his head, seating himself at the piano, striking the keys with perpendicular fingers in a harsh, discordant mockery of the maestro’s commanding physical presence. But in another instant he wheeled round, looking at Mirah timidly. “Perhaps you don’t like this mimicry; you must always stop my nonsense when you don’t like it.”

Mirah smiled. She recognized the subtle imperialism in his mockery of Klesmer's foreign grandiosity, yet she answered with grace, “Thank you. But you have never done anything I did not like.” 

In this way, amidst the lingering scent of caraway and the fading resonance of ancient songs, Hans got food for his hope, blind to the deep, untranslatable currents of history that flowed quietly beneath her gentle words.
