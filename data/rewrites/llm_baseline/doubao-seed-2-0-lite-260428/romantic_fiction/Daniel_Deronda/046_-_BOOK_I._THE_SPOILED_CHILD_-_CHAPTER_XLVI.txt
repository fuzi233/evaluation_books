> “If any one should importune me to give a reason why I loved him, I feel it could no otherwise be expressed than by making answer, ‘Because it was he, because it was I.’ There is, beyond what I am able to say, I know not what inexplicable power that brought on this union.” — MONTAIGNE: *On Friendship*

The long-planned moment had arrived: the moment to tell Mordecai that the little lost sister he had grieved for decades was alive, and to move him to a sunlit Brompton lodging a mere ten minutes’ walk from the Meyrick home, so that when Mirah at last met the brother she’d never dared hope to find, they would both be wrapped round in Mrs. Meyrick’s warm, motherly care. Deronda had confided every detail of Mirah’s story to the “little mother” except the strange, unspoken bond that had tied Mordecai’s hopes to him, and she had guarded the secret fiercely—no hint had slipped to her daughters, or to Hans, for any accidental whisper reaching Mirah would have stirred up restless, painful suspicion and spoiled the gentle reveal they had carefully orchestrated to secure her long-sought independence, that quiet, dignified kind of self-sufficiency that lets a loved one care for you without robbing you of your pride.

Mrs. Meyrick’s mix of Scottish steadiness and Gaelic warmth had made her an ideal keeper of the secret, though she harbored small, soft misgivings of her own. She was sure Mordecai was good, of course—Deronda’s judgment was never wrong—but she had quietly hoped Mirah’s sharp, painful longing for her Jewish roots would soften over time, tangled gently into the new, bright life she was building with the Meyricks. Her own secret daydream had never centered on long-lost kin: she had been quietly waiting for the romance between Hans and Mirah to bloom, and this fervent, dying brother who would drag Mirah’s heart back to the deep, vivid dye of Jewish devotion felt like an unlooked-for complication, the sort of intense real-life figure you read about with delight after midnight, but panic a little to find knocking on your front door at breakfast.

“I’m as glad as you are the coarse pawnbroker wasn’t her brother,” she admitted to Deronda, as they walked through the empty Brompton lodgings, tucking soft wool bedding into place. “There are Ezras and Ezras in the world, and I can’t bear to think Mirah’s only remaining kin would be the sort of shopkeeper who’d hustle you out the door before you’d even finished looking at a teacup. But the things he said to you about his mother and his little sister? That makes my heart soft for him. I’m just not fond of fanaticism, that’s all. Heard too much fire-and-brimstone preaching as a girl, lost my taste for it.”

“Mordecai isn’t a fanatic,” Deronda said gently. “A fanatic’s enthusiasm is narrow, blind to all that doesn’t fit his small path, cruel to those who walk a different road. Mordecai is an enthusiast—the kind of man whose heart burns for great, widespread good for all his people. He’s not strictly orthodox, he makes allowance for every man’s flaws, and the Cohen family who shelter him adore him even though they don’t understand a word of his grandest, dearest dreams.”

“Alright, alright,” Mrs. Meyrick laughed, patting the drafty bedroom window with a fresh sheet of thick paper to block the cold draught. “I’ll take your word for the parts I don’t get. My poor old husband used to say the world would be a dismal place if there was nothing but common sense in it. I’ll have his bedding turned down soft, and when we break the secret to the girls and Hans, they’ll deck these rooms out with every little pretty trinket they own. But promise me you won’t tell Mirah before I warn the children the evening before, so they can slip out the next morning and leave the two of them to their first meeting, no fuss, no prying eyes?”

Deronda agreed, teasing her playfully for not comparing Mordecai to the fire-breathing Covenanter Habakkuk Mucklewrath from Scott’s novels when she talked to the girls, and she waved him off promising she’d talk them all into being overjoyed, even if she had to work herself into a happy state to do it.

He headed for the Cohen home that evening, careful to write ahead that he wished to speak to Mordecai in his own workroom, that he would not intrude if the family thought it rude. He did not want to hurt these kind, rough people who had given Mordecai a home and love when no one else in the world had cared for him.

The whole Cohen household had dressed up for his visit: the women had fresh ribbons in their hair, the children wore their cleanest stockings, and Cohen himself held his tongue out of respect for the mysterious “great business” his odd lodger had going on with the handsome, gentle young gentleman. Little Jacob, the bright scruffy boy who adored Mordecai, trotted up to Deronda’s knee before he could reach the workroom door.

“What are you going to talk to Mordecai about?” he demanded.

“Something that will make him very happy,” Deronda said, pinching his ear. “Something you won’t understand yet.”

To prove he was far more learned than the stranger assumed, Jacob poured out a long string of throaty, nasal Hebrew verses he’d memorized from Mordecai, nodding his head fiercely as if he’d delivered an unbeatable challenge. When Deronda admitted he could not recite a single line of it, the boy whooped in triumph, danced around in his scuffed scarlet socks, and dragged the workroom door wide open yelling “Mordecai! Here’s the young swell!” before his mother and grandmother could hush him.

The room had been fussed over too: a scrap of soft carpet laid by the hearth, Deronda’s chair pulled close to the fire, the lamps trimmed bright. Mordecai was standing by the flames, his face bright with a solemn, trembling expectation, as if he had spent the whole day waiting for Deronda to tell him the secret the two of them had danced around for months: that Deronda was his long-lost blood kin, the heir to his people’s legacy he’d dreamed of finding.

“You have come to tell me my soul’s dearest wish is true,” he said, clasping his hands tight as Deronda sat beside him. “That you are my brother by blood, that the heritage is yours, there is nothing left to divide us.”

Deronda’s heart ached at the look on his face. “I have learned nothing new about my own origins yet,” he said softly, breaking the inevitable disappointment gently. Mordecai sank back in his chair, eyes closing, the whole day’s sharp tension draining away into sickening, hollow reaction.

“Except,” Deronda hurried on, “that some time ago I found myself connected to you through someone you love more than almost anyone. Someone who was precious to your mother, and precious to you. Someone you thought lost to the world.”

Mordecai’s eyes flew open, his hand spasming tight around Deronda’s wrist, his face white with terror—afraid, Deronda knew, that he was about to hear that his sister had come to harm.

“What you prayed for has come to pass,” Deronda said, his own voice soft with tremor. “Mirah is safe. She was delivered from all the evil that chased her, and she is here in London, looking for you.”

Mordecai’s grip loosened, he panted out a tearless sob, sinking back against his chair as the wave of joy hit him. His eyes filled with a soft, luminous sweetness Deronda had never seen before, and for the first time he could see the clear family resemblance to Mirah, the same gentle, glowing light in his face that lit hers when she spoke of their mother. For long minutes he whispered quiet Hebrew prayers under his breath, as if he was talking face to face with the mother they had both lost.

Deronda told him the rest then, softening the worst parts of Mirah’s story, skipping the part where she had nearly drowned herself in despair, only telling him she had run away to England to find her mother and brother, that she had found a loving home with the Meyricks, that she had never stopped talking about the brother she barely remembered, but had always ached for.

“I came to this very house looking for Ezra Cohen, thinking he was Mirah’s brother,” Deronda said, smiling. “I feared for so long that her only kin would be coarse, unkind people who would hurt her. But I found the brother she deserved long before I knew your name was Ezra Mordecai Cohen.”

The only kinship Mordecai shared with the Cohen family was the broad, beloved kinship of the Jewish people, he told Deronda. “They sheltered me, gave me bread and love when no one else would. I would not trade that kindness for anything. But I will go with you to Mirah,” he added, before Deronda could even make his plea. “Our mother would have wished it. I will accept the home you have prepared for us.” His voice softened. “But I cannot tell the Cohens myself. My heart will fail me. You must speak for me.”

They walked back out to the front parlor together, all the Cohens huddled by the fire: baby wrapped in her scarlet quilt in the corner, little Adelaide Rebekah on grandmother’s lap, Jacob squeezed between his father’s legs. The warm firelight gilded their dark, gentle eyes as Deronda broke the news: Mordecai’s long-lost little sister was alive, a talented, lovely young singer who would care for him for the rest of his days, they had a soft new home waiting for him not far away in Brompton.

The wail that went up was immediate. The grandmother sniffled, wiping her eyes on her apron: “So you’re going to leave us, Mordecai, no more little ones running around to cheer you up?” The young mother took up the wail, and Cohen blurted that Jacob would have to go to proper school now, no more casual Hebrew lessons from their lodger. At that, Jacob’s face crumpled, he burst into loud sobs, Adelaide Rebekah wailed in sympathy, and the baby woke screaming in her cradle, the whole household in an uproar of soft, unselfish grief at the thought of losing the man they had loved like family.

Cohen, gruff and big-hearted, wiped his own eyes and made a stolid speech to Deronda, squaring his elbows proudly: “We’re not the sort to grudge a man his good luck, sir. Not one bit. The women and the kids are just soft, that’s all—any Jewish woman’s heart is tender to anyone she’s fed bread to, you know that. We’ll be glad for him soon enough, once we’ve gotten used to the idea.”

Mordecai reached out for Jacob, pulling the sobbing little boy against his chest, and said gently that Jacob could visit him any time he liked, Brompton was only a short walk away, he would teach him all the Hebrew verses he’d promised him, still. Jacob stopped crying mid-sniffle at the word “visit,” brightening at the thought of treats and adventures on the trip. The grandmother sighed in resignation, saying only that she hoped he would get good kosher meat in his new home, and Cohen winked at Deronda, saying he had always known there was some grand, lovely secret between the two of them, far more interesting than boring lessons.

As Deronda walked home that night, his chest felt light, warm with that soft, quiet joy that comes when every long, careful plan at last falls into place. Tomorrow, he would bring Mirah to the new lodgings. The brother and sister she had spent half her life praying for would finally be face to face.
