CHAPTER LXVI. “One day still fierce ’mid many a day struck calm.” —BROWNING: _The King and the Book_.

While Gwendolen’s thoughts remained fixed on Deronda, untouched by the quiet drama unfolding elsewhere, Ezra and Mirah found their lives increasingly entwined with his presence—though not in the way she might have imagined. Their bond deepened, not through choice, but through fate’s unrelenting hand.

Lapidoth had left his daughter at the threshold of her new life, driven not by love or duty, but by the insistent pull of chance—a game he could neither resist nor master. The moment he held Mirah’s purse, the weight of hunger seemed to vanish, replaced by the intoxicating hope of gain. Yet even as he walked away from her door, his mind already turned to what came next: whether to pawn the purse for more stakes, or return it with a lie that would spare him shame. He was not without pride, and he believed, with the stubbornness of a man who has lived too long on illusions, that he had rights over his children’s possessions—even if they were earned through sacrifice and silence.

He had spent the morning at a lowly gambling den marked only by a faded sign reading “Pyramids,” where thirty shillings had vanished into thin air. Now, with the purse empty and his pockets bare, he wandered toward Brompton Square, drawn by the faint possibility that Mirah might be out, or returning. If he could catch sight of her, perhaps he could slip inside without confrontation. But evening had fallen, and after waiting in the cold street, weariness made him decide to ring the bell.

As he approached, however, he heard music—her voice, clear and pure, rising from the piano. Mirah sat there, lost in song, singing “_Herz, mein Herz_” with all the emotion of one who carries sorrow within her soul. Ezra sat beside her, eyes closed, listening—not merely to the melody, but to the memory it stirred. Then the door opened, and Mrs. Adam stood there, hesitant. “A gentleman below says he is your father, miss.”

Mirah rose immediately, her hands clasping together as though to steady herself. She looked at Ezra, pleading silently. But before she could speak, he said, “No, Mirah. Not so. Let him come up, Mrs. Adam.”

She stood frozen, heart pounding, watching her brother rise. His face was transformed—brow furrowed, lips tight, eyes sharp with something she had never seen before: not anger, but a terrible clarity. When the door opened again, Lapidoth stepped in, his expression carefully arranged into one of sorrow, though his body trembled beneath the mask.

Mrs. Adam glanced between them—the younger man, gaunt and grave; the elder, worn and hollow—and whispered to herself, “Father, sure enough.” The resemblance was unmistakable in outline, yet the depth of character had been shaped by years of divergence. One was a man of quiet strength, forged in suffering; the other, a shadow of what he once was.

“Ezra,” Lapidoth began, voice cracking, “my boy… you hardly know me after so many years.”

“I know you—too well—father,” Ezra replied, each word deliberate, heavy with meaning. “You have not changed.”

“Oh, I see you’re angry,” Lapidoth said quickly, regaining some of his old charm. “I don’t blame you. Things have gone wrong. A man in trouble can’t always do right. I’ve suffered, I tell you—I’ve known hardship.” He turned to Mirah, holding out her purse. “Here’s your little purse, my dear. I emptied it to pay for food and lodging. I knew you’d want me to clear myself. And now I stand here—penniless—before you, my children. You can turn me out if you wish. Say the word, Mirah. Say, ‘Father, I’ve had enough of you.’ Say it, and I’ll go like a spark into the night. I won no longer spoil your peace.”

Tears glistened in his eyes—habitual, perhaps, but real enough in their effect.

“You know I could never say it, father,” Mirah answered softly, her voice thick with pain. “But I feel the truth behind your words—only not the part about leaving.”

“Mirah, my sister,” Ezra interrupted, his voice firm, “leave us.”

She hesitated, her eyes full of fear and longing. She moved close to him, took his hand, and whispered, “Remember, Ezra—you said our mother would not have shut him out.”

“Trust me,” Ezra said, and she withdrew, climbing the stairs slowly, her heart racing. If he drove him away—if he sent him into the cold world again—what then?

Lapidoth watched his son, sensing the storm gathering. He had expected rebuke, perhaps even rejection—but not this. Not the voice of a man speaking from the depths of grief and justice, shaped by years of silent endurance. Ezra spoke now, not as a son, but as a judge.

“This home,” he began, “is built on kindness and labor. My friend supports me. My sister earns her own living. We have nothing to give freely, but we will not cast you out. You are our father. Though you broke every bond, we honor ours. But I will never trust you.”

He paused, breath catching.

“You fled with money, leaving debts unpaid. You abandoned my mother. You stole her child and broke her heart. You became a gambler—where shame and conscience should have been, there is only craving. You tried to sell Mirah—you had sold her, but the price was denied. Such a man cannot expect trust again.”

His voice faltered, but did not break.

“We will feed you. We will give you shelter, clothing, a bed. Because you are our father. But you will never be trusted. You are an evil man. You caused our mother’s suffering. That such a man is our father is a wound upon our flesh—one that will never heal. But God has laid this burden upon us. Even if justice demanded your punishment, even if your body failed under public scorn—we would still say, ‘This is our father. Make way, that we may carry him out of your sight.’”

Lapidoth had braced himself for condemnation, but not for this—this raw, honest fire from the soul of his son. It struck him like lightning, not from outside, but from within. The tears came then—uncontrolled, violent. He collapsed into a chair, burying his face in his hands, sobbing as if his heart would break.

Yet even in that moment, he knew it was not just grief—it was strategy. A reflex honed over years: the ability to use emotion as armor. As a young man, he had once used such vulnerability to disarm criticism. Now, it served him again.

Ezra sat back, exhausted. His hands shook. He felt as though he had spoken words that could not be unsaid, as though he had stepped across a threshold from which there was no return. He did not speak. He did not look at his father. He simply sat, drained.

But Mirah—her ears tuned to every sound—heard the sobs. She could not stay away any longer. She opened the door, her first thought not for her father, but for her brother. She rushed to Ezra, taking his trembling hand. He gripped hers tightly, finding strength in her touch, though he said nothing.
