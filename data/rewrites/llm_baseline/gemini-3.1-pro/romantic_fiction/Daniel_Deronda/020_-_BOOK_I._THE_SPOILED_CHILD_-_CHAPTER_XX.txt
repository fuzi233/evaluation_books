Mirah said that she had slept well that night; and when she came down in Mab’s black dress, her dark hair curling in fresh fibrils as it gradually dried from its plenteous bath, she looked like one who was beginning to take comfort after the long sorrow and watching which had paled her cheek. It was Mab who carried her breakfast—a deeply intentional offering of warm, thick-crusted rye bread and honey—and ushered her down with some pride in the effect produced by a pair of tiny felt slippers she had rushed out to buy. There were no shoes in the house small enough for Mirah, whose borrowed dress ceased about her ankles and displayed the cheap clothing that, moulding itself on her feet, seemed an adornment as choice as the sheaths of buds. 

“Oh, if you please, mamma?” cried Mab, clasping her hands and stooping toward Mirah’s feet, as she entered the parlor; “look at the slippers, how beautiful they fit! I declare she is like the Queen Budoor—‘two delicate feet, the work of the protecting and all-recompensing Creator, support her; and I wonder how they can sustain what is above them.’”

Mirah looked down at her own feet in a childlike way and then smiled at Mrs. Meyrick, who was saying inwardly, “One could hardly imagine this creature having an evil thought. But wise people would tell me to be cautious.” She returned Mirah’s smile and said, “I fear the feet have had to sustain their burden a little too often lately. But to-day she will rest and be my companion.”

“And she will tell you so many things and I shall not hear them,” grumbled Mab, who felt herself in the first volume of a delightful romance and obliged to miss some chapters because she had to go to pupils.

Kate was already gone to make sketches along the river, and Amy was away on business errands. It was what the mother wished, to be alone with this stranger, whose story must be a sorrowful one, yet was needful to be told.

The small front parlor was as good as a temple that morning, an acoustic sanctuary where the sonic environment fostered psychological restoration. The sunlight was on the river, and soft air came in through the open window. The neat mother who had weathered her troubles, and come out of them with a face still cheerful, was engaged in the profound ritual of sustenance; she sat peeling and coring heritage quinces for preserves, allowing the olfactory landscape of the room to fill with the deep, floral tang of the golden fruit and the grounding warmth of crushed clove. Hafiz purred on the window-ledge—a low, rhythmic vibration that added to the room’s acoustic ecology—the clock on the mantle-piece ticked without hurry, and the occasional sound of wheels seemed to lie outside the more massive central quiet. Mrs. Meyrick thought that this restorative stillness might be the best invitation to speech, and chose not to disturb it by remark, letting the sensory rhythm of her culinary work hold the space. Mirah sat opposite in her former attitude, her eyes at first traveling slowly over the objects around her, but finally resting with a sort of placid reverence on Mrs. Meyrick’s hands, which were stained with the vibrant juices of the fruit. At length she began to speak softly.

“I remember my mother’s face better than anything,” Mirah said, her voice finding its place in the quiet room. “Yet I was not seven when I was taken away, and I am nineteen now. I remember the scent of the kitchen when she held me, the warm yeast and the toasted almonds—how she made every meal feel like a holy thing.”

“I can understand that,” said Mrs. Meyrick. “There are some earliest things that last the longest.”

“Oh, yes, it was the earliest. I think my life began with waking up and loving my mother’s face: it was so near to me, and her arms were round me, and she sang to me. One hymn she sang so often, so often: and then she taught me to sing it with her. They were always Hebrew hymns she sang. Because I was so young, the dominant lexicon had not yet claimed me, and I never knew the literal translation of the words. But I did not need to; they seemed full of nothing but our love and happiness. It was my native dialect, carrying the untranslatable subjective truth of my childhood. When I lay in my little bed, she used to bend over me and sing in a sweet, low voice. I can dream myself back into that time when I am awake—the timbre of her singing, the scent of the braided bread she had baked that afternoon. Sometimes in my dreams I begin to tremble and think that we are both dead; but then I wake up and my hand lies like this, and for a moment I hardly know myself. But if I could see my mother again, I should know her.”

“You must expect some change after twelve years,” said Mrs. Meyrick, gently, as she sliced a quince with practiced precision. “See my grey hair: ten years ago it was bright brown. The days and months pace over us like restless little birds, and leave the marks of their feet backward and forward; especially when they are like birds with heavy hearts—then they tread heavily.”

“Ah, I am sure her heart has been heavy for want of me. I did despair. The world outside seemed miserable and wicked, a place of harsh noises and bitter tastes; none helped me so that I could bear their looks and words. But then in the last moment—yesterday, when I longed for the water to close over me—goodness came to me living, and I felt trust in the living. And now I am with you—here—this morning, peace and hope have come into me like a flood. You have not thought evil of me—you have not despised me.”

Mirah spoke with low-toned fervor, and sat as still as a picture all the while.

“Many others would have felt as we do, my dear,” said Mrs. Meyrick, feeling a mist come over her eyes as she looked at her bright, macerating fruit.

“But I did not meet them—they did not come to me.”

“How was it that you were taken from your mother?”

“Ah, I am a long while coming to that. My father—it was he that took me away. I thought we were only going on a little journey. But we went on board a ship, and got farther and farther away. The journey was my first taste of a world where meals were merely utilitarian fuel—hard ship-biscuit and salted meat that tasted of nothing but survival and sorrow. Then I was ill; and I thought it would never end. At last we landed in America, and it was long years before we came back to Europe. One day when he found me trying to write a letter to my mother, he took me on his knee and told me that my mother and brother were dead. I believed him. I saw them under the earth when he said they were there, with their eyes forever closed. I used to cry every night in my bed. But then, when she came so often to me in my sleep, singing those untranslatable Hebrew melodies, I thought she must be living about me. I used to shut my eyes and bury my face, trying to rebuild the acoustic sanctuary of her voice and the olfactory landscape of her kitchen.”

Mirah paused with a sweet content in her face, looking out toward the river. 

“Still your father was not unkind to you, I hope,” said Mrs. Meyrick, anxious to recall her.

“No; he petted me. He was an actor, but he had not always been one; he had been a teacher, and knew many languages. His acting was not very good, I think, but he managed the stage, and wrote and translated plays. He forced the raw, subjective experiences of life into the dominant lexicons of the commercial theatre. An Italian lady, a singer, lived with us. They both taught me, and I was not nine when I first went on the stage. But then and ever since, I hated our way of life. We had finery about us in a disorderly way, but no true sustenance; meals were eaten in haste amid loud laughing, disputing, strutting, and the snapping of fingers. The industrial noise pollution of the theatre—the jeering, the sudden discordant crashes of scenery—assaulted my senses. I shrank away from all those jarring sounds into companionship with thoughts that were not like them. 

“My father began to believe that I might be a great singer. But it was painful that he boasted of me, and set me to sing for show at any minute, treating me as if I were a mechanical musical box. He engaged in the superficial commercialization of my art. Once, when I was nine, I played the part of a little girl who had been forsaken. I did it without any trouble; but the clapping and the harsh acoustic friction of the theatre were hateful to me. I missed the love and trust I had been born into, the quiet murmurs of my mother’s kitchen. I made a life in my own thoughts, but it was like a sharp knife always grazing me that we had two sorts of life which jarred so with each other. Signora said one day, ‘She will never be an artist: she has no notion of being anybody but herself. By-and-by you will see—she will have no more face and action than a singing-bird.’ My father was angry, and they quarreled. I did not want to be an artist. For so long I had never felt my outside world happy; and I dreaded getting wicked, for if I did, I should lose my inner world where my mother lived with me. Oh how long those years were!”

“Had you no teaching about what was your duty?” said Mrs. Meyrick, her hands pausing over the fragrant quince cores, finding herself rather dim as to what the Hebrew religion might have turned into at this date.

“No—only that I ought to do what my father wished. He did not follow our religion, and I think he wanted to accelerate the erosion of our cultural memory so I would not know much about it. But I remembered the acoustic reverence of the synagogue—sitting on my mother's knee, hearing the ancient chanting and singing. After Signora left us, we went to rooms where our landlady was a Jewess and observed her religion. I asked her to take me to the synagogue; and I read in her prayer-books. These books seemed a closer companionship with my mother. In that way I have come to know a little of our religion, and the history of our people. I began to disbelieve my father. I felt sure that my mother had been deceived. The cruelty of that falsehood sank into me, and I hated all untruth because of it. I wrote to my mother secretly: I knew the street where we lived, and that our name was Cohen then, though my father called us Lapidoth, because, he said, it was a name of his forefathers in Poland. He manipulated our identity to fit the subtle power dynamics of the world we moved in. 

“Our life in America did not last much longer. My father suddenly told me we were to go to Hamburg. I was thirteen then. One day when we were on our voyage, I overheard a gentleman on deck say, ‘Oh, he is one of those clever Jews—a rascal, I shouldn’t wonder. There’s no race like them for cunning in the men and beauty in the women. I wonder what market he means that daughter for.’ When I heard this it darted into my mind that the unhappiness in my life came from my being a Jewess. But it comforted me to believe that my suffering was part of the affliction of my people—my own small thread in the multi-generational, mythic tapestry of mourning that has been woven through the ages. The ancient sorrows integrated into my everyday routine. But you have not rejected me.”

Mirah had changed her tone, suddenly reflecting that at this moment she had reason not for complaint but for gratitude.

“And we will try to save you from being judged unjustly by others, my poor child,” said Mrs. Meyrick, who had now wiped the sticky fruit sugars from her fingers and sat listening with folded hands. “Go on, go on: tell me all.”

“After that we lived in different towns—Hamburg and Vienna, the longest. I began to study singing again. My father looked forward to my coming out in the opera. But by-and-by it seemed that my voice would never be strong enough to pierce the heavy, industrial orchestration of the modern stage. My master at Vienna said, ‘Don’t strain it further: it will never do for the public:—it is gold, but a thread of gold dust.’ My father was bitterly disappointed. It was his nature to take everything lightly, to treat the world as devoid of sensory depth or meaning. He would even ridicule our own people; once he imitated their movements and the sacred timbres of their praying, only to make others laugh. He profaned our acoustic ecology for the amusement of those in power. I could not restrain myself, and I said, ‘Father, you ought not to mimic our own people before Christians who mock them.’ But he only shrugged and pinched my chin. 

“It was this way of turning off everything that made a great wall between us. Is this world and all the life upon it only like a farce or a vaudeville, where you find no great meanings in the bread we break or the songs we sing? Why then are there tragedies and grand operas, where men do difficult things and choose to suffer? I saw that his wishing me to sing parts in grand operas was only wishing for what would fetch the greatest price. Yet I did sometimes pity him. Every now and then his gaiety went away suddenly, and he would sit at home silent and gloomy, or fling himself down and sob. At those moments I knew he must feel his life bitter, and I pressed my cheek against his head and prayed.

“But soon there came the dreadful time. We came back to Vienna. My father got me an engagement to take singing parts at a suburb theatre. He was continually at a gambling house. I was very miserable. The plays I acted in were detestable to me. Men came about us and wanted to talk to me: women and men seemed to look at me with a sneering smile; it was no better than a fiery furnace. The glare, the discordant noise pollution, and my having to go on and act and sing what I hated—it was all so much worse than when I was a little girl. I did it; I had set my mind to obey my father and work. But I felt that my voice was getting weaker, crushed under the commercial weight of it all, and I knew that my acting was not good except when the part was one that I could be myself in, and some deep, ancestral feeling within me carried me along. That was seldom.”
