Gerard met a monk who had built the great sundial in the prior’s garden, a water-drawing wheel, and a winnowing machine for grain—always at work on some clever device. He had crafted several psalteries and two dulcimers, and now was fashioning a set of regalles, a small organ for the choir. Gerard could play the psaltery a little, but the monk played it with such grace and skill that he made Gerard feel like a mere beginner. The monk also illuminated manuscripts beautifully, though his handwriting did not match Gerard’s elegance.

As they compared their talents—each with genuine warmth and sincerity, as men of their time did when learning reflected true passion—young and old quickly grew close, like brothers. The monk pressed Gerard to stay the night. Gerard consulted Denys, who nodded reluctantly, shrugging as if to say, “What can we do?”

Gerard told the monk where he was headed and recounted their recent adventures, softening the tale of the bolstered man. “Alack!” said the old monk, “I’ve traveled far in my day, but never once was I troubled.” He warned Gerard to avoid inns—they were full of rogues and rowdies, places where one’s soul might suffer even if the body escaped unharmed. Instead, he advised lodging in peaceful monasteries each night. Then, suddenly sharp-eyed and intense, he asked, “How long has it been since you were shriven?”

Gerard flushed and murmured, “Better than a fortnight.”

“And thou an exorcist! No wonder perils have found thee,” the monk replied. “Come—thou must be absolved at once.”

“Yes, father,” said Gerard humbly, “with all my heart,” and began to kneel, hands clasped. But the monk stopped him sharply.

“Not to me! Not to me! I am as worldly as any man alive. My soul is wrapped up in these wooden pipes and leather stops—things that will perish, along with those whose minds are fixed upon such vanities.”

“Dear father,” Gerard said gently, “these instruments serve the Church. Surely that sanctifies the labor?”

“That,” the monk said, wagging a finger with half-playful warning, “is exactly what the devil has been whispering into my ear. He reminds me how Solomon built a house for the Lord with fine hangings—and that was counted good, not sinful. Oh, he quotes Scripture well!”

“But I am no simpleton,” he declared, turning abruptly toward empty air as if addressing a hidden presence, “no fool who thinks toys are holy. This one project done—vigils, fasts, prayers: standing, lying on the chapel floor, even in a tub of cold water. Nothing frightens the devil more than seeing monks pray while soaked to the chin. For *corpus domat aqua*—the body loves water.”

He winked at Gerard. “Now go confess your little sins—youthful, secular, pardonable—and leave me to mine, sweet as honey, and to be atoned for in kind.”

Gerard bowed, but couldn’t help asking, “Where may I find a confessor more holy and merciful?”

“In each cell here,” the monk replied simply. “Go to Brother Anselm—there.”

Gerard walked down the corridor, following the direction. The doors stood close together, and he seemed to misjudge. Just as he raised his hand to knock, he heard a hushed cry from his friend: “Nay! nay! nay!”

He turned. There stood the monk at his own door, agitated, pacing back and forth, clapping his hands like a sawyer. Gerard thought the cell he’d approached must house a beast—or worse, the very figure whose presence had been so plainly announced in the monastery.

He looked back questioningly, then moved to the next door. Instantly, the monk nodded rapidly, his face softening into relief, and vanished inside. He took out his hourglass, flipped it over, and returned to his work on the regalles. Again and again he glanced up, muttering, “Well-a-day, how swift the sands run when a man is bent on earthly things.”

Brother Anselm was a venerable monk, with a broad head and a face radiating both dignity and kindness. As Gerard confessed, answering the gentle yet probing questions, he found himself thinking, “Here is a face—I wonder if I may draw it after this?” And in that moment, his thoughts wandered, and he forgot a few sins.

But this time he did not downplay the incident with the bolster, nor did he minimize the pagan nature of the man involved.

The penance was simple: enter the church, prostrate himself before the altar, kiss its lowest step three times, then kneel and recite three Our Fathers and one Credo. “When done, return to me immediately.”

Gerard completed his task and returned to find Brother Anselm spreading plaster on his leg.

“After the soul, the body,” the monk said. “Know that I am the surgeon here, lacking a better. This is to cool your wound—not burn it. The saints forbid.”

During the treatment, Anselm, naturally intrigued by the Dusseldorf episode, sided with Denys on the matter of bleeding.

“We Dominicans rarely bleed patients anymore. Lay doctors say it’s due to cowardice or incompetence. But truthfully, we’ve long seen that simples—herbs—can cure most ills that can be cured at all. They don’t kill when used wisely; other remedies strike like thunderbolts. And remember: the Vulgate says plainly, ‘The blood is the life of a man.’ In medicine, law, or theology, to think oneself wiser than God is folly. Besides, simples are powerful. A small creature bitten by a venomous snake finds a herb strong enough to counteract the poison—stronger, faster than any human sickness. We learn from her wisdom and our traditions, still seeking the gifts God has scattered across the earth—some to feed, others to heal. Only in dire cases do we mix heavenly and earthly virtue. We steep relics—hair or bone of a saint—in the remedy, and miracles follow.”

“Do you truly believe it’s the relic?” Gerard asked. “Peter a Floris, a learned physician and no pagan, denies it.”

“What does Peter a Floris know? And what do I know? I cannot claim we command the saints, or force them to lend their power. But I see that patients, drinking such remedies in faith, often improve—as if by charm. Faith in the receiver plays a great part. And so it always has. A woman healed by touching Christ’s garment—had she not touched it, she would not have been healed. Had she worn it without faith, it would have meant nothing. But let us not probe too deeply. All around us calls for faith. Have patience. One day we shall understand. Meanwhile, as your confessor, I strictly forbid you, on your soul’s health, to heed learned laymen on matters of religion. Arrogance is their downfall—it shuts heaven’s door in their own faces. Remember: I speak of *learned* laymen. Unlearned ones have often taught me humility—may they teach you too.”

Your wound is cared for. In three days, it will be only a scar. God speed you, and may the saints make you as good and happy as you are thoughtful and kind.”

Gerard wished he need not leave just yet—he was to dine in the refectory. But Brother Anselm said, with a faint regret, that he would not leave his cell this week—he was in penance. With gentle hands, he lifted Gerard’s head and kissed his brow. Before the door closed, he was already back to his devotions.

Gerard left, chilled by the loneliness of monastic life, saddened. “Alas,” he thought, “this kind face I shall never see again. That voice—gone from my ears, from my heart, forever. There is only meeting and parting in this sorrowful world. Well-a-day, well-a-day!”

His melancholy was broken by a young monk who led him to the refectory. There, several monks sat at a table, and Denys stood stiff as a poker, being questioned about the towns ahead. The friars pooled their knowledge, charting a route and marking every religious house along the way. They handed Gerard the map.

Then came supper. Afterward, the old monk guided Gerard to his cell. They talked eagerly, and the friar finally revealed the reason for his earlier pantomime.

“You nearly fell into Brother Jerome’s hands. That was his cell.”

“Is Brother Jerome a wicked man?” Gerard asked.

“A wicked man?” the friar crossed himself. “A saint—an anchorite, the very pillar of this house! He would have sent you barefoot to Loretto. Ah, I forgot—you’re bound for Italy. He’d have sent you to Canterbury or Compostella instead. But Jerome was born old and cloaked. Anselm and I were boys once—wicked beyond imagining” (Gerard looked skeptical). “That keeps us humble, and makes us lenient toward youth and hot blood.”

At Gerard’s request, the monk played one last melody on the psaltery—a heavenly strain that calmed the troubled spirit and soothed the weary heart. Then came rest.

I have described this day in full—not because it was extraordinary, but because it stands as a symbol of many such days that followed. For the travelers, worn and weather-beaten, found that life, like the road, is not evenly filled with drama. Great events come in clusters, separated by stretches of quiet. Perhaps they draw each other through unseen links. But there is more—life itself is an intermittent fever.

All storytellers, whether of history or fiction, must either skip these quiet intervals or risk dragging the narrative. Yet this practice gives readers a false sense of proportion—one I wish to avoid here. So I ask your understanding: though no vivid adventure followed for a long while, one journey succeeded another. One monastery after another welcomed them freely—kindly, sometimes warmly—feeding and sheltering them without charge. Though their only enemies were winter and harsh weather—challenges not to be underestimated—they marched across vast distances, covering more ground than I have detailed.

And so, Gerard—bronzed and travel-stained from head to foot—and Denys, whose shoes were in tatters, feet swollen and sore, drew near the Burgundian frontier.
