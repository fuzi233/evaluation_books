“The meanest thing to which we bid adieu,
Loses its meanness in the parting hour.” — ELLIOTT.

Mrs. Shaw could not bear Milton. Not that she was naturally a harsh judge of places or people—quite the contrary—but Milton, with all its smoke and noise, its ragged streets and over-dressed ladies, seemed to her a place that had no room for peace or decency. The men wore clothes ill-fitted and slovenly; the poor looked weary and unclean; even the air itself felt heavy and oppressive. She feared for Margaret’s health, and worse still, for her own nerves. She insisted, with gentle but firm urgency, that Margaret must leave Milton at once and return with her to London. Margaret, weak and worn down by grief and illness, gave in at last, promising that as soon as the funeral on Wednesday passed, she would prepare to go, leaving Dixon to manage the house, settle debts, and oversee the sale of their belongings.

Before that somber day—when Mr. Hale would be laid to rest far from either home he had known, and far from his grieving wife who now lay alone among strangers—Margaret received a letter from Mr. Bell.

“MY DEAR MARGARET:—I meant to return to Milton on Thursday, but it turns out I’m needed in Plymouth this week, one of those rare occasions when duty calls. Captain Lennox and Mr. Thornton are here. The former seems a decent fellow, though perhaps too eager to help—offering to come to Milton and assist you in searching for the will. Of course, there is no will, or you’d have found it already if you followed my instructions. Still, he insists on taking you and your mother-in-law back to London. Given his wife’s condition, he can’t stay beyond Friday. But don’t worry—your Dixon is reliable. She’ll hold things together until I arrive. If no will exists, I’ll entrust matters to my Milton solicitor. Though I doubt the captain has much head for business. Still, his moustache is magnificent.

There will need to be a sale. Choose what you wish to keep—or send me a list later. Two other points, and I’m done. You know, or if you don’t, your father did, that upon my death, my money and property will pass to you. Not that I intend to die soon—I merely state this so you understand what’s coming. The Lennoxes seem fond of you now—whether they will remain so is another matter. So let us begin with an agreement: you shall pay them two hundred and fifty pounds a year, as long as you both find it agreeable to live together. (This includes Dixon—don’t let yourself be pressured into paying more for her.) That way, if one day the captain decides he wants his house to himself, you won’t be left stranded. You can take your two hundred and fifty pounds and go elsewhere—unless I’ve claimed you first to come and keep house for me.

As for dress, personal expenses, confectionery—every young lady eats sweets until wisdom arrives—I’ll consult a woman I know to determine how much your father left you, and then fix these matters accordingly.

Now, Margaret—have you already flown off in anger before reading this far, wondering what right this old man has to dictate your life so freely? I expect you have. Yet I do have a right. I’ve loved your father for thirty-five years. I stood beside him at his wedding. I closed his eyes in death. I am your godfather—and since I cannot give you spiritual comfort, knowing full well you surpass me in that regard, I would at least try to provide material support. And I have no living kin. Who will mourn Adam Bell? My whole heart is set on this one purpose. And Margaret Hale is not the girl to say no.

Write back, even just two lines, to tell me your answer. But _no thanks_.”

Margaret picked up a pen and, with trembling fingers, wrote:
“Margaret Hale is not the girl to say him nay.”

She couldn’t think of anything else. Her strength was gone. Even this small act exhausted her. She had to lie down again, shutting her eyes against the weight of thought.

“My dearest child,” Mrs. Shaw said softly, “did that letter upset you?”

“No,” Margaret whispered. “I shall feel better once tomorrow is over.”

“I’m certain you won’t truly feel better until you’re out of this dreadful air. How you’ve endured it for two years, I cannot imagine.”

“Where could I have gone? I couldn’t leave papa and mamma.”

“Well, don’t fret about it. It may have been best, though I never dreamed how poorly you lived. Our butler’s wife lives in a better house than this.”

“It’s lovely in summer—you can’t judge it now. I’ve been very happy here,” she murmured, closing her eyes to end the conversation.

The house was now warm and comfortable, fires lit in every bedroom as Mrs. Shaw insisted. She doted on Margaret, buying every delicacy, every soft luxury she herself would have cherished. But Margaret cared little for them—only noting them as signs of her aunt’s kindness. She was restless, yet drained. All day, she wandered from room to room, sorting through familiar objects, touching them gently, as if reluctant to part with memories. Dixon followed her, ostensibly to receive orders, but secretly instructed to soothe her into quiet.

“These books, Dixon, I’ll keep. Send the rest to Mr. Bell—they’ll mean something to him, as well as to papa. This one—I’d like you to give this to Mr. Thornton after I’m gone. Wait—let me write a note.”

She sat down quickly, afraid to dwell on thoughts, and scribbled:

“DEAR SIR,—The book enclosed I am sure you will value for the sake of my father, to whom it belonged.
Yours sincerely,

She resumed her walk through the house, pausing at each object from childhood, touched by affection and sorrow. She spoke little. Dixon reported to Mrs. Shaw that “Miss Hale didn’t seem to hear a word I said, though I talked the whole time to distract her.”

By evening, the exhaustion had brought her some relief—her first good night’s sleep since hearing of her father’s death.

At breakfast the next morning, Margaret asked to visit a few friends before leaving.

Mrs. Shaw hesitated. “My dear, I don’t think you have any friends here close enough to justify calling on them so soon—before church.”

“But today is my only chance. If Captain Lennox arrives this afternoon, and if we really must go tomorrow…”

“Oh, yes—we must go. I’m more convinced than ever this air harms you. You look pale and frail. Edith expects us, and she may be waiting. And you cannot be left alone, my dear. If you insist on visiting, I’ll go with you. Dixon can arrange a coach.”

So Mrs. Shaw accompanied her, along with the maid, to ensure shawls and cushions were properly managed. Margaret’s face remained solemn, untouched by the effort of such a journey. She dreaded one stop above all—Nicholas Higgins’ cottage. She hoped her aunt might refuse to alight, especially given the damp, wind-swept court where wet clothes flapped between houses.

But Mrs. Shaw, torn between comfort and propriety, chose comfort. With many warnings about catching cold, she allowed the visit.

Nicholas was out. Only Mary and a few of the Boucher children were home. Margaret regretted not timing her visit better. Mary, though kind-hearted, had a blunt mind. As soon as she understood why Margaret came, she burst into tears, sobbing uncontrollably. Margaret could not speak—her words dissolved in the flood of emotion. She could only offer vague hopes of future meetings, and ask Mary to tell Nicholas how much she wished he could come see her after work.

As she turned to leave, she paused. “I’d like something small to remember Bessy by.”

Mary’s face lit up. “Oh, take something better—this cup only cost fourpence!”

“That will do, thank you,” Margaret said, and hurried away, while Mary smiled, pleased to give.

“Now to Mrs. Thornton’s,” Margaret thought. “It must be done.”

But the thought made her stiffen. She struggled to explain to her aunt who Mrs. Thornton was and why she wished to bid farewell.

They were shown into the drawing-room, where a fire had just been lit. Mrs. Shaw wrapped herself tightly in her shawl and shivered. “What an icy room!” she muttered.

They waited. When Mrs. Thornton entered, she was softer than usual—perhaps because Margaret was leaving. She remembered not only Margaret’s endurance, but her spirit. Her manner held a rare tenderness as she saw the pale, tear-streaked face and the quiver in Margaret’s voice.

“Allow me to introduce my aunt, Mrs. Shaw. I’m leaving Milton tomorrow. I wanted to see you once more—to apologize for my behavior last time we met, and to say I know you meant well, however much we misunderstood each other.”

Mrs. Shaw looked puzzled—apologies for rudeness, gratitude for kindness? Unusual.

Mrs. Thornton replied, “Miss Hale, I am glad you acknowledge my intentions. I acted as I believed was my duty. I always sought to be a friend to you.”

“And,” Margaret said, blushing, “will you believe me when I say that though I cannot—nor choose to—explain my actions, I did not behave as you imagined?”

Her voice was gentle, her eyes pleading. For once, Mrs. Thornton was moved—by the charm Margaret had always possessed, yet which she had refused to admit.

“Yes, I believe you. Let us say no more.”

“Where will you live?” Mrs. Thornton asked.

“With my aunt,” Margaret replied.

“My niece will live with me in Harley Street. She is almost like a daughter,” Mrs. Shaw said, looking proudly at Margaret. “And I am grateful for any kindness shown her. Should you and your husband ever come to London, my son and daughter—Captain and Mrs. Lennox—will join me in welcoming you.”

Mrs. Thornton thought silently: Margaret hadn’t explained the relationship between Mr. and Mrs. Thornton—nor had she mentioned that Mr. Thornton was her son. She answered curtly: “My husband is dead. Mr. Thornton is my son. I never go to London. Your offers are kind, but I shall not be able to accept them.”

At that moment, Mr. Thornton entered. He had just returned from Oxford. His mourning suit marked the reason for his absence.

“John,” his mother said, “this is Mrs. Shaw, Miss Hale’s aunt. I’m sorry to say, Miss Hale is bidding us farewell.”

“You are going?” he asked, quietly.

“Yes,” Margaret said. “We leave tomorrow.”

“My son-in-law arrives this evening,” Mrs. Shaw added.

Mr. Thornton turned away, as if absorbed in something on the table—perhaps a letter he had just noticed. He didn’t even seem aware when they rose to leave. But as they reached the door, he stepped forward to help Mrs. Shaw into the carriage.

They stood side by side on the doorstep. The memory of the riot flooded both their minds—the chaos, the speeches, her fierce declaration that no man in that crowd mattered less to her than he did. Now, seeing her pale beauty and stony composure, he clenched his jaw.

“No,” he whispered. “I tested it once—and lost it all. Let her go—with her cold heart and her beauty. She fears I’ll say something she cannot endure. Let her go. Beauty and heiress as she may be, she will find it hard to meet a truer heart than mine. Let her go.”

His voice held no sorrow, no emotion. The hand she offered was taken firmly, then dropped as if it were nothing.

No one in the house saw him again that day. He said he was busy.

Margaret was utterly spent by the visits. Her aunt watched over her, sighing “I-told-you-so” with tender concern. Dixon declared she looked as ill as the day she heard of her father’s death. They debated delaying the journey—but when Mrs. Shaw suggested it, Margaret writhed in pain and said, “Oh, let us go. I cannot wait. I won’t recover here. I want to forget.”

The arrangements continued. Captain Lennox arrived, bringing news of Edith and the baby. His calm, indifferent talk—though kind—soothed Margaret. She grew stronger. By the time Nicholas Higgins came, she was able to sit quietly in her room.

“Eh!” he said, stepping in. “To think the old gentleman passed so suddenly! I nearly fell over when I heard. ‘Mr. Hale?’ I asked. ‘The parson?’ ‘Ay,’ they said. ‘Then,’ I replied, ‘there’s a better man gone than ever lived on this earth!’ I came to see you, to tell you how sorry I was—but the women in the kitchen wouldn’t let me in. Said you were ill. Butter me, but you don’t look like the same girl. And you’re going to be a grand lady in London, aren’t you?”

“Not a grand lady,” Margaret said, half-smiling.

“Well,” Higgins continued, “Thornton said the other day, ‘Higgins, have you seen Miss Hale?’ ‘No,’ I said, ‘a pack of women won’t let me near her. But I’ll wait.’ We’ve known each other well enough. And I won’t pretend I’m not grieved for the old gentleman—just because I can’t get to tell you so.”

“And he said, ‘You won’t have long to try. She’s not staying a day longer than necessary. She’s got rich relations taking her away. We shan’t see her again.’”

“Measter,” I said, “if I don’t see her before she goes, I’ll make it my business to come up to London next Whitsuntide. I won’t be stopped by any relation. But bless you, I knew you’d come. I only pretended I thought you might leave without seeing me.”

“You’re quite right,” Margaret said. “You only do me justice. And you’ll not forget me, I’m sure. If no one else remembers me, I know you will—and papa too. You know how gentle and kind he was. Look, Higgins—here is his Bible. I’ve kept it for you. I can hardly spare it, but I know he’d want you to have it. I’m sure you’ll care for it and study it—for his sake.”

“You may say that,” Higgins replied. “If it were the devil’s own writing, and you asked me to read it for your sake and the old gentleman’s, I’d do it. What’s this, wench? I’m not taking your money—don’t think it. We’ve been honest friends—no coin passed between us.”

“For the children—for Boucher’s children,” Margaret said quickly. “They may need it. You’ve no right to refuse it for them. I wouldn’t give you a penny,” she added, smiling. “Don’t think there’s any for you.”

“Well, wench,” Higgins said, “I can only say—Bless you! And bless you!—and amen.”
