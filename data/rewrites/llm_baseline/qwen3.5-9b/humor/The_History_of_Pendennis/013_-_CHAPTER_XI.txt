The Major and Captain Costigan were veterans of the field, seasoned men who faced enemies without losing their composure. However, the rest of the gathering in Cos's sitting-room found themselves somewhat rattled by Pendennis's sudden appearance. Miss Fotheringay's heart, usually steady, began to flutter; a deep, healthy flush colored her cheeks as Lieutenant Sir Derby Oaks fixed her with a scowl.

Perched in the window-seat, a little crooked old man who had been trying to transcribe theatre music while watching the two gentlemen fence—a spectacle of stamping and jumping that had forced him to abandon his pen—looked up eagerly at the newcomer. As the Major, distinguished by his impeccably blacked boots, swept into the room offering the most graceful bows to everyone, the old man leaned forward. "Me daughter—me friend, Mr. Bows—me gallant young pupil and friend, I may call 'em, Sir Derby Oaks," announced Costigan, waving his hand grandly to introduce the trio. "In one moment, Meejor, I'm your humble servant!"

With surprising speed, the energetic Captain dashed into his adjoining bedroom. In less than a minute, he transformed himself: twisting his lank hair with an ancient brush, tearing off his worn stock to reveal a fresh one crafted by Emily, donning a crisp new collar, and slipping into a handsome coat ordered specifically for Miss Fotheringay's benefit. Following him, Sir Derby emerged from the same chamber, encasing his large frame in a tiny shell-jacket that fit so snugly it looked ridiculous, yet which Miss Fotheringay, Costigan, and even poor Pen admired immensely.

While the actress and the new arrival exchanged pleasantries regarding the weather, Costigan returned, now sporting his new "shoot" jacket. "I needn't apologize to ye, Meejor," he said in his richest, most courteous tone, "for receiving ye in me shirt-sleeves."

"An old soldier can't be better employed than in teaching a young one the use of his sword," the Major replied gallantly. "I recall hearing in old times that you wielded yours with skill, Captain Costigan."

"What? Ye've heard of Jack Costigan, Major?" the other exclaimed, feigning great surprise. The Major had indeed pried details from his nephew about this Irish officer. Whether he truly remembered the man or merely relied on second-hand gossip was unclear, but Major Pendennis was a man of honor and veracity. He stated firmly that he perfectly recalled meeting Mr. Costigan and hearing him sing at Sir Richard Strachan's table during the Walcheren campaign.

At this revelation, delivered with such bland cordiality, Bows looked up, utterly bewildered. "But we will discuss these matters another time," the Major continued, perhaps wishing to avoid committing himself further. "It is to Miss Fotheringay I have come today to pay my respects." He executed another bow so courtly and gracious that, if she had been a duchess, he could hardly have improved upon it.

"I had heard of your performances from my nephew, madam," the Major added. "Who raves about you, as I believe you know well. But Arthur is but a boy, a wild enthusiast whose opinions one must not take literally. I confess I was anxious to judge for myself. Permit me to say your performance delighted and astonished me. I have seen our finest actresses, and on my word, I think you surpass them all. You are as majestic as Mrs. Siddons."

"Faith, I always said so," Costigan winked at his daughter. "Major, take a chair."

Milly rose, retrieved an unripped satin garment from the only empty seat, and wheeled the chair toward the Major with one of her finest curtsies. "You are as pathetic as Miss O'Neill," the Major continued, settling in. "Your songs remind me of Mrs. Jordan in her prime, Captain Costigan; your manner evokes Mars. Have you ever seen the Mars, Miss Fotheringay?"

"There were two Mahers in Crow Street," remarked Miss Emily. "Fanny was decent enough, but Biddy wasn't much."

"Sure, the Major means the god of war, Milly, my dear," interjected the parent.

"It is not that Mars I meant, though Venus may be pardoned for thinking about him," the Major replied, directing a smile squarely at Sir Derby Oaks, who had re-entered in his shell-jacket. Yet, the lady failed to grasp the words, and the compliment did nothing to soothe Sir Derby. Far from pleased, the young baronet received it with sullen stiffness, scowling uneasily at Miss Fotheringay with an expression that clearly asked, *What the deuce is this man doing here?*

Major Pendennis was not in the least annoyed by the gentleman's ill humor; on the contrary, it delighted him. "So," he thought, "a rival is in the field." He silently vowed that Sir Derby might become not only a rival but a victor in this love match involving himself and Pen.

"I fear I interrupted your fencing lesson," the Major said aloud. "But my stay in Chatteris is brief, and I was eager to introduce myself to my old fellow-campaigner, Captain Costigan, and to see a lady who had charmed me so much from the stage. I was not the only man smitten last night, Miss Fotheringay—if I may call you so, though your family name is ancient and noble. There was a reverend friend of mine who went home in raptures over Ophelia; and I saw Sir Derby Oaks throw a bouquet that no actress ever deserved better. I should have brought one myself had I known what awaited me. Are not those the very flowers in a glass of water on the mantelpiece yonder?"

"I am very fond of flowers," Miss Fotheringay replied, casting a languishing glance at Sir Derby Oaks—but the Baronet remained sullen.

"Sweets to the sweet—isn't that the expression from the play?" Mr. Pendennis asked, determined to remain good-natured.

"'Pon my life, I don't know. Very likely it is. I ain't much of a literary man," answered Sir Derby.

"Is it possible?" the Major feigned surprise. "Then you don't inherit your father's love of letters, Sir Derby? He was a remarkably fine scholar, and I had the honor of knowing him well."

"Indeed," the other muttered, giving a sulky wag of his head.

"He saved my life," continued Pendennis.

"Did he now?" cried Miss Fotheringay, rolling her eyes first in surprise at the Major, then with gratitude toward Sir Derby—but the latter was immune to such glances. Far from being pleased that the Apothecary, his father, had saved Major Pendennis's life, the young man actually looked as if he wished the event had turned out differently.

"My father, I believe, was a very good doctor," the young gentleman said by way of reply. "I'm not in that line myself. I wish you good morning, sir. I've got an appointment—Cos, bye-bye—Miss Fotheringay, good morning."

Despite the young lady's imploring looks and appealing smiles, the Dragoon bowed stiffly out of the room. The clatter of his sabre echoed as he strode down the creaking stairs, followed by angry tones cursing little Tom Creed, who was playing in the passage, and whose peg-top Sir Derby had kicked away with an oath into the street.

The Major did not smile, though he had every reason to be amused. "Monstrously handsome young man that—as fine a-looking soldier as ever I saw," he told Costigan.

"A credit to the army and to human nature in general," answered Costigan. "A young man of refined manners, polite affability, and princely fortune. His table is sumptuous; he's adored in the regiment; and he rides sixteen stone."

"A perfect champion," said the Major, laughing. "No doubt all the ladies admire him."

"He's very well, in spite of his weight, now he's young," said Milly. "But he's no conversation."

"He's best on horseback," Mr. Bows noted.

On this, Milly replied that the Baronet had ridden third in the steeplechase on his horse Tareaways. The Major began to comprehend that the young lady herself lacked particular genius and wondered how she could act so well despite her stupidity.

Costigan, exercising Irish hospitality, pressed refreshments upon his guest. The Major, who was no hungrier than one would be after a Lord Mayor's dinner, declared he desired a biscuit and a glass of wine above all else, claiming he felt faint from fasting. He knew, however, that accepting small kindnesses flattered donors and ensured goodwill.

"Some of the old Madeira, Milly, love," Costigan winked at his child.

That lady, exchanging a glance of intelligence with her father, exited the room and descended the stairs, where she softly summoned her little emissary, Master Tommy Creed. Giving him a coin, she ordered him to rush to the Grapes for a pint of Madeira and the baker's for sixpence-worth of assorted biscuits, returning quickly so he might save two for himself.
