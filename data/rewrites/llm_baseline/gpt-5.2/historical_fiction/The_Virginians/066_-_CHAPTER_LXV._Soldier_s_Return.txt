Great Powers! will the vanity of men—of Frenchmen above all—never tire? A rear-guard cut off upon a strip of sand; a handful of brave fellows hunted down by ten thousand, and because Fortune, who always snaps at the hindmost, took her usual toll, the gentlemen at Paris must needs blaze powder at the Invalides and make their old stone walls clap and thunder, as if cannon-bursts could translate slaughter into glory.

I had once a mind to draw—true to the life—an honest picture of London in her own triumphs: the polished guns dragged in show by sunburnt tars; the horse-grenadiers prancing, their cuirasses ringing; the mob roaring, cheering, and filching handkerchiefs with equal fervour; our friends in some Fleet Street balcony craning forward, blessing the spectacle of British success. But since the French have been so vulgar as to mimic the Tower’s fireworks with their own, setting up St. Cas against our St. Malo, I scorn the stale comparison. I say Nolo, not Malo; and I am content—content, at least—if Harry Warrington has come back from one expedition and t’other with a whole skin.

He did come back. The sea air had tanned him to the hue of well-browned crust; the salt had stiffened the seams of his gaiters, and left, in the folds of his coat and hair, that sharp, honest smell of brine and tar which clings to a man longer than praise or blame. A ball had whistled close by his side-curl and spared it; the ocean had taken his stockings for its own a dozen times over, but had not swallowed his body. He had, it is true, shown the lapels of his coat to the enemy—those reverses which coats, like men, are made to wear—yet for as short a time as possible, withdrawing out of their sight as quick as might be.

They were at table when he told it: the Lambert parlour full of the soft domestic stir that goes on even while men speak of death. In the pauses of his narrative one heard the small music of life—china set down, a spoon clicking the rim of a bowl, the kettle’s low murmur like a contented creature on the hob; from the street beyond the window, a cart-wheel’s creak and the distant call of a seller crying his wares. Such sounds, homely and unconsidered, seemed to argue against the roar of cannon as if peace were the older language and war a brutal dialect that could never quite master a hearth.

“I can tell you,” said Harry, speaking with that plain exactness which belongs to men who have looked on a thing and do not embroider it, “we all had to run for it; and when our line broke, it was he who could get to the boats who was most lucky. The French horse and foot pursued us down to the sea, and were mingled among us, cutting our men down, and bayoneting them on the ground. Poor Armytage was shot in advance of me, and fell; and I took him up and staggered through the surf to a boat. It was lucky that the sailors in our boat weren’t afraid; for the shot were whistling about their ears, breaking the blades of their oars, and riddling their flag with shot; but the officer in command was as cool as if he had been drinking a bowl of punch at Portsmouth, which we had one on landing, I can promise you.”

At the word punch, Mrs. Lambert’s hand, which had been dividing a little triangle of candied peel for the syllabub, paused as though memory itself had tasted the thing: rum warmed with lemon and sugar, nutmeg grated fine as brown dust; the sort of bowl that, in happier hours, makes a company bold and merry. Yet Harry’s voice did not brighten; it was merely that soldier’s habit of fastening upon the familiar—upon a bowl on a table—when the mind has too much blood and shouting in it.

“Poor Sir John was less lucky than me,” he continued. “He never lived to reach the ship, and the service has lost a fine soldier, and Miss Howe a true gentleman to her husband. There must be these casualties, you see; and his brother gets the promotion—the baronetcy.”

“It is of the poor lady I am thinking,” says Miss Hetty Lambert—haply it is to her, and for her judgement, the volunteer chiefly tells his story—“and the King. Why did the King encourage Sir John Armytage to go? A gentleman could not refuse a command from such a quarter. And now the poor gentleman is dead! Oh, what a state his Majesty must be in!”

“I have no doubt his Majesty will be in a deep state of grief,” says papa, wagging his head; and there was in the old General’s voice that dry crackle which in a well-governed room is as audible as a snapped twig.

“Now you are laughing! Do you mean, sir, that when a gentleman dies in his service, almost at his feet, the King of England won’t feel for him?” Hetty asks, her colour rising quick as if some pepper had been put on her tongue. “If I thought that, I vow I would be for the Pretender!”

“The sauce-box would make a pretty little head for Temple Bar,” says the General, who could see her meaning behind her words, and knew well enough what tumult of remorse, consternation, and gratitude beat under the bodice that had driven Harry into danger and now thanked Heaven he came back. “No,” says he, “my dear. Were kings to weep for every soldier, what a life you would make for them! I think better of his Majesty than to suppose him so weak; and, if Miss Hester Lambert got her Pretender, I doubt whether she would be any the happier. That family was never famous for too much feeling.”

“But if the King sent Harry—I mean Sir John Armytage—actually to the war in which he lost his life, oughtn’t his Majesty to repent very much?” asks the young lady, catching herself, too late, on the name that mattered.

“If Harry had fallen, no doubt the court would have gone into mourning: as it is, gentlemen and ladies were in coloured clothes yesterday,” remarks the General.

“Why should we not make bonfires for a defeat, and put on sackcloth and ashes after a victory?” asks George Warrington, who sat opposite his brother, watching him with that quiet, elder-brother measure which is half pride and half rebuke. “I protest I don’t want to thank Heaven for helping us to burn the ships at Cherbourg.”

“Yes you do, George! Not that I have a right to speak, and you ain’t ever so much cleverer. But when your country wins you’re glad—I know I am. When I run away before Frenchmen I’m ashamed—I can’t help it, though I done it,” says Harry, and there was no swagger in the confession, only that grave honesty which is, in its way, courage. “It don’t seem to me right somehow that Englishmen should have to do it,” he added, very low.

George smiled; yet he did not choose to ask what, on the other hand, was the Frenchman’s opinion. Outside, the street’s noises went on: a hammer tapping, a dog barking, the faint chiming of a bell. How strange that men can lay waste a coast and then come home to listen to such small music, as though Providence had meant the world for kettles and conversations rather than cannon.

“’Tis a bad business,” continued Harry, “but ’tis lucky ’twas no worse. The story about the French is, that their Governor, the Duke of Aiguillon, was rather what you call a moistened chicken. Our whole retreat might have been cut off, only, to be sure, we ourselves were in a mighty hurry to move. The French local militia behaved famous, I am happy to say; and there was ever so many gentlemen volunteers with ’em, who showed, as they ought to do, in the front. They say the Chevalier of Tour d’Auvergne engaged in spite of the Duke of Aiguillon’s orders. Officers told us, who came off with a list of our prisoners and wounded to General Bligh and Lord Howe. He is a lord now, since the news came of his brother’s death to home, George. He is a brave fellow, whether lord or commoner.”

“And his sister, who was to have married poor Sir John Armytage, think what her state must be!” sighs Miss Hetty, who has grown of late so sentimental, and takes sorrow as some ladies take spices—eagerly, as if the stronger flavour made life truer.

“And his mother!” cries Mrs. Lambert. “Have you seen her ladyship’s address in the papers to the electors of Nottingham? ‘Lord Howe being now absent upon the publick service, and Lieutenant-Colonel Howe with his regiment at Louisbourg, it rests upon me to beg the favour of your votes and interests that Lieutenant-Colonel Howe may supply the place of his late brother as your representative in Parliament.’ Isn’t this a gallant woman?”

“A Laconic woman,” says George.

“How can sons help being brave who have been nursed by such a mother as that?” asks the General.

The two young men looked at each other.

“If one of us were to fall in defence of his country, we have a mother in Sparta who would think and write so too,” says George.

“If Sparta is anywhere Virginia way, I reckon we have,” remarks Mr. Harry, with that half-smile of his which does not quite know how to sit upon his face. “And to think that we should both of us have met the enemy, and both of us been whipped by him, brother!” he adds, pensively.

Hetty looks at him and cannot help seeing him only as he was—tottering through the water towards the boats, a comrade bleeding on his shoulder, the enemy pressing, the shot snapping and whining like angry wasps round his head. And it was she who drove him into the danger! Her words provoked him. He never rebukes her now he is returned. Except when asked, he scarcely speaks about his adventures at all. He is very grave and courteous with Hetty; with the rest of the family especially frank and tender. But those taunts of hers wounded him. “Little hand!” his looks and demeanour seem to say, “thou shouldst not have been lifted against me! It is ill to scorn any one, much more one who has been so devoted to you and all yours. I may not be over quick of wit, but in as far as the heart goes, I am the equal of the best, and the best of my heart your family has had.”

Harry was wrong—wrong in the judgement he had made of her steadiness, and wrong in the debt he thought her owing him; and yet his magnanimous endurance of the wrong served him to regain in Miss Hetty’s esteem that place which he had lost during the previous months’ inglorious idleness. The respect which the fair pay to the brave she gave him. She was no longer pert in her answers, or sarcastic in her observations regarding his conduct. In a word, she was a humiliated, an altered, an improved Miss Hetty.

And all the world seemed to change towards Harry, as he towards the world. He was no longer sulky and indolent: he no more desponded about himself, or defied his neighbours. The colonel of his regiment reported his behaviour as exemplary, and recommended him for one of the commissions vacated by the casualties during the expedition. Unlucky as its termination was, it at least was fortunate to him.

His brother-volunteers, when they came back to St. James’s Street, reported highly of his behaviour. These volunteers and their actions were the theme of everybody’s praise. Had he been a general commanding, and slain in the moment of victory, Sir John Armytage could scarce have had more sympathy than that which the nation showed him. The papers teemed with letters about him; and men of wit and sensibility vied with each other in composing epitaphs in his honour. The fate of his affianced bride was bewailed. She was, as we have said, the sister of the brave Commodore who had just returned from this unfortunate expedition, and succeeded to the title of his elder brother—an officer as gallant as himself—who had just fallen in America.

My Lord Howe was heard to speak in special praise of Mr. Warrington; and so Harry had a handsome share of the fashion and favour which the town now bestowed on the volunteers. Doubtless there were thousands of men employed who were as good as they; but the English ever love their gentlemen, and love that they should distinguish themselves; and these volunteers were voted Paladins and heroes by common accord. As our young noblemen will, they accepted their popularity very affably. White’s and Almack’s illuminated when they returned; St. James’s embraced its young knights.

Harry was restored to full favour amongst them. Their hands were held out eagerly to him again. Even his relations congratulated him; and there came a letter from Castlewood, whither Aunt Bernstein had by this time betaken herself, containing praises of his valour, and a pretty little bank-bill, as a token of his affectionate aunt’s approbation. This was under my Lord Castlewood’s frank, who sent his regards to both his kinsmen, and an offer of the hospitality of his country-house, if they were minded to come to him.

And besides this, there came to him a private letter through the post—not very well spelt, but in a handwriting which Harry smiled to see again—in which his affectionate cousin, Maria Esmond, told him she always loved to hear his praises (which were in everybody’s mouth now), and sympathised in his good or evil fortune; and that, whatever occurred to him, she begged to keep a little place in his heart. Parson Sampson, she wrote, had preached a beautiful sermon about the horrors of war, and the noble actions of men who volunteered to face battle and danger in the service of their country. Indeed, the chaplain wrote himself, presently, a letter full of enthusiasm, in which he saluted Mr. Harry as his friend, his benefactor, his glorious hero.

Even Sir Miles Warrington despatched a basket of game from Norfolk: and one bird (shot sitting), with love to my cousin, had a string and paper round the leg, and was sent as the first victim of young Miles’s fowling-piece. When the hamper was opened in Southampton Row, the room filled at once with the green, woodland smell of feathers and cold earth; and Mrs. Lambert, ever mistress of the house’s small ceremonies, began at once to contrive how it should be roasted so the skin might crackle and the flesh stay juicy—how a pinch of salt, rubbed in with a firm hand, and a little butter slipped under the breast, could turn even a dead creature into comfort for the living. Such is the way of households: they season grief with supper, and keep memory alive by feeding it.

And presently, with joy beaming in his countenance, Mr. Lambert came to visit his young friends at their lodgings, and announced that Mr. Henry Warrington was forthwith to be gazetted as Ensign in the Second Battalion of Kingsley’s, the 20th Regiment, which had been engaged in the campaign, and which now at this time was formed into a separate regiment, the 67th. Its colonel was not with his regiment during its expedition to Brittany. He was away at Cape Breton, and was engaged in capturing those guns at Louisbourg, of which the arrival in England had caused such exultation.
