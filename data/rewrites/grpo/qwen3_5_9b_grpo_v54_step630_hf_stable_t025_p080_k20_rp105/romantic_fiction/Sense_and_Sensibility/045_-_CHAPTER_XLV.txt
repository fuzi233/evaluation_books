For a long time after Willoughby's carriage had vanished, Elinor remained paralyzed by a chaotic storm of conflicting thoughts, all converging on a single, heavy note of sorrow. She could not summon the energy to turn her mind to her sister. The man she had despised only moments prior as the epitome of worthlessness now stirred a strange, aching pity within her. Despite his glaring faults, she found herself imagining his eternal separation from her family with a tenderness that she quickly admitted was born less of his virtues than of his desperate plight. She recognized that his hold on her mind was fueled by charms that reason demanded she ignore: his striking appearance, his open and affectionate demeanor, and that burning, albeit misguided, love for Marianne. Yet, she felt the pull of these influences long before she could muster the strength to resist them.

When she finally returned to the sleeping Marianne, she found her just awakening, revitalized by a deep, restorative slumber. Elinor's heart swelled with emotion. The weight of the past, the precariousness of the present, the memory of Willoughby's visit, Marianne's survival, and the imminent arrival of her mother created a whirlwind of agitation that banished all signs of exhaustion. Her only fear was revealing her turmoil to her sister. But this fear lasted barely half an hour before another carriage rattled up the drive. Driven by a desperate need to spare her mother further agony, Elinor rushed to the hall and met Mrs. Dashwood at the threshold, steadying her as she stumbled inside.

Mrs. Dashwood, whose terror upon approaching the house had nearly convinced her that Marianne was dead, possessed no breath for questions or greetings. Instead, Elinor, seizing the moment, poured out the joyful news. Her mother caught the words with her characteristic fervor, and in an instant, the crushing weight of fear was replaced by overwhelming elation. Supported between her daughter and Colonel Brandon, she wept tears of relief, embracing Elinor repeatedly before turning to press the Colonel's hand with a gaze that spoke of profound gratitude and the certainty that he shared in their bliss. He offered his congratulations in a silence even deeper than hers.

Once her mother regained her composure, her sole desire was to see Marianne. Within minutes, she was beside her beloved child, who had become even dearer to her through the trials of absence, suffering, and danger. Elinor's own delight at the reunion was tempered by a fear that the emotional intensity might rob Marianne of needed rest. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood, pragmatic in the face of mortal peril, remained calm. Satisfied by her mother's presence and too weak for conversation, Marianne submitted willingly to the quiet prescribed by the nurses. Mrs. Dashwood insisted on staying by her side through the night, while Elinor, yielding to her mother's plea, retired to her room.

However, the rest that a sleepless night and hours of agonizing anxiety demanded was denied her by a restless spirit. "Poor Willoughby" became a constant refrain in her thoughts. She found herself torn between a desire to hear his defense and a self-reproach for judging him so harshly before. The promise she had made to tell Marianne the truth now loomed as a terrifying prospect; she dreaded the impact it might have on her sister's fragile heart, doubting whether Marianne could ever find happiness again. For a fleeting, dark moment, she wished Willoughby were a widower. Then, recalling Colonel Brandon, she chastised herself, realizing that the reward of her sister's affection belonged to Brandon's enduring constancy, not her rival's fleeting passion, and praying fervently against the death of Mrs. Willoughby.

The shock of Colonel Brandon's mission at Barton had been significantly softened for Mrs. Dashwood by her own preceding panic. So consumed was she with worry for Marianne that she had already resolved to travel to Cleveland that very day, settling her arrangements before Brandon even arrived. Consequently, the Careys were expected any moment to escort Margaret away, as her mother refused to take her to a place potentially tainted by infection.

Marianne improved daily, and the radiant cheerfulness of Mrs. Dashwood's countenance declared her, as she repeatedly proclaimed, to be one of the happiest women alive. Elinor could not hear these declarations without occasionally wondering if her mother remembered Edward. But Mrs. Dashwood, trusting Elinor's temperate report of her own disappointment, was swept away by the exuberance of her joy, focusing solely on what would amplify it. Marianne had been rescued from a danger in which her mother's own poor judgment regarding the attachment to Willoughby had played a part; yet, in her recovery, she discovered another source of joy that Elinor had not anticipated.

It was revealed during a rare moment of privacy between them.

"At last we are alone, my Elinor. You do not yet know the full extent of my happiness. Colonel Brandon loves Marianne. He has told me so himself."

Her daughter, feeling a complex mix of pleasure and pain, surprise and lack thereof, listened in silent attention.

"You are never like me, dear Elinor; I should wonder at your composure now. If I had sat down to wish for any possible good for my family, I would have fixed on Colonel Brandon marrying one of you as the most desirable outcome. And I believe Marianne will be the happier of the two."

Elinor was half-inclined to ask for the reasoning behind this, satisfied that no argument based on an impartial assessment of their ages, characters, or feelings could support it. However, her mother was always carried away by her imagination on such subjects. Instead of an inquiry, Elinor merely smiled and let the thought pass.

"He opened his whole heart to me yesterday as we traveled. It came quite unawares, quite undesignedly. I, you may well believe, could talk of nothing but my child; he could not conceal his distress. I saw that it equaled my own, and he perhaps, thinking that mere friendship, as the world goes, would not justify such warm sympathy—or rather, not thinking at all, I suppose—gave way to irresistible feelings and made me acquainted with his earnest, tender, constant affection for Marianne. He has loved her, my Elinor, ever since the first moment he saw her."

Here, Elinor perceived—not the actual words or professions of Colonel Brandon, but the natural embellishments of her mother's active fancy, which shaped every detail to suit her delight.

"His regard for her infinitely surpasses anything Willoughby ever felt or feigned. It is far warmer, far more sincere, or whatever we are to call it. It has persisted through all knowledge of dear Marianne's unhappy infatuation for that worthless young man! Without selfishness, without encouraging false hope! Could he have seen her happy with another? Such a noble mind! Such openness, such sincerity! No one can be deceived by him."

"Colonel Brandon's character," said Elinor, "as an excellent man, is well established."

"I know it is," replied her mother seriously, "or after such a warning, I should be the last to encourage such affection, or even to be pleased by it. But his coming for me as he did, with such active, such ready friendship, is enough to prove him one of the worthiest of men."

"His character, however," answered Elinor, "does not rest on one act of kindness, to which his affection for Marianne, were humanity out of the question, would have prompted him. To Mrs. Jennings, to the Middletons, he has been long and intimately known; they equally love and respect him. Even my own knowledge of him, though recently acquired, is considerable. I value and esteem him so highly that if Marianne can be happy with him, I shall be as ready as yourself to consider our connection the greatest blessing in the world. What answer did you give him? Did you allow him to hope?"

"Oh! my love, I could not then speak of hope to him or to myself. Marianne might have been dying at that moment. But he did not ask for hope or encouragement. His was an involuntary confidence, an irrepressible effusion to a soothing friend, not an application to a parent. Yet, after a time, I did say—for at first I was quite overcome—that if she lived, as I trusted she might, my greatest happiness would lie in promoting their marriage. Since our arrival, since our delightful security, I have repeated it to him more fully, giving him every encouragement in my power. I tell him, a very little time will do everything; Marianne's heart is not to be wasted forever on such a man as Willoughby. His own merits must soon secure it."

"To judge from the Colonel's spirits, however, you have not yet made him equally sanguine."

"No. He thinks Marianne's affection is too deeply rooted for any change, even under a great length of time. And even supposing her heart were free again, he is too diffident of himself to believe that, with such a difference in age and disposition, he could ever attach her. There, however, he is quite mistaken. His age is only slightly beyond hers, making it an advantage that ensures his character and principles are fixed. His disposition, I am well convinced, is exactly the one to make your sister happy. And his person, his manners too, are all in his favor. My partiality does not blind me; he certainly is not so handsome as Willoughby; but at the same time, there is something much more pleasing in his countenance. There was always something in Willoughby's eyes at times which I did not like."

Elinor could not remember it; but her mother, without waiting for her assent, continued.

"And his manners, the Colonel's manners, are not only more pleasing to me than Willoughby's ever were, but they are of a kind I well know to be more solidly attaching to Marianne. Their gentleness, their genuine attention to others, and their manly, unstudied simplicity are much more accordant with her real disposition than the liveliness, often artificial and often ill-timed, of the other. I am very sure myself that had Willoughby turned out as really amiable as he has proved himself the contrary, Marianne would yet never have been so happy with him as she will be with Colonel Brandon."

She paused. Her daughter could not quite agree with her, but her dissent went unheard and thus caused no offense.

"At Delaford, she will be within an easy distance of me," added Mrs. Dashwood, "even if I remain at Barton. And in all probability—for I hear it is a large village—indeed there certainly must be some small house or cottage close by that would suit us quite as well as our present situation."

Poor Elinor! Here was a new scheme to send her to Delaford! But her spirit was stubborn.

"His fortune too! For at my time of life, you know, everybody cares about that; and though I neither know nor desire to know what it really is, I am sure it must be a good one."

Their conversation was interrupted by the entrance of a third person, and Elinor withdrew to think it all over in private, to wish success to her friend, and yet, in wishing it, to feel a pang for Willoughby.
