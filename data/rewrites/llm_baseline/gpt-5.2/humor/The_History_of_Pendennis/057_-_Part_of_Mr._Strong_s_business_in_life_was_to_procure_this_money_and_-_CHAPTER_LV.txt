Since Arthur’s illness had brought him to that helpless state in which a man is grateful for a glass of water and yet incapable of finding his own other boot, Mrs. Pendennis had taken entire possession of him—of his pillows, his pulses, his draughts, his temperature, his conscience, and (what is far more intimate than a pulse) his drawers and closets. There was nothing in those domestic repositories which her motherly hand did not rearrange with a kind of tender ferocity: shirts had their buttons restored as if to moral respectability; stockings were darned with the solemnity of little funerals; collars were re-folded until they looked like the very emblem of propriety.

And then—among these humble and fragrant linens, where soap and lavender ought to have reigned alone—there lay letters.

It is astonishing how many sorts of paper can breed in a young gentleman’s chamber. There are the quiet, well-meaning envelopes from tradesmen, which smell faintly of leather and ledger: bootmakers with their beseeching “next Saturday” and hatters with their firm belief that a man’s head may be covered on credit, but his conscience must be settled in cash. Pen, being free-handed and careless, had a little colony of such missives; not enough to ruin him, but quite enough to ruin his mother’s digestion.

So she did what a vigilant commander does in a beleaguered city: she seized the gates. The brass knocker on the inner door—the postman’s cheerful instrument of terror—was actually unscrewed, lest the startling rat-tat should disturb her patient’s sleep. The very sound of a letter arriving is a kind of kitchen-knife to an uneasy conscience. Blessed, indeed, is the man who can hear the postman and think of nothing but affection. The good are made brisk by that knock; the guilty feel as if the world itself were tapping them on the shoulder.

Mrs. Pendennis intercepted everything. She paid some of the accounts out of her own small hoard—those savings pinched from ribbons and trifles, stored up by a simplicity that had the look of parsimony, though it came of love. If a young man’s correspondence is ever to be surrendered to parental hands, it is usually at the price of having one’s debts quietly slain and buried.

But what she wanted most was not a bill.

She wanted the key to the dreadful Fanny Bolton mystery—an anxiety she had never once confessed to Arthur, and yet which lay in her mind like an undigested crust: always there, always scratching. She opened other letters only to get at that one—like a cook lifting pot-lids in a panic, not because she expects a stew, but because she smells smoke.

And now, out at Richmond, with the river murmuring its mild domestic music beyond the windows, and the terrace outside full of summer light, she could bear herself no longer. She drew Major Pendennis into consultation, like a nervous housewife dragging the family lawyer into the pantry and whispering over the jam.

Pen and Warrington were on the terrace in the sunshine, talking and laughing like men who have never had a letter to dread; and Helen, holding her terrors as one holds a hot dish one is afraid to drop, addressed her brother-in-law.

When she asked what she ought to do—whether he knew anything—Major Pendennis puckered his face so that it was impossible to tell whether he was about to pronounce wisdom or enjoy himself. His little eyes gave her one queer glance, then dropped demurely.

“My dear, good creature,” he said, “I don’t know anything about it; and I don’t wish to know anything about it; and, as you ask my opinion, I think you had best know nothing about it too. Young men will be young men; begad, and if you think our boy is a Jo—”

“Pray, spare me this!” Helen cut in, growing suddenly stately, as if virtue itself had stiffened her spine.

“My dear creature, I did not commence the conversation,” the Major returned, with a bow that looked as if it had been ironed.

“I can’t bear to hear such a sin—such a dreadful sin—spoken of in such a way,” she cried; tears started, not only from sorrow but from sheer vexation. “I can’t bear to think that my boy should commit such a crime. I wish he had died, almost, before he had done it. It is breaking my heart, Major Pendennis, to think that his father’s son—my child—whom I remember so good—oh, so good, and full of honour!—should be fallen so dreadfully low, as to—as to—”

“As to flirt with a little grisette, my dear creature?” said the Major. “Egad, if all the mothers in England broke their hearts because—Nay, nay. Don’t cry. I can’t bear tears—I never could. But how do we know that anything serious has happened? Has Arthur said anything?”

“His silence confirms it,” sobbed Mrs. Pendennis, behind her pocket-handkerchief, as if the cambric were a confessional grille.

“Not at all,” said the Major, slipping in a little worldly truth as delicately as a cook slips anchovy into a sauce. “There are subjects, my dear, about which a young fellow cannot surely talk to his mamma.”

“She has written to him,” Helen blurted.

“What, before he was ill? Nothing more likely.”

“No, since,” gasped the lady. “Not before; that is, I don’t think so—that is, I—”

“Only since; and you have—yes, I understand,” said the Major, with the quick comprehension of a man who has seen many cupboards opened in his time. “I suppose when he was too ill to read his own correspondence, you took charge of it?”

“I am the most unhappy mother in the world,” she cried.

“The most unhappy mother in the world, because your son is a man and not a hermit!” the Major said, almost briskly. “Have a care, my dear sister. If you have suppressed any letters to him, you may have done yourself a great injury; and if I know anything of Arthur’s spirit, you may cause a difference between him and you which you’ll rue all your life—a difference a dev’lish deal more important, my good madam, than the little—little—trumpery cause which originated it.”

“There was only one letter,” Helen burst out—“only a very little one—only a few words. Here it is. Oh—how can you, how can you speak so?”

When she said “only a very little one,” Major Pendennis had to exercise all the discipline of old campaigners not to laugh outright; for the smallest letter can contain enough to set a whole household clattering like an overturned tray. But he composed his features into gravity.

“I recommend you,” he said, “if you can, to seal it up—those letters ain’t unfrequently sealed with wafers—and to put it amongst Pen’s other letters, and let him have them when he calls for them. Or if we can’t seal it, we mistook it for a bill.”

“I can’t tell my son a lie,” said the widow, in the tone of one who would rather be executed than equivocal.

The truth was, that the little paper had poisoned her bag for two days. Martha had brought it; Helen had never seen Fanny Bolton’s handwriting, but the instant the letter lay in her palm she knew its author—as surely as a cook knows the smell of an onion the moment the skin is nicked. She had been watching for it daily, her nerves pricked to attention by every footstep on the stair. She had opened other letters simply as one opens cupboards in search of a single forbidden jar.

Now she held the thing out to the Major.

He read only the address in the timid, sprawling hand—“Arther Pendennis, Esq.”—and his lip curled, not so much at the girl as at the whole human comedy.

“No, my dear,” he said, “I won’t read any more. But you who have read it may tell me what it contains—only prayers for his health in bad spelling, and a desire to see him? Well, there’s no harm in that. And as you ask me—” Here he grew demure, as if about to confess to a pastry-theft. “—I don’t mind telling you that Morgan, my man, has made some inquiries; and my friend Doctor Goodenough looked into it; and it appears this person was greatly smitten with Arthur; that he paid for her and took her to Vauxhall Gardens, as Morgan heard from an old acquaintance of ours—an Irish gentleman, who was very nearly once having the honour of being the—from an Irishman, in fact. The girl’s father, a violent man of intoxicated habits, has beaten her mother; the mother persists in declaring the daughter entirely innocent to her husband, while telling Goodenough that Arthur has acted like a brute to the child. So you see the story remains a mystery. Will you have it cleared up? I have but to ask Pen, and he will tell me at once—he is as honourable a man as ever lived.”

“Honourable!” Helen repeated, with a bitterness that made the word taste like burnt sugar. “Oh, brother, what is this you call honour? If my boy has been guilty, he must marry her. I would go down on my knees and pray him to do so.”

“Good God! are you mad?” Major Pendennis cried, loud enough to startle the very upholstery. And then—because old memories and old fears can move faster than any carriage—he saw in a flash what such a prayer might do. Arthur was obstinate enough, wild enough, and chivalrous enough (in the dangerous way) to commit any folly where a woman was concerned.

“My dear sister,” he continued, after a pause in which his mind had galloped, “have you lost your senses? What right have we to suppose that anything has passed between this girl and him? Let’s see the letter.”

He took it then, and let his eye run over it quickly—over the misspellings that offended Helen as if they were stains, over the little broken phrases that betrayed a heart without education but not without feeling.

“Her heart is breaking; pray, pray, write to me—home unhappy—unkind father—your nurse—poor little Fanny,” he muttered, half-mocking the orthography and half acknowledging the pathos. “But, good heavens! what is there in this? Only that the little devil is making love to him still. Why, she didn’t come into his chambers until he was so delirious he didn’t know her. What-d’you-call-’em—Flanagan, the laundress—told Morgan so. She came in company of an old fellow, old Mr. Bows, who came most kindly down to Stillbrook and brought me away—by the way, I left him in the cab and never paid the fare; dev’lish kind it was of him. No, there’s nothing in the story.”

“Do you think so? Thank Heaven—thank God!” Helen cried, a laugh and a sob tumbling into one another. “I’ll take the letter to Arthur and ask him now. Look at him there—on the terrace with Mr. Warrington—talking to some children. My boy was always fond of children. He’s innocent, thank God—thank God! Let me go to him.”

Old Pendennis had his own opinion—an opinion he kept as neatly folded as a dress-shirt. He had argued “not guilty” briskly enough; but he judged Arthur by what Arthur’s uncle would have done with a pretty girl, a summer evening, and a ticket to Vauxhall. If Helen went to Arthur and Arthur spoke truth (as rascals will, when cornered), it would spoil everything. So the old diplomatist tried once more, turning persuasion as deftly as one turns an omelette—without letting the tender part break.

“My dear, good soul,” he said, taking her hand and kissing it, “as your son has not acquainted you with this affair, think if you have any right to examine it. As you believe him to be a man of honour, what right have you to doubt his honour in this instance? Who is his accuser? An anonymous scoundrel who brings no specific charge. If there were any such, wouldn’t the girl’s parents have come forward? He is not called upon to rebut, nor you to entertain, an anonymous accusation; and as for believing him guilty because a girl of that rank happened to be in his rooms acting as nurse—begad you might as well insist upon his marrying that dem’d old Irish gin-drinking laundress, Mrs. Flanagan.”

Mrs. Pendennis burst out laughing through her tears—so sudden and helpless a laugh that it seemed as if the very idea of Mrs. Flanagan in bridal white had driven out the devil for the moment.

“Marry Mrs. Flanagan, by Ged!” the Major continued, tapping her slender hand as if sealing the treaty. “No. The boy has told you nothing, and you know nothing. The boy is innocent—of course. And what is the course for us to pursue? Suppose he is attached to this girl—don’t look sad again, it’s merely a supposition—and begad a young fellow may have an attachment, mayn’t he? Directly he gets well he will be at her again.”

“He must come home! We must go off directly to Fairoaks,” Helen cried, snatching at the safety of old rooms and familiar fields as if they were a shawl.

“My good creature, he’ll bore himself to death at Fairoaks,” said the Major. “He’ll have nothing to do but think about his passion there. There’s no place in the world for making a little passion into a big one like a lonely country-house where there’s nothing to do. We must occupy him: amuse him: take him abroad. He’s never been abroad except to Paris for a lark. We must travel a little. He must have a nurse with him—Goodenough says he had a dev’lish narrow squeak of it (don’t look frightened)—and so you must come and watch; and I suppose you’ll take Miss Bell; and I should like to ask Warrington to come. Arthur’s dev’lish fond of Warrington. He can’t do without Warrington. Warrington’s family is one of the oldest in England, and he’s one of the best young fellows I ever met. I like him exceedingly.”

“Does Mr. Warrington know anything about this—this affair?” Helen asked. “He had been away, I know, two months before it happened; Pen wrote me so.”

“Not a word—not a word,” the Major cried, with a touch too much eagerness, as if he were slamming a cupboard door on a rattling pan. “I’ve asked him. I’ve pumped him. He never heard of the transaction—never. I pledge you my word. And I think you had much best not talk to him about it—much best not—of course not. The subject is delicate and painful.”

The widow pressed her brother’s hand with all the gratitude of a woman who has been starving on worry and suddenly gets a morsel of comfort.

“Thank you, brother,” she said. “You have been very, very kind. You have given me a great deal of comfort. I’ll go to my room and think of what you have said. This illness and these—these emotions—have agitated me a great deal; and I’m not very strong, you know. But I’ll go and thank God that my boy is innocent. He is innocent. Isn’t he, sir?”

“Yes, my dearest creature, yes,” said the old fellow, kissing her affectionately, softened in spite of himself—moved by her tenderness, and yet seasoning it with a private, worldly scorn. When she was gone he looked after her and murmured, “Innocent! I’d swear till I was black in the face he was innocent, rather than give that good soul pain.”

Then, having won his little battle, the fatigued warrior threw himself on the sofa, laid his yellow silk pocket-handkerchief over his face like a curtain at the end of a play, and took a snug nap, snoring with a refreshing regularity—an amiable domestic bass note against the lighter treble of the summer day.

Outside, the terrace was full of sun. Pen and Warrington dawdled away the hours, very happy, and Pen (at least) very talkative. He unfolded to George an immense plan for a new novel and a new tragedy; and when Warrington laughed at the idea of his writing tragedy, Pen swore by Jove he would show he could, and began to spout lines, as if Richmond were Drury Lane and the sparrows his audience.

The Major’s solo—performed, as it were, upon the instrument of his own nose—was interrupted by Miss Bell’s entrance. She had been visiting her old friend Lady Rockminster, who had taken a summer villa nearby; and the great lady, hearing of Arthur’s illness and Helen’s arrival, had called—bringing, for the benefit of the invalid she did not like, grapes, partridges, and other attentions that smelt deliciously of condescension and the larder. For Laura, however, Lady Rockminster had a real fondness, and longed to carry her off; but Laura would not leave Mrs. Pendennis now, for Helen, worn by watching and worry, had fallen into a frailty of her own, so that Doctor Goodenough prescribed for mother and son alike.

Major Pendennis started up at once—his slumbers were light, as if he suspected even his own dreams of plotting. He made Miss Bell a gallant speech; he had been full of gallantry toward her of late. Where had she been gathering the roses that bloomed upon her cheeks? How happy he was to be disturbed from visions by so charming a reality!

Laura, who had humour and honesty in good supply, possessed also something like contempt for the old gentleman; it amused her to draw out his worldliness and hear his club-wisdom on great people and morals. But now she was not satirical. She had brought home game for Pen and flowers for mamma; and she looked grave—grave enough to make the room seem quieter.

She had just been with Mrs. Pendennis. Helen was very worn; she feared she was very, very ill. Laura’s large eyes filled with sympathy that was as simple and nourishing as broth. Could not that good dear Doctor Goodenough cure her?

“Arthur’s illness,” Major Pendennis said slowly, “and other mental anxiety, no doubt, have shaken Helen.”

A burning blush came on Laura’s face; she understood his allusion at once. She looked him full in the face and made no reply, but thought, fiercely and silently, He might have spared me that. What is he aiming at by recalling that shame?

He was aiming at something, likely enough; the Major seldom opened his mouth without meaning to put something inside someone else’s. He went on: Goodenough had spoken of their dear friend’s health. She wanted rest and change of scene—yes, change. Painful circumstances must be forgotten and never alluded to. He begged pardon for even hinting at them; he never should do so again—nor, he was sure, would she. Everything must be done to soothe and comfort Helen; and his proposal was that they should go abroad for the autumn, to a watering-place in the Rhine neighbourhood, where Helen might rally her exhausted spirits and Arthur try to become a new man. Of course Miss Bell would not forsake her mother?

Of course not. It was Helen—Helen only (and Arthur for Helen’s sake)—that Laura cared about now. She would go abroad or anywhere with Helen.

And Helen herself, after an hour alone in her room, had grown as eager for the tour as a schoolboy made sea-mad by a book of voyages. Whither should they go? The farther the better—somewhere so remote that even recollection could not follow them; somewhere so delightful that Arthur should never wish to leave; anywhere, so that he could be happy.

She opened her desk with trembling fingers, took out her banker’s book, counted her little savings as if each figure were a prayer, and thought, If more is wanted, there is the diamond cross. I will borrow from Laura again. Let us go—let us go—directly he can bear the journey. Come, kind Doctor Goodenough; come quick, and give us leave to quit England.

The good Doctor drove over to dine with them that very day, and found her in a state of flutter that made even her breathing seem overworked.

“If you agitate yourself so,” he told her, “and if your heart beats so, and if you persist in being so anxious about a young gentleman who is getting well as fast as he can, we shall have you laid up, and Miss Laura to watch you; and then it will be her turn to be ill; and I should like to know how the deuce a doctor is to live who is obliged to come and attend you all for nothing? Mrs. Goodenough is already jealous of you, and says—quite justly—that I fall in love with my patients. And you must please to get out of the country as soon as ever you can, that I may have a little peace in my family.”

When the plan was proposed to Arthur, it was received with the greatest alacrity. He longed to be off that instant. He began to let his moustachios grow from the very moment—perhaps to put his mouth into training for French and German—only to become seriously disquieted when those moustachios, arriving with slow importance, proved to be of a decidedly red colour. He had looked forward to an autumn at Fairoaks; and perhaps the prospect of two or three months there did not delight him.

“There is not a soul to speak to in the place,” he said to Warrington. “I can’t stand old Portman’s sermons and pompous after-dinner conversation. I know all old Glanders’s stories about the Peninsular war. The Claverings are the only Christian people in the neighbourhood, and they’re not to be at home before Christmas, my uncle says; besides, Warrington, I want to get out of the country. Whilst you were away—confound it—I had a temptation, from which I’m very thankful to have escaped, and which I count that even my illness came very luckily to put an end to.”

And here he narrated to his friend the whole Vauxhall affair.

Warrington listened gravely. Putting moral delinquency aside, he was very glad Arthur had escaped a danger that might have made his whole life wretched—and which certainly would have occasioned the wretchedness and ruin of the other party.

“And your mother,” Warrington added, “and—and your friends—what pain it would have been to them!”

“Not a word to my mother!” Arthur cried, alarmed as if he heard, not the postman, but the judge. “She would never get over it. An esclandre of that sort would kill her, I do believe. And the best way—when a danger of that sort menaces—is not to face it, but to turn one’s back on it and run.”

“And were you very much smitten?” Warrington asked.

“Hm!” said Arthur, with the knowing air of a young man who would like to be thought a veteran in affairs of the heart. “She dropped her h’s, but she was a dear little girl.”

Poor Clarissas of the world—poor little girls with their unguarded hearts and their unguarded spelling—if you could but hear how the Lovelaces speak of you when you are not present; if you could but see your tender little letters pulled from a case like cigars and handed round for amusement, you would be less eager to write, and far less eager to believe. There is a sort of baseness that is not complete unless it boasts; and the man who betrays your honour is, nine times out of ten, eager to betray your secret too.

“It’s hard to fight, and it’s easy to fall,” Warrington said gloomily. “And as you say, Pendennis, when danger like this is imminent, the best way is to turn your back on it and run.”

After this discourse—upon which Arthur would have been eloquent enough a month earlier—the talk returned to travel. Arthur pressed Warrington to come with them. Warrington was part of the family now—part of the cure. Arthur declared he should not have half the pleasure without him.

But George said no. He could not go. He must stay and take Arthur’s place at home.

“That’s needless,” Arthur urged; “Shandon’s back in London. I’m entitled to a holiday.”

“Don’t press me,” Warrington said. “I can’t go. I’ve particular engagements. I’m best at home. I’ve not got the money to travel—that’s the long and short of it—for travelling costs money, you know.”

This little obstacle fell on Arthur like a wet cloth. He mentioned it to his mother. Mrs. Pendennis was very sorry; Mr. Warrington had been exceedingly kind; but she supposed he knew best about his affairs. And then, no doubt, she reproached herself—quietly, as women do—for the selfish wish to carry her boy away and have him wholly to herself.
