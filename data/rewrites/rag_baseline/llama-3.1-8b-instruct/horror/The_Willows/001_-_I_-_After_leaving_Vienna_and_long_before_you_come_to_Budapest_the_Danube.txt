As we navigated the Danube River, leaving Vienna's urban sprawl behind, the landscape transformed into a realm of solitude and desolation. The river's waters began to disperse, spreading out in every direction, and the surrounding terrain morphed into a seemingly endless expanse of willow-bushes, a sea of vegetation that stretched as far as the eye could see. This vast, eerie landscape was designated on maps as the Sümpfe, a German term for marshes, a testament to the region's unique hydrological characteristics.

During periods of high water, the terrain was nearly submerged, with the willows' slender stems barely visible above the surface. However, in normal seasons, the wind whipped the bushes into a frenzy, causing their silver leaves to dance and sway in an ever-changing display of mesmerizing beauty. Unlike traditional trees, these willows lacked sturdy trunks, instead boasting supple stems that bent and flexed in response to even the slightest breeze. Their rounded tops and soft outlines gave the impression that the entire landscape was in constant motion, as if the wind itself was alive and in perpetual flux.

The Danube, now unencumbered by the strict confines of its banks, meandered freely through the labyrinthine network of channels that crisscrossed the islands. The resulting currents created whirlpools, eddies, and foamy rapids, as the river carved out new paths and reshaped the landscape with each passing moment. It was a dynamic, ephemeral world, where the very existence of the islands was precarious, subject to the whims of the floodwaters, which could erase their forms in a matter of days.

Our journey had begun just outside of Pressburg, where we had navigated the river's treacherous currents, dodging whirlpools and sandbars as we made our way downstream. We had left Vienna behind, slipping through the still-sleeping city just as the sky was beginning to redden before sunrise. A short time later, we found ourselves beneath the boughs of a birch grove, the wind whipping through the trees as we paused to break our fast. From there, we continued on, the river carrying us past the ancient Roman settlement of Carnuntum, where the mighty Marcus Aurelius once walked, and under the watchful gaze of the Carpathian Mountains, where the March River flowed gently in from the left, marking the border between Austria and Hungary.
