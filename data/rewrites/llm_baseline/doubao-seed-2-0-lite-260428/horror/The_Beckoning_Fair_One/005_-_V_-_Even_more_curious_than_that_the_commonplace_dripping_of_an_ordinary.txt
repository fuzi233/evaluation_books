Far stranger than the way a common, banal dripping tap had fallen into perfect sync with a ghostly, unplaceable draft snaking through the walls was the effect that rhythm left on Oleron: it pried open the edges of his perception, and suddenly his ears hummed with a terrible, sharpened sensitivity to every small sound the hundred-year-old house exhaled.

It is an old truth that the deepest, most suffocating silence in the world is not the absence of noise, but a quiet held taut by a thousand almost-too-small-to-hear disturbances. This house was never still. Maybe the soft, damp spring air seeped into its waterlogged, torpid old timbers, waking them from decades of frost-numbed sleep. Maybe the fires Oleron lit each evening stoked heat into its knobbly, jointed skeleton, making it stretch. Every joist and oak baulk was riddled with burrowing, chittering insect life, carving tiny tunnels through the old dark wood like so many secret scribbles. Sit still long enough in his armchair, and Oleron’s hearing would shift, the same way a hiker who thinks the midsummer woods are dead quiet suddenly realizes the air thrums with the whir and creak of ten thousand tiny hidden lives.

He found himself smiling, half uneasy, at the foolish arbitrary line mankind draws between things that live and things that do not. Never mind the pattering of mice behind the wainscot, the soft thud of crumbling plaster falling down inside the walls, the little popping gasps from burning logs in his hearth: the whole house was talking, in a language he almost thought he could learn if he just waited long enough. Beams settled into their mortises with long, tired human sighs. Things ticked slow, rhythmic counts inside the walls, as if the wood itself had a heartbeat. Joints cracked, floorboards whined soft complaints under his feet, and sash windows shifted in their frames with a soft, deliberate thud even when not a breath of wind stirred the curtains.

Whether the house was truly alive or not, it had a personality, soft and winsome, that curled around him like a well-loved coat. After an hour of dozy musing he hit on the fancy that if his own soul and body fit so easily together, he might slip so far into the bones of this house that he would bleed into its walls. It amused him to picture some future tenant, years from now, walking in and feeling that faint, unnameable prickle of a presence. A harmless hack of a novelist, whose only sin was deciding he had to burn through his half-finished novel and start it all over, laying the groundwork for a new ghost. A ridiculous joke. Almost.

But the deeper his quiet, creeping attachment to the house grew, the more Elsie Bengough recoiled from the place, her aversion sharpening into something that made her skin prickle the second she stepped over the threshold. She did not bother hiding it.

“It doesn’t belong to this century, not even a little bit,” she said, sharp as a shard of broken glass, staring at the panelled walls. “And it’s poison for you, Paul. You already drift too far away from the real world, slip right into apathy if no one yanks you back. You should be in a flat with concrete floors, a cheap patent gas meter, a tradesman’s lift, somewhere loud and busy that forces you to rub elbows with other people. I could get you a little part-time work, three days a week, plenty of time left for your writing—would you say yes?”

Oleron felt a petty, unnameable resentment rise in his throat, as if the words were being put in his mouth before he could think. He thanked her, no smile on his face. “No, I don’t think so. Everyone has to live their own life, after all.”

“Live your own life?” She huffed, crossing her arms. “When was the last time you went out for more than a stamp run? When was the last time you took a proper walk, stretched your legs?”

“Some time, I suppose. I haven’t kept track.”

“Since I was last here?”

“I haven’t gone out much.”

“And is *Romilly* turning out better for you being locked up in here, wrapped in all this quiet?”

“I think she is. I’m laying her foundations. I’ll start the proper draft soon.”

She seemed to have forgotten their fight over the first, abandoned draft of *Romilly*. She frowned, turned half away, then snapped back around. “Oh, you’re still clinging to that ridiculous idea?”

“If you mean I threw out the old version and started a new one from scratch, yes,” Oleron said slow, his voice colder than he meant it to be.

That uncordial edge pricked her, but Elsie was a fighter. His unaccountable stiffness made her firmer. She huffed a sharp, exasperated pshaw. “Where is the old manuscript? I want to see it. I want to read you parts of it, knock this wool-gathering nonsense out of your head before you throw years of work in the bin.”

This time Oleron turned his back to her. When he faced her again, his tone was softer, but unyielding. “It’s no use, Elsie. I get to decide what path I take, even if you think it’s wrong. I’ve thought this all through. The manuscript? I almost burned it, but I didn’t. It’s in that window seat, if you absolutely have to look.”

Elsie stepped quick to the window seat, lifted the wooden lid. Then she gave a sharp, gasping little cry, clapping the back of her free hand to her mouth. She spoke over her shoulder, voice tight. “You were supposed to knock those nails flat, Paul.”

Oleron was at her side in two strides. “What? What’s happened?”

“I did pull every last one of them out,” he said, staring.

“Left one sticking far enough to gut someone,” she said, holding up her hand. A raw, welling red gash ran all the way from her wrist to the knuckle of her little finger, the rust from the nail already leaving dark brown streaks in the blood.

“Good God,” Oleron breathed. He grabbed her uninjured hand, tugging her toward the bathroom. “Come on, we need to bathe that right now.”

He ran warm water over the gash, scrubbing away the dirt and rust, then switched to cold, holding her hand under the stream as he rambled in shock. “How did I miss it? I ran my whole palm over the edge of that box a dozen times, there was nothing there. Is the water too cold? Does that hurt? I swear I pulled every nail out of that wood.”

“Leave it a little longer,” she murmured, eyes half closed. “I can bear it.”

He led her back to the sitting room after a few minutes, wrapped the gash tight in one of his clean linen handkerchiefs, but his face was still twisted with bewilderment. He had spent half a whole day restoring those three window seats, prying out every old nail and patching the wood, he knew he had. He’d even opened each of the lids a dozen times after that, no sharp edge, no snag, no nail sticking up an inch and a half like a tiny waiting fang. But there it was.

“I’ll yank the damned thing out right now,” he muttered, grabbing a pair of pincers. He got it in one clean pull, no mistakes this time.

Elsie had sunk into his armchair, her face as pale as parchment, but she still had the crumpled old *Romilly* manuscript clutched in her uninjured hand. She wasn’t done arguing.

“Paul, if you throw this manuscript away and start over, it will be the worst mistake you ever made,” she said, voice thick with earnest worry.

Oleron hung his head. He couldn’t stop seeing that gash, that impossible nail. For once, *Romilly* was the last thing on his mind. But Elsie kept pressing, until he spoke, voice soft, almost apologetic. “What can I say? I have to follow what feels right to me, Elsie. When you read the new version, you’ll understand. And if you still don’t like it, well…” He made a hopeless little gesture. “You have to let me go by my own lights.”

She went quiet.

“Come on,” he said gently. “We’ve gotten along so well up to now. Don’t let this tear us apart.”

The words had barely left his mouth before he regretted them. Her lips and eyelids quivered at the same time, and her voice shook when she spoke. “I can’t help saying this, Paul—you’ve changed. So much.”

“Hush, you’re still in shock,” he soothed. “Rest a minute. How could I change?”

“I don’t know, but you have. You haven’t been the same since the day you moved into this house. I wish you’d never even seen the place. It’s ruined your work, it’s turned you into someone I barely recognize, I’m so scared for you.” She winced, shifting her bandaged hand. “Oh, God, it’s starting to throb.”

“Poor thing,” he said. “Let me walk you to a doctor to get that dressed properly.”

“No, I’ll be fine,” she said. She propped her elbow on the back of the chair, and her bandaged hand rested light on his shoulder.

At that touch, a sudden, strange, sharp new anxiety coiled tight in his chest. A hundred times before, on their walks and trips, she’d slipped her hand into his arm like a sister, and he’d never thought twice about it. But now, staring at her, her eyes half closed, her head tilted back, that soft, secret smile on her parted lips, the truth hit him like a blow to the chest. Good God. He’d never noticed. She was in love with him.

And the strangest thing of all was that instead of softening him, filling him with guilt or warmth, a hot, ugly surge of resentment bubbled up, unbidden, completely alien to him. What was she doing here? Why wasn’t she minding her own work? Why was she poking into his life, trying to drag him out of the only place that had ever felt like home? She was the one who had changed, not him.

By the time she opened her eyes again, he’d pushed the ugly feeling down, but his voice was distant, careful. “Let me take you to the doctor, Elsie.”

She stood up. “No, thank you, Paul. I’ll go now. I’ll stop by the chemist if it needs a new dressing. Take my other arm, please. Goodbye.”

He didn’t try to stop her. He walked her to the bottom of the stairs. Halfway down the narrow alley outside, she turned back. “No point me trekking all the way out here if you’re not home,” she called. “I’ll send you a postcard before I come next time.”

At the front gate, she turned one last time, her face mournful. “Leave this house, Paul. Everything about it is wrong.”

Then she was gone.

Oleron walked slowly back into his silent sitting room. He went straight to the window seat, lifted the lid, and stood there staring down at the patch of wood where the nail had been, the faint dark stain of old, old dried blood already seeping back into the grain, no trace of Elsie’s fresh red wound on the splintered surface.

A cold chill ran down his spine.

“That’s not right,” he whispered to the empty, quiet room. “It’s impossible. I know I pulled every last nail out of this wood.”

Somewhere deep inside the walls, a soft, slow sigh echoed. The house was smiling.
