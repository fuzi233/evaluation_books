Letters of fire had appeared on the church wall, boldly questioning—though with a curious air of sincerity—why no mass was offered for the duke in this time of turmoil. The supernatural message had been seen by many and slowly faded from view, leaving the onlookers transfixed in stunned silence. The result was swift: the city corporation, eager not to fall behind even celestial powers in loyalty to their sovereign, hastily commissioned numerous masses. Among those who profited was our old acquaintance, the cure—a man with a particular fondness for coin. But I would not detain you over such a commonplace miracle if it were not for what followed.

Denys begged for his arms back. “Why disgrace him as well as break his heart?” he asked.
“Then swear upon the cross of thy sword,” came the reply, “that thou wilt not leave the bastard’s service until this rebellion is crushed.”
He yielded, offering three solemn oaths before reclaiming his weapons and freedom.

The troops halted at an inn called “The Three Fish.” There, Marion saw Denys and exclaimed, “I’m out of luck—how could I have expected to see you again?” Noticing his somber mood, and sensing that her jest had fallen flat, she asked gently what troubled him. He told her. She responded with quiet optimism. Gerard, she said, was too noble and well-mannered to come to harm. Women would always stand by him. And besides, things would either go well or ill for him. If well, he’d join some respectable company bound for Rome; if ill, he’d return home—perhaps even before Denys himself. “You’ve still a little fighting to do in Flanders, by all accounts,” she added, handing him his gold pieces. Though he pressed her repeatedly, she refused to accept a single one.

Denys found her argument persuasive—not only because it aligned with his own hopes, but because her honesty stirred something within him. It made him feel a little better about the world into which his dear friend had ventured alone.

Meanwhile, foot soldiers—small groups of two or three—were already making their way toward Flanders, many penniless, yet carried from town to town by bailiffs who issued orders for food and lodging at local inns. Anthony of Burgundy intercepted many of these stragglers, gathering them under his banner. Thus, he entered Flanders at the head of six hundred men.

Upon crossing the frontier, he met his brother Baldwyn, who brought reinforcements, arms, and supplies. Together they organized their forces and marched through several towns—not merely unopposed, but greeted with cheers and acclamations. Such loyalty drew sharp comments. “This rebellion of ours is a bite,” grumbled Simon, a soldier who had chosen Denys as his comrade. Denys said nothing, but silently vowed to St. Mars that he would kill Anthony of Burgundy should this uprising prove no rebellion at all.

That afternoon, they caught sight of a strongly fortified town. A whisper ran through the ranks: this place was disaffected. Yet when they approached, the great gate stood wide open, flanked by towers each guarded by a single sentinel. The advancing army, lulled into carelessness, broke formation and marched forward without caution.

Only when they were within a furlong did the drawbridge rise slowly, creaking upward until it stood vertical against the fort. At that very moment, the portcullis descended with a thunderous crash, and the towers and walls bristled with lances and crossbows. A low murmur spread through the front ranks and rippled to the rear.

“Halt!” shouted the bastard.

The order echoed down the line. They stopped.

“Herald to the gate!”

A pursuivant rode out, halting twenty yards from the gate. With herald’s flag hanging down around his bugle, he blew a summons. A tall figure in gleaming brass armor appeared above the gate. Words passed between them—too faint to hear, but their meaning clear. The herald then sounded a single sharp, menacing note and galloped back, face set with war.

The bastard rode forward to meet him. No sooner had they exchanged words than he turned in his saddle and bellowed, “Pioneers, to the van!” In moments, hedges were leveled and the army took the field, encamping just beyond arrow range. Mounted officers darted south, east, and west to nearby friendly towns, summoning catapults, palisades, mantelets, raw hides, tar-barrels, carpenters, provisions—everything needed for a siege.

One soldier, whose spirits had flagged, brightened at the clang of the portcullis. His temple flushed. When the herald returned with battle in his eyes, he understood instantly—and for the first time in days, he cried out, “Courage, tout le monde, le diable est mort!”
If the great warrior heard, how he must have grinned!

The besiegers camped a furlong from the walls, building roads, keeping pikemen ready for assault, and sending forward sappers, pioneers, catapultiers, and crossbowmen. They began filling the moat, mining beneath the walls, breaching defenses—work done under constant fire of arrows, bolts, stones, and small rocks. These engineers required “a hundred eyes,” acting with vigilance, intelligence, daring, and skill that made a siege both compelling and, to spectators, even amusing.

First, they advanced carpenters behind rolling mantelets to build a high, strong stockade right at the edge of the moat. Lives were lost, but not many—thanks to a concentrated barrage from crossbowmen, including Denys, who shot over the workers’ heads at any defender who dared show his face or peer through a loophole. Under this cover, the carpenters soon raised a solid palisade.

But the defenders anticipated this. By night, they extended wooden penthouses atop the curtain walls—structures supported by square holes near the top, allowing them to launch missiles more freely than through narrow loopholes or sloping crenelets. This gave them greater range and flexibility.

In response, the besiegers brought up mangonels and hurled massive stones at these wooden shelters, smashing them apart. Simultaneously, they constructed a triangular wooden tower as tall as the curtain wall, keeping it ready for use just outside bowshot. This tower was a terrifying sight to the defenders—such towers had taken many a town.

They began mining beneath the moat where the tower loomed, preparing for its arrival. The besiegers also mined elsewhere, aiming to collapse the square barbican. All this time, Denys remained behind his mantelet, alongside another arbalestrier, protecting the workmen and landing excellent shots. His precision earned him the attention of an unseen archer who occasionally sent a quivering shaft into his mantelet—one so close it struck within an inch of Denys’s eye slit.

“Peste!” he muttered. “You shoot well, my friend. Come forth and receive my congratulations! Shall merit such as thine hide its head?”
Then he laughed grimly. “Comrade, it’s one of those cursed Englishmen—with his half-ell shaft. I’ll not die till I’ve had a shot at London wall.”

On the side of the besieged, a figure soon drew notice: a tall knight clad in complete brass armor, carrying a long, slender lance. He directed the defenders' movements with calm authority. Whenever disaster struck the attackers, he was usually involved. My young reader may ask, “Why didn’t Denys shoot him?”

Denys did shoot him—every day. So did other crossbowmen, archers, every man who could aim. The knight was there to be shot. Yet he seemed utterly indifferent. Worse—he appeared not to know when he was hit. He walked his battlements under fire like a stout captain pacing his deck in a storm, oblivious to the raindrops falling on his woolen armor.

Eventually, the besiegers grew bitter. They refused to waste good steel on him anymore. Instead, they cursed him and his impervious coat of mail. He took the missiles like the rest.

Gunpowder has spoiled war. War has always harmed mankind’s true interests. But in ancient times, it served a purpose: it painted vividly, sang divinely, birthed epics. Now, invisible slaughter under a thick pall of smoke—what benefit is there in that? A poet might write, “Suave etiam belli certamina magna tueri,” but the sentiment is hollow, like a cuckoo’s call. You can’t *see* anything but chaos.

They managed better in the Middle Ages. This siege was modest, but it was enough for a minstrel or writer to witness and sing of it, earning an honest living. So far, the sport was justified.

It was a clear, crisp day, not quite frosty. The attackers focused their efforts on a stretch of about two hundred fifty yards—two curtains and two towers. One was the square barbican; the other, topped with a pointed roof resting on a stone machicolade, featured a row of deadly crenelets between roof and wall—like the gunports of a modern frigate, nearly closed.

The curtains bore shattered penthouses, damaged by mangonels and trebuchets. On the besiegers’ side of the moat stood what looked like a colossal arsenal—longer than broad, teeming with human ants, busy with industry, displaying the full mechanical ingenuity of the age. Winches, pulleys, cranes, capstans—all at work. Piles of stones, fascines, mantelets, tar-barrels. Horses and carts streamed in endlessly.

Yet closer inspection revealed arrows flying constantly, cranes hurling stone with reckless abandon, and among the tools, here and there, a worker or soldier lying flat—feathers protruding, blood seeping out.

At the moat’s edge opposite the wooden tower, a strong penthouse—called a “cat”—was creeping forward, slowly filling the moat with fascines and rubble. Pulled by ropes wound around pulleys and windlasses, it moved steadily.

The knight burned the first cat by tossing flaming tar-barrels onto it. So the besiegers built a steeper roof and covered it with raw hides. The tar-barrels now failed. Then the knight signaled with his spear, and a small trebuchet behind the wall began dropping stones just clear of the wall—until one pierced the cat’s roof, creating a terrible hole.

Baldwyn of Burgundy saw this and lost his temper. He ordered the great catapult battering the curtain’s woodwork to be turned and aimed slantwise at the knight.

Meanwhile, Denys and his English counterpart went to dinner. These two, ever watchful of each other, had developed a silent camaraderie, communicating through signs—especially on matters vital in peace or war. Sometimes Denys would place a piece of bread atop his mantelet; the Englishman would hang something similar by string. Or vice versa. Always, they dined together.

Now, the engineers took an extraordinary step: aiming fifty-pound stones at a single man. The catapult was a marvel—simple, elegant, effective. Imagine a fir tree cut down, mounted horizontally on uprights, unbalanced so three-quarters rested on one side. A half-ton weight fastened to the short end. The long tapering part was forced down to the ground using a winch and secured with a bolt. The stone was placed in a sling attached to the tip. The butt end rose high, struggling under the weight. The bolt was drawn—gravity took over—the short end swung down violently, the long end whipped up, and the stone flew from the sling with immense force.

This time, the stone missed the knight but crashed into the penthouse beside him, shattering roof and floor. Two figures fell through: the stone itself and a mangled body. They plunged down the high wall, down, down, into the dark waters of the moat, which closed over them, holding both stone and bone for two hundred years—until cannon disturbed the waters and civilization drained them.

“Aha! A good shot!” cried Baldwyn.

The knight retreated. The besiegers jeered. He reappeared on the barbican platform, helmet barely visible above the parapet. He seemed busy. Soon, a massive Turkish catapult emerged, elevated, and launched a twenty-pound stone two hundred forty yards into the air. It bounced, striking dirt into Lord Anthony’s eye and making him curse. The next stone killed a horse hauling arrows, knocking it dead like a rabbit and spilling the cart.

The engine then turned on the besiegers’ wooden tower—supposedly out of range. Sir Turk hurled specially shaped stones, striking it repeatedly, breaking it in places. The besiegers turned two engines on the monster, slinging smaller stones onto the barbican platform, killing two engineers. But the Turk ignored them. He flung a forty-pound stone at the great catapult, hitting near the axis and destroying it entirely. Engineers fled, yelling.

In the afternoon, Simon raced back to his mantelet after shooting at the defenders. Denys, peering through his slit, saw Simon suddenly freeze, arms outstretched, then fall face down. An arrow protruded from his back. The archer showed himself briefly, proud of his mark. It was the Englishman.

Denys, already prepared, fired. The bolt struck the archer, who staggered away wounded. But Simon never stirred. His wars were over.

“I am unlucky in my comrades,” said Denys.

The next morning, the besieged saw an unwelcome sight: the cat, now covered in mattresses and raw hides, was filling the moat. The knight bombarded it in vain, threw burning tar-barrels—useless. Then, with his own hands, he lowered a bag of burning sulphur and pitch by rope, stinking out the workmen. But Baldwyn, armored like a lobster, rushed forward, cut the rope, and the work continued.

The knight sent new engineers into the mine, undermining the area, reinforcing it with beams, and coating them thickly with grease and tar.

At dawn, the moat was filled. The wooden tower began moving on wheels toward a section of curtain already under attack by two catapults. Its approach was eerie—no visible agency, driven by internal rollers worked by leverage. On top stood a platform with the first assault party, protected by a vertical drawbridge and full armor.

The defenders attacked the tower with stones, striking often—but to no effect. It was well defended with mattresses and hides. Soon, it reached the moat’s edge.

The knight ordered the mine beneath it ignited. The Turkish engine then hurled a stone of half a hundredweight directly at the knights on the tower, sweeping two off into the plain. One writhed; the other lay motionless.

Now, the besieging catapults hurled blazing tar-barrels, setting fire to the hoards on both sides. Assailants climbed ladders behind the tower, lowering the drawbridge onto the battered curtain. Catapults fired tar-barrels, igniting adjacent works to drive defenders away.

Armored men sprang across the bridge, led by Baldwyn. The invulnerable knight and his men met them. A fierce combat erupted—figures falling headlong from the narrow bridge. But fresh attackers kept coming. The defenders were driven back.

Another minute, and the town was taken. But the mine had been timed perfectly—just as the breach occurred, the supports collapsed. The tower sank abruptly from the wall, tearing the drawbridge loose and dumping soldiers onto the dry moat.

The defenders roared and surrounded Baldwyn and his men—strangely offering quarter. While some disarmed them, others lit the turret with hand grenades. Who was busiest? The tall knight. He thrust fire-bags onto his long spear, driving them into the doomed structure once so fearsome.

To do this, he had to stand on a projecting beam of the shattered hoard, gripping a pikeman’s hand to steady himself.

This enraged Denys. He dashed from his mantelet, hoping to escape notice amid the chaos. He raised his crossbow—missed the knight completely—but his bolt struck the pikeman in the brain. The knight fell heavily from the wall, lance and all.

Denys stared, stunned. In that instant, he felt his arm grow hot, then cold—an English arrow skewering it.

No one noticed. The knight, armor glinting in the morning sun, fell headlong, turning midair, and slapped the water with a sound heard a mile away. None thought to see him again.

But he landed at the edge of the fascines, his spear lodged underwater. With great effort, he pulled himself to the side—but couldn’t climb out.

Anthony sent twelve knights with a white flag to take him prisoner. He surrendered quietly, saying nothing.

Taken to Anthony’s tent, the latter laughed at his muddy armor—then frowned. “I marvel, sir, that so good a knight should know his duty so poorly as to rebel and cause us all this trouble.”

“I am nun-nun-nun-nun-nun-no knight.”

“What then?”

“A hosier.”

“A what? Then thy armor shall be stripped off, and thou shalt be tied to a stake before the works, riddled with arrows as a warning to traitors.”

“N-n-n-n-no! Duda-duda-duda-duda-don't do that.”

“Why not?”

“Tuta-tuta-tuta-townsfolk will-h-h-h-hang t'other buba-buba-buba-buba-bastard.”

“What, whom?”

“Your bub-bub-bub-brother Baldwyn.”

“What? Have you knaves taken him?”

The warlike hosier nodded.

“Hang the fool!” Anthony snapped.

The hosier watched his eyes, then removed his helmet. From the lining, he pulled an intercepted letter from the duke—ordering Anthony to court immediately, to represent Burgundy at England’s court, to escort the English king’s sister to her bridegroom, the Earl of Charolois. A mission flattering to Anthony’s pride and appetite for pleasure. Edward the Fourth ruled the most splendid court in Europe.

The hosier saw Anthony’s longing to leave. “We’ll gega-gega-gega-give ye a thousand angels to raise the siege.”

“And Baldwyn?”

“I’ll gega-gega-gega-go and send him with the money.”

Dinner-time arrived. A flag of truce flew on both sides. The false knight and the true one dined together, reaching a peaceful understanding.

“But what is your grievance, my good friend?” asked Anthony.

“Tuta-tuta-tuta-too much taxes.”

Denys, finding the arrow in his arm, turned his back—protected by a long shield—and walked sulkily into camp. He was met by Comte de Jarnac, who had seen his brilliant shot and, learning he was wounded, handed him a handful of broad coins.

“Hast got the better of thy grief, arbalestrier, methinks.”

“My grief, yes; but not my love. As soon as this rebellion is put down, I go to Holland. There I shall meet him.”

He was closer than he knew. Relieved from duty the next day, though his wound was serious, he set out with firm resolve to rejoin his friend in Holland.
