For a long stretch, our footsteps dragged over a desolate moorland, a landscape choked with dry, brittle vegetation and ancient mosses. Suddenly, emerging from the jagged horizon, I spied a solitary cottage, shrinking in the distance. The sun hung suspended, shrouded in a pallid, suffocating gray cloud. The heath offered no hint of past warmth; instead, a wind cut through us with a strange, chilling intensity, as if blown directly from some frozen realm where night reigns eternal.

"Finally!" exclaimed the raven, flapping his wings with a weary sigh. "What a tedious journey! In half the time, I could have flown to Paradise to visit my cousin—the one who never returned to Noah, remember?—and back again. Oh, dearest, it feels almost like winter!"

"Winter?" I cried, bewildered. "It seems to me as though we departed but yesterday!"

"That is because you travel so swiftly in your realm," the raven replied, his voice carrying a metallic edge. "There, you cannot simply loosen the thread of gravity you call 'pull' and let the world spin beneath your heels. Here, however, is my dwelling! My wife is kind enough to let me reside with her, calling it the Sexton's Cottage."

"But where lies your churchyard?" I asked, scanning the barren expanse. "Where are the graves you tend?"

The raven extended his long neck, holding his beak perfectly horizontal. He slowly rotated it, pointing successively north, south, east, and west, offering no verbal answer.

I followed the direction of his beak with my eyes. And there it was: without walls, without stones, the entire flat heath *was* a graveyard. As the dreary wind swept across the land, it brushed against countless invisible tombs. He was the sexton of every square inch he surveyed, the lord of everything laid aside in the dust. I stood within the burial ground of the universe itself, bounded by the unenclosed moor and circled by a gray, starless horizon that bowed low. Spring, summer, autumn, and sunshine—all lay behind me. I had arrived at a winter that waited in ambush, a season that had slumbered through the endless night wrapped in sheets of ice. Only now did it awake, smiling with childish innocence as the dawn broke. At noon, Summer blazed forth in terrifying glory, and as the afternoon waned, Old Autumn crept in, dying slowly until the first breath of the ghostly, vapor-filled night extinguished him.

As we approached the cottage, the clouded sun plummeted down the steepest slope of the western sky, sinking below the ridge just as we reached the threshold. The moment I crossed the door, a cold assault hit me—a physical, material weight that gripped my limbs like the claws of an icy death. I stumbled inside, slamming the door shut as a ferocious wind battered the exterior, seeking entry.

Instantly, silence descended. Inside, a single candle flickered on a rough wooden table. My first sight was what I took to be a coffin lid propped against the wall. Yet, it swung open silently, revealing not wood, but a doorway. Through it stepped a woman. She was clad entirely in white, as pristine as fresh snowfall, yet her countenance possessed a warmth that snow lacked. Her features were flawless, almost sculptural, but it was her eyes that arrested me. All the vitality of her being, her very essence, converged there, condensing into a light that burned with an inner fire. It might have been the approach of death that rendered her face so luminous, but those eyes held a life vast enough to sustain a nation. They were dark, plunging into depths that grew darker the longer I gazed, a whole night-sky compressed into two pupils where stars flashed and danced. Encircling them like a horizon was an iris of eternal twilight. To know what such eyes truly *were*, one needed only God; surely they poured straight from His hand. The stillness of her face suggested a primordial perfection, while those living eyes represented a continuous creation.

"He brings Mr. Vane, wife!" called the raven.

"He is welcome," she answered. Her voice was low, rich, and gentle, resonant with treasures of immortal sound that seemed buried deep within its tones.

I stared, speechless.

"I knew you would be pleased to see him," the raven added.

The woman stood rigid before the door she had passed through, refusing to advance further.

"Shall he sleep?" she asked quietly.

"I fear not," the raven replied. "He is neither weary nor heavily laden."

"Why, then, have you brought him?"

"My fears suggest it may be precipitate."

"Do I not understand you?" I interjected, a chill unease settling in my stomach mixed with a faint, desperate hope for escape. "Surely a man must perform a day's labor first!"

I gazed into the woman's pale face, and my heart began to flutter wildly. She met my stare in silence.

"First, let me return home," I insisted, trying to regain footing. "I shall return after I have found, created, or at least discovered something!"

"He has not yet learned that the day commences with sleep!" the woman murmured, turning to address her husband. "Tell him he must rest before he can accomplish anything."

"Men," the raven corrected, "are so obsessed with the notion of having *done* things that they fall asleep upon them. They cannot empty an egg without turning into the shell, lying down!"

These words tore my gaze away from the woman and fixed them on the raven. For a fleeting second, I saw no bird at all. Instead, I beheld the Librarian—the same slender, elderly figure in a rusty black coat, broad-bodied and long-tailed. Until now, I had seen only his back; now, his face revealed itself. It was gaunt, bony, the skeletal structure visible beneath the skin, a visage that suggested the skulls of his final claimed profession. Yet, in truth, I had never witnessed a face so vivid, a look so keen, or an expression so friendly as the one in his pale blue eyes. Those eyes, however, held a hazy veil, as if worn from much weeping.

"You knew I was not a raven!" he said, a smile playing on his thin lips.

"I knew you were Mr. Raven," I admitted, "but somehow, I thought you a bird as well!"

"What caused you to believe I was a bird?"

"You appeared as a raven, and I saw you digging worms from the earth with your beak."

"And then?"

"Toss them into the air."

"And then?"

"They transformed into butterflies and flew away."

"Have you ever seen a raven do that? I told you I am a sexton!"

"Does a sexton toss worms into the air and watch them become butterflies?"

"Yes."

"I have never seen one do it!"

"You saw me do it!—Yet, I remain the Librarian in your house, for I was never dismissed, nor did I resign the post. Now, I am Librarian here as well."

"But you just told me you are the Sexton here!"

"So I am. It is largely the same vocation. With books, they are merely dead bodies to you, and a library is nothing more than a catacomb!"

"You confuse me!"

"That is perfectly acceptable!"

Moments passed in silence. The woman remained motionless, a statue beside the open door of the coffin.

"On certain occasions," the sexton spoke finally, "it is more convenient to project one's bird-self to the forefront. Everyone, as you should know, possesses a beast-self, a bird-self, a dull fish-self, yes, even a creeping serpent-self—all requiring significant effort to crush and subdue! Truly, he also harbors a tree-self and a crystal-self, and I cannot count how many others exist—all striving for harmony. One can discern the nature of a man by observing which creature most frequently steps forward."

He turned to his wife, and I studied him anew. He towered above average height, standing with a posture more erect than I had previously observed. Like his wife, his face was exceedingly pale. His nose elegantly framed the beak that had retreated within it; his lips were very thin, devoid of color, yet their curves were beautiful. About them quivered a shadowy smile that blended humor, love, and profound pity.

"We require sustenance, wife," he said, "for we have traveled a great distance."

"You know, husband," she answered, "we can offer only to those who ask."

She turned her unchanging, radiant face toward me.

"Please, give me something to eat, Mrs. Raven," I pleaded, "and something—to whatever it may be—that will quench my thirst."

"Your thirst must be greater before you receive what will satisfy it," she replied, "yet whatever I can give, I will willingly provide."

She moved to a cupboard embedded in the wall, retrieved bread and wine, and placed them upon the table.

We sat down to a perfect meal. As I ate, the bread and wine seemed to penetrate far deeper than mere physical hunger or thirst could explain. Anxiety and discomfort dissolved completely, replaced by a calm, quiet expectation.

An overwhelming drowsiness seized me, and for the first time, I felt genuine weariness.
