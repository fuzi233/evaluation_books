The loggia atop Bardo’s house rose above the surrounding buildings, forming a gallery around a square courtyard. On the street-facing side, columns supported the roof; on the other three, a wall pierced with arched openings allowed for sheltered views beyond. From this rear side, Romola could gaze over a jumble of uneven, poorly constructed homes toward the distant hill of Bogoli—unseen by any passerby below. Near one of these arches, close to the door through which she would enter, Tito waited, his heart heavy with the weight of sunlight that fell across the stone floor and mingled with the wreckage of his hopes.

He had never truly believed that Romola’s love for him could overcome the shock of learning his secret. He lacked the arrogance that might have blinded him to the truth: her affection was rooted in her belief in who he was, not in what he might be. Yet as he imagined her approaching—her radiant beauty softened by the gentle warmth of her hazel eyes—he found himself wishing she were less noble, less pure, so that he might hold her tightly, kiss her once more, before they parted forever. He had never known true intimacy with her—only fleeting glances, brief kisses, the pressure of hands. So often, he had longed for solitude with her. Now, they would be alone. But as he saw her silhouette appear at the doorway, he felt her presence not as closeness, but as distance—cold, unyielding, inevitable.

Then the door opened.

Romola stood there.

It happened like lightning—fleeting, overwhelming. He did not see her clearly, but *felt* the light around her head, the sorrow in her eyes, the cry of love that escaped her lips: “Tito!” And in an instant, she was in his arms, weeping into his chest, her face buried against his shoulder.

How deeply Romola had longed to see that face again! The vision of death, the bewildering tale of a life she could not comprehend, the haunting echo of her brother’s last breath—these had made her realize more than ever that it was Tito who first brought warmth and hope into her world, who had eased the pain of remembering Dino. She must tell him everything. There was no one else. All morning, she had held herself together in front of her father. Now, the dam broke.

Proud and composed before all others, Romola was, in Tito’s presence, as open and tender as a child. She had been content with silence, with mere glances. But now, needing only to be near him, she clung without hesitation.

“My Romola! My goddess!” Tito whispered, cradling her gently, pressing his lips to the golden waves of hair at her neck. In that moment, he was in paradise. Shame, disgrace, separation—none of it mattered anymore. This joy was too strong to be tainted by the knowledge that she still loved him, unaware of the truth. Indeed, he rejoiced in her ignorance. Concealment had been wisdom. Only the fear he had carried—unnecessary, perhaps—remained a shadow, though even that seemed worth enduring for the rapture now flooding his soul.

The sob passed. Romola lifted her head. Neither spoke. They simply looked at each other, caught in the quiet wonder of young love—she with her pale hands resting on his dark curls, he with his fingers tracing the gold of her hair. Each was beautiful to the other. Each experienced, for the first time, that perfect mutual recognition, untouched by doubt or distance.

A new sadness settled on Romola’s heart, making her linger longer in the comfort of their silence. Tito dared not move closer, afraid that even a kiss would break the spell.

“Tito,” she said at last, “it has been terrible. But I must tell you everything. Your strength will help me resist the thoughts that reason cannot shake.”

“I know,” Tito replied softly. “I know he is dead.” His voice was calm, but his eyes betrayed the storm within—the longing that had shaped so many silent prayers, the wish that had burned in his heart long before the deed was done. Yet Romola saw only her own purity reflected in those deep, lustrous eyes, as if reading a letter written in dreams.

“So changed,” she murmured. “To think it was Dino. And so strange—no word to my father. Only a vision, as if he wanted to speak to me. And yet… it was heartbreaking. The labored breath, the eyes fixed on the crucifix, yet seeing nothing. I shall never forget it. It feels as though it will stand between me and every sight I meet.”

Her voice faltered. Her heart swelled, and she paused. But the need to share with Tito pressed upon her, urging her to push past the grief.

“It was strange, Tito,” she continued, her thoughts drifting. “The vision was about our marriage. Yet he knew nothing of you.”

“Tell me, my Romola,” Tito said, leading her to the bench nearby. A sudden fear stirred in him—what if the vision somehow concerned Baldassarre? The shift in emotion prompted him to change their position, to create space, however slight.

Romola recounted every detail from her entrance into San Marco, sparing none of her brother’s words—each spoken sentence seared into her memory. When she reached the end of the vision, she stopped. The rest remained too vivid to speak aloud. She sat staring into the distance, half-unaware of Tito beside her.

His mind, meanwhile, was at peace. The vision had passed over him like mist, leaving no trace. He waited silently, expecting her to continue.

“I took it,” she said, as if he had read her thoughts. “I took the crucifix. It’s downstairs in my room.”

“And now, my Romola,” Tito said, pleadingly, “banish these terrible thoughts. It was merely a monkish vision—born of fasting and fanaticism. It means nothing to you.”

“No, Tito,” she answered quietly. “But poor Dino—he believed it was divine. It’s strange, how men live in such fervent beliefs that seem madness to others. Dino wasn’t a vulgar zealot. And Fra Girolamo—his voice… it seemed to pierce me, as if there were truth in what moves them—truth I do not understand.”

“It was only your emotions, Romola,” Tito insisted. “Your brother’s state was no different from the theosophy that has plagued dreamers since time began—the same ideas Marsilio Ficino studies in the New Platonists. But Dino’s passion drove him to act, while others only write. And Fra Girolamo? He’s just a narrow-minded monk with a gift for preaching and terrifying crowds. Any voice would have shaken you then. Once your mind rests, you’ll judge these things as you always have.”

“Not about Dino,” Romola said firmly. “I was angry with him. My heart closed against him while he spoke. But since then… I’ve thought less of myself, more of him. Oh, Tito—how pitiful it was, watching his young life fade like that. That look at the crucifix, gasping for air—I can never forget it. Last night, I stared at the crucifix for hours, trying to believe it helped him. Until the lamplight made it seem… as if the suffering face looked down with pity.”

“Promise me,” Tito urged, “you won’t dwell on such thoughts. They belong to weak nuns, not to my golden-haired Aurora—made to scatter shadows, not gather them. Don’t think of them now. We won’t be alone much longer.”

His tone was tender, beseeching. With a gentle touch, he turned her face toward him.

Romola had been gazing absently through the archway, not seeing the hill beyond. She had been back in the chapter house, among the pale images of sorrow and death. Tito’s touch, his voice, pulled her back. Now, in the warm sun, she saw the world anew—rich, dark, alive. Purple vines wound between elms, corn ripened under the heat, birds darted among flowers, dancers beat cymbals in rhythm, melodies danced on strings—nature reveling in its strength.

What a transition—from the pallid visions of suffering to this vibrant youthfulness, as if a sun-god walked without knowing night. How could the anguish on Dino’s face, that desperate yearning for something unseen, coexist with this fullness of life? Was there no reconciliation? Only blind worship of conflicting gods—first in ecstasy, then in mourning?

For the first time, Romola felt this question rise like a dizzying ache—a need to grasp something, anything. It lasted only a breath, yet in that moment, centuries of human inquiry condensed into her single, trembling mind. But there was no answer. The feeling vanished, swept away by the return of youthful joy, the radiant beauty of Tito, like dawn after a glance at the grey west.

“Your mind wanders from us, my Romola,” Tito murmured, softly reproachful. “It seems forgotten to you.”

She looked into his eyes, silent. Then the sadness melted, replaced by warmth.

“My joy!” she said, her voice clear and bright.

“You care enough to banish those cold fancies?” Tito asked, smiling. “Or will you always suspect me as the Great Tempter?”

“How could I not care for you more than anything?” she replied. “Everything I’ve ever felt—about my father, about loneliness—was preparation for loving you. You’d laugh if you knew what kind of man I imagined marrying—some scholar with deep lines, like Alamanno Rinuccini, grey hair, agreeing with my father on Aristotelian matters, willing to live with him. I read about love in poems, but never dreamed it could happen here, in Florence, in our old library. Then you came, Tito—so dear to my father—and I began to believe life could be happy for me too.”

“My goddess,” Tito breathed, awed by the majesty and simplicity in her. “But if you truly cared about our marriage, you’d persuade your father and Messer Bernardo to stop delaying.”

“I do care,” Romola said. “But I know my godfather will insist on more delay because of Dino’s death. He’s never agreed with my father about disowning Dino. And you know he’s always said we should wait until you’ve been in Florence a year. Don’t blame him. He’s prejudiced, yes—but noble. He says it’s folly for my father to keep the library separate, so it bears his name—yet he’d still try to fulfill his wish. That, to me, is great nobility—the ability to respect a feeling he doesn’t share.”

“I have no resentment toward Messer Bernardo,” Tito said. “For thinking you too precious for me.”

“But does your father know?” Romola asked.

“Yes. I told him. I couldn’t help it. I told him where I’d been, that I saw Dino die—but nothing else. He’s forbidden me to speak of it again. But he’s been silent today, restless—those movements that wound me so. It’s as if he’s trying to escape the prison of his blindness. Let us go to him now. I persuaded him to sleep—he didn’t rest well last night. Your voice calms him, Tito. Always.”

“And not one kiss?” Tito asked gently, with a tone that made him seem both dependent and charming—rare gifts that excused his boldness.

A blush, swift as light, spread across Romola’s cheeks and neck as she leaned toward him. It seemed impossible their kisses could ever grow common.

“Let us walk once round the loggia,” she said, “before we go down.”

“There’s something grim about Florence,” Tito remarked, pausing at the front of the house, where they could see across rooftops to the river. “Even in its merriment, it’s shrill, hard—biting rather than joyful. I wish we lived in Southern Italy, where thought is broken not by weariness, but by sweet languor—something the ‘ingenia acerrima Florentina’ never seem to know. I’d love to see you under that southern sun, lying among flowers, lulled into pure delight, while I sang to you on the lute—something unconscious, born of light and warmth. You’ve never known that joy, my Romola.”

“No,” she said, “but I’ve dreamed of it since you came. I thirst for a deep draught of joy—for a life as bright as you. But let’s not think of it now, Tito. It seems to me there will always be pale faces among the flowers, eyes looking in vain. Let us go.”
