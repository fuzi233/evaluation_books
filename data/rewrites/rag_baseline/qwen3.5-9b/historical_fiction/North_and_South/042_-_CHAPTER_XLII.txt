The silence that descended upon the room was not merely an absence of sound; it was a physical weight, a suffocating fog that pressed against the ribs until breathing became a labored act of defiance. Margaret lay upon the sofa, a statue carved from marble and despair, her eyes sealed shut against a world that had suddenly lost its color. The shock of her beloved voice failing her, replaced by a silence that ached like a fever, had stripped her of all capacity for speech or movement. She was prostrate, not in sleep, but in a paralysis of the soul, waiting for a music that would never undo the crushing quiet.

Mr. Bell sat by the fire, his stout limbs stiff with the rigors of travel, trapped in a limbo between duty and helplessness. He dared not summon the carriage to carry her back to Oxford; the journey would demand a strength she possessed in no measure. Instead, he watched her, a sentinel guarding a fallen queen, while the dinner prepared by Dixon below grew cold. The savory aroma of deviled chicken drifted up, but to Mr. Bell's senses, it tasted of sawdust and ash. With a tender, futile gentleness, he minced the meat, seasoning it with care, only to watch Dixon attempt to coax a bite from Margaret. The languid shake of her head was a final decree: in this state, food was poison, not nourishment.

"I cannot leave her," Mr. Bell muttered, rising with a groan that echoed the room's heaviness. "I must write to Oxford. The preparations can wait. Can Mrs. Lennox not come?"

Dixon wiped her eyes, her own grief spilling over for two souls, and delivered the blow: Mrs. Lennox was confined, too near the precipice of childbirth to undertake a journey.

"Well!" Mr. Bell coughed, clearing his throat as if to swallow the disappointment. "We must have Mrs. Shaw. She has returned to England, surely?"

"Yes, sir," Dixon replied, her voice tinged with a defensive protectiveness. "But I doubt she wishes to abandon Mrs. Lennox during such a... significant time."

"Significant time," Mr. Bell repeated, the phrase tasting bitter on his tongue. He cleared his throat again, unable to finish the thought. "She could be content to linger in Venice or Naples, or some of those Papish havens where her own 'significant times' unfolded in Corfu. But what does a prosperous woman's convenience mean compared to this? Look at her! Lying there as still as an altar-stone, a helpless, homeless, friendless creature named Margaret. Tell Mrs. Shaw to come. Secure a room for her by tomorrow night. I will ensure she arrives."

And so, the letter was written. Mrs. Shaw received it with tears streaming down her cheeks, declaring it so reminiscent of the General's pre-gout rages that she would treasure it forever. Had Mr. Bell merely requested her aid, pleading with a tone that suggested refusal was an option, her natural *vis inertiae* might have held her fast. True sympathy alone would not have moved her; it required the sharp, uncourteous command of authority to break her resistance. Once the order was absolute, she surrendered instantly, allowing her maid to pack her trunks with frantic efficiency.

As Captain Lennox escorted his mother to the carriage, Edith burst forth from the house, a whirlwind of caps, shawls, and tears. "Do not forget, Mamma! Margaret must come and live with us. Sholto departs for Oxford on Wednesday; you must instruct Mr. Bell regarding our arrival. And if you require Sholto, he can proceed directly from Oxford to Milton. Do not forget, Mamma—you are to bring Margaret back."

Inside the drawing-room, Mr. Henry Lennox sat cutting open the pages of a new review, seemingly oblivious to the storm gathering around him. "If you find Sholto's prolonged absence disagreeable, Edith," he remarked without looking up, "perhaps I should descend to Milton myself to render assistance."

"Oh, thank you," Edith replied, her voice softening. "Old Mr. Bell will manage everything; additional help may not be necessary. One rarely expects much *savoir-faire* from a resident Fellow. But dearest, darling Margaret—it will be wonderful to have her here again. You were such great allies years ago."

"Were we?" Mr. Henry asked indifferently, his gaze fixed on a passage in the journal.

"Well, perhaps not—I forget. I was so consumed with thoughts of Sholto. But isn't it providential? If Uncle is to pass, it is precisely now, when we have returned home, settled in the ancestral house, and fully prepared to receive Margaret? Poor thing! What a transformation awaits her from Milton! I shall procure new chintz for her bedroom, make it bright and cheerful, to lift her spirits."

Mrs. Shaw's journey to Milton was a tapestry woven from dread and determination. She oscillated between fearing the initial confrontation and plotting the swift removal of Margaret from "that horrid place" back to the plush comforts of Harley Street. "Oh dear!" she confided to her maid, staring out at the looming chimneys of the distant town. "Look at those chimneys! My poor sister Hale! I could not have rested in Naples had I foreseen this. I must have fetched her and Margaret away sooner." Privately, she acknowledged a truth she had long suppressed: she had always deemed her brother-in-law weak, but never so utterly diminished as he appeared now, having traded the radiant sanctuary of Helstone for this gray exile.

Within the house, Margaret remained unchanged. Pale, motionless, speechless, tearless. They informed her of her aunt's arrival, yet she offered neither surprise nor joy, nor did she register any aversion. Mr. Bell, his hunger finally returning, urged her to taste sweetbreads stewed with oysters, praising Dixon's culinary efforts. She shook her head with the same quiet obstinacy as the day before. Deprived of her company, he ate the entire dish himself, finding sustenance in the act of service.

It was Margaret who first perceived the halting of the cab arriving from the station. Her eyelids fluttered, her lips colored and trembling. Mr. Bell hastened downstairs to greet Mrs. Shaw. When they ascended, Margaret stood, fighting the vertigo that threatened to unbalance her. Upon seeing her aunt, she stepped forward into the open embrace awaiting her. First came the passionate release of tears soaking her aunt's shoulder, dissolving the numbness that had encased her heart. All the quiet habits of love, the tenderness of years, the bond forged with the dead—all melted away in that instant. An inexplicable likeness in look, tone, and gesture surged forth, reminding her with terrifying clarity of her mother, turning her frozen spirit into an overflowing river of warmth.

Mr. Bell slipped out of the room and retreated to the study, ordering a fire and attempting to distract himself by examining the books lining the shelves. Every volume whispered of his deceased friend, a constant reminder of the void left behind. Though his duties had shifted from watching Margaret to managing correspondence, his thoughts remained tethered to her. Just then, the sound of Mr. Thornton's voice reached the door. Dixon, emboldened by the arrival of Mrs. Shaw's entourage, dismissed him with cavalier brevity. The presence of the maid conjured visions of former grandeur, the Beresford lineage, and the "station"—a word she used with relish—from which her young mistress had been cast out and to which she was destined to return. These fantasies, shared complacently with Mrs. Shaw's maid, fueled Dixon's condescension toward anyone associated with Milton. Thus, despite her lingering awe of Mr. Thornton, she barred his entry with curt precision.

The discomfort of being contradicted vanished when Mr. Bell opened the study door. "Thornton! Is that you? Enter for a moment; I wish to speak with you."

Mr. Thornton complied, forcing Dixon to retreat to the kitchen, where she bolstered her ego with a prodigious tale of Sir John Beresford's coach-and-six during his tenure as High Sheriff.

"I confess, I am unsure why I summoned you," Mr. Bell admitted, pacing the room. "It is dreary enough to sit among objects that whisper of a dead friend. Yet, Margaret and her aunt must possess the drawing-room exclusively."

"Has Mrs.... has her aunt arrived?" Mr. Thornton asked.

"Arrived? Yes! Maid and all. One might have expected her to come alone at such a juncture! Now I shall be forced to vacate and navigate my way to the Clarendon."

"You must not go to the Clarendon. We have five or six vacant bedrooms at home."

"Well aired?"

"I trust my mother to ensure that."

"Then I shall merely ascend to bid that wan girl goodnight, pay my respects to her aunt, and depart with you immediately."

Mr. Bell lingered upstairs longer than anticipated. Mr. Thornton, impatient with business matters and having barely spared time to inquire after Miss Hale at Crampton, began to wonder at the delay.
