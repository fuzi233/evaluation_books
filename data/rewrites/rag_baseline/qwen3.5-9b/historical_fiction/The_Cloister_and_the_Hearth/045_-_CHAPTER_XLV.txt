The gathering at the hosier's dwelling hung suspended in a fragile equilibrium, the little party seated around the table digesting the weight of recent revelations. The atmosphere shattered only when their mother entered, her presence cutting through the murmurs like a blade. She swept a piercing gaze across the circle, her eyes searching for purchase, before laying the letter flat upon the wood. With deliberate slowness, she traced the inked lines with a trembling finger, feigning literacy to anchor herself and the room in the illusion that she could decipher the script. Then, abruptly, she lifted her head, locking eyes with Cornelis and Sybrandt. Their gazes dropped, unable to meet hers. In that instant, the tempest that had simmered beneath the surface erupted, crashing down with violent clarity.

Catherine seemed to inflate, swelling like an agitated bird ruffing its feathers against the wind. From her lips poured a torrent—a *Rhone* and *Saône* of wisdom and nonsense, of grand invectives and petty insults, a verbal deluge no man born of earth could muster in a single stream, let alone a woman. Her speech was a chaotic mosaic of accusation and revelation: "For ages I harbored suspicions that you kindled the flame between Gerard and your father, setting that old rogue, Ghysbrecht, upon the path. Now, here stand Gerard's own words, written in blood and ink, proving your treachery. You have driven your own flesh and blood into a distant, foreign land, robbing the mother who bore you of her darling, the pride of her eye, the joy of her heart. Yet, you remain consistent from end to end. When we were all boys, my others were a comfort; you were a curse—mischievous, sly, requiring a woman half a day merely to mend your torn garments. Why? Work wears cloth, but play cuts it. Prudence arrives with the beard, but none came to you. You were the last to retire and the last to rise, for honesty sleeps early and industry wakes betimes. Where two lie-in-beds exist in a house, there dwells a pair of ne'er-do-wells.

Often I have sat watching your ways, bewildered by your origins. You bear no resemblance to your father, nor are you akin to me save as a wasp differs from an ant. Surely you were altered in the cradle, or perhaps the cuckoo dropped you upon my floor, for you possess neither our hands nor our hearts. Of all my blood, none but you ever mocked those God afflicted. Behind my back, I heard you sneer at Giles for lacking size, and at my lily Kate for failing the strength of a Flemish mare. If you can mock men and women thus, surely you will rob a church next! For in the eyes of Him who created Kate and Giles, and in mine who suffered for them as I suffer for you, you are paltry, unfeeling, treasonable curs!

No, I will not hush, my daughter; the cup has overflowed. It takes a monumental effort to turn a mother's heart against the sons she has nursed upon her knees. Many times I have blinked, refusing to see too clearly, biting my tongue lest your father discover what I knew; he would have cast you to the door without hesitation. But now, the cup is full. And whence came this wealth? This past month you have rolled in gold. You earned not a single coin. May I never hear from another mouth how you acquired it. Since that night you lingered so late, returning with a swollen head, Cornelis, sloth and greed are ill-matched companions. Those who love money must sweat or steal. Well, if you plundered any poor soul, it was likely a woman, I swear it; for a man would drive you with his bare hand.

It matters not; it serves one purpose. It reveals how you intend to manage our affairs once they fall into your hands. I have watched you, my lads, for some time. You have squandered a groat today between you. I spend scarce a groat a week yet sustain you all, good and bad. I renounce waiting for the shoes that might walk behind your coffins. This shop and this house shall never be yours. Gerard is our heir—the poor Gerard you have banished and strived to destroy. Never address me as 'mother' again! Yet, you have rendered him tenfold dearer to me. My poor lost boy! Soon I shall see him again, hold him in my arms, set him upon my knees. Yes, you may stare! You are too cunning, yet not cunning enough. You severed the stalk, but you left the seed—the seed that shall outgrow you and outlive you. Margaret Brandt is swift, and this child is Gerard's, and what belongs to Gerard belongs to me. I have prayed the saints it may be a boy, and it shall be—it must be.

Kate, when I learned the truth, my bowels yearned over her unborn child as if it were my own. He is our heir. He will outlive us. You will not; for a corrupt heart within a carcass is like the worm in the nut, swiftly reducing the body to dust. So, Kate, retrieve Gerard's bib and tucker from the drawer you know well. One day we shall carry them to Sevenbergen. We shall borrow Peter Buyskens' cart and go to comfort Gerard's wife under her burden. She is his wife. Who is Ghysbrecht Van Swieten? Can he interpose himself between a couple and the altar, sundering those united by God and the priest? She is my daughter, and I am as proud of her as I am of you, Kate. As for you, stay out of my sight for a while, for you resemble the black dog in my eyes."

Cornelis and Sybrandt seized the implicit command and retreated, their movements sluggish, weighed down by a triad of emotions: remorse, impenitence, and a festering hate. They avoided her gaze as diligently as possible. For many days thereafter, she spoke not a word—not good, not bad, not indifferent—to either of them. Thus, she liberated her spirit.
