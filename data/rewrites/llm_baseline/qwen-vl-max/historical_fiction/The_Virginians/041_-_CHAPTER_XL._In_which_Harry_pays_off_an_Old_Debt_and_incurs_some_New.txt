The Tunbridge season was drawing to a close, and the Esmond party, weary of the endless rounds of tea and cards, prepared to depart. Madame de Bernstein had her sights set on Bath—where life was livelier, company more plentiful, and the gaming tables ever inviting. She would make her way there after visiting a few country friends. Harry Warrington, with only mild reluctance, agreed to accompany Lady Maria and Parson Sampson to Castlewood.

As they passed through Oakhurst village once more, they came upon the same hospitable cottage where Harry had been so warmly received. Lady Maria could not resist teasing him about the young ladies of the village—how they all seemed to be setting their caps at him, how his hostess plainly wished to snare him for one of her daughters. Her remarks grew sharper, more pointed, until Harry, annoyed, declared he would ride past their door—Lady Maria’s disapproval of the family was well known, and he felt no desire to linger under their roof. That evening, at the inn several miles down the road, his mood was sour. Lady Maria’s smiles brought no warmth to his face; her tears, so readily summoned, stirred no sympathy in him. He growled back at her complaints, and when she tried to draw him into private conversation, he refused. The chaplain, as if by design, remained in the room. Had Harry ordered Sampson to stay? She sighed and retired, leaving him to bow her away with cold politeness, entrusting her care to the landlady and her maid.

Ten minutes later, a horse galloped out of the inn yard—its rider unseen, its purpose unknown.

An hour after supper, Mrs. Betty returned for her lady’s smelling salts and found Parson Sampson alone, pipe in hand. “Mr. Warrington?” she asked. “Gone to bed.” “To walk in the moonlight,” Sampson corrected. “How should I know where Mr. Harry is?”

Next morning, Harry took his place beside Lady Maria’s carriage. But his brow remained dark. The storm within had not passed. He barely spoke during the journey. *Great heavens!* thought Lady Maria. *She must have told him she stole it!*

And indeed, she had. As they walked up the steep hill near Oakhurst on the Westerham road, Lady Maria, leaning heavily on Harry’s arm and quite smitten with him, poured forth sentimental vows—affectionate declarations, poetic promises, and exaggerated raptures. The more she loved, the colder he became. The sun beat down on her face, still fresh but marked by the lines of sixty years. Poor Harry felt the weight of her arm like a burden, and the walk uphill became unbearable. To think that this—this drag—would follow him all his life! He cursed the night air, the wine, and the foolish pledge he’d made under its influence.

Her words grated on him. She quoted fragments from plays she remembered, trying desperately to charm him. “My champion,” she called him. “My Henrico, my preserver!” And again: “Molinda will be faithful forever!” She clung to him. “Ah, child!” she sighed, pressing her fingers to her heart. “Have I not your image, your hair, your writing here? Shall it not go with me to the grave? It would, sir, were I to meet unkindness from my Henrico!”

A strange revelation. Madame Bernstein had given Harry the silken case—then burned the contents. Yet here was Maria, still carrying it. At that moment, as Harry jerked his arm away, she felt the first pang of guilt—not for lying, but for being caught in the lie. Heaven help us—if every falsehood demanded penance, we’d never escape sackcloth and ashes.

At Castlewood, Harry’s temper did not improve. The lord of the house was absent; the ladies were gone. Only Mr. Will Esmond was home, returning from partridge-shooting just as the carriage arrived. His face turned pale at the sight of his cousin, who greeted him with a sullen scowl.

Still, Harry tried to appear civil. They met at supper, where conversation began politely, though without warmth. Mr. Will had been to races? Several. Successful? “Pretty well,” he said. “And you’ve brought back my horse sound?” Harry asked sharply.

“What horse?” Will replied.

“The brown horse I played against you—won from you the night before you rode off.”

“Protest, I don’t understand,” Will said.

“You remember the horse, Mr. Esmond.”

“I remember playing you for a horse,” said Will. “But mine was not brown.”

“Mr. Sampson was present,” Harry said, turning to the chaplain. “Will you serve as umpire?”

“I must admit,” Sampson said, glancing nervously toward the absent master, “that Mr. Warrington played for the brown horse.”

“And he got the other one,” Will grinned. “Sold it for thirty shillings!”

“Not a word more,” Harry said calmly. “It’s merely a matter of a bet, my dear Lady Maria. Shall I serve you more chicken?”

He was courteous, even cheerful, while she remained. When she rose, he followed her to the door, closing it with a courtly bow. Then, once she was gone, he motioned the servants to leave. After they departed, Harry locked the heavy door and pocketed the key.

The click echoed through the hall. Will, sitting over his punch, glanced up. “What the devil do you mean by that?” he snapped.

“I guess there’s going to be a quarrel,” Harry replied, blandly. “No use having servants watch two gentlemen fight.”

“Who’s fighting?” Will demanded, eyes wide, hand gripping a knife.

“Mr. Sampson,” Harry said, “you were present when I played Mr. Will fifty guineas against his brown horse?”

“Against his horse?” Will bellowed.

“I’m not such a fool as you take me for,” Harry said, voice steady. “Though I do come from Virginia.” He repeated his question.

“I must own it,” Sampson said, looking apologetic.

“I don’t own any such thing,” Will insisted, forcing a laugh.

“No, sir,” Harry said, stepping forward. “Because it costs you no effort to lie—and cheat.”

“Hands off, Mr. Chaplain!” Harry warned. “Fair play!”

Before the chaplain could react, Will hurled a wine bottle at Harry’s head. Harry ducked just in time—the bottle shattered against the wall, destroying a portrait of an ancestor and splashing port wine across Sampson’s wig and face.

“Great heavens!” the parson cried, dripping red liquid. “I beg you, gentlemen, cease!”

But gentlemen, especially when provoked, rarely heed such pleas. The bottle missed. Will grabbed a large silver-handled knife and lunged. Harry caught his wrist—just as he’d seen boxers do at Marybone—and delivered a sharp blow to Will’s nose. He staggered backward, hitting the oak paneling, then dropped the knife, which Harry kicked under the table.

Will, too, knew boxing tricks. He charged forward, head lowered like a battering ram, aiming for Harry’s stomach.

Harry had seen such tactics before—in London, and among the negroes on his mother’s plantation. He sidestepped, then struck a crushing blow to Will’s ear. The man fell, head cracking against the table, and collapsed unconscious.

“Mr. Sampson,” Harry said, breathless but composed, “you’ll bear witness—it was fair.”

He unlocked the door. Servants rushed in, startled by the crash and shouts. They found two men covered in blood—one drenched in wine, the other in his own.

“Mr. Sampson will testify I struck fairly,” Harry said. “And Mr. Esmond threw the first blow. Undo his neckcloth—see if he lives. Gumbo, fetch a fleam. Stop—he’s waking. Lift him up. Tell a maid to clean the floor.”

In moments, Will regained consciousness. His eye was swollen shut. He groaned, then began cursing freely.

“He’s coming around,” Harry said.

“Oh, praise be Mussy!” sighed Betty.

“Ask him, Gumbo,” Harry said, with dry humor, “if he’d like another.”

“Massa Harry say, wool you like any maw?” Gumbo asked, bowing low.

“No, curse you, you black devil!” Will spat, swinging at the servant. (“So he nearly cut my tongue in half!” Gumbo explained later to Betty.)

“No—that is, yes!” he added, furious. “You infernal Mohock! Why doesn’t someone kick him out?”

“Because nobody dares,” Harry said, adjusting his ruffles with calm dignity.

“And nobody won’t neither,” the men muttered. They liked Harry. Will had no allies.

“We know all’s fair,” one said. “It ain’t the first time Master William’s been served so.”

“And I hope it won’t be the last,” Betty chimed in. “To strike a poor black gentleman so!”

By now, Will had sat up, wiped his face, and slunk off to bed.

“Surely it’s manners to say goodnight,” Harry said. “Goodnight, Mr. Esmond.”

His joke was clumsy—but he laughed inwardly at his own wit.

“He’s ad his zopper, and he goes to baid!” Betty exclaimed in dialect, making everyone laugh except Will, who left trailing curses like smoke.

The next morning, Harry wrote a note to Will, asking if he wanted a ride to town. If so, he’d frighten the highwaymen on Hounslow Heath and look “very genteel” at the Chocolate House. Will responded with a furious reply, demanding Harry go to hell—*not* Hounslow.

Later, a maiden appeared at Harry’s door. Gumbo stepped forward, holding a white triangle.

Harry knew what it was. “Of course,” he groaned. A letter from Molinda—her Enrico, her beloved. She hadn’t slept. Was he sleeping well in his ancestral halls? Would he never be cruel again? Kai ta loipa. He wouldn’t quote more. Ah, golden tablets—now faded leaves. Where is the juggler who once enchanted you?

After the brawl, Harry could not remain at Castlewood. He wrote a formal letter to the lord of the house, explaining the incident—with Sampson supervising, ensuring no spelling errors. He told Lady Maria that staying after assaulting a son of the house would be ill-bred. She wept at parting. He’d go to London, see younger beauties—none, she insisted, could love him as she did. Harry showed no emotion. The moment he crossed Castlewood Bridge, he cheered up. He urged his horses to ten miles an hour, sang to them, nodded at girls by the roadside, and chucked the landlady under the chin. He was not broken-hearted. He longed for London—St. James’s, Newmarket, the fashionable world. Tunbridge’s petty society now seemed childish.

By the time he reached London, nearly all his forty pounds had vanished. More money was needed. But Harry soon eased his mind. There were two sums of five thousand pounds—his and his brother’s—over which he held control. He’d borrow a little, win it back at cards, and live as befit his station. He would explain to Madam Esmond that a gentleman of his rank could not maintain proper company or appear in society on a mere two hundred a year.

He stayed briefly at the Bedford Coffee-House, then moved to Bond Street. He took lodgings, installed Gumbo, and placed his horses at a livery stable. Tailors, mercers, and shoemakers were summoned. With regret, he discarded his mourning clothes and donned a laced hat and waistcoat. Gumbo powdered his hair—now Harry looked as fashionable as any man in the Mall. He drove in his phaeton at the Ring. Rumors of his wealth preceded him. Curiosity swirled around the “fortunate Virginian.”

Until he could join the club officially, Lord March had entered his name at White’s Chocolate-House as a distinguished American gentleman. Fashionable London was small, but a young man with money at twenty-one could thrive—even out of season. Harry meant to enjoy himself.

He ordered Mr. Draper to sell five hundred pounds’ worth of stock. What would his mother say, had she known her son was already squandering his inheritance? He dined at taverns, supped at clubs, where Jack Morris introduced him with glowing praise. Life, youth, pleasure—wine flowed freely, and Harry drank greedily. Far away, perhaps, the pious widow prayed for her son. Behind the trees at Oakhurst, a tender heart beat for him. When the Prodigal Son wandered, were not love and forgiveness still watching?

Among the unpublished letters of the late Lord Orford, one exists—omitted by editor Peter Cunningbam, perhaps doubted. I myself saw a copy in Madam Esmond’s handwriting, labeled: *“Mr. H. Walpole’s account of my son Henry at London, and of Baroness Tusher—written to General Conway.”*

“ARLINGTON STREET, Friday Night.

“I’ve come away from my devotions to our Lady of Strawberry. Have I not knelt three weeks straight? My old joints ache with rheumatism. So I took a fit to visit London—go to Vauxhall, Ranelagh. Quoi! May I not have my rattle like other elderly babies? Suppose I tire of virtue and crave cakes and ale—shall your reverence forbid me? George Selwyn, Tony Storer, and I took a boat from Westminster. Was it Tuesday? No—Tuesday I was with the Dukes of Norfolk, just returned from Tunbridge. Wednesday, then. How should I know? Wasn’t I dead drunk after a whole pint of lemonade at White’s?

“The Norfolks told me about a young savage—Iroquois, Choctaw, or Virginian—who’s been making noise in London. An offshoot of the disreputable Esmond family of Castlewood—all the men gamblers, spendthrifts; the women—well, I won’t say. My father told me both late lords were in his pay. The last one, a beau of Queen Anne’s reign, rose from viscount to earl thanks to his notorious sister, Bernstein, late Tusher, née Esmond—a great beauty, favorite of the Pretender. She sold secrets to my father, whom she later betrayed. Now she’s loyal to the Hanoverians. ‘Will Horace Walpole’s tongue never stop scandal?’ says your wife over your shoulder. I kiss your hand. I am dumb. The Bernstein is a model of virtue. She married her father’s chaplain—not out of shame, but choice. Many nobles skip marriage altogether. She wasn’t ashamed to be Mrs. Tusher, nor did she take a German Baroncino for a second husband—no one outside Hanover ever saw him. The Yarmouth bears no grudge. Esther and Vashti are friends—cheating each other at cards all summer.

“‘And what has this to do with the Iroquois?’ asks your ladyship. The Iroquois was at Tunbridge too—winning, not cheating. They say he bled Lord March of thousands. Lord March, who’s quarreled with everyone, fought everyone, ridden over everyone, and been loved by everyone’s wife—except Mr. Conway’s, and not even hers. And the Queen herself, the Countess of England, Scotland, France, and Ireland, Queen of Walmoden and Yarmouth—may Heaven preserve her.

“You know Jack Morris, that impudent creature who slips into every house in London. At Vauxhall, he nodded to a young fellow—pretty enough, leaning on Jack’s arm, delighted by the garden. Lord, how he stared at the fireworks! Gods, how he huzzayed at the singing woman—horrible, painted wench who shrieked till my ears bled! Twopence worth of glass beads and tawdry cloth are treasures in Iroquois-land, and our savage valued them accordingly.

“A buzz spread: this was the fortunate youth. He won three hundred at White’s last night—from Rockingham and my nephew. Here he was, bellowing at music, so loud it pleased the ear. I hate puppet-shows, but I love treating children to one, Miss Conway! I send your ladyship my compliments—we shall see the dolls together.

“When the singer descended, Jack introduced the Virginian. I saw him blush crimson, make a fine bow—such grace, I never thought existed in wigwams. ‘There’s a certain jenny squaw about her,’ George said—joke not as bright as a firework. Then they retreated together.

“I’d eaten and drunk too much earlier. My companions wanted chicken and rack-punch. George fell asleep. I told Tony Storer about the Virginian’s family—especially the Bernstein’s past, and Lady Maria, who was once close to the late Prince of Wales. What did I say? Not half I knew, certainly not a tenth. But then—out bursts my savage, face flushed, in war paint. He’d been drinking fire-water in the next box!

“He cocked his hat, drew his sword, demanded: ‘Which gentleman is maligning my family?’ I had to hush him—lest he wake George. ‘I assure you, sir,’ I said, ‘I didn’t know you were near. I sincerely apologize.’

“But the damage was done.”
