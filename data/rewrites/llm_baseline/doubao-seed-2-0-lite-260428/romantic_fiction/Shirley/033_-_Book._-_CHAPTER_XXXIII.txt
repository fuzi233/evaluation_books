Fifteen-year-old Martin Yorke had a very specific, very mischievous plan to pull off that day—and the first rule of his scheme was that he could not, under any circumstances, attend grammar school.

So he picked at his breakfast until his plate sat practically untouched, and by the time Mark was slinging his schoolbag over his shoulder to leave for class, Martin was clutching his chest, face pale enough to sell a dramatic story of a sudden, stabbing pain around his heart. It was a very convincing performance, convincing enough that his mother fussed over him, and his father waved him off to claim the best armchair by the parlour fire and the morning paper, rather than tramping through the cold to Mr. Summer’s classes.

By the time the front door clicked shut behind Mark, and Matthew and Mr. Yorke had headed off to the counting house, Martin had four tasks to tick off his list. The first, and most urgent for a growing boy who’d skipped his morning meal on purpose, was to make up for that missed breakfast. The next three? Get his mother, Hortense Moore, and the housekeeper Mrs. Horsfall well and truly out of the way before four o’ clock that afternoon.

He slipped quiet as a cat to the larder, the servants all behind closed kitchen doors eating their own late breakfast, his mother and Miss Hortense gossiping on the lawn. He took his time picking out the very best treats: three blushing rosy apples resting on a bed of straw, a buttery apricot puff, a small damson tart, a currant tea-cake still slightly warm from the hearth, a tender wing of cold fowl and a slice of cured ham he sliced free with his clasp knife, and a thick wedge of cold custard pudding to round it all out. His arms loaded high, he was halfway across the hall, three steps from the back parlour where he intended to eat his feast in peace, when the front door opened.

It was Matthew. The older boy had never bought Martin’s fake chest pain for a single second, had been muttering about him “shamming Abraham” ever since breakfast, and the sight of Martin staggering under that mountain of forbidden snacks was all the proof he needed to prove he was right.

Martin froze for all of one heartbeat—then a bright, devious little grin spread across his face. This accident wasn’t a disaster. It was an opportunity.

He knew exactly how his elder brother picked fights: dry snide laughs, taunts about being a lazy little faker, sneers at his fake illness. Martin snapped back with a cutting, deliberately provocative retort, and before ten seconds were up the two Yorke boys were rolling and shouting in the hall loud enough to rouse the whole house. The servants came running, his mother and Hortense darted in from the lawn, no one could pry them apart, and Mr. Yorke stormed out of the counting house to threaten to throw whichever of the two started it out of the house for good.

When they finally pulled Martin off Matthew, he was completely calm, not a scratch on him, smiling to himself. He’d known their fight would send his mother straight into one of her well-known hysterics, and that by mid-afternoon she’d be tuckered out enough to spend the rest of the day napping in her bedchamber. That was the hardest part of his plan, already done.

He lounged on the sofa pretending to be a delicate invalid for the rest of the morning, at one point half-convincing himself this whole scheme to play matchmaker for Caroline Helstone wasn’t worth the hassle—until he glanced at the half-finished portrait of Robert Moore Hortense had left on the dining room easel, the one with wild unbound hair and eyes dim with grief, and shook his head. No, he wasn’t going to quit. He wanted to see that sad girl smile, and he wanted to outwit every single stuffy grown woman in the neighbourhood while he did it.

After dinner, just as he’d predicted, his mother dragged herself up the stairs to her bed, declaring the scene with the boys had worn her out completely. Next on his list: Hortense. He found her settled in the back parlour mending stockings, and launched into a long, rambling story about Sarah the maid at the Hollow, the one who was now running the cottage all by herself. He casually dropped the news that Sarah supposedly had three suitors tripping over themselves to court her, and that the neighbours had seen her feeding them lavish meals every night. Hortense, who could not resist a chance to snoop on a household scandal, grabbed her shawl within five minutes and set off for the Hollow to see the state of things for herself.

That left only Mrs. Horsfall. Martin slipped to his mother’s work basket, fished out the key to the sideboard cupboard, pulled out a bottle of sweet spiced cordial and a small glass, left them out on the parlour table, then knocked on the door to Robert Moore’s bedchamber where the nurse sat keeping watch. He told her the family had all stepped out, and she was welcome to go sit by the back parlour fire and help herself to a nice glass of refreshment with no one to disturb her. He watched her go in, then shut the door softly behind her. The nurse was safely occupied for hours.

Martin grabbed his cap and ran for the wood. It was only half past three, but the sky had turned dark and heavy, soft fat snowflakes starting to drift down, the wind cutting sharp through the bare branches of the old twisted oaks. He loved it, the wild, spectral hush of the snow coming down. He paced the narrow path back and forth, the snow falling faster, the wind picking up from a soft moan to a low howl.

“Took her long enough,” he muttered, when he finally heard the quick, light footstep on the path, barely making a sound over the dead leaves. It was Caroline Helstone, her shawl wrapped tight around her, her cheeks pink, not even seeming to notice the bitter cold or the blinding snow swirling around her.

“Martin—how is he?” she burst out, the first words from her mouth.

Martin’s chest did a strange little soft flip. She didn’t care a bit about the snow, or the cold, or what anyone would say if they saw her, all she cared about was him. He almost felt bad for not having brought a spare cloak to wrap around her.

He teased her a little, told her he wasn’t going to run messages for her anymore, that he was far too “delicate” after his terrible chest pain that morning to traipse through the snow, and before long he’d talked her into trusting him, into letting him lead her down the quiet lane to Briarmains, telling her he could get her inside to see Robert herself. She hesitated for only a second before nodding, and slipping her hand through his arm.

The whole house was quiet, exactly as Martin promised. He kicked his shoes off on the mat at the door, so no sound of their steps would carry, and Caroline followed him barefoot up the staircase, her feet light as a shadow. He tapped on Robert’s bedchamber door three times before a low, weak, familiar voice called out for them to enter.

Martin stepped in first, telling the wounded man that a lady had come to call on him, that none of the staff had been around to turn her away. Robert, surprised, ordered Martin to send her in—and then he froze, wide-eyed, when Martin stepped aside and said “Come in, Miss Caroline.”

Caroline stepped into the middle of the room, and the tall, thin, wasted figure of Robert Moore stumbled forward to take both her hands. Outside, the storm raged so hard the whole house seemed to rattle, but neither of them heard a single howl of wind, or saw the snow piling up against the window panes. They only saw each other. They talked of all the times Caroline had tried to visit before, turned away at the door, of the long, bleak weeks the wounded man had spent alone, of the quiet, sick despair Caroline had suffered through when she thought she would never see him again. When she leaned down to brush her soft lips over his thin, cold, work-worn fingers, a great, trembling tear rolled down his hollow, gaunt cheek, and he swore he would hold that gentle kiss close in his heart for as long as he lived.

Martin slipped out, shutting the door behind him, grinning to himself like he was the most powerful king in the shire. He’d never pulled off a scheme half as good as this one. He waited in the gallery, keeping watch, making sure no stray servant or family member wandered up to disturb them, until he heard a faint cough from his mother’s bedchamber down the hall. Panicked, he threw open the door to Robert’s room. They’d had twenty whole minutes, way more than the quarter of an hour he’d promised.

“Come on, now, out you go!” he hissed. Caroline protested for half a second, but when he reminded her his mother was up and moving around, she let Martin wrap her shawl tight around her, and hurried down the stairs, slipping out the front door just as Mrs. Yorke’s footsteps creaked on the gallery above them. She skimmed across the snowy lane, her feet barely sinking into the drifts, gone before Martin could even work up the nerve to ask her for the kiss he thought he’d earned for pulling off the best secret reunion the two of them had been dreaming of for months.

He leaned against the doorframe, watching her go, the snowflakes landing on his rosy cheeks. He told himself he’d only missed claiming his reward because he’d been in a rush, not because a soft, boyish shyness had held him back. But even without the kiss, he knew, somewhere quiet in his heart, he’d done a far kinder, far more wonderful thing than he’d ever planned to do that day.
