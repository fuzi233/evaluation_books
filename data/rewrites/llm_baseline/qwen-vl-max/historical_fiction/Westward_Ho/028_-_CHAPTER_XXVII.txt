“All precious things, discover'd late, To them who seek them issue forth; For love in sequel works with fate, And draws the veil from hidden worth.”

—The Sleeping Beauty.

Thus Ayacanora took up her place in Lucy’s cabin, officially welcomed as a member of the crew.

Yet she proved no easy companion. From the moment she stepped into the world of the white man, she entered that perilous transition which has undone so many native souls—when simplicity meets civilization, when wonder overwhelms identity. It was the same crisis that would later hasten the downfall of North American tribes, and had it not been for Amyas’s unwavering sense and steadfast care, Ayacanora might have met a fate even darker than that of Pocahontas, daughter of the Virginia king. Once honored at the court of King James I as a sister sovereign, and later claimed by noble families as an ancestor, Pocahontas ended her days in poverty, shut away in some wretched garret in Wapping.

For the mind of the savage, overwhelmed by the superiority of the white man’s skill, wealth, and knowledge, often collapses under the weight of shame. The body, fed without labor, grows weak and idle. Self-respect fades. Self-reliance vanishes. And with them go the few virtues that once sustained the wilderness-born. Then begins the slide—toward theft, sloth, drunkenness, and despair. Easy, if not certain.

And so, at first, it seemed Ayacanora was slipping down that path fast. The warrior-prophetess of the Omaguas, once fierce and proud, now appeared little more than a willful child. Diana of the Meta, after marveling at the great floating house—wandering decks, peering into cupboards and corners—developed a curious habit: stealing trinkets that caught her fancy. She never asked; she simply took. When Amyas forbade her, she threatened to drown herself and sulked in her cabin all day.

She obeyed him, save in one thing: sweets. Perhaps her blood craved the fruits of her forest home. Whatever the cause, the bishop’s stores of fruit and confections dwindled swiftly. Worse still, the sweet Spanish wine Amyas had reserved for Lucy’s daily comfort vanished too.

Another stern lecture followed. Amyas told her how shameful it was to steal from poor, sick Lucy. She responded, as always, with threats of suicide—and ran toward the rail, intent on fulfilling them. But Amyas caught her just in time, forgave her, and held her close.

Then came a violent fit of weeping, repentance, and promises. A week later, he discovered she had tricked the Portuguese steward into giving her another bottle of wine instead. Lucky for her, the wine affected her as he had warned: she acted foolishly one evening, woke with a splitting headache the next morning. The truth came out. Amyas summoned the steward for punishment—but Ayacanora intervened, begging mercy, even offering to take the flogging herself. She confessed the steward had spoken truth: she had threatened his life, and he had given her the wine only out of fear.

Her own headache and Amyas’s cold gaze taught her enough. After another failed attempt at drowning herself, the stubborn beauty calmed—at least for a while. And better still, she vowed never again to touch fermented liquor, a vow she kept until her last breath.

Yet Amyas still wrestled with worry. How could he guide a girl who, at the slightest provocation, chose death over discipline? Punish her? He dared not. Send her ashore? He felt a deep conviction—no superstition, but a quiet certainty—that her affection for the English was divinely sent, that God had placed her in his care, and that He would demand an account of her soul.

At his wit’s end, he turned to prayer. Again and again, he begged God to show him what to do—before she destroyed herself or someone else. At last, he decided: let Parson Jack teach her the basics of Christianity. That way, she could be baptized upon their return to England.

But here arose a new problem. She refused Jack outright—any teacher but Amyas himself. And Amyas had good reason to decline such a role. So for weeks, Ayacanora remained untaught, except in English, which she mastered with astonishing speed.

Then came yet another trial. She developed a sharp dislike—not only for John Brimblecombe, whose walk and voice she mimicked for the sailors’ amusement—but also for Will Cary, whom she barred from speaking to her or approaching her. Was she jealous of his closeness to Amyas? Or did she, with a woman’s instinct, sense that he was the only other man aboard bold enough to court her—though, to be fair, Will had no such intention, nor did Amyas.

When questioned, her reply was firm: “Cary is a cacique, like Amyas. There should not be two caciques.” One day, she even suggested Amyas kill his rival and claim the ship for himself. She sulked for days when Amyas laughed and repeated her advice to its intended victim.

Even the black crew suffered. Ayacanora regarded them with deep disgust. At first, she avoided them. Later, she hurled anything within reach—bottles, tools, bits of cloth—until the poor men, fearing for their lives, complained to Amyas. He granted them peace—for a time.

Over the rest of the crew, she ruled like a queen. She called them from their work to run errands, make toys, obey her commands—sometimes enforced with a sharp slap across the ear. Yet they loved her, especially old Yeo. Sailors, true to their nature, doted on her, obeyed her, joked with her—as one might with a tame leopard, knowing its claws could strike at any moment. But she entertained them. And Amyas, too. They needed a pet—what finer one could they have? As for Amyas, her presence, though often trying, kept his mind occupied. Her wildness stirred him, distracted him from the heavy thoughts of meeting his mother, from the terrible tale he must tell her. Without her, those memories might have crushed him entirely, making all his victories and escapes seem meaningless.

At last, events reached a breaking point.

For many days, the ship had sailed steadily before a favorable wind. They’d passed the West Indian islands and now drifted safely through the vast weed-choked waters of the Sargasso Sea. Here, for the first time, they relaxed their strict discipline and began to “rummage” their prize—the spoils of war.

What they found—gold, silver, jewels, goods—mattered little to readers. Suffice it to say, there was enough to make Amyas rich beyond measure, after paying all claims: Cary’s share, the crew’s, Mr. Salterne’s third as owner of the vessel.

In the captain’s cabin, however, were two chests. One contained magnificent Mexican feather garments. The other, Spanish and East Indian finery—likely meant for a senora in Caracas, having passed through Havana and Cartagena.

At Cary’s suggestion, the crew voted these two chests to Ayacanora—her due, in recognition of her Amazonian courage and service.

She seized them greedily, carried them to Lucy’s cabin, and spent hours kneeling among them, gazing with delight. The Mexican clothes she dismissed as crude. But the Spanish finery—those were treasures. For days, she appeared on deck, dazzling in Seville mantillas, Madrid hats, brocade farthingales, and every glittering trifle she could wear.

The men tittered. Amyas felt like crying. Nothing is more pitiful than a child’s vanity—except perhaps a grown person pretending to be childish. And nothing more agonizing than a child’s heartbreak when she realizes she’s been mocked instead of admired.

Amyas wanted to speak—but feared her reaction. Yet the cure came naturally. The spectacle lasted three days. Then, unable to endure the laughter any longer, she turned to Amyas: “Ayacanora quite English girl now—is she not?”

A titter echoed behind her. She looked around, saw a dozen faces laughing openly. In an instant, she understood. She darted down the companion-ladder and disappeared.

Amyas, expecting her to leap overboard, rushed after her. But she had locked herself in with Lucy. He heard her sobbing violently, and Lucy’s faint voice asking what had happened.

He knocked. No answer. She refused to come out all day. By evening, they were forced to break the door open to prevent Lucy from starving.

Inside, Ayacanora sat amid torn clothes scattered on the floor. Her finery lay in ruins. She wept as if her heart would burst. Poor Lucy wept too—half from hunger, half from shared sorrow.

Amyas tried to comfort her. “They’ll never laugh at you again,” he promised.

“But then,” he added hesitantly, “you must not be so… so…”

“So what?” she asked, weeping harder.

“So like a wild girl, Ayacanora.”

Her hands fell to her knees. A shudder ran through her chest. She dropped to her knees before him, looking up with pleading eyes.

“Yes—wild girl. Poor, bad wild girl. But I will be English girl now!”

“Fine clothes won’t make you English, my child,” said Amyas.

“No—English clothes. English heart! Good heart, like yours! Yes—I will be good. Sir John shall teach me!”

“There’s my good maid,” said Amyas. “Sir John shall begin tomorrow.”

“No! Now! Now! Ayacanora cannot wait. She will drown herself if she is bad another day! Come, now!”

She made him fetch Brimblecombe. The honest parson spoke patiently for over an hour. That very night, Ayacanora told Lucy everything he had said.

From then on, whenever Jack went to read and pray with Lucy, Ayacanora stayed—no longer fleeing to the deck. She stood quietly, trying to understand. She knelt when he knelt. She tried to pray—so she might have an English heart. And though her prayers were silent, they were not unheard.

Days passed, hopeful and peaceful. Then, one morning, shortly after they left the Sargasso Sea, the ship sailed on quietly. The men worked under the awning—repairing, chatting, tinkering. Brimble7combe catechized Ayacanora in the cabin. Amyas and Cary, cigars in hand, discussed plans for future adventures against the Spaniards. Amyas poured out his bitter hatred—his desire for revenge for his brother and Rose—a darkness settled deep in his soul.

Suddenly, a commotion below. A scuffle. A shout. Both men leapt to their feet.

Jack Brimblecombe burst onto deck, clutching his head.

“Save me! Save me from that she-fiend! She’s possessed by a legion! She broke my nose—ripped out half my hair! I’ve none to spare! Here she comes! Stand by me, gentlemen! Satanas, I defy thee!”

He hid behind Amyas and Cary as Ayacanora stormed up, wild-eyed, like a Maenad. Seeing Amyas, she stopped.

“If you had defied Satan down below,” Cary laughed, “I suspect he wouldn’t have attacked you so boldly, Master Jack.”

“I am innocent!” Jack cried. “As pure as the babe unborn! Oh, Mr. Cary! This is too much!”

“What happened?” Amyas asked.

“He looked at me,” she declared, fiercely.

“Well, a cat may look at a king.”

“But he sha’n’t look at Ayacanora. Only you—no one else—or I’ll kill him!”

Jack protested he hadn’t even glanced at her. But the idea—whether real or imagined—had taken root. She refused to return below. Jack left, grumbling, hairless and sporting a black eye for a week.

“At least,” Cary said, relighting his cigar, “it’s a fault on the right side.”

“God grant me grace,” Amyas replied, “or it may be on the wrong side for me.”

“He will, old heart-of-oak,” Cary said, putting his arm around Amyas’s neck—to Ayacanora’s clear annoyance. She walked to the rail, pulled out a fishing line, and amused herself watching flying fish dart past bonitos.

Eventually, bored, she moved forward to listen to John Squire, the armorer, who sat mending a helmet and humming a tune—altered, of course—about his homeland:

“Oh, Bideford is a pleasant place, it shines where it stands,
And the more I look upon it, the more my heart it warms;
For there are fair young lasses, in rows upon the quay,
To welcome gallant mariners, when they come home from say.”

“It’s Sunderland, John Squire,” said his mate. “Not Bidevor.”

“Well, Bidevor’s as good as Sunderland any day,” Squire replied. “No say-coals to blacken the place, but just as fine harmonies, Tommy Hamblyn—”

“Oh, if I was a herring, to swim the ocean o’er,
Or if I was a say-dove, to fly unto the shoor,
To fly unto my true love, a waiting at the door,
To wed her with a gold ring, and plough the main no moor.”

Then Salvation Yeo interrupted:

“Aren’t you ashamed, John Squire, to your years, singing such carnal vanities after all the providences you’ve seen? Let the songs of Zion be in your mouth, man, if you must caterwaul all day!”

“You sing ‘em yourself then, gunner.”

“Well,” said Yeo, “and why not?” And he pulled out his psalm-book and began:

“Such as in ships and brittle barks
Into the seas descend,
Their merchandise through fearful floods
To compass and to end;
There men are forced to behold
The Lord’s works what they be;
And in the dreadful deep the same,
Most marvellous they see.”

“Humph!” said Squire. “Very good and godly. But still I du like a merry catch now and then. Wouldn’t you let a body sing ‘Rumbelow’—even when he’s heaving the anchor?”

“Well, I don’t know,” said Yeo. “But the Lord’s people had better praise the Lord then too, and pray for a good voyage, instead of howling about—”

“A randy, dandy, dandy O,
A whet of ale and brandy O,
With a rumbelow and a Westward-ho!
And heave, my mariners all, O!”

“Is that fit talk for immortal souls?” Yeo demanded. “How does that child’s-trade sound beside the Psalms, John Squire?”

It was precisely this moment that sealed the discovery.

For Salvation Yeo, wishing to mock the old song, had sung it with its full nasal twang—just as he had done twelve years earlier, hoisting John Oxenham’s anchor in Plymouth Sound.

And Ayacanora, standing beside Amyas, listening to the men, suddenly started. Then, half to herself, she sang the tune back—word for word, note for note, in the very same voice.

Yeo froze. His face turned pale.

“Who sang that?” he asked sharply.

“The little maid here,” Amyas said. “She’s learning English well.”

“The little maid?” Yeo whispered, growing paler still. “Why do you scare an old servant by talking of little maids, Captain Amyas? Well…” he muttered to himself, “as I am a sinful saint, if I hadn’t seen where the voice came from, I could have sworn it was her—just as we taught her by the river, I and William Penberthy of Marazion, my dear comrade. The Lord have mercy on me!”

All grew silent when Yeo spoke of that lost child. Ayacanora, pleased by Amyas’s praise, hummed softly:

“And heave, my mariners all, O!”

Yeo jumped up from his gun.

“I can’t bear it! As I live, I can’t! You, Indian maiden—where did you learn that?”

Ayacanora looked up, startled by his intensity, then at Amyas to see if she had done wrong. Then she turned away, saucy, and hummed on.

“Ask her, for mercy’s sake—ask her, Captain Leigh!”

“My child,” Amyas said, switching to Indian speech, “how is it you sing that so perfectly? Have you ever heard it before?”

Ayacanora looked puzzled, shook her head.

“If you speak Indian to Ayacanora, she is dumb. She must be English girl now, like poor Lucy.”

“Well then,” Amyas said, “do you remember—Ayacanora—anything from when you were a little girl?”

She paused. Then raised her hands above her head.

“Trees—great trees like the Magdalena—always nothing but trees—wild and bad everything. Ayacanora won’t talk about that.”

“Do you remember anything that grew on those trees?” Yeo asked eagerly.

She laughed. “Silly! Flowers and fruit, nuts—monkey-cups too. Ayacanora climbed up after them—when she was wild. I won’t tell any more.”

“But who taught you to call them monkey-cups?” Yeo asked, trembling.

“Monkey’s drink. Mono drink.”

“Mono?” Yeo asked, testing another clue. “How did you know beasts were called monos?”

“She might have heard it coming down with us,” Cary said, joining them.

“Ay, monos,” she replied, defensively. “Faces like little men, tails. And one very dirty black one, with a beard, says Amen in a tree to all the other monkeys—just like Sir John on Sunday.”

This brought laughter from all but Yeo.

“But don’t you remember any Christians? White people?”

She was silent.

“Don’t you remember a white lady?”

“Um?”

“A woman—very pretty—with hair like his?” pointing to Amyas.

“No.”

“What do you remember, besides Indians?” Yeo asked, desperate.

She turned her back, annoyed.

“Try again,” Amyas urged. She focused.

“Ayacanora remembers great monkeys—black, oh, so high,” she said, raising her hand above her head, then making a gesture of disgust.

“Monkeys? With tails?”

“No—like man. Ah! Yes—just like Cooky there—dirty Cooky!”

The unfortunate son of Ham, passing nearby, narrowly escaped a marlingspike thrown by Ayacanora.

“Ayacanora,” Amyas said, “if you throw anything else at Cooky, I must have you whipped.”

“I’ll kill you then,” she answered, flatly.

“She means negurs,” Yeo muttered. “Where could she have seen them? Could it be the Cimaroons?”

“But why would someone who saw whites forget them, yet remember negroes?” Cary asked.

“Let us try again,” Amyas said. “Do you remember any great monkeys but those black ones?”

“Yes,” she said slowly. “Devil.”

“Devil?” all three asked—knowing well that Indians believed the devil appeared to their shamans.

“Ay—him Sir John tells about on Sundays.”

“Save and help us!” Yeo gasped. “And what was he like?”

She made signs—a monkey’s face, a gray beard like Yeo’s. Then came gestures around her neck—confusing them all.

“I know!” Cary burst out, laughing. “Sir Urian had a ruff! Trunk-hose too, my fair dame? Wait—let me test. Was his neck like the senor commandant’s, the Spaniard?”

Ayacanora clapped her hands—understood!

“The ‘devil’ was a monkey, with a gray beard, wearing a ruff—humph!”

“Ay!” she said, in fluent Spanish. “Mono de Panama; viejo diablo de Panama.”

Yeo threw up his hands. “Oh Lord of all mercies! Those were the last words of Mr. John Oxenham! Ay—and the devil is surely none other than Don Francisco Xararte! Oh dear! oh dear! my sweet young lady! My pretty little maid! Don’t you know me? Don’t you know Salvation Yeo, who carried you over the mountains, who climbed for monkey-cups for you, my dear? And William Penberthy, who picked flowers for you? And your poor father—just like Mr. Cary there, only he had a black beard, black curls, and swore terribly, like a Spaniard—my dear young lady?”

And the old man fell to his knees, kissing her hands.

The crew, thinking him mad, crowded aft.

“Steady, men—don’t vex him!” Amyas said. “He thinks he’s found his little maid.”

“And so do I, Amyas,” Cary said.

“Steady, steady, my masters! If this turns out false, his wits will crack. Mr. Yeo—can’t you think of any other token?”

Yeo stamped impatiently. “What need? It’s her! I tell you—it’s her! What a beauty she’s grown! Oh dear! Where were my eyes all this time? Beholding her, and not seeing her? ‘Tis her very mortal self! And don’t you remember me, my dear? Salvation Yeo, who taught you to sing ‘Heave my mariners all, O!’ sitting on a log by the boat on the sand—where red lilies grew in the moss, dear, wasn’t there? And we made posies to put in your hair?”

The old man spoke on, pleading, suggesting, as if he could coax her into being the child he had lost.

Ayacanora watched him—first angry, then amused, then attentive, then utterly absorbed.

Then—suddenly—she flushed crimson. Snatching her hands from his, she covered her face.

“Do you remember any of this, my child?” Amyas asked gently.
