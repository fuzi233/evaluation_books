Winter was deepening, yet the days grew longer without bringing the promised brightness of hope that usually greets a February sun. Mrs. Thornton had entirely withdrawn from the household. Mr. Thornton visited occasionally, but his calls were strictly limited to the study and addressed solely to her father. Mr. Hale remarked that Mr. Thornton remained unchanged; indeed, the scarcity of their meetings seemed to heighten the value Mr. Hale placed upon them. From what Margaret could discern of their conversations, there was no trace of resentment or vexation in Mr. Thornton's conduct regarding the cessation of his visits. Rather, his business affairs, complicated by the recent strike, demanded a closeness of attention he had neglected the previous winter. Even Margaret noted that he spoke of her from time to time, always in a calm, friendly manner, neither avoiding nor actively seeking mention of her name.

Margaret herself lacked the spirits to lift her father's mood. The dreary peace of the present moment followed a prolonged period of anxiety and turmoil, interspersed with storms, which had drained her mind of its elasticity. She attempted to occupy herself by teaching the two younger Boucher children and striving diligently toward goodness. Yet, she worked hard, truly hard, for her heart seemed dead to the fruits of her efforts. Though she performed her duties punctually and painfully, she remained as far removed from cheerfulness as ever; her life continued to feel bleak and desolate. The only task she executed well was born of unconscious piety: the silent comforting and consoling of her father. Every mood of his found a ready sympathizer in Margaret; every wish of his, however quiet or hesitant, she strove to foresee and fulfill. Her spirit of obedience was meek and complete.

March brought news of Frederick's marriage. Letters arrived from him and Dolores; hers were written in a mix of Spanish and English, natural enough, while his were peppered with linguistic inversions showing how deeply his bride's idiom was taking root. Upon receiving Henry Lennox's letter—which announced the slim hope of Frederick clearing himself at a court-martial due to missing witnesses—Frederick had penned a vehement missive to Margaret. He renounced England as his country, wishing to "unnative" himself, declaring he would refuse a pardon or live in the land if permitted. At first, these words caused Margaret to weep bitterly, seeming so unnatural. However, upon reflection, she perceived them merely as the poignant expression of a hope crushed by disappointment. She realized there was nothing left but patience. In his subsequent letters, Frederick spoke so joyfully of the future that he showed no thought for the past, allowing Margaret to find a purpose for the patience she had been cultivating.

The charming, timid letters from Dolores began to hold a fascination for both Margaret and her father. The young Spaniard was clearly eager to make a favorable impression on her lover's English relations; her feminine care peeked out at every erasure. The announcement of the marriage was accompanied by a magnificent black lace mantilla, chosen by Dolores herself for her unseen sister-in-law—a woman Frederick described as a paragon of beauty, wisdom, and virtue. This union elevated Frederick's worldly status to heights they could scarcely desire. Barbour and Company, one of Spain's most extensive trading houses, welcomed him as a junior partner. Margaret smiled faintly and then sighed as memories of her old tirades against trade resurfaced. Here was her *preux chevalier*, her knightly brother, transformed into a merchant and trader! Yet, she immediately rebelled against herself, protesting silently against the confusion implied between a Spanish merchant and a Milton mill-owner. Regardless, trade or no trade, Frederick was exceedingly happy. Dolores must be charming, and the mantilla was exquisite! With that, she returned to her present reality.

That spring, her father occasionally suffered from a difficulty in breathing that distressed him greatly. Margaret was less alarmed, noting that the condition vanished completely during intervals, yet she remained so desperate to see him rid of it entirely that she urged him urgently to accept Mr. Bell's invitation to visit him in Oxford in April. Mr. Bell's invitation extended to Margaret as well; indeed, he wrote a special letter commanding her attendance. Still, Margaret felt it would be a greater relief to remain quietly at home, entirely free from responsibility, resting her mind and heart in a way she had not managed for over two years.

When her father finally drove off toward the railroad station, Margaret acutely felt the immense weight of pressure that had previously borne down on her time and spirits. It was astonishing, almost staggering, to realize how much liberty she possessed: no one depended on her for cheering care or even happiness; there was no invalid to plan for or think for. She could be idle, silent, and forgetful—and, most surprisingly, she could be unhappy if she chose. For months, her personal cares and troubles had been stuffed into a dark cupboard, but now she had the leisure to retrieve them, to mourn over them, to study their nature, and to seek the true method of subduing them into elements of peace. For weeks, she had been vaguely conscious of their existence, hidden from view. Now, she would confront them all at once, assigning each its proper role in her life. Sitting almost motionless for hours in the drawing-room, she reviewed the bitterness of every memory with unwavering resolve. Once, at the stinging thought of the faithlessness that spawned such an abasing falsehood, she cried aloud.

She refused to acknowledge the force of the temptation anymore. Her plans for Frederick had all failed; the temptation lay there as a dead mockery, never possessing life within it. The lie had been despicably foolish in the light of subsequent events, proving that faith in the power of truth was infinitely wiser.

In her nervous agitation, she unconsciously opened a book belonging to her father lying on the table. The words that caught her eye seemed crafted specifically for her current state of acute self-abasement:

*"Je ne voudrois pas reprendre mon cœur en ceste sorte... mais je voudrois le corriger par voye de compassion. Or sus, mon pauvre cœur, nous voilà tombe dans la fosse... Revelons-nous, et quittons-la pour jamais, reclamons la misericorde de Dieu... remettons-nous au chemin de l'humilité. Courage, soyons meshuy sur nos gardes..."*

"The way of humility," Margaret thought. "Ah, that is what I have missed! But courage, little heart. We will turn back, and by God's help, we may find the lost path."

Rising immediately, she determined to engage in work that would draw her out of herself. Passing the drawing-room door on her way upstairs, she summoned Martha and tried to pierce the grave, respectful, servant-like manner that had crusted over her individuality with almost mechanical obedience. It was difficult to coax Martha into speaking of personal matters, but eventually, mentioning Mrs. Thornton struck the right chord. Martha's face brightened, and under slight encouragement, she spun a long tale of how her father, in his early life, had been connected to Mrs. Thornton's husband—perhaps even doing him a kindness, though Martha barely knew the details as it happened when she was a small child. Circumstances had separated the families until Martha was nearly grown, when her father, having sunk lower from his original occupation as a warehouse clerk, and her mother deceased, faced ruin. It was Mrs. Thornton who sought them out, providing for them and caring for them.

"I had the fever and was delicate," Martha explained. "Mrs. Thornton and Mr. Thornton never rested until they nursed me up in their own house and sent me to the sea. The doctor said the fever was catching, but they cared not for that—not even Miss Fanny, who went visiting the folks she intended to marry into. So, though she was afraid at the time, it all ended well."

"Miss Fanny going to be married!" Margaret exclaimed.

"Yes; to a wealthy gentleman, though he is much older than she. His name is Watson, and his mills are somewhere beyond Hayleigh. It's a very good match, despite his gray hair."

At this news, Margaret fell silent long enough for Martha to regain her propriety and habitual brevity. She swept up the hearth, asked when to prepare tea, and departed with the same wooden countenance with which she had entered. Margaret had to check herself from indulging a habit she had recently acquired: imagining how every event concerning Mr. Thornton would affect him, wondering if he would like or dislike it.

The next day she taught the little Boucher children, took a long walk, and concluded with a visit to Mary Higgins. To Margaret's surprise, she found Nicholas already home; the lengthening daylight had deceived her regarding the late hour. He, too, seemed to have entered further into the path of humility; he was quieter and less self-asserting.

"So the old gentleman is away on his travels, is he?" Nicholas said. "The little ones told me so. Eh! but they're sharp ones, I almost think they beat my own wenches for sharpness, though 'tis wrong to say so, and one of 'em is in her grave. There's something in the weather, I reckon, that sets folk wandering. My master, the one at the shop yonder, is spinning about the world somewhere."

"Is that why you're home so early tonight?" Margaret asked innocently.
