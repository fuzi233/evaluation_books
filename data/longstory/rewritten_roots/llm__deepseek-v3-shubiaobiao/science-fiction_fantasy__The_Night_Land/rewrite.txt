After eating three of the tablets and drinking sparingly, I lay down, thoughts drifting to Naani—until sleep claimed me before I could dwell long upon her.

I woke abruptly, drowning. Hot water surged around me, filling the basin, steaming and pungent. I scrambled upright, coughing, and saw that a fissure in the rock had belched forth boiling water. The pool drained slowly, as it must have done for ages, and I realized I had slept through its cycles.

Stripping off my armor, I spread my garments upon heated stone to dry, then bathed in the warm pool, keeping the Diskos close. Though I sensed no evil here, the memory of the Night Lands kept me wary. Refreshed, I dressed again and slept six hours more, lulled by the rhythmic gurgling of the rising water.

At last, I rose, ate, and set forth, determined to cover great distance. The forest loomed to my left, the sea to my right. Often the trees grew so thickly that I waded through their shadows, their scent wild and sweet—so unlike the tame groves of the Underground Fields. Life stirred in the undergrowth; eyes gleamed in the dark, watching, retreating. Nothing attacked, yet I kept the Diskos ready.

Three times that day, I passed small fire-hills, their eruptions shaking the earth. Around each lay a wasteland of dead trees, yet already new growth crept inward, reclaiming the scorched earth between eruptions. Thirty-seven boiling springs I counted, some roaring like beasts, others silent but for their hissing steam.

By the eighteenth hour, exhaustion forced me to halt. I chose a spot against a great rock, where nothing could approach unseen, and slept—or so I thought. Yet my spirit remained alert, sensing danger.

I woke with a jolt. The luminous air revealed six hunched figures crouched at the forest’s edge, their eyes gleaming like beasts’. They watched, motionless. I gripped the Diskos, heart pounding, but before I could act, they vanished—gone as silently as shadows.

Uneasy, I ate and drank, then pressed onward, moving swiftly. For thirty hours I marched, pausing only to eat and drink at intervals. Twice, I felt unseen things tracking me through the woods, matching my pace. Fear drove me faster.

At last, I reached a river flowing into the sea. An island stood midstream, a possible refuge. With no way to swim, I lashed fallen trees into a crude raft and poled across, collapsing on the island to sleep nine hours of dreamless exhaustion.

The next day, I crossed the remaining stretch of river and continued, the land unfolding in wonders and perils. Fire-hills rumbled, creatures lurked, yet none attacked. My thoughts flickered often to Naani, though danger left little room for longing. Every step was a prayer for her.

Near evening, I climbed a great rock, seeking shelter. At its summit, I found not mere stone, but something stranger—a long, shaped mass like a fallen pillar, its underside familiar, its top overgrown. A place to rest, I thought, and slept—until instinct woke me.

Two Humped Men scaled the rock, swift and silent. Their thick necks and shoulders bulged with brute strength, their eyes fixed on me. I readied the Diskos. One must die quickly, lest the other strike from behind.

The battle for my life was at hand.

In a single moment, the weight of my despair shattered into wild hope. There, rising from the blackness of that cursed land, stood the stark outline of a pyramid—massive, silent, reaching into the endless night. My heart surged. If this was the Lesser Refuge, then my search was over. Naani was near.

I ran, reckless, down the slope of the great volcano, my body driven by a feverish need to reach her. But the land betrayed me. My foot caught, and I fell hard, pain lancing through me as I struck the earth. For long minutes, I lay there, gasping, helpless. If any creature had found me then, I would have been easy prey.

When I could move again, I rose with caution. The pyramid loomed ahead, a darker shadow against the distant glow of fire-pits. Yet doubt gnawed at me. Why was it utterly dark? If it were the Refuge, there should be lights, life. Unless—

I pushed the thought away. It had to be the Refuge.

The descent took hours, the land treacherous underfoot. By the time I reached the plain, the pyramid was hidden behind the bulk of its hill. I pressed on, guided by memory and the dull red flickers of distant flames. Twice, unseen things moved in the night—scuttling, hunting. Once, a roar shook the air, followed by a scream that froze my blood.

At last, I climbed the hill, my pulse hammering. But as I neared the summit, dread coiled in my gut. The pyramid stood before me, a monstrous silhouette, its sides smooth and lifeless. No light. No sound. Only an emptiness that screamed wrongness.

My spirit recoiled. This was no sanctuary. Something evil waited within.

I retreated, swift and silent, back into the night. Four hours I walked, numb, until I found myself on the shore of an ancient sea, though I had no memory of how I came there. My legs gave out. I sat, hollow. The pyramid was a tomb. Naani was lost.

Then—desperation. I sent the Master-Word pulsing into the dark, and with it, her name.

An answer came, faint but clear—the Master-Word echoing back, and then, like a whisper in my mind, her voice. *Naani. Mirdath.* My name, spoken as it had been in another life.

Joy and terror warred in me. She lived—but she was far, so far. And then—

A sound. A sob.

I turned, heart wild, and saw her.

Kneeling by a fire-hole, a small, ragged figure, her face streaked with tears. She did not see me, did not know I was near. She only listened, desperate, for a voice she thought came from across the world.

I stepped forward. "Mirdath."

She startled, eyes wide, trembling. Then—recognition. A cry broke from her, and she stumbled toward me, collapsing against my armor. She was thin, starved, her clothes torn, her hands shaking.

I wrapped her in my cloak, fed her broth from my supplies. She drank greedily, her strength returning in slow, shuddering breaths. Between sips, she spoke—halting, broken—of the fall of the Lesser Refuge, of monsters and slaughter, of her flight into the night.

Her father was dead. The others, scattered or slain. She had wandered alone, hunted, surviving on moss and bitter water, hiding from things that stalked the dark.

And always, she had called for me.

Now, she was here. In my arms. Alive.

The spider-crabs still watched from the shadows, patient, waiting. I rose, kicked them away, disgusted. They scattered, cowards.

Naani—*Mirdath*—watched me, her eyes bright with tears and something else. Trust. Belonging.

I knelt beside her again, and she pressed her hands into mine.

No words were enough. But in that moment, none were needed.

The gloom of the old sea-bed fell behind us as we emerged into the lighter expanse of the Night Land. After the suffocating darkness below, even the dim glow of the distant fire-holes seemed a relief. I glanced at the Maid, her face pale with exhaustion, her eyes lifting to mine with a quiet trust that warmed me more than any fire.

I drew her close, feeling the delicate weight of her against me. She was so small, so weary—so unlike the iron-hard strength that had carried me through the wastes. Guilt pricked at me for pushing her so far, but she only shook her head, pressing nearer as if to silence my reproach. Her fingers curled into the fabric of my cloak, and I kissed her, then turned my gaze outward, scanning the Land for the path ahead.

To our left, the great blue Shine pulsed against the horizon, a constant, eerie glow that had guided me across the sea-bed. But I knew better than to venture too near. The tales of the Fixed Giants lurking within that light were not to be dismissed. And closer, the red smolder of the giants’ pit reminded me of the perils still ahead.

The Maid—*Naani*—studied the landscape with a quiet intensity. She knew this terrain, not as I did, through brute wandering, but through the careful records of the Lesser Redoubt.

“We cannot go that way,” she said, pointing toward a stretch of fire-holes tinged with green. “The gas there is death.”

I frowned. “Then how?”

She turned to me, her eyes sharp with thought. “We must return to the sea-bed. Follow its edge southwest until we are past the gas. Then up again—but cautiously. The giants are always near their fires.”

Her plan was sound—better than any I might have formed in haste. And there was a sweetness in hearing her voice shape our survival, a trust that bound us closer than any vow.

Yet weariness dragged at us both. Seventeen hours had passed since our last rest, and the Maid’s steps had grown slow, her body trembling with fatigue. We needed sleep before pressing further.

A fire-hole glowed not far off, promising warmth against the Land’s bitter chill. Yet I hesitated. Fire meant life—and in this place, life was seldom friendly. Still, the cold gnawed too deep to refuse.

We crept toward the hollow where the fire burned, moving like shadows. Below, the flames roared among jagged rocks, casting long, twisting shadows. No monsters lurked in sight—only the faint scuttle of small, venomous things that fled our approach.

The heat was a blessing, but not without cost. Even as we huddled near, I watched the edges of the light, wary. The Maid sensed my unease.

“We should not stay,” she murmured.

I agreed. A small cave, high in the rock face, offered refuge. We climbed, settling into its dry shelter, the fire’s glow painting the walls in shifting orange. Wrapped together in the cloak, we slept—but lightly, our minds still half-lost to the dread of the Land.

***

A scream shattered our rest.

I jolted awake, the Maid gasping beside me. Out in the night, human voices wailed in terror, cut through by deep, monstrous bellows. *Giants.*

I peered from the cave mouth. Below, a cluster of ragged figures fled past the hollow—wild, desperate things, their bodies torn by thorns and stones. Behind them, thunderous footsteps shook the earth. Four massive shapes loomed into the firelight—three dark and brutish, the fourth a sickly, livid white. They passed without pause, vanishing into the dark.

A woman’s scream ended abruptly. Then silence.

The Maid trembled against me, her breath shallow. I tightened my grip on the Diskos, fury boiling in my blood. But there was nothing to do. Nothing but wait.

Then—movement. A naked girl darted into the hollow, her eyes wild. She scrambled beneath an overhang, but too late. A squat, hairy figure stalked into view, its gaze locking onto her hiding place.

I leapt without thought.

The fall should have broken me, but rage carried me through. The Diskos hummed to life as I charged, its blade hungry. The brute turned—just in time to meet its death. My first strike missed as it dropped low, but the second sheared through its shoulder, sending it crashing to the earth.

The Maid was already at my side, my belt-knife clutched in her hand. She had come to fight for me.

Her eyes met mine, wordless. There was no need for speech. The horror was past.

We left the hollow soon after, our path set toward the sea-bed once more. The giants’ roars had faded, but their memory lingered. The Night Land was never still.

And we had far yet to go.

I carried the Maid for an hour down the Gorge, my strength fraying with each step. My feet faltered, my body numb with exhaustion. If I did not find shelter soon, I would collapse beneath her weight.

The Gorge loomed unfamiliar, as though I had walked it in a dream. Only when I forced myself to focus did memory return—the fire-holes, the caves I had marked on my first journey. One in particular, high in the cliff, had stirred longing in me then: a safe place for her to rest, if only we could reach it.

Now, we had.

Seven hollows scarred the left wall, the highest snug and dry, warmed by hidden fires in the rock. The glow from the pit below cast flickering light inside, enough to see by. It was perfect.

I woke Naani gently, kissing her until she stirred. She blinked up at me, drowsy, then gasped as she saw where we stood. "You carried me all this way?" she murmured, dismayed. I silenced her with another kiss. She melted against me, whispering, "I love you utterly."

Climbing to the cave was no easy task, but we managed. Inside, the air was warm, the floor smooth. I set her down, then descended again to fetch a boulder—large enough that, balanced at the cave’s mouth, it would warn us of any intruder.

Naani spread the cloak for us to sleep upon, arranging it with unconscious intimacy. She hesitated only a moment before lying beside me, her head pillowed against my armored shoulder. Within minutes, we were both asleep.

---

Twelve hours later, I woke to the sound of water hissing in the cup. Naani knelt nearby, preparing our meal. She laughed when she saw me stir, bright-eyed, and came to kiss me. I pulled her close, savoring the warmth of her.

"You must let me tend to you," she insisted, pressing a tablet to my lips. I obeyed, letting her spoil me. But when I tried to rise, pain lanced through me—my body stiff from the battle with the Yellow Beast.

Naani had already foreseen this. She helped me remove my armor, then worked the ointment into my bruises with slow, firm strokes. I watched her face, the way her brow furrowed in concentration, the softness in her eyes when she glanced up.

"You’re so strong," she murmured, tracing the lines of muscle along my arms. Then, abruptly, she knelt and pressed her forehead to my hands. "I never dreamed a man like you could exist."

I lifted her, holding her gently. She trembled against me, not with fear, but with a quiet, overwhelming emotion. Words were unnecessary.

---

After eating, we set out again. The Gorge stretched before us, fire-pits casting their ruddy glow, steam hissing from cracks in the rock. Naani walked beside me now, her steps light despite her wounded feet.

Six hours passed before we halted. I recognized the cave where I had rested after escaping the Slugs—the same spring, the same fire-hole. A strange comfort, to return.

We built a crude wall across the cave’s mouth, blocking out the world. Naani arranged our bedding with quiet care, then curled against me, her breath steadying into sleep.

I lay awake a while, feeling the weight of her trust. Her fingers twitched against my side, as though even in dreams, she sought to reassure herself I was there.

When sleep took me, it was deep.

I woke to her hands brushing my face, her lips pressing shyly to mine. She pretended innocence, but her smile betrayed her.

"Did you kiss me while I slept?" I asked.

Her eyes danced. "Perhaps."

I laughed, pulling her close. The Gorge waited, but for now, we had this moment—safe, together.

We emerged at last from the desolate stretch of the Upward Gorge, that grim passage between the mountains, and paused where the land sloped downward into the open Country of the Seas.

Naani stood beside me, her breath quick with wonder, her eyes alight. She turned often, drinking in the vastness—the sweep of the land, the distant shimmer of the sea. Then she looked back, her gaze lifting toward the black mouth of the gorge we had escaped, and a shadow of fear passed over her. She stepped closer to me, as if to assure herself of safety, then laughed softly, half in relief, half in sheer astonishment.

Never before had she known such openness, such light. The air itself seemed new to her, and she drew it in deeply, as if it were a forgotten luxury. She called out once, just to hear her voice carry, and the sound rang clear before fading into the distance.

Then—an answer.

A faint echo, or something else? We turned sharply, peering back into the gloom of the gorge. Whether it was mere reverberation or something more sinister, we could not say. But unease prickled along my skin, and we moved swiftly downward, putting distance between us and that dark cleft in the mountains.

Soon, we found a broad, flat rock, well-suited for rest. We climbed atop it and shared our meal, sitting close, speaking in hushed tones despite the openness around us. Still, we kept watch, for dangers lurked even here—Humpt Men, or worse.

Naani’s gaze wandered ceaselessly over the landscape, lingering on the distant sea. A strange yearning flickered in her expression, as if half-remembered dreams stirred within her. Once, she wept suddenly, and I held her until the storm passed.

She spoke often of the air, its clarity, its sweetness—things I, too, had marveled at when first I left the darkness of the Underground World. But for her, the wonder was sharper, fresher. At times, her words slipped into fragments of old speech, as if her mind brushed against memories buried deep. Then, just as suddenly, she would return to the present, her hand finding mine, her silence more telling than words.

Far to our left, where the shore met the sea, a great mist hung—the same I had passed on my outward journey. She questioned me about it, and I explained as best I could, though much remained unknown. The volcanoes that burned beneath the sea sent up their steam, and the earth trembled with hidden forces.

She listened, rapt, then abruptly kissed me—once, twice, thrice—her fingers pressing against my shoulders, her eyes alight with mischief. I laughed, caught between delight and exasperation, and she only grinned, unrepentant.

Later, I combed her hair loose over her shoulders, relishing the softness of it. She protested only halfheartedly, then settled into contentment. For a time, we forgot the journey, the dangers, all of it.

But duty called. We gathered our gear, and I helped her with her boots before we set off again, descending toward the shore.

Hours later, the hiss of boiling water grew louder, the air thickening with steam. We entered the mist, and I took the lead, wary of hidden pools. The ground shuddered beneath us, and strange noises echoed—shrieks, whistles, the groans of the earth itself.

Naani stayed close, her fingers laced with mine. The steam swallowed us whole, and for a time, we moved blindly, guided only by the sound of the sea to our left.

Then—release. The mist thinned, the noise faded, and we emerged once more into open air.

Before us stretched vast forests, their trees towering and strange. Naani stared in wonder, touching leaves, breathing in their scent. Something in her stirred, though she could not name it.

We pressed on until we reached the great tree where I had slept days before. She asked to climb it, to see the branch where I had rested. I agreed, and we ate there, looking out over the land. The fire-hill burned in the distance, its glow a beacon.

She cut a small piece of bark as a keepsake, then kissed the place where I had lain.

Later, I showed her the tracks of the beast that had hunted me—a monstrous thing, though dwarfed by the Slug. She traced the marks with her fingers, awed.

As we walked, I realized with a pang that the armor had bruised her shoulders. Anger flared—at myself, at her for not telling me. She only laughed, unrepentant, and when I scolded her, she grew defiant.

For a moment, we stood in silence, her small fists clenched. Then, with a sigh, she relented, pressing close. I held her, my sternness crumbling.

We walked on, her hand in mine, the sea whispering beside us. The journey was long, the dangers many—but in that moment, none of it mattered.

We were together.

I awoke to pain and confusion, my senses slow to return. Weakness held me down like an unseen weight. I lay on my back, the sound of labored breathing near me. Turning my head with effort, I saw her—the Maid, still unclothed, straining with desperate strength against a great pole—the very one I had cut when the Humpt Men attacked.

Memory flooded back. The raft, the battle, my collapse. She had dragged me here while I lay senseless, saving me before the creatures could finish their work. How she had managed it, I never fully learned—only that love had lent her a strength beyond nature.

Later, she told me of waking beneath my limp body, my blood staining the earth around her. She thought me dead at first, until she felt the faint pulse of life. Then came the instinct of danger, the frantic effort to haul me to the raft, the snatched fragments of her torn garments still clutched in the dead Humpt Man’s grip. She barely escaped, the surviving beasts howling after her as she pushed us into the current.

Now, on the island, I drifted between fevered dreams and brief clarity. Pain was a constant haze, but through it all, I knew her presence—gentle hands, quiet words, the steady rhythm of her care.

The first true waking came softly. I lay cushioned on something soft, the ache in my body dulled. She knelt beside me, her face alight with such love and relief that it seemed to pour strength back into me. She kissed me, her tears warm against my skin, and I kissed her in return, too weak for more.

She lifted my head, gave me broth, and I slipped back into sleep.

Three times this happened. By the fourth, I could whisper, *"I love you."* She wept then, pressing my hand to her heart.

By the fifth waking, I understood more—the bandages from her torn clothes, the careful way she moved me. She had dressed herself again, her garments mended with threads pulled from the ruined fabric, needles carved from thorns. She blushed when I noticed, hiding her face with a kiss.

On the seventh day, I grew stronger. She fed me whole tablets now, counting them carefully, ensuring I had enough to rebuild my blood. We shared them as always, kissing each other’s portions before eating.

She washed me, changed the bandages, and laughed when I teased her. Once, I tied her toes together with a strand of her hair, and we laughed like children. She plaited a lock of my hair with hers, tucking it into her bosom, and I kept its twin beneath my bandages, close to my heart.

We spoke of the Mighty Pyramid, her eyes wide with wonder. I told her of its vastness, its lights, its people—so strange to her, yet she listened as if I spoke of heaven.

And once, in quietness, I began a tale—a man from an ancient time, his love lost, then found again in a future age. She wept, her fingers tightening on mine, as if something half-remembered stirred in her. But the moment passed, and we let the past fade.

That day was pure happiness. Weakness still held me, but her nearness was enough. We jested, spoke foolishness, and for a while, the Night Land seemed far away.

Yet always, beneath it all, was the unspoken truth—we were not yet safe. But for now, on this little island, we had peace.

In the tenth hour of that day, we pushed the raft into the water. The paddles moved smoothly in their rests, as I had designed, and the raft glided forward with little effort. The Maid stood at the fore paddle, while I took the rear, and we fell into a steady rhythm, propelling ourselves at a pace just slower than walking.

By the twelfth hour, we paused to eat and drink, then resumed our quiet labor. The motion became so natural that we scarcely noticed it—only the gentle rocking of our bodies, back and forth, as the hours slipped past. We spoke softly, and often the Maid would glance back at me with love in her eyes, her lips teasing, though she would shake her head in playful refusal when I made to leave my paddle and go to her.

When the eighteenth hour came, we drew in the paddles. The Maid spread the cloak carefully for our bed, and after eating, we slept deeply, as if slipping into oblivion in an instant.

Eight hours later, we woke together, disoriented for a fleeting moment before remembering where we were—safe, untouched. We laughed, joyously, and after a kiss, washed in the sea’s water before breaking our fast. Then we took up the paddles again, moving steadily along the coast, peaceful and content through the day.

That voyage lasted four full days, for we did not hurry, allowing my strength to rebuild. Nothing troubled us, save once when a great beast heaved itself from the sea onto the shore, browsing on the vegetation. It did not frighten us, only made us glad for the distance between us. It seemed a natural thing, not monstrous, as if the creatures of that land belonged to some ancient order of life.

Once, we passed near a Fire-Hill rising from the sea, its base boiling, jets of flame roaring upward. Strange grunts echoed from the water as gases burst forth—a reminder of the power sleeping in that deep world. We stared back at it long after we had passed, awed.

Otherwise, the journey was uneventful. We navigated carefully where the Great Sea fractured into smaller channels, slipping through the watery passages until, on the fourth day, we reached land—a flat shore leading to the mouth of the First Gorge.

It was the tenth hour, as it had been when we began. A peaceful voyage, yet I knew greater dangers lay ahead. Still, our hearts were set; we would conquer what came, for the prize was a life together in the Mighty Home.

We grounded the raft as best we could. The Maid cut a small piece of wood from it—a keepsake—before we gathered our gear. I donned my armor once more, the Scrip and Pouch at my back, the Diskos in hand. The Maid secured her bundle (now much diminished) and her knife at her belt.

Then she knelt and kissed the raft, a silent farewell to the past. I lifted her gently, and we turned toward the dark mouth of the Gorge.

To our right, miles distant, loomed the great Mountain with its four fire-peaks, ash-hills piled below from ages of eruptions. The Maid marveled at it, as any would.

We entered the Gorge, pausing only to look back one last time at the Country of Seas—that hidden, living land in the abyss of the world. Perhaps we were the last humans who would ever see it. A solemn thought.

Then we rounded the bend, and the darkness swallowed us.

For five days, we journeyed through that desolate place. We kept to sixteen hours of travel, eight of sleep, careful not to overtire. The Gorge was lifeless but for the gas-fires, their moans and whistles the only sounds in that vast silence. The Maid often asked when we would reach the Night Land, her excitement growing, and I shared her eagerness—though beneath it lay a fear that some final disaster might yet strike.

On the fifth day, distant echoes reached us—the monstrous piping of the Great Gas Fountain. The sound grew, and soon we saw its flicker ahead, a blue flame dancing in the darkness. We stood at last before it, deafened by its roar, tiny in the face of that eternal force.

After a time, we turned away, beginning the long climb up the Mighty Slope. For eight days, we crawled upward through unending night, tethered together by straps, blind and numbed by the dark. Often, I paused, calling the Maid to me, holding her close for comfort before we pressed on.

And so we left the Flame behind, ascending into the blackness, toward the terror and wonder of the Night Land.

The Mighty Pyramid loomed before me, a mountain of light in the eternal dark, its vast bulk rising like a beacon of life against the consuming night. Yet despair clung to me, a weight heavier than the miles I had carried her. Naidok, my beloved, lay still in my arms, her breath shallow, her spirit flickering. So close to salvation—yet still too far.

I pressed forward, my stride unbroken, when a figure emerged from the hollow of a fire-pit—a hulking, hair-covered man, his hands monstrous with claws like a beast’s. He lunged, eager, ravenous.

I set Naidok down swiftly, my heart cold with fury. No delay. No mercy. I struck, but the brute twisted aside, seized my helm, and wrenched it free, flinging me backward. I rolled, unharmed but shaken, and surged to my feet as the Diskos roared to life in my grip. One swing—the blade sheared through him as if he were mist, leaving his halves to crumple grotesquely in the firelight.

No pause. I snatched Naidok up and ran.

The night trembled with unseen watchers. The Millions whispered their awe, their hope, their terror. Then, from the blackness, more shapes slithered—twisted things from the rocks, the brush, the shadows. I cut through them, a relentless storm, my mind numb but my body sharp with desperation. The land itself seemed to wake, snarling, howling, laughing in the dark.

And then—the baying.

The Night-Hounds.

Close now, too close, their cries tearing through the silence. A mile? Less? I ran harder, my gaze locked on the Pyramid, its light dimming as if the Earth-Current itself faltered.

A flash—blue, searing—split the sky. The Humans had unleashed the Current upon the hounds. The world erupted in flame and thunder, the ground rupturing, rocks hurled like shrapnel. I staggered, shielding Naidok, then forced myself onward. The silence afterward was deafening.

But the land was not done.

Creatures lurched from the dark, blocking my path. Tusked, squat, their fingers grasping. The Hundred Thousand watched from the Circle, helpless, for beyond its weakened glow swayed the Black Mounds—living Evil, waiting to devour any who stepped out.

I fought like a thing possessed, the Diskos a blur. Blood sprayed, bodies fell. The Hundred Thousand shouted, hurled their own blades into the fray. I broke through—stumbled—crossed the Circle.

Hands reached but did not touch me. Silence fell.

A Master of the Diskos saluted. A physician came.

Naidok was laid upon the earth.

A breath. A pause. A quiet shake of the doctor’s head.

Dead.

The word echoed in my bones.

I lifted her again, cradling her for the last march through the lane of grey-armored men, their Diskoi reversed in salute. The Great Gate groaned open. The Masters stood in silence, the Monstruwacan at their head, his face stricken.

They knew.

And so I carried her home.

When I awoke, it was to the hum of the Lift carrying me upward, cradled upon the same bed where I had once believed I would never rest again—never rise from the silent abyss below. The deep thunder of the Underground Organs reverberated through the earth, a distant hymn beyond death’s veil, rolling like the chant of unseen multitudes. At times, it was a murmur, the echo of wind through unseen chasms; at others, clear and resonant, the ancient melody of the *Song of Honour*.

I knew, as if in a dream, that the Millions of that Deep Country rejoiced, their voices woven into a tribute to the miracle that had been wrought. Yet all was dim to me, my eyelids heavy, my body adrift on sluggish tides. The scent of flowers—real, sweet—drifted from the Fields as the Lift climbed mile after mile through the heart of the Pyramid.

I must have stirred, for the Master Doctor’s voice came soft beside me: *"Rest. She is safe."*

Then came a haze of days—half living, half dreaming—where I floated between worlds, untroubled, questioning nothing. Time blurred. And then, one day, a face bent over mine, eyes alight with a quiet love. *Naani.* My own. She spoke no words, only held my hands, and warmth seeped back into me. Contentment. Sleep.

The day came when they let me rise, carrying me to one of the Quiet Gardens. There, amid the whispering leaves, I waited—until *she* stepped from behind the foliage, hesitant, yet glowing. I had never seen her like this—not as the ragged survivor of the Night Land, but as a maid adorned in the gentle finery of the Pyramid. Dainty. Lovelier than memory had painted.

I tried to stand—foolish, in my weakness—but she darted forward, pressing me back with gentle hands. Then she settled beside me, cradling my head against her breast, her lips meeting mine. In that moment, she was both lover and guardian, wordless in her happiness.

When the attendants returned, the Master Doctor among them, satisfaction lined his face.

From then on, she was with me daily. Love mended me faster than any physic. Soon, I walked again—through private paths in the Fields, away from the crowds who would have followed, curious and kind. Naani never left my side. The Master Monstruwacan and the Master Doctor had seen to it: we were wed, quietly, spared the spectacle until I was strong enough to bear it.

(That later ceremony—oh, the Millions made it a marvel! A Guard of Honour stretching eight miles high, from the Pyramid’s peak to its roots. But that was for show. *This*—these stolen days—was ours.)

We wandered the Fields, hidden, nameless. We slept where the flowers grew thickest, ate in secluded villages, laughed under skies that knew no Night. She teased me, called me a glutton as I devoured the meals she prepared, then kissed me silent before I could protest. She was everything—joy, comfort, *home*.

Once, we ventured back to the Country of Silence. Memory weighed heavy there, but in time, we returned often—not with sorrow, but with reverence, and the quiet understanding that love outlasts even death.

As we left that place, I confessed: *"When the House held you, I remembered—how I never woke to catch you kissing me as I slept."*

She flushed, eyes wide. She hadn’t known I’d been aware of her stolen moments. Then her expression softened—she understood the agony of those hours when I’d believed her lost.

The Doctors had told her, she explained. The Force had suspended her, frozen her very spirit—until the Earth-Current revived her. Such a thing had not happened in ages.

In our wanderings, she learned my stories—those I’d lived, those I’d dreamed. She, too, had suffered, rising from her sickbed when the Master Doctor feared I would slip away, holding my hands though she could barely stand.

——

At last came the day she led me, sweetly cunning, to the Hall of Honour. The great chamber was filled, yet hushed, as though the crowd merely lingered without purpose. Then I saw it—at the center, where only the greatest of the dead were remembered, stood a statue: a man in shattered armor, a maid forever in his arms.

No honor in all the Pyramid was higher. And I—I was no hero, only a man who had loved fiercely enough to defy death.

The silence around us was thick with unspoken respect. No one approached. They let us depart quietly, her hand in mine.

I walked away, humbled. Honour is but ash without love.

And I? I had love.

Love, which mothers honour, nurtures faithfulness—and builds, stone by stone, the House of Joy.
