As soon as the throng had swept past, Romola hurried down to the street and raced toward the steps of San Stefano. Cecco had drifted with the crowd into the Piazza, leaving her to find Baldassarre standing solitary against the church door. He held his horn-cup in his hand, waiting.

There was a startling transformation in him. The vacant, dreamy stare of a half-awaking consciousness had vanished, replaced by a ferocity that struck Romola with the force of a lightning bolt aimed directly at her. It was the gaze of a caged beast sensing its prey moving safely beyond the bars. Romola flinched as the look locked onto her, yet her immediate thought was that he had spotted Tito. As the hatred grated against her spirit, a fragile hope ignited within her: perhaps this man *was* the criminal, and her husband innocent. If she could bring Tito face-to-face with him now, she could finally find peace.

"If you will come with me," she said softly, "I can offer you shelter and food until you are strong again. Will you come?"

"Yes," Baldassarre replied, his voice low. "I shall be glad to regain my strength." He repeated the words as if murmuring to himself rather than addressing her.

"Come!" she invited, guiding him beside her down the quieter path toward the Arno and the Ponte Rubaconte.

"I suspect you are not a Florentine," she remarked as they approached the bridge.

He glanced around, silent. His suspicion was sharpened by physical weakness, the fog of confusion deepening around him. Yet, Romola studied him too, and there was a gentleness in her eyes that eventually drew a response.

"No," he answered cautiously. "I am no Florentine. I am a lonely man."

She noted his reluctance to speak further, fearing he wished to leave, so she did not press him. As they walked, her mind worked tirelessly, extinguishing the faint hope that her husband's situation was free of pain. If this old man had been in the wrong, why the dread and secrecy? They proceeded in silence until they reached the entrance of the Via de' Bardi. Suddenly, Baldassarre stopped, turning to look at her with a jolt, as if a shock had pierced him.

Moments later, Romola paused at the half-open door of the courtyard and faced him.

"Ah!" he exclaimed before she could speak. "You are his wife."

"His wife?" Romola asked, bewildered.

It would have been impossible for Baldassarre to summon a name. The sheer weight of Tito's image seemed to expel any verbal response. He offered no reply, staring at her with a strange, fixed intensity.

Romola pushed the door open wide, revealing a courtyard strewn with straw where four or five sick people lay. Tiny, pale children crawled or sat amidst them, gnawing on straws and gurgling.

"If you will come in," Romola said, her voice trembling, "I will find you a comfortable spot and bring you more food."

"No," Baldassarre refused. "I will not come in."

Yet he remained frozen, paralyzed by the crushing burden of impressions that left his mind too confused to choose a course.

"Is there nothing I can do for you?" Romola pleaded. "Let me give you money to buy food. It will be more plentiful soon."

She reached into her *scarsella*, holding out her palm with several *grossi*. She deliberately offered more than she would to anyone else in such dire straits.

He stared at the coins for a moment, then nodded. "Yes, I will take them."

She poured the coins into his palm, and he clutched them tightly.

"Tell me," Romola begged, "what shall you—"

But Baldassarre had turned away, resuming his walk toward the bridge. Crossing it, he headed straight up the Via del Fosso and turned abruptly into the shop of Niccolò Caparra, as though it were his destination.

Niccolò was currently leading a procession with the city's armorers, leaving only an apprentice behind. Weapons hung everywhere, gleaming in the dim light. Baldassarre's eyes scanned the array, hungry for something far more potent than bread. Though Niccolò would likely refuse to sell arms to a man bearing the marks of imprisonment, the apprentice, less observant and scrupulous, sold a sharp hunting-knife for three *grossi* without hesitation.

It was a compact blade, easily concealed within Baldassarre's tunic. He walked on, feeling a surge of renewed strength. That sharp edge could lend deadly precision to the thrust of an aging arm; at the very least, it was a companion, a power allied with him, even if it failed. It might shatter against armor, but was the armor guaranteed to remain? During those long months of confinement, vengeance had lain dormant, allowing baseness to grow complacent and secure.

The knife had been purchased with the traitor's own money. That was justice. Before accepting the coin, he had known exactly what he would do with it: acquire a weapon. And if possible, food as well—food to nourish the arm that would wield the steel, food to sustain the body that served as the temple of vengeance. Once he had eaten enough, he would be able to think and act. First, he must consider how to hide, lest Tito have him dragged away once more.

With this plan forming in his mind, Baldassarre navigated the narrowest alleys, buying meat and bread, then sat beneath the first loggia to eat.

The bells began to toll, swelling into louder and louder peals of joy. They vibrated through the air, binding him to a world that stood firmly against him. To Baldassarre, the sound was merely part of that hostile universe.

Romola watched him until he vanished around the corner into the Piazza de' Mozzi. Half relieved by his departure, half reproached for failing to seek the truth with greater determination, she wondered if she had missed a chance to alleviate some guiltless suffering. Yet, what could she have done if the truth carried the heavy burden of a painful secret regarding her husband? Was a wife not permitted to desire ignorance of her spouse's wrongdoing, especially when she alone must refrain from protesting or warning others against him? The thought stirred complex fibers of feeling, but in her weariness, she let it pass.

It was a time for rejoicing, for help had arrived for Florence. Turning back into the courtyard, she went to share the good news with her patients lying on their straw beds. Closing the door to muffle the overwhelming clamor of the bells, she threw back the black drapery covering her head so the women could see her clearly. Standing among them, she announced that corn was coming and that the bells rang in gladness at the news.

They all sat up to listen. Children trotted and crawled toward her, pulling at her black skirts, eager to be near her face. Exhausted as she was, Romola yielded to them, sitting down on the straw. The tiny, pale faces peered into her basket, tugging at her hair, while the weak voices around her chanted, "The Holy Virgin be praised!"

"It was the procession!" someone cried. "The Mother of God has had pity on us!"

Finally, rising from the heap of straw, too weary to attempt a smile, she said as she climbed the stone steps, "I will come by-and-by to bring you your dinner."

"Bless you, madonna! Bless you!" came the faint chorus, echoing the same tone they had used moments earlier to praise the unseen Madonna.

Romola cherished that music profoundly. Unlike some women who found comfort in the mere routine of caring for the sick and clothing the ragged, her early training had kept her aloof from such domestic labors. Without the inspiration of her deepest feelings, the work would have been irksome to her. Instead, it had become the sole unshaken refuge of her mind, the narrow path upon which clarity shone brightest.

Though the widening gulf between herself and Tito, bridged only by her futile attempts at submission, bred doubts about the validity of the bond she had sworn to uphold—even as she withdrew from her confessor, Fra Salvestro, or encountered the rigid disciples of Savonarola, feeling a sickening revulsion at their narrowness and a fierce resurgence of her old contempt for their superstition—she found stability in her acts of compassion.

Whatever else caused her uncertainty, the aid she gave to her fellow citizens confirmed that Fra Girolamo had been right to call her back. As his unforgettable words suggested, her place had never been empty; it had been filled with her love and labor. Florence needed her. The heavier her personal sorrow pressed, the greater her gladness in recalling the hours over the past two years when she had lightened the burdens of others.

All the ardor of her nature, which could no longer express itself solely in maternal tenderness for father and husband, had transmuted into an enthusiastic sympathy for the common life. She had ceased to believe her own lot could be happy, indeed, she had ceased thinking of happiness altogether. The single end of her existence appeared to be the diminution of sorrow. This enthusiasm was constantly revitalized by Savonarola's influence. Despite recoiling in disgust from the wearisome visions and allegories when repeated by others, her profound affinity for his passionate sympathy and the splendor of his aims retained its full power.
