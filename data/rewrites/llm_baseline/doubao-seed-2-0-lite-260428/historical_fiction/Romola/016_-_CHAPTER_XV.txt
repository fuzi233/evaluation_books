The sharp, cool air of late Florentine autumn brushed Romola’s cheeks as she reached the stone entrance of San Marco, where one of the white-robed friars waited, clearly expecting her arrival. Monna Brigida, her older companion, slipped away into the dim, incense-draped nave of the adjoining church, for no woman was permitted to step past the outer cloister’s threshold. Romola was led straight to the closed door of the chapter house, where the dying man had been carried to rest.

When the door swung open, soft, muted daylight blended with the glow of two beeswax tapers set behind a low, narrow truckle bed, revealing Fra Luca’s gaunt, emaciated face. A thin crown of golden hair circled his tonsured scalp, and his deep-sunken hazel eyes were fixed unwaveringly on the small wooden crucifix he clutched in bony, trembling fingers. He was propped almost upright against a pile of rough wool blankets, and as Romola set aside her veil, she registered only in passing that a second hooded monk stood by the far side of the bed, gliding toward the door the moment she stepped in. Behind them, the tall pale fresco of the crucified Christ rose over the far wall, a row of sorrowful, pale saint faces gazing down from beneath its feet.

The next instant, her eyes locked with Fra Luca’s as he lifted his gaze from the crucifix, and a sharp, stabbing pang tore through her: this wasted, otherworldly monk was the same fair, bright young brother she had loved through her childhood. “Dino,” she breathed, the sound half a cry of raw pain. She did not step closer. She stood rigid, two yards away from the bed, an unshakable repulsion coiling in her chest. This monk’s coarse habit felt like a brand, marking the dastardly, unfilial choice that had left their blind father isolated and desolate for years, the grovelling superstition that had dressed that desertion up as piety.

Raised by Bardo, a proud, unapologetic humanist who was one of Florence’s few frank, unashamed pagans of the era, Romola had always been taught to see the Church’s claims over cultivated reason as nothing to do with their quiet, studious household, a feature of the messy, uneducated masses they had always kept apart from. She had never thought of her brother’s flight as anything other than a source of indignant, uncurious contempt. Even so, the deep well of love in her soul clung to the old memory of the boy she had known, and her eyes searched his face, hungry for a flicker of the old, familiar Dino that was almost impossible to trace.

No corresponding warmth stirred in the monk’s face. He looked at his little sister, now grown to full, bright womanhood, with the far-off, unblinking gaze of a spirit that has already half left the mortal world. “My sister,” he said, his voice thin and ragged, but shockingly distinct through his ragged breath, “it is well you did not delay. My time is very short, and I have a message I am bound to deliver.”

Romola took one tentative step nearer, her heart softening. The message, she assumed, would be a penitent plea for forgiveness from their father. Nothing could erase the long years of abandonment, but a dying man looking back on the wrong he had done would surely draw pity, bring understanding at the last. Dino would ask after the speed of their father’s fading sight, how he filled his long dark days, how their home felt now without him, and his last words would be full of tenderness and regret.

“Romola,” Fra Luca began, “I have had a vision concerning you. Three times in the last two months, and each time it grew clearer. That is why I travelled from Fiesole: I knew it was a message from heaven, and I had no right to keep it from you. That my breath has been preserved long enough to give it to you is a sign God holds mercy for you—” A fit of laboured, gasping breath cut his words short.

Romola’s heart chilled again. A vision. The very sort of fantastical nonsense her father had always scoffed at with bitter amusement. Indignation rose hot to her lips. “Dino, I thought you had words to send to our father. You forsook him when his sight was failing, you made his life so desolate. Have you never once cared? Never repented? What sort of religion puts these silly visions before the duties you owe your own blood?”

The sunken hazel eyes turned slowly to her, and he held her gaze in silence for long moments, as if weighing whether he should answer her at all. “No,” he said at last, his tone low, passionless, as if a spirit not quite human was speaking through his dying frame. “I have never repented fleeing that stifling poison breath of sin, thick and hot around me, that threatened to steal over my senses like besotting wine. Our father could not hear the voice that called me night and day; he knew nothing of the demon tempters that dragged at my sleeves to pull me back. He has lived all his life in the middle of human sin and misery and never believed they existed, like a man picking up pretty shining pebbles in a mine while the whole world above him dies of plague. I tried to tell him, but he only scorned me. The studies he wanted me to live for were childish, dead toys, or else they were only given life by worldly ambitions and fleshly lusts—the only things that give weight to the old poetry and history he wanted me to pore over forever.”

“Has our father not lived a pure, noble life then?” Romola burst out, her face flushing bright pink, her whole body trembling. “He seeks no honours, he never lies, he denies himself every luxury, he lives like one of the ancient sages. He never wanted you to chase ambition or lust! He wanted you to live as he has done, by the purest maxims of the philosophy he raised us on.” She spoke half out of the rote of the beliefs he had instilled in her, but with the full, burning conviction of every ardent young idealist.

Her brother paused for a long while, staring at her passionate face with his strange, emotionless eyes. “What were those philosophical maxims to me? They told me to be strong, when I felt so weak I would have rolled myself in thorns like Saint Benedict just to smart my body raw and drive temptation away. Divine love found me, penetrated me, planted a great hungry need in me like a seed that demands space to grow. I was raised in ignorance of the true faith, but it flooded over me like a rising tide. I knew there was a life of perfect love and purity for the soul, where there would be no hungry craving for pleasure, no tormenting unanswerable questions, no fear of suffering. Before I had ever read the lives of the saints, I foresaw their ecstasy: even pagan philosophy stumbled on that truth, that man can reach bliss by dying to mortal wants, and living only for the unseen perfect God. But to reach that I had to forsake the world. No affection, no hope could bind me to anything that passes away. I could only live among other people as souls linked to that eternal unseen life. That need drove me constantly. It came to me in visions when my mind grew weary poring over the vain words that record the passions of dead men. It came to me after I had stumbled into sin, and turned away loathing from the stench of the emptied cup. And in those visions, I understood the true meaning of the Crucifix.”

He stopped, gasping hard for a minute or two, and Romola made no move to speak. There was no point trying to reach the mind of this unearthly brother; it was as useless as trying to grasp a shadow with her bare hand. When he spoke again, his heaving chest had grown calmer.

“I knew who I had to follow, but I saw that even among the servants of the Cross who claimed to renounce the world, my soul would have been choked by the fumes of hypocrisy, lust, and pride. God did not choose me, as he chose Saint Dominic and Saint Francis, to wrestle with evil in the Church and the world. He called me to run. I took my vows and I fled—fled to lands where danger, scorn, and want carried me like angels, straight to rest on the bosom of God. I have lived as a hermit, I have ministered to pilgrims, but my task is almost done. The veil between me and my everlasting rest has worn very thin. I came back to Florence so—”

“Dino, you *did* want to know if father is alive,” Romola interrupted, the thought of all his years of wandering suffering softening her, making her crave once more for reconciliation.

“—so that before I died I might urge our brethren to study Eastern tongues, as I never did, and go out to do greater work than I managed. And I found they are already set on that path. But since I came back, Romola, I have known I was sent partly to you—not to renew the bonds of earthly affection, but to deliver the heavenly warning given to me in my vision. For three times I have seen it. All the years since the divine voice first called me, when I was still in the world, I have been guided by visions. In our waking thoughts, we can never be sure we have not mixed our own error in with the light we pray for, but in visions we are passive, our souls are as an instrument in God’s hand. So listen, and do not speak again. My time is too short.”

Romola’s mind recoiled hard from the thought of listening to this vision. Her indignation had faded, but only because she could feel the gulf between her and her brother widening, impossible to cross. But as Fra Luca spoke, the second hooded monk had stepped fully around the bed, and now stood on the side opposite her, cowl drawn low over his face.

“Kneel, my daughter,” said a deep, rich, ringing voice, startlingly different from Fra Luca’s thin, breathless tone. “The Angel of Death is present here, waiting while the heavenly message is delivered. Bend your pride before it is bent for you by an iron yoke.”

The tone was not imperious, but calm, assured, steeped in quiet benignity. Romola, vibrating at the sound, looked up at the figure across the bed. His face was almost hidden in the shadow of his cowl, and her gaze fell first to his hands, folded across his chest, resting on the edge of his black mantle. They were strikingly, beautifully formed, almost transparently delicate, and their grace amplified the strange pull of his voice. Romola’s usual instinct to rebel against monks, whom she had been raised to despise, would have latched onto any repulsive detail to resist him—but his face was hidden, and his hands seemed to hold a gentle plea against all hardness.

The next moment, his right hand reached out to take the crucifix from Fra Luca’s fatigued grasp, and his left lifted a damp sponge to touch the dying man’s parched lips. As he bent, his cowl slipped back, and the full glow of the tapers fell on his features: high arched nose, prominent full lower lip, a thick crown of dark curly hair above his brow, all speaking of fierce energy and passion. His blue-grey eyes shone mild beneath soft auburn lashes, and like his hands, they spoke of sharp, tender sensitiveness. Romola knew at once it was Fra Girolamo Savonarola, the prior of San Marco, a man she had always considered more obnoxious than every other monk in Florence, simply because his preaching was louder and more unyielding. Her rebellion stirred against the strange soft influence that had almost made her want to bend.

“Kneel, my daughter,” the penetrating voice said again. “The pride of the body is a barrier against the gifts that purify the soul.” He looked at her, steady and mild, and Romola felt that subtle, unaccountable pull of a rare personality that few men on earth wield, to move the people around them. Slowly, she sank to her knees. In the very act of abandoning her proud, straight stance, a tremor ran through her, and her whole frame of mind shifted. She fell into a strange state of passive, open receptivity.

Her brother began to speak again, his voice thinner now than ever. “Romola, in the deep of the night, as I lay awake, I saw our father’s room—the library, all the books, the marbles, the reading desk where I used to stand to read. I saw you, your hair long and fair, sitting by our father’s chair. At the desk there stood a man whose face I could not see. I looked and looked, but his face was blank, like a painting scrubbed away. I saw him move, take you by the hand. Then you took our father by the hand, and all three of you walked down the stone steps out into the streets, the faceless man leading the way. You stood at the altar in Santa Croce, and the priest who married you had the face of death. The graves opened, and the dead in their shrouds rose and followed you like a bridal train. You passed through the streets and the gates, out into the valley. The man leading you hurried you faster than you could bear, and the dead grew tired of following, and turned back to their graves. At last you reached a stony waste, no water, no trees, no grass. Instead of water, unrolling parchments spread out everywhere. Instead of trees and grass, men of bronze and marble sprung up, crowding around you. Our father fainted of thirst, and fell to the ground. The faceless man let go of your hand, and fled. As he went, I saw his face. It was the face of the Great Tempter. You wrung your hands, searching for water, but there was none. The bronze and marble figures mocked you, holding out cups of water, but when you grasped them and lifted them to our father’s lips, they turned to parchment. Then the bronze and marble figures turned to demons, and snatched our father’s body out of your arms. The parchments shrivelled up, blood ran everywhere, and fire burned on top of the blood, until they all vanished. The plain was bare and stony again, and you were all alone in the middle of it. Then night fell, and I saw no more. Thrice I have had this vision, Romola. It is a revelation meant for you: a warning against marriage, as a temptation of the enemy. It calls on you to dedicate yourself—”

His pauses had grown longer, more frequent, and now a terrible fit of gasping seized him, his eyes rolling up to fix on the crucifix as if it were a light that was fading fast. Presently he found a little more strength to speak, in a tone so faint it was barely audible. “To renounce the vain philosophy and corrupt thoughts of the heathens: for in the hour of sorrow and death, their pride will turn to mockery, and the unclean gods will—”

The words died away entirely.

Even as a little part of Romola’s mind told her this was nothing but a dream, fed by old childhood memories and his own fervent religious convictions, a strange, unearthly awe had settled over her. She was not prone to sickly fancies; she had a sharp, vivid intellect and healthy human passions, too grounded in the ordinary relations of the world to crave morbid, exceptional events. But the images of the vision, which she despised, jarred at her like cruel, piercing cries. This was the first time she had ever stood beside a man in the throes of death: her young life had been sombre, but she had never known acute suffering, never a grief that cut to the bone. This brother, returned to her at the very edge of his final agony, felt like a sudden, awful apparition from a world she had never dared to think about. The pale sorrowful faces on the fresco behind the bed seemed to draw closer, keeping company with the pale face on the bed.

“Frate,” the dying voice whispered. Savonarola leaned down, but no other words came for long, dragging moments. “Romola,” the voice said next. She leaned forward, but again silence fell. The words were struggling in vain, in his closing throat. “Fra Girolamo, give her—”

“The crucifix,” said Savonarola’s steady voice. No other sound came from the dying lips.

“Dino!” Romola cried, the sound low and piercing, as the full realization hit her that all the years of misunderstanding between them would never be mended now.

“Take the crucifix, my daughter,” Savonarola said, after a few moments of quiet. “His eyes behold it no more.”

Romola stretched out her hand and took the small wooden cross, and the action seemed to break the terrible tension that had wrapped around her. A great, ragged sob burst out of her. She bowed her head beside her dead brother, and wept aloud. It felt to her as if this first encounter with death would change the colour of daylight for her forever.

Savonarola moved to the door, and called in a lay brother who had been waiting outside. Then he stepped back to Romola, and spoke in a tone of gentle command. “Rise, my daughter, and be comforted. Our brother is with the blessed now. He has left you the crucifix, in remembrance of the heavenly warning—that it may be a beacon to you in the darkness.”

She rose from her knees, trembling, pulled her veil back over her head, and hid the crucifix under her mantle. Savonarola led the way out into the cloistered courtyard, lit now only by starlight and a single lantern held by a servant near the entrance. A group of San Marco’s regular lay visitors stood clustered there, waiting to see the prior, who they had been told was tending a dying friar in the chapter house. Romola was dimly aware of forms stepping back to make way for her, of Savonarola’s low murmur that “our brother is departed”, of a light, gentle hand resting on her arm. A moment later, the main door swung open, and she was standing in the wide, open Piazza di San Marco, with no one but Monna Brigida and the servant holding the lantern beside her.

The fresh night air revived her, helped her regain her self-control. The scene that had just ended was still horribly clear and vivid in her mind, but as the ordinary, familiar world outside the chapter house pressed back on her, it began to shrink to the size of a terrible, lingering memory.
