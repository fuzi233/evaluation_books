Briarmains lay closer to the Hollow than the Rectory, and it was there Mr. Yorke had deposited his injured comrade. He had seen him laid in the finest bed of the house, tended with a meticulous care that mirrored the affection of a father for his own son. The sight of the blood, welling from the treacherous wound, struck a chord deep within the Yorkshire gentleman's heart. The tableau was irresistible: the sudden collapse of a tall, proud figure prostrate upon the road, the fine southern head bowed low in the dust, the prime of youth suddenly extinguished, leaving a pallid, lifeless, helpless form before him. This convergence of tragedy secured Mr. Yorke's most fervent interest.

With no other hand to raise, no other voice to offer kindness, and no other mind to devise a plan, Mr. Yorke was forced to assume total responsibility. This absolute dependence of the speechless, bleeding youth upon his benevolence acted as a powerful magnet for his own. Mr. Yorke relished power, and to wield it over a fellow creature's life suited him perfectly.

Equally, the incident appealed to his saturnine better half. Where some women might have been paralyzed by terror at the sight of a gory man brought over their threshold to lie in the "howe of the night," Mrs. Yorke found her spirits lifted. She reserved her hysterics for domestic slights—when Jessie refused to leave the garden for knitting, or when Martin proposed fleeing to Australia to escape her tyranny. But an attempted murder near her door, resulting in a half-dead man in her best bed, set her mind at ease, sharpened her wit, and gave her cap the dash of a turban.

Mrs. Yorke was the type of woman who would reduce a simple maid to misery while nursing a hospital full of plague victims like a heroine. She almost loved Moore. Her tough heart yearned toward him when she found him committed to her charge, left in her arms as dependent as her youngest child in a cradle. Had she seen a domestic or daughter give him water or smooth his pillow, she would have boxed their ears. She chased Jessie and Rose from the upper rooms and forbade the housemaids to set foot in the chamber.

If the accident had occurred at the rectory gates and old Helstone had taken in the martyr, neither Yorke nor his wife would have pitied him; they would have deemed him rightly served for his tyranny. But as it was, he became the apple of their eye.

Strangely, Louis Moore was permitted to sit on the edge of the bed, lean over the pillow, hold his brother's hand, and press his pale forehead with fraternal lips. Mrs. Yorke bore this well, allowing him to stay half the day, even letting him sit up all night. She rose at five on a wet November morning, lit the kitchen fire herself, made breakfast, and served it to the brothers. Majestically arrayed in a boundless flannel wrapper, shawl, and nightcap, she sat watching them eat with the complacency of a hen observing her chicks. Yet she warned the cook for daring to bring up sago-gruel, and the housemaid lost favor for airing Moore's surtout and helping him on with it, accepting a smile and a shilling in return. Two ladies called one day, pale and anxious, begging to see Moore; Mrs. Yorke hardened her heart and sent them packing with opprobrium.

But how was it when Hortense Moore arrived? Surprisingly, the whole family suited Mrs. Yorke better than any other. Hortense and she shared an exhaustless mutual theme: the corrupt propensities of servants. Their views were identical; they watched the staff with the same suspicion and judged them with the same severity. Hortense showed no jealousy of Mrs. Yorke's attentions to Robert, allowing her to remain the nurse with little interference. Instead, she found ceaseless occupation fidgeting about the house, surveilling the kitchen, reporting on its activities, and generally making herself useful. Together, they excluded visitors sedulously, holding the young mill-owner captive, hardly letting the air breathe or the sun shine on him.

Dr. MacTurk, the surgeon to whom Moore's case was committed, pronounced the wound dangerous but not hopeless. Initially, he wished to place a nurse of his own selection with Moore, but neither Mrs. Yorke nor Hortense would hear of it. They promised faithful obedience to his directions, and so he was left in their hands.

They executed the trust to the best of their ability, yet something went wrong. Bandages were displaced or tampered with, leading to great loss of blood. MacTurk, summoned, arrived with his steed foaming. He was one of those surgeons dangerous to vex—abrupt in his best moods and savage in his worst. Seeing Moore's state, he relieved his feelings with flowery language unnecessary to record here. A bouquet or two of the choicest blossoms fell on the unperturbed head of Mr. Graves, his stony young assistant; another nosegay went to a second young gentleman in his train, an interesting facsimile of himself, indeed his own son; but the full corbeille of blushing bloom fell to the lot of meddling womankind, en masse.

For most of one winter night, MacTurk and his satellites were busy about Moore. There, at his bedside, shut up alone in the chamber, they wrought and wrangled over his exhausted frame. Three of them stood on one side of the bed, and Death on the other. The conflict was sharp, lasting until daybreak, when the balance between the belligerents seemed so equal that either party might have claimed victory.

At dawn, Graves and the younger MacTurk were left in charge, while the senior surgeon sought additional strength, securing it in the person of Mrs. Horsfall, the best nurse on his staff. He gave Moore into her charge with stern injunctions regarding the responsibility laid upon her shoulders. She accepted this responsibility stolidly, as she did the easy-chair at the bedhead. That moment began her reign.

Mrs. Horsfall possessed one virtue: orders from MacTurk were obeyed to the letter. The Ten Commandments were less binding in her eyes than her surgeon's dictum. In other respects, she was no woman but a dragon. Hortense Moore fell effaced before her; Mrs. Yorke withdrew, crushed. Yet both were personages of dignity in their own estimation and of significant bulk in the eyes of others. Perfectly cowed by the breadth, height, bone, and brawn of Mrs. Horsfall, they retreated to the back parlour. She, for her part, sat upstairs when she liked and downstairs when she preferred it. She took her dram three times a day and her pipe of tobacco four times.

As for Moore, no one ventured to inquire about him. Mrs. Horsfall had him at dry-nurse. It was she who was to do for him, and the general conjecture ran that she did so accordingly.

Morning and evening MacTurk came to see him. His case, complicated by this new mischance, became one of interest to the surgeon. He regarded him as a damaged piece of clockwork, which it would be creditable to his skill to set going again. Graves and the younger MacTurk—Moore's sole other visitors—contemplated him in the light in which they usually viewed the occupant of the dissecting-room at Stilbro' Infirmary.

Robert Moore had a pleasant time of it—in pain, in danger, too weak to move, almost too weak to speak, with a sort of giantess as his keeper and the three surgeons as his sole society. Thus he lay through the diminishing days and lengthening nights of the whole drear month of November.

In the beginning of his captivity, Moore feebly resisted Mrs. Horsfall. He hated the sight of her rough bulk and dreaded the contact of her hard hands, but she taught him docility in a trice. She made no account of his six feet, his manly thews and sinews; she turned him in his bed as another woman would turn a babe in its cradle. When he was good, she addressed him as "my dear" and "honey," and when he was bad, she sometimes shook him. If he attempted to speak when MacTurk was present, she lifted her hand and bade him "Hush!" like a nurse checking a forward child. If she had not smoked or taken gin, he thought, it would have been better; but she did both. Once, in her absence, he intimated to MacTurk that "that woman was a dram-drinker."

"Pooh! my dear sir, they are all so," was the reply he got for his pains. "But Horsfall has this virtue," added the surgeon, "drunk or sober, she always remembers to obey _me_."

At length the latter autumn passed; its fogs and rains withdrew from England their mourning and tears; its winds swept on to sigh over lands far away. Behind November came deep winter—clearness, stillness, frost accompanying.

A calm day settled into a crystalline evening. The world wore a North Pole coloring; all its lights and tints looked like the reflections of white, violet, or pale green gems. The hills wore a lilac blue; the setting sun had purple in its red; the sky was ice, all silvered azure; when the stars rose, they were of white crystal, not gold; gray, cerulean, or faint emerald hues—cool, pure, and transparent—tinged the mass of the landscape.

What is this by itself in a wood no longer green, no longer even russet, a wood of neutral tint—this dark blue moving object? Why, it is a schoolboy—a Briarfield grammar-school boy—who has left his companions, now trudging home by the highroad, and is seeking a certain tree, with a certain mossy mound at its root, convenient as a seat. Why is he lingering here? The air is cold and the time wears late. He sits down. What is he thinking about? Does he feel the chaste charm Nature wears tonight? A pearl-white moon smiles through the gray trees; does he care for her smile?

Impossible to say; for he is silent, and his countenance does not speak. As yet it is no mirror to reflect sensation, but rather a mask to conceal it. This boy is a stripling of fifteen—slight, and tall for his years. In his face there is as little amenity as servility; his eye seems prepared to note any incipient attempt to control or overreach him, and the rest of his features indicate faculties alert for resistance. Wise ushers avoid unnecessary interference with that lad. To break him in by severity would be a useless attempt; to win him by flattery would be an effort worse than useless. He is best let alone. Time will educate and experience train him.

Professedly Martin Yorke (it is a young Yorke, of course) tramples on the name of poetry. Talk sentiment to him, and you would be answered by sarcasm. Here he is, wandering alone, waiting duteously on Nature, while she unfolds a page of stern, silent, and solemn poetry beneath his attentive gaze.

Being seated, he takes from his satchel a book—not the Latin grammar, but a contraband volume of fairy tales. There will be light enough yet for an hour to serve his keen young vision. Besides, the moon waits on him; her beam, dim and vague as yet, fills the glade where he sits.

He reads. He is led into a solitary mountain region; all round him is rude and desolate, shapeless, and almost colorless. He hears bells tinkle on the wind. Forth-riding from the formless folds of the mist dawns on him the brightest vision—a green-robed lady, on a snow-white palfrey. He sees her dress, her gems, and her steed. She arrests him with some mysterious question. He is spell-bound and must follow her into fairyland.

A second legend bears him to the seashore. There tumbles a strong tide, boiling at the base of dizzy cliffs. It rains and blows. A reef of rocks, black and rough, stretches far into the sea. All along, and among, and above these crags dash and flash, sweep and leap, swells, wreaths, drifts of snowy spray. Some lone wanderer is out on these rocks, treading with cautious step the wet, wild seaweed; glancing down into hollows where the brine lies fathoms deep and emerald clear, and seeing there wilder and stranger and huger vegetation than is found on land, with treasure of shells—some green, some purple, some pearly—clustered in the curls of the snaky plants. He hears a cry. Looking up and forward, he sees, at the bleak point of the reef, a tall, pale thing—shaped like man, but made of spray—transparent, tremulous, awful. It stands not alone. They are all human figures that wanton in the rocks—a crowd of foam-women—a band of white, evanescent Nereids.

Hush! Shut the book; hide it in the satchel. Martin hears a tread. He listens. No—yes. Once more the dead leaves, lightly crushed, rustle on the wood path. Martin watches; the trees part, and a woman issues forth.

She is a lady dressed in dark silk, a veil covering her face. Martin never met a lady in this wood before—nor any female, save now and then a village girl come to gather nuts. Tonight the apparition does not displease him. He observes, as she approaches, that she is neither old nor plain, but, on the contrary, very youthful; and, but that he now recognizes her for one whom he has often wilfully pronounced ugly, he would deem that he discovered traits of beauty behind the thin gauze of that veil.
