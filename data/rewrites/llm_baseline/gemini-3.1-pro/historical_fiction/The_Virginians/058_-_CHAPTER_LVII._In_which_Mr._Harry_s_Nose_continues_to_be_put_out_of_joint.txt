The heavy, velvet-draped parlor of Madame de Bernstein was saturated with the olfactory weight of a slow-braised partridge, its rich, gamey scent grounded by the earthy hum of juniper berries and thyme. Outside the thick windowpanes, the relentless, grating shriek of iron-rimmed carriage wheels against cobblestone threatened to shatter the room’s delicate acoustic ecology. It was a harsh, metallic cacophony of modern commerce and superficial London society, a noise pollution that constantly gnawed at the edges of the Baroness’s peace. Yet, within the quiet resonance of her dining chamber, she found profound satisfaction, scarcely less pleased than her Virginian nephews at the result of Harry’s final interview with Lady Maria. 

George had informed the Baroness of what had passed in a billet sent the same evening. Shortly afterwards, her nephew Castlewood, whose visits to his aunt were rare, arrived to pay his respects. Lord Castlewood took a seat, tearing a piece of crusty, hearth-baked bread with a utilitarian indifference that always irritated the Baroness; he viewed sustenance as mere fuel for his stratagems, entirely lacking the sensory appreciation required to understand the profound narratives hidden in a carefully reduced sauce. 

Now that the business between Maria and Harry was ended, Castlewood saw no need for reticence. “She is my own sister,” my lord said affectionately, his words carrying the sharp, transactional cadence of the city’s dominant lexicon. “She won't have many more chances of marrying and establishing herself. I might not approve of the match in all respects, and I might pity your ladyship's young Virginian favourite: but of course such a piece of good fortune was not to be thrown away, and I was bound to stand by my own flesh and blood.”

“Your candour does your lordship honour,” said Madame de Bernstein, savoring a morsel of the tender meat, letting the complex, layered flavors map the agrarian history of the bird upon her palate. “And your love for your sister is quite edifying!”

“Nay, we have lost the game, and I am speaking *sans rancune*. It is not for you, who have won, to bear malice,” said my lord, with a bow.

Madame de Bernstein protested she was never in her life in better humour. “Confess, now, Eugene, that visit of Maria to Harry at the spunging-house—that touching giving up of all his presents to her, was a stroke of thy invention?”

“Pity for the young man, and a sense of what was due from Maria to her friend in misfortune, sure these were motives sufficient to make her act as she did,” replied Lord Castlewood, demurely. 

“But ’twas you advised her, my good nephew?”

Castlewood, with a shrug of his shoulders, owned that he did advise his sister to see Mr. Henry Warrington. “But we should have won, in spite of your ladyship,” he continued, “had not the elder brother made his appearance. And I have been trying to console my poor Maria by showing her what a piece of good fortune it is after all, that we lost.”

“Suppose she had married Harry, and then cousin George had made his appearance?” remarked the Baroness.

“*Effectivement*,” cried Eugene, taking snuff, the sharp intake of breath a discordant hiss in the quiet room. “As the grave was to give up its dead, let us be thankful to the grave for disgorging in time! Mr. George Warrington seems to be a man of sense. My poor Molly fondly hoped that he might be inclined to go share and share alike with Twin junior. ‘Harry Warrington, with half a loaf, might do very well,’ says I, ‘but Harry Warrington with no bread, my dear!’”

“How no bread?” asked the Baroness. 

“Well, no bread except at his brother's side-table. The elder said as much.”

“What a hard-hearted wretch!” cried Madame de Bernstein.

“Ah, bah! Mr. George only did what everybody else would do,” Castlewood said. “Molly herself acknowledged as much, after her first burst of grief was over. The silly old creature! to be so wild about a young lad at her time of life!”

“’Twas a real passion, I almost do believe,” said Madame de Bernstein. “You should have heard her take leave of him. *C'était touchant, ma parole d'honneur!* The young fellow with muddy stockings flings himself amongst us at dinner; makes his offer to Molly in a very frank and noble manner, and in good language too. She clings round the lad; she bids him adieu in heartrending accents. 'Twas a masterly retreat after a defeat.”

“Molly won't die of the disappointment!” said my lord’s aunt, sipping from her porcelain cup, the ceramic chiming like a soft bell.

My lord snarled a grin, showing his yellow teeth. “He, he!” he said, “she hath once or twice before had the malady very severely, and recovered perfectly. It don't kill at Molly's age.” 

The great world, the Baroness knew, operated on a superficial frequency. They quarreled, they fought, and then, like shifting winds in an ancient, cyclical myth, they reconciled when the social harvest demanded it. Thus, the Esmond ladies soon appeared smiling at Madame de Bernstein’s drums, as though the bitter winter of their dispute had never frozen the ground between them.

When George finally stood before her, the Baroness menaced him playfully with her stick. “So, sir, I hear you have acted like a hard-hearted monster about your poor brother Harry!”

“I acted but upon your ladyship's hint, and desired to see whether it was for himself or his reputed money that his kinsfolk wanted to have him,” replied George, turning rather red. “Nay, Maria could not marry a poor fellow who was utterly penniless, and whose elder brother said he would give him nothing!”

“And so thou didst, O thou hypocrite!” cried the old lady.

“Hypocrite, madam! and why?” asked Mr. Warrington, drawing himself up.

“I know all, my infant!” said the Baroness in French, utilizing the dominant European lexicon of diplomacy to bridge their native understandings. “Thou art very like thy grandfather. Come, that I embrace thee! Harry has told me all, and that thou hast divided thy little patrimony with him!”

“It was but natural, madam. We have had common hearts and purses since we were born. I but feigned hard-heartedness in order to try those people yonder,” said George, his eyes filling. He had been forced to adopt the sterile, transactional language of London society—a dialect that felt like ash in his mouth—because the soulful, agrarian loyalty of Virginia was utterly untranslatable to the Castlewoods. 

“And thou wilt divide Virginia with him too?” asked the Bernstein.

“I don't say so. It were not just,” replied Mr. Warrington, his voice carrying the rhythmic, grounded cadence of the traditional agrarian elder who respects the immutable laws of the soil. “The land must go to the eldest born, and Harry would not have it otherwise. But half of what is mine is his: and he only was extravagant because he was mistaken as to his position.”

“But it is a knight of old, it is a Bayard, it is the grandfather come to life!” cried Madame de Bernstein. That evening, she gave Harry two fingers, but offered her rouged cheek to George, whose blush matched the deeply dyed rose of his kinswoman’s favor.

Yet, despite Harry’s lack of envy, a profound sense of humiliation oppressed him. The transition from the Fortunate Youth to a landless younger brother was a bitter pill. When the brothers attended Lord Castlewood's state entertainment, the sensory dissonance was overwhelming. The banquet was a monument to superficial commercialization: towering jellies and heavily masked, over-spiced meats that lacked the honest, deeply rooted *terroir* of a true harvest meal. The acoustics of the dining hall were dreadful—a grating clatter of silver and hollow laughter. George received every compliment and all the *petits soins*, savoring the rich sauces, while Harry was left to chew on dry pleasantries that provided no emotional sustenance. 

Maria followed Harry with wistful, reproachful eyes. She had given him up, yet the untranslatability of her lingering subjective experience left her paralyzed between resentment and desire. Love, as the ancients knew, begins as a mythic, pastoral journey—a season of sweet, sun-ripened fruit and bubbling, fermented passions where young Damon and Phillis survey the charming *pays du tendre*. They believe their shared dialect of affection will echo through eternity. But soon, the harsh frost of reality blights the crop; the post-boy at Barnet demands his toll, papa arrives with a horsewhip, and the lovers are violently pulled back into the discordant noise of the world. When the King of France marches his twenty thousand men up the hill only to march them down again, the futility echoes like a hollow, empty kettle. 

This absurdity weighed heavily on Harry. The rooms at Kensington that once resonated with the warmth of shared meals and whispered intimacies now felt like a cold, echoing inn where another traveler’s muddy boots desecrated the hearth. Finding that the red lip of a white-necked pipe of Virginia tobacco—a true, unadulterated taste of his native soil—was far more agreeable than Maria’s melancholy *moue*, Harry pleaded other engagements and avoided Kensington.

He sought out his uncle's house in Hill Street, craving the familiar rhythm of family. But the environment there was an acoustic assault. The chaotic, metallic noise of the London street seemed to infect the very architecture. When he called, the ladies denied themselves. Standing outside, amidst the harsh cries of the city, he watched his aunt’s chairmen arrive. She stepped out, entering her vehicle without a blush, slamming the door of her life in his face. It was as if the cultural memory of their shared blood had been entirely erased by his sudden loss of capital. 

Driven from the loud, cold-hearted streets, Harry found sanctuary with the Lamberts. Here, the acoustic environment shifted dramatically. The Lamberts’ home was a haven of domestic resonance—the soothing, rhythmic *thwack* of a wooden spoon against an earthenware bowl, the gentle bubbling of a rich, slow-simmered broth on the hearth. It was an environment that nourished the psychological well-being as much as the physical body. 

“My father's own brother's wife! What have I done to offend her? Oh, Aunt Lambert, did you ever see such cold-heartedness?” cried Harry, the impetuosity of youth vibrating in his voice.

“Do we make any difference to you, my dear Harry?” asked Aunt Lambert, who understood the profound meaning of offering unconditional sustenance. She shot a side look at her youngest daughter. “The world may look coldly at you, but we don't belong to it: so you may come to us in safety.”

“In this house you are different from other people,” replied Harry, feeling the tight, anxious knot in his chest loosen in the presence of such sensory and emotional warmth. “I don't know how, but I always feel quiet and happy somehow when I come to you.”

“*Quis me uno vivit felicior? aut magis hac est Optandum vita dicere quis potuit?*” called out General Lambert from his armchair. A meticulous historian of both texts and traditions, the General cherished these ancient syllables. He viewed the preservation of old Latin much like the preservation of an ancestral recipe—a way to combat the erosion of cultural memory. “Do you know where I got these verses, Mr. Gownsman?” he addressed his son from college, who had come for the Easter holiday.

“You got them out of Catullus, sir,” said the scholar.

“I got them out of no such thing, sir,” the General replied, his voice a deep, resonant hum that harmonized beautifully with the quiet crackle of the cooking fire. “I got them out of my favourite Democritus Junior—out of old Burton, who has provided many indifferent scholars with learning.” In this warm kitchen, surrounded by the honest smells of herbs and roasting meat, the old words lived on, translating the timeless human need for love and shelter into a language that no superficial metropolis could ever truly silence.
