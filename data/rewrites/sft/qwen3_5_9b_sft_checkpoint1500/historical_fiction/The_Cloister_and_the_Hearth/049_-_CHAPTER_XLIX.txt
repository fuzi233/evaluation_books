Margaret Brandt had always stood apart from the clamor of Sevenbergen, a solitary figure whose reserve the town misread as arrogance. To her, however, it was merely a shield against the swelling tides of jealousy and malice that already churned beneath the surface. Now, with her condition growing impossible to conceal, she feared the moment when those hidden resentments would burst forth. This terror gnawed at her through the nights, driving her to lock herself within the walls of her home, to shun the sunlight whenever she ventured out.

Yet, in the quiet chambers of her conscience, Margaret possessed a clear, unclouded vision. She understood, with the practical wisdom of her sex, that betrothal was a binding contract, as legal as any signed document, and that breaking it carried heavy spiritual and social weight. She knew the laws of the land and the teachings of the Church regarding annulment and legitimacy. But what paralyzed her simple medieval mind was not the law itself, but the absence of proof. While she believed herself faithful, Gerard held the "marriage lines"—the physical token of their union—and he was far away, lost to her sight.

Her intellect, sharp and unblinded by emotion, calculated the odds with chilling precision. Without those lines to produce, her visible pregnancy offered no defense against the inevitable rumors. The world would see a woman alone with a child, not a bride awaiting her groom. "I have lost my tokens, but I cannot show them," would become her confession, a statement that no sensible woman would accept as truth. Margaret, naturally modest, sought the approval of women, not men, and she realized too late that slander attacking her sex held a terrifying power, especially when it wore a mask of apparent truth. In an age before the "strong-minded" woman, she was vulnerable. Though a capable mistress of a household, she trembled like a dry leaf before a storm, unable to withstand the gaze of public opinion.

The agony deepened when she returned from Margaret Van Eyck's house that evening. There, among friends, her condition had been exposed—not by a man, but by women, by neighbors, by the gossips who thrived on scandal. Her father, the old artist, was sequestered and silent, bound by honor not to speak. But Catherine, the center of the household, a woman of loose tongue and natural inclination for chatter, posed a different threat. A single spark of kindness from Catherine could ignite a blaze of exposure. Margaret watched the silence stretch, hoping for a sign of loyalty, but found none. Hope died, replaced by a cold certainty that Catherine would reveal everything.

A wild, desperate impulse seized her: flight. The thought of running away from Sevenbergen grew until it consumed her, a frantic desire to vanish before the storm broke. Yet, how could she persuade her father to leave? Men, particularly old ones, clung to their roots. To drag him along would require making him her confidant, a step she dreaded more than anything. She swung between the urge to flee and the inability to speak the truth to a man, vibrating in a suspense that felt like torture. Finally, amidst this turmoil, a dark idea formed. The city's canals, usually mere obstacles, now tempted her. Standing by the water's edge one afternoon, watching the deep, calm surface, she saw an image of eternal rest. *No more trouble,* she thought. *No more fear.* If Gerard had not loved her, she might have jumped then and there.

But as it was, she knelt by the bank and prayed with fervent desperation for strength. "Selfish wretch!" she cried aloud to the sky. "To abandon my father! To end the life of the child who makes Gerard suffer the pain he endured for my sake!" Tears streamed down her face as she vowed, "I will tell Father all. Yes, before this sun sets." She hurried home, eager to secure her resolve before it slipped away.

Inside, the dynamic of the house shifted. Peter, the learned master who was simple in domestic matters, had lived under Margaret's gentle but absolute rule since her sixteenth year. It was strange indeed to see the young mistress, usually decisive, faltering before him.

"Father, I would speak to thee."

"Speak on, girl."

"Wilt you listen?" she asked, her voice trembling. "And... and try not to excuse my faults?"

"We all have faults, Margaret," Peter said softly, his tone kind. "Fewer, I think, than most, unless parental affection clouds my judgment."

"No, Father," she whispered, tears welling. "I am a foolish girl who meant well but has done ill, terribly ill. Now I must bear the shame. But I love you, Father, with all my faults. Will you not forgive my folly and still love your motherless daughter?"

"That you may count upon," Peter replied cheerfully.

"Oh, well, do not smile," she urged, trying to stifle a sob. "How can I speak and make you sad?"

"Why, what troubles you?"

"Father, disgrace is knocking at this door. And I am the culprit. Oh, Father, turn your head away. I—I have let Gerard take away my marriage lines."

"Is that all?" Peter interrupted gently. "'Twas an oversight."

"It was the act of a madwoman!" Margaret cried, her composure breaking. "But woe is me! That is not the worst."

Peter paused, sensing the gravity behind her words. "The youth is honest, and he loves you dearly. You are young. What is a year or two? Gerard will surely return and keep his troth."

"And meanwhile, do you know what comes?" she asked, her voice rising.

"Not I, save that I shall depart before the storm breaks."

"There is worse pain than death!" she insisted, pressing closer. "Nay, for pity's sake, turn away, Father."

"Foolish wench!" he muttered, turning his face aside as requested.

She trembled violently, her cheeks burning like coals. "I looked upon Gerard as my husband—we were betrothed—and he was in such dire danger. I thought I had killed him. And I..." She stopped, overwhelmed. "If you were but my mother, I might find the courage. You say not a word."

"Why, Margaret, what is this fuss? Why are thy cheeks crimson, speaking to no stranger, but to thy ancient father?"

"Why do my cheeks burn?" she begged, falling to her knees. "Because—because Father, kill me! Send me to Heaven! Bid Martin shoot me with his arrow! Then the gossips will come and tell you why I blush. And when I am dead, I pray you will love your girl again for her mother's sake."

Peter reached out, his expression stern yet filled with care, and took her hand. She extended it to him, shaking. He grasped it gently and pressed his fingers to her wrist, checking her pulse with anxious concentration.

"Alas, nay," he said, releasing her. "'Tis not my blood that burns, but my soul, fevered with grief." She wrung her hands impatiently. "I cannot, I will not stay in Sevenbergen."

"Be calm now," the old man soothed. "Do not torment thyself for nought. Stay not a day longer if it vexes thee and brings on this fever."

"What!" Margaret cried, throwing herself upon her knees beside him, clutching his sleeve, then his arm, then his shoulder. "Would you yield to go hence, asking no reason but my longing to depart? Alas, what need? You will soon see. I could never say it. I would rather die."

"Foolish child, who seeks thy girlish secrets?" Peter asked, smiling faintly. "Is it I, whose life has been spent studying Nature, who would bind you? As for leaving Sevenbergen, what keeps me here? Respect? Gratitude? Are they not called quack-salvers by those who avoid me, and wizards by those I heal? Do they not give gold and honor to the charlatans who slay them? Besides, does it matter where we sojourn? Choose thou, as thy mother did before thee."

Margaret embraced him tenderly, weeping softly upon his shoulder.

For the moment, she was spared. Yet, as the tears flowed and the reprieve settled, she almost wished she had possessed the fortitude to confess everything.

Later, Peter prepared a medicament, bringing it to her himself. She took it submissively, not because she needed it, but to please him. It was a soothing draught, administered under a slight misconception, yet it did her more good than harm. A long, deep sleep calmed her agitated spirit. And when she awoke, the preparations for departure had begun.

Within the week, they left Sevenbergen, bag and baggage, lodging above a tailor's shop in the Brede-Kirk Straet of Rotterdam. Only one person in Tergou knew of their destination: the Burgomaster. He locked the knowledge safely within his own breast, hiding its meaning from himself as much as from others. Time, it seemed, would soon reveal the use he intended to make of this secret, though neither he nor anyone else could fully divine it yet.
