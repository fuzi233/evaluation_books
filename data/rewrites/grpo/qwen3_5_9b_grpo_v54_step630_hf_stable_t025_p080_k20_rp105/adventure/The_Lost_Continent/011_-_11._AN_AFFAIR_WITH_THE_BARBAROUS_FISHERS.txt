The Empress's jealousy was a storm in a teacup, a mighty ruler reduced to sulking over a mere prisoner. My mind, honed by military discipline for moments of crisis, snapped to action the instant her words left her lips. I was bound by the High Council to sacrifice myself for Atlantis, and Nais was a secondary casualty in that equation. Yet, even as the thought of her death crossed my mind, a counter-scheme ignited.

I bowed low before Phorenice. "In this, and in all other matters where a human hand holds sway, I shall execute your will, Empress," I declared. Her response was immediate; she patted my arm, and a tide of emotion surged from her captivating eyes. The gods had indeed gifted her with a form and voice that turned battles into victories before they began.

The harbor merchants and neighbors soon descended, offering clothes and shelter. Phorenice accepted graciously. While I cared little for the dampness of my coat, the waters where the mammoth had tossed us were foul, and I gladly followed her lead. I said nothing to Nais, offering not even a final glance. Such a small gesture, however, was insufficient for Phorenice. Before departing, she issued strict orders to the port captain to guard his prisoner.

Inside the merchant's house, I was bathed and stripped of my plain garments, leaving the owner relieved and pleased. But before Phorenice returned, I spent an hour on the pavement listening to the distant rumble of bombardment. When she finally emerged, accompanied by a ruined merchant, a grim amusement touched my lips. He had lost everything—his jewels and gauds, bought in partnership, now adorning Phorenice.

"Are they not pretty?" she asked, flashing the stolen treasures. "I am glad the mammoth's ride brought me this gift. Tell this man that when he earns more, I shall visit again." She dismissed the remaining servants, opting instead to share a litter with me. "We shall play at being lovers today, even if true love awaits us tomorrow. Do not summon more bearers; my own slaves suffice. I am slender, Deucalion, and I dislike being overshadowed by a gross, overfed man."

"To the royal pyramid?" I asked.

"No," she replied, her mood shifting instantly. "I wish neither to fight nor rule today. I am Phorenice, the maiden, for a few hours. If someone worthy woos me, it shall be a luxury. Drive us around the harbor's rim. Tell the starers that if they follow, I shall rain fire upon them as I did upon the sacrifice."

I knew the streets were unruly, having heard hints from the pyramid guard, but I held my tongue. Dismissing the initial guard, I drove away the gaping crowd with sharp words and settled into the litter. As we swung along the wide pavement bordering the merchant houses and the harbor waters, I felt a pang of sadness. The basin was nearly empty. Ships that once lined the docks so closely they spilled into the estuary were now scarce. Trade, once roaring with life, had withered. Merchants, once portly and generous, now curled their beards in courtly fashion, neglecting their wares for banquets. The smaller traders, ground down by taxes and war, faced starvation. A massive cargo of slaves had recently been dumped across the estuary to die, for no one in Atlantis would accept another.

I suppressed these thoughts, knowing they would only sour our ride. Phorenice, sensing my mood, matched my silence with her own. We traveled to the westernmost wall, where she led me to a solitary stone house on a tidal promontory. Here, she revealed her secret: gold collectors.

While the Sacred Mountain taught that gold existed dissolved in seawater, no one had devised a way to extract it. Phorenice had stumbled upon the trick. "I tell Atlantis only the gods possess this secret," she laughed, patting my arm. "But I am no goddess to you, Deucalion. I must have dreamed it." She explained the system: seawater sluiced through culverts over rough metal plates, trapping the yellow metal in the ridges. Her workers melted the catch into bars. "My fellows tried to cheat me," she admitted, her tone lightening, "until I towed two of them behind a galley until the cannibal fish devoured them. Since then, they have given me credit for... divinity."

"More divinity," I suggested.

"I suppose so. But you too shall become a god among these silly Atlanteans if you apply your wit." Her words oscillated between mocking and earnest, leaving me increasingly mystified by her supple mind and body. The thought that Atlantis could be saved by my agency, even after marrying her, grew fainter. The only logical solution seemed to be ending her mischief with a dagger, yet the idea revolted me. I clung to the hope that the High Gods would command such a deed if it were necessary.

Yet, the fascination of Phorenice was a powerful drug. When we left the gold collectors and encountered danger, I found myself fighting with a ferocity I rarely displayed. The cause was the pervasive unrest and poverty of the common people. We were attacked by "barbarous fishers"—desperate men driven to violence by the harshness of their trade. Their lives were precarious, dependent on trained sea-birds that could be snatched by larger beasts, leaving them destitute and scorned by their guilds. Starving and desperate, they sought any chance to survive, often through theft or robbery.

As we rode, Phorenice spoke of her new sumptuary laws, having confiscated gold from a smith who dared wear it. Suddenly, clumsy fishing boats paddled toward us, and savage men ran from the narrow lanes, forming threatening clusters.

"I will scatter this rabble," I said, stepping from the litter.

"Oh, the people always cluster," she dismissed, drawing her weapons. "Scatter them. I am taking a holiday, Deucalion, while you learn to woo me."

I moved to confront the crowd, ordering them to clean their lanes and disperse. "Give us money," a shrill voice demanded.

"I will send a torch and rig a gallows if you annoy me further," I replied grimly. "To your kennels!"

Most retreated into the shadows, but the men in the boats called them back. Realizing I could not protect her alone if she remained a glittering target, I signaled the bearers to move. But Phorenice stopped them. Stepping out, she strapped a sword to her hip and donned a golden target on her forearm.

"An unfair trick," she laughed. "If you keep a fight to yourself now, where will your greediness lead when I am your shrinking wife?"

The crowd surged forward, drawn by her jewels. Fishers leaped from their boats, smashing the fragile vessels. Women joined the men, pouring out of the huts. "She called down fire once today!" one shouted. "She can burn us all!"

"Nay, I will not burn any," Phorenice retorted, her voice dripping with malicious amusement. "But you shall watch my sword-play till you are tired."

I knew she could not conjure fire here, but they gave us no time for pleasantries. Closing in, the reek of their bodies made us cough. A grimy hand reached for her jewels; I lopped it off at the elbow. Then we engaged.

"Your back to mine, comrade!" she cried, striking with fine dexterity. It was a slaughter. The crowd pushed inward with such greed that there was no room to retreat, and we maintained a deadly circle by sheer whirling of steel. Phorenice was a past-mistress of the blade, touching life with every thrust. It was clear the throne of Atlantis had been won not just by beauty, but by blood.

Above the uproar came her battle-shout: "Deucalion!" The flattery tickled me, and in the fury of the engagement, the memory of Nais vanished completely. Driven by the thrill of combat and the desire to protect her from these savage men, I fought with renewed vigor. We stood amidst a ring of twisted dead, the crowd shrinking back.

This was the moment for a truce. Strong words could have sent them fleeing. Instead, Phorenice gloated over our invulnerability, demanding payment for the bearers they had killed and threatening burnings and skinnings for the quarter.

"It makes your backs creep, does it?" she taunted. "Why do you not put it beyond my power to give the order? Either you must think yourselves gods or me no goddess."

Stubborn and sullen, they refused to flee. A stone whizzed between us, striking down a fisher. They had adapted. I looked for a rescue, finding none.

"Phorenice," I said in the court tongue, "a whiff of sea-breeze would be welcome. Will you cut a path to the beach, and I will keep these creatures at bay?"

"Why have you kept back your flattering speeches till now?" she teased, leaping over the ring of dead. With a charge of terrifying speed, she cut a way through the crowd to the crashing surf. I followed, guarding her rear, marveling at her agility.

Two boats drifted in the surf. One was crushed by the waves; the other was our only hope. The fishers saw our intent and shouted, "Guard the boat! Smash her! Slit her skin!"

Phorenice was a legged snake, reaching the boat before anyone could stop her. I, slower of foot, fought off attackers on the sand, eager to reduce the number of stone-throwers. We reached the beach, and I tipped the flimsy craft into the water.

"Fighting is no trade for me," I cried, "while you are here, Phorenice. Guard my back and walk into the water."

I waded with the boat, but the fishers swarmed like sharks. I wielded a short stabbing knife, while Phorenice danced with her dripping sword. Then, a great fish surged into the shallows, dragging a fisher down. I grabbed Phorenice and threw her into the boat, pushing off with all my might.

The fishers pressed on, indifferent to the loss of their kin, eager only for the jewels. Two dove beneath to slit the boat's skin; I killed them. Phorenice dispatched those climbing the gunwales. Suddenly, a huge long-necked turtle rose from the mud, jaws gaping. Phorenice, quick with a remedy, thrust a fisher's body toward the beast. The turtle snapped it up, vanishing into the depths.

Now in deep water, Phorenice balanced the boat while I paddled. She held two shields—her golden target and the rough hide buckler I had won—deflecting the volley of jagged stones. We drew away from the shore, safe for the moment, the barbarous fishers content with lopped arms and split faces, forgetting the morning of a tomorrow they might not see.
