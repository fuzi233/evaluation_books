When little Gerard was almost three months old, a messenger arrived from Tergou, breathless and urgent, bearing word for Catherine. “Tell them I cannot come,” she said firmly, “and I will not. They have Kate already.” The man turned and left without another word.

Catherine resumed her thoughts aloud: “There, child, I must go. They’re all in confusion—this is the third time they’ve sent. And tomorrow he’ll come himself, dragging me home by the ear, with a flea in his ear to boot.”

She then reviewed the lessons of motherhood she had learned through years of experience, instructing Margaret on how to handle every possible emergency that might arise. She pressed money into Margaret’s hands, but Margaret refused it, bowing her head with gratitude. Catherine insisted, growing stern. Margaret offered excuses—reasonable ones—but Catherine dismissed them with quiet disdain. To her, such reserve lacked the warmth and tenderness befitting a woman.

“Come now,” she said, “speak plainly. We are parting, and who knows if we shall ever see each other again?”

“Oh, mother, do not say that!” Margaret pleaded.

“Alas, girl,” Catherine replied, “I’ve seen it too often. At every farewell, the thought comes to me. When I was your age, I never dreamed such things could happen. We believed we would live forever. So speak your heart.”

“Well then, mother—there’s something I’ve hesitated to tell you. Your Cornelis once said to me, ‘So you are come to share with us, eh, mistress?’ That was his very words. I told him I would be deeply sorry.”

“Beshrew his ill tongue! What does it matter? He’ll never know.”

“Perhaps he would, sooner or later.”

“But whether he does or not, I will accept no charity that carries a grudge. Unless I saw my child starving—Heaven alone knows what I might do. But no, mother, give me only your love. I value that above silver, and they cannot take that from me. Not a stiver shall I take from their house.”

“You are a foolish lass,” Catherine said, shaking her head. “Were it me, I’d take it just to spite him.”

“No, you wouldn’t,” Margaret replied gently. “You and I are apples from the same tree.”

Catherine smiled, yielding at last. And when the moment of parting came, both women clung to one another, tears streaming freely.

After Catherine left, the baby cried incessantly. All efforts to soothe him failed until Margaret suspected a pin caught between his clothes and skin. Upon searching, she found a gold angel pressing against his spine.

“There now, Gerard,” she whispered to the infant, “I thought granny gave in rather suddenly.”

She carefully removed the coin, wrapped it in a scrap of linen, and placed it at the bottom of her chest. Then she looked down at the child and said softly, “Observe, my dear, that I can be as resolute as granny herself.”

Catherine later told Eli of Margaret’s refusal and her stubborn pride. Eli said she was right, and Catherine wrong. Catherine tossed her head in disagreement. Eli remained silent, deep in thought.

Margaret, meanwhile, faced daily struggles. She still had two men to feed—Martin and the old man—and could no longer work as fiercely as before. Her days were filled with tending the house, caring for the child, and cooking for all. Yet she had saved a little money, and she often told her son, “Your father will return soon to help us before this runs out.” With that hope, and the joy of her boy, she passed several peaceful months.

But time wore on. No sign of Gerard. No word. And stranger still, no news at all.

Her mind began to wander, contrary to her practical nature. She would sit lost in sorrowful thoughts, sighing quietly. While her heart grew heavy, her savings dwindled.

One day, she opened the cupboard to find it empty. She looked into the faces of those who depended on her—Martin, the old man—and felt a wave of pity surge within. She knelt beside the cradle and asked the baby, “Can I let grandfather and poor Martin go hungry?” Then, without hesitation, she took out Catherine’s hidden angel.

As she unfolded the cloth, a single tear fell upon the gold—a quiet shame for having to use it.

She sent Martin to exchange it for coins. While he was gone, a Frenchman arrived with one of the dealers who had earlier offered her so little for her illuminated manuscripts. This man explained he worked for his sovereign, collecting masterpieces for a book of hours.

Margaret showed him her best pieces. He was enchanted by one—the delicate flowers, raspberries, and creeping creatures shaded by Margaret Van Eyck herself. He offered a price beyond anything she had ever imagined.

“Do not mock my need,” she said, “three mouths depend on me, and none to fill them but myself.”

“Indeed, fair mistress,” he replied, “I would not overreach. My princess commands me to seek only the finest artisans. She insists on fairness—no hard bargains. Let me satisfy you, and you satisfy me.”

The next moment, Margaret was kneeling beside the cradle, kissing little Gerard, showering four gold coins upon him again and again, speaking to him in broken Dutch: “Oh, what a kind princess—wasn’t she? We will pray for her, won’t we, my lambkin, when we are old enough?”

Martin returned furious. “They will not change it. I suspect they think I stole it.”

“I am truly grateful,” Margaret said quickly, snatching the coin back, wrapping it again, and returning it to its hiding place.

Before these unexpected funds ran out, she resumed her ironing and starching. But soon after, Martin fell ill and died after nine days. Nearly all her money went toward giving him a decent burial.

He was gone. An empty chair sat by the hearth—where he had always preferred the warmth of the fire to the sun, especially since the Busy Body had departed. Margaret refused to let anyone else sit there. Yet whenever her eyes lingered too long on the vacant seat, a tear would fall.

Now there was no one to carry her laundry home. If she did it herself, she would have to leave little Gerard with a neighbor—but she dared not trust such a treasure to mortal hands. And besides, she could not bear to be away from him for hours.

So she began seeking a boy to carry her basket on Saturdays and Mondays.

A plump, rosy-faced youth named Luke Peterson appeared—seeming fifteen, though he was eighteen. Blushing and fidgeting with his hat, he asked, “Would a man serve you better than a boy?”

Even before he spoke, Margaret had thought, *This one will do.* But she took his cue and said, “Nay, but a man might ask more than I can afford.”

“Not I,” Luke declared warmly. “Mistress Margaret, I’m your neighbor. I work as a cooper. I’ll carry your basket before or after my day’s labor, gladly. You don’t need to pay me. We’re not strangers, you know.”

“Master Luke,” she replied, “I recognize your face, but I cannot recall ever speaking to you.”

“Oh yes, you did,” he said. “One day you were trying to carry your baby and a pitcher full of water. I said, ‘Let me take the baby.’ You said, ‘No, I’ll give you the pitcher—I’ll keep the child.’ I carried the pitcher home, and you took it from me here at your door. You said, ‘I am muckle obliged to you, young man,’ in such a sweet voice—unlike how folks around here talk to a fellow.”

“I remember now,” she said softly. “And I suppose it was the least I could say.”

“Well then, Mistress Margaret,” he continued, “if you’ll say as much every time I carry your basket, I’ll bear it any number of times, no matter how far.”

“Nay, nay,” she said, blushing faintly. “I would not burden your kindness. You are young and kind-hearted. Say I offer you supper on Saturday night when you bring the linen home, and your morning meal on Monday—would that balance things?”

“As you please,” he answered, “but don’t say I asked for two meals! Such a trifle!”

With Luke’s helpful presence and the strength Heaven granted this young woman—though she bore many sorrows—the household managed for a while.

But her heart grew heavier with each passing day. Gerard’s silence stretched on—now mysterious, now terrifying. And Suspense, that cruel tormentor, began to gnaw at her soul with hot pincers, until she wept aloud: “Oh, that I could know the worst!”

One afternoon, as she sat in quiet despair, she heard heavy footsteps climbing the stairs. She started, trembling. “That is not a step I know. Bad news?”

The door opened. It was Eli, looking grave yet gentle.

Margaret stared at him, her heart pounding, unable to speak.

“Girl,” he said softly, “the skipper has returned.”

“One word,” she gasped. “Is he alive?”

“I hope so. No one has seen him dead.”

“Then they must have seen him alive?”

“No, girl,” Eli said quietly. “Neither dead nor alive has he been seen in Rome for many months. My daughter Kate believes he has gone to another city. She asked me to tell you that.”

“Ay,” Margaret murmured, darkly. “Like enough. Like enough. My poor babe!”

Eli, his voice weak, asked for a morsel to eat—he had come fasting. Margaret, touched despite her turmoil, prepared a meal, trembling and half unaware of what she did.

Before he left, he laid his hand upon her head and said, “Whether he lives or dies, I see you as my daughter. Can I do nothing for you today? Think well.”

“Ay, old man,” she whispered. “Pray for him. And for me.”

Eli sighed deeply and descended the stairs slowly, heavily.

She listened, dazed, to his footsteps fade away. Then she sank down beside the cradle, drawing little Gerard close to her breast.

“Oh, my poor fatherless boy,” she moaned. “My fatherless boy!”
