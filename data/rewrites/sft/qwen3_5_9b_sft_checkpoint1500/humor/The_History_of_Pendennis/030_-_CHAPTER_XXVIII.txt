The ballroom at Baymouth was a cauldron of chaos. High words flew like shattered glass, and the crowd pressed together around a corner window with the desperate energy of trapped bees. Through the open pane, the street below roared with sarcasm—"Pitch into him!" "Where's the police?"—while inside, the air crackled with tension. On one side stood Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant, surrounded by a ring of admirers including the formidable Madame Fribsby; on the other, Arthur Pendennis, flanked by a circle of concerned gentlemen and ladies. Just as Strong pushed his way through the crush, the situation escalated.

The trouble stemmed from a tiny spark of pride involving Sir Francis Clavering's angry little chef. Earlier, Mr. Pen, humiliated by a disastrous waltz and stung by Miss Amory's icy behavior, had sought solace at the window, murmuring calmly to the sparkling sea. In his attempt to cool his temper, he felt a hand land heavily on his shoulder. Turning, he encountered Mirobolant, the French artist, with eyes blazing like coals beneath his pale ringlets. Being tapped by a kitchen staff member was an affront to every fiber of Pendennis's aristocratic sensibility.

"You speak French?" Mirobolant demanded, switching seamlessly to the tongue of his ancestors.

"What is that to you?" Pen retorted, stepping back.

"At any rate, you understand it?" Mirobolant bowed, rolling his R's with the force of a gasp. "Oui, *monsieur*, vous me comprendrez... quand je vous dis que vous êtes un lâche."

"A coward?" Pen stared, stunned. "The consequences of that word among men of honor are clear: I'll fling you out of this window!"

With a roar, Pen lunged forward, intent on executing his threat. The window was inches away, and the artist, though a formidable foil-fighter, was no match for the young gentleman. But the moment dissolved into a cacophony of screams, clattering fiddles, and rushing bodies. Captain Broadfoot and a burly officer interposed themselves like human walls, while Laura, her face a mask of terror, peeked over the crowd to ask, "What is wrong?"

Enter Strong, bursting from the refreshment room like a hurricane. He found Mirobolant grinding his teeth and spitting oaths in a garbled Galleon French, while Pen looked terrifyingly wicked, trying desperately to maintain composure.

"Qué pasa?" Strong asked the chef in rapid Spanish.

"I am Chevalier de Juillet!" the tiny man snapped, slapping his chest. "He insulted me!"

"He called me 'cook'," hissed the little Frenchman.

Strong couldn't suppress a chuckle. "Come away, poor Chevalier. We must not brawl before ladies. I'll carry your message to Mr. Pendennis. The poor fellow isn't right in his head."

Gathering round Pen, the group demanded answers. Stammering, Pen admitted he had accused the man of being a cook when the chef attempted to escort a young lady, leading to the "coward" remark and the challenge. "If you gentlemen hadn't stopped me, I'd have thrown him out!" he confessed.

"Damn him! Serve him right!" the gentlemen grumbled, though some suggested a "little thrashing" wouldn't hurt the arrogant foreigners.

Then Laura stepped forward, her face softening the storm. "Pen, you must shake hands with him before you sleep. Foreigners have different manners. If you hurt a poor man's feelings, you'd be the first to apologize, wouldn't you, dear Pen?"

Her gaze was pure forgiveness, an angelic plea. Pen, touched, took both her hands and promised compliance. As she lingered, gazing at him, he thought, *How fond she is!* Yet, he needed to resolve the absurdity of the quarrel first.

Laura then asked, "Won't you stop and waltz with me, Pen? I'm not afraid."

This was the fatal slip. Pen immediately visualized himself prostrate on the floor, having tripped over Miss Roundle and the dragoon, sending Blanche flying against the wall, all while the room erupted in laughter—including Laura and Pynsent.

"I shall never dance again," Pen declared, his face darkening with resolve. "Never. I'm surprised you'd ask."

"Why?" Laura pressed, her tone turning sharp. "Is it because you can't get Blanche?"

"No," Pen lied, his vanity flaring. "Because I don't wish to make a fool of myself. Especially not for you to laugh at me, Laura. I saw you and Pynsent. By Jove! No man shall laugh at me."

"Poor Pen!" Laura cried, wounded by his morbid sensitivity and savage vanity. "Who thinks the worse of you for stumbling? Why are you so ready to think evil?"

Just then, Mr. Pynsent arrived, bowing gracefully. "Lady Rockminster commands me to inquire if I may take you to supper?"

"I was going with my cousin," Laura stammered, glancing nervously at Pen.

"Oh, pray, no!" Pen interjected hastily. "You're in excellent hands. I can't do better than leave you. I'm going home."

"Good night, Mr. Pendennis," Pynsent said dryly—a polite wrapper for a wish to box the insolent jackanapes' ears. Pen merely bowed and departed, leaving Laura pleading behind.

Outside, the night air was crisp, the sea murmuring softly. Pynsent remarked on the strangeness of the gathering, mentioning the seafaring man who had bought him champagne and the various local dignitaries. Laura feigned ignorance, claiming she had been frightened by the quarrel.

"Pynsent said your cousin was rather unlucky tonight," Pynsent noted gently. "Three or four people haven't been pleased."

"Didn't he leave me in charge to you?" Laura asked, looking up with a guilty, coquettish flicker.

"Easily forgiven," Pynsent cried, taking her arm. They walked toward the supper room, where Laura, though eager to eat, felt deeply distressed about Pen. She wondered how her mother could expect her to wed a man so capricious and jealous. Relief finally came when a waiter delivered a small pencil note from Pen: *"I met Cooky below ready to fight me; I asked his pardon. I'm glad I did. Wanted to speak to you tonight, but will wait till you come home. God bless you. Dance away all night with Pynsent. — PEN."*

Pen descended the stairs, his heart heavy with regret for his harsh words to Laura, yet he couldn't apologize in front of Pynsent. Instead, he resolved to make peace with the Frenchman. He found Mirobolant in the hall, pacing with mischief.

"I've got him in the coffee-room," Mirobolant grinned. "With pistols and a candle. Swords on the beach? Mirobolant is a dead hand with foils."

"I can't fight a cook!" Pen fumed.

"He is a Chevalier of July!" Mirobolant countered. "They present arms to him!"

"And do you ask me to duel a servant?" Pen snarled. "I'll call a policeman!"

Strong laughed. "I'm but joking. I came to settle quarrels, not fight them. I've been soothing Mirobolant. He tells me you used the word 'cook' not as an insult, but because it's customary for a hired officer not to arm himself with a lady's arm. And here's the real secret: Madame Fribsby told me the poor artist is laboring under a violent passion."

Upon hearing this, Pen burst into a hearty laugh, his rage vanishing instantly. He realized his own jealousy throughout the evening had been absurd. Remembering his own youthful infatuations, he forgave the artist easily. He entered the coffee-room, extended his hand, and delivered a fluent speech in French, declaring he was "sincerely sorry" (*sincèrement fâché*) for the expression that had caused offense, promising he would never, *jamais, jamais* intend harm.

"Bravo!" cried Strong, delighted by Pen's fluency and magnanimity. Mirobolant withdrew, regretting his words, and Arthur felt a newfound confidence. Before departing with Strong for the "Butcher Boy" tavern, Pen dispatched a final note to Laura: *Dance away all night with Pynsent, and be very happy.*

At daybreak, they reached the "Brawl," separating as the ball at Baymouth wound down. Back at Fairoaks, Pen returned home without delivering his intended confession to Laura. He told his mother, Helen, of the "calamity" at Baymouth in a gay tone, dismissing the whole affair as a mere mishap unsuitable for serious declarations.

While Pen prepared for departure—packing books, polishing his gun, dreaming of entering the Inns of Court and becoming a successful playwright to secure his future and eventually marry Laura—life continued its chaotic rhythm. Laura returned home the following evening, driven by Pynsent, a sight that darkened Pen's brow. Helen, eager for news, retired early, leaving Laura alone with Pen, though both claimed fatigue.
