The three or four "To Let" boards had leaned propped against the low paling for so long no living resident of the tiny triangular square could remember a time they stood straight. Hunched at their own uneven, lopsided angles, they looked less like signage than a row of weathered wooden sentinels, perpetually on the verge of toppling down onto any passerby slow enough to linger at the gate—yet for years, not one of them had ever snared a tenant for the old, brooding house tucked away behind them. There was hardly any foot traffic to snare, in any case: the busy main thoroughfare ran a full furlong off, cut off from the square by a maze of warren-like tenements, blind alleys, and forgotten byways that had sprung up long after the old red-brick house was erected, hemming it in completely. It was common local gossip the old place was only spared demolition while a handful of long-held leases ran out, and the whole block would be cleared for new slum housing the second those papers lapsed.

The house’s brickwork bloomed dark with centuries of damp, half-buried in its facades the carved crowns, clasped hands, and forgotten insignia of insurance companies that had collapsed and vanished so long ago no one in the district could name them now. Local children had swung from the low wooden gate at the end of the entrance alley until all that remained was a single thick iron top bar; boarded basement windows lining the narrow path were scrawled over with tramps’ cryptic, looping chalk marks that never quite looked like ordinary hobo code, curving into faint, unrecognizable faces if you squinted at them long enough. Water spilling continuously from the gutter of the encroaching next-door house had worn the stone path into uneven, slimy ruts, and stray cats and dogs had long claimed the unlit alley as their own, leaving scraps of half-eaten carrion and paw prints caked in mud across every flat surface. The agents had never bothered to keep the To Let boards repainted or legible, and no one could blame them—there seemed no chance at all anyone would ever agree to move in.

For six months, the struggling writer Oleron had passed the old house twice a day, on his short walk between his overpriced lodgings and the separate workroom he rented an easy ten minutes away. All that time he had kept strictly to the far side of the square, never once brushing close enough to see the peeling paint on the lurching notice boards. Then one gray, rain-damp morning, he took the near side by accident, and found himself pausing right in front of one of the tilted boards. Beneath the agent’s smudged name, the faded lettering—scrawled in ink that had yellowed to the color of an old, healing bruise, seemingly around the time Oleron himself was a small boy—stated the key to the house could be collected at Number Six.

Oleron was already paying far more than a writer with no private income, who all but ignored his dwindling public, could afford for two separate spaces: a bedroom at his lodgings, and the workroom half a mile away. He paid extra, too, to store most of his grandmother’s heavy old furniture in a warehouse across town. Worse, he was constantly plagued by small, infuriating inconveniences: the book he wanted to read in bed was always at his workroom, the urgent note he needed mid-day was always tucked in the pocket of a coat hanging behind his bedroom door. The split domicile was a constant, quiet thorn in his side, and it had never occurred to him to fix it until that very second.

He leaned over the low privet hedge, glancing first at the chalk-marked boarded basement windows, then up at the smudged, grimy first-floor panes, then higher still to the flat stone leads on the roof, pale and blank as an upturned face staring up at the overcast sky. He stood for a minute, thumbing his lean, clean-shaven jaw, then darted one last look at the notice board and walked quickly across the square to Number Six.

He knocked, waited two or three minutes. The front door stood ajar, but no one answered. He raised his hand to knock a second time when a long-nosed man in rolled shirt sleeves appeared, his mouth still half-full of food. “I was saying a blessing over our dinner,” he snapped, severe and affronted that Oleron had interrupted his grace. Oleron asked for the key to the old empty house, and the man vanished back inside. Five more minutes passed before he reappeared, still masticating, and announced flatly the key had been lost years ago. “But you won’t need it,” he added. “The front door’s never shut, and a hard push’ll pop every other one. I’m Barrett, the agent for the place, if you’re thinking of taking it.”

Oleron walked back across the square, stepped down the two broken steps at the splintered gate, and followed the damp alley to the old wide front doorway. When he pushed it open, the house breathed out at him: a thick, cold smell of damp rot, mildew, and the faint, sickly sweet tang of something long left to fester under a floorboard. To his right, dark steps led down to gaping, cavernous cellars, and the broad, handsome main staircase stretched before him, its carved handrail crusted with decades of black grime. Oleron climbed carefully, avoiding all contact with the rail and the grimy walls, and stopped on the first landing. The door facing him was boarded over top to bottom, but he pushed at the door to his right, and a rusted old staple gave with a high, squealing crack that echoed through the empty rooms. He stepped across the threshold into the empty first-floor flat.

He spent a full quarter hour wandering the bare, sun-dappled rooms, then stepped back out and crossed the square again to Barrett’s house. “How much is the rent?” he asked. The sum Barrett named was absurdly, insultingly low, the price easily explained by the neglected neighborhood and the house’s total state of disrepair. When Oleron asked if he could rent only the single first floor, Barrett hesitated, said he didn’t know, it might be possible. The lawyers who owned the whole property were a firm in Lincoln’s Inn, he added, and Oleron ought to mention his name when he called on them.

Oleron was swamped with work that afternoon, so he made the trip to Lincoln’s Inn the next day. The lawyers tried immediately to push him into buying the entire house for fifty pounds down, with the rest of the purchase price left on mortgage. It took him half an hour to dissuade them, to make them understand he had no interest in owning the whole rotting building, he only wanted to rent the one first floor flat. The lawyers hemmed and hawed, claimed at first they had no authority to make that kind of arrangement, before finally caving—on three conditions: the lurching old To Let boards had to stay in place by the paling, no removal permitted; the second they found a tenant for the full house, Oleron’s lease would terminate automatically with no notice required, no questions asked. Oleron laughed off the risk of the whole ever house letting out, promised them his final decision within a week.

The next day he visited the house again, wandering all the way to the top floor, the floorboards creaking under his feet as if something inside the walls was shuffling silently out of his path. He went home giddy, ran a bath and daydreamed nonstop about the space. Scrubbed clean, repainted, filled with his grandmother’s old furniture, it would be perfect. He stopped by the storage warehouse to refresh his memory of the half-forgotten pieces, take measurements, then called on a local decorator. He was busy with a half-finished novel, frustrated that he’d stumbled on the house at such a busy time of year, but decided the fastest solution was to put all writing work on pause until he was fully moved in.

A fortnight later, every wall on his first floor was painted a soft, tender elderflower white, the paint fully cured. Oleron was halfway through moving in, light with giddy delight, rubbing his hands as he polished and arranged his grandmother’s belongings: the tall lattice-paned china cabinet holding his grandmother’s Derby, Mason, and Spode ware, the big folding Sheraton table, the low long bookshelves (he’d ordered two more exact copies to match), the carved dining chairs, the polished Sheffield candlesticks, the dented riveted rose-bowls. All set against the pale white panelled walls, moulded and coffered around the deep window recesses with a soft, quiet warmth no modern builder could ever replicate. The ceilings were lofty, faintly patterned with a faded old design of stars; even the tapered mouldings of his cast iron fireplace were carved as delicately as fine jewellery. Oleron drifted from room to room rubbing his hands, stopping every few steps just to savor the soft, glowing sight of one bright white room opening onto the next.

“Charming, absolutely charming,” he murmured to himself, grinning. “I can’t wait to show Elsie Bengough all this.” He fitted a heavy new bolt and a Yale lock to his entry door, sealing his first-floor quarters off completely from the rest of the empty, rotting house. Now, if he wanted to read in bed, his book was only a few steps away, no half mile walk needed. He thought over and over how absurdly lucky he was, to stumble on this perfect little flat for next to nothing. He hung a heavy hat-rack in his tiny square entry hall, strung up all his coats and hats, and late at night, passersby cutting through the quiet triangular square would glance up past the hunched, waiting line of wooden To Let boards, and see the warm glow of his red blinds glowing against the dark old brick, or the shadow of his candlestick moving as he drifted from room to room, still arranging his things, getting ready to go back to the writing work his move had interrupted. None of them could see the second, fainter shadow that stretched out behind his own, moving slow along the white panelling every time the candle flame dipped and flickered.
