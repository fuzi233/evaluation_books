*“Within the gentle heart Love shelters him,
As birds within the green shade of the grove.
Before the gentle heart, in Nature’s scheme,
Love was not, nor the gentle heart ere Love.”*
—Guido Guinizelli (Rossetti’s Translation)

In the quiet lanes of Pennicote, the news of Grandcourt’s death rippled through more than one home, stirring emotions long held in check. Among those affected was the Meyrick household, where Hans, ever the bearer of gossip and tidings, made it his habit to deliver the *Times* to his mother. Mrs. Meyrick, with her insatiable appetite for news—from the grand theater of politics to the intimate announcements of marriages—found a peculiar pleasure in the latter, as if skimming the endings of society’s unwritten novels.

This particular Wednesday, however, Hans arrived with more than the usual rustle of paper. Bursting into the parlor just as Mirah was concluding Mab’s singing lesson, he brandished the *Times* with uncharacteristic fervor, interrupting Mab’s earnest but imperfect rendition of *Lascia ch’io pianga*. The sudden noise startled Mirah, her fingers stilling on the piano keys, while Mab groaned, “Hans! Must you drown out my singing with something worse?”

“What on earth has happened?” Mrs. Meyrick demanded, her thoughts leaping to Deronda—always the unspoken concern in their little circle.

“Nothing dire,” Hans replied, though his tone carried a weight that belied his casual words. “Only that the Duke Alfonso has drowned, and his Duchess has been rescued—by none other than our friend Deronda, who happened to be in Genoa at the time.”

Mirah’s hands clenched in her lap, her breath catching. Mrs. Meyrick murmured sympathy for the widow, but Hans, ever irreverent, quipped, “A fortunate accident for the Duchess, wouldn’t you say? Now she’s free to marry a man with better hair and fewer freezing glances.”

Mirah stood abruptly, her dark eyes flashing. “How can you speak so lightly?” she demanded, her voice trembling. “Mr. Deronda would never call such a thing *luck*. You don’t know what pain you might be stirring—what if this brings sorrow to others?”

Hans faltered, his jest crumbling under her unexpected fury. “I—I take it back,” he stammered, flushing. “I was thoughtless.”

Mirah turned away, her anger cooling as swiftly as it had flared, but the sting remained. She resumed her seat at the piano, her back rigid, while Mab scolded Hans for his insensitivity.

When the lesson ended, Mirah excused herself, her usual warmth subdued. She could not meet Mrs. Meyrick’s eyes, ashamed of her outburst, but the older woman saw the turmoil beneath and drew her close. “God bless you, my dear,” she whispered, and Mirah felt the unspoken forgiveness.

Hans, chastened, offered to walk her home. She did not refuse.

Their steps were quiet, the tension between them unspoken but palpable. Hans, sensing the depth of her feelings, wrestled with his own realization: *She loves him. Not as a benefactor, but as a woman loves a man.* The thought settled heavily in his chest.

When they arrived, Mordecai greeted them with rare brightness, a letter clasped in his thin hands. “From Deronda,” he said, his voice alight. “He writes that he will return soon.”

Mirah’s heart twisted. She forced a smile and excused herself, retreating to her room where she could wrestle with her emotions in private.

Alone, she pressed her hands to her temples, as if to still the storm within. *This is jealousy,* she realized with a pang. The love she had nurtured so innocently had sharpened into something painful—something she had read of in stories but never imagined feeling herself.

Later, as she prepared tea, she moved with practiced calm, but her thoughts were a tempest. She had always known Deronda belonged to a world beyond hers—a world of grand houses and society, where she and Ezra were but passing shadows. But now, the possibility of him binding himself to *her*—Mrs. Grandcourt—felt like a door closing.

Mordecai, lost in his own joy, spoke of unity and sacrifice, of love that gives without possession. But Mirah, though she listened, could not share his lofty detachment.

“Women are framed for love that renounces,” he said softly.

Mirah shook her head. “Not always, Ezra. Sometimes love wants to be seen. Sometimes it wants to *matter*.”

He studied her, but she did not explain further.

That night, as she lay in bed, she whispered to the darkness, “I never meant to feel this. I never meant to want what I cannot have.”

But the heart, once awakened, does not ask permission.
