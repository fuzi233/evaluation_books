For moments following that dreadful laugh's final echo, which had vibrated through the cavernous stone room, Tars Tarkas and I stood frozen in a silence heavy with tension. Nothing moved within our line of sight; no sound broke the oppressive stillness.

Eventually, Tars Tarkas chuckled softly, a sound characteristic of his peculiar kin when confronted with the horrific or terrifying. It was not a hysterical cackle, but a genuine expression of the perverse pleasure they derive from scenes that drive Earthmen to loathing or despair.

I have witnessed them frequently rolling upon the ground in frantic fits of uncontrollable mirth while witnessing the death throes of women and children under the torture of that infernal green Martian spectacle known as the Great Games.

Looking up at the Thark, I allowed a smile to grace my own lips. In truth, here was a situation demanding a smiling face far more than a trembling chin.

"What do you make of it all?" I asked. "Where in the deuce are we?"

He regarded me with surprise.

"Where are we?" he repeated. "Do you tell me, John Carter, that you know not where you be?"

"That I am upon Barsoom is all I can guess," I admitted. "And but for you and the great white apes, I wouldn't even guess that, for the sights I've seen today are as alien to the Barsoom I knew ten years ago as they are to the world of my birth. No, Tars Tarkas, I truly know not where we be."

"Where have you been since you opened the mighty portals of the atmosphere plant years ago, after the keeper perished and the engines stalled, leaving all Barsoom to die of asphyxiation—or having already died? Your body was never found, though the men of a whole world searched for it for years. The Jeddak of Helium and his granddaughter, your princess, offered such fabulous rewards that even princes of royal blood joined the hunt.

"There was but one conclusion when all efforts failed: you had taken the long, final pilgrimage down the mysterious River Iss to await in the Valley Dor, upon the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus, the beautiful Dejah Thoris, your princess.

"Why you went, no one could guess, for your princess still lived—"

"Thank God!" I interrupted. "I dared not ask you, fearing I might be too late to save her. She was very low when I left her in the royal gardens of Tardos Mors that long-gone night; so low that I scarcely hoped to reach the atmosphere plant before her spirit fled forever. And she lives yet?"

"She lives, John Carter."

"You haven't told me where we are," I reminded him.

"We are where I expected to find you, John Carter—and another. Years ago, you heard the story of the woman who taught me the thing green Martians are bred to hate: love. You know the cruel tortures and awful death her love won for her at the hands of the beast, Tal Hajus.

"She, I thought, awaited me by the Lost Sea of Korus. You know that it was left for a man from another world—for yourself, John Carter—to teach this cruel Thark what friendship is; and you, I thought, also roamed the carefree Valley Dor.

"Thus were the two I most longed for at the end of the long pilgrimage I must someday take. As the time elapsed which Dejah Thoris had hoped might bring you back to her side (for she always believed you had only temporarily returned to your own planet), I finally yielded to my great yearning. A month ago, I started upon this journey, the end of which you have witnessed today. Do you understand now where you be, John Carter?"

"And that was the River Iss, emptying into the Lost Sea of Korus in the Valley Dor?" I asked.

"This is the valley of love, peace, and rest to which every Barsoomian since time immemorial has longed to pilgrimage at the end of a life of hate and strife and bloodshed," he replied solemnly. "This, John Carter, is Heaven."

His tone was cold and ironic; its bitterness reflected the terrible disappointment he had suffered. Such a fearful disillusionment, such a blasting of lifelong hopes and aspirations, such an uprooting of age-old tradition might have justified a vastly greater demonstration on the part of the Thark.

I laid my hand upon his shoulder.

"I am sorry," I said, finding no other words adequate.

"Think, John Carter, of the countless billions of Barsoomians who have taken this voluntary pilgrimage down this cruel river since the beginning of time, only to fall into the ferocious clutches of the terrible creatures that now assail us. There is an ancient legend that once a red man returned from the banks of the Lost Sea of Korus, returned from the Valley Dor, back through the mysterious River Iss. The legend says he narrated a fearful blasphemy of horrid brutes inhabiting a valley of wondrous loveliness—brutes that pounced upon each pilgrim as he ended his journey and devoured him on the banks of the Lost Sea where he sought love and peace. But the ancients killed the blasphemer, as tradition ordains that anyone returning from the bosom of the River of Mystery shall be slain.

"But now we know it was no blasphemy; the legend is true, and the man told only what he saw. Yet, what does it profit us, John Carter, since even if we escape, we would be treated as blasphemers? We are trapped between the wild *thoat* of certainty and the mad *zitidar* of fact—we can escape neither."

"As Earth men say, we are between the devil and the deep sea, Tars Tarkas," I replied, unable to suppress a smile at our dilemma.

"There is naught we can do but take things as they come. At least, we have the satisfaction of knowing that whoever slays us eventually will have far greater numbers of their own dead to count than they will gain in return. Whether white ape, plant-man, green Barsoomian, or red man, whosoever takes the last toll from us will know it is costly in lives to wipe out John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, and Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, at the same time."

I couldn't help laughing at his grim humor, and he joined me in one of those rare laughs of genuine enjoyment, a trait marking him apart from his fiercer kin.

"But about yourself, John Carter," he cried finally. "If you have not been here all these years, where indeed have you been, and how is it that I find you here today?"

"I have been back to Earth," I replied. "For ten long Earth years, I have prayed and hoped for the day that would carry me once more to this grim old planet of yours. For all its cruel and terrible customs, I feel a bond of sympathy and love for it greater than for the world that gave me birth.

"For ten years, I endured a living death of uncertainty and doubt regarding whether Dejah Thoris lived. Now, for the first time in all these years, my prayers are answered and my doubts relieved. Yet, through a cruel whim of fate, I find myself hurled into the one tiny spot of all Barsoom from which there appears to be no escape. If there were, it would be at a price extinguishing the last flickering hope I cling to of seeing my princess again in this life—and you have seen today with what pitiful futility man yearns toward a material hereafter.

"Only a bare half-hour before I saw you battling the plant-men, I was standing in the moonlight upon the banks of a broad river tapping the eastern shore of Earth's most blessed land. I have answered you, my friend. Do you believe?"

"I believe," replied Tars Tarkas, "though I cannot understand."

As we spoke, I scanned the interior of the chamber with my eyes. It was perhaps two hundred feet long and half as broad, hewn from the cliff material, appearing mostly dull gold in the dim light diffused by a single minute radium illuminator centered in the roof. Here and there, polished surfaces of ruby, emerald, and diamond patched the golden walls and ceiling. The floor was of another material, very hard, worn by much use to the smoothness of glass. Aside from the two doors, I discerned no other aperture. Knowing one was locked against us, I approached the other.

As I extended my hand to search for the controlling button, that cruel, mocking laugh rang out once more, so close that I involuntarily shrank back, tightening my grip on the hilt of my great sword.

Then, from the far corner of the great chamber, a hollow voice chanted: *"There is no hope, there is no hope; the dead return not, the dead return not; nor is there any resurrection. Hope not, for there is no hope."*

Our eyes instantly turned toward the source, but no one was visible. Cold shivers raced along my spine, and the short hairs at the base of my neck stiffened and rose, just as they do on a hound's neck when it sees uncanny things hidden from human sight.
