The young stranger allowed himself to be steered clear of the clattering carts and braying mules by Nello, whose lively chatter flowed as freely as the Arno in flood.

“To be honest,” the Greek said, once they had found smoother footing, “I’m not sorry to exchange my rag-selling patron for a man who speaks without chewing his words like a stale crust. Since when do peddlers of broken glass claim to traffic in lutes and blades?”

Nello’s laugh was bright as the glint of his razor. “Common? Ha! Our Bratti is as common as a unicorn. He’s a philosopher of haggling, squeezing every drop of pleasure—meaning profit—from life, then bargaining for a swift passage through purgatory by bequeathing his gains to Holy Church. I’ve told him, ‘Bratti, your arithmetic limps. What will you barter in Paradise? Saints don’t need rusty nails, and the only secondhand robes belong to poor flayed Bartolomeo.’ But forgive me—” He crossed himself abruptly. “Lorenzo lies cold today, and the Muses rend their hair—a grievous sight for a barber. And you, *Messere*, with your fine speech and threadbare lodging, wear misfortune like a shadow. What storm cast you ashore here?”

The stranger’s gaze sharpened. “Which Lorenzo do you speak of?”

“Which Lorenzo?” Nello spread his hands. “The sun of Florence! Lorenzo de’ Medici, whose death shakes the very lantern of the Duomo and sets the Marzocco lions gnashing their stone teeth. Our Pericles—if you’ll pardon the comparison in a Greek’s ear.”

The young man’s lips curved. “Why not? I doubt even Athens in her prime boasted a barber so learned.”

“Ah! I knew it.” Nello clapped his hands. “The lilt of your Italian betrays you—born under Italian skies, no? A Grecian sapling grafted onto our soil, like those Persian peaches in the Medici gardens. But time and travel have steeped you anew in the old dyes. And if I may guess—” He lowered his voice. “Those same Grecian hues, along with a few antique gems, are all the waves left you, eh? Well, when towers fall, the sparrows must flit. Lorenzo’s death makes Rome the wiser nest, but—”

“A certain Augustinian monk sang Florence’s praises,” the Greek admitted wryly. “‘The market of scholars,’ he called it.”

“*Gnaffè!* And so it remains,” Nello declared. “Lorenzo wasn’t our only patron. Bernardo Rucellai, Alamanno Rinuccini—but enough! A thousand years yawn until I can serve a scholar like you. First—” He brandished his razor. “This beard must go. Florentine maidens prefer lips unburied by brambles.”

The Greek touched his chin. “A dire sacrifice. Especially if your maids are as sweet as the little Tessa I kissed this morning.”

“Tessa?” Nello snorted. “A milkmaid’s hands are rough as her cheese. You’ll rise to ladies who scent their gloves with civet, not stable straw. But mark me—you must shed the look of a *sgherro*. Cultivate the air of a Pietro Crinito: sin among silks, not tavern dregs.”

“Then let the razor work,” the Greek conceded. “Though I’d keep these curls, if your Graces permit.”

“Like Pico in his prime!” Nello shepherded him toward the Piazza San Giovanni, where the Duomo’s vast dome loomed beside Giotto’s slender campanile. The Greek tilted his head, but his admiring gaze soon cooled to scrutiny.

Nello bristled. “You stare as if our cathedral were a cow byre! Has Constantinople finer marbles? Or a cupola to rival Brunelleschi’s?”

The young man laughed. “Spare the scimitar—I confess your stones are fair. But inside?” He shuddered. “Smoke-blackened Virgins, skeletal saints glaring like starved cats. Ghiberti’s gates show promise, though.”

“Promise?” Nello huffed. “Those doors are fifty years old! Our artists have studied the antique since—”

“—Since monks let them glimpse Olympus between penances,” the Greek finished. “A pity.”

Nello steered him firmly toward his shop, its sign proclaiming *Apollo and the Razor*. Inside, Sandro, the solemn assistant, mixed lavender-scented lather while the Greek eyed the inner sanctum—a snug retreat with a stone Hermes half-hidden among laurels.

“Your *eruditi* gather here?”

“Here, and amid the razors,” Nello said. “For inspiration strikes clearest with a smooth chin. Now—” He swathed the Greek in linen. “Consider this: Is Virgil spelled with *i* or *e*? You’ll find the answer comes swifter once the blade passes. Though if you favor *i*, keep it quiet until your fortune’s made—Florence clings to *e*.”

The Greek studied his shaven reflection in a Murano mirror. “You’ve stolen years from me. I’ll pass for a boy in doublet.”

“Nonsense!” Nello snipped a curl. “Poliziano lectured with less beard. Though—” He hesitated. “Your Greek blood may weigh against you. Some scholars call your learning weedy till transplanted to Italian minds.”

The stranger’s jaw tightened.

“Peace!” Nello raised his hands. “I but repeat what’s said. And between us—” He leaned close. “Change your stand on leaven, tweak your Doxology, and chairs might open for you. Ah, but here’s true counsel: Praise Cicero, smirk at Argiropulo’s blunders, and you’ll fare well. Now—” He dusted the Greek’s shoulders. “For patronage, try young Piero de’ Medici, though his tastes run to falconry over philosophy. Or Bernardo Rucellai, if you’ve gems to sell.”

The Greek drew a sardonyx Cleopatra from his doublet lining. “And this ring?” He twisted a gold band set with an emerald. “Know you an honest jeweler?”

Nello’s eyes gleamed. “Ah! Let me make introductions…”
