For several days, Tito saw little of Romola. The next morning, he approached her with a gentle demeanor, suggesting it would be prudent for her to clear away the smaller personal items from the library, as agents were arriving to pack the antiquities. Leaning in to kiss her forehead, he advised her to retreat to her own chamber, where the small painted tabernacle stood, keeping herself away from the clamor of unfamiliar footsteps. Romola assented quietly, offering no sign of emotion. The night had been a prolonged vigil for her; despite her robust constitution, sensation had dulled into a continuous, dull ache, as though she had been stunned and bruised. Tito sensed her illness but dared not speak of it. Seeing her hand and brow turned to ice, he merely fetched a fur-lined mantle and draped it lightly over her shoulders. In every brief interval he returned, the scene remained unchanged: he attempted to soothe her with unobtrusive acts of tenderness, yet she seemed to have lost the capacity to speak to him or even to look at him.

"Patience!" he murmured to himself. "She will recover, and forgive at last. The bond between us must remain the strongest." When a struck person lies slow to rise and appears untouched, the striker often slides effortlessly into the role of the aggrieved party, feeling no pain himself while acutely aware of his own benevolence since delivering the blow. Yet Tito was not naturally inclined to feel aggrieved; his mind bent constantly toward reconciliation. He would have endured much just to feel Romola's hand rest upon his head again, as it had that morning when he first recoiled from gazing at her.

He found it easier to wait patiently for the return of domestic happiness because his life outside the walls grew increasingly captivating. While a course of action is often the slow outgrowth of an entire character, it usually traces back to a single impression as its apparent origin. Since the moment in the Piazza del Duomo, when Tito, mounted on bales, had tasted the keen pleasure of pleasing the ears of men with any phrase that suited them, his imagination had drifted toward a form of political activity that Florence's turbulent public life might soon demand. However, a fresh dread of Baldassarre, awakened at that same instant, had lain like an immovable rock across that path, urging him to sell the library as preparation for fleeing Florence at the very moment he began to feel a new attraction to the city. That dread was nearly lifted now. He must still wear his armor, preparing himself for demands on his coolness and ingenuity, but he no longer felt compelled to take the inconvenient step of leaving Florence to seek new fortunes. His father had refused the offered atonement, forcing him into defiance; an old man in a strange land, with his memory gone, was too weak to defy such resolve.

Tito's implicit desires were now unfolding into explicit thoughts. As the freshness of young passion faded, life took on for him the aspect of a game blending skill and chance. The game Florence promised was rapid and exciting—a struggle of revolution and party, certain to include ample unavowed action where brilliant ingenuity, discarding all inconvenient beliefs save that "ginger is hot in the mouth," sees the path of superior wisdom.

No sooner had the French guests departed than Florence became as agitated as a colony of ants when an alarming shadow is removed and the camp must be hastily repaired. "How shall we raise funds for the French king? How manage the war with the obstinate Pisan rebels? Above all, how mend our plan of government to ensure magistrates are chosen and laws voted correctly?" Until these questions were answered, trade faced stagnation, and the large body of working men, excluded from citizenship and lacking even a vote to soothe their stomachs, were likely to grow impatient. Something had to be done.

First, the great bell rang, summoning the citizens to a parliament in the Piazza de' Signori. When the crowd was wedged tight and hemmed in by armed men at every outlet, the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and the eight Priors—emerged and stood by the stone lion on the platform before the Old Palace. They proposed that twenty chief men of the city be granted dictatorial authority for one year, empowered to choose all magistrates and set the government in order. The people shouted their assent, feeling themselves the electors of the Twenty. This kind of "parliament" was an old Florentine fashion, designed to make the will of the few appear as the choice of the many.

The shouting in the square soon ceased, but the debate inside the palace continued. Was Florence to adopt a Great Council in the Venetian mode, where all officers and laws were elected by a wide number of qualified citizens regardless of rank or party? Or was it to be governed by a narrower scheme, preserving the hereditary influence of good families against the dilution of shopkeepers' votes? Doctors of law disputed day after day, far into the night. Messer Pagolantonio Soderini argued excellently for the popular scheme; Messer Guidantonio Vespucci countered with equally excellent reasons for a more aristocratic form. It was a question of boiled or roast meat, prejudged by the palates of the disputants; their excellent arguing might have been prolonged indefinitely without more result than deferring the cooking. The majority inside the palace, holding power already, sided with Vespucci, believing change should be moderate. The majority outside, conscious of little power and many grievances, feared change less.

Outside the palace, a force was gradually giving the vague desires of that majority the character of a determinate will: the preaching of Savonarola. Impelled partly by a spiritual necessity to guide the people and partly by public men who could carry no measures without his aid, he rapidly shifted in his daily sermons from the general to the specific. He moved from urging hearers to postpone private passions for the public good, to telling them precisely what government they must have to achieve it—from "Choose whatever is best for all" to "Choose the Great Council," and "The Great Council is the will of God."

To Savonarola, these were identical propositions. The Great Council was the only practical plan to express a public will large enough to counteract the corrupting influence of party interests; it was a plan that could make honest, impartial public action possible. The purer Florence's government became—the more secure from those who profited from the moral debasement of their fellows—the closer the Florentine people would approach the character of a pure community, worthy to lead the renovation of the Church and the world. Fra Girolamo's mind never stopped short of that sublimest end; the objects toward which he worked always possessed the same moral magnificence. He harbored no private malice and sought no petty gratification. Even in the last terrible days, when ignominy, torture, and the fear of torture laid bare every hidden weakness of his soul, he could tell his importunate judges: "Do not wonder if it seems to you that I have told but few things; for my purposes were few and great."
