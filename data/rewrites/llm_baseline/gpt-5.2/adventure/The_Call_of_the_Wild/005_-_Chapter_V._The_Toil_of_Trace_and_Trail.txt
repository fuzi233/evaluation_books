Thirty days out of Dawson the Salt Water Mail dragged itself into Skaguay with Buck and his mates still in the lead, and the town met them the way a kitchen meets the end of a long shift: with the sour tang of sweat, wet wool, and old fish clinging to everything, and with eyes that could tell at a glance what the body could not admit.

They were used up. Buck, who had once carried one hundred and forty pounds as if it were a proud coat, had boiled down to one hundred and fifteen, his frame pared as clean as a stock after a hard simmer—no fat left to soften the edges. The others, lighter to begin with, had been reduced even more cruelly. Pike, who had made a life’s art of limping to avoid a pull, limped now because the joint truly burned. Sol-leks favored a leg in the dumb, loyal way he did all things. Dub’s shoulder-blade was wrenched and never set right, a torn hinge in a door that still must open.

Worst of all were their feet. There was no spring in them, no rebound—only the heavy, jarring fall of pads bruised into tenderness, each step a dull thud through bone and sinew. It wasn’t the clean exhaustion of a sprint that sleep can cure like a hot broth. It was the slow emptying-out of months, the sort of fatigue that drains not only muscle but the very wish to lift muscle again. In less than five months they had eaten up twenty-five hundred miles of trail, and in the last eighteen hundred they had been granted only five days’ rest. They came down Skaguay’s main street as if the traces were made of lead and their bodies of ash. On down grades they did not so much run as simply try not to be ground under the sled.

“Mush on, poor sore feets,” their driver coaxed, the old phrase tasting in the air like something said over a pot to keep it from sticking. “Dis is de las’. Den we get one long res’. Eh? For sure. One bully long res’.”

The men who drove them believed it. They had earned it. Any fair reckoning would have allowed loafing after twelve hundred miles with two days’ rest. But the mail had swollen like dough left too long near the stove—too many stamped envelopes, too many bundles for sweethearts and wives and kin who had stayed behind while men poured north. Orders came down with the same chill authority as winter itself: fresh Hudson Bay dogs were to replace the trail-worn; the worn were to be disposed of. Dogs counted for little against dollars. Even the language of it—worthless—was a knife: a dominant word that could cut life down to a ledger-mark.

Three days passed, and in those days Buck and the team learned how close to the end they truly were. On the morning of the fourth day two men from the States bought them harness and all, cheaply enough that the deal smelled of bargain more than justice. Buck watched the chaffering, saw money flash from hand to hand, and understood—without any word being spoken to him—that the Scotch half-breed and the mail-train drivers were slipping out of his life the way Perrault and François had done before.

The men called each other Hal and Charles. Charles was middle-aged, pale, with watery eyes and a mustache twisted up fiercely as if it could lend backbone to the weak lip beneath. Hal was nineteen or twenty and made noise without meaning: a big Colt’s revolver on his hip, a hunting-knife, a belt bristling with cartridges like a rack of shiny utensils in a kitchen owned by someone who couldn’t yet cook. The belt announced him—callow, loud, convinced the trappings were the craft.

At their camp Buck saw disorder the way a seasoned animal sees it the way a seasoned cook does: instantly, in the placement of things, in the waste, in the sourness beginning. The tent was only half stretched. Dishes lay unwashed. Bundles were scattered like ingredients left out to spoil. And there was a woman—Mercedes—Charles’s wife and Hal’s sister. The three of them made a family party, and for all their talk they moved like people who had never learned the humble grammar of work.

They set about taking down the tent and loading the sled with great effort and no method. The canvas was rolled into an awkward bundle three times the size it should have been, like dough overhandled until it toughens. Tin dishes were packed away still crusted, old grease and cold porridge smells clinging to them. Mercedes fluttered in and out of their way, chattering advice as incessantly as boiling water. When a clothes-sack was put at the front she insisted it belonged on the back; when it was moved to the back and covered over, she remembered something that “could abide nowhere else” but in that very sack, and the whole load was undone and done again.

Three men from a neighboring tent stepped out to watch. They grinned and winked at one another the way old hands do when they see a beginner about to burn the stew.

“You’ve got a right smart load as it is,” one said. “An’ it’s not me should tell you your business, but I wouldn’t tote that tent along if I was you.”

“Undreamed of!” Mercedes cried, hands up in dainty dismay. “However in the world could I manage without a tent?”

“It’s springtime,” the man answered. “You won’t get any more cold weather.”

She shook her head, and Charles and Hal piled on the last odds and ends until the sled looked like a pantry overturned and lashed down with hope.

“Think it’ll ride?” another asked.

“Why shouldn’t it?” Charles snapped.

“Oh, that’s all right,” the man soothed, backing away from offense. “Just a-wonderin’. Seems a mite top-heavy.”

Charles turned his back and drew the lashings down as best he could, which was to say poorly, the knots like bad stitching that will not hold when strain comes.

“And of course the dogs can hike along all day with that contraption behind them,” a second onlooker said, voice dry as smoke-cured fish.

“Certainly,” Hal replied with a politeness so cold it rang. He took the gee-pole and swung his whip. “Mush!” he shouted. “Mush on there!”

The dogs sprang against their breast-bands, strained until their lungs sawed at the air, then relaxed. The sled did not move. They could not shift it an inch.

“The lazy brutes, I’ll show them,” Hal cried, raising the whip.

Mercedes snatched it, a quick, pretty gesture like brushing flour from a sleeve. “Oh, Hal, you mustn’t. The poor dears! Promise you won’t be harsh with them for the rest of the trip, or I won’t go a step.”

“Precious lot you know about dogs,” Hal sneered. “They’re lazy. You’ve got to whip them. That’s their way. Ask any one.”

Mercedes turned her face to the men, imploring, her repugnance to pain plain as a white cloth. Their answer came blunt as an axe.

“They’re weak as water,” one said. “Plum tuckered out. They need rest.”

“Rest be blanked,” Hal snapped, and Mercedes flinched as if the oath itself were a blow.

But she was clannish. She whirled instantly to defend her brother against the stranger’s voice. “Never mind that man. You’re driving our dogs. Do what you think best.”

Hal’s whip fell. The team heaved themselves low, claws biting packed snow, shoulders shaking with the effort. The sled held fast as if rooted. After two tries they stood panting, and Hal’s whip whistled again—until Mercedes dropped to her knees before Buck and put her arms around his neck, tears in her eyes.

“You poor, poor dears,” she sobbed. “Why don’t you pull hard? Then you wouldn’t be whipped.”

Buck did not like her. Her hands smelled of perfumed soap and worry, not of trail-smoke and competent labor. But misery makes even dislike a thin thing; he endured the embrace as one endures a badly fitted harness.

One of the onlookers, jaw clenched, finally spoke, voice sharp with the effort not to curse.

“It’s not that I care a whoop what becomes of you,” he said, “but for the dogs’ sakes—break out that sled. The runners are froze fast. Throw your weight against the gee-pole, right and left. Break it out.”

A third attempt was made. Hal worked the pole as directed, and the runners cracked free from the snow with a sound like a crust giving way. The overloaded sled lurched forward. Buck and the others threw themselves into the pull, frantic under the rain of blows. A hundred yards ahead the trail turned and pitched steeply into the main street. It would have taken an experienced man to keep that top-heavy load upright, and Hal was not such a man.

On the turn the sled went over. Loose lashings spilled half the load. The dogs did not stop. Lightened, angered by the unjust burden and the club and whip, they surged forward. Buck, rage burning through weakness, broke into a run, the team following his lead. Hal shouted “Whoa! Whoa!” but he was hauled off his feet. The capsized sled ground over him as it skated on its side, and the dogs dashed up the street, scattering pots and blankets and sacks along Skaguay’s chief thoroughfare as if the town itself were to be seeded with their owners’ foolishness.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the dogs and gathered the scattered belongings. They offered advice the way elders offer it at a table where a young cook has ruined a pot: without much tenderness, because tenderness doesn’t fix hunger.

“Half the load and twice the dogs,” they said, “if you ever expect to reach Dawson.”

Hal and Charles and Mercedes listened as if the words were an insult. They pitched the tent again and began to overhaul the outfit. Canned goods were turned out that made men laugh—canned goods on the Long Trail, as absurd as carrying a piano into a blizzard. One man shook his head and said, “Blankets for a hotel. Half as many’s too much. Throw away that tent, and all those dishes—who’s going to wash them anyway? Good Lord, do you think you’re travelling on a Pullman?”

So began the ruthless cutting down—the inexorable elimination of the superfluous. Mercedes cried when her clothes-bags were dumped onto the ground. She cried over each discarded thing as if every object held a word of her past that could not be spoken again once it was thrown away. She rocked with her hands around her knees and vowed she would not go an inch, not for a dozen Charleses. She appealed to everyone and everything, then wiped her eyes and began, with sudden fierce zeal, to throw away even what a wiser woman would have called necessity. When she finished with her own things she attacked her husband’s and brother’s belongings and stripped them like a tornado.

Even halved, the outfit remained bulky. That evening Charles and Hal went out and bought six Outside dogs. Added to the original six and Teek and Koona—the huskies gained at the Rink Rapids—this made fourteen. Hal and Charles glowed with pride at the number; it was showy, like laying out a feast of tins and wrappers instead of real food. They had watched other sleds come and go, but never had they seen one harnessed to fourteen dogs. There was a reason for that, a simple arithmetic that any food historian would have smiled at bitterly: one sled cannot carry food for fourteen mouths for the length of the trail. But Hal and Charles did not know the old northern wisdom that lives not in books but in bodies. They had “worked the trip out” with a pencil—so much to a dog, so many dogs, so many days, Q.E.D.—and Mercedes nodded over their shoulders as if it were a recipe any fool could follow.

The next morning Buck led the long team up the street. There was no snap in him now, no eagerness, no proud lift. They were starting dead weary. Buck had traveled between Salt Water and Dawson four times already. The knowledge that he faced that same trail again, jaded and thin, made him bitter. His heart was not in the work, and no dog’s heart was. The Outside dogs were timid and frightened, their bodies untrained to the hard economy of the North; the Inside dogs had no confidence in their new masters.

Buck felt, in the dim, practical way he felt most truths now, that these two men and the woman could not be depended upon. They did not know how to do anything. As days passed it became clear they could not learn. They were slack in all things, without order or discipline. Half the night went to pitching a slovenly camp. Half the morning went to breaking it and loading the sled so badly that all day they were stopping to rearrange bundles. Some days they did not make ten miles. Other days they could not get started at all. And on no day did they make more than half the distance their pencil reckoning required.

It was inevitable they would go short on dog-food, but they hastened it with their ignorance. They overfed at first, bringing the famine sooner. The Outside dogs, not trained by months of hunger to make a mouthful last, ate with frantic appetite. When the worn huskies pulled weakly, Hal decided the orthodox ration was too small and doubled it. And when Mercedes, tears shining, could not cajole him into giving even more, she stole from the fish-sacks and fed the dogs slyly, as if kindness could be measured in handfuls and not in the far rarer currency of rest. But it was rest Buck and the huskies needed, not extra mouthfuls. The heavy load kept draining them, no matter what went down their throats.

Then the underfeeding came, as it always does when people mistake a calculation for a living world. Hal woke one day and saw the truth: half the dog-food was gone and only a quarter of the trail covered; and no more dog-food could be bought for love or money. So he cut even the orthodox ration and tried to increase the day’s travel. Charles and Mercedes seconded him with anxious voices and useless hands. It was easy to give the dogs less. It was impossible to make them go faster under a load still too heavy, with masters who could not rise early or work cleanly. Not only did they not know how to work dogs, they did not know how to work themselves.

Dub went first. Poor, blundering thief who was always getting caught and punished, he had been faithful in the traces. His wrenched shoulder, never treated, never given rest, went from bad to worse until Hal shot him with the big Colt’s revolver. The sound cracked across the spring air—an ugly, blunt noise that did not belong among the trickle of thaw-water and the returning birds. It was the sort of violent note that industrial life makes—sudden, careless, and deaf to the living soundscape around it.

They said in the country that an Outside dog starves on the ration of a husky. The six Outside dogs under Buck did no less than die on half the ration of a husky. The Newfoundland went first, then the three short-haired pointers. The two mongrels, with no spirit to break, clung longer, but they too went in the end.

By then the Southland gentlenesses had fallen away from the three people like frosting melting off a cake left in the sun. Arctic travel, stripped of its romance, became a reality too harsh for their manhood and womanhood. Mercedes stopped weeping over the dogs; she was too busy weeping over herself and quarrelling with her husband and brother. Quarrelling was the one thing they were never too weary to do. Their irritability rose out of misery, doubled with it, outdistanced it. They did not learn the wonderful patience the trail can teach to those who toil and suffer and yet keep sweet speech. They had no inkling of such a dialect of endurance. Their muscles ached, their bones ached, their hearts ached, and sharp words came first in the morning and last at night.

Charles and Hal wrangled whenever Mercedes gave them the smallest opening. Each believed he did more than his share, and neither hesitated to announce it. Mercedes, shifting alliances between husband and brother, made a beautiful and unending family quarrel. A dispute over who should chop a few sticks for fire would swell grotesquely—fathers and mothers dragged in, uncles, cousins, dead relatives, people thousands of miles away, as if old grievances were canned and opened now to spill their brine into the present. Hal’s opinions on art, or the society plays his mother’s brother wrote, somehow became relevant to the chopping of a Yukon fire. Charles’s political prejudices became proof of his laziness. Mercedes found Charles’s sister’s “tale-bearing tongue” relevant to building a camp. While they fought, the fire stayed unbuilt, the tent half pitched, the dogs unfed.

Mercedes nursed a special grievance—the grievance of sex. She was pretty and soft and had been treated chivalrously all her days. Here her husband and brother were everything but chivalrous, and because helplessness had been her accepted role, their complaints struck at what she believed her right. She made their lives unendurable. She stopped considering the dogs at all and, because she was sore and tired, insisted on riding the sled. Pretty and soft, she still weighed one hundred and twenty pounds—an added sack of grain on a starving team. She rode for days until the dogs fell in the traces and the sled stood still. Charles and Hal begged her to walk, pleaded, entreated, and she answered with tears and prayers that told Heaven of their brutality.

Once they took her off the sled by main strength. She went limp like a spoiled child and sat down in the trail. They traveled three miles without her. She did not move. They unloaded, came back, and by main strength put her on again. They never tried it a second time.

In the excess of their own misery they became callous to the suffering of their animals. Hal’s theory was that one must get hardened. He had preached it at first to his sister and brother-in-law; failing there, he hammered it into the dogs with a club. At the Five Fingers the dog-food gave out entirely. A toothless old squaw offered to trade a few pounds of frozen horse-hide for Hal’s Colt’s revolver—the same revolver that had taken Dub. Hal gave it up. The hide was a poor substitute, stripped from starved horses months before. Frozen, it was like galvanized iron. When a dog worried it into his stomach it thawed into thin, innutritious leather strings and a mass of short hair—irritating, indigestible, a mockery of sustenance. It filled the mouth but did not feed the body, like a word from a dominant tongue that names hunger without knowing its taste.

Through it all Buck staggered on at the head of the team as in a nightmare. He pulled when he could; when he could not, he fell and lay until whip or club drove him up again. The stiffness and gloss had gone from his coat. The hair hung limp and draggled, or clotted with dried blood where the club had bruised him. His muscles had wasted to knotty strings; the flesh pads were gone so each rib and bone stood out clean beneath loose hide wrinkled in folds of emptiness. It was heartbreaking—only Buck’s heart was unbreakable. The man in the red sweater had proved that long ago.

As it was with Buck, so it was with his mates. They were perambulating skeletons. In their misery they had grown dull to pain. The lash and the club were distant sensations, like sounds heard through thick walls. Their eyes saw dimly; their ears heard dimly. They were not half living. They were bags of bones with faint sparks fluttering. When a halt came they dropped in the traces like dead dogs and the spark seemed to gutter out. When the club fell, it flickered up just enough to make them totter to their feet and stagger on.

A day came when Billee, good-natured to the last, fell and could not rise. Hal had traded away the revolver, so he took the axe and knocked Billee on the head where he lay in the traces, then cut the carcass out of the harness and dragged it aside. Buck saw it. The others saw it. They knew the circle was closing.

The next day Koona went. Then only five remained besides Buck: Joe, too far gone to be malignant; Pike, crippled and limping, no longer conscious enough to malinger; Sol-leks, one-eyed, still faithful, mournful because he had so little strength left to give; Teek, who had not traveled so far that winter and so was beaten more because he was fresher; and Buck, still in lead, but no longer enforcing discipline, blind with weakness half the time, keeping the trail by its loom and by the dim feel of his feet.

It was beautiful spring weather, but neither dogs nor humans had room in themselves to notice it. Each day the sun rose earlier and set later; dawn came by three in the morning and twilight lingered until nine. The long day blazed. The ghostly winter silence had given way to the great spring murmur of awakening life, and that murmur came up from all the land like music returning to a house after a long illness. Sap rose in the pines. Willows and aspens burst into young buds. Shrubs and vines dressed themselves in fresh green. Crickets sang at night; in the day creeping, crawling things rustled out into the sun. Partridges boomed and woodpeckers knocked in the forest. Squirrels chattered. Birds sang. Overhead, wild-fowl honked north in cunning wedges that split the air.

From every hill slope trickled running water, the music of unseen fountains. All things thawed—bending, snapping, letting go. The Yukon strained to break loose the ice that bound it; water gnawed from beneath while sun ate from above. Air-holes formed. Fissures sprang and spread. Thin sections fell bodily into the river. And amid that bursting, rending, throbbing of life returning—under blazing sun and through soft-sighing breezes—staggered, like wayfarers to death, the two men, the woman, and the huskies.
