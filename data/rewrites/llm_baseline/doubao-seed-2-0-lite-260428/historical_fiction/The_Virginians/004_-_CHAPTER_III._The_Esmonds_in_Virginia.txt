Colonel Henry Esmond, a veteran who had earned his rank fighting for Queen Anne through all her continental wars, found himself caught up in the final, failed Jacobite plots to restore the Stuart line to the British throne when the queen died. The nation had set its heart on the new Hanoverian dynasty, and those few loyalists who opposed King George were forced to flee the three kingdoms for safety. Among them, Esmond’s friends soon counselled him to go abroad, though his sincere regret for his part in the uprising, paired with the new king’s famously forgiving nature, meant it was not long before they secured him a full pardon.

As has long been documented, Esmond belonged to the noble house that took its title from Castlewood in Hampshire. It was common knowledge that James II and his exiled son had twice offered him and his father the rank of Marquis, and that he could have claimed the hereditary Irish peerage of his line had he bothered to correct a trivial legal informality in the family papers. Tired to the bone of decades of political strife, and worn thin by messy, lingering family disputes back in Europe, he chose to settle far across the Atlantic, where he took possession of a vast plantation granted to one of his ancestors by King Charles I nearly a century prior. Here, he built his new home, which he named Castlewood, after the old Hampshire patrimony he would never see again. His daughter was born here, and his two grandsons; it was also here that his wife died. The lady who shared his Virginia home had been, in her first marriage, the widow of his kinsman, the ill-fated Viscount Castlewood, killed in a notorious duel with Lord Mohun in the final years of King William’s reign.

Every custom of Virginia in that era was modelled lovingly after the old English ways. It was a fiercely loyal colony, so proud of its early support of the exiled Charles II that it boasted it had been a kingdom of the Stuarts years before the Restoration brought him back to the English throne. The Anglican church and the Crown were honoured side by side, without question. The planter gentry were connected by blood or marriage to some of the finest old English families, and held their heads high above the sharp Dutch traders of New York, and the thrifty, money-obsessed Roundhead descendants of Pennsylvania and New England. No people on the entire continent were less republican than this great province, which would soon step to the forefront of the historic revolt against the British Crown a generation later.

The great Virginia estates ran on a rhythm almost patriarchal in its simplicity. For the rough, fertile work of the tobacco fields, every plantation had scores of hands: indentured assigned servants shipped over from Europe, and generations of enslaved African people, all bound to obey the master of the house. The land gave them all their food, their livestock, their game. The wide rivers teemed with fish for the taking, and the private wharves jutting out into the James or Potomac saw ships come right up to the plantation banks to take on tobacco, the only cash crop the gentry deigned to cultivate, bound for London or Bristol. Those same ships brought back every fine manufactured good, every bolt of English cloth, every luxury trinket the family could want, in direct exchange for their harvest.

Their hospitality was boundless. No stranger who knocked at their gates was ever turned away empty-handed, or sent to find lodging elsewhere. The gentry travelled between each other’s estates for weeks at a time, in a state of ease that verged on the feudal. The great moral conflict over chattel slavery had not yet been born in the era we recount: no Virginia gentleman felt the least moral shock at owning black servants, and the system, for all its inherent cruelty, was rarely administered with deliberate brutality. Food was plentiful on the great plantations; the enslaved people were by and large easygoing, and lived lives far more comfortable than the poorest labourers back in the British Isles. You might as soon have tried to convince Madam Esmond of Castlewood to let all her prize horses run loose, unfed and unbroken, as to argue for negro emancipation. She was fully convinced that the whip and the corn bag were equally good for both her stable stock and her black household.

Her father, for all his quiet scepticism about most established pieties, never pushed the point. His doubts never broke out into open rebellion; he was disaffected with much of the world, but never angry at it. A great old grief in his past had left a faint, permanent tinge of melancholy over every part of his later life. He was not unhappy: he was the kindest, most affectionate of men to everyone around him, gentle and even yielding to the women of his family, whom he almost never contradicted. But some old bankruptcy of the heart had left his spirit too tired to fight for joy, and he submitted to life far more than he ever revelled in it. He was never in better, lighter spirits than in his final hours, when he knew he would soon lay his burdens down.

When his wife died, his daughter took over the full management of his affairs, and he handed them over to her without a single complaint. So long as he had his books and his quiet afternoons, he wanted nothing more. When guests arrived at Castlewood he played the gracious, generous host, with a dry, pleasant, sarcastic wit that charmed every visitor. He was never the least bit sorry when they rode away.
“My dear, I shan’t be sorry to go myself,” he told his daughter once, “and you, for all the love you bear me, will console yourself after a while. Why should a man my age be romantic? You still are young enough for that, after all.” He spoke more lightly than he meant, for the lady he addressed was a thoroughly practical, matter-of-fact little woman, with barely a spark of romance in her entire nature.

Fifteen years after they first settled at Castlewood, the plantation’s profits had grown so large that Esmond easily agreed to his daughter’s plan to build a grand, permanent new stone mansion to replace the plain little wooden house he had been perfectly content to live in, a home worthy of the noble name their heirs would inherit. Several of her neighbouring planters had already built great handsome houses of their own, and Madam Warrington – as she was then known – was determined to take her rightful place at the very top of colonial society. Colonel Esmond cared not a fig for fine lodgings or aristocratic quarterings, but his daughter held the antiquity and merit of the Esmond line in the highest possible regard. In his serene, gentle old age, he humoured all her little peculiarities with easy, teasing good nature, using his own considerable skill as a painter and his deep stores of antiquarian knowledge to help her.

A hundred years ago, a working knowledge of heraldry was a standard part of the education of every noble gentleman and lady. On a long tour of Europe in her youth, Miss Esmond had pored eagerly over every scrap of family history and pedigree she could find, returning to Virginia with a hoard of old documents about the Esmond line that she accepted as unassailable fact, plus all the grand new heraldry volumes just published in France and England. These, to her complete satisfaction, proved the Esmonds were descended not just from the Norman warriors who sailed to England with William the Conqueror, but from ancient English royalty itself. Two magnificent hand-painted heraldic trees, executed by the Colonel’s own brush, showed the family springing in one line from the Emperor Charlemagne, painted in full gilded plate armour, imperial mantle and jewelled diadem, and in the other from Queen Boadicea, whom the Colonel insisted on rendering in the light, furlined costume of an ancient British warrior queen, with a massive gilded crown, a tiny fur mantle, and a handsome, symmetrical figure tastefully tattooed all over in bright woad blue. These two illustrious lines merged somewhere in the 13th century, on the person of the fortunate Esmond ancestor who could claim descent from both.

Good Madam Rachel thought very little of the Warrington family she had married into. She wrote her name Esmond Warrington, but after her father’s death, when she took full rule of the Castlewood estate, everyone in the colony called her Madam Esmond of Castlewood. She did not scruple to get into petty quarrels over precedence at the Governor’s assemblies in Jamestown, so determined was she to assert her rank. Even though her father had burned and disowned the Jacobite patent that would have made him a Marquis, she acted at all times as if that document was still fully in force. She considered the English branch of the Esmond family to be of far lower dignity than her own Virginian line, and openly asserted her superiority over every other member of the colonial aristocracy.

What use is recalling those old trivial squabbles? The people who fought them are long beyond the reach of petty disputes, and the new republic that would rise after Independence would sweep all those old social inequalities away. Before the Revolution, though, there was no more fiercely aristocratic society in the whole world than Virginia. Which means that the two little boys whose story we tell – the Esmond twins of Castlewood – were raised to honour every old English tradition, and were two of the most loyal, devoted little subjects the rightful British king ever had.

When the twins’ grandfather died, their mother proclaimed the elder, George, her formal successor and heir to the entire estate, in great state. Harry, his younger brother by exactly half an hour, was instructed by everyone in the household to honour and defer to his senior. The entire household followed suit: the large, happy extended family of enslaved people, the indentured servants from Europe, every soul on the plantation was taught to treat George as their future master. The only dissenters in the whole household were Madam Mountain, Madam Esmond’s oldest friend and companion, and Harry’s negro foster mother, who could never, for the life of her, understand why her boy should not be the heir – she swore constantly that Harry was handsomer, stronger, and far cleverer than George, though in truth the two boys were so nearly identical in height, strength, and beauty that no one but their mother could reliably tell them apart, save for the faint difference in the shade of their hair. When they were tucked into bed under those enormous, ribbon-trimmed nightcaps all children of the era wore, not even the most sharp-eyed visitor could distinguish one from the other.

For all their identical faces, their temperaments were wildly different. The elder twin was quiet, peaceful, studious, and reserved. The younger was loud, boisterous, and born with an unmistakable martial streak. Harry could learn anything very quickly once he set his mind to it, but he would drag his heels for days before he settled to his lessons. No threat of the ferule could ever make Harry study when he was in an idle mood, and no amount of scolding could stop George from sneaking time to help his brother catch up on his work. Harry loved nothing more than to drill the little enslaved boys on the plantation like a corporal, cane them for misbehaviour, and challenge them to boxing matches – he never bore a grudge for a second if he lost the fight. George, meanwhile, almost never struck anyone, and was gentle to every creature on the estate.

As was the custom, each of the boys was assigned a special little personal servant of their own. It became famous around the plantation that once, George found his small black assigned servant fast asleep on his own bed, and instead of waking him, sat down beside him and fanned the flies off the child with a feather fan. Old Gumbo, the boy’s father, was horrified when he stumbled on the scene, and Madam Esmond was furious, ordering the young negro off to the overseer to be whipped. George begged, pleaded, burst into passionate tears, and implored her to reverse the sentence, but she was inflexible. A fierce, blazing quarrel between mother and son followed. George refused to be pacified. He said the punishment was a shame – that he was the boy’s master, and no one, not even his own mother, had any right to touch him without his say so. He swore, so fiercely it shocked his usually gentle mother, that the very day he came of age, he would set young Gumbo free. Then he ran off to the slave quarters to visit the boy mid-punishment, and gave him his own favourite wooden toy.

The old Colonel had laughed when Madam Esmond told him of the incident, and only shrugged when George turned on him and said, “You let mamma rule you in everything, grandpapa.”
“Why, so I do,” the old man smiled. “Rachel, my love, it’s so plain even this baby has noticed I’m thoroughly petticoat-ridden.”
“Then why don’t you stand up like a man?” little Harry put in, always ready to back his brother.
Grandpapa looked at him, his eyes crinkling with amusement.
“Because I like sitting down best, my dear. I’m an old gentleman, and standing up fatigues me.”

Harry was the grandfather’s favourite, for his sharp, impish drollery and his love of many of the old man’s own pastimes. He would chatter and laugh away the whole of an afternoon with the old Colonel, while quiet George rarely said more than a few words to him. George, for his part, came alive in the library, where his brother would fidget and grow gloomy after ten minutes. He could recognise every book on the shelves before he was even big enough to carry one, and spent hours poring over their pages long before he could properly read or understand the words. Harry, by contrast, was never happier than when he was out in the stables or the woods, desperate to join every hunt or fishing trip, and showing all the signs of becoming an expert sportsman from toddlerhood.

When the boys were still small, their grandfather’s ship was preparing to sail for England, and Captain Franks asked them what presents he ought to bring back for them. George hesitated between a set of new books and a fiddle. Harry, without a second’s pause, declared he wanted a real little working gun. Madam Warrington was upset that her elder son could want something so trivial and low-bred as a fiddle, and applauded Harry’s choice as far more worthy of his noble name and lineage.
“Books, papa, I will admit are a respectable choice,” she argued to her father, “though I am sure you have nearly every book in the world already. But I never can – I may be wrong, but I never can – bring myself to wish that my son, the grandson of the Marquis of Esmond, should be a fiddler.”
“Should be a fiddlestick, my dear,” the old Colonel chuckled. “Remember that Heaven’s ways are not our own. Every soul born into the world has a little private kingdom of thought all its own, and it is a sin for any of us to invade it. If George loves music, you can no more stop him than you can order a rose not to smell sweet, or a bird not to sing.”
“A bird sings by nature,” she tossed her head. “George did not come into the world with a fiddle tucked in his cradle. I hated the harpsichord when I was a little chit at school in Kensington, and only learned it to please my own mamma. Say what you will, dear sir, I cannot believe that fiddling is proper work for a person of quality.”
“And King David played the harp, my dear?”
“I wish my papa would not speak of him in that tone,” she huffed.
The old man never argued further. As he had written in his own memoir years before, it had always been his nature to be led by the women he loved. After his wife died, he spoiled his daughter, humoured every one of her caprices, laughed at her prejudices, and let her have her own way. He believed that harsh, overbearing authority over children never changed their natures for the better, and only made hypocrites of them.

At last, the time came for Mr. Esmond to lay down all the affairs of this life, and he did so as if he were glad to be rid of their weight. We will not open this young story with tolling bells or a lengthy funeral sermon, though everyone who heard Parson Broadbent of Jamestown deliver his eulogy was astonished by the richness of its rhetoric and the fluency of its Latin. The boys’ Scottish tutor, Mr. Dempster, likely knew the truth of the matter, for he had corrected the proofs of the oration, which was printed by special request of the Governor and dozens of leading gentry, at Mr. Benjamin Franklin’s press in Philadelphia.

No such lavish, grand funeral had ever been seen in the whole colony as the one Madam Esmond Warrington arranged for her father – a ceremony he would have been the first to laugh at, for all its pomp. The two little lads of Castlewood, nearly smothered beneath their heavy black mourning trains and broad hatbands, led the procession. They were followed by no less a personage than Lord Fairfax from Greenway Court, the Governor of Virginia in his fine coach, the Randolphs, the Careys, the Harrisons, the Washingtons, and every leading family in the county. All the neighbourhood had loved the departed Colonel, whose great goodness, quiet high talent, benevolence and unassuming courtesy had earned him the unqualified respect of everyone who knew him.

When news of his death reached England, the family of Colonel Esmond’s stepson, the current Lord Castlewood of Hampshire, volunteered to pay for the fine marble monument to mark his grave. After a proper period of mourning, the monument was erected, carved with the Esmond arms and coronet, supported by a group of plump, weeping cherubs, and inscribed with an epitaph that, for once, did not tell a single falsehood.
