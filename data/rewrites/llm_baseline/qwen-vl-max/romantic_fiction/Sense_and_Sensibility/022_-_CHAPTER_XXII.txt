Marianne, who had never been known for her patience with anything resembling impertinence, vulgarity, or a lack of refinement, was now especially unkindly disposed—her spirits too low, her heart too full—to welcome the Miss Steeles or to encourage their attempts at friendship. Her constant coolness toward them discouraged any effort on their part to grow closer, and Elinor, observing this, soon noticed how the sisters began to turn their attention more toward her. Lucy, in particular, seemed determined to win her favor, seeking every opportunity to draw her into conversation, sharing her thoughts with an ease and openness that made their acquaintance feel less like a formal exchange and more like a genuine connection.

Lucy was naturally quick-witted; her observations were often sharp and amusing, and for brief moments, Elinor found her pleasant company. Yet beneath this charm lay a troubling lack of education. She was ignorant of the world’s most basic knowledge, unrefined in speech and manner, and though she tried earnestly to appear refined, her deficiencies could not be hidden. Elinor pitied her—the waste of natural talent left untended by learning—but she also saw something far deeper: a lack of moral sincerity. The way Lucy flattered, fawned, and sought approval at the Park revealed a mind without delicacy, without integrity. To be near someone so clever yet so dishonest, so eager to impress while lacking honesty, filled Elinor with unease. How could she truly enjoy the company of one whose ignorance kept them from equal conversation, and whose conduct rendered every kind word or gesture meaningless?

One day, as they walked together from the park to the cottage, Lucy broke the silence with a question that startled Elinor.

“You’ll think my question strange, I’m sure,” Lucy said, “but do you know Mrs. Ferrars personally? Edward’s mother?”

Elinor did think it odd—and her expression betrayed her surprise—as she replied, “I’ve never seen her.”

“Indeed?” Lucy said, her eyes narrowing slightly. “I thought you might have met her at Norland. Then perhaps you don’t know what sort of woman she is?”

“I know nothing of her,” Elinor answered carefully, unwilling to share her true feelings about Edward’s family, and not inclined to satisfy what seemed like idle curiosity.

“I must seem very strange to you,” Lucy continued, watching her closely. “But there may be reasons—I wish I could tell you. Still, I hope you’ll believe I mean no harm.”

Elinor gave a polite reply, and they walked in silence for a few minutes. Then Lucy spoke again, hesitantly but with growing urgency.

“I can’t bear the idea of you thinking me prying. I’d rather suffer anything than lose your good opinion. And I wouldn *’t* trust anyone else—no, I’d want your advice if I were in such a difficult situation. But I won’t trouble you. I’m sorry you don’t happen to know Mrs. Ferrars.”

“I am sorry I don’t,” Elinor said, stunned. “If it mattered to you, I suppose. But I didn’t realize you had any connection to the family at all. I’m surprised you’d ask so seriously about her.”

“I know you are,” Lucy said gently. “But if I could tell you everything, you wouldn’t be so shocked. Mrs. Ferrars means nothing to me now—but someday, perhaps soon, we might be very close indeed. It depends entirely on her.”

She looked down, blushing, but cast a fleeting glance at Elinor to gauge her reaction.

“Good heavens!” Elinor exclaimed. “What do you mean? Are you acquainted with Mr. Robert Ferrars? Could you possibly be?”

She felt no warmth at the thought of such a sister-in-law.

“No,” Lucy replied, “not Robert Ferrars. I’ve never even seen him. But,” she said, fixing her gaze on Elinor, “his eldest brother.”

At that moment, Elinor felt a surge of disbelief so strong it nearly overwhelmed her. She turned to Lucy, silent and amazed, unable to grasp why such a claim would be made—or what purpose it served. Her face flushed, but her mind remained firm in denial. She felt no fainting sickness, no wave of hysteria—only a cold, hard certainty that this could not be true.

“You may well be surprised,” Lucy went on. “You couldn’t have guessed. He never mentioned it to you or anyone—because it’s always been meant to be secret. And I’ve kept it faithfully, only telling Anne. I wouldn’t have told you at all, if I hadn’t trusted you completely. And I knew your questions about Mrs. Ferrars would seem odd, so I thought I should explain. I’m sure Mr. Ferrars won’t mind—he holds your family in the highest regard, and sees you all as his own.”

She paused, letting the words sink in.

Elinor said nothing for a moment. Her astonishment was too great. Then, forcing herself to speak calmly, she asked, “May I ask—how long has this engagement been going on?”

“Four years,” Lucy said quietly.

“Four years!” Elinor whispered, her voice barely steady.

“Yes,” Lucy confirmed.

Though deeply shaken, Elinor still refused to believe it. “I didn’t even know you were acquainted until recently.”

“Our acquaintance goes back many years,” Lucy said. “He was under my uncle’s care—Mr. Pratt. You’ve heard of him, haven’t you?”

“I think I have,” Elinor replied, summoning strength as her emotions surged. “He was with your uncle for four years—near Plymouth, at Longstaple. That’s where we first met. My sister and I often stayed with our uncle, and that’s where we became engaged—though not until a year after he left as a pupil. But he came back often after that.”

She sighed. “I didn’t want to enter into it without knowing his mother’s approval. But I was young—and I loved him too much to be wise.”

“You must have seen enough of him,” Lucy said, “to know he’s capable of making a woman truly devoted.”

“Certainly,” Elinor murmured, though her mind was racing. Then, with renewed confidence in Edward’s honor and her own judgment, she added, “Engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars? I confess I’m utterly astonished. There must be some mistake—perhaps of name or person. We cannot mean the same man.”

“We mean no other,” Lucy said, smiling. “Edward Ferrars, eldest son of Mrs. Ferrars of Park Street, brother to your sister-in-law, Mrs. John Dashwood—that is the man I mean. I wouldn’t be mistaken about the man on whom all my happiness depends.”

“It’s strange,” Elinor said, her voice trembling, “that I never heard him mention your name.”

“That’s not surprising,” Lucy replied. “We’ve kept it secret. You didn’t know me or my family, so there was no reason to mention me. And he feared his sister might suspect—so he never spoke of it.”

They walked in silence for a moment.

Then Lucy pulled out a small miniature from her pocket. “To avoid any confusion, please look at this face. It doesn’t do him justice, but I’m sure you’ll recognize him. I’ve had it over three years.”

Elinor took it. As she looked at the portrait, every doubt vanished. It was undeniably Edward’s face. She returned it quickly, acknowledging the likeness.

“I’ve never been able to give him my picture in return,” Lucy said, “which troubles me greatly. He’s always wanted it. But I’ll have it painted the very first chance I get.”

“You’re right to wait,” Elinor said, her voice calm but her heart breaking.

They walked a few steps in silence before Lucy spoke again.

“I know you’ll keep this secret,” she said. “You must understand how important it is—not to let his mother find out. She’d never approve. I have no fortune, and I believe she’s extremely proud.”

“I didn’t seek your confidence,” Elinor said, “but I assure you, it is safe with me. Still, I must admit—this revelation seems unnecessary. Your knowing me shouldn’t make the secret safer.”

As she spoke, she studied Lucy’s face, hoping to catch a flicker of falsehood. But Lucy’s expression remained unchanged.

“I feared you’d think I was overstepping,” she said. “I’ve known you by reputation for a long time, and when I saw you, I felt like an old friend. Besides, after asking so many questions about Mrs. Ferrars, I thought I owed you an explanation. And I have no one else to confide in. Anne knows—but she has no sense. She’d ruin everything. I was terrified when Sir John mentioned Edward’s name—I thought she’d blurt it out.”

She dabbed her eyes with her handkerchief.

“I wonder sometimes,” she said, after wiping her tears, “if we should end it altogether.”

She looked directly at Elinor.

“But then I can’t bring myself to do it. I couldn’t bear to see him so miserable. And for myself—how could I live without him? What would you do, Miss Dashwood? What would you advise?”

“I cannot advise you,” Elinor said, startled. “This is not a matter for advice. Only your own judgment can guide you.”

After a pause, Lucy said, “Of course, his mother will have to provide for him eventually. But poor Edward—how low-spirited he is! Did you notice how depressed he seemed when he visited Barton?”

“He was miserable,” Elinor admitted. “Especially when he left us.”

“Ah, yes,” Lucy said. “He’d just spent two weeks with us at Longstaple. I begged him to cheer up—for fear you’d guess what was wrong. But it broke his heart to leave so soon. And seeing me so upset—poor fellow!”

She pulled out a letter and casually showed it to Elinor.

“He writes in terrible spirits,” she said. “I received this just before leaving Exeter. You know his handwriting, I’m sure—it’s lovely. But this one isn’t quite as neat. He must have been tired—he filled the page completely.”

Elinor looked at the writing. It was unmistakably Edward’s. This was not a painting or a rumor—this was proof. A correspondence between them could only exist under a formal engagement. For a moment, she felt her world collapse—her heart sank, her breath caught. But she forced herself to stand firm. She could not fall apart. Not now.

“Writing to each other,” Lucy said, tucking the letter away, “is all we have during these long separations. I have his picture. Poor Edward has nothing—except a lock of my hair set in a ring. I gave it to him last time he was at Longstaple. He said it helped, but not as much as a portrait. Perhaps you noticed the ring when you saw him?”

“I did,” Elinor said, her voice steady, though her soul was in torment.

She remembered the ring—remembered Edward’s quiet sadness, his evasiveness about his time at Plymouth. Now, everything made sense. And everything was ruined.

They reached the cottage. The Miss Steeles lingered briefly, then returned to the Park. Elinor remained alone.

And then, at last, she allowed herself to grieve.
