The letter from Tunbridge Wells arrived on a Saturday morning, dated the 25th of August, 1756. It was written by Mr. Warrington, his hand moving with the brisk confidence of a man who believes he has solved the equation of life. He reported success: three bets won, a journey from Bromley executed in record time, and a safe arrival at the Wells. He mentioned the horses, the postilion, the black man driving Mrs. Betty, and the lack of trouble on Blackheath despite suspicious riders. He ended with a promise of repayment for a game of piquet played the previous night. The tone was one of triumph, a declaration that the chaos of the road had been tamed by skill and speed.

Inside the house of captivity, the atmosphere was thick with the scent of stale food and suppressed fear. Lady Maria and the chaplain had been playing cards past midnight, a futile distraction from the reality of their confinement. The sound of wheels breaking the silence was not a gentle approach; it was a mechanical intrusion, a disruption of the stagnant air. The wheels whirred, slowed, and halted directly before the door. A pause followed—a tense negotiation at the gate—before Mrs. Betty emerged. Her face was a mask of conflicting emotions, radiating joy while her eyes overflowed with tears. Behind her stepped a figure that shattered the remaining tension: a tall young gentleman.

The chaplain dropped his cards, the sound sharp against the table, a signal of total surrender to the moment. Lady Maria rose, her color draining until she resembled a sheet of paper, before collapsing into the arms of her cousin. The reunion was immediate and overwhelming, a physical release of months of pressure. Kisses were exchanged, a torrent of affection that seemed to defy logic, flooding the small room. He had come to rescue her. The narrative of her imprisonment, her shame, her isolation—all of it evaporated under the weight of his presence. She wept, a genuine flood of relief, and leaned heavily on his arm. For a moment, stripped of her captors and her fears, she appeared more vibrant than she ever had in the history of her suffering. She did not faint; instead, they walked out, a united front against the world.

"You are both free," Harry said, his voice steady, cutting through the emotional haze. "Betty, fetch my lady's hat and cloak. Chaplain, we shall smoke a pipe at our lodgings; the ride has tired me." The chaplain, a man constructed entirely of sensitivity, reacted with a violence of feeling. He seized Harry's hand, kissing it fervently, praying aloud for divine blessing upon his patron. Harry felt a surge of warmth, a chemical reaction in his blood that confirmed his worthiness. To save the suffering was the highest function of existence. He marched beside his rescued princess, his hat cocked, imagining himself crowned by invisible trumpeters. He saw himself as a conqueror, a hero entering a hall of comfort. Yet, the reality was simpler: he had simply used a key to unlock a door. He had driven away a dragon with a gesture and silenced a tyrant with a coin. His vanity inflated, puffing him up like a balloon filled with hot air, but beneath it lay a core of good nature. He had saved two lives, extracted tears of gratitude, and now, perhaps, he would swagger a bit about London and White's Club. Let him brag; it was harmless.

Sampson, however, was not merely relieved; he was transformed. His nature was a sponge, absorbing pain and pleasure with equal intensity. As a preacher, he had trained his emotions to be tools, and now they were unleashed. He wept, laughing, roaring, and offering money with reckless abandon. He was in a state of rapturous gratitude, viewing Harry not just as a savior but as a god. In London, such a companion would be dangerous, but tonight, he was merely a vessel for adoration.

"The younger branch of our family," Harry announced with a superior air, "have treated you poorly. But by Jove, Sampson, I shall stand by you!" He spoke of the splendor of his lineage, a topic that always ignited his eloquence. He offered a banknote to cover his sister's schooling, a tangible proof of his benevolence. Sampson accepted the gift, overwhelmed, swearing oaths of unworthiness.

"I do not deserve this!" Sampson cried, dropping his head. "By George, I am a villain!"

"Pshaw," Harry dismissed, waving a hand. "That pocket-book I lost last week was empty anyway."

"No, sir! There was no money!" Sampson insisted, his voice trembling.

"How do you know?" Harry demanded, startled.

"Because I am a thief, sir. I found the book that night at Barbeau's when you were drunk."

"And the letters?" Harry asked, rising, his face flushing red.

"They told me everything, sir. Spies surround you, young and simple as you are. They cannot hide their secrets."

"Stories about Lady Fanny? About my cousin Will?"

"Yes, they are true," Sampson sighed. "The House of Castlewood has been cursed since the elder branch departed."

Harry lunged forward. "Do you dare breathe a word against Lady Maria?"

"Not for worlds!" Sampson replied, glancing sideways. "Though I must confess, she seems older than you, and perhaps a match ill-suited to your youth. Still, she possesses every virtue."

"She is too old, I know," Harry declared with majestic certainty. "But she has my word. Bring me the letters, and I will forgive your theft."

"My benefactor, let me forgive myself!" Sampson cried, retreating to his chamber.

He returned moments later, pale and shaken. He produced the pocket-book. "It bears your name, sir. But the letters are gone. My trunk and valise were tampered with. I am a guilty man, unable to restore what is owed." He knelt, clasping his hands in a posture of pathetic despair.

Who had entered the room? The landlady swore no one had come in. Nothing else was missing from Sampson's sparse belongings. The theft was specific, targeted. Was it Lady Maria? She had been imprisoned all day, unaware of the breach. She could not have stolen the letters. The clues pointed elsewhere: Case, the house steward's secret trip to London, his knowledge of the shoemaker where Sampson lodged, his access to the Esmond family's private affairs. These threads suggested the Baroness Bernstein. But why arrest Lady Maria? The chaplain knew nothing of the specific letter she had lost; she had not trusted him with her deepest secret.

Harry, intoxicated by his own success—the three bets, the rescue, the fine supper of partridges and Burgundy sent by Monsieur Barbeau—accepted Sampson's confession. He forgave the theft, shook the chaplain's hand, and invited him to drink.

"To the House of Esmond-Warrington!" Sampson toasted, tears streaming down his face.

"We are the elder branch," Harry corrected, his voice noble but slightly slurred. "My grandfather was the Marquis of Esmond. Here's to you, Chaplain—I forgive you—and God bless you. If you had been caught for three times as much, I would have paid it. Look, the sun has risen! We need no candles." He dismissed the chaplain to bed, secure in his victory.

At noon, Madame de Bernstein summoned him. She noted the changes in his attire, evidence of his recent shopping sprees in London. She called him her dearest child, thanking him for his generosity toward Maria. She recounted the shock of the church seizure and her own gambling losses, explaining why she had sent Case to London for funds.

"So Case went to London?" Harry asked.

"Of course. The Baroness de Bernstein cannot remain in debt." She turned to him, eyes pleading. "Can you lend me some, child?"

"I can give you twenty-two pounds," Harry blushed, counting his coins. "I have forty-four left until my Virginian remittances arrive. I have been extravagant buying horses and clothes."

"And rescued your relations in distress, you prodigal good boy. No, child, I do not want your money. Take this note for fifty pounds. Spend it, be merry! Your mother will repay me, even if she does not love me." She held out her hand, affectionate and commanding.

"When you need me, come to me."

Beatrix Bernstein's anger bubbled beneath her grace. "I love you, child, and yet I am furious. You have engaged yourself to poor Maria, who is as old as your mother? What will Madam Esmond say? You will starve before she dies."

"I have ten thousand pounds from my father," Harry argued. "Now that my brother is gone."

"The interest will not buy enough cards."

"We must stop playing cards," Harry conceded.

"It is beyond Maria's capacity. She will pawn your coat to play. The madness runs in our family—in me too. I warned you. I prayed you not to gamble with them. A lad of twenty engaging a woman of forty-two! Writing letters on his knees, signing with his heart's blood, promising to marry no other woman than his adorable cousin, Lady Maria Esmond. Oh, it is cruel! Cruel!"

"Madam, who showed you my letter?" Harry asked, burning with shame.
