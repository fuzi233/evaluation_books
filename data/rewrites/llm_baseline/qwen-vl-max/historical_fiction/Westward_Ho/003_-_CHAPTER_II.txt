“*If men remain silent, the stars will make you known; the Sun does not forget its comrade.*”
—Old Epigram on Drake.

Five years have passed. It is nine o’clock on a crisp, bright November morning, and yet the bells of Bideford Church still ring out, two hours beyond their usual hour. They do not toll in solemn order, but burst into joyful peals every five minutes, as if they too could not contain their mirth. The streets of Bideford are transformed into a riot of color—garlands strung across lanes, tapestries draped from windows, and crowds of seamen, merchants, and townsfolk dressed in their finest holiday attire. Ships in the harbor fly all their flags, and cannon roar in celebration. Horses crowd every stable, and Sir Richard Grenville’s house thrums with feasting, laughter, and servants running to and fro.

Along the churchyard path, packed with women in bonnets and shawls, moves the noble blood of North Devon—tall men, stately ladies, heirs of ancient houses whose names once echoed through England’s courts and fields. Among them is the Countess of Bath, escorted by Sir Richard himself, her cap in hand (for her husband, Earl Bourchier, is in London at the queen’s court). There are Bassets from Umberleigh, Carys from Clovelly, Fortescues from Wear and Buckland, Coles from Slade, Stukelys from Affton, St. Legers from Annery, Coffins from Portledge, and even Coplestones from Eggesford, thirty miles distant. And last—but not least—comes Sir John Chichester of Ralegh, followed by his eight daughters and three sons in single file, a sight straight from old patriarchal times. One son is absent, fighting in Ireland to avenge his brother’s murder, and who will one day rule there as Lord Deputy and Baron of Belfast. At the gate, he meets his cousin from Arlington, whose own procession stretches behind him—four daughters and nineteen sons, the youngest not yet past the town hall, the eldest already at the Lychgate. They laugh and yield the way to the elder branch, a living tree of noble stock.

Into the church they go, each placed according to rank or near enough to it—though not without sharp glances, whispered complaints, and quiet jostlings among high-born matrons. The churchwardens and sidesmen, overwhelmed by such grand company, grow flustered and red-faced, finally begging Sir Richard himself to sort out the nobility: who is senior, who junior, which family bears eight quarterings, which only four—lest they spark a feud between half the fine ladies of the region. The older men sit safely in the corporation pews; the younger ones care only for a view of the ladies. At last, silence falls. All eyes turn to the door. Then, from afar, music approaches—flutes, hautboys, drums, and trumpets—blaring, shrieking, thundering up to the very threshold. Then, silence. The churchwardens descend with rods in hand. A hush, then a rustle, and sudden tears and blessings from many women—and some men too—as the long-awaited heroes enter. The rector begins not the morning service, but the ancient rite of thanksgiving after a great sea victory.

What has stirred this joy in old Bideford? Why do all eyes fix upon four weather-beaten mariners, adorned with ribbons and knots by loving hands? And especially upon the towering figure before them—a boy without beard, yet built like Hercules, golden hair flowing over his shoulders, rising head and shoulders above the congregation? And why, as they move instinctively toward the altar and kneel before the rails, does Mrs. Leigh of Burrough hide her face in her hands, her hood trembling with sobs?

Because these are Devon men—men of Bideford. Their names are Amyas Leigh of Burrough, John Staveley, Michael Heard, Jonas Marshall, and Thomas Braund of Clovelly. They are the first English sailors ever to sail around the world, having voyaged with Francis Drake. And now they return home—not just to rest, but to give thanks to God.

It is a tale that must be told. To understand how they came to this moment, we must look back, nearly to where we began.

For more than a year after Mr. Oxenham’s departure, young Amyas had lived quietly, as promised, though prone to occasional bursts of temper—common enough in strong-willed boys. His studies did not improve much, but his training at home flourished. He became a skilled archer, rider, and swordsman (in the old style of buckler fencing). But then, while attending the Exeter Assizes on business, his father caught the prison fever common in those days, fell ill in court, and died within a week.

Now Mrs. Leigh was left alone—widowed, with a fierce young son in her care. She had loved her husband deeply and truly. He had been a man of talent—scholar, courtier, soldier—but also one of disappointment. His estate had been ruined by his father’s extravagance and his own youthful ambition, which led him to court, where he spent his heart and purse chasing shadows. Though gifted, he lacked the strength to use them wisely. Shyness, pride, or a delicate honor kept him from seizing success, even as he resented the world that denied him. Finally, he met Miss Foljambe, a fair young lady from Derbyshire at Queen Elizabeth’s court—also weary of court life. They married despite the queen’s disapproval, and fled Whitehall to live quietly at Burrough. In her, he found peace and love—and knew it.

Mrs. Leigh was one of the noble English women of that age—devout, gentle, free from superstition or harshness. Yet she carried a shadow: memories of persecution. Twenty-five years earlier, she had seen Joan Waste burned at Derby, blind and innocent, and Mistress Joyce Lewis, a lady like herself, perish at the stake. Sometimes, in dreams, she heard her mother’s cries—pleading for mercy or strength to endure the fire. Her mother, a Yorkshire gentlewoman and former maid-of-honor to Queen Catherine, had been a close friend of Anne Askew. She had watched, horror-stricken, as Askew, crippled by torture, was carried to Smithfield in a chair, to die beside Lascelles. The heavens themselves seemed to mourn, thunder roaring and rain hissing on the flames.

This sorrow stayed with Mrs. Leigh all her life, deepening under Mary’s reign, when she had to flee for her life through the hills of the Peak District, hidden by loyal tenants and protected by her husband’s bold declaration: no constable, priest, bishop, or cardinal would lay hands on her. She survived—but the sadness remained, a dark mantle trailing behind her. That same sorrow touched her daughter, who, after marriage, gave herself wholly to God.

Yet her devotion was not withdrawn from the world. She served her husband, her children, the poor of Northam Town. She was respected by the gentry—Grenvilles, Fortescues, Chichesters—all who admired her husband’s wit and talents. She practiced self-denial, often drawing gentle rebukes from her husband—but never out of fear of God, nor to earn favor. Her faith was shaped by Luther’s teachings, by the “Alt-Deutsch Theologie,” a mystic theology the Reformer said he valued more than any book save Scripture and Augustine. It guided her by day and night.

Now, barely forty, she was widowed—still beautiful, still radiant with a calm that seemed divine. The dove of peace rested upon her words, her gestures, her presence. It was no wonder Sir Richard and Lady Grenville cherished her. No wonder her children adored her. And no wonder young Amyas, once the storm of grief passed, felt a new life begin. He realized: his mother would no longer act for him alone—he must now act for her.

So, the day after his father’s funeral, instead of returning home, he walked boldly into Sir Richard Grenville’s house and asked to see his godfather.

“You must be my father now, sir,” he said firmly.

Sir Richard looked at the boy’s broad, strong face and swore a mighty oath—by oak, ash, and thorn—that he would be father to him and brother to his mother, for Christ’s sake. Lady Grenville took the boy by the hand and walked home with him to Burrough. There, the two women embraced, weeping—one for loss, the other in foreboding. For Mrs. St. Leger knew well that her husband’s fiery spirit would not leave his body peacefully. Death, she prayed nightly, would find him sword in hand, on the field of duty and fame.

And so they vowed eternal sisterhood—and kept it. Life at Burrough continued as before. Amyas rode, shot, boxed, wandered the quay beside Sir Richard. Mrs. Leigh saw no need to change the training her husband had set for his son. It was enough that her elder son, Frank, had chosen the path she had secretly wished for both.

Frank—God’s gift to her pure love—had earned honor at home and abroad. First at Bideford school, then at Exeter College, where he befriended Sir Philip Sidney and others of promise. In 1572, on his way to Heidelberg, he stopped in Paris with letters to Walsingham at the English Embassy. There, he met Sidney again—and saved his own life, as Sidney did his, during the Massacre of St. Bartholomew’s Day.

At Heidelberg, he stayed two years, gaining respect from princes and scholars alike. He refused Sidney’s urging to go to Italy, choosing instead to become tutor to two young German princes. After living with them at their father’s court, he brought them down to Padua, writing home that he would “perfect them, according to his insufficiency, in all princely studies.”

Sidney returned to England, but Frank made friends wherever he went—carrying recommendations from princes, magnificos, and learned doctors who admired his learning, modesty, and virtue. He soaked in the wonders of Italy: debated poetry with Tasso, history with Sarpi, listened to Galileo’s bold theories, took his pupils to Venice to be painted by Veronese, saw Palladio’s palaces, the Rialto merchants, the Ragusan argosies. He watched Tintoretto paint “tempestuous glories o’er the scene.” By secret intercession, he entered Titian’s sacred studio—the old master, silver-bearded, undimmed-eyed, amidst his creations—where he spoke of Bellini, Raphael, Michelangelo, the building of St. Peter’s, the fire in Venice, the sack of Rome, kings and poets long gone, and showed the brush that Francis I once picked up for him.

He visited Rome—though forbidden to Sidney by Languet—and saw the Pope, whom Sidney called “a right good fellow.” He saw the Vatican frescoes, heard Palestrina conduct his music beneath St. Peter’s dome, and nearly fell in love with the melodies—until he remembered the prayers offered under that same dome for the murdered Protestants of Paris.

Finally, months before his father died, he returned his pupils to Germany, dismissed with rich gifts. Mrs. Leigh’s heart soared—her wanderer would come home. But within a month of her husband’s death, a letter arrived from Frank—describing the Alps, the valleys of the Waldenses, and his meeting with Stephanus Parmenius (Budaeus), a famed scholar. He had visited Geneva with Budaeus, heard the doctors debate, and disliked their harsh judgments, though admiring their subtlety. He wrote in Latin, full of quotations—yet the letter never reached its intended reader. The widow wept over it alone, and wept harder at the end, where Frank confessed he had set out with Budaeus down the Danube to Buda, to experience Hungarian learning before finishing his travels.

After that, he wrote again and again to his father—only to receive no reply for nearly two years. Fearing disaster, he rushed back to England, only to find his mother a widow, and his brother Amyas gone to the South Seas with Captain Drake of Plymouth.

Still, even then, Frank was not allowed to stay. Sir Richard, who abhorred idleness, sent him to Court to serve Lord Hunsdon. There, delicate and fair as Amyas was strong, he quickly gained favor through Carew, Sidney, and Leicester. He entered the queen’s household, basked in royal favor, and became a favorite of Gloriana’s court—surrounded by ladies, chivalry, and the elegance of Elizabethan life. He found Sidney again—now twenty-five, renowned across Europe: patron of scholars, counselor to warriors, confidant of William of Orange, Languut, Plessis du Mornay, and leaders of Protestantism. And he found that the son of a Devon squire was welcomed by the most favored of fortune and nature.

Mrs. Leigh submitted silently. “You took away my mastiff-pup,” she smiled at Sir Richard, “and now you must take my fair greyhound too.”

“Would you have your greyhound grow into a true Cotswold dog—capable of pulling down a ten-point stag—or a smooth-skinned poppet, led by Florence ladies with bells and farthingales?”

She accepted. Months later, she received a letter—through Sir Richard—from Queen Elizabeth herself. The queen thanked her for “the loan of that most delicate and flawless crystal, the soul of your excellent son,” praised him beyond measure, and exalted her above Cornelia: “Cornelia showed her jewels, but kept them. You, madam, have lent both your sons to your country and your queen. I am in your debt, and shall repay it as becomes both me and you.”

The letter, drenched in holy tears, was placed in her cedar chest beside Frank’s diplomas. She read the Latin aloud, though she understood none of it—hoping to find, somewhere, the phrase *excellentissimus Noster Franciscus Leighius Anglus*, the treasure of her heart.

But why did Amyas go to the South Seas?

Two reasons—each one powerful enough to send many a youth to far worse fates: first, a schoolmaster. Second, a beauty.

Vindex Brimblecombe, once a servant of Exeter College, Oxford—called “Sir Vindex” in the fashion of the time—was master of Bideford’s grammar school. He was, at heart, a kind and godly man, but long accustomed to punishing weaker boys. Such power, though useful for discipline, hardened the soul of the teacher. Over time, he grew crueler. When Amyas became fatherless, Sir Vindex felt a new duty—to care for the boy. But the only result was an increase in beatings—from two per week to one per day.

All the while, Amyas had never lost his dream of the sea. When he couldn’t walk the quay or sit on the pebble ridge at Northam, staring at the endless ocean, he drew ships and maps on his slate—imaginary islands, castles, dragons, gallant vessels with lances like porcupine quills, and little round o’s for heads—himself and his friends slaying the dragon, rescuing the princess.

One afternoon, he was deeply absorbed in such a drawing—when the other boys gathered around, laughing. Sir Vindex, as usual after dinner, slept soundly in his chair.

And so, the story goes, the boy’s dream took flight—into the real world.
