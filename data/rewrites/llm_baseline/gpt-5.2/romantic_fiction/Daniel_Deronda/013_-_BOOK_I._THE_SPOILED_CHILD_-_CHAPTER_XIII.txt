Grandcourt, having once determined that Miss Harleth should be his wife, displayed that grave, unsmiling talent of his for fitting acts to an end. Scarce a day passed, through that fortnight of ripening fields and shortening evenings, in which he did not contrive to be near her, or at least to let her feel his nearness as one feels a presence in a room before one has turned one’s head. His cousin, Mrs. Torrington, now presided at Diplow; and thus Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen were invited among a large company, where there were abundant eyes to take note how the master of the house singled out the portionless beauty, and wore an air of indifference toward the heiress.

All that world within visiting distance of Pennicote—Mr. Gascoigne and the families worth naming—began to speak as if the thing were already sealed, and the rector’s assurance hardened quickly into a resolve that he must “do his duty” by his niece and see that the settlements were such as became the position. Even to Mrs. Davilow, the wonder was less that Grandcourt meant it than that he had not yet said it; and to Grandcourt himself there was a faint, irritated surprise that a matter so clear in his intention still hung on a formality undischarged. More than once he had risen with the quiet promise to himself that before night he would put Gwendolen into the position of accepting him; and more than once the evening came and he had not done it. This delay, instead of cooling him, acted as an acrid spice: it sharpened determination, as a too-sweet dish asks for salt.

He asked leave that a beautiful horse should be brought for her to ride. Mrs. Davilow was to follow in the carriage; they were to go to Diplow for luncheon, Grandcourt conducting them. It was mid-harvest—days when the land seems to give forth its very meaning in smell and sound: the dry, warm breath from stubble where the cut straw prickles under foot; the greener, milk-sweet damp by a hedge-bottom; the faint metallic tang where iron has fretted in a gate; and everywhere that soft, incessant language of the season—finches ticking in the thorn, a far scythe’s whisper, the creak of a loaded wain, and, underneath all, the steady hush of corn-heads shifting their multitude as the breeze moved through them like a sociable spirit. Poppies flamed along the field-borders; here sheaves stood in ranks; there horses strained under the last load from a broad expanse of gray-brown stubble; and beyond, the downs lay in their tender, cloud-shadowed pallor. The road ran for a time through a country of dairy-farms that had kept the old look—low barns, quiet yards, slow cattle under wide boughs—so that one might believe peace had found a refuge away from the railway’s distant rush, that new harsh note in the landscape’s ear.

But peace does not always pass, like a cool hand, over the fevered forehead. It did not soothe Mrs. Davilow. The sight before her—Gwendolen and Grandcourt cantering, then slackening to a walk till the carriage overtook them—was precisely what a mother’s hopes had hung upon; yet it only fed that inward conflict of longing and foreboding which had become her habit. If Grandcourt spoke to-day, the uncertainty would end. If he spoke and Gwendolen refused—what then? If he spoke and Gwendolen accepted—would that be safety or another kind of peril? Mrs. Davilow, who could not imagine her daughter content in what are traditionally called “mean circumstances,” tried to quiet herself with the thought, “Perhaps she will be as happy as she could be with any one—or as most other women are.” Yet the question returned, like a refrain one cannot silence: Is he a man she would be happy with?

Grandcourt, too, wanted the uncertainty ended—the uncertainty of not having spoken. Other uncertainty—he would not name it—had no reasonable foundation; it was merely like an irritant in the air.

Gwendolen enjoyed the ride—the measured lift of the horse beneath her, the clean clink of bit and curb, the warm animal smell rising from a glossy neck, the little drum of hoofs on firm ground. But her pleasure did not overflow into that unpremeditated girlish chatter and laughter which had come so easily with Rex. Her words were few, her laughs light as an echo heard from far off. There was for her also “something in the air,” though she would have denied that it was any subjugation of her will. She desired—oh, keenly—that this dignified gentleman, and every one else, should understand she meant to do as she liked. If she chose to take him, he must know she would not be managed into the pattern of other women. She would not renounce her freedom.

Grandcourt spoke, as usual, in that brief manner which makes a man appear to have depths by merely not using them. His sentences ended as if he had clipped them with shears; he left pauses that might have contained meaning, and which others filled with conjecture.

“How do you like Criterion’s paces?” he said, when they had entered the park and fallen from canter to walk.

“He is delightful to ride. I should like to have a leap with him, if it would not frighten mamma. There was a good wide channel we passed five minutes ago. I should like to gallop back and take it.”

“Pray do. We can take it together.”

“No, thanks. Mamma is so timid—if she saw me it might make her ill.”

“Let me go and explain. Criterion would take it without fail.”

“No—indeed—you are very kind—but it would alarm her too much. I dare take any leap when she is not by; but I do it and don’t tell her about it.”

“We can let the carriage pass and then set off.”

“No, no—pray don’t think of it any more: I spoke quite randomly.” Even as she said it, she felt a strange recoil from her own proposal, as if some inner caution had pulled the rein.

“But Mrs. Davilow knows I shall take care of you.”

“Yes, but she would think of you as having to take care of my broken neck.”

A pause lengthened between them; the carriage wheels were a soft, regular murmur behind, and the park’s air had that faint scent of damp earth under grass, as if water were stored somewhere in the ground. Grandcourt looked toward her and said at last, as if he were remarking on the weather,

“I should like to have the right always to take care of you.”

Gwendolen did not turn her eyes on him. She felt, in that moment, blood rush and retreat—first the heat of a blush, then a pallor that seemed to take away breath; yet she answered quickly enough, in the lightest flute-tone, with a careless movement of her head.

“Oh, I am not sure that I want to be taken care of: if I chose to risk breaking my neck, I should like to be at liberty to do it.”

As she spoke she checked her horse and turned in her saddle, looking toward the advancing carriage. Her eyes swept over Grandcourt in the movement, but there was no language in them to correct the carelessness of her words. She knew—keenly—that she was risking something, not her neck but the forward course of this courtship; and she was not content with that risk, even while she made it.

“Damn her!” thought Grandcourt, checking his horse. He was not given to running his thoughts into speech, even inwardly; the phrase stood for a whole press of irritation and resolve. Was she trying to force him into protestations—to make him perform the lover with kneelings and vows? That was not the gate by which she would enter on the privileges he could give. Did she expect a letter, a written proposal? Equally absurd. He would not put himself in any posture that could be definitely rejected. Besides, had she not already accepted him in accepting his attentions? Any rupture would be understood to her disadvantage. It was coquetry, then—nothing else.

The carriage came up; the moment of solitude was broken, and there could be no more _tête-à-tête_ until they reached the house, where a crowd made a moving screen of faces and voices. There, Gwendolen—still in her riding-dress, her hat laid aside—came into the bright pressure of observation. The talk, the laughter, the clink of glass, the shifting of chairs, the flutter of skirts, made a kind of domestic music in which she could hide a little from her own inward tumult; and since Mr. Lush was not there to look at her with that offensive familiarity, the admiring attention acted like wine on her spirits, dispersing for the time the uneasy consciousness of divided impulses. Whether Grandcourt had been offended she could not tell; his manners did not change. Yet her quickness had not reached the delusion that his manners were any clue: because they were unchanged, she was not the less afraid of him.

She had not been at Diplow before except to dine; and when luncheon was over and some guests had departed, Lady Flora Hollis proposed that the remaining party should explore the grounds, since certain views from windows and garden were worth showing in the sloping afternoon light. There were opportunities enough for Grandcourt to draw Gwendolen aside and speak unheard; but he did not. He spoke to no one else; yet what he said to her was no more eager than in their first interview. He looked at her not less than usual; and a fresh return of defiance rose in her—she met his eyes fully, not caring, rather preferring, that they seemed to have no expression.

At last it seemed as if he had contrived his moment. The party stopped by the pool, amused with Fetch’s accomplishment of bringing a water-lily to the bank, like Cowper’s Beau; the dog, having failed once, insisted on trying again, plunging and snorting, sending little rings of sound across the water so that even the rushes seemed to listen.

Grandcourt stood with Gwendolen a little outside the group. He turned deliberately, fixed his eyes on a knoll planted with American shrubs, with a winding path up it, and said languidly,

“This is a bore. Shall we go up there?”

“Oh, certainly—since we are exploring,” said Gwendolen. She was pleased, and yet afraid: pleased with being chosen out of the crowd, afraid of what might be said when there were no ears to hear it.

The path was too narrow for him to offer his arm. They went up in silence. At the small platform at the top, Grandcourt said,

“There is nothing to be seen here: the thing was not worth climbing.”

How was it that she did not laugh? She stood perfectly still, holding up the folds of her skirt like a statue, tightening her grasp on the handle of her whip—which she had snatched up automatically with her hat when they set out.

“What sort of a place do you prefer?” Grandcourt asked.

“Different places are agreeable in their way. On the whole, I think I prefer places that are open and cheerful. I am not fond of anything sombre.”

“Your place of Offendene is too sombre....”

“It is, rather.”

“You will not remain there long, I hope.”

“Oh, yes, I think so. Mamma likes to be near her sister.”

A silence, brief but heavy.

“It is not to be supposed that _you_ will always live there, though Mrs. Davilow may.”

“I don’t know. We women can’t go in search of adventures—to find out the North-West Passage or the source of the Nile, or to hunt tigers in the East. We must stay where we grow, or where the gardeners like to transplant us. We are brought up like the flowers, to look as pretty as we can, and be dull without complaining. That is my notion about the plants; they are often bored, and that is the reason why some of them have got poisonous. What do you think?” She ran on with a nervous quickness, lightly whipping the rhododendron bush before her, as if the glossy leaves could take her agitation in their leathery patience.

“I quite agree. Most things are bores,” said Grandcourt, as if her talk had pushed his mind into an easy current away from the track he meant to follow. Then, after a pause, he went on in his refined, broken drawl: “But a woman can be married.”

“Some women can.”

“You, certainly, unless you are obstinately cruel.”

“I am not sure that I am not both cruel and obstinate.” Suddenly she turned her head and looked full at him. She was less concerned with the effect on him than with what that steady regard might do within herself.

He stood perfectly still, half a yard away. It flashed through her mind that a kind of lotus-eater’s stupor had begun in him and was stealing over her too: a languor that dulled resistance, that made will seem like something far off. Then he said,

“Are you as uncertain about yourself as you make others about you?”

“I am quite uncertain about myself; I don’t know how uncertain others may be.”

“And you wish them to understand that you don’t care?” There was a touch of hardness now.

“I did not say that,” she answered, hesitating, turning her eyes away and whipping the rhododendron again. She wished she were on horseback—she could have fled in a canter. One cannot canter down a knoll.

“You do care, then,” said Grandcourt, not more quickly, but softened again into that drawl which seemed to caress without warmth.

“Ha! my whip!” Gwendolen cried, with a little scream of distress. She had let it go—nothing more natural in agitation—and it had sprung away with an odd force, lodging in the branches of an azalea half-way down the knoll. Here was escape, neatly offered by accident. She ran down, laughing prettily, and Grandcourt was obliged to follow; but she rescued the whip before he could reach it, and went on to the level ground. There she paused and looked at him with an exasperating brightness and heightened colour, as if she had carried off a small triumph and still wore the signs of it.

These signs were still visible to Mrs. Davilow when they joined the rest of the party.

“It is all coquetting,” thought Grandcourt. “The next time I beckon she will come down.”

It seemed likely enough to him that the beckoning might occur the very next day, at the picnic and archery meeting in Cardell Chase, planned on the evening of the ball.

In Gwendolen’s mind, too, that result presented itself as one of two alternating likelihoods—as if she were being hurried toward a boundary-line and did not know on which side she would fall. This subjection to a possible self, a self she could not absolutely predict, filled her with astonishment and a kind of terror. Her favourite key to life—doing as she liked—seemed to fail her. She could not foresee what, at a given moment, she might like.

The prospect of marrying Grandcourt had become more alluring than she had ever believed marriage could be. Dignities and luxuries, the power to do a great deal of what she liked—these were no longer airy imaginings but close, palpable, like a rich scent suddenly brought under one’s very senses: the thought of fine rooms, bright with firelight; of table-linen smooth as water under the hand; of silver that caught and returned the candle’s flame; of a life where the very daily bread should be chosen rather than endured. And Grandcourt himself? He seemed as little of a flaw in his fortunes as lover and husband could be. She wanted, in her fancy, to mount the chariot and drive the plunging horses herself, with a spouse beside her who would fold his arms, lend his countenance, and never look ridiculous. He had been everywhere, seen everything—an enviable preamble to his supreme preference for Gwendolen Harleth. He did not appear to enjoy anything much. That, she told herself, was not necessary; indeed, the fewer tastes and ardours he had, the freer his wife might be to follow her own. With all her perspicacity, with all the reading that seemed to her mamma dangerously instructive, her judgement was curiously at fault before him. He was adorably quiet, free from absurdities—he would be a husband with whom a woman could make the best appearance.

And yet—why this constraint? Why did her talk with him lack the daring playfulness she had shown with other admirers? The very absence of demonstrativeness she was glad of worked on her like a charm that numbed as well as attracted. Grandcourt was formidable—like a handsome lizard of some unknown species, not one of the quick bright darting kind. But Gwendolen knew hardly anything about lizards; and ignorance offers a wide pasture to probability. This splendid creature might be gentle, suitable even as a boudoir pet: what might not a lizard be, if one had never been bitten? Her acquaintance with Grandcourt was such that no accomplishment suddenly revealed in him would have surprised her; yet he was so little suggestive of drama that it scarcely occurred to her to picture, in any detail, how his six-and-thirty years had been spent. She imagined him always cold and dignified, never committed. He had hunted the tiger—had he ever been in love? Had he ever made love? Those experiences seemed as remote from the Grandcourt of Diplow as a far country’s language heard through a wall—one catches cadence without meaning.

On the whole she wished to marry him; he suited her purpose. Her prevailing, deliberate intention was to accept him.

But was she going to fulfil that intention? Already, her evasions had gone farther than necessary. She began to be afraid of herself—of that caprice she had always treated as a royal prerogative.

On the drive home she sat, as was her habit, with her back to the horses; and thus she lay open to her mother’s constant observation. Mrs. Davilow saw the excitement and changefulness in her eyes, her unwonted silence, her absence of mind, and took them for sure signs that something unprecedented had occurred. Anxiety urged her at last into speech. The Gascoignes were to dine at Offendene; perhaps there was reason to consult the rector. She did not expect influence over Gwendolen—neither the rector nor herself had much hope of that—but she longed to disburden her mind.

“Something has happened, dear?” she began, in a tender, questioning tone.

Gwendolen turned, as if roused suddenly to the sense of her own body, and took off her gloves, then her hat, letting the soft moving air touch her head as if it could cool thought itself. They were in a retired part of the road, long afternoon shadows lying across it from bordering trees; there were no eyes on them. Gwendolen met her mother’s look and said nothing.

“Mr. Grandcourt has been saying something?—Tell me, dear.” The last words were a beseeching.

“What am I to tell you, mamma?” Gwendolen returned perversely.

“I am sure something has agitated you. You ought to confide in me, Gwen. You ought not to leave me in doubt and anxiety.” Mrs. Davilow’s eyes filled with tears.

“Mamma, dear, please don’t be miserable,” said Gwendolen with pettish remonstrance. “It only makes me more so. I am in doubt myself.”

“About Mr. Grandcourt’s intentions?” Mrs. Davilow asked, gathering courage from fear.

“No; not at all,” said Gwendolen curtly, with a pretty little toss as she put on her hat again.

“About whether you will accept him, then?”

“Precisely.”

“Have you given him a doubtful answer?”

“I have given him no answer at all.”

“He _has_ spoken so that you could not misunderstand him?”

“As far as I would let him speak.”

“You expect him to persevere?” Mrs. Davilow asked anxiously, and, receiving no reply, ventured on: “You don’t consider that you have discouraged him?”

“I dare say not.”

“I thought you liked him, dear,” her mother said timidly.

“So I do, mamma, as liking goes. There is less to dislike about him than about most men. He is quiet and _distingué_.” Gwendolen spoke with a pouting gravity; but suddenly mischief returned, and her face broke into a smile as she added, “Indeed he has all the qualities that would make a husband tolerable—battlement, veranda, stable, etc., no grins and no glass in his eye.”

“Do be serious with me for a moment, dear. Am I to understand that you mean to accept him?”

“Oh, pray, mamma, leave me to myself,” Gwendolen said, with a pettish distress that was near a sob.

And Mrs. Davilow said no more.

When they reached home, Gwendolen declared she would not dine. She was tired; she would come down in the evening, after rest. The likelihood that her uncle would hear something did not trouble her. Whatever he said would be on the side of accepting Grandcourt; and she wished to accept him—if she could. At that moment she would willingly have had weights hung upon her own caprice.

Mr. Gascoigne did hear—not Gwendolen’s words repeated exactly, but a softened, generalized account. Mrs. Davilow conveyed—so vaguely as the rector’s keen questions would allow—that Gwendolen was uncertain of her own mind, yet inclined on the whole toward acceptance. The result was inevitable: the uncle felt called upon to interfere. He did not believe he could do his duty by withholding direction from his niece at such a crisis. Mrs. Davilow ventured a hesitating opinion that it might be safer to say nothing—Gwendolen was so sensitive (she did not like to say wilful). But the rector’s mind was firm: it held to first judgements tenaciously and acted on them promptly; counter-judgements, to him, were no more than shadows passing over solid ground.
