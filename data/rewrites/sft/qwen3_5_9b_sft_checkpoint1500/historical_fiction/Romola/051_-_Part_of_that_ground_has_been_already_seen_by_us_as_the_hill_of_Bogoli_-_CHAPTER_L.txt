That morning, the Piazza was filled with the bustle of the Carnival. Among the throng, a figure approached, instantly recognizable not by the somber black of mourning, but by the vibrant hues of old red, green, and white. This was Tessa. Though she no longer resided on the Hill of San Giorgio following the tumultuous events involving Baldassarre and Tito, she had secured a new abode. Tito, seeking peace for both her sake and his own, had found her a quiet, airy house bordering the vast gardens north of the Porta Santa Croce.

Tessa had not ventured forth without explicit permission. The night prior, Tito had accompanied her. She had suppressed a rising tide of emotion within her breast until she beheld him in a state of radiant tranquility. With one arm encircling the sturdy Lillo and the other resting gently upon her shoulder, he helped steady the toddler Ninna on her wobbly legs. The profound weariness that had previously settled upon his brow and lips when he returned home had completely evaporated. Tessa, quick to master the subtle arts of persuasion required to navigate life with Naldo while retaining a measure of autonomy, understood perfectly how to read the shifting landscapes of her husband's countenance.

Indeed, the charming, gentle-humored spirit of Tito, who had awakened beneath the Loggia de' Cerchi five years ago unburdened by deception, rarely manifested in anyone as fully as in the man currently seated in the straight-backed, carved armchair he had procured for his comfort. In these fleeting moments, Tito felt a swelling sense of relief. He required no guile toward Tessa; her innocence and ignorance rendered her incapable of suspecting his duplicity. The soft, childish coos of "Babbo" were music to his ears. When he contemplated departing Florence, he could never imagine abandoning Tessa and their little ones behind. He harbored a deep, tender affection for these round-faced, wide-eyed creatures who clung to him, untouched by malice. Wherever genuine affection takes root, it blooms like a green leaf or flower, pure and breathing purity regardless of the soil. Conversely, poor Romola, despite her tireless self-sacrifice, inadvertently contributed to the hardening of Tito's nature, chilling it with a disdain that seemed impossible for him to harbor. Yet, Tessa kept the fountains of kindness flowing.

"Ninna is remarkably well-behaved without me," Tessa began, feeling a plea swell in her throat as she guided Ninna to sit on the floor. "I can leave her with Monna Lisa whenever I please. And if she cries in the cradle, Lillo is quite sensible; he immediately goes to thump Monna Lisa."

Lillo, whose large dark eyes appeared even deeper in shade against his light-brown curls, leaped from his father's knee and promptly demonstrated his wit by striking Monna Lisa, who sat at the far end of the room, slowly shaking her head over her spinning wheel. "A wonderful boy!" Tito exclaimed, laughing. "Is he not?" Tessa asked eagerly, stepping closer. "Might I venture to see the Carnival tomorrow, merely for an hour or two? Mightn't I?"

"Oh, you wicked pigeon!" Tito chuckled, pinching her cheek. "Are those your longings? What business does an old woman with two children have with carnivals?"

"But old ladies enjoy seeing sights," Tessa replied, her lower lip trembling slightly. "Monna Lisa mentioned she would like to go, though she is so deaf she cannot hear what follows behind her, and she fears we cannot manage both children."

"No, indeed, Tessa," Tito said, his expression turning grave. "You must not consider taking the children into the crowded streets, or I shall be angry."

"But I have never visited the Piazza without leave," Tessa pleaded in a frightened voice, "since Holy Saturday, and I believe Nofri is dead, for you recall the poor *madre* perished. I shall never forget the Carnival I witnessed once; it was so lovely—all roses, a king, a queen beneath them, and singing. I preferred it to San Giovanni."

"There is nothing comparable anymore, my Tessa," Tito responded. "They intend to construct a bonfire in the Piazza—that is all. However, I cannot allow you to walk alone in the evenings."

"Oh, no! I do not wish to go in the evening. I only desire to witness the procession by daylight. Surely there *will* be a procession?"

"Yes, in a manner," Tito conceded. "As lively as a flight of cranes. Do not expect roses and glittering monarchs, my Tessa. Nevertheless, I suppose any assembly of people termed a procession will delight your blue eyes. Furthermore, there is a structure they have erected in the Piazza de' Signori for the bonfire; you might wish to view that. But return home early, and maintain the appearance of a solemn little old matron. Should you encounter men adorned with feathers and swords, steer clear of them; they are exceedingly fierce and prone to decapitating elderly women."

"Santa Madonna! Whence do they emerge?" Tessa gasped, though Tito's laughter reassured her. "But I shall keep away from them. Only..." she whispered, leaning close to Naldo's ear, "...if I might take Lillo? He is very sensible."

"But who will thump Monna Lisa if she remains unheard?" Tito asked, struggling to suppress a smile while maintaining a serious facade. "No, Tessa, you could not safeguard Lillo amidst a crowd, and he is too heavy for you to carry."

"It is true," Tessa admitted with a touch of sadness. "He enjoys running off. I forgot that. Then I shall go alone. But now, observe Ninna; you have neglected her too much."

Ninna was a blue-eyed tot, at that teetering, tumbling stage of development—a solid, reliable entity akin to a loaded die that invariably finds its base, warranting accurate prediction. Tessa snatched her up, and when Tito finally bestowed due attention on the infant's recent dental developments, Tessa whispered, "Shall I purchase some confetti for the children?"

Tito extracted a handful of small coins from his purse (*scarsella*) and deposited them into her palm.

"That will procure endless treats," Tessa declared, delighted by the abundance. "I shall scarcely mind going without Lillo so long as I bring him something."

Thus, Tessa departed toward the great Piazza where the bonfire awaited. She considered the February breeze insufficiently cold to necessitate additional covering beyond her green woolen gown. A mantle would have been oppressive, concealing a new necklace and a new clasp, mounted in silver—the sole ornamental gifts Tito had ever bestowed upon her. Tessa gave no thought to displaying her figure, for no one had ever complimented it; however, she was convinced her jewelry represented the finest ever worn by the wealthiest peasant woman. She adjusted her white hood to ensure the front of her necklace was prominently displayed. These ornaments, she reasoned, must inspire respect for her status as the wife of a man capable of affording them.

She trotted along cheerfully in the February sunlight, preoccupied with purchasing sweets for her little ones to fill her small basket, oblivious to any observers. Yet, her descent from her upper story into the street had been noted. She was being followed by a man who had frequently waited in vain to determine if Tessa indeed resided in the house to which he had once trailed Tito. Baldassarre carried a package of yarn, a constant employment to earn his meager sustenance and keep the sacred flame of vengeance alive. He had made this detour, as he often did, specifically to pass the house where he had shadowed Tito at night. His prolonged imprisonment had sharpened his timidity and conviction regarding a diabolical fortune favoring Tito. He dared not pursue him openly, fearing exposure to ridicule and a return to prison unless concealed within a crowd or darkness. He felt an instinctive horror that if Tito's gaze fell upon him, he would again be castigated, stripped of his weapon, and imprisoned helplessly. His fierce resolve had become as stealthy as a serpent, relying on a single strike of the fang. Justice remained weak and friendless; he could not again hear the voice echoing promises of vengeance in the Duomo. Though he had returned repeatedly, that voice appeared stifled by cunning, strong-armed wickedness. For a considerable time, Baldassarre's primary objective had been to verify whether Tito still wore armor, for at last, his fading hope would have been satisfied by a successful thrust on this side of the grave. Yet, he would never again risk his precious knife. It was a weary existence, waiting for a chance to answer this question by brushing Tito's back in the crush of the street. Since then, the knowledge that his sharp steel was useless, and that his only hope lay in some novel scheme, had weighed heavily upon his enfeebled mind. A dim vision of securing one of the two wives to assist him haunted him continually, only to slip away again. The wife who had lived on the hill was no longer there. If he could locate her anew, he might seize a thread of a project and forge a path toward greater clarity.

And this morning, he had succeeded. He was certain now of her location. As he walked, slightly bowed under his burden of yarn, yet keeping the green-and-white figure in his sight, his mind dwelled upon her and her circumstances, as feeble eyes scrutinize lines and colors, striving to interpret them into coherent meaning.
