I drifted into consciousness of my own existence, and of a cold so deep, so infinite it felt like a living, gentle embrace. I was flooded with a bliss sharper and softer than any I could have imagined in my earthly life, a blessing I know my fumbling, waking heart can no longer fully recall. Warmth was a faint, half-forgotten memory, no more appealing than a faded dream of summer rain to a man who has never known anything but the hush of snow. The cold had smoothed every sharp edge of care, dissolved every lingering ache, folded every old sorrow into itself until nothing remained but soft, bright expectancy. Not softened, not comforted—sorrow had been swallowed whole, for the life drawing near would restore every good, every lovely thing a hundredfold. I lay still, breathing the damp, rich scent of Earth’s dark, fertile bosom, aware of the slumbering souls of primroses, daisies, snowdrops, curled patient beneath the frost waiting for spring. There are no words to do justice to that frozen, half-aware sleep: no need to stand, no need to strive, only to stretch out and rest. No tongue can tell how cold I was, and every breath drew me deeper into that cold, and every breath made me welcome it more. I grew ever less conscious of my small, individual self, ever more wrapped in a bliss no mind could map, unasked, unearned, mine simply because I existed, and my existence was rooted in a Will that dwelt at the core of mine.

Then the dreams came, crowding fast one after another.
I lay naked on the wind-scoured peak of a snow mountain, white mist billowing below me like a storm-tossed sea. The cold moon hung level with me, and above us both stretched a sky even colder, where the moon and I dwelt as equals. I was Adam, new-formed from the loam, waiting for God to lean close and breathe the first spark of life into my nostrils.
Then I was not Adam at all, but a small child curled in the bosom of a mother whose skin glowed with a whiteness no earthly light could match. Then I was a youth on a white stallion, leaping from cloud to cloud across a blue heaven, riding calm and unhurried toward some unnameable blessed end. For centuries I dreamed—for chiliads? Or was it all one single, unbroken night? Time had no hold on me then: I was far deeper in the land of thought than any mapped seven dimensions, any imagined ten senses. I think I was right where I still am, in the very heart of God.
I drifted through dim, eon-long cycles slumbering at the center of a melting glacier. The spectral moon drew nearer and nearer, the wind’s low song and the roar of a new-born torrent swelling in my ears. I lay still and listened: wind, water, moon, all three sang a hymn of quiet waiting, for redemption was drawing close. Those cycles might have lasted a million years, they might have been one single pregnant second heavy with all eternity—there was no way to tell the difference.

Then, without breaking even a thread of my quiet bliss, every wrong I had ever done rose up around me, stretching far back past the edges of my mortal memory right to the present moment. In every memory of every harm I had caused, my fully awake, conscious self was there: confessing, abjuring, grieving, making full atonement one by one to every soul I had ever hurt, ever offended, ever made carry an unneeded troubled thought. Every human I had wronged was unspeakably dear to me now, and I bowed low before each, fighting to cast the lingering weight of my offence out between us. I wept at the feet of the mother whose gentle commands I had ignored as a boy; I stood burning with bitter shame before my father, confessing the two small lies I had told him, then forgotten for decades, until they had festered in my chest until I could finally lay them at his feet. I was the eager, willing slave of every person I had ever wronged. I dreamed up a thousand grand services to make it right: for one I would build a house such as no hand had ever raised from the earth, for another I would breed horses swifter and more beautiful than any that had ever run across any world. For a third I would grow a garden no spring had ever seen, strung with still, glassy pools and alive with the glint of running water. I would write songs that made hearts swell until they ached, and tales that made listeners glow with unnameable fire. I would twist the raw forces of the universe into new channels of invention that would make them laugh out loud with the joy of unending wonder. Love owned me entirely. Love was my life. Love was, as it was for the One that made me, all in all.

Suddenly I was wrapped in a solid, lightless blackness so thick even the faint ghost of light that lingers in the back of closed eyes could not cast a single imagined glimmer. My heart feared nothing, hoped infinitely, and I was still at peace. I lay there wondering what the first light would look like when it came, what new creation it would drag in with it—when, all at once, without any conscious choice, I sat bolt upright and stared around.
The moon was slanting through the low, horizontal, crypt-like windows of the death chamber, her long pale light streaking across the stone floor. For one wild second I thought it fell across the fallen, ripening sheaves of the Great Husbandman’s harvest. But no. That harvest was gone. Not a single sacred sheaf remained. My dead were all gone. I was alone.

There were depths of desolation in that empty room I had never guessed existed. Had the ripening dead ever been real? Had I only dreamed them, and their soft, quiet loveliness? But then why these cold stone walls? Why these empty couches lined up along the stone? No—they had all risen. They were all out in the new eternal day, and they had forgotten me. They had left me behind, all alone. The tomb was ten thousand times more terrible now its inhabitants were gone. The quiet breathing of the sleepers had wrapped me in their blissful peace before; now I had no friend, no lover left near me. I sat frozen, horror-stricken, for a long minute. Once I had been alone with the moon on a mountain top high in the sky; now I was alone with her inside a vast empty cenotaph, and even she stared wildly about, searching for her own dead with a ghastly, hollow gaze. I sprang to my feet and staggered out of that terrible place.

The cottage was empty. I stumbled out into the night. The moon was gone. Even as I crossed the threshold of the death chamber, a thick wall of cloud had risen up to cover her. But a broad, shimmering glow stretched far across the heath, tangled with a ghostly, murmuring music as if the hidden moon was raining down light that plashed and splashed against the earth as it fell. I ran stumbling across the moor until I found a small, lovely lake fringed with reeds and rushes. Behind the cloud the moon gazed down on the quiet, glass-clear water, still as polished glass. But the soft, musical murmur went on, filling the air, drawing me further onward. I circled the edge of the mere and climbed the low line of hills beyond.

The sight that met my eyes took my breath away. The whole expanse I had once tramped across a hundred times on hot, aching feet, crisscrossed with the deep scored ruts and ravines of the long dry dead riverbed, was now alive with streams, torrents, glassy pools—a river deep and wide, surging full from end to end. The moon flashed bright on the crests of the waves, and the water flashed back its own white sparks, breaking everywhere where it crashed against the rocks along its bed. A great jubilant song rose from its bosom, the song of new-born, unshackled liberty. I stood staring for a long moment, and my own heart began to exult. My life had not been all a waste. I had helped set this river free. My dead were not lost. I only had to follow the water to find them. I would walk and walk and walk, following it wherever it led, until I reached the place they had gone ahead to meet. Our reunion might be thousands of years away, but at last—at long last—I would hold them again. What other reason could the floods have to clap their hands for joy? I hurried down the slope of the hill. My pilgrimage had begun. I did not know which way to turn my steps, but I would go on moving until I found my living dead.

A wide, swift torrent ran at the foot of the hills. I rushed into it, and it did not drag me under. I waded through it. The next stream I sprang across. The third I swam, and the next I waded again. I stopped every few paces to stare at the endless, flashing loveliness of the flowing water, to listen to the thousands of tiny overlapping threads of music that rose from it. Every now and then a clear, perfect melody would seem to pull itself free of the soft, dulcet roar, only to melt back into the unified, thunderous song. At moments the whole world of moving water would wash over me, not to drown me with the force of its seaward rush, but with the enormity of a silence so deep it had turned itself into sound.

As I stood lost in that delight, a hand rested light on my shoulder. I turned. A man stood before me in the prime of his strength, so beautiful he looked as if he had just stepped straight from the glad heart of the Creator, young as the One who never grows old. I stared. It was Adam. He stood tall and grand, wrapped in a flowing white robe, strands of moonlight woven through his hair.

“Father!” I cried. “Where is she? Where are all the dead? Did the great resurrection come and go while I slept? The terror of my loneliness was too heavy to bear. I could not rest without my dead, so I ran from that empty desolate chamber. Where must I go to find them?”

“You are mistaken, my son,” he answered. The soft breath of his voice was pure consolation. “You are still in the chamber of death, still lying on your couch, asleep and dreaming, with all the dead sleeping around you.”

“Alas, how am I ever supposed to know that while I am dreaming? The very best dream feels more real than waking truth.”

“When you are fully dead, you will never dream a false dream. A soul that is true cannot generate anything that is not true, and nothing false can ever enter it.”

“But father,” I faltered, “how am I supposed to tell the difference between true and false when both feel exactly equally real?”

“Do you not understand?” he returned, with a smile gentle enough to slay every sorrow of every one of his children. “You cannot perfectly tell true from false while you are not yet quite dead—nor will you ever need to, once you are quite dead, which is to say, fully alive. For then the false will never come near you at all. Believe me, at this very moment you are lying on your bed in the house of death.”

“I am trying so hard to believe you, father. I do believe you, even if I cannot see or feel the truth of what you say.”

“You are not to blame for that. And because even in a dream you choose to believe me, I will help you. Reach out your left hand, open your palm, and close it gently. You will clasp the hand of your Lona, who lies asleep right where you are, while you dream you are awake.”

I stretched my hand out. It closed around Lona’s hand—firm, soft, deathless, warm.

“But father,” I cried, “she is warm!”

“Your hand is just as warm to her. Cold is a thing that does not exist in our country. Neither of you have reached the fields of home yet, but you are both alive, and warm, and whole to each other.”

My heart flooded with joy. But a sharp, stinging doubt pricked at me a second later. “Father,” I said, “forgive me—how do I know for certain that this, too, is not just part of this lovely dream I am walking through with you right now?”

“You doubt because you love the truth. There are people who would happily believe the whole of life is nothing but a phantasm, if it meant they could live forever in a world of pleasant dreams. You are not one of those people. Be content for a little while to not know for certain. The hour will come, and it will not be long now, when, being true, you will look upon the very face of truth itself, and doubt will be dead forever. By then you will barely even remember what those false phantoms looked like. You will know things you cannot even begin to dream right now. You have not yet looked the Truth in the face—you have only ever seen it dimly, as if through a cloud. That thing you have never seen save in a dark mirror, that can never be known except when its own innate splendor shines straight into pure, unclouded eyes—you cannot help but doubt it now, and you are blameless for that doubt, until the day you stand face to face with it and can no longer doubt at all. But to anyone who has even once seen a shadow of the truth, and even when that shadow is gone, strives to obey it anyway— to that one the real vision, the Truth Himself, will come, and He will never leave, but abide with them forever.”

“I think I see, father,” I said. “I think I understand.”

“Then remember. Hold fast to what you have seen. Hard trials still await you, of a weight and a kind you cannot now imagine. Recall what these things looked like to you, what they meant to you. Hold fast to what you will see in the dreams that come next. Truth is all in all, and the truth of every thing is both hidden and revealed in the shape it takes when it appears before you.”

“How can that be, father?” I said, lifting my eyes to ask the question, for I had been listening with my head bowed, aware of nothing but the sound of his voice.

He was gone. There was nothing in my ears but the roaring silence of the swift-flowing waters. I stretched out my hands to search for him, but no warm answering touch met my fingers. I was alone again, adrift in the land of dreams. I knew I looked wide awake, but I believed I was still dreaming, because Adam had told me so. But even in a dream, you cannot just sit still and refuse to move until the dream grows bored and fades away. I picked up my wandering and walked onward.

I crossed a dozen more channels until I reached a wide stretch of bare rock. There, dreaming I was tired, I lay down and ached to wake up. I was just about to stand and resume my journey when I saw I was lying right beside a dark pit cut straight down into the rock, its mouth shaped exactly like the mouth of a grave. It was deep, pitch black, no bottom visible at all. I remembered the trick I had figured out as a small child, in my childhood dreams: a fall would always wake me up. Whenever I wanted a dream to end, I would climb to some high place and throw myself off, to wake safe in my bed. With one last glance at the soft peaceful sky and one at the rushing waters, I rolled myself right over the edge of the pit.

Consciousness left me for a heartbeat. When it returned, I was standing in the garret of my own house, in the tiny wooden chamber that held the hooded chair and the magic mirror. An unspeakable, blank, bleak despair flooded into me. There was an impassable abyss between me and my Lona now. A distance no chain could ever measure. Space, time, the whole gulf between different modes of being, stood around me like unscalable walls of adamant, locking me away from her. It was true I could still try to pass back through the light-door, and make the journey back to the house of the dead—all that lay between me and that chamber was a wide stretch of heath, and the pale starry night that separated me from the sun, the only thing that could open the mirror door for me, and which was now all the way on the other side of the world. But an immeasurably wider gulf lay between us now: she was still asleep, and I was wide awake. I was no longer worthy to share that holy sleep with her, and I could never hope to wake up beside her ever again. I had done wrong. I had run away from my dream. The dream was no more of my making than my life itself. I should have stayed and seen it through to the end. By fleeing it, I had left the whole holy sleep behind me.

I would go find Adam, tell him the truth, and bow to whatever decree he chose to lay on me. I crept to my bedroom, threw myself on my bed, and passed a completely dreamless night.

I rose the next morning and dragged myself listlessly toward the library. I met no one on the way. The whole house felt dead. I sat down with a book to wait for noon, and could not make a single sentence make sense. The mutilated manuscript on the masked door drifted into my view, and the sight of it turned my stomach. What did the princess and all her devilry matter to me now? I stood up and looked out the window. It was a brilliant, bright morning. The great fountain shot high into the air, then fell roaring back to its basin, the sun sitting right in its feathery crest. Not a single bird sang. Not a single living thing was visible. No raven, no librarian came near me. The whole world was dead around me. I picked up another book, sat down, and kept waiting.

Noon drew near. I climbed the stairs up to the shadowy, silent roof. I shut the door of the little wooden chamber behind me, and turned to open the door out of my dreary world. I stepped before the mirror with a heart of stone. No matter what I did, nothing worked. I pulled the chains, adjusted the hood over and over, rearranged the mirrors a dozen different ways. Nothing happened. I waited and waited to give the magic time to wake up, but it never came. The mirror stood blank, its dim old depths holding nothing but the reflection of the opposite mirror, and my own haggard, worn face staring back.
