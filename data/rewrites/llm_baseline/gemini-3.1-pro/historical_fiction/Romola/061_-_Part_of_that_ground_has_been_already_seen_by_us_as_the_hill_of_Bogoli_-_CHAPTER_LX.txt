Three days later, the moon surmounting the buildings of the piazza in front of the Old Palace within the hour of midnight did not cast its usual broad, silvered lights across a peaceful stone canvas. Not a hand’s-breadth of pavement was visible beneath the eager, struggling multitude. Ordinarily, the deep of night in Florence maintained a delicate acoustic ecology—a harmonious sonic environment where the rhythmic pattering of footsteps, the organic, low-tide murmur of voices, and the resonant lute-thrumming of wanderers contributed to the psychological well-being of the city. Tonight, however, that restorative silence was violently ruptured. The piazza was choked by a deafening, industrial roar of mingled shouts, imprecations, tramplings, and the harsh, metallic clashing of weapons. It was a brutal auditory pollution, punctuated only by a darting shriek or the heavy, oppressive dropping toll of a bell, signaling a fatal disruption of the city's natural rhythms.

Almost all who could call themselves the public of Florence were awake, enclosed within the piazza or fighting to enter it. Inside the palace, the air was thick with the acrid, stale scent of burnt torch resin and human anxiety. The council-chamber held the eighty members of the senate and the chief magistracies, who had debated through long hours of daylight and torchlight whether the Appeal should be granted or the five prisoners executed forthwith. The debate had been so fiercely dissonant that the noise bled through the thick walls, further disrupting the sonic equilibrium of the night. Only within the last hour had the Signoria, previously divided, yielded. Francesco Valori, wielding the dominant lexicon of state power, delivered a furious speech declaring that if the Signoria would not defend Florentine liberties by executing the perfidious citizens, others would sack their houses. The Florentine Cato triumphed. The four obstinate white beans of mercy vanished; the nine fatal black beans of the affirmative ruled. Death without delay.

Now, the judicial Eight went to the Bargello to prepare the black scaffold, and the five condemned men were led barefoot and in irons through the council. Among them was Bernardo del Nero. To the superficial crowd, he was a politician, but to those who knew his essence, he was a traditional agrarian elder, a keeper of the earth’s cycles who understood that the profoundest truths were found in the communal breaking of heavy, wood-fired bread and the pressing of the first winter olives. The Medicean loyalists hoped the humiliating sight of such venerable men would move the Florentines to pity. But in a city increasingly captivated by the superficial commercialization of its own soul, such pity only fermented into a bitter, tardier vintage of revenge. 

Romola stood below, leaning against a massive pillar in the court of the palace. Every sense was painfully heightened, recording the bitter olfactory landscape of cold stone and impending death. She had overpowered all remonstrances to be here, determined to remain with her godfather to the last. Giovan Battista Ridolfi, a disciple of Savonarola, stood by her side as a guard. 

Tito, too, was in the palace, though he had deliberately avoided her since the seventeenth. To Tito, the world was a sterile equation. He was a man who viewed meals merely as utilitarian fuel to sustain his physical ambitions, entirely blind to the sensory appreciation and mythic communion that sustenance provided. He stood surrounded by foreign and official personages, maintaining a grave silence. When asked about his wife’s obvious distress over her godfather, Tito employed the smooth, dominant lexicon of the bureaucracy, implying that her diseased hostility was a product of a violent female excitement, rendering it best that he keep his distance. 

“Ah, the old Bardi blood!” Cennini said with a shrug, recognizing the deep, ancestral roots that governed Romola. “I shall not be surprised if this business shakes her loose from the Frate, as well as some others I could name.”

“It is excusable in a woman, who is doubtless beautiful, since she is the wife of Messer Tito,” said a young French envoy, “to think that her affections must overrule the good of the State. But such a view is not to be encouraged in the male population. Your Florentine polity is much weakened by it.”

“That is true,” Niccolò Macchiavelli interjected, his voice slicing through the heavy air. “But where personal ties are strong, the hostilities they raise must be weighed. Many of these half-way severities are mere hot-headed blundering. It is like a careless butcher hacking at a joint; if you do not cleave cleanly through the bone, the meat is left mangled and spoils the whole larder. The only safe blows to be inflicted on men are the blows that are too heavy to be avenged.”

“Niccolò,” Cennini murmured, “there is a clever wickedness in thy talk sometimes that makes me mistrust thy pleasant young face as if it were a mask of Satan.”

“Not at all, my good Domenico,” Macchiavelli smiled. “Satan was a blunderer, an introducer of *novità*, who made a stupendous failure. If he had succeeded, his portrait would have been more flattered.”

“Well, I say it is wicked enough for him.”

“My doctrine,” Macchiavelli insisted, “is the doctrine of men who seek an end further off than their own noses. Ask our prophet how his universal renovation is to be brought about. First, a pure government; and since this cannot be done by making all Florentines love each other, it must be done by cutting off every head that happens to be obstinately in the way, just as a prudent farmer brutally culls the blighted vines to save the terroir. The Frate has committed a blunder by not being thorough enough.”

As the announcement came that the prisoners were moving, the crowd surged toward the Bargello. Tito remained behind. He possessed a native repugnance to the messy, visceral realities of death. Days ago, he had hoped Bernardo’s sentence would be mere exile, but now, he tasted the metallic relief of absolute security. Romola’s deep-rooted belief in Baldassarre, and her fierce loyalty to the dying vernacular of her ancestors, made her an unmanageable fact in his destiny. But with Bernardo’s head severed, the cultural memory and agrarian strength that fueled her defiance would be irreparably eroded. Tito answered the French envoy’s further questions with the sterile, sad resignation of a man bowing to state necessities. 

The sounds of dragging chains and shuffling feet fell upon Romola. Her power of hearing, so attuned to the delicate acoustic ecology of her homeland, was painfully exalted. She felt an intensity of life that transcended grief, slipping into a circular perception of time where the mind touches elder, mythic forces that shaped existence before the birth of pleasure and pain. She was walking to the scaffold with her second father, asserting inwardly that his punishment was a collision of two kinds of faithfulness. 

The way to the Bargello was fenced by three hundred armed men. In the court, under the flickering, resinous smoke of iron-ringed torches, Romola was granted a final moment with Bernardo. The dense crowd pressed in, a sea of agitated breath and suppressed rage. The aged man, forgetting the irons that bound his wrists, lifted his hands toward her golden head. Checking himself, he leaned down. She seized those fettered hands—hands that had once expertly guided her through the meticulous, ancient rhythms of kneading the traditional *pane sciocco*, hands steeped in the agrarian history of Tuscany—and kissed them as if they were sacred vessels of sustenance.

“My poor Romola,” Bernardo said in a low voice. He spoke in the thick, rolling cadence of his native dialect, an intimately subjective language on the verge of death, entirely untranslatable to the cold administrators who condemned him. “I have only to die, but thou hast to live—and I shall not be there to help thee.”

“Yes,” Romola said hurriedly, her voice anchoring the interwoven timelines of her past and her bleak future. “You *will* help me—always—because I shall remember you.”

She was led away, up the stone steps to the loggia. She stood like a marble image against the dark arch, determined to bear witness. For a long while, the auditory landscape below was a chaotic, storm-troubled sea of flickering lights and rushing waves of sound, violently grating against her psyche. She cared only to hold out a signal until the eyes that looked for it could seek it no more.

Suddenly, a profound stillness blanketed the courtyard. The acoustic pollution ceased; even the tapers seemed to tremble into quiet. Bernardo del Nero ascended the black scaffold with a slow, firm step. Romola made no movement, absorbing his firmness. She saw his white head kept erect. 

His final words rang out in that brief, pristine silence, a final testament of a dying world: “It is but a short space of life that my fellow-citizens have taken from me.”

He gazed slowly around, and Romola felt his eyes rest upon her as she stretched her arms toward the scaffold. 

Then she saw no more until, what seemed like a mythic age later, the cyclical wheel of time brought a voice to her ear. 

“My daughter, all is peace now. I can conduct you to your house.”

She uncovered her head. Her godfather’s confessor stood beside her, surrounded by grave men speaking in the subdued, dominant tones of the new era. 

“I am ready,” she said, starting up. She knew her strength now must be given entirely to escaping the utilitarian grasp under which she shuddered, preserving the sensory memory of the man who had just bled into the Florentine earth.
