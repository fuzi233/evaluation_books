The soul possesses a faculty capable of weaving veils that conceal and illuminate alike, transforming mere contingencies into the grandeur of pomp. Like the moon rising behind a dense, lofty grove on a summer evening, burning with an unconsuming fire that kindles the leafy shadows until the dusky foliage becomes a substance glorious and incorporated with her own radiance, so too does this inner power operate.

Deronda emerged from the narrow confines of his Chelsea lodging with a spirit heavy enough to demand physical exertion, a remedy for the fumes of his own irritable temper. Heading toward the city, the sight of the Chelsea Stairs and the waiting boats instantly resolved his dilemma: he would reject the stagnant passivity of a carriage ride. Instead, he hailed a wherry, seizing an oar to propel himself through the water.

His destination was Ram's bookshop. He had arrived too late the previous day to catch Mordecai during his midday vigil, having been informed that the remarkable inmate of the Cohen household invariably returned between five and six. Deronda felt a pressing necessity to deepen his acquaintance with this enigmatic figure, viewing it as a prerequisite for reclaiming his ring. He feared their conversations would once again terminate abruptly, severed by that fleeting drop of Mordecai's interest akin to a retracting drawbridge, threatening to seal off any future ease of communication. As the rhythm of rowing warmed his limbs and sharpened his focus on the errand at hand—specifically regarding Mirah—he underwent a familiar shift in mental clarity. He moved his perspective from seeing Mordecai merely as a tool for his own ends to recognizing the humanity of the man he had been taunting internally as little more than a recruiting sergeant, oblivious to the dramatic tragedy that supplied his recruits.

"If I secured the information I desperately seek from this man," Deronda mused, "I would be content provided he held no inclination to reveal more of himself, or explain why he seemed to harbor an expectation from me that remained unfulfilled. Such curiosity would likely fade; yet, perhaps they had passed each other like two ships, each carrying an exile who would have recognized the other had they been able to gaze face-to-face. Though there seems little chance of a peculiar bond between myself and this poor fellow, whose voyage, I suspect, is drawing to a close... I wonder if there exists a profound mutual longing between strangers who exchange vacant glances, or even those who secretly crave one another's absence amidst a crowd. Regardless, one creates opportunities for connection by adopting the recruiting sergeant's strategy."

As the wherry approached Blackfriars Bridge, where Deronda intended to disembark, the clock struck half-past four. The gray daylight was dying gloriously, its western clouds fractured into narrowing strata of purple before a vast, spreading saffron clarity. This hue possessed a monumental calm in the sky, yet upon the river, animated by its shifting forms, it manifested as a luminous dance—the alternating flash of ripples, the sudden glow of a brown sail, the transition of laden barges from shadow to color—all responding actively to that brooding splendor.

Now sufficiently heated, Deronda abandoned the oar and draped his Inverness cape over his shoulders. Just as he fastened the final button, his gaze met a well-known face framed against the bridge's parapet, illuminated by the western light into startling brilliance. It was a visage of bodily emaciation and spiritual urgency. It was Mordecai. Caught in his own watch toward the west, Mordecai had spotted the approaching boat, holding it fast in his gaze. First drawn by motion, then seized by a recovering intuition that made him tremble with foreknowledge, he watched until the nearing figure lifted its face toward him—the face of his visions. Immediately, with a white hand raised high, Mordecai beckoned repeatedly.

Anticipating recognition, Deronda had signaled without delay, and the reply came instantaneously. Mordecai lifted his cap and waved it, feeling in that singular moment that his internal prophecy was realized. Obstacles, inconsistencies, everything dissolved into the overwhelming sense of completion flooding his soul with the satisfaction of his longing. His exultation mirrored that of an experimenter observing the first stirrings of change corresponding to what his feverish concentration had previously foreseen. The prefigured friend emerged from the golden background to signal him; this was actuality; the remainder was yet to be written.

Within three minutes, Deronda had landed, settled his fare with the boatman, and joined Mordecai, who stood perfectly still, waiting for him by instinct.

"I was exceedingly glad to find you standing here," Deronda said. "I intended to proceed to the bookshop and search for you again. I was there yesterday—did they mention it?"

"Yes," Mordecai replied, his tone simple yet profoundly grave. "That was precisely why I came to the bridge."

This answer, delivered with such weight, struck Deronda as mysteriously cryptic. Did the peculiarities of this man truly stem from some form of mental alienation, as Cohen had hinted?

"You knew nothing of my presence in Chelsea?" Deronda asked after a pause.

"No; but I expected you to descend the river. I have been waiting for you these five years." Mordecai's deep-set eyes locked onto those of the friend who had finally arrived, filled with a look of affectionate dependence that was both heartbreaking and solemn. Deronda's sensitivity responded fully, unable to deny that this strangely revealed relationship was rooted in an illusion.

"It will be a satisfaction to me if I can prove of any genuine utility to you," Deronda answered earnestly. "Shall we take a cab and drive wherever you wish? You have surely walked enough with your short breath."

"Let us go to the bookshop. It will soon be time for me to be there. But now, look up the river," Mordecai said, turning back toward the water. He spoke in tones of an excited calm, so consumed by a sense of fulfillment that he perceived no barriers to a total understanding between them. "Observe the sky as it slowly fades. I have always loved this bridge; I stood upon it as a child. It is a gathering place for spiritual messengers. It is true, as the Masters said, that every order of existence has its angel—that signifies the full message of each from afar. Here, I have listened to the messages of earth and sky. When I was stronger, I would linger to watch the stars in the deep heavens. Yet, this time, just about sunset was what I cherished most. It has sunk into me, dwelling within me—fading, slowly fading. It was my own decline; it paused, it waited, until finally it brought me my new life, my new self, who will live when this breath is entirely spent."

Deronda remained silent, feeling strangely transformed. The initial suspicion that Mordecai might suffer from hallucinations, or be a monomaniac obsessed with a subject that had strained his diseased organism, yielded to a more submissive expectancy. His nature was too expansive, too willing to conceive realms beyond his immediate experience, to settle quickly on the facile diagnosis of "madness" whenever a consciousness displayed such fullness and conviction where his own was empty. Consistent with his habitual disposition, he found himself inclined to meet rather than resist claims arising from another's need. This claim carried a solemnity that seemed to radiate from Mordecai, utterly nullifying his outward poverty and elevating him into authority, as if he were that preternatural guide seen in universal legends who suddenly sheds his mean disguise to stand as a manifest Power. This impression was reinforced by a resolved quietude in Mordecai's demeanor, born of his persuasion of fulfillment. After a moment of shared silence, Mordecai said, "Let us go now." Once they were in the cab, he added, "We will alight at the end of the street and walk to the shop. You may examine the books; Mr. Ram is departing shortly and will leave us alone."

It appeared this enthusiast was as cautious and attuned to the judgments of others as if he were the antithesis of all enthusiasm, known as "a man of the world."

As the cab rattled along, Mirah remained present in Deronda's mind amidst this strange experience, though he foresaw that the dialogue would be dictated by Mordecai, not himself. He was no longer certain what questions he could pose. Reacting to his own mood, he thought inwardly, "I suppose I am in a state of complete superstition, as if awaiting a destiny capable of interpreting an oracle. Yet, some powerful connection must exist between me and this man, for he feels it intensely. Great heaven! What relation has proven more potent in the world than faith, even when mistaken? Than expectation, even when perpetually disappointed? Will my side of this relation be disappointing or fulfilling? Well, if it is ever possible for me to fulfill, I will not disappoint."

Ten minutes later, the two men, possessing an intensity of consciousness akin to two undeclared lovers, found themselves alone in the small, gas-lit bookshop. They turned face to face, each bare-headed by an instinctive urge to see the other fully. Mordecai stepped forward to lean his back against the small counter, while Deronda stood against the opposite wall, barely four feet apart.
