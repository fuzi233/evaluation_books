To comprehend the magnitude of the moment, one must first grasp the suffocating weight of the silence that had settled over Barsoom. As Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I stood transfixed before the magnificent vessel—a floating fortress of steel and ambition that promised salvation—we watched as a second, then a third, crested the distant hills, gliding with predatory grace to join their sisters. From the upper decks of the nearest warship, a score of one-man air scouts erupted like hornets from a hive, diving in long, screaming arcs toward the earth below us.

Before the dust of their engines had settled, we were encircled by armed sailors, a phalanx of red skin and gleaming armor. An officer stepped forward, his posture rigid with protocol, ready to issue commands, until his gaze locked upon Carthoris. Then, the mask of military detachment shattered. With an exclamation of raw, relieved joy, he surged forward, seizing the boy's shoulders as if anchoring himself against a tidal wave.

"Carthoris, my Prince!" he cried, his voice cracking with emotion. "Kaor! Kaor! Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and of her husband, John Carter. Where have you been, O my Prince? All Helium has been plunged into a mourning so profound it threatens to swallow the sun itself. Terrible have been the calamities that have beset your great-grandfather's mighty nation since the fatal day you vanished from our midst."

"Grieve not, my good Hor Vastus," Carthoris replied, his young face etched with a maturity beyond his years. "For I do not return alone to cheer my mother's heart and the hearts of my beloved people. I bring back also the one whom all Barsoom loved best—her greatest warrior, her savior—John Carter, Prince of Helium!"

Hor Vastus turned, following the direction of the boy's gesture. As his eyes fell upon me, the air seemed to vanish from his lungs; he swayed, a statue of shock carved from marble.

"John Carter!" he gasped, the name tearing from his throat. A sudden, troubled pallor washed over his features. "My Prince," he stammered, his voice trembling, "where hast thou—" and then he faltered, unable to articulate the question that burned behind his eyes. The loyal soldier dared not force from my lips the terrible truth: that I had returned from the bosom of the Iss, the River of Mystery; that I had walked the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus and the Valley Dor.

"Ah, my Prince," he continued, forcing a smile that did not reach his haunted eyes, as though no interruption had marred his greeting. "That you are back is sufficient. Let Hor Vastus' sword have the high honor of resting first at thy feet." With these words, the nobleman unbuckled his scabbard and flung his blade onto the ground before me.

If you possess knowledge of the customs and the soul of the red Martians, you would understand the seismic shift that occurred in the presence of that simple act. It was an invocation older than empires, a declaration that transcended mere loyalty. It meant: *My sword, my body, my life, my soul are yours to wield as you see fit. Until death and beyond, I look to you alone for the authority of my every action. Whether you walk in light or shadow, your word shall be my only truth. Whosoever raises a hand against you shall answer to my blade.*

It is the oath of fealty that mortals occasionally pay to a Jeddak whose character shines with such radiant chivalry that it inspires the fervent devotion of his followers. Yet, I had never witnessed such a high tribute bestowed upon a lesser mortal. There was but one response possible. I stooped, lifting the sword from the earth, raised the hilt to my lips in a silent vow, and then, stepping forward, buckled the weapon upon Hor Vastus's hip with my own hands.

"Hor Vastus," I said, placing my hand firmly upon his shoulder, "you know best the promptings of your own heart. That I shall need your sword, I have little doubt. But accept from John Carter, upon his sacred honor, the assurance that I will never call upon you to draw this blade except in the cause of truth, justice, and righteousness."

"That I knew, my Prince," he replied, his eyes shining with tears, "before I ever threw my beloved blade at your feet."

As we spoke, other fliers darted between the ground and the battleship, weaving a chaotic tapestry of motion. Presently, a larger craft was launched from above, capable of carrying a dozen souls, and dropped lightly near us. As it touched down, an officer sprang from its deck to the surface, advancing to Hor Vastus with a crisp salute.

"Kantos Kan desires that this party whom we have rescued be brought immediately to the deck of the *Xavarian*," he announced.

As we moved toward the waiting craft, I scanned the faces of my companions, and for the first time, I realized with a jolt of cold dread that Thuvia was absent. Inquiry revealed that none had seen her since Carthoris had sent her *thoat* galloping wildly toward the hills, driven by a desperate hope to carry her out of harm's way.

Instantly, Hor Vastus dispatched a dozen air scouts in every conceivable direction to scour the landscape for her. It could not be possible that she had strayed far given the short interval since we last beheld her. We boarded the craft that had been sent to fetch us, and moments later, we were aboard the *Xavarian*.

The first to greet me was Kantos Kan himself. My old friend had ascended to the highest echelons of the Helium navy, yet to me, he remained the same brave comrade who had endured the privations of a Warhoon dungeon, survived the terrible atrocities of the Great Games, and faced the perils of our search for Dejah Thoris within the hostile city of Zodanga. Once, I had been an unknown wanderer upon a strange planet, and he a simple *padwar* in the navy of Helium. Today, he commanded all of Helium's aerial terrors, and I was a Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium.

He did not ask me where I had been. Like Hor Vastus, he too dreaded the truth and refused to be the instrument that would wrench a confession from me. He knew it must come eventually, but until that hour arrived, he seemed content merely to know that I was once more beside him. He greeted Carthoris and Tars Tarkas with the keenest delight, yet he asked neither of them where they had wandered. He could scarcely keep his hands off the boy, his affection overflowing.

"You do not know, John Carter," he said to me, his voice thick with suppressed emotion, "how we of Helium love this son of yours. It is as though all the great love we bore his noble father and his poor mother had been distilled and centered entirely in him. When it became known that he was lost, ten million people wept."

"What mean you, Kantos Kan," I whispered, a chill running down my spine, "by 'his poor mother'?" For the words carried a sinister resonance that I could not immediately fathom.

He drew me aside, lowering his voice.

"For a year," he confessed, "ever since Carthoris disappeared, Dejah Thoris has grieved and mourned for her lost boy. The blow of years ago, when you did not return from the atmosphere plant, was somewhat mitigated by the duties of motherhood, for your son broke his white shell that very night."

"That she suffered terribly then, all Helium knew," I murmured. "Did not all Helium suffer with her the loss of her lord? But with the boy gone, there was nothing left to anchor her spirit. After expedition upon expedition returned with the same hopeless tale of no clue as to his whereabouts, our beloved Princess drooped lower and lower, until all who saw her felt that it could be but a matter of days ere she joined her loved ones within the precincts of the Valley Dor."

"As a last resort," Kantos Kan continued, his face grim, "Mors Kajak, her father, and Tardos Mors, her grandfather, took command of two mighty expeditions. A month ago, they sailed away to explore every inch of ground in the northern hemisphere of Barsoom. For two weeks, no word has come back from them, but rumors were rife that they had met with a terrible disaster and that all were dead."

"About this time," he paused, choosing his words carefully, "Zat Arras renewed his importunities for her hand in marriage. He has been forever after her since you disappeared. She hated him and feared him, but with both her father and grandfather gone, Zat Arras was very powerful, for he remains Jed of Zodanga—to which position, you will remember, Tardos Mors appointed him after you had refused the honor."

"He had a secret audience with her six days ago. What transpired therein, none knows. But the next day, Dejah Thoris had disappeared, and with her had gone a dozen of her household guards and body servants, including Sola, the green woman—you recall, Tars Tarkas' daughter."
