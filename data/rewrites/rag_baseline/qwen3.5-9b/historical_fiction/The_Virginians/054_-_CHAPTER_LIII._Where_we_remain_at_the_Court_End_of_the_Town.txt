George Warrington had repeated the tale to Madame de Bernstein the night before, though the narrative suffered a disjointed rhythm under her attention. The old lady nodded off frequently, her consciousness drifting only when George paused to declare the story fascinating, prompting her to demand its continuation. In response, the young gentleman faltered, his voice trembling, cheeks flushing, compelled to speak despite his reluctance. He spoke far less fluently than he did for his small audience in Hill Street, where Hetty's wide-eyed wonder, Theo's sympathetic gaze, and their parents' gentle expressions provided applause sufficient to sustain any modest youth. Regarding his mother's conduct, the General remarked it resembled Mr. Addison's trunk-maker; she possessed the capacity to elevate any tragedy simply by engaging in the front boxes to weep. This was precisely why they selected Lord Wrotham's house as the theater for George's debut performance, hoping he might speak to advantage rather than to the sleepy, cynical ear of the old lady.

"Very good and most interesting, I am sure, my dear sir," Madame Bernstein murmured, raising three delicate fingers encased in lace to conceal a convulsive twitch of her mouth. "And your mother must have been delighted to see you." George offered a slight shrug and a low bow as his aunt lifted her keen, aged eyes. "Delighted indeed," she continued dryly, "to see you, and to kill the fatted calf—and that sort of thing. Though why I invoke the calf, nephew George, I cannot say; you were never the prodigal son. I may call it a calf to thee, my poor Harry! Thou hast certainly dwelt amongst the swine. Evil companions have stripped the coin from thy pocket and the coat from thy back."

"He came to his family in England, madam," George replied with rising heat, "and his friends were your ladyship's."

"He could not have chosen worse advisers," the old lady retorted, tossing her head. "Had she condescended to write to me through him, I would have informed my sister sooner." Later that night, arranging herself for the rout, she whispered to her maid, "This young gentleman's mother is half sorry he has returned to life; I saw it in his face. Half sorry, and I am perfectly furious! Why did he not lie still beneath the tree? Why did that young Florac carry him to the fort? I knew those Floracs in Paris, under Monsieur le Regent. They were of the Floracs of Ivry. No great house prior to Henri IV. His ancestor was the King's favorite. His ancestor—he! he!--his ancestress! Brett! Entendez-vous?" She demanded her card-purse. "I dislike the grand airs of this Monsieur George, yet he bears a striking resemblance to his grandfather—the same look, sometimes the same tone. Have you heard of Colonel Esmond when I was young? This boy possesses his eyes. I suppose I favored the Colonel's because he loved me."

Engaged in a card party—an amusement she never missed, weekday or Sabbath, so long as she retained the strength to hold trumps or occupy a chair—she soon dismissed her two nephews. To the elder, she offered a couple of fingers and a stately curtsy; to Harry, two hands and a kindly pat on the cheek. "My poor child, now thou art disinherited, thou wilt see how differently the world treats thee!" she declared. "There is only, in all London, a wicked, heartless old woman who will treat thee as before. Here is a pocket-book for you, child! Do not lose it at Ranelagh tonight. That suit becomes your brother far better than it did upon you! Present your brother to everyone, and pace the room for at least two hours, child. Were I you, I would retire to the Chocolate-House and play as if nothing had occurred. While you are there, your brother may return to me and consume a morsel of chicken. My Lady Flint serves wretched suppers, and I wish to discuss your mother's letter with him. Au revoir, gentlemen!" She departed toward her toilette, her chairmen and flambeaux already awaiting at the door.

The gentlemen proceeded to Ranelagh, where only a handful of Mr. Harry's acquaintances were present. They walked the circular path, encountering Mr. Tom Claypool with country friends; they listened to the music and drank tea in a box. Harry served as master of ceremonies, introducing his brother to the curiosities of the venue, while George displayed even greater excitement than Harry had on his initial arrival at this palace of delight. George loved music more profoundly than Harry ever did; he heard a full orchestra for the first time, witnessing a satisfactory performance of a piece by Mr. Handel. A notable instance of Harry's humility and deference to his elder brother emerged: he held George's love for music in high regard at a time when fiddling was deemed effeminate and unmanly in England, and when patriotic prints daily urged Britons to sneer at the frivolous accomplishments of Squallinis and Monsieurs.

Nobody in Britain prides itself on ignorance anymore. Conceit has vanished. Dulness is nonexistent. Arrogance is entirely unknown. Art has secured her letters of naturalization, residing here on terms of near-equality. If Mrs. Thrale chooses to marry a music-master today, her friends need not shudder. If she possessed a good fortune and maintained a competent cook, people would dine with her despite the misalliance, treating Mr. Piozzi with civility.

After Ranelagh, following Madame Bernstein's counsel, George returned to her ladyship's house, while Harry presented himself at the club, where gentlemen assembled nightly to sup and gamble. Naturally, no one alluded to Mr. Warrington's brief absence. Mr. Ruff, his former landlord, visited him with utmost gravity and civility, acting as if no rift had ever existed between them. Mr. Warrington had instructed his trunks and belongings to be removed from Bond Street that morning; he and his brother were now established in separate apartments. Yet, once supper concluded and the gentlemen prepared to ascend to the macco-table, Harry announced he would not play further. He had already burned his fingers and could afford no further extravagance.

"Why," Mr. Morris asked in a flippant tone, "must you not have won more than you lost, Mr. Warrington, after all is said and done?"

"And of course I do not understand my own affairs as well as you, Mr. Morris," Harry replied sternly, remembering the other's behavior upon hearing of his arrest. "But I have another reason. A few months or days ago, I was heir to a great estate and could afford to lose a little money. Now, thank God, I am heir to nothing." He glanced around, blushing noticeably, at the knot of gentlemen—his gaming associates—who lounged at the tables or gathered by the fire.

"How do you mean, Mr. Warrington?" cried Lord March. "Have you lost Virginia, too? Who has won it? I always fancied playing you myself for that stake."

"And grow an improved breed of slaves in the colony," another added.

"The rightful owner has won it. You have heard me tell of my twin elder brother?"

"Who was killed in that affair of Braddock's two years ago! Yes. Gracious goodness, my dear sir, I hope in heaven he has not come to life again?"

"He arrived in London two days since. He was a prisoner in a French fort for eighteen months; he escaped only a few months ago and left our house in Virginia shortly thereafter."

"You haven't had time to order mourning, I suppose, Mr. Warrington?" Mr. Selwyn asked with good-natured simplicity, though Harry struggled to grasp the jest until his brother explained it.

"Hang me, if I don't believe the fellow is absolutely glad of the reappearance of his confounded brother!" Lord March exclaimed as they continued discussing the matter after the young Virginian had taken his leave.

"These savages practice the simple virtues of affection—they are barely civilized in America yet," yawned Selwyn. "They love their kindred, and they scalp their enemies," simpered Mr. Walpole. "It is not Christian, but natural. Wouldn't you like to attend a scalping match, George, and watch a fellow skinned alive?"

"A man's elder brother is his natural enemy," Mr. Selwyn stated placidly, arranging his money and counters.

"Torture is like broiled bones and pepper. You wouldn't relish simple hanging afterwards, George!" continued Horry.

"I'm hanged if there's any man in England who would like to see his elder brother alive," said Lord March.

"No, nor his father either, my lord!" cried Jack Morris. "First time I ever knew you had one, Jack. Give me counters for five hundred."

"I say, 'tis all mighty fine about dead brothers coming to life again," Jack continued. "Who is to know that this wasn't a scheme arranged between these two fellows? Here comes a young fellow calling himself the Fortunate Youth, claiming to be a Virginian Prince and the devil knows what else, getting into our society..." A great laugh erupted at Jack's phrase "our society."

"Who is to know that this wasn't a cross?" Jack pressed. "The younger one comes first. He marries an heiress, secures her, and then the elder brother rises! When did this elder brother appear?
