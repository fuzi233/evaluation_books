As Paul Oleron settled into his new home, a sense of calm enveloped him, like the aroma of freshly baked bread wafting from the kitchen. The old house, with its brick walls bearing the subtle scars of insurance marks, seemed to hold secrets, whispers of the past waiting to be unearthed. His fingers, accustomed to the tactile sensation of pen scratching against paper, moved with deliberate precision as he arranged his belongings, his mind already turning to the task ahead – the completion of his magnum opus, Romilly Bishop.

In the kitchen, the warm glow of morning sunlight danced across the countertop, illuminating the rows of cookbooks that lined the shelves. The scent of coffee beans, freshly ground and brewing, filled the air, a comforting ritual that marked the start of another day. Paul's stomach growled in anticipation as he waited for Mrs. Barrett, the stout Welsh woman with a voice like a songbird, to arrive with his breakfast. Her Merionethshire accent, a reminder of her rural roots, added a touch of warmth to the otherwise sterile atmosphere of the city.

As he sipped his coffee, Paul's gaze wandered to the large closet adjacent to the kitchen, its door adorned with a small, intricately carved wooden handle. A relic from a bygone era, the closet had once served as a powder room, its hatch a portal to a world of elaborate coiffure and powdered wigs. Paul's curiosity got the better of him, and he pushed open the door, revealing a narrow space with shelves that stretched deep into the wall. Amidst the dusty relics, two mushroom-shaped wooden wig-stands caught his eye. How had they ended up here? Had the painters discovered them in some forgotten corner of the house and tucked them away?

The discovery sparked a train of thought that carried Paul back to his writing desk, where Romilly Bishop lay waiting. The manuscript, now nearing its climax, seemed to be taking on a life of its own, its characters and plot twists unfolding like the delicate layers of a soufflé. Paul's fingers itched to return to the page, to coax the story towards its inevitable conclusion. Yet, he knew better than to rush the creative process, to force the words onto the page without allowing them to simmer, like a rich stew left to bubble on the stovetop.
