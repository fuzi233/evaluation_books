The feud between the two girls didn’t last long—mostly because Laura was too eager to forgive, and Blanche’s grudge had never been serious in the first place. Nobody really minds being called wicked; it doesn’t scratch the ego. In fact, Blanche found her friend’s outrage oddly flattering—like a compliment disguised as an accusation. And since they both knew the real reason behind the drama (though neither would say a word), the tension melted away like sugar in tea.

So Laura sighed and admitted that the romantic spark of their friendship had fizzled out—and that Blanche was now just someone worth ordinary regard. As for Blanche, she immediately penned a heart-wrenching poem about betrayal and unrequited devotion. It was the classic tale: love met with indifference, loyalty returned with neglect. She even found a new confidante among the daughters of some London newcomers who’d recently arrived at Clavering Park. Miss Amory, ever the social climber, took this young lady under her wing and poured out all her sorrows into her eager ears.

Meanwhile, the tall footmen stopped coming to Fairoaks with notes for sweet Laura, and the pony-carriage barely left the drive anymore. When Laura did visit, Blanche would adopt a look of tragic martyrdom—eyes downcast, lips trembling—while Laura just laughed and treated the whole performance like a particularly silly play.

But if Blanche found solace in female friendships, she also discovered male companionship that seemed to comfort her just as well. If ever she chatted with a young man—whether strolling through a garden, leaning out a drawing-room window, or pausing mid-waltz—she’d turn on the charm: eyes wide, voice soft, tone full of tender appeal. Then, like clockwork, she’d vanish, leaving the poor fellow to replay the entire scene in his head—just so he could do it again with the next suitor.

When the Claverings first arrived at the Park, there weren’t many eligible men around. Pen had the field all to himself. In Clavering town? No young men. Nearby? Just a few clergymen and country squires with ill-fitting coats and feet like trunks. The Baronet didn’t invite the dragoons stationed at Chatteris—his own regiment, no less—and had left on bad terms over a horse deal, a disputed gambling account, and a scandal involving a white feather and blind-Hookey. But let’s not dwell on the past unless it helps move the story forward.

Then came autumn—the end of Parliament, the London season, and a wave of county families descending upon their ancestral homes. Baymouth, the nearby seaside resort, buzzed with activity. Mr. Bingley’s Theatre Royal in Chatteris reopened. The Assizes and Race Balls drew their usual crowds. And finally, after months of polite distance, the great families began to call.

The Earl of Trehawk, Lord Lieutenant of the County, arrived at Eyrie Castle. The Countess Dowager of Rockminster, whose son was a local magnate, settled into a mansion on the Marine Parade. They made their grand entrance at Clavering Park, and suddenly every noble family in the region followed suit—carriages rolling down the avenue like a parade of peacocks.

And thus began Mirobolant’s golden age—when art could distract him from the agony of unrequited love. The footmen were too busy delivering messages to be seen near Fairoaks. Blanche had new friends, new walks, new places to avoid Pen’s fishing rod. He came every day, casting lines into the stream, but the fish refused to bite, and the Peri—well, she wasn’t appearing.

Now, in strict confidence (and please don’t tell anyone), we must mention the hollow tree. You may recall Pen used it during his romance with Miss Fotheringay. Later, he repurposed it—not for fishing gear, but as a secret post office. Under moss and a flat stone, he tucked little poems and letters addressed to an Undine or Naiad who supposedly haunted the river. Occasionally, he’d find a flower in return—or a note in delicate handwriting, in French or English, written on pink scented paper.

Yes, Miss Amory did walk by that stream. Yes, she did use pink scented paper. But after the aristocrats descended upon Clavering Park, nobody came to collect Pen’s letters. The white notes stayed under the stone, untouched. The tree reflected in the water. The stream rolled on.

There wasn’t much in those letters—just a few words, half-joking, half-sympathetic, the kind any young woman might write. But oh, you foolish Pendennis—if you wanted her, why didn’t you *say* so? Maybe neither of you meant it seriously. Maybe you were just playing at love, and she was playing along.

But when a man is shut out of a game he thought he was winning, he often gets angry. Pen began to take his poems very seriously. He felt tragically romantic again—like in his first crush. He decided he needed answers.

One day he went to the Hall and found a room full of visitors. Another time, Miss Amory was resting—preparing for a ball. Pen cursed balls, cursed his lack of money, cursed his lowly position in the county that kept him off the guest lists. On a third attempt, he found her in the garden—but walking with none other than the Bishop and Bishopess of Chatteris, who glared at him like he’d stolen their communion wine. The Bishop remembered him. Remembered the incident in the Dean’s garden.

“The Bishop says you’re a sad young man,” Lady Clavering whispered kindly. “What have you done? Nothing, I hope, to vex your dear Mar? How is she? Why doesn’t she come see us? We haven’t seen her in ages. We’ve been going about, gadding, so we don’t see neighbors. Give my love to her and Laura. Come to dinner tomorrow.”

Mrs. Pendennis was too ill to attend, but Laura and Pen came. There was a large party. Pen managed a brief word with Miss Amory.

“You never come to the river now,” he said.

“I can’t,” she replied. “The house is full of people.”

“Undine has left the stream,” Pen said, trying to sound poetic.

“She never should have gone there,” Miss Amory answered. “She won’t go again. It was foolish. Wrong. Only play. Besides,” she added, looking him straight in the eye before dropping her gaze, “you have other consolations at home.”

If he wanted her, why didn’t he speak? She might have said yes. But when she mentioned “other consolations,” he thought of Laura—so kind, so pure—and of his mother, who had hoped so desperately to unite him with her adopted daughter.

“Blanche!” he began, irritated.

“Miss Amory!”

“Laura is looking at us, Mr. Pendennis,” she said. “I must go back.” And she ran off, leaving Pen to chew his nails and stare into the moonlight.

Laura *was* watching. She was talking with Mr. Pynsent, Lord Rockminster’s son—grandson of the Dowager Lady—seated in state, receiving Lady Clavering’s awkward grammar and patronizing Sir Francis. Pynsent and Pen had once been classmates at Oxbridge, where Pen had been the more fashionable, perhaps even a bit arrogant toward the younger gentleman. They’d met again today and exchanged that peculiar English semi-nod—the one that says, “You’re here? Well, I suppose I’ll pretend to recognize you.”

“I knew that man at Oxbridge,” Mr. Pynsent said to Miss Bell. “A Mr. Pendennis, I think.”

“Yes,” she said.

“He seems rather sweet on Miss Amory,” he continued.

Laura looked at them. Perhaps she thought the same. But she said nothing.

“A man of large property in the county, isn’t he?” asked Pynsent. “He used to talk about representing it. Whereabouts are his estates?”

Laura smiled. “His estates lie on the other side of the river, near the lodge-gate. He’s my cousin. I live there.”

“Where?” Pynsent asked, laughing.

“On the other side of the river—at Fairoaks,” she answered.

“Many pheasants there? Cover looks good,” he said.

Laura smiled again. “We have nine hens and a cock, a pig, and an old pointer.”

“Pendennis doesn’t preserve, then?” Pynsent asked.

“You should come and see him,” Laura said, laughing. She was amused by the idea that her Pen was a great landowner.

“Indeed, I quite long to renew our acquaintance,” Pynsent said gallantly, his eyes saying clearly, “It’s *you* I want to see.” To which Laura gave a small smile and a graceful bow.

Then Blanche arrived, flashing her most dazzling smile, and begged Laura to join her for a song. Laura, always obliging, went to the piano. Pynsent listened politely until Miss Amory began singing, then walked away.

“What a nice, frank, amiable, well-bred girl that is, Wagg,” Pynsent said to his companion. “The tall one—ringlets, red lips—monstrous red, aren’t they?”

“What do you think of the girl of the house?” Wagg asked.

“I think she’s a lean, scraggy humbug,” Pynsent replied. “She drags her shoulders out of her dress. Never leaves her eyes alone. Simpers and ogles like a French maid.”

“Pynsent, be civil,” the other man said. “Someone might hear.”

“Oh, it’s Pendennis of Boniface,” Pynsent said. “Fine evening, Mr. Pendennis—we were just talking of your charming cousin.”

“Any relation to Major Pendennis?” Wagg asked.

“His nephew,” Pen replied, offering his best manner. “Had the pleasure of meeting you at Gaunt House.” Instantly, the acquaintanceship was sealed.

The next afternoon, Pen returned from a fruitless fishing trip to find the two gentlemen seated in his mother’s drawing room—chatting comfortably with the widow and her ward.

Pynsent, tall and gaunt with red whiskers and a prominent chin tuft, was perched on a chair near Miss Laura. She was enjoying his conversation—simple, direct, slightly humorous, laced with slang. It was the first time she’d seen a true London dandy. Back when Mr. Foker visited, she’d been just a child, and he’d been more boy than man.

Wagg, meanwhile, observed everything. “Old gardener,” he muttered, seeing John at the lodge. “Red livery waistcoat—clothes drying on the gooseberry bush—blue aprons, white ducks—gad, they must be Pendennis’s ducks. Nobody else wears ’em. Shy place for a rising politician, eh, Pynsent?”

“Snug little crib,” Pynsent said. “Pretty cosy lawn.”

“Mr. Pendennis at home, old man?” Wagg asked.

“No, Master Pendennis is agone out,” John replied.

“Are the ladies at home?” the younger visitor asked.

“Yes, they be,” John said. As they walked up the gravel path, Wagg noted every detail: the barometer, the letter bag, the umbrellas, the clogs, Pen’s hats, the tartan wrapper. Old John opened the door. Wagg took it all in.

“Old fellow does all the work,” he whispered. “Caleb Balderstone. Shouldn’t wonder if he’s the housemaid.”

They entered the drawing room. Pynsent recognized two perfectly well-bred women. Wagg bowed extravagantly, with florid gestures and knowing winks. Pynsent ignored the signals, choosing instead to be haughty toward Wagg and deferential to the ladies.

To Wagg, poverty was comedy. He had the soul of a butler who’d been promoted to the drawing room. His jokes were endless, his good nature genuine—but he couldn’t grasp that a gentleman might wear an old coat, or that a lady could be respectable without a carriage or a French milliner.

“Charming place, ma’am,” he said, bowing to the widow. “Noble prospect—delightful for us Cocknies, who seldom see anything but Pall Mall.”

The widow simply said she’d never been to London except once—before her son was born.

“Fine village, ma'am, fine village,” Wagg continued. “Increasing every day. It’ll be a large town soon. Not a bad place for those who can’t get to the country. Will repay a visit.”

“My brother, Major Pendennis, has often mentioned your name,” the widow said. “And we’ve enjoyed some of your droll books, sir,” Helen added—though she hated them.

“He is my very good friend,” Wagg said, bowing low. “One of the best-known men about town. Where known, appreciated—I assure you, appreciated. He’s with Steyne in Aix-la-Chapelle. Steyne has the gout. So does your brother. I’m going to Stillbrook for pheasant-shooting, then Bareacres—where I’ll likely meet Pendennis.” He launched into a torrent of fashionable chatter, naming peers and rattling on breathlessly. The widow listened in stunned silence. *What a man,* she thought. *Are all London gentlemen like this? Pen will never like him.*

Pynsent, meanwhile, was chatting with Laura. He named houses he planned to visit, hoped to see her at them, suggested she spend a season in London, hinted he might canvass the county in the next election, and asked for Pendennis’s support. He praised Pen’s oratory at Oxbridge, asked if he was entering politics too. He spoke pleasantly, charmingly—until Pen returned.

Pen greeted them courteously. Though he remembered a debate at Oxbridge where he’d declared his intention to enter Parliament and delivered a grand speech thanking everyone for electing him—he didn’t hold it against Pynsent. The young man was so open and friendly that Pen hoped he’d forgotten the boastful moment.

Pen adjusted his tone, talked about Plinlimmon, Magnus Charters, the old Oxford set—with easy familiarity, as if he lived among dukes every day.

Then, at six o’clock, Betsy the maid burst in—door flung wide, tray in hand—carrying three tea cups, a teapot, and a plate of thick bread-and-butter.

All of Pen’s dignity vanished. He froze. “What will they think?” he thought.

Wagg stuck his tongue in his cheek, found the tea pathetic, and winked at Pynsent.

But Pynsent saw nothing wrong. People drank tea at six if they wanted to. He later asked Wagg, “What the devil were you grinning at?”

“Didn’t you see how the cub was ashamed of the thick bread-and-butter? I bet they’re having treacle if they’re good. I’ll tell old Pendennis when we get back to town.”

“Don’t see the fun,” Pynsent said.

“Never thought you would,” Wagg growled. They walked home in silence.

At dinner, Wagg recounted the visit with hilarious precision. He described old John, the clothes drying, the clogs, the drawing room, the pictures: “Old man with a beak and bald head—feu Pendennis, I bet two to one. Sticking-plaster portrait of a youth in cap and gown—the present Marquis of Fairoaks, of course. The widow in miniature—Mrs. Mee. She wore that dress when we came, or one made the year after. Tips cut off her gloves—she stitches her son’s collars with them. Then the servant came in with tea, so we left the Earl and Countess to their bread-and-butter.”

Blanche, sitting beside him, adored witty men and burst out laughing. “You’re such an odd, droll creature!”

Pynsent, however, was disgusted. “I don’t know what sort of ladies you’re used to, Mr. Wagg,” he said loudly, “but I never met two better-bred women in my life. And I hope, ma'am, you’ll call upon them,” he added, turning to Lady Rockminster.

Sir Francis whispered to his neighbor: “That’s what I call a sticker for Wagg.”

Lady Clavering tapped Pynsent with her fan, winked, and said, “Mr. Pynsent, you’re a good feller.”

After the Blanche affair, a subtle change passed over Laura’s conversation with Pen. A hint of melancholy, a touch of bitterness. She seemed to examine him—and find him lacking. Her mother noticed the way her clear eyes watched him, the look of almost scorn when he lounged with the women, smoked lazily on the lawn, or lay under a tree reading a book he wasn’t reading.

“What’s happened?” Helen asked. “Something has. Has that wicked little Blanche been meddling?”

“Nothing has happened,” Laura said.

“Then why do you look at Pen so?” the widow asked sharply.

“Look at him, dear mother?” the girl replied. “We two women are no society for him. We don’t interest him. We’re not clever enough for a genius like Pen. He wastes his life among us, tied to our apron-strings. He cares for nothing. Won’t go beyond the garden gate. Even Captain Glanders and Captain Strong bore him,” she added with a bitter laugh. “And they’re men, you know—our superiors. He’ll never be happy here. Why, is he not facing the world? Without a profession?”

“We have enough,” the widow said, her heart pounding. “Pen hasn’t spent a penny in months. He’s very good. He could be happy with us.”

“Don’t distress yourself, dear mother,” Laura said gently. “I don’t like to see you upset. Don’t be sad because Pen is unhappy. All men are. They must work. They must make names for themselves. Look—those captains fought battles. Mr. Pynsent is rich and works hard. He wants a reputation. He said Pen was one of the best speakers at Oxbridge. Pen laughs at Wagg’s fame—and calls him a dunce. Says anyone could write his books.”

“I’m sure they’re vulgar,” the widow interjected.

And so the chapter ends—quietly, like a sigh in the evening air.
