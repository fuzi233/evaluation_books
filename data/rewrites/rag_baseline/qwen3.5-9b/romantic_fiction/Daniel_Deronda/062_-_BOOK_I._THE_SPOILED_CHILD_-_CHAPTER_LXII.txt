The German epigraph hung in the air like a prophecy fulfilled: *Fortune is a light harlot, / And loves not to abide in one place.* She had watched for this moment as the realization of a threat, yet here it was, the familiar sorrow returning with the heavy, abundant luggage of a decade's neglect.

Mirah stepped out of Knightsbridge, the evening air cool against her skin. She had just finished singing at a charitable morning concert in a wealthy house, recommended by Klesmer, surrounded by the usual throngs of admirers waiting to catch a glimpse of the departing company. Her dress was simple black, draped with a dust-cloak, rendering her invisible to the prying eyes of the street. Yet, she felt the weight of footsteps keeping a precise, predatory rhythm with her own. Another kind of alarm, sharper than social scrutiny, tightened her chest. She thought instantly of her father. To turn and face him would be to invite a torrent of emotion she had previously deemed intolerable; to ignore him was impossible. She must wait for the compulsion to strike.

She walked on, neither hastening nor slowing, picturing the man behind her as her father with absolute certainty. Alongside this grim visualization came a bitter regret: she had promised Mrs. Meyrick not to conceal his existence. This regret became a driving force, urging her to intercept the inevitable collision before it caused her brother an unnecessary shock. As she approached the entrance of the small square where her home lay, she prepared to turn, to seize control of the encounter. Just as she resolved to act, a presence slipped to her side, a hand grasped her wrist, and a voice, curled with a persuasive accent, whispered, "Mirah!"

She paused instantly, unstartled. It was the voice she feared most, and the eyes she dreaded. Her face remained grave, composed as if facing an executioner, while his expression shifted rapidly between desperation and a calculated attempt at soothing. Once handsome, with vibrant color, his face was now sallow and deeply lined, bearing the peculiar stamp of impudent suavity that arises when one courts favor while simultaneously accepting disrespect. He was lightly built, active, possessing a deceptive vitality that masked his age; he was barely fifty-seven. His attire was shabby, a mirror of his previous appearances. The sight of this irreverent father struck Mirah with a compounded anguish of shame and grief, repulsion and pity—a mixture intensified now that her own world had transformed into one devoid of the comradeship needed to shield him from scorn.

"It is you, father," she said slowly, her voice tremulous.

"Why did you run away from me, child?" he began, his speech rapid, laced with a tone of tender reproof, accompanied by jerky, abbreviated gestures. "What were you afraid of? You knew I never forced you against your will! It was for your sake I broke your engagement in the Vorstadt; I saw it suited you not, and you repaid me by abandoning me to the ruin that followed. I had secured a better position for you at the Dresden theater; I withheld it to surprise you. Instead, you left me stranded, forcing me to vanish to avoid breach of contract. Hard lines for a man who sacrificed everything to secure an education that was to be your fortune! What father devoted himself more to his daughter than I to you? You know how I bore that disappointment in your voice, making the best of it. When I had no one but you, and was breaking down fighting for my livelihood with my brain alone—you chose that moment to leave. Who else did you owe everything to? Where was your gratitude? For what I cared for, I might have died in a ditch."

Lapidoth halted abruptly, not for lack of words, but having reached a pathetic crescendo. He gave a sudden sob, indistinguishable from a woman's, pulling hastily from his pocket an old yellow silk handkerchief. He genuinely believed his daughter had treated him poorly—a sentimentality natural to those who are unscrupulous yet possess a heightened sensitivity to what is owed to them, ignoring any counter-debt. Despite the sob, Mirah possessed the energy to prevent him from deceiving himself. She answered more firmly, using accusing words for the first time.

"You know why I left you, father; and I had reason to distrust you, because I felt sure you had deceived my mother. Had I trusted you, I would have stayed and worked for you."

"I never intended to deceive your mother, Mirah," Lapidoth replied, tucking the handkerchief away, his voice struggling against fresh tears. "I meant to take you back to her, but chance hindered me at the critical moment, and then news of her death arrived. It was better for you that I remain where I was; your brother could care for himself. No one claimed me but you. A particular friend managed affairs for me, and I sent him money for expenses. There is one possibility, however—" he pivoted quickly to guard against an unlikely yet plausible scenario— "that he lied to extract money from me."

Mirah offered no response. She could not bear to speak the only truth: *"I do not believe a word you say."* Instead, she signaled a desire to move forward, fearing that standing still would attract unwanted attention. Even as they walked, their pairing was conspicuous. Mirah, her beauty highlighted by the quiet, careful dignity of an English lady, formed a strange pendant to the shabby, foreign-looking, gesticulating man beside her. His air possessed an ineffaceable jauntiness, perhaps born of his bushy, grizzled curls, his diminutive stature, and his light step.

"You seem to have prospered, Mirah? You show no want," the father observed, examining her with emphatic scrutiny.

"Good friends who found me in distress assisted me in securing work," Mirah replied, her words automatic as she focused on what she must soon confess. "I teach lessons. I sing in private homes. I have just performed at a private concert." She paused, adding with significant gravity, "I have very good friends who know all about me."

"And you would be ashamed if they saw your father in this state? No wonder. I came to England with no prospects save the hope of finding you. It was a mad quest; but a father's heart is superstitious—it feels drawn like a loadstone. I might have flourished abroad; without you to burden me, I could have rolled or settled as easily as a ball. But loneliness breaks a spirit. I hoped my little Mirah would repent leaving her father when she looked back. I've suffered a sharp pinch to survive; I cannot foresee my next fall. Talents like mine are useless here. When a man is reduced to rags, no one believes in him. I could not secure decent employment with my appearance. I've been driven to depths for mere pennies."

Mirah's anxiety surged at the image of her father sinking further into degradation, a fate she felt bound to prevent. Before she could respond to his fluent, rote-like stream of inventions, he asked promptly, "Where do you live, Mirah?"

"Here, in this square. Not far from the house."

"In lodgings?"

"Yes."

"Anyone to care for you?"

"Yes," Mirah repeated, locking eyes with his keen, searching gaze. "My brother."

The father's eyelids fluttered as if struck by lightning; his shoulders twitched. After a perceptible pause, he asked, "Ezra? How did you know—how did you find him?"

"That requires a long explanation. We are at the door. My brother would not wish me to shut it on you."

Mirah stood on the doorstep, her face turned toward her father below on the pavement. Her heart hammered with the anticipation of confronting Ezra. In this posture of granting permission to the father she had once obeyed, seeing him shrink beneath the indirect demand for entry, she felt a pang of peculiar, sympathetic humiliation—the stabbed heart of reverence belonging to a nature intensely filial.

"Stay a minute, *Liebchen*," Lapidoth said in a lowered tone. "What sort of man has Ezra become?"

"A good man—a wonderful man," Mirah answered, emphasizing each word slowly as she struggled to master the agitation shaking her voice. She felt compelled to prepare him for the total revelation awaiting him. "But he was very poor when my friends discovered him—a poor workman. Twelve years ago, he was strong and happy, planning to go to the East, a place he loved to dream of. My mother called him back because... because she had lost me. He went to her, caring for her through immense suffering, working until she died in grief. Ezra, too, lost his health and strength. The cold seized him on his return, for she was forsaken. For years he has weakened, always poor, always working, yet filled with knowledge and greatness of mind. All who approach him honor him. To stand before him is like standing before a prophet of God." She ended with difficulty, her heart throbbing, "Falsehoods are no use."

She cast down her eyes to avoid witnessing the ignoble look of frustration gathering in his face. Yet, he was swift in invention and decision.

"Mirah, *Liebchen*," he cooed in his old, caressing manner, "would you not like me to appear slightly more respectable before my son sees me? With a little sum of money, I could outfit myself properly and return home as a father should. Then I could seek decent employment. With a good shirt and coat, people would welcome me.
