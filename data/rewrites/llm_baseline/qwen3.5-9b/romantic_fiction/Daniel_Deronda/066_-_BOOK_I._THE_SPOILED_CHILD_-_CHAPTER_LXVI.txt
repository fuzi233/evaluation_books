"One day still fierce 'mid many a day struck calm."

While Gwendolen remained oblivious to the shifting currents of Daniel Deronda's life, two others—Ezra and Mirah—were drawn inexorably closer to him, their relationship illuminated with painful clarity.

Lapidoth had abandoned his daughter at the threshold, driven by a sudden, desperate gamble. The mere prospect of wagering a sum greater than what was needed to quell immediate hunger had seized his mind, erasing all other considerations. Until he was utterly ruined, he never paused to weigh whether he should seek Mirah again or face his son. In that initial panic, he recoiled from confronting Ezra as one might shrink from any unbearable social constraint; possessing Mirah's purse was sufficient to banish thoughts of future need entirely.

The craving for chance is often more absolute than the need for food. Hunger can be soothed by distraction or excitement, but the obsession with watching odds—the mental suspension required by play—numbs all other impulses. In its final, tyrannical stage, it resembles the aimless wandering of demons seeking amusement on the scorched earth of damnation. Yet, even the most abstract selfishness demands sustenance. Though Lapidoth's appetite for food and drink was naturally modest, he had slid into a squalid existence where survival required cash on hand.

When, during a brief stop at a house marked "Pyramids" on the blind, he had doubled and trebled Mirah's thirty shillings only to lose them all, he stepped out with her empty purse in his pocket. Already, his mind was racing between two options: pawn the purse for an immediate stake, or return it to her with a forced smile, claiming he had used the money to settle a debt. Among the few sensibilities remaining in Lapidoth was a stubborn belief in his own rights; he convinced himself that his claim on his children's possessions outweighed the justice of his son's anger. Ultimately, he reasoned that living with his children was the best path forward. The more he contemplated facing Ezra, the less he flinched, his imagination intoxicated by the fantasy of winning money safely without effort, rather than terrified by the threat of private humiliation.

Luck had recently turned against him, but he expected a reversal. Could not the tide turn with an influx of funds from his daughter's affairs or the generous friends she had mentioned? Lapidoth clung to the illusion of his own cleverness—a habit forged by early experience. It is not only women who fail to recognize their fading allure or pretend they are untouched by exhaustion.

His rapid calculations led him to the small square in Brompton. Hoping to spot Mirah entering or leaving the house, he believed his entry would be smoother. But it was already evening—the day following his first encounter with her. After a short wait, weariness took hold, prompting him to consider ringing the bell and asking when she might return. However, as he approached, he knew she was inside; he heard her singing.

Mirah sat at the piano, pouring out the melody of *"Herz, mein Herz,"* while Ezra listened with his eyes closed. Suddenly, Mrs. Adam opened the door, her voice tinged with embarrassment: "A gentleman below says he is your father, miss."

"I will go down to him," Mirah replied, rising instantly and glancing at her brother.

"No, Mirah, not so," Ezra said firmly. "Let him come up, Mrs. Adam."

Mirah stood frozen, fingers twisting nervously, her stomach churning with anxiety. She watched Ezra, who had also risen, visibly shaken. There was an expression on his face she had never seen before: his brow knitted, his lips set in a severe hardness that mirrored the cold fire in his eyes.

As Mrs. Adam admitted the stranger, she glanced at the tense pair. Comparing the younger man to the older, she murmured to herself as she closed the door, "Father, sure enough."

The resemblance was primarily one of outline, strikingly apparent at first glance. Yet, subtle, hidden differences had carved the strongest contrasts into his features, transforming the spirit of a Cromwell within the visage of a man who had once been merely a respectable parishioner. Lapidoth had adopted a mournful demeanor beforehand, but a genuine wince tightened his frame as he spoke.

"Well, Ezra, my boy, you hardly know me after so many years."

"I know you—too well, father," Ezra replied, his tone slow, biting, and solemn, rendering the word *father* a sharp reproach.

"Oh, you are displeased with me. No wonder. Circumstances have been cruel. When a man falls into straits, he cannot act freely, nor can anyone else control him. I have suffered enough, I know," Lapidoth said quickly.

In speech, he always regained a slickness and boldness. Turning then to Mirah, he extended her purse. "Here is your little purse, my dear. I feared you'd worry about that note. I've emptied it; you'll see. I had a score to pay for food and lodging. I knew you'd want me to clear my name, and here I stand—with not a penny in my pocket—at the mercy of my children. You can turn me out if you wish, without calling the police. Just say the word, Mirah. Say, 'Father, I've had enough of you. You made a pet of me and spent your all on me when I couldn't live without you, but I can manage without you now.' Say that, and I'm gone, vanishing like a spark. I won't ruin your happiness again." Tears welled in his voice before he finished.

"You know I could never say that, father," Mirah answered, her anguish undiminished despite recognizing the falsehood of his words, save for his underlying desire to remain.

"Mirah, my sister, leave us!" Ezra commanded, his tone authoritative.

She looked at her brother vacantly, pleadingly. Awe of his decision paralyzed her, yet she could not depart without making a plea for this father, who felt like a part of her own flesh grown through pain. Moving close to her brother, she placed her hand in his, whispering softly—but loud enough for Lapidoth to hear—"Remember, Ezra, you said my mother would not have shut him out."

"Trust me, and go," Ezra said.

She retreated from the room. A few steps up the stairs later, she collapsed onto a seat, her heart pounding. If her brother's words drove him away...

Lapidoth sensed the storm brewing in his son's mind. He began to adjust, seeking a perspective that granted him a cool superiority over any attempt to humiliate him. This gaunt son, speaking as from a tomb, possessed the jarring incongruity that selfishness learns to admire in suffering. Only when the relentless claws of disease seize its own flesh does such hypocrisy crumble. Any sermonizing delivered must be dismissed as routine, like a passerby ignoring the religious wailing echoing from an open cathedral while seeking shelter from a hailstorm. Lapidoth was not born callous; he had cultivated it.

"This home we have here," Ezra began, "is sustained partly by the generosity of a beloved friend who supports me, and partly by the labor of my sister, who supports herself. As long as we have a roof, we will not eject you. We will not cast you out to the vagaries of your vices. For you are our father, and though you have broken your bond, we acknowledge ours. But I will never trust you. You absconded with money, leaving debts unpaid; you deserted my mother; you stole her child and shattered her heart. You became a gambler, and where shame and conscience once resided, now sits an insatiable void. You were ready to sell my sister—you had offered her, but the price was refused. A man who has done these things can never expect trust again. We will share our food with you; you shall have a bed and clothing. We fulfill this duty because you are our father. But you will never be trusted. You are an evil man; you caused the misery of our mother. That such a creature is our father is a brand upon our flesh that will never cease to sting. Yet the Eternal has decreed it. Even if human justice were to scourge you for your crimes and your body failed beneath public scorn, we would still declare, 'This is our father; make way, that we may carry him out of our sight.'"

Lapidoth, preparing himself for the blow, had not anticipated the precise intensity of the lightning or its trajectory—it would not strike around him but pierce right through his frame. He could not foresee the novelty of this voice emerging from the soul of his son. It tapped into that spring of hysterical excitability Mirah had witnessed when he sat at home sobbing.

As Ezra concluded, Lapidoth threw himself into a chair and wept like a woman, burying his face in the table. Strangely, while this hysterical breakdown was an inevitable physiological reaction to his son's words, it also served as a conscious resource. Just as in his youth, when he was a bright-faced, curly-haired young man, he had learned to exploit this finely tuned physical susceptibility to blunt resentment or disapproval.

Ezra sat silently, exhausted by the shock of his own uncontrollable outburst—the release of feelings he had carried alone and in silence for years. His thin hands trembled on the chair arms; he would barely have found the strength to answer a question. He felt as though he had taken a step toward summoning death.
