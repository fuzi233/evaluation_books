Margaret had not anticipated deriving much personal joy from Mr. Bell's visit; she had merely looked forward to it for her father's sake. However, when her godfather arrived, she instantly assumed the most natural posture of friendship. He declared that she possessed no merit in being exactly what she was—a girl entirely to his liking—and attributed her charm to an hereditary power that allowed her to step into his regard and claim it. In response, she generously credited him with remaining so fresh and youthful despite wearing his Fellow's cap and gown.

"Fresh and young in warmth and kindness, I mean," Mr. Bell added. "I must admit, however, that I consider your opinions to be the oldest and mustiest I have encountered in a long time."

"Hear this daughter of yours, Hale! Her residence in Milton has thoroughly corrupted her. She is a democrat, a red republican, a member of the Peace Society, a socialist—"

"Papa, it is simply because I am advocating for the progress of commerce. Mr. Bell would have preferred keeping trade stagnant, exchanging wild-beast skins for acorns."

"No, no. I would dig the earth and grow potatoes. I would shave the wild-beast skin and turn the wool into broadcloth. Do not exaggerate, missy. But I am weary of this bustle. Everyone rushing over one another in their frantic haste to get rich."

"It is not everyone who can sit comfortably in college rooms and let their wealth accumulate without any exertion of their own. Undoubtedly, there are many men here who would be grateful if their property increased as yours has, without them needing to take any trouble," Mr. Hale remarked.

"I do not believe they would. It is the bustle and the struggle they crave. As for sitting still, learning from the past, or shaping the future through faithful work done in a prophetic spirit—Pooh! I do not believe there is a single man in Milton who knows how to sit still; and it is a great art."

"The people of Milton, I suspect, believe that Oxford men do not know how to move. It would be a very good thing if they mixed a little more."

"It might benefit the Miltoners. Many things might be beneficial for them that would be quite disagreeable for others."

"Are you not a Milton man yourself?" Margaret asked. "I should have thought you would be proud of your town."

"To be honest, I cannot see what there is to be proud of. If you will only come to Oxford, Margaret, I will show you a place worthy of glory."

"Well!" Mr. Hale interjected. "Mr. Thornton is coming to drink tea with us tonight, and he is as proud of Milton as you are of Oxford. The two of you must try to make each other a little more liberal-minded."

"I do not wish to be more liberal-minded, thank you," Mr. Bell replied stiffly.

"Is Mr. Thornton coming to tea, Papa?" Margaret whispered.

"Either to tea or shortly thereafter. He could not decide. He instructed us not to wait."

Mr. Thornton had resolved not to ask his mother how far she had progressed in her project of confronting Margaret regarding the impropriety of her conduct. He felt reasonably certain that if such an interview occurred, his mother's account of it would only serve to annoy and chagrin him, albeit colored by her own perspective. He shrank from hearing Margaret's name mentioned. While he blamed her, while he was jealous of her, while he renounced her, he nonetheless loved her sorely, in spite of himself. He dreamed of her approaching him with outstretched arms, radiating a lightness and gaiety that made him loathe her even as it allured him. Yet, the impression of this figure—devoid of Margaret's actual character, as if some evil spirit had possessed her form—was so deeply stamped upon his imagination that upon waking, he struggled to distinguish the Una from the Duessa. His dislike for the latter seemed to envelop and disfigure the former. Nevertheless, he was too proud to acknowledge his weakness by avoiding her sight. He neither sought opportunities to be in her company nor avoided them. To prove his self-control, he lingered over every task that afternoon, forcing every movement into unnatural slowness and deliberation. Consequently, it was past eight o'clock before he reached Mr. Hale's.

There were business arrangements to be settled in the study with Mr. Bell. The latter continued to sit by the fire, talking wearily long after all business was concluded, suggesting they might as well retire upstairs. Mr. Thornton refused to suggest moving; he chafed, finding Mr. Bell a most tedious companion. Secretly, Mr. Bell considered Mr. Thornton as brusque and curt as anyone he had ever met, lacking both intelligence and manners. Finally, a slight noise from the room above indicated it was time to move. They found Margaret with a letter open before her, eagerly discussing its contents with her father. Upon the gentlemen's entrance, the letter was immediately set aside, but Mr. Thornton's keen senses caught a few words exchanged between Mr. Hale and Mr. Bell.

"A letter from Henry Lennox. It makes Margaret very hopeful."

Mr. Bell nodded. Margaret turned red as a rose when Mr. Thornton looked at her. He had the strongest urge to rise and leave the room instantly, never to set foot in the house again.

"We were thinking," said Mr. Hale, "that you and Mr. Thornton had taken Margaret's advice and were each trying to convert the other, given how long you remained in the study."

"And you thought there would be nothing left of us but an opinion, like the Kilkenny cat's tail? Whose opinion did you suppose would possess the most obstinate vitality?"

Mr. Thornton had no idea what they were discussing and disdained to inquire. Mr. Hale politely explained.

"Mr. Thornton, we were accusing Mr. Bell this morning of a certain Oxonian medieval bigotry against his native town; and we—Margaret, I believe—suggested that it would do him good to associate a little with Milton manufacturers."

"I beg your pardon. Margaret thought it would do the Milton manufacturers good to associate a little more with Oxford men. Was that not so, Margaret?"

"I believe I thought it would do both parties good to see a little more of the other—I did not realize it was my idea any more than Papa's."

"So you see, Mr. Thornton, we ought to have been improving each other downstairs, instead of talking over vanished families of the Smiths and Harrisons. However, I am willing to do my part now. I wonder when you Milton men intend to live. All your lives seem spent gathering materials for existence."

"By 'living,' I suppose you mean enjoyment."

"Yes, enjoyment—I do not specify the type, because I trust we should both consider mere pleasure as very poor enjoyment."

"I would prefer the nature of the enjoyment be defined."

"Well! Enjoyment or leisure—enjoyment of the power and influence which money grants. You are all striving for money. What do you want it for?"

Mr. Thornton fell silent. Then he said, "I really do not know. But money is not what *I* strive for."

"What then?"

"It is a home question. I shall have to submit myself to such a catechist, and I am not sure I am prepared to do it."

"No!" said Mr. Hale. "Let us not be personal in our catechism. You are neither of you representative men; you are each too individual for that."

"I am not sure whether to consider that a compliment or not. I should like to be the representative of Oxford, with its beauty, its learning, and its proud old history. What do you say, Margaret; ought I to be flattered?"

"I do not know Oxford. But there is a difference between being the representative of a city and the representative man of its inhabitants."

"Very true, Miss Margaret. Now I recall, you were against me this morning, and were quite Miltonian and manufacturing in your preferences."

Margaret perceived the quick glance of surprise Mr. Thornton gave her and was annoyed at the construction he might place on Mr. Bell's remark. Mr. Bell continued—

"Oh! I wish I could show you our High Street—our Radcliffe Square. I am omitting our colleges, just as I give Mr. Thornton leave to omit his factories when speaking of the charms of Milton. I have a right to abuse my birthplace. Remember, I am a Milton man."

Mr. Thornton was annoyed more than he should have been by Mr. Bell's remarks. He was not in a mood for joking. At another time, he could have enjoyed Mr. Bell's half-testy condemnation of a town where life varied so greatly from his habits; but now, he was sufficiently galled to attempt defending what was never meant to be seriously attacked.

"I do not set up Milton as a model of a town."

"Not in architecture?" Mr. Bell asked slyly.

"No! We have been too busy to attend to mere outward appearances."

"Do not say *mere* outward appearances," Mr. Hale said gently. "They impress us all, from childhood upward—every day of our lives."

"Wait a little while," said Mr. Thornton.
