My hand slipped down from the curtain, yet I must assure you, do not imagine that the sheer, paralyzing embarrassment of my situation occupied my mind. Far from it! The fervor with which I regarded Mr. Godfrey eclipsed all self-consciousness. I never paused to question why he was absent from the concert. Instead, my thoughts whirled around those startling words he had spoken moments ago. "I will do it today," he had declared, his voice thick with a terrible resolve. What, pray, did he intend to accomplish? Was he prepared to apostatize from the faith he had so vigorously championed? Had he truly abandoned us at the Mothers'-Small-Clothes? Was the angelic smile I had witnessed in the committee-room a fleeting illusion? Was the unrivaled eloquence delivered at Exeter Hall a prelude to ruin? I was so agitated by the terrifying possibilities of such a man turning against his principles that I believed I would have burst forth from hiding to demand an explanation. Yet, before I could act, a new sound pierced through the heavy drapes. It was a voice, loud, bold, and devoid of any feminine coquetry: the voice of Rachel Verinder.

"Why have you come up here, Godfrey?" she demanded. "Why did you not enter the library?"

A soft laugh escaped him. "Miss Clack occupies the library."

"Clack in the library!" She immediately settled onto the ottoman in the back drawing-room. "You are perfectly right, Godfrey. This is far preferable."

For a moment, I had burned with feverish anxiety, unsure of my next move. Now, a sudden chill seized me, and uncertainty vanished. To reveal myself after overhearing this conversation would be impossible. To retreat anywhere save perhaps into the fireplace was equally futile. A martyrdom awaited me. With scrupulous justice to my own conscience, I silently adjusted the curtains to ensure I remained within sight and earshot, preparing to endure my trial with the stoic spirit of a primitive Christian.

"Do not sit on the ottoman," Rachel continued, addressing Godfrey. "Fetch a chair. I prefer my interlocutors to sit directly opposite me when I speak."

He selected the nearest seat, a low chair that seemed comically small for his towering frame. Never before had I seen his legs so exposed, nor did he fit the furniture with such evident disproportion.

"Well?" she pressed. "What did you say to them?"

"Just what you said to me, dear Rachel," he replied smoothly. "That Mamma was not well today, and that I did not wish to leave her for the concert."

"That is correct," she confirmed. "They were grieved to see you depart, yet they understood. Everyone sent their love, expressing a cheerful hope that Lady Verinder's indisposition would pass quickly."

"You do not consider it serious, do you, Godfrey?"

"Not at all! Within a few days, I am certain everything will return to normal."

"I share your optimism," she said. "I was frightened at first, but now I trust. It was very kind of you to explain my absence to those who are barely acquaintances with you. Yet, it seems cruel that you missed the music as well."

"Oh, do not say that, Rachel! If you knew how much happier I am—here, with you!"

He clasped his hands and gazed at her. From this angle, his posture inadvertently turned toward me. I felt a wave of nausea wash over me as I recognized the exact same pathetic, pleading expression on his face that had once moved thousands to tears on the platform at Exeter Hall.

"It is difficult to shake one's bad habits, Godfrey," Rachel murmured. "But strive to abandon the habit of paying compliments—for my sake."

"I have never paid *you*, Rachel, a compliment in my life," he insisted. "Successful love may occasionally adopt the language of flattery, I grant you. But hopeless love, my dearest, always speaks the naked truth."

He pulled his chair closer and took her hand as he uttered those words. A silence fell between them. He, who had so often thrilled the crowds, undoubtedly thrilled her now. Then the truth of his earlier statement, "I'll do it today," crystallized in my mind. Indeed, the most rigorous code of propriety could scarcely have failed to detect what he was doing in that very instant.

"Have you forgotten our agreement, Godfrey, when you spoke to me in the country?" she asked firmly. "We agreed that we were to remain cousins, and nothing more."

"I break that agreement, Rachel, every single time I see you."

"Then do not see me."

"Nonsense! I break it every time I think of you. Oh, Rachel! How kindly you told me only the other day that my place in your esteem was higher than it had ever been before! Am I mad to build hopes upon those cherished words? Am I mad to dream of a future when your heart might soften toward me? Do not tell me otherwise if I am insane! Leave me this delusion, dearest! I need *something* to cherish, something to comfort me, if I possess nothing else!"

His voice trembled, and he pressed a white handkerchief to his eyes. Exeter Hall resurfaced in the memory, lacking only the audience, the cheering, and a glass of water to complete the parallel. Even her stubborn nature yielded slightly. I observed her leaning closer, and a new note of interest colored her speech.

"Are you truly sure, Godfrey, that you love me as deeply as you claim?"

"Absolutely sure! You know what I was, Rachel. Let me tell you what I am. I have lost every interest in life except my interest in you. A transformation has overtaken me, one I cannot explain. Would you believe it? My charitable work has become an unendurable nuisance. When I encounter a Ladies' Committee now, I wish myself at the uttermost ends of the earth!"

If the annals of apostasy contain anything comparable to such a declaration, I can only state that the case before me is not recordable in the libraries of my reading. My thoughts drifted to the Mothers'-Small-Clothes, the Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision, and the myriad other societies constructed upon Mr. Godfrey as a pillar of strength. I thought of the struggling Female Boards, which breathed the lifeblood of their existence through his nostrils—this same Mr. Godfrey who had just denounced their virtuous labors as a "nuisance" and confessed his desire to flee the earth whenever found among them! My younger female friends will likely find encouragement in knowing that this display tested my discipline severely before I could swallow my righteous indignation in silence. Nevertheless, it remains fair to admit that I did not lose a single syllable of the conversation.

Rachel broke the spell next. "You have made your confession," she stated. "I wonder if revealing mine would cure you of this unhappy attachment."

Both he and I started. I believed, and so did he, that she was on the verge of unveiling the mystery of the Moonstone.

"Would you think, upon looking at me," she continued, "that I am the wretchedest girl alive?"

"It is true, Godfrey," she admitted. "What greater misery exists than to live degraded in one's own estimation? That is my life now."

"My dear Rachel! It is impossible that you have any cause to speak of yourself in such terms!"

"How do you know I have no cause?"

"Can you possibly ask me that! I know it, because I know *you*. Your silence has never diminished you in the eyes of your true friends. The disappearance of your precious birthday gift may seem strange; your unexplained connection to that event may seem stranger still..."

"Are you speaking of the Moonstone, Godfrey?"

"I certainly thought you referred—"

"To nothing of the sort," she interrupted sharply. "I can hear of the loss of the Moonstone, let whoever wishes speak of it, without feeling degraded in my own estimation. If the story of the diamond ever emerges, it will be known that I accepted a dreadful responsibility; it will be known that I entangled myself in guarding a miserable secret—but it will be as clear as the noonday sun that I did nothing mean! You have misunderstood me, Godfrey. It is my fault for failing to speak more plainly. Cost me what it may, I shall be plainer now. Suppose you were not in love with me? Suppose you loved some other woman?"

"Yes?"

"And suppose you discovered that woman to be utterly unworthy of you? Suppose you were convinced it was a disgrace to waste another thought on her? Suppose the mere idea of marrying such a person burned your face with shame?"

"Yes?"

"And, despite all that, you could not tear her from your heart? Suppose the feeling she inspired in you, during the time you believed in her, was a feeling not meant to be hidden? Suppose the love this wretch inspired in you...? Oh, how can I find words to express this? How can I make a *man* comprehend that a feeling which horrifies me at myself can simultaneously fascinate me? It is the breath of my life, Godfrey, and it is the poison that kills me—both in one! Go away! I must be out of my mind to speak thus. No! You must not leave me—you must not carry away a wrong impression. I must defend myself.
