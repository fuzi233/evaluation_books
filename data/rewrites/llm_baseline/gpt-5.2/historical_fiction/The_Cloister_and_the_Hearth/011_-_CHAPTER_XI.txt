The burgomaster’s look had been like a blade turned in the light—one flash, and Gerard understood it meant to cut him from the world. Imprisonment sealed the lesson: Ghysbrecht was no magistrate to him now, but an enemy with patience enough to wait for dark. So when he slipped along the angle of the tower, he did not truly expect spirits. He expected cord, knife, and treachery—some contrivance set for a man whose escape could scarcely be guessed.

A soft, brave hand slid into his.

Margaret was at his side. Not to counsel him from peril, but to step into it with him, as if danger were a hearth they must both tend.

They had only to clear the last shadow to see the tower itself—and then their steps stopped of their own accord, as if the stones had grown a voice and said, Be still.

Halfway up the wall, something climbed.

It was no man. Its head burned—steady, intent—like an enormous glowworm, a fistful of fire in the night air. The body behind that light was dark, yet its outline could be read in the red spill cast over the masonry: long, nearly four feet, moving with a purpose that made the skin tighten. At the foot of the tower stood another thing: white, upright, the exact semblance of a woman, motionless as a candle set before an altar.

Gerard’s breath rasped. Margaret’s fingers clenched his.

“The rope,” Gerard whispered, and the whisper sounded too loud in the meadow’s hush. “The rope—look—’tis going up the rope.”

Now he saw it: a new rope, running from the window of his late cell down to the grass, taut as a drawn bowstring. The fiery-headed creature climbed by it, and, reaching the window, vanished inward; at once the cell was lit from within, the little square reddening as if a forge had been kindled where his chains had been.

The white figure below did not move.

Awe has its own cold. It benumbs the knees and makes the heart beat against the ribs like a fist on a door. And then—stranger still—terror will sometimes turn into a wild desire to rush upon the very thing that should not be touched, as if the mind would end the suspense by ending itself.

That impulse took Margaret.

She loosened Gerard’s hand as quietly as if she had only set down a cup. Her face was bewildered; then, with a cry that tore through the night like cloth, she darted toward the white shape.

To Gerard it was plain as scripture: the evil one was drawing her to perdition.

He dropped to his knees on the damp grass. The earth smelled of crushed stalks and cold stone, and somewhere, beyond the tower’s bulk, a night-bird clicked and paused, as if listening too.

“Exorcizo vos,” he cried, voice breaking on the Latin he had learned with such care. “In nomine beatae Mariae, exorcizo vos!”

The words came in a rush, less prayer than weapon. And—mercifully—he heard, not a hellish laugh, but a feeble cry: fear, unmistakable, human in its thinness.

Hell, it seemed, had its little weaknesses.

He redoubled his exorcisms; and when he dared look up, he saw the white shape on its knees at Margaret’s, hands lifted not to seize but to plead, and he heard it praying—piteously—for mercy.

Footsteps came—hasty, uneven—Kate and Giles, drawn by dread and loyalty. Their lantern made a small trembling sun, and its flame shook in the night wind as though the air itself were disturbed by what it carried.

They halted, astonished, at the sight of the rope dangling from the prisoner’s window.

“I see how ’tis,” said Giles at once, his mind taking facts as they landed. “Our Gerard hath come down this rope. He is clear. Up I go and see.”

“No, Giles—no!” Kate clutched at him, her voice full of that quick, frightened wisdom that sees a snare everywhere once it has been bitten by one. “Seest thou not ’tis glamour? This rope is a line the evil one casts out to wile thee to destruction. He knows the weakness of each heart. He hath seen how fond thou art of going up things. Where should our Gerard come by a rope? And how fasten it in the sky like this? ’Tis not in nature. Holy saints protect us, for hell is abroad.”

“Stuff,” said the dwarf, with the plain logic of a body made for climbing. “The way to hell is down, and this rope leads up. I never had the luck to go up such a long rope. It may be years ere I fall in with so long a rope all ready for me. As well be knocked on the head at once as never know happiness.”

And he sprang to it with a little cry of delight, as a cat will leap to a board where fish lie cooling—smell and promise in one. His huge arms took hold; his legs, light as feathers, began to work; and up he went so fast it seemed the rope itself drew him.

Kate’s fear fought her love. She could win only one concession.

“Stay—let me fasten the lantern on thy neck.” Her hands shook, but her knot was sure. “A light scares ill spirits.”

So the lantern hung at the nape of Giles’s neck, and he became, in the night’s eye, the very glowworm they had seen: a moving head of fire climbing the tower.

Kate watched him, trembling so that even her crutches quivered under her. Then, suddenly, the white figure did what stone cannot do: it seemed to start from the solid masonry itself and flew at her with more than mortal speed.

Kate’s cry was small, strangled by terror; her tongue clove to her palate. She dropped her crutches. Down she sank on her knees, hiding her face, moaning as if bargaining with the dark.

“Take my body,” she sobbed, “but spare my soul!”

Margaret, panting from her rush and her fear, stopped short.

“Why—it is a woman!”

Kate, peering through her fingers, voice quivering with the same astonishment: “Why—it is a woman!”

“How you scared me!” Margaret gasped, and her hand went to her breast as if to keep her heart from leaping out.

“I am scared enough myself,” Kate answered, tears breaking loose. “Oh! oh! oh!”

The two stood close in the lantern’s weak circle, women made suddenly sisters by fright. Above them the rope creaked softly, and the tower’s stones drank the light without giving any back. Far off, a dog barked once and was silent; the night seemed to measure every sound as carefully as a cook measures salt, for too much would spoil the whole.

“This is strange,” Kate whispered, forcing her mind to order the pieces. “But the fiery-headed thing? Yet it was with you, and you are harmless! Why are you here at this time o’ night?”

“Nay—why are you?” Margaret returned, and then, as the answer shaped itself, her eyes widened. “Perhaps we are on the same errand. Ah—you are his good sister, Kate.”

“And you are Margaret Brandt.”

“Yes.”

“All the better,” Kate breathed, relief and fear mingled. “You love him. You are here. Then Giles was right. He has won free.”

Gerard stepped forward then, his very presence like a door opened onto certainty. The sight of him drew a sound from Kate that was half sob, half laugh strangled in her throat. For an instant the tower and its trickery—if trickery it was—fell away, and there was only family, only the sudden hunger of the heart satisfied by the plain fact of a loved one breathing in front of you.

But the night had not done with them.

From above came a noise that made the hair lift at the nape: hollow and unearthly, like a voice thrown up out of a grave, a sepulchre made ventriloquist.

“PARCHMENT!—PARCHMENT!—PARCHMENT!”

Each time it was louder, as if the tower itself learned the word and enjoyed it.

They stared upward, and there was Giles at the window, both hands crammed with rolled skins, his face lit from below by the lantern at his neck. The light turned him into a little demon in a glass shrine—lurid, transparent at the edges, his grin too wide for any saint’s carving.

With a shout of “PARCHMENT!” he hurled the first bundle down.

They came like wounded wild-ducks: some dropping in a dead collapse, others fluttering and catching air, others spreading and wheeling slowly, pale as bleached bones as they spiraled toward the grass. They slapped down, one upon another, whitening the dark ground.

Again: “PARCHMENT—PARCHMENT!”

Another flock fell. Then another. The meadow filled with records and skins—things that had once lain in quiet chests, smelling of calf and smoke and old ink, the slow labor of scribes and clerks turned suddenly into a storm.

At last Giles slid down the rope like a falling star, his light racing after him, and landed with the briskness of one who has done a night’s work and is hungry for his pay. Business before sentiment, his concave claw went out.

“Settle,” he said, eyes bright, “for the merchandise I delivered.”

“Hush,” Gerard snapped, listening to the night as if it were an enemy with ears. “Thou speakest too loud. Gather them up, and follow us to a safer place than this.”

Kate, still kneeling, lifted her face. “Wilt thou come home with me, Gerard?”

“I have no home.”

“You shall not say so,” she pleaded, pain and devotion in her voice. “Who is more welcome than thou wilt be, after this cruel wrong, to thy father’s house?”

“Father!” Gerard answered, and the word came out like a bitter herb crushed between the teeth. “I have no father. He that was my father is turned my gaoler. I have escaped from his hands; I will never come within their reach again.”

“An enemy did this,” Kate insisted, “and not our father.” And in a rush she told him what she had overheard Cornelis and Sybrandt say—how those two had put their tongues to the burgomaster’s ear and their malice into the town’s law.

But the wound was too fresh. Gerard’s indignation, long held like a simmering pot, boiled over at last.

“Cornelis and Sybrandt are ill curs,” he said, voice low and fierce, “that have shown me their teeth this long while. But they could do no more. My father it is that gave the burgomaster authority, or he durst not have laid a finger on me, that am a free burgher of this town. So be it, then. I was his son. I am his prisoner. He hath played his part; I shall play mine. Farewell the burgh where I was born and lived honestly and was put in prison. While there is another town left in creation, I’ll never trouble you again, Tergou.”

“Gerard—Gerard,” Kate sobbed, reaching toward him and finding only air.

Margaret leaned close to Kate, whispering with the quick, practical tenderness of a woman who knows when words will scald. “Do not gainsay him now. Give his choler time to cool.”

Kate turned, searching Margaret’s face as though reading a recipe by poor light—looking for the true measure of her. “Let me look at thee.”

Whatever she found there eased her; she whispered, “’Tis a comely face—and no mischief-maker’s.”

“Fear me not,” Margaret murmured back. “I could not be happy without your love, as well as Gerard’s.”

“These are comfortable words,” Kate said, and the sob in them was softer now. Then, with a sudden honesty that made Margaret’s throat tighten, she said, “I little thought to like thee so well. My heart is willing, but my infirmity will not let me embrace thee.”

At that, Margaret went to her and wound her arms gently round the slight, hampered body, and kissed her with the warmth of a promise. “Often he hath spoken of thee to me, Kate; and often I longed for this.”

“You, too, Gerard,” Kate said, turning her cheek to him. “Kiss me ere you go; for my heart lies heavy at parting with you this night.”

Gerard bent and kissed her—briefly, as men do when they are trying not to feel too much—and Kate took up her crutches again. She went homeward, tapping softly into the dark; the last thing they heard of her was a little patient sigh, as if the night itself had exhaled through her.

Then tears stood thick in Margaret’s eyes. She watched the black lane swallow Kate’s small figure. Gerard, already facing the road to Sevenbergen, did not notice the sigh; the noise of his own anger filled his ears.

They turned to go. Behind them, Giles nudged Gerard with his bundle of parchments and held out his claw again, greedy as hunger.

Margaret caught Gerard’s sleeve. “Nay—why take what is not ours?”

“Oh, spoil an enemy how thou canst.”

“But may they not make it a handle for fresh violence?”

“How can they?” Gerard said harshly. “Thinkest thou I shall stay in Tergou after this? The burgomaster robbed me of my liberty. I doubt I should take his life for it, if I could.”

“Oh, fie, Gerard.”

“What!” he flung back. “Is life worth more than liberty? Well—I can’t take his life, so I take the first thing that comes to hand.”

He tossed Giles a few small coins. The urchin’s face lit as if the lantern had been moved into it; he clutched the money, then his parchments, and shuffled away after his sister, the tower’s shadow swallowing his small, eager shape.

Martin joined them as they made for Sevenbergen, stepping into their company like a stitch drawn through cloth—quiet, firm, binding their flight together. And away they went, leaving the haunted tower behind them with its rope and its pale papers strewn like spilled flour on the grass, while the night’s sounds closed in again: the whisper of leaves, the far-off bark, the thin creak of wood, and the steady, living music of the road under their feet.
