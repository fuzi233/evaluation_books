In every household that has ever hung a print of Apollo over the mantel or kept a cracked gilt frame for the sake of what it once held, there is a quiet superstition: that Art is a kind of inheritance. You are “born to it,” people say, as if genius were a field you might walk into at will, with a deed in your pocket and a gate that will open because you are handsome, or well-bred, or praised at dinner.

But Art is not a landed estate. It is more like a bread you do not get by wanting it, nor by imagining its taste, but by hard daily work at the kneading-trough—by learning the exact temper of water and yeast, the stubbornness of flour that will not be bullied into smoothness, the discipline of heat that gives nothing to vanity. It is prophecy without possession until you have earned the loaf with burned fingers and a watchful ear.

The beliefs we carry most obstinately are often those we have never tested—beliefs that live in us like a pulse, self-fed, asking no evidence beyond our own pleasure in feeling them. And there are young creatures, especially, who have been so consistently applauded for being themselves that they cannot imagine the world asking for anything else.

The next morning was Sunday, and the air had the clean, mild edge of autumn—sunlight thinned by a veil of melancholy, lying in slanting bars on the half-strewn grass and entering the drawing-room like a benediction that had no words. The house was quiet in that particular way country houses are quiet on a Sunday: not silence, but a soft arrangement of sounds—the distant rustle of skirts, a door cushioned in its closing, the small click of china somewhere far off, and then the faint, steady hush that comes when a family is preparing itself to be seen as orderly.

“Pray go to church, mamma,” Gwendolen said, with that tone which made a request a decree. “I prefer seeing Herr Klesmer alone.”

Mrs. Davilow’s anxious face took on the look of one who hears an instrument slightly out of tune and cannot bear it. “That is hardly correct, I think.”

“Our affairs are too serious for us to think of such nonsensical rules,” said Gwendolen, and her contempt fell like salt on a wound. “They are insulting as well as ridiculous.”

“You would not mind Isabel sitting with you? She would be reading in a corner.”

“No; she could not. She would bite her nails and stare. It would be too irritating. Trust my judgment, mamma—I must be alone. Take them all to church.”

She had her way, of course. Yet decorum—like a cook who cannot help saving the drippings—found a compromise: Miss Merry and two of the girls remained at home, planted as if by design at the dining-room windows, to give the place the look of habitation.

Gwendolen stayed in the drawing-room where so many of her triumphs had been staged in miniature. The old furniture received the sun as if it had known it longer than any living face: tapestried chairs with their faded flower-wreaths; dark pictures that looked back with an enigmatic patience; that superannuated organ at which she had once pleased herself with acting Saint Cecilia, making religion into applause; and, through open doors, the pale, dusty knick-knacks of the antechamber—witnesses of her earlier play at being Hermione in a Greek dress. That memory was busy now, not for its own charm but because Herr Klesmer had been struck then, had admired her pose and expression. Whatever he had said, and whatever she had decided he must have thought, was now sharpened into importance, like a remembered aroma which one believes will decide whether a dish succeeds or fails.

For perhaps she had never before been so inwardly dependent—so consciously in need of another person’s opinion. Something had altered in her self-estimate: a new fluttering, a new deliberation, as if the easy certainty with which she had always judged herself were no longer sufficient. Yet the refrain within her was still that he had seen so little of her; any unfavorable conclusion must rest on too narrow a base. She felt clever enough for anything. She only needed the world to agree.

To occupy the minutes before eleven, she gathered her volumes and pieces of music, and laid them in a neat heap on the piano, setting herself to classify them with an air of method, as if order on a polished surface could command order in a life. And then, catching her own image in the glass panel that reflected the room, she was drawn toward it—as many women are drawn, not by vanity but by the hunger for confirmation.

She wore black, without ornament. The warm whiteness of her skin was set like cream against a tawny crust—between her light-brown coronet of hair and the square-cut bodice. The effect was severe and antique: a statue in black, white, and tawny marble. Watching the reflection advance, she thought, “I am beautiful”—not with a laugh, not even with exultation, but with a grave decision. Beauty was the one claim she needed others to attest. If anyone objected to the turn of her nose or the line of her chin, she could not answer as she might answer any criticism of skill by showing she could learn; there is no practice that reshapes bone.

The sound of wheels came at last, then the loud ring, and the opening doors—each noise distinctly placed in the house’s acoustic map, each a signal that her privacy was about to be entered. The arrival slightly increased her flutter. For all her confidence, she dreaded Herr Klesmer as one dreads a strong acid: something of the world that does not melt away because you smile at it, that burns in spite of your will. Poor Gwendolen—she was at a higher crisis of her woman’s fate than in that last experience which had revolved around Grandcourt. Then, the question had been whether she would take a particular man as husband. Now, more deeply folded within her, was the question whether she need take a husband at all—whether ambition might be satisfied without bondage, whether she might win bread and praise by herself.

Herr Klesmer entered with his most deferential bow in the wide doorway of the antechamber—deference conveyed not only by manner but by the perfect gloves and finest gray kerseymere trousers, as if even genius knew the value of exact finish. Gwendolen met him with unusual gravity, holding out her hand.

“It is most kind of you to come, Herr Klesmer. I hope you have not thought me presumptuous.”

“I took your wish as a command that did me honor,” he said. His gravity answered hers, yet there was in his tone a slight edge, as if his own feelings—quickened by yesterday—had not yet settled into repose.

For once, Gwendolen was too strained to remember social flourishes. She remained standing near the piano; he took his stand at the other end, his back to the light, his terribly omniscient eyes on her. There was no use in prettiness now, no room for charade. She began at once.

“I wish to consult you, Herr Klesmer. We have lost all our fortune; we have nothing. I must get my own bread, and I desire to provide for my mamma, so as to save her from any hardship. The only way I can think of—and I should like it better than anything—is to be an actress—to go on the stage. But, of course, I should like to take a high position, and I thought—if you thought I could”—her voice faltered, not from timidity but from the effort of holding herself steady—“it would be better for me to be a singer—to study singing also.”

He set his hat down on the piano and folded his arms as if to compress his attention into a smaller space.

“I know,” she continued, cheeks flushing and paling in turns, “I know that my method of singing is very defective; but I have been ill taught. I could be better taught; I could study. And you will understand my wish—to sing and act too, like Grisi, is a much higher position. Naturally, I should wish to take as high rank as I can. And I can rely on your judgment. I am sure you will tell me the truth.”

She had always believed truth, when solemnly requested, would come dressed in favor.

But he did not speak. He drew off his gloves quickly, tossed them into his hat, rested his hands on his hips, and walked to the other end of the room. Compassion was in him—more than she could imagine—yet he needed to lay a guard on his speech, as a careful cook guards heat from scorching what is delicate. Turning again, he looked at her with a mild frown of inquiry.

“You have never seen anything, I think, of artists and their lives?—I mean of musicians, actors, artists of that kind?”

“Oh, no,” said Gwendolen, not abashed by the bareness of the fact. She had been well provided for; ignorance was one of the luxuries of her condition.

“You are—pardon me,” he said, pausing again near the piano, “in coming to a conclusion on such a matter as this, everything must be taken into consideration—you are perhaps twenty?”

“I am twenty-one,” she answered quickly. A small fear rose like a sudden cold draught. “Do you think I am too old?”

He pouted his under lip and shook his long fingers upward in a gesture that was enigmatic as a foreign idiom one cannot translate.

“Many persons begin later than others,” Gwendolen said, instinctively offering information as if she were still in a room where her thoughts were welcomed for their own sake.

He took no notice. His gentleness became studied, as if he were choosing words that might not bruise.

“You have probably not thought of an artistic career until now: you did not entertain the notion, the longing—what shall I say?—you did not wish yourself an actress, or anything of that sort, till the present trouble?”

“Not exactly: but I was fond of acting. I have acted; you saw me, if you remember—you saw me here in charades, and as Hermione.” Her anxiety clung to that moment when she had felt herself most vivid, most applauded.

“Yes, yes,” he said quickly, “I remember—I remember perfectly.” And again he moved away, as if his body must pace while his mind argued.

She felt she was being weighed, and the delay was like the long pause before a dish is tasted by a judge. Yet she did not conceive the verdict could be truly against her. To seem graceful, she said, “I shall be very much obliged to you for taking the trouble to give me your advice, whatever it may be.”

“Miss Harleth,” he began at last, and there was a solemnity in him that made the room feel colder, “I will veil nothing from you in this matter. I should reckon myself guilty if I put a false visage on things—made them too black or too white. The gods have a curse for him who willingly tells another the wrong road. And if I misled one who is so young, so beautiful—who, I trust, will find her happiness along the right road—I should regard myself as a—Bösewicht.”

The German word fell with a loud whisper, heavy with meaning that was not quite hers; and she felt, in that moment, how much of experience remains untranslatable—how a single foreign syllable can carry a whole cultural weight that an English equivalent cannot lift.

Her heart sank under the unexpected gravity. She watched his face as one watches a knife that will either slice bread or cut flesh.

“You are a beautiful young lady,” he went on, “you have been brought up in ease—you have done what you would—you have not said to yourself, ‘I must know this exactly,’ ‘I must understand this exactly,’ ‘I must do this exactly,’”—with each terrible must he lifted a long finger in succession, like marks on a ledger. “In sum, you have not been called upon to be anything but a charming young lady, whom it is an impoliteness to find fault with.”

He paused only an instant, then thrust out his powerful chin.

“Well, then, with that preparation, you wish to try the life of an artist; you wish to try a life of arduous, unceasing work, and—uncertain praise. Your praise would have to be earned, like your bread; and both would come slowly, scantily—what do I say?—they may hardly come at all.”

The discouragement pricked her into resistance. She turned her head slightly away, with an air of pique.

“I thought that you, being an artist, would consider the life one of the most honorable and delightful. And if I can do nothing better?—I suppose I can put up with the same risks as other people do.”

“Do nothing better?” His voice sharpened with sudden fire. “No, my dear Miss Harleth, you could do nothing better—neither man nor woman could do anything better—if you could do what was best or good of its kind. I am not decrying the life of the true artist. I am exalting it. I say, it is out of the reach of any but choice organizations—natures framed to love perfection and to labor for it; ready, like all true lovers, to endure, to wait, to say, I am not yet worthy, but she—Art, my mistress—is worthy, and I will live to merit her. An honorable life? Yes. But the honor comes from the inward vocation and the hard-won achievement: there is no honor in donning the life as a livery.”

His fervor, which might have seemed like a halo over the subject to another girl, did not yet strike Gwendolen as exclusion. She heard only that excellence existed and that honor belonged to it; she did not quite hear him saying she was not made for it. Besides, she had always believed herself able to learn whatever she chose, and the notion that she could simply step upon a public stage and produce the effect she produced in drawing-rooms was in her like blood: you could not draw it out without pain.

“I am quite prepared to bear hardships at first,” she said, insisting. “Of course no one can become celebrated all at once. And it is not necessary that every one should be first-rate—either actresses or singers. If you would be so kind as to tell me what steps I should take, I shall have the courage to take them. I don’t mind going up hill. It will be easier than the dead level of being a governess. I will take any steps you recommend.”

He understood then that delicacy would only leave her with false hopes. His plainness came as a knife comes when one must cut away what is rotten.

“I will tell you the steps,” he said, “not that I recommend, but that will be forced upon you. It is all one, so far, what your goal will be—excellence, celebrity, second, third rateness—it is all one. You must go to town under the protection of your mother. You must put yourself under training—musical, dramatic, theatrical: whatever you desire to do you have to learn”—she looked as if she would interrupt, but he lifted his hand decisively—“I know. You have exercised your talents—you recite—you sing—from the drawing-room Standpunkt. My dear Fräulein, you must unlearn all that. You have not yet conceived what excellence is: you must unlearn your mistaken admirations. You must know what you have to strive for, and then you must subdue your mind and body to unbroken discipline. Your mind, I say. For you must not be thinking of celebrity: put that candle out of your eyes, and look only at excellence. You would of course earn nothing—you could get no engagement for a long while. You would need money for yourself and your family. But that”—here his frown deepened as if to dismiss a trifling ingredient in a larger recipe—“that could perhaps be found.”

Her pride winced as if the edge had been set deliberately against it. The last sentence stung worst, because it hinted at dependence—at charity disguised as practicality. She felt herself flushing and paling again, and hated being seen moved. Seeking refuge, she walked suddenly to a seat and pointed out a chair to him.

He did not sit. He turned slightly to face her and leaned against the piano, a posture of firmness, as if he meant to keep the weight of his counsel steady.

In that instant she wished she had never sent for him. In all her life, she had rarely been met on any ground but rank and beauty; now she was being handled like an incompetent apprentice, and the bitterness rose to her throat.

“Now,” he went on, still with that grave, unaltered tone, “what sort of issue might be fairly expected from all this self-denial? You would ask that. It is right that your eyes should be open to it. I will tell you truthfully. This issue would be uncertain, and, most probably, would not be worth much.”

He looked at her through his spectacles with a monstrous impartiality, as if beauty were a thing as irrelevant as gilt on a cracked plate.

Her eyes began to burn, but dread of weakness drove her into harder self-control. She forced herself to speak in a dry tone.

“You think I want talent, or am too old to begin.”

He made a sort of hum, then came down emphatically.

“Yes! The desire and the training should have begun seven years ago—or a good deal earlier. A mountebank’s child who helps her father to earn shillings when she is six years old—a child that inherits a singing throat from a long line of choristers and learns to sing as it learns to talk, has a likelier beginning. Any great achievement in acting or in music grows with the growth. Whenever an artist has been able to say, ‘I came, I saw, I conquered,’ it has been at the end of patient practice. Genius at first is little more than a great capacity for receiving discipline. Singing and acting, like the fine dexterity of the juggler with his cups and balls, require a shaping of the organs toward a finer and finer certainty of effect. Your muscles—your whole frame—must go like a watch, true, true to a hair. That is the work of spring-time, before habits have been determined.”

“I did not pretend to genius,” she said, still clinging to the belief that some opening must remain. “I only suppose that I might have a little talent—enough to improve.”

“I don’t deny that,” he answered. “If you had been put in the right track some years ago and had worked well you might now have made a public singer, though I don’t think your voice would have counted for much in public. For the stage your personal charms and intelligence might then have told without the present drawback of inexperience—lack of discipline—lack of instruction.”

He sounded cruel; but his impulse was compassion, sharpened by knowledge. He saw, with a painful definiteness, the humiliations, the long rehearsals, the cheap lodgings, the harsh voices of drill and correction, the auditory assaults of a world that makes its living amid din and judgement—the clang of modern life that leaves no quiet corner for a young woman’s pride to recover in. He could not convey those details to her; she had lived too sheltered, her ear accustomed to praise and the muffled sounds of comfort.

And she was not convinced. Her self-opinion rallied, as it always did when opposed. She began to think his judgement fallible—perhaps biased. It struck her that she might have done better to write directly to a London theatre manager and demand an appointment; she might have obtained at once what Herr Klesmer was denying her. She would say no more about singing—he had set himself against that. But she felt able to argue about acting.

“I understood, of course, that no one can be a finished actress at once,” she said, resistance in her tone. “It may be impossible to tell beforehand whether I should succeed; but that seems to me a reason why I should try. I should have thought that I might have taken an engagement at a theatre meanwhile, so as to earn money and study at the same time.”

“Can’t be done, my dear Miss Harleth—I speak plainly—it can’t be done. I must clear your mind of these notions which have no more resemblance to reality than a pantomime. Ladies and gentlemen think that when they have made their toilet and drawn on their gloves they are as presentable on the stage as in a drawing-room. No manager thinks that. With all your grace and charm, if you were to present yourself as an aspirant to the stage, a manager would either require you to pay as an amateur for being allowed to perform or he would tell you to go and be taught—trained to bear yourself on the stage, as a horse, however beautiful, must be trained for the circus; to say nothing of that study which would enable you to personate a character consistently, and animate it with the natural language of face, gesture, and tone. For you to get an engagement fit for you straight away is out of the question.”

“I really cannot understand that,” she said, haughtiness returning like a stiff collar. Then she checked herself and altered her tone, striving for reasonableness. “I shall be obliged to you if you will explain how it is that such poor actresses get engaged. I have been to the theatre several times, and I am sure there were actresses who seemed to me to act not at all well and who were quite plain.”

“Ah, my dear Miss Harleth,” he said, and there was a weary patience in him, “that is the easy criticism of the buyer. We who buy slippers toss away this pair and the other as clumsy; but there went an apprenticeship to the making of them. Excuse me; you could not at present teach one of those actresses; but there is certainly much that she could teach you. For example, she can pitch her voice so as to be heard: ten to one you could not do it till after many trials. Merely to stand and move on the stage is an art—requires practice. It is understood that we are not now talking of a comparse in a petty theatre who earns the wages of a needle-woman. That is out of the question for you.”

“Of course I must earn more than that,” said Gwendolen, with a sense of wincing rather than being answered. “But I think I could soon learn to do tolerably well all those little things you have mentioned. I am not so very stupid. And even in Paris, I am sure, I saw two actresses playing important ladies’ parts who were not at all ladies and quite ugly. I suppose I have no particular talent, but I must think it is an advantage, even on the stage, to be a lady and not a perfect fright.”

“Ah, let us understand each other,” he said, and something new flashed out, as if he had at last found the exact word in a language both could grasp. “I was speaking of what you would have to go through if you aimed at becoming a real artist—if you took music and the drama as a higher vocation in which you would strive after excellence. On that head, what I have said stands fast. You would find—after your education in doing things slackly for one-and-twenty years—great difficulties in study; you would find mortifications in the treatment you would get when you presented yourself on the footing of skill. You would be subjected to tests; people would no longer feign not to see your blunders. You would at first only be accepted on trial. You would have to bear what I may call a glaring insignificance: any success must be won by the utmost patience. You would have to keep your place in a crowd, and after all it is likely you would lose it and get out of sight. If you determine to face these hardships and still try, you will have the dignity of a high purpose, even though you may have chosen unfortunately. You will have some merit, though you may win no prize. You have asked my judgment on your chances of winning. I don’t pretend to speak absolutely; but measuring probabilities, my judgment is: you will hardly achieve more than mediocrity.”

The words fell like cold water. She sat motionless, looking down at her hands lying one over the other in her lap, as if she could read her future there. The room’s quiet pressed on her; outside, even the Sunday soundscape seemed to mock her—those distant wheels, that faint stir of leaves—nature going on in its old rhythms while her imagined life was being unmade.

Then the deep-toned, long-drawn “But—” startled her, as a sudden chord startles one out of reverie, and she looked up again.

“But—there are certainly other ideas, other dispositions with which a young lady may take up an art that will bring her before the public. She may rely on the unquestioned power of her beauty as a passport. She may desire to exhibit herself to an admiration which dispenses with skill. This goes a certain way on the stage: not in music: but on the stage, beauty is taken when there is nothing more commanding to be had. Not without some drilling, however: as I have said before, technicalities have in any case to be mastered. But these excepted, we have here nothing to do with art. The woman who takes up this career is not an artist: she is usually one who thinks of entering on a luxurious life by a short and easy road—perhaps by marriage—that is her most brilliant chance, and the rarest. Still, her career will not be luxurious to begin with: she can hardly earn her own poor bread independently at once, and the indignities she will be liable to are such as I will not speak of.”

Every sentence struck her with a confused sting. She heard, beneath his careful restraint, a contempt for the very course she had half-imagined as her salvation; and she felt, too, the pressure of his vocabulary—how his terms separated “artist” from “woman who exhibits herself,” as if language itself were a gate guarded by those who possessed the right words. She, who had always ruled by ease of speech, suddenly felt the power of a lexicon that could exclude.

“I desire to be independent,” she said, and her voice trembled with wounded pride. “That was my reason for asking whether I could not get an immediate engagement. Of course I cannot know how things go on about theatres. But I thought that I could have made myself independent. I have no money, and I will not accept help from any one.”

She could not bear that he should imagine she had sent for him to solicit anything but counsel. If she must be denied her dreams, she would at least keep her pride upright, like a table set with nothing on it yet kept spotless for the sake of dignity.
