The next morning dawned bright and early, and we rose with the sun. After breakfast, we set to work right away on making bird-lime—a thick, sticky mixture of caoutchouc oil and turpentine that turned out perfectly. The boys brought long wooden rods, which I coated thoroughly with the adhesive substance and placed high in the trees where fruit was abundant and birds gathered in large numbers.

The number of pigeons this year was astonishing—far greater than ever before. It struck me that last year we had only seen the stragglers at the end of their season; now, entire flocks were descending upon the trees, bending branches under their weight. Even the sweet acorns of the evergreen oaks attracted huge groups, and from the mess beneath the trees, it was clear these birds roosted there at night.

This gave me an idea. I decided to launch a nighttime raid, just as the colonists in Virginia did—using torchlight to flush out the birds and catch them by surprise.

Meanwhile, our bird-lime worked wonderfully. As the pigeons landed, they stuck fast. The more they flapped and struggled, the deeper they sank into the gooey trap, until, crying out in distress, they tumbled to the ground, dragging the limed sticks with them. We quickly removed the birds, cleaned the traps, and reset them for another round.

Soon, the boys could manage the operation without my help, so I left them to it and went to prepare torches—made from pinewood soaked in turpentine—for our planned night attack.

Jack soon came running with a beautiful pigeon unlike any other he’d caught. He didn’t want to kill it. It looked like one of our own European breed—the kind we’d once kept but lost when we first arrived. I took the trembling bird gently, cleaned its feet and wings with oil and ashes to remove the sticky residue, and placed it in a wicker cage. I told Jack to bring me any others like it.

He did, and we collected several pairs. This made me very happy. When we first landed, these birds had flown free and become wild again. Now, thanks to our efforts, we had a small flock we could raise—and enjoy pigeon pie whenever we wanted.

As evening fell, we headed for the grove of sweet acorn trees, armed only with long bamboo poles, torches, and canvas sacks. The children thought our gear looked odd and weak—but soon they saw how effective it would be.

Darkness fell almost as we reached the woods. I lit the torches, and instantly the scene transformed. Every branch was packed with ortolans and wild pigeons, roosting in dense clusters. The sudden glare startled them. Chaos broke out—birds fluttered wildly, blinded and confused. Some dropped straight down before we even touched the branches. We scooped them up and stuffed them into our sacks.

When we began beating the trees with our poles, my wife and Franz could barely keep up with gathering the fallen birds. The sacks filled quickly. We turned back toward Falconhurst, safely stowed our prize, and retired to bed, satisfied and weary.

The next day was spent entirely plucking, boiling, roasting, and stewing the feast. There was no time for anything else.

But the following morning, we prepared for a new mission: a full-scale expedition to Woodlands, where we hoped to stop the monkey raids once and for all. I believed if we could catch the mischievous creatures in the act, we could teach them a lesson they’d never forget.

My wife packed enough food for several days, while she stayed behind with Franz and Turk. I took along extra-strong bird-lime (thicker than what we used for pigeons), short stakes, plenty of string, and a collection of coconut shells and gourds. The buffalo carried everything, plus two of the boys. I rode the ass, and we arrived at a hidden spot deep in the forest near Woodlands, shielded by thick bushes. We pitched a small tent, tied up the animals, and secured the dogs so they wouldn’t wander and give us away.

The cottage stood quiet and empty. I wasted no time setting up our trap. We drove stakes into the ground in a zigzag pattern around the house, weaving strings between them to form a tangled maze. Everything was smeared with the powerful bird-lime. Bowls of the sticky mix were scattered about, laced with rice, maize, and other tempting treats.

Night passed peacefully. With the trap complete, we rested beneath the tent.

Just before dawn, we heard a strange commotion—rustling, cracking, shrieking, chattering. The sound meant one thing: the monkeys were coming.

We grabbed clubs and cudgels, held the dogs on leashes, and crept silently through the underbrush. From our hiding place, we watched as the chaos unfolded.

The forest erupted in noise. A massive horde of monkeys burst from the trees, scrambling, leaping, racing across the clearing toward the cottage. Drawn by the unfamiliar objects, they rushed to the bowls and jars.

They seemed endless. Their frantic movements made it hard to count them. They charged through the palings, some climbing onto the roof, pulling at pegs to break in.

But slowly, one by one, they became trapped. Bird-lime clung to their heads, paws, backs. Some sat down, trying to scrub themselves clean with clumsy, comic gestures. Others got caught in the strings and stakes, dragging them like burdens. Some tried to help each other—only to stick together. The harder they pulled, the worse it got.

Many grabbed the gourds and coconuts, only to have their hands glued to them. One old monkey found a calabash filled with palm wine. He drank greedily—then, as the shell stuck to his face, covering his eyes, he stumbled blindly, spinning and hopping like a madman.

Some fled—but many more became fixed to the nearby trees, hanging from branches, shrieking in terror.

With panic spreading, I released the dogs. They lunged forward, snarling and barking, attacking the helpless apes as if they were hunting criminals.

The scene turned grim. We joined in with clubs, striking where needed. Soon, the noise died—howls, yelps, screams—all silenced. The clearing lay still, littered with bodies.

At least forty monkeys lay dead or dying. The boys grew somber. I shared their sadness and quickly redirected them—helping bury the slain, burn the stakes, strings, and bowls, destroying every trace of our trap.

Then we began restoring the cottage. We searched for the scattered sheep, goats, and poultry, gradually rounding them up and returning them to their pens.

As we worked, we heard a heavy thud from above. Looking up, we found three magnificent birds caught on limed sticks in the trees.

Two were Blue Molucca pigeons. The third, with shimmering green, bronze, and steel-blue feathers, looked like the rare Nicobar pigeon. I smiled at the thought of raising them—making them the first residents of a new home near the cave.

“First tenants, Father?” Fritz asked. “Do you think we’ll catch more?”

“Not exactly catch,” I replied. “I mean to use a secret art. Magic, Fritz!”

I said no more.

Within days, Woodlands was restored. We returned to Falconhurst, welcomed with joy.

Everyone agreed we must go to Tentholm next—to build a proper pigeon house in the rock. There were other tasks too, so we planned to stay longer.

My plan was to carve a spacious chamber into the cliff, facing Jackal River, close to our rocky home. Inside, I’d add partitions, perches, and nesting boxes. A wooden front would cover the opening, with sliding doors, shutters, and a wide platform where birds could walk and rest.

After weeks of work, the dove-cot was ready. I sent the other boys to work far from the cave, then called Fritz.

"Now, my faithful assistant," I said, "it’s time to summon the new settlers. Yes—time to perform a real pigeon-dealer's trick."

Fritz blinked. “You mean… magic?”

“Exactly,” I laughed. “Pigeon dealers are clever. Not only do they protect their own birds—they lure others in. All I need is soft clay, aniseed, salt, and oil. I’ll make a special mixture—delicious to our birds, and scented so strongly that wild ones will come to share it.”

“I can get those things, Father.”

“And I’ll need oil of aniseed,” I added. “To coat the pigeon holes. That way, as the birds pass in and out, their feathers will absorb the scent—drawing others to follow.”

“I’ll bring the mortar and oil,” Fritz promised.

Once the oil was pressed from crushed seeds and strained through cloth, it carried a strong, lasting fragrance—perfect for the task.

All preparations done, we moved the pigeons into their new home. The slides were closed. Our European birds had grown friendly with the three newcomers. When the others returned and climbed the ladder to peek through a glass pane I’d installed, they saw the birds calmly feeding on grain and nibbling at the “magic food,” as Fritz called it—though he kept my secret safe.

On the third morning, I woke Fritz early. I told him to climb the rope ladder and attach a cord to the sliding door, so we could open or close it from below. Then he spread fresh aniseed oil around the entrance.

We returned and woke the family. “If you hurry,” I said, “you might see me release the pigeons.”

Everyone gathered. I waved a wand dramatically, muttered a pretend spell, and signaled Fritz to pull the cord.

One by one, the birds peered out—soft eyes scanning the sky. They retreated, then stepped forward again. Slowly, they walked onto the platform—what little Franz called “the verandah.” Suddenly, they took flight with a sharp whirring sound, circling higher and higher, until they vanished.

We stared after them—then, to our delight, they reappeared, landing softly on the dove-cot.

We cheered—thinking they’d accepted their new home. But then the three blue pigeons rose again, soaring in wide circles, heading straight for Falconhurst.

Their flight was bold, determined. I feared we’d lost them forever.

We tried to comfort ourselves by petting the four remaining birds, but the disappointment lingered. All day, the dove-cot remained the center of attention.

Nothing was seen of the fugitives—until midday the next day.

Jack burst into the cave, breathless. “He’s there! He’s come! He really is!”

“Who? What do you mean?”

“The blue pigeon!” he shouted. “Hurrah!”

“Nonsense,” Ernest scoffed. “You’re joking.”

“Why should it be nonsense?” I asked. “I believe we’ll see them all soon!”

Everyone ran outside. And there, sure enough, stood the blue pigeon—billing and cooing to a mate. She was a stranger, clearly of his kind. He darted in and out of the doorway, bowing and cooing, so charmingly that she finally stepped in.

“Close the door!” the boys yelled, rushing for the cord.

“Wait!” I called. “Don’t frighten them. Besides, the others are coming—would you shut the door in their faces?”

“Here they come!” Fritz cried, spotting them from afar.

And indeed, the second blue pigeon arrived—with a mate. After a playful display of modesty, she followed him inside.

The third and most splendid of the trio came last. Perhaps he’d taken longer to find a partner worthy of him. But we expected him. The boys already spoke of “Mr. and Mrs. Nicobar” as if it were certain.

Late that afternoon, Franz and his mother went out for supper. Franz returned quickly, excited. “Come quick! You’ve got to see something beautiful!”

We all rushed to the dove-cot. There, the third bird had returned—with a lovely bride. Encouraged by the others, they settled in at once.

Soon, nest-building began. Among the materials, I noticed a long grey moss—lichen, possibly the same kind used in the West Indies to stuff mattresses instead of horsehair.

My wife heard about it immediately and sprang into action. “If we gather enough,” she said, “I’ll clean and sort it. We’ll make bolsters, pillows, saddles—anything we need!”

We also discovered nutmegs—thanks to the pigeons. We planted them carefully in our orchard.

For a while, life continued quietly—until Jack, as usual, got himself into trouble.

He set off early on one of his private adventures—always hoping to return with a surprise.

This time, he came back covered in mud and green slime. A bundle of Spanish cane was strapped to his back, muddy and wet. One shoe was gone. He looked miserable—so much so that we couldn’t laugh, though we wanted to.

“Dear boy! What happened?”

“Only the swamp behind the powder magazine,” he said. “I went to collect reeds for baskets and hen-coops. I saw better ones farther out in the marsh—so I jumped from one dry patch to another. Then I slipped—and sank past my ankles.”

“I tried to reach the reeds, but I went deeper and deeper—above my knees in thick mud. I was stuck!”

“I screamed, but no one came. I was terrified.”

“Then Fangs appeared! He knew my voice and came right over the swamp. But all he could do was bark and run around. I wonder you didn’t hear us!”

“In desperation, I cut down all the reeds near me, tied them into a bundle, and used it as a raft. I climbed on top, balanced myself, and kicked and scrambled until I freed my legs.”

“Then I grabbed Fangs’ tail. He pulled, I dragged, crawled, waded—sometimes on the reeds, sometimes dragging them—until we made it back to dry land. I had a close call!”

“A lucky escape,” I said. “And God be thanked. Fangs acted bravely. You showed great courage. Now go wash up—you’ve brought me excellent canes, just what I need.”

I had a plan. I wanted to build a loom for my wife—knowing she understood weaving. I selected two strong reeds, split and bound them tightly so they’d dry straight and firm. Smaller reeds were cut and sharpened for the comb’s teeth.

The boys helped without knowing the purpose—calling the tools “father’s monster toothpicks.”

Eventually, the loom was assembled. I kept it a secret, telling the children it was an “outlandish musical instrument”—one only Mother could play.

When the time came to reveal it, her joy was immense. The children watched in fascination as she “played the loom,” laughing and cheering.

Around this time, a fine foal—son of the onager—was born. Strong and gentle, he was named Swift. He would be trained for my riding.

Inside the cave, progress continued. I began designing an aqueduct to bring fresh water directly to our home. The journey to Jackal River was long—especially on washing days. Hollow bamboo pipes worked well. A large cask served as a reservoir. The supply was steady, and my wife declared she was as pleased with this engineering feat as if we’d built a marble fountain with mermaids.

With the rainy season approaching, I pushed ahead with winter preparations. We gathered roots, fruits, grains—potatoes, rice, guavas, sweet acorns, pinecones. Load after load arrived. My wife sewed constantly, making more sacks and bags.

Casks and barrels were repurposed. Even the raft was dismantled—its tubs now stored in the cellar.

The weather grew stormy. Dark clouds rolled in. Wind, thunder, lightning, and torrential rain swept across the land. The sea surged against the cliffs. Nature itself sang the prelude to the great rains.

It was early June. Twelve weeks of rough weather lay ahead.

We moved some animals into the salt cave—the cow, the ass, Lightfoot, Storm, and the dogs. Knips, Fangs, and the eagle would provide amusement during long evenings.

The boys often rode to Falconhurst to check on things and fetch supplies.

More work remained to make the cave comfortable—especially since we’d be living indoors for months.

The darkness inside bothered me. So I devised a solution. I called Jack and we found a tall, sturdy bamboo pole. We planted it firmly in the floor, wedging it tight.

Jack climbed it, carrying hammer and chisel. He widened a crack in the ceiling and fixed a pulley. Then, descending, I raised a large ship’s lantern—filled with oil and burning four wicks—up through the hole. It cast a warm, steady glow.

Days passed arranging rooms. Ernest and Franz organized the library. Jack and his mother decorated the sitting room and kitchen. Fritz and I handled the workshops—setting up the carpenter’s bench, turning lathe, and hanging tools on the walls.

Our home was becoming a true haven.
