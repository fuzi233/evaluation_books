“Full fathom deep thy father lies…” The old song went about the quays and church-porches, not as a stage-trick, but as folk-memory—salt on the tongue, and bell-sound in the ear—fitting enough for a year when so many men, Spanish and English, were already being turned by the sea into other substances: into weed-sway, into crab-meat, into coral-crumb, into the hard pearls of rumor.

It was over. The Armada, that vast pantry of war—casks, biscuit, horses, saints’ banners, brass guns, priests, and princes’ pride—had been beaten, and the long, high quarrel of Europe had, for a season, dropped from the sky into the plain talk of shot and splinter. A day came when even the simplest sailor could taste which side was light and which was darkness, as plainly as he could tell fresh water from bilge. And when it was done, and men counted the cost, it seemed again—as it always seems after such hours—that the loudest boast had been no more than a bladder blown up for a fair, bursting at one prick, leaving only stink behind.

Across England and the Low Countries the thanksgiving rose like a great oven-heat, that steady roar you hear in the bakehouse when the loaves are setting and the work is going well. There were appointed fasts and prayers; there were sermons that handled no other argument than praise; there were processions, and in London the Queen rode in a triumph that smelt of wet woollen liveries, pitch from the street-hangings, and the cold iron tang of captured ensigns. The colors of the vanquished hung out of St. Paul’s like fish on a line. The city shouted till the stones seemed to take the sound up and pass it along; for a people’s well-being is not only in bread and law, but in the very quality of the noises above their heads—whether they are the honest peals of bells and the measured chanting of a multitude, or the thin clatter of fear.

And whither had that great host fled? Northward—away and away, like deer breaking cover and splitting into scattered bodies, each beast for itself. Past Orkney and Shetland, where poor fishermen were caught up as unwilling guides; past Norway, where the old Viking blood refused them water and food; on toward the Faroes and the strange never-sleeping light near the Pole. Their water failed. Their cattle were heaved overboard, and the shrieks of drowning horses went up into that bleak air and were snatched away, as if even the wind was ashamed to carry them long. Medina Sidonia, with the soundest ships and best victuals, led his portion off and left the rest to grope homeward as they might, appointing Cape Finisterre like a market-cross where all should meet—if they ever did.

Meanwhile Parma, on the Dunkirk dunes, watched and waited and could not believe his idol would fail him, and spun fresh rigging and cast anchors as if patient industry could conjure back a sea that had chosen its own answer. His ladies might sit long with fans in hand.

As for Amyas Leigh and the Vengeance, they were not among those who turned home.

At the fifty-seventh degree the English fleet, short of provisions and long out of powder and ball, bore southward for Harwich, leaving the Spaniard to those “uncouth and boisterous northern seas.” But when the Lord High Admiral gave the word, Amyas asked leave to follow. He went to Sir John Hawkins and begged ammunition and victual such as might be spared, pledging repayment like an honest man—out of plunder if he lived, out of his estate if he died—and lodged bills in Hawkins’s hands. Hawkins, who could stow a kingdom’s hazard in the same pocket with string and tobacco, took them without ceremony.

Then Amyas called his men together. The smell of tar was in their clothes; salt had dried on their faces like a crust. He spoke to them again of the Rose of Torridge and Don Guzman de Soto, so that an old wrong might serve as yeast to make their courage rise.

“Men of Bideford, will you follow me? There will be plunder for those who love plunder; revenge for those who love revenge; and for all of us—(for we all love honor)—the honor of having never left the chase as long as there was a Spanish flag in English seas.”

And every soul aboard, with mouths that still remembered hard biscuit and beer gone sour in cask, answered that they would follow Sir Amyas Leigh round the world.

There were sixteen days of weary hunting after that—days whose hours were measured by tack and tide, by the scrape of rope through block, by the dull percussion of the sea against plank, by the cook’s calls and the thin comfort of a hot mess. They sighted the Sta. Catharina and struck at her and were forced off when help came down on them. When Medina Sidonia abandoned crippled ships, Amyas thrust straight into the thick of the Spanish mass rather than let the main quarry slip. He crippled her so she could not claw off to windward into the ocean, but must run south, past Orkney, down through the Minch between Cape Wrath and Lewis.

The younger hands snarled that he was mad, because in his stubborn haste he ran past noble prizes all but disabled—among them a great galliass, and the two great Venetians La Ratta and La Belanzara—golden mouths waiting to be picked clean. Those same ships would afterward, with thirty more, be wrecked on the west coast of Ireland, and men would scramble over their broken ribs like gulls over a carcass. Amyas took fresh water in spite of the “Hebridean Scots” of Skye, who reviled him in an unknown tongue—words that struck the ear like stones clacking in a bucket—then fought awhile, then embraced him and his men with howls of affection, half wild, half merry, and as nakedly honest as the wind. Cary christened a strange island, half black and half white, “the drowned magpie,” though the people called it Raghary, and the name sat in the air like a taste you cannot translate—something between peat-smoke and sea-fog.

Amyas let his crew pick the bones of a great Venetian going ashore upon Islay, where they got booty enough to sweeten their tempers and pay for the voyage; and then soured them again by refusing to land and plunder two great Spanish wrecks on the Mull of Cantire. He fought the Sta. Catharina off Castleton after she had taken refuge there—where Manx-men slew a boat’s crew with arrows—and shot away her mainyard after a day’s work. The Spaniard blundered down the coast of Wales like a man in a strange kitchen who cannot find the door, and both ships were nearly lost on Holyhead and again on Bardsey. In Cardigan Bay, with a lee shore under them and a heavy gale behind, the Sta. Catharina struck on Sarn David—those pebble-dykes drowned under water, said to be the remnants of lost Gwalior, ruined by Prince Seithenin the drunkard. Every loyal Welshman, they say, spits at his name. The galleon got off on the rising tide and fought again, then the wind shifted and she fetched round St. David’s Head.

So much for the long chase. The end was brewing, and the air itself began to taste of it.

On the fifteenth evening they had seen St. David’s Head, and the Welsh coast by Milford Haven looming black and sharp under the blaze of an inland thunder-storm. All through the fore part of the night lightning played—white, then violet, then that hard blue that leaves your eyes aching. A light south-western breeze carried the damp smell of land for a moment, then lost it again in the larger salt.

They strained their eyes through the dark for the Spaniard’s masts, glimpsed now and then by a flash; but the night swallowed all. Still, with the wind as it was, she could not have gone far west. To pass them and go north was more than she dared. She was likely lying-to ahead, perhaps between them and the coast.

A little after midnight the wind chopped west and blew stiffly till daybreak. If she had not tried the desperate trick of running past them in the dark, she was trapped in the mouth of the Bristol Channel. Dawn came slowly on a day like the aftermath of thunder: sunless, drizzling, roofed with low dingy cloud, netted and barred and hung with black fringes, as if the storm were only taking breath. The horizon lay narrow and spongy with vapor, drifting before a chilly breeze. Later the wind died, and the sea fell to a long glassy roll flecked with foam, while overhead the inky sky brooded and around them a leaden mist shut out both shore and chase.

Amyas paced the wet deck—sloppy with brine and rain—fierce as a man who has been starved at the very moment the roast is set down before him. He knew the Spaniard could not escape, and yet each idle minute was vinegar in his mouth. The men sat sulkily, whistling for a wind. The sails flapped and slapped the masts with that empty, scolding noise that can drive men mad at sea; the ship rolled till the yard-arms seemed almost to dip alternately into the gray.

“Take care of those guns,” Amyas growled. “You will have something loose next.”

“We will take care of the guns, if the Lord will take care of the wind,” said Yeo.

“We shall have plenty before night,” Cary answered. “And thunder too.”

“So much the better,” said Amyas. “Let it roar till it splits the heavens, if it does but let me get my work done.”

“He’s not far off, I warrant,” said Cary. “One lift of the cloud, and we should see him.”

“To windward of us, as likely as not,” Amyas said. “The devil fights for him, I believe. Sixteen days on his heels—and not sent this through him yet!” And he shook his sword like a hungry man shaking a knife.

Hour after hour—no gull, no fin, no life but their own ship’s creaks and the sea’s slow breathing. Even sound seemed dulled by the mist, as if the world’s ear were stuffed with wool. Amyas’s black mood deepened with the low sky, and the thought of losing the prey after all made his whole body shudder.

“No, God!” he cried aloud, as if the deck itself were a pulpit. “Let me but once feel this in his accursed heart, and then—strike me dead, if Thou wilt!”

“The Lord have mercy on us,” cried John Brimblecombe. “What have you said?”

“What is that to you, sir? There, they are piping to dinner. Go down. I shall not come.”

Brimblecombe went below, and his whisper carried through the mess like a draft under a door. Most took it for a bad sign, except old Yeo, who had the plain man’s courage of being neither grand nor delicate.

“Well, Sir John,” said he, “and why not? What better can the Lord do for a man, than take him home when he has done his work? Our captain is wilful and spiteful, and must needs kill his man himself; while for me, I don’t care how the Don goes, provided he does go. I owe him no grudge, nor any man. May the Lord give him repentance, and forgive him all his sins: but if I could but see him once safe ashore—as he may be ere nightfall, on the Mortestone or the back of Lundy—I would say, ‘Lord, now lettest Thou Thy servant depart in peace,’ even if it were the lightning which was sent to fetch me.”

“But, master Yeo, a sudden death?”

“And why not a sudden death, Sir John? Even fools long for a short life and a merry one, and shall not the Lord’s people pray for a short death and a merry one? Let it come as it will to old Yeo. Hark! there’s the captain’s voice!”

“Here she is!” thundered Amyas from above; and in an instant men scrambled up the hatchway, boots slipping on wet ladders, shoulders knocking in the press.

The cloud had lifted like a curtain jerked aside, and southward a ragged bore of blue sky opened, letting a long spear of sunshine fall upon tall masts and a stately hull. The Sta. Catharina lay rolling some four or five miles to the eastward, golded for a moment and then grayed again; but no land showed—only that false emptiness that makes a sailor’s stomach feel hollow.

“There she is; and here we are,” said Cary. “But where is here? and where is there? How is the tide, master?”

“Running up Channel by this time, sir.”

“What matters the tide?” Amyas said, devouring the ship with cold, terrible blue eyes. “Can’t we get at her?”

“Not unless some one jumps out and shoves behind,” Cary answered. “I shall down again and finish that mackerel, if this roll has not chucked it to the cockroaches under the table.”

“Don’t jest, Will! I can’t stand it.” Amyas’s voice quivered; Cary looked at him and saw the trembling that ran through him like wind through reeds. Cary took his arm and drew him aside to the bulwarks, where the ship’s wet wood smelt of salt and old tar.

“Dear old lad,” said he low, “what is this? You are not yourself, and have not been these four days.”

“No. I am not Amyas Leigh. I am my brother’s avenger. Do not reason with me, Will: when it is over I shall be merry old Amyas again.” He rubbed his brow as if to wipe off heat.

After a moment he said, “Do you believe that men can be possessed by devils?”

“The Bible says so.”

“If my cause were not a just one, I should fancy I had a devil in me. My throat and heart are as hot as the pit. Would to God it were done, for done it must be! Now go.”

Cary went away with a shudder. As he passed down the hatch, he glanced back. Amyas had drawn a hone from his pocket and was whetting his sword-edge again—stroke after stroke—like a man under a doom to sharpen forever, listening to that thin, exact rasp as though it were the only honest sound left in the world.

The day dragged on. The strip of blue sky was curtained over. The air grew sultrier; now and then a distant mutter rolled from the west. They could not lessen the distance—boats were gone, all but one too small to tow. The men went below to finish dinner; the smell of mackerel and hot fat rose for a moment through the hatch, sharp and comforting, then was swallowed again by damp air. Amyas stayed above, working the blade and looking up now and then at the Spaniard as if afraid she might vanish like a mirage.

About two, Yeo came up.

“He is ours safely now, sir. The tide has been running to the eastward for this two hours.”

“Safe as a fox in a trap. Satan himself cannot take him from us!”

“But God may,” said Brimblecombe simply, behind them.

“Who spoke to you, sir? If I thought that He—There comes the thunder at last!”

As if the sky answered, an angry growl rolled nearer and nearer, then crashed overhead against some cloud-cliff and ceased, leaving a silence that was almost louder. Men looked into one another’s faces. Amyas did not move.

“The storm is coming,” he said. “And the wind in it. It will be Eastward-ho now, for once, my merry men all!”

“Eastward-ho never brought us luck,” Jack muttered to Cary; but by then all eyes were on the north-west. A black line began to harden along the horizon, marking sea from air.

“There comes the breeze.”

“And there the storm, too.”

The thunder, which had been slow and distant, now began to ring peal on peal, as if someone were dragging cannon across the sky’s floor. The yards creaked round. The sea crisped. Hot air swept their cheeks, tightened rope, filled sail, and bent the Vengeance over. A cheer burst from the men as the helm went up; and they staggered away before the wind, straight down upon the Spaniard, who lay still becalmed, rich prey in a poor man’s sight.

“There is more behind, Amyas,” Cary shouted through the growing noise. “Shall we not shorten sail a little?”

“No. Hold on every stitch. Give me the helm, man. Boatswain, pipe away to clear for fight.”

In ten minutes they were at quarters, while the thunder rolled louder and the breeze freshened fast.

“The dog has it now. There he goes!” Cary cried.

“Right before the wind. He has no liking to face us.”

“He is running into the jaws of destruction,” said Yeo. “An hour more will send him either right up the Channel, or smack on shore somewhere.”

“There! he has put his helm down. I wonder if he sees land?”

“He is like a March hare beat out of his country,” Cary said, “and don’t know whither to run next.”

So it was. The Spaniard fell off again and fled dead down wind. The Vengeance gained fast; in two hours the four miles dwindled to one. The storm came nearer—lightning flashing like blue knives through the murk—and from a vast black arch of cloud poured a fiercer breeze. The hints to shorten sail grew to murmurs; Amyas yielded, unwilling.

They rushed on with scarcely lessened speed, the black arch following like a hunter’s cloak, curtained by a flat gray sheet of rain. Ahead of that curtain the water boiled in a long white line; behind it keen sparks leapt down into the sea or darted zigzag through rain so thick it looked like a net thrown over the world.

“We shall have it now, and with a vengeance; this will try your tackle, master,” Cary said.

The master only shrugged and turned up his collar as the first stinging drops struck ears and neck. Then the squall burst full upon them—rain that cut like hail, hail that lashed the sea into froth, and wind that tore the heads off surges and swept the waters into one white seething waste. Lightning ran and leapt, dazzling; thunder changed from deep roar to sharp cracks that hurt the ear like a whip.

“Get the arms and ammunition under cover, and then below with you all!” Amyas shouted from the helm.

“And heat the pokers in the galley fire,” Yeo added, “to be ready if the rain puts our linstocks out. I hope you’ll let me stay on deck, sir, in case—”

“I must have some one, and who better than you? Can you see the chase?”

No. The Sta. Catharina was wrapped in the gray whirlwind. She might be within half a mile for all they could tell.

Now Amyas and his old liegeman stood alone on deck, two men with one thought. The squall worsened. Rain poured heavier. The ship’s timbers groaned and sang—each rope with its own note, each block with its own clack—until the whole rigging seemed an instrument played by the storm. In such a chaos, language itself felt small; every shouted order was swallowed or broken, as if the wind refused to carry human words faithfully. What is untranslatable is not only a Gaelic curse or a Latin prayer, but the exact measure of terror in a man’s chest when the sea changes color.

“If he has laid-to, we may overshoot him, sir!” Yeo cried.

“If he has tried to lay-to, he will not have a sail left in the bolt-ropes, or perhaps a mast on deck. I know the stiff-neckedness of those Spanish tubs. Hurrah! there he is, right on our larboard bow!”

There she was indeed, two musket-shots off, staggering away with canvas split and flying, her greatness reduced to a thing battered and noisy, like a pantry-door torn from its hinges in a gale.

“He has been trying to hull, sir, and caught a buffet,” Yeo said, rubbing his hands. “What shall we do now?”

“Range alongside, if it blow live imps and witches, and try our luck once more. Pah! how this lightning dazzles!”

They swept in, gaining fast.

“Call the men up, and to quarters; the rain will be over in ten minutes.”

Yeo sprang forward to the gangway—and sprang back again, face white under its weathering.

“Land right ahead! Port your helm, sir! For the love of God, port your helm!”

Amyas jammed the helm down with bull-strength while Yeo shouted below. The Vengeance swung. The masts bent like whips. Crack went the foresail like a cannon.

What matter? Within two hundred yards was the Spaniard; and in front of her, rising through the dense hail, a huge dark bank heaved up from the sea and mingled with cloud—at its foot pillars and spouts of leaping foam.

“What is it, Morte? Hartland?”

It might have been anything—any deadly mile of coast.

“Lundy!” Yeo cried. “The south end! I see the head of the Shutter in the breakers! Hard a-port yet, and get her close-hauled as you can, and the Lord may have mercy on us still! Look at the Spaniard!”

Yes—look.

On their left, as they broached-to, the granite wall sloped down from cloud toward an isolated peak of rock, two hundred feet high. Then came a hundred yards of roaring breaker upon a sunken shelf, across which the tide-race poured like a cataract; then, amid a column of salt smoke, the Shutter rose—huge, black, fang-like—waiting. And between that fang and the land, the great galleon loomed dimly through the storm.

He, too, had seen his danger and tried to broach-to. But his clumsy bulk refused the helm. He struggled a moment, half hidden in foam; fell away again, and rushed upon his doom.
