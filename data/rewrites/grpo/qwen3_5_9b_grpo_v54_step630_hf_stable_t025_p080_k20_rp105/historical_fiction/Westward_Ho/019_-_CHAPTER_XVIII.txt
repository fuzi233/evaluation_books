The vessel slipped silently past the southern tip of Grenada under the cover of darkness, finally entering the archipelago known as the "Fairy Ring." To the seasoned eyes of the crew, these waters were a paradox: a realm where nature had concentrated her most breathtaking beauty, yet humanity had concentrated its greatest sins. While the newcomers might have viewed Barbados with a sense of strange grandeur, the seas surrounding these islands offered a different kind of splendor—calm, smiling waters untouched by the hurricanes that roared miles to the north. The sky, sea, and land merged into a single, vast rainbow, a spectacle that left even the pragmatic sailors, driven by thoughts of Spanish gold, momentarily awestruck.

Amyas, however, saw beyond the scenery. Accustomed to the tropics, his mind was fixed on a singular, dangerous objective: the extirpation of the Spanish presence and the annexation of the West Indies to Queen Elizabeth's dominions. Yet, even his unpoetic gaze could not fail to register the awe-inspiring nature of their surroundings. These were lands famous yet entirely new to Englishmen, territories where failure meant not merely death, but the tortures of the Inquisition. Few English ships had dared these waters before; those who had, under Captain John Hawkins, had learned the hard way that Spanish jealousy required English steel to overcome. As the old mariners of the *Pelican* and the *Minion* recounted tales of every isle and bird, Frank stood silent, his soul drinking in the glory of the approaching horizon.

He murmured to himself, "This is the gate of heaven," watching the world dissolve into a blazing phantom. Islands and capes hung suspended in the air, mirrored below in the shimmering heat haze. Giant cacti twisted like candelabra against glaring slopes, and the sun itself seemed distorted into strange, pear-shaped figures by beds of crimson mist. But while Frank marveled, Yeo rejoiced. To the south of the setting sun, a cluster of tall peaks rose from the sea—the mountains of Macanao at the western end of Margarita, the Isle of Pearls. It was a place renowned across the Mediterranean and German fairs, second only to the pearl-rich island in the Gulf of Panama, which had claimed the life of John Oxenham fifteen years prior.

The following day, they ran along the northern coast, passing undiscovered the fortress guarding the eastern pearl fisheries. Eventually, they opened into a deep, wooded bight where a Spanish caravel and three small boats lay at anchor. Instantly, the deck erupted with activity. Opinions flew regarding the approach; some advocated sailing directly into the roadstead with the afternoon breeze, while others, fearing hidden reefs, favored a quiet approach via the pinnace. Yeo suggested flying Spanish colors to avoid alarm, but Amyas firmly rejected the deception, deeming it a mean act unless necessary for extreme danger or statecraft.

They held their course until the next point blocked their view, then launched the assault. Cary took the largest boat with twenty men, while Amyas commanded the smaller vessel with fifteen, including John Brimblecombe. The priest arrived in his cassock and bands, clutching an old sword belonging to his uncle, which he treasured deeply.

Approaching the bight's mouth, they encountered a formidable barrier of coral rocks. Forced to navigate the reef's edge, the crew gazed in wonder at the subaquatic gardens of Nereus, where living flowers swayed beneath the clear water. Suddenly, a "school of sharks" emerged from the depths. Some were nearly as long as the boat, their wicked eyes fixed upward with a hungry intensity.

"Jack," Amyas whispered, pointing to the largest predator, "look how that fellow eyes thee. He has taken a fancy to that plump hide and thinks thou wouldst taste as tender as a sucking porker."

Jack turned pale but remained silent. Just then, the massive shark spotted a parrot-fish emerging from a coral cleft. Driven by instinct, the fish leaped into the air, nearly aboard the boat, while three or four great brown snouts clashed together within two yards of Jack, displaying rows of saw-like teeth before sinking back into the water. Overwhelmed by the sight, Jack softly muttered, "In manus tuas, Domine!" and turned his eyes away from the sea.

Once through the reef, they entered the bight with a fair breeze. Cary's boat closed the distance before the Spaniards could mount their defenses. Standing in the stern-sheets, Cary shouted for surrender, noting their numerical superiority. When the Spanish captain demanded to know in whose name they acted, Cary retorted, "In the name of common sense, ye dogs! Do you not see that you are but fifty strong to our twenty?" Before the captain could fire, Cary scrambled up the side, knocking him over with a blow intended to spare bloodshed. The crew surrendered immediately, some kneeling, others leaping into the water.

Meanwhile, Amyas secured the second boat, which yielded without resistance, while the negroes swam ashore. The third boat, however, attempted to flee. Seizing an opportunity to distinguish himself, John Brimblecombe grabbed a boat-hook and clawed onto the fleeing vessel's stern, shouting, "Stay, ye Papists! Stay, Spanish dogs!" Expecting ten to one, he was instantly pulled overboard.

Seized by a panic born of his imagination, which filled the sea with the sharks he had just witnessed, Jack began to roar like a bull. In his confusion, he struck out after the Spanish boat, seemingly in a madness of valor or a desperate hope to grab the trailing hook. Dressed in his cassock, he floated like a great black monk-fish, howling and puffing with salt water in his mouth. "Stay, ye Spanish dogs! Help, all good fellows! See you not that I am a dead man? They are nuzzling already at my toes! He hath hold of my leg! My right thigh is bitten clean off! Oh that I were preaching in Hartland pulpit!"

His cries grew more frantic than any seasickness Panurge ever endured. The English, expecting him to be snapped up by sharks or struck by oars, fired a volley into the fugitives. The Spaniards, terrified, leaped overboard. Jack scrambled into the boat, drew his sword with one hand, and wiped water from his eyes with the other, slashing at the empty air and crying, "Yield, idolaters!" Realizing there was no one left to fight, he sat panting in the sternsheets and began stripping off his hose.

Amyas, assuming his friend had gone mad from the sun or heatstroke, ordered the boat alongside and demanded to know what he was doing with his "nether tackle." Amidst uproarious laughter, Jack vowed that his right thigh had been bitten through to the bone and that his hose was full of blood. He would have swooned from imaginary blood loss had not his friends, through argument and anger, convinced him he was whole and sound.

They then overhauled their prize, finding it stocked with hides and salt-pork, but also containing crates of brown pearls. The Spaniards had damaged the color by opening the shells with fire rather than allowing gradual decay. After a malicious prankster set the captured ship ablaze—a fitting end for a vessel laden with hides—they departed, though Amyas chided the wanton destruction. Cary joked that as "Lutheran devils," they were bound to vanish with an evil savor.

Once aboard, Amyas confronted Brimblecombe, advising him to play the man more seriously and keep his breath for rowing rather than roaring before injury. Frank joined the rebuke, questioning Jack's logic. "Tell me, thou recreant Jack," Frank challenged, "is not a priest more than a layman? If a priest shows himself no man, he shows himself all the more no priest."

Jack, realizing the trap, stammered that sharks did not understand logic. Frank pressed further, asking if it was the part of a man to howl like a pig because he feared what was not there. Jack fell silent. Frank concluded that Jack's behavior was not cowardice but Manicheanism—blaming an evil creator for sharks made to devour useless carcasses. As a Brother of the Rose, Jack should have leaped joyfully into the shark's mouth.

"You are a cause of scandal," Frank declared, "and a rotten branch to be excised." He stripped Jack of his office as chaplain, warning that unless he performed deeds equal to the Seven Champions within seven days, he would be allowed to carry no iron but a pen. Amyas added his weight, criticizing the clergy for being soft-handed men who could not dig or beg, and whose failure to stand firm in the hour of need unpreached their entire lives. Together, they boarded Jack with heavy sarcasm until he covered his ears and fled, leaving Frank laughing so heartily that Amyas found relief in the smile it brought to his own steady face.

The next day, Sunday, they held divine service, though Amyas read the articles of their agreement rather than letting Jack preach, given his recent shame. Seeing a sloping beach with fresh water, they decided to land to wash clothes and replenish supplies, scarce since Barbados. Jack insisted on joining, carrying his sword and arquebuse, claiming a dream of Spanish attack that he felt compelled to fulfill, even if it meant death. He preferred dying to being mocked as an ape or a scarecrow for his cowardice.

Upon landing, they washed and stretched their legs, admiring the beauty before setting out a seine net. Among their catch was a seven-foot sea-cow, or manati, described as a pious beast that guarded corpses until they decayed, carried its young under its arm, and sang melodiously when wounded. They also secured two large black fowls, curassos, which provided a hearty meal of roasted and boiled meat.

While the crew filled casks near the river's outflow, John Brimblecombe sat apart under a great tree, reading his Bible with a shamefaced expression, his sword and arquebuse nearby. His solitude ended abruptly when a cry signaled the enemy's approach. Fifty Spanish soldiers, accompanied by negroes and an officer on horseback with a plumed hat, emerged from the woods, swords drawn.

"Stand, for your lives!" Amyas shouted. Ten minutes were lost in scrambling for formation, but when Jack saw the Spaniards, he acted as if he had expected them. Plucking a leaf to mark his page, he laid down his book, caught his arquebuse, and ran like a mad dog straight at the Spanish captain. He shot the officer through the body, killing him instantly, then threw the weapon at the next man and charged with his sword.

The Spaniards, viewing him as a fiend or Luther incarnate, retreated pell-mell, firing five or six shots at once. Whether from fear or the risk of hitting their own men, their aim was poor; Jack received only one shrewd ball in the thigh, which left him limping for days. As the Spaniards fled, nearly forty Englishmen rallied in good order. The Spaniards turned and ran, and Cary noted, "The dogs had had such a taste of the parson, that they had no mind to wait for the clerk and people."

"Come back, Jack! Are you mad?" Amyas shouted.

But Jack, silent until then, pursued them fiercely. Reaching a great blow at an arquebusier, he caught his foot in a root, fell, and struck his head against the ground, knocking out the last of his breath. When Amyas saw the Spaniards gone, he picked up the unconscious Jack. Staring about wildly, Jack cried, "Glory be! Glory be!--How many have I killed? How many have I killed?"

"Nineteen, at the least," Cary replied, "and seven with one back stroke." He showed Brimblecombe the dead captain and several wounded men.

"There!" Jack paused, blowing hard. "Will you laugh at me any more, Mr. Cary; or say that I cannot fight, because I am a poor parson's son?"

Cary took his hand, apologized for his earlier scoffing, and admitted that Jack had played the best man of them all. Jack, never bearing malice, began to laugh in return, recalling Cary's schoolboy pranks with Bideford. They returned to the boats, grateful for their deliverance. As the crew rejoiced over Jack, he grew faint from unseen bleeding, making as little of his real wound as he had of his imaginary one the day before.

That evening, Frank asked how Jack had shown such cool valor after his previous panic. Jack explained that he had been reading the eleventh chapter of Hebrews to fortify his spirits, specifically the verse, "out of weakness were made strong, waxed valiant in fight." Hearing the cry of the Spaniards, he believed he fought in a just cause with equal faith, granting him a strange assurance of victory. "There is no credit due to me," he modestly stated. "There was no valor in me whatsoever, but only a certainty of safety; and any coward would fight if he knew that he were to have all the killing and none of the scratches."

The next Sunday, Jack repeated this sermon, which edified all, even Salvation Yeo, who acknowledged him as a godly preacher and a valiant comrade despite his simple ways.

They took the Spanish officer's sword and a heavy gold chain as prizes. Jack accepted the sword but steadfastly refused the chain, urging Amyas to put it into the common stock. When Amyas refused, Jack cut the chain into links and distributed them among the crew members who had helped him, winning their enduring goodwill.

The chronicler noted that he had never seen such a lack of covetousness in the worthy man, from their school days until his peaceful burial in Hartland. Though ugly, lowly, and simple in understanding, Brimblecombe possessed a spirit so loving and cheerful, lifted from base purposes to the worship of duty, that he seemed to believe it more blessed to give than to receive. All the wealth he gained in war he dispersed among his sisters and the poor of his parish.
