Jeeves—my man, you know—is really a most extraordinary chap. So capable. Honestly, I wouldn’t know what to do without him. On broader lines, he’s like those chappies who sit peering sadly over the marble battlements at the Pennsylvania Station in the place marked “Inquiries.” You know the type. You go up to them and say: “When’s the next train for Melonsquashville, Tennessee?” and they reply, without blinking, “Two-forty-three, track ten, change at San Francisco.” And they’re right every time. Well, Jeeves gives you just that same impression of knowing everything.

As an instance of what I mean, I remember meeting Monty Byng in Bond Street one morning, looking the last word in a grey check suit, and I felt I should never be happy till I had one like it. I dug the address of the tailors out of him, and had them working on the thing inside the hour.

“Jeeves,” I said that evening, “I’m getting a check suit like that one of Mr. Byng’s.”

“Injudicious, sir,” he said firmly. “It will not become you.”

“What absolute rot! It’s the soundest thing I’ve struck for years.”

“Unsuitable for you, sir.”

Well, the long and the short of it was that the confounded thing came home, and I put it on, and when I caught sight of myself in the glass I nearly swooned. Jeeves was perfectly right. I looked like a cross between a music-hall comedian and a cheap bookie. Yet Monty had looked fine in absolutely the same stuff. These things are just Life’s mysteries, and that’s all there is to it.

But it isn’t only that Jeeves’s judgment about clothes is infallible—though, of course, that’s really the main thing. The man knows everything. There was the matter of that tip on the “Lincolnshire.” I forget now how I got it, but it had the aspect of being the real, red-hot tabasco.

“Jeeves,” I said, for I’m fond of the man and like to do him a good turn when I can, “if you want to make a bit of money, have something on Wonderchild for the ‘Lincolnshire.’”

He shook his head.

“I’d rather not, sir.”

“But it’s the straight goods. I’m going to put my shirt on him.”

“I do not recommend it, sir. The animal is not intended to win. Second place is what the stable is after.”

Perfect piffle, I thought, of course. How the deuce could Jeeves know anything about it? Still, you know what happened. Wonderchild led till he was breathing on the wire, and then Banana Fritter came along and nosed him out. I went straight home and rang for Jeeves.

“After this,” I said, “not another step for me without your advice. From now on, consider yourself the brains of the establishment.”

“Very good, sir. I shall endeavour to give satisfaction.”

And he has, by Jove! I’m a bit short on brain myself; the old bean would appear to have been constructed more for ornament than for use, don’t you know? But give me five minutes to talk the thing over with Jeeves, and I’m game to advise anyone about anything. And that’s why, when Bruce Corcoran came to me with his troubles, my first act was to ring the bell and put it up to the lad with the bulging forehead.

“Leave it to Jeeves,” I said.

I first got to know Corky when I came to New York. He was a pal of my cousin Gussie, who was in with a lot of people down Washington Square way. I don’t know if I ever told you about it, but the reason why I left England was because I was sent over by my Aunt Agatha to try to stop young Gussie marrying a girl on the vaudeville stage, and I got the whole thing so mixed up that I decided it would be a sound scheme for me to stop on in America for a bit instead of going back and having long cosy chats about the thing with Aunt. So I sent Jeeves out to find a decent apartment, and settled down for a bit of exile. I’m bound to say that New York’s a topping place to be exiled in. Everybody was awfully good to me, and there seemed to be plenty of things going on, and I’m a wealthy bird, so everything was fine. Chappies introduced me to other chappies, and so on and so forth, and it wasn’t long before I knew squads of the right sort—some who rolled in dollars in houses up by the Park, and others who lived with the gas turned down mostly around Washington Square—artists and writers and so forth. Brainy coves.

Corky was one of the artists. A portrait-painter, he called himself, but he hadn’t painted any portraits. He was sitting on the side-lines with a blanket over his shoulders, waiting for a chance to get into the game. You see, the catch about portrait-painting—I’ve looked into the thing a bit—is that you can’t start painting portraits till people come along and ask you to, and they won’t come and ask you to until you’ve painted a lot first. This makes it kind of difficult for a chappie. Corky managed to get along by drawing an occasional picture for the comic papers—he had rather a gift for funny stuff when he got a good idea—and doing bedsteads and chairs and things for the advertisements. His principal source of income, however, was derived from biting the ear of a rich uncle—one Alexander Worple, who was in the jute business. I’m a bit foggy as to what jute is, but it’s apparently something the populace is pretty keen on, for Mr. Worple had made quite an indecently large stack out of it.

Now, a great many fellows think that having a rich uncle is a pretty soft snap: but, according to Corky, such is not the case. Corky’s uncle was a robust sort of cove, who looked like living for ever. He was fifty-one, and it seemed as if he might go to par. It was not this, however, that distressed poor old Corky, for he was not bigoted and had no objection to the man going on living. What Corky kicked at was the way the above Worple used to harry him.

Corky’s uncle, you see, didn’t want him to be an artist. He didn’t think he had any talent in that direction. He was always urging him to chuck Art and go into the jute business and start at the bottom and work his way up. Jute had apparently become a sort of obsession with him. He seemed to attach almost a spiritual importance to it. And what Corky said was that, while he didn’t know what they did at the bottom of the jute business, instinct told him that it was something too beastly for words. Corky, moreover, believed in his future as an artist. Some day, he said, he was going to make a hit. Meanwhile, by using the utmost tact and persuasiveness, he was inducing his uncle to cough up very grudgingly a small quarterly allowance.

He wouldn’t have got this if his uncle hadn’t had a hobby. Mr. Worple was peculiar in this respect. As a rule, from what I’ve observed, the American captain of industry doesn’t do anything out of business hours. When he has put the cat out and locked up the office for the night, he just relapses into a state of coma from which he emerges only to start being a captain of industry again. But Mr. Worple in his spare time was what is known as an ornithologist. He had written a book called *American Birds*, and was writing another, to be called *More American Birds*. When he had finished that, the presumption was that he would begin a third, and keep on till the supply of American birds gave out. Corky used to go to him about once every three months and let him talk about American birds. Apparently you could do what you liked with old Worple if you gave him his head first on his pet subject, so these little chats used to make Corky’s allowance all right for the time being. But it was pretty rotten for the poor chap. There was the frightful suspense, you see, and, apart from that, birds, except when broiled and in the society of a cold bottle, bored him stiff.

To complete the character-study of Mr. Worple, he was a man of extremely uncertain temper, and his general tendency was to think that Corky was a poor chump and that whatever step he took in any direction on his own account was just another proof of his innate idiocy. I should imagine Jeeves feels very much the same about me.

So when Corky trickled into my apartment one afternoon, shooing a girl in front of him, and said, “Bertie, I want you to meet my fiancée, Miss Singer,” the aspect of the matter which hit me first was precisely the one which he had come to consult me about. The very first words I spoke were, “Corky, how about your uncle?”

The poor chap gave one of those mirthless laughs. He was looking anxious and worried, like a man who has done the murder all right but can’t think what the deuce to do with the body.

“We’re so scared, Mr. Wooster,” said the girl. “We were hoping that you might suggest a way of breaking it to him.”

Muriel Singer was one of those very quiet, appealing girls who have a way of looking at you with their big eyes as if they thought you were the greatest thing on earth and wondered that you hadn’t got on to it yet yourself. She sat there in a sort of shrinking way, looking at me as if she were saying to herself, “Oh, I do hope this great strong man isn’t going to hurt me.” She gave a fellow a protective kind of feeling, made him want to stroke her hand and say, “There, there, little one!” or words to that effect. She made me feel that there was nothing I wouldn’t do for her. She was rather like one of those innocent-tasting American drinks which creep imperceptibly into your system so that, before you know what you’re doing, you’re starting out to reform the world by force if necessary and pausing on your way to tell the large man in the corner that, if he looks at you like that, you will knock his head off. What I mean is, she made me feel alert and dashing, like a jolly old knight-errant or something of that kind. I felt that I was with her in this thing to the limit.

“I don’t see why your uncle shouldn’t be most awfully bucked,” I said to Corky. “He will think Miss Singer the ideal wife for you.”

Corky declined to cheer up.

“You don’t know him. Even if he did like Muriel he wouldn’t admit it. That’s the sort of pig-headed guy he is. It would be a matter of principle with him to kick. All he would consider would be that I had gone and taken an important step without asking his advice, and he would raise Cain automatically. He’s always done it.”

I strained the old bean to meet this emergency.

“You want to work it so that he makes Miss Singer’s acquaintance without knowing that you know her. Then you come along——”

“But how can I work it that way?”

I saw his point. That was the catch.

“There’s only one thing to do,” I said.

“What’s that?”

“Leave it to Jeeves.”

And I rang the bell.

“Sir?” said Jeeves, kind of manifesting himself. One of the rummy things about Jeeves is that, unless you watch like a hawk, you very seldom see him come into a room. He’s like one of those weird chappies in India who dissolve themselves into thin air and nip through space in a sort of disembodied way and assemble the parts again just where they want them. I’ve got a cousin who’s what they call a Theosophist, and he says he’s often nearly worked the thing himself, but couldn’t quite bring it off, probably owing to having fed in his boyhood on the flesh of animals slain in anger and pie.

The moment I saw the man standing there, registering respectful attention, a weight seemed to roll off my mind. I felt like a lost child who spots his father in the offing. There was something about him that gave me confidence.

Jeeves is a tallish man, with one of those dark, shrewd faces. His eye gleams with the light of pure intelligence.

“Jeeves, we want your advice.”

“Very good, sir.”

I boiled down Corky’s painful case into a few well-chosen words.

“So you see what it amounts to, Jeeves. We want you to suggest some way by which Mr. Worple can make Miss Singer’s acquaintance without getting on to the fact that Mr. Corcoran already knows her. Understand?”

“Perfectly, sir.”

“Well, try to think of something.”

“I have thought of something already, sir.”

“You have!”

“The scheme I would suggest cannot fail of success, but it has what may seem to you a drawback, sir, in that it requires a certain financial outlay.”

“He means,” I translated to Corky, “that he has got a pippin of an idea, but it’s going to cost a bit.”

Naturally, the poor chap’s face dropped, for this seemed to dish the whole thing. But I was still under the influence of the girl’s melting gaze, and I saw that this was where I started in as a knight-errant.

“You can count on me for all that sort of thing, Corky,” I said. “Only too glad. Carry on, Jeeves.”

“I would suggest, sir, that Mr. Corcor:an take advantage of Mr. Worple’s attachment to ornithology.”

“How on earth did you know that he was fond of birds?”

“It is the way these New York apartments are constructed, sir. Quite unlike our London houses. The partitions between the rooms are of the flimsiest nature. With no wish to overhear, I have sometimes heard Mr. Corcoran expressing himself with a generous strength on the subject I have mentioned.”

“Oh! Well?”

“Why should not the young lady write a small volume, to be entitled—let us say—*The Children’s Book of American Birds*, and dedicate it to Mr. Worple! A limited edition could be published at your expense, sir, and a great deal of the book would, of course, be given over to eulogistic remarks concerning Mr. Worple’s own larger treatise on the same subject. I should recommend the dispatching of a presentation copy to Mr. Worple, immediately on publication, accompanied by a letter in which the young lady asks to be allowed to make the acquaintance of one to whom she owes so much. This would, I fancy, produce the desired result, but as I say, the expense involved would be considerable.”

I felt like the proprietor of a performing dog on the vaudeville stage when the tyke has just pulled off his trick without a hitch. I had betted on Jeeves all along, and I had known that he wouldn’t let me down. It beats me sometimes why a man with his genius is satisfied to hang around pressing my clothes and what-not. If I had half Jeeves’s brain, I should have a stab at being Prime Minister or something.

“Jeeves,” I said, “that is absolutely ripping! One of your very best efforts.”

“Thank you, sir.”

The girl made an objection.

“But I’m sure I couldn’t write a book about anything. I can’t even write good letters.”

“Muriel’s talents,” said Corky, with a little cough, “lie more in the direction of the drama, Bertie. I didn’t mention it before, but one of our reasons for being a trifle nervous as to how Uncle Alexander will receive the news is that Muriel is in the chorus of that show *Choose Your Exit* at the Manhattan. It’s absurdly unreasonable, but we both feel that that fact might increase Uncle Alexander’s natural tendency to kick like a steer.”

I saw what he meant. Goodness knows there was fuss enough in our family when I tried to marry into musical comedy a few years ago. And the recollection of my Aunt Agatha’s attitude in the matter of Gussie and the vaudeville girl was still fresh in my mind. I don’t know why it is—one of these psychology sharps could explain it, I suppose—but uncles and aunts, as a class, are always dead against the drama, legitimate or otherwise. They don’t seem able to stick it at any price.

But Jeeves had a solution, of course.

“I fancy it would be a simple matter, sir, to find some impecunious author who would be glad to do the actual composition of the volume for a small fee. It is only necessary that the young lady’s name should appear on the title page.”

“That’s true,” said Corky. “Sam Patterson would do it for a hundred dollars. He writes a novelette, three short stories, and ten thousand words of a serial for one of the all-fiction magazines under different names every month. A little thing like this would be nothing to him. I’ll get after him right away.”

“Fine!”

“Will that be all, sir?” said Jeeves. “Very good, sir. Thank you, sir.”

I always used to think that publishers had to be devilish intelligent fellows, loaded down with the grey matter; but I’ve got their number now. All a publisher has to do is to write cheques at intervals, while a lot of deserving and industrious chappies rally round and do the real work. I know, because I’ve been one myself. I simply sat tight in the old apartment with a fountain-pen, and in due season a topping, shiny book came along.

I happened to be down at Corky’s place when the first copies of *The Children’s Book of American Birds* bobbed up. Muriel Singer was there, and we were talking of things in general when there was a bang at the door and the parcel was delivered.

It was certainly some book. It had a red cover with a fowl of some species on it, and underneath the girl’s name in gold letters. I opened a copy at random.

“Often of a spring morning,” it said at the top of page twenty-one, “as you wander through the fields, you will hear the sweet-toned, carelessly flowing warble of the purple finch linnet. When you are older you must read all about him in Mr. Alexander Worple’s wonderful book—*American Birds*.”

You see. A boost for the uncle right away. And only a few pages later there he was in the limelight again in connection with the yellow-billed cuckoo. It was great stuff. The more I read, the more I admired the chap who had written it and Jeeves’s genius in putting us on to the wheeze. I didn’t see how the uncle could fail to drop. You can’t call a chap the world’s greatest authority on the yellow-billed cuckoo without rousing a certain disposition towards chumminess in him.

“It’s a cert!” I said.

“An absolute cinch!” said Corky.

And a day or two later he meandered up the Avenue to my apartment to tell me that all was well. The uncle had written Muriel a letter so dripping with the milk of human kindness that if he hadn’t known Mr. Worple’s handwriting Corky would have refused to believe him the author of it. Any time it suited Miss Singer to call, said the uncle, he would be delighted to make her acquaintance.

Shortly after this I had to go out of town. Divers sound sportsmen had invited me to pay visits to their country places, and it wasn’t for several months that I settled down in the city again. I had been wondering a lot, of course, about Corky, whether it all turned out right, and so forth, and my first evening in New York, happening to pop into a quiet sort of little restaurant which I go to when I don’t feel inclined for the bright lights, I found Muriel Singer there, sitting by herself at a table near the door. Corky, I took it, was out telephoning. I went up and passed the time of day.

“Well, well, well, what?” I said.

“Why, Mr. Wooster! How do you do?”

“Corky around?”

“I beg your pardon?”

“You’re waiting for Corky, aren’t you?”

“Oh, I didn’t understand. No, I’m not waiting for him.”

It seemed to me that there was a sort of something in her voice, a kind of thingummy, you know.

“I say, you haven’t had a row with Corky, have you?”

“A row?”

“A spat, don’t you know—little misunderstanding—faults on both sides—er—and all that sort of thing.”

“Why, whatever makes you think that?”

“Oh, well, as it were, what? What I mean is—I thought you usually dined with him before you went to the theatre.”

“I’ve left the stage now.”

Suddenly the whole thing dawned on me. I had forgotten what a long time I had been away.

“Why, of course, I see now! You’re married!”

“Yes.”

“How perfectly topping! I wish you all kinds of happiness.”

“Thank you, so much. Oh Alexander,” she said, looking past me, “this is a friend of mine—Mr. Wooster.”

I spun round. A chappie with a lot of stiff grey hair and a red sort of healthy face was standing there. Rather a formidable Johnnie, he looked, though quite peaceful at the moment.

“I want you to meet my husband, Mr. Wooster. Mr. Wooster is a friend of Bruce’s, Alexander.”

The old boy grasped my hand warmly, and that was all that kept me from hitting the floor in a heap. The place was rocking. Absolutely.

“So you know my nephew, Mr. Wooster,” I heard him say. “I wish you would try to knock a little sense into him and make him quit this playing at painting. But I have an idea that he is steadying down. I noticed it first that night he came to dinner with us, my dear, to be introduced to you. He seemed altogether quieter and more serious. Something seemed to have sobered him. Perhaps you will give us the pleasure of your company at dinner tonight, Mr. Wooster? Or have you dined?”

I said I had. What I needed then was air, not dinner. I felt that I wanted to get into the open and think this thing out.

When I reached my apartment I heard Jeeves moving about in his lair. I called him.

“Jeeves,” I said, “now is the time for all good men to come to the aid of the party. A stiff b.-and-s. first of all, and then I’ve a bit of news for you.”

He came back with a tray and a long glass.

“Better have one yourself, Jeeves. You’ll need it.”

“Later on, perhaps, thank you, sir.”

“All right. Please yourself. But you’re going to get a shock. You remember my friend, Mr. Corcoran?”

“Yes, sir.”

“And the girl who was to slide gracefully into his uncle’s esteem by writing the book on birds?”

“Perfectly, sir.”

“Well, she’s slid. She’s married the uncle.”

He took it without blinking. You can’t rattle Jeeves.

“That was always a development to be feared, sir.”

“You don’t mean to tell me that you were expecting it?”

“It crossed my mind as a possibility.”

“Did it, by Jove! Well, I think you might have warned us!”

“I hardly liked to take the liberty, sir.”

Of course, as I saw after I had had a bite to eat and was in a calmer frame of mind, what had happened wasn’t my fault, if you come down to it. I couldn’t be expected to foresee that the scheme, in itself a cracker-jack, would skid into the ditch as it had done; but all the same I’m bound to admit that I didn’t relish the idea of meeting Corky again until time, the great healer, had been able to get in a bit of soothing work. I cut Washington Square out absolutely for the next few months. I gave it the complete miss-in-baulk. And then, just when I was beginning to think I might safely pop down in that direction and gather up the dropped threads, so to speak, time, instead of working the healing wheeze, went and pulled the most awful bone and put the lid on it. Opening the paper one morning, I read that Mrs. Alexander Worple had presented her husband with a son and heir.

I was so darned sorry for poor old Corky that I hadn’t the heart to touch my breakfast. I told Jeeves to drink it himself. I was bowled over. Absolutely. It was the limit.

I hardly knew what to do. I wanted, of course, to rush down to Washington Square and grip the poor blighter silently by the hand; and then, thinking it over, I hadn’t the nerve. Absent treatment seemed the touch. I gave it him in waves.

But after a month or so I began to hesitate again. It struck me that it was playing it a bit low-down on the poor chap, avoiding him like this just when he probably wanted his pals to surge round him most. I pictured him sitting in his lonely studio with no company but his bitter thoughts, and the pathos of it got me to such an extent that I bounded straight into a taxi and told the driver to go all out for the studio.

I rushed in, and there was Corky, hunched up at the easel, painting away, while on the model throne sat a severe-looking female of middle age, holding a baby.

A fellow has to be ready for that sort of thing.

“Oh, ah!” I said, and started to back out.

Corky looked over his shoulder.

“Halloa, Bertie. Don’t go. We’re just finishing for the day. That will be all this afternoon,” he said to the nurse, who got up with the baby and decanted it into a perambulator which was standing in the fairway.

“At the same hour tomorrow, Mr. Corcoran?”

“Yes, please.”

“Good afternoon.”

“Good afternoon.”

Corky stood there, looking at the door, and then he turned to me and began to get it off his chest. Fortunately, he seemed to take it for granted that I knew all about what had happened, so it wasn’t as awkward as it might have been.

It’s a well-known fact—though I can’t swear to the source—that when you’re feeling particularly on top of the world, Fate decides it’s time for a little surprise visit. Shakespeare might’ve said it, or maybe it was some other clever fellow with a penchant for poetic doom. But whatever the origin, it’s absolutely true: just as you’re basking in the golden glow of perfect contentment, life sneaks up behind you with a lead pipe and gives you a good whack. And me? I’m a prime example.

Take this whole business with Lady Malvern and her son Wilmot. A moment before they showed up, I was blissfully convinced that everything in my life was exactly as it should be. The sun was shining (or at least the sky wasn’t threatening rain), I’d just emerged from a cold shower feeling like a newborn colt, and—best of all—I’d finally stood up to Jeeves. That’s right. After months of being treated like a slightly confused child, I had *asserted myself*. Not just once, but twice! First, he tried to make me give up a perfectly good suit—fair enough, I suppose, since his taste is impeccable—but then he dared to forbid me from wearing my beloved cloth-topped boots. That was the final straw. Then came the hat crisis: he wanted me to wear the Longacre (as worn by John Drew, apparently), while I insisted on the Country Gentleman (worn by another famous actor, though I forget his name). After a tense standoff involving several rounds of firm refusal and one rather dramatic purchase, I won. I wore the Country Gentleman. I felt like a man again. A free man.

So there I was, standing in the bathroom, towel-drying my spine like a Roman emperor preparing for battle, humming slightly off-key, when the door tapped.

“Hullo?” I called.

“Lady Malvern wishes to see you, sir,” said Jeeves, voice smooth as silk.

“Eh?”

“Lady Malvern, sir. She is waiting in the sitting-room.”

I blinked. “Pull yourself together, Jeeves,” I said, trying to sound stern. “You know perfectly well no one’s in the sitting-room. It’s barely ten o’clock!”

“I gathered from her ladyship, sir, that she arrived early this morning from an ocean liner.”

Ah. That made it marginally less absurd. I remembered arriving in America myself about a year prior—shot out onto a foreign shore at six-thirty in the morning like a startled badger. So perhaps it was possible.

“Who on earth is Lady Malvern, Jeeves?”

“Her ladyship did not confide in me, sir.”

“Is she alone?”

“She is accompanied by Lord Pershore, sir. I believe he may be her son.”

“Oh. Well, put out something suitable, and I’ll be dressing.”

“Our heather-mixture lounge is ready, sir.”

“Lead the way.”

As I dressed, I tried to recall who Lady Malvern could possibly be. It wasn’t until I’d wrestled my shirt over my head and reached for the studs that it clicked.

“I’ve placed her, Jeeves. She’s a friend of Aunt Agatha’s.”

“Indeed, sir?”

“Yes. I met her at lunch one Sunday before I left London. Vicious woman. Writes books. Did one on social conditions in India after the Durbar.”

“Yes, sir. Pardon me, sir, but not that tie!”

“What?”

“Not that tie with the heather-mixture lounge, sir.”

I froze. This was serious. I thought I’d broken the ice with Jeeves. But here he was, back to his old ways—criticizing my fashion choices like I was still a boy who needed guiding.

“What’s wrong with it?” I demanded. “It’s cheerful pink. Nothing more.”

“Too ornate, sir.”

“Nonsense! I’ve seen you glare at it before. Speak plainly!”

“Unsuitable, sir.”

“Jeeves,” I said, clenching my jaw, “this is the tie I wear.”

“Very good, sir.”

He turned away. Dashed unpleasant. I could see the wound in his soul. But I held firm. I tied the tie, slipped into my coat and waistcoat, and marched into the sitting-room.

“Halloa! Halloa! Halloa!” I boomed. “What?”

“Ah! How do you do, Mr. Wooster? You haven’t met my son, Wilmot, I think? Motty, darling, this is Mr. Wooster.”

Lady Malvern was a force of nature—a large, energetic, radiant woman who seemed to occupy more space than the room itself. She sat in my biggest armchair as if it had been custom-built for her, and she radiated warmth and cheer like a furnace. Bright eyes, yellow hair, and fifty-seven front teeth visible at all times. She had the kind of presence that made you feel instantly inadequate—like a small boy caught in a drawing-room full of adults.

Motty, her son, was quite the opposite. Tall, thin, pale, and shy. He looked like a ghost who’d forgotten how to speak. Same yellow hair, plastered down and parted in the middle. Eyes dull grey with pink rims. Chin drooping. No eyelashes. Mild, furtive, sheepish—basically, the sort of chap who would nod politely if someone asked him to sign a confession.

“Awfully glad to see you,” I said. “So you’ve popped over? Making a long stay?”

“About a month,” she replied. “Your aunt gave me your address and told me to be sure and call on you.”

I felt a wave of relief. Aunt Agatha had been distant ever since I’d gone to New York to rescue Cousin Gussie from a music-hall girl—only to discover that Gussie had married her and joined the stage himself. Aunt Agatha had been furious. I hadn’t dared return. So hearing that she was now recommending people to visit me was like finding out I hadn’t been banned from England after all.

“You aunt said you’d do anything in your power to help us,” she continued.

“Rather! Absolutely!”

“Thank you so much. I want you to put dear Motty up for a little while.”

I didn’t get it.

“Put him up? For clubs?”

“No, no! Darling Motty is essentially a home bird. Aren’t you, Motty?”

Motty, who had been sucking the knob of his walking stick, managed a weak:

“Yes, mother.”

“I wouldn’t have him belong to clubs. I mean, have him live with you while I’m away.”

The words landed like a sack of bricks. I stared at her. She seemed utterly unaware of the horror she was inflicting.

I glanced at Motty. He was staring blankly at the wall, mouth still nuzzling his stick. The idea of having this creature lodged in my flat for a month—or longer—was enough to make me consider jumping out the window.

But before I could protest, she went on, rolling over me like a steamroller.

“I’m leaving New York by the midday train. I must visit Sing-Sing prison—I’m very interested in American prison reform. Then I’ll travel across to the coast, visiting points of interest. I’m here primarily on business. You’ve read my book, _India and the Indians_, I hope? My publishers want a companion volume on the United States. I’ll only be here a month, but that should suffice. I was less than a month in India, and Sir Roger Cremorne wrote his _America from Within_ after just two weeks. I’d love to take Motty with me, but poor boy—he gets sick on trains. I’ll pick him up on my return.”

From my vantage point, I could see Jeeves in the dining-room, laying the breakfast table. I wished I could talk to him. I knew he’d have a solution. But instead, I heard the door open and closed again.

“Jeeves!” I called. “What about it?”

“Sir?”

“What’s to be done? You heard it all. That… *excrescence* is coming to stay.”

“Pill, sir?”

“The what?”

“Pill, sir.”

I narrowed my eyes. This was unlike Jeeves. He was usually composed, never petty. But then I realized—ah! The tie. He was still upset about the pink tie. He was getting revenge.

“Lord Pershore will be staying from tonight, Jeeves,” I said coldly.

“Very good, sir. Breakfast is ready.”

I could have wept. There was no sympathy. None. Just silence and cold professionalism. I almost gave in and told him to destroy the hat and tie if he hated them so much—but no. I wouldn’t let him treat me like a serf again.

And so, with a heavy heart, I accepted my fate. If I threw Motty out, he’d tell his mother. She’d tell Aunt Agatha. And Aunt Agatha—well, she could turn a minor indiscretion into a national tragedy. I couldn’t risk it. I’d have to endure.

Midday came, and Motty’s luggage arrived—along with a massive parcel labeled “Books.” I brightened. Maybe this was going to be bearable. A year’s reading material? Perhaps he’d be too busy to cause trouble.

I donned my Country Gentleman hat, adjusted the pink tie, and headed out for lunch with friends. The afternoon passed pleasantly. By dinner, I’d almost forgotten about Motty.

I returned late, expecting to find him asleep. But the book parcel was still unopened. And then—scrabbling at the front door. A thud.

“Better go see what that is, Jeeves.”

“Very good, sir.”

He returned moments later.

“If you would step this way, sir, I think we might carry him in.”

“Carry him in?”

“His lordship is lying on the mat.”

I rushed to the door. There was Motty, curled up on the floor, moaning.

“He’s had a fit,” I said. “Jeeves! Someone’s fed him meat! He’s a vegetarian!”

“Sir?”

“He must’ve eaten steak! Call a doctor!”

“I hardly think so, sir. If you would take his legs—”

“Great Scot! You don’t think—he can’t be—”

“I am inclined to think so, sir.”

And indeed, he was. Once on the right track, you couldn’t miss it. Motty was under the surface.

“You never can tell, Jeeves!”

“Very seldom, sir.”

“Remove the eye of authority and where are you?”

“Precisely, sir.”

“Well, we’d better bring him in.”

We carried him inside. Jeeves tucked him into bed. I lit a cigarette and sat down, dreading what was to come.

Next morning, I went to check on him. I expected a wreck. Instead, he was sitting up, reading Gingery stories, chirpy as a bird.

“What ho!” I said.

“What ho!” he replied.

“What ho! What ho!”

“What ho! What ho! What ho!”

I stopped. Conversation was impossible.

“How are you feeling?” I asked.

“Topping!” he declared. “That fellow of yours—Jeeves—is a corker. I had a frightful headache, but he gave me this dark drink—his own invention—and I was right as rain. I must see more of him. He seems distinctly one of the ones!”

I couldn’t believe it. This was the same boy who’d sucked his stick like a nervous squirrel?

“You ate something that disagreed with you last night?” I ventured.

“No!” he snapped. “I drank too much! Much too much! Lots and lots too much! And I’m going to do it again—every night! If you ever see me sober, tap me on the shoulder and say, ‘Tut! Tut!’ and I’ll apologize and fix it.”

“But I’m responsible for you!”

“Can’t help your troubles,” he said firmly. “This is my first chance to yield to the temptations of a great city. What’s the point of temptations if no one yields? Makes it discouraging for the city. Besides, mother told me to collect impressions.”

I sat on the edge of the bed, stunned.

“I know how you feel,” he said kindly. “But duty first! This is my first real freedom. We’re only young once. Young man, rejoice in thy youth! Tra-la! What ho!”

Well. He had a point.

Over the next few days, Motty became a whirlwind. He started bringing pals back at three in the morning. One night, I came home to find a bull-terrier named Rollo—won in a raffle—grabbing my trouser leg.

“Jeeves!” I yelled.

“That would be Rollo, sir.”

“Eh?”

“His lordship’s dog. His temper is uncertain. I didn’t hear you come in.”

I watched as Jeeves walked into the room—calm, collected, fearless. The dog rolled over, paws in the air, as if hypnotized. Then, seeing me again, it lunged.

And that was the moment I realized: I was living in a house ruled by chaos, with a dog who hated me and a guest who’d turned into a human tornado—all because of a single, ill-advised request from a woman whose idea of kindness was dumping her son on a stranger.

And Jeeves? Still silent. Still judgmental. Still refusing to acknowledge that the pink tie had been a triumph.

I sighed. Life, I decided, was not fair. But at least I had a hat I could wear.

There are moments—usually early in the morning, when I’m half-asleep and sipping tea like a man who’s just discovered it’s a liquid—when I’ve caught myself wondering what on earth I’d do if Jeeves ever decided to leave me. It’s not that he’s particularly attached to me; in fact, I suspect he views me more as a slightly amusing inconvenience than a human being with feelings. But still, the thought of life without him is enough to make my soul shiver.

Now, don’t get me wrong—I’m not saying he’s *my* best friend or anything sentimental like that. He’s just… very good at everything. Like, for instance, putting on a shirt. You know how some valets fumble with studs? Not Jeeves. He slides them into place with the precision of a surgeon performing an operation on a tiny clockwork mouse. It’s unnerving. And yet, oddly comforting.

He’s also the only person I can count on when things go sideways—which, let’s face it, they tend to do whenever I’m involved. Whether it’s a crisis involving a stolen hat, a misplaced monocle, or a sudden desire to grow a moustache (more on that later), Jeeves always appears, calm as a cucumber, with a plan that somehow makes sense even though it probably shouldn’t.

Take the case of Bicky and his uncle—the Duke of Chiswick, otherwise known as “the hard-boiled egg.” That’s what they called him in England: a man so frugal he once refused to buy a new pair of socks because the old ones were still technically wearable. If you asked me, he was less a nobleman and more a walking savings account with a title.

It all began after I’d been in New York for a few months. I came home late one night, feeling rather tired and vaguely guilty about having skipped dinner with the club, when Jeeves handed me my final drink with the news:

“Mr. Bickersteth called twice, sir, while you were out.”

“Oh?” I said, taking a sip of whisky. “And?”

“He appeared a trifle agitated, sir.”

“Pipped?”

“That impression, sir.”

I didn’t mind much—Bicky was always pipped, usually because he’d spent the last of his allowance on something ridiculous like a monkey costume or a set of fake teeth. But this time felt different. And anyway, I had my own troubles: namely, the moustache.

Yes, I’d grown one. A little thing, really—just a faint shadow above my lip—but it had sent Jeeves into a state of near-melancholy. He couldn’t stand it. Every time I walked into a room, he’d look at me like I’d committed a crime against fashion. It wasn’t personal, of course. He simply believed in symmetry and elegance. And apparently, facial hair doesn’t fit into either category unless it’s carefully groomed, which mine most certainly was not.

So we’d been living in silence, me with my moustache, him with his disapproval. It was tense. Like two people sharing a train compartment but refusing to acknowledge each other’s existence.

Then came Bicky.

He showed up looking like someone had run over him with a steamroller. His eyes were sunken, his suit was askew, and he smelled faintly of despair.

“Halloa, Bertie!” he croaked. “Jeeves told you I was trying to reach you?”

“Indeed he did,” I said. “What’s the matter, old chap?”

“I’m in a hole,” he said, sinking into a chair. “A deep one.”

I glanced at Jeeves, who stood motionless, hands behind his back, as if waiting for instructions from a superior officer.

“Jeeves,” I said, “this is serious. Can you help?”

“Certainly, sir,” he replied, as though he’d already written the solution in invisible ink on the ceiling.

Bicky looked at him like he was a miracle worker.

“It’s about my uncle,” he said. “He’s coming tomorrow. The Duke of Chiswick.”

I blinked. “The *Duke*?”

“Yes,” said Jeeves, calmly. “His Grace.”

“You’re related to a duke?” I said. “That’s news.”

“Not directly,” said Bicky. “My father was his sister’s husband. Captain Rollo Bickersteth of the Coldstream Guards. Dead now. Left nothing.”

Ah. That explained why Bicky was perpetually broke. He had a duke for an uncle, yes—but one who treated money like it was made of glass and could crack if you looked at it too hard.

“Anyway,” Bicky continued, “the old boy wanted me to go to Colorado and learn ranching. I said no. Horses bite, Bertie. They’re vicious. I hate them. So instead, I cabled and told him I’d found a great business opportunity in New York. He wrote back saying fine, carry on. I’ve been here ever since, pretending I’m making a fortune.”

“And now he’s coming to visit?”

“Exactly. And I’ve been telling him I live in luxury. In a flat. With servants. All that jazz. But I’m staying in a boarding house. What am I supposed to do?”

I turned to Jeeves.

“What should he do?”

Jeeves paused. Then spoke.

“If I may suggest, sir, perhaps Mr. Bickersteth could borrow your flat.”

“Borrow it?” I said. “You mean… pretend it’s his?”

“Precisely, sir. With your permission, I could assume the role of his valet. You would be his guest. His grace would occupy the second spare bedroom. And a wireless message could be sent to inform him of the change of address.”

Bicky stared.

“You mean… I could tell him I own this place?”

“Absolutely, sir.”

We both stared at Jeeves. He gave a small nod, as if confirming a fact everyone already knew.

“Well,” said Bicky, “that’s clever. How do you think of these things?”

“I believe it has something to do with the shape of my head,” Jeeves replied. “It sticks out at the back.”

Bicky laughed. “I’ll take your word for it.”

The next morning, I woke early to greet the arrival of the Duke. Ocean liners, as anyone who’s ever been near one knows, arrive at unearthly hours. By nine o’clock, I was dressed, had my tea, and was leaning out the window, watching the street below.

And then—chaos.

A taxi pulled up. Out stepped a man in a top hat, waving his arms like a conductor leading a symphony of outrage. He was arguing with the cab driver over the fare.

“The trip from the dock to here would have cost eightpence in London!” he shouted.

The cabby shrugged. “This is New York, pal. Eight shillings.”

The Duke looked like he’d been punched in the stomach.

I called to Jeeves.

“The Duke’s arrived.”

“Yes, sir.”

Sure enough, Jeeves opened the door and ushered in the Duke of Chiswick, who looked like he’d just survived a war.

“How do you do, sir?” I said, stepping forward. “Your nephew met you at the dock, but you must have missed him. I’m Wooster. Great pal of Bicky’s. I’m staying here. Would you like a cup of tea?”

He sank into an armchair.

“Does this flat belong to my nephew Francis?”

“Absolutely.”

“It must be terribly expensive.”

“Well, yes,” I said. “Everything costs more here. Especially taxis.”

He sighed. “A terrible country! Nearly eight shillings for a short ride! Iniquitous!”

Jeeves brought the tea. The Duke took a sip and nodded slowly.

“Have you any idea how much he pays for this place?”

“About two hundred dollars a month,” I said.

“Forty pounds!” he gasped. “In England, that would pay for a modest cottage!”

I saw where this was going. He was suspicious. And rightly so. Poor old Bicky wasn’t exactly known for his financial acumen.

“So,” I said, “you might say Bicky has changed. New York brings out the best in people. Something in the air. Makes them sharp. Efficient. Businesslike. He’s doing quite well now. Everyone says so.”

“Really?” said the Duke. “What does he do?”

“Oh, just business,” I said. “Like Carnegie. Or Rockefeller. Big names.”

I left before he could ask another question.

Later that evening, I returned to find Bicky sitting in a chair, staring blankly at the wall, cigarette dangling from his mouth.

“Rotten luck,” he muttered. “He thinks I’m doing well. So he canceled my allowance.”

“What?”

“He said I don’t need it anymore. I’m making my own way.”

I felt sorry for him.

“We must do something,” I said.

Jeeves appeared, as if summoned by a silent bell.

“Sir?”

“Any ideas?”

“Yes, sir. Since we last spoke, I’ve considered a new approach.”

“Go on.”

“His grace is a prominent personage. Americans love shaking hands with prominent personages. Why not offer introductions—for a fee?”

Bicky snorted. “You want us to charge people to shake hands with my uncle?”

“I have an aunt,” said Jeeves, “who paid five shillings for a young man to bring a movie star to tea. It boosted her social standing.”

Bicky hesitated.

“If you think it could work…”

“I believe it will, sir.”

And so we launched the “Duke of Chiswick Experience.” We advertised locally. We offered handshake opportunities for $2.50. We even printed leaflets.

But nobody bit.

Until Jeeves stepped in.

One morning, he entered with tea and a smile.

“Might I speak to you, sir?”

“It’s off,” I said. “No one wants to meet the Duke.”

“I believe I’ve arranged it, sir.”

“Who?”

“Eighty-seven gentlemen from Birdsburg, Missouri.”

I dropped my tea.

“Birdsburg?”

“Yes, sir. Missouri.”

“How?”

“I sent a letter to the local newspaper. I mentioned that His Grace is visiting and is available for handshakes. The response was immediate.”

I sat back, stunned.

“Eighty-seven men from Missouri are coming to shake hands with my uncle?”

“Precisely, sir.”

And so they did.

They arrived in a convoy of wagons, wearing hats shaped like corn cobs, and demanded their introductions. The Duke, though bewildered, played along. And by the end of the week, Bicky had earned enough to cover his rent—and then some.

As for me? I learned a valuable lesson: never underestimate the power of a man who can turn a grumpy duke into a cash cow.

And Jeeves? Well, he just smiled, poured me another cup of tea, and said, “I believe the market is beginning to respond, sir.”

Which, of course, meant nothing to me—but sounded impressive.

Let me tell you about Bobbie Cardew. It’s a tale so rich in moral instruction that even the most hardened skeptic might find himself nodding sagely, like a man who’s just realized he left his hat on the bus and can’t remember where he was going. I won’t pretend to write like a novelist—no flowery prose or poetic flourishes here. The story stands on its own merits, and those merits are entirely due to the lesson it teaches: if you’re a man, read this and shudder; if you’re a woman, read it and laugh—because it’s all about how one lady managed to make a gentleman feel profoundly, deeply, and rather permanently embarrassed.

Now, if you’ve only recently met Bobbie, you might be surprised to learn that once upon a time, he was known less for his charm and more for his memory—or lack thereof. In fact, it wasn’t uncommon for people to say, “Oh, poor Bobbie! He forgets everything!” And they weren’t exaggerating. I mean, I’ve been called a scatterbrain myself, but compared to Bobbie? I’m a walking encyclopedia of half-remembered facts. He was the human equivalent of a faulty alarm clock: set it, forget it, and hope someone else remembers to wake you up.

When I first knew him, Bobbie was the undisputed champion of absent-mindedness within a five-mile radius. If I wanted him to dinner, I’d send a letter Monday morning, a telegram Tuesday night, a phone call Wednesday afternoon, and finally—just thirty minutes before the appointed hour—a messenger in a taxi with strict instructions to stand at the door until Bobbie arrived, preferably still wearing his slippers and holding a sandwich.

And yet—he wasn’t *entirely* an idiot. Deep down, beneath layers of woolly thinking and vague intentions, there was a kernel of sense. But you had to blow it out with dynamite. Or so I thought.

Then came marriage—the real dynamite, the soul-shaking kind. That’s what changed Bobbie. You know how a puppy chases a bee? It sees it, thinks it’s fun, doesn’t understand the sting until it’s too late. That’s how Bobbie fell into love. One moment he was chasing a pretty nurse, the next he was married—on impulse, with a whoop, as though tying the knot was just another game of polo.

Her name was Mary Anthony. Five feet six inches tall, with hair like spun gold and a chin that could cut glass. She worked as a hospital nurse, which, to a man who’d never lifted a finger for work in his life, seemed oddly romantic—like a knight rescuing a damsel who also happened to be a qualified medical professional.

Bobbie broke his leg playing polo (a classic move), and Mary was assigned to look after him. Within a week, they were off to the registry office, arm-in-arm, like two kids skipping school. A true romance—except maybe not quite the kind you’d expect.

I met them both at the club one evening. Bobbie announced the news proudly. The next day, he introduced me to Mary. I liked her instantly. I’ve never worked a day in my life—my name’s Reggie Pepper, by the way. My uncle Edward ran Pepper, Wells & Co., coal merchants, and left me a tidy sum. So I don’t need to earn a living, but I do admire anyone who does—especially women who’ve had to fight for every penny. Mary had been orphaned early, raised herself, and clawed her way through life with grit and grace. I respected her.

We got along famously at first. Not anymore, but we’ll get to that. Back then, she looked at Bobbie like he was made of moonlight and magic. He returned the favor with equal enthusiasm. I figured, if only Bobbie remembered the wedding date, they might actually survive.

Fast forward a year. They’d moved into a flat. I dropped by often. Everything seemed perfect—until the quiet dinner incident.

I ran into Bobbie in Piccadilly. He invited me to dinner at home. Being the fool I am, I accepted. What was I thinking?

When we arrived, Mary was waiting. And let me tell you—she looked like a queen who’d just discovered she was about to be deposed. Her hair was piled high with diamonds, her dress was something from heaven, and her smile was pure ice. I thought, *If this is how she dresses for quiet dinners, no wonder Bobbie loves coming home.*

“Here’s old Reggie,” said Bobbie cheerfully. “I’ve brought him to share a bite. I’ll phone the kitchen—what?”

She stared. Then turned red. Then white. Then gave a tiny, strained laugh. I wanted to vanish. She recovered quickly.

“I’m so glad you could come, Mr. Pepper,” she said, smiling at me like a diplomat negotiating a peace treaty.

Dinner went smoothly. She joked, played ragtime, laughed. But I saw the strain. I saw the effort. I knew she was holding back a scream that would have shaken the foundations of the building. I left as soon as I could.

The next day, I found Bobbie at the club looking like a lost sock in a blizzard.

“Do you know how long I’ve been married?” he asked.

“About a year?”

“No,” he said, voice cracking. “Exactly a year—yesterday.”

Lightbulb moment.

“Yesterday was……?”

“Our anniversary. I’d planned to take Mary to the Savoy, then to Covent Garden. She really wanted to hear Caruso. I had the ticket in my pocket. All through dinner, I kept feeling like I’d forgotten something—but couldn’t place it.”

“Until your wife mentioned it?”

He nodded slowly.

“She mentioned it,” he said, as if the phrase held the weight of the world.

I didn’t ask for details. Women like Mary—strong, determined, with hair like fire and chins like armor—can be angels. But when they lose their patience, they don’t half-hearted about it.

“To be perfectly honest,” Bobbie said, voice breaking, “my stock’s pretty low at home.”

I lit a cigarette and said nothing. He didn’t want talk. He walked out. I watched him go. He stopped, hesitated, then entered a jeweler’s. That, I thought, was the first sign of intelligence in years.

From that point on, I became genuinely invested in Bobbie’s marital crisis. Most marriages are mildly interesting. This one was a full-blown disaster waiting to happen. Bobbie was a gentle soul, yes, but fundamentally a dunce. Mary was determined to fix him. Nature, however, was on Bobbie’s side. When Nature creates a dunce, she protects it with a special gift: short-term memory loss. It keeps the dunce forever a dunce. Take me, for instance. I’ve forgotten more things than I’ve learned. If I’d remembered half the advice I’ve been given, I’d be wearing a size nine hat. As it is, I wear a ten—and it’s still too big.

For a week, Bobbie was energized by shame. He remembered things. Elephants, I’ve read, have excellent memories. But they were no match for Bobbie during that week. Yet the shock was too small. It dented his mental armor, but didn’t break it. Soon, he was back to normal.

It was heartbreaking. Mary loved him. But she was afraid. She knew that if a man forgets his wedding anniversary after one year, he’ll forget he’s married at all by year four. She had to act now.

I tried to help. One afternoon, he came to me, distressed.

“It’s such a little thing,” he said, “but she made a fuss. I forgot to bring home that book she asked for.”

“She knows you forget things,” I said.

“Of course. But it’s not fair. I can’t remember anything. Never could.”

He rambled on. Then pulled out two sovereigns.

“Oh, by the way,” he said. “I owe you.”

“For the bet on Tuesday?”

“Yes. Murray and Brown playing hundred-up. I gave you two-to-one on Brown. Murray beat him by twenty.”

“So you remember some things?”

He bristled. “Of course I do! Don’t think I’m the sort who forgets to pay debts!”

“Subside, laddie,” I said.

Then I spoke like a father.

“What you need to do, my dear old friend,” I said, “is pull yourself together. You’re heading for trouble. You’ve shown you *can* remember things—like bets. So why not birthdays? Anniversaries? Make it part of your list. It may hurt your brain, but you can’t avoid it.”

“I suppose you’re right,” he said. “But why does she care so much about dates? Does it matter if I forget when we got married or when she was born or when the cat had the measles? She knows I love her.”

“That’s not enough for a woman,” I said. “They want to *see* it. Remember that. Forget it, and there’ll be trouble.”

He chewed his cane.

“Women are frightfully strange,” he said.

“You should have thought of that before marrying one,” I replied.

I did what I could. I laid it bare. But Bobbie didn’t listen. He went back to his old ways.

Weeks passed. Then, one spring morning, I was smoking by the window when Bobbie burst in, eyes bulging, face pale as a ghost, waving a piece of paper.

“Reggie!” he cried. “She’s gone!”

“Who?”

“Mary! Left me! Gone!”

“Where?”

Silly question? Maybe. But Bobbie nearly foamed at the mouth.

“How should I know? Read this!”

I took the letter. It was brief, sharp, devastating:

> MY DEAR BOBBIE,—I am going away. When you care enough about me to remember to wish me many happy returns on my birthday, I will come back. My address will be Box 341, _London Morning News_.

I read it twice.

“Well,” I said, “why don’t you?”

“Why don’t I what?”

“Why don’t you wish her many happy returns? Seems simple.”

“But she says ‘on her birthday.’”

“And when is that?”

“Can’t you understand?” he snapped. “I’ve forgotten.”

“Forgotten?”

“Yes.”

“How?” I asked. “Just the date? Or completely?”

“I know it was somewhere between January 1st and December 31st. That’s how close I get.”

“Think.”

“Think? What’s the use? I’ve been trying since I opened the letter. I’ve knocked sparks out of my skull.”

I rang for drinks.

“Well,” I said, “this is a tough case. Suppose someone came to Sherlock Holmes and said, ‘Mr. Holmes, when is my wife’s birthday?’ Even Sherlock would blink. But I know enough to know we need clues. Can you remember the last birthday? What was the weather?”

“Ordinary.”

“Warm?”

“Warmish.”

“Cold?”

“Fairly cold, maybe. I can’t recall.”

I ordered more drinks. “You’re a great help, Bobbie. Truly invaluable.”

Then—miracle of miracles—he had an idea.

“I’ve got it!” he said. “I gave her a present last year. We just go to the shop, find the receipt, and—done!”

“What did you give her?”

He slumped. “I can’t remember.”

But then—another idea! Like golf, sometimes inspiration comes in bursts. He suggested buying twelve little books called *When Were You Born?* Each month has its own volume, telling you your character, talents, weaknesses—all for fourpence halfpenny. We’d buy all twelve and find which one matched Mary.

Clever plan. We bought them. Half each. We sat down.

But every month described Mary perfectly. December: “Keeps secrets, travels widely.” Mary had kept her secret and traveled far. October: “Original ideas, loves movement.” Exactly her style. February: “Wonderful memory.” Yes, indeed.

Bobbie favored May: “Capricious—barrier to happiness.” I preferred February: “Determined, earnest, expects full return.” He agreed.

Eventually, he tore the books up, stamped on them, burned them, and went home.

But something had changed. Bobbie was thinking. Really thinking. For the first time in his life, he was using his brain. It was painful to watch, but I knew it was good. He was improving. Maybe not becoming brilliant, but becoming… less of a disaster.

He came to me often. I gave him sympathy. And always, when he was weak, I delivered the Moral Lesson.

One day, he arrived with a glimmer of hope.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’ve got it. I remember something vital.”

“What?”

“We went to the Coliseum on her birthday. That helps, doesn’t it?”

“It’s a start,” I said. “But how?”

“They change the program weekly. I remember one act—Professor Somebody’s Terpsichorean Cats.”

“Ah! Now we’re talking.”

So we went to the Coliseum. They dug through archives and found the cats performed in mid-May.

“I told you it was May,” Bobbie said triumphantly.

“If you’ve any sense,” I said, “there won restrained.”

He agreed.

That night, my phone rang. Bobbie.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’ve got it. We saw the cats at a matinee.”

“Ah. So it must have been Wednesday the 7th or Saturday the 10th.”

“Unless they had daily matinees,” I said.

I heard a groan.

Then I remembered: “You had lunch at the Ritz that day. You wrote a cheque.”

“For ten pounds. To the hotel. Check your book.”

He gasped. “Reggie, you’re a genius!”

He called back.

“It was the 8th. Reggie, old man, I——”

“Topping,” I said. “Good night.”

I rang the hotel near the Strand.

“Put me through to Mrs. Cardew.”

“It’s late,” said the operator.

“And getting later,” I said. “Hurry up.”

She answered.

“What is it?”

“My feet are cold,” I said. “But I’m calling to tell you—Bobbie’s remembered.”

She screamed.

I smiled. I’d done my job.

And that, my dear reader, is the story of how one man learned the hard way that love isn’t just feeling—it’s remembering.

I don’t want to bore you—no, really, I don’t—but I’ve got to tell you about dear old Freddie Meadowes. I’m not much of a writer, mind you—more the sort who’d rather have a good cigar and a decent game of croquet than wrestle with prose—but once I get this down, I’ll hand it over to some literary type to polish up. That way, it’ll sound like something out of a book instead of just a bloke rambling on after too many whiskies.

Freddie’s been my mate for years—solid, dependable, and all that sort of thing. So when I walked into the club one morning and found him slumped in a dark corner, staring blankly at the wall like a man who’d just seen the last of his fortune vanish into thin air, I knew something was wrong. Normally, Freddie is the life and soul of any party—full of jokes, charm, and an uncanny ability to sing “The Rosary” with one finger. But this? This was different. He looked like he’d lost the last sixpence in his pocket—and then swallowed it.

Jimmy Pinkerton was there with me. Jimmy’s a playwright—a clever fellow, though he spends half his time trying to convince people that he’s not actually a madman. Between us, we managed to coax the truth out of Freddie. As we suspected, it was a woman. Angela West—the girl he was engaged to—had broken things off. She wouldn’t speak to him, wouldn’t answer his letters, wouldn’t even let him near her phone. The whole business had left him looking like a man who’d just discovered that his favorite hat had gone missing.

I felt for him. I know what it’s like. Once, I was madly in love with a girl named Elizabeth Shoolbred. She couldn’t stand me. It’s all in my autobiography, if you ever read it. So I knew exactly what Freddie needed.

“Change of scene,” I said. “Come with me to Marvis Bay. I’ve rented a cottage there. Jimmy’s coming down on the twenty-fourth. We’ll be a cozy little group.”

“Absolutely right,” said Jimmy. “Change of scenery is the cure. I once knew a man whose fiancée refused him. He went abroad. Two months later, she wired: ‘Come back. Muriel.’ He started writing a reply—then suddenly realized he couldn’t remember her surname. Never sent it. And never saw her again.”

Freddie didn’t seem convinced. He just stared at the ceiling like a man who’d forgotten how to blink. But eventually, he agreed to come. “Might as well be there as anywhere,” he muttered.

Now, do you know Marvis Bay? It’s in Dorsetshire. Not exactly the kind of place where adventures happen—unless you count being eaten alive by gnats as an adventure. You spend your days swimming, sitting on the sand, and in the evenings, walking along the shore while the insects swarm around you like tiny, irate helicopters. At nine o’clock, you rub antiseptic on your bites and go to bed. Peaceful. Quiet. Boring.

But it seemed to agree with Freddie. Once the moon came out and the wind whispered through the trees, he became inseparable from the beach. The gnats adored him. They’d hang around waiting for him like loyal subjects, ignoring perfectly respectable passersby just so they could be ready when he emerged.

It was nice, really. But by the end of the first week, I began to wish Jimmy had arrived earlier. Because Freddie, bless him, wasn’t exactly the most lively companion. When he wasn’t chewing on a pipe and glaring at the carpet, he was at the piano, playing “The Rosary” with one finger. He couldn’t play anything else. And even “The Rosary” was a disaster—about halfway through, a fuse would blow, and he’d have to start over.

One morning, I came in from a swim to find him mid-performance.

“Reggie,” he said, voice hollow, “I’ve seen her.”

“Seen who?” I asked.

“Miss West. I was at the post office. We met in the doorway. She cut me.”

He started “The Rosary” again, but missed the second bar.

“You ought never to have brought me here,” he said. “I must go away.”

“Go away?” I said. “Don’t be absurd. This is exactly what you need. This is where you come out strong.”

“She cut me.”

“Never mind. Be a sportsman. Try again.”

“She looked clean through me!”

“Of course she did. But don’t worry. Put this in my hands. I’ll see you through. What you need,” I said, “is to make her owe you something. Get her thanking you timidly. That’s the key.”

“But why would she thank me?”

I paused. Then it hit me.

“Save her from drowning,” I said.

“I can’t swim,” he replied.

Ah, there it was. Freddie—dear, sweet Freddie—was the kind of man who could win a polo match blindfolded but couldn’t rescue a cat from a tree without getting himself wet.

He cranked the piano again. I dashed outside.

I strolled along the beach, thinking. Freddie wasn’t much help. He had his strengths—he was brilliant at polo, and his imitation of cats fighting in a backyard was uncanny—but when it came to initiative, he was about as useful as a rubber duck in a hurricane.

Then, rounding some rocks, I saw a blue dress. And by Jove—it was her. Angela West. I hadn’t met her, but Freddie had sixteen photos of her scattered around his room, so I knew it was her. She was helping a small, fat child build a sandcastle. Nearby, an elderly lady sat reading a novel. “Aunt,” the girl called her. So, using my best Sherlock Holmes logic, I deduced the kid was her cousin. I imagined Freddie would’ve tried to sentimentalize the situation—maybe say the kid reminded him of his own childhood. Me? I just thought he looked like a round, slightly grumpy potato.

After finishing the castle, the kid grew bored and started whimpering. The girl took him to a sweets stall. I walked on.

Now, if you ask most fellows, they’ll tell you I’m a chump. And honestly? I don’t argue. I *am* a chump. The Peppers have always been chumps. But every now and then—when you least expect it—I get a brainwave so hot it could fry an egg on a winter’s day. And that’s what happened now.

On my way back, I saw the kid smacking a jellyfish with a spade. The girl was nowhere in sight. I stopped. Then it struck me.

What if I kidnapped the kid? Just for a moment. If Angela starts panicking, and Freddie suddenly appears, leading the child by the hand, telling a dramatic story about how he found him wandering alone and practically saved his life—well, wouldn’t she feel obliged to forgive him?

I grabbed the kid and made off with him. On the way back, I pictured the scene: Freddie stepping forward, heroic, Angela melting into his arms. I could see it so clearly I nearly choked on emotion.

When I burst into the cottage with the kid, Freddie didn’t exactly jump for joy. The child had started screaming. Poor Freddie looked like he’d been handed a live eel.

“Stop it!” he yelled. “Do you think nobody has troubles except you? What the deuce is going on?”

The kid answered with a yell that rattled the windows. I ran to the kitchen, fetched a jar of honey. It worked. The kid stopped crying and began spreading the honey on his face like it was art.

“Well?” Freddie said, when silence returned.

I explained the plan. After a few moments, he finally grasped it.

“You’re not such a fool as you look, sometimes, Reggie,” he said, impressed. “This seems promising.”

He pulled the kid off the honey jar and headed out to find Angela.

I was thrilled. I loved Freddie. Knowing he’d soon be his old self again made me feel like I’d just won the lottery. I was lounging on the veranda, smoking peacefully, when I saw Freddie returning—still with the kid. And he looked like he’d just lost a fight with a badger.

“Hello!” I said. “Couldn’t find her?”

“Yes,” he said, with a hollow laugh. “I found her.”

“Well, then——?”

Freddie sank into a chair and groaned.

“This isn’t her cousin, you idiot!” he said. “He’s not related to her at all. She just met him on the beach. Never seen him before.”

“What? Who is he?”

“I don’t know. Oh, Lord, I’ve had a time! Thank goodness you’ll probably spend the next few years in Dartmoor for kidnapping. That’s my only comfort. I’ll come and jeer at you through the bars.”

“Tell me everything,” I said.

It took him ages. He kept interrupting to call me names, but I pieced together the story. She’d listened to his heroic tale like an iceberg. Then—without calling him a liar—she made it clear that if he and Dr. Cook ever met and compared stories, it would be the biggest duel in history. He’d crawled away, humiliated.

“And mind,” he concluded, “this is your mess. I’m not involved. If you want to avoid prison, go find the kid’s parents and return him before the police show up.”

By Jove, I didn’t realize how hard it would be to return a kidnapped child. I searched Marvis Bay like a bloodhound. No one claimed him. It was as if he belonged there. Then, by inspiration, I asked the sweet-stall man. He told me the kid’s name was Medwin, and his parents lived at Ocean Rest, Beach Road.

I raced there and knocked. No answer. I knocked again. I heard movement inside, but no one came. Just as I was about to take the knocker apart, a voice shouted from above:

“Hi!”

I looked up. A round, pink face with grey whiskers stared down from a window.

“Hi!” it shouted again.

“What do you mean by ‘Hi’?” I asked.

“You can’t come in,” the face said. “Hello, is that Tootles?”

“My name’s not Tootles,” I said. “And I don’t want to come in. Are you Mr. Medwin? I’ve brought back your son.”

“I see him. Peep-bo, Tootles! Dadda can see ’oo!”

The face vanished. Voices echoed. It reappeared.

“Hi!”

I kicked the gravel furiously.

“Do you live here?” I asked.

“I’m staying here for a few weeks.”

“What’s your name?”

“Pepper. But——”

“Pepper? Any relation to Edward Pepper, the colliery owner?”

“My uncle. But——”

“I used to know him well. Dear old Edward Pepper! I wish I was with him now.”

“I wish you were,” I said.

He beamed.

“This is most fortunate,” he said. “We were wondering what to do with Tootles. My daughter Bootles has mumps. Tootles mustn’t be exposed. We couldn’t think what to do. It was most fortunate you found him. He strayed from his nurse. I wouldn’t trust him to a stranger, but you’re different. Any nephew of Edward Pepper’s has my full confidence. You must take Tootles to your house. It will be ideal. I’ve written to my brother in London to fetch him. He may arrive in a few days.”

“May?”

“He’s busy, of course. But he should be here within a week. Until then, Tootles can stay with you. Very much obliged. Your wife will like Tootles.”

“I haven’t got a wife,” I yelled—but the window slammed shut, as if the man had just trapped a germ escaping.

I sighed. The window flew open again.

“Hi!”

A heavy package hit me on the head and exploded like a bomb.

“Did you catch it?” the face asked. “Dear me, you missed it! Never mind. You can get it at the grocer’s. Ask for Bailey’s Granulated Breakfast Chips. Tootles takes them with milk.”

My spirit was crushed. I accepted my fate. Taking Tootles by the hand, I walked away. Napoleon’s retreat from Moscow was a picnic by comparison.

As we turned the corner, we met Angela.

Tootles pointed at her and said, “Wah!”

She smiled. I let go of him. He ran to her.

“Well, baby?” she said, bending down. “So father found you again, did he? Your little son and I made friends on the beach this morning,” she said to me.

I froze. Coming on top of the whiskered lunatic, this was too much. She had nodded goodbye and was halfway down the road before I could gasp, “Wait! I’m not his father!”

I didn’t expect Freddie to be joyful. But I thought he might show a bit of grit. Instead, he leaped up, clutched his head, and didn’t speak for ages. When he did, it was a torrent of insults and emotional outbursts I didn’t know he was capable of.

“Well,” he said finally, “say something! Man, why don’t you say something?”

“You don’t give me a chance,” I said gently.

“What are you going to do about it?”

“What can we do?”

“We can’t spend our lives nursing this—this exhibit.”

He stood up.

“I’m going back to London,” he said.

“Freddie!” I cried. “Would you desert a pal like this?”

“I would. This is your problem. You’ve got to fix it.”

“Freddie,” I said, “you’ve got to stand by me. You must. Do you realize this child needs undressing, bathing, dressing again? You wouldn’t leave me to do it alone? We were at school together. Your mother likes me. You owe me ten bob.”

He sat down.

“Oh, well,” he said, resigned.

“Besides,” I said, “I did this all for you.”

He looked at me strangely.

“Reggie,” he said, voice strained, “one moment. I’ll stand a lot—but not being expected to be grateful.”

Looking back, I realize what saved me from madness was buying out the local sweet shop. By feeding Tootles constantly, we survived the day. At eight o’clock, he fell asleep in a chair. We undressed him—unbuttoning everything, pulling where buttons weren’t—and carried him upstairs.

Freddie stared at the pile of clothes. I knew what he was thinking: how on earth do we get him dressed again?

The next morning, we borrowed a nurse from the next bungalow. Women are amazing. She had the kid dressed and looking like a champion in eight minutes. I paid her handsomely. She promised to come daily.

Breakfast was almost pleasant. Then Tootles spilled milk on Freddie’s trousers.

When Freddie returned, he launched into a monologue about King Herod. “The more I see of this kid,” he said, “the less I wonder at his views on infanticide.”

Two days later, Jimmy Pinkerton arrived. He took one look at the screaming child and picked up his suitcase.

“For me,” he said, “the hotel. I can’t write dialogue with that noise. Whose idea was this? Which of you adopted this little treasure?”

I explained the mumps situation. Jimmy perked up.

“I might turn this into a farce,” he said. “Act two, perfect setup.”

“Farce!” snarled Freddie.

“Exactly. Curtain falls on hero—well-meaning, half-baked idiot—kidnapping the child. Second act: his misadventures. I’ll sketch it tonight. Come on, Reggie, show me the hotel.”

On the way, I told him about Angela.

He dropped his suitcase and stared at me like an owl.

“What?” he said. “Why, this is a play! Ready-made! Parted lovers. Lisping child. Reconciliation over the cradle. Classic. Child center stage. Girl L.C.; Freddie upstage, by the piano. Can Freddie play?”

“He can play a bit of ‘The Rosary’ with one finger.”

Jimmy shook his head. “We’ll cut the music. But the rest is gold. Look.” He squatted in the sand. “This stone is the girl. This seaweed is the child. This nutshell is Freddie. Dialogue builds to child’s line. ‘Boofer lady, does i’oo love dadda?’ Business of hands reaching. Freeze. Freddie crosses, takes girl’s hand. Swallows lump. Big speech. ‘Ah, Angela, has not this gone on too long? A little child rebukes us!’ And so on. Freddie writes his own part. We need a better line for the kid. ‘Kiss Freddie.’ Short, crisp, punchy.”

“But Jimmy,” I said, “there’s no way to get the girl here. She cuts Freddie.”

Jimmy frowned. “Awkward. But we’ll make it exterior. We’ll corner her on the beach. Meanwhile, we must rehearse the kid. First rehearsal—eleven sharp.”

We decided not to tell Freddie. He was already in a bad mood. So we focused on Tootles. And quickly realized: the only way to teach him lines was with sweets.

“The challenge,” said Jimmy after the first rehearsal, “is linking the line to the reward. Once he understands that saying those words brings acid-drops, we’re golden.”

It was thrilling. Some days, he nailed it. Other days, he said “Kick Freddie.” Jimmy vetoed that.

But fate had other plans.

Freddie was at the piano. I was taking Tootles out. Suddenly, Angela appeared on the path.

Tootles screamed. She stopped at the steps.

“Hello, baby!” she said. “Good morning. May I come up?”

She didn’t wait. She walked in, fussed over the kid. Six feet away, Freddie pounded the piano.

I tried to break it up.

“We were just going to the beach,” I said.

“Yes?” she replied. “So you’re tuning the piano? My aunt’s been looking for a tuner. Mind if I go in and tell him to come to us?”

“Er—not yet!” I said. “He can’t be disturbed. Artistic temperament. I’ll tell him later.”

“Very well,” she said. “Ask him to call Pine Bungalow. West is the name. Oh, he seems to have stopped. I suppose he’ll be out soon. I’ll wait.”

“Don’t you think—shouldn’t we go?”

She ignored me, rummaging in her pocket.

“The beach,” I babbled.

And that was it. The curtain rose. The performance began. And none of us had rehearsed Freddie’s part.

If you’ve ever had the misfortune to be tangled up in someone else’s drama—especially when it involves mistaken identities, secret siblings, and a prince getting clocked in the dark—you’ll understand why I still shudder at the memory of George Lattaker’s escapade in Monte Carlo. I wouldn’t normally go on about such things, but this one was too absurd not to share.

We’d arrived in Monte Carlo aboard the _Circe_, a yacht belonging to an old sportsman named Marshall. On board were myself, my valet Voules (a man whose emotional range is usually limited to “yes, sir” and “no, sir”), Mrs. Vanderley, her daughter Stella, Mrs. Vanderley’s maid Pilbeam, and George—my dear, slightly dim-witted friend who’d been dragged along by me because I thought he deserved a holiday before his big inheritance.

George had just turned twenty-five, which meant his uncle Augustus, the trustee of a legacy left by Aunt Emily, was supposed to hand over a tidy sum. The money had been sitting in trust since George was a baby, and frankly, it wasn’t just *an income*—it was *a pile*. And since Uncle Augustus was already in Monte Carlo, I suggested George come meet him there instead of waiting for him to come to London. A two-for-one deal: settle affairs and enjoy the Riviera. George agreed. We anchored in Monaco Harbour, and the next day was supposed to be the big reveal.

But as fate would have it, the trouble began at 7 a.m., when I was jolted awake by what sounded like a domestic tragedy unfolding outside my cabin door. A woman sobbing “Oh, Harold!” and a male voice—deep, angry, and unmistakably Voules’s—shouting back. It was enough to wake the dead.

“Voules!” I bellowed.

The noise stopped instantly. Sobs receded into the distance. Then came a knock. Voules entered with that perfectly composed, “Your carriage awaits, sir,” expression. You’d never guess he’d just been involved in a full-blown emotional meltdown.

“Voules,” I said, “are you under the impression I’m running for Queen of the May? You woke me at seven!”

“I understood you to summon me, sir.”

“I summoned you to explain why you were screaming like a banshee.”

“I owe you an apology, sir. In the heat of the moment, I raised my voice.”

“It’s a wonder you didn’t bring down the ceiling. Who was that woman?”

“Miss Pilbeam, sir—Mrs. Vanderley’s maid.”

“What was the fuss about?”

“I was breaking our engagement, sir.”

I nearly fell off the bed. Voules? Engaged? This was news. But I didn’t pry—after all, I wasn’t paid to meddle in love lives, just to keep my trousers pressed. So I changed the subject.

“I think I’ll get up,” I said.

“Yes, sir.”

“I can’t wait for breakfast. Can you rustle something up?”

“Yes, sir.”

So I ate alone and went topside to smoke. The morning was glorious—blue sea, sparkling Casino, perfect weather. Soon the others started appearing. Stella Vanderley was first. She looked pale. Said she hadn’t slept well. Fair enough—unless you get your eight hours, you’re no good to anyone.

“Seen George?” I asked.

She froze. Odd, because George and Stella had been practically inseparable during the trip. I half expected George to pop out and whisper, “I’ve done it, old scout—she loves me!”

“I haven’t seen Mr. Lattaker,” she said.

I let it drop. George was clearly low on the totem pole that morning.

Then the papers arrived.

Mrs. Vanderley opened hers and gasped.

“The poor, dear Prince!” she wailed.

“Shocking!” said Marshall.

“I knew him in Vienna,” she added. “He danced beautifully.”

I read mine. Turns out His Serene Highness the Prince of Saxburg-Leignitz—why do they call them “Serene”?—had been attacked in a dark alley after leaving the Casino. No escort, apparently, so some ruffian took advantage and gave him a thorough beating. He’d been found unconscious by a passerby and taken back to his yacht, where he remained comatose.

“This isn’t going to end well,” I said. “What happens if you hit a Serene Highness? Do they hang you?”

“Later,” read Marshall, “the pedestrian who discovered the Prince was Mr. Denman Sturgis, famed private investigator. He’s offered his services and claims to have a vital clue.” That’s the guy who cracked the Chicago kidnapping case. If anyone could catch the culprit, it was him.

Five minutes later, a boat pulled alongside. A tall, thin man climbed aboard, scanned the group, and fixed on Marshall.

“Good morning,” he said. “I believe you have a Mr. George Lattaker on board?”

“Yes,” said Marshall. “He’s below. Want to see him? Whom shall I say?”

“He wouldn’t know my name. Urgent business. Just a moment.”

“Take a seat. He’ll be up shortly. Reggie, go fetch him.”

I went to George’s cabin.

“George!” I yelled.

No answer. I opened the door. Empty. And the bunk hadn’t been slept in. I blinked. I walked back on deck.

“He’s not here,” I said.

“Not here?” Marshall frowned. “Perhaps he went ashore. He’ll be back soon. Breakfast is starting. Join us?”

The man said yes. Then the gong rang and everyone headed down, leaving me alone.

I sat smoking, thinking. Then I heard a faint, hoarse whisper—“Reggie?”—and looked over my shoulder.

There, at the top of the gangway, stood George—dusty, hatless, in evening clothes, looking like a fugitive from a bad dream.

“Great Scott!” I cried.

“Shh!” he whispered. “Anyone around?”

“They’re all at breakfast.”

He sank into my chair, eyes closed. Poor fellow looked like he’d been through a war.

“I say,” I touched his shoulder.

He jumped like a startled rabbit.

“Don’t do that! What’s the idea? How am I supposed to make friends if you keep startling me? My nerves are shot, Reggie!”

“Sorry. What happened?”

“I did a murder last night.”

I stared.

“You—what?”

“It could happen to anyone. After Stella broke off our engagement—”

“Broke off? How long were you engaged?”

“Two minutes. Maybe less. I proposed at ten last night in the saloon. She said yes. I was about to kiss her when we heard footsteps. I ducked out. Coming down the hall was that dreadful girl—Pilbeam. Have you ever been accepted by the girl you love, Reggie?”

“Never. I’ve been rejected dozens of times.”

“So you won’t understand. I was ecstatic. I didn’t care who I kissed. Could’ve been the ship’s cat. It wasn’t. It was Pilbeam.”

“You kissed her?”

“I kissed her. Then the saloon door opened—and out came Stella.”

“Great Scott!”

“Exactly what I said. I realized she’d think I was a cad. She dumped me. I grabbed a dinghy and rowed off. I was mad. I didn’t care. I wanted to forget. I went ashore. I—well, I might have drowned my sorrows. I don’t remember much except fighting someone in a dark street, someone falling, me falling, and then legging it. I woke up in the Casino gardens. I lost my hat.”

I grabbed the paper.

“Read this,” I said.

He read it.

“Good heavens!”

“You didn’t attack the Prince, did you?”

“Reggie, this is terrible.”

“Cheer up. They say he’ll recover.”

“That doesn’t matter.”

“It does to him.”

He read again.

“They’ve got a clue.”

“They always say that.”

“But—my hat! I must’ve dropped it during the fight. That man—Sturgis—must’ve found it. It had my name in it!”

“George,” I said, “you need to move fast. Oh!”

He jumped.

“Don’t bark like that! What’s wrong?”

“The man!”

“What man?”

“A tall, thin man with a gimlet eye. He arrived before you. He’s having breakfast now. He wouldn’t give his name. I didn’t like him. It’s Sturgis. It has to be.”

“No!”

“I feel it. I’m sure.”

“Did he have a hat?”

“Of course.”

“Fool! I mean *my* hat. Was he carrying one?”

“By Jove—he was carrying a parcel. George, you’ve got to run. Slugging a Serene Highness is *lèse-majesté*. Worse than hitting a cop. You’ve got seconds.”

“But I’ve got no money. Lend me ten pounds, Reggie. I’ll wire my uncle—”

“Look out!” I shouted. “Someone’s coming!”

He vanished just as Voules appeared, tray in hand, holding a letter.

“What’s the matter?” I snapped.

“I beg your pardon, sir. I thought I heard Mr. Lattaker. A letter for him.”

“He’s not here.”

“No, sir. Shall I remove it?”

“No. Give it to me. I’ll pass it on.”

“Very good, sir.”

“Oh, Voules—are they still at breakfast? The gentleman who came to see Mr. Lattaker?”

“He is currently enjoying a kippered herring, sir.”

“Ah. That’ll keep him busy. Full of bones.”

I called George. He emerged.

“Who was it?”

“Only Voules. He brought a letter. Everyone’s still eating. The detective’s on kippers.”

“That’ll hold him. Full of bones.” He read the letter. Then grunted.

“Well, I’m hanged,” he said.

“What?”

He handed it to me. Here’s what it said:

> *My dear George—I shall see you tomorrow, I hope; but before we meet, I must prepare you for a curious situation regarding the legacy your father inherited from Aunt Emily. You may have heard of your twin brother Alfred, who disappeared or was kidnapped—no one knows which—when you were babies. For years, we assumed he was dead. Yesterday, however, I received a letter from him claiming he’s been living in Buenos Aires as the adopted son of a wealthy South American and only recently discovered his identity. He says he’s en route to meet me. Of course, he may be an impostor, but until we verify his credentials, I fear the transfer of funds will be delayed. I’ll explain everything when we meet.—Your affectionate uncle,*
> *Augustus Arbutt.*

I read it twice. Then, like lightning, it struck me—a brainwave so brilliant it almost made me dizzy.

“Why, old top,” I said, “this saves you.”

“Saves me from half the money? If he’s real, we split it. I thought it was just me. Now I’ve got competition.”

“Nonsense. This is your ticket out. You don’t have to flee. All you need to do is pretend you’re Alfred. Your uncle will vouch for you. And once we get rid of Sturgis, we sail home tomorrow.”

He stared.

“You ought to be locked up, Reggie.”

“Ass! Don’t you see? Twins look alike. Who’s to prove you’re not Alfred? Your uncle will back you. And Alfred won’t be around to contradict you. Only for a few hours. Then we’re gone.”

It finally clicked. His face lit up.

“You’re right,” he said. “And as Alfred, I can talk to Stella and fix things for George.”

“Genius,” I said modestly.

“You *are*.”

“Only sometimes. I can’t keep it up.”

Then—coughing behind us.

We spun around.

“What the devil are you doing here, Voules?” I said.

“I beg your pardon, sir. I’ve heard all.”

George and I exchanged glances.

“Voules is trustworthy,” I said. “Decent Voules! You wouldn’t betray us, would you?”

“Yes, sir.”

“You would?”

“Yes, sir.”

“But, Voules, what would you gain?”

“Financially, nothing.”

“Whereas, if you stay quiet”—I tapped his chest—“you might earn a considerable sum.”

“Am I to understand, sir, that because you are rich and I am poor, you think you can buy my self-respect?”

“Oh, come on.”

“How much?” Voules asked.

We negotiated. You’d think a loyal servant would accept five pounds. Not Voules. It took a hundred upfront and another hundred upon safe departure. But we settled. George went below to change.

Just as the breakfast party returned, I said:

“Did you meet him?”

“Meet whom?” Marshall asked.

“George’s twin, Alfred.”

“I didn’t know George had a brother.”

“Nor did he—until yesterday. Long story. He was kidnapped as a baby. Everyone thought he was dead. George got a letter from his uncle. I suspect that’s where George went—to meet him. Meanwhile, Alfred has arrived. He’s in George’s cabin, freshening up. You’ll be amazed—the likeness is uncanny. Look—here he comes.”

George emerged—clean, neat, in yachting gear.

They were stunned. I introduced him.

“Mr. Pepper tells me my brother isn’t on board,” said George.

“It’s astonishing,” said Marshall.

“Is my brother like me?” George asked cheerfully.

“No one could tell you apart,” I said.

“Twins are always alike,” said George. “But if it came to identification, there’s one way. Do you know George well, Mr. Pepper?”

“He’s a dear old pal.”

“You’ve swum with him?”

“Every day last August.”

“Well, then, you’d have noticed if he had a mole like this on the back of his neck, wouldn’t you?” He turned and showed the mole—hidden by his collar. I’d seen it often while swimming.

“Has George a mole like that?”

“No,” I said. “Oh, no.”

“You’d have noticed if he had?”

“Yes. Definitely.”

“I’m glad,” said George. “It would be awkward not being able to prove who I am.”

That settled it. They couldn’t argue. George seemed confident. He even went below for lunch without a hitch.

Everything was fine—until lunchtime. George spent most of it chatting with Stella. When the gong rang, he pulled me aside.

“It’s all right,” he said. “I told you.”

“What did I tell you?”

“About Stella. I told her she looked worried. She confessed. I explained that George was a decent chap who’d just lost his mind temporarily. She agreed.”

“You must be quick to win her confidence after knowing her for two hours.”

“Maybe,” said George. “I didn’t realize how persuasive my brother was.”

We headed below, but then a boat hailed us.

“It’s my uncle,” said George.

A stout man climbed aboard.

“Could he say that clearer, Bertie?” he asked. “It sounded promising at first, but then it kind of fizzled. What’s the plan?”

“My dear old man, it’s simple. We can rely on Jeeves. All you need is someone to go around New York, take notes, and send them to you. Then you write the letters based on those notes. That’s it, isn’t it, Jeeves?”

“Precisely, sir.”

A spark of hope lit up Rocky’s eyes. He stared at Jeeves, stunned by the man’s genius.

“But who would do it?” he asked. “It’d have to be a sharp fellow—someone who notices things.”

“Jeeves!” I said. “Let Jeeves do it.”

“But would he?”

“You would do it, wouldn’t you, Jeeves?”

For the first time in our long history, I saw Jeeves almost smile. His lips curved a quarter-inch. His eye lost its fish-like meditativeness.

“I should be delighted to oblige, sir. In fact, I have already visited several of New York’s attractions during my evening walks. It would be most enjoyable to continue.”

“Perfect!” I said. “I know exactly what your aunt wants to hear. Cabaret life. First stop: Reigelheimer’s, Forty-second Street. Anyone can show you the way.”

Jeeves shook his head.

“Pardon me, sir. People are no longer going to Reigelheimer’s. The place now is Frolics on the Roof.”

“You see?” I said to Rocky. “Leave it to Jeeves. He knows.”

We were all blissfully happy. Jeeves loved exercising his mind and enjoying the bright lights. I saw him one night at the Midnight Revels, sitting at a table near the dance floor, smoking a fat cigar and sipping a bottle of the finest. He looked almost human—his face wore an expression of serene benevolence, and he was making notes in a small book.

I was happy too—because I liked Rocky and enjoyed helping him. Rocky was content—still able to watch worms in his pyjamas. And the aunt? She seemed delighted. She was getting Broadway second-hand, but it was hitting her right.

Rocky’s letters, based on Jeeves’s reports, were electrifying. It was odd, really. I loved New York, but just mentioning it made Rocky shudder. Yet here’s a letter I wrote to a friend in London:

> DEAR FREDDIE,—Well, here I am in New York. It’s not bad. I’m not having a bad time. Everything’s pretty all right. The cabarets aren’t bad. Don’t know when I’ll be back. How’s everybody? Cheer-o!—Yours,
> P.S.—Seen old Ted lately?

Not that I cared about Ted. But if I hadn’t mentioned him, I couldn’t have gotten the letter onto the second page.

Now here’s Rocky’s letter on the same subject:

> DEAREST AUNT ISABEL,—How can I ever thank you enough for giving me the chance to live in this astonishing city! New York grows more wonderful every day. Fifth Avenue is at its best. The dresses are magnificent!

Then pages and pages of fashion commentary—something I never knew Jeeves was capable of.

“I was out with some of the crowd at the Midnight Revels the other night. We had dinner at a new place on Forty-third Street, then took in a show. Georgie Cohan dropped in at midnight and gave a great speech about Willie Collier. Fred Stone stayed only a minute, but Doug. Fairbanks did amazing stunts and made us roar. Diamond Jim Brady was there, as usual. Laurette Taylor showed up with a party. The show at the Revels is excellent. I’m enclosing a programme. Last night we went to Frolics on the Roof——

And so on. Clearly, the artistic temperament helps. It’s easier for someone used to writing poetry to spice up a letter than for someone like me. Still, Rocky’s correspondence was hot stuff. I called Jeeves in.

“Jeeves, you’re a wonder!”

“Thank you, sir.”

“How you notice everything beats me. I couldn’t tell you a thing about those places except that I had a good time.”

“It’s just a knack, sir.”

“Well, Mr. Todd’s letters ought to cheer Miss Rockmetteller up, don’t you think?”

“Undoubtedly, sir.”

And they did! They certainly did! About a month later, I was lounging in the apartment, smoking, when the door opened and Jeeves’s voice cut through the silence like a bombshell.

Not loud—Jeeves has a soft, soothing voice that drifts through the air like a distant sheep’s bleat. But what he said made me jump like a startled gazelle.

“Miss Rockmetteller!”

And in walked a large, solid woman.

I was stunned. I’d come to see Rocky’s aunt as a permanent fixture in Illinois. Seeing her in New York was like finding a glacier in a swimming pool.

She looked less like an invalid than anyone I’d ever met—except perhaps my Aunt Agatha. She had a lot of Aunt Agatha about her, actually. She looked like someone who could snap a man’s neck if provoked. And I suddenly realized she’d consider herself provoked if she ever found out about the ruse.

“Good afternoon,” I managed.

“How do you do?” she said. “Mr. Cohan?”

“Er—no.”

“Mr. Fred Stone?”

“Not absolutely. As a matter of fact, my name’s Wooster—Bertie Wooster.”

She seemed disappointed. The name “Wooster” meant nothing to her.

“Isn’t Rockmetteller home?” she asked. “Where is he?”

She had me with the first question. I couldn’t say he was in the country watching worms.

There was a faint cough—the respectful signal that Jeeves was about to speak.

“If you remember, sir, Mr. Todd went out in the automobile with a group this afternoon.”

“So he did, Jeeves,” I said, checking my watch. “Did he say when he’d return?”

“He indicated, sir, that he would be somewhat late.”

Jeeves vanished. The aunt sat down in the chair I’d forgotten to offer. She looked at me with a strange, unpleasant expression. It made me feel like a stray dog she planned to bury later. My Aunt Agatha had given me that look many times. It always made my spine curl.

“You seem very comfortable here, young man. Are you a close friend of Rockmetteller’s?”

“Oh, yes, rather!”

She frowned as if she’d expected better of Rocky.

“Well, you must be,” she said, “the way you treat his flat as your own!”

I was speechless. I’d seen myself as the charming host. Now I was being treated like a trespasser. Not just a visitor—more like a plumber who’d wandered in by mistake. It hurt.

At that moment, an idea struck me: tea. The eternal lifeline.

“Would you care for a cup of tea?” I asked.

“Tea?”

She spoke as if she’d never heard of the drink.

“Nothing like a cup after a journey,” I said. “Bucks you up! Gives you a bit of zip. Restores you, you know. I’ll go tell Jeeves.”

I stumbled down the hall to Jeeves’s domain. He was reading the paper as if nothing mattered.

“Jeeves,” I said, “we need tea.”

“Very good, sir.”

“I say, Jeeves, this is awkward, what?”

I needed sympathy. The nerves were shot.

“She thinks this place belongs to Mr. Todd. Where did that idea come from?”

Jeeves filled the kettle with dignified calm.

“No doubt because of Mr. Todd’s letters, sir,” he said. “It was my suggestion, sir, that they be addressed from this apartment to give the impression he lives here.”

I remembered. We’d thought it clever at the time.

“Well, it’s bally awkward, you know. She sees me as an intruder. By Jove! She probably thinks I’m a free-loader, borrowing his shirts and eating his food.”

“Yes, sir.”

“It’s rotten, you know.”

“Most disturbing, sir.”

“And there’s another thing: What about Mr. Todd? We’ve got to get him here immediately. When you bring the tea, go out and send him a telegram—next train.”

“I’ve already done so, sir. I wrote the message and dispatched it via the lift attendant.”

“By Jove, you think of everything, Jeeves!”

“Thank you, sir. Buttered toast with the tea? Just so, sir. Thank you.”

I returned to the living room. She hadn’t moved. Still rigid, gripping her umbrella like a weapon. She gave me another of those looks. For some reason, she clearly disliked me. Probably because I wasn’t George M. Cohan. It was hard on a chap.

“This is a surprise, what?” I said after a few minutes of silence, trying to revive the conversation.

“What is a surprise?”

“Your coming here, don’t you know.”

She raised her eyebrows and studied me through her glasses.

“Why is it surprising that I should visit my only nephew?”

That made sense.

“Oh, rather,” I said. “Of course. What I mean is——”

Jeeves entered with the tea. I was glad to see him. Business saved me.

“Tea, tea, tea—what? What?” I said.

Not what I’d intended. But it worked. I poured her a cup. She sipped it, then set it down with a shudder.

“Do you mean to say,” she said frostily, “that you expect me to drink this?”

“Rather! Bucks you up, you know.”

“What do you mean by ‘bucks you up’?”

“Well, makes you full of beans. Makes you fizz.”

“I don’t understand a word. You’re English, aren’t you?”

And so it went. The situation remained tense, but at least the tea was served.
