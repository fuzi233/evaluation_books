Margaret stood at the crossroads of fortune, a day that tasted both like honey and gall. For within its span, she had gained a soulmate in Denys, yet lost the hope of her husband's return. The truth settled heavy upon her: Gerard was utterly alone in that distant, strange land. Reason whispered that he should have arrived weeks ago; logic dictated he would be here by now. Yet, Denys remained her anchor. He was her "she-comrade," a constant source of cheer who banished despair with his wit and formulas. She doted on him, treating him with the affection usually reserved for kin, while simultaneously lecturing him with a feigned sternness. She would not allow him to take a single meal outside her roof, nor suffer any foul language from his lips.

"It ruins you, Denys," she chided, watching his transformation from rough sailor to polite gentleman. "Good heavens, to hear such ugly words escape such a handsome head! Desist, or I shall be truly angry; behave yourself." Instantly, Denys fell into line, the comedy of his struggle between innate courtesy and acquired ruffianship playing out before her eyes.

But as the saying goes, when it rains, it pours. Soon, others sought her friendship. She had penned a humble missive to Margaret Van Eyck, acknowledging her own diminished status in the household and promising to keep her distance, yet pleading that she could not erase the memory of past kindness. In the letter, she recounted her desperate battles for survival and implored secrecy regarding her whereabouts—especially from Gerard's former associates. "I loathe them," she wrote, her bitterness seeping through the ink. "They drove him from me. Even after his departure, their hearts did not turn toward me as they might have, had they repented their cruelty."

Van Eyck was perplexed by this sudden shift. Eventually, she confided in Reicht, who carried the secret like water through a pipe straight to Catherine.

"Indeed, has she turned so bitter against us?" the good woman asked, her voice tinged with sorrow. "She stole our son from us, and now she hates us for failing to rush into her embrace. Still, it is a blessing she lives and remains no further than Rotterdam."

Meanwhile, across the northern duchy, a spectacle unfolded. The English Princess, now Countess Charolois, made a grand procession accompanied by her stepdaughter, Marie de Bourgogne, the young heiress. The old Duke, Europe's most magnificent prince, displayed his full splendor. Armies of dazzling knights and crowds of gorgeously dressed ladies trailed behind them, joined by minstrels, jesters, bards, musicians, actors, and tumblers. Fencing matches, dances, and merriment filled every town they visited. Richart summoned all his people to Rotterdam to witness the pageant.

Days passed in Rotterdam before fate intervened again. Denys encountered Catherine by chance in the street. After a warm exchange of greetings, he urged her to rejoice, boasting that he had finally located her "she-comrade." But Catherine quickly cooled his enthusiasm, pointing out that he would have found her sooner had he stayed quietly at Tergou rather than wandering aimlessly through Holland.

"And now that we are found, does it make things better?" she asked cynically. "Her heart is set firmly against us."

"Oh, let that flea bite," Denys dismissed lightly. "Come with me to her house."

"No," Catherine refused flatly. "Those who arrive unbidden remain seated nowhere." She suggested instead that Denys act as a mediator to smooth things over. He accepted the task with great pomp and confidence, trotting off to Margaret.

"She-comrade," he announced, "I met today a friend of yours."

"You looked into Rotterdam, then, and saw yourself?" Margaret retorted sharply.

"Nay, 'twas a woman seeking your regard. 'Twas Catherine, Gerard's mother."

"Oh, was it?" Margaret replied coldly. "Then you may tell her she has come too late. There was a time I yearned for her, yes; but she kept aloof during my hour of greatest need. So now, let us remain exactly as we were."

Denys attempted to soften her resolve, coaxing and pleading, but she remained bitter and sullen, unmoved. Finally, he scolded her, and the pressure broke her; she erupted into hysterics. Terrified by the result of his own eloquence and caught off guard, Denys stumbled into a solemn promise never to raise the subject again.

He returned to Catherine crestfallen and delivered the news. Catherine flared with anger, recounting the reception of his overtures to the family. The feud ignited, burning hotter with each passing day. Only little Kate dared offer excuses for Margaret.

The following day, another visitor arrived. The household appeared enslaved and degraded: Martin was buried to his elbows in soap suds, Denys ironing with clumsy ineptitude, and Margaret plaiting ruffs, though her eyes held the sharp gaze of a mistress surveying her raw recruits. Into this scene stepped an old man. Venerable at first glance, he revealed himself upon closer inspection to be keen-eyed and wizened.

"Ah!" cried Margaret.

Swiftly turning her back, she hid her face with invincible revulsion. "Oh, that man! That man!"

"Nay, fear me not," said Ghysbrecht gently. "I come on a friend's errand. I bring you a letter from foreign parts."

"Do not mock me, old man," she snapped, turning slowly around.

"Nay, see;" he held out an enormous envelope.

Margaret lunged forward, snatching the letter with trembling hands and glistening eyes. It bore Gerard's handwriting.

"Oh, thank you, sir, bless you for this! I forgive you all the ill you ever wrought me."

Clutching the letter to her bosom with one hand, she glided swiftly from the room, leaving Ghysbrecht behind. He departed without returning, casting a final scowl at Martha. Margaret was left alone with her letter for hours, the weight of the world suspended in those few folded pages.
