“Edith,” said Margaret again, softly, as if a quieter syllable might coax wakefulness without breaking it. “Edith!”

But Edith had slipped away from speech as easily as a ribbon slides from a careless knot. Curled upon the crimson damask sofa in the back drawing-room of Harley Street, she lay in white muslin and blue bows, the very picture of a delicate creature who belonged more to a painted tale than to a London house that smelt, faintly and continually, of lamps and polished wood and the last course of dinner. If Titania had ever been fashioned for a Season—white muslin instead of gossamer, blue ribbons instead of dew—she might have looked as Edith looked now, asleep with her lips slightly parted, her silken curls loosened by the warmth of the room.

Margaret watched her cousin’s face as if she had only lately learned how to see it. They had grown up side by side; Edith’s prettiness had been remarked on by everyone except Margaret, who had taken it for granted like daylight. But in these last days—these days measured out by fittings and visits and lists—beauty had acquired an edge, as things do when one is about to lose them. Edith was to be married; the word had been spoken so often that it had begun to sound less like a promise and more like a summons, calling them all to hurry.

Before Edith’s drowsiness had deepened, they had been whispering of wedding dresses and the arrangement of lace, of what Captain Lennox had told her about Corfu—its sea-wind and white glare, its quarters that were to be home—and, with equal seriousness, of the difficulty of keeping a piano in tune in such a place, as if the strings’ obedience were the chief safeguard of married happiness. They had planned Scotland after the wedding: what gowns would do, what cloaks, what colours Edith could bear. Then the talk had softened, thinned, and fallen away; and now Margaret, with no listener, found herself turning inward to her own change.

She had been on the verge of speaking of Helstone—the country parsonage where her father and mother lived, where her happiest holidays had always been passed, though for ten years Harley Street had been called her home. In place of speech she had silence; in place of Edith’s answers she had the hum of another room.

From the front drawing-room drifted the sound of women’s voices: a cultivated ripple, now rising, now sinking, threaded through with the occasional sharp bark of laughter. Mrs. Shaw was entertaining the five or six ladies who had dined there, their husbands still lingering below among the deeper sounds of male conversation and wine. These were the familiar neighbours of the house; friends, in Mrs. Shaw’s sense—people with whom one exchanged dinners and favours, and for whom one did not scruple to call before luncheon if there was anything to ask or anything to offer.

They had been invited in honour of Edith’s approaching marriage, to eat a farewell dinner. Edith had objected, for Captain Lennox was expected by a late train; but objection was not Edith’s talent for long. She yielded, as she always did, the more readily because her mother had ordered “those extra delicacies of the season” which London women believed capable of soothing any grief, provided it was properly fed. Edith had sat at dinner leaning back, playing with her food as if it were a set of toys, her mind absent and her face grave, while those around her enjoyed Mr. Grey’s pointed little sayings. Mr. Grey, who always took the bottom of Mrs. Shaw’s table, had been particularly agreeable; the gentlemen had stayed downstairs longer than usual—a mercy, judging by what Margaret caught through the doorway now.

“I suffered too much myself,” Mrs. Shaw’s voice said, soft and plaintive as a flute heard through a wall. “Not that I was not extremely happy with the poor dear General; but still, disparity of age is a drawback—one that I was resolved Edith should not have to encounter. Of course, without any maternal partiality, I foresaw that the dear child was likely to marry early. Indeed I had often said I was sure she would be married before she was nineteen. I had quite a prophetic feeling when Captain Lennox—” Her voice dropped to a whisper; Margaret could fill the rest as well as any of them.

The course of true love, in Edith’s case, had run smooth enough to look almost planned. Mrs. Shaw had “given way to the presentiment,” and had urged on the marriage though it was below the expectations others had formed for a young, pretty heiress. She declared her only child should marry for love, and sighed as if love had not figured in her own marriage to General Shaw. Mrs. Shaw enjoyed the romance far more than Edith did.

Not that Edith was not properly in love; she was. Still, she would have preferred a good house in Belgravia to all the picturesque makeshift Captain Lennox described in his soldier’s future. The very bits that made Margaret’s heart warm—camp talk, sudden moves, foreign rooms with bare floors and thin curtains—made Edith pretend to shiver, partly for the pleasure of being coaxed out of her dislikes, partly because she truly hated discomfort. And yet, had some splendid man come with title and estate and wide staircases and servants in livery, Edith would still have held to her Captain while the temptation lasted. Afterwards—when the glitter had passed—it was possible she might have felt a little prick of regret that her love could not have been attached to a person who united everything that was desirable. In this she was her mother’s child. Mrs. Shaw, who had married the General with respect rather than affection, had spent years quietly lamenting, with the air of one enduring a martyrdom, that she was bound to a man she could not love.

“I have spared no expense in her trousseau,” came Mrs. Shaw again. “She has all the beautiful Indian shawls and scarfs the General gave to me, but which I shall never wear again.”

“She is a lucky girl,” replied Mrs. Gibson, whose voice Margaret knew—quick, practical, with an edge of self-satisfaction. One of Mrs. Gibson’s daughters had been married within the last few weeks; she listened with a double ear, measuring Edith’s good fortune against her own. “Helen had set her heart upon an Indian shawl, but really when I found what an extravagant price was asked, I was obliged to refuse her. She will be quite envious when she hears of Edith having Indian shawls. What kind are they? Delhi? with the lovely little borders?”

Margaret heard her aunt’s tone change, as if Mrs. Shaw had turned her head toward the dimmer back room. “Edith! Edith!” she called; and then her voice sank again with a little sigh of fatigue, as though even raising it were an exertion that cost her.

Margaret stepped forward at once. “Edith is asleep, Aunt Shaw. Is it anything I can do?”

Every woman in the other room chimed in with “poor child!” at the news, as if sleep were a calamity and not a refuge. In Mrs. Shaw’s lap a minute dog—Tiny—began to bark, stirred up by the burst of pity as by a gust of wind.

“Hush, Tiny! you naughty little girl! You will waken your mistress.” Mrs. Shaw’s hand smoothed the dog’s glossy head. “It was only to ask Edith if she would tell Newton to bring down her shawls. Perhaps you would go, Margaret dear?”

Margaret went.

Up, up, to the old nursery at the very top of the house, where the air always felt thinner and the sounds from below grew faint—London’s endless clatter reduced to a distant breathing. Newton was there among boxes and tissue-paper, busy with the lace required for the wedding. The room held the smell of stored things: starch and old linen, and the faint metallic tang of pins. Margaret paused at the threshold, looking in.

This had been her first familiar room in Harley Street nine years ago, when she had been brought, untamed and bewildered, from the forest-like freedom of Helstone to share her cousin’s home, play, and lessons. She remembered the London nursery’s dimness—coal-darkened light at the window, and the rule of an austere, ceremonious nurse with hands as clean as her conscience, who was terrible about torn frocks. Margaret remembered that first tea up here, separate from her father and aunt, who were dining somewhere down below at what seemed, to her childish mind, an immeasurable depth of stairs—as if, unless she were up in the sky, they must be far down in the bowels of the earth.

At home, in the country parsonage, her mother’s dressing-room had been her nursery. They kept early hours there; Margaret had always eaten with her father and mother. The contrast had wounded like a sudden cold. Even now, tall and stately at eighteen, she could recall the wild tears of nine-year-old grief, smothered under bedclothes that first night; could hear the nurse bidding her not to cry because it would disturb Miss Edith; could feel the child forcing her sobs into silence until her newly-seen grand aunt had come softly upstairs with Mr. Hale to show him his little sleeping daughter.

She had hushed herself then, lying as if asleep, for fear of making her father unhappy. She had not dared to show grief before her aunt; she had even half believed it wrong to feel sorrow after all the long hoping and planning and contriving at Helstone—after the careful arranging of her wardrobe to suit grander circumstances, after papa’s leaving his parish to come up to London even for a few days.

Now she had come to love this old nursery, dismantled as it was. The idea of leaving it for ever in three days touched her with a kind of cat-like regret, an instinctive clutching at what had sheltered her.

“Ah, Newton,” she said, “I think we shall all be sorry to leave this dear old room.”

“Indeed, miss, I shan’t for one,” Newton replied, tugging at a length of lace with practised fingers. “My eyes are not so good as they were, and the light here is so bad I can’t see to mend laces except just at the window, where there’s always a shocking draught—enough to give one one’s death of cold.”

“Well, I dare say you will have both good light and plenty of warmth at Naples,” Margaret said. “You must keep as much of your darning as you can till then. Thank you, Newton; I can take them down—you’re busy.”

Newton went, not without a muttered grumble, to undo the shawls which had already been produced and admired four or five times that day. When she returned, she handed Margaret a pile of soft, heavy folds.

Margaret carried them down carefully, the weight warm against her arms. As she descended, she drew in their scent: not London’s soot and beeswax polish, but something spiced and dry and far-travelled—like cedar chests and sun-baked markets, like pepper and sandalwood crushed together in memory. The smell rose each time the fabric shifted, as if the shawls themselves breathed out a foreign heat.

In the drawing-room her aunt asked her to stand as a lay figure on which to display them, since Edith still slept.

No one remarked on it, but Margaret’s tall, finely made figure in her plain black silk—mourning for some distant relative of her father’s—set off the gorgeous folds far better than Edith’s slight form would have done. The shawls would have half-smothered Edith; on Margaret they hung in long, splendid lines, colour deepening under the chandelier’s light—ruby and ochre and indigo, patterned with minute borders that seemed like writing in a language the eye could almost, but not quite, read.

Margaret stood silent and passive while her aunt adjusted the draperies. Occasionally, as she was turned, she caught her own reflection in the mirror over the chimney-piece: the familiar face framed by foreign splendour, a princess costume laid over a parson’s daughter. She smiled at the sight—quietly, as a child smiles when allowed to dress up—then touched the fabric with her fingertips, enjoying its softness, its coolness where it lay in shade, the slight roughness where the weave grew denser. She liked, without vanity, the sensation of beauty laid upon her.

At that moment the door opened, and Mr. Henry Lennox was announced.

Some of the ladies started back as if caught in a guilty pleasure, half ashamed of their absorbed interest in dress. Mrs. Shaw held out her hand to the new-comer. Margaret remained very still, thinking she might still be wanted as a block for the shawls, but she looked at Mr. Lennox with a bright, amused face, sure of his sympathy in the ridiculousness of being surprised in such an occupation.

Her aunt was absorbed at once in questions—about his brother the bridegroom, his sister Janet coming from Scotland to be bridesmaid, and various other members of the Lennox family. Margaret saw she was no longer required as shawl-bearer and began to attend, instead, to the other visitors her aunt had forgotten.

Almost immediately Edith came in from the back room, winking and blinking at the stronger light. She shook back her slightly ruffled curls and looked, all at once, like the Sleeping Beauty startled from dreams into ceremony. Even asleep, it seemed, she had instinctively understood that a Lennox was worth waking for.

She began at once with a multitude of questions about dear Janet—the future unseen sister-in-law for whom Edith professed such eager affection that, had Margaret been less proud, she might have felt a flicker of jealousy at the speed with which a stranger could be welcomed. As Margaret sank a little into the background, she saw Henry Lennox’s eyes go toward the vacant chair near her; and she knew, as certainly as if he had spoken, that he meant to sit there as soon as Edith loosed him from questioning.

She had not been sure he would come that night. It was a pleasant surprise; and with it came the sense of a pleasant evening. He liked and disliked nearly what she did. Her face brightened into honest openness.

By-and-by he came to her.

She received him with a smile that held no shyness, no self-consciousness. It was the smile of someone who was glad of an equal mind near at hand.

“Well,” he said, settling himself, “I suppose you are all in the depth of business—ladies’ business, I mean. Very different to my business, which is the real true law business. Playing with shawls is very different work to drawing up settlements.”

“Ah, I knew how you would be amused to find us so occupied in admiring finery,” Margaret answered. “But really Indian shawls are very perfect things of their kind.”

“I have no doubt they are,” he said. “Their prices are very perfect too. Nothing wanting.”

The gentlemen began to drop in one by one; the room’s sound thickened, voices overlapping like the layers of a city street. Even here, behind curtains and polish, the world had its own noise—London’s perpetual buzz finding its way into every pause.

“This is your last dinner-party, is it not?” Mr. Lennox asked. “There are no more before Thursday?”

“No. I think after this evening we shall feel at rest—at least, that kind of rest when the hands have nothing more to do, and all the arrangements are complete for an event which must occupy one’s head and heart. I shall be glad to have time to think, and I am sure Edith will.”

“I am not so sure about her,” he said, “but I can fancy that you will. Whenever I have seen you lately, you have been carried away by a whirlwind of some other person’s making.”

“Yes,” Margaret replied, and sadness shaded her voice. The month’s commotion about trifles—visits, lists, tries-on, consultations over effect—pressed upon her suddenly with a tired weight. “I wonder if a marriage must always be preceded by what you call a whirlwind, or whether in some cases there might not rather be a calm and peaceful time just before it.”

“Cinderella’s godmother ordering the trousseau, the wedding-breakfast, writing the notes of invitation, for instance,” he said, laughing.

“But are all these quite necessary troubles?” Margaret asked, looking straight up at him. She wanted, truly, some quiet idea of marriage—something that belonged to real life and not to show.

“Oh, of course,” he replied, and his tone sobered. “There are forms and ceremonies to be gone through, not so much to satisfy oneself, as to stop the world’s mouth—without which stoppage there would be very little satisfaction in life. But how would you have a wedding arranged?”

“Oh, I have never thought much about it,” she said, “only I should like it to be a very fine summer morning; and I should like to walk to church through the shade of trees; and not to have so many bridesmaids, and to have no wedding-breakfast. I dare say I am resolving against the very things that have given me the most trouble just now.”

“No, I don’t think you are,” he said. “The idea of stately simplicity accords well with your character.”

Margaret did not like that; it pricked her, not because it was unkind, but because it slid too easily into that familiar way of his—trying to draw her into talk about herself, with himself as the interpreter. She cut it short.

“It is natural for me to think of Helstone church, and the walk to it, rather than of driving up to a London church in the middle of a paved street.”

“Tell me about Helstone,” he said. “You have never described it to me. I should like to have some idea of the place you will be living in, when ninety-six Harley Street will be looking dingy and dirty, and dull, and shut up. Is Helstone a village, or a town, in the first place?”

“Oh, only a hamlet; I don’t think I could call it a village at all. There is the church and a few houses near it on the green—cottages, rather—with roses growing all over them.”

“And flowering all the year round, especially at Christmas—make your picture complete,” he said.

“No,” Margaret replied, annoyance sharpening her words. “I am not making a picture. I am trying to describe Helstone as it really is. You should not have said that.”

“I am penitent,” he answered. “Only it really sounded like a village in a tale rather than in real life.”

“And so it is,” Margaret said eagerly, her eyes lighting. “All the other places in England that I have seen seem so hard and prosaic-looking after the New Forest. Helstone is like a village in a poem—in one of Tennyson’s poems. But I won’t try and describe it any more. You would only laugh at me if I told you what I think of it—what it really is.”

“Indeed, I would not. But I see you are going to be very resolved.” He paused, then added, “Well, then, tell me that which I should like still better to know: what the parsonage is like.”

“Oh, I can’t describe my home,” she said. “It is home, and I can’t put its charm into words.”

“I submit. You are rather severe to-night, Margaret.”

“How?” She turned those large soft eyes full upon him, surprised into openness. “I did not know I was.”

“Why, because I made an unlucky remark, you will neither tell me what Helstone is like, nor will you say anything about your home, though I have told you how much I want to hear about both—the latter especially.”

“But indeed I cannot tell you about my own home,” she said. “I don’t quite think it is a thing to be talked about, unless you knew it.”

“Well, then,” he said, after a moment, “tell me what you do there. Here you read, or have lessons, or otherwise improve your mind, till the middle of the day; take a walk before lunch, go a drive with your aunt after, and have some kind of engagement in the evening. There, now fill up your day at Helstone. Shall you ride, drive, or walk?”

“Walk, decidedly. We have no horse—not even for papa. He walks to the very extremity of his parish. The walks are so beautiful, it would be a shame to drive—almost a shame to ride.”

“Shall you garden much? That, I believe, is a proper employment for young ladies in the country.”

“I don’t know. I am afraid I shan’t like such hard work.”

“Archery parties—picnics—race balls—hunt balls?”

“Oh no!” She laughed. “Papa’s living is very small; and even if we were near such things, I doubt if I should go to them.”

“I see,” he said. “You won’t tell me anything. You will only tell me that you are not going to do this and that. Before the vacation ends, I think I shall pay you a call, and see what you really do employ yourself in.”

“I hope you will,” Margaret answered. “Then you will see for yourself how beautiful Helstone is. Now I must go. Edith is sitting down to play, and I just know enough of music to turn over the leaves for her; and, besides, Aunt Shaw won’t like us to talk.”

Edith did sit down to play, and played brilliantly—her fingers swift and sure, her face turned a little upward as if to catch applause in advance. Margaret stood by to turn the leaves, listening not so much to the melody as to the way the room changed under it: the talk subdued, the feet stilled, the very air seeming to arrange itself around sound. For a moment the house held a kind of order that London noise seldom allowed—music making a clearing, as a wind makes a clearing in smoke.

In the middle of the piece the door half-opened. Edith’s eyes flew there; she saw Captain Lennox hesitating on the threshold. The music stopped at once. She flung down the sheets and rushed out, leaving Margaret with her hands still lifted, caught mid-duty, blushing and confused. The guests stared; Margaret had to explain, stumbling a little, what vision had appeared to cause Edith’s sudden flight.

Captain Lennox had come earlier than expected—or was it really so late? Watches were drawn out; exclamations followed; people were properly shocked at the hour and took their leave with hurried politeness.

Then Edith came back, glowing, half shy and half proud, leading in her tall handsome Captain as if she were bringing a prize into a room that had been waiting for it. His brother shook hands with him. Mrs. Shaw welcomed him in her gentle, kindly way that always carried something plaintive in it—an echo of long habit, of years spent considering herself a victim to an uncongenial marriage. Now the General was dead, she had ease and money and freedom with very few drawbacks; yet she had been perplexed to find herself anxious all the same. Of late she had settled on her own health as a suitable source of apprehension, and produced a nervous little cough whenever she thought of it. A complaisant doctor had ordered precisely what she desired: a winter in Italy.

Mrs. Shaw had wishes as strong as most people’s, but she disliked doing anything from the open motive of her own pleasure. She preferred to be compelled into gratification by another person’s command or necessity; it enabled her to moan in her soft manner while taking what she wanted. In this way she began to speak of her journey to Captain Lennox, who assented, as duty required, though his eyes were always seeking Edith.

Edith, meanwhile, was at the tea-table, rearranging it with bright importance, ordering up all sorts of good things in spite of her lover’s assurances that he had dined within the last two hours. She was determined to show how well she could behave as a soldier’s wife—competent, bustling, cheerful in a way that looked, to her, like courage.

Mr. Henry Lennox stood leaning against the chimney-piece, amused by the family scene. Close beside his handsome brother, he seemed the plain one in a singularly good-looking family; but his face was intelligent, keen, and mobile. Margaret wondered, now and then, what thoughts moved behind his silence. He observed everything with an interest that had a faintly sarcastic edge—called out, perhaps, by Mrs. Shaw’s plaintive self-portrayal—separate from the warmer interest awakened by what he saw.

He thought it pretty, the sight of the two cousins busying themselves about cups and plates, sugar and cream, napkins folded just so. Domestic ritual had its own gravity. It was all very well to speak of Corfu and Scotland and Naples; it was here, in the small acts of serving and arranging, that women were trained into the shape of their futures.

Edith chose to do most herself. She discovered the water in the urn was cold and ordered up the great kitchen tea-kettle. The consequence was that, meeting it at the door and insisting on carrying it, she found it too heavy. She came in pouting, with a black mark on her muslin gown and a little round white hand indented by the handle. She held it up to Captain Lennox like a hurt child; and the remedy was as inevitable as in childhood—his quick taking of her hand, his murmured concern, her pleasure in being fussed over.

Margaret, more practical, set up a spirit-lamp with swift quiet skill. The flame steadied under the kettle, heat gathering without commotion—an effective contrivance, though not at all like the gypsy encampment Edith sometimes declared to be the nearest resemblance to barrack life. Still, the water warmed; tea was made; the room filled with the scent of it—dark, comforting, familiar—rising into the overlay of Eastern spice from the shawls and the faint lemony bite of wax and polish.

After that evening, all was bustle until the wedding was over.
