A shadow descended upon a noble mind, extinguishing the polar star that had once been his pure, unrivalled love for Margaret. With that light quenched, he drifted aimlessly across a gloomy sea of hopeless expectation. Yet, his torment was not merely the paralysis of despair; it was compounded by a searing exasperation at the sheer waste of his own self-denial, fortitude, peril, and virtue. Every act of restraint burned like a brand, stinging him with the bitter realization that had he chosen the lazy, selfish comfort of home, he might have saved Margaret's life. These twin poisons, raging in the fevered blood of his youth, drove him mad and dismantled his morality. He rushed furiously into pleasure, diving headlong into vice as a drowning man lunges blindly into the enemy's gunfire.

In those days, pleasure was synonymous with destruction. Wine, women, gambling—whatever could procure an hour of frenzied excitement or a fleeting moment of oblivion—he consumed with reckless abandon. The substantial savings he had amassed for Margaret now funded his debauchery, and he quickly ascended to leadership among a circle of loose companions he had previously kept at arm's length. His heart rotted alongside his morals. He sulked violently against his old landlady for offering gentle counsel, eventually moving to a distant quarter of the city to escape both remonstrance and painful memories. As time wore on, his hand grew unsteady; the pen, once his faithful confidant, betrayed him. His patience eroded as the habits of indulgence took root, leading him to abandon painting in ink for portraiture in color. Yet, even this craft was abandoned whenever his idle cronies summoned him. Thus, he dove deeper into foul waters, chasing the hollow shell of oblivion.

It is not my duty to chronicle in minute detail the coarse vices in which this unfortunate young man now wallowed. My sole purpose is to establish the undeniable truth: he had become a rake, a debauchee, a drunkard, and one of the most wicked youths in Rome. Those who conceal or soften the wickedness of the virtuous, robbing the despondent sinner of hope through their lack of candor, are no lovers of truth or mankind. Enough said: the man was not born to do things by halves, nor was his vice partial.

News of his decline rippled through the city. His humble female acquaintances gossiped freely; his old landlady informed Teresa that he was going to the devil, praying her to locate him. Teresa relayed the tragic tale to her husband, Lodovico, urging him to search for the young man's current whereabouts. "Do you remember his face, Lodovico mio?" she asked. "Teresa, a man in my line of work never forgets a face, least of all a benefactor's. But you know I rarely venture abroad by daylight." Teresa sighed. "And how long will this continue, Lodovico?" "Until some cavalier drives his sword through me," he replied grimly. "They will not allow a poor fellow like me to pursue any honest trade."

Pietro Vanucci, who bore prosperity far worse than adversity, was one such figure. Having been ignominiously ejected by his landlady for keeping late hours, he encountered Gerard in the street. Despite Gerard's apparent transformation, Vanucci greeted him warmly and shortly thereafter secured lodging in the same house. He arrived accompanied by a lad named Andrea, who ground his colors and served as both pupil and model—a youth of rare beauty, sharp as a needle. Vanucci claimed a warm friendship for Gerard, though Gerard, in whom all warmth of sentiment seemed extinguished, accepted this overture with icy reserve. What a strange alliance it proved to be: Vanucci, a libertine, half-misanthrope, and open infidel, paired with a man who had lost his soul. They wandered the streets as unlikely companions, united by nothing but circumstance.

One day, having undermined his health, stripped his beauty, and squandered most of his fortune, Vanucci organized a raucous expedition up the Tiber in a boat drawn by buffaloes. Lorenzo de' Medici had introduced these beasts to Florence three years prior, but they were novelties in Rome. Nothing satisfied Vanucci, the "beggar on horseback," except to be pulled upstream by the brutes. Each libertine was required to bring a lady, who must be handsome, lest he pay a fine. The bearer of the loveliest damsel would be crowned with laurel and hailed as a public benefactor—a twisted interpretation of *vir bonus est quis?* They procured a splendid galley and twelve buffaloes, and soon the libertines and their female accomplices gathered at the embarkation point. But Gerard was absent.

They waited, first with patience, then with impatience. Vanucci offered an excuse: "I heard him say he forgot to procure a fardingale. Comrades, the good lad is hunting for a beauty worthy to stand among these peerless dames. Consider the difficulty, ladies, and be patient!" Finally, Gerard appeared in the distance, holding a female by the hand. "She is long enough," critiqued one woman from afar. "Gemini! What strides she takes!" exclaimed another. "Oh, it is wise to hasten into good company," Vanucci muttered, feigning concern.

When the pair approached, however, satire choked in their throats. Gerard's companion was a peerless beauty who extinguished the boatload like the rising sun swallows the stars. She was tall yet not towering, straight as a dart yet supple as a young panther. Her face was a perfect oval, her forehead pale, her cheeks a rich olive flushed with eloquent blood, and her glorious eyes framed by long, thick silken lashes seemingly designed to sweep up sensitive hearts. Her lips were saucy red, her teeth the whitest ivory. The women were visibly depressed by this wretched sight; the men were in ecstasy. They welcomed her with loud shouts and waving caps, and one enthusiast even knelt upon the boat's gunwale, hailng her of divine origin. Only his mistress, pulling his hair, and the goddess delivering a swift kick, stopped him. He lay supine, and the peerless creature frisked over his body without deigning him a glance, taking her seat at the prow. Pietro Vanucci sat in a collapse, glaring at her, his mouth agape like a dying codfish.

The drover cracked the reins, the ropes tightened, and the buffaloes moved upstream. "What think ye of this new beef, mesdames?" Vanucci boomed. "We ne'er saw monsters so vilely ill-favored," retorted a lady, shuddering at their nasty horns and foul nostrils held high. "Holes be they, not nostrils." "Signorina, the beeves are a present from Florence the beautiful. Would you look a gift beef in the nose?" queried another. "They are so dull," objected a lively lady. "I went up the Tiber twice as fast last time with but five mules and an ass." "Nay, that is soon mended," cried a gallant. Jumping ashore, he drew his sword and, ignoring the drivers' protests, charged the dozen buffaloes, goading them forward.

The animals snorted and whipped their tails but refused to accelerate. The boatload erupted in laughter. Finally, he goaded a patriarchal bull who instantly turned on the sword. With a furious jerk of his prodigious neck, the bull sent the man flying over his head. The spark described a bold parabola and crashed sitting, unconsciously waving his glittering blade, into the yellow Tiber. The laughing ladies screamed and wrung their hands—all but Gerard's fair companion. She uttered something resembling an oath, seized the helm, and steered the boat away. The gallant surfaced sputtering, gripped the gunwale, and was dragged aboard, dripping.

He glared around him, confused. "I understand not that," he said, peevishly, puzzled and thus discontented. Finding he was not slain, but rather his doublet perforated, they began to laugh louder than ever. "What are ye cackling at?" the spark demanded. "I desire to know how 'tis that one moment a gentleman is out yonder pricking African beef, and the next moment—" Gerard's lady interjected, "Disporting in his native stream." "Tell him not, a soul of ye," cried Vanucci. "Let him find out his own riddle." "Confound ye all," the spark lamented. "I might puzzle my brains till doomsday and ne'er find it out. Also, where is my sword?" "Ask Tiber!" the lady replied. "Your best way, signor, will be to do it over again; and, in a word, keep pricking African beef until your mind receives light. So shall you comprehend the matter by degrees, as lawyers mount heaven and buffaloes ascend the Tiber."

A chevalier remarked that the last speaker transcended the sons of Adam in wit as much as she did the daughters of Eve in beauty. At this, and indeed at all their compliments, Pietro Vanucci's behavior became peculiar. He ceased his staring and gaping bewilderment, coiling up snake-like on the bench, his mouth muffled, two bright eyes fixed on the lady, twinkling and scintillating most comically. Yet, he failed to interest or amuse her in return. Her glorious eyes and lashes swept him calmly at times, scarcely distinguishing him from the benches and things.
