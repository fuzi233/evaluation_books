Let us kindly skip ahead a few months in the biography of Mr. Arthur Pendennis. During this interval, many thrilling adventures surely occurred that would have made his autobiography a bestseller, but for the weary reader of *his* memoirs, they would likely prove as dry as dust. Last time we met him, he had officially signed up as a professional "hack"—a term Mr. Warrington, his bosom buddy, uses with relish. Now, imagine the life of a hack, whether legal, literary, clerical, or mercantile. It is a monotonous treadmill of boredom. One day's labor mirrors the next with terrifying precision. A literary man often battles time, his own will, his failing health, his crippling laziness, and his deep-seated hatred for the subject matter he is forced to tackle, much like any other daily drudge. When you need to cash in on Pegasus (assuming he owns no other marketable assets), goodbye to soaring poetry and celestial flights. Pegasus now rises only on schedule, like Mr. Green's balloon, strictly adhering to advertised times and ticket prices. He trots in harness over cobblestones, pulling a cart or cab behind him. Often, Pegasus works with panting sides and trembling knees, and frequently receives a sharp cut from his driver.

Do not, however, pour too much pity on this unfortunate steed. There is no divine exemption for animals—or humans—from labor, sickness, or decay. If Pegasus gets whipped, he often deserves it. I, for one, am prepared to defend my friend George Warrington against the sentimental notion held by poetic enthusiasts: namely, that letters and "genius" are entitled to a tax-free, duty-free existence, exempt from the prosaic grind of bread-winning and paying taxes like everyone else.

So, the *Pall Mall Gazette* was duly launched, and Arthur Pendennis's merits were recognized as those of a flippant, witty, and amusing critic. He toiled away every week, churning out reviews of whatever fell into his department. He certainly wrote with flippancy, but with honesty and to the best of his ability. Perhaps a historian of seventy, who had spent twenty-five years composing a magnum opus, was not treated fairly by a young gentleman who disposed of the material in a couple of days' reading at the British Museum. Or perhaps a poet, sweating over sublime sonnets intended for immortality, was annoyed by two or three dozen snappy lines in Mr. Pen's review where the critic acted like a lord on the bench and the author a trembling suitor. The theatergoers complained bitterly, and he likely was too harsh on them. Yet, little harm was done. Times have changed since then; back then, there were so few truly great historians, poets, or actors that almost none appeared before his critical desk. Those who received a little whipping generally got what was good for them. Not that the judge was any wiser than those he sentenced, nor did he ever fancy himself superior. Pen possessed a robust sense of humor and justice, meaning he lacked an overbearing reverence for his own work. Furthermore, he had his friend Warrington at his elbow—a terrifying critic if Pen grew conceited, and far savagier toward Pen than toward anyone else he tried at his literary assizes.

Through these critical labors and occasional contributions to leading articles, when conscience allowed him to speak his mind without offending the paper, Mr. Pendennis earned four pounds, four shillings weekly, with considerable pain and effort. He also supplied magazines and reviews with his compositions and was rumored, though he preferred silence on the matter, to be the London correspondent of the *Chatteris Champion*, which at the time featured some brilliant metropolitan letters. These endeavors enabled the fortunate youth to earn nearly four hundred pounds a year. On the second Christmas after arriving in London, he actually brought his mother a hundred pounds as a dividend on the debt he owed Laura. Mrs. Pendennis read every word of her son's works, considering him the profoundest thinker and most elegant writer of the day. She viewed his repayment of the hundred pounds as an act of angelic virtue. She feared he was ruining his health and delighted when he described the society he met and the great men of letters and fashion he encountered. This is how all readers understand the worship of sons by mothers, and that charming, simple love with which country women track the careers of their darlings in London. Whether John holds a brief, Tom is invited to a ball, or George meets a famous man at dinner, what joy fills the hearts of mothers and sisters at home in Somersetshire! How eagerly young Hopeful's letters are read and remembered! What a topic for village chatter and friendly congratulations! In the second winter, Pen visited for a very brief spell, cheering the widow's heart and lighting up the lonely house at Fairoaks. Helen had her son entirely to herself; Laura was visiting old Lady Rockminster; the folks of Clavering Park were away; the few old friends, led by Dr. Portman, called on Mr. Pen and treated him with marked respect. Between mother and son, it was pure fondness, confidence, and affection. It was the happiest fortnight of the widow's life, perhaps both theirs. The holiday vanished far too quickly, and Pen returned to the busy world, leaving the gentle widow alone again. She sent Arthur's money to Laura. I do not know why this young lady chose to leave home precisely when Pen was coming, nor whether he felt more piqued or relieved by her absence.

By this time, through his own merits and his uncle's introductions, Pen was reasonably well integrated into London, known in both literary and polite circles. His fashionable reputation served him well; he was considered a gentleman of good present means and better prospects, who wrote for pleasure rather than necessity—a supreme recommendation for a young literary aspirant. Bacon, Bungay & Co. proudly accepted his articles. Mr. Wenham invited him to dinner. Mr. Wagg regarded him favorably. They recounted how they met him at fashionable houses, where he was quite welcome, as they cared little for his current financial standing or future; his appearance and address were impeccable, and he had cultivated a character as a clever fellow. Ultimately, he was invited to one house because he had been seen at another. Thus, a vast array of London life unfolded before the young man. He became familiar with all sorts of people, from Paternoster Row to Pimlico, feeling as much at home at Mayfair dining tables as at the tavern boards where some of his pen-friends assembled.

Brimming with high spirits and curiosity, easily adapting to everyone he met, the young fellow delighted in this strange variety and jumble of humanity, making himself welcome or at least at ease wherever he went. For instance, he might breakfast at Mr. Plover's in the morning alongside a Peer, a Bishop, a parliamentary orator, two blue ladies of fashion, a popular preacher, the author of the latest sensation novel, and the newest lion imported from Egypt or America. Then, he would depart this distinguished society for the back room of the newspaper office, where pens, ink, and wet proofs awaited him. Here sat Finucane, the sub-editor, with the latest news from the Row. Soon, Shandon would arrive, nodding to Pen, and begin scribbling his leading article at the other end of the table, flanked by a pint of sherry—which, when the attendant boy spotted him, was always silently produced for the Captain. Or Mr. Bludyer's roaring voice would echo from the front room, where that truculent critic would seize books from the counter despite the timid protests of Mr. Midge, the publisher. After examining the volumes, he would sell them at his stall, drink and dine on the proceeds in a tavern box, then demand ink and paper to proceed to "smash" the author of his dinner and the novel. Towards evening, Mr. Pen would stroll toward his club to pick up Warrington for a constitutional walk. This exercise cleared the lungs and stimulated an appetite for dinner, after which Pen enjoyed the privilege of bowing at some delightful houses open to him, or exploring the town for amusement. There was the Opera, the Eagle Tavern, a ball in Mayfair, a quiet night with a cigar, a book, and a long chat with Warrington, or a wonderful new song at the Back Kitchen. At this stage of his life, Mr. Pen beheld all sorts of places and men; very likely, he did not realize how much he enjoyed himself until long afterwards, when balls gave him no pleasure, farces failed to make him laugh, tavern jokes produced no excitement, and the loveliest dancer ever to show her ankles failed to stir him from his chair after dinner. At his present mature age, all these pleasures are past, and the times have passed away. It has been but a very few years since... but the time is gone, and most of the men. Bludyer will no longer bully authors or cheat landlords of their scores. Shandon, the learned and thriftless, the witty and unwise, sleeps his last sleep. They buried honest Doolan the other day; never again will he cringe or flatter, nor pull a long bow or empty a whisky-noggin.

The London season was now blooming in full vigor. Fashionable newspapers teemed with information about grand banquets, routs, and balls enlivening the polite world. Our gracious Sovereign was holding levees and drawing-rooms at St. James's. The bow-windows of the clubs were crowded with the heads of respectable, red-faced, newspaper-reading gentlemen.
