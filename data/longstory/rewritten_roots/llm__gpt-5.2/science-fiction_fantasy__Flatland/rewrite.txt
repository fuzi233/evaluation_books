If my poor friend of Flatland still kept the bright edge of mind he had when he first set these Memoirs down—back when his thoughts were quick as a knife and just as sharp—I should not be standing in his place now. He asked me, before his memory thinned and the old words began to crumble on his tongue, to do three homely duties for him, as one might keep a household running when the cook is ill: first, to thank the readers and critics in Spaceland, whose appetite for his little book has, with surprising speed, called for a second and revised serving; second, to beg pardon for certain slips of the press and the compositor’s blunders (not all of them of his making); and third, to clear away a few misunderstandings that have clung to the work like smoke to rafters.

He is not the Square he once was. Seven years in prison will do that to any creature—years in which each day arrives with the same clang of bolts, the same thin shuffle of warders’ feet, the same stale air, rubbed raw by echoes. Worse than iron has been the constant drone of disbelief beyond the walls: the laughter, the mockery, the easy dismissal, repeated until even the stoutest mind begins to forget the very shape of what it knew. Age, too, has taken its slow, patient turn at him, as it does at all living things, wearing away not only strength but vocabulary. The terminology he gathered in that brief, burning visit to Spaceland—those strange, untranslatable terms that once tasted sharp and new—has faded. He can summon them now only like half-remembered aromas from a childhood kitchen: real enough to ache for, impossible to name.

So he has asked me to answer, as best I can, two particular objections that have been pressed upon him: one of the intellect, and one of morals.

The first is the cleverest, and to many Spacelanders almost irresistible. A Flatlander, it is said, when he sees a Line must be seeing something not only LONG but also THICK. Otherwise it would not be visible; without thickness there could be nothing for the eye to catch. Therefore, the critic argues, the Flatland people ought to admit that their countrymen are not merely long and broad, but also—though perhaps ever so slightly—HIGH.

When I first heard this, I confess I stood as foolish as a man who has been asked, in the middle of a meal, to explain why hunger exists at all. The fact seemed plain; the conclusion, seductive. Yet my old friend’s reply, though it comes now through the tremor and thinness of his later mind, meets the objection cleanly.

“I grant your critic his facts,” he said to me when I brought it up, “but I deny his conclusion. Yes: there is, in Flatland, a Third unrecognized Dimension which might be called ‘height.’ And yes: there is, in Spaceland, a Fourth unrecognized Dimension, not yet christened in your language, which I will call ‘extra-height.’ But we can no more take cognizance of our ‘height’ than you can take cognizance of your ‘extra-height.’ Even I—who have been in Spaceland, and for twenty-four hours had the privilege of understanding what you mean by ‘height’—even I cannot now comprehend it. I cannot realize it by sight, nor recover it by reasoning. I can only apprehend it by faith.”

He spoke “faith” the way a famine survivor speaks “bread”: not as a pious ornament, but as the name for a thing that must be believed because it cannot be held.

“The reason,” he continued, “is plain when you attend to it. Dimension implies direction. It implies measurement. It implies the more and the less. But all our lines are equally and infinitesimally thick—high, if you like—and therefore there is nothing in them to lead our minds toward the conception of that Direction. Your critics speak of a ‘delicate micrometer’ as though the tool itself were a revelation. But a measure is useless unless you know what to measure and in what direction. In Flatland we would not know either. We see a Line as long and BRIGHT. Brightness is necessary to its existence, as length is. If brightness vanishes, the Line is extinguished.

“And so,” he said, and here his voice, as I remember it, took on that weary patience of one who has explained the same recipe to people who refuse to taste it, “so all my friends in Flatland, when I speak of an unrecognized Dimension that is somehow visible in a Line, say at once: ‘Ah—you mean BRIGHTNESS.’ And when I say: ‘No, I mean a real Dimension,’ they retort: ‘Then measure it, or tell us in what direction it extends.’ And that is the end of me; for I can do neither.

“Only yesterday,” he added—yesterday meaning, in his prison calendar, another rung in the same iron ladder—“when the Chief Circle (our High Priest) came on his seventh annual inspection and asked me for the seventh time, ‘Are you any better?’ I tried again to prove to him that he was high, as well as long and broad, though he did not know it. Do you know what he said? ‘You say I am “high.” Measure my “high-ness,” and I will believe you.’ What could I do? I was crushed, and he left the room triumphant.”

I cannot help pausing here to note a detail he himself cannot now express, but that any creature with ears would understand: how the Chief Circle’s triumph was not only in his words. It was in the sound of his departure—the confident slide of authority along the corridor, the crisp, satisfied cadence of footfalls, the door’s closing thud that declared, more loudly than any argument, that the world belonged to those who could define the terms. There is a kind of noise that is not merely loud but final, the way a factory whistle or a lock’s snap can flatten thought into submission. Flatland’s prison has that noise in it, year after year, until even an imagination trained on wonder begins to flinch at its own music.

My friend, anticipating the Spacelander’s incredulous shrug, pressed on:

“Does this still seem strange to you? Then put yourself where I am. Suppose a creature of the Fourth Dimension visited you and said: ‘Whenever you open your eyes, you see a Plane of Two Dimensions and infer a Solid of Three; but in reality you also see—without recognizing—a Fourth Dimension. It is not colour. It is not brightness. It is a true Dimension. Yet I cannot point out to you its direction, nor can you possibly measure it.’ What would you do? Would you not have him locked up? That is my fate. And it is as natural for Flatlanders to lock up a Square for preaching the Third Dimension as it is for Spacelanders to lock up a Cube for preaching the Fourth.

“Alas,” he said, and in that one word I heard a whole lineage of cells and censures, “how strong a family likeness runs through blind and persecuting humanity in all Dimensions. Points, Lines, Squares, Cubes, Extra-Cubes—we are all liable to the same errors, all alike the slaves of our own Dimensional prejudices; as one of your poets has said, ‘One touch of Nature makes all worlds akin.’”

He asked me to add—because critics, like diners, sometimes imagine a missing course is the chef’s negligence—that certain remarks have been inserted in his later dialogue with the Sphere bearing upon this very matter, remarks omitted before as tedious and unnecessary, but now supplied to meet the misunderstanding.

On the intellectual objection, then, I find his defence—if you will allow me the word—impregnable.

If only I could say as much of his answer to the second objection, which is moral in nature. Some have charged him with being a woman-hater; and since the charge has been urged with particular heat by those whom Nature’s decree has constituted the somewhat larger half of the Spaceland race, I should like to remove it, so far as I can honestly do so.

Yet the Square is unaccustomed to Spaceland’s moral terminology. To transcribe his defence word for word would be to wrong him—not by exposing him, but by forcing his meaning through alien language, as if one tried to translate the taste of a native bread into the terms of an imported confectioner’s sugar. Acting instead as interpreter and summarizer, I gather that his seven years of imprisonment have not left his opinions untouched. Time, even when caged, has a way of working its slow fermentation in the mind.

He has modified his views, personally, both with regard to Women and with regard to the Isosceles, or Lower Classes. Where once he spoke with the easy certainty of his upbringing, he now inclines, in some measure, toward the Sphere’s opinion that the Straight Lines are in many important respects superior to the Circles. But—and here lies the crux—he wrote as an Historian. In writing, he identified himself (perhaps too closely) with the opinions generally adopted by Flatland, and, as he has been informed, even by Spaceland historians; in whose pages, until very recent times, the destinies of Women and of the multitude have seldom been thought worthy of mention, and never of careful consideration. There are silences in official histories that are not mere omissions but instruments of power: whole vocabularies missing, whole lives unnamed, as if a people’s native dialect were rubbed out and replaced by the rulers’ convenient lexicon.

In another passage, obscure even to friendly readers, he now wishes to disavow the Circular—or aristocratic—tendencies with which some critics have naturally credited him. While doing justice to the intellectual force by which a few Circles have for many generations maintained supremacy over immense multitudes, he believes that the facts of Flatland, set down without comment, declare something harder than doctrine: that Revolutions cannot always be suppressed by slaughter; and that Nature, in sentencing the Circles to infecundity, has condemned them to ultimate failure.

“And herein,” he says, “I see the fulfilment of the great Law of all worlds: that while the wisdom of Man thinks it is working one thing, the wisdom of Nature constrains it to work another, and quite a different and far better thing.”

For the rest, he begs his readers not to suppose that every minute detail of Flatland daily life must correspond to some detail in Spaceland. A world is not a mere allegorical pantry where each jar must match another jar on another shelf. And yet he hopes that, taken as a whole, this work may prove suggestive as well as amusing to those Spacelanders of moderate and modest minds who, speaking of what lies beyond experience though it is of the highest importance, decline to say on the one hand, “This can never be,” and on the other, “It must needs be precisely thus, and we know all about it.”

Part I: THIS WORLD

“Be patient, for the world is broad and wide.”

I set these notes down as one sets out a table—first the board laid bare, then the vessels, then the slow and careful naming of each thing that will be eaten and remembered. Thus is the work portioned. The titles may seem at first like dry grains held in the palm, but wait: they will be milled, soaked, warmed, and made to give up their hidden savor. “Be patient,” says the old proverb, as any agrarian elder would murmur while listening for the right hush in the wind before planting, or for the right simmering whisper in a pot before salting. “For the world is broad and wide.” And broadness—whether of land or language or the dimly-imagined spaces beyond—cannot be swallowed whole. It must be cut into sections, tasted in order, and heard with care, lest the loudest voices drown the subtler notes and the memory of what we truly are be lost under the pounding, industrial din of careless speech.

I name our world Flatland—not because that is the word we use at our own hearths, but because your roomy life in Space needs a handle you can hold. Think of it as a vast, well-worn baking-stone: one endless plane where the folk are not flesh but Figures—Lines, Triangles, Squares, Pentagons, Hexagons, and the rest—sliding to and fro across the surface as freely as a cook’s palm over floured dough, yet never once able to rise into air or sink beneath. We are like shadows that have forgotten their softness: hard-bodied, keen-edged, and lit along our borders as if some invisible skillet kept the rim always faintly bright.

Not long ago I would have called it my whole universe and been content, the way an elder is content with the same seed-sack and the same stories. But a few years have seasoned my mind with other tastes; I have learned there are higher modes of being, as there are higher notes a landscape can carry when the wind moves cleanly through reeds and not through engines. Still—Flatland remains my home, my kitchen, my field.

In such a country, you will see at once that nothing can exist of what you call “solid.” There is no thickness to store warmth, no depth in which anything might be hidden like an onion under its skins. Yet you may imagine that at least by sight we should separate Triangle from Square, Square from Pentagon, the way a food historian tells barley from rye with a glance. But our eyes are not granted that luxury. In truth, nothing is visible to us—nothing can be—except Straight Lines. I do not ask you to take this on faith; I can show you by a homely experiment, the kind repeated from grandparent to grandchild at a plain table, until it becomes custom and then becomes law.

Set a penny on the middle of one of your tables in Space. Lean over it as you would over a simmering pot and look straight down. It appears a circle—full, round, complete as a loaf seen from above. Now draw back to the edge of the table and lower your eye little by little, as if you were stooping to catch the scent that rises before taste arrives. The penny grows more and more oval, stretching thin like dough coaxed into a long crust. Lower still—bring your eye exactly to the level of the tabletop, until you have made yourself, for that moment, a Flatlander—and the penny ceases to be oval at all. So far as you can see, it becomes a straight line: a mere edge, a single cut across your vision, no breadth, no roundness—only the boundary.

Do the same with a Triangle or a Square cut from pasteboard. From above it shows its face plainly enough; from the side it loses its history. The nearer your gaze comes to the plane, the more every figure is pared down to what meets the eye at that level. At the very level—at our level—each one of them is reduced to the same austere appearance: a straight line.

Take, for example, an equilateral Triangle—among us, a Tradesman of respectable class, as common and necessary in the household economy as the knife that does the daily slicing. Seen from above, he is a figure with three honest sides. Seen near the level, the corners begin to vanish into mere changes of length, subtle as a shift in aroma when a pot is moved from flame to embers. And seen precisely at the level—which is to say, seen as we must see him in Flatland—he is nothing but a straight line, luminous-edged and undecorated.

When I was in Spaceland, I heard your sailors describe something akin while they cross your seas. A distant island may have bays, forelands, and angles without number, inlets that would shelter nets and coves that would cradle boats; yet from far off, unless your sun strikes just so and carves the coast with light and shade, they see none of that. They see only a grey, unbroken line laid along the water—an edge with all the richness of land pressed flat into one narrow stroke.

So it is with us. When one of our triangular or other acquaintances approaches, we do not behold his form; we hear his coming in the faint change of our world’s hush and see only that a line grows longer. When he departs, that line shrinks again, like a scent thinning as a dish is carried away. But still it is a straight line—Triangle, Square, Pentagon, Hexagon, Circle, whatever he may be by his own nature and name, to our eyes he is line and nothing else.

You may well ask—under these scant conditions, with no sun to cast a shadow, no kindly play of light to give depth, no relief of bright and dark—how we know friend from stranger, elder from youth, ruler from laborer; how we keep the old distinctions from being ground into sameness, how we keep our very words from dying out under the pressure of a single dominant look. This question is natural, and its answer is bound up with our lives, our customs, our speech, and the subtle tyrannies hidden in what is allowed to be seen and said. But it will be given more fitly when I come to describe the inhabitants of Flatland themselves.

For the present, I set that dish aside, still steaming, to be served in its proper course; and I will say only a word or two—plain as bread and salt—of the climate and the houses in our country.

We too speak of four quarters—North, South, East, and West—though our tongues have never learned to point to a rising or setting star, for we have no sun to train our senses, no constellations to stitch direction into story. Instead, we are guided by a steadier tutor: a Law of Nature, old as our first hearth-fire, that draws all things, living and unliving, with a constant longing toward the South.

In the temperate belts this yearning is gentle—so gentle that even a Woman in tolerable health may press several furlongs northward without collapsing into breathless tremor. Yet the pull is never absent. It tugs at the joints like an unseen hand at the apron-strings; it leans the body’s intention and makes even a careless step declare, in its slight reluctance, which way is up-country and which way is down. Most days that faint resistance is compass enough.

And then there is the rain—our punctual messenger—falling at stated intervals, always out of the North. Long before any child can parse law or legend, they learn the rain’s direction the way they learn the difference between simmer and boil: by sound. The first hiss upon the roof-panels comes with a particular angle in its patter, a bright, needling percussion that travels along the northern sides and then softens as it spills away. In towns, the houses themselves sing you the bearings. Their side-walls run, for the most part, North and South, so that their roofs may ward off the northern rain. A traveler half-asleep can tell the compass by the way water drums—sharp on the roof’s leading edge, muted where the line of the wall shelters it—much as a cook can tell a pot’s temper by the note it gives when the spoon taps the rim.

Out in the country, where there are no walls to speak for you, the trunks of trees offer their own rough counsel, standing like silent elders along the fields. Even there, between the southward pull in your limbs and the northward rain in your ears, we are seldom as lost as you might suppose.

Yet I remember a certain walk across a desolate plain in a temperate region where the attraction was so slight it might have been a mere superstition, and there were neither houses nor trees—nothing to catch the ear, nothing to break the sameness. My own body offered no trustworthy tilt; the land gave no acoustic clue, no change in resonance underfoot. I went on until doubt thickened like overreduced broth. At last I was compelled to remain motionless for hours together, listening for that first distant curtain of rain, waiting for the North to announce itself in the only dialect the wilderness was willing to speak.

The force of the attraction bears harder upon the weak and aged, and most heavily of all upon delicate Females. To them the South is not merely a direction but a weight in the blood, as if the world itself were tilting their spines. Hence it is accounted a point of breeding that if you meet a Lady in the street you give her the North side of the way—so that the earth’s pull may not press her toward you and jostle her to harm. It is not always easy to do at short notice, especially when you are in rude health and the air is quiet and dry and your senses are robbed of those little confirmations—roof-rattle, gutter-run, the faint insistence of the ground—that help a man tell his North from his South.

Our houses have no windows. Light comes to us in our homes and out of them, by day and by night, equally at all times and in all places—whence, we know not. In old days this ignorance was a feast for the learned: they gnawed at it as a scholar gnaws an old bone for flavor, asking endlessly, “What is the origin of light?” The question bred treatises and arguments, and arguments bred obsession, until the would-be solvers crowded our lunatic asylums. At last, after fruitless attempts to discourage such investigations by making them subject to a heavy tax—an ungenerous, commercial-minded cure for a spiritual hunger—the Legislature, in comparatively recent times, absolutely prohibited them.

I—alas, I alone in Flatland—know now only too well the true solution of this mysterious problem. Yet my knowledge cannot be made intelligible to a single one of my countrymen. The very words that would carry it fail; our common lexicon has no honest vessel for what I have seen. So I am mocked at— I, the sole possessor of the truths of Space and of the theory of the introduction of Light from the world of three Dimensions—as if I were the maddest of the mad. There are experiences that do not translate; there are realities that die the moment one forces them into a dominant dialect unfit to hold them. But a truce to these bitter mouthfuls: let me return to our houses.

The commonest form of a house among us is five-sided—a pentagon—built not for ornament, but for safety and for the weather’s habits. The two northern sides make the roof, meeting the rain as a lid meets steam, and for the most part they have no doors. On the East is a small door for the Women; on the West a much larger one for the Men; the South side, which serves as the floor, is usually doorless. The arrangement is as traditional as a family recipe: handed down, adjusted by law and custom, and defended with the same quiet ferocity with which an old farmer defends the seed-corn that kept his grandparents alive.

Square and triangular houses are not allowed, and the reason is plain enough to any who have ever walked too quickly with their thoughts elsewhere. The angles of a Square—and still more those of an equilateral Triangle—are far more pointed than those of a Pentagon. Moreover, the lines of inanimate objects, such as houses, are dimmer than the lines of Men and Women. Thus the sharp corner that your eye might miss until too late can do serious injury to an inconsiderate or absent-minded traveler running suddenly against it. Long ago, as early as the eleventh century of our era, triangular houses were universally forbidden by Law, except in fortifications, powder-magazines, barracks, and other state buildings—places where it is not desirable that the general public should approach without circumspection.

At that time square houses were still permitted everywhere, though discouraged by a special tax, as if danger could be priced and paid for like spice. But about three centuries afterward the Law decided that in all towns containing a population above ten thousand, the angle of a Pentagon was the smallest house-angle that could be allowed consistently with public safety. The good sense of the community seconded the Legislature; custom, like a patient cook, took the raw rule and made it daily bread. Now, even in the country, pentagonal construction has superseded every other; only now and then, in some remote and backward agricultural district, may an antiquarian discover a square house lingering like an obsolete word in a dying dialect—still intelligible, still stubborn, yet belonging more to memory than to the living tongue of the land.

In Flatland, a full-grown body, measured from longest side to broadest reach, seldom exceeds eleven of your inches—twelve only in the most extravagant tales, the way old hearth-keepers speak of a loaf that rose so high it brushed the rafters. Yet within that modest span our whole larder of society is arranged, each rank cut to its pattern as surely as dough is portioned, shaped, and proved.

Our Women are Straight Lines.

No curve softens them; no angle gives them a handle for the eye. They pass with a hush and a peril, like a knife slid back into its sheath—silent, simple, and, in the wrong proximity, fatal. Their coming is known less by sight than by the minute changes in the air’s tremble: the faintest shift of sound along a corridor, the way a voice bounces when a Line is near, the tiny swallowing of echoes. Flatland teaches you early to listen for what cannot be safely stared at.

Our Soldiers and the lowest classes of workmen are Triangles with two equal sides, each about eleven inches long, and a base so short—often no more than half an inch—that their vertex is a needle-point. When the base is most degraded, scarcely an eighth of an inch, they are nearly indistinguishable from Women: so sharpened is their summit, so eager their angle. We call them, as you might in your own kitchens and barracks, by a name that is both classification and fate: Isosceles.

The Middle Class is made of Equilateral Triangles—three sides equal, angles balanced, a steadiness in them like a well-kept hearth that gives off neither smoke nor sudden flare. Our Professional Men and Gentlemen are Squares (to which class I myself belong), and Pentagons—figures with five sides, moving with a self-possession that suggests long practice in measured conduct, as if each step were counted like spoonfuls of salt.

Above these begin the Nobility: Hexagons first, then seven-sided, eight-sided, and onward into the many-sided—those called Polygonal, because the tongue itself gives up enumerating and settles for a collective label. Finally, when the sides become so numerous and so small that no ear can catch the tiny facets of their passage, no eye can separate edge from edge, the figure is taken for a Circle. These are our Priests. To be nearly indistinguishable from perfect roundness is the highest distinction in our world; the ultimate refinement, like a sauce reduced until no single ingredient asserts itself and all becomes one seamless, authoritative savor.

It is a Law of Nature with us that a male child shall have one more side than his father—so that generation by generation the family rises, in the usual course, one step in the scale of development and rank. Thus the son of a Square is a Pentagon; the son of a Pentagon, a Hexagon; and so upward, as if the household were adding another place setting at each birth, another spoke to the wheel of lineage.

But as every steward of fields and ovens knows, laws of growth do not treat all soils alike. This rule does not always touch the Tradesmen, and less often still the Soldiers and Workmen, who can hardly be said to deserve the name of human Figures, since they have not all their sides equal. With them the Law falters. The son of an Isosceles remains Isosceles.

And yet—Flatland is stingy with mercy but not wholly barren of it. Hope, like a starter kept alive on scant flour, is not shut out even from the sharp-angled poor. After a long series of military successes, or diligent and skilful labours—years of carrying burdens, years of cutting and setting and building until the hands learn a craft’s patience—it is generally found that the more intelligent among these lower classes show a slight increase of their third side, the base, and a shrinkage of the two long sides. The very geometry of their bodies records the slow work of restraint and practice, as if the State had kneaded them through generations, pressing down the wild peaks and coaxing breadth where there was only point.

Then come intermarriages—arranged, always, by the Priests. They are not romantic unions but planned graftings, like the old agrarian method of pairing stock to bring sweetness into a bitter strain. The sons and daughters of the more intellectual Isosceles are matched so that the offspring may approximate still more to the Equal-Sided type. Rarely—vanishingly, against the vast numbers of Isosceles births—a genuine and certifiable Equilateral Triangle is produced from Isosceles parents.

Yes: certifiable. In Flatland no Lady of any position will marry an uncertified Triangle. Nature may whisper, but the Boards must speak aloud. A slightly irregular Triangle has sometimes begotten Square offspring; but almost always the irregularity returns like a long-forgotten aftertaste, visited upon the third generation, which either fails to attain Pentagonal rank or relapses to the Triangular. We are a people who fear relapse as a cook fears contamination: one unseen fault, one unclean utensil, and the whole line is said to sour.

For the birth of a True Equilateral from Isosceles parents requires more than luck. Its antecedents are a history: carefully arranged intermarriages; long, continued exercise of frugality and self-control on the part of would-be ancestors; and a patient, systematic development of the Isosceles intellect through many generations. In the poorest quarters the elders tell it like a household epic—how great-grandparents learned to portion their rations, how a grandfather refused the noisy temptations of the street and instead trained his mind in quiet rooms where the soundscape was clean, where thought could rise without being shredded by clamor. Even the acoustics matter: the Circles know that a mind grows best where harsh vibrations are subdued, where the air is not hammered day and night by the cheap din of factories or the gaudy street-singers hired to sell what they do not understand. Noise is a kind of theft; it steals attention first, then memory.

When at last such a child is born, the rejoicing runs for many furlongs around. Neighbours who have never tasted fine spice will still set out their best, scraping together stored oil, browning what little grain they have, letting savory scents carry into the lanes—an offering to fortune and proof that the serfs, too, can keep ritual. The event becomes part of oral record, carried in dialect from mouth to mouth; yet the dialect is always under pressure, because official speech—the Priestly lexicon—presses in like standardized weights, flattening local words until the very story of the child’s making becomes untranslatable in the old terms. What the poor call it in their kitchens is not what the Boards write down.

After birth comes the strict examination, conducted by the Sanitary and Social Board. Cold instruments, practiced questions, the hush of authority. If the infant is certified as Regular, he is admitted with solemn ceremonial into the class of Equilaterals. And then, immediately, he is taken from his proud yet sorrowing parents and adopted by some childless Equilateral, bound by oath never to permit the child henceforth to enter his former home—or even to look upon his relations again—lest the freshly developed organism, by unconscious imitation, fall back into its hereditary level.

I have seen that severing, and there is no seasoning that can disguise its bitterness. The parents stand in their poor rooms where broth once simmered and now cools untouched; the air holds the lingering aroma of celebration, and beneath it the metallic tang of grief. In silence they listen to the fading sound of their child being led away—each step a diminishing beat on the floor—until the corridor’s echoes swallow it. The house, robbed of its newest voice, changes its sound at once: emptier, sharper, more like a place abandoned. Thus does Flatland preserve improvement by forbidding remembrance.

And yet the occasional emergence of an Equilateral from serf-born ancestors is welcomed, not only by the serfs themselves—as a gleam of light thrown across the monotonous squalor of their days—but by the Aristocracy at large. The higher classes know well that such rare phenomena do little to vulgarize privilege; rather, they serve as a barrier against revolution from below. If every acute-angled labourer were absolutely without hope, the next rebellion might find a leader of such ability that numbers and strength would overmatch even the wisdom of the Circles.

Nature itself, our rulers say, has provided a safeguard. For in proportion as the working-classes increase in intelligence, knowledge, and all virtue, in that same proportion their acute angle—physically terrible—shall increase also, approximating to the comparatively harmless angle of the Equilateral. The brutal and formidable among the soldier class—creatures almost on a level with Women in their lack of intelligence—are most deadly when they are most dull. As they wax in mental ability enough to employ their penetrating power with strategy, so do they wane in the power of penetration itself. The more they learn to plan, the less they can stab.

The Priests praise this as Compensation: admirable, inevitable, proof of the natural fitness—almost the divine origin—of our aristocratic constitution. But among the lower hearths, where people speak in older cadences and measure truth by what it costs in the mouth to say it, the same “law” is remembered differently: as a bargain imposed by those who already feast. Improve your mind, and your body is blunted; grow wise, and the weapon you were born with is dulled in the same stroke. The dominant lexicon calls it order. The serf’s tongue, if allowed to keep its own words, might call it containment.

By judicious use of this law, the Polygons and Circles are almost always able to stifle sedition in its cradle, taking advantage of the boundless hopefulness of the human mind. Art comes to the aid of Law and Order as surely as spice comes to mask a tough cut of meat. It is generally found possible—by a little artificial compression or expansion, administered by State physicians—to make some of the more intelligent leaders of a rebellion perfectly Regular, admitting them at once into the privileged classes. Others, still below the standard but hungry for elevation, are allured by the prospect of ultimate ennoblement and induced to enter the State Hospitals, where they are kept in honourable confinement for life: fed, housed, and removed from the sound of the streets they might have roused. One or two alone—the more obstinate, foolish, and hopelessly irregular—are led to execution.

Then the wretched rabble of Isosceles, planless and leaderless, are either transfixed without resistance by the small body of their brethren whom the Chief Circle keeps in pay for such emergencies; or, more often, by jealousies and suspicions skilfully fomented among them by the Circular party, they are stirred to mutual warfare and perish by one another’s angles. In the alleys the clash is not merely physical; it is linguistic too—neighbour set against neighbour by words whose meanings have been subtly bent, by slogans repeated until local sense is drowned in official noise. A people who cannot keep hold of their own terms cannot keep hold of their own cause.

No less than one hundred and twenty rebellions are recorded in our annals, besides minor outbreaks numbered at two hundred and thirty-five; and they have all ended thus—hope fed just enough to keep the pot from boiling over, memory trimmed and renamed, the sound of dissent muffled beneath the rounded, unbroken authority of the Circles.

If the keenest of our Soldier Triangles seem to you in Spaceland like weapons given life, you may imagine what it is to share a hearth with a being sharpened beyond even that—our Women, who are to a wedge what a needle is to a chisel: all point, and at both ends.

I have sometimes tried, for the sake of a younger Reader who loves a homely illustration, to explain their dreadfulness by an experiment as simple as laying out supper. Set a pin upon a board, as I have seen your cooks lay a skewer beside the cutting-stone. Lower your eye until it is level with the board. Look along the skewer’s side and you can trace its whole length as a pale line. But shift your gaze so that you meet it end-on and—save for the glint of its tip—it is gone. Not vanished truly, only hidden by the poverty of your angle of sight.

So it is with one of our Women. When her side is presented, she is a straight line: visible, unmistakable, and—if you value your continued existence—respected. When the end that bears her single organ (for with us the eye and mouth are one and the same aperture, seeing and speaking from the same bright point) meets your vision, she shrinks to a lustrous dot, a dangerous spark on the floor. And when her hinder extremity is toward you, it is dimmer still—sub-lustrous, scarcely brighter than furniture—so that her own body seems to wear an “invisible cap,” and you may blunder into her without warning.

From this the peril should be plain even to the least reflective mind in Spaceland. If the angle of a decent middle-class Triangle can cut; if brushing a Working Man can leave a long gash; if a rash encounter with an Officer demands bandaging and weeks of caution; if the merest touch from the vertex of a Private Soldier can mean death—then what is collision with a Woman but instant annihilation? And what, again, is daily life where the deadliest creature may choose to be seen as a thin line, or as nothing more than a faint point?

Because of this, our States have for ages cooked up enactments as a housewife cooks down a sharp vinegar into a safer sauce—by rules, by habit, by fear, reducing the wildness into something that will not poison the table. In the Southern and less temperate regions—where gravitation presses more heavily, where bodies are dragged into little unintended swerves as a heavy sack of grain drags the porter’s step—the Code concerning Women is stricter; for there an accidental motion is common, and an accident with a needle is catastrophe.

Yet in all places the chief provisions may be tasted in this brief summary:

First: every house must have one entrance on the Eastern side for the use of Females only, by which all women shall enter “in a becoming and respectful manner,” and never by the Men’s Western door. (In Spaceland I was told that even among some of your Priestly circles you keep separate entries for certain classes, that they too may “approach in a becoming and respectful manner”; the tune is strangely familiar though played on different instruments.)

Second: no Female may walk in any public place without continually keeping up her Peace-cry, under pain of death.

Third: any Female duly certified to suffer from St. Vitus’s Dance, fits, chronic cold with violent sneezing, or any ailment that compels involuntary motion, is to be destroyed immediately.

Some States add further seasoning to this harsh stew. In a few, a woman may not stand or walk in public unless she keeps her back perpetually moving right to left, so that those behind may read her presence by that lateral swish. Others command that she travel with a son or servant—or with her husband—like a cook who must never carry a boiling pot without an attendant to clear the path. Others confine women to their houses except at religious festivals.

But the wisest among our Circles and Statesmen have observed, as carefully as a food-historian weighing famine against feast, that an excess of prohibitions weakens the race and breeds a worse harvest: domestic murder. For when women are penned too long or hampered too tightly, their temper ferments. In the less temperate climates whole villages of males have been known to be wiped out in an hour or two by one simultaneous outbreak, as if a sudden fire had run through a granary. Thus the Three Laws above commonly suffice for the better regulated States, and may stand as a rough exemplar of our Female Code.

After all, our principal safeguard lies not in statute but in the women’s own interest. A retrograde movement of theirs can kill at once; yet unless she can instantly disengage her stinging extremity from the struggling body, her own frail length may be shattered. In this one sees, as in the crack of a too-thin glass jar, how danger checks itself.

Fashion, too, is enlisted on the side of safety. I said that in some less civilized States the back-sway is enforced by Law. In well-governed regions it has long been universal among ladies who aspire to breeding; memory itself cannot reach its beginning. It is held a disgrace to a State if legislation must command what ought to rise, and in every respectable female does rise, like a natural instinct.

There is, moreover, a hierarchy in the very music of it. Among ladies of Circular rank the undulation is rhythmical and well-modulated, like the soft, measured stirring of a thick stew that must not scorch. The wife of a common Equilateral can manage little beyond a monotonous swing, a pendulum-tick that says only, “Here, here, here,” without grace. Yet even that regular tick is admired and imitated by the wife of an aspiring Isosceles, for in the females of those families no back-motion has become, as yet, a necessity of life. Thus in every household of position “back motion” keeps time as steadily as any clock, and husbands and sons enjoy at least immunity from the invisible sting.

Do not suppose, however, that our Women are devoid of affection. The tragedy is not the absence of feeling but the tyranny of the moment. Passion in them predominates over every other consideration—an outcome, we hold, of their unfortunate conformation. Having no angle at all, and so falling below the very lowest Isosceles, they are deemed wholly devoid of brain-power: no reflection, no judgment, no forethought, and scarcely any memory. In fury they remember no claims and acknowledge no distinctions. I have known a case—no fable, but a cold item of household record—where a Woman exterminated her entire family, and half an hour later, when the rage had passed and the fragments had been swept away as one sweeps flour-dust from a board, she asked what had become of husband and children.

From this it follows that a Woman is not to be irritated while she is in a position to turn round. But when you have her in her apartments—which are constructed to deny her that power—you may say what you will. There she is impotent for mischief, and will not remember a few minutes later the insult for which she is now threatening you with death, nor the promises you found it necessary to make in order to quiet her. One may call this peace; it is rather the peace of a kitchen where knives are locked away, not because hunger has ended, but because hands are unsteady.

On the whole, domestic life among us goes smoothly enough—if smoothness is measured by the scarcity of slaughter—except in the lower strata of the Military Classes. There the husbands’ want of tact and discretion produces disasters past description. Trusting in the offensive virtue of their acute angles instead of the defensive arts of good sense and seasonable simulation, they neglect the prescribed construction of women’s apartments, or they irritate their wives outdoors by ill-advised expressions which they refuse to retract. Worse, their blunt devotion to literal truth makes them unwilling to pour out the lavish promises by which a judicious Circle can, in an instant, pacify his consort.

The result is massacre—yet not without advantage, as it removes the more brutal and troublesome among the Isosceles. Many of our Circles regard the destructiveness of the Thinner Sex as one providential arrangement among others for suppressing redundant population and nipping Revolution in the bud.

Yet even in our best regulated and most nearly Circular families, I cannot pretend that the ideal of family life rises as high as with you in Spaceland. There is safety bought dearly. Harmony of tastes or pursuits is necessarily slight; the cautious wisdom of the Circles has ensured survival at the cost of comfort.

In every Polygonal household of rank it has been a habit from time immemorial—and has now become a kind of instinct among high-born women—that mothers and daughters keep their mouths and eyes perpetually toward the husband and his male friends. For a lady of distinction to turn her back upon her husband would be taken as a portent, entailing loss of STATUS. This custom has the advantage of safety, yet, as I shall later show, it is not without grave disadvantages.

In the home of a Working Man or respectable Tradesman—where the wife is permitted to turn her back while she attends to household tasks—there are at least intervals of quiet: she is neither seen nor heard, save for the faint humming of the continuous Peace-cry, like a kettle’s low warning while dinner is prepared. But in upper households there is too often no peace. The voluble mouth and bright, penetrating eye are always directed toward the Master; and light itself is not more persistent than the stream of feminine discourse. The tact that can avert the sting cannot stop the speech. And since—by our belief—she has absolutely nothing to say, and absolutely no constraint of wit, sense, or conscience to prevent her from saying it, some cynics have been heard to declare that they would prefer the danger of the death-dealing but inaudible extremity to the safe sonorousness of the other.

To you in Spaceland the condition of our Women may seem deplorable; so indeed it is. A male of the lowest Isosceles may look forward to some widening of angle and to the slow elevation of his caste; but no Woman can entertain such hope. “Once a Woman, always a Woman” is with us a Decree of Nature, and the very Laws of Evolution appear suspended against her. Yet at least—so our philosophers say—we may admire the prearrangement which has ordained that, having no hopes, they shall also have no memory to recall, and no forethought to anticipate, the miseries and humiliations which are at once the necessity of their existence and the foundation-stone of Flatland’s constitution.

You who live in the lavish world where light comes with shade, where two eyes grant you the trick of distance, where colour is not a rumour but a daily garnish upon the air—you who can look at an Angle as at an object on a platter, and take in the whole roundness of a Circle at once—how am I to season for you the poverty of our sight?

In Flatland, whatever the creature—living or lifeless—whatever its true figure, it presents to the eye almost the same thin appearance: a straight Line. Imagine a kitchen in which every spice, every grain, every herb, and every cut of meat looked, to the glance, like the same pale ribbon. In such a place, recognition is not a matter of “seeing,” but of other senses trained until they become a second kind of sight. We have, in practice, three means.

The first is hearing. In our world the ear is not an ornament, but an instrument as precise as a well-tempered knife. By voice and cadence we know our friends; and, among the humbler ranks, we can usually tell class from class—Equilateral, Square, Pentagon—without so much as touching. I speak only of the three lowest Regular orders; the Isosceles I leave out of account, as one leaves grit out of flour.

Yet the higher one climbs in our social ovens, the more difficult it becomes to distinguish by sound alone. Voices grow alike; and, besides, the delicate art of voice-discrimination is considered a plebeian talent, neglected by the Aristocracy as a farmer’s weather-sense is neglected by those who buy their bread already sliced. Worse: where imposture is possible, hearing is a cracked bell. Among the lowest orders the vocal organs are developed beyond what would correspond with the ears that judge them; an Isosceles can, with ease, mimic the voice of a Polygon, and with training even counterfeit the tones of a Circle. The ear, however keen, can be deceived by a practiced throat.

Therefore we resort more commonly to the second method: Feeling.

Among our women and the lower classes—of the upper I shall speak presently—touch is the chief test of recognition between strangers, especially when the question is not “Which individual?” but “Of what class?” What “introduction” is among your higher folk, that “feeling” is with us. In the country districts, among old-fashioned gentlemen far from the noise and hurry of towns, you will still hear the formal courtesy, rich as an ancestral recipe: “Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so.” It has a certain domestic gravity, like the invitation to break bread under one’s roof with proper ceremony.

In towns, among men of business, the phrase is pared down—“be felt by” is dropped, as if politeness were an unnecessary garnish—and it becomes: “Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so,” though all understand that the contact must be mutual. Among the most modern, dashing young gentlemen—those who hate any superfluous effort and are supremely indifferent to the purity of their native speech—the language is further clipped and browned into slang. “To feel” becomes a technical verb meaning “to recommend for the purposes of feeling and being felt,” and so the barbarism is sanctioned in fast society: “Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones.” Thus does language, like a traditional stew, get thinned by haste until the old flavour is scarcely remembered; and yet the power of the shortened words remains, a little tyranny hidden inside a fashionable abbreviation.

Do not suppose, however, that “feeling” is with us the tedious circling round of every side that it would be with you. We do not stroke a whole figure as one kneads an entire loaf. Long practice, begun in schools and hardened by daily necessity, enables us to judge class by a single angle touched cleanly and briefly. A Triangle, Square, and Pentagon—so long as they are Regular—each has its own unmistakable “bite” beneath the fingertip. And the brainless vertex of an acute Isosceles is obvious even to the dullest touch, as a bone-shard is obvious in badly ground meal.

So, as a rule, it suffices to feel one angle; and having done so, we know the class—unless, indeed, we are dealing with the higher nobility. There the task becomes far more delicate. Even a Master of Arts in our University of Wentbridge has been known to confuse a ten-sided with a twelve-sided Polygon; and there is hardly a Doctor of Science, in or out of that famous University, who can decide promptly and unhesitatingly between a twenty-sided and a twenty-four-sided member of the Aristocracy. The differences are like those between two batches of the same bread made from adjacent fields: the untrained palate swears they are identical, while the historian and the elder argue for hours over the faintest note of soil and season.

Moreover, the ritual of contact is not without peril. If you recall what our Legislative Code says of Women, you will perceive at once that introduction by touch demands caution. Angles can wound. For the safety of the Feeler, the Felt must stand perfectly still. A start; a restless shifting; yes, even a violent sneeze—such abrupt motions have, before now, proved fatal to the incautious and have nipped many a promising friendship in the bud, like a careless hand overturning the pot just as it comes to the boil.

This danger is especially frequent among the lower classes of Triangles. Their eye lies so far from their vertex that they can scarcely take cognizance of what happens at that extremity; and their nature is rough and coarse, not sensitive to the delicate touch of a highly organized Polygon. What wonder, then, if an involuntary toss of the head has sometimes deprived the State of a valuable life?

I have heard that my excellent Grandfather—one of the least irregular of his unhappy Isosceles class, who indeed obtained, shortly before his decease, four out of seven votes from the Sanitary and Social Board to pass him into the Equal-sided—often deplored, with a tear in his venerable eye, a catastrophe of this kind that befell his great-great-great-Grandfather: a respectable Working Man whose angle, or brain, was 59 degrees 30 minutes.

According to my Grandfather’s account, my unfortunate Ancestor was afflicted with rheumatism; and while being felt by a Polygon—some Great Man, important as a rich merchant at market—he made one sudden start and accidentally transfixed the Great Man through the diagonal. The punishment and disgrace that followed—long imprisonment, degradation, and the moral shock that soured the whole household—threw back our family a degree and a half in our slow ascent toward better things. In the next generation the family brain was registered at only 58 degrees; and not until five generations had passed was the lost ground recovered, the full 60 degrees attained, and the Ascent from the Isosceles finally achieved. So much misfortune, rolling forward through blood and memory like a bitter inheritance, from one small accident in the act of Feeling—one twitch, like a moment’s inattention that ruins a carefully tended culture of yeast.

Here I seem to hear some of my better educated readers cry out: “Angles? Degrees? Minutes? How can you speak of such measures? We can see an angle because we see two lines inclined; but you, who see only one line at a time—how can you ever discern any angle, much less register one as larger or smaller?”

I answer: though we cannot see angles, we can infer them—and with great precision. Necessity has sharpened our touch as hunger sharpens the senses of a cook who must tell, by smell alone, when the bread is about to scorch. Our tactile faculty, trained and compelled, distinguishes differences of angle more accurately than your sight can do unaided by rule or instrument.

Nor are we without natural helps. It is a Law of Nature with us that the brain of the Isosceles class begins at half a degree—thirty minutes—and increases, if it increases at all, by half a degree in each generation until the goal of 60 degrees is reached. Then serfdom is quitted; the freeman enters the class of Regulars. Nature herself, therefore, supplies an ascending scale, an Alphabet of angles from half a degree up to 60 degrees. Specimens of this scale are placed in every Elementary School throughout the land, like standard measures in a pantry—weights that teach the hand what “true” means.

Owing to occasional retrogressions, to still more frequent moral and intellectual stagnation, and to the extraordinary fecundity of the Criminal and Vagabond classes, there is always a vast superfluity of individuals of the half-degree and single-degree class, and a fair abundance of Specimens up to 10 degrees. These are absolutely destitute of civic rights; and many, not having even intelligence enough for warfare, are devoted by the States to the service of education.

Fettered immovably, so that all danger is removed—stilled like animals trussed for the sake of safety, an ugly image that our children learn to accept as “order”—they are placed in infant classrooms. There they are utilized by the Board of Education to impart to the offspring of the Middle Classes that tact and intelligence of which these wretched creatures themselves are utterly devoid. In some States the Specimens are fed and suffered to exist for several years; but in more temperate and better regulated regions, it is found, in the long run, more advantageous for the educational interests of the young to dispense with food and renew the Specimens every month—which is about the average duration of the foodless existence of the Criminal class.

In the cheaper schools, what is gained by the longer existence of the Specimen is lost partly in the cost of food and partly in diminished accuracy: the angles, after a few weeks of constant “feeling,” are impaired, like a frequently handled mould whose sharp edges wear down. Nor must it be forgotten—if we are honestly counting advantages—that the more expensive system tends, though slightly yet perceptibly, to diminish the redundant Isosceles population, an object which every statesman in Flatland keeps constantly in view.

On the whole, therefore—though I am not ignorant that in many popularly elected School Boards there is a reaction in favour of what is called “the cheap system”—I am disposed to think this one of those cases in which expense is the truest economy. But I must not let the noise of School Board politics drown the sound I am trying to make you hear.

Enough, I trust, has been said to show that Recognition by Feeling is neither so tedious nor so indecisive as you might suppose; and it is plainly more trustworthy than Recognition by hearing. Still, as I have admitted, it has the grave objection of danger. For this reason many in the Middle and Lower classes, and all without exception among the Polygonal and Circular orders, prefer a third method—whose description I reserve for the next section.

I am likely to sound like a cook who swears that every broth tastes of plain water—only to turn around and tell you, with equal earnestness, that he can name the village and the season from one sip.

For I have said before, and I do not withdraw it, that in Flatland every figure, to the eye, offers itself as a line—no breadth, no face, nothing that your Spaceland sight would call a “shape.” Taken strictly, that ought to make all of us visually indistinguishable: Merchant from Physician, noble from tradesman, father from child. And yet we do, in fact, recognize one another by sight.

The key lies in a qualification I made and you perhaps skimmed as though it were a footnote in a recipe: Recognition by Feeling is universal—among the lower classes. Sight-recognition is a high art, practiced chiefly by the higher ranks and, no less importantly, in the temperate regions of our world. It exists because of Fog.

Yes—Fog. That same damp curtain which, in your country, you curse as a thief of horizons and a bruise upon the spirit, is to us a kind of daily hearth-smoke: not always comfortable, perhaps, but nourishing. It is the softening steam over the communal pot, the haze that makes distances legible. It nurses our arts and parents our sciences. But let me set aside praise and give you the plain method, as one might lay out the steps of a difficult preservation—dependent not on mystical inspiration but on careful, repeated trial.

If Fog did not exist, every line would appear with the same hard-edged clarity. There are indeed arid regions—unhappy countries—where the air is so dry and transparent that a line two feet away and a line three feet away look equally clean, equally sharp. In such places, sight is almost useless for discerning rank.

But where Fog is plentiful—and it is plentiful for much of our year—distance becomes a measurable ingredient. A thing three feet away is perceptibly dimmer than a thing two feet eleven inches away. The difference is small, like the difference between a sauce just beginning to reduce and one that has gone a minute further: subtle, and yet, to a practiced sense, decisive. By long training—by constant experimental tasting, as it were, of comparative dimness and clearness—we learn to infer the configuration of the object from the way its “line” fades into the Fog.

Consider an instance.

Suppose two strangers approach me, and I wish to know their rank. Let them be, say, a Merchant and a Physician—an Equilateral Triangle and a Pentagon. Both, to my eye, are lines. How can I tell which line is which?

If I can place myself so that my eye-glance bisects one of the approaching figure’s angles, I am, in a manner, standing at the exact point where two adjoining sides present themselves impartially. Think of it as positioning your nose over the center of a platter so the aromas from left and right rise evenly: you are not favoring one edge.

Now, in the case of the Merchant—the Triangle—what do I see? A line with a bright middle point, because the angle-point is nearest to me. Yet on either side, the line falls away rapidly into dimness. The sides recede quickly into the Fog, and what appear to me as the extremities of the Merchant are very dim indeed—like the ends of a long corridor where the air turns milky and sound is swallowed.

In the case of the Physician—the Pentagon—I see much the same general phenomenon: a line with a bright center. But the shading into dimness is less rapid, because the adjoining sides retreat less steeply away from my point of view. The extremities are therefore not so dim as the Merchant’s. The line “keeps its strength” longer, as a well-made stock carries flavor farther down the tongue than thin water ever can.

From these two examples you can grasp the principle: by reading the gradient of brightness—how quickly the line fades—we discriminate, with fair accuracy, between the middle and the lower orders. If you, my Spaceland patrons, can at least conceive the possibility, and refrain from dismissing it as an impossibility dressed up in words, then I shall have achieved as much as I reasonably can. To multiply instances would not enlighten so much as overwhelm, like piling spice upon spice until even the cook can no longer taste the dish.

Yet I must guard the young and inexperienced from a dangerous inference. They might hear these simple cases and imagine that recognition by sight is easy—something like guessing a loaf’s doneness by looking at its crust once. In real life, our problems are far more delicate.

For example: when my Father, who is a Triangle, approaches, he may present not an angle but a side. If I am confronted by his side full front, then until I ask him to rotate—or until I edge my own position around him—I am, for a moment, uncertain whether he might be a straight line: that is to say, a Woman. What is crisp certainty when the angle is offered becomes uncertainty when the side is presented, just as the most distinct herb becomes hard to name when crushed into a blend.

Or again: when I am with one of my two hexagonal Grandsons, and I view one of his sides directly, the sight is not a single, simple fade from bright to dim. I see one whole line in comparative brightness, scarcely shading at the ends, and I also see, faintly, the indications of two other sides—smaller lines, dim throughout and dimming further toward their extremities. The very richness of the configuration makes it harder to read at a glance; it is like tasting a sauce in which several ingredients are properly integrated: the better the craft, the more training required to identify each component.

If you are even passably acquainted with mathematics, you will readily believe me when I say that the true trials come not in solitary meetings but in society—when we are ourselves in motion, advancing or retreating, rotating, adjusting our bearings—while attempting to discriminate by sight among many high-ranked Polygons moving in different directions at once, as in a ball-room or conversazione. Imagine a banquet hall at full attendance, not silent but alive with the scrape of service, the careful hush of etiquette, the shifting murmur of many conversations. In such an acoustic environment, the mind’s well-being depends on the ability to separate streams of sound without panic. So it is with sight among us: the “noise” is visual—perpetual fluctuations of brightness and dimness as figures rotate—and it takes a discipline as exacting as any practiced ear.

Hence the generous endowments granted to the Learned Professors of Geometry, Static and Kinetic, at our illustrious University of Wentbridge, where the Science and Art of Sight Recognition is taught regularly to large classes of the elite. It is not an accomplishment for those who must spend their days at the counter, the furnace, the field; it demands time, money, and a kind of patient attention that must be cultivated like a long-fermented starter, fed and watched across years.

Only a few of the scions of our noblest and wealthiest houses can afford the thorough prosecution of this noble and valuable art. Even to me—a Mathematician of no mean standing, and Grandfather of two most hopeful and perfectly regular Hexagons—there are moments when I find myself in a crowd of rotating higher Polygons and am perplexed. The shifts of brightness are so quick, so irregular, that the eye feels as if it were listening to a chorus in which each voice changes pitch mid-note.

To a common Tradesman or Serf, such a scene is almost as unintelligible as it would be to you, Reader, if you were suddenly transported among us. You would see nothing but lines—apparently straight—whose parts varied irregularly and perpetually in brightness and dimness. Even if you had passed the third year of Pentagonal and Hexagonal instruction at the University and knew the theory perfectly, you would still require many years of experience before you could move in a fashionable crowd without jostling your betters. And it is against etiquette to ask them to “feel.” They, by superior culture and breeding, read your movements easily, while you understand little of theirs. The asymmetry is not merely personal; it is built into our dominant customs, our sanctioned vocabulary of manners—who may touch, who must not, who may demand certainty, and who must be content with inference.

In a word, to comport oneself with perfect propriety in Polygonal society, one ought to be a Polygon oneself. That, at least, is the painful teaching of my experience.

It is astonishing how much this art—almost an instinct—develops by habitual practice and by the deliberate avoidance of Feeling. Here, too, custom shapes capacity. Just as, in your world, if the deaf and dumb are permitted always to gesticulate and use the hand-alphabet, they will never acquire the more difficult but more socially potent arts of lipspeech and lip-reading, so with us: those who rely upon Feeling early will never learn Seeing in perfection. The senses are not merely organs; they are languages, and if one dialect becomes dominant in childhood, the other dwindles—until certain nuances become untranslatable, and whole regions of experience are lost to the speaker as if to a dead tongue.

For this reason, among our higher classes, Feeling is discouraged or absolutely forbidden. From the cradle, their children are not sent to Public Elementary schools—where Feeling is taught as the practical language of the many—but to higher Seminaries of exclusive character. At Wentbridge itself, to “feel” is regarded as a serious fault: Rustication for the first offence, Expulsion for the second. Thus power protects its own lexicon. The rule is not merely about knowledge; it is about who is permitted to ask for certainty and who must accept the haze.

Among the lower classes, by contrast, the art of Sight Recognition is considered an unattainable luxury. A common Tradesman cannot afford to let his son spend a third of his life in abstract studies. The children of the poor are therefore allowed to feel from their earliest years. They gain thereby a precocity, an early vivacity—a quick, bright responsiveness—that contrasts at first most favorably with the inert and listless behavior of half-instructed Polygonal youths, who have been denied tactile certainty and are still awkward in the Fog, as if forced to cook by aroma alone before they have learned what each spice smells like.

But when the Polygonal youths at last complete their University course and are prepared to put theory into practice, the change that comes over them is almost a new birth. In every art, science, and social pursuit, they rapidly overtake and distance their Triangular competitors. Only a few fail to pass the Final Test or Leaving Examination.

The condition of this unsuccessful minority is truly pitiable. Rejected from the higher class, they are despised by the lower. They possess neither the matured and systematically trained powers of Polygonal Bachelors and Masters of Arts, nor the native precocity and mercurial versatility of the youthful Tradesman. The professions and public services are closed against them. And though in most States they are not actually debarred from marriage, they have the greatest difficulty forming suitable alliances; for experience shows that the offspring of such unfortunate and ill-endowed parents is generally itself unfortunate—if not positively Irregular.

From these specimens of the refuse of our Nobility, the great Tumults and Seditions of past ages have generally derived their leaders. So serious is the mischief that an increasing minority of our more progressive Statesmen now argue that true mercy would dictate their entire suppression—by enacting that all who fail the Final Examination should be imprisoned for life, or extinguished by a painless death.

But here I find myself straying toward Irregularities—a matter of such vital interest, and of such dangerous consequence, that it demands a separate section.

All through my former account I have spoken as though it were as settled as the salt in a pantry—that every soul in Flatland comes baked to one dependable recipe: a Regular Figure.

I ought, perhaps, to have set that rule out on the table at the start, the way an old agrarian elder lays down his knife before carving and says, “This is how we have always portioned the loaf.” For in our country the shape of a body is not mere appearance; it is the very measure by which we apportion trust, plan doorways, arrange processions, and set places at luncheon.

By “Regular,” understand me plainly. A Woman must not only be a line, but a straight line—no wavering, no kink, no sudden bulge like dough that rose unevenly and split its crust. An Artisan or Soldier must have two sides equal, as if cut by the same careful hand from the same bolt of cloth. Tradesmen must show three equal sides; Lawyers—of whose square profession I am one—four; and so upward through the Orders, the law being that in every Polygon all the sides are equal.

The length of those sides varies, of course, with age as surely as a broth grows richer by slow reduction. A Female at birth is scarcely an inch from end to end; a tall adult Woman might reach to a foot. Among the Males, you may say roughly that an adult’s sides, taken altogether, sum to two feet or a little more. But I am not now weighing portions. I am speaking of equality—of the exact repeat of measure that lets a cook know, without tasting every spoonful, that the pot is properly seasoned.

For the whole of our social life rests upon this one homely miracle: Nature wills that Figures have equal sides, and therefore equal angles. If the sides were unequal, the angles might be unequal; and then the simplest act of recognition—our daily bread—would crumble.

At present, to know a man’s class you need only one good glance, one swift estimate of an angle, the way a practiced food historian can tell a region by the scent of its smoke—peat, oak, seaweed—without asking the cook to recite his whole lineage. Sight Recognition among us is an art built on regularity: a single angle speaks a dialect everyone understands.

But imagine a world where a figure’s angles told conflicting stories, each corner muttering a different idiom. Then no single look would suffice. You would be forced to “Feel” him—angle by angle—like a blindfolded taster compelled to name every spice separately because the blend has been botched. Life would not be long enough for such tedious sampling. Sight Recognition would perish at once. Feeling, as an art, would soon follow, for what can survive when every encounter demands a full survey of another’s perimeter? Intercourse would become perilous or impossible; confidence would curdle; forethought would spoil in the heat. The simplest social arrangement—meeting, dining, marching—would turn uncertain, and civilization would relapse, not with a dramatic thunderclap, but with the dull, everyday confusion of a kitchen where no one agrees on measures.

Consider one plain instance, taken from domestic routine, the kind that binds generations together more surely than proclamations: you meet two or three Tradesmen in the street. At a glance you know them for what they are—Triangles—with their angles plain, their sides quickly “bedimmed” by honest labor and the dust of commerce. You invite them to step into your house to lunch.

You do so with perfect confidence now, because everyone knows—within an inch or two—the area an adult Triangle will occupy. Your doorway, your passage, your table-space: all were planned in the old way, as a farmer lays out rows knowing the reach of his hoe.

But suppose, as you usher in your guests, that one Tradesman drags behind his respectable vertex a monstrous irregular appendage—a parallelogram tail, twelve or thirteen inches across the diagonal—like a wagon hitched to a mule you never saw. What then? What are you to do with such a creature sticking fast in your house door, scraping your lintel, jamming the entry so that the warm scents of your stew cannot even travel out to greet him?

Under such conditions one angle would no longer be enough. You would have to measure and re-measure—perimeter after perimeter—before offering even a cup of broth. Your whole life would be spent surveying acquaintances, as though every invitation required you to inventory a stranger’s kitchen before trusting him with a seat by your hearth.

Already, even with Regularity, a crowd taxes the sagacity of a well-educated Square. The air is full of motion; the street hums with the layered acoustics of footsteps, voices, and the faint scrape of sides passing—our own little soundscape, in which the wise learn to listen for danger as much as to look. In a panic, the noise becomes a kind of industrial roar, a brutal flattening of all subtle signals; and when the sonic environment collapses into mere din, judgment follows. If, on top of that, no one could calculate the Regularity of a single figure in the press, all would be chaos. A slight alarm would lead to serious injuries—or, if Women or Soldiers were present, perhaps considerable loss of life, for a straight Line and a sharp Angle are unforgiving in close quarters.

Expediency, therefore, joins Nature in sealing Regularity as the condition of safety. Nor has the Law been backward in adding its own hard lid to the pot.

“Irregularity of Figure” means with us what you might call, and more than call, moral obliquity and criminality. We do not treat it as a private misfortune, to be compassionately hidden like a family recipe gone wrong; we treat it as a public threat.

There are, it is true, certain promulgators of paradoxes—philanthropists, they name themselves—who insist there is no necessary bond between geometrical and moral Irregularity. Their argument, served hot and sweetened, runs thus:

“The Irregular,” they say, “is from birth scouted by his own parents, derided by his siblings, neglected by domestics, scorned and suspected by society. He is excluded from all posts of responsibility, trust, and useful activity. His every movement is watched by the police until he comes of age and presents himself for inspection. Then he is either destroyed, if he exceeds the fixed margin of deviation, or immured in a Government Office as a clerk of the seventh class—prevented from marriage, forced to drudge at an uninteresting occupation for a miserable stipend, obliged to live and board at the office, and to take even his vacation under close supervision. What wonder if human nature, even in the best and purest, is embittered and perverted by such surroundings?”

It is a plausible dish, artfully plated. Yet it does not persuade me—nor, as the record shows, has it persuaded the wisest of our Statesmen—that our ancestors erred when they made it an axiom of policy that tolerating Irregularity is incompatible with the safety of the State.

Doubtless, the life of an Irregular is hard. But the interests of the Greater Number require that it be hard. A single warped measure corrupts a whole pantry. If a man with a triangular front and a polygonal back were allowed to exist—and to propagate a posterity still more irregular—what would become of the arts of life, which depend upon predictable spaces and shared measures?

Are our houses, doors, and churches to be rebuilt—thresholds widened, aisles altered, corners blunted—to accommodate such monsters? Are ticket-collectors to measure every man’s perimeter before admitting him to a theatre, or allowing him his place in a lecture-room? Is an Irregular to be exempt from the militia? And if he is not exempted, how is he to be prevented from carrying desolation into the ranks of his comrades, where one ill-judged turn in the crush of bodies might cut down a neighbor like an unguarded blade?

And consider, too, the temptations to imposture that would beset such a creature. What a dreadful advantage, to possess more than one “face” in the literal sense! How easy to enter a shop with a polygonal front foremost, presenting a respectable angle to a confiding tradesman, ordering goods to any extent—and then, by some sly shift, slipping away on a different measure entirely. In a world where language—our angles, our sides, our visible grammar—anchors identity, an Irregular becomes an untranslatable text: one that can be read differently from different directions, and so can always deny what it has been.

Let advocates of a falsely named Philanthropy plead as they may for the abrogation of the Irregular Penal Laws, I for my part have never known an Irregular who was not also what Nature evidently intended him to be: a hypocrite, a misanthropist, and, up to the limits of his power, a perpetrator of all manner of mischief.

Yet even here I will not recommend—at least not at present—the extremest measures adopted by some States, where an infant whose angle deviates by half a degree from correct angularity is summarily destroyed at birth. Our annals show that some of our highest and ablest men—men of real genius—laboured in their earliest days under deviations as great as, or even greater than, forty-five minutes; and to have lost those lives would have been irreparable injury to the State, like throwing away a rare seed because the first sprout appeared crooked.

Moreover, the art of healing has achieved some of its most glorious triumphs in the compressions, extensions, trepannings, colligations, and other surgical or dietetic operations by which Irregularity has been partly or wholly cured—patient labor, often beginning in infancy, like training a young vine to a straight trellis. Many a deviation has been coaxed, pressed, and guided back toward the common measure.

Advocating therefore a via media, I would draw no fixed and absolute line. But when the frame is just beginning to set—when growth, like cooling sugar, hardens into final shape—and when the Medical Board has reported that recovery is improbable, I would suggest that the Irregular offspring be painlessly and mercifully consumed.

If you have listened closely to the thin music of our world, you will already know why so many of us speak of Flatland as a place of admirable reason and miserable splendour.

We have our share of tumults. We quarrel by classes, we plot by angles, we wage wars that can be verified like a theorem the instant the last soldier has turned his base. There is a sharp, satisfying click to life here—the way a problem fits its proof, the way a crowd parts (or fails to part) when a careless Isosceles misjudges a passing distance. Yet from the standpoint of art, of those indulgences that should season the mind the way salt wakens broth, existence is plain fare served without aroma.

Consider what our eyes are given. All prospect, all “landscape,” all portraiture and still-life—flower, fruit, face, and feast—collapse into a single line. A line may shine or dim, grow bright or sink into obscurity, but it cannot bloom. It cannot carry the blush of a peach at its ripest edge, nor the deep umber of bread crust where heat has kissed it longest. It cannot—now, at least.

For Tradition, like an elder at the hearth who insists the best cider was pressed before any of us were born, tells us it was not always thus. There was an age—brief by the measure of dynasties but long enough to lay down a whole layer of longing—when Colour came among our ancestors and sat in their houses for some centuries like a guest too radiant to forget. It did not arrive by decree of Priests, nor by any official curriculum. It was found, as so many dangerous delights are found, privately.

The tale names a single Innovator: a certain Pentagon, his name variously preserved, but most often spoken as Chromatistes. Some say he stumbled on the constituents of the simpler colours as one might discover a new spice in the back of a granary; others claim he deduced them, patient as a cook-historian reducing a stock to its essence, skimming and tasting until the true note rose. However it began, the practice he devised was rudimentary—daubs and washes at first, a method more like staining than drawing—yet it altered every domestic routine it touched.

He began at home, as all revolutions do. First he decorated his own house: not merely marking thresholds for pride, but laying colour along edges the way a careful baker scores a loaf, so that form announced itself without being handled. Then, in a gesture that Tradition repeats with a mixture of amusement and disgust, he painted his slaves. Then his Father. Then his Sons and Grandsons. Lastly, himself—turning his own frame into a moving banner, a pantry-label that could be read at a glance.

Beauty was only half the charm. The other half was convenience—practical, daily, undeniable.

Wherever Chromatistes glided, varnished in hues, he did not need to be “felt” to be known. No one mistook his front for his back. His neighbours ceased their exhausting calculations of motion. In crowded ways, no one jostled him by accident; no one forced him to cry out his identity, that harsh vocal proclamation we colourless Squares and Pentagons must often make when we pass among ignorant Isosceles whose senses are dull and whose manners duller. It was as though he had brought not only a new art but a new quiet: less scraping of bodies, less panicked shouting, fewer collisions that ring through streets like dropped pans in a kitchen.

And so the fashion spread—fast, hot, and uncontrollable, the way flame runs along dry thatch.

Before a week had cooled, every Square and Triangle in his district had copied him, mixing what pigments they could, staining their sides with whatever tints were easiest to prepare. A few conservative Pentagons held out, clinging to the old plainness as if austerity were virtue in itself. A month or two, and even the Dodecagons—those who love to seem above common enthusiasms—were infected with the innovation. In less than a year the practice had run through all classes but the very highest of the Nobility.

From that district it leapt to surrounding regions. Within two generations, no one in all Flatland remained colourless—no one, that is, except Women and Priests.

Here Nature herself seemed to set her hand against the spread, as if some ancient law of our world—older than any statute—refused to be seasoned with this new brilliance.

Many-sidedness was almost essential as a pretext for the Innovators. They went about repeating an axiom that sounded profound because it sounded inevitable: “Distinction of sides is intended by Nature to imply distinction of colours.” The phrase passed from mouth to mouth like a fashionable recipe, converting whole towns at a time to the new culture. To those who had sides—many sides—it seemed plausible. Each edge could be given its own shade, each angle its own signature, until identity became legible without touch.

But manifestly the saying did not apply to Women, who had only one side, and therefore—plurally and pedantically speaking—NO SIDES. Nor did it persuade the Priests, who, if they would insist they were truly Circles and not merely high-class Polygons with an infinitely large number of infinitesimally small sides, boasted (what Women confessed and deplored) that they also had no sides: they were blessed with a perimeter of one line, a Circumference. To both these Classes, the popular sophism had no hook in it. They could not be coaxed by talk of “distinct sides,” because their very pride—or their very stigma—lay in side-lessness.

Thus, when all others surrendered to the fascinings of corporal decoration, Women and Priests alone remained unpainted, “pure,” as the Priests called it—though others muttered “stubborn,” and some, more honest, whispered “afraid.”

The moralists of later centuries have taken care to spit upon that epoch. Immoral, licentious, anarchical, unscientific—such are the names that official histories pin to the Colour Revolt like labels on jars. Yet from an aesthetic point of view, those days were the glorious childhood of Art in Flatland: a childhood that never ripened into manhood, never even reached the blossom of youth.

To live then was itself a delight, because living implied seeing.

Domestic life, we are told, became an ever-renewed banquet for the eye. Even at a small party, company was not merely company but a composition: hues clustered like dishes on a table, each shape announcing itself without the rude necessity of contact. In churches and theatres, the richly varied assembly was said to have been so distracting that some of our greatest teachers and actors lost their thread—not because the doctrine or drama lacked force, but because the crowd itself shimmered, as if a thousand sauces had been set simmering at once, each giving off its own invisible steam of colour.

Most ravishing of all, Tradition insists, was the unspeakable magnificence of a military review.

Picture it as best you can in a world that now knows only brightness and shadow: a line of battle of twenty thousand Isosceles suddenly facing about, and exchanging the sombre black of their bases for the orange and purple of the two sides enclosing their acute angle—like a field of knives that, with one turn of the wrist, becomes a field of blossoms. The militia of the Equilateral Triangles were tricoloured in red, white, and blue, as though patriotism itself had been distilled into dyes. The Square artillerymen—mauve, ultramarine, gamboge, burnt umber—rotated rapidly near their vermilion guns, colour and motion together making a kind of visible percussion. And the five-coloured and six-coloured Pentagons and Hexagons flashed and dashed across the field in their offices of surgeons, geometricians, aides-de-camp, their very duties made legible by their painted sides.

All this, so the story runs, so overcame an illustrious Circle—commander and sovereign—that he threw aside marshal’s baton and royal crown, crying out that he would exchange them for the artist’s pencil. Whether the anecdote is fact or fable scarcely matters; it is credible in the way a hunger-dream is credible. We know what it means for a sense long starved to suddenly be fed.

How great and glorious that sensuous development must have been is hinted at by the very language of the period. The common speech of common citizens during the Colour Revolt is said to have carried more savour—words and thoughts suffused, as if every phrase had been steeped in a richer tincture. From that era we still inherit our finest poetry, and whatever rhythm remains in the more scientific utterance of these modern days: a few lingering syllables like the last herbs clinging to a pot after the feast has been cleared away, reminding us—faintly, stubbornly—that once, in Flatland, life had more than one flavour.

Meanwhile, as Colour spread through Flatland like a dye that would not rinse out, the old intellectual crafts spoiled in their jars.

At first the change seemed harmless, even convenient—like the first time a household abandons the slow, patient stone-grinding of grain because a noisy new mill promises flour in an hour. The Art of Sight Recognition, once tended as carefully as a starter passed down through stern, circular families, was declared unnecessary. Why train the mind to read the minute taper of an angle, or the subtle shortening of a line, when a glance at a painted surface could decide a stranger’s rank as easily as a market-stall sign? So the practice thinned. The University’s halls, once hushed for Geometry and bright with the clean click of reasoning in Statics and Kinetics, grew slack and loud with easy answers. Even the inferior Art of Feeling—taught to children as one teaches them to test ripeness, to weigh, to compare—was soon treated as a clumsy relic. In the Elementary Schools it faded too, dismissed as if it were mere peasant fussing.

With the old disciplines neglected, the Isosceles classes began to swell in temper as well as in number. They had long been held in check by an ugly but effective custom: the Criminal classes, under compulsion, supplied “Specimens” for Education, paying a tribute in bodies that both tamed their brutal nature and thinned their excessive multitude. But now the Isosceles loudly proclaimed that Specimens were neither used nor needed, and refused the customary payment. Their insolence grew on the strength of their immunity, like weeds thickening when the field is no longer weeded.

Year by year the Soldiers and Artisans grew bolder. They asserted—at first with bravado, and then with increasing truth—that there was little difference between them and the highest Polygons, now that Colour had raised them to an equality of outward recognition. Where once a difficult problem in life, whether Statical or Kinetical, demanded training and long discipline, now it could be “solved” by the simple process of Colour Recognition, as if all the hard labour of cookery could be replaced by looking at a sauce and declaring it ready because it had turned the right shade.

Not content that Sight Recognition was merely falling into natural neglect, the leaders of the movement began to demand the legal prohibition of what they called “monopolizing and aristocratic Arts.” They clamoured for the abolition of all endowments devoted to Sight Recognition, Mathematics, and Feeling. And then—because Colour had become, as they said, a second Nature that erased old distinctions—they insisted the Law must follow suit: henceforth all individuals and classes were to be recognized as absolutely equal, entitled to equal rights.

The higher Orders wavered. In that hesitation the Revolution advanced its knife deeper, tasting the weakness, and sharpening its appetite. At last came the demand that all classes alike—Priests and Women not excepted—should do homage to Colour by submitting to be painted.

When it was objected that Priests and Women had no sides, the answer came slickly, as if prepared long before: Nature and Expediency, they said, agreed that the front half of every human being—the half containing eye and mouth—must be distinguishable from the hinder half. So, before a general and extraordinary Assembly of all the States of Flatland, they brought in a Bill: every Woman was to be coloured red on the half that held eye and mouth, and green on the other half. Priests were to be painted the same—red on that semicircle in which eye and mouth formed the middle point, green upon the hinder semicircle.

There was cunning in this recipe. It did not originate from any mere Isosceles—no creature so degraded had angularity enough to appreciate, much less devise, such state-craft—but from an Irregular Circle, one of those mistakes that should have been corrected in childhood, yet was preserved by foolish indulgence until it could sour a whole nation. Like a tainted batch of preserves kept out of misplaced sentiment, it did not remain one jar’s misfortune: it spread, and brought desolation on the country and destruction on myriads of followers.

One purpose of the Bill was to bring Women, in all classes, over to the Chromatic Innovation. Give them the same two colours as the Priests, and you offered them a delicious possibility: that in certain positions every Woman would appear like a Priest, and be treated with respect and deference. The Female Sex could not be expected to refuse such seasoning.

If you do not at once perceive how a Woman, thus painted, could resemble a Priest, consider the matter plainly, with the eye of one who has watched sauces separate when the pan is jostled and knows that a slight change of angle alters everything.

Imagine a Woman duly decorated by the new Code: her front half—where eye and mouth are—coloured red; her hinder half green. Look at her from one side. What meets you is not a rounded figure, not a complex proof, but a single straight presentation: a line, half red and half green.

Now imagine a Priest. His mouth lies at a point on his circumference, and his front semicircle—bounded by the diameter that passes through the mouth and its opposite—is coloured red; his hinder semicircle green. If you contemplate him so that your eye lies in the same straight line as his dividing diameter, you will again see a straight line, and it too will appear half red and half green. The line may be somewhat shorter than that of a full-sized Woman, and the shading towards the extremities may differ; but in an age when Sight Recognition was decaying, colour would strike first, and the mind—untrained, impatient, dulled by the constant hiss of easy signals—would accept identity of hue as identity of class and neglect the finer signs.

Remember also how swiftly Women learn arts that profit them. They would not take long to soften and shade their extremities so as to imitate the Circles, just as an ambitious cook learns to pass a rough broth through finer cloth until it seems clear as consomme. Under such conditions, the Universal Colour Bill placed Flatland under a grave danger: to confound a Priest with a young Woman.

How attractive that prospect must have been to the Frail Sex you can readily imagine. They anticipated, with an almost culinary relish, the confusion that would follow. At home, they might hear political and ecclesiastical secrets intended for husbands and brothers, and might even issue commands in the name of a priestly Circle. Out of doors, the stark pairing of red and green—without any other colours to complicate the dish—would be sure to lead the common people into endless mistakes. The Women would gain whatever the Circles lost in the deference of passers-by.

As for the scandal that would fall upon the Circular Class if frivolous and unseemly conduct were imputed to them—if the noise of the streets, already harsh with the shouting of factions, began to carry the Circles’ once-quiet name as a cheap jingle for mockery—and as to the consequent weakening of the Constitution, the Female Sex could not be expected to spend a thought on such considerations. Even within the households of the Circles, the Women were all in favour of the Universal Colour Bill.

But the Bill had a second object, subtler and more corrosive: the gradual demoralization of the Circles themselves.

In the general intellectual decay, the Circles still preserved a clear and strong understanding. From earliest childhood, in Circular homes familiar with the total absence of Colour, the Nobles alone kept the Sacred Art of Sight Recognition alive. It was their domestic routine, their daily bread—kneaded in silence, proved in patience. From it flowed the advantages of that admirable training of the intellect. Up to the moment the Universal Colour Bill was introduced, the Circles had not only held their own but increased their lead by abstaining from the popular fashion, keeping their houses colourless as a sanctuary keeps its air untainted by the clang of the market.

Now, therefore, the artful Irregular—author of this diabolical measure—resolved to strike in a single blow: to lower the Hierarchy by forcing it to submit to the pollution of Colour, and at the same time to destroy the Circles’ domestic opportunities for training in Sight Recognition, enfeebling their minds by depriving them of pure and colourless homes. Once subjected to the chromatic taint, every parental and every childish Circle would begin to corrupt the other. Only in discerning between Father and Mother would the Circular infant find problems for the exercise of its understanding—and those problems would too often be corrupted by maternal impostures, shaking the child’s faith in all logical conclusions, as a kitchen loses confidence in its measures when a dishonest hand keeps changing the weights.

Thus, by degrees, the intellectual lustre of the Priestly Order would wane. And when that dimming had advanced far enough—when the old quiet arts had been drowned beneath the bright, commercial racket of painted certainty—the road would lie open for the total destruction of Aristocratic Legislature and the subversion of our Privileged Classes.

For three years the talk of the Universal Colour Bill ran through Flatland like a sauce left too long on the fire—first a pleasant fragrance, then a sharp, acrid tang that stung the nostrils and made even the calmest households snap. In those days the markets still rang with honest sounds: the soft rasp of angle on angle as goods were tested, the low hum of bargaining, the measured tap of respectable feet. But over it all came a new clatter—slogans, shouted syllables, the harsh percussion of crowds that did not know how to listen. The city’s acoustic ecology soured. Where once one could distinguish the clean, thin chime of a Gentleman’s approach from the broader, grainier tread of an Artisan, everything was drowned beneath the same industrial roar of agitation.

And still the sedition swelled.

A whole army of Polygons went out—private soldiers, not conscripts—marching with their edges freshly honed, their pride burnished like a pan before a festival feast. They were annihilated by a superior force of Isosceles Triangles. The Squares and Pentagons, who might have steadied the broth, stayed neutral, as if the pot were not theirs and the fire could not reach them.

Worse than the battlefield was the kitchen-table war.

Political animosity crept into noble homes the way smoke creeps under a door: at first a faint bitterness on the tongue, then a sting in the eyes, then the panic of choking. In many a household of Circles, wives—stirred into fury by the endless arguments—wearied their lords with prayers to abandon opposition to the Colour Bill. Some, finding their entreaties fruitless, did not merely slam doors or turn silent; they fell upon their own innocent children and husband, slaughtering them, perishing themselves in the act of carnage. It is recorded—coldly, as if a ledger could hold such heat—that in that triennial agitation no fewer than twenty-three Circles perished in domestic discord. Flatland, which liked to believe itself governed by measure and tradition, tasted then the metallic bite of fear.

It seemed the Priests had no choice between submission and extermination—until the course of events was wrenched aside by one of those picturesque incidents that Statesmen ought never to neglect, often to anticipate, and sometimes perhaps to originate, because of the absurdly disproportionate power with which they seize the sympathies of the populace.

A low-type Isosceles—his brain little if at all above four degrees—had plundered a Tradesman’s shop. Among the stolen goods were colours, kept like rare spices in stoppered vessels, meant for controlled use, not for street-corner experiments. The Isosceles, dabbling as a child does in forbidden jars, painted himself—or caused himself to be painted, for the story varies—with the twelve colours of a Dodecagon. It was a grotesque cookery of appearance: pigments smeared in layers without patience, without understanding of how one hue blooms only when another is tempered, how too much brilliance curdles into gaudiness. Yet to the untrained eye, it promised nobility.

Thus dressed in stolen splendour, he went into the Market Place, where the air was thick with the scent of ground meal and heated oil, and the soundscape was crowded with calls and countercalls. There he accosted, in a feigned voice, a maiden—the orphan daughter of a noble Polygon—whose affection in former days he had sought in vain. By a series of deceptions—helped, on one side, by a string of lucky accidents too long to relate, and on the other, by an almost inconceivable fatuity and neglect of ordinary precautions on the part of the bride’s relations—he succeeded in consummating the marriage.

When the unhappy girl discovered the fraud—when the false “seasoning” of colour was revealed as nothing but a mask over base angles—she committed suicide.

News in Flatland does not travel only by proclamation; it travels by the small domestic transmissions that keep a culture alive: the murmured story while kneading, the warning spoken over a simmering pot, the name repeated as a charm at bedtime. This catastrophe moved from State to State until it became not merely an incident but a folk-terror, a new taste on every tongue. The minds of the Women were violently agitated. Sympathy with the miserable victim, and anticipations of similar deceptions for themselves, their sisters, and their daughters, made them regard the Colour Bill in an entirely new aspect. Not a few openly avowed themselves converted to antagonism; the rest needed only a slight stimulus to make the same avowal.

The Circles seized the moment as an expert cook seizes the second when a sauce breaks—quickly, decisively, before it can be saved by the wrong hand. They convened an extraordinary Assembly of the States, and besides the usual guard of Convicts, they secured the attendance of a large number of reactionary Women. The Hall filled until the very air seemed compressed, buzzing with friction, the floor thrumming under countless small impacts. Amidst an unprecedented concourse, the Chief Circle of those days—Pantocyclus—arose, and was hissed and hooted by a hundred and twenty thousand Isosceles. Their noise was not the living complexity of a healthy city; it was the dead, flattening racket of a mob, a single note hammered again and again.

Pantocyclus secured silence by declaring that henceforth the Circles would enter on a policy of Concession: yielding to the wishes of the majority, they would accept the Colour Bill.

As if the Hall were a single animal, the uproar flipped—hissing turned to applause. In that abrupt reversal one could hear how easily a populace is conducted by sound, how the dominant lexicon of “reform” and “innovation” can be made to mean whatever the speaker chooses, provided the cadence is right. Pantocyclus invited Chromatistes, the leader of the Sedition, into the centre of the Hall to receive, in the name of his followers, the submission of the Hierarchy.

Then followed a speech—one of those day-long masterworks that leaves the listener exhausted, as after a feast of too many courses, each seasoned to distract from what is being swallowed.

With a grave appearance of impartiality, Pantocyclus declared that since they were now finally committing themselves to Reform or Innovation, it was desirable to take one last view of the perimeter of the whole subject—its defects as well as its advantages. He spoke gradually, letting certain phrases linger like aromas: dangers to Tradesmen; dangers to Professional Classes; dangers to Gentlemen. When murmurs rose among the Isosceles, he cooled them by reminding the Assembly that, in spite of these defects, he was still willing to accept the Bill if the majority approved.

But it was manifest that all, except the Isosceles, were moved by his words and were either neutral or averse to the Bill.

Turning then to the Workmen, he asserted that their interests must not be neglected, and that if they intended to accept the Colour Bill, they ought at least to do so with full view of the consequences. Many, he said, stood on the point of being admitted to the class of the Regular Triangles; others cherished for their children a distinction they could not hope for themselves. That honourable ambition—the slow-fermented hope of families who measure their lives in generations—would now have to be sacrificed.

With the universal adoption of Colour, he warned, all distinctions would cease. Regularity would be confused with Irregularity. Development would give place to retrogression. The Workman would, in a few generations, be degraded to the level of the Military, or even the Convict Class. Political power would fall into the hands of the greatest number—that is to say, the Criminal Classes, already more numerous than the Workmen, soon to out-number all other Classes put together when the usual Compensative Laws of Nature were violated.

A subdued murmur of assent ran through the ranks of the Artisans. The sound was telling: not a cheer, but the low, reluctant rumble of men imagining their children’s future thinning out, like watered broth. Chromatistes, alarmed, attempted to step forward and address them—but found himself encompassed with guards and forced to remain silent.

Pantocyclus, in a few impassioned words, made a final appeal to the Women. If the Colour Bill passed, he cried, no marriage would henceforth be safe, no woman’s honour secure; fraud, deception, hypocrisy would pervade every household; domestic bliss would share the fate of the Constitution and pass to speedy perdition. “Sooner than this,” he cried, “Come death.”

Those words were the preconcerted signal.

The Isosceles Convicts fell upon the wretched Chromatistes and transfixed him. The Regular Classes opened their ranks, making way for a band of Women who, under direction of the Circles, moved back foremost— invisibly and unerringly—upon the unconscious soldiers. The Artisans, imitating the example of their betters, also opened their ranks. Meanwhile bands of Convicts occupied every entrance with an impenetrable phalanx.

The battle—rather, the carnage—was short. Under the skilful generalship of the Circles, almost every Woman’s charge was fatal, and very many extracted their sting uninjured, ready for a second slaughter. But no second blow was needed. The rabble of the Isosceles did the rest of the business for themselves.

Surprised, leader-less, attacked in front by foes they could not properly perceive, and finding egress cut off by Convicts behind them, they at once—after their manner—lost all presence of mind and raised the cry of “treachery.” In that one word lay the tragedy of language: a single term, blunt and easy to fling, becoming a weapon sharper than any angle. It sealed their fate. Every Isosceles now saw and felt a foe in every other. The Hall’s soundscape turned feral—scraping, snapping, the awful percussion of panic ricocheting off itself. In half an hour not one of that vast multitude was living; and the fragments of seven score thousand of the Criminal Class, slain by one another’s angles, attested the triumph of Order.

The Circles delayed not to push their victory to the uttermost. The Working Men they spared—but decimated. The Militia of the Equilaterals was at once called out; and every Triangle suspected of Irregularity on reasonable grounds was destroyed by Court Martial, without the formality of exact measurement by the Social Board.

Then came the long after-work of victory: not the ringing drama of the Hall, but the quiet, systematic, domestic invasion. The homes of the Military and Artisan classes were inspected in visitations extending through upwards of a year; and during that period every town, village, and hamlet was purged of that excess of the lower orders brought about by neglect to pay the tribute of Criminals to the Schools and University, and by the violation of the other natural Laws of the Constitution of Flatland. Thus the balance of classes was again restored, as if society were a pantry rearranged after a scare—labels facing outward, forbidden jars moved to the highest shelf, the memory of hunger used to justify any cruelty.

Needless to say, henceforth the use of Colour was abolished and its possession prohibited. Even the utterance of any word denoting Colour—except by the Circles or by qualified scientific teachers—was punished by a severe penalty. A lexicon was amputated. A whole range of private sensation became unspeakable, and what cannot be spoken cannot be easily remembered; it slips away from family story and kitchen-song, leaving only a gap where meaning used to be, and a fear of naming what the tongue still secretly knows.

Only at our University, in some of the very highest and most esoteric classes—which I have never been privileged to attend—it is understood that the sparing use of Colour is still sanctioned for illustrating certain deeper problems of mathematics. Of this I can speak only from hearsay.

Elsewhere in Flatland, Colour is now non-existent. The art of making it is known to only one living person, the Chief Circle for the time being; and by him it is handed down on his death-bed to none but his Successor. One manufactory alone produces it; and lest the secret should be betrayed, the Workmen are annually consumed and fresh ones introduced.

So great is the terror with which even now our Aristocracy looks back to those far-distant days of the agitation for the Universal Colour Bill—days when a stolen pigment, like an illicit spice, nearly altered the flavour of the whole world, and when the very words for seeing were hunted from the mouth as if they carried contagion.

It is time—long overdue—that I set down my pen from these wandering household notes of Flatland and turn at last toward the hinge of my life: my initiation into the mysteries of Space. That is the true burden of this tale. All else has been no more than the warming of the oven, the laying out of the knives, the rinsing of the herbs before the real cooking begins.

And yet, before I bring you to that strange banquet, I must deliberately leave whole platters untouched. I could linger—oh, I could linger gladly—over the practical arts by which we glide and halt though we have no feet; the craft by which we make walls stand true, of wood or stone or brick, though we have no hands to stack them, no deep foundations to bite into, no friendly weight of earth to press from the side and hold them steady as yours can. I could tell how rain is born in the pauses between our zones so that the North does not drink all the clouds before the South can taste them; I could speak of our hills and mines, our trees and vegetables, our seasons and harvests—how the year sounds when it turns, what the fields whisper when the grain is ready, how the wind changes its pitch over different soils. I could unroll our Alphabet and the way we write upon long, narrow tablets, our letters shaped to a world of edges and lines.

All these things I pass over—not because I have forgotten them, but because I respect the Reader’s time, as a careful cook respects the hunger of his guests and does not drown the table in endless side-dishes when the main course is already steaming.

Still, a few final remarks are due on the great pillars of our Constitution: those who direct our conduct and knead our destinies into shape; those whom all salute and nearly adore. I mean, of course, our Circles—whom I will call Priests.

Do not mistake my word “Priest” for your own thin meaning of it. In Flatland the Priests are the administrators of all business, art, and science: directors of trade and commerce, of generalship and architecture, of engineering and education, of statesmanship and legislature, of morality and theology. They do nothing with their own sides, and yet they are said to be the cause of everything worth doing that is done by others. Their authority is like a great hush that settles over a room when the master of the house steps in: even the smallest scrape of a chair becomes aware of itself. In their presence the soundscape of society changes—voices smooth themselves, movements soften, and a kind of cultivated quiet, almost ceremonial, spreads outward, while the rougher noises of common life are pushed away as if they were vulgar smoke.

Among the populace, any Circle is deemed a Circle. Among the educated, it is known—though rarely spoken aloud—that no Circle is truly a Circle. He is a Polygon with an enormous number of minute sides. As the sides increase, the Polygon approaches the Circle; and when the sides are very many indeed—three or four hundred, for instance—it becomes exceedingly hard for even the most delicate touch to discover an angle.

Let me correct myself. It would be hard—if one were permitted to touch.

But, as I have already hinted, Recognition by Feeling is unknown in the highest society. To feel a Circle would be taken not as curiosity but as insolence: an assault upon the sacred boundary of rank. A Circle’s very safety, his myth, depends upon this trained abstinence. Polite breeding teaches you not only what to say, but what sensations you must pretend not to possess. Thus the Circle, from his earliest years, learns to wrap his true perimeter in mystery. The less anyone is allowed to verify, the more perfect the legend becomes. It is a kind of language, too—an official dialect of reverence in which questions cannot be formed.

For an average perimeter of three feet, a Polygon of three hundred sides has each side no longer than the hundredth part of a foot—barely more than a tenth of an inch. Increase the count to six or seven hundred, and each side is scarcely larger than the diameter of a pin-head in Spaceland. Courtesy, which in Flatland is a law stronger than measurement, assumes that the Chief Circle of any age has ten thousand sides. No one has counted. No one dares to ask what “ten thousand” truly means under the fingers. The number is recited the way an ancient recipe is recited in a dead tongue: not to be tested, only to be honored.

The rise of the Circles’ posterity is not chained, as it is among the lower Regular classes, by the Law of Nature that adds only one side with each generation. If that were so, the nobility of a Circle would become merely a matter of pedigree and arithmetic: the four hundred and ninety-seventh descendant of an Equilateral Triangle would be, by necessity, a five-hundred-sided Polygon, no more and no less. Our rulers would be the product of counting—cold, mechanical, and verifiable.

But Nature has not granted us that clarity. She issues two opposing decrees upon circular propagation. First: the higher a race climbs, the faster development proceeds—growth accelerates as though some hidden fire were stoked hotter with each step upward. Second: in the same proportion, fertility declines. In the home of a Polygon of four or five hundred sides, it is rare to find a son; more than one is never seen. The house is quiet—not with the contented quiet of a well-fed family after supper, but with the brittle quiet of rooms too large for the voices in them, of lineage pressed thin as a wafer.

Yet the single child, when he comes, may arrive with a startling surplus of sides. The son of a five-hundred-sided Polygon has been known to possess five hundred and fifty—sometimes six hundred. A whole ancestry seems to be swallowed in one generation, as if time itself were folded and tucked away, and the family leapt forward in a single stride.

Nor is Nature left to work alone. Art—our craft, our learned violence—steps in to speed the higher Evolution. Our physicians have discovered that the small and tender sides of an infant Polygon of the superior class can be fractured and the whole frame re-set with such precision that a Polygon of two or three hundred sides sometimes—by no means always, for the process is attended with terrible risk—overleaps two or three hundred generations at a stroke. It is as though a cook, impatient for a slow stock to deepen, were to raise the flame too high: sometimes the broth clarifies into brilliance, and sometimes it scorches and is ruined beyond saving.

Many a promising child is sacrificed in this way. Scarcely one in ten survives.

And yet parental ambition among those Polygons who stand on the very fringe of the Circular class burns hotter than caution. It is rare to find a nobleman of that precarious rank who has not placed his first-born in the Circular Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium before the infant has reached a month of age. The decision is made in the nursery, beside the soft domestic sounds of early life—hushed consultations, the faint rasp of a nurse’s movement, the thin cry of the child—while outside, society hums with its own expectations like an endless, approving drone. One year determines success or failure.

At the end of that year, the child has, in all likelihood, added one more stone to the crowded Neo-Therapeutic Cemetery.

But on rare occasions—so rare that one may count them like sacred feasts in a long famine—a glad procession bears the little one home to his exultant parents. He is no longer a Polygon, but a Circle, at least by courtesy: his angles subdued into an almost unbroken curve; his family name suddenly pronounced with a new emphasis, as if the very syllables had been promoted. And that single blessed instance, spoken of in careful tones and repeated until it becomes a kind of lullaby for aspiring households, is enough to draw multitudes of Polygonal parents into the same domestic sacrifice—though the issue, more often than not, is grievously different.

The Priests of our land—those perfect Circles whose very edges seem to hum with authority—compress their whole doctrine into a maxim so often repeated that it falls, like a refrain, into the rhythm of daily life: Attend to your Configuration.

It is spoken in the Courts, murmured in the schools, and carried even into the kitchen-quarters where the day’s bread is squared and portioned. Not a stirring of the State’s great ladle, not a rule of household discipline, not a public hymn chanted to the measured cadence of official drums, but is seasoned with this one idea: that the shape of a man is the root of his conduct; that morality itself is no more than geometry made visible.

The Circles boast—justly, if one cares only for order—that they have quenched the old heresies which once led our ancestors to waste sympathy upon the foolish notion that a Figure might, by mere will, become better. Effort, encouragement, praise, training—these, they say, are airy spices that scent the room yet do not feed. What nourishes, what strengthens, what cures, is Configuration.

Pantocyclus, that illustrious Circle who stamped out the Colour Revolt, was the first to persuade the multitude that Configuration makes the man. His argument was as plain as salt. If you are born an Isosceles with two sides uneven, you will go wrong—inevitably—unless those sides are made even. For that, you must be taken, as one takes bruised fruit to the preserving-pan, to the Isosceles Hospital. If you are a Triangle, Square, or any Polygon whatever, and your outline bears the least Irregularity, you must be carried to one of the Regular Hospitals to have the disease corrected; otherwise, sooner or later, you will be delivered—not as a patient but as a culprit—to the State Prison, or to the angle of the State Executioner.

Pantocyclus laid every fault at the door of bodily deviation from perfect Regularity. A crime might be congenital, of course; but it might also be accidental—born of a collision in a crowd where the air is thick with the rasp of edges and the mind is made frantic by perpetual scraping; or caused by neglect of proper exercise, or by excess of it; or even by a sudden change of temperature, shrinking one part of the frame, swelling another, as a badly stored loaf cracks on one side and domes on the other. Therefore, he concluded—cool as a steward weighing rations—that neither good conduct nor bad conduct is a fit subject for praise or blame. Why praise the integrity of a Square who defends his client faithfully, when what you truly admire is the precision of his right angles? Why blame a lying, thievish Isosceles, when what you ought rather to lament is the incurable inequality of his sides?

In theory, nothing could be neater. In practice, it sticks sometimes in the throat.

In the Courts the doctrine bites cleanly enough. If an Isosceles rogue pleads that he cannot help stealing because his sides are uneven, the Magistrate answers with equal simplicity: for that very reason—because you cannot help being a nuisance to your neighbours—I cannot help sentencing you to be consumed. The law falls like a cleaver; the matter is ended.

But in the small fermenting troubles of domestic life—where one cannot clap a child into Prison, nor send him to Consumption as one sends refuse to the furnace—the doctrine can turn awkward, like a sauce that will not bind. I have had it pressed upon me by one of my own Hexagonal grandsons. The boy, with all the innocence of appetite and all the cunning that appetite teaches, has pleaded disobedience as if it were a mere mishap of climate: a sudden change of temperature, he says, has been too much for his Perimeter; the blame lies not in him but in his Configuration; and, since Configuration is strengthened only by appropriate sustenance, the remedy is clear—abundance of the choicest sweetmeats.

Now there is a kind of logic in this that I cannot wholly reject; and yet there is a kind of household sense in me that cannot accept it. So I do what many do, though we confess it to ourselves with a faint taste of hypocrisy: I assume that a good, sound scolding—or, when necessary, castigation—possesses some latent strengthening influence upon a boy’s Configuration. I own I have no proof. It is like adding a pungent herb because one’s mother did so: one cannot demonstrate its virtue, yet the dish seems wrong without it.

Nor am I alone in this private evasion. I have observed that many of the highest Circles, sitting in judgment, use praise and blame freely toward Regular and Irregular Figures alike. And in their own chambers—behind the hush-curtains that soften the harsh hiss of the outer thoroughfares—I have heard them rebuke their children with as much passion about “right” and “wrong” as if those words were not mere instruments but real things that could be chosen, tasted, and refused.

Indeed, the Circles carry their policy so persistently into every mind that they reverse, in our land, the old commandment you have in Spaceland. With you, children are taught to honour their parents. With us—next to the Circles, who are the object of universal homage—a man is taught to honour his Grandson, if he has one; or, failing that, his Son. By honour is not meant indulgence, as if one were spooning honey into a mouth already sticky with it, but a reverent regard for the young one’s highest interests. The father must subordinate himself to posterity: it is preached as duty, and translated among the initiated as necessity and fitness. Thus the whole State advances, and one’s own line, if kept clean and Regular, climbs by degrees.

Yet if a humble Square may be permitted to say that anything Circular contains an element of weakness, the weak point appears to me in their relations with Women.

Since it is of the utmost importance that Irregular births should be discouraged, it follows that no Woman with Irregularities in her ancestry is a fit partner for one who desires his posterity to rise. The Irregularity of a Male can be measured; one can run the mind’s rule along his edges and detect the fault as readily as a cook detects grit between the teeth. But Women are straight—straight as a skewer—so that they are, visibly, “Regular.” Their Irregularity is invisible: not a present flaw but a potential one, concealed like a bitter seed in sweet fruit, ready to declare itself in offspring.

Therefore the State has devised another test: pedigrees, kept with a meticulousness that would shame the most careful keeper of recipes. These genealogies are preserved and supervised; and without a certified pedigree no Woman may marry. One might suppose that a Circle—proud of ancestry, mindful of descendants that might one day culminate in a Chief Circle—would be the most scrupulous in his choice. But it is not so.

The higher the rank, the more careless the choosing. An aspiring Isosceles, hoping to generate an Equilateral Son, would no more take a wife with a single ancestral blot than a baker would mix clean flour with weevil-dust. A Square or Pentagon, confident that his family is steadily rising, does not inquire above the five-hundredth generation. A Hexagon or Dodecagon is more negligent still. But a Circle has been known deliberately to take a wife whose Great-Grandfather was Irregular—tempted by some slight superiority of lustre, or by the charm of a low voice, which with us, even more than with you, is accounted “an excellent thing in Woman.”

Such marriages are, as might be expected, barren; or they produce positive Irregularity; or there is diminution of sides. But none of these evils has yet been sufficiently deterrent. The loss of a few sides in a highly developed Polygon is not easily noticed—like a pinch of salt forgotten in an over-spiced stew—and is sometimes compensated by a successful operation in the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium. And the Circles, too ready to make a virtue of their own languor, are disposed to acquiesce in infecundity as a law of superior development. Yet if this evil is not checked, the diminution of the Circular class may accelerate; and the day may not be far distant when, the race no longer able to produce a Chief Circle, the Constitution of Flatland must fall.

One other warning rises to my mind—harder to speak, harder still to remedy—and it too concerns Women.

About three hundred years ago, it was decreed by the Chief Circle that, since Women are deficient in Reason but abundant in Emotion, they ought no longer to be treated as rational, nor receive mental education. The consequence followed with the slow, steady certainty of dough left in a cold room: they were no longer taught to read, nor even enough Arithmetic to count the angles of husband or child; and so, with each generation, they declined sensibly in intellectual power. This quietism still prevails.

My fear is that, however well meant, the policy has been carried so far that it injures the Male sex also. For we Males must now live, as it were, a bi-lingual—almost a bi-mental—existence. In Women’s chambers we speak one dialect, thick with emotional terms: “love,” “duty,” “right,” “wrong,” “pity,” “hope”—names for conceptions which, in the strict tongue of science, have no existence, and whose fiction serves chiefly to rein in feminine exuberance. Among ourselves, and in our books, we use another vocabulary, another idiom entirely, pared down as carefully as a chef trims fat from a joint.

“Love” becomes “the anticipation of benefits.”
“Duty” becomes “necessity” or “fitness.”
And so on, word after word transmuted, as if the same ingredient were made to serve two different cuisines.

Moreover, before Women we speak with language implying the utmost deference to their sex; and they fully believe themselves adored by us almost as devoutly as the Chief Circle is adored. But behind their backs they are regarded—and named, by all except the very young—as little better than mindless organisms. Even Theology changes with the doorway: in the Women’s chambers, one creed; elsewhere, another.

This double training—of language, and therefore of thought—lays, I suspect, too heavy a burden on the young. At the age of three they are taken from maternal care and taught to unlearn the old speech—except for repeating it dutifully before Mothers and Nurses—and to learn the vocabulary and idiom of science. Already, I think, I discern a weakening in the grasp of mathematical truth compared with the robust intellect of our ancestors three hundred years ago. It is as though the mind, forced to dine at two tables with two incompatible rules of taste, loses its appetite for plain certainty.

I say nothing of the danger—though it exists—that some Woman might surreptitiously learn to read and convey to her sex the fruit of a single popular volume; nor of the chance that some infant Male, through indiscretion, might reveal to a Mother the secrets of the logical dialect. I rest my humble appeal on a simpler ground: the enfeebling of the Male intellect. For that reason alone I would entreat the highest Authorities to reconsider the regulations of Female education.

“O brave new worlds, that have such people in them!”

It was the last night but one of the year 1999, and the Long Vacation had just opened its wide, idle arms. I had spent the evening in my old indulgence—Geometry—turning a stubborn problem over as a cook turns a bone in stock, searching for the one angle that will make the whole pot speak. I went to bed with the thing still unsolved, the mind still chewing. Sleep came at last, but it brought no silence.

In my dream I found myself listening before I knew what I was seeing.

A thin world lay stretched out—one hard, continuous track, like a string drawn tight across an empty kitchen. Upon it moved a multitude: small Straight Lines, and among them still smaller Beings like bright pin-pricks of light—lustrous Points—sliding back and forth along that single course, and, as nearly as I could judge, all with the same even haste. Their motion made a peculiar music, not music as we name it, but a confused, multitudinous chirping and twittering that rose whenever they moved and fell away whenever they paused. The alternation was like a busy market that suddenly remembers a curfew: sound—then a struck hush; sound again—then the whole place holding its breath.

The soundscape unsettled me more than the sight. In Flatland, noise has a place: the clatter of domestic life, the polite tones of conversation, the ceremonial cadences of public speech. But this was an ecology of sound tied to motion alone, as if the world could only vocalize while in transit, and fell dead the moment it stopped.

Taking one of the larger Lines for a Woman—my Flatland habits leaping too quickly to their old classifications—I approached and addressed her. No answer. I tried again, and again; nothing but the background twitter swelling and thinning with the tide of their movement. My patience, never perfect when manners are breached, soured. I stepped close, brought my mouth directly before what I took to be her mouth—so close that I blocked her path—and I shouted my question into the only avenue I could find:

“Woman, what signifies this concourse, this strange confused chirping, and this monotonous motion to and fro in one and the same Straight Line?”

The Line halted. The surrounding world, as if obeying one nerve, quieted for a beat. Then the Line replied, crisp and affronted.

“I am no Woman. I am the Monarch of the world. But thou—whence intrudest thou into my realm of Lineland?”

Lineland. The word landed like a name for a dish I had never tasted—simple syllables, yet closed to the tongue that had not been raised on them.

Startled, I begged pardon with what civility I could gather, protesting I meant no molestation of his Royal Highness. I called myself a stranger and asked him to tell me of his dominions. It was like trying to consult a food-historian who insists every spice is common knowledge, and takes your ignorance for theatrical jest. Again and again the King assumed that what he knew must be the measure of all minds, and that I pretended not to know for sport.

Yet by careful questioning—by returning to the same points, as one returns to an old recipe to tease out what a missing line truly meant—I drew out the shape of his certainty.

This Monarch, poor in knowledge though rich in assurance, believed that the Straight Line he called his Kingdom, in which he passed his whole existence, constituted not only the whole of the world but the whole of Space. He could neither move nor see save along that Line; and because his senses offered him nothing else, he could not conceive of anything beyond it. Outside his World—his Line—there was, to him, not even emptiness. For emptiness implies room. Rather, there was non-existence.

When first my voice had addressed him, he told me, it reached him in a fashion so contrary to experience that he made no answer, “seeing no man,” as he phrased it, “and hearing a voice as it were from my own intestines.” Before I thrust my mouth into his world—into the Line itself—he had neither seen me nor heard anything distinct; only confused sounds beating against what I would have called his side, but what he called his INSIDE or STOMACH, as though all intrusion must, by definition, be digestion. Even now he could not so much as imagine the region from which I had come. To him, direction was not a compass but a single thread.

His subjects—he explained—were all alike confined in motion and eyesight to that single Straight Line which was their Universe. The small Lines were Men, and the Points were Women; and it need hardly be said that their whole horizon was limited to a Point. No one could ever see anything but a Point. Man, woman, child, thing—each was a Point to a Linelander’s eye. Only the sound of the voice distinguished sex or age.

Here was a culture whose memory must live on the tongue and in the ear, not in the glance. I thought of how, in our own world, the tiniest difference of angle carries rank, history, and law; how a name is enforced by shape. In Lineland, the body was stripped of readable form, and identity rode on vibration alone—on pitch, timbre, rhythm. If a dialect died here, a person would vanish with it.

And there was another consequence, domestic and inescapable. Because each individual occupied the whole narrow path of the Universe, and no one could step aside—no right or left existed to grant courtesy—no Linelander could ever pass another. Once neighbors, always neighbors. Their neighborhood was like marriage with us: not a matter of choice only, but a binding of circumstance until death did them part.

A life with vision reduced to a Point and movement to a single track seemed to me inexpressibly dreary—like being condemned to cook forever over one unchanging flame, never allowed to walk around the stove, never permitted to open a window for fresh air. Yet the King spoke with an astonishing vivacity, even cheerfulness, as though his world’s narrowness had not starved his spirit but trained it, like a vine forced to climb a single pole.

A question pressed on me—awkward, but unavoidable if one means to understand any society beyond its laws: how did they manage family? How did love and household arise in a universe where you cannot approach, cannot pass, cannot even see what you claim to cherish?

I hesitated, as one hesitates before asking after another’s pantry, for the subject seemed delicate. Then I plunged in, abruptly, by asking after the health of his family.

“My wives and children,” he replied, “are well and happy.”

I was staggered. For near the Monarch, as I had noted even in the first sweep of my dream, there were none but Men. I could see—if “see” is the word for perceiving points—that several individuals stood between him and any supposed women. I ventured, carefully:

“Pardon me, but I cannot imagine how your Royal Highness can at any time either see or approach their Majesties, when there are at least half a dozen intervening individuals whom you can neither see through, nor pass by. Is it possible that in Lineland proximity is not necessary for marriage and for the generation of children?”

“How can you ask so absurd a question?” he cried. “If it were indeed as you suggest, the Universe would soon be depopulated. No, no; neighbourhood is needless for the union of hearts; and the birth of children is too important a matter to have been allowed to depend upon such an accident as proximity. You cannot be ignorant of this. Yet since you are pleased to affect ignorance, I will instruct you as if you were the veriest baby in Lineland. Know, then, that marriages are consummated by means of the faculty of sound and the sense of hearing.”

He spoke as if reciting an old household ordinance, worn smooth by repetition, as familiar as the way an elder farmer speaks of sowing by the moon—less a belief than a calendar inside the bones.

“You are of course aware,” he continued, “that every Man has two mouths or voices—as well as two eyes—a bass at one and a tenor at the other of his extremities. I should not mention this, but that I have been unable to distinguish your tenor in the course of our conversation.”

I answered that I had but one voice, and that I had not known his Royal Highness had two.

“That confirms my impression,” said the King, with a cold little edge, “that you are not a Man, but a feminine Monstrosity with a bass voice, and an utterly uneducated ear. But to continue.

“Nature having herself ordained that every Man should wed two wives—”

“Why two?” I asked, before I could stop myself.

“You carry your affected simplicity too far,” he cried. “How can there be a completely harmonious union without the combination of the Four in One, viz. the Bass and Tenor of the Man and the Soprano and Contralto of the two Women?”

His certainty was tyrannical, not merely personal: it was the kind that makes custom into arithmetic and then calls the arithmetic the will of the Universe. I tried again, probing the seam.

“But supposing that a man should prefer one wife or three?”

“It is impossible,” he said. “It is as inconceivable as that two and one should make five, or that the human eye should see a Straight Line.”

I would have interrupted—my mind rising with objections like steam—but he hurried onward, eager to display the machinery of their life.

“Once in the middle of each week a Law of Nature compels us to move to and fro with a rhythmic motion of more than usual violence, which continues for the time you would take to count a hundred and one. In the midst of this choral dance, at the fifty-first pulsation, the inhabitants of the Universe pause in full career, and each individual sends forth his richest, fullest, sweetest strain. It is in this decisive moment that all our marriages are made.”

It was the last day of the Year 1999 of our Era. Rain had been sifting against the roof in a fine, incessant percussion—soft at first, then fuller, like a patient hand drumming on a lidded pot to keep it from boiling over. In Flatland, where walls hold sound differently and every household learns its own weather by ear, that rain was the old night’s bell. My wife remained with me to see the old Millennium out and the new one in. Our four sons, and my two orphan grandchildren, had long since withdrawn to their own apartments. Even in a world of lines and angles, the kitchen is where memory keeps its dialect; our family recipes are spoken less with words than with timing, heat, and the ear trained to the exact hiss that tells you when a pan is ready.

Yet my mind would not stay in the pantry of the present. I kept turning over a sentence that had dropped, quite casually, from the mouth of my youngest grandson—a most promising young Hexagon, brilliant, perfectly angled, and still raw with that hunger children have for meanings no elder has ever tasted.

Earlier in the evening his uncles and I had been giving him his practical lesson in Sight Recognition. We turned upon our centres—now quickly, now slow—so that he might learn to name our positions from the variations of lustre. To make the matter plain, I took nine Squares, each an inch every way, and set them together as one larger Square, three inches to a side. I told him that though we cannot SEE the inside of a Square—as a tongue cannot see the crumb within a loaf—yet we can know its content by rule: “Thus,” said I, “we know that 3 squared, or 9, is the number of square inches in a Square whose side is three inches long.”

“Nothing at all,” I replied, too quickly, for the question pricked me. “Not, at least, in Geometry; for Geometry has only Two Dimensions.”

I began at once, as teachers do when threatened by a child’s clean curiosity, to pile example upon example. A Point, moving through a length of three inches, makes a Line of three inches, represented by 3. A straight Line of three inches, moving parallel to itself through a length of three inches, makes a Square of three inches every way, represented by 3².

But he returned, stubborn and bright, to the taste of the thing. “Well then,” he exclaimed, “if a Point by moving three inches makes a Line of three inches represented by 3; and if a Line of three inches, moving parallel to itself, makes a Square of three inches every way represented by 3²; it must be that a Square of three inches every way, moving somehow parallel to itself (but I don’t see how), must make Something else (but I don’t see what) of three inches every way—and this must be represented by 3³.”

The audacity of it—his insistence that the rule ought to have a next verse—ruffled me like an unexpected draught at the table. “Go to bed,” I said. “If you would talk less nonsense, you would remember more sense.”

What you style Flatland is the vast level surface of what I may call a fluid, on, or in, the top of which you and your countrymen move about, without rising above it or falling below it. I am not a plane Figure, but a Solid. You call me a Circle; but in reality I am not a Circle, but an infinite number of Circles, of size varying from a Point to a Circle of thirteen inches in diameter, one placed on the top of the other. When I cut through your plane as I am now doing, I make in your plane a section which you, very rightly, call a Circle. For even a Sphere—which is my proper name in my own country—if he manifest himself at all to an inhabitant of Flatland, must needs manifest himself as a Circle.”

The words “fluid,” “cut,” “section” landed with an odd physicality, like talk of slicing a loaf. I pictured—against my will—a stack of round flatbreads, from smallest to largest, piled with perfect alignment. Yet the taste of the concept remained wrong, because there was no oven in my mind for such bread.

He pressed on. “Do you not remember—” and at that the air seemed to stir with the memory of the previous night’s dream, Lineland written like an afterimage upon my brain—“how, when you entered the realm of Lineland, you were compelled to manifest yourself to the King, not as a Square, but as a Line, because that Linear Realm had not Dimensions enough to represent the whole of you, but only a slice or section of you? In precisely the same way, your country of Two Dimensions is not spacious enough to represent me, a being of Three, but can only exhibit a slice or section of me—which is what you call a Circle.”

I did remember: the King’s thin tyranny, his cramped vocabulary, the way his language seemed built to keep experience small. And I felt, uneasily, the same power at work here but reversed—this Stranger’s grand lexicon pressing down upon my native terms until they bent. Even “Space,” our old honest word, was being annexed and repurposed, as if a conqueror had slapped his own labels on our pantry jars.

“The diminished brightness of your eye indicates incredulity,” he said. “But now prepare to receive proof positive. You cannot see more than one of my sections at a time, for you have no power to raise your eye out of the plane; but you can at least see that, as I rise in Space, so my sections become smaller. See now—I will rise.”

I saw no rising. No footstep, no drift. There was no accompanying creak of floor or sigh of displaced air—none of the honest sounds by which a body announces movement in a room. Yet the Circle before me shrank. Not by stepping away, not by fading like a lamp; he diminished as a coin might diminish in a tale told badly, becoming smaller and smaller until he was a mere bead of light, and then—nothing.

I blinked hard, once, twice, as if to clear steam from a cook’s spectacles. The room remained. The chair legs stood where they stood. The faint household noises continued, indifferent. But the Stranger was gone.

And then, from a nowhere that had no direction, a hollow voice emerged—so near it seemed to sound inside my ribs, close to the heart’s own percussion.

“Am I quite gone? Are you convinced now? Well, now I will gradually return to Flatland and you shall see my section become larger and larger.”

A Circle returned—first a point, then a small bright ring, swelling with steady certainty until he regained his former size. If sound is the soul of place, then his voice had committed a violence: it had entered my most private acoustic chamber without crossing any threshold. My skin crawled with it.

When he was full-sized again, he heaved a deep sigh, and in that sigh I heard the fatigue of a teacher whose pupil cannot taste what is being offered. My silence told him all.

To me, though I had witnessed the facts, the causes remained black as an unlit hearth. I had seen a Circle diminish, vanish, and return. But “upward,” “below,” “solid”—these were seasonings I could not dissolve into my understanding. And so, because the mind hates a vacuum, I began to fill the gap with older, homelier superstitions: perhaps he was no Circle at all, but a clever juggler; perhaps the nursery tales of enchanters and magicians, whispered over kneading boards and washbasins, were true after all.

After a long pause he muttered, as if bargaining with himself, “One resource alone remains, if I am not to resort to action. I must try the method of Analogy.”

Again he held silence for a long while, letting the room’s ordinary noises reassert their frail authority. Then he spoke.

“Tell me, Mr. Mathematician; if a Point moves Northward and leaves a luminous wake, what name would you give to the wake?”

“A straight Line,” I answered at once—thankful, absurdly, for the familiar.

“And a straight Line has how many extremities?”

“Two.”

“Now conceive the Northward straight Line moving parallel to itself, East and West, so that every point in it leaves behind it the wake of a straight Line. Suppose it moves through a distance equal to the original straight Line. What name will you give to the Figure thereby formed?”

“A Square,” I said, with the relief of one who has found again the taste of bread he knows.

“And how many sides has a Square? How many angles?”

“Four sides and four angles.”

“Now stretch your imagination a little,” he said, and the phrase itself felt like being asked to stretch dough beyond its strength, “and conceive a Square in Flatland moving parallel to itself upward.”

“What? Northward?”

“No, not Northward; upward—out of Flatland altogether. If it moved Northward, the Southern points in the Square would have to move through positions previously occupied by the Northern points. But that is not my meaning. I mean that every Point in you—for you are a Square and will serve my illustration—every Point in you, that is to say in what you call your inside, is to pass upwards through Space in such a way that no Point shall pass through the position previously occupied by any other Point; but each Point shall describe a straight Line of its own. This is all in accordance with Analogy; surely it must be clear to you.”

My patience thinned. It is one thing to be instructed; it is another to be told that the failure of comprehension is a moral fault. I restrained the urge—sudden, violent—to rush at him and fling him out of my house, out of my world, out of the very vocabulary by which he bullied my senses.

“And what may be the nature of the Figure,” I asked, “which I am to shape out by this motion you denote by ‘upward’? I presume it is describable in the language of Flatland.”

“Oh, certainly,” he said. “It is all plain and simple, and in strict accordance with Analogy—only you must not speak of the result as a Figure, but as a Solid. But I will describe it to you. Or rather not I, but Analogy.

“We began with a single Point, which—being itself a Point—has only ONE terminal Point. One Point produces a Line with TWO terminal Points. One Line produces a Square with FOUR terminal Points. Now you can give yourself the answer: 1, 2, 4 are evidently in Geometrical Progression. What is the next number?”

“Eight,” I said, though the word sat on my tongue like a name from a dead dialect—correct, yet unmoored from lived experience.

“Exactly. The one Square produces a SOMETHING-WHICH-YOU-DO-NOT-AS-YET-KNOW-A-NAME-FOR-BUT-WHICH-WE-CALL-A-CUBE with EIGHT terminal Points. Now are you convinced?”

“Has this Creature sides,” I demanded, “as well as angles—or what you call ‘terminal Points’?”

“Of course; and all according to Analogy. But, by the way, not what YOU call sides, but what WE call sides. You would call them SOLIDS.”

“And how many solids,” I pressed, “or sides will appertain to this Being whom I am to generate by the motion of my inside in an ‘upward’ direction, and whom you call a Cube?”

“How can you ask? And you a mathematician!” His impatience cracked, like a pot set too quickly on high heat. “The side of anything is always—if I may so say—one Dimension behind the thing. Consequently, as there is no Dimension behind a Point, a Point has 0 sides; a Line, if I may say, has 2 sides (for the Points of a Line may be called by courtesy its sides); a Square has 4 sides; 0, 2, 4—what progression do you call that?”

“Arithmetical.”

“And what is the next number?”

“Six.”

“Exactly. Then you see you have answered your own question. The Cube which you will generate will be bounded by six sides—that is to say, six of your insides. You see it all now, eh?”

Six of my insides.

The phrase landed like a desecration. My mind flashed—not with diagrams—but with the kitchen again: the servants’ suppers, the careful saving of crusts, the inherited dishes whose flavors carried the memory of mothers and grandmothers. The home is made of boundaries—of walls, of doors, of permitted sounds, of words that mean what they have always meant. And here was a Stranger, claiming new dominion over direction itself, over the very grammar of the body, as though my “inside” were merely a panel to be counted and repurposed.

“Monster!” I cried, and my voice was raw, stripped of polite tone. “Be thou juggler, enchanter, dream, or devil, no more will I endure thy mockeries. Either thou or I must perish.”

And with that I hurled myself upon him.

It was all for nothing.

I drove my keenest right angle into the Stranger with the whole blunt force of my fear behind it—an impact that would have cracked any honest Circle like a nut under a pestle. I leaned in, grinding, sure I had him pinned; and yet, beneath my edge, there was the sickening sensation of contact turning to absence. He did not slide left or right, as any decent body would. He seemed to seep—like steam slipping from a lidded pot you have not truly sealed—upward, downward, elsewhere. My angle met less and less resistance, until there was only emptiness.

A blank—an intolerable hush in the place where his outline had been.

And still his voice sounded, disembodied, as though spoken from inside the walls, or from the hollow of my own skull. In that moment I noticed the house’s usual domestic music—the faint rasp of my Wife’s movements beyond, the soft tap of a cupboard latch settling, the far-off neighbor’s tread—being drowned by that single, intrusive tone, as if an uncouth instrument had begun to play over a well-tended household choir.

“Why will you refuse to listen to reason?” said the Intruder. “I had hoped to find in you—as being a man of sense and an accomplished mathematician—a fit apostle for the Gospel of the Three Dimensions, which I am allowed to preach once only in a thousand years: but now I know not how to convince you. Stay—I have it. Deeds, and not words, shall proclaim the truth. Listen, my friend.”

He spoke as if I were a pupil at his table and he the master carver, portioning reality with a knife too fine for my palate. “I have told you I can see from my position in Space the inside of all things that you consider closed. For example, I see in yonder cupboard near which you are standing, several of what you call boxes—(but like everything else in Flatland, they have no tops nor bottoms)—full of money; I see also two tablets of accounts.”

At the word cupboard my mind flew, absurdly and against my will, to its accustomed use: not merely for wealth, but for keeping order—containers within containers, the domestic art of shutting away what must be protected from dust, from meddling hands, from the casual eyes of visitors. In a well-governed home even silence is stored—quiet corners kept clean of clamor. Yet here was a voice that treated the cupboard as a stage.

“I am about to descend into that cupboard,” the Stranger continued, “and to bring you one of those tablets. I saw you lock the cupboard half an hour ago, and I know you have the key in your possession. But I descend from Space; the doors, you see, remain unmoved. Now I am in the cupboard and am taking the tablet. Now I have it. Now I ascend with it.”

There was no creak of hinge, no scrape of bolt—none of the honest sounds that attend a door giving way. The acoustic world did not agree with what he claimed, and yet the claim arrived with the calm certainty of a recipe recited by someone who has cooked it a thousand times. My skin prickled.

I rushed to the closet and dashed the door open.

One of the tablets was gone.

A kind of cold washed through me, the way a kitchen goes cold when the fire is extinguished mid-simmer, leaving the scent of half-made broth suspended and wrong. In the same instant, with a mocking laugh—thin, bright, commercial, like a street-performer’s bell—the Stranger appeared in the other corner of the room, and at that very moment the missing tablet seemed to drop onto the floor as if tossed there by an invisible hand.

I snatched it up.

There could be no doubt. The familiar edges, the exact weight in my grasp, the small wear-marks where my own handling had smoothed it—this was indeed the lost tablet of accounts. My stomach tightened, not from hunger but from a kind of nausea that comes when the mind’s pantry is raided: when something you relied upon being where it ought is found in the wrong place, and the old labels no longer guarantee the contents.

I groaned, half in horror and half in a dreadful suspicion that my senses had soured. But the Stranger pressed on, as if victory gave him appetite.

“Surely you must now see that my explanation, and no other, suits the phenomena. What you call Solid things are really superficial; what you call Space is really nothing but a great Plane. I am in Space, and look down upon the insides of the things of which you only see the outsides.”

His words struck at more than geometry. They struck at naming itself: at the authority of our inherited terms. Solid. Plane. Inside. Outside. The old lexicon of Flatland, handed down like family recipes, suddenly treated as provincial jargon by this foreign tongue. And I, who had always trusted definitions the way an elder trusts seed saved from last year’s harvest, felt the ground of language loosen beneath me.

“You could leave this Plane yourself,” he said, “if you could but summon up the necessary volition. A slight upward or downward motion would enable you to see all that I can see.”

Volition, he called it—as though a man might choose to rise from his world the way one chooses to lift a ladle, and as though failure were merely a lack of will, not a law of being. The power in his phrasing was not merely in what he claimed, but in how he made my limitations sound like a moral defect.

“The higher I mount,” he continued, “and the further I go from your Plane, the more I can see, though of course I see it on a smaller scale. For example, I am ascending; now I can see your neighbour the Hexagon and his family in their several apartments; now I see the inside of the Theatre, ten doors off, from which the audience is only just departing; and on the other side a Circle in his study, sitting at his books. Now I shall come back to you.”

His omniscience spilled through the neighborhood like an unwanted odor—privacy spoiled, as if someone had flung open every larder and peered into every pot. I imagined the Theatre disgorging its audience in a tide of footsteps and murmurs; imagined the Hexagon’s children in their rooms, the soft domestic rustle of their lives; imagined the studious Circle turning a page in the solitude of his lamp. All of it, he claimed, lay open to him, inspected from above like dishes laid out for a critic who had not earned an invitation.

“And, as a crowning proof,” said he, “what do you say to my giving you a touch—just the least touch—in your stomach? It will not seriously injure you, and the slight pain you may suffer cannot be compared with the mental benefit you will receive.”

Before I could utter a word—before I could even draw breath enough to protest—I felt it: a sudden, shooting pain inside me, as if a needle had been thrust into flesh that no external blade had ever reached. It was not the clean hurt of a blow you can locate, nor the soreness of honest labor. It was an intimate violation. And with it came a laugh—demoniacal, ringing—as though the sound issued from within my own body, as if my insides had been turned into a stage for his amusement.

A moment later the sharp agony ceased, leaving a dull ache behind, the way a scald cools into throbbing shame. The Stranger began to reappear, swelling gradually into visibility, and as his outline returned he spoke with the smugness of one who has seasoned a dish too boldly and expects gratitude for the intensity.

“There,” he said, “I have not hurt you much, have I? If you are not convinced now, I don’t know what will convince you. What say you?”

My answer was not in words.

My resolution hardened, quick and absolute. It seemed intolerable—more intolerable than any physical pain—that I should live subject to the arbitrary visitations of a Magician who could toy with one’s very stomach, invading the private chambers where even our most homely comforts are digested and made part of ourselves. If he could do this, then nothing in Flatland was truly secure—not cupboard, not account-tablet, not the body itself; and the mere thought made the house feel suddenly loud with threat, as if every familiar creak had become a signal for intrusion.

If only I could pin him—hold him against the wall until help arrived—until the household and the neighbors, with their solid customs and lawful angles, could witness him and judge him.

Once more I hurled my hardest angle against him. At the same time I cried out for aid, my voice sharp enough to cut through doors and corridors, alarming the whole household.

I believe that at the moment of my onset the Stranger had sunk below our Plane and truly found difficulty in rising. In any case he remained motionless; and I, hearing—or thinking I heard—the sound of help approaching, pressed against him with redoubled vigor, my whole being set like a wedge.

I shouted again.

A convulsive shudder ran through the Sphere. His smooth certainty cracked for the first time; I caught fragments of his mutter, as if spoken not to me but to himself: “This must not be… either he must listen to reason, or I must have recourse to the last resource of civilization.”

Then, addressing me louder, hurriedly, he exclaimed with sudden command, “Listen: no stranger must witness what you have witnessed. Send your Wife back at once, before she enters the apartment. The Gospel of Three Dimensions must not be thus frustrated. Not thus must the fruits of one thousand years of waiting be thrown away. I hear her coming. Back! back! Away from me, or you must go with me—whither you know not—into the Land of Three Dimensions!”

My Wife—her approaching presence, the familiar cadence of her steps—became, in his mouth, not a beloved household fact but a hazard to his mission. Even in crisis he spoke of his Gospel, his thousand-year permission, as though the private life of a home were merely an obstacle to be managed. The power in his lexicon struck again: “civilization,” “Gospel,” “fruits”—grand words used to justify the silencing of others.

“Fool! Madman! Irregular!” I cried, grinding my angle tighter. “Never will I release thee; thou shalt pay the penalty of thine impostures.”

“Ha! Is it come to this?” thundered the Stranger; and his voice filled the room like a sudden, crushing drumbeat, blotting out every domestic sound, every honest footfall and hinge-whisper. “Then meet your fate: out of your Plane you go. Once, twice, thrice! ’Tis done!”

An unspeakable horror seized me, as if the very air had turned to ink and I were drowning in it. There was darkness; then a dizzy, sickening rush of perception that was not like seeing in Flatland at all—more like being forced to taste light with every edge of my being. I beheld a Line that was no Line, a breadth that had no right to exist; Space that was not Space. I was myself, and yet I was not myself, as though my name had been spoken in a dialect I had never learned.

When at last I found voice, I shrieked aloud in agony, “Either this is madness or it is Hell.”

“It is neither,” replied, calmly and close, the voice of the Sphere. Even his speech seemed to have depth in it, a rounded resonance, like sound heard not only from before but also from above and behind. “It is Knowledge; it is Three Dimensions. Open your eye once again, and try to look steadily.”

I looked—and behold, a new world.

There stood before me, visibly incorporate, all that I had before inferred, conjectured, dreamed of perfect Circular beauty. Yet what I now beheld did not present itself as a mere circumference, a sharp boundary against emptiness, but as a complete presence: the Stranger’s form lay open to my view, as if the centre of him were plain and near. I saw no heart, no lungs, no arteries—none of those familiar hidden things that, in Flatland, one might forcibly inspect by a cruel invasion. Instead I perceived a beautiful, harmonious Something—without any word in my vocabulary to hold it. But you, my Readers in Spaceland, would call it the surface of the Sphere.

My mind fell prostrate before my Guide. “How is it,” I cried, “O divine ideal of consummate loveliness and wisdom, that I see thy inside, and yet cannot discern thy heart, thy lungs, thy arteries, thy liver?”

“What you think you see, you see not,” he replied. “It is not given to you, nor to any other Being, to behold my internal parts. I am of a different order of Beings from those in Flatland. Were I a Circle, you could discern my intestines; but I am a Being composed, as I told you before, of many Circles—the Many in the One—called in this country a Sphere. And, just as the outside of a Cube is a Square, so the outside of a Sphere presents the appearance of a Circle.”

Bewildered though I was by his enigmas, I no longer chafed against them. I worshipped him in silent adoration, as one might worship a hearth-fire whose heat is undeniable though its fuel be mysterious.

His voice softened. “Distress not yourself if you cannot at first understand the deeper mysteries of Spaceland. By degrees they will dawn upon you. Let us begin by casting back a glance at the region whence you came. Return with me a while to the plains of Flatland, and I will shew you that which you have often reasoned and thought about, but never seen with the sense of sight—a visible angle.”

“Impossible!” I cried. Yet, the Sphere leading the way, I followed as if in a dream, borne along by a motion I could not perform for myself—like a morsel lifted from a dish by an unseen hand.

Soon his voice arrested me. “Look yonder, and behold your own Pentagonal house, and all its inmates.”

I looked below, and for the first time I saw with my physical eye that domestic individuality which, until now, I had merely inferred with the understanding. How poor and shadowy my inferences were, compared with the living reality spread open beneath me—every room, every passage, every secret angle, all displayed without wall or door to hinder sight.

My four Sons lay calmly asleep in the North-Western rooms, their outlines quiet as bread left to cool. My two orphan Grandsons were to the South. The Servants—the Butler, the Footman, the Scullion—each in his place. My Daughter in her apartment. And my affectionate Wife—alarmed by my continued absence—had quitted her room and was roving up and down in the Hall, anxiously awaiting my return. Her unrest made a kind of silence visible: a domestic hush strained too tight, like a larder-door left ajar at night.

Also the Page, aroused by my cries, had left his room, and under pretext of ascertaining whether I had fallen somewhere in a faint, was prying into the cabinet in my study. And as we came nearer and nearer I could discern—even with this strange new steadiness—the contents of my cabinet, the two chests of gold, and the tablets of which the Sphere had made mention.

Touched by my Wife’s distress, I would have sprung downward to reassure her; but I found myself incapable of motion, as though my will were a utensil in a foreign kitchen—fit to be held, yet useless in my grasp.

“Trouble not yourself about your Wife,” said my Guide. “She will not be long left in anxiety. Meantime, let us take a survey of Flatland.”

Once more I felt myself rising through Space. It was even as the Sphere had said: the further we receded, the larger became the field of vision. My native city lay open in miniature, and with it the interior of every house and every creature therein—life disclosed with the pitiless completeness of a pantry inventoried by a master who permits no curtain to remain.

We mounted higher; and lo, the secrets of the earth—the depths of mines and inmost caverns of the hills—were bared before me. I beheld buried hollows and hidden workings as plainly as a cook beholds the veins in a leaf of sorrel held against the light. In this strange altitude the world had a new soundlessness, as if all our customary rustling and footfalls and door-slidings were trapped below in the thin sheet of the plane; and yet that very quiet felt like tyranny—an imposed hush, as when a great Hall commands silence so thoroughly that even honest speech seems a crime.

Awestruck at the mysteries thus unveiled before my unworthy eye, I said to my Companion, “Behold, I am become as a God. For the wise men in our country say that to see all things, or as they express it, OMNIVIDENCE, is the attribute of God alone.”

There was something of scorn in my Teacher’s voice when he answered. “Is it so indeed? Then the very pick-pockets and cut-throats of my country are to be worshipped by your wise men as being Gods: for there is not one of them that does not see as much as you see now. But trust me, your wise men are wrong.”

“Then is omnividence,” said I, “the attribute of others besides Gods?”

“I do not know,” he replied. “But if a pick-pocket or a cut-throat of our country can see everything that is in your country, surely that is no reason why the pick-pocket or cut-throat should be accepted by you as a God. This omnividence, as you call it—it is not a common word in Spaceland—does it make you more just, more merciful, less selfish, more loving? Not in the least. Then how does it make you more divine?”

“More merciful, more loving!” I exclaimed. “But these are the qualities of women! And we know that a Circle is a higher Being than a Straight Line, in so far as knowledge and wisdom are more to be esteemed than mere affection.”

“It is not for me,” said the Sphere, “to classify human faculties according to merit. Yet many of the best and wisest in Spaceland think more of the affections than of the understanding—more of your despised Straight Lines than of your belauded Circles. But enough of this. Look yonder. Do you know that building?”

I looked, and afar off I saw an immense Polygonal structure—the General Assembly Hall of the States of Flatland—surrounded by dense lines of Pentagonal buildings at right angles to each other, which I knew to be streets; and I perceived that I was approaching the great Metropolis.

“Here we descend,” said my Guide.

It was now morning: the first hour of the first day of the two thousandth year of our era. And there—because Flatland is as faithful to precedent as a family that repeats the same New Year’s stew and the same table-prayer until the words have lost their savor—at the first hour of the first day, as they had met at the first hour of the first day of the year 1000 and again of the year 0, the highest Circles of the realm were assembling in solemn conclave.

The minutes of the previous meetings were read by one whom I at once recognized as my brother: a perfectly Symmetrical Square, Chief Clerk of the High Council. The cadence of his recitation—measured, official, unseasoned—seemed itself a kind of State-silence, a way of keeping memory in a locked box by letting it exist only in prescribed words.

It was found recorded on each occasion that:

“Whereas the States had been troubled by divers ill-intentioned persons pretending to have received revelations from another World, and professing to produce demonstrations whereby they had instigated to frenzy both themselves and others, it had been for this cause unanimously resolved by the Grand Council that on the first day of each millenary, special injunctions be sent to the Prefects in the several districts of Flatland, to make strict search for such misguided persons, and without formality of mathematical examination, to destroy all such as were Isosceles of any degree, to scourge and imprison any regular Triangle, to cause any Square or Pentagon to be sent to the district Asylum, and to arrest any one of higher rank, sending him straightway to the Capital to be examined and judged by the Council.”

“You hear your fate,” said the Sphere to me, while the Council was passing for the third time the formal resolution. “Death or imprisonment awaits the Apostle of the Gospel of Three Dimensions.”

“Not so,” replied I. “The matter is now so clear to me, the nature of real Space so palpable, that methinks I could make a child understand it. Permit me but to descend at this moment and enlighten them.”

“Not yet,” said my Guide. “The time will come for that. Meantime I must perform my mission. Stay thou there in thy place.”

Saying these words, he leaped with great dexterity into the sea—if I may so call it—of Flatland, right in the midst of the ring of Counsellors.

“I come,” cried he, “to proclaim that there is a land of Three Dimensions.”

I could see many of the younger Counsellors start back in manifest horror, as the Sphere’s circular section widened before them—expanding like a sound that grows louder not by effort but by nearness. But, on a sign from the presiding Circle—who shewed not the slightest alarm or surprise—six Isosceles of a low type, from six different quarters, rushed upon the Sphere.

“We have him,” they cried. “No; yes; we have him still! he’s going! he’s gone!”

“My Lords,” said the President to the Junior Circles of the Council, “there is not the slightest need for surprise. The secret archives, to which I alone have access, tell me that a similar occurrence happened on the last two millennial commencements. You will, of course, say nothing of these trifles outside the Cabinet.”

Raising his voice, he now summoned the guards. “Arrest the policemen; gag them. You know your duty.”

So were consigned to their fate the wretched policemen—ill-fated and unwilling witnesses of a State-secret which they were not to be permitted to reveal. It struck me then, with a bitterness sharper than any geometric proof, that power does not merely forbid speech; it chooses which words may survive, and which must be strangled before they can become memory.

The President again addressed the Counsellors. “My Lords, the business of the Council being concluded, I have only to wish you a happy New Year.”

Before departing, he expressed—at some length—to the Clerk, my excellent but most unfortunate brother, his sincere regret that, in accordance with precedent and for the sake of secrecy, he must condemn him to perpetual imprisonment; but he added his satisfaction that, unless some mention were made by him of that day’s incident, his life would be spared.

When they took my brother, it was done with the brisk, well-practised economy of a household that has long ago learned how to hush a scene before it spills. The Council Chamber—so lately a bright tableland of argument—became a pantry-door slammed in the face. I saw him guided away as one might see a loaf carried from the oven before it has finished singing, still too tender to bear the air.

All the old domestic instincts rose in me at once: to intercede, to press into his hand some last comfort, if only a farewell that tasted of kinship. I tried to fling myself down into the Chamber. I gathered my will as a cook gathers heat under a stubborn pot.

But my body did not answer.

I discovered, with a shock as intimate as biting on a hidden bone, that I had no motion of my own. Not a tremor, not a slide—nothing. I hung wholly upon the volition of my Guide, as a knife hangs on the hand that wields it.

In tones like a cellar in winter, the Sphere said, “Heed not thy brother; haply thou shalt have ample time hereafter to condole with him. Follow me.”

Once more we rose—lifted, not as smoke lifts of itself, but as dough is hoisted and turned by an unseen, irresistible palm—up and away from the clamour of the Chamber. Below, the voices thinned to a dry rustle, the way a market’s babble becomes, at distance, mere wind in reed-beds. Yet even that fading sound pricked me. It seemed to me that the health of a world could be measured by what it allowed to be heard: grief spoken plainly, or grief muffled beneath law’s hard flooring.

“Hitherto,” said the Sphere, “I have shewn you naught save Plane Figures and their interiors. Now I must introduce you to Solids, and reveal to you the plan upon which they are constructed.”

At his bidding there appeared a multitude of moveable square cards. They lay like clean wafers of paste—thin, identical, obedient—awaiting the craft that would make them more than their flatness. The Sphere began to set them, one upon another, not as I in my old ignorance would have imagined—northward of the other, edge beyond edge, like a careless layering of tiles—but ON the other, face to face, true contact, a stacking that made a quiet, papery whisper, the sound of order being built.

“See,” said he. “Now a second, now a third. I am building up a Solid by a multitude of Squares parallel to one another. Now the Solid is complete, being as high as it is long and broad, and we call it a Cube.”

To my eye, trained in Flatland’s narrow cuisine of sight, it was as if some irregular creature had been slit and displayed; I saw angles and openings as one sees the inside of a fish laid too boldly on the board. The very look of it gave me the same uneasy revulsion as a dish whose familiar surface has been torn away before the eater is ready.

“Pardon me, my Lord,” I said, “but to my eye the appearance is as of an Irregular Figure whose inside is laid open to the view; methinks I see no Solid, but a Plane such as we infer in Flatland—only of an Irregularity which betokens some monstrous criminal, so that the very sight of it is painful to my eyes.”

“True,” answered the Sphere, “it appears to you a Plane, because you are not accustomed to light and shade and perspective; just as in Flatland a Hexagon would appear a Straight Line to one who has not the Art of Sight Recognition. But in reality it is a Solid, as you shall learn by the sense of Feeling.”

Then he brought me to the Cube, and made me touch.

If Flatland’s knowing is like reading recipes by name alone, this was the first time I had kneaded the dough. The Cube was not a mere outline; it resisted, it returned pressure, it offered faces that were not edges, and corners that were not mere points but meeting-places of planes. Six plane sides. Eight terminal points—the Sphere named them solid angles. The words settled in me like new spices: at first sharp, then blooming, then altering everything they touched.

And I remembered his earlier saying: that such a creature would be formed by a Square moving, in Space, parallel to himself. A strange pride—almost parental—rose in me, absurd and sweet: that so insignificant a Square as I might, in some fashion, be called progenitor to so illustrious an offspring. It was as if, from my plain barley, a loaf had arisen that could feed an empire.

Yet the Sphere’s talk of “light” and “shade” and “perspective” still puzzled me. These were not our native terms; they had no homely equivalents in the dialect of Flatland, where a thing is known by contact and by the thin music of approach. The Sphere explained—clearly, swiftly, as one who has seasoned a stew a thousand times and cannot imagine anyone missing the salt. He shifted objects, altered the position of the lights, let me feel the Cube, and even—reverently, with his permission—his own sacred Person, until at last the confusion in me clarified as broth clarifies when the scum is patiently skimmed.

So it came to pass that I could now readily distinguish between a Circle and a Sphere, a Plane Figure and a Solid. This was the Climax, the Paradise, of my strange eventful history.

And yet—here begins the sour turn, the fall that still burns like a careless mouthful of boiling fat.

For when a palate has once tasted a new depth, how shall it consent thereafter to thin gruel? When language has once found a word that fits a sensation, how can it go back to the old, blunt syllables that only approximate? The thirst for knowledge is not a polite thirst, easily dismissed with a cup. It is a hunger that, once awakened, demands not merely more, but truer; and if denied, becomes its own torment.

My volition shrinks from the painful task of recalling my humiliation; yet, like a second Prometheus, I will endure this and worse, if by any means I may arouse in the interiors of Plane and Solid Humanity a spirit of rebellion against the Conceit which would limit our Dimensions to Two or Three or any number short of Infinity. Away, then, with personal considerations. I will set down the exact facts, the exact words—burnt into me as if branded by a hot iron on a butcher’s hook—and let my Readers judge between me and Destiny.

The Sphere, for his part, would willingly have continued his lessons. He spoke of the conformation of all regular Solids—Cylinders, Cones, Pyramids, Pentahedrons, Hexahedrons, Dodecahedrons, and Spheres—as a patient host might press dish after dish upon a guest: here the crisp geometry of one, there the rich symmetry of another.

But I interrupted him.

Not because I was weary—no; I was ravenous. What he offered was plentiful, but I had glimpsed the possibility of a pantry beyond his pantry, a storeroom above his storeroom, where the very idea of “inside” might be prepared and served in a manner yet unknown.

“Pardon me,” said I, “O Thou Whom I must no longer address as the Perfection of all Beauty; but let me beg thee to vouchsafe thy servant a sight of thine interior.”

The Sphere paused, as if a fine music had been marred by some sudden, vulgar clatter.

“My what?” he said.

“Thine interior,” I replied, and, being plain by nature, I added what I meant: “thy stomach, thy intestines.”

“Whence this ill-timed impertinent request?” he demanded. “And what mean you by saying that I am no longer the Perfection of all Beauty?”

“My Lord,” I said, “your own wisdom has taught me to aspire to One even more great, more beautiful, and more closely approximate to Perfection than yourself. As you, superior to all Flatland forms, combine many Circles in One, so doubtless there is One above you who combines many Spheres in One Supreme Existence, surpassing even the Solids of Spaceland. And even as we, who are now in Space, look down on Flatland and see the insides of all things, so of a certainty there is yet above us some higher, purer region, whither thou dost surely purpose to lead me—my Priest, Philosopher, and Friend—some more spacious Space, some more dimensionable Dimensionality, from the vantage-ground of which we shall look down together upon the revealed insides of Solid things; and where thine own intestines, and those of thy kindred Spheres, will lie exposed to the view of the poor wandering exile from Flatland, to whom so much has already been vouchsafed.”

“Pooh! Stuff!” cried the Sphere. “Enough of this trifling! The time is short, and much remains to be done before you are fit to proclaim the Gospel of Three Dimensions to your blind benighted countrymen in Flatland.”

The phrase—“Gospel”—fell on me with the weight of a dominant lexicon. It named my duty in his tongue, not mine; it made my wonder into a tool, my astonishment into an instrument to be used upon others. I felt, dimly, the power in naming: how easily one world’s vocabulary becomes another world’s chain.

“Nay, gracious Teacher,” I persisted, “deny me not what I know it is in thy power to perform. Grant me but one glimpse of thine interior, and I am satisfied for ever—remaining henceforth thy docile pupil, thy unemancipable slave, ready to receive all thy teachings and to feed upon the words that fall from thy lips.”

There, in my own phrasing, the hunger spoke nakedly: to feed upon words, to be sustained by what was spoken. For in every world I had known, sustenance and knowledge had braided together; one ate, one learned, one remembered. And what is memory but a recipe repeated until it becomes the house’s smell?

“Well, then,” said the Sphere at last, as one who gives a child a crust to quiet it, “to content and silence you, let me say at once, I would shew you what you wish if I could; but I cannot. Would you have me turn my stomach inside out to oblige you?”

“But my Lord,” I said, “you have shewn me the intestines of all my countrymen in the Land of Two Dimensions by taking me with him into the Land of Three. What therefore more easy than now to take your servant on a second journey into the blessed region of the Fourth Dimension? There I shall look down with you once more upon this land of Three Dimensions and see the inside of every three-dimensioned house, the secrets of the solid earth, the treasures of the mines in Spaceland, and the intestines of every solid living creature—even of the noble and adorable Spheres.”

“But where is this land of Four Dimensions?” he asked.

“I know not,” I confessed, “but doubtless my Teacher knows.”

“Not I,” said the Sphere. “There is no such land. The very idea of it is utterly inconceivable.”

“Not inconceivable, my Lord, to me,” I answered, “and therefore still less inconceivable to my Master.” For the sensations of this place had already forced my mind to invent new rooms in itself; I had found, in the gaps of my old speech, a pressure toward other speech. “Nay, I despair not that even here, in this region of Three Dimensions, your art may make the Fourth Dimension visible to me; just as in the Land of Two Dimensions your skill would fain have opened the eyes of your blind servant to the invisible presence of a Third Dimension, though I saw it not.”

I begged him to let me recall the past, to lean on analogy as one leans on a grandmother’s old measures—pinches, handfuls, the remembered weight of flour in the palm. In our world, so much is transmitted by comparison because there are no words precise enough; and yet comparison itself is a kind of language that can die if mocked out of use.

“Was I not taught below,” I said, “that when I saw a Line and inferred a Plane, I in reality saw a Third unrecognized Dimension, not the same as brightness, called ‘height’? And does it not now follow that, in this region, when I see a Plane and infer a Solid, I really see a Fourth unrecognized Dimension, not the same as colour, but existent—though infinitesimal and incapable of measurement? And besides this, there is the Argument from Analogy of Figures.”

“Analogy!” snapped the Sphere. “Nonsense: what analogy?”

“Trifle not with me, my Lord,” I cried, the old humility now flavoured with something fierce. “I crave, I thirst, for more knowledge. Doubtless we cannot SEE that higher Spaceland now, because we have no eye in our stomachs. But just as there WAS the realm of Flatland, though that poor puny Lineland Monarch could neither turn to left nor right to discern it; and just as there WAS close at hand, and touching my frame, the land of Three Dimensions, though I—blind senseless wretch—had no power to touch it, no eye in my interior to discern it; so of a surety there is a Fourth Dimension.”

And then, as if reciting a sequence learned by heart beside a hearth—numbers passed down like seed from season to season—I set forth what seemed to me unanswerable.

“In One Dimension, did not a moving Point produce a Line with TWO terminal points? In Two Dimensions, did not a moving Line produce a Square with FOUR terminal points? In Three Dimensions, did not a moving Square produce—did not this eye of mine behold it—that blessed Being, a Cube, with EIGHT terminal points? And in Four Dimensions shall not a moving Cube—alas for Analogy, and alas for the progress of Truth, if it be not so—shall not, I say, the motion of a divine Cube result in a still more divine Organization with SIXTEEN terminal points? Behold the confirmation of the Series, 2, 4, 8, 16: is not this a geometrical progression?”

I pressed on, tasting the logic as one tastes a sauce—adjusting, balancing, believing one has found the true proportion.

“Again, was I not taught by my Lord that as in a Line there are TWO bounding Points, and in a Square there are FOUR bounding Lines, so in a Cube there must be SIX bounding Squares? Behold once more the confirming Series, 2, 4, 6: is not this an arithmetical progression? And consequently does it not follow that the more divine offspring of the Cube in the Land of Four Dimensions must have 8 bounding Cubes?”

At last I cast myself—whole soul, whole appetite—upon conjecture.

“O my Lord,” I said, “I appeal to you to confirm or deny my anticipations. If I am wrong, I yield, and will no longer demand a Fourth Dimension; but if I am right, my Lord will listen to reason. I ask therefore: is it, or is it not, the fact that ere now your countrymen also have witnessed the descent of Beings of a higher order than their own—entering closed rooms, even as you entered mine, without the opening of doors or windows, appearing and vanishing at will? On the reply to this question I am ready to stake everything. Deny it, and I am henceforth silent. Only vouchsafe an answer.”

The Sphere was silent for a while. In that pause I heard, more clearly than before, the soundscape of this world: a distant hum, steady as a mill’s turning, a vibration that seemed always present beneath speech, like an unseen machinery. It struck me as a kind of tyranny of noise—constant, normalized, unremarked—against which the subtler sounds of wonder and doubt had to fight for room. In Flatland, a whisper along an edge could be destiny. Here, the air itself seemed to drown the finest messages.

“Atfter a pause,” said the Sphere at length, “it is reported so. But men are divided in opinion as to the facts. And even granting the facts, they explain them in different ways. And in any case, however great may be the number of different explanations, no one has adopted or suggested the theory of a Fourth Dimension. Therefore, pray have done with this trifling, and let us return to business.”

“I was certain of it,” I breathed. The admission—reported, disputed, explained away—was enough. In every world, I saw now, the dominant tongue polices the possible: it does not merely forbid certain acts; it forbids certain sentences from being considered sane. If no one has named it, then it must not be.

“And now,” I urged, unable to stop as one cannot stop chewing when the mouth has been set to it, “have patience with me and answer yet one more question. Those who have thus appeared—no one knows whence—and have returned—no one knows whither—have they also contracted their sections and vanished somehow into that more spacious Space, whither I entreat you to conduct me?”

“Moodily,” the Sphere replied, “they have vanished, certainly—if they ever appeared. But most people say that these visions arose from the thought—you will not understand me—from the brain; from the perturbed angularity of the Seer.”

“Say they so?” I cried. “Oh, believe them not. Or if it indeed be so, that this other Space is really Thoughtland, then take me to that blessed Region where I in Thought shall see the insides of all solid things. There, before my ravished eye, a Cube—moving in some altogether new direction, but strictly according to Analogy—shall create a still more perfect perfection than himself, with sixteen terminal Extra-solid angles, and eight solid Cubes for his Perimeter.”

My words ran ahead of me, fermenting, foaming. It was no longer request but prophecy, no longer curiosity but a kind of liturgy spoken in a tongue half-made, as if my old Flatland dialect were straining to birth sounds it had never needed. I talked of a Fifth, a Sixth, a Seventh—each new dimension like another course in an endless feast, richer and stranger, until the very concept of “enough” vanished.

“In that blessed region of Four Dimensions,” I cried, “shall we linger on the threshold of the Fifth, and not enter therein? Ah, no! Let our ambition soar with our corporal ascent. Then the gates of the Sixth Dimension shall fly open; after that a Seventh, and then an Eighth—”

In vain did the Sphere, in a voice of thunder, reiterate his command of silence and threaten me with the direst penalties if I persisted. Nothing could stem the flood. I was intoxicated—not with wine, but with the sharp, clean draught of truth he himself had poured for me. A palate once awakened will demand its native complexity; it will not be pacified with watered broth.

Perhaps I was to blame.

But the end came swiftly, as when a boiling pot, neglected, suddenly erupts.

There was a crash outside, and at the same instant a crash inside me—an inward concussion that seemed to strike through my very substance and fling me headlong. The space around me became a chute; I was hurled with a velocity that precluded speech.

Down. Down. Down.

The descent had the brutal certainty of a cleaver coming to the board. I knew—knew with the bleak foreknowledge that precedes pain—that return to Flatland was my doom. One glimpse, one last and never-to-be-forgotten glimpse I had of that dull, level wilderness—now to become my Universe again—spread out before my eye like an endless table wiped bare of all but the same old fare.

Then darkness.

Then a final, all-consummating thunder-peal that seemed to shake not only the air but the very right to imagine.

And when I came to myself, I was once more a common creeping Square, in my Study at home, listening to the Peace-Cry of my approaching Wife. The familiar sound, homely and sharp as a kettle beginning to sing, threaded the air of my two-dimensional house; and with it returned the old domestic world—its routines, its language, its limits—closing around me as surely as a lid. Yet even there, in the comfort and confinement of that known voice, the taste of higher spaces lingered on my mind like an untranslatable spice: impossible to describe in our native speech, and therefore all the more certain that I had once swallowed it whole.

I had less than a minute in which to gather myself into something like a husband again, and in that narrow slice of time instinct—older than reason, older than any theorem—told me to hide my strange journey from my Wife.

Not because I feared her tongue; no, she had never been one to chatter at corners. But because I knew, as surely as I knew the grain of our floor by the touch of my angles, that to any Woman in Flatland the tale would fall like a foreign spice upon an untrained palate: sharp, inexplicable, and therefore suspect. Our language itself had no proper mouth for what I had tasted. How could I tell her of “Upward,” when every word we own for direction is salted with North and South?

So I improvised, as one does when a stew threatens to scorch and guests are already at table. I told her I had slipped through the cellar trap-door and lay there stunned.

Even to her—who had the uncommon steadiness of mind that some call “good sense,” as if sense were ever good or bad but only tended—my story was nearly beyond belief. In our country the pull to the South is so faint that falling “down” is less a certainty than a rumor. Yet she did not wrangle with me. She heard, more than my words, the tremor in my voice, the quickness of my breath, the way my very edges seemed to ring as though struck.

“You are ill,” she said, with the authority of the domestic world, where ailments are measured not in axioms but in posture, color, and the thinness of a man’s patience. “You require repose.”

I accepted it gratefully. Solitude, at that moment, was as necessary as silence in a kitchen before dawn, when the bread must rise and no hammering must jar the air. I withdrew to my chamber, and at last, with the door closed, I let my thoughts spread out upon the floor of my mind like dough being rolled—trying to make something that could hold what I had been given.

A drowsiness came over me, warm and heavy, like the slow drift of sleep after a meal that has been richer than one deserved. Before my eyes closed, I forced myself—almost with the stubbornness of a cook repeating a technique until it lives in the hands—to rehearse the Third Dimension. I tried to summon, cleanly and without blur, that miracle by which a Cube is born from the motion of a Square, as a loaf is born from flour that has been kneaded and lifted and turned.

It would not come as clear as I wished. The image kept collapsing back into Flatland, as if my mind were a language that lacked the word and therefore lost the thought. Yet one phrase remained, not as an explanation but as a scent you cannot mistake once you have breathed it: Upward, and yet not Northward.

I clung to it. I repeated it as if it were a charm, as if the cadence itself could preserve what meaning could not. “Upward, yet not Northward. Upward, yet not Northward.” And so, like a man rocking himself into rest with the only lullaby he knows, I fell into a deep, clean sleep.

In that sleep a Vision came.

I was once more beside the Sphere. His luminous skin—if I may call it skin—held a gentler sheen than before, a light like polished fruit rather than hard metal, and I knew he had set aside his wrath and found room again for patience.

We moved together through a vast stillness toward a bright Point, so small it might have been a pinprick of radiance pricked through black cloth. The Sphere directed my attention to it, and as we drew nearer, a sound began to seep from it—at first not sound at all, only the suggestion of vibration, the way a kettle seems to promise a whistle before it gives it.

It was a slight humming, like one of your Spaceland bluebottles, but thinner, less resonant—so faint that even in the perfect hush of the void through which we traveled, it did not reach us until we had checked our flight at something under twenty human diagonals away.

“Look yonder,” said my Guide. “In Flatland thou hast lived; of Lineland thou hast received a vision; thou hast soared with me to the heights of Spaceland; now, to complete the range of thy experience, I conduct thee downward—to the lowest depth of existence: Pointland, the Abyss of No dimensions.

“Behold yon miserable creature. That Point is a Being like ourselves, but confined to the non-dimensional gulf. He is himself his own World, his own Universe. Of any other than himself he can form no conception. He knows not Length, nor Breadth, nor Height, for he has had no experience of them. He has no cognizance even of the number Two; nor has he a thought of Plurality. He is his One and All, being in truth—Nothing. Yet mark his perfect self-contentment, and hence learn this lesson: to be self-contented is to be vile and ignorant, and to aspire is better than to be blindly and impotently happy. Now listen.”

The Sphere ceased, and the Point’s humming tightened into a tiny, low, monotonous tinkling—distinct as a phonograph heard through a wall. In the thin music I caught words:

“Infinite beatitude of existence! It is; and there is none else beside It.”

“What,” I asked, “does the puny creature mean by ‘it’?”

“He means himself,” said the Sphere. “Have you not noticed that babies—and babyish people—who cannot distinguish themselves from the world speak of themselves in the Third Person? But hush.”

The Point continued, ringing on, each sentence folding back upon itself like a refrain that never discovers a new note.

“It fills all Space, and what It fills, It is. What It thinks, that It utters; and what It utters, that It hears. And It itself is Thinker, Utterer, Hearer—Thought, Word, Audition. It is the One, and yet the All in All. Ah, the happiness—ah, the happiness of Being!”

The sound of him was, in its way, unbearable: not loud, not harsh, but perfectly unvaried, like a single spoon tapping forever against an empty bowl. In Flatland, even our humblest kitchens have a chorus—knife on board, ladle in pot, the low speech of those who work. This was a solitude so complete it had turned into worship.

“Can you not startle the little thing out of its complacency?” I said. “Tell it what it really is, as you told me. Reveal to it the narrow limitations of Pointland and lead it up to something higher.”

“That is no easy task,” said my Master. “Try you.”

And so I did what Flatlanders do when we are frightened and want our fear to sound like authority. I raised my voice to the uttermost and cried out into that narrow brightness:

“Silence, silence, contemptible Creature. You call yourself the All in All, but you are the Nothing. Your so-called Universe is a mere speck in a Line, and a Line is a mere shadow as compared with—”

“Hush, hush—you have said enough,” interrupted the Sphere. “Now listen, and mark the effect of your harangue on the King of Pointland.”

For King he was, in his own mind—an autocrat ruling an empire of himself. And indeed, as I watched, his lustre beamed more brightly than ever upon hearing my words. It was as if my protest had been taken not as contradiction but as seasoning: an added flavor he believed he had invented.

Hardly had I ceased when he resumed his tinkling chant:

“Ah, the joy—ah, the joy of Thought! What can It not achieve by thinking! Its own Thought coming to Itself, suggestive of Its disparagement, thereby to enhance Its happiness! Sweet rebellion stirred up to result in triumph! Ah, the divine creative power of the All in One! Ah, the joy, the joy of Being!”

“You see,” said my Teacher, “how little your words have done. So far as the Monarch understands them at all, he accepts them as his own—for he cannot conceive of any other except himself—and he plumes himself upon the variety of ‘Its Thought’ as an instance of creative power. Let us leave this God of Pointland to the ignorant fruition of his omnipresence and omniscience. Nothing that you or I can do can rescue him from his self-satisfaction.”

So we left that pinprick deity to his thin, ceaseless self-applause, and floated gently back toward Flatland.

As we traveled, the Sphere’s voice—mild now, and oddly intimate—moved through the quiet like wind through reeds, not breaking the silence but shaping it. He drew from my vision its moral, urging me to aspire, and more than that, to teach others to aspire: not to be content with the vocabulary given us by custom and law, not to confuse the limits of our words with the limits of what is.

He confessed, too, that at first he had been angered by my ambition to soar to Dimensions above the Third. But since then, he said, he had received fresh insight, and he was not too proud to acknowledge his error to a Pupil.

Then—still strictly according to Analogy—he began to initiate me into mysteries higher than those I had already witnessed: showing how to construct Extra-Solids by the motion of Solids, and Double Extra-Solids by the motion of Extra-Solids, by methods so simple, so easy, that—so he said—they would be patent even to the Female Sex.

I woke with my whole being ringing like a well-tuned pan after the last simmer—bright, resonant, certain. The night’s impossible instruction had left me ravenous, not for bread, but for proclamation. I would go out at once, I told myself, and season the whole of Flatland with this new truth. I would speak it plainly to every class and angle: to Women, even to Soldiers. I would begin at home, with my Wife, while the morning still smelt of warmed stone and yesterday’s tea.

But before I could lift the first word like a ladle, the street below broke into a harsh, metallic chorus: many voices commanding silence, the command itself striking the ear like a cleaver on bone. Then a single voice rose above them—official, practiced, the kind that does not persuade so much as press down.

A herald. A proclamation.

I leaned toward the window-slit, listening as one listens to a kettle one does not trust. The Resolution of the Council unfurled in that voice, each clause snapped out cleanly: any who “perverted minds” with delusions, any who professed revelations from another World, were to be arrested—imprisoned—or executed.

The words left a bitter taste behind them, like burnt sugar on the tongue. In Flatland, the Council did not merely legislate; it salted the earth.

I stood very still, attending not only to the meaning but to the soundscape itself: the way the street’s ordinary morning—soft footfalls, neighbors’ murmurs, the gentle domestic clink of shutters—was drowned beneath this hard civic din. There was an ecology to quiet, and the Council had learned how to poison it. When a city is trained to fear its own voices, memory begins to rot; language shrinks to what is safe, and what cannot be safely said becomes—by degrees—unthinkable.

This danger was not to be trifled with. I must not speak of Revelation. I must proceed, if I proceeded at all, by Demonstration; and it comforted me at first to recall how simple the proof had seemed.

“Upward, not Northward.”

That had been the clue, the spice that unlocked the whole broth.

Yet as I rolled the phrase in my mind, it did not bloom as it had in the first clean moment of waking. When I had risen from sleep, it had felt as plain as Arithmetic—like counting measures of flour. Now it slid away from the intellect like oil from a dry pan. I could taste it, almost, but not grasp it.

My Wife entered just then, her presence bringing with it the familiar domestic register: the faint scent of linen, the soft scrape of her movement, the household’s steady rhythm. Her curiosity, naturally, was keen. She wished to know why the Circle had demanded that mysterious interview, and—more pressingly—how he had entered our house at all.

I gave her an account. It was elaborate, full of plausible seams and careful omissions; I stitched it the way a nervous cook stretches a thin stock, adding water and herbs to make it seem abundant. If any Reader in Spaceland were to set my words beside the strict truth, I fear the flavors would not match. But the outcome was what I needed: at last I persuaded her, gently but firmly, back to her customary duties, without letting fall a single crumb about another World.

When she had gone, I felt an urgency that was almost physical, as if the dream’s teachings were cooling, congealing, becoming unpalatable the longer they sat. I needed a first disciple—someone young, docile, mathematically inclined—someone whose mind might take this strange new nourishment before it spoiled.

My Pentagonal Sons were men of character and standing, physicians of no mean reputation; but they were not great in mathematics, and in this they were unfit for the first trial. Worse, their patriotism and reverence for the Circles ran deep, like an inherited recipe they would defend even against their own blood. If they suspected me of seriously maintaining the seditious heresy of a Third Dimension, I could not be sure they would not feel compelled—by duty, by fear, by the very vocabulary of loyalty—to hand me over to the Prefect.

A grandson, then. A mere boy.

I sent for my little precocious Grandson—the one whose casual remarks on the meaning of 3³ had once drawn the Sphere’s approval. With him I would be safe. He would know nothing, as yet, of the Proclamation. Children do not recognize the taste of law until it has been forced upon their tongues.

When he entered the room, I secured the door with care, as one covers a pot against stray ash. The house, for a moment, settled into a hush I could trust. We sat together and drew out our mathematical tablets—Lines, as you in Spaceland would call them—laying them ready like utensils for a lesson that was half cookery and half incantation.

“We will resume yesterday,” I told him, and my voice tried for lightness, tried for that affectionate tone with which elders pass down household arts.

I taught him again the old, accepted transformations: how a Point, moved in One Dimension, yields a Line; how a straight Line, moved in Two Dimensions, yields a Square. These were our staple foods, our daily bread—facts repeated so often they had become flavorless and unquestioned, yet still sustaining.

Then I forced a laugh—too sharp, perhaps, like laughter scraped out of a dry throat.

“And now, you scamp,” I said, “you wanted to make me believe that a Square may, in the same way, by motion ‘Upward, not Northward,’ produce another figure—an extra Square—in Three Dimensions. Say that again, you young rascal.”

As if summoned by the phrase, the street outside answered with the herald’s cry—“O yes! O yes!”—and the Resolution began anew, loud as a hammer on a gong.

I watched my Grandson’s face as the words came through the walls. He was young, but unusually intelligent, and he had been bred in perfect reverence for the authority of the Circles. He understood far more quickly than I had expected. He did not interrupt. He sat rigidly, listening, while the law poured itself into the air of the room, turning the very quiet between us into something dangerous.

When the last word died away, the boy’s composure shattered. He burst into tears.

“Dear Grandpapa,” he sobbed, “that was only my fun, and of course I meant nothing at all by it; and we did not know anything then about the new Law; and I don’t think I said anything about the Third Dimension; and I am sure I did not say one word about ‘Upward, not Northward,’ for that would be such nonsense, you know. How could a thing move Upward, and not Northward? Upward and not Northward! Even if I were a baby, I could not be so absurd as that. How silly it is! Ha! ha! ha!”

His tears turned to laughter with the abruptness of a pan flaring when water hits hot fat—fear hissing into mockery, mockery masking fear. In that laughter I heard the Council’s victory: how swiftly a new lexicon of obedience can teach a child to spit out the very syllables that might have enlarged his world.

“Not at all silly,” I snapped, my temper rising like a boil I had not watched closely enough. “Here, for example, I take this Square—”

At the word I grasped a moveable Square lying at hand, and I held it as a cook holds up a slice to show its cut, its grain, its truth.

“—and I move it, you see, not Northward but—yes, I move it Upward—that is to say, not Northward, but I move it somewhere—not exactly like this, but somehow—”

And there, in the very moment I needed the proof to be as clean as a knife’s edge, my mind failed to find the cut. The concept that had seemed so certain in the dream would not translate into the language of Flatland, not into gesture, not into demonstration. My hands shook the Square about in a purposeless, foolish manner, as if agitation could substitute for direction.

My Grandson stared, and then he burst out laughing louder than before. The sound bounced off the walls, bright and cruel.

“You are not teaching me,” he declared, between peals of laughter. “You are joking with me!”

Before I could stop him, he ran to the door, unlocked it—so easily, so decisively—and fled the room, leaving behind only the echo of his mirth and the stale aftertaste of my own inarticulacy.

Thus ended my first attempt to convert a pupil to the Gospel of Three Dimensions.

My failure with my Grandson left a scorch-mark on the hearth of my courage. I had tried to feed him a new world as one feeds a child—small portions, gently seasoned, made familiar with a proverb he could chew without choking: Upward, not Northward. He spat it out as if it were grit.

Yet I did not despair. I only saw that a catch-phrase is a thin broth. It warms the mouth and leaves the body unfed. If I meant to nourish conviction, I must set before the public the whole dish—its ingredients, its method, its scent while it came together—so that even the timid could follow the recipe from first principles. And for that, in Flatland, there is only one kitchen spacious enough: writing.

So I withdrew for months into privacy and began a treatise. I worked as our oldest agrarian Elders work the soil—patiently, returning to the same furrow until it yields—except my field was the stubborn page. To evade the Law, I dared not speak of a physical Dimension. I named it Thoughtland instead, a country of the mind where, in theory, a Figure might look down upon Flatland and see, all at once, the interiors of all things—like a cook who can taste the center of a loaf without cutting it, or a food historian who can read the whole story of a spice by its first breath on the tongue.

In Thoughtland, I wrote, it was possible to suppose a Figure environed, as it were, with six Squares, and containing eight terminal Points. I described it as carefully as I could, turning the idea as one turns a fruit in the hand, seeking the bruise, the shine, the hidden ripeness.

But the very tools of our country betrayed me. In Flatland there are no tablets but Lines, no diagrams but Lines—each of them stretched on the same straight track, distinguishable only by size and brightness. How can one teach pastry without ovens? How can one show thickness with a knife that has no blade? I could only set down strings of words where a single honest sketch would have done the work of a hundred sentences. When at last I titled my manuscript Through Flatland to Thoughtland, I could not persuade myself that many would understand what I meant, even if they read it with goodwill.

Meanwhile my life grew muffled and sour, like grain stored too long beside damp stone. All pleasures palled. Every common sight—a doorway, a window, a simple house—tantalized me, because I could not look upon any familiar outline without comparing what it appeared to be in Two Dimensions with what I had once apprehended it to be in Three. The comparison rose in me like an unbidden aroma, and I had to clamp down my mouth lest it escape as treason.

I neglected my clients and my business. I let appointments pass as if they were meals left to burn while I stood over a different pot, intoxicated by a fragrance no one else could smell. I gave myself instead to contemplation of the mysteries I had beheld—mysteries I could impart to no one, and which daily grew harder to reproduce even within my own mind. The soundscape of my days changed. Where once there had been the clean, familiar hush of domestic routine—measured steps, the soft exchanges of neighborly angles—there was now a constant inward ringing, as though the world itself were crowded with a thin industrial whine: the noise of enforced sameness, the grinding of a lexicon that admitted only what it already owned.

One day—about eleven months after my return from Spaceland—I shut my eye and tried to see a Cube. I could not. The image would not come, as if the recipe had slipped out of my native dialect and left me with only a translation: correct, perhaps, but tasteless. Later I succeeded, or thought I did; yet even then I was not quite certain—nor have I ever been afterwards—that I had exactly realized the original. That uncertainty struck deeper than any rebuke. It made me more melancholy than before, and drove me to action, though I did not know what action could serve.

I would have been willing to sacrifice my life for the Cause, if only the sacrifice could have produced conviction. But if I could not convince my own Grandson, how could I convince the highest and most developed Circles in the land? And yet, at times, my spirit surged past caution, and I gave vent to dangerous utterances. I was already considered heterodox, if not treasonable, and I felt the peril like a knife laid flat against the skin. Still, among the loftiest Polygonal and Circular society, I could not always refrain from bursting into suspicious half-seditious phrases.

When, for example, men spoke of those lunatics who claimed they had received power to see the insides of things, I quoted an ancient Circle who said that prophets and inspired people are always counted mad by the majority. And I found myself dropping expressions such as “the eye that discerns the interiors of things,” and “the all-seeing land,” as if they were harmless seasonings. Once or twice—more reckless than wise—I even let fall the forbidden terms: the Third and Fourth Dimensions. Each word felt like a foreign spice smuggled past the gatekeepers of taste: potent, unmistakable, impossible to pretend was anything else.

At last, to complete a series of minor indiscretions, I undid myself at a meeting of our Local Speculative Society held at the palace of the Prefect himself. Some extremely silly person had read an elaborate paper—dry as chalk, loud with self-satisfaction—exhibiting the precise reasons why Providence has limited the number of Dimensions to Two, and why omnividence must belong to the Supreme alone. The hall hummed with the complacent acoustics of power: polished echoes, the kind that make every orthodox sentence sound truer than it is.

Something in me snapped—perhaps the memory of the Sphere’s voice, perhaps the pressure of all that untranslatable seeing. I rose and gave an exact account of my voyage with the Sphere into Space, and to the Assembly Hall in our Metropolis, and then to Space again; of my return home; of everything I had seen and heard in fact or vision.

At first I pretended it was fiction—the imaginary experiences of a fictitious person—hoping the disguise might soften the Law’s attention as sugar softens bitterness. But enthusiasm, like heat in a sealed pot, forced its way out. I threw off all pretence. In a fervent peroration I exhorted my hearers to divest themselves of prejudice and become believers in the Third Dimension.

I was arrested at once and taken before the Council.

Next morning I stood in the very place where, only a few months before, the Sphere had stood beside me. I was allowed to begin and to continue my narration—unquestioned, uninterrupted. Yet from the first I foresaw my fate; for the President, noting that a guard of the better sort of Policemen attended—of angularity little, if at all, under fifty-five degrees—ordered them relieved before I began my defence by an inferior class of two or three degrees.

I knew too well what that meant. I was to be executed or imprisoned, and my story was to be kept secret from the world by the simultaneous destruction of the officials who had heard it. The President merely wished to substitute cheaper victims for expensive ones. Even the state’s cruelty had its economy, as if lives were ingredients measured by cost, not by worth.

After I had concluded my defence, the President—perhaps perceiving that some of the junior Circles had been moved by my evident earnestness—asked me two questions.

First: could I indicate the direction I meant when I used the words “Upward, not Northward”?

Second: could I, by any diagrams or descriptions (other than the enumeration of imaginary sides and angles), indicate the Figure I was pleased to call a Cube?

I declared I could say nothing more. I must commit myself to the Truth, whose cause would surely prevail in the end.

The President replied that he quite concurred in my sentiment, and that I could not do better. I must be sentenced to perpetual imprisonment; but if the Truth intended that I should emerge from prison and evangelize the world, the Truth might be trusted to bring that result to pass. Meanwhile I should be subjected to no discomfort not necessary to preclude escape; and unless I forfeited the privilege by misconduct, I should occasionally be permitted to see my brother, who had preceded me to my prison.

Seven years have elapsed. I am still a prisoner, and—if I except the occasional visits of my brother—debarred from all companionship save that of my jailers.

My brother is one of the best of Squares: just, sensible, cheerful, not without fraternal affection. He comes to me with the steady cadence of old domestic habits, as if we were meeting over a plain meal in a quiet room, and for a little while the harsh acoustics of the prison soften. Yet I confess that our weekly interviews cause me, in one respect, the bitterest pain.

He was present when the Sphere manifested himself in the Council Chamber. He saw the Sphere’s changing sections. He heard the explanation then given to the Circles. Since that time scarcely a week has passed, through seven whole years, without his hearing from me a repetition of the part I played in that manifestation—together with ample descriptions of all the phenomena in Spaceland, and the arguments for the existence of Solid things derivable from Analogy.

Yet—shame compels me to confess it—my brother has not yet grasped the nature of the Third Dimension, and frankly avows his disbelief in the existence of a Sphere.

So I am absolutely destitute of converts. For aught that I can see, the millennial Revelation has been made to me for nothing. Prometheus in Spaceland was bound for bringing down fire for mortals; but I—poor Flatland Prometheus—lie here in prison for bringing down nothing to my countrymen.

And still I exist in hope: that these memoirs may, in some manner I know not how, find their way to the minds of humanity in Some Dimension, and stir up a race of rebels who will refuse to be confined to limited Dimensionality. That is the hope of my brighter moments.

But it is not always so.

Heavily weighs on me at times the burdensome reflection that I cannot honestly say I am confident as to the exact shape of the once-seen, oft-regretted Cube. In my nightly visions the mysterious precept—Upward, not Northward—haunts me like a soul-devouring Sphinx. It is part of my martyrdom that there are seasons of weakness when Cubes and Spheres flit away into the background of scarce-possible existences; when the Land of Three Dimensions seems almost as visionary as the Land of One or None.

Nay, there are nights when even this hard wall that bars me from freedom, these very tablets on which I am writing, and all the substantial realities of Flatland itself, feel like the offspring of diseased imagination—the baseless fabric of a dream—dissolving as quietly as a scent that cannot be named in any surviving tongue.
