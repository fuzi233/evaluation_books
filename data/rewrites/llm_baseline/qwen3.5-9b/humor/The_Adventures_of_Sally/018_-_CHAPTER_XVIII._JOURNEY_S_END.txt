Darkness was gathering with a distinct sense of apology, as though it felt bad about interrupting what had been a flawless summer day. To the west, past the tree line, a faint afterglow still clung to the horizon, while a new moon hung overhead like a polished silver sickle above the great barn.

Sally stepped out onto the porch and performed a solemn triple bow for luck. Standing on the gravel, she breathed in the sweet scents of the evening and decided that life was simply wonderful. By now, however, the darkness had shaken off its initial hesitation and set about its work with vigorous efficiency. The sky settled into a uniform, deep blue, speckled with quiet stars, until the cement road leading toward Patchogue and Babylon dissolved completely into invisibility.

Lights flickered on in the houses across the meadow. From the direction of the kennels came a single, sleepy bark. The small, white, woolly dog—Toto—who had been trotting at Sally's heels, halted abruptly and emitted a sharp, challenging squeak. The evening was so still that Ginger's footsteps, pounding home from the village where he had procured provisions, evening papers, and yarn for Sally's sweater, were audible long before he turned into the gate.

Sally couldn't see him yet, but she looked toward the sound and felt that familiar, cozy thrill of happiness that had visited her every evening for the past year.

"Ginger!" she called out.

"What ho!"

The woolly dog, issuing another important squeak, scurried down the drive to investigate, only to be greeted with icy indifference. Ginger, despite his professed love for dogs, had never managed to warm to Toto. When Sally, a month prior, had found Mrs. Meecher distraught because her pet had fallen into a terrible lethargy, she had begged Ginger to take the invalid away for some country air.

"It's wonderful what you've done for Toto, angel," Sally remarked as Ginger approached, evading the animal's enthusiastic leaps with frosty precision. "He's a different dog."

"Bit of luck for him," Ginger replied. "All through my time at Mrs. Meecher's, I never saw him move faster than a stately walk. Now he's running around all the time."

"The blighter had been overeating since birth," Ginger explained. "That was the whole problem. A little judicious dieting fixed him up."

"We'll be able to," Ginger continued, brightening, "ship him back next week."

"I shall quite miss him."

"I nearly missed him this morning—with a shoe," Ginger admitted. "He was up on the kitchen table wolfing down bacon, and I took action."

"My cave-man!" Sally murmured. "I always said you had a frightfully brutal streak in you. Ginger, what an evening!"

"Good Lord!" Ginger exclaimed suddenly as they stepped into the light of the open kitchen door. "Now what?"

He stopped and stared at her intently. "Do you know you're looking prettier than you were when I headed down to the village!"

Sally gave his arm a gentle squeeze. "Beloved!" she cooed. "Did you get the chops?"

Ginger froze in his tracks, horror washing over his face. "Oh, my aunt! I completely forgot them!"

"Oh, Ginger, you old chump," Sally laughed. "Well, you'll have to undergo a little judicious dieting yourself, just like Toto."

"I say, I'm dreadfully sorry. But I did get the wool."

"If you think I'm going to eat wool..."

"Isn't there anything else in the house?"

"Vegetables and fruit."

"Fine! Though, of course, if you're desperate for chops..."

"Not at all. I'm spiritual. Besides, people say vegetables are excellent for blood pressure or something."

"Didn't you get the mail either?"

"Absolutely not! I was on top of it like a knife. Two letters from fellows wanting Airedale puppies."

"No! Ginger, we're getting on!"

"Pretty bloated," Ginger agreed complacently. "Pretty bloated. If things keep buzzing along like this, we might even manage to get that two-seater. There was a letter for you. Here it is."

"It's from Fillmore," Sally said, examining the envelope as they moved inside the kitchen. "About time, too. Haven't heard a peep from him for months."

She sat down and opened the letter. Ginger hauled himself onto the table, wriggled into a comfortable position, and began reading his evening paper. After skimming the sports section, he lowered the paper and watched Sally's bent head with a profound sense of contentment. Despite being married for nearly a year, Ginger still wandered through a magical world in a state of dazed disbelief, unable to fully grasp that such bliss existed. In all his travels, he had seen many things that looked promising from afar, but none that held up under close inspection—except for this business of marriage. With Sally as his partner, marriage seemed to be one of the rare endeavors in life without any hidden catches. His honest eyes gleamed as he observed her.

Sally broke into a fit of giggles. "Ginger, look at this!"

He leaned forward and took the slip of paper she offered. Bold letters caught his eye:

Ginger squinted at the cryptic message with a puzzled frown. "What is it?" he asked.

"It's Fillmore," Sally gurgled.

"How do you mean?"

"Fillmore and Gladys have started a little restaurant in Pittsburgh."

"A restaurant!" There was a note of genuine shock in Ginger's voice. Even though he knew that the managerial career of his brother-in-law, that modern Napoleon, had ended somewhat spectacularly, he had never entirely lost his reverence for someone he considered a mastermind. For Fillmore Nicholas, the Man of Destiny, to descend to running a restaurant—and a *small* one at that—seemed almost indecent.

Sally, however—for sisters often fail to maintain proper reverence for their brothers' greatness—was thrilled.

"It's the most splendid idea," she declared enthusiastically. "It really looks as if Fillmore is finally going to amount to something. They apparently started on a tiny scale, just making pork pies..."

"Why 'Popp'?" Ginger interrupted, voicing a question that baffled him deeply.

"Just a trade name, silly," Sally dismissed. "Gladys is a wonderful cook; she makes the pies while Fillmore goes around selling them. They did so well that they've launched a full-blown restaurant, which is also a success. Listen to this."

She giggled again and turned the page. "...sound financial footing. In fact, our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a truly massive scale. It is Big Ideas that lead to Big Business. I am contemplating a vast expansion of this venture. In a very short time, I shall organize branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, and all the major cities, each managed by a dedicated manager and featuring, in addition to standard cuisine, our famous Popp's Outstanding Pork Pies. Once those branches are established as going concerns, I shall sail for England and introduce Popp's Pork Pies there..."

"Isn't he a little wonder!" Sally exclaimed.

"Dashed brainy chap. Always said so."

"I must admit, I was rather uneasy when I first read that. I've seen so many of Fillmore's 'Big Ideas.' That's usually his pattern: he gets something good and then overdoes it until it bursts. However, it seems all right now that he has Gladys to look after him. She added a postscript. Just four words, but oh, how comforting to a sister's heart. 'Yes, I don't think!' is what she wrote, and I haven't read anything more cheering in ages. Thank heavens she has poor dear Fillmore well in hand."

"Pork pies!" Ginger mused, as the pangs of hungry appetite began to gnaw at his insides. "I wish he'd sent us one of those outstanding little chaps. I could certainly use one."

Sally rose and tousled his red hair. "Poor old Ginger! I knew you'd never be able to stick to it. Come on, it's a lovely night. Let's walk to the village and feast at the inn. We're going to be millionaires before we know where we are, so we can easily afford it."
