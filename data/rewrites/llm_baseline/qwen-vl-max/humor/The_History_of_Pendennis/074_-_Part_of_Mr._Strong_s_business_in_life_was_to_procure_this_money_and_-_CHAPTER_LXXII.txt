CHAPTER LXXII. In which the Decks Begin to Clear (With a Side of Sarcasm and a Dash of Mourning)

When Arthur Pendennis, resplendent in his dressing-gown like a man who’d just wrestled with the morning and won, made his way up to Warrington’s chambers—his usual post-interview ritual—he found not George, nor even a ghostly echo of him, but Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress, presiding over the ruins of a once-happy literary life.

George was gone. Vanished into the Suffolk countryside like a shy fox fleeing the spotlight. His carpet-bag was packed, his address scribbled on an envelope: “To my brother’s house.” The table was littered with half-finished manuscripts, inkstands oozing like sad tears, pens abandoned mid-sentence, and a blotting-paper that looked like it had seen more drama than a Victorian melodrama. One candle had burned out. The other? Still burning, but only because someone forgot to blow it out—probably George, too busy writing about human folly to notice his own.

“Found him at the desk, sir,” Mrs. Flanagan announced, as if she were reporting on a murder scene. “Writing away like a madman. Hard bed, no sleep. Not a wink all night.”

Ah, yes. The noble life of the writer: where genius is born in exhaustion, and inspiration comes only after 3 a.m., when the world has given up on you and your liver has already surrendered.

Pen stared around, taking in the wreckage—the open drawers, the torn-up pages, the sealing wax chewed into something resembling abstract art. And then he saw it: a gap in the bookcase. Right next to the old College Plato with the Boniface Arms. Helen’s Bible was gone.

Of course it was. George had taken it. Pen didn’t need to read between the lines. He knew why George had fled—not just from family pressure, but from love itself. Love, like a poorly timed sneeze, had caught him off guard. And now, dear, dear old George was off to Norfolk, or wherever the winds blew him, leaving behind only silence and a few scattered thoughts.

Pen sank into George’s chair, feeling suddenly very small. He missed him. He missed his voice, his laughter, his unwavering decency. How could anyone be so good? So honest? So *unbearably* admirable?

“I suppose I’m just a selfish little worm,” Pen mused aloud, though no one was there to hear. “He’s a knight. I’m a tourist in a castle.”

Then, descending to his own chambers, he noticed a note in the letter-box. From George. Written in that familiar, slightly sloping hand:

> “Dear Pen,—
> I shall be half-way home when you breakfast, and intend to stay over Christmas, in Norfolk, or elsewhere.
> I have my own opinion of the issue of matters about which we talked in J——— St. yesterday; and think my presence _de trop_.
> Vale. G. W.
> Give my very best regards and adieux to your cousin.”

And thus, George vanished. The laundress remained. The world moved on.

Pen, dutiful son that he was, went to see his uncle the next day. Not being admitted, he turned to Lady Rockminster’s apartments—where the old lady immediately demanded Bluebeard.

“Bluebeard is gone,” Pen said, pulling out the note.

Laura took it, glanced at it, handed it back without looking at him, and walked away.

Pen, undeterred, launched into a full-blown eulogy of George—so passionate, so heartfelt, that Lady Rockminster blinked in surprise. She’d never heard him praise anyone this much. “You’ve always been cold, Arthur,” she said. “I didn’t know you cared so deeply.”

“Well,” Pen replied, “he’s not just a friend. He’s a man who’d give you his last crust—and then ask if you wanted the crumbs.”

---

Later, walking through Waterloo Place (because what else does one do when one is emotionally drained and duty-bound?), Pen spotted a familiar figure emerging from Messrs. Gimcrack’s famous shop. It was Foker—Harry Foker, heir to the great brewery empire, and now, by the looks of it, the new king of the ale trade.

He was in deep mourning—black hat, black gloves, black brougham, black horse, black driver. Even the springs seemed mournful. A grieving man, clearly, though one might question whether grief or greed was the real emotion beneath the velvet.

“Hail, Foker!” Pen called out, catching up with him. “What brings you to the land of jewels and regret?”

Foker’s tiny hand—gloved in deepest ebony, wristband snow-white—extended toward Arthur. The other hand clutched a morocco case, likely containing a watch, a ring, or perhaps a lock of hair from a recently deceased fiancée.

Pen’s mind raced. This wasn’t just mourning. This was *accessory*. He saw the pattern: the heir, fresh from his father’s funeral, indulging in trinkets while the world watched. Like Horace’s young prince pouring wine from his father’s casks—only here, the wine was diamonds and gold.

“Le Roi est mort. Vive le Roi!” Pen declared.

“Ah,” said Foker, blushing like a schoolgirl caught stealing cake. “Yes. Thank you. Very much obliged. How do you do, Pen?—very busy—good-bye!”

And off he went, jumping into his black carriage like a tiny, anxious monarch, sipping tea and trying not to look guilty.

Pen chuckled. “Ah, the wheel turns. Old Harry’s dead. New Harry’s drunk on inheritance. The bankers bow. The draymen cheer. And somewhere, a poor soul wonders if they’ll ever get their twenty thousand a yard.”

---

Later, speaking with Laura, Pen mused aloud on the nature of money and loyalty.

“You know,” he said, “there was no thought of money between me and our dear mother. Laura.”

“Of course not,” she said, eyes brightening. “You would have spurned it!”

“Oh, come now,” Pen said, feigning seriousness. “Don’t pretend I’m some angelic martyr. I might have felt the pull. But thank God, I didn’t have to choose. I’m glad I didn’t face the temptation.”

“That’s not true,” Laura insisted, her voice soft. “You wouldn’t have sold yourself. You couldn’t.”

“And yet,” Pen said, “we’re all taught to pray for deliverance from evil. I’m glad I was spared that sin. But I wonder—what if I had been tempted? What if I had known the taste of betrayal?”

Laura looked at him, heartbroken. “Arthur, don’t say such things. You frighten me.”

“Do I?” he asked gently. “Then let me tell you what But said to me just now.”

“But?” she whispered.

“Yes. But. That sneaky little word. It creeps in when you least expect it. ‘If that girl had reason as well as love,’ But said, ‘she’d love you no more.’ If she knew you as you are—the flawed, selfish creature you know—you’d lose her. You’d lose everything.”

Laura shivered.

“And yet,” Pen added, “you’re pure. You’re untouched. You’re like a child who doesn’t know what a lie is. I think some of you—some women—were never fallen. You bear sweet thoughts like flowers. You can’t help it.”

“Then what am I?” Laura asked.

“A miracle,” he said. “And I’m afraid I’m the skeptic who keeps trying to dissect it.”

---

Lady Rockminster entered, having completed her daily ritual of transformation—like a phoenix rising from the ashes of bathwater and perfume.

“Mr. Pendennis,” she said, “you’re always coming here.”

“It’s pleasant,” Arthur said. “We were just talking about Foker.”

“He’s a fine young man,” she said. “Fifteen thousand a year. My cousin. He must come and see me.”

She paused, then added, “He’s engaged to Lady Ann.”

“Engaged?” Arthur asked.

“Engaged? No,” she said, frowning. “She broke it off. Married someone else. Mr. Hobson. Her father’s chaplain. Red-haired widower. Two children. Poor Lord Rosherville is distraught.”

“Married?” Arthur repeated. “In Drummington Park?”

“Yes. Last Thursday. After breakfast. With her maid. In the church. No notice. No ceremony. Just—gone.”

Pen blinked. “So Foker’s free?”

“Free?” Lady Rockminster said. “He’s his own master now. And he’ll choose for himself. Probably Alice or Barbara. Alice has smallpox. Barbara is ten years older. Not promising.”

Pen smiled. “Well, that’s one less problem for me.”

But his mind wandered. Who was Foker buying those bracelets for? Was it Lady Ann? Or someone else? Someone younger? Someone French? Someone who wore feathers?

The world was full of mysteries. And none of them mattered quite as much as the one unfolding in Tunbridge Wells.

Because Laura, bless her, was determined to marry Blanche *now*. She wrote letters. She implored. She threatened to run away. She even offered to go to Tunbridge—despite Lady Rockminster’s protests—just to be near her new sister.

“Must go,” Laura wrote. “Will go. Will not be deterred.”

And so began the correspondence:

**Blanche, No. 1:** “Oh, dearest Laura! I shall welcome you with open arms! We’ll sing duets! Wander through golden woods! I count the hours!”

**Laura, No. 2:** “How lovely! I hope our friendship grows stronger each day. We’ll share secrets. We’ll make each other happy.”

**Blanche, No. 3:** “Wait. What? Your letter says nothing about Arthur. What’s happened? What is this mystery?”

And thus, the mystery deepened. Until Arthur, finally, could no longer resist. He picked up his pen. He folded the paper. And wrote the letter that would change everything.

But that, dear reader, is a story for another chapter.
