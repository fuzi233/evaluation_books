All great literary men are shy—myself included, though I’m told it’s hardly noticeable. And for that, I am grateful. Once, my shyness was painfully obvious, a source of misery to me and discomfort to everyone around me—particularly my lady friends, who complained bitterly about it.

A shy man’s life is not a happy one. Men dislike him, women despise him, and worst of all, he despises himself. No amount of practice eases his torment, and time is the only cure—though I once stumbled upon a delightfully absurd remedy in a weekly journal’s advice column:

Poor wretch! I can only imagine his despair upon reading those words. *"Adopt an easy and pleasing manner"*—as if one could simply *decide* not to be shy! My dear afflicted friend, do not attempt such artifice. Any effort to appear otherwise will only render you either absurdly effusive or unbearably awkward. Better to remain your natural self—though be warned, you will still be deemed either surly or dull.

Yet the shy man does exact a small revenge upon society for the tortures it inflicts upon him. His misery is contagious. He casts a pall over any gathering, draining the cheer from even the most jovial souls. This is often misunderstood—his nervous silence mistaken for arrogance, his stammering for indifference. When terror grips him at the first word addressed to him, rendering him mute and flushed, he is accused of being hot-tempered.

But the shy man’s greatest curse is perpetual misunderstanding. His jokes are taken as solemn declarations; his sarcasm, as earnest opinion. If he attempts flattery, it is received as biting satire, earning him lifelong resentment. The world delights in his discomfort, finding endless amusement in his plight—yet beneath the comedy lies something tragic.

A shy man is a lonely man, forever separated from his fellow creatures by an invisible wall. He longs to join the laughter, to speak and be heard—but his voice fails him. In crowded streets, in lively rooms, amid work and merriment, he stands apart, a silent spectator to the world’s joy. His heart brims with love and sympathy, but his lips remain sealed. His indignation at injustice chokes unspoken in his throat, turning inward to sour his spirit.

Yes, shy men—like ugly women—endure a harsh existence in a world that demands thick skin. A trembling, blushing creature, knees knocking and hands twitching, is an embarrassment to all. If he cannot cure himself, he might as well hang himself and be done with it.

But take heart, fellow sufferers—for I speak from experience. Once, I was the shyest of the shy, a man who could scarcely meet a pretty maid without his knees betraying him. Yet now—well, just yesterday, I confronted a railway refreshment-room attendant with stern reproach for her lack of courtesy. I held her gaze, demanded proper service, and—

—then immediately fled without my tea. But that was because I changed my mind, not because I was frightened.

Shyness, let it be known, is not stupidity. The loud and brash may mock it, but sensitivity is no flaw. The horse is not inferior to the sparrow, nor the deer to the pig. Shyness is merely excess of feeling—not to be confused with conceit, though fools often mistake it as such.

Indeed, conceit is the surest cure for shyness. Once convinced of your own brilliance, bashfulness evaporates. When you regard your fellow men as intellectual infants, their presence ceases to unnerve you. True conceit is armor—impenetrable, unshaken by petty slights. It renders a man genial, unpretentious, at ease with high and low alike.

The shy man, by contrast, is humble—too anxious, too doubtful. But youth tempers with time. Experience wears away his diffidence, or the world’s rough handling hardens him. You seldom meet a truly shy man past his youth—except in novels, where he is portrayed as saintly, noble, and adored by women.

Stage shyness is a pretty fiction. The shy hero whispers devotion in asides, endures mockery with gentle patience, and is ultimately revealed as wise and good. Real shyness is less poetic. The real shy man is clumsy, graceless, and far less interesting—but at least he is constant in love. Why? Because the mere thought of courting *one* woman terrifies him; the idea of pursuing *two* is unthinkable.

His bolder brethren, meanwhile, flirt recklessly, distracted by every pair of laughing eyes. The shy man, fixed upon his boots, knows no such temptation. Happy? Not at all—he *wishes* he could flirt, curses his own timidity, and occasionally, in a burst of desperation, attempts it—only to retreat in humiliated failure.

And how the world delights in his discomfort! His blushes, his fumbling hands, his stammering tongue—all are fodder for ridicule. Friends mimic his nervous walk, his awkward handshake. Fathers advise him to *"be bold!"* Mothers chirp platitudes about shame. Brothers call him *"worse than a girl,"* and sisters indignantly deny that any girl could be half so hopeless.

And they are right. There is no such thing as a shy woman—or if there is, I have yet to meet her. The notion that women are timid fawns, blushing and fleeing from masculine boldness, is charming nonsense. A girl of twelve will face a room with cool composure while her brother stammers beside her. A woman will stride late into a concert, unbothered by stares, while her husband trails in apologetic misery.

In matters of love, especially, women are fearless. From the first coy glance to the last wedding vow, they command the field while men flounder. But then, love is *her* business—and in business, we all set aside our weaknesses. Even the shyest man becomes a bold salesman when his livelihood depends on it.

So take courage, fellow sufferers. Shyness fades—or if it does not, you may at least take grim satisfaction in knowing you are not alone. And if all else fails, remember: even the most wretched among us may yet find refuge in the quiet certainty of his own conceit.
