The long road stretched northward from London, its edges fraying into the countryside like a threadbare ribbon. Between clusters of shops and the occasional field stood a peculiar house—white and pale green, with a veranda and sun-blinds, as if built for warmer climes. It had an air of mystery, as though it held a story waiting to unfold.

Father Brown and Flambeau stood at its doorstep, the priest puffing on his pipe while his tall companion flicked ash from a cigarette. The house was oddly constructed, shaped like a **T**, with a long frontage and a short rear wing. At the back lay a conservatory, a riot of tropical blossoms, their colors glowing unnaturally in the fading sunlight.

Leonard Quinton, the poet who lived here, was a man consumed by Eastern fantasies—opium dreams of jeweled kings and blood-red sunsets. His wife, practical and weary, tolerated his eccentricities, including the presence of a silent Indian mystic who drifted through the house like a ghost.

As the visitors stepped away, a disheveled young man—Atkinson, Mrs. Quinton’s brother—burst in, demanding to see the poet. Dr. Harris, Quinton’s physician, blocked him, insisting the poet was asleep. The doctor then led the others around the conservatory, where they glimpsed Quinton inside, reclining among crimson flowers.

Outside, they encountered the Indian mystic, robed in white, his bronze face impassive. He spoke only once: *“I want nothing.”* The words lingered, heavy with meaning.

The evening darkened. Mrs. Quinton passed them, her expression unreadable. The doctor excused himself to administer Quinton’s sedative, locking the study door behind him. Moments later, a storm broke, and with it came a cry—Quinton was dead.

They found him slumped in the conservatory, a dagger in his side. His last words, scrawled on paper: *“I die by my own hand; yet I die murdered!”*

Father Brown studied the note, frowning. *“This paper is the wrong shape,”* he murmured.

The storm raged. The mystery had only just begun.
