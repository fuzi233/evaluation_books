Her presence was meant to stir compassion, not dread, in eyes unclouded by prejudice. Her chest heaved like a trapped bird, her cheeks flushed with alternating waves of crimson and pallor, and her hand clutched the stone wall—the instinctive grip of a frightened creature. She trembled violently, her gaze a bewildered mixture of adoration, reverent awe, pity, and tender remembrance fixed upon the gaunt, glaring figure of the hermit. Such a scene was, in itself, a marvel of nature.

"Aha!" he cried, his voice jagged. "At last thou comest in flesh and blood; come to me as thou camest to Saint Anthony. Yet I am wary. I suspected thy wiles were not yet spent. I am armed." Snatching up his small crucifix, he thrust it outward at her, his expression one of stunned disbelief, while holding a flickering candle in his other hand. Both objects shook with the violence of his agitation. "Exorcizo te."

"Ah, no!" she cried, her voice a pitiful plea, raising two delicate, supplicating palms. "Alas, do me no harm! It is Margaret."

"Liar!" the hermit shouted, his face contorted. "Margaret was beautiful, yes, but not with a supernatural radiance like thine. Thou didst shrink at that sacred name, thou subtle hypocrite!" He raised the candle higher, invoking the Divine Name with fervor. "In Nomine Dei exorcizo vos."

"Oh, Jesus!" Margaret gasped, the terror absolute in her extremity. "Curse me not! I will return home. I thought I might have visited. By manhood, Latinize me not! Oh, Gerard—is this how we meet after all? After all?"

She cowered almost to her knees, weeping with a superstitious fear mingled with wounded affection.

Though steeped in a deep-seated hatred of Satan, Clement might have remained doubtful whether this distressed creature, so wholly human and natural, was not merely artifice and a fiend. But her spontaneous invocation of that holy name dissolved his illusion. Let him see with his eyes, let him hear with his ears.

A cry of self-reproach escaped his lips. He attempted to lift her, but fasts and the overwhelming shock of his prolonged solitude being broken rendered him unable. "What," he gasped, shaking over her, "is it thou? And have I met thee with harsh words? Alas!" They were both choked by emotion, speechless for a moment.

"I heed it not," Margaret said bravely, fighting back tears. "Thou took me for another—for a devil! Oh! Oh! Oh!"

"Forgive me, sweet soul!" As soon as he could form more than a fragment of a sentence, he continued, "I have been plagued by the Evil One since I arrived here."

Margaret glanced around with a shudder. "Likely enough. Then, take my hand, and let me lead thee from this filthy place."

He stared at her, astounded.

"What? Desert my cell and return to the world? Is that why thou hast come to me?" he asked, his tone heavy with sadness and reproach.

"Yes, Gerard. I am come to take thee to thy pretty vicarage. Thanks to Heaven and thy good brother Giles, thou art Vicar of Gouda. Mother and I have made it so neat for thee, Gerard. 'Tis quite comfortable in winter, I promise thee. But wait until the hawthorn blooms, and anon thy walls shall don their robe of brave roses and sweet woodbine. Hast we forgotten thee, and the foolish things thou lovest? And, dear Gerard, thy mother awaits; 'tis late for her to leave her bed: pray, pray, come! And the moment we escape this foul hole, I will show thee a treasure thou hast acquired, of which thou knowest naught—or surely thou wouldst never have fled from us so. Alas, what have I done? What have I ignorantly said to warrant this treatment?"

For he had drawn himself up into a rigid heap, regarding her with a gaze that blended fear and suspicion.

"Unhappy girl," he said solemnly, yet deeply agitated, "wouldst thou have me risk my soul and thine for a miserable vicarage and the flowers growing upon it? But this is not thy doing: the bowels-less fiend sends thee, poor simple girl, to me with this lure. But oh, cunning fiend, I will unmask thee, even unto this thine instrument, and she shall see thee and abhor thee as I do. Margaret, my lost love, why am I here? Because I love thee."

"Oh! No, Gerard, thou lovest me not, or thou wouldst not have hidden from me; there was no need."

"Let there be no deceit between us twain, who have loved so truly; and after this night, let us meet no more on earth."

"Now God forbid!" she exclaimed.

"I love thee, and thou hast not forgotten me, or thou wouldst have married ere this, and wouldst not be the one to find me, buried here from sight of man. I am a priest, a monk: what but folly or sin can ensue from thee and me living neighbors, feeding a passion innocent once, but now (so Heaven wills it) impious and unholy? No, though my heart breaks, I must be firm. 'Tis I that am the man, 'tis I that am the priest. We must meet no more, till I am schooled by solitude, and thou art wedded to another."

"I consent to my doom, but not to thine. I would ten times liefer die; yet I will marry, ay, wed misery itself, sooner than let thee lie in this foul, dismal place, with yon sweet manse awaiting thee." Clement groaned; with every word she spoke, two things stood clearer and clearer—his duty, and the agony it must cost.

"My beloved," he said, with a strange mixture of tenderness and dogged resolution, "I bless thee for granting me one more sight of thy sweet face. May God forgive thee, and bless thee, for destroying in a minute the holy peace it hath taken six months of solitude to build. No matter. A year of penance will, Dei gratia, restore me to my calm. My poor Margaret, I seem cruel: yet I am kind. 'Tis best we part; ay, this moment."

"Part, Gerard? Never! We have seen what comes of parting. Part? Why, thou hast not heard half my story; no, nor the tithe. 'Tis not for thy mere comfort I take thee to the Gouda manse. Hear me!"

"I may not. Thy very voice is a temptation with its music, memory's delight."

"But I say thou shalt hear me, Gerard, for henceforth I go not unheard."

"Then must we part by other means," said Clement sadly.

"Alack! what other means? Wouldest thou put me to thine own door, being the stronger?"

"Nay, Margaret, well thou knowest I would suffer many deaths rather than use force upon thee; thy sweet body is dearer to me than my own; but a million times dearer to me are our immortal souls, both thine and mine. I have withstood this direst temptation long enough. Now I must flee it: farewell! Farewell!"

He moved toward the door, having actually opened it and stepped halfway out, when she darted after him, catching hold of his arm.

"Nay, then another must speak for me. I thought to reward thee for yielding to me; but unkind that thou art, I find I need help; turn then this way, one moment."

"Nay, nay."

"But I say ay! And then turn thy back on us, if thou canst." She relaxed her grasp slightly, thinking he would never deny her so small a favor. But at this, he saw his opportunity and seized it.

"Flee, Clement, flee!" he almost shrieked. Religious enthusiasm momentarily restoring his old strength, he burst wildly away from her. After a few steps, he bounded over the little stream and ran alongside it, but finding he was not pursued, he stopped and looked back.

She lay upon her face, her hands spread out.

Yes, without meaning it, he had thrown her down and injured her.

When he saw this, he groaned and turned back a step; but suddenly, by another impulse, he flung himself into the icy water.

"There!" he cried, standing there up to his throat in liquid ice, "kill my body, but save my soul!"

While he stood thus, Margaret uttered one long, piteous moan and rose to her knees.

He saw her plainly, almost as clearly as in midday sun. He beheld her pale face and eyes glistening, and then, in the still night, heard these words:

"Oh, God! Thou that knowest all, Thou seest how I am used. Forgive me then! For I will not live another day." With this, she suddenly started to her feet and fled like some wild creature, wounded to death, close by his miserable hiding-place, shrieking:
