No Place for Repentance.

Messer Naldo returned sooner than anyone anticipated, arriving on the evening of the twenty-eighth of November, merely eleven days after his previous visit. This brevity proved he had not ventured far beyond the mountains, and the scene that unfolded in the Via de' Bardi that night serves to illuminate the impulse that drew his steps toward the hill of San Giorgio.

When Tito first secured this dwelling for Tessa upon his return from Rome, more than a year and a half prior, he convinced himself he acted solely under the constraint of his own benevolence following the unfortunate incident that led the innocent Tessa to believe he was her husband. While it was true that his kindness flowed naturally toward such a trusting, pretty creature, it was equally true that Tito harbored feelings of affection for her distinct from any calculated gain. Though he might have preferred freedom from her company had he not been captivated by her charm, he was not in love with Tessa. His heart belonged, for the first time, to an entirely different woman—Romola. Her presence possessed him with such intensity that the mere sweep of her long tresses across his cheek seemed to vibrate through the hours. All the youthful, ideal passion within him had been awakened by her. His fiber was too refined, his intellect too bright, to succumb to the habits of a gross pleasure-seeker. Yet, he had spun a web around himself and Tessa that he felt incapable of breaking. In the initial moments after the mock marriage, he had been prompted to leave her under an illusion by a distinct calculation of his own potential needs; but since that critical moment, it seemed the web had spun itself in spite of him, growing like a vine over which he held no power. The elements of kindness and self-indulgence were difficult to distinguish in a soft nature like Tito's. The annoyance he had felt under Tessa's persistent pursuit on the day of his betrothal, coupled with his thorough intention to reveal the truth when he fulfilled his promise to see her again, served as sufficient proof to him that he had ultimately left Tessa under her delusion and provided her a home because he was overcome by his own kindness. In these days of his first devotion to Romola, he required a self-justifying argument. He had learned to find solace in the fact that she was deceived about certain matters. Yet, every strong feeling constructs its own conscience, possesses its own piety—much like the bond between a son and a mother, which can sometimes endure even amidst the foulest corruption. Tito could not yet find peace committing a secret offense against his wedded love.

Consequently, he exercised extreme caution to preserve the secrecy of this transgression. Monna Lisa, who, like many of her class, rarely left her habitation except to visit a few specific shops or attend confession once a year, knew nothing of his true name or whereabouts. She only knew that he paid her handsomely to ensure her comfort and cared little for the rest, save that she had grown fond of Tessa and found pleasure in the duties for which she was compensated. There was clearly some mystery at play, given that Tessa was a peasant girl (*contadina*) while Messer Naldo was a gentleman; but for all Monna Lisa knew, he might have been a real husband. Tito had thoroughly frightened Tessa into silence regarding the circumstances of their union by warning her that if she broke that silence, she would never see him again. Furthermore, Monna Lisa's deafness, which made it impossible to communicate with her without significant premeditation, had saved Tessa from any incautious revelation to her, such as the slip of the tongue she had made while speaking with Baldassarre. For a long while, Tito's visits were so infrequent that it seemed plausible he undertook journeys between them. These were prompted chiefly by a desire to ensure all was well with Tessa. Although he always found the visit more pleasant than the prospect of it—always renewing his appreciation for that pretty, ignorant lovingness and trust—he had not yet felt a pressing need for it. However, he was determined, if possible, to preserve the simplicity upon which this charm depended; to keep Tessa a genuine peasant and not place the small field-flower among conditions that would rob it of its grace. He would have been horrified to see her dressed in the attire of any rank higher than her own; the piquancy of her conversation would vanish if things began to acquire new relations for her, if her world expanded and her pleasures became less childish. The squirrel-like enjoyment of nuts at her discretion marked the standard of the luxuries he had provided. By this means, Tito saved Tessa's charm from being sullied, and he also, through a convenient coincidence, spared himself from aggravating expenses that were already becoming burdensome to a man whose funds were entirely consumed by his avowed habits of life.

Briefly, this was the history of Tito's relationship with Tessa up until a very recent date. It is true that once or twice before Bardo's death, the knowledge that Tessa resided up the hill, with whom it was possible to spend an agreeable hour, had served as an inducement for him to escape the slight weariness of the old man, when, lacking any positive engagement, he might otherwise have borne the weariness patiently and shared Romola's burden. But the moment when he first felt a genuine hunger for Tessa's ignorant lovingness and her belief in *him* had arrived only quite recently, distinctly marked by circumstances as unforgettable as the onset of a malady that permanently vitiated sight and hearing. It was the day he first saw Baldassarre and purchased the armor. Returning across the bridge that night with the coat of mail in his hands, he had felt an unconquerable shrinking from an immediate encounter with Romola. She, too, knew little of the actual world; she, too, trusted him. Yet, he possessed an uneasy consciousness that behind her frank eyes lay a nature capable of judging him, and that any ill-founded trust of hers sprang not from a pretty, brute-like incapacity, but from a nobleness that might prove an alarming touchstone. He desired a little ease, a little repose from self-control, after the agitation and exertions of the day; he wished to be where he could adjust his mind to the morrow without caring how he behaved in the present moment. And there was a sweet, adoring creature within reach whose presence was as safe and unconstraining as that of her own kids—one who would believe any fable and remain utterly unimpressed by public opinion. Thus, on that evening, when Romola waited and listened for him, he turned his steps up the hill.

No wonder, then, that his steps took the same course on this evening, eleven days later, when he had been forced to recoil under Romola's first outburst of scorn. He could not wish Tessa in his wife's place, nor refrain from wishing that his wife should be thoroughly reconciled to him; for it was Romola, and not Tessa, who belonged to the world where all the larger desires of a man possessing ambition and effective faculties must necessarily lie. Yet, he sought a refuge from a standard disagreeably rigorous, from which he could not make himself independent simply by dismissing it as folly; and Tessa's little soul was that inviting refuge.

It was not much past eight o'clock when he ascended the stone steps to the door of Tessa's room. Usually, she heard his entrance and ran to meet him, but not that night. When he opened the door, he saw the reason. A single dim light burned above the dying fire, illuminating Tessa in a kneeling attitude by the head of the bed where the baby lay. Her head had fallen aside onto the pillow, and her brown rosary, which usually hung above the pillow over the picture of the Madonna and the golden palm-branches, lay in the loose grasp of her right hand. She had fallen fast asleep over her beads. Tito stepped lightly across the small room and sat down close to her. She had probably heard the opening of the door as part of her dream, for she had not been looking at him two moments before she opened her eyes. She opened them without any start and remained quite motionless, gazing at him as if the sense of his presence smiling at her shut out any impulse that could disturb that happy passiveness. But when he placed his hand under her chin and stooped to kiss her, she said—

"I dreamed it, and then I said it was dreaming—and then I awoke, and it was true."

"Little sinner!" said Tito, pinching her chin, "you have not said half your prayers. I will punish you by not looking at your baby; he is ugly."

Tessa did not like those words, even though Tito was smiling. A pouting distress clouded her face as she said, bending anxiously over the infant—

"Oh, it is not true! He is prettier than anything. You do not think he is ugly. You will look at him. He is even prettier than when you saw him before—only he is asleep, and you cannot see his eyes or his tongue, and I cannot show you his hair—and it grows—isn't that wonderful? Look at him! It's true his face is very much alike when he is asleep; there is not so much to see as when he is awake. If you kiss him very gently, he won't wake: you want to kiss him, is it not true?"

He satisfied her by giving the small mummy a butterfly kiss, then placing his hand on her shoulder and turning her face toward him, he said, "You like looking at the baby better than looking at your husband, you false one!"

She was still kneeling, and now rested her hands on his knee, looking up at him like one of Fra Lippo Lippi's round-cheeked adoring angels.

"No," she said, shaking her head; "I love you always best, only I want you to look at the bambino and love him; I used only to want you to love me."

"And did you expect me to come again so soon?" said Tito, inclined to make her prattle. He still felt the effects of the agitation he had undergone—still felt like a man who has been violently jarred; and this was the easiest relief from silence and solitude.

"Oh, no," said Tessa. "I have counted the days—to-day I began at my right thumb again—since you put on the beautiful chain-coat that Messer San Michele gave you to take care of you on your journey. And you have got it on now," she said, peering through the opening in the breast of his tunic. "Perhaps it made you come back sooner."

"Perhaps it did, Tessa," he said. "But don't mind the coat now. Tell me what has happened since I was here. Did you see the tents in the Prato, and the soldiers and horsemen when they passed the bridges—did you hear the drums and trumpets?"

"Yes, and I was rather frightened, because I thought the soldiers might come up here. And Monna Lisa was a little afraid too, for she said they might carry our kids off; she said it was their business to do mischief. But the Holy Madonna took care of us, for we never saw one of them up here. But something has happened, only I hardly dare tell you, and that is what I was saying more Aves for."

"What do you mean, Tessa?" said Tito, rather anxiously. "Make haste and tell me."

"Yes, but will you let me sit on your knee? Because then I think I shall not be so frightened."

He took her on his knee and put his arm round her, but looked grave; it seemed that something unpleasant must pursue him even here.

"At first I didn't mean to tell you," said Tessa, speaking almost in a whisper, as if that would mitigate the offense; "because we thought the old man would be gone away before you came again, and it would be as if it had not been. But now he is there, and you are come, and I never did anything you told me not to do before. And I want to tell you, and then you will perhaps forgive me, for it is a long while before I go to confession."

"Yes, tell me everything, my Tessa." He began to hope it was after all a trivial matter.

"Oh, you will be sorry for him: I'm afraid he cries about something when I don't see him. But that was not the reason I went to him first; it was because I wanted to talk to him and show him my baby, and he was a stranger that lived nowhere, and I thought you wouldn't care so much about my talking to him. And I think he is not a bad old man, and he wanted to come and sleep on the straw next to the goats, and I made Monna Lisa say, 'Yes, he might,' and he's away all the day almost, but when he comes back I talk to him, and take him something to eat."

"Some beggar, I suppose. It was naughty of you, Tessa, and I am angry with Monna Lisa. I must have him sent away."

"No, I think he is not a beggar, for he wanted to pay Monna Lisa, only she asked him to do work for her instead. And he gets himself shaved, and his clothes are tidy: Monna Lisa says he is a decent man. But sometimes I think he is not in his right mind: Lupo, at Peretola, was not in his right mind, and he looks a little like Lupo sometimes, as if he didn't know where he was."

"What sort of face has he?" said Tito, his heart beginning to beat strangely. He was so haunted by the thought of Baldassarre that it was already him whom he saw in imagination sitting on the straw not many yards from him. "Fetch your stool, my Tessa, and sit on it."

"Shall you not forgive me?" she said, timidly, moving from his knee.
