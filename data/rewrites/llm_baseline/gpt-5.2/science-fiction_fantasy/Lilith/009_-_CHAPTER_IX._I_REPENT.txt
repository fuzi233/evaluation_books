I laid the manuscript down, and for a time its paper-smell—dust, ink long dried, the faint sourness of old paste—was enough to steady me. My father had not merely brushed the edge of that other country: he had known Mr. Raven. That comfort came like broth to an empty stomach; it warmed first, and then it revealed the ache beneath.

For I remembered, with a sudden shame that tasted like iron on the tongue, that I had never been told how my father died, nor why there was always a pause—like a door gently closed—whenever his end approached in conversation. A thought pushed up, inexorable: he must at last have followed Mr. Raven, and not come back. And I—fool that I was—had fled.

What might I not have learned by now of life and death and the wide fields beyond ordinary seeing? The Ravens were good folk. A night in their house would not have harmed me. Strange, yes; but his strangeness had the clean edge of faculty, and hers the calm of a beauty that made one lower one’s voice without knowing why. And I had suspected them—treated them as if they harboured a plot against me, as if hospitality were a trap.

The more I chewed on my behaviour, the more bitter it grew. Why should I have feared such dead? Their stillness—sweet, stately—returned to me like the remembered fragrance of bread cooling under linen: you do not eat it yet, but it calls you to patience. To share their holy rest would have been an honour; I had proved myself unworthy. What could that sleeping king have done me? What could the lady with the wound in her palm have done—she whose hand had lain open, quiet as a bowl waiting?

I fell longing after their faces, and wept; and weeping, I threw myself on a couch and slid at once into sleep, as if my grief had been a tide pulling me under.

I woke as suddenly as I had fallen, with the sharp conviction of having been called—no voice I could name, but an insistence as definite as a knock. The house lay still as an empty church, its very timbers holding their breath. Outside, a blackbird sang on the lawn: clear notes dropped into the silence and spread through it, each sound cleanly bounded, as if the air itself were a cup that could be filled without spilling. The song made me think how noise—mere noise—can bruise the mind, and how this single bird, unbought and unadvertised, restored order more truly than any performed music in a bright hall with its clapping and talk.

“I will go and tell them I am ashamed,” I said aloud, “and will do whatever they would have me do.”

I rose and went straight up the stairs to the garret. The wooden chamber was as it had been when first I saw it: boards dry as old bones, the dim mirror returning everything with a film over it, like breath on glass. It was nearly noon; the sun would be a little higher than when first I came. I must raise the hood a little and adjust the mirrors accordingly. If only I had watched Mr. Raven do it—seen the small sure motions, the way one watches an elder’s hands when he makes something that cannot be learned from words alone.

I pulled the chains and let the light fall upon the first mirror. Then I turned to the other. There were the shapes of the former vision—plain enough to recognize, and yet all tremulous, like a landscape seen in a pool ruffled by the faintest breathing wind. I touched the glass: it yielded nothing, hard and sealed. Suspecting polarisation—some shifting of relation that would make the one surface speak truly to the other—I altered and altered them, tiny changes, angle after angle, as one keeps tasting a sauce and adding a grain of salt, a drop of acid, until, not by inspiration but by patient correction, the thing comes right. At last, in great degree—so far as I was concerned—by chance the relations fell into place, and the mountains stood blue, steady, and clear between the mirrors.

I stepped forward. My feet were among the heather.

The air there had the scent of crushed thyme and cold stone. Each stalk that brushed my boot released its own small bitter-sweetness, as if the ground itself were a pantry of dried herbs. I knew only that the way to the cottage had passed through a pine-forest. I pushed through thickets and small fir-woods, forever thinking I recognized a rise of ground, a twist of stream, a particular boulder—only to find my certainty crumble. I met no great forest. The day slipped; the sun sank; the air sharpened with the coming winter until it felt like drinking water from a metal cup.

Then, when the sun was near the horizon and my hope had begun to thin, I saw a little black object coming toward me. It bobbed and glided with that peculiar decision of a bird on business.

It was the raven.

Relief flooded me so strongly I nearly laughed, but my shame held me. I hastened to meet him.

“I beg your pardon, sir,” I said, “for my rudeness last night. Will you take me with you now? I heartily confess I do not deserve it.”

“Ah!” he returned, and looked up at me—one bright bead of an eye taking in more than my face. After a brief pause he said, “My wife does not expect you to-night. She regrets that we at all encouraged your staying last week.”

The words fell like cold ash. “Take me to her that I may tell her how sorry I am,” I begged, forcing humility into my voice as one forces down a mouthful that will not go.

“It is of no use,” he answered. “Your night was not come then, or you would not have left us. It is not come now, and I cannot show you the way. The dead were rejoicing under their daisies—they all lie among the roots of the flowers of heaven—at the thought of your delight when the winter should be past and the morning with its birds come: ere you left them, they shivered in their beds. When the spring of the universe arrives—but that cannot be for ages yet! how many, I do not know—and do not care to know.”

His speech had a cadence like an old dialect heard in childhood: half familiar, half beyond translating. The words were mine, yet their meanings kept slipping, as if he spoke in the deeper sense of them and I in the shallow. I could grasp only the sting: that my fear had chilled those I had wronged.

“Tell me one thing, I beg of you, Mr. Raven,” I said, hunger rising in me like a physical pain. “Is my father with you? Have you seen him since he left the world?”

“Yes; he is with us, fast asleep. That was he you saw with his arm on the coverlet, his hand half closed.”

The image returned at once—linen, a hand resting as if it had just set down a tool. My throat tightened. “Why did you not tell me? That I should have been so near him, and not know!”

“And turn your back on him!” corrected the raven.

The rebuke struck home. “I would have lain down at once had I known!”

“I doubt it. Had you been ready to lie down, you would have known him! Old Sir Up’ard, and your twice great-grandfather, both are up and away long ago. Your great-grandfather has been with us for many a year; I think he will soon begin to stir. You saw him last night, though of course you did not know him.”

“Why of course?” I demanded, smarting.

“Because he is so much nearer waking than you. No one who will not sleep can ever wake.”

“I do not at all understand you!”

“You turned away, and would not understand!”

I held my peace. His words were like a dish made in a tongue you do not speak: you can smell the richness, you can tell it nourishes, but you cannot name the ingredients. Yet if I said nothing, he would go, and I could not bear that.

“And my grandfather—is he also with you?” I asked.

“No; he is still in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead.”

“Where is the Evil Wood, that I may find him?”

“You will not find him; but you will hardly miss the wood. It is the place where those who will not sleep, wake up at night, to kill their dead and bury them.”

“I cannot understand you!”

“Naturally not. Neither do I understand you; I can read neither your heart nor your face. When my wife and I do not understand our children, it is because there is not enough of them to be understood. God alone can understand foolishness.”

At that, I felt stripped—naked and of small worth, as if my very language had been taken from me and I must stand silent in another’s market. The power in his words was not loud; it was the quiet authority of one whose meanings are rooted deeper than the listener’s.

“Then,” I said at last, “will you be so good as show me the nearest way home? There are more ways than one, I know, for I have gone by two already.”

“There are indeed many ways.”

“Tell me, please, how to recognise the nearest.”

“I cannot,” answered the raven; “you and I use the same words with different meanings. We are often unable to tell people what they need to know, because they want to know something else, and would therefore only misunderstand what we said. Home is ever so far away in the palm of your hand, and how to get there it is of no use to tell you. But you will get there; you must get there; you have to get there. Everybody who is not at home, has to go home. You thought you were at home where I found you: if that had been your home, you could not have left it. Nobody can leave home. And nobody ever was or ever will be at home without having gone there.”

“Enigma treading on enigma!” I cried, anger flaring to cover fear. “I did not come here to be asked riddles.”

“No; but you came, and found the riddles waiting for you! Indeed you are yourself the only riddle. What you call riddles are truths, and seem riddles because you are not true.”

“Worse and worse!” I exclaimed.

“And you must answer the riddles!” he continued. “They will go on asking themselves until you understand yourself. The universe is a riddle trying to get out, and you are holding your door hard against it.”

“Will you not in pity tell me what I am to do—where I must go?”

“How should I tell your to-do, or the way to it?”

“If I am not to go home, at least direct me to some of my kind.”

“I do not know of any. The beings most like you are in that direction.” He pointed with his beak.

I looked. I could see nothing but the setting sun, its glare whitening the world and making a mockery of sight. The day’s last light had the harshness of a bright shop-window: it showed, but it did not reveal.

“Well,” I said bitterly, “I cannot help feeling hardly treated—taken from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and refused instruction as to where I am to go or what I am to do!”

“You forget,” said the raven, “that, when I brought you and you declined my hospitality, you reached what you call home in safety: now you are come of yourself! Good night.”

He turned and walked slowly away, his beak toward the ground as if listening to the earth with it. I stood dazed. It was true: I had come of myself. Yet had I not come to atone? My heart ached; my brain held neither quest nor purpose—no hope with a name, no desire with a direction. I gazed after him and would have followed, but a knowledge like lead settled in me: it was useless.

All at once he pounced upon a spot, throwing the whole weight of his body on his bill, and for some moments dug vigorously, scraping and thrusting as if he were unearthing a buried seed. Then, with a flutter of wings, he flung back his head, and something shot from his bill, cast high into the air.

That moment the sun set. The air darkened at once, as if a hand had drawn a curtain. But the thing he had thrown opened into a soft radiance, and came pulsing toward me like a fire-fly—only larger, and with a yellower, fuller light, not sharp but mellow as lantern-light through skin of ripe fruit.

It flew over my head.

I turned and followed it.

Here I must break the straight thread of my telling, because what follows is forever resisting the words I have. To set it down is like trying to write a forgotten dialect with borrowed letters: the shapes may resemble the sounds, but the life of it escapes between. The things I record were, in their nature and in the nature of the creatures concerned in them, so different from any events of this economy, that I can only present them by the forms and language of this world—by the way they struck my senses and altered my heart. Even so, I write under a continual sense of failure. At best I can give one phase of a meaning that had many, like rings within rings; one circle of embodiment where there were graduated spheres.

A single thing would sometimes seem to be and to mean many things at once, with an uncertain identity at the core, shifting and changing its look. I am driven, again and again, to set down what I know is but a clumsy representation of the feeling aimed at—none of our media fit to carry the peculiar strangeness with clearness or certainty. Even to one who knew the region better than I, I could not be sure of transmitting the reality of my experience. For while I had no doubt I was regarding a scene of activity, I might at the same moment be conscious—as if with another sense—that I was perusing a metaphysical argument, sounded rather than read, like meaning conveyed through an air that had its own acoustics, its own laws of listening.
