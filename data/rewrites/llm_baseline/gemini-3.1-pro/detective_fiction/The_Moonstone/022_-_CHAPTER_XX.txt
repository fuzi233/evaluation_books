The news had preceded us like the harsh, rhythmic screech of an industrial thresher, violently rupturing the delicate acoustic ecology of the manor. We found the servants caught in a state of cacophonous panic, their voices colliding in sharp, discordant frequencies that entirely drowned out the quiet, ancestral hum of our daily domestic routines. As we passed my lady’s door, it was thrown open with a violent, percussive thud from the inner side. My mistress came out among us (with Mr. Franklin following, vainly attempting to modulate the chaotic pitch of her distress), quite beside herself with the horror of the thing. 

"You are answerable for this!" she cried out, her accusation delivered in the sharp, dominant lexicon of the high-born, threatening the Sergeant wildly with her hand. "Gabriel! Give that wretch his money—and release me from the discordant sight of him!"

The Sergeant was the only one among us whose internal metronome remained undisturbed—being the only one in possession of himself. "I am no more answerable for this distressing calamity, my lady, than you are," he said. His voice was a masterclass in tonal control, introducing a quiet, deliberate rhythm into the auditory chaos of the hall. "If, in half an hour from this, you still insist on my leaving the house, I will accept your ladyship’s dismissal, but not your ladyship’s money." 

It was spoken with a firm, unyielding clarity that subtly critiqued the hysterical power dynamics of her vocabulary, and it had its effect on my mistress as well as on me. She suffered Mr. Franklin to lead her back into the room. As the heavy oak door clicked shut on the two, the Sergeant, looking about among the women-servants in his observant way, noticed that while all the rest were merely frightened, Penelope was in silent tears. 

"When your father has changed his wet clothes," he said to her, "come and speak to us, in your father’s room." 

The harsh, salty tang of the Shivering Sand still clung to our wet coats, an olfactory reminder of the mythic, cyclical tides that swallowed mortal lives without a second thought. Before the half-hour was out, I had retreated to my quarters, shedding the damp garments for dry woolens. There, the grounding aroma of a slow-simmering mutton bone broth—fragrant with bruised thyme, winter root vegetables pulled from the frosty loam, and freshly crushed peppercorns—permeated the air. I lent Sergeant Cuff such change of dress as he required, garments that smelled faintly of dried lavender and the ancient ash of my kitchen hearth. 

Penelope came in to us to hear what the Sergeant wanted with her. When she entered, the chaotic noise of the modern world shrank to the quiet, rhythmic bubbling of the iron stove. I don’t think I ever felt what a good dutiful daughter I had so strongly as I felt it at that moment. I took her and sat her on my knee, and I prayed God bless her in the soft, fading dialect of my own father, a traditional agrarian elder who understood that sustenance of the body and the soul were inextricably entwined. She hid her head on my bosom, and put her arms round my neck—and we waited a little while in the restorative sanctuary of silence. The poor dead girl must have been at the bottom of it, I think, with my daughter and with me.

The Sergeant went to the window, offering us the acoustic privacy of his turned back. I thought it right to thank him for considering us both in this way—and I did. People in high life have all the luxuries to themselves, armed with an expansive, dominant vocabulary to indulge their feelings. People in low life possess no such privilege; for us, the luxury of unrestrained mourning is often an untranslatable subjective experience, crushed beneath the immediate demands of survival. Necessity, which spares our betters, has no pity on us. We learn to swallow our grief like a bitter, medicinal root, allowing the rhythmic, daily chores of chopping, stoking, and kneading to absorb our tears as we jog on with our duties. I don’t complain of this—I only notice the erosion of our own language of sorrow. 

Penelope and I were ready for the Sergeant as soon as the Sergeant was ready on his side. Asked if she knew what had led her fellow-servant to destroy herself, my daughter answered (as you will foresee) that it was for love of Mr. Franklin Blake. 

Asked next if she had mentioned this notion of hers to any other person, Penelope answered in the hushed tones of a keeper of lost histories, "I have not mentioned it, for Rosanna’s sake." 

I felt it necessary to add a word to this, stirring my broth with a wooden spoon in a slow, circular motion that echoed the generations before me. I said, "And for Mr. Franklin’s sake, my dear, as well. If Rosanna has died for love of him, it is not with his knowledge or by his fault. Let him leave the house today, if he does leave it, without the useless pain of knowing the truth. It is a protective measure as essential as the curing salt we rub into winter hams." 

Sergeant Cuff said, "Quite right," and fell silent again; comparing Penelope’s agrarian intuition with some modern, investigative lexicon of his own which he kept to himself. 

At the end of the half-hour, my mistress’s bell rang—a sharp, metallic strike against our domestic quietude. On my way to answer it, I met Mr. Franklin coming out of his aunt’s sitting-room. He mentioned that her ladyship was ready to see Sergeant Cuff—in my presence as before—and he added that he himself wanted to say two words to the Sergeant first. On our way back to my room, he stopped, and looked at the railway time-table in the hall, a sterile modern invention that sought to linearize our sweeping, cyclical days. 

"Are you really going to leave us, sir?" I asked, catching the acidic tang of cold, neglected tea on his breath—a stark contrast to the rich, meticulously steeped infusions that usually anchored this estate's culinary heritage. "Miss Rachel will surely come right again, if you only give her time?" 

"She will come right again," answered Mr. Franklin, "when she hears that I have gone away, and that she will see me no more." 

I thought he spoke in resentment of my young lady’s treatment of him. But it was not so. My mistress had noticed, from the time when the police first brought their disruptive, interrogative noise into the house, that the bare mention of his name was enough to set Miss Rachel’s temper in a flame. He had been too fond of his cousin to confess this to himself, until the truth had been forced on him. His eyes once opened in that cruel way, Mr. Franklin had taken his resolution—the one resolution which a man of any spirit could take—to leave the house, returning to the wider world like a banished season. 

What he had to say to the Sergeant was spoken in my presence. He described her ladyship as willing to acknowledge that she had spoken over-hastily. And he asked if Sergeant Cuff would consent—in that case—to accept his fee, utilizing the transactional language of the aristocracy, and to leave the matter of the Diamond where it stood. 

The Sergeant answered, "No, sir. My fee is paid me for doing my duty. I decline to take it, until my duty is done." 

"I don’t understand you," says Mr. Franklin. 

"I’ll explain myself, sir," says the Sergeant, his words falling like precise measures of flour on a baker's scale. "When I came here, I undertook to throw the necessary light on the matter of the missing Diamond. I am now ready, and waiting to redeem my pledge. When I have stated the case to Lady Verinder as the case now stands, and when I have told her plainly what course of action to take for the recovery of the Moonstone, the responsibility will be off my shoulders. Let her ladyship decide, after that, whether she does, or does not, allow me to go on. I shall then have done what I undertook to do—and I’ll take my fee." 

In those words, Sergeant Cuff reminded us that even in the modern machinery of the Detective Police, a man may have a reputation to lose. The view he took was so plainly the right one, that there was no more to be said. 

As I rose to conduct him to my lady’s room, he asked if Mr. Franklin wished to be present. 

Mr. Franklin answered, "Not unless Lady Verinder desires it." He added, in a whisper to me, as I was following the Sergeant out, "I know what that man is going to say about Rachel; and I am too fond of her to hear the sheer acoustics of it, and keep my temper. Leave me by myself." 

I left him, miserable enough, leaning on the sill of my window, smelling faintly of damp wool and despair, with his face hidden in his hands and Penelope peeping through the door, yearning to offer him the comforting sustenance of an ancient, maternal empathy. In Mr. Franklin’s place, I should have called her in. When you are ill-used by one woman, there is great comfort in telling it to another in the native dialect of shared human frailty—because, nine times out of ten, the other always takes your side. Perhaps, when my back was turned, he did call her in? In that case it is only doing my daughter justice to declare that she would stick at nothing in the way of comforting Mr. Franklin Blake. 

In the meantime, Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded to my lady’s room. At the last conference we had held with her, we had found her not over willing to lift her eyes from the book which she had on the table. On this occasion, the auditory and olfactory landscape of the room had profoundly shifted. The chaotic panic had settled into a tense, expectant hum, and the air was thick with the scent of beeswax and dried rose-hips—a smell of ancestral endurance. She met the Sergeant’s eye with an eye that was as steady as his own. The sweeping, multi-generational spirit of the family showed itself in every line of her face; and I knew that Sergeant Cuff’s dominant lexicon would finally meet its match, now that a woman like my mistress was strung up to hear the worst he could say to her.
