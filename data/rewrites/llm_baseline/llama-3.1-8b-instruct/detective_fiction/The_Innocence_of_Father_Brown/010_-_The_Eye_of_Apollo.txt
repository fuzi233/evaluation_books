As the morning sun climbed over the rooftops of Westminster, its rays danced across the surface of the Thames, imbuing the city with a warm, golden light. Two figures emerged onto Westminster Bridge, each lost in their own thoughts. One, a towering figure, was none other than M. Hercule Flambeau, private detective, on his way to his new offices in a sleek, modern building adjacent to the majestic Westminster Abbey. Beside him walked the Reverend J. Brown, a humble cleric from St. Francis Xavier's Church in Camberwell, who had come to visit his friend after a somber visit to a local deathbed.

The skyscraper before them was a marvel of American ingenuity, its gleaming steel and glass façade a testament to the era's love of innovation. Though still under construction, the building already housed three tenants, including the office just above Flambeau, which was occupied by a young woman with a flair for the dramatic. The floor below was home to another occupant, a quiet, unassuming individual who seemed content to keep to themselves. But it was the floor above Flambeau's office that commanded attention – or rather, the enormous, gilded statue of an eye that dominated the exterior, surrounded by a halo of golden rays that stretched across nearly half the facade.

"What in the world is that?" Father Brown asked, his voice laced with curiosity, as he halted mid-step.

"A new cult, I suppose," Flambeau replied with a chuckle. "One that preaches forgiveness without repentance, much like Christian Science. The fellow calling himself Kalon – I'm not sure of his full name, but it's certainly an unusual one – has taken the flat above me. My typists below are a pair of ladies with an air of quiet competence, while this self-proclaimed New Priest of Apollo above us worships the sun."

"May he beware," Father Brown cautioned. "The sun was known to be a cruel deity in its time."

"I believe their symbol, the open eye, represents the idea that a person can endure any trial if their mind remains steadfast," Flambeau explained. "They claim that staring directly at the sun is a test of one's inner strength."

"If one were truly whole, they wouldn't need to test their resolve by gazing at the sun," Father Brown observed dryly.
