Ghysbrecht Van Swieten lay awake, his mind a torment of restless dread, though the tempest raging outside offered him no solace. It was not the thunder that kept him from slumber, nor the flashing lightning; rather, it was a gnawing fear that had taken root in his chest. Had he been struck by such terror, he might have joined the frantic search for Peter, but his anxiety was of a different, more personal hue. The moment the storm broke and silence returned to the night, the burgomaster rose with a jerky, agitated motion. He crept down the creaking stairs, saddled his mule with trembling hands, and rode swiftly to the "Three Kings" inn at Sevenbergen.

There, he found his men sprawled in a drunken stupor—some slumped over chairs, others lying upon the tables, a few curled up on the floorboards. Ghysbrecht roused them with furious shouts, demanding answers for their failure. They recounted the tale of their unsuccessful search, weaving their report with exaggerated boasts of their own zeal.

"Fool!" the burgomaster cried, his voice cracking with rage. "To let you go without me! My life depends on it; he was there all the time. Did you look under the girl's bed?"

"No," one of the men replied, rubbing his eyes. "There was no room for a man there."

"How do you know that, if you did not look?" Ghysbrecht snarled, his face contorted. "You should have looked under her bed, inside it too, and sounded every panel with your knives. Come, rise now, and I shall show you the proper way to search."

Dierich Brower stood up, shaking the drowsiness from his frame. "If you find him, call me a horse and no man," he challenged, though his tone lacked true conviction.

Within moments, Peter's house was once again surrounded by the burghers. The fiery old man left his mule in the care of Jorian Ketel and, accompanied by Dierich and the rest, burst through the door.

The house was utterly empty. Not a soul remained within its walls, not even Peter. They climbed the stairs, their footsteps echoing in the hollow rooms, until suddenly, one of the men gave a sharp cry. He pointed through Peter's window, which stood wide open to the night air. The others leaned in, and there, visible in the moonlight at a short distance, walked the man they sought. He moved quietly across the fields, accompanied by Margaret and Martin.

With an exulting yell of triumph, Ghysbrecht descended the stairs and threw himself onto his mule. He and his men spurred their animals forward, setting off in hot pursuit of the fugitives.
