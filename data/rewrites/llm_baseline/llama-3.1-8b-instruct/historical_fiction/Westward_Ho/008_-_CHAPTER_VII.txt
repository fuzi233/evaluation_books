As the morning sun cast its golden light upon the rolling hills of Cornwall, Sir Richard and Amyas strolled through the tranquil gardens of Stow House, situated on the northern slope of a picturesque combe. The air was alive with the sweet fragrance of blooming flowers and the gentle hum of bees as they flitted from bloom to bloom. It was a scene of serenity, a world away from the turmoil of the past, yet somehow reminiscent of a bygone era.

The old house, a testament to the passing of time, stood as a relic of a more tumultuous age. Its walls, once fortified against siege, now stood as a testament to the ingenuity of medieval craftsmen. The machicolated turrets, loopholes, and dark crannies that had once served as a means of defense now seemed almost quaint, a reminder of a time when conflict was a constant presence.

As they walked, Amyas spoke little, lost in thought, while Sir Richard, ever the diplomat, pondered the intricacies of language and culture. He was a scholar, fascinated by the ancient tongues that had given rise to the modern languages spoken today. The clicks and chirps of the Cornish dialect, still spoken by some of the local inhabitants, held a certain allure for him, a key to unlocking the secrets of the past.

Their conversation turned to the subject of the Cistercian monks of Neath, who had once received gifts of land from Sir Richard's ancestor, the first Sir Richard, son of Hamon Dentatus. The tale of how he had slain the Prince of South-Galis and the Lord of Glamorgan, and then gifted his conquests to the monks, was a testament to the complex web of alliances and rivalries that had shaped the history of the region.

As they reached the eastern end of the garden, Sir Richard pointed out the changes that had taken place over the centuries. The Norman keep, once a symbol of power and strength, had given way to the stately architecture of the Tudors. It was a reflection of the shifting tides of power, as one dynasty rose and another fell. Yet, amidst the ruins, there was a sense of continuity, a reminder that even in the midst of change, some things remained constant.
