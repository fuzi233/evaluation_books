Stepping out into the cavernous lobby of the club, Archer collided with Ned Winsett, the sole member of Janey's circle of "clever people" with whom he felt compelled to dig beneath the surface of ordinary club chatter and chop-house gossip. Across the room, Archer had spotted Winsett's familiar, shabby, round-shouldered silhouette, his gaze fixed intently on the Beaufort box. The two men exchanged a handshake, and Winsett, ever the pragmatist, suggested a beer at a small German eatery around the corner. Archer, however, was not in the mood for the kind of cynical dissection they usually indulged in there; he feigned a pressing engagement at home. Winsett, unfazed, merely shrugged, declaring, "Oh, well, so have I, for that matter. I'll be the Industrious Apprentice today too."

They strolled side by side through the bustling hall until Winsett broke the silence. "Look here," he said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "What I'm really after is the name of the dark lady in that swell box of yours—the one with the Beauforts, wasn't it? The one your friend Lefferts seems so hopelessly smitten by."

Archer felt a flicker of annoyance he couldn't quite name. What did Ned Winsett want with Ellen Olenska's name? And why, above all, did he link her so casually to Lefferts? It was unlike Winsett to display such specific curiosity, yet Archer recalled that he was, after all, a journalist.

"It's not for an interview, I hope?" Archer laughed, trying to deflect the tension.

"Well—not for the press; just for myself," Winsett replied smoothly. "The truth is, she's a neighbor of mine. A queer quarter for such a beauty to settle in. She's been awfully kind to my little boy, who fell down her steps chasing his kitten and gave himself a nasty cut. She rushed in bareheaded, carrying him in her arms, his knee beautifully bandaged, and was so sympathetic and radiant that my wife was completely dazzled and forgot to ask her name."

A warm glow spread through Archer's chest. The tale itself was unremarkable; any woman would have acted so for a neighbor's child. Yet, it felt distinctly like Ellen to rush in bareheaded, cradling the boy, and to possess such a luminous presence that it rendered Mrs. Winsett speechless.

"That is the Countess Olenska—a granddaughter of old Mrs. Mingott's," Archer supplied.

"Whew—a Countess!" Winsett whistled. "Well, I didn't know Countesses were so neighborly. The Mingotts certainly aren't."

"They would be, if you'd let them," Archer retorted.

"Ah, well—" It was their perennial, interminable debate regarding the obstinate unwillingness of the "clever people" to frequent the fashionable set. Both men knew there was no point in prolonging it.

"I wonder," Winsett broke off, "how a Countess happens to live in our slum?"

"Because she doesn't care a hang about where she lives—or about any of our little social sign-posts," Archer said, feeling a secret pride in his own idealized image of her.

"H'm—been in bigger places, I suppose," the other commented dryly. "Well, here's my corner."

He slouched off across Broadway, and Archer stood watching him go, musing on his final words. Ned Winsett possessed flashes of penetrating insight; they were the most fascinating aspect of him, and they always left Archer wondering why they had allowed him to accept failure so stolidly at an age when most men were still struggling.

Archer knew Winsett had a wife and child, though he had never seen them. Their meetings were invariably at the Century Club or in haunts of journalists and theatrical types, like the restaurant where Winsett had proposed a drink. Winsett had hinted that his wife was an invalid—a claim that might be true, or merely a euphemism for a lack of social grace, evening attire, or both. Winsett himself harbored a savage abhorrence of social observances. Archer, who dressed in the evening because he found it cleaner and more comfortable, viewed Winsett's attitude as part of the tedious "Bohemian" pose that made fashionable people, who changed clothes without comment and never harped on the number of servants, seem so simple and self-assured. Nevertheless, Winsett always stimulated him. Whenever Archer caught sight of the journalist's lean, bearded face and melancholy eyes, he would drag him out of his corner for a long, stimulating talk.

Winsett was not a journalist by choice. He was a pure man of letters, untimely born in a world that held no need for his talents. After publishing a single volume of brief, exquisite literary appreciations—one hundred and twenty copies sold, thirty given away, and the rest destroyed by the publishers to make room for marketable material—he had abandoned his true calling. He now took a sub-editorial job on a women's weekly, where fashion plates and paper patterns alternated with New England love stories and advertisements for temperance drinks.

On the subject of "Hearth-fires"—the paper's name—he was inexhaustibly entertaining. But beneath his humor lurked the sterile bitterness of a young man who had tried and given up. His conversation always forced Archer to measure his own life against Winsett's and feel how little it contained. Yet Winsett's life contained even less. While their shared fund of intellectual curiosities made their talks exhilarating, their exchange of views usually remained within the limits of a pensive dilettantism.

"The fact is, life isn't much a fit for either of us," Winsett had once said. "I'm down and out; nothing to be done about it. I've got only one ware to produce, and there's no market for it here, and won't be in my time. But you're free and you're well-off. Why don't *you* get into touch? There's only one way to do it: to go into politics."

Archer threw his head back and laughed. There, instantly, appeared the unbridgeable chasm between men like Winsett and Archer's own kind. Everyone in polite society knew that in America, "a gentleman couldn't go into politics." But since he could hardly say that to Winsett, he answered evasively: "Look at the career of the honest man in American politics! They don't want us."

"Who's 'they'? Why don't you all get together and be 'they' yourselves?"

Archer's laugh lingered on his lips as a slightly condescending smile. It was useless to prolong the discussion; everyone knew the melancholy fate of the few gentlemen who had risked their clean linen in municipal or state politics in New York. The day was past when such things were possible. The country was now in the possession of the bosses and the emigrants, and decent people were forced to retreat to sport or culture.

"Culture! Yes—if we had it! But there are just a few little local patches, dying out here and there for lack of... well, hoeing and cross-fertilizing: the last remnants of the old European tradition your forebears brought with them. But you're in a pitiful little minority. You've got no center, no competition, no audience. You're like the pictures on the walls of a deserted house: 'The Portrait of a Gentleman.' You'll never amount to anything, any of you, till you roll up your sleeves and get right down into the muck. That, or emigrate... God! If I could emigrate..."

Archer mentally shrugged his shoulders and turned the conversation back to books, where Winsett, if uncertain, was always interesting. Emigrate! As if a gentleman could abandon his own country! One could no more do that than roll up one's sleeves and go down into the muck. A gentleman simply stayed at home and abstained. But you couldn't make a man like Winsett see that; and that was why the New York of literary clubs and exotic restaurants, though a first glance made it seem like a kaleidoscope, ultimately revealed itself to be a smaller box with a more monotonous pattern than the assembled atoms of Fifth Avenue.

The next morning, Archer scouring the town in vain for more yellow roses, arrived late at the office. He perceived that his tardiness made no difference to anyone and was filled with sudden exasperation at the elaborate futility of his existence. Why should he not be, at that very moment, on the sands of St. Augustine with May Welland? No one was deceived by his pretense of professional activity. In old-fashioned legal firms like Mr. Letterblair's, which managed large estates and "conservative" investments, there were always two or three young men, fairly well-off and devoid of professional ambition, who sat at their desks accomplishing trivial tasks or simply reading newspapers for a certain number of hours each day. Though it was considered proper for them to have an occupation, the crude fact of money-making was still regarded as derogatory, and the law, being a profession, was accounted a more gentlemanly pursuit than business. But none of these young men had much hope of really advancing, nor any earnest desire to do so; and over many of them, the green mold of the perfunctory was already perceptibly spreading.

It made Archer shiver to think that it might be spreading over him too. He had, to be sure, other tastes and interests; he spent his vacations in European travel, cultivated the "clever people" May spoke of, and generally tried to "keep up," as he had somewhat wistfully told Madame Olenska. But once he was married, what would become of this narrow margin of life in which his real experiences were lived? He had seen enough of other young men who had dreamed his dream, perhaps less ardently, and who had gradually sunk into the placid and luxurious routine of their elders.

From the office, he sent a note by messenger to Madame Olenska, asking if he might call that afternoon and begging her to let him find a reply at his club. But at the club he found nothing, nor did he receive any letter the following day. This unexpected silence mortified him beyond reason. Though the next morning he saw a glorious cluster of yellow roses behind a florist's window-pane, he left them there. It was only on the third morning that he received a line by post from the Countess Olenska. To his surprise, it was dated from Skuytercliff, whither the van der Luydens had promptly retreated after putting the Duke on his steamer.

"I ran away," the note began abruptly, skipping all usual preliminaries, "the day after I saw you at the play, and these kind friends have taken me in. I wanted to be quiet, and think things over. You were right in telling me how kind they were; I feel myself so safe here. I wish that you were with us." She ended with a conventional "Yours sincerely," and made no allusion to the date of her return.

The tone of the note surprised the young man. What was Madame Olenska running away from, and why did she feel the need to be safe? His first thought was of some dark menace from abroad; then he reflected that he did not know her epistolary style, and that it might run to picturesque exaggeration. Women always exaggerated; and moreover, she was not wholly at ease in English, often speaking as if translating from French. "Je me suis évadée"—put in that way, the opening sentence immediately suggested she might merely have wanted to escape a boring round of engagements, which was very likely true, for he judged her to be capricious and easily wearied of the pleasure of the moment.

It amused him to think of the van der Luydens having carried her off to Skuytercliff on a second visit, this time for an indefinite period. The doors of Skuytercliff were rarely and grudgingly opened to visitors, and a chilly weekend was the most ever offered to the few thus privileged. But Archer had seen, on his last visit to Paris, the delicious play of Labiche, "Le Voyage de M. Perrichon," and he remembered M. Perrichon's dogged and undiscouraged attachment to the young man whom he had pulled out of the glacier. The van der Luydens had rescued Madame Olenska from a doom almost as icy; and though there were many other reasons for being attracted to her, Archer knew that beneath them all lay the gentle and obstinate determination to go on rescuing her.

He felt a distinct disappointment upon learning she was away, and almost immediately remembered that only the day before he had refused an invitation to spend the following Sunday with the Reggie Chiverses at their house on the Hudson, a few miles below Skuytercliff. He had had his fill long ago of the noisy, friendly parties at Highbank, with coasting, ice-boating, sleighing, long tramps in the snow, and a general flavor of mild flirting and milder practical jokes. He had just received a box of new books from his London bookseller and had preferred the prospect of a quiet Sunday at home with his spoils. But now, he went into the club writing-room, wrote a hurried telegram, and told the servant to send it immediately. He knew that Mrs. Reggie didn't object to her visitors suddenly changing their minds, and that there was always a room to spare in her elastic house.
