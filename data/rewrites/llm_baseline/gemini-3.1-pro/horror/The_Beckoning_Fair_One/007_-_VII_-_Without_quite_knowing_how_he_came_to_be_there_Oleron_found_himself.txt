Without quite knowing how he came to be there, Oleron found himself striding over the loose board he had temporarily placed on the step broken by Miss Bengough. The wood groaned underfoot, a sudden, dissonant spike in the hallway’s delicate acoustic ecology. Hatless and breathless, he descended. Only later would the memory return to him in cyclic fragments, interwoven with his everyday domestic routines: the warm, rendered-animal-fat scent of the candle left burning on the table, the door opened merely a sliver to allow his passage, and the soft, deliberate click of the latch as he sidled out into the gloom. 

At the foot of the stairs, the sonic landscape shattered. A flurry of motion dashed up from the disused cellars, bringing with it the subterranean, olfactory notes of damp loam and ancient, calcified mortar. It was only a cat, but the sudden auditory rupture tore a childish sob from Oleron's throat. He passed out of the gate and stood for a moment under the "To Let" boards, plucking foolishly at his lip as he looked up at the glimmer of light behind one of his red blinds, where mythic occurrences seemed to be bleeding seamlessly into the fabric of his home. 

Moving stumblingly up the square, eager to escape the silent, historical gravity of the house, he sought refuge in a small public-house round the corner. He had never entered it before, but the heavy, gustatory-rich air of the place—thick with the scent of fermented yeast, aged malt, and damp sawdust—offered a grounding contrast to the ethereal, untranslatable silence of his own rooms. He put down a shilling that missed the counter by inches, the metallic clink swallowed by the low murmur of the room. 

"B--b--bran--brandy," he requested, then stooped to look for the coin. 

He had the little sawdusted bar to himself. What company there was—carters, labourers, and the agrarian elders of the neighborhood's vanishing trades—was gathered in the farther compartment, beyond the space where the white-haired landlady moved among her taps and bottles. Oleron sat on a hardwood settee with a perforated seat and drank. The brandy was an unrefined distillation, yet it carried a complex, fiery terroir—notes of charred oak and bruised, overripe fruit that burned a fierce path down his throat. He drank half, and then, finding profound meaning in the physical sustenance and unwilling to treat the ancient craft of distillation as something to be carelessly spilled, he finished it. 

He fell to wondering which of the men whose rough, native dialects drifted across the acoustic space of the public-house would undertake the removal of his effects on the morrow. In the meantime, he ordered more brandy. He could not return to that pith-white room where he had left the candle. The sonic anomalies of the house—nails that drove themselves back into the wood with a dull thud, steps that snapped beneath human weight, and the terrifying, intimate sound of an invisible woman brushing her hair in the dark—were reason enough to disrupt his daily rhythms. He felt querulous and injured. He had taken the place for himself, not to share his domestic sanctuary with spectral inhabitants; that lawyer fellow in Lincoln's Inn should be told so, too, before many hours were out. It was outrageous, letting people in for agreements like that!

A cut-glass partition divided the compartment where Oleron sat from the space where the white-haired landlady moved, stopping seven or eight inches above the level of the counter. Presently, Oleron saw that faces were watching him through the aperture. The faces disappeared when he looked at them. He moved to a corner where he could not be seen from the other bar, but this brought him into line with the landlady. She knew him by sight, and presently she made a remark on the weather. Oleron did not know how to reply, trapped in the untranslatability of his own subjective terror, but her words sufficed to bridge the gap. She employed the dominant, sanitized lexicon of small talk, noting that the winter had been a bad one for influenza, but that the agrarian promise of spring weather seemed to be coming at last. 

Even this slight contact with the commonplace steadied him. An idle, nascent wonder whether the landlady brushed her hair every night, and, if so, whether it disrupted the room's acoustic field with little electric cracklings, was shut down with a snap. Oleron was better.

With his next glass, the rich, botanical warmth of the brandy fortified him. Not go back? Indeed, he would go back! They should very soon see whether he was to be turned out of his hearth like that! He began to wonder why he was sitting hatless in a public-house, treating alcohol as mere utilitarian fuel for his courage. Suppose he were to try and translate his experience to the white-haired landlady? To explain the scratched hand, the rotten stair, the minute auditory disturbances from which he had bolted? The modern lexicon had no vocabulary for such things; she would think him mad. The real truth was that he hadn't been honoring his work. He had been dreaming his days away, neglecting his sweeping, multi-generational saga, the sixteenth chapter of *Romilly*. Fancy, he had actually been fool enough to think of destroying fifteen chapters of cultural memory! Thenceforward, he would remember his obligations to his fellow-men and the profound importance of his work. 

He finished his brandy and went out. He had walked for some time before the cool night air began to clear his mind. The urban clamor around him—a grating industrial noise pollution—faded, replaced by a deep, resonant fear that had nothing to do with himself. It was a fear for Elsie. The house’s sonic environment was actively hostile to her. She had felt the untranslatable shift in the atmosphere. "*I'm not wanted here...*" she had said. The subtle, territorial power dynamics of the space were rejecting her. He must hold her to her intention of staying away, protecting her from the house's cyclic malice. 

With Elsie made secure, he could not abandon his home. He mastered the faint, metallic taste of fear that coated his tongue and turned abruptly in his walk. By the time he reached the short street leading to the square, he was too late for more brandy. The little public-house was still lighted, but closed, and one or two men standing on the kerb fell into a sudden, heavy silence as he passed—a jarring shift in the acoustic ecology. The long-nosed Barrett, whom he passed further down, did not return his good-night, withholding the simplest social grace. 

Oleron turned in at the broken gate, hesitated merely an instant in the alley, and mounted his stairs. Using the last inch of tallow in the Sheffield stick, he deliberately forced himself to make the tour of his five rooms before retiring. In the little hall outside his kitchen, he noticed a letter on the floor. It was unstamped, delivered by hand. He carried it to the sitting-room and glanced at the envelope. The handwriting was clumsy, a physical manifestation of language death, running from beginning to end without a single comma or period to dictate its breath or rhythm. It was from Barrett. It informed Oleron that he must make other arrangements for the preparation of his breakfasts and the cleaning of his rooms—a violent severance of Oleron's daily culinary and domestic sustenance. 

The true sting lay in the postscript. It consisted of a text of Scripture, weaponizing the dominant lexicon of morality in a thinly veiled, insulting allusion to Elsie Bengough. A seldom-seen frown cut deeply into Oleron's brow. So! That was it! 

His suppressed rage broke out. The foul-minded lot! The devil himself could not find fault in the passionate, yet entirely platonic, shared history between Paul Oleron and Elsie, yet this nosing rascal sought to impose his crude worldview upon them. Oleron crumpled the paper, held it to the candle flame, and inhaled the sharp, acrid olfactory note of burning sulfur and carbonized wood pulp as he ground the ashes under his heel. The wrathful blaze effectively banished the pale shadows of the house. 

Yet, one other puzzling circumstance was to close the day. As he undressed, the house's mythic cycle asserted itself once more: the coverlets on his bed bore a distinct, physical impress, seamlessly integrated into the domestic routine, as if a body had recently lain upon them. Oleron could not remember resting there during the day, though the untranslatability of his own fatigue made him uncertain. His indignation for Elsie, acting with the final, warming notes of the fermented grape in his blood, overwhelmed all other sensory inputs. He put out his candle, lay down, and passed immediately into a deep, dreamless sleep, which, devoid of Mrs. Barrett's morning culinary clatter to wake him, lasted almost once round the clock.
