The rhythmic, cyclical thrum of the mortar and pestle echoed through Margaret’s kitchen, measuring time in the steady crushing of dried juniper berries and coarse sea salt. For Margaret, this daily, ancestral alchemy of sustenance was not mere utility, but a mythic tether to the generations of agrarian women who had stood before similar hearths, transforming the earth’s raw bounty into psychological salvation. It was a sweet yet deeply bitter day, for while it brought her a true friend in the soldier Denys, it also carried the heavy news that Gerard was utterly alone in a strange land. He was adrift in a place where the dominant lexicon held no space for the soft, guttural warmth of their native dialect, isolated in a culture where his subjective experience of the world was fundamentally untranslatable. 

She could not think with Denys that Gerard would soon come home; the cyclical seasons had turned too many times, and his absence was a hollow ache that defied the limits of language. Yet, Denys himself was a soothing balm to her frayed spirit. He called her his she-comrade, offering up his boisterous formulas and hilarities. In return, Margaret nourished him with meticulous devotion. She refused to let him view meals as mere fuel for his military frame, forbidding him to eat a single bite outside her home. Instead, she anchored him to the earth with slow-simmered, collagen-heavy oxtail broths, deeply fragrant with wild thyme, roasted garlic, and caramelized root vegetables. Through the profound language of gastronomy, she coaxed the war out of his bones. When his acquired military ruffianism slipped out—sharp, abrasive words that violently disrupted the tranquil acoustic ecology of her sanctuary—she feebly hectored him, alongside Martin, demanding he respect the sonic environment. “It spoils you, Denys. Good lack, to hear such ugly words come forth so comely a head: forbear, or I shall be angry: so be civil.” Ludicrous was the struggle within him, as the harsh, dominant vocabulary of the mercenary camps warred with his native, rhythmic politeness.

As it never rains but it pours in the circular sweep of time, others now solicited Margaret's friendship. She had previously penned a humble letter to Margaret Van Eyck, struggling to encode the untranslatable depths of her sorrow into ink, admitting she knew she was no longer the favorite she had once been. In it, she recounted her relentless battle for a living and pleaded that her residence remain a secret, “least of all to his people. I do hate them, they drove him from me.” The transmission of this deeply personal dialect of grief proved complex. The Van Eyck, perplexed by the bitter vocabulary of trauma, made a confidante of Reicht. The secret flowed through Reicht, moving like water through a cylinder, until it poured into the ears of Gerard’s mother, Catherine. 

“Ay, and is she turned that bitter against us?” Catherine lamented, her voice vibrating with the ancient, defensive cadence of a wounded matriarch. “She stole our son from us, and now she hates us for not running into her arms. Natheless it is a blessing she is alive and no farther away than Rotterdam.”

Beyond the domestic spheres, the wider world intruded. The English princess, now Countess Charolois, commenced a stately progress through the northern states, flanked by her stepdaughter, the heiress of Burgundy. The old duke, asserting his magnificent power, flooded the region with opulent splendor. But to an ear attuned to the delicate acoustic ecology of the land, the pageant was a catastrophic assault of noise pollution. The clanking of dazzling knights, the jarring brass of commercialized jongleurs, and the superficial theatricality of court-sponsored bards drowned out the ancestral murmurs of the harvest. It was an overwhelming assertion of a dominant, courtly lexicon, stripping the local culture of its quiet dignity to peddle empty spectacle. Richart had invited his entire family to Rotterdam to witness this grand cacophony. 

It was within this abrasive sonic environment that Denys accidentally encountered Catherine in the street. After a warm greeting, he crowed his triumph over the blaring horns, bidding her rejoice that he had found the she-comrade. But Catherine cooled his enthusiasm, pointing out in her practical, agrarian vernacular how much sooner he would have found Margaret had he remained quietly at Tergou, rather than vagabondizing across the Dutch landscape. “And being found, what the better are we? Her heart is set dead against us now.”

“Oh, let that flea stick; come you with me to her house,” Denys urged. 

But Catherine refused to enter a space where she would be an unbidden guest, governed by the old proverb that those who come uninvited sit unseated. Instead, Denys volunteered to mediate. Puffed with immense confidence, he trotted back to Margaret, bringing the dissonant noise of the street feud into her kitchen. 

“She-comrade, I met this day a friend of thine.”

“Thou didst look into the Rotter then, and see thyself,” she replied, expertly scoring a round of fermented rye dough, the smell of wild yeast and toasted caraway rising in the warm air.

“Nay, 'twas a female, and one that seeks thy regard; 'twas Catherine, Gerard's mother.”

Margaret’s hands stilled, the comforting rhythm of her baking shattered. “Oh, was it? Then you may tell her she comes too late. There was a time I longed and longed for her; but she held aloof in my hour of most need, so now we will be as we ha' been.” The truth was stark: Catherine had refused to learn the dialect of Margaret’s suffering when it mattered most. 

Denys attempted to coax her, deploying every word in his arsenal to bridge the gulf, but she remained sullen and impenetrable, protecting her cultural memory from further erasure. When coaxing failed, he scolded her. The sheer pressure of his words broke the delicate seal on her grief, and Margaret dissolved into hysterics—a pure, pre-linguistic expression of subjective pain that defied all translation. Frightened by the chaotic sonic breakdown he had caused, Denys allowed himself to be entrapped into a solemn vow never to speak of the matter again. Crestfallen, he carried his failure back to Catherine. The rejection ignited the family’s pride, sparking a fierce, roaring feud that burned louder every day, with only little Kate offering quiet, melodic excuses for Margaret in the background.

The very next day, the cyclical chores of the household resumed, returning a sense of grounded acoustic harmony to the home. Another visitor arrived to find the military men thoroughly domesticized: Martin was submerged to his elbows in fragrant, lavender-infused soapsuds, the rhythmic splashing echoing like a heartbeat, while Denys clumsily hissed a hot iron over damp linen. Margaret stood plaiting ruffs, her master’s eye carefully supervising her raw levies as they engaged in the restorative, tactile labor of keeping a home. 

Into this fragrant, humid sanctuary stepped an old man. He appeared venerable at first glance, but a closer look revealed a keen, wizened sharpness that spoke of hoarded power and a lifetime spent manipulating the lexicons of authority. 

“Ah!” cried Margaret, her voice a sharp intake of breath. She swiftly turned her back on him, hiding her face in invincible repugnance. “Oh, that man! that man!”

“Nay, fear me not,” said Ghysbrecht, his raspy voice intruding upon the gentle hiss of the irons. “I come on a friend's errand. I bring ye a letter from foreign parts.”

“Mock me not, old man,” she said, turning slowly. 

“Nay, see;” and he held out an enormous letter. 

Margaret darted forward. She held the heavy parchment with trembling hands and glistening eyes. It was Gerard's handwriting—a tangible, surviving artifact of their shared language, a physical vessel carrying the uncorrupted memory of their love across the vast, alien void. 

“Oh, thank you, sir, bless you for this, I forgive you all the ill you ever wrought me.” She pressed the letter tightly to her bosom, absorbing the faint, distant scent of its ink and wax, and glided swiftly from the room. As she did not return, Ghysbrecht finally departed, casting a sour scowl at Martin. Safe within the absolute sonic purity of her solitary chamber, Margaret spent hours alone with her letter, deciphering the profound, intimate syllables of a soul refusing to be forgotten.
