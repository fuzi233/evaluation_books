The labyrinthine alleys of Loo concealed our escape, guided by Infadoos and his chiefs through shadowed pathways untouched by Twala’s sentinels. For an hour we moved like ghosts beneath the obscured moon, until at last the eclipse began to wane. A sliver of silver pierced the darkness, then a crimson glow spilled across the heavens—a celestial firebrand against the void. Stars dissolved as dawn’s pallid light revealed our position: we had cleared the city and now approached a broad, flat-topped hill, its horseshoe shape bristling with boulders.

This was no natural fortress but a military stronghold, its summit a sprawling camp capable of housing regiments. As we climbed, the returning moonlight exposed row upon row of warriors—thousands already roused from sleep, huddled in superstitious dread. We pushed through the murmuring crowds to a central hut, where two attendants stood with our abandoned belongings, including Good’s long-lost trousers. His jubilant reunion with them drew a sigh from Infadoos. “Must my lord conceal such legs?” But Good, ever modest, surrendered only once more to public display. Henceforth, the Kukuanas would have to content themselves with his eyeglass, solitary whisker, and unreliable teeth.

At dawn, the army assembled—twenty thousand of Kukuanaland’s finest, arrayed in a dense square. Infadoos, a born orator, wove a tale of usurped thrones and tyranny: Ignosi’s father murdered, his people groaning under Twala’s cruelty, the white lords descending from the stars to restore justice. He spoke of the moon’s extinction, Scragga’s death, and the promise of a new era. When Ignosi stepped forward, his words were thunder.

“I am your king,” he declared, battle-axe raised. “Let any man deny it and face me now. Stand with me, and victory shall bring honor. Oppose me, and your blood will water the earth.” The regiments roared approval. Twala’s answer would come swiftly—messengers already sprinted to summon reinforcements. War was inevitable.

We spent the day fortifying the hill. Stones barred pathways; boulders were poised to crush attackers. By dusk, a herald arrived, sneering. “Twala offers mercy: one in ten dies. But you, Incubu, and the pretender Ignosi, will scream for the Silent Ones’ pleasure.” Laughter followed my retort: “Tell Twala his corpse will greet the sunset tomorrow.”

Night fell. The camp hummed with preparation. Sir Henry and I walked among the sleeping warriors, their moonlit faces already death-pale in my imagination. “How many live by tomorrow?” I wondered aloud. Curtis grinned. “Fortune favors the brave.”

At dawn, Twala’s forces advanced—three columns, like rivers of spears. Infadoos grimaced. “They attack from all sides.” We braced, chain-mail heavy on our shoulders. Sir Henry, clad as a Kukuana general, looked every inch a mythic hero. Good and I, less impressively armored, clutched spears we barely knew how to wield.

On the koppie, we watched the enemy swarm. Thirty thousand against our twenty. The math was cruel, but the choice was gone. Somewhere in the chaos ahead, Twala waited. And so did fate.
