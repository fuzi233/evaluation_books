My trek across the vast, haunted continent of Atlantis was fraught with peril, yet I need not recount every shadowed mile. There was but one halt of consequence, forced by necessity. One day, I had tracked and slain a stag in the open savannah, its breath heavy in the air. I scanned the horizon for lurking beasts, satisfied that the clearing was safe, and began to butcher the carcass, bundling the meat for the journey ahead.

Beneath the tall grass, however, waited a monstrous green worm, a many-legged horror with venom sharp enough to dissolve bone. As I moved, my heel brushed it. Like a dart of light, it struck, its tiny head snapping forward to drive fangs deep into my lower thigh. With my knife, I severed its neck; the creature writhed, twisting its hundred legs in a grotesque dance before dying. I cleaned my blade on the earth and drove it deep around the wound, forcing the blood to flow freely, washing the poison from my flesh. With the crimson stream trickling down my leg, I hoisted the meat and strode on, grateful to have escaped what could have been a gruesome end.

Yet, as I walked, a tightness seized my leg, a throbbing that grew hourly until the skin stretched taut like parchment. A sickness racked my body, violent and consuming; had a great beast found me then, I would have been devoured without a struggle. As darkness fell, I dragged myself into the crook of a tree, enduring wakefulness and agony through the night.

At dawn, when the night predators retreated to their dens, I climbed down, leaning heavily on my spear. I limped through the sombre forests, guided by the moss on the northern sides of the trees. But soon, I noticed moss growing on all sides, and my head grew light with pain and swelling. Still, I pressed on, driven by the High Council's command to secure the enlargement of Nais.

My last conscious memory was of being met in the black forest by a stranger who aided me, after which my senses fled into oblivion.

When I awoke, I lay on a bed of leaves in a rocky cleft, sheltered by stakes and crude fortifications against marauding beasts. My wound was poulticed with herbs, and squatting by a fire of sticks at the cavern's edge was a woman cooking a stew of wood-grubs and honey.

"How came I here?" I asked.

"I brought you," she replied.

"And who are you?"

"A nymph," she said. "I collect herbs, cure the sick, tell fortunes, and make predictions. In return, I accept what I can get. If they cannot pay, I curse them to wither. It is a lean existence when war and pestilence have thinned the land."

"Do you visit Atlantis?"

"Not I. Phorenice would boil me in brine if she could catch me. Our Empress tolerates no magic but her own. They say she is tearing the Priests from their Mountain now."

"Does she still reign?"

"Most vilely."

"As a maiden?"

"As the mother of twin sons. Tatho is her husband now, and has been for three years."

"Tatho! The viceroy of Yucatan?"

"There is no Yucatan," she corrected. "A vast nation of little hairy men from the West overran the country. Armed only with stone-tipped clubs, their numbers were their weapon. They desired no plunder or slaves, only the flesh of Atlanteans. They came like bees in a swarm, eating each city clean before moving on. Tatho and the few survivors sailed home, leaving their wives to be eaten by the horde. They are curious, persevering things. The Gods surely do not allow them to cross the sea to Atlantis, or they would be a plague worse than Phorenice."

I recalled tales of these creatures—beings between man and beast, held in check by the Gods, released only as a devastating army when punishment was needed. I said nothing to the nymph, deeming her a gullible commoner, though my heart bled for the fate of the Yucatan colony where I had labored so long.

"It does not suit me to have my name blazoned abroad yet," I lied, steering the conversation elsewhere. "What of Egypt?"

"In its accustomed darkness," she sighed. "Who cares for Egypt these days? Or anything but oneself? Once, folk brought offerings out of piety. Now, they only come for profit. It is a poor life for a nymph in these times; the fashion is to believe in no mysteries."

"What pestilence did you speak of?"

"I have not seen it. Thank the Gods it has not reached this way. They say it grows from the bodies Phorenice has left unburied. She slays so many that the dead lie thicker than beasts can consume them. For what sin did the Gods let her rise? I wish she and her twins were boiled alive before they crossed my path."

"She has placed images of herself in all the temples," she continued, offering me the grub-stew. "She orders prayers to her alone, declaring other gods inferior. Those insufficiently devout have their hamstrings slit by tormentors to force devotion. Will you eat? You carried meat when I found you, but now you are empty."

I stayed with the nymph for six days, her charms healing my leg. Upon recovery, I hunted a fat horse in the plains as payment and departed.

The land beyond had once thrived. Houses perched in trees or built of massive, dovetailed logs offered safety from cave-bears and tigers. Yet, not one in ten of these settlements remained. Fires had gone cold, vegetable patches reclaimed by scrub, and village gates blocked by rotting logs. Phorenice's influence spread like a blight over what was once a smiling land.

Men I encountered mostly fled into the forest at my approach. Some threatened but did not attack, sensing my lack of malice. A few offered shelter, but as I neared the city, passing through denser populations, the danger of capture grew. Watchers in towers would cry, "Here is a masterless man!" and mobs would rush to strip me of my weapons and conscript me as labor.

Filled with pity for their fallen state, I often used words to dissuade them, striking lightly only when necessary to keep moving. I avoided villages entirely, circling through the forest to escape their gaze. Similarly, I kept beyond the walls of greater towns, unwilling to risk delay under new, arbitrary laws. My progress was slow, but my pride burned hot, fueled by a fever of haste.

Finally, I found myself within a half-day's march of Atlantis itself. The Sacred Mountain loomed high, its ring of eternal fires burning beside the city. Caution became paramount. Everywhere, evidence showed the fighting men had been drained away. Women prayed for the rout of the Priests or the death of Phorenice, hoping for peace.

An army besieged the Sacred Mountain, guarding its single approach with deadly precision. After my long journey, I feared I would fail the High Council's orders. Force was useless; I would face one glorious fight against an army, ending my usefulness. No. This required guile. I hid in the outskirts of a wood, my mind churning for a plan.

Before me lay the grim city walls, temples, palaces, and pyramids. The royal pyramid's steps, overlaid with Phorenice's gold, caught my eye. But it was not the gold that moved me. In the square before the pyramid stood a granite throne carved with a snake and an outstretched hand. Beneath it slept Nais, my love, drugged and unconscious for eight years, yet alive, waiting for me. I must reach the place where Zaemon defied the Empress and announce my arrival.

I lay in the woods for a day and night, raging at my lack of a plan. Then, in the morning, a man ran toward me.

"You need not threaten me with your weapons!" he cried. "I mean no harm. You are Deucalion, though I should not know you in these rags and tangled beard. You will give me your good word. I have not loitered."

He was Ro, a lower priest I knew and little esteemed—a greedy man unworthy of trust.

"From whom do you come?" I asked.

"Zaemon commanded me. He came to my house, though how he bypassed Phorenice's blockade, I cannot tell. I told him I wished no part of his schemes. I am a peaceful man, Deucalion, with a wife who needs nourishment. I still serve the temple, though we have swept out the old gods for the Empress's image. The people are pious now, and living is easy. Offerings are plentiful. I had no wish to risk my snug post or my head."

"I can believe all that, Ro."

"But Zaemon could not be denied. He burst into a black fury. While he spoke, I felt as good as dead. You know his powers?"

"I have seen some."

"Well, the Gods alone know who is true. I serve the one who employs me. But those Zaemon serves grant him power. Look at this right hand of mine? It is dead and paralyzed from the wrist, a gift from Zaemon to 'collect my attention.' He spoke hard things of my apostasy, refusing my defense that the change was forced. He gave me a choice: obey his bidding exactly, or suffer a paralysis that would lead to my death in some gutter."

"If Zaemon said those words, he meant them. Did you accept?"

"With a wife depending on me, what choice did I have? I asked his pleasure. He said I must find you when you arrive from the distant lands and deliver his message."

"'Where is the meeting place?' I asked.

'There is none appointed,' he said. 'Watch and search always. When he comes, you will be guided.'"

Ro explained that he believed he had been guided to find me. "If there is gratitude, restore my hand," he pleaded. "It is awful to live like this. Zaemon could cure it from afar."

"You seem to have faith in the old priests," I noted. "But your errand is incomplete. I have received no message."

"The message is simple. Make a great blaze. Fire the grasses before this wood if you choose. On the following night, go to the flank of the Sacred Mountain where the rocks drop sheer. They will lower a rope and haul you up."

"It seems easy. I will ask Zaemon to restore your hand."

"You shall have my prayers. And look, Deucalion, it is a small matter. Better to do it now on landing. Later, you may be disturbed. Phorenice will pull you down once she sets her mind to it. She never fails. She has all the ancient learning of our Priests, plus more. She has discovered the Secret of Life these recent months—"

"She has found that?" I cried, startled. "How? Only the Three know that. It is beyond our knowledge even who are members of the Seven."

"I know nothing of her means. But she has the secret. She is as good an immortal as any of them."

Ro hurried off, asking for a small coin or link of bronze for his wife's food. I gave him nothing, not wishing to burden myself further.

Almost before Ro's heels cleared the grass, I lit the blaze. That night, under the cover of darkness, I ventured to the appointed spot. No rope dangled, but soon one descended the smooth cliff like a slender snake. I made a loop, slipped it over my leg, and pulled. Those above hauled, and I ascended the Sacred Mountain after years of toil and war. None disturbed the ascent; Phorenice's troops ignored the gaunt, seamless precipice.

The men hauling me up were old and panting, puzzling why younger priests were not assigned. I asked no questions. Among the Priest's Clan, it is assumed orders are given for the best. They took me directly to Zaemon.

The old man greeted me with the royal sign. "All hail to Deucalion," he cried, "King of Atlantis, duly called by the High Council."

"Is Phorenice dead?" I asked.

"It remains for you to slay her and take your kingdom, if there is any land left to govern. The sentence has gone out that she is to die. We shall carry it out, even if we must unleash the dreadful powers stored in the Ark of the Mysteries and wreck this continent. We have borne her infamies by command of the High Gods, but now she has gone beyond endurance. They have given the word for her cutting off."

"You are one of the Three; I am only one of the Seven. You best know the cost."

"There can be no counting the cost now, my brother, my king. It is an order."

"It is an order," I repeated formally. "So I obey."

"If it were not impious, I could justify this decision. The woman usurped the throne yet was forgiven. She tampered with holy religion yet was forgiven. She killed the peoples of Atlantis in greedy wars and destroyed our trade yet was forgiven. She desecrated temples and set up her own images yet was forgiven. But at last, her evil cleverness discovered the Secret of Life and Death. She overstepped the boundary of the High Gods' forbearance."

Zaemon recounted how he had delivered a final warning in the banqueting hall. Phorenice had chosen defiance, claiming her magic matched theirs and that she would share the secret with those she pleased, making death from age a mere legend. She cared not how she upset the laws of Nature. She was Phorenice, the highest law.

"She began to collect an army from that moment," Zaemon concluded. "Our arts discovered you still lived. By solemn election, you were made King. Here you are, my brother. Being King, you must order all things for the preservation of your high estate. We live only to give you obedience."

"Then, as King, I can speak without threat. I must have my Queen first, or I cannot give my whole mind to ruling."
