In my youth, fortune brought me into close acquaintance with a gentleman of irreproachable character who hailed from an island widely regarded—by those whose judgment in matters maritime is beyond reproach—as the most exquisite jewel in the ocean’s crown. The tales he told of his kinfolk dwelling in that distant land were such that they once made my young blood run cold with horror. For there was scarcely a crime under heaven that had not been committed by one or other of them: the Ten Commandments shattered like glass; deceptions so audacious that no fiction could invent them; murders and thefts more shocking than those ever attributed to Thurtell or Turpin—all recounted with chilling accuracy and unreserved candor by my friend concerning his nearest relations, whenever anyone cared to listen.

It seemed miraculous that any member of this family still walked free, rather than rotting in a hulk. One brother, Tim, had carried his father's grey hairs to the grave with sorrowful reverence; another, Mick, had repeatedly robbed the parish church; sister Annamaroia had abandoned her Captain for the Ensign, forged her grandmother’s will, and stolen the silver spoons—a deed for which Larry the knife-boy was hanged. The house of Atreus might have seemed mild in comparison to the race of O’What-d’ye-call-'em from which my companion descended. Yet I dare not name the country from whence he came.

How then did I react when, time after time, I found these same murderers, thieves, parricides, habitual forgers of bills and letters of credit, writing to each other with affectionate phrases—"my dearest brother," "my dearest sister"—and living together in perfect harmony for months on end? With hands stained with the blood of murdered parents, Tim would mix a screeching tumbler and offer Maria a drink from it. With lips blackened by perjuries sworn in court over his grandmother’s stolen will or the murder of his brother Thady’s helpless orphans, Mick would kiss his sister Julia’s cheek, and they’d spend the night laughing, weeping, reminiscing about old times—the dear old Castle What-d’ye-call-'em, where they were born, the fighting Onetyoneth quartered there, the Major’s proposal to Cyaroloine, and the tomb of their beloved mother (who had chattered them out of the property). Heaven bless her soul! Their embraces at meeting and parting were so heartfelt, so full of emotion, that one forgot the grim stories told earlier—stories that implied they could hang each other if only they dared to speak all they knew.

What nobler gift can there be than forgiveness? What more rational act than, after hurling every insult under the sun at a man, to apologize, regret your words, withdraw the decanter you flung at his head, and return to friendship as though nothing had passed? Some people possess this admirable, angelic gift. It was beautiful, for example, to see our two ladies at Tunbridge Wells forgiving each other—smiling, jesting, almost fondling one another despite the bitter words of yesterday—yes, even forgetting past grievances, though both remembered them perfectly well. Can you and I do the same? Let us strive, my friend, to cultivate this gentle, Christian spirit.

I believe one may learn to forgive harsh language directed toward oneself—but only through practice, and only if one has grown accustomed to hearing and using it. After a quarrel marked by mutual insults, you embrace again. Heaven bless us! Bad words mean little when one is used to them; they scarcely ruffle the temper. So the aunt and niece played cards amicably, drank to each other’s health, shared a wing of chicken, pulled a bone from the merry-thought—and yet, in conversation, scratched their neighbors' eyes out, not each other’s.

We have read how Peninsular warriors, when the bugles sang truce, fraternized, exchanged tobacco pouches and wine, ready to raise their firelocks and knock each other’s heads off when the truce ended. Likewise, our seasoned soldiers—skilled in war but wise in peace—laid down their arms temporarily and enjoyed themselves gaily. Of course, while drinking with Jack Frenchman, you keep your pistol within reach, ready to blow his brains out should he make a hostile move. But meanwhile, it is *A votre santé, mon camarade!* Here’s to you, mounseer! And everything is pleasant enough.

As for Aunt Bernstein’s threatened gout? The twinges had passed. Maria was delighted! As for Maria’s fainting fits? There had been no recurrence—only a slight one last night. The Baroness was deeply sorry! Her niece must consult the best physician, take nourishing remedies, continue the steel, even after leaving Tunbridge. How thoughtful of Aunt Bernstein to offer sending bottled waters after her!

But suppose Madame Bernstein confides to her maid: “Fainting fits—nonsense! Epilepsy! Inherited from that dreadful scrofulous German mother!” What means do we have of knowing what passes between the old lady and her attendant? Suppose Lady Maria orders Mrs. Betty, her maid, to taste every medicinal glass first, declaring her aunt capable of poisoning her? Very likely such conversations occur. These are merely precautions—the firelocks loaded and cocked, lying quiet upon the grass.

With Harry’s bond safely in her pocket, the veteran Maria chose not to press for payment. She knew the world too well for that. He was bound to her, but she gave him ample time and leave on parole. It was not her intention to unnecessarily provoke or anger her young slave. She understood the difference in age, and that Harry must enjoy his pleasures and diversions.

“Take your ease and amusement, cousin,” said Lady Maria. “Frisk about, pretty little mousekin,” murmured grey Grimalkin, purring in the corner, watching with green eyes.

Of course, the ladies had discussed at length what Harry ought to see and do during his first visit to London. Both knew perfectly well what amused a young gentleman in those days, and spoke of it with the frankness typical of that easy era. Our wily Calypso consoled herself fully in the absence of her wandering youth, taking whatever diversion came her way. Mr. Jack Morris, the gentleman we’ve mentioned as enjoying the company of Lord March and Mr. Warrington, was one such diversion.

To live among titled persons was Jack Morris’s greatest joy; losing money at cards to an earl’s daughter was almost a pleasure to him. Now, Lady Maria Esmond was an earl’s daughter who was very glad to win. She obtained permission to bring Mr. Morris to the Countess of Yarmouth’s assembly, and they played cards together—everyone satisfied.

Thus, the first forty-eight hours after Mr. Warrington’s departure passed cheerfully at Tunbridge Wells, until Friday arrived—the day when the sermon was to be delivered, which we saw Mr. Sampson preparing. The company at the Wells were eager to hear it. Sampson had earned a reputation for being both amusing and eloquent; and if there were no breakfast, conjurer, dancing bears, or concert going on, the good folk of the Wells would endure a sermon.

He knew Lady Yarmouth was coming—and what power she wielded in granting livings and dispensing bishoprics. The Defender of the Faith of that day placed great trust in her opinion on such matters. So we may be certain Mr. Sampson prepared his finest discourse for her ears.

When the Great Man arrives home at the Castle and walks over to the little country church in the park, bringing with him the Duke, the Marquis, and two Cabinet Ministers—has it ever been your lot to sit among the congregation and watch Mr. Trotter the curate and his sermon? He glances anxiously at the Great Pew; falters as he announces his text, thinking: *Ah! Perhaps his lordship may give me a living!* Mrs. Trotter and the girls watch the Great Pew too, observing the effect of papa’s sermon—the familiar favorite—upon the assembled big-wigs. Papa’s initial nervousness fades; his noble voice clears, warms to the topic—he kindles—he takes out his handkerchief—he is approaching that exquisite passage which has always moved the parsonage to tears—he begins it! Ah! What is that humming sound filling the edifice, making hob-nailed Melibaeus grin at smock-frocked Tityrus? It is the Right Honourable Lord Naseby snoring in the pew by the fire! And poor Trotter’s visionary mitre vanishes with the music.

Sampson served as domestic chaplain to Madame Bernstein’s nephew. The two ladies of the Esmond family supported him. On the day of the sermon, the Baroness hosted a small breakfast in his honor. Sampson appeared rosy and handsome, wearing a fresh-flowered wig and a smart, rustling new cassock—purchased on credit from a church-loving mercer at the Wells.

Accompanied by his patronesses, their ladies’ lacqueys trailing behind them with their large gilt prayer-books, Mr. Sampson marched from breakfast to church. Everyone noted how well the Baroness looked—she laughed, was especially warm with her niece, offering a bow and stately smile to all as she moved forward with her tortoiseshell cane.

At the door, a dazzling gathering of rank and fashion entered—the fine company of the Wells trooping in, led by her ladyship of Yarmouth, conspicuous with vermilion cheeks and a robe of flame-colored taffeta. Among them were shabby figures too, though the fashionable greatly outnumbered them.

What odd-looking pair, for instance, were those in ragged coats—one with carroty hair peeking beneath a scratch-wig—who entered just as the organ ceased? No, he couldn’t have been a Protestant—he mechanically crossed himself upon entering, muttering to his companion: “Bedad, Tim, I forgawt!” From which I conclude he came from the island mentioned at the start of this chapter. Wherever they go, a rich fragrance of whisky spreads.

A man may be a heretic and still possess genius—these Catholic gentlemen came to pay homage to Mr. Sampson. Indeed, not only Catholics were present, but followers of an even older creed. Who were those two men with hooked noses and sallow complexions, who forced their way into the church despite resistance from the beadle?

Seeing the greasy appearance of these Hebrew strangers, Mr. Beadle was inclined to deny them entry. But one whispered: “We wants to be conwerted, gov'nor!” Another slipped money into his hand. Mr. Beadle raised his mace, barred the doorway lifted, and the Hebrew gentlemen entered.

The organ swelled—the doors closed. Shall we enter and listen to Mr. Sampson’s sermon, or lie on the grass outside?

Preceded by the beadle in gold lace, Sampson walked up to the pulpit—rosy and jolly as any man could wish to see. Then, as he rose from his plump cushion, why did his Reverence turn as pale as death? He glanced toward the western church door—there, on either side, stood those terrible Hebrew caryatides.

Then he turned to the vestry door, near the rector’s pew, where Sampson had sat beside his patronesses during the service. Suddenly, two perfumed Hibernian gentlemen slipped from an adjacent seat and took positions on a bench near the vestry door and rector’s pew, sitting quietly until the sermon ended, eyes cast humbly downward.

How shall we describe that sermon, if the preacher himself never knew how it concluded? Nevertheless, it was considered excellent. When it ended, the fine ladies buzzed in their pews, exchanging praise and commentary.

Madame Walmoden, seated next to our friends, declared it was *bewdiful* and made her *dremble all over*. Madame Bernstein called it excellent. Lady Maria was pleased that the family chaplain had distinguished himself. She looked up at him, trying to catch his eye as he remained seated in the pulpit; she greeted him with a flutter of her handkerchief and a wave. He barely acknowledged it—his face pale, his gaze fixed on the font, where the Hebrews still lingered.

The stream of people passed them—rushing, thronging, marching in pairs, dribbling one by one. Everyone left. The two Hebrews remained by the door.

The Baroness de Bernstein and her niece lingered in the rector’s pew, where the old lady engaged in deep conversation with the clergyman.

“Who are those horrible men at the door?” Lady Maria asked Mrs. Brett, her aunt’s maid, “and what a smell of spirits there is!”

“Farewell, doctor; you have a darling little boy—is he to be a clergyman, too?” asked Madame de Bernstein.

“Are you ready, my dear?” And the pew opened. Madame Bernstein, whose father was only a viscount, insisted her niece, Lady Maria—an earl’s daughter—go first.

As she stepped forward, the individuals she had called “horrible men” advanced. One pulled a long strip of paper from his pocket. She started and turned pale. She rushed toward the vestry, hoping to escape and bar the door behind her. But the two men followed swiftly—one actually laid a hand on her shoulder.

“At the suit of Mistress Pincott, of Kensington, mercer, I have the honour of arresting your ladyship,” he said. “My name is Costigan, madam, a poor gentleman of Ireland, bound by circumstances to follow a disagreeable profession. Will your ladyship walk, or shall my man fetch a chair?”

Lady Maria Esmond gave three shrieks and collapsed, swooning to the ground.

“Keep the door, Mick!” shouted Mr. Costigan.

“Best let in no one else, madam,” he said politely to Madame Bernstein. “Her lady-ship has fallen in a fainting fit and will recover here, at her ease.”

“Unlace her, Brett!” cried the old lady, her eyes twinkling oddly. As soon as done, Madame Bernstein seized a small bag hanging from a hair chain around Lady Maria’s neck and cut the necklace in two.

“Dash some cold water over her face—it always recovers her!” said the Baroness. “You stay with her, Brett. How much is your suit, gentlemen?”

Mr. Costigan replied: “The debt we have against your ladyship is one hundred and thirty-two pounds, in which she is indebted to Mistress Eliza Pincott.”

Meanwhile, where was the Reverend Mr. Sampson?

Like the fabled opossum who, spotting the gunner from his gum-tree, said: “It’s no use, Major—I’ll come down,” so Sampson surrendered without resistance.

“At whose suit, Simons?” he asked sadly.

Sampson knew Simons—they had met many times before.

“Buckleby Cordwainer,” said Mr. Simons.

“Forty-eight pounds and charges, I know,” sighed Mr. Sampson. “I haven’t the money. What officer is here?”

Mr. Simons’s companion, Mr. Lyons, stepped forward. “My house is most convenient,” he said, “often used by gentlemen. I shall be proud to accommodate your reverence.”

Two chairs happened to wait outside the chapel. In those chairs, Lady Maria Esmond and Mr. Sampson took their seats and were escorted to Mr. Lyons’s residence by the men we’ve just met.

Shortly after the arrests, the Baroness sent Mr. Case, her trusted servant, with a note to her niece—full of expressions of affection, but regretting that her heavy losses at cards made paying such a sum impossible. However, she had already written to Mrs. Pincott, begging for time, and would inform her niece as soon as she received a reply.

Mrs. Betty came to console her mistress. The two women searched for funds to send Betty to London—she had nearly suffered misfortune too. Both Lady Maria and her maid had lost heavily at cards, and together could muster only eighteen shillings. They agreed Betty should sell a gold chain belonging to her lady and travel with the proceeds.

Betty took the chain to the very toy-shop man who had sold it to Mr. Warrington, who had given it to his cousin. The shopkeeper, suspecting theft, threatened to summon a constable. Betty had to explain—how her mistress was imprisoned—and by nightfall, all of Tunbridge Wells knew Lady Maria Esmond was in the hands of bailiffs.

Yet, meanwhile, the money was found, and Mrs. Betty hurried to London in search of the champion whom the prisoner trusted.

“Don’t say anything about that paper being gone! Oh, the wretch, the wretch! She shall pay it me!” I assume Lady Maria meant her aunt by “wretch.”

Mr. Sampson read a sermon to her ladyship, and they spent the evening discussing revenge and playing backgammon, confidently expecting Harry Warrington to rush to their rescue upon hearing of their plight.

Though by evening’s end, every soul at the Wells knew what had happened—Lady Maria taken into custody, the house where she lay, the exact amount—nay, ten times the amount—for which she was arrested, and that she had pawned her jewelry to raise a little money to stay in jail—yet everyone maintained perfect civility. At Lady Trumpington’s card party that night, where Madame Bernstein appeared, not a word was spoken about the morning’s events as long as she remained within hearing.

Lady Yarmouth inquired after the Baroness’s nephew and learned Mr. Warrington was in London. Was Lady Maria coming tonight? No—she was indisposed, had fainted at church, and was confined to her room.

Cards were dealt, fiddles sang, wine flowed, the gentlefolk talked, laughed, yawned, chattered, footmen intercepted supper, chairmen drank and swore, stars climbed the sky—just as though no Lady Maria was imprisoned, no poor Sampson arrested.

’Tis certain, dearly beloved brethren, that the little griefs, stings, annoyances we feel acutely in ourselves do not prevent our neighbors from sleeping; and when we slip from the world, the world does not miss us. Is this humiliating to our vanity? So much the better. But on the other hand, is it not a comforting truth? And may we not be thankful for our humble condition?

If we were not selfish—*passez-moi le mot*, please—and if we cared as much for others’ sorrows as our own, how unbearable human life would be! If my neighbor’s tight boot pinched my corn; if slander against Jones set Brown into fury; if Mrs. A’s death plunged Messrs. B, C, D, E, F into despair—could we bear the burden of the world?

Let us not be angry or surprised that the company continued playing, laughing, happy, although Lady Maria had come to grief.

Countess, the deal is yours! Are you going to Stubblefield to shoot as usual, Sir John? Captain, we shall have you running off to Bath after the widow!

So the clatter goes on; the lights burn; the beaux and ladies flirt, laugh, ogle; the prisoner rages in his cell; the sick man tosses on his bed.

Perhaps Madame de Bernstein stayed until the very end, unwilling to allow the company to speak of her as soon as her back turned. Ah, what a comfort it is—once again—that we have backs, and that our ears don’t grow upon them! He who has ears to hear, let him stuff them with cotton.

Madame Bernstein might have heard folks say it was heartless of her to attend assemblies and play cards when her niece was in trouble. As if she could help Maria by staying home! At her age, it is dangerous to disturb an old lady’s tranquility.

“Don’t tell me!” said Lady Yarmouth. “The Bernstein would play cards over her niece’s coffin. Talk about her heart! Who ever said she had one? That old spy lost it to the Chevalier a thousand years ago and has lived perfectly well without one since. For how much is Maria put in prison? If it were only a small sum, we would pay it—it would vex her aunt so. Find out, Fuchs, in the morning, for how much Lady Maria Esmond is imprisoned.”

And the faithful Fuchs bowed and promised to obey.

Meanwhile, about midnight, Madame de Bernstein returned home and fell into a deep sleep, waking only late the next morning when her usual attendant arrived with her tea.

If I told you she took a dram with it, you’d be shocked. Some of our great-grandmothers used cordials in their “closets.” Have you not read of the fine lady in Walpole who said, “If I drink more, I shall be 'muckibus!'”? As surely as Mr. Gough is alive now, our ancestresses freely partook of strong waters.

After finishing her cordial, Madame Bernstein stirred and asked Mrs. Brett for news.

“He can give it you,” said the waiting-woman, sulkily.

“Who?”

Mrs. Brett named Harry, saying Mr. Warrington had arrived about midnight—and Betty, Lady Maria’s maid, was with him.

“And my Lady Maria sends your ladyship her love and duty, and hopes you slept well,” said Brett.

“Excellently, poor thing! Is Betty gone to her?”

“No; she is here,” said Mrs. Brett.

“Let me see her directly,” cried the old lady.

“I’ll tell her,” replied the obsequious Brett, and went off on her mistress’s errand, leaving the old lady peacefully resting on her pillows.

Presently, two pairs of high-heeled shoes clicked across the deal floor of the bedroom. Carpets were luxuries unknown in bedrooms of that time.

“So, Mrs. Betty, you were in London yesterday?” called Bernstein from behind her curtains.

“It is not Betty—it is I! Good morning, dear aunt! I hope you slept well?” called a voice that made old Bernstein start on her pillow.

It was Lady Maria’s voice. She drew aside the curtains and dropped a low curtsey.

Lady Maria looked radiant, rosy, and joyful.

And with the surprise of her sudden appearance through Madame Bernstein’s curtains, I think we may close this chapter.
