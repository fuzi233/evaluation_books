Lieutenant Sutch, despite retiring late, rose with the dawn. He roused the household, packed and unpacked his luggage repeatedly, creating such a commotion that every task took double its usual duration. Never before had the house witnessed such noise and frenzy during the thirty years of Sutch's residency. His servants scrambled frantically through the corridors, searching for forgotten items of his travel gear, unable to keep pace with his demands. Sutch himself burned with a boyish fervor. It was understandable; for three decades he had lived in stagnation, drifting on the world's half-pay list, until miraculously, duty had finally called. Something significant, requiring energy, tact, and resolve, had fallen to him. In short, Lieutenant Sutch was to be employed once more. He was eager to begin, dreading the brief delay before his departure, fearing some unforeseen obstacle might cast him back into idleness.

"I shall be ready this afternoon," he declared briskly to Durrance over breakfast. "I'll catch the night mail to the Continent. Perhaps we could drive up to London together, since London lies on your route to Wiesbaden."

"No," replied Durrance. "I have one final visit to make in England. I hadn't considered it until I lay in bed last night. You've put the idea in my head."

"Oh," Sutch remarked, "who do you intend to visit?"

"General Feversham," Durrance answered.

Sutch dropped his knife and fork, staring at his companion in astonishment. "Why on earth do you wish to see him?"

"I want to tell him how Harry has redeemed his honor, and how he continues to do so. Last night you mentioned being bound by a promise not to reveal Harry's intentions—or his success—until the son returned himself. But I am under no such vow. I feel that promise places too heavy a burden on the General. Nothing in the world would wound him more than the certainty that his son was a coward. Harry might have robbed or murdered; the old man would have preferred either crime to this. I shall cross into Surrey this morning and assure him that Harry was never a coward."

Sutch shook his head. "He won't comprehend it. Naturally, he'll be grateful. He'll rejoice that Harry has atoned for his disgrace, but he'll never grasp why he fell into it in the first place. Ultimately, he'll only be happy because the family honor is restored."

"I disagree," Durrance insisted. "I believe the old man loves his son deeply, though he would never admit it. I actually rather like General Feversham."

Sutch had seen little of the General in the past five years. He could never forgive him for his role in Harry's ruin. Had the General possessed the capacity to sympathize with or truly understand the boy's nature, the white feathers would never have been dispatched to Ramelton. Sutch imagined the old man sitting sternly on his terrace at Broad Place, entirely unaware of his own culpability, instead posing as a martyr whose son had become a shame to all the deceased Fevershams whose portraits lined the high walls of the hall. Sutch felt he could never converse patiently with the General, convinced that no argument could sway that stubborn man from his convictions. He had made no effort to decide whether Durrance's news should reach Broad Place.

"You are remarkably thoughtful for others," Sutch observed.

"It is not to my credit," Durrance replied. "I practice thoughtfulness for others purely out of an instinct of self-preservation. That is all."

"Selfishness is the natural, encroaching flaw of the blind," Durrance continued. "I know this, so I am vigilant against it."

True to his word, Durrance traveled that morning via branch lines from Hampshire into Surrey, arriving at Broad Place bathed in the glow of the afternoon sun. General Feversham was now approaching his eightieth birthday. Though his back remained stiff and his posture as upright as it had been years prior when he first introduced Harry to his Crimean comrades, he appeared shrunken in stature, his face seeming smaller. Durrance had walked with the General on his terrace merely two years ago; even though the General was blind, Durrance noted the changes. Old Feversham walked with a heavier step, and a childish tone had crept into his voice.

"You have joined the veterans ahead of schedule, Durrance," the General said. "I read of it in a newspaper. I would have written had I known where to send my letter."

If he suspected the purpose of Durrance's visit, he showed no sign. He rang the bell, and tea was served in the grand hall where the portraits hung. He inquired after various officers from the Sudan with whom he was acquainted, lamented the corruption of the War Office, and fretted that the nation was heading toward destruction.

"Everything, through sheer misfortune or poor management, is going to the devil, sir!" he exclaimed irritably. "Even you, Durrance, you are not the same man who walked with me on my terrace two years ago."

The General had never been gifted with tact, and his solitary existence had offered no improvement. Durrance could have retorted with a *tu quoque*, but he held his tongue.

"But I come bearing news of the same affair," Durrance stated.

Feversham sat up rigidly in his chair. "And I offer you the same response. I have nothing to say regarding Harry Feversham. I will not discuss him."

He spoke in his customary hard, emotionless tone, as if addressing a stranger. Even the name was uttered without a trace of sorrow. Durrance began to wonder if the springs of affection had completely dried up within the General's heart.

"It would not please you, then, to know where Harry Feversham has been, and how he has lived these past five years?"

A pause followed—not lengthy, yet distinct—before the General responded: "Not in the least, Colonel Durrance."

The reply was absolute, yet Durrance placed his trust in the silence that preceded it. "Nor on the business he has undertaken?" he pressed.

"I am not interested in the slightest degree," Feversham declared. "I do not wish him to starve, and my solicitor informs me that he receives his allowance. That is sufficient knowledge for me, Colonel Durrance."

"I shall risk your anger, General," Durrance said. "There are moments when it is wise to disobey one's superior officer. This is one of them. Naturally, you may eject me from the house. Otherwise, I shall recount to you the history of your son and my friend since he vanished from England."

General Feversham laughed. "Of course, I cannot throw you out of the house," he admitted, adding severely, "but I warn you that you are abusing your position as my guest."

"Yes, there is no doubt of that," Durrance answered calmly. He proceeded to tell his tale—the retrieval of the Gordon letters from Berber, his encounter with Harry Feversham at Wadi Halfa, and Harry's subsequent imprisonment at Omdurman. He concluded with the events of that very day, ending on the news of Lieutenant Sutch's departure for Suakin.

General Feversham listened to the entire account without interruption, not even shifting in his chair. Durrance could not gauge the spirit in which he listened, but he found solace in the fact that the General did listen and offered no argument. For some time after Durrance finished, the General remained silent. He raised a hand to shade his eyes, as though the speaker were visible, maintaining the gesture even when he finally spoke. Pride prevented him from showing the portraits on the wall that he felt the natural joy of having his son reclaim his honor.

"What I fail to understand," he said slowly, "is why Harry ever resigned his commission. I could not comprehend it before; I understand it even less now that you have described his great bravery. It is one of those strange, inexplicable occurrences. Things simply happen, and that is all that can be said. However, I am very glad you compelled me to listen, Durrance."

"I acted with a specific objective. Whether you approve is for you to decide; for my part, I see no reason why Harry should not return home and reclaim everything he lost."

"He cannot regain everything," Feversham countered. "It is unjust that he should. He committed the sin, and he must pay the price. He cannot recover his career, for one thing."

"No, that is true; but he can find another path. He is not yet too old to secure another opportunity. And that is all he needs to lose."

General Feversham lowered his hand and shifted in his chair. He glanced quickly at Durrance, opening his mouth to ask a question, then closing it again.

"Well," he said briskly, as if dismissing the matter entirely, "if Sutch can arrange Harry's escape from Omdurman, I see no reason why he should not return home."

Durrance rose. "Thank you, General. If you can arrange transport to the station, I can catch the six o'clock train to town."

"But surely you will stay the night," cried General Feversham.

"It is impossible. I depart for Wiesbaden early tomorrow."
