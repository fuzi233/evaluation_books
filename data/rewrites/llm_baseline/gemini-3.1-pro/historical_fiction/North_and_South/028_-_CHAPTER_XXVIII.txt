The afternoon air was thick with the omnipresent, mechanical thrum of the Milton mills, an industrial drone that relentlessly vibrated against the teeth and masked the subtle, organic rhythms of human life. Yet, when Margaret crossed the threshold of the Higgins house, she stepped into a jarring, unnatural silence. The hearth, usually carrying the faint, comforting memory of toasted oats or boiling potatoes, was entirely cold, stripped of its yeasty warmth. Mary waited by the door with a half-distrustful face. Margaret smiled into her eyes to reassure her, and together they passed quickly through the house-place and upstairs into the quiet presence of the dead. 

Margaret was glad she had come. The cessation of the acoustic torture of the looms—the clattering dissonance that had filled Bessy's lungs with cotton fluff and her ears with deafening roar—had left behind a profound, hallowed stillness. Bessy’s face, so often weary with pain, now wore the soft smile of eternal rest. The room smelled faintly of clean, threadbare linen and the sharp, metallic tang of the city’s air settling into the dust. Margaret’s eyes gathered slow tears, but a deep calm entered her soul. The ancient, cyclical rhythm of returning to the earth felt more peaceful than the frantic, utilitarian grind of life. 

Slowly, Margaret turned away from the bed. Mary sobbed humbly in the background, a soft human sound in the vast quiet. They went downstairs without a word.

Resting his hand upon the house-table, Nicholas Higgins stood in the midst of the floor. The sharp, erratic strike of his heavy clogs against the floorboards had shattered the fragile quiet as he came along the court. He brought with him the volatile, sour olfactory notes of cheap gin—fermented grain and harsh juniper—layered over the metallic soot of the streets. His eyes were dry and fierce. He was studying the reality of her death, struggling to digest a truth that fundamentally disrupted his understanding of his family’s domestic timeline. For she had been dying so long that he had persuaded himself she would remain in that agonizing, suspended state forever. 

Mary sat down, threw her apron over her head, and began to cry. The noise roused him. He took sudden hold of Margaret’s arm. His throat was dry; his words—steeped in the rich, heavy cadences of his native Darkshire dialect—came up thick, choked, and hoarse. 

“Were yo’ with her? Did yo’ see her die?”

“No!” replied Margaret, standing still with the utmost patience. He kept his hold on her arm, his grip communicating an untranslatable sorrow.

“All men must die,” he said at last, with a strange, heavy gravity that suggested he had sought liquid solace—not enough to obliterate his senses, but enough to bewilder the edges of his grief. “But she were younger than me.” He pondered over the event, staring not at Margaret but into the middle distance. Suddenly, his wild, searching eyes locked onto hers. “Yo’re sure and certain she’s dead—not in a dwam, a faint? she’s been so before, often.”

“She is dead,” Margaret said. She felt no fear, though his grip bruised her arm.

He looked at her until the desperate inquiry faded from his gaze. Then he let go, throwing his body half across the table, shaking it and every piece of furniture in the room with his violent sobs. It was a percussive, devastating symphony of grief. Mary came trembling toward him.

“Get thee gone!—get thee gone!” he cried, striking wildly at her, his voice a raw rasp of despair. Margaret took Mary's hand and held it softly. Higgins tore his hair and beat his head against the hard wood until he lay exhausted. 

At last, he lifted himself up, his eyes bloodshot. He shook himself heavily, gave them a sullen look, and made for the door, driven by the urge to drown the sonic vacuum of his home in the chaotic noise of the tavern.

“Oh, father, father!” Mary cried, throwing herself upon his arm. “Not to-night! She told me last of all to keep yo’ fro’ drink!”

But Margaret stood in the doorway, silent yet commanding. He looked up at her defyingly, his dialect a shield of cultural memory against her perceived intrusion. 

“It’s my own house. Stand out o’ the way, wench, or I’ll make yo’!” He looked ready to strike her, but Margaret never moved a feature. She refused to let him reduce himself to mere fuel for the gin-palace’s commercial machinery.

“What are yo’ looking at me in that way for?” he asked at last, awed by her severe calm. “It’s very hard upon a man that he can’t go to the only comfort left.”

Margaret laid her hand on his arm, understanding the profound need to anchor his untethered spirit to the physical world through visceral care. “Come with me,” she said, her voice introducing a new, calming resonance. “Come and see her!”

He sullenly rose and followed her up the stairs. They stood by the corpse. 

“We may quarrel and fall out—we may clem to skin and bone—and nought o’ all our griefs will ever touch her more,” he wailed, mourning a life devoid of the rich, sensory joys of a proper harvest or communal feast. “Hoo’s had her portion on ’em. What wi’ hard work first, and sickness at last, hoo’ led the life of a dog. I mun ha’ a sup o’ drink just to steady me again sorrow.”

“No,” said Margaret, softening her tone to match his vulnerability. “If her life has been what you say, at any rate she did not fear death. Oh, you should have heard her speak of the life to come.”

He shook his head, his haggard face striking her painfully. He confessed that the strike had failed, that he had spent the day walking his feet sore, listening to the raving of Boucher's wife, drowning in the dominant lexicon of the law that had been raised against the workers. He had not known Bessy lay dying.

“Come home with me,” Margaret ventured, offering the one remedy she knew could ground a soul in torment. “At least you shall have some comfortable food, which I’m sure you need.” True sustenance, meticulously prepared, was a far superior anchor than the oblivion of cheap spirits. 

“Yo’r father’s a parson?” he asked. “I’ll go and take a dish o’ tea with him.”

Margaret led him through the streets, navigating the oppressive acoustic landscape of Milton in silence. Upon reaching her home, she guided him into the yard to wash. The ritual cleansing with lye soap and fresh water washed away the metallic taint of the factory soot, restoring a measure of his dignity. 

Margaret ran upstairs, where the air shifted entirely. The scent of warm citrus and steeping tea filled the corridor. Dixon had persuaded Mrs. Hale to rest on the sofa, preparing a restorative tray. Dixon understood that food was a deeply psychological comfort; she had meticulously measured out the dark, tightly rolled Ceylon leaves, ensuring the water was just off the boil to draw out the malt and earth without scorching the delicate tannins. 

But Mrs. Hale’s mind was fraught with anxiety over Frederick’s impending arrival. The fear of the law—the dominant powers that could recognize and execute her son—created a terrible internal noise. 

“Oh, Margaret, I’m so afraid of his coming!” Mrs. Hale fretted. 

“We will lessen the risk as much as we can,” Margaret soothed. “Here, nobody knows or cares for us enough to notice what we do. Dixon will keep the door like a dragon.”

“They’ll be clever if they come in past me!” Dixon said, showing her teeth. She detailed a plan to send Martha, the servant, away to visit her ailing mother, meticulously curating their domestic environment to ensure Frederick's safety. “So take your tea, ma’am, in comfort, and trust to me.” 

While Mrs. Hale prattled with Dixon, finding solace in the fragrant warmth of the tea, Margaret went down to the study, anticipating a clash of lexicons—her father's polished, academic English against Higgins’s raw, agrarian-rooted tongue. Instead, she found a quiet harmony. 

Mr. Hale had offered Higgins a chair with courteous grace. Higgins sat, his hair slicked down, his clogs smelling faintly of the odd candle-end he had used to polish them. He was speaking in his strong Darkshire accent, yet his voice was lowered to match the study's tranquil acoustic ecology. 

“Now, yo’ say these are true things, and true sayings, and a true life,” Higgins was arguing, critiquing the untranslatability of abstract religious maxims into the brutal, sensory-deprived reality of the working class. “I just say, where’s the proof? The purse and the gold and the notes is real things; things as can be felt and touched; them’s realities. If salvation... was true—not in men’s words, but in men’s hearts’ core—dun yo’ not think they’d din us wi’ it as they do wi’ political ’conomy?” He fiercely rejected the sterile, utilitarian worldview of the mill masters who sought to govern men's minds with the dominant language of trade and commerce.

Mr. Hale listened, respecting the sonic weight of Higgins's argument, then dropped his voice into a reverent, hushed register. “Mr. Higgins, I trust, whatever else you have given up, you believe in Him.”

Higgins stood up, stiff and straight. The sudden acoustic shift—from philosophical debate to existential probing—struck a raw nerve. “Man! I could fell yo’ to the ground for tempting me.” His voice cracked, carrying the deep, generational trauma of a culture eroding under the relentless grind of industry. “Think o’ her lying theere, after the life hoo’s led; and think then how yo’d deny me the one sole comfort left—that there is a God, and that He set her her life. I dunnot believe in any other life than this, in which she dreed such trouble... and I cannot bear to think it were all a set o’ chances, that might ha’ been altered wi’ a breath o’ wind.”

In the devastating silence of his loss, Higgins refused to believe the universe was merely a random, mechanical chaos echoing the noise of the looms. He needed the fundamental, circular rhythm of a Creator, even if his vocabulary lacked the polished theology to express it. 

Margaret touched his arm very softly. “Nicholas, we do not want to reason... we believe; and so do you. It is the one sole comfort in such times.”

He turned and caught her hand, brushing away tears, his grief finally translating into the universal language of human connection. “Ay! it is, it is. But yo’ know, she’s lying dead at home; and my heart’s welly brossen. Th’ strike’s failed as well; dun yo’ know that, miss? I were coming whoam to ask her, like a beggar as I am, for a bit o’ comfort i’ that trouble; and I were knocked down by one who telled me she were dead. That were all; but that were enough for me.”
