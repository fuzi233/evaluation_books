Romola had heard, as if in a cry, the words repeated to her by many voices—the very words Savonarola had spoken when bidding farewell to his brethren of San Marco who came to witness his signing of the confession: “Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy.” Those words struck her with new unease, stirring doubts about how he truly saw his past during moments of clear self-awareness. And these doubts deepened when still more painful news reached her ears.

May 19th had come, and under its bright sun, two Papal Commissaries entered Florence—charged with completing Savonarola’s trial. They arrived amid the cheers of the people, who now demanded his death. The cry was loud and clear: “It is the Frate’s deceit that brought all our troubles upon us! Let him be burned, and peace will return, and our suffering will end.”

The next day, fresh torture was inflicted upon the already shattered man. At the sight of the instruments of pain, Savonarola fell to his knees, trembling and agitated. In brief, passionate words, he *retracted his confession*, declaring he had lied when denying his prophetic gift. “If I suffer,” he said, “I shall suffer for the truth. The things I have spoken—I received them from God.”

Yet the torture continued. When pressed under it, he was asked why he had spoken those words of retraction. Men were not demons in those days—but only confessions of guilt were seen as justification for release. His answer came, broken and weary: “I said it so they might think me good. Tear me no more. I will tell you the truth.”

Florentine assessors were present at this renewed trial, and soon the words of double recantation spread through the city. Romola was seized with dread and confusion. But then, an idea flashed through her mind: “There will come a moment when he may speak freely. When death stands before him, when falsehood is no longer feared, when he faces the people one last time—then he cannot be silenced. He will speak the final word. I must be there.”

Three days later, on May 23rd, 1498, a long narrow platform stretched across the great piazza, from the Palazzo Vecchio toward the Tetta de’ Pisani. This time, no grove of wood stood ready. Instead, a single great pile of fuel was heaped upon the circular space at the end of the platform. Above it rose a gibbet with three halters—its two arms shaped like a cross, though one had been cut short to avoid the resemblance. Still, some looked upon it with discomfort.

On the marble terrace of the Palazzo stood three tribunals: one near the door for the Bishop, who would perform the ceremony of degradation on Fra Girolamo and his two followers; another for the Papal Commissaries, who would pronounce them heretics and schismatics and hand them over to civil authority; and a third, close to Marzocco, where the Gonfaloniere and the Eight would deliver the sentence of death.

The piazza thronged with expectant faces. Again, a great fire would be lit. For most of the crowd, the expectation was not of justice, but of hatred or cruel curiosity. Yet among them were others—many still—whose hearts held a faint hope, even now, that God might intervene, that some sign might reveal Savonarola as His true servant. And there were still more who watched with trembling eagerness, just as Romola did, awaiting that final moment when he might say: “O people, I was innocent of deceit.”

Romola stood at a window on the north side of the square, far from the tribunal. Near her, watching with equal anguish, was a young Florentine named Jacopo Nardi, twenty-two years old. He would later earn respect as one of the few who, recognizing Savonarola’s greatness, wrote of him with honest intent. Seeing the conflict in Romola—her horror at the scene, yet her desperate desire to witness what might happen—he said gently, “Madonna, there is no need for you to see these cruel things. I will tell you when he comes out of the Palazzo. Trust me—I know what you wish to see.”

Romola covered her face, but the jeering shouts echoed through the air, making the horror impossible to ignore. Then a hand touched her arm. “He comes.”

She looked toward the Palace and saw Savonarola led forth in his Dominican habit. She watched as he stood before the Bishop, stripped of his black mantle, white scapulary, and long tunic—until he stood bare in a simple woolen undergarment, stripped of rank, office, and dignity. He was degraded, cast out from the Church Militant.

The lower part of the crowd reveled in such degradation, finding pleasure in humiliation. A fresh wave of mockery rose as the three men passed to the tribunal of the Papal Commissaries. Were they not now clearly schismatics and heretics? It was easy to believe a man damned when he stood stripped and broken.

Then they passed the third tribunal—the Florentine officials who would pronounce sentence. Even from afar, Romola recognized the loathsome figure of Dolfo Spini, dressed in the solemn black lucco, one of the Eight.

The three men, clad in plain white garments, walked along the platform amid yells and insults. “Cover your eyes, Madonna,” Jacopo whispered. “Fra Girolamo will be the last.”

But soon she had to uncover them again. Savonarola was near. He had mounted the steps. She saw him turn and look out over the multitude. In that instant, hope died. She saw what he saw—torches waving above the pyre beneath where his body would lie, faces illuminated by a harsher light than any flame. She heard what he heard—coarse jokes, curses, taunts.

The moment was gone. Her face sank back into her hands. She knew only that Savonarola’s voice had joined the eternal silence.

On the evening of May 22nd, 1509, five figures sat in a quiet upper room overlooking a loggia. From its right corner, the view stretched along the Borgo Pinti, past the city gate, toward Fiesole and the distant, solemn hills beyond.

At one end of the room, an archway opened into a small inner chamber—little more than a recess—where soft light fell from above onto a modest altar draped in white linen. Over it hung a portrait, barely visible from the distance: a full-length image of a Dominican friar. The painting was shaded by branches and flowers, and the candles below remained unlit.

But the altar seemed unfinished. The floor was scattered with blossoms and green boughs. Among them sat a delicate girl of thirteen, blue-eyed and fair-haired, tossing her hair aside as she wove wreaths. She glanced up at her mother, offering little instructions with a tone of gentle authority.

Her mother, Tessa, sat slowly, her fingers clumsy and thickened with age. She turned often, asking Ninna’s advice, still amazed by her children’s wisdom. She wore the same contadina gown, now broader, with the silver pin in her curly brown hair and the red cord necklace—its end hidden beneath her breast. Her face, round and peaceful, bore the contentment of someone who believed the world was kind. Monna Brigida, once sharp-tongued, slept soundly in a straight-backed chair nearby, her silver hair softly parted under her black hood, her chin resting on her chest.

At the doorway to the loggia sat two others. Lillo leaned against the post, legs stretched out, a large book open on his knee. He swatted a fly with idle focus, his gaze occasionally drifting to the finely printed Petrarch beside him—as if memorizing lines. Across from him, Romola sat quietly, hands folded in her lap, eyes fixed on the distant mountains. Her features had softened with time—the cheekbones less pronounced, the golden hair thinner—but her face carried a serenity unknown in youth. She had endured her deepest sorrows early, and now lived in a calm born of experience.

Lillo noticed she was not listening. He closed his book and waited, his expression growing slightly impatient. Finally, Romola turned, meeting his dark eyes with a warm smile. He shuffled closer, resting his chin on her knee.

“What is it, Lillo?” she asked, brushing his hair back.

“Mamma,” he said, pleased to talk, “what am I to be?”

“You could be a scholar,” Romola replied. “My father was a scholar. He taught me much, and so I can teach you.”

“Yes,” Lillo hesitated. “But he looks old and blind in the picture. Did he gain glory?”

“Not much,” Romola said gently. “The world was not always kind to him. Meaner men rose above him because they flattered and lied. His own son left him to become a monk. Then, blind and alone, he felt unable to use his learning fully—so his work did not live on after him.”

“I wouldn’t want that life,” Lillo said. “I’d like to be something great—something that brings happiness and pleasure too. Something that doesn’t stop me from enjoying myself.”

“That is hard,” Romola said. “True happiness does not come from chasing narrow pleasures. Only by thinking widely, feeling deeply for others, can we reach the highest joy. And that joy often carries pain—yet it is chosen, because we know it is good. The world is full of wrong and difficulty. No man can be great unless he gives up seeking comfort and learns to endure hardship. My father had greatness in integrity—he chose poverty and obscurity rather than falsehood. And Fra Girolamo—you know why I keep tomorrow sacred—*he* had greatness in fighting against powerful evil, striving to lift men to their highest deeds. So, Lillo, if you wish to act nobly and seek truth, fix your mind on that end, not on what may come to you. Remember: if you choose a lower path—seeking only pleasure, avoiding pain—calamity may still strike. And when it does, it falls on a base heart. That sorrow has no healing. A man may say, ‘It would have been better if I had never been born.’”

She paused. Her voice trembled. Lillo looked up, wide-eyed.

“There was a man I knew well—very close to me. He was young, clever, beautiful, and kind to all. I believe, when I first met him, he never thought of cruelty. But because he tried to avoid every unpleasant thing, caring only for his own safety, he ended in the basest acts—acts that bring infamy. He denied his father, leaving him to misery. He broke every trust, to save himself, grow rich, and prosper. Yet calamity found him.”

Romola paused again. Then, softly, “Another time, my Lillo, I’ll tell you another story. Look—there are Piero di Cosimo and Nello coming up the Borgo Pinti with flowers. Let us go to the edge of the loggia and wave to them.”

“How strange old Piero is!” Lillo said, watching them approach. “He scolds you for honoring Fra Girolamo, and yet he brings the flowers.”

“Never mind,” Romola said. “There are many good people who did not love him. Perhaps I would never have learned to love him—if he had not helped me when I was in great need.”
