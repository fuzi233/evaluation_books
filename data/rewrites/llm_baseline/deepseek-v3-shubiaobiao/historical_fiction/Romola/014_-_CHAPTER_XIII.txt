The languid heat of a September afternoon draped Florence in drowsy stillness. In Nello’s shop, the barber himself lay sprawled across his bench, cap pulled low over his eyes, one leg dangling toward the floor where a volume of poetry had slipped from his grasp, its pages crumpled beneath him. Nearby, Sandro sat hunched in a corner, engrossed in a solitary game of *mora*, his fingers moving with solemn deliberation as if conducting some private ritual.

Tito stepped lightly across the threshold, his movements as fluid as the melody he plucked from the lute strings. Leaning over Nello, he sang softly:

*“Quant’ è bella giovinezza,
Che si fugge tuttavia!
Chi vuol esser lieto sia,
Di doman non c’è certezza.”*

Nello startled awake like a bird roused from its perch, his cap flying off as he sat bolt upright. “Ah, my radiant Apollo!” he exclaimed, rubbing his eyes. “I see I’ve overindulged in poetic slumber. A dangerous habit—too much verse turns the mind to mush, like wine left too long in the cask. But what brings you here, my Orpheus? That coaxing lilt in your voice tells me you want something.”

Tito laughed, fingers still tracing idle patterns on the lute. “You know me too well. I’ve come for a touch of your art—not the razor, but the comb. My curls are in dire need of your skill before I present myself at the Scala palace. The young Cardinal de’ Medici will be there, along with that sharp-witted Bernardo Dovizi. I’d rather outshine them with scent than wit.”

Nello seized his comb with a flourish. “Ah, you needn’t fear rivals, my golden one. Dovizi may be quick, but he’s a weasel, all teeth and no soul. You? You’re the man who’s charmed even the sour-tongued Messer Angelo—no small feat!”

Tito’s smile flickered. “Messer Angelo could swallow me whole and never notice. But flattery will do for now.”

Nello worked deftly, his chatter winding on. “Ah, but you’re too modest! The city adores you. And why not? You’re the very model of devotion—always hurrying back to the Via de’ Bardi, to that grand labor of yours. Though I’ve heard whispers that even posterity may struggle to decipher its meaning.”

Tito’s amusement was thin. “Perhaps. But for now, I’ll settle for Romola’s favor.”

Nello paused, comb hovering. “Ah! So the betrothal nears? Unless old Bardo sets you some Herculean task first.”

“If only his demands were so simple,” Tito murmured. Then, as if struck by a sudden thought, he added, “You mentioned Cronaca earlier—something about San Marco?”

Nello shrugged. “Only that your scholar’s runaway son has returned. Fra Luca, they call him now. Cronaca saw him there.”

A ripple of tension passed through Tito, though his fingers remained steady as he smoothed the fallen book’s pages. “I thought he was still in Fiesole.”

“Not according to Cronaca. But what do I know of monks and their comings and goings?” Nello waved a dismissive hand. “There! You’re fit to dazzle the Medici. Though I’d avoid the Piazza della Nunziata tonight—the *Fierucoloni* are in, and their hymns to the Virgin won’t drown out the occasional flying stone.”

Tito left with a jest on his lips, but unease coiled in his chest. The Scala palace offered little distraction—the young cardinal had not appeared—and as he departed, the sinking sun cast long shadows across his path.

Then, rounding a corner, he halted in surprise. A veiled figure moved toward him, unmistakable in her bearing despite the somber drapery. Romola. At her side waddled Monna Brigida, her usual finery replaced by drab simplicity.

“Romola?” Tito stepped forward, his voice low. “What’s happened?”

She lifted her veil just enough to meet his eyes. “I’m going to San Marco,” she said softly. “My brother—Fra Luca—is dying. He’s asked for me.”

Tito’s pulse stuttered. “You’re defying your father?”

“For the first time.” Her gaze held his, steady but sorrowful. “He thinks I’m with Brigida. My godfather knows the truth. You remember when we spoke of Dino?”

“Yes.” The word was a whisper.

Romola’s voice was firm. “I must go. He was once dear to me, and if he seeks forgiveness at the end, who am I to deny him? You understand, don’t you?”

Tito’s smile was a fragile thing. “You are always right, *mia Romola*. Even in trusting me too much.”

She touched his hand briefly before drawing her veil down once more. “Go now. We mustn’t be seen together.”

Monna Brigida huffed. “Yes, off with you! The fewer eyes on us, the better. And if anyone asks, we’re just two pious women off to vespers, not—”

Tito bowed, pressing Romola’s fingers to his lips before turning away. The gesture was courtly, foreign, and it left him with a bitter taste.

Alone, he paced the shadowed streets, his mind racing. Fra Luca would ask questions. Romola would answer. The past would unravel, and with it, every carefully spun lie.

By the time he reached the Piazza dell’ Annunziata, the sun had fled. The lanterns of the *Fierucoloni* flickered like distant stars, their hymns rising into the dusk. He stood at the edge of the crowd, unmoored, a man without a path—ready, in that moment, to follow any voice that called.
