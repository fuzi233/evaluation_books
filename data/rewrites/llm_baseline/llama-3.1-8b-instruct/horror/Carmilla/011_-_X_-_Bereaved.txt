My father and I exchanged a knowing glance; this was a man transformed by the trials of the past year.

It was ten months since we'd last seen him, and yet the General's appearance seemed to have aged a lifetime. His words, however, were laced with the same unyielding passion that had characterized our conversations in the past. As we drove deeper into the woods, he spoke of the bereavement that had shaken him to his core – the loss of his beloved niece and ward. His voice trembled with rage and sorrow as he inveighed against the "hellish arts" that had claimed her life, and he wondered aloud at the heavens' tolerance for such monstrous indulgences.

My father, ever the astute observer, sensed that something profound had befallen the General. He asked, with gentle solicitude, if the General would not find it too painful to recount the circumstances surrounding his niece's passing. The General's response was immediate and fervent. "I should tell you all with pleasure," he declared, "but you would not believe me."

"Why should I not?" my father inquired, his voice calm and even.

The General's expression turned testy. "Because you believe only in what confirms your own prejudices and illusions," he said. "I, too, was once like you, but I have learned better. I have seen things that defy explanation, things that have shattered my faith in the rational world."

My father's eyes narrowed, his gaze piercing the General's words. "Try me," he said. "I am not a dogmatist, and I respect your conclusions all the more for being based on evidence."

The General's eyes flashed with a deep-seated conviction. "I have been forced to confront the impossible," he said, his voice barely above a whisper. "I have been a pawn in a preternatural conspiracy, one that has left me changed forever."
