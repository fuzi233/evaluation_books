But it is not thus with all. What then? Let His will be done—done it will be, whether we bow to it with meekness or break ourselves against it. There are powers that urge the world forward, visible and veiled; they have their charge, and they fulfil it. If proof must be written, it will be written—if need be—in fire and in blood. We read that scripture in the scorched seams of the earth, in the red sap of the vine, in the quick pulse under our own ribs when joy is plucked away. Sufferer, do not faint at the heat of such evidence. Wayfarer, tighten what is loose; look up; go on. Pilgrims and fellow-mourners, keep close company. For most of us the road runs dark through this wilderness; let our step be even, our burden borne, our banner held. For staff—promise. For present help—providence. For end—home. Let us run that we may obtain; let us endure; let us keep the faith, and trust the issue: “Art thou not from everlasting, mine Holy One? WE SHALL NOT DIE!”

On a Thursday morning we were gathered, as usual, in classe for the literature lesson. July lay warm upon the city; the glass-door stood ajar, and a fresh current came in from the garden, lifting the leaves of the plants trained round the lintel. They bent and waved as if they had gossip to whisper—tidings drawn from outside air and carried, green-tongued, into our close routine. Even the soundscape of the room was softened: the scratch of pens, the small rustle of paper tied with ribbon, the quiet breathing of girls who had been schooled into stillness. The compositions lay ready—white bundles, neatly bound—waiting for the quick hand and quicker eye of the Professor who always came with a kind of keen weather about him: swiftness, fire, a step that struck sparks from the boards.

The hour came. We expected that step.

Instead, the door opened, and it was Madame Beck who entered—quietly, cautiously, with a light shawl drawn round her shoulders as if she had stepped in from a chill, though the day was hot.

She went straight to M. Emanuel’s desk and stood before it. Her voice, when she spoke, was low and firm; her gaze fixed, not roaming.

“This morning there will be no lesson of literature.”

She paused—two minutes, perhaps—long enough for the room to feel the gap where a voice should have been. Then she went on.

“It is probable the lessons will be suspended for a week. I shall require at least that space of time to find an efficient substitute for M. Emanuel. Meanwhile, it shall be our study to fill the blanks usefully.

“Your Professor, ladies, intends, if possible, duly to take leave of you. At the present moment he has not leisure for that ceremony. He is preparing for a long voyage. A very sudden and urgent summons of duty calls him to a great distance. He has decided to leave Europe for an indefinite time. Perhaps he may tell you more himself. Ladies, instead of the usual lesson with M. Emanuel, you will, this morning, read English with Mademoiselle Lucy.”

She bowed—courteous as a rule-book—drew her shawl closer, and passed out.

Silence dropped at once, heavy as a coverlet. Then the room stirred. A murmur travelled desk to desk; one or two began to cry. It spread—not deep, not noble, but contagious. Sobs broke the air in little gusts, and with each gust discipline loosened as if a hand had slipped off the reins.

Habit and duty—those old, hard-fed servants—rose in me quickly. I stood, took the book, fixed my voice into its usual tone, and brought the room to order. I made the English reading long, close, exacting. I held them to it, as one holds dough to the board and kneads because to stop would be to think; because the hands can bear what the heart cannot. I remember a sharp impatience at the sobbing girls. Their distress had that hysteric edge which frays rather than moves; I told them so, with a dryness that half ridiculed. I was severe; I could not endure the tears, that gasping sound. It seemed to call something in me to the surface which I dared not let rise.

One weak-minded, low-spirited pupil persisted when the rest had been shamed into quiet. Relentless necessity made me accost her so that she conquered the convulsion. She might have had a right to hate me—except that, when school was over and the others had gone, I ordered her to stay, and when we were alone I did what I had never done to one among them before: I pressed her to my heart and kissed her cheek. The poor child broke again at that tenderness, more bitterly; and I—afraid of what it unlocked—sent her away at once.

All that day I stuffed with occupation, minute upon minute, as one stuffs a jar to the brim so there shall be no room for air. I would have sat up all night if the light might have been kept burning; but night was a treacherous larder, cold and empty, and it left bad effects, preparing me ill for the next day’s ordeal—gossip.

The news went through the school like a spoon tapping the rim of every cup. At first there was a little reserve—surprise wears modesty for an hour—but it soon fell off. Teachers, pupils, the very servants mouthed the name of “Emanuel.” That he—whose connection with the school was as old as its beginning—should withdraw thus suddenly, startled all. They talked until talk itself yielded a kind of intelligence: by the third day it was said he would sail in a week; then that he was bound for the West Indies.

I watched Madame Beck for disproof or confirmation as one watches a hearth for a draught: the slightest change of flame, the faintest shift of smoke. But her face was smooth, unperturbed; her eyes disclosed nothing but commonplace.

“This secession is an immense loss to me,” she said publicly—in classe, at dinner, audibly to Zélie St. Pierre. “I do not know how I shall fill up the vacancy. I am so used to my kinsman; he has become my right hand. What shall I do without him? I opposed the step, but M. Paul has convinced me it is his duty.”

Why was it his duty? The question burnt in me like spice on a raw tongue. I had impulses—sudden, fierce—to catch her sleeve as she passed, to grip and say: Stop. Finish the matter. Why must he go into banishment? But Madame always addressed some other teacher; she never looked at me, never seemed conscious that I could care.

The week wore on. Nothing more was said of his coming to take leave; no one seemed anxious; no one questioned whether he would appear; they gossiped incessantly, and never touched that point which was life to me. As for Madame—she could see him when she chose, speak to him as she pleased. What should she care if he came into the schoolroom or not?

The week was consumed. We were told his destination—“Basseterre in Guadaloupe.” The business, they added, concerned a friend’s interests, not his own. I thought as much.

“Basseterre in Guadaloupe.” I slept little, and whenever sleep caught me it was only to be broken by those words, pronounced over my pillow or written across the dark in jagged characters of red and violet light. Even the night had its acoustics: the house creaked, a distant cart rumbled on the stones, a shutter clicked, and every small sound seemed to arrange itself into that one refrain—Basseterre, Guadaloupe—as if the city were learning a foreign word and repeating it without understanding, wearing it down with use.

For what I felt there was no help; how could I help feeling? He had been kind of late—hourly kinder. It was a month since we had settled the theological difference; in that time no quarrel had risen. Our peace was not that cold daughter of divorce; we had not lived aloof. He came oftener; he talked with me more; he spent hours with me with temper soothed, with eye content, with manner home-like and mild. Kind subjects had grown between us. He inquired into my plans of life; I told him. The school project pleased him; he made me repeat it more than once, though he called it an Alnaschar dream—my basket of eggs carried too gaily. Still, the mutual understanding was settling, fixing; feelings of union and hope made themselves profoundly felt; affection, deep esteem, dawning trust—each had fastened its bond.

What quiet lessons I had then! No more taunts on my “intellect,” no more threats of grating public exhibitions. In place of the jealous gibe, and the more jealous half-passionate eulogy, came mute, indulgent help; fond guidance; a tender forbearance which forgave but never praised. Sometimes he would sit many minutes and not speak at all; and when dusk or duty brought separation, he would leave with words like, “Il est doux, le repos! Il est précieux le calme bonheur!”

One evening—not ten days ago—he joined me while I walked in my alley. He took my hand. I looked up; I thought he meant to arrest my attention.

“Bonne petite amie,” he said softly; “douce consolatrice!”

Through his touch and with his words came a new feeling—a strange thought finding its path. Could he be becoming more than friend or brother? Did his look say something beyond fraternity? His eyes had more to speak; his hand drew me forward; his lips stirred to interpret—

No. Not now. The twilight alley was broken by an interruption, double and ominous: two forms faced us, bodeful as a shadow and its echo—a woman’s and a priest’s—Madame Beck and Père Silas.

The Père’s aspect I shall never forget. At first it wore a kind of Jean-Jacques sensibility, stirred by the affection he had surprised; then instantly it darkened with the jaundice of ecclesiastical jealousy. He spoke to me with unction; he looked on his pupil with sternness. As for Madame Beck, she saw nothing—nothing; though her kinsman, in her presence, retained the hand of the heretic foreigner, clasping it close and fast.

After that, when the sudden announcement of departure struck, my first reception of it was disbelief. Only repetition—many tongues, many minds accepting—forced it upon me. Of that week of suspense—blank, burning days—bringing no word from him, no explanation: I remember, but cannot describe its passage.

The last day broke. Now he would visit us; now he would come and speak farewell—or vanish mute and be seen no more.

That alternative seemed to exist in the mind of not one creature in the school. All rose at the usual hour; all breakfasted; all went to ordinary duty with the same trained phlegm. The house moved on its rails, tame, oiled, oblivious. I could scarcely breathe in an atmosphere so stagnant, so smothering. Would no one lend me a voice? Had no one a wish, a word, a prayer to which I could say Amen?

They had been unanimous in demanding trifles—a holiday, a treat, a remitted lesson. They would not now band together to besiege Madame Beck and insist upon a last interview with a master who had been loved—loved as they could love. Yet what is the love of the multitude? A sound that rises loud and falls at once, like applause in a theatre—ready for the next act.

I knew where he lived; the distance was scarcely a stone’s throw. Had he been in the next room—unsummoned, I could make no use of my knowledge. To follow, seek, remind, recall—there I had no faculty. M. Emanuel might have passed within reach of my arm; had he passed silent and unnoticing, silent and stirless I should have let him go.

Morning wasted; afternoon came. I thought all was over. My heart trembled; my blood ran troubled; I was sick, scarcely knowing how to keep at my post. Yet the little world plodded on indifferent—jocund even. Those very pupils who, seven days before, had sobbed at the news, now seemed to have forgotten both it and their emotion. Habit had swallowed feeling, as a loud street swallows birdsong.

A little before five—dismissal hour—Madame Beck sent for me to her chamber to read and translate an English letter, and to write the answer. Before we began, I noticed she closed both doors softly; she even shut and fastened the casement, though the day was hot and she generally required free air. Why this precaution? Suspicion leapt in me—keen, almost fierce. Did she wish to exclude sound? What sound?

I listened as I had never listened—like a wolf in winter, nose in the snow, sensing prey; hearing far off the traveller’s tramp. Yet I could listen and write. Halfway through the letter I heard a tread in the vestibule; my pen stopped. No bell had rung: Rosine—acting, no doubt, by orders—had anticipated such awakening. Madame saw me halt. She coughed, made a bustle, spoke louder. The tread passed towards the classes.

“Proceed,” she said.

My hand was fettered, my ear enchained, my thoughts carried off captive.

The classes were in another building; a hall parted them from the dwelling-house. Even so, through distance and partition, I heard a sudden stir of numbers—one whole division rising at once.

“They are putting away work,” said Madame.

It was indeed the hour; but why that sudden hush—that instant quelling of the tumult, as if a presence had entered and drawn a net over sound?

“Wait, Madame—I will see what it is.”

I set down my pen and left her. Or tried. She would not be left: powerless to detain, she rose and followed—close as my shadow. On the last stair I turned.

“Are you coming, too?” I asked.

“Yes,” she said, meeting my glance with a peculiar look—clouded, yet resolute.

We went on, not together, but she in my steps.

He had come.

Entering the first classe, I saw him—once more the form most familiar, standing in the centre of the girls. I do not doubt they had tried to keep him away; but he was there.

The pupils stood in a semicircle. He passed round, giving farewells—pressing each hand, touching each cheek with his lips. Foreign custom permitted it at such parting; yet it seemed to me, in that room, a ceremony solemn enough to last a lifetime. The air carried small sounds—skirts brushing, benches shifting, the soft kiss of farewell—each one a little cut.

Madame Beck’s closeness goaded me. I felt her breath at my neck and shoulder; I shrank under it, fevered. He was approaching; the semicircle was almost travelled. He came to the last pupil; he turned—

And Madame stepped before me.

Suddenly she magnified her proportions, amplified her drapery. She eclipsed me. I was hidden. She knew my weakness; she could calculate the moral paralysis that could strike me in crisis—the total default of self-assertion. She hastened to her kinsman, broke upon him volubly, mastered his attention, hurried him to the glass-door opening on the garden.

I think he looked round. Had I caught his eye, courage might have rushed in to aid feeling; there might have been a charge—perhaps a rescue. But already the room was confusion: the semicircle broke into groups; my figure was lost among thirty more conspicuous. Madame had her will. She got him away, and he had not seen me; he thought me absent.

Five o’clock struck. The loud dismissal-bell rang—metallic, tyrannous, cutting through every other sound. The school separated; the room emptied.

There is, in my memory, an entire darkness over some minutes I then passed alone—a grief inexpressible over a loss unendurable. What should I do—what should I do—when all my life’s hope was torn by the roots out of my riven, outraged heart?

What I should have done I know not, when a little child—the least in the school—broke with simplicity into the silent centre of that inward storm.

“Mademoiselle,” lisped the thin treble voice, “I am to give you that. M. Paul said I was to seek you all over the house, from the grenier to the cellar, and when I found you, to give you that.”

And the child delivered a note—the little dove dropped on my knee, its olive leaf plucked off. There was neither address nor name, only these words:

“It was not my intention to take leave of you when I said good-by to the rest, but I hoped to see you in classe. I was disappointed. The interview is deferred. Be ready for me. Ere I sail, I must see you at leisure, and speak with you at length. Be ready; my moments are numbered, and, just now, monopolized; besides, I have a private business on hand which I will not share with any, nor communicate—even to you.—PAUL.”

“Be ready.” Then it must be this evening. Was he not to go on the morrow? Of that I was certain: I had seen the date of his vessel’s departure advertised. I would be ready. Yet could that meeting be achieved? Time was short; the schemers watchful, active, hostile. The way of access looked strait as a gully, deep as a chasm; Apollyon straddled across it, breathing flames. Could my Greatheart overcome? Could my guide reach me?

Who could tell? Yet courage began—small, stubborn—to feed on that note, as one feeds on a crust when there is nothing else. It seemed to me I felt a pulse of his heart beating true to the whole throb of mine.

I waited my champion, and Apollyon came trailing his Hell behind him. If Eternity held torment, I do not think its form would be fiery rack, nor its nature despair. I think it would be suspense. Imagine a day among those days which never dawn and will not set: an angel entering Hades—standing, shining, smiling—delivering a prophecy of conditional pardon, kindling a doubtful hope of bliss, not now, but at an hour unlooked for; revealing, in his own grandeur, the height and compass of promise; then, towering, becoming a star and vanishing into his own Heaven. His legacy would be suspense—a worse boon than despair.

All that evening I waited—trusting the dove-sent olive leaf, yet, in the midst of trust, terribly fearing. Fear pressed heavy, cold and peculiar; I knew it for the partner of a rarely-belied presentiment. The first hours dragged. In spirit I clung to the flying skirts of the last; yet they passed like drift-cloud, like wrack scudding before storm.

Day burned away like a Yule-log—hot, consuming, red at the heart. Its crimson close perished; and I was left bent among cool blue shades, over pale ashen gleams of night.

Prayers were over; it was bed-time. My co-inmates retired. I still remained in the gloomy first classe, forgetting—disregarding—rules I had never broken before.

How long I paced I cannot tell. I must have been afoot many hours. Mechanically I moved benches and desks, making myself a path down the length of the room, as if I were laying out a narrow furrow in hard ground and must keep to it because it was the only way forward. There I walked; and when I was certain the whole household lay abed, quite out of hearing—there, at last, I wept. Reliant on Night, confiding in Solitude, I kept my tears sealed no longer. They heaved my heart; they tore their way. In this house, what grief could be sacred?

Soon after eleven—a very late hour in the Rue Fossette—the door unclosed quietly, not stealthily. A lamp’s flame invaded the moonlight, and Madame Beck entered with the same composed air as if she came on ordinary business at an ordinary season. She did not address me at once. She went to her desk, took her keys, and seemed to seek something. She loitered over the feigned search long—too long. She was calm—too calm; my mood could scarcely endure the pretence. Two hours earlier I had left behind my wonted respects and fears; now no yoke could be borne, no curb obeyed.

“It is more than time for retirement,” she said. “The rule of the house has already been transgressed too long.”

I gave no answer; I did not check my walk. When she came in my way I put her out of it.

“Let me persuade you to calm, Meess; let me lead you to your chamber,” she said, trying to speak softly.

“No,” I answered. “Neither you nor another shall persuade or lead me.”

“Your bed shall be warmed. Goton is sitting up still. She shall make you comfortable: she shall give you a sedative.”

“Madame,” I broke out, “you are a sensualist. Under all your serenity, your peace, your decorum, you are an undenied sensualist. Make your own bed warm and soft; take sedatives and meats, and drinks spiced and sweet, as much as you will. If you have any sorrow or disappointment—and, perhaps, you have—nay, I know you have—seek your own palliatives in your own chosen resources. Leave me. Leave me, I say!”

“I must send another to watch you, Meess: I must send Goton.”

“I forbid it. Let me alone. Keep your hand off me, and my life, and my troubles. Oh, Madame! in your hand there is both chill and poison. You envenom and you paralyze.”

“What have I done, Meess? You must not marry Paul. He cannot marry.”

“Dog in the manger!” I said—for I knew she secretly wanted him, had always wanted him. She called him “insupportable,” railed at him for a “dévot;” she did not love, but she wanted to marry that she might bind him to her interest. Deep into some of her secrets I had entered—how, I know not; by intuition, by inspiration that came I know not whence. Living with her had taught me too that, unless with an inferior, she must ever be a rival. She was my rival, heart and soul—secretly, under the smoothest bearing—unknown to all save her and myself.

For two minutes I stood over her, feeling that the whole woman was in my power; for in certain moods—stimulated states like that instant—her habitual disguise, mask and domino, became to me a mere network full of holes. I saw underneath a being heartless, self-indulgent, ignoble.

She retreated quietly. Meek and self-possessed—though uneasy—she said that if I would not be persuaded to rest, she must reluctantly leave me. Which she did at once, perhaps more glad to get away than I was to see her vanish.

This was the sole flash—truth-extorting—rencontre that ever passed between me and Madame Beck. It was never repeated. It did not change her manner to me; I do not know that she revenged it; I do not know that she hated me the worse for my fell candour. I think she bucklered herself with her strong mind’s secret philosophy and resolved to forget what it irked her to remember. To the end of our mutual lives there occurred no repetition of, no allusion to, that fiery passage.
