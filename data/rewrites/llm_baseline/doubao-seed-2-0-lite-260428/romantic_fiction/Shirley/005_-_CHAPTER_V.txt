The light, unruly good humour that had carried Robert Moore through the long, fraught night after the frame attack still clung to him when he roused at dawn. He and his overlooker Joe Scott had bunked down on makeshift beds cobbled from spare blankets and mill crates tucked in the nooks of the front and back counting houses, and Moore—who was always up before the first workman stirred—rose even earlier than usual that morning, humming a lively French folk tune under his breath as he laced his boots, loud enough to rouse Joe where he snored on his pallet.

"Ye haven't gone and lost all your brass then, maister?" Joe squawked, blearily pushing himself up.

"Not a stiver, mon garçon—my lad, that means. Up with you, now. We'll do a turn round the mill before the hands show their faces, and I'll lay out the whole of my new plan for you. We'll get the new machinery running yet, Joseph. You've heard of Bruce, haven't you? The Scottish king and the spider?"

"Th' arrand? Aye, I've read me history o' Scotland, know it as well as thee, I reckon. And I take your meaning: ye'll stick it out, no quitting."

"That's exactly what I mean."

"Is there many of your sort back in your country?" Joe asked, tucking his fold-up bed away in its cupboard.

"My country? And which do you think that is?"

"Why, France, isn't it?"

"Not a bit of it. The French seized Antwerp, sure, but that doesn't make me French.

"Holland then?"

"Don't muddle Antwerp and Amsterdam, man, I'm no Dutchman."

"Flanders?"

"Joe, you insult me! A Fleming? Me? Clumsy great nose sticking out, sloped-back thin forehead, pale blue eyes sitting right flat on my face? All body and no legs, like a Fleming is built? You don't know those Netherlanders, do you? I'm an Anversois, born and bred. My mother was Anversoise too, though her line was French, that's why I speak the language so easy."

"But your father were Yorkshire, so that makes you half one of us. Any fool can see it—ye're that keen to turn a profit, to get forrards, ye fit right in."

"You're an impudent rascal, Joe, but I grew up around Belgian factory workers who spoke their minds far rougher than you, so your bluntness doesn't ruffle me. The classe ouvrière, the working folk there, were brusque as anything to their employers."

"We allus speak our minds here in the north, that's the truth. Them soft young parsons and grand London folks get all shocked at our 'incivility'—and we love giving 'em something to gasp over, watching 'em roll their eyes and wave their hands like they've been stung by nettles, stammering 'Dear me! What dreadful vulgarity! How very coarse!'"

"You're a pack of savages, Joe. Don't tell me you think you're civilized?"

"Middling, maister, middling. Us manufacturing lads up north are a deal sharper than them slow farming folk down south. Trade sharpens wits, and a mechanic like me? You've got to think, ain't you? Working round the machinery, you see an effect, you hunt straight for the cause, and nine times out of ten you find it. I read a fair bit too, curious to know what the folks that run this country think they're doing with us. There's hundreds smarter than me—greasy mechanics reeking of machine oil, dyers with blue-stained hands that know more about what makes a fool of a law tick than soft ninnies like Christopher Sykes of Whinbury, or that strutting Irish curate Peter over in Helstone."

"Go on, I know you think you're a genius, Scott."

"Aye, I'm fair clever. Can tell cheese from chalk, and I've made the most of what chances I had, better than a whole lot of folks that reckon themselves above me. But Yorkshire's full of lads as good as me, and a few a damn sight better."

"Oh, you're a sublime, magnificent fellow—just a conceited prig with it all. Don't go thinking because you picked up a bit of practical math, or scraped a little chemistry knowledge out of the bottom of a dye vat, that you're some neglected unappreciated man of science. Don't go convincing yourself that because trade hits a rough patch sometimes, and you and your mates go short of work and bread, that all you working folk are martyrs, and the whole government's rotten to the core. And don't you dare try and tell me all the virtue lives in cottages and none at all in slated big houses. Human nature's the same no matter if you live under tiles or thatch—vice and virtue mixed up in every single soul, rich or poor, no exceptions. I've met villains with gold, villains with nothing, and villains sitting pretty on a modest little income exactly like Agar's old wish. The clock's about to strike six. Go ring that mill bell, Joe, sharp."

It was mid-February, so the dawn that crept up was thin, pale, and slow, just a faint silver ray piercing the dark brown night, turning the thick opaque shadows of the dale soft and half-translucent. No colour tinged the east, no warm flush touched the hills. The sun's fire felt all but quenched, as if last night's rain had drowned it. The wind that stirred was raw and biting, peeling back layers of night cloud to leave a pale silvery ring all round the horizon, no blue sky to be seen, just a thin stratum of pale grey vapour. The rain had stopped, but the earth was sodden through, every puddle and little rivulet brim full.

The mill windows glowed with lamplight, the bell clanged loud across the yard, and soon the little factory children came running, hurrying so fast we can hope they barely felt the bite of the sharp wind—especially since that winter they'd trundled to work through blizzards, torrential rain, and hard killing frost so bad it turned the puddles to ice.

Mr. Moore stood in the mill entrance to watch them file past, counting each head as they went. He called out a sharp, quiet reprimand to the stragglers, which Joe repeated a little more firmly once they reached the work floor. Neither master nor overlooker was cruel, even if they were strict: a boy who turned up far too late got fined a penny, which Moore made him hand over before he crossed the threshold, warning him the next offence would cost him twopence.

Rules are necessary for a mill, of course, and cruel masters make cruel rules—rules that were often enforced tyrannically in this era—but there are no child-beating slave-drivers here. I will not sully these pages with tales of whippings and torture. Neither Mr. Moore nor Joe Scott ever laid a hand to a child in their mill. Joe had once thrashed his own son hard for lying and sticking to it, that was true, but both men were too calm, too reasonable, to use corporal punishment for anything but the rarest of exceptions.

Moore wandered the mill, the yard, the dye house, and the warehouse until that sickly grey dawn thickened into full day. The sun even peeked over the dark ridge of the far hill: a white, disc of light, almost as chill and colourless as ice, gilding the top edge of the clouds above it, and staring solemnly down the whole length of the narrow little dale. It was eight o'clock. All the mill lamps were blown out. The whistle blew for breakfast, and the children poured out of the work rooms for their half hour off, clustering round their little tin cans of coffee and small wicker baskets of bread. Let us hope every last one of them had enough to eat. It would be a sad, shameful thing if they did not.

At last Moore left the mill yard, and turned his steps towards his little cottage home, a short walk away down a lane banked high with hedges that gave the small whitewashed place a quiet, secluded air. A green wooden porch arched over the front door; the garden beds at its feet poked up bare brown stalks, budless and flowerless now, but promising climbing roses and honeysuckle for the summer ahead. The lawn and borders were nothing but black mud right then, save for a few sheltered nooks where the very first emerald green snowdrop and crocus shoots were peeking up through the earth. Winter had been long and harsh, the last deep snow only melted three days prior in yesterday's rains, patches of white still lingering on the high hill hollows and peaks. The lawn grass was not green yet, bleached pale and pale from frost. Three graceful, small trees stood clustered beside the cottage, tall enough to feel imposing with no other trees nearby, framing the little white house perfectly. It was a snug, peaceful nest for quiet reflection, but far too small to hold all of Moore's ambition and restless energy for long.

He did not go inside straight away. He fetched a spade from the little tool shed by the back door, and started turning the cold soil in his garden, digging for a full fifteen minutes undisturbed, before an upstairs window creaked open, and a woman's voice called out to him.

"Eh, bien! Tu ne déjeûnes pas ce matin?"

"Breakfast ready, Hortense?" Moore called back, speaking in their shared native French.

"Of course it is. It has been sitting hot for half an hour."

"Then I'm ready too. I'm ravenous enough to eat a dog."

He threw down his spade and stepped inside. The narrow front hall led straight to a tiny parlour, where breakfast was laid out: coffee, bread and butter, and the very un-English addition of slow-stewed pears. Hortense Moore, his older sister, presided over the table. She was a tall, sturdy woman of thirty-five, with thick black hair twisted up in curl papers, high colour in her cheeks, a small neat nose, and bright little black eyes. The lower half of her face was broader than the upper, her forehead small and slightly lined, her expression a little fretful but not unkind, the kind of look you half want to tease and half feel sorry for. She was dressed for her morning housework in a simple woven petticoat and striped cotton camisole, the petticoat cut short enough to show a pair of stocky ankles and feet that were far from elegant.

Do not mistake her for a slattern. Hortense was the most orderly, thrifty housekeeper alive. This was the customary morning dress she had worn her whole life back in Antwerp, and she refused to adopt English conventions just because she was living in a remote Yorkshire dale, convinced sticking to her old Belgian ways was a mark of virtue. She thought very highly of herself, and not without reason—she was loyal, hardworking, and unshakably honest, even if she overestimated her own wisdom, and never noticed her little flaws of being narrow-minded, overly touchy about her own dignity, and quick to take offence over tiny trifles. Still, when no one threatened her pride or her family's honour, she was warm and generous. She adored both her brothers, as the last remaining members of their once wealthy old family, and she thought Robert, the older of the two, was the greatest man alive. Any opposition to him, or to her, was nothing short of monstrous injustice.

So the moment Robert sat down at the breakfast table, and she had served him his stewed pears and cut him a thick slice of buttered Belgian tartine, she launched into a full tirade of horror about the previous night's attack on the new frames.

"The idea! To destroy them! Disgraceful, shameful behaviour! The working people here are both stupid and cruel, exactly like the English servants, especially that girl Sarah. She is unbearable!"

"She looks clean and hard-working enough," Moore murmured.

"Looks! I don't care how she looks, I'm not saying she's dirty or lazy—but she is insolent! She argued with me for a full quarter of an hour yesterday about how to boil beef. Said I boiled it to rags, that no English person would eat our bouilli, that the broth was nothing but greasy warm water. And that barrel of sauerkraut I prepared with my own hands down in the cellar? She called it a tub of pig slop! I'm half driven mad by that girl, and I can't even fire her, because I'll only end up with someone worse. You're just the same with your workmen, poor dear brother."

"I'm afraid you're not very happy in England, Hortense."

"It is my duty to be happy where you are, of course, but otherwise there are a thousand things that make me long for Antwerp. Everyone here is ill-bred. The mill girls snicker when they walk in the kitchen and find me in my jupon and camisole making dinner—you know I can't trust Sarah to cook a single dish properly. The last time I was invited out to tea, they treated me like I was invisible, no respect at all for my family name, the Gérards and the Moores, we are perfectly entitled to deference! Back in Antwerp everyone treated me with the utmost distinction, here I think they must laugh at my accent, though I know I pronounce English perfectly."

"Hortense, in Antwerp everyone knew us as rich. Here, everyone only ever knew us as poor."

"See? That's how mercenary everyone is! And last Sunday, it was pouring rain, so I walked to church in my neat black sabots—perfectly respectable for muddy country roads in winter, we all wear them at Mons and Leuze outside Brussels. I walked up the aisle as calm as you please, and four ladies and four gentlemen hid their faces behind their prayer books and laughed at me!"

"Then don't wear the sabots again. I told you before, they don't suit this country."

"They are not the rough peasant clogs! They are clean, proper black sabots! Let anyone try and walk the muddy Flemish chaussées in delicate Paris boots, they'll change their tune fast!"

"Forget Mons and Leuze and the Flanders roads. When in Rome, do as the Romans do. And I'm not so sure about the camisole and petticoat either. No English lady dresses like that in the morning. Ask Caroline Helstone."

"Caroline? *I* ask Caroline for fashion advice? *I* consult her? She is the child who should be consulting me! She is nothing but a baby."

"She's seventeen, almost eighteen. Old enough to know all about gowns and shoes."

"Do not spoil her, Robert. Do not make her think she is more important than she is. She is modest and unassuming now, let us keep her that way."

"Fair enough. Is she coming this morning, like usual?"

"She will be here at ten for her French lesson."

"She doesn't sneer at you, does she?"

"Of course not. She respects me more than anyone else here, she sees that I am educated, intelligent, well-bred. I do not care for her deeply, of course, I do not take sudden silly fancies, my regard for her is steady. She is my cousin, after all, and her position is lonely, her uncle is far too strict with her. She is a good pupil, though of course she is not perfect. There is much that needs correcting in her."

"Pour me one last cup of coffee, then, and tell me what those faults of hers are."

"I am so glad you have a good appetite after that terrible night. Well, Caroline is sometimes quiet and reserved in a way that is not properly girlish and submissive, and she has little bursts of wild, unsettled hurry that quite unsettle me, though most of the time she is far too quiet, far too thoughtful, pensive for no reason I can name. I will train her, of course, give her a proper system for her thoughts, full control over her silly feelings. The other day I set her to memorize some short light French poetry to practice her pronunciation, and she was turning the pages so fast I could see her curl her lip in absolute scorn. She said 'All this bores me to death.' And then she suddenly stopped, memorized one little piece by Chénier, *La Jeune Captive*, and came and recited it to me so passionately, so full of feeling, you would have thought that scribbler Chénier was better than Racine and Corneille put together. That unruly excess of feeling? That is the 'unsettled hurry' I mean. She has no regulated mind at all, but I will fix that for her."

"Quite right, Hortense. Wait, isn't that her shadow passing the window? She's here."

"Ah, yes. She is half an hour early. My child, what brings you here before your lesson time?"

The door had swung open to reveal Caroline Helstone, wrapped in a heavy winter mantle gathered gracefully around her slight, slender frame, cheeks pink from the sharp cold wind, eyes bright and worried.

"I ran all the way here to make sure you two are alright. I only heard about the attack on the frames this morning at breakfast, my uncle told me. I knew you would both be so grieved."

"Unspeakable, the whole thing," Hortense sighed. "You sympathize with us? Your uncle sympathizes?"

"He was furious! He rode out with you last night, didn't he, to Stilbro' Moor? I thought he went with you to meet the wagons?"

"Aye, we set out ready for a fight, Caroline," Robert smiled, the faint ghost of amusement on his face. "But the lads we went to rescue met us halfway, no battle needed."

"No one got hurt, then? No one was injured?"

"Not a scratch. Only Joe Scott's wrists a little sore, they tied them behind his back too tight."

"You were not there, were you? Not when the wagons got attacked?"

"No. You hardly ever get to be on the spot for the kind of fight you actually want to help with."

"Where are you going this morning, then? I saw Murgatroyd saddling your horse out in the yard."

"To Whinbury. It's market day."
