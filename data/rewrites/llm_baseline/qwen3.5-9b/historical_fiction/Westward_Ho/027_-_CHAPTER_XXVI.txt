"When captains courageous, whom death could not daunt,
Did march to the siege of the city of Gaunt,
They muster'd their soldiers by two and by three,
But the foremost in battle was Mary Ambree.
When brave Sir John Major was slain in her sight,
Who was her true lover, her joy and delight,
Because he was murther'd most treacherously,
Then vow'd to avenge him fair Mary Ambree."

With one final gaze at the golden tropic sea and the radiant tropical evenings along the coast of New Granada, the scene shifted to the shimmering Bay of Santa Marta. Before the land breeze, the bay rippled like a single sheet of living flame. The vast forests sparkled with countless fireflies, while a lazy mist clinging to the inner hills glowed golden in the fading sunlight. High above, nineteen thousand feet in the air, the majestic peak of Horqueta pierced the abyss, its silhouette rose-red against the dark blue dome of the heavens. As the rosy cone faded to a dull leaden gray, the stars began to emerge one by one. Venus, shining like a second moon, tinted the snowy summit with gold and cast a long, rippling line of yellow light across the water. Everywhere, there was glory and abundance. It was no wonder that the earth in this enchanted land was rich beneath its surface as it was upon it; the sky, the hills, and the sea formed a sparkling garland of jewels—surely the soil itself must be encrusted with emeralds, rubies, grains of gold, and wreaths of native silver.

Thus pondered the Bishop of Cartagena, seated in the state cabin of the great galleon *The City of the True Cross*. Looking pensively out the window toward the shore, the good man was in a state of holy calm. His sturdy figure reclined in one easy chair, his thick ankles resting on another, beside a table laden with oranges, limes, guavas, pineapples, and every fruit of the Indies. An Indian girl, adorned with scarves and gold chains, waved a feather fan to keep the flies at bay. Beside her, in a bucket of ice brought down from Horqueta—a gift from a pious Spanish lady who had "spent" an Indian or two to deliver it—stood several flasks of virtuous Alicant wine. Yet, the bishop was not so selfish as to enjoy the ice or wine alone. At his special request, Colonel Don Pedro, Intendant Don Alvarez, and Captain Don Paul had joined him that evening, accompanied by two friars seated at the far end of the table. Together, they strove to console the prelate regarding the unsatisfactory state of his cathedral town, which had recently been sacked and burned by an old acquaintance, Sir Francis Drake.

"We have suffered greatly, señores—ah, greatly suffered!" sniffled the bishop, quoting Scripture in the fashionable manner of the day. His delivery was glib, though often irreverent, with texts twisted boldly and interspersed with complaints about Tita and the mosquitoes. "Great suffering, truly; but there shall remain a remnant—a remnant like the shaking of the olive tree and the gleaned grapes after the vintage is done. Ah! Gold? Yes, I trust Our Lady's mercies are not closed, nor Her arms shortened. Look, señores!" He pointed majestically out the window. "It looks like gold! It smells of gold, as I might say, by poetic license. Indeed, the very waves, as they ripple past us, sing of gold, gold, gold!"

"It is a great privilege," remarked the Intendant, "to receive such graceful comfort simultaneously from a clergyman and a scholar."

"A poet as well," added Don Pedro. "You have no idea what sweet sonnets—"

"Hush, Don Pedro—hush! If I, a mateless bird, have wasted an idle hour teaching lovers how to sing, so what? I am a clergyman, señores; but I am a man, and I can feel, señores; I can sympathize; I can mitigate; I can excuse. Who understands better than I how much human nature lurks within us, fallen sons of Adam?" He turned to the trembling girl. "Tita!"

"Um?" she responded with a genuine Indian grunt.

"Fill His Excellency the Intendant's glass. Does much more treasure descend, illustrious señor? Might the poor of Mary hope for a few more crumbs from their Mistress's table?"

"Not a peso, I fear," the Intendant replied. "The great white cow up there"—he pointed to Horqueta—"has been milked dry for this year."

"Oh!" He gazed upward at the magnificent snow-covered peak. "Good only for cooling wine, eh? And as safe for the time being as Solomon's birds."

"Solomon's birds? Please explain your obscure allusion, my lord."

"Enlighten us, Your Excellency; enlighten us."

"Ah! There lies a tale. You know the holy birds that run up and down the Prado in Seville among the ladies' delicate feet—with hooked noses and cinnamon crests? Of course. Hoopoes—the *Upupa*, as the classics call them. Well, señores, once upon a time, the story goes, these hoopoes all wore golden crowns upon their heads. And, señores, they paid the price—eh? One day, all the birds and beasts gathered to pay homage at the court of His Most Catholic Majesty, King Solomon, and among them came these same hoopoes. They had a small request to make, poor rogues. Can you guess what it was? Why, that King Solomon should pray for them that they might wear any kind of crown except these golden ones. For—listen, Tita, and observe the trap of riches—mankind hunted, shot, trapped, and snared them solely for the sake of these golden crowns, making life a unbearable burden. So Solomon prayed, and instead of golden crowns, they all received crowns of feathers. Ever since, señores, they live as merrily as crickets in an oven, and also hold the honor of bearing the name of His Most Catholic Majesty, King Solomon."

"Tita! Fill the Señor Commandant's glass. Fray Gerundio, what are you whispering down there, sir?"

Fray Gerundio had merely murmured to his brother regarding the Bishop's story of Solomon's birds:

"Then, friend," replied the other, a Dominican like Gerundio but of a darker, sterner countenance, "within a week corrupt human nature would discover some fresh bauble for which to kill and be killed in vain."

"What is that, Fray Gerundio?" asked the Bishop again.

"I merely remarked that it would be well for the world if all mankind offered the same prayer as the hoopoes."

"World, sir? What do you know of the world? Convert your Indians, sir, if you please, and leave matters of state to your superiors. You will excuse him, señores" (lowering his voice to address the Dons). "A very worthy and pious man, but a poor peasant's son; and besides—you understand. A little error here; too much fasting and vigilance, I fear, good man." The Bishop tapped his forehead knowingly, indicating that Fray Gerundio's faculties were in an unsatisfactory condition.

The Friar heard and saw with a quiet smile. He belonged to that rare order of excellent men whom the cruelties of their compatriots had inspired (just as darkness highlights light by mere contrast), inspiring names like Las Casas and Gasca, and many others inscribed in the Book of Life, to acts of love and pious daring worthy of any creed or age. True Protestants, they protested even before kings against the evils closest to them, the sins that truly plagued them; true liberals, they did not hesitate to call the dark-skinned heathen their brothers; and asserting in terms that astonish us today, remembering the era in which they spoke, the inherent freedom of every being wearing the flesh and blood their Lord wore; true martyrs, they witnessed for Christ and too often received the rewards of such testimony in slander and contempt. Such was Fray Gerundio: a poor, humble, clumsily tongued peasant's son who could rarely string three sentences together unless he became eloquent, clutching a crucifix amidst groups of Indians or Negroes. Accustomed to such rebukes as the Bishop's, he accepted them for what they were worth and sipped his wine in silence while the conversation continued.

"They say," observed the Commandant, "that a very small Plate fleet will sail to Spain this year."

"What else?" retorted the Intendant. "What have we to send, in the name of all saints, since these accursed English Lutherans have swept us clean?"

"And if we had anything to send," added the Sea-Captain, "what vessel would we send it in? That fiend incarnate, Drake—"

"Ah!" exclaimed His Holiness. "Spare my ears! Don Pedro, you will oblige my weakness by not mentioning that man; his name is Tartarean, unfit for polite lips.
