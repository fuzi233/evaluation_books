The ink on my Oxford diploma had barely dried when I retreated to the family estate, seeking respite before the weight of inheritance settled on my shoulders. My parents had left me alone in this world long ago, and the house stood as a silent monument to generations I scarcely knew—generations whose ghosts lingered not in portraits, but in the spines of ancient books.

The library was a living thing, sprawling across the ground floor like an organism that had outgrown its shell. Rooms bled into one another through arches and narrow passages, each shelf a fossil record of shifting intellectual tides. I preferred the heart of it, the great chamber where books climbed the walls like ivy, their leather and vellum whispering under the weight of centuries. Ptolemy, Paracelsus, and the elder Bacon kept company with Maxwell and Darwin, their contradictions a comfort. Truth, after all, was a shapeshifter.

That evening, the rain had painted the windows gray until sunset split the clouds. A bloody light spilled through the glass, igniting the fountain on the lawn—then, as I turned, it found the only painting in the room. The portrait hung like an afterthought in a shallow niche, a man whose name I barely recalled, though his eyes now seemed to lock onto mine with unsettling clarity.

Then—movement. A flicker at the edge of vision. Near the farthest shelf, a figure stretched toward a book, gaunt and deliberate. I blinked; the air settled. A trick of the light, I told myself. Yet when I reached later for a volume on electromagnetism, the gap in the shelf yawned empty. By morning, it was back.

Three nights later, the library played another trick. The closet door—a sly thing disguised as shelves, its false backs labeled with lost titles—had always charmed me. But now, the slim vellum corner of a manuscript, wedged decades ago between facades, was gone. The butler, a man who had served my father as a boy, paled when I asked. He knew nothing. Yet his silence was heavier than his words.

I saw the old man again at dusk—his coat dark and polished with age, slipping through the closet door. I chased the shadow, found nothing. But when I returned to my chair, the manuscript was back in its place, snug as a bone in a grave.

The butler folded like parchment under my questions. There were stories, of course. A librarian named Raven, centuries old, who had served an ancestor obsessed with "unwholesome" books. They vanished together. Only Raven returned—sometimes.

"Let him visit," I said, running a finger along the restored manuscript’s edge. It didn’t budge. "But keep the maids from gossiping."

The butler nodded. He’d never seen the ghost. Only the books it touched.

Days passed without incident. Then, about a week later, something extraordinary occurred.

The manuscript fragment still haunted my thoughts. I had tried—and failed—to free it from whatever unseen force held it in place. But today, my focus shifted to the books themselves. The closet’s stale air made me uneasy about their condition, and I resolved to inspect them properly.

Just as I rose from my chair, I glimpsed movement near the closet door—a figure, slight and stooped, drifting toward the far end of the room. A shadow, perhaps, but one with unnerving clarity: a man in a threadbare coat, its tails nearly brushing the floor, revealing spindly legs clad in black stockings and wide, slipper-like shoes. Without hesitation, I followed.

He led me out of the library, across the hall, and up the grand staircase to the first floor. Past the stately rooms we went, down a corridor, then up a narrower stair to the second floor. Here, the house grew unfamiliar—a maze of passages I had never explored as a child.

At last, we reached a wooden stair, winding and creaky. Halfway up, my guide vanished. No sound, no trace. The top of the stair opened into a vast garret, its high beams crisscrossing beneath the roof. Dust-laden light seeped through cobwebbed skylights, painting the air with a dull glow. Doors stood ajar, hinting at unseen chambers.

Then I saw it—an unpainted wooden enclosure, its door slightly open. Hoping the stranger might be inside, I pushed it wider.

The small room was bathed in a lifeless light, as if abandoned by time itself. Sunbeams, weak and diffuse, slanted through swirling motes, illuminating a tall mirror with a tarnished surface. Its ebony frame bore a black eagle, wings outstretched, gripping a golden chain from which hung a dark sphere.

At first, I thought it merely reflected the room—until I realized it showed nothing at all. No chamber. No me.

Then the wall seemed to dissolve.

Before me stretched a desolate landscape: heathland, barren and flat, broken by low, oddly shaped hills. Beyond them, distant mountains cut the horizon. A lone raven, grizzled with age, hopped toward me with deliberate grace, pecking at the ground.

Unthinking, I stepped closer—then stumbled. My foot caught on something (the mirror’s frame?), and suddenly, I stood not before the glass, but *within* the scene. The air was real, the earth solid beneath my feet. The raven lifted its head, fixing me with a gleaming eye.

I was no longer in the house.

I was *elsewhere*.

I turned, searching the landscape behind me, but the world had dissolved into a haze—no clear lines, no familiar shapes. Was it mist or meadow? Cloud or cliff? My senses refused to settle. Only one truth stood firm: I recognized nothing.

To test the illusion, I stretched out my hands, stepping blindly, hoping touch might correct sight. But my fingers grasped only air. The ground beneath me was solid, the wind whispered through low plants, yet the world refused to make sense.

Then my gaze fell upon the raven. It stood a short distance away, watching me with an expression both solemn and amused. For a moment, I considered speaking to it—absurd, of course. Yet when I turned away, unease coiled tighter in my chest. Had I crossed into a place where the laws of my world no longer held? Could a man step beyond order and become prey to the lawless?

And yet—the raven remained. The earth beneath me. The wind in my ears.

*“How did I get here?”* The words left my lips before I could stop them.

A voice answered—harsh, croaking, yet deliberate. *“You came through the door.”*

I spun, searching for a speaker, but there was only the raven, its beak parted as if waiting. The sunless air cast no shadows, yet the bird seemed to hold its own darkness within it.

I must make this clear, though words falter. I was in a place—or a *state*—so unlike the world I knew that language buckled under the weight of description. Already I feel the truth slipping, reshaping as I grasp for it, like a dream dissolving upon waking.

The raven spoke again. *“You did not see the door because it was not a door *in*—it was a door *out.* The more doors you pass through, the deeper you go.”*

*“Where am I?”* I demanded.

*“Unanswerable,”* it said. *“You know nothing of ‘where.’ To understand, you must first make yourself at home.”*

*“By *doing.* The sooner you begin, the better. Until then, neither in nor out will make sense.”*

*“I didn’t mean to come here,”* I muttered.

*“Stumbling in doesn’t mean you’ll stumble out. Whether your arrival was *unfortunate* remains to be seen.”*

I bristled. *“Do you never leave?”*

*“Depends on the man. Or the bird.”* It tilted its head. *“Now, my turn: Who are you?”*

The question struck like a blade. Who *was* I? My name—if I ever had one—meant nothing here. I had no answer.

The raven laughed, a sound like dry sticks breaking. *“If you don’t know *yourself,* how can you know *me?*”* Then it shifted—not in form, but in *essence.* One moment, a bird; the next, a gaunt man in a black coat; then a raven again.

*“You—you were the librarian,”* I stammered.

*“Does it matter? You called me a raven. That was truth enough.”* It stepped back, its voice fading. *“No one is *himself* until he first knows *that* he is, then *what* he is. Home is the only place where you can go out and in. Find it.”*

Then it was gone.

I wandered, unmoored. Was I dead? Was this the afterlife? The air hummed with unseen presences. The raven’s words gnawed at me: *Do something.* But what?

A pine forest rose ahead. I entered, desperate for shelter. Between the trees, a shimmering *something* hovered—not light, not substance, but a trembling in the air. I reached for it, stumbled, and fell.

When I opened my eyes, I was back in the garret, the mirror looming before me. The eagle atop it seemed to smirk. Terror seized me. I fled, the house itself now alien, its corridors hungry.

Down, down, until I collapsed in the library. The garret was a weight upon the house—upon *me.* Its unseen inhabitants could descend at any moment. I was not safe.

*“If I don’t know my own mind,”* I thought, *“what protects me from it?”*

Then, as if summoned, the raven’s voice echoed: *Who are you?*

I had no answer.

Desperate, I turned to the hidden door, the mutilated book jutting from the shelf. Inside, fragmented verses teased my understanding—phrases that stirred something deeper than thought. I copied what I could, chasing meaning, but the words slipped like smoke.

Exhaustion took me. That night, I slept without dreams.

By morning, the garret’s dread had faded.

But the question remained.

The morning sun burned bright, but the air hung thick, restless—a storm brewing beneath the blue. I twisted the milky sapphire on my finger, searching for the star trapped within its depths. The light caught it wrong, smearing the gem’s heart into a haze. Then, for an instant, a black eye flickered in the stone’s core, staring back at me. I fumbled the ring, and it struck the floor with a sound like a lock clicking shut.

By the time I retrieved it, the eye was gone. So was the sun.

Clouds swallowed the sky whole. The wind rose, then the lightning—a single, bone-shaking crack—and the rain fell like a blade between worlds. I leaned into the open window, breathing the charged air, when a shape cut through the downpour: a raven, black as a hole in the world, striding toward me as if the storm were nothing at all.

It bowed at the sill, wings tucked like a gentleman’s coattails, then stepped inside.

“You,” I said.

The bird didn’t answer. It stalked past me, tail feathers dragging a wet trail across the floor, and paused at a narrow door leading to the garden. I opened it—not out of courtesy, but curiosity. The raven hopped into the porch, where the vines shuddered under the rain’s weight. Thunder rolled again, longer this time. The bird turned its head, slow as a clock’s hand, and looked at me.

*You hear that?* it might as well have said.

“Lovely weather for worms,” I muttered.

“Yes,” the raven croaked. “Easy digging. You should see the steppes of Uranus—rains like this for weeks. Splendid for the burrowers.”

I blinked. “Burrowers?”

“The things that live under. Not like your earthworms. Bigger. Stranger.” It tilted its head. “Ever seen a dry-bog serpent uncoil? No? Pity.”

The storm broke as suddenly as it came. The raven shook water from its wings. “Time to go.”

“Go where?”

“Where we’re needed.”

“I’m needed here.”

The bird laughed—a sound like a nail dragged over slate. “You’ve never been needed here. Come.”

It leapt onto the sodden grass. I hesitated, then followed. The sun tore through the clouds, turning every raindrop into a shard of glass. The raven stabbed its beak into the earth and flung a worm skyward. The thing unfurled wings—red and black, a living ember—and vanished into the light.

“Worms don’t turn into butterflies,” I said.

“Here they do.” The raven shrugged. “But I’m no scholar. Just a graveyard’s keeper.”

I turned, and the world turned with me.

The house was gone. The garden. Only pines remained, their shadows stretching like fingers.

“What have you done?” My voice sounded thin, foreign.

The raven pecked at the dirt. “You did it. You stepped through.”

“Take me back.”

“Can’t.”

“Why not?”

“Because,” it said, “you’re not lost. You’re found.”

The trees whispered. The air smelled of roses and old music.

“Where are we?” I demanded.

“Somewhere. Nowhere. The place between dimensions.” The raven fluffed its feathers. “That tree? It’s growing through your kitchen hearth. Walk into it, and you’ll walk through the fire.”

I laughed, sharp and brittle. “Nonsense.”

“Is it? You didn’t believe in me either.”

Silence. Then, softer: “Your housekeeper’s niece is playing the piano. The hyacinths are tangled in the strings. Listen.”

I strained—heard nothing. But the scent of roses thickened, sweet and strange.

“This isn’t possible,” I whispered.

The raven’s eye gleamed. “Neither were you. Until you were.”

It hopped ahead, tail flicking. “Come. My wife will want to meet you.”

I had no choice.

The adventure had already begun.

The raven led me deeper into the pine forest, where the air hummed with quiet reverence. We walked in silence, the ancient trees bending over us like sentinels of another age. Their trunks twisted into shapes that whispered of time long past, their bark etched with stories only the wind could read.

At last, the woods thinned, and the raven paused. His beak lifted toward the edge of the heath.

*"Do you see that hawthorn?"* he asked.

I squinted at the gnarled shape in the distance. "I see an old man, bent and white with years."

*"Look again,"* he insisted.

The image shifted—a thorned tree, its branches heavy with blossoms despite the season.

"But it isn’t spring," I protested.

*"The hawthorn blooms when it chooses,"* the raven replied. *"That one grows in the ruins of the old church on your land. You meant to tell your bailiff to leave its yard wild, didn’t you? Before the storm came."*

A memory flickered—the scent of roses, the quiet of graves untouched by ploughs.

*"Listen,"* the raven murmured.

I strained my ears. Was that music? A whisper of hymns long forgotten, or just the wind threading through stone?

*"They still gather there,"* he said.

"Who?"

"Why do they go?"

"Do they pray aloud?"

*"No. Prayer is quieter now. A thing of the heart."* His wing twitched upward. *"Look—there goes one."*

A white pigeon spiraled into the sky, wings catching the sun.

"A bird," I said.

*"A prayer,"* he corrected. *"Alive. Taking flight."*

"But a prayer is a thought—how can it have wings?"

*"You think only in words. But some hearts think in living things."* His gaze dropped suddenly to the base of a granite slab. *"And here’s another."*

A flower bloomed where none had been before—petals like pale fire, a center of gold. It was unlike anything I had ever seen, and it filled me with a longing I couldn’t name.

*"A prayer-flower,"* the raven said.

"How do you know?"

"Could you teach me to recognize them?"

*"No. That’s between you and the flower."* He tilted his head. *"The universe will make a fool of you until you learn to see for yourself."*

I stared at the blossom, its edges trembling in the breeze. It was more than a plant—it was a voice. And if I listened, perhaps I’d hear what it was saying.

Awe settled over me. Somewhere, a heart was speaking—and the earth was answering.

The moor stretched before us—a barren expanse of rock and withered moss, lifeless under a leaden sky. The sun hung low, smothered in gray, casting no warmth, only a dull glow that seemed to mock the idea of light. The wind sliced through the air, carrying with it a chill that spoke of places untouched by day.

“At last,” the raven croaked, his voice rough with fatigue. “A journey that should have taken wings. I could have flown to Paradise and back by now.”

“Winter already?” I asked, disoriented. “It feels as though we left only hours ago.”

“Time bends when you move beyond the pull of the world,” he replied. “But here we are. My wife’s house—though she lets me call it the sexton’s cottage.”

I glanced around. No churchyard, no graves—only the endless moor.

The raven tilted his head, his beak sweeping the horizon. “Look again.”

And then I saw it. Not a cemetery of stone and sod, but the earth itself—a vast, unmarked grave. The wind howled over it, whispering names forgotten. The raven was no mere caretaker; he was the keeper of all things laid to rest, his domain as boundless as the sky.

Winter slept here, wrapped in ice. Spring stirred at dawn, fleeting as a dream. Summer blazed, brief and fierce, before autumn crept in, dying with the first breath of night.

The cottage loomed ahead, its door a dark mouth in the fading light. As we neared, the sun plunged below the horizon, and the cold struck like a blade. I staggered inside, the wind slamming the door shut behind me.

Silence.

A single candle burned on a rough-hewn table. Against the wall stood what I took for a coffin lid—until it swung open, revealing a woman.

She was white—white as bone, as untouched snow—yet her face held a warmth that defied the chill. Her eyes eclipsed all else, deep and dark, twin voids where stars flickered. They were alive in a way the rest of her was not, as if the universe had pooled into them and left the shell behind.

“Mr. Vane,” the raven announced.

“Welcome,” she said, her voice a buried melody.

I could not speak.

“He is not weary,” the raven observed.

“Then why bring him?” she asked.

“A suspicion,” he replied. “Precipitate, perhaps.”

“A man must work before he rests,” I protested, though the words felt hollow.

Her gaze held mine, unreadable. “The day begins with sleep.”

The raven—no, the *man*—shifted before me. No longer a bird, but the librarian I had known, his coat frayed, his face gaunt beneath the pallor of long vigils. His eyes, pale blue and ringed with shadows, held a kindness that belied their sharpness.

“You knew I was no raven,” he said.

“I saw you dig worms from the earth,” I admitted. “They became butterflies.”

“A sexton’s work,” he said. “Books and graves are much the same—both hold what once was alive.”

I shook my head. “You confuse me.”

“Good.”

His wife stood motionless by the door, a specter in white.

“We’ve traveled far,” the sexton said to her. “We hunger.”

“You know I give only when asked,” she replied.

I swallowed. “Please. Food. Drink.”

She brought bread and wine, simple yet potent. With each bite, the weight of the journey lessened. Drowsiness crept over me.

“I’ve earned neither,” I murmured.

“Sleep is a gift,” the sexton said. “Not a wage.”

A small black cat leapt onto his lap. He stroked it absently, as though smoothing earth over a fresh grave.

“One of Mara’s,” he said. “Send it out. She may need it.”

His wife took the creature and vanished.

“How do I rest here,” I asked, “if none wake on their own?”

“By surrendering,” he answered.

“And if I don’t wake?”

His gaze was steady. “Do you trust me?”

The candle flickered. The silence deepened.

“Yes,” I said.

“Then come.”

She returned, lifting the light. The sexton rose, and I followed them into the dark.

A breath of glacial air rushed over me as I crossed the threshold. The door swung shut behind us, sealing us inside. The sexton murmured something to his wife, and she turned toward me—transfigured. Her eyes, once luminous, now blazed with an unbearable radiance, as if life itself, eternal and untamed, had settled upon her features. Her beauty was terrifying, a brilliance too fierce to meet. Even her hands glowed, every delicate line shimmering like moonlit pearl. I averted my gaze, relieved when she looked away.

The candle’s frail light barely pierced the darkness. At first, I saw nothing—then a pale gleam, something raised from the floor. A bed? Could anything living sleep in such cold? And if it did, no wonder it would never wake. Beyond, fainter glimmers flickered, scattered like dying stars.

We moved forward. The first shape resolved into a human form beneath a snow-white sheet—still, silent, faceless in the dimness. Then another. And another. An aisle of couches stretched before us, each bearing its own shrouded sleeper. Dread coiled in my chest. The chamber yawned endlessly, vanishing into shadow. Were these the sexton’s books? His silent, unwaking library?

*"One of the cellars I tend,"* the sexton murmured, as if wary of disturbing the air. *"Wine set here to ripen. But it’s dark for a stranger’s eyes."*

*"The moon comes,"* his wife replied, her voice low and sweet, carrying the weight of ancient sorrows long surrendered.

As she spoke, moonlight spilled through an opening in the wall, igniting a thousand icy reflections. The couches stretched beyond sight, each a solitary island in a sea of stillness. The sleep here was not death—not quite. It was something deeper, something I could not name.

The moon climbed higher, her light cold as the void between stars. Where it touched the sheets, they gleamed blue, and the faces beneath—pale, serene—seemed to leach warmth from the very air. Some bore the faint scars of past suffering, not yet erased by time. A man in his prime, his dark beard like frozen ink. A girl, her parted lips still holding the ghost of a sigh. A white-haired woman, her expression not submission but *certainty*, as if she had seen the axis of the universe and found it just.

*"Pardon me,"* some faces seemed to whisper. *"I died only yesterday."*

We reached three empty couches, beyond which lay a woman, her hand upturned, a dark stain at its center. Beside her, a man, his fist clenched as if still gripping a sword.

*"Does it heal?"* the sexton asked his wife, peering at the woman’s hand.

*"Well enough,"* she replied. *"The nail found nothing left to wound."*

I could bear it no longer. *"Are they not dead?"*

The sexton’s voice was a hushed thing. *"I scarcely remember what your world means by *dead.* Here, it is... different."* He gestured to the endless rows. *"This is but one vault. Outside, they lie thicker than autumn leaves. The moon reads them all night."*

*"Our moon does not rot,"* he said. *"She preserves. Your world buries its dead beneath stones, as if to pin them down. Here, I wait for the hour to wake them."*

Madness. All of it. But how could I flee? I was adrift in a realm where space itself seemed to fold, where the dead were not dead, only waiting.

*"This is your couch,"* the sexton said, pointing.

*"Why *this* one?"* I demanded, my voice shaking.

*"I am *alive.* They are—"*

*"Not as much as you think,"* he interrupted, smiling faintly. *"Blessed is the life whose pauses are not death."*

The cold gnawed at me. *"I’ll freeze before I sleep."*

*"They do not feel it,"* he said. *"It heals them. Will you be a coward, Mr. Vane? Face what comes."*

His wife stood motionless, the candle’s glow no longer touching her face. She was marble again, her light withdrawn.

*"You expect me to bed down in a tomb?"* I shouted.

No answer. Only silence, vast and waiting.

The sexton turned to his wife. *"We keep the shepherd’s flock."* Then to me, stern: *"You brought the stench of death in with you. The air will not be clean until you’re gone."*

They retreated into the dark, leaving me alone with the sleepers. Fear seized me. I ran, stumbling past the couches, bursting through the door—

—and found myself in my library, gripping the handle of the masked door.

Had it been a dream? Or had I slipped into something deeper?

The first sunbeam struck the mirror, then the door, as if pointing the way back. I flung it open. The light leaped ahead, catching the gilt edges of a misplaced book.

And there, in the corner, a half-open drawer.

Inside, my father’s papers.

I took the first page and read.

The golden sun hung heavy above me, casting its light over the blue expanse of the sea—a world I had known since childhood, yet one I now suspected was merely a veil over something far stranger.

It was in my father’s library, amidst the scent of old paper and leather, that I first encountered the man who would unravel the fabric of my reality. I had been idly studying the portrait of a long-dead ancestor when he appeared—not from the door, but as if stepping from the shadows between myself and the painting. He was gaunt, clad in faded black, with a sharp nose that put me in mind of a storybook crow.

“Mr. Vane,” he said, his voice dry but not unkind, “I thought it time we met. Your grandfather knew me well—better, perhaps, than your father ever cared to.”

“You knew my father?” I asked.

His lips curled in a knowing smile. “I knew him. He did not know me.”

He introduced himself as Raven—a name that tugged at the edges of my memory, whispered in tales of the uncanny. He spoke of my ancestor, Sir Upward, with the familiarity of an old friend, though by any measure of time, that should have been impossible.

Then came the revelation.

“There is a door in your house,” he said, “a threshold that does not lead to another room, but to another world.”

I laughed, but the sound withered in my throat. His eyes were too steady, too certain.

“A better world?” I asked.

“Different,” he corrected. “The laws are not the same. But morality? That remains.”

He led me then, through the winding halls of my own home as if they were his, up to a forgotten garret where the roof slanted and the floorboards groaned beneath us. There, against the wall, stood an oblong mirror, its surface clouded with age, crowned by an eagle with wings outstretched as if in warning.

“This,” said Raven, “is the door.”

I watched as he pulled chains hidden in the shadows. The ceiling above us groaned, shifting, and a sliver of noon light pierced the dust. It struck another mirror, then the one before us—but instead of reflecting, the light vanished, swallowed whole.

“Where does it go?” I whispered.

“Where all lost things go,” he said. “Into the unseen.”

Then the glass before us shimmered. The mist within it parted like a curtain, revealing a vast heath beneath a foreign sky. And there, walking away, was a figure—a man in a wide black coat, his hair lifted by a wind I could not feel.

It was Raven.

I turned to demand an explanation—but he was gone. When I looked back, the mirror showed only my own face, pale with terror.

I fled.

The house was silent around me, but the air hummed with the echo of his words: *There are more doors than you will think of in many years.*

And I knew, then, that the world was not what I had believed.

I set the manuscript aside, strangely comforted by the knowledge that my father had glimpsed the same unfathomable world I now wandered—that he, too, had known the enigmatic Mr. Raven. Then, like cold water dashed upon my face, came the memory: I had never learned how my father died. Had he, like so many others, followed Raven into that other place and never returned? Shame coiled in my gut. What truths had I fled from? What wisdom might I have gained about life, death, and the vast, unseen realms that lay beyond?

The Ravens were not monsters. They were keepers of mysteries. The woman, with her eerie grace and wounded palm; the king in his endless slumber—what had I feared from them? Their stillness had been sacred, and I had turned my back on it like a coward. Disgust rolled through me. I had been given a gift and spat upon it.

Tears stung my eyes. I collapsed onto a couch, and sleep took me like a thief.

A voice, or the ghost of one, jerked me awake. The house was silent as a tomb. Outside, a blackbird sang, its melody too bright for the weight in my chest. *I will go back*, I decided. *I will beg their forgiveness.*

I climbed to the garret, where the wooden chamber waited, unchanged. The mirror was a dull slate, indifferent to my presence. Noon light pressed against the shutters—I had to adjust the hood, align the mirrors just so. If only I’d watched Raven more closely! My fingers fumbled with the chains until, at last, light spilled across the glass.

The second mirror trembled with shapes—faint, wavering, like reflections in a wind-stirred pond. I pressed my palm to the surface. Cold. Unyielding. Polarization, perhaps? I twisted the mirrors, shifting angles blindly, until—

—the world steadied. Mountains, blue and sharp, cut the horizon. Heather crunched beneath my boots.

I remembered only the pine forest. Hours bled away as I wandered, the sun sinking low, winter’s teeth gnawing at the air. Then—movement. A black speck resolved into the raven, picking his way toward me.

“I was wrong,” I blurted before he could speak. “Take me back. I’m sorry.”

He cocked his head. “My wife does not expect you.”

“Let me apologize to her.”

“No.” His voice was final. “Your night had not come then. It has not come now. The dead rejoiced when you walked among them—did you know that? They dreamed of your joy when winter passed. But you left. You made them shiver in their graves.”

A fist closed around my throat. “My father—is he with you?”

“Yes. Asleep. You saw him. His hand half-curled on the coverlet.”

*That was him?* I nearly retched. “Why didn’t you tell me?”

“You turned away,” said the raven, merciless. “Had you been ready to see, you would have known.”

I swallowed bile. “And my grandfather?”

“Fighting in the Evil Wood.”

“Where is it?”

“You’ll know it when you wake there. It’s where those who refuse sleep go to murder their dead.”

Frustration seared me. “None of this makes sense!”

“Of course not,” said the raven. “You are a riddle to yourself. The universe is shouting its truth, and you’ve barred the door.”

The sun dipped below the horizon. Darkness pooled around us.

The raven lunged suddenly, beak stabbing the earth. He wrenched something free—a flicker of light, tossed into the air. It unfurled like a flower, pulsing gold.

Then it flew. And I, with no other compass, followed.

(*A note, here: I write these things knowing they are clumsy shadows. The truths of that place do not fit the language of this one. Even now, I cannot say if I walked through a land or a metaphor—only that it was real, and that I failed to grasp it.*)

The air thickened with darkness as winter’s grip tightened around me. Ahead, the flickering fire of the winged creature pulsed brighter, halting mid-flight as if waiting. When I stepped beneath its glow, it drifted forward again, lingering over patches of rocky ground. Each time I glanced up, it had grown larger—now casting my own shadow beside me.

This was no ordinary insect. Its wings, vast and nearly square, shimmered with impossible colors, rippling through hues I had no name for. Enthralled, I barely noticed the earth beneath me—until my foot caught on stone, and I fell.

When I awoke, the creature hovered above me, its light now a symphony of radiance, painting the air in shades beyond any spectrum I knew. I rose, but my gaze stayed fixed on it, and soon I stumbled again. Fearful of another fall, I sat, watching, longing—until, to my astonishment, it descended toward me.

Slowly, then swiftly, it neared, expanding as it came. My heart surged—here was the universe offering itself to me! I reached out, and my fingers closed around it.

Darkness.

The light vanished. In my palm lay nothing but a cold, dead thing—a book, stiff and lifeless. I flung it away, heard it thud into the heather, and buried my face in my hands. The cold gnawed at me, forcing me to stand.

Then—a glimmer.

Not the creature, but the edge of a rising moon, sharp as a blade. She cast her light, indifferent, offering no guidance. Her face was unfamiliar, her glow alien, as if lit by some distant, unknown sun. She stared at me, unblinking, her gaze icy yet curious. *Why are you here?* she seemed to ask.

And for the first time, I understood the terror of being truly awake in the cosmos. I *was*—and there was no undoing it.

I trudged on, the ground beneath me shifting to something spongy, dry as powdered peat. Then it *moved*. A ripple passed through the earth ahead, a shadow in the moonlight. Before I could react, a wave erupted, and from it lunged a beast—tiger-like, jaws dripping clods of soil, eyes blazing. It snarled soundlessly, then plunged back into the ground.

*The moon is twisting my mind,* I told myself. *These are phantoms.*

But the truth was worse. The moon’s light was all that held the horrors at bay.

A worm the size of a bear heaved itself from the soil, mane white as bone, neck crimson. It writhed, exhausted, then collapsed, unable to burrow again. Others came—serpents feathered in iridescence, things with too many limbs, mouths where eyes should be. Yet none touched me.

I didn’t understand then: the moon paralyzed them.

At last, I neared the hills. The moon sagged low, weary. Then—movement. A woman walked, draped in mist that clung and fled her in turns. Beautiful, yes, but her face was a mask of pride and torment. Her eyes were hollow, and at her side, a dark stain pulsed. She clutched it, gasping.

Her hair, pale gold, tangled with the mist. Then—she fell.

I ran to her.

Her body convulsed. Legs slithered away as serpents. Arms fled her shoulders, twisting into scaled things. Something dark—a bat?—leapt from her, and she was gone.

The earth surged like a tidal wave. Terror seized me. I fled toward the hills as the moon’s light faded behind the peaks.

A cry rose behind me—frustrated, ravenous. The first of the creatures lunged, but as we crossed into the moonlight, it dissolved into nothing. The moon’s glare was a weapon. One by one, they fell, howling.

I reached the ridge. The horizon swallowed the moon. The dark returned.

I waited for dawn.

I awoke to the pale glow of dawn cresting the horizon. Climbing back to the vantage point of the rocks, I scanned the hollow I had crossed under the moon’s cold gaze. It lay still and lifeless now—no trace of the unseen things that had slithered through the dark. Ahead stretched a barren expanse, a desert of cracked riverbeds and wind-scoured stone, broken only by a distant smudge of green—perhaps a forest. No smoke, no movement, no sign of life. The sky was an empty bowl, flawless and pitiless.

I descended and began my march toward that uncertain promise of shelter. The plain was a graveyard of ancient waters, its channels dry as bone, its rocks scabbed with lichen. No whisper of moisture lingered, yet as I walked, my ears played tricks: the ghost of rushing streams hissed at the edges of my hearing, a cruel illusion in that lifeless waste.

By sunset, I reached the forest’s edge. Not the pines I had expected, but a congregation of strange trees—some familiar in twisted parody, others utterly alien. One loomed above me, its blossoms grotesque: skull-like calyxes gaping open to spill frothy white blooms like brains from a shattered cranium. Exhausted, I slumped against its trunk, watching as the last light bled away between the branches.

Then the forest folded in on itself. Shadows thickened, and the cold came sharp as a blade.

Night fell, and with it, the wood awoke.

At first, it was only the wind, stirring the leaves into phantom shapes. A pack of wolves writhed in the branches, their spectral forms straining against an unseen leash. Nearby, the foliage contorted into skeletal horses, their fleshless necks snapping up and down as if tormented by bits and bridles. One swayed with the weight of a half-rotten skull, jawless and grim. High above them all, a woman floated, her arms outstretched in command.

I shut my eyes, willing the visions away. But the darkness only deepened the horror.

By the time the moon rose, the forest was screaming.

A battle raged around me—phantoms and skeletons locked in endless slaughter. Bones shattered and reknit mid-swing; curses and prayers tangled in the air. Horses of bleached bone trampled the fallen, while specters on mist-steeds howled their creeds like war cries. Truth and lies were weapons here, wielded with equal fury. Through it all, that woman moved untouched, her dead eyes gleaming, her voice a lash: *"Ye are men—slay one another!"*

I pressed myself into the earth, praying the chaos would not notice me.

Just before dawn, a whisper cut through the din: *"Let the dead bury their dead."*

Silence.

When the sun climbed above the trees, there was nothing left but scattered twigs and the memory of violence. The forest stood still, holding its breath. No birds sang. No creature stirred. Even the wind had died.

I fled, eyes fixed straight ahead, refusing to let the shapes in the periphery take form. Every rustle of leaves might have been the scrape of a shovel, the clatter of bones.

By afternoon, I broke free of the trees—only to face another skeletal riverbed, another expanse of dust and stone. The sun dipped low, and exhaustion dragged me down. As I lay on the dry moss, the phantom murmur of water rose again, singing me into a fitful sleep.

When I woke, the land was striped with shadows, the rocks drinking back the night’s darkness. My throat ached for the sound of a human voice. Even the raven’s cryptic words haunted me now—*those nearest your sort*.

I turned my face to the northwest and by south, and walked on.

I knelt beside a strange little shrub, its leaves trembling as if alive. A single fruit clung to its branches—small, unfamiliar, and tempting. Hunger gnawed at me, but caution held my hand. I did not yet know that unseen eyes watched from the rocks, waiting to see if I would dare to taste it.

Moving deeper into the channel, the shrubs grew taller, thickening into dwarf trees. Their gnarled roots twisted across my path, forcing me to leap and scramble. A sudden rustle—like the beating of countless wings—made me freeze, but nothing emerged. Ahead, the land dipped into a hollow, the ghost of an ancient lakebed. Trees crowded its basin, heavy with coarse, unappetizing fruit.

Exhausted, I collapsed onto a patch of soft moss. Nearby, a tiny tree bore rosy apples no larger than cherries. I plucked one and bit into it—sweetness burst across my tongue. I reached for another.

Then came the laughter.

High, clear voices rang out like chimes. *"He likes our apples! He likes our apples!"* A chorus of giggles followed. *"He's a good giant!"*

I turned my head slowly. Dozens of children surrounded me—some barely able to toddle, others on the cusp of adolescence. Their eyes sparkled with mischief and delight. A few lingered apart, quieter, watchful. The rest chattered like birds, their words tumbling over one another in joyous chaos.

One small boy, his face alight with mischief, darted forward and pressed a massive green apple into my hand. Silence fell.

*"Eat, good giant,"* he urged.

I took a bite—and recoiled. The taste was foul, rotten. I flung it away.

The children erupted into cheers. They swarmed me, clambering onto my shoulders, hugging my arms, their tiny hands patting my face. *"We knew you were good!"* they cried. *"We knew you’d come!"*

Then, without warning, their laughter died. The crowd stilled. Small hands tugged at me urgently.

*"Get up! Hurry!"* a girl whispered, her eyes wide with fear.

I staggered to my feet just as a shadow fell across the slope. A hulking figure loomed there—broad, brutish, his face twisted in a snarl. The children vanished like mist.

The brute lumbered down toward me. I braced myself, but before I could react, a second attacker struck from behind. Blows rained down. I fought, but they overpowered me, dragging me into the woods where their tribe dwelled in squalor—huts of broken branches, the air thick with the stench of spoiled fruit.

That night, I lay bruised and aching. The next morning, they bound my feet and forced a crude stone tool into my hand. *Scrape the bark,* their kicks commanded. *Work.*

But as I toiled, I stole glances at the dwarf-tree nearby, its branches heavy with those small, red fruits. One by one, I plucked them and ate—each bite a spark of strength, each moment a step closer to escape.

I had scarcely begun my work when faint rustlings and whispers surrounded me. The Little Ones emerged from the brushwood between the towering trees, their laughter like wind chimes.

“The giants are blind,” one giggled. “They don’t see us!”

I gestured toward the retreating footsteps, but they shook their heads. “The wind carries their scent away,” a girl said, her fingers deftly loosening the rope around my ankles. “They never notice.”

The children swarmed over me, feeding me sweet fruits, tumbling in play, their joy infectious. The sun dipped low when heavy footsteps thudded nearby. At once, the Little Ones vanished—all but the girl who had freed me.

“A stomp from their clumsy feet could crush one of us,” she whispered before melting into the shadows.

The giant yanked me up, dragged me back, and kicked me into his hut. Yet I no longer cared. I had found friends—tiny, bright, and fearless.

That night, whispers fluttered around me like moth wings. Small hands tucked close, warming me. By dawn, they returned, bearing gifts—a newborn boy nestled in the eldest girl’s arms.

“We found him in the wood,” she said, placing him in mine. The baby blinked sleepily, then dozed.

“Where do they come from?” I asked.

“The wood,” she said, as if it were obvious. “We just find them.”

“But how?”

She frowned. “We don’t ask. It’s better not to know.”

The baby suckled plum juice from her fingers, content. Yet among them, I noticed one—Blunty—greedy, sullen, clutching a giant’s apple.

“He’ll grow,” the girl murmured sadly. “They always do.”

“Into a giant?”

She nodded. “He’ll forget us. They all do.”

Days passed. Blunty swelled, his face hardening. Then one morning, he was gone—replaced by a dull-eyed hulk who didn’t recognize us.

“That’s him now,” the girl whispered. “Eating their apples forever.”

I shuddered. Was I any different? Had I once been small, forgetting as I grew?

The Little Ones clung to me, singing, telling stories, their voices weaving magic. But the question haunted me: *Where did they come from?* And worse—*Where do they go?*

Yet in their laughter, I found something rare. A hope. A warning.

For a time, I was content to live among the Little Ones, their innocence a balm to my weary soul. Yet soon, restless thoughts stirred within me—a gnawing sense that I was meant for more than idle comfort, that this world held mysteries I was bound to unravel. If I truly cared for these children, I owed it to them to understand the forces that shaped their lives.

Their growth was stunted—unnaturally so. Lona, their de facto mother, seemed no older than fifteen, yet she spoke as if she had always existed. The others, though wise in ways I could not grasp, knew nothing of time or age. They feared growth, equating it with the corruption of the giants. But was such fear justified? Could perfection truly demand stagnation? Life, I believed, was meant to flourish.

I longed to teach them—mathematics, music, the art of preserving their fleeting melodies in written form. Yet how could I guide them without first understanding the roots of their condition? My presence, I realized, might even endanger them further. The brutish giants already saw me as a threat; if they viewed the Little Ones as my accomplices, their fragile peace could shatter.

My resolve hardened: I would leave, if only to return wiser. But before I could prepare them, fate intervened.

One afternoon, as I spoke of my affection for them, they swarmed over me in a cascade of laughter—tiny limbs clinging, small bodies piling upon mine until I was buried beneath their joy. So absorbed were we that none noticed the approaching giant until his shadow fell across us.

A single cry—*"Take care, good giant!"*—and they scattered like leaves in a storm. Then came the blow: a club crashing against my skull, darkness swallowing me whole.

I awoke beneath a star-pricked sky, my head throbbing, my throat parched. The moment my ear touched the earth, I heard water—sweet, beckoning. Small hands pressed fruit to my lips until my thirst eased. Only then did I hear the soft, unfamiliar sound of weeping.

"You must go," Lona whispered, her voice steady despite the tears glinting in her eyes. "They will kill you if you stay wounded."

The others echoed her, their faces solemn. One boy, breathless from eavesdropping, reported the giant’s threats: *Wizard. Moles and squirrels. Knock him or no peace.*

I nodded, though every step sent pain lancing through me. "I’ll return when I’ve learned how to make you strong—how to grow without becoming *them*."

Their eyes darkened. "We *won’t* grow," they insisted. "We are strong now. You don’t understand."

And perhaps I didn’t.

They escorted me to the valley’s edge, feeding me as we walked, fretting over the "red juice" I’d lost. When the moon rose, Lona pressed her baby into my arms for a farewell kiss. "She will guide you," she said, nodding to the silver light. Then, softer: "The Cat-woman will not harm you."

The others chimed in with warnings—a desert-dwelling giantess, claws sharp, heart cruel. I stored the tales away, though my path lay elsewhere.

As they vanished into the night, their laughter ringing like wind chimes, I turned toward the unknown. Behind me, Lona walked apart, her child in her arms, her gaze lingering until the shadows claimed her.

The moon stretched my silhouette long and thin across the rocks. Somewhere ahead lay answers—or deeper questions. And though my heart ached to leave, I knew this was the only way to protect them.

To help, one must first learn. And to learn, one must sometimes walk alone.

The moon, ever full, traveled with me as I walked. That night, as she dipped below the horizon, I thought I saw a smile flicker across her luminous face. Just before she vanished, her light painted upon her disc the image of a cottage—its door and window open, as if inviting me inside. The vision faded with the moon, leaving the night dark and treacherous. I chose to rest in a sandy hollow, ate the fruit the Little Ones had given me, and slept.

I woke beneath unfamiliar stars. Nearby, a figure sat in silence—tall, shadowed against the sky, her back turned to me.

“Will you not come with me?” Her voice was rich, gentle.

“I’m comfortable here,” I said, testing her. “Where would you take me?”

“The night here is not safe,” she replied. “Things roam that you would not wish near as you sleep.”

“I’ve had no trouble.”

“Because I’ve watched over you.”

Her words unsettled yet reassured me. She called herself the Cat-woman, though not by choice. The children had warned me of her ugliness, but her voice held no malice.

“What should I call you?” I asked.

“The name you give me will tell me who *you* are,” she said. “Few guess rightly.”

I followed her through the dark, unable to match her pace or glimpse her face. The silence gnawed at me.

“Do you know of a land—green, smooth underfoot?” I asked.

“Bulika,” she answered. “A city ruled by a princess, ancient and cruel. She stole the waters of this land, leaving only dust.”

“And the Little Ones—the Lovers?”

“In Bulika’s library lies a prophecy: They will grow, yet not grow. They will master the giants.”

Her words twisted like riddles. Before I could question further, we reached her cottage—a stark, windowless place. Inside, she veiled herself in white linen, then offered me a loft to sleep.

That night, I woke to a howl. Peering out, I saw her beneath the moon, pointing a path to a great white beast—a panther, or something like it. The creature vanished into the night.

By morning, she was brusque. “No one stays two nights,” she said, directing me toward Bulika. As I left, I turned back—and for an instant, saw her unveiled: a face of sorrow and stars, weeping yet hopeful.

The door was gone. A scream pierced the air. A gray cat fled toward the river. And I knew—I would return, though not willingly.

I rose to continue my journey, my feet dragging across the endless desert. The sun hung low, casting long shadows that stretched like skeletal fingers over the cracked earth. My throat burned, my limbs ached, and yet—there was no choice but forward.

Then, a flicker of green. A ragged line of trees broke the horizon, and hope surged in my chest. I stumbled toward them, the air thickening with the scent of pine and damp soil. The forest swallowed me whole, its towering trunks forming a cathedral of silence.

But silence was a lie.

The deeper I ventured, the more the trees seemed to watch. Their branches twisted into grotesque shapes, their bark peeling like flayed skin. And then—movement. Not wind, not animal. A whisper of fabric, the faintest chime of laughter.

I froze.

Before me, the forest floor opened into a clearing—no, a ballroom. Moonlight spilled through the canopy, painting the scene in silver and shadow. Figures swirled in an endless waltz, their movements fluid, hypnotic.

Their faces were wrong.

Skulls.

Bone-white, gleaming, yet alive. Eyes burned in hollow sockets, jewels glinting where ears had been. They danced as if nothing were amiss, as if flesh still clung to their frames. The women’s gowns billowed, the men’s coats flared—yet beneath, only death.

I should have run.

Instead, I stepped closer.

The music swelled—no instrument, just the rustle of leaves, the creak of branches. The dancers turned as one, their empty gazes locking onto me. Not with hunger, not with malice. With recognition.

*You are like us,* their silence said. *You will be like us.*

Then—her.

A woman, untouched by decay, stood at the edge of the clearing. Radiant. Whole. Her lips curled in disdain as she surveyed the macabre assembly. For a heartbeat, the dancers stilled, their hollow eyes widening.

She looked down.

A shadow clung to her side, dark and pulsing. Her perfect face twisted in horror. A choked gasp escaped her—then she fled.

The skeletons shuddered.

A wind howled through the trees, extinguishing the moonlight. The dancers’ forms wavered, then collapsed like puppets with severed strings. Bones clattered to the ground, gowns crumpling into dust.

The forest exhaled.

I stood alone, the air thick with the scent of rot and roses. The night was silent once more.

I walked on.

The moon watched, cold and indifferent, as I left the ghastly ball behind.

But the laughter followed.

Soft. Mocking.

Waiting.

I hadn’t taken ten steps before something strange caught my eye—a carriage, ancient and crumbling, yet standing upright on its heavy wheels. The skeletons of two horses lay before it, still harnessed to the pole, their bony heads stretching toward the reins held by the skeletal coachman on his tattered seat. The carriage doors had long since fallen away, and inside sat two more skeletons, slumped in opposite corners.

Then, as I watched, they jerked awake. Bones clattered as they lurched upright, each scrambling from its side. One collapsed immediately, a heap of yellowed bones. The other swayed precariously before shuffling around the carriage, gripping it for support, its joints creaking with the effort. It knelt beside the fallen one, fumbling to help it rise—only to nearly topple itself in the attempt.

At last, the first skeleton sat up, its hollow skull swiveling as if taking in its surroundings. Ignoring its companion, it clutched the spokes of the rear wheel and hauled itself upright, hands gripping one knee as if in pain. The second skeleton rose with equal difficulty, its voice thin and distant, like wind through dead branches.

*“Yes, I have,”* the other snapped, voice rough and brittle. *“You did nothing to help, and now this damned knee is out!”*

*“No doubt, madam—your best was poor indeed! I thought I’d never stand again!”* He paused, then let out a rasping laugh. *“But—bless my soul! Are you out in your bones?”*

She glanced down at herself. *“I’ve nothing else to be out in. And you can hardly complain.”* Her tone sharpened. *“What is this? Am I dreaming?”*

*“You may be. I can’t say. But this knee won’t let me pretend.”* He shifted, grimacing. *“And by the devil—I’m bones myself! Are these mine? Every joint aches worse than the last!”*

*“Likely you were too drunk to notice the damp,”* she remarked dryly.

*“How do you know what my fools call me?”* he demanded. *“I don’t recall you!”*

They bickered like long-familiar enemies, each word a barb. She bound his knee with a strand of grass, and he tested it with a cautious stamp.

*“You used to stamp differently,”* she observed.

*“Did I?”* He studied her. *“Now that I look—I think I hated you.”*

*“Ah.”* He gave a rattling chuckle. *“Then we’re damned together.”*

She snatched up a broken branch to steady herself and turned away.

*“Give me that stick!”* he barked.

*“I think I’ll keep it,”* she replied, hobbling off.

He tried to follow, but his knee gave way again. She laughed as he cursed.

*“Now you’ll need splints forever,”* she called back. *“Dry bones don’t mend.”*

I stared, bewildered. *What hell is this?*

Then a voice spoke beside me—the raven’s, though I saw no bird. *“They are in hell. You are not.”*

A moment later, the old librarian stood before me, his coat flapping like wings.

*“They were handsome once,”* he said. *“Now even their bones cling to pride. But hatred cannot last forever. Even they may yet learn.”*

*“Will they?”* I asked.

*“We shall see.”* His eyes gleamed. *“Love runs deeper than hate, in the end.”*

And with that, he was gone—only a raven darting low across the ground, leaving me to wonder at the grotesque tragedy before me.

I walked on beneath the bruised copper moon, her dull glow spilling through the branches like tarnished light. She hung low, dented and weary, as if the weight of the night had pressed her into decay. No clouds lingered to share her vigil; the stars burned too fiercely, leaving her isolated in the vast indifference of the sky.

She and I moved in opposite directions, yet for a long while we traveled side by side—she a dim, distant lantern, I a living shadow. My eyes were fixed on the earth, but I felt her gaze, heavy and mournful, upon me.

Then I saw it—a pale form sprawled beneath the outstretched arms of a great tree. At first, I thought it another skeleton, another relic of this strange land. But as I knelt, my fingers brushed not bone but flesh, cold and stiff, yet unmistakably human.

She lay curled on her side, her body so emaciated that every rib pressed sharply against her skin, every joint stood defined beneath its thin sheath of leathery tissue. Her lips had shrunk back from her teeth, exposing them in a ghastly grin. Black hair, impossibly long and fine, spilled around her like spilled ink.

A woman. Or what remained of one.

Had she wandered here, lost and starving? Collapsed in exhaustion and never risen? The forest was not cruel, but neither was it kind—it would not clothe her, would not bury her. It had left her naked, hollowed out by time and hunger.

I pressed a hand to her side. No breath stirred. No heartbeat answered. Yet beneath her, the pine needles were faintly warm.

I had no tools, no strength to dig. But I could not leave her to the indifferent air. I gathered armfuls of dry leaves, spread my own coat beneath her, and lifted her onto it. Then I covered her, layer upon layer, a cocoon against the night’s chill.

I had no food but the last of the grapes I carried. I pried her jaws apart—stiff, but not locked—and squeezed a drop of juice onto her tongue. No swallow followed. No flicker of life.

Still, doubt was no excuse for surrender.

I crawled beneath the leaves beside her, pressed my warmth against her terrible cold. The moon sank; the stars wheeled. I waited for dawn.

---

When light came, it brought movement. A snake slid past, sinuous and purposeful. Then deer-like creatures, small and delicate. Then horses—perfect in form but no larger than ponies—trotting toward some unseen destination.

They were heading for the river.

I left the woman long enough to follow, and there it was—a narrow, swift-running stream, its waters strangely warm, its surface veiled in mist. The animals drank eagerly, then crossed to the grasslands beyond.

Hope flared. If the water held heat, it might drive the death from her bones.

I ran back, gathered her up—so light, so terribly light—and carried her to the bank. She was not rigid; her limbs yielded as I moved her. A good sign, perhaps.

I bathed her first with handfuls of water, then, when she did not stir, submerged her body, cradling her face above the current. The warmth seeped into her, but still she did not wake.

I needed shelter. A place to keep her warm.

Upriver, the land rose into rocky hills. At their base, the water surged from a cleft in the stone, as if the mountain itself had birthed it. Inside the cave, the river ran swift and deep, its heat radiating through the rock.

I built a bed over the water—branches lashed together, padded with leaves and moss. A cradle of warmth.

Then I carried her there.

---

Seven days. Seven nights.

I bathed her in the river each morning, laid her back on her heated bed, watched for the slightest twitch of muscle, the faintest flutter of breath.

Once, a shadow of bruising appeared on her side—my heart clenched—but by the next dawn, it had vanished.

I fed her drops of grape juice, though most spilled from her slack lips. I ate little myself, sustained by the water’s strange vitality.

When I slept, I dreamed of angels with broken wings. When I woke, I saw only her sunken face, her hollow cheeks.

But I waited.

Because if she woke—*if*—then I would no longer be alone.

And that was worth any vigil.

I woke in the dim glow of the cave, my hand throbbing. The skin was swollen, marred by a triangular wound—like the bite of some unseen predator. By evening, the pain had faded, leaving only a faint scar. I scoured the cavern, turning over stones, but found nothing.

Days dragged. The figure on the bed of leaves never stirred, never opened her eyes. Yet she was not dead—no decay touched her, no taint in the air. If anything, the sharp angles of her bones had softened, her parchment skin grown smoother. Life was returning, slow as the tide reclaiming the shore.

One night, restless, I rose to seek cooler air. Moonlight spilled into the cave, and I paused, gazing at her. My breath caught—had her eyes opened? Dark, fathomless, like voids cut from the night itself, they seemed to gleam beneath her lids. I crept closer, heart hammering.

But no. Her lashes were sealed shut. Hope had tricked me. I turned away, crushed, and wept into my hands.

Then—a thought. *Had* they opened, just for a moment? Perhaps she had blinked, then slept again. Or perhaps she watched me now, feigning stillness. The possibility soothed me. I slept.

That night, I was bitten again. A burning thirst woke me. By dawn, the wound had nearly healed. Some creature of the hot springs, I reasoned—a leech, drawn to blood. But as long as I was its target, she was safe.

Then, one morning, as I pressed a peeled grape to her lips, they moved—ever so slightly—closing around it. She lived.

I worked feverishly, crafting garments from the fibrous husks of desert plants, stitching layers into something modest, something she could rise in. Days passed. Her form filled out, her skin regained its luster—but her eyes remained shut.

And the bites continued. Every third night, without fail, the unseen thing found me as I slept. My strength waned; blood loss left me dizzy. But I didn’t care. She was waking. That was enough.

Then—a rustle. A voice, weak but clear: *"I caught the vile thing."*

I froze. *She spoke.*

"What creature?" I whispered.

"The one biting you."

"What was it?"

"A great white leech."

My skin prickled. "How big?"

"Six feet, perhaps."

"You saved me," I breathed. "How could you touch it?"

"I had to." Her voice was steel. "I threw it in the river."

"It’ll return," I said, then swayed, the cave tilting. I stumbled outside, collapsed.

When I woke, dawn lit the cave. She stood over me, clad in the rough garments I’d made, her eyes—*finally open*—black and depthless as a starless sky.

"You brought me here?" she demanded.

"Yes."

"From where?"

"The wilds. You had no clothes, no jewels—just bones and skin."

Her lip curled. "Why not leave me?"

"Because you were alive. And I was alone."

Her laugh was sharp. "You’d keep me like a trophy?"

"There was no beauty left to keep," I snapped.

Her face darkened. "Then why?"

"Because you were human."

"*Human?*" Contempt dripped from the word.

I met her glare. "I’d have done the same for anyone."

She turned away, silent. Then: "How long was I asleep?"

"Months."

Her fingers twined in her hair—long, untangled. "You bathed me in that river?"

"Yes. It was the only way."

A shudder ran through her. When she faced me again, her eyes were cold. "You’ve done me two great wrongs. You forced me back to life. And you’ve shamed me."

Her hand lifted. A gust of frigid air struck me—

Then nothing.

I woke on the ground, drenched and shaking. She was gone.

I staggered to my feet, my mind reeling. She was gone—vanished like a phantom into the night. The loneliness that crashed over me was worse than the abyss I had pulled her from. Had I wronged her by dragging her back to life? I would spend every breath I had left sharing the weight of existence I had forced upon her.

My eyes caught movement—a swift, ghostly figure gliding across the meadow, away from the river. Without hesitation, I plunged into the water one last time, letting its cold shock revive me, then surged forward in pursuit. My body ached from the white leech’s final visit, from the woman’s blow, but strength returned with each stride. I would not lose her.

*Is this the end?* My heart whispered a dirge. Her eyes—those furious, hate-filled eyes—haunted me. I understood her rage at being dragged back to life, but what else had I done to earn such loathing? Had my touch, my care, been so repulsive? I had handled her like sacred things, like a father cradling a child. Had all my devotion only birthed ingratitude?

*No.* The thought flared in defiance. *Beauty must have a heart. Somewhere, buried deep, it beats. And if it sleeps, then waking it is a greater gift than life itself.*

She moved like a storm given form, effortless, inevitable. The distance between us grew. I pushed harder, my limbs humming with sudden vitality, as if the air itself buoyed me. I closed the gap, three paces behind her—then she whirled, graceful and terrible.

Her gaze was a blade. "Why do you follow me?" Her voice was ice.

"Because I have lived too long in the shadow of your eyes to turn away now."

"You will not be spared," she warned.

"Not until I know you’re safe."

Her lips curled. "Then suffer the consequences."

She walked on. But as she turned, her eyes struck me like a physical blow. It wasn’t scorn—it was annihilation. Her beauty was a weapon, and she wielded it without mercy. Despair gave way to stubbornness; I stumbled after her like a discarded dog.

"Have pity!" My voice broke.

She ignored me.

"I’ll be your slave!" I reached for her arm.

She recoiled as if burned. Her eyes blazed. I flinched but held my ground.

"Pity me," I begged again.

Silence. She walked. The sun climbed, peaked, sank. She never paused. Neither did I.

Night fell. Without warning, she collapsed onto the grass, her body limp. Exhaustion, finally. I stood over her, torn. Did I love her? She was cruel. Did I hate her? I couldn’t leave.

I knelt.

"Begone." Her voice was a whip. "Don’t touch me."

Her arms lay limp—then suddenly lashed around my neck, steel traps. She yanked me down, her lips sealing against my cheek. A sharp, hot pain lanced through me, then dulled into numbness. My limbs turned leaden. Darkness swallowed me.

I woke to moonlight, weak and dazed. My cheek stung. Blood? My neck ached—more wetness. I turned my head sluggishly.

And saw *her*.

The moon’s glow clung to her like a second skin. She stood radiant, terrible.

I staggered toward her.

"Down!" she snarled. "Another step, and you die."

"I will," I slurred, dragging myself forward.

She stepped back, raised her arm—and struck.

An iron weight smashed into my skull. I dropped.

But when I lurched upright, I was drenched, clear-headed. Had the blow revived me? No pain, no wound—only cold dampness. The moon hadn’t moved.

She stood yards away, her back to me. Doing—*something*.

Then, in a flash, she shed her garments.

Pale as death, she stood naked under the moon.

One heartbeat.

Then she *fell forward*—

And *streaked* away.

Not a woman. A *thing*, long-bodied, low, racing in monstrous bounds. Shadows rippled along its back like fleeing ghosts.

"God save them," I choked. "Is it heading for the city?" In my mind, I heard screams, chaos, as it tore through homes.

Before I could move, another shape shot past me—pure white, an arrow of fury. It streaked toward where she had fallen.

My voice died in my throat. I lunged after it.

But the spot was empty. Only her discarded clothes remained.

The white beast was already a speck in the distance, vanishing.

Had the first creature taken her? No—it couldn’t have carried her so swiftly. Unless...

A darker thought rose.

Unless it *was* her.

I searched the ground. Nothing.

Then I ran, following the beasts’ trail into the night.

The moon vanished behind a shroud of cloud as I hurried forward, and from the gloom emerged a woman in white, clutching a child to her chest. She ran hunched, her movements frantic—each step a silent scream of terror. She hadn’t seen me, her path parallel to mine.

I pivoted, planting myself between her and whatever stalked the night. Before I could turn fully, something launched over my head—a flash of white—striking my brow with a force that sent me sprawling. Blood trickled into my eye as I scrambled up, but the creature was already gone, a phantom in the mist. Then a shriek split the air, raw and final.

I ran.

Ahead, a hunched shape lurched through the moonlight—not fleeing, but limping, dragging a forepaw. Spots mottled its pale hide, and a dark trail glistened behind it. Blood. *A lot of blood.* Some hunter had wounded it, yet as it passed, I felt no triumph—only pity. An axe wouldn’t have touched it now.

The woman sat in the grass, her child cradled tight. She flinched when I spoke.

“I mean no harm,” I said, crouching to seem smaller. “The beast is bleeding out. It won’t hurt you now.”

Her breath hitched. “You don’t know what it is.”

“Then tell me.”

Her fingers trembled over the child’s unmarked skin. “It’s *hers*. The princess sends it to drink the blood of babies. Those who survive grow up hollow.” She swallowed. “I thought I had more time. But I heard it—*sniffing*—and ran.”

“You fought it?”

“A stone to its paw. That scream you heard wasn’t mine.”

I stared. This ragged woman had crippled a monster.

“The princess believes a child will kill her,” she whispered. “So she makes sure none live. Witches teach barren curses. The beast hunts those who slip through.” Her voice dropped lower. “If it reached home, another will come.”

I offered to carry the child. She recoiled.

“Don’t go to Bulika,” she said, rising. “The palace isn’t stone and gold—it’s a throat. At night, the walls *try* to scream.”

Then she was gone, swallowed by the dark.

The blood trail gleamed under the moon, leading back to a city of nightmares.

I had wandered so long in the dark that I no longer knew where I was. My patience had worn thin when suddenly I stumbled into the path of the leopardess—her wounded paw left a trail of blood, a rivulet rushing against my ankles. The force of it surprised me, and a cold suspicion crept into my mind: *Whose blood was this?*

I followed the sound of the stream, letting it guide me toward Bulika, though I soon questioned how any creature, no matter how vast, could sustain such a flow. No leopardess, no beast of legend, could bleed so endlessly without emptying. Cautiously, I dipped a finger into the current—and found it was not blood at all, but water, clear and murmuring, flowing without a channel over the grass. Its song was sweet, yet I dared not drink. Instead, I walked beside it, letting the coolness on my feet revive me as I pressed on through the thinning dark.

At last, the first light of dawn revealed the silhouette of a city—ancient, its towers jagged against the pale sky. I glanced down, expecting to see the brook still at my feet, but it had vanished. Only bent grass and scattered pools marked its passage. The earth remembered what I could no longer see.

The city walls loomed ahead, surrounded by gardens choked with strange, unrecognizable plants. No flowers bloomed; no animals stirred. Heaps of refuse piled against the stonework, as if the place disgorged its waste but never tended to it. The nearest gate stood half-open, unguarded, its hinges rusted into stillness. I stepped through, into a street frozen in silence.

No movement. No voices. Had I wandered into a tomb? Unease prickled my skin. I retreated, circling outside the walls until I found another gate, another street—this one slowly filling with sluggish figures. Their eyes slid over me, indifferent or hostile. One man shoved me; others threw stones. None would speak. Here, poverty was a crime, and I wore its mark plainly.

When a lumbering brute by the gate hurled his bread at me instead of a rock, I seized it and fled. Beyond the walls, their courage withered. I ate, slept under the sun’s gaze, and woke to moonlight—the same moon, I fancied, that had watched over my first night in this world.

The wind carried a foul stench from the city as I slipped back inside. Shadows clung to corners, figures hunched against the cold. Then—movement. A flash of white. A leopardess, luminous in the dark, slinking after a man who cast no shadow. No, not a man—a *thing*, flat and black, a slice of darkness given form. Where moonlight touched him, he drank it in, turning deeper black, while the leopardess blazed like polished silver.

I froze as they passed. The shadow-man turned—or seemed to—his profile vanishing into a razor-thin line. And then the wind pierced me, a gust so cold it rattled my bones. My heart shuddered like a stone in a child’s rattle.

The city held its breath. And I stood alone, caught between the hunter and the hunted, wondering which I should fear more.

The narrow alley swallowed me as I stepped into its gloom, seeking refuge beneath a shallow arch. Moonlight pooled on the cobblestones like spilled mercury, and I hesitated at the threshold, watching. Then she came—a woman, slipping in behind me, breath ragged, her body pressed to the wall as if it could shield her. She trembled, her gaze fixed on the alley beyond.

A heartbeat later, the leopard came.

It was a streak of ivory and shadow, muscles coiled beneath a pelt mottled like old parchment. The woman gasped, her fingers digging into my arm. I pulled her closer.

"If it turns on us," I said, low and steady, "I’ll hold it. Run."

Her voice was a whisper, frayed with fear. "Thank you."

"You know this creature?"

Her nod was stiff. "She belongs to the princess. You must be a stranger—everyone in Bulika knows her."

"I am." My eyes tracked the beast’s vanished path. "They let it roam free?"

"A muzzled thing, they say. Gloved claws, chains." A bitter laugh escaped her. "Yet she slips her cage. Hunts children when she can. Few mothers remain in Bulika to mourn."

Her tears came then, silent and sharp. "I should never have left home," she choked. "The princess returned last night, and already the beast is loose. She waits for me—I know it. Watching my door like a specter." She wiped her face, sudden suspicion tightening her features. "Why am I telling you this? You could be anyone."

"Not all strangers bring harm," I said. The wind howled through the alley, a living thing. "You have a home to return to. That’s more than most."

She studied me, then nodded once. "Take me there safely, and I’ll give you shelter from this accursed night."

We waited in the hush, and I asked of Bulika—its people, its ways.

"They dig," she said. "Not for bread, not for trade. For gems in the dark beneath their houses. They are wealthy enough to let others labor for them."

"Why?"

"Work is a stain here. Only the lowborn toil."

"And when the gems run out?"

She scoffed. "The veins are endless. And if they weren’t—we are Bulika. The princess ensures our wealth, our safety. No outsider dares challenge her."

Every few words, her eyes darted past me, scanning the dark.

"Your people despise strangers," I noted.

"They defile us. We are ancient. Pure." Her chin lifted. "The princess has ruled for millennia. The earth obeys her. The air, the waters—perhaps even fire. She answers to no one."

When she deemed it safe, we moved, winding through serpentine passages until we reached her door—wedged between two towering houses, a steep stair leading up. Halfway, panic seized her. She fled the last steps, and before I could follow, the door slammed shut.

I stood alone on the narrow landing, the wind clawing at my back. Exhaustion weighed heavy. Let Bulika scorn me; I would rest here, in the space between doors, and wait for dawn.

Moonlight spilled across the street below, the wind whispering through the empty lanes like a thief. Yet not a breath of it touched me in my refuge. I had just settled into uneasy rest when a glimmer of movement caught my eye—the sleek, luminous head of the creature I had seen trailing the Shadow rose above the topmost step.

The moment our eyes met, she froze, then retreated, tail first. I leapt to my feet; with no room to turn, she tumbled backward in a flash of white, scrambled up, and vanished down the stairs. I chased her to the street, scanning the shadows, but she was gone.

Two predators now haunted Bulika—one spotted, one pure. I cared little for its treacherous citizens, but a child’s life? That was worth my meager existence. I would stand guard.

The latch clicked—slow, deliberate. The door creaked open. Not the woman I had aided, but the veiled figure of the desert stood there, silent. She led me to a barren chamber, pointed to a rug, and left. The outer door sighed shut.

Then came the sounds—a stifled moan, a child’s cry, a shriek that tore the night. I burst into the passage. There, the white leopardess, a newborn clutched in her jaws like a cub. I threw myself at her, wrenching the infant free. It wailed as it struck stone.

The shrouded woman appeared, stepping over our struggle. She took the child and vanished. When she returned, she pulled me from the beast and pushed me into the night—the leopardess at my heels.

*Betrayed again,* I thought. *Given to the hunter.* But as I fled down the steps, she followed, docile. I turned, braced for attack—instead, she crouched at my feet. Hesitant, I stroked her moonlit fur. Her rough tongue rasped against my skin.

Perhaps she, too, was trapped. If I refused every gesture of kinship, how would I ever find the truth buried in this damned world?

A wet thud broke the silence—a woman’s broken body sprawled in the street. Nearby, the Shadow walked, the leopardess now trailing him once more. I pursued, but as I neared, she whirled, fangs bared, eyes blazing green fire. A snap, a bite—then a look almost pleading. *Why did you make me?*

I turned away, furious. Enough wasting time. The palace loomed ahead, its walls crumbling, gates agape. It slept under the moon, silence thick as a curse.

Exhaustion overtook me. I curled into a recess, a stone bench my bed. Then warmth—weight—a tongue rough as bark against my cheek. The leopardess. I braced for teeth, for blood. Instead, she pressed close, her heat seeping into my bones. I slept.

Dawn came with the illusion of home—the scent of gardens, the comfort of a bed. Then the cruel truth: cold stone, the stink of the city. Yet her warmth lingered.

I rose. Hope, shapeless but fierce, kindled in my chest. I would find the princess. I had saved her once. That had to count for something.

Even if it killed me.

I circled the castle, passing again through its open gates and crossing the moat’s shadowed ravine. Inside the courtyard, towering poplar-like trees stood in rigid formation, their leaves whispering secrets to the wind. At its heart loomed the tallest of them all, its crown splayed like a spectral hand against the sky. Between the trunks, glimpses of the palace teased my curiosity—a structure of strange, foreign grandeur, low and sprawling, with corner towers clawing at the clouds and a massive central dome swelling like a bubble of molten silver.

The entrance yawned before me, an elliptical arch, dark and unguarded. I stepped inside.

The hall stretched before me, an elongated oval of polished stone. And there, to one side, crouched in a cage, was the leopardess—pale as moonlight, her dark spots like scattered ink. Steel bound her neck; her jaws were muzzled, her paws muffled. She did not move, but her eyes—great emerald discs with slitted pupils—tracked me with unnerving stillness. Was she alive? A statue? A phantom?

Narrow passages branched from the hall. I chose one at random, its winding path splitting into many. In the tightest of these, a page collided with me. He recoiled, fear flashing across his face before arrogance replaced it.

“What business have you here?” he sneered.

“To see the princess.”

He laughed. “As if she would receive you! I haven’t even seen her today myself.”

I seized him by the scruff, shaking him like a rat. “Take me to her, or I’ll drag you through every corridor until I find her. Let her judge how her servants treat guests.”

His bravado crumbled. He led me to a cavernous kitchen where servants moved sluggishly, their faces pale as they stared—not at me, but past me. I turned.

The leopardess stood at my back, silent, watching.

The servants recoiled. One, bolder than the rest, stepped forward.

“Take me to the princess,” I demanded.

“She has not yet risen, my lord.”

“Then wake her. Tell her one who knows the white leech seeks an audience.”

Fear flickered in his eyes. “She’ll kill me for such a message.”

I glanced at the leopardess. The man swallowed and fled.

The others remained frozen, their gazes locked on the beast. She paid them no mind, pressing close to me. I stroked her head. She rose onto her hind legs, her paws resting on my shoulders, then vanished like mist.

The servant returned. “Follow me.”

My pulse quickened.

He led me through a labyrinth of corridors, finally ushering me into a vast, lightless chamber. The air was cold, the walls invisible. A single dim glow pooled on the floor, and high above, an oval aperture pierced the darkness. The architecture was alien, deliberate—a place designed to unsettle.

Then, like a star flaring to life, she appeared.

Clad in white, her hair a torrent of black silk, the princess stood before me. Her eyes were fathomless, her skin luminous. She smiled—a girl’s smile, innocent, disarming.

Could this be the same woman who had struck me, scorned me, abandoned me?

She stepped closer, her fingers brushing my shoulder. “You’ve found me at last,” she murmured. “I knew you would.”

Her touch sent a shudder through me—not of fear, but of warring instincts. Attraction and revulsion twisted together.

“You’re cold,” she observed. “Come.”

She led me to a bath carved into the floor, its water clear as crystal. But something about it unnerved me. A scent clung to the air—sweet, yet wrong. Had she enchanted it? Poisoned it? I remembered the leopardess’s wounded paw, the howl in the night.

I did not enter.

Instead, I donned the robe laid out for me—white wool, embroidered with silver—and descended to a round chamber of alabaster, its walls shimmering with ghostly light. The princess awaited me there, clad in a gown of woven moonlight, her arms bare.

A feast lay before us: fruit, milk, wine the color of dawn.

“We do not kill to eat here,” she said. “But I hope this pleases you.”

I drank the wine. It was like swallowing a dream.

She watched me, her gaze heavy with meaning. “Men live to die,” she said. “I live to live on. Age does not wither me—it perfects me. I have walked this world for millennia. And yet, until you, no lover has ever proved worthy.”

Her voice was honey, but her eyes held something sharper.

“You passed my test,” she continued. “You followed not out of pity, but devotion. And so I offer you what no other has had: my power, my beauty, my love.”

She knelt before me, her hands resting on my knees. Then I saw it—the glove on her left hand, crude and out of place. Beneath it, I imagined claws.

A roar shook the chamber. The walls trembled.

The princess flinched. “The wine,” she said hastily. “Too strong for you. Rest now. We will speak later.”

She led me to my room, her smile tight with unease.

As the door closed behind her, I wondered: Was that roar the leopardess? And if so—why had it sounded like a warning?

I collapsed onto the bed, my mind churning through the princess’s tale. She had slipped—revealed more than she intended. That single careless word unraveled one mystery: the leopardess had *bounded* across the stream, while the princess lay helpless on the bank. The water had broken her spell. Her own confession laid bare the peril facing the Little Ones—and I, fool that I was, had saved their greatest enemy. The realization coiled in my gut as sleep dragged me under.

The wine, no doubt, had played its part.

When I woke, the chamber was bathed in the soft glow of a hanging lamp. A languid sweetness clung to me, as if I floated on a twilight sea, untethered from pain or fear. Dying, perhaps.

Then—*agony*. A searing sting through my heart. Again. And again. My body refused to move, pinned beneath an invisible weight. Something vile pressed down on me, smothering my will. I lay paralyzed, drowning in silent terror.

A hand brushed my face, gentle yet firm, pushing my head into the pillow. The weight lifted from my chest. I gasped, eyes fluttering open.

The princess stood over me on the bed, her gaze distant, dreaming. Her eyes were calm, her mouth smeared with red. She caught me staring and struck—a swipe of her handkerchief like a blade across my vision. Darkness.

A thud. The whisper of something heavy landing.

By the time my sight returned, only the flick of a tail vanished through the door. I lunged after it.

The hall of alabaster lay moonlit, a pale ghost of itself. No princess. She could be anywhere. Every shadow now held teeth.

Beyond, the black hall seethed with shapes half-seen. Phantoms wove through the dark, a chaotic dance of skull-faced women, skeletal riders, and burrowing horrors. No order, only hunger and motion. The princess was nowhere.

I fled to the vestibule.

Moonlight silvered the leopardess’s cage—where two forms writhed, locked in a soundless battle. Human and beast, tangled, furious. A blink, and the spotted leopardess slipped free, padding toward the open door. Behind her, the cage stood undisturbed, its occupant once more still.

The moon watched, expectant.

The shadow from the night before glided through the trees, the leopardess trailing. I followed, silent as they.

The city slept beneath the moon.

At the stair, the shadow ascended. The leopardess followed. Then—a cry. Something heavy thudded down, landing at my feet.

The woman who had led me home lay broken, her body blackened, twisted.

Before I could move, the spotted leopardess bounded down, a baby clamped in her jaws.

I lunged—

A streak of white lightning. The pale leopardess struck, teeth sinking into the other’s throat.

The battle was swift, brutal. Claws scrabbled on stone; growls tore the air. The spotted one was larger, but the white held on, relentless.

A howl—rising, twisting into a woman’s scream.

The white leopardess released her.

The spotted beast reared, melting into the princess, her skin swallowing the last of the leopard’s spots. For a heartbeat, she stood radiant, moonlight glinting off the blood streaking her throat.

Then—collapse. The spots surged back. She fled, a beast once more.

The white leopardess turned to me. In one motion, she wrenched the baby from my arms and vanished into the night.

The spotted leopardess vanished like a shadow up the slope, her tawny form melting into the dark mouth of the palace. By the time I reached the entrance hall, the princess stood waiting, her discarded robe now clasped around her. The wounds on her neck had crusted over, dried by the wind of her flight.

Her eyes flashed when she saw me, then softened into a practiced smile. "A trifling mishap," she said, tilting her chin. "I went to rid the city of that wretched cat-woman, but one of her beasts set upon me before I could strike." A shudder passed through her, and for a moment, she looked as she had in the cave—raw, unguarded. Pity twisted in my chest, though I knew the lie in her words. Had I abandoned her too soon?

"Foolish child," she murmured, catching my hesitation. "No tears, now. Wait here. I must go to the black hall—then you’ll fetch something to soothe these scratches."

I followed. I would not let her slip away.

The moment we crossed the threshold, a murmur rose like the drone of unseen wings. Light flickered erratically, illuminating fragments of the vast chamber—a dance of shadows and sudden clarity. Scenes unfolded in fractured glimpses: the skull-headed revelers in the forest hall, the princess watching from the doorway; the battle in the Evil Wood, her voice urging it onward. Yet all the while, she stood motionless before me, head bowed.

Then the light settled on the hollow in the heath, and there she was again, pacing, clawing at the mist that refused to cloak her. The old librarian emerged, studied her—and she collapsed, her form unraveling like smoke. A scream tore through the hall, and the princess, rigid until now, crumpled at my feet.

I fled. I would not revive her again.

Outside, I gripped the bars of the empty cage, my breath ragged. The leopardess’s tail twitched once in the stillness. Then—

"Come." The princess’s voice, cool and unhurried, beside me. "I have need of you."

She led me into the moonlit courtyard, where the great central tree towered, its branches woven tight at the crown like a silver pinecone. She gazed up, whispering as if to herself, "There’s a flower at the top—one touch would heal me. I could become a dove and pluck it, but a snake lurks in the leaves. A single bite, and I’d fall faster than if a tiger’s jaws found my throat." Her lips curled. "How I loathe that cat-woman."

Then she turned, her smile sweetly pained. "Can you climb?"

The question was a blade sheathed in velvet. I studied the trunk—knotted with old growth, scaled like a serpent’s spine. "I can."

"Not barefoot," she chided.

I had no sandals, but it didn’t matter. "I’ve gone without before."

Her fingers brushed her wounded neck, and against my judgment, I relented. Kneeling, she ripped the embroidered borders from her robe and bound my feet. "The ends will dangle," I noted.

"They won’t catch," she said.

I began the climb. The higher I went, the colder the air bit, until my hands and feet numbed to uselessness. The branches, though still, felt treacherously unstable. When I breached the canopy, moonlight drenched me—

And then I was drowning.

The world upended. I was thrown, spun, dragged under some invisible current. Water roared in my ears, my lungs burning as I plunged deeper. My knees struck solid ground at last, and a voice rasped in the dark:

"I told you so."

The cold water stung my skin as I blinked it from my eyes. Dawn’s pale glow shimmered across the raven’s obsidian feathers where he perched on the rim of the great stone basin. Below him, I floated on my back, half-submerged in the fountain’s pool—the same one my father had built, its steel-bright pillar spearing skyward before bursting into foam.

*"You told me nothing,"* I spat, nettled by his calm.

The raven tilted his head. *"I told you to refuse any request from those you distrust."*

"An easy thing to forget in the moment," I muttered.

"And yet, you will not forget the consequences," he replied, extending a hand. I took it, and in an instant, I stood dripping on the lawn beside him.

"You’ll need dry clothes," he observed. "Where you come from, a little water means nothing. Here, it carries weight."

With that, he was a raven again, striding toward the open door of the house, his gait oddly regal.

"I haven’t much to change," I admitted, thinking of the robe I’d discarded.

The raven cast a glance over his wing. *"A luxury I lack. It’s been an age since I shed a feather."*

Inside, the house lay silent, untouched by stirring life. I found a dressing-gown in my room and made for the library. The librarian emerged from the shadows of the shelves as I entered, and I sank onto a couch. The raven—now a man again—settled into a chair beside me.

For a long moment, neither of us spoke.

"What does any of this mean?" I finally asked.

"A fine question," he mused. "No one knows *what* things are—only what they *mean*. And that depends on what you do with them."

"I’ve done nothing with any of it."

"Not yet. But you recognize that, which is more than most manage in a lifetime. At least you *wanted* to be of use."

"I did. For the children—the Little Ones."

"And yet you began all wrong."

"I didn’t know the right way."

"No. But that’s no excuse for not seeking it."

I exhaled. "Then tell me plainly. What should I have done?"

"Stayed. Acted where you stood. You left to find your purpose, only to circle back empty-handed."

"But how could I have helped them? They were already beyond me."

"Beyond your *knowledge*, perhaps. Not beyond your care. They weren’t growing—not truly. You saw that. They feared the very idea of it."

"And I was supposed to force growth upon them?"

"No. Only to clear the obstacles in their path."

"What obstacles?"

He leaned forward. "The lack of water, for one. They had none to weep with."

"I’d have spared them tears if I could."

"Of course you would. Like every well-meaning fool. But without weeping, your world would never have been worth saving."

I flinched. "I thought—perhaps water *was* what they needed. But I never considered digging a well."

"Not even when you heard the rivers beneath the earth?"

"It crossed my mind once. But the giants—"

"Ah, yes. The giants." His voice sharpened. "You let fear rule you. You bowed to brutes while the Little Ones looked to you as their protector. You showed them cowardice disguised as caution."

Heat rose in my chest. "I served the giants to shield the children!"

"Did you? Or did you delay their strength by coddling their fears? You could have taught them to stand. Given them water. Let them learn their own power. Instead, you let the giants keep their chokehold."

The truth of it settled like a stone in my gut.

"And now?" I asked quietly.

"Now," said the raven, "you carry the weight of silence. The chance is gone."

The words hung between us, heavier than any chains.

I sat in silence, burning with shame. Every word he spoke was true—I had failed the Little Ones. Worse, I had failed myself.

Mr. Raven regarded me with unsettling calm. "You wronged them twice over. For creatures like them, a little discipline might have been a mercy. A lesson or two from their own branches, and they might have stood a chance against what’s coming."

"Cowards," I muttered, though the word tasted bitter.

"Cowardice in others doesn’t justify cruelty in you," he said. "A man who preys on weakness is weaker still." His voice darkened. "And now, because of your dealings with *her*—"

"I despise her!" I snarled.

"Did you ever let her know it?"

My silence was answer enough.

His gaze sharpened. "Not even hatred could make you honest. But quiet now—we weren’t alone at the fountain."

"I saw nothing. Only a wretched cat slinking into the bushes."

"A Persian," he corrected, "though you’d never know it by the state of her. Fur matted, eyes wild—hardly the regal creature she pretends to be."

A chill gripped me. "What are you saying?"

"We’ll see soon enough," he murmured. "Cats have their tells, and I know a few." He rose, crossed to the closet, and returned with the book—the *whole* book, intact, no longer torn.

I stared. "But the other half—"

"Lodged in my library," he said, as if that explained everything.

Perhaps it did. I held my tongue. Some questions had no safe answers.

He opened the ancient volume, its pages yellowed, one stained dark as old blood. His fingers traced the lines, then he began to read—not in any language I knew, yet every word seared into my mind. Fragments took shape in my memory:

A hiss slithered through the room. I jerked upright, scanning the shadows. Nothing.

He turned a page.

Another turn. Another verse.

A shriek tore through the air, raw and guttural. Not human. Not wholly animal.

Mr. Raven didn’t flinch. He read on, relentless.

The scream came again. This time, movement—a streak of white, a blur of matted fur. A massive cat, spine arched, claws splayed, scrambled toward the hearth.

He slammed the book down in its path. The creature froze, eyes locked on the pages.

Still, his voice rolled on, as if the words themselves were a cage:

The cat convulsed. Its yowl was a sound of rending flesh.

Mr. Raven stood, took a charred stick from the fireplace, and drew a symbol on the floor. Then he faced the trembling creature.

"Lilith," he said, quiet as a grave. "You didn’t expect *me* here, did you?"

The cat’s pupils swallowed its eyes.

He continued, merciless. "When God shaped me, He gave me a wife—not a servant, not a slave. A queen. But you wanted worship, not love. So you bled yourself dry to flee me, wed the Shadow instead, and crowned yourself in Hell."

The air thickened. The cat’s form shuddered, stretched—

A woman crouched on the carpet, naked, glorious, venom in her smile. "I am *beautiful*," she hissed. "And I am *eternal*."

"You are a burning bush," he said. "All ash, no flame."

Her hand flew to her side. A spasm twisted her face.

He stepped closer. "What’s festering under your ribs, Lilith? A leopard’s spot? Or the rot of your choices?"

She bared her teeth. "I will *drink* the blood of your child."

His voice changed. Not louder. *Deeper.* As if the earth itself spoke.

"**Down.**"

She collapsed. Fur rippled over skin. The leopard writhed, then shrank—

A scrawny gray cat again.

He seized her by the neck, hauled her to the closet, and flung her inside. With a final mark on the threshold, he shut the door. The lock clicked.

When he turned back to me, he was just an old librarian, weary and sad.

But his eyes were still full of storms.

“Stay vigilant,” Adam warned, his voice low. “She will deceive us if she can. Even the wise have fallen to her cunning.”

“How do we guard against her?” I asked.

“In every way,” he answered. “She fears her own child—hates her. To her, children are death to their parents, each generation an enemy to the last. She sees her daughter as a breach in her immortality, a wound bleeding her essence away. From the moment of the child’s birth, she has sought to destroy her. But her schemes have failed. Instead, a refuge of children has grown in the land she claims as hers—a sanctuary where her daughter is both shield and heart.

“My Eve once longed for the child, would have loved her as her own, but we were unfit then to raise her. She was taken into the wild, and for ages, we knew nothing of her fate. Yet she was tended by unseen hands, raised among spirits. She knew no sorrow until she found a babe in the woods, and the mother in her awoke. Since then, she has gathered more, each one a thread in the weave of her purpose. She does not rule them—she *is* them. They are her life, and she has forgotten any time before them.”

“But I saved *her*,” I said, glancing back toward the sealed chamber. “Doesn’t that bind me to her now?”

“You saved the enemy of them all. Your life is forfeit to their safety. She was on her way to slaughter them when the river struck her down. You intervened. Now, she lingers, waiting for her chance.”

“She can’t know of the door,” I said, though doubt crept in. “She can’t open it.”

Adam raised a hand, silencing me. “She hears through stone and shadow. Go—now. Seek my wife’s cottage. I will stand guard.”

“Let me go to the children instead.”

“No. That path is perilous. Trust my wife.”

I hesitated. Why delay? If not to shield them, why go at all? Yet still, I questioned instead of obeyed.

“Why keep her in that closet?” I pressed. “The night I fled your house, I stumbled straight into it.”

“Distance here is not as you know it,” he said. “That door is no closer to our home than any other place.”

“But—the books,” I argued. “You draw whole volumes from where I see only fragments. How can part of a book be *here* and part *there*?”

He sighed. “Some things are beyond your grasp—not because you lack wit, but because you lack the framework to hold them. You do not understand the air you breathe, yet you accept it. This is no different. I cannot make you comprehend, only believe.”

A low whistle from him, and a white dove appeared, alighting on his shoulder. They spoke without sound. Then, in a blink, she was gone.

“Why whistle?” I asked. “Sound here doesn’t carry *there*.”

“No. But meaning does.” He stood abruptly. “Go. Now.”

A crash echoed from the closet—the scrape of claws on oak. I tensed.

“She’s alone in there,” I muttered. “With that wound—”

“A wound that will never close,” he said grimly. “Evil does not die. It only ceases when replaced by good.”

A howl tore through the hall. We ran.

The mirror chamber loomed ahead. Adam adjusted the hooded frame, aligning the reflections. The mountains wavered into view.

Then—a snarl. The leopardess burst between us, a streak of spotted fury. She leaped through the glass as if through an open gate, vanishing into the distance.

I made to follow, but Adam caught my arm.

“No need to chase. She is beyond catching. Our path lies another way.”

“We can’t abandon the children to her!”

“Mara has been warned,” he said calmly. “She will act. But you must sleep first.”

I glanced back. A white shape moved across the plains—Mara, cutting an intercepting path.

“The leopardess is strong,” Adam murmured. “But the white lioness is stronger.”

“I’ve seen them fight. It was no clear victory.”

“You saw with unopened eyes. The princess fled. She always does—until the day she surrenders. Then, perhaps, her dawn will come.” He turned away. “Now, come. He who cannot act must first rest.”

I lingered, watching the last shimmer of the white leopardess vanish into the shadows before turning to follow the librarian—though every instinct rebelled. *Sleep?* Here, among the dead, while the world hummed with unseen urgency? Time slipped past like smoke. The sun bled into the horizon, and the air grew thick with the creeping chill of the tomb. My pulse faltered as the librarian’s form blurred into the gloom, his footsteps fading.

Then came the raven.

Its wings cut the silence, slow and deliberate. Fireflies winked in the void; the moon, pallid and reluctant, dragged itself above the land.

*"You are weary, Mr. Vane,"* the raven observed, perching on a jagged stone. *"You’ll need the horse by morning."*

It whistled—a sound like wind through bone.

A shadow blotted the moon. Hooves drummed, distant at first, then thunderous. The beast erupted from the lunar face itself—a specter of sinew and gleaming bone, its mane a storm of silver, its tail lashing like a whip. Nineteen hands tall, its hide stretched taut over a frame built for ruin. Death’s own mount.

Yet I *wanted* it.

Greed, raw and clawing, coiled in my chest. I ran my hands over its ribs, each one sharp beneath satin skin. The moon’s reflection pooled in its flanks. I whispered to it, breathed into its nostrils—and it answered with a hot gust, its eyes twin pits of blue fire over smoldering coals.

*"Good,"* croaked the raven. *"He’ll bear you better if he knows you."*

"Let me ride him now," I demanded.

I hauled myself onto its back, fingers twisted in its mane. The horse stood motionless.

*"Why delay?"* the raven asked.

"The leopardess—" I swallowed. "I *must* follow her."

But the horse beneath me was power incarnate. I *ached* to let it run.

Adam’s voice cut through the dark, cold as a blade. *"You’ve done nothing of worth, Mr. Vane. Do you know why?"*

"Because I’m a fool."

*"Dead men,"* he said, *"cannot wake the dead."*

The words meant nothing. I dug my heels in.

The horse *moved*.

Night became a blur. The wind screamed; the earth vanished beneath us. The moon shuddered in its arc—then *rolled*, tumbling like a dropped coin. Darkness swallowed us mid-leap.

The horse fell.

I staggered up, hands skimming its lifeless bulk. No breath. No pulse. Just a carcass.

I sat atop the corpse, face in my hands, as the world turned to ash around me.

The night was a blade of ice against my skin. My body numbed beneath me, the cold gnawing deeper with each breath. Then came the wolves—soft paws on stone, panting breaths thick with hunger. Their eyes, embers in the dark, closed in. I stood, defenseless.

They lunged.

A blur of green eyes shattered the pack’s advance. Smaller shapes, silent as shadows, tore into the wolves with a sound like wind through thorns. Cats. Led by *him*—the gray beast whose eyes I knew even in the blackness. The fight was chaos, a storm of snarls and claws I could only hear. Then, silence. The wolves fled.

Victory was brief.

Teeth sank into my legs. Cats swarmed me, a living tide of claws and fury. I fought, tearing them away, but they clung like burrs. They scratched, bit, hissed—then, as if obeying some unseen command, ceased their torment and drove me forward instead. All night they harried me through the dark, herding me like prey until dawn broke over the orchard valley. Exhausted, I collapsed into sleep.

A kick woke me.

Ropes bit into my wrists and ankles. Giants loomed, their laughter coarse as gravel. "Fitting," I spat, laughing back—a hollow sound. They dragged me to the flint pile, mocking with thrown fruit. I worked, biding time. *The Little Ones will come.*

And they did.

A chorus of bells and trumpets erupted from the brush. Children rode in on miniature beasts—horses, bears, elephants—their voices a symphony of joy. The giants faltered. Stones flew. Animals charged. In moments, I was free, surrounded by tiny hands and laughter.

Lona fed me fruit, her silence tender. Then they fashioned a litter between two elephants, tails knotted, trunks linked. I lay cradled in the hollow of their backs as the procession moved, children chattering until I drowsed. When I woke, the forest was alive with birdsong.

"Close your eyes!" a voice chirped.

I obeyed. A rustle, then silence—*true* silence, the kind that hums in the bones.

"Open them!"

The trees erupted. Children dropped like ripe fruit, white blossoms against the dark. They were *nesting* now, they explained, learning bird-song from their winged teachers. One boy, smug in his walnut-tree perch, called down: "Kings don’t need wings! Arms and legs are better!"

As night deepened, their voices faded into dreams. The last thing I heard was a sleepy murmur from the branches:

"Up again, king. Good night. I seepy."

And then, only the wind.

I lay beneath a tree as the children, one by one or in small clusters, climbed to their nests for the night. Though weary myself, I remained awake, certain Lona would come—she had not yet bid me goodnight.

When I first saw her again, I was struck by her resemblance to the princess, and I knew at once she was the daughter Adam had spoken of. Yet where Lilith’s beauty was harsh and dazzling, Lona’s was softened by innocence, deepened by a mother’s tenderness. *She must be with the child,* I thought—the one belonging to the woman I had met fleeing, the woman Lona had already told me was *not half mother enough.*

At last she came, settling beside me with a quiet joy, stroking my face and hands before she began to recount all that had happened since I left. Moonlight flickered through the leaves, illuminating her face—so full of thought, of care that only made her love more radiant. How such a child could have sprung from such a mother was beyond me—but then, she had not one parent, nor two, but *three.*

She drew me to her by the part of me most like herself, and I loved her as one who, no matter how she grew, would only ever become more of a child. I realized now that I had loved her even as I left her, and the hope of seeing her again had been my greatest comfort. Every word she spoke went straight to my heart, purifying it like truth itself.

She told me that after my departure, the giants had grown more hostile, half-convinced of the Little Ones’ existence but still blind to their presence. Sometimes the giants would stamp and rage, sensing—or imagining—some sign of them, while the children stood nearby, laughing silently at their fury.

But soon the giants’ anger took a darker turn: they began destroying the fruit trees the Little Ones depended on. This forced their mother to act. She set the sharpest-eared among them to listen at night and learned that the giants believed *I* was hiding nearby, waiting to strike them down in their sleep.

So she devised a plan. The Little Ones would leave the valley, retreating into the forest—far enough to escape the giants’ notice, but close enough to return for fruit under cover of darkness. The forest had little undergrowth for shelter, but if birds could live there, why not the Little Ones? They could learn from the wild creatures, take refuge in the treetops, build nests where the earth offered no hollows.

She proposed it, and they agreed eagerly. Already they could climb like squirrels; they had watched birds build nests countless times. The forest trees, though vast, did not frighten them—they stretched toward the sky, their branches like open arms, inviting. Perhaps, in the highest boughs, they might even find the bird that laid baby-eggs, the one that tumbled them down when they were ripe!

And so they became creatures of the canopy, as at home among the leaves as the birds themselves. The giants, finding no trace of them, decided they had fled—and soon forgot them entirely.

Lona asked if I had noticed how many of the children had grown. I admitted I had not, but I could believe it. She said their minds had grown too—a comfort, for at first their increasing size had alarmed her.

They gathered fruit in the twilight or under the moon—hours the giants feared, as if darkness and moonlight were poison to them. But soon the Little Ones learned that night-picked fruit spoiled faster, and they began to wonder: why not drive the giants away instead?

Already they knew the forest’s animals—had made friends with horses, elephants, even bears, addressing each as *brother* or *sister.* (Only caterpillars were left alone—until they emerged as butterflies, at which point the children greeted them as *Sister Butterfly,* as if their transformation were a kind of repentance.)

One evening, as they went to gather fruit, they stumbled upon a woman seated on the ground, a baby in her lap—the same woman I had met on the road to Bulika. Mistaking her for a giantess who had stolen one of their own, they swarmed her in a whirl of small fists and feet. But when she surrendered the child without resistance—kissing her as tenderly as they would have—they realized she was no enemy. They brought her fruit, offered her kindness.

The woman, afraid to return to Bulika (where the princess would surely punish her for the leopardess’s injury), chose to stay. Here, at least, her child would be safe.

The woman knew the legend—that the princess feared a child destined to destroy her. The people of Bulika knew it too, though none guessed the horrors she committed to preserve her youth. The woman saw an opportunity: if she could rouse the Little Ones to attack Bulika, she might slip away in the chaos, return to her husband, and vanish.

She began by teaching the children to throw stones—a game they took to with surprising skill. Soon they were pelting targets with deadly accuracy.

Then, while gathering fruit, they found me—asleep, guarded by giants. They rushed home, plotted, returned with elephants and horses, overwhelmed my captors, and carried me off.

That night, Lona and I spoke for hours—of the children’s growth, of what it might mean. Her wisdom astonished me; had I been more of a child myself, perhaps it would not have.

Near dawn, a fluttering stirred me awake. The Little Ones were descending from their nests, scattering into the forest.

*“They’re gathering stones,”* Lona explained. *“If the giants come, they’ll rain them down from the trees.”*

She spoke with pride. *“Wait until you see them throw.”*

Then she told me of the night before last—of two leopardesses, one white, one spotted, locked in battle beneath the trees. The children, thinking it play, had rushed to pet them—until the white one roared, scattering them like birds. By morning, only Whitey remained.

*“I know them both,”* I said. *“Spotty hates the children. Whitey loves them—she only scared them to keep them safe.”*

The Little Ones returned, noisy with triumph, lugging stones. They climbed back to their nests, clumsy with their burdens, and slept at once.

Lona withdrew to her tree. I stayed where I was, comforted by the thought that Whitey might still linger nearby.

At sunrise, the giants came—straggling in small groups until over a hundred had gathered. The children slept on, and I dared not wake them.

But then a giant stumbled over me. I struck him, and at once a storm of stones hailed down—every one missing me, every one striking true. The giants fell, roaring, until the forest floor was littered with them.

The children descended like monkeys, mounted the waiting elephants, and rode over the groaning giants. When their noise grew tiresome, the elephants silenced them with a few swift blows of their trunks.

By the next morning, the giants were gone—vanished. They never returned to the forest. The Little Ones moved deeper into the orchard valley, and the war was over.

Victory had been claimed, but the true battle lay ahead. The woman from Bulika spoke endlessly of her city—its crumbling walls, its cowardly denizens, the wickedness festering in its heart. Soon, the children chattered of nothing else, though none had ever seen a city, nor understood what it meant to siege one. Only then did I grasp the woman’s design—though the *why* of it still eluded me.

Lona, ever the flame to my kindling, embraced the idea. The children were no longer helpless; their minds and bodies sharpened daily. What stood in our way? The specter of Lilith, yes—but I knew her weakness. The prophecy of her downfall clung to her like a curse, and the people feared it. If there was risk, it was a worthy one. Success would mean more than survival—it would forge these children into a people, a force for righteousness. They would rule with the fierce love of the innocent, and Bulika would be remade.

And I? I confess, ambition coiled in my chest. Lona, rightful heir to that tainted throne, with me beside her—minister, protector, companion. A life in her service was all I desired. Together, with these children as our foundation, what kingdom might we build? (Foolish, too, were my fleeting dreams of gem-trade between worlds—a greed-stained fantasy best forgotten.)

Adam’s plea echoed in me: *Water might hasten their growth.* But Lona, ever prudent, shook her head. "They are what they are without it. When the city is ours, we will search for rivers."

So we prepared.

Lona saw to provisions—food, shelter, the needs of the littlest. I drilled the rest. Slingstones flew like angry bees; bows thrummed; girls hefted aloe-spears, needle-sharp and deadly. The smallest clung to their elder sisters, a living chain of care. My greatest struggle was discipline—to make them rally at a cry, to move as one.

And Lona—how she had bloomed! Taller now, her hair a cascade of sunlight, yet every grace of childhood lingered. When we met after long absence, she flung her arms around me, wordless, radiant—then sprang away to soothe a whimpering babe. To watch her with the children was to witness something holy. She was grandmother, sister, mother, all in one.

My heart knew no division: she was my north star, my purpose. Yet never did she seek me idly—only near, always near, her presence a steady hum beneath my thoughts. Her love was not in kisses, but in the quiet certainty of her steps beside mine.

The forest birds, plumed in jeweled splendor, gave me an idea. I fashioned Lona a mantle of feathers, layer upon iridescent layer. She watched, curious, as I worked. When I clasped it over her shoulders, she removed it at once—not in refusal, but in some unspoken ritual. Days later, she returned wearing it, and presented me with armor of her own making: scaled leaves, tough as leather. From then on, we wore them like royalty.

Then came the horses—great, gentle beasts that bowed to no fear of our strangeness. Two I trained for riding, and Lona’s joy as she mounted was a thing to behold. The creature beneath her seemed to preen, proud of its burden.

Still, doubt gnawed at me. A city was no small thing to take. But the woman of Bulika scoffed. "The princess’s magic is dust before these children. As for her soldiers—let them come. I’ll crack their skulls myself." Only the leopardess gave her pause, but I was ready for that fight.

Yet I hesitated. "Should the smallest stay behind?" I asked Lona.

She shook her head. "Their mothers must see them. Hearts will break open at the sight."

Her faith shamed me. I, who had faltered before, now marched because she led. There was no choice but forward.

We stockpiled hay for the elephants, nuts for the bears, dried fruit for ourselves. The horses stood laden, the children eager. On the eve of departure, I gathered them.

"You were taken from your mothers," I said. "Stolen by a princess who feared you. Now we go to Bulika—to find them, to free them."

"We can fight!" they cried, small fists raised.

"You can," I said. "But listen. Strike true. Draw your bows to the ear. When you sling a stone, mean it."

"We will!"

"Some of you may be hurt."

A boy on a nimble bull calf grinned. "I don’t mind!"

"Some may die."

Lona’s voice, clear as a blade, cut through. "I would let my mother kill me," she said, "if first I could kiss her."

A girl leaped onto a rock. "Come on! To our mothers!"

My heart ached. But there was no turning back.

The morrow would march us toward glory—or ruin.

The morning sun had barely crested the horizon when we set out, a vibrant procession cutting across the vast plain—children astride bears and deer, elephants laden with hay, and a fluttering escort of butterflies and dragonflies. The very forest seemed to migrate with us, birds leading the way as if they were heralds of some grand procession. Even the insects stayed with us, a shimmering cloud of life that refused to turn back until the gates of Bulika loomed before us.

The journey was not without its whimsy. Little riders dozed on the backs of their patient mounts, and those perched atop the elephants chattered to their unseen companions below. One child, digging hay from his elephant’s load, murmured, *"Nosy dear, I’m coming! Just a little longer and you’ll poke your trunk up to kiss me!"* The beasts themselves seemed to understand, their deep rumbles and whickers blending with the laughter of the children.

But when the city rose before us—its jagged walls like the teeth of some great beast—a hush fell over the company. For the first time, fear flickered in their eyes. These were creatures of the open sky and soft earth; stone nests were alien, unwelcoming. Yet they pressed on, trusting in Lona and, to my shame, in me.

The thunder of hooves and paws echoed through the archway as we entered, a sound so foreign to Bulika’s streets that even the horses startled at their own noise. The Little Ones, usually so fearless, trembled. The three eldest girls clutched the infants tighter, and even the bears seemed uneasy. Only Lona rode steady beside me, her gaze unshaken.

The city awoke sluggishly. Faces peered from windows, first blank, then horrified as they took in the army of children and beasts. Women crept into the streets, drawn despite their terror—until one boy, spotting a woman who reminded him of his mother, flung himself into her arms. A man wrenched him away—only to be met with the swift justice of a girl’s spear.

*"They are just bad giants,"* Lona said, her voice cold as she urged her horse toward a towering brute who dared raise a club. He faltered, and a hail of stones sent him sprawling. Another tried to intercept us, striking a child’s mount—but an elephant’s foot settled the matter.

Yet the worst blow came when we saw the women of Bulika up close. Lona’s face darkened. These were not the mothers her Little Ones dreamed of. Before we could regroup, two boys were snatched away. We gave chase, only to be met by a white leopardess—familiar, yet terrifying to those who remembered the forest’s battles. But little Odu, brave as ever, ran to her. She tumbled in her haste not to harm him, and before she could rise, he was on her back. The sight of a child mastering their terror struck the crowd dumb.

We found one stolen boy in the bowels of a house, tossed into a pit by a woman who sneered that the leopardess would have him. Instead, the beast carried him to safety. The other child was not so fortunate—slain before we could reach him. Lona’s grief was sharp, but she whispered, *"At least he will never become one of them."*

The leopardess turned her wrath on the murderer, dragging him into the street like a cat with prey. *"Let us leave this place,"* Lona said, her voice hollow. *"There are no mothers here."*

As night fell, the Little Ones curled against their animals, asleep in moments. The city lay quiet, but I kept watch. Bulika was not finished with us yet. The princess had not shown herself—and silence was never a good sign.

The children dreamed, their breaths a soft chorus beneath the stars. The animals dozed lightly, ready to wake at a whisper. And all around us, the city held its breath, waiting.

The stench of the city’s corruption clung to us, but retreat was not an option. Lona’s disgust was palpable—she wanted to flee, to leave these hollow-eyed wretches to their fate. But I knew better. To run now would be to betray the Little Ones, to leave them vulnerable to the storm that would surely follow. And above all, it would deny the prophecy its due.

Mother and daughter *had* to meet.

Lona’s radiance might disarm Lilith’s cruelty. Or, if violence stirred, I would stand between them. If words failed, I would strike without mercy. The universe had woven this moment—doom coiled around Lilith’s throat, and we were the hands meant to tighten it.

I did not tell Lona the truth of their bond. She agreed to march to the palace regardless.

---

The princess had seen us coming.

At dawn, from her tower, she had watched the approach of the Little Ones—an army of innocence she had failed to obliterate. Terror, raw and choking, seized her. The prophecy was no longer a whisper in the dark. It was here.

She descended to the black hall, to the ellipse’s northern focus, beneath the open roof. Here, she *thought*—if one could call it thinking. To her, it was the act of shaping herself into the illusion she preferred, a queen unmarred by rot or regret.

Above her, unseen in the gloom, hung a mirror. She waited for the sun to climb, to illuminate her reflection, to gift her the vision of her own splendor. Shadows flitted at the edges of her sight, but she ignored them. Even the one that stood beneath the mirror, the silent attendant of her steps, went unnoticed.

The city fell. The people cowered. The Little Ones camped in the square, their strange mounts grazing on cobblestones. The sun rose, and for a fleeting moment, Lilith saw herself glorious—until the light shifted, and the mirror showed her the truth.

The black stain on her side had spread.

She recoiled, clutching her robe. The Shadow slipped away, and this time, she saw him go.

---

We rode to the palace at midday—twelve of the tallest boys alongside us, astride their small horses and elephants. The great hall waited, a maw of darkness.

Lilith sat in her chair, a shaft of sunlight crowning her, when the thunder of hooves shook the stones. Her breath hitched. No such sound had ever breached her sanctum.

We entered.

To us, she was a blaze in the gloom, a fallen star trapped in human form. To her, we were invaders, silhouettes against the light.

Then Lona leapt from her horse.

"Mother! *Mother!*"

The word tore through the hall, sweet and terrible.

Lilith’s face twisted. Hatchered her beauty into something monstrous. She stood, rigid, as Lona sprinted to the dais, arms outstretched—

And then, in a movement too quick to stop, Lilith seized her.

Lona’s body hit the marble with a sound that would haunt me forever.

I was too late.

I dropped to my knees, gathering her broken form against me. Blood slicked my hands, dripped onto the floor. The horses screamed, scenting it. Mine reared, bolted blindly into the dark, the others following in a panicked stampede. The Little Ones’ mounts crashed into the walls, their riders thrown clear.

Lona’s horse stood trembling over her, trunk outstretched as if to nudge her awake.

The princess slumped back in her chair, her face a corpse’s mask, her eyes burning. The rot had reclaimed her—her skin withered, the stain on her side a brand of damnation.

But I saw none of it. Only Lona.

"Mother…" she sighed.

Then nothing.

---

I carried her into the courtyard, the sun glaring down on her pallid face. Her head lolled. She was gone.

The world narrowed to silence.

I forgot the Little Ones. Forgot the murderess on her throne. Forgot even the weight in my arms. I wandered, numb, until the emptiness of the square jolted me back.

I had lost them too.

Staggering to the broken pillar, I collapsed at its base. And then—

A flicker. A ghost of a smile on Lona’s still lips.

I knew it was illusion. And yet I believed it.

---

The city was waking, its people gathering like vultures. They jeered, but none dared approach—not with the white leopardess slinking at my heels. I kicked at her. She hesitated, then followed.

At the gate, the crowd grew bold. A man shoved me, greedy hands reaching for my burden.

I broke his ribs with a kick.

Then—chaos. A roar from behind. The mob scattered as the Little Ones surged through, astride their elephants.

And there, bound atop two of the beasts, was Lilith.

Her eyes rolled, wild and unseeing. The elephants’ trunks probed her bonds, ensuring they held.

Behind her, on Lona’s horse, rode two more children.

We marched out of the city, victorious and ruined.

We had taken the princess.

And lost our queen.

A murmur of delight rippled through my companions as they spotted their kin in the distance. The two astride Lona’s horse spurred forward to meet them, greeted by a faltering cheer—one that dissolved into silence as swiftly as it had risen. When I drew near, the air thickened with the sound of stifled sobs, like the hushed breaking of waves upon some unseen shore.

The faces of the Little Ones were hollowed by an unfamiliar dread, their eyes too old for their small frames. No ordinary sorrow could have carved such weariness into their features. A few approached me hesitantly, their arms outstretched, wordlessly pleading to bear Lona’s lifeless form. I surrendered her to them, my chest tightening at the sight of their hopeless tenderness. They cradled her as if she might wake at any moment, stroking her arms, pressing kisses to her cold cheeks. But she did not stir.

They carried her to a patch of soft grass and laid her down gently, their sobs muffled against one another’s shoulders. The rest clustered around, a grieving circle of small, broken hearts. Beyond them, the elephants stood silent sentinel, while I lingered at the edges, my gaze fixed on Lona over the bowed heads of her children.

Then, one of the Little Ones—Odu—noticed the princess. His small frame tensed, his whisper sharp with recognition.

"I’ve seen her before," he hissed to the child beside him. "She’s the one who fought the white leopardess! The night they woke us with their screams!"

"Don’t be stupid," the other retorted. "That was a beast, spotted and wild!"

Odu’s voice dropped lower. "Look at her eyes. She’s bad. A giantess, yes—but a beast too. I know it’s her."

His companion edged forward, only for Odu to yank him back.

"Don’t!" Odu warned, his own gaze flickering between fear and fascination. "She’ll devour you in a breath. She’s the shadow. The wicked princess!"

"But they said she was beautiful!"

"She was," I cut in. "Until wickedness twisted her."

The princess heard. The venom in her glare could have withered stone.

Odu frowned, his small face crumpling with guilt. "I shouldn’t have run away."

"Why did you?" I asked.

He hesitated. Another child answered instead. "We were scared."

"Of what?"

"A man," murmured a third. "He came from the palace, down the hill."

Odu shook his head. "Not a man. A shadow. He had no *thick* to him."

I knelt to his level. "Tell me."

Odu’s fingers twisted together. "He was black. All black, like spilled ink, walking like a giant but flat—just darkness shaped like a man. We didn’t run at first. We watched. He kept coming, spreading wider and wider until… he wasn’t there anymore. Then he was *inside* us."

A shudder ran through him.

"How did you know?"

"I wasn’t me anymore," he whispered. "I wanted to hurt Sozo. Not really! But the shadow made me think it." He flung his arms around Sozo, trembling. "It wasn’t *me*. Deep down, I still loved him. But the shadow… it hated through me."

His voice cracked. "Then I heard laughter. Ugly, shaking laughter. And I ran. I didn’t mean to—my legs just *went*. Naughty legs!" He kicked his own feet, as if punishing them for their betrayal.

"And the shadow?" I pressed.

Odu lifted his shoulders. "Gone, maybe. Back to the dark where shadows live."

I turned away, my thoughts spiraling. Where was Lona now? Not this empty shell in the grass. Kneeling, I gathered her limp form into my arms and whispered against her ear, "Where are you, Lona? I love you."

No answer. Only silence.

After pressing a kiss to her unresponsive lips, I laid her back down and set a guard over her. Night was falling. The Little Ones needed protection.

By sunset, I had stationed sentinels around the camp and a watch near the princess. The rest I ordered to sleep. They curled into the grass, their exhaustion claiming them instantly.

When the moon rose, a flash of white caught my eye—the leopardess, gliding soundlessly around the camp’s perimeter. Three times she passed between the princess and the slumbering children, a silent guardian. Satisfied, I dismissed the watch, letting them rest. Then I stretched out beside Lona’s body, keeping vigil through the long, hollow night.

The night was a chasm of whispers and unseen terrors. The children tossed in restless sleep, their dreams invaded by phantoms of the house’s hidden workings. Silence coiled around them, thick as smoke, broken only by the imagined rush of a wind that made no sound—a phantom gale that shuddered through the walls yet left no mark. They woke gasping, certain the house itself was alive, breathing in the dark.

Water rose in their dreams, a black flood seeping from below, swallowing the rooms whole. It came without ripple or echo, drowning the air until they choked on nothing. Then, as suddenly, it receded, leaving them dry and trembling. Sleep reclaimed them, only to hurl them into another nightmare—cats, countless cats, swarming over them, claws sheathed in malice but never breaking skin. Then, piercing the dark like a blade, the distant howl of the great desert cat, the matriarch of shadows. At her call, the swarm stilled. The children slept at last, but the night was far from done.

***

I remained with the veiled woman and the fallen princess, a witness to the unraveling. The white leopardess cowered in the corner, her golden eyes wide with dread. Shadows pooled and shifted, restless as storm clouds. The princess lay motionless on the settle, a corpse awaiting resurrection or damnation.

When Mara returned, she positioned Lilith between us, the fire’s glow painting her hollow cheeks. The air thickened, charged with something unspeakable. Time stretched, taut as a bowstring, until midnight struck its silent chord.

Mara rose. Slowly, deliberately, she unwound the veils from her face. Beneath them was sorrow incarnate—not despair, but sorrow that had long since made peace with its weight. Tears fell, unheeded, onto Lilith’s brow as she bent and breathed upon it.

"Will you turn from what you have done?" Mara asked, thrice.

Lilith’s lips parted, her voice a thing of echoes, not sound. "I will not. I am myself, and no other."

"Then you are lost," Mara said, and stepped back.

The serpent of light came then, the worm of living fire, slithering from the hearth to Lilith’s breast. It vanished into her, and the torment began. Sweat beaded like poison on her skin. She convulsed, arched, then stood—not by her own power, but as a puppet of revelation. She saw herself. Not the queen she had believed, but the ruin she had wrought.

"Unmake me," she begged.

"You must unmake yourself," Mara replied.

Lilith’s defiance crumbled, then resurged—a final, desperate rebellion. She cursed the light, the life, the very hand that had shaped her. But the truth was inescapable. She was a dead thing that could not die, a shadow that had devoured its own soul.

Then came the nothingness. Not emptiness, but negation—an abyss that swallowed even the concept of being. Lilith screamed, not in pain, but in the horror of ceasing without end. "Let me die!" she howled.

Mara caught her as she fell. "You shall," she murmured. "But not as you think."

Dawn crept in, soft and insistent. The wind carried rain, gentle as forgiveness, and Lilith wept—not for repentance, but because she could do nothing else. The tears were a beginning.

Mara laid her down. For the first time in eternity, Lilith slept.

The Mother of Sorrows rose, veiling her face in the folds of her mantle, and went to rouse the Little Ones. They lay as if untouched by time, their breath steady and deep, but the moment her voice reached them, they sprang to their feet, eyes bright, limbs supple, as though the night had never been. Laughing, they followed her down the stair, their bare feet whispering against the stone, until she led them to where the princess lay, her tears still flowing even in sleep.

The children’s joy faltered. They looked from the weeping woman to the rain beyond, then back again, their small faces solemn.

“The sky is crying too,” murmured one.

“The princess is leaking!” cried another, wide-eyed. “Is it the same as rivers?”

Odu, ever curious, reached out to touch the wet trails on Lilith’s hollow cheeks. “I thought rivers were louder,” he said, glancing at me for confirmation.

Mara smiled. “These are the quiet rivers—the ones that water the soul.”

“Will they wake the ground?” asked Luva, pressing close.

“Yes, little one. Soon the earth will drink and grow green again.”

Odu frowned, poking his own cheek as if testing for moisture. “But why does the princess cry?”

“Because she is learning,” Mara said softly. “And learning hurts before it heals.”

Lilith stirred, her eyes fluttering open. The children gasped as she lifted her gaze to them—no longer burning with fury, but heavy with sorrow.

“We must take her home,” Mara announced.

“Is home a nest?” asked Sozo, tilting his head.

“A kind of nest,” Mara agreed. “But first, we must bring her to a greater place—a house of rest.”

Luva wrinkled her nose. “Are there cats there?”

“None,” Mara assured her. “Only dreams too sweet for claws.”

With that, the Little Ones scattered to gather their belongings, all but Luva, who lingered, staring at Lilith’s tear-streaked face.

“Her robe is all wet,” she whispered. “Will the elephants mind?”

“Not at all,” Mara said. “Tears cleanse more than dirt.”

I had slept by the fire, but now I rose, my limbs stiff yet my mind clear. Mara turned to me.

“It is time, Mr. Vane.”

“Is there no safer path?” I asked. “Must we face the monsters again?”

“The riverbed is easiest,” she replied. “But the den cannot be avoided. Fear not—the children will pass unharmed.”

We stepped into the dawn. The beasts waited, patient as shadows. Odu was already perched on the neck of one elephant, grinning. I mounted Lona’s horse, and Mara placed her body gently in my arms. When she emerged again with Lilith, the children gasped—not in fear, but awe. Mara had unveiled herself, and her beauty struck them silent.

The elephants took Lilith with infinite care, their trunks cradling her as if she were made of glass. The procession began.

The moor stretched before us, vast and barren, but the children danced around Mara, their laughter weaving through the wind. We crossed the dry channels without trouble, yet as we neared the ridge, the sun dipped low, staining the sky with fire.

“Mount,” I urged the Little Ones. The moon hung hesitant, as if reluctant to rise.

Mara led us forward, her steps sure. The elephants followed, Lilith swaying between them. The leopardess trailed behind, her tail twitching.

Then we reached the edge—the nightmare’s maw.

The earth trembled.

Monsters surged from the ground, their forms a delirium of teeth and talons, all reaching for Lilith. She stiffened, eyes wild, but the children, blind to the horrors, chattered obliviously. The beasts did not touch them.

“Keep close,” I called.

Mara walked on, unshaken. A head, severed and grinning, rolled into our path. The leopardess pounced, but it vanished with a shriek. The moon wavered, then plunged downward.

Darkness fell.

A shape loomed—a corpse-flower of a face, jaws gaping. The leopardess leapt.

Then blackness.

A cry echoed. I whirled, but the elephants were already safe on the far bank. Behind me, dust swirled where the leopardess had fought.

A voice, gentle and familiar, spoke from the void.

Mara answered, her voice thick. “But I will miss her.”

We stumbled toward a distant light—a candle in a window. Adam stood there, his face lined but kind.

“Welcome,” he said.

Lilith, pride crumbling, stepped forward.

Adam’s eyes softened. “We have waited long for you.”

She said nothing.

Eve appeared, radiant, her gaze lingering on Lilith. “The enemy of my children,” she murmured.

“No longer,” Mara said.

Lilith shuddered. “I do not know your Father.”

“That is why you suffer,” Adam replied.

She turned as if to flee, but Mara stopped her. “You must open your hand.”

Lilith stared at her clenched fist. “I cannot.”

Adam returned with a sword—the blade like frozen lightning. Without a word, he took her wrist.

One flash. One drop of blood.

Her hand fell into Mara’s lap.

Lilith sighed—and slept.

The children, watching, crept close.

“She’s pretty now,” one whispered.

“I’ll sleep with her,” declared another, climbing beside her.

Mara covered them both. “Rest well.”

We left them there, the dead and the dreaming, and stepped back into the night. The leopardess lay across Lilith’s feet, a guardian in white.

And the house of death held its breath, waiting for morning.

I turned to Eve, my voice raw with exhaustion. “Mother, one couch beside Lona lies empty. I know I am unworthy—but may I not keep vigil with my dead tonight? I surrender myself. I am sick of my own weakness. Let me sleep the sleep of surrender.”

Her gaze softened. “The place beside Lona is already yours. But first, there is a task you must complete.”

“I am ready,” I said.

She arched a brow. “How do you know you can do it?”

“Because you command it,” I replied.

She turned to Adam. “Is he forgiven, husband?”

Adam’s voice was steady. “From my heart.”

“Then tell him what must be done.”

Adam took my arm. “Come. We will speak in the cottage.”

The wind howled as we stepped outside, its fury dying to a mournful whisper. Inside, Adam sat, and I stood before him, waiting.

“You remember,” he said, “the dry rock where you first glimpsed the desert. Return there now. Walk deep into the wastes. Listen with your ear to the sand. If you hear water beneath, continue. If silence answers, you have strayed. Follow the sound until it grows loudest—there, you will dig.”

He handed me a spade. “Bury the hand where the water sings clearest. But heed this: carry it with care. Let nothing touch it. Do not stop. Do not turn. Speak to no one. Look at no one. The night is dark, but you must go at once.”

I took the spade and stepped into the blackness.

The cold bit deep, the path invisible—yet with each step, a faint glow pulsed beneath my feet, guiding me. The bad burrow lay still, lifeless. The moon rose, casting long shadows, revealing the way.

As dawn neared, a figure materialized before me—a woman veiled in mist.

“Have you forgotten me?” she whispered.

I walked on.

“You left me in the dark!” she shrieked. “I am alive—take my hand!”

Her fingers brushed my arm—cold, grasping. I did not slow.

Armored phantoms marched behind me, their footsteps fading as I pressed forward.

Then, another—a woman seated in the path, face hidden.

“You’ve come at last,” she crooned. “Rest now. Give me your burden.”

Her voice coiled around me, sweet as poison. My limbs ached. My will wavered.

But I knew her deceit.

I stepped through her.

The Shadow came next—a specter with a helmeted skull, exhaling decay. I choked on the stench, my strength failing—yet I walked on, and it passed through me like smoke.

The wind shifted. Dawn breathed against the horizon.

Mara’s cottage stood open, a loaf and pitcher waiting. No wails. No whispers.

I climbed the precipice, into the desert.

There, I listened—until the hidden water roared loudest in my ears.

I dug. The sand fought me, shifting, sliding. But at last—dampness.

I laid the hand in the earth. Water seeped upward, swallowing it.

I filled the grave.

Then, spent, I collapsed beside it—and slept.

The ground was damp when I awoke, the river swelling in its ancient bed, hungry to reclaim the valley. Soon it would thunder over the precipice, split into twin torrents—one to drown the orchard, the other to scour the den of monsters—and between them, the Evil Wood would stand isolated. I had to return to those who sent me.

At the cliff’s edge, I walked between the branching waters, my path now familiar even if the river surged behind me. I paused at Mara’s cottage, hoping to find her, but the door yawned empty, bread and water untouched on the table. Silence pooled thick in the air. I called out, but no answer came.

Further on, an old man sat weeping in the sand.

"Why do you weep?" I asked.

"They deny me death," he said, voice cracked like drought-stricken earth. "The Lady of the House refuses me. Plead for me, stranger."

"She refuses none who are ready," I said.

"Ready?" He scoffed. "You’re too young to understand."

"Age isn’t the measure," I replied. "You must *want* to live before you can die rightly. Go to Mara’s cottage. Eat her bread, drink her water. Wait for her. She’ll teach you."

He wept harder, deaf to counsel. Mara would find him.

The moon had not yet risen when I passed the lair of the horde. Only stillness answered—until I crossed. Then, a distant roar of water, a shriek at my back. I didn’t turn.

Cold seeped into my bones as I reached the House of Death, a darkness so thick it clung like frost. But Eve’s candle glowed in the window, a beacon. The door stood open. Inside, emptiness.

Loneliness swallowed me. Thousands slept nearby, yet I was utterly alone. They lived; I was dead—but not dead enough to bridge the gulf. The silence gnawed.

Then—movement. A whisper not of sound but of the air itself trembling. The coffin-door creaked open.

Lona’s face emerged, pale as moonlit marble, eyes closed yet fixed on me.

"Come, king," she murmured, voice like wind through dry grass. "I can’t rest without you. The river waits."

"My love!" I cried, gathering her icy form. "I thought you lost."

"Carry me back," she sighed. "The air hurts."

I laid her on her couch. "Sleep. I’ll be here when you wake."

She was gone before I finished. I collapsed onto my own bed, aching for the cold to still my heart.

Then—light. Adam stood over me.

"You didn’t wait," he chided gently.

"My child couldn’t sleep," I said.

"She never woke," he corrected. "You held her dream."

I tried to rise, but my limbs were stone.

"Rest," Eve’s voice floated from the dark. "You’re home."

Mara brought bread and wine. Adam breathed warmth into my chest.

"Your mother lies beside you," he said, candlelight brushing her face.

"She’s younger," I whispered.

"All things return," he said. "Even Lilith begins to fade."

They dressed me in the white shroud. The cold no longer bit.

Around me, the sleepers’ faces glowed faint in the dark. Then—I was among them.

And knew nothing more.

I awoke—or perhaps I did not. Awareness crept over me like the slow unfurling of dawn, but there was no warmth to greet it, only the infinite embrace of cold. A cold so deep it was not absence, but presence—a stillness that cradled me, a silence that hummed with unspoken promises.

I had known warmth once, but memory eluded me. The cold had soothed, had dissolved pain into nothingness, had comforted sorrow until sorrow itself was forgotten. Comfort? No. Something greater. Sorrow had been swallowed whole by the approach of life, a life that would restore all things a hundredfold.

I lay at peace, breathing in the damp earth-scent of a world waiting beneath frost. The souls of primroses, daisies, snowdrops—patient, unyielding—lingered beneath the soil, dreaming of spring. And I dreamed with them.

Dreams came like shifting tides.

I was naked upon a mountain peak, the white mist below heaving like a restless sea. The moon hung in the air beside me, and beyond it, the vast indifferent sky. I was Adam, waiting for the breath of life.

I was a child cradled against a radiant figure, white as untouched snow.

I was a rider on a pale horse, leaping from cloud to cloud across a blue eternity.

Centuries passed—or was it moments? Time had no claim here. I existed in thought, beyond dimensions, beyond senses. I was where I had always been: in the heart of something vast, something unnamed.

Then—betrayal.

I sat up. The moon peered through the crypt-like windows of the death chamber, casting long, solemn light over empty couches.

They were gone.

My dead—my beloved vanished ones—had left me behind.

Panic seized me. Had they ever been real? Or were they phantoms, figments of a mind desperate for companionship? The walls mocked me. The silence accused.

I fled.

Outside, the world was a contradiction—alive yet lifeless. A shimmering lake reflected a sky where no moon lingered. Torrents of water carved through the land, singing a song of freedom. I had done this. I had set these waters loose.

But to what end?

A hand touched my shoulder.

I turned.

Adam stood before me, radiant, ageless. His voice was the whisper of creation itself.

*“You are still dreaming,”* he said.

“How can I know?” I begged. “How can I tell dream from truth?”

His smile held the weight of eternity. *“You cannot. Not yet. But the truth will come, and when it does, doubt will be impossible.”*

Before I could plead further, he was gone.

I wandered, lost, until I stood at the edge of a yawning pit—a grave without end. In dreams past, a fall had always woken me.

So I fell.

Darkness.

Then—light.

I stood in the wooden chamber of the mirror, the place where worlds crossed. Despair crushed me. Lona was beyond reach, separated by gulfs of time and existence. I had fled my dream, abandoned her, and now the door was closed to me.

I was awake. And she was not.

I would find her again. Even if it took death. Even if it took begging the universe itself.

I would not rest until I held her—or dissolved into nothingness trying.

The fourth night, I slipped into what felt like sleep—only to wake into true awakening. My eyes opened upon absolute darkness, yet I knew with certainty: I lay in the house of death. Every moment since my arrival had been a dream, and now, at last, I was *alive*.

*"Finally,"* my heart whispered, and it surged with joy.

I turned—and there stood Lona, waiting for me. I had never lost her, only the sight of her. She needed no mourning. Though the room was black, *she* was not. Light shimmered from her eyes, radiant as the Mother’s own, and her face glowed with the same unearthly luminescence. Her death-garment, now luminous with resurrection, gleamed like fresh snow under the sun. The girl who had fallen asleep had awakened a woman, brimming with the essence of life itself.

I pulled her into my arms. *This* was reality.

*"I woke first,"* she murmured, smiling as if the thought still astonished her.

*"You did,"* I said. *"And woke me with you."*

*"I only watched you,"* she replied, *"and waited."*

A candle floated toward us through the dark, and moments later, Adam, Eve, and Mara joined us. Their greeting was serene, as though such awakenings were ordinary.

*"I trust the darkness treated you kindly?"* Eve asked.

*"Not entirely,"* I admitted. *"But the waking makes it worth every shadow."*

*"This is only the beginning,"* she said. *"You are barely roused."*

Adam’s voice was warm. *"He is clothed in Death—which is but Life’s brightest garment."* He embraced Lona, clasped my shoulder, studied the slumbering princess, and gave the leopardess an affectionate pat. *"I expect we’ll meet you both again soon."*

*"Must we die once more?"* I asked.

*"No,"* he said, echoing Eve’s smile. *"You’ve died into life. Now you need only *live*—fully, fiercely. The more you live, the stronger you become."*

*"The will alone summons the strength,"* Eve said. *"Pure life knows no weariness."*

Mara kissed my brow. *"Did I not promise all would be well? Tell others this: *What will be well, is already well.*"* She sighed—not in sorrow, but as though she knew few would believe it. *"Now you know me,"* she finished, her smile luminous.

*"I do,"* I said. *"You are the voice that cried in the wilderness before the dawn. The shepherd who drives the lost sheep home before night falls."*

*"One day, my work will end,"* she said softly. *"Then I shall rejoice with the great Shepherd who sent me."*

Adam’s voice cut through like dawn. *"All night long, the morning lingers near."*

A sound like beating wings shuddered through the air.

*"The Shadow stirs,"* Adam said. *"He claims one here as his own—but what is ours can never be his again."*

I turned toward my parents’ resting places—only to find their couches empty, claimed instead by the Little Ones. A pang of that old, desolate dream struck me, and I flinched.

Lona touched my arm. *"What troubles you?"*

*"Their absence,"* I whispered.

*"They left long ago,"* Adam said. *"They kissed you as they departed and whispered, *‘Come soon.’*"*

*"How could you, lost in that hollow house of yours?"* He turned to Lona. *"Your parents, child, will come for *you.*"*

The time had come. Lona pressed a kiss to her murderous mother’s brow, then embraced her father.

*"That kiss will call her home,"* Adam said.

*"Who were her parents?"* Lona asked.

*"My father is hers as well,"* Adam answered.

We knelt then, thanking them—for death, for life. Their breath swept over us like a blessing.

A sound like thunderous wings filled the air.

*"The sun comes,"* Adam said.

And I heard it—the roar of a star, the crescendo of creation itself. The three exchanged a glance, and their smiles became a three-petaled flower of light, drifting upward in silent praise.

Then—they *changed.*

Adam and Eve stood before me as angels of resurrection, Mara as the Magdalene at the empty tomb. Adam’s face burned like lightning; Eve’s napkin scattered brilliance like sparks. A wind rose, pulsing like a dying beast’s breath.

*"You hear *his* wings now,"* Adam said—not the sun’s, but the Shadow’s. *"He is restless, for he carries his own torment within."*

*"Is there nothing deeper in him?"* I asked.

*"A shadow cannot exist without substance,"* Adam said. *"Nor substance without light behind it."*

Then—a sound. Distant, golden, triumphant.

*"The cock crows!"* Adam cried. *"After eons of silence, he *sings!*"*

And I heard it—the defiance of death, the herald of dawn. The chaos yearning for kingdom.

A great flapping echoed.

*"The black bat flees,"* Mara said.

*"Amen, golden cock!"* Adam shouted, and his voice shook the house.

At that word, the Little Ones sprang upright, blinking. *"Crow! Crow again!"* they cried, as if they’d seen the bird in dreams. They turned to their sleeping companions, kissed them tenderly, then bounded from their beds—only to freeze at the sight of the princess, still slumbering.

Odu ran to the leopardess, stroking her head. *"Wake up!"* he pleaded. When she didn’t stir, his face crumpled. *"She’s cold!"*

*"She waits for the princess,"* Mara said gently.

Odu touched the princess’s cheek, then recoiled. *"She’s cold too! Why won’t they wake?"*

Adam knelt beside him. *"Her waking isn’t ripe. She must forget enough to remember. Then—she’ll rise."*

*"But the cock has *crowed!*"* Odu protested, shaking his friends. *"Peter! Crispy! Wake up!*" When they didn’t stir, tears spilled down his face.

*"Let them sleep,"* Adam said. *"Would you leave the princess alone when she wakes? They are happy. So is the leopardess."*

Odu wiped his eyes—a gesture both new and ancient.

Eve led us out. No farewells were spoken. The horse and elephants waited, but we walked, too buoyant to ride. They followed, and the world stretched before us, alive with dawn.

The darkness had lifted, replaced by a soft twilight that carried the breath of spring. The world had transformed—or perhaps it was we who had changed. Though the sky held no light to cast shadows, every blade of grass, every heather-bush, stood visible, as if illuminated from within or seen through eyes that now perceived deeper truths. The air hummed with an unseen radiance, and the boundaries between myself and the world dissolved.

I walked barefoot, each step a communion with the earth. The plants whispered their secrets—not in words, but in shapes and colors that revealed the essence of their being. I was no longer separate from them; their life flowed into mine, and mine into theirs. To know a thing was to understand its origin, its purpose, its home—to recognize that all existence was rooted in the same unfathomable source.

New senses awakened within me—faculties beyond description, for no language could capture their nature. My awareness expanded endlessly, an open vessel receiving the world’s wonders. When the breeze stirred the heather, its purple bells chimed, and I was the breeze, the sound, the joy of hearing it—all at once. I was an ocean of peace, yet alive with waves of eternal delight.

This was life as it was meant to be: pure, untainted bliss. Existence itself was a celebration. My beloved walked beside me, and together we journeyed toward the Father. The first dawn of our freedom had not yet broken, yet already we knew a joy beyond measure. What then would the fullness of eternity bring?

We passed the hollow where once the earth’s horrors had writhed—now a serene lake, its waters clear as crystal. Beneath the surface, illuminated by an eerie glow, lay the remnants of those grotesque forms: coiled, knotted, frozen in their malice. They did not stir, but they were not dead. So long as corruption lingered in the hearts of men, such abominations would endure.

Then came the herald of the sun—the dawn wind, soft yet triumphant. The sun surged above the horizon, a blazing ember from the Father’s altar, scattering light like a sacrifice of molten gold. The flowers straightened, lifting their faces in expectation. Something greater than the sun approached, something beyond time. It did not matter how long the wait—Love himself would come, and all creation strained to see him.

The morning was a resurrection. The children danced ahead; beasts followed. Butterflies and dragonflies wove through the air, a living storm of color and light. This was summer perfected, an earth reborn. The rocks drank the sunlight; the world itself seemed to exhale life. Lona and I burned with quiet joy, our desires rising like incense.

The once-dry riverbeds now surged with water, singing over stones. We walked alongside the torrent, not crossing, but ascending toward the great cataract. There, the desert had vanished—replaced by lush plains and deep, silent rivers. The Little Ones plunged into the water, laughing, and the beasts followed. Forests had sprung from the barrenness, their canopies alive with birdsong.

We climbed higher, the land growing ever richer. The trees towered, their branches forming a cathedral of leaves. The children scrambled into the heights, calling to the unseen elephants below, their voices weaving a dialogue only Lona fully understood. Squirrels led them in playful chases; birds alighted on their shoulders, sang prophecies, and vanished.

Yet no monkeys appeared. The woods held only beauty, only peace.

We were going home.

Lona and I walked beneath the canopy of leaves when a sudden shout echoed from above. Moments later, the Little Ones came tumbling down from the branches, their eyes alight with wonder.

"We saw something!" they cried. "Far across the plain, where the mountain scrapes the sky, there’s a city—or maybe a castle—hidden in the clouds!"

I climbed to see for myself. Through the shifting blue mist, I glimpsed towering spires and jagged cliffs, their edges blurred where stone met sky. The city seemed alive, breathing with the wind, its highest towers swallowed by the heavens.

We pressed onward, the river beside us growing clearer, purer—as if the water itself had awakened. The Little Ones raced ahead, their laughter ringing like silver.

Then the storm came.

A flash tore through the clouds, and for an instant, the world stood still. The Little Ones gasped.

"Did you see?" one whispered.

"I saw him," murmured another.

"What did he say?"

A small voice—Luva’s—answered in delight: *"He said, 'All of you are mine—come along!'"*

I had seen only lightning, but the children heard a voice. Lona, too, nodded as if she understood.

Another burst of light—this time, faces flickered within the glow, radiant and fleeting. A third strike, and the air itself shimmered with figures—horses, lions, birds of impossible colors, their wings catching fire from the sky.

Lona laughed. "Serpents turning into birds! Why did I ever mourn the past?"

Rain fell, soft and glistening, painting the air with rainbows. The river swelled, its current smooth and strong, its bed lined with flowers that glowed beneath the surface.

At last, we reached the mountain’s foot. A grand stairway of green serpentine and deep red porphyry led up to the city gates. The Little Ones dashed ahead, vanishing into the arms of waiting angels—beings of light who welcomed them with laughter.

A towering angel, his armor flashing like contained lightning, turned to us. **"Welcome home,"** he said, and his voice was warmth itself.

Beyond the gates, the city unfolded—a labyrinth of living stone, each gem and pillar humming with unseen life. The river roared from the heights, cascading down steps carved into the mountain’s heart.

At the summit, the clouds parted just enough to reveal a vast throne—empty, yet waiting. The river gushed from beneath it, endless and new.

The angel could go no further. **"This climb is yours alone,"** he said.

Hand in hand, Lona and I began the ascent. The rocks were steep, the air thin. But as we neared the clouds, a hand—firm and gentle—took mine and guided me forward. A door opened in the mist.

I turned to see Lona, but the door closed softly behind me.

Silence.

I stood alone—in my library.

I still have not found Lona, but Mara walks beside me often. She has been both teacher and guide, unraveling mysteries I had not even known were knotted within me. Yet sometimes, I wonder—was that last awakening just another layer of the dream? Am I still trapped in the chamber of death, caught between sleep and waking, not yet ready to rise? Or did I never truly surrender to slumber, and so emerge half-formed, too soon?

If that waking was itself a dream, then it must have been a promise—a glimpse of a dawn yet to come. Surely such a vision could not be mere deception. Could even God craft illusions so radiant?

*"Then where did the dream begin?"* Hope whispers.

*"From the depths of my own shadow,"* I reply.

*"And who first placed it there?"* Hope counters. *"Was it your mind that conjured it? The fever in your veins that shaped it?"*

*"Or,"* she continues, *"was your mind only the instrument, and the fever the hand that played it? But who carved the instrument? Who drew the bow?"*

Man dreams. God breathes life into the dream. When a man dreams his own desires, he is at their mercy; when the dream is given to him, it is a covenant—a thing that will be fulfilled.

I have not sought the mirror again. The hand that cast me back was clear: that door is closed to me now. *"All the days of my appointed time, I will wait until my change comes."*

Sometimes, when I walk among my books, they ripple like water, as though some unseen wind stirs them—as though another world presses against the edges of this one. Other times, beneath the open sky, the earth itself shudders, the trees trembling as if they might dissolve. But then the moment passes, and all is solid again.

There are whispers, too—voices I almost recognize, murmuring just beyond hearing. When I strain to listen, silence returns. I do not know if they rise from within me or drift in from some unseen place. I do not chase them. They come; I let them pass.

Fragments of memory flicker at the edges of my thoughts—faces half-recalled, moments that refuse to take shape. Yet I do not dream, not as I once did. Or perhaps I am dreaming now, more deeply than ever before.

But when I wake at last—when I step into that greater life which cradles this one like a child—I will know. There will be no doubt then.

Until then, I wait. Asleep or awake, I wait.

Novalis once wrote: *"Our life is no dream, but it should—and perhaps will—become one."*

And so the horizon stretches, endless.
