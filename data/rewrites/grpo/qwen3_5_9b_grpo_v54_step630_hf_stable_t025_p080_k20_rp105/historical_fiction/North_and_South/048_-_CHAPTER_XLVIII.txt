"My own, my father's friend!
I cannot part with thee!
I ne'er have shown, thou ne'er hast known,
How dear thou art to me."

The social gatherings hosted by Mrs. Lennox were constructed with a specific alchemy: her friends supplied the beauty, Captain Lennox provided the effortless mastery of current topics, and Mr. Henry Lennox, accompanied by a select circle of rising young men, contributed wit and sharp intellect, all deployed with such finesse that they appeared spontaneous rather than calculated. These dinners were undeniably delightful spectacles. Yet, even within this glittering arena, Margaret's dissatisfaction remained an uninvited guest. She observed that every talent, every sentiment, and even every inclination toward virtue was consumed as fuel for fireworks; the sacred, hidden fire of the soul was reduced to mere sparkle and crackle. They discussed art through a purely sensuous lens, fixated on surface effects rather than seeking deeper truths. They fanned themselves into artificial enthusiasm about lofty subjects in company, only to let those thoughts wither when alone, squandering their capacity for genuine appreciation into a fluent stream of empty words.

One evening, after the gentlemen had retired to the drawing-room, Mr. Lennox approached Margaret. It was nearly the first voluntary word he had spoken to her since her return to Harley Street.

"You did not look pleased at what Shirley was saying at dinner," he observed.

"Didn't I? My face must be remarkably expressive," Margaret replied.

"It always has been. It has not lost the art of eloquence."

"I did not like," she said quickly, "his manner of advocating what he knew to be wrong—so glaringly wrong, even in jest."

"But it was exceedingly clever. How every word landed! Did you recall the apt epithets?"

"Yes."

"And yet you would like to despise them. Please do not hesitate, though he is my friend."

"There! That is precisely the tone in you, that—" she broke off abruptly.

He paused, listening to see if she would complete her thought. She only flushed deeply and turned away. Before she could withdraw completely, she heard him speak in a low, clear voice:

"If my tones, or my modes of thought, are what you dislike, will you grant me the justice of telling me so? And thus give me the opportunity to learn how to please you."

For weeks, there had been no news of Mr. Bell's departure for Milton. He had mentioned it at Helstone as a potential journey in the near future, yet he must have handled his affairs by correspondence. Margaret reasoned that if he could, he would avoid traveling to a place he disliked and one he would struggle to comprehend, especially regarding the secret significance she attached to an explanation that demanded the intimacy of speech. She knew he felt the necessity of it, yet whether in summer, autumn, or winter, the timing mattered little. It was August now, and there had been no mention of the Spanish voyage he had hinted at with Edith. Margaret tried to reconcile herself to the fading of this illusion.

Then, one morning, a letter arrived. It stated that next week he intended to come to town to discuss a plan forming in his mind; furthermore, he planned to indulge in some medical treatment, having begun to agree with her view that it was preferable to attribute his irritability to his health rather than his character. There was a tone of forced cheerfulness in the letter, which Margaret noted only in retrospect. At the time, her attention was captured by Edith's exclamations.

"Coming up to town! Oh dear! And I am so exhausted by the heat that I doubt I have the strength for another dinner. Besides, everyone has left except our dear, stupid selves, who cannot decide where to go. There would be no one to meet him."

"I am certain he would much prefer dining with us alone rather than with the most agreeable strangers you could assemble. Moreover, if he is unwell, he will not seek invitations. I am glad he has finally admitted it. I was sure he was ill from the entire tone of his letters, yet he refused to answer me when I asked, leaving me with no third party to whom I could turn for news."

"Oh! He is not very ill, or he would not think of Spain."

"He never mentions Spain."

"No! But his proposed plan evidently concerns it. However, would you truly go in weather like this?"

"Oh, it will cool down daily. Yes! Think of it. I am only afraid I have thought and wished too much—in that absorbing, wilful way that is destined either to be disappointed or gratified to the letter, yielding no spiritual pleasure."

"But that is superstitious, I'm sure, Margaret."

"No, I do not think so. Only it ought to warn me and check me from yielding to such passionate wishes. It is a sort of 'Give me children, or else I die.' I fear my cry is, 'Let me go to Cadiz, or else I die.'"

"My dear Margaret! You will be persuaded to stay there; and then what shall I do? Oh! I wish I could find someone for you to marry here, so I could be sure of you!"

"I shall never marry."

"Nonsense, and double nonsense! Why, as Sholto says, you are such an attraction to the house that he knows many men who would gladly visit next year for your sake."

Margaret straightened herself haughtily. "Do you know, Edith, I sometimes think your life in Corfu has taught you just a shade or two of coarseness."

"Well!"

Edith began to sob bitterly, declaring with such vehemence that Margaret had lost all love for her and no longer regarded her as a friend, that Margaret realized she had expressed too harsh an opinion to soothe her own wounded pride. She ended the day as Edith's slave, while the younger lady, overwhelmed by hurt feelings, lay like a victim on the sofa, heaving profound sighs until she finally fell asleep.

Mr. Bell did not appear on the day he had twice postponed his visit. The following morning, a letter from Wallis, his servant, arrived. It stated that his master had been unwell for some time—the true reason for delaying his journey—and that at the very moment he was scheduled to depart for London, he had suffered an apoplectic fit. Wallis added that the medical men believed he could not survive the night, and it was more than probable that by the time Miss Hale received this letter, her poor master would be no more.

Margaret received this letter at breakfast and turned pale as she read it. Silently, she handed it to Edith and left the room.

Edith was terribly shocked, crying in a sobbing, frightened, childish manner that distressed her husband greatly. Mrs. Shaw was eating breakfast in her own room, and the task of reconciling his wife to the terrifying proximity of death fell to him, for the first time in her life. Here was a man who was to have dined with them today, lying dead or dying instead! It took some time before she could think of Margaret. Then she sprang up and followed her upstairs into her room. Dixon was packing a few toiletries, and Margaret was hastily putting on her bonnet, weeping throughout, her hands trembling so violently she could barely tie the strings.

"Oh, dear Margaret! How shocking! What are you doing? Are you going out? Sholto would telegraph or do anything you like."

"I am going to Oxford. There is a train in half an hour. Dixon has offered to go with me, but I could have gone alone. I must see him again. Besides, he may be better and need care. He has been like a father to me. Do not stop me, Edith."

"But I must. Mamma will not like it at all. Come and ask her about it, Margaret. You do not know where you are going. I should not mind if he had his own house; but in his Fellow's rooms! Come to Mamma and ask her before you go. It will not take a minute."

Margaret yielded and missed her train. In the suddenness of the event, Mrs. Shaw became bewildered and hysterical, and the precious time slipped away. However, another train departed in a couple of hours. After various discussions regarding propriety and impropriety, it was decided that Captain Lennox should accompany Margaret, for the one thing she held constant was her resolution to go, alone or otherwise, by the next train, regardless of what was said about the step's propriety. Her father's friend, her own friend, was lying at the point of death; the thought of this struck her with such vividness that she was surprised at the firmness with which she asserted her right to independent action. Five minutes before departure, she found herself sitting in a railway carriage opposite Captain Lennox. It was always a comfort to her to think that she had gone, even if only to hear that he had died during the night. She saw the rooms he had occupied and associated them ever after most fondly in her memory with the idea of her father and his one cherished, faithful friend.

They had promised Edith before starting that, if all ended as they feared, they would return to dinner; thus, the long, lingering look around the room where her father had died had to be interrupted, replaced by a quiet farewell to the kind old face that had so often offered pleasant words and merry quips.

Captain Lennox fell asleep on their journey home, and Margaret could cry at leisure, reflecting on this fatal year and all the woes it had brought her. No sooner was she fully aware of one loss than another came—not to supersede her grief for the one before, but to reopen wounds and feelings scarcely healed. Yet, at the sound of the tender voices of her aunt and Edith, at the merry glee of little Sholto at her arrival, and at the sight of the well-lit rooms with their mistress, pale yet full of eager sorrowful interest, Margaret roused herself from her heavy trance of almost superstitious hopelessness. She began to feel that even around her, joy and gladness might gather. She claimed Edith's place on the sofa; Sholto was taught to carry Aunt Margaret's cup of tea with great care; and by the time she went up to dress, she could thank God for having spared her dear old friend a long or painful illness.
