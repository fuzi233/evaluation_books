The wintry hours in the plague-stricken valley where Romola had laboured for weeks stretched soft and still as frost settling on cypress boughs, as she lay resting her worn limbs. Her mind drifted back over all the roads that had led her there, and peered out across the unwritten stretch of the future, and every familiar shape of the past looked new, viewed from this quiet, unburdened vantage ground.

The experience that had begun the moment she woke adrift in a small boat on the Arno had sealed itself into her soul as deeply as fresh wax holds the stamp of a seal. Not long before, she had felt all bonds cut, no purpose left to hold her to the world, sinking into bitter, selfish grief that life had nothing left to give her. She had thought it no sin to whisper, “I am tired of life. I want to die.” That very thought had throbbed in her throat as she fell asleep lulled by the river’s current — but the second she woke to the cry of a drowning man, the call of unmet need, she had not paused to reason, as she once would have in Florence, that she was glad to live only to lighten other people’s sorrow. She had simply lived, moved by an urgent, unthinking impulse to share the lives of the people around her, to answer every call for help, to do the work that shouted out to be done until there was no room left for arguments over why she should keep breathing, keep enduring, keep labouring.

It was a new baptism for Romola. Back in Florence, the simple, sacred ties between one human being and another had been tangled up for her with the complicated weights of a false marriage, the fickle demands of the city’s state, and the fervid tensions of discipleship to Fra Girolamo. When all those ties had broken one by one and betrayed her trust, the shock had knocked her loose from the whole of life, and numbed her sympathies. Now, she thought with sharp, burning shame: it was nothing but base cowardice in me to wish for death. If every other thing I once believed in is doubtful, this suffering I can ease is certain. If the glory of the cross that Fra Girolamo preached is an illusion, the sorrow of these people is only the truer. While my arm still has strength to lift, I will stretch it out to the fainting. While my eyes can still see, they will search out the forsaken.

Then the past rose up to her with a new, soft, unignorable call. Her work in the green valley was done, and all the love and care that had been tied up in the villagers’ lives rushed back to fill the old, deep, familiar channels of duty and affection she had thought she abandoned forever. That rare, clear moment of self-reflection that comes when you are cut completely loose from every old habit and old company let her judge herself as she never had before. The compulsion of her sympathetic heart, always quick to imagine the pain of others, stirred in her stronger every hour. She questioned every conclusion she had drawn, every choice she had made back then: she had been rash, arrogant, forever bitter that no one around her was good enough to meet her standards, while she herself had betrayed the very best her soul had once known to be right. She began to condemn her flight from Florence. It had been nothing but cowardly self-preservation, after all. The reasons Fra Girolamo had once dragged her back from running away had been truer, deeper than the reasons she had found to flee a second time. If she could feel the urgent needs of these valley strangers so keenly, how could she ignore the needs of the people who were nearest to her in the whole world?

Then the tide turned, and she pushed back against that sharp self-reproach. The memory of her years with Tito rose up, all those impossible conditions that kept any real union between them out of reach, even while their public marriage bound her to a thousand false “duties” that amounted to nothing more than hiding and sanctioning the very sins her soul revolted against. That flight, she told herself, had been her only possible way out. No person who feels things deeply, who has not been dulled by thick insensibility, can avoid this constant back-and-forth conflict when the tangled, twisted conditions of life have made it impossible to keep a bond you once swore to honour. There is no replacing those old ties. The new good you do never erases the fact that the old one broke, that life has been maimed, that a perfection you once hoped for is gone forever. Until the scars over those old wounds have healed completely, conscience will keep casting those doubtful, longing glances backward.

Romola shrank in dread at the thought of being close to Tito again — but she was ill at ease, too, that she had deliberately put herself out of reach of ever knowing his fate. She was tormented by the thought that one day soon he might be lost in terrible misery, crying out for help, and she would not be there. That thin, stinging thread of pain still wove through her heart, bearing out Fra Girolamo’s old words: she could not cease to be a wife. Nothing that had once mingled itself with the current of her heart’s blood could ever be completely erased for her.

Florence, and every small detail of her old life there, called to her like hunger. Her feelings did not wander after vague, impossible dreams: every living part of them was fed on the memory of familiar things — the smell of her blind father’s old manuscript library, the boom of the Duomo’s bells across the Arno, the narrow stone alleys where the vendors called out their wares at dawn. The thought that she had left all of that behind forever grew more and more insistent every hour she was not busy tending to the villagers. What if Fra Girolamo was not perfect? What if the whole life of Florence was nothing but a tangled web of inconsistencies? Had she been so high and mighty that she could shake the dust of the city off her feet and declare, “This world is not good enough for me”? If she had truly been that much better than the people she left behind, she would never have lost all her trust so easily. The bitter grief she had carried for her godfather no longer burned hot and consuming, and her debt of gratitude to Savonarola rose up to take its place. Nothing that had happened, or could ever happen, could erase the fact that the great fire of inspiration in his preaching had woken a whole new life in her. No one else she had ever met deserved that same gratitude from her. His mistakes, his flaws — might they not bring terrible calamities down on the city?

She could not rest. She hardly knew if it was her strength returning with the first budding leaves on the fig trees that made her restless, or if it was her eager, gnawing longing to get closer to Florence. She did not dare imagine walking through the city gates just yet, but the urge to get near enough to learn what was happening to the people she loved swelled so strong in her that it pushed all other plans out of her head.

One bright March morning, all the villagers gathered in the square to see their blessed lady depart. Sturdy Jacopo, the mountain man who had fished her half-dead from the river, had fetched a sure-footed mule for her, and would guide her over the slopes of the hill of Bogoli — ground we have seen on earlier journeys through these hills — to the nearest market town. The old Padre would walk with them too, to help her find the safest path onward to Pistoja. The small store of trinkets and gold she had brought with her to the valley had never been touched, and was more than enough to cover her needs for the journey.

If Romola had not been so pulled by the sharp, hungry longing drawing her onward, that last walk down the village street would have torn her heart in two. The Padre and Jacopo waited by the well with the mule, but her steps were slowed by the wailing villagers, who knelt in the mud to kiss her hands, clung to the hem of her grey wool gown to kiss its worn folds, crying, “Ah, why do you leave us now, when the good warm season is coming, and the crops will grow fat and plentiful? Why do you go?”

“Do not grieve,” Romola told them, her voice thick. “You are all well now, and I will never forget you. I must go and see if my own people are in need of me.”

“Ah, yes! If they have the pestilence, too!” one old woman called out.
“Come back to see us again, Madonna!” a little girl shouted.
“Yes, yes, we will look after little Benedetto, we promise!” the whole crowd chorused.

At last Romola mounted the mule — but little Benedetto, the orphan boy she had nursed back to health, set up such a vigorous scream of protest when he saw her turn away from him that the whole crowd followed along, insisting he must ride perched on the mule’s neck all the way down to the foot of the slope. The final parting could not be put off forever, but Romola turned again and again, long after the path curved upward, to wave her hand. Long after she could hear their voices no more, she could still see that small, loving flock of villagers lingering on the green valley floor, waiting to catch the last flash of her hand, before she turned her face fully toward the distant smoke of Florence, over the familiar Bogoli heights.
