The frost of the quarrel melted faster than the snows of winter. Laura, a woman of quick forgiveness, sighed and let the matter slide, while Miss Blanche, whose grudges were as fleeting as a summer shower, found the accusation of wickedness oddly flattering rather than stinging. Vanity, after all, rarely bruises under charges of moral frailty; Blanche was merely pleased by her friend's theatrical indignation, a performance that required no real cause. Thus, Laura confessed that the romantic chapter of their early friendship had closed, leaving Blanche worthy only of ordinary regard.

Blanche, however, was a poet at heart. Instantly composing verses of "desertion" and "disenchantment," she lamented the coldness of love and the neglect of fidelity. Just as her sorrow needed an outlet, new neighbors arrived from London, bringing daughters who became her eternal sisters. With them, Blanche shared her woes. Meanwhile, the tall footmen ceased delivering notes to Laura, and the pony-carriage rarely ventured to Fairoaks. When Laura finally visited Blanche, the latter adopted a look of suffering martyrdom, which Laura, in turn, laughed at with a good-humored disrespect.

Yet, if Blanche found consolation in female friendship, she discovered others of the fair sex who offered her sympathy. Whenever she encountered a young man—even for ten minutes in a garden or a drawing-room window—she would deploy her beautiful eyes, speak in tones of tender appeal, and retreat to repeat the drama for her next suitor. At Clavering Park, when the house was quiet, Pen enjoyed the monopoly of her glances. The local county, however, was sparse in eligible young men; the nearest rivals were a curate or two and a rustic squire with ill-made clothes. The local dragoons, belonging to the Baronet's own regiment, were off-limits due to past squabbles over horses, accounts, blind-Hookey, and white feathers.

But the autumn session and the London season changed things. Families from the counties descended upon Baymouth and filled Mr. Bingley's theatre at Chatteris. The old, shrewish families of Drummington and Tozely Park, previously shy of the Claverings, suddenly took notice. Tales circulated wildly about Sir Francis, his wife, and especially Miss Amory and Captain Strong. Then came the heavy hitters: the Earl of Trehawk and the Countess Dowager of Rockminster. Their arrival in state triggered a cascade of carriages, filling the avenues of Clavering Park.

With the house crowded, Mirobolant, the valet, found distraction in his duties, forgetting the pangs of love. Footmen were too busy to ferry notes to Fairoaks. Blanche wandered new paths, leaving the riverside where Pen usually fished. Pen, however, remained by the stream, whipping it with empty lines. The "fish" refused to bite, and the "Peri"—Miss Amory—did not appear.

Recalling his old haunt, a hollow tree where he once wooed Miss Fotheringay, Pen transformed it into a post-office. Beneath moss and stone, he hid little poems addressed to an "Undine." Occasionally, a reply arrived on pink-scented paper, a flower, or a faint word of acknowledgment. These exchanges were playful, half-jocular, half-sympathetic. But when the great folks invaded Clavering Park, the routine broke. The white paper stayed buried under the stone; the pink notes ceased. The tree reflected in the stream as the Brawl rolled by. Pen, baulked in his game, grew serious. He demanded an explanation.

He found the Hall full of visitors, or Miss Amory absent at a ball, or walking in state accompanied by the Bishop and Bishopess of Chatteris, who scowled at him with dignified disapproval. The Right Reverend Prelate remembered the little transaction in the Dean's garden. "You're a sad young man," Lady Clavering whispered kindly. "How is your dear Mar?" Pen, vexed by the narrowness of his means and the humiliations of country life, tried to catch her eye.

"You never come to the river now," he said, trying to be poetic.
"I can't," Blanche replied. "The house is full of people."
"Undine has left the stream," Pen pressed.
"She never ought to have gone there," Miss Amory answered firmly. "It was only play... Besides, you have other consolations at home." She dropped her eyes, offering a final chance. Yet, thinking of Laura's purity and his mother's hopes, Pen hesitated. "Blanche!" he started, then stopped as she fled back to the company, leaving him biting his nails in the moonlight.

Later, Laura sat with Mr. Pynsent, Lord Rockminster's son, exchanging a sharp, impertinent nod typical of Oxford men. Pynsent, a tall, gaunt dandy with red whiskers, discussed Laura's estates with amused curiosity. When Laura jokingly revealed they owned "nine hens and a cock, a pig, and an old pointer," Pynsent laughed, mistaking her for a country gentlewoman unaware of the city's ways. Soon, Blanche joined them, singing a duet that drew Pynsent away, though he later called her a "lean, scraggy humbug" to a friend.

Two days later, Pen returned from a fruitless fishing trip to find Pynsent and his companion, Mr. Wagg, in his mother's drawing-room. Pynsent, striding over chairs, discoursed on his travels and political ambitions. Wagg, a man of flamboyant wit and poor taste, observed the domestic details with a butcher's eye: the old gardener John, the clothes drying on gooseberry bushes, the ladies' clogs, and the widow's miniature portrait. Wagg mocked Pen's apparent poverty, noting the "thick bread-and-butter" served at six, while Pynsent remained oblivious, treating the situation with polite indifference. Wagg, however, relished the tale at dinner, describing the "old man with a beak" and the "sarving maid," much to Blanche's delight and Pynsent's growing disgust.

Back at the house, the atmosphere shifted. Laura, observing Pen's listlessness and his failure to engage with the local captains or even the new arrivals, grew concerned. "He wastes his life here," she told her mother, Helen. "He is not clever enough for us. He ought to go away." Helen, realizing Pen's unhappiness, agreed. After much deliberation, they decided to give him £200 to travel to London and study law. When they revealed this plan to Pen, he was transformed. He embraced his mother with fervor, tears in his eyes, declaring his resolve to redeem his past and honor them. He sang and laughed, happier than she had seen him since his university days.

Meanwhile, Laura was visiting the Dowager Countess of Rockminster at Baymouth, preparing for a ball. Pen, delighted by the prospect of seeing her, arranged to ride over with Captain Strong. As they approached the Ballroom at the Baymouth Hotel, the room buzzed with aristocrats and commoners alike. Mirobolant, sporting a blue ribbon and three gold crosses, mingled with the crowd, while Colonel Altamont, a man of purple-dyed whiskers and immense jewels, nodded familiarly to the Chevalier.

The music swelled with "See the Conquering Hero Comes." The Dowager Countess, Mr. Pynsent, Sir Francis Clavering, and the rest of the elite filed in. Pen rushed through the crowd, bypassing Blanche, and seized Laura's hand. "God bless you!" he cried. "I must speak to you."
"Not for three dances, dear Pen," she smiled, pulling away gently.
He fell back, biting his nails in vexation, forgetting to salute Pynsent, as the ball spun on around them.
