“Surely whoever speaks to me in the right voice, him or her I shall follow. As the water follows the moon, silently, with fluid steps anywhere around the globe.” —WALT WHITMAN.

“Now my cousins are at Diplow,” Grandcourt said, “would you like to go there? Tomorrow? The carriage will come for Mrs. Davilow. You can tell me what you’d like done in the rooms. Things must be arranged properly while we’re away at Ryelands. And tomorrow is the only day.”

He sat sideways on a sofa in the drawing-room at Offendene, one arm draped over the back, the other tucked between his crossed knees—posture of a man deeply absorbed in watching the person beside him. Gwendolen, who had never cared much for needlework, had taken it up since her engagement, now holding a piece of white embroidery that, upon closer inspection, revealed many uneven stitches. For the past week or so, their days had mostly been spent riding together, but always a few hours remained for this quieter companionship—one she found surprisingly agreeable. She was content with Grandcourt. His answers to her eager questions about his travels and experiences were delivered with a calm ease that suited her well. From the start, she’d noticed he knew exactly what to say; and increasingly, she sensed not just that he wasn’t foolish, but that somehow he made her feel that folly belonged elsewhere—among those who did things he didn’t care to do. A man who seems to have had everything within reach possesses a quiet power to belittle others. And Grandcourt’s behavior as a lover had never overstepped the bounds of tender homage—soft as a breath of roses, leaving no trace but a quiet sense of pride. Once, yes, he had kissed not her cheek but the delicate skin just below her ear. Startled, she had jerked upright, heart pounding, making him rise too and murmur, “I beg your pardon—did I disturb you?”
“Oh, nothing,” she replied, trying to sound light, though afraid of herself. “It’s just that I don’t like being kissed there.” She sat again, laughing lightly, but beneath it all, her pulse fluttered with a vague unease. She could no longer treat him as she once had treated poor Rex—flaunting, indifferent. Her reaction had seemed almost flattering to him, and he had not repeated the gesture.

That day, rain kept them from riding. But in compensation, a parcel arrived from London, and Mrs. Davilow had just left after presenting the beautiful items—Grandcourt’s choice—scattered across the tables. Gwendolen, basking in the glow of her present life, let her hands fall into her lap and asked, with a teasing air,

“Why is tomorrow the only day?”

“Because the next day is the first meet with the hounds,” Grandcourt replied.

“And after that?”

“I’ll need to leave for two days—it’s tedious—but I’ll go one day and return the next.” He noticed a shift in her expression, and releasing his hand from under his knees, placed it gently over hers. “You mind my going?”

“It’s no use objecting,” she said, coldly. She fought hard against the urge to confess her suspicion—that he was going to see someone else. To speak plainly, without restraint, would have been both freeing and dangerous.

“Yes, it is,” he said, enfolding her hand. “I’ll delay my trip. And I’ll travel at night—so I’m only gone a single day.” He thought he understood the cause of this small flare-up, and she was especially captivating to him now.

“Then don’t delay,” she said, feeling a sudden surge of control. “Just travel at night.” It was a command, sharp and decisive—a tiny release of irritation.

“So you’ll go to Diplow tomorrow?”

“Oh, yes—if you wish,” she answered, her tone casual, almost dismissive. Her focus was elsewhere; she hadn’t even realized her hand was still in his.

“How you treat us poor men!” Grandcourt murmured, lowering his voice. “We always end up getting the short end.”

“_Are_ you?” she asked, looking at him more honestly than usual. She longed to believe this banter was true—her lover truly suffering. If so, then she, too, was justified. If she knew everything, Mrs. Glasher might seem more to blame than Grandcourt. “_Are_ you always getting the worst?”

“Yes,” he said, gazing into her eyes. “Are you as kind to me as I am to you?”

Gwendolen felt struck. She was aware of how much she had received—how much he had given—and her sense of power faded. She saw now that no matter how she looked, she couldn’t turn back. It was as if she had climbed into a chariot where another held the reins. And it was not in her nature to leap out before the world. She had not entered blindly; any words she spoke now would be an admission she had known better. Her right to question was gone. All she could do was adjust—so that the thorns of conscience wouldn’t wound her. With a quiet shiver, she shifted her inner stance. There had been a brief silence, during which she had not looked away. Then, breaking into a smile, she said:

“If I were as kind to you as you are to me, it would ruin your generosity. It wouldn’t be as great as it is now—and it *is* great.”

“Then I may not ask for one kiss?” Grandcourt asked, pleased to pay such a high price for this new form of affection—this love that led quietly toward marriage.

“Not one?” she teased, nodding at him with defiance.

He lifted her small left hand to his lips, then released it with respect. To say he was not repulsive was faint praise—he was nearly charming. And in that moment, she felt sure she could never love another man as deeply as this one. His reserve stirred something inexplicable, delightful within her.

“Apropos,” she said, picking up her work again, “is anyone besides Captain and Mrs. Torrington at Diplow? Or are they alone? I suppose he talks in cigars, and she replies with her chignon.”

“She has a sister,” Grandcourt said, smiling faintly. “And two men besides—one you know, I think.”

“Oh,” she said, shaking her head. “Then I have a low opinion of him.”

“You saw him at Leubronn—young Deronda. A young fellow with the Mallingers.”

Gwendolen felt her heart give a sudden leap. Her fingers, clutching her embroidery, turned cold.

“I never spoke to him,” she said, careful not to betray herself. “Is he unpleasant?”

“No, not really,” Grandcourt replied, in his usual languid tone. “He thinks too highly of himself. I thought he’d been introduced to you.”

“No. Someone told me his name the evening before I left. That was all. What is he?”

“A sort of ward of Sir Hugo Mallinger’s. Nothing important.”

“Oh, poor thing! How terribly sad for him!” she said, half-joking, without meaning to mock. “I wonder if the rain has stopped,” she added, rising to look out the window.

Fortunately, it had cleared by the next day, and Gwendolen rode to Diplow on Criterion—the same horse she’d ridden when returning with her mother in the carriage. She always felt bolder in riding dress, and liked to believe she looked her best in it—a comforting assurance when facing something daunting. Her anger toward Deronda had softened into a strange dread—perhaps because of the way he had influenced her thoughts—fearing that his first intrusion into her life might foreshadow a deeper influence. Such feelings often birth superstitions: intense emotions about ourselves that make the stars seem to warn us, or a beggar’s blessing feel like prophecy. And superstitions bring consequences that often confirm their fears or hopes.

Before luncheon, Gwendolen toured the rooms with Mrs. Torrington and Mrs. Davilow. She assumed, if she saw Deronda, there would be little more than a polite bow between them. She meant to ignore him completely.

Yet, despite herself, she found she was drawn to him. From the moment they were in the same room, she felt herself doing nothing but notice him—everything else felt automatic, routine.

At lunch, Grandcourt said, “Deronda, Miss Harleth tells me you weren’t introduced to her at Leubronn?”

“Miss Harleth hardly remembers me,” Deronda replied, looking at her simply as they bowed. “She was very absorbed when I saw her.”

Had he known she suspected him of retrieving her necklace?

“On the contrary,” Gwendolen said, nerves prickling but her composure intact. “I remember you well. You disapproved of my playing roulette.”

“How did you come to that conclusion?” he asked, gravely.

“Oh, you cast an evil eye on my game,” she said, turning her head with a smile. “I started losing the moment you came near. Before that, I’d been winning.”

“Roulette in a place like Leubronn is sheer boredom,” Grandcourt remarked.

“_I_ found it boring when I began to lose,” Gwendolen said, glancing at him, then quickly at Deronda. She saw his eyes fixed on her—not with mockery, but with a depth that pierced her more than Klesmer’s criticism ever had. She turned her head sharply as if listening to others, though she was thinking only of him. His face carried that unsettling quality—something that challenged judgment, as if one’s own standards were flawed. (Who hasn’t met someone whose presence subtly undermines belief?) His voice, heard for the first time, contrasted sharply with Grandcourt’s flat drawl—like the deep tones of a cello compared to idle chatter in the sunlit afternoon. Perhaps Grandcourt was right in saying Deronda thought too much of himself—just a way of dismissing a superiority that humbles. But the conversation turned to rinderpest and Jamaica, and the subject of roulette passed.

Grandcourt declared the Jamaican negro a beastly sort of Caliban; Deronda said he always sympathized with Caliban, who naturally had his own view and could sing a good song. Mrs. Davilow mentioned her father had an estate in Barbados, though she’d never been to the West Indies. Mrs. Torrington insisted she’d never sleep among blacks. Her husband corrected her: “They’d be manageable enough if it weren’t for the half-breeds.” Deronda added, “The whites have only themselves to blame for the half-breeds.”

While these polite exchanges flowed, Gwendolen played with her jelly, watching each speaker so she could feel safe looking at Deronda.

“What does he really think of me?” she wondered. “He must have been interested—I mean, why else would he send back my necklace? What does he think of my marriage? What makes him so serious? Why is he here?”

These questions swirled in her mind, born of an uneasy longing to be admired by him—unmixed admiration. This desire had begun with her resentment of his critical gaze. Why did she care so much about a man who was “nothing of consequence”? She had no time to find the answer—she was too caught up in caring.

Later, in the drawing-room, when Grandcourt stepped away, she walked unthinkingly to Deronda, who stood alone at a table, flipping through prints.

“Will you hunt tomorrow, Mr. Deronda?” she asked.

“Yes, I believe so.”

“You don’t mind hunting?”

“I find reasons to enjoy it. It’s a sin I’m prone to—when I can’t get boating or cricket.”

“Do you mind my hunting?” she asked, tilting her chin saucily.

“I have no right to object to anything you choose.”

“You thought you had a right to object to my gambling,” she persisted.

“I was sorry for it. I don’t recall telling you I objected,” he said, his gaze steady, large-eyed and grave—innocent of intent. His eyes had a peculiar power—dark yet gentle, conveying a special interest in whoever he looked at. They could easily inspire demands, as if kindness were a reason for excessive claims. And this effect was beginning to stir in Gwendolen.

“You stopped me from gambling again,” she said. But the moment she spoke, her cheeks flushed. Deronda blushed too, aware of the questionable freedom he’d taken with the necklace.

No further words could be exchanged. She turned to the window, ashamed of having said too much, yet strangely glad she had stepped into this quiet understanding. Deronda, too, welcomed it. Gwendolen seemed more compelling than before. And surely, changes had occurred in her since Leubりnn—the struggle of realizing her mistake had awakened something new, something more complex than her old confidence: a soul stirring, with both nobler and darker possibilities. Among the forces she now feared was something inside herself that disturbed peace.

That evening, Mrs. Davilow asked, “Was it really true, or just a joke, about Mr. Deronda spoiling your play, Gwen?”

Her curiosity was piqued, and she dared ask a question not involving Grandcourt.

“Oh, it just happened that he was watching when I started losing,” Gwendolen said casually. “I noticed him.”

“I wouldn’t be surprised,” Mrs. Davilow said. “He’s a striking young man. Reminds me of Italian paintings. One could guess, without being told, that he has foreign blood.”

“Does he?”

“Mrs. Torrington says so. I asked who he was, and she said his mother was some foreign noblewoman.”

“His mother?” Gwendolen asked sharply. “Then who was his father?”

“Well—everyone says he’s Sir Hugo Mallinger’s son, raised by him. Though he’s called a ward. She adds that if Sir Hugo had his way, he’d have left his estates to Mr. Deronda, since he has no legitimate heir.”

Gwendolen said nothing. But her mother saw the change in her face and regretted speaking. It seemed inappropriate for her daughter, whom she disliked calling worldly. And indeed, she wished she hadn’t known such things herself.

An image formed instantly in Gwendolen’s mind: the unknown mother—no doubt dark-eyed, perhaps sorrowful. Hardly any face could be less like Deronda’s than the portrait of Sir Hugo at Diplow. A dark-eyed woman, no longer young, became a figure in her conscience.

That night, lying in her dimly lit bed, she whispered,
“Mamma, do men usually have children before marriage?”

“No, dear,” Mrs. Davilow replied. “Why do you ask?”

“If it were true, I ought to know,” Gwendolen said, indignantly.

“You’re thinking of what I said about Mr. Deronda and Sir Hugo. That’s very unusual, dear.”

“Does Lady Mallinger know?”

“She knows enough to be satisfied. That’s clear, because Mr. Deronda has lived with them.”

“And people don’t think worse of him?”

“Well, of course he’s at a disadvantage. It’s not as if he were Lady Mallinger’s son. He doesn’t inherit, and isn’t influential. But people don’t need to know. He’s well received.”

“I wonder if he knows. And whether he’s angry with his father?”

“My dear, why would you think that?”

“Why?” Gwendolen asked, sitting up abruptly. “Don’t children have reason to resent their parents? How can they help their parents marrying or not marrying?”

But then a sudden realization struck her—she fell back onto her pillow. It wasn’t just the old fear of reproaching her mother for her second marriage. Now she felt something deeper: that her own marriage seemed tainted, forbidden—by the very story she’d just heard.

There was no more talk. Until sleep claimed her, Gwendolen wrestled with arguments against her marriage—arguments newly urgent, mirrored in the tale of a man whose fleeting connection with her had somehow rooted itself in her deepest feelings. Characteristically, she never questioned whether her marriage was indefensible because she’d chosen Grandcourt not for love, but for convenience—not as a duty-bound partner, but as a means to an end. Gwendolen’s ideas were painfully simple; yet many of life’s greatest dilemmas press upon us in our simplicity. And to judge wisely, perhaps we must understand how things appear to the unwise—because that appearance shapes most of history.

The next morning brought double excitement. She was going to hunt—once threatened by propriety, until Mrs. Torrington proved capable enough to ride with her—her first hunt since her escapade with Rex. And she would see Deronda again. Since the previous night, her interest in him had grown, and she expected to see something new in him—something she’d missed before.

What would he become? What life awaited him—though he was “nothing of consequence”? With only slight differences, he might have been as powerful as Grandcourt—even, her imagination ran, holding the very estates Grandcourt would inherit. Yet now, Deronda might someday see her mistress of Topping Abbey, bearing the title that should have been his wife’s. These futile speculations about what might have been marked a turning point for Gwendolen. She, who had always taken the best as her due, now saw her position differently—as a harsh, unjust exclusion of others. The news about Deronda seemed to group him with Mrs. Glasher and her children—before whom she now felt a need to apologize—she who had once been surrounded by people who owed her apologies.

Perhaps Deronda was thinking of these things too. Could he know of Mrs. Glasher? If he knew she knew, he’d despise her. But he couldn’t possibly. Would he despise her for marrying Grandcourt? His possible judgment weighed on her as heavily as Klesmer’s critique of her talent. Yet she found more resistance to disapproval of her marriage—because it’s easier to justify our actions than to impress others with our ability. “How can I help it?” is not our favorite excuse for failure. But Gwendolen found strength in saying:

“How can I help what others have done? Things wouldn’t be right if I turned back now and refused to marry Mr. Grandcourt.” And turning back was impossible. The horses in the chariot she’d boarded were racing forward.

This mood of youthful desperation returned like a tide. She could face anything ahead rather than retreat into shame. And it was oddly comforting to think that harm lay equally in either path. But the immediate joy was the hunt—where she would see Deronda, and he would see her. Always lurking behind other thoughts was the impression that he was deeply interested in her. Yet today, she resolved not to repeat yesterday’s mistake—no eager words, no seeking contact. The hunt would be absorbing enough.

And so it was—for a long while. Deronda was there, often within sight, but this only heightened the pleasure—something Gwendolen had tasted only once before, and which seemed to promise delight beyond any obstacle except the loss of riding itself. No accident brought them together. The run ended near home, and as the gray November afternoon settled into a soft yellow glow in the west, Gwendolen returned with the party from Diplow, heading toward Offendene. The thrill had passed, and she grew irritable—disappointed she’d had no chance to speak to Deronda, whom she wouldn’t see again, since he was leaving in two days. What would she say? She wasn’t sure. But she needed to speak to him.

Grandcourt rode beside her; Mrs. Torrington, her husband, and another gentleman ahead; and Deronda’s horse could be heard behind. The desire to talk to him—to hear him speak—was becoming overwhelming. Unless she simply asserted her will and defied convention, there would be no chance. Where Miss Gwendolen willed it, order must yield. They had just emerged from a pine and beech wood, where twilight pressed down heavily, increasing her impatience. The hoofbeats behind, growing louder, became an irritation. She reined in her horse and glanced back. Grandcourt paused after a few steps. But she waved her whip, nodded sideways with playful authority, and said:

“Go on! I want to speak to Mr. Deronda.”
