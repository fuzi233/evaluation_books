There is a peculiar alchemy in human spirits, a strange capacity for joy that defies the logic of circumstance. One might assume that a drizzling night, a perilous trek across the moors, and the grim business of hunting down lawbreakers would dampen the mood of any sensible man. Yet, as Mr. Helstone and Mr. Moore emerged from the mill-yard gates, leading their small retinue, they moved with a buoyancy that seemed entirely at odds with the weather. Lantern light flickered across their faces, illuminating a spark of lively defiance in Moore's dark eyes and a radiant, almost manic gleam in the rector's usually stern visage. To an observer from Stilbro' Moor, this pair would have been fair game for a shot from behind a hedge; they were walking straight into danger with the exhilaration of men who knew exactly what they were doing.

It is a well-known truth that a clergyman should be a man of peace, a gentle shepherd of souls, not a warrior clad in black. Yet, Mr. Helstone was neither diabolical nor devoid of principle. He was simply a man who had missed his true calling; he was born for the battlefield, but bound by duty to the pulpit. He was a creature of iron will, hard-handed and unyielding, a man of rigid conscience who could be cruel in his adherence to truth. He was loved by some and hated by many, a figure of such stark contrast that he seemed to embody the very friction of the age.

For his part, Mr. Moore was a man of equally hard lines, though of a different texture. A Whig by political necessity and a foreigner by birth, he possessed a bitterness that Helstone found both irritating and strangely compelling. As they rode side by side, the air between them crackled with the static of opposing ideologies. The war was the sole topic that united them, even as it drove them apart. Helstone, a high Tory, viewed the conflict as a sacred crusade; Moore, a man of pragmatic opposition, saw it as a futile struggle against an inevitable tide.

"The Peninsular news still holds your faith, I trust?" Moore asked, his voice cutting through the damp air.

Helstone's response was curt. "What do you mean?"

"Faith in the wooden-faced idol of England? Do you truly believe Wellington can send fire from heaven to consume the French holocaust?"

"I believe Wellington will flog Bonaparte's marshals into the sea whenever he chooses to lift his arm."

Moore scoffed, the sound sharp in the cold night. "My dear sir, you speak as if you are serious. Bonaparte commands an omnipotent spirit; your Wellington is a mere martinet, a dull-witted drover acting for a farmer no less dull. Against such genius, your 'good cause'—the restoration of a disgraced king—is a pathetic farce."

Helstone's temper flared. "Against legitimacy stands usurpation! Against modest, righteous resistance stands boastful, treacherous ambition! God defend the right!"

"God often defends the powerful," Moore countered calmly.

Helstone seized the biblical imagery with the fervor of a preacher. He spoke of the Israelites crossing the Red Sea, of the weak against the mighty, of the rod of Moses triumphing over the chariots of Pharaoh. "Crime and the lost archangel generalled the ranks of Pharaoh," he declared, his voice rising above the rain. "Which triumphed? We know the answer. The Lord saved Israel that day. The right hand of the Lord dashed in pieces the enemy!"

Moore listened, his expression unreadable, before offering his own twist on the metaphor. "France is Israel, and Napoleon is Moses. Europe, with her rotten dynasties, is corrupt Egypt. Gallant France is the Twelve Tribes, and her vigorous Usurper is the Shepherd of Horeb."

Helstone recoiled, unable to find a retort to such audacity. Moore pressed on, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "In Italy, he was a true Moses, a regenerator of nations. It puzzles me how the conqueror of Lodi could condescend to become an emperor—a vulgar humbug—and how a people who once dreamed of liberty could sink back into slavery. I despise France! If England had marched as far as France, she would not have retreated so shamelessly."

"You imply that imperial France is no worse than republican France?" Helstone demanded fiercely.

"I mean to say nothing," Moore replied smoothly, "but I can think what I please. About revolutions, regicides, the divine right of kings, and the sanity of war..."

His sentence was abruptly cut short by the thunderous roll of wheels and the sudden halt of a gig in the middle of the road. Both men had been so engrossed in their philosophical duel that they had failed to hear the approach until it was upon them.

"Nah, maister; did th' wagons hit home?" a voice called out from the vehicle.

"Can that be Joe Scott?" Helstone asked.

"Ay, ay!" confirmed a second voice. Inside the gig sat two figures, illuminated by a single lantern. "Ay, Mr. Moore, it's Joe Scott. I'm bringing him back in a bonny pickle. I found him on the top of the moor, him and three others. What will you give me for restoring him to you?"

"Why, my thanks, I believe," Moore replied graciously. "I could better have afforded to lose a better man. That is you, I suppose, Mr. Yorke, by your voice?"

"Ay, lad, it's me," came the reply. "I was coming home from Stilbro' market, and just as I got to the middle of the moor... I heard a groan. I pulled up. Some would have whipped on faster, but I've naught to fear. I don't believe there's a lad in these parts who would harm me. I asked what was wrong, and Joe said they were lying in a ditch, teed with a bit of bandage. So I got down, loosened 'em with my penknife, and Scott rides with me to tell me all how it happened."

"Well, I am greatly obliged to you, Mr. Yorke," Helstone said stiffly.

"Are you, my lad? You know you're not," Yorke laughed, a rough, hearty sound. "However, here are the rest approaching. And look, by the Lord, another set with lights in their pitchers, like the army of Gideon! And as we've the parson with us—" he turned to Helstone with a grin—"good-evening, Mr. Helstone. We'll do."

Helstone returned the greeting with the rigidity of a statue. Yorke, however, was in a jovial mood. "We're eleven strong men, and there's both horses and chariots among us. If we could only fall in with some of these starved ragamuffins of frame-breakers, we could win a grand victory. We could be every one a Wellington—that would please you, Mr. Helstone—and such paragraphs as we could contrive for the papers! Briarfield should be famous. But we'll have a column and a half in the *Stilbro' Courier* over this job, I dare say. I expect no less."

"And I'll promise you no less, Mr. Yorke," Helstone replied, his tone sharpening. "I'll write the article myself."

"To be sure—sartainly! And mind ye recommend weel that them 'at brake t' bits o' frames, and teed Joe Scott's legs wi' band, should be hung without benefit of clergy. It's a hanging matter, or should be. No doubt of that."

"If I judged them, I'd give them short shrift!" cried Moore. "But I mean to let them alone this bout, to give them rope enough, certain that in the end they will hang themselves."

"Let them alone, will ye, Moore? Do you promise that?"

"Promise! No. All I mean to say is, I shall give myself no particular trouble to catch them; but if one falls in my way..."

"You'll snap him up, of course. Only you would rather they would do something worse than merely stop a wagon before you reckon with them."

"Well, we'll say no more on the subject at present," Yorke interrupted, steering his horse toward a distant light. "Here we are at my door, gentlemen. I hope you and the men will step in. You will none of you be the worse of a little refreshment."

Both Moore and Helstone initially opposed the invitation, citing the propriety of their mission and the inclemency of the night. Yet, the courtesy of the offer, combined with the inviting glow of the muslin-curtained windows, proved irresistible. They yielded, and Mr. Yorke, alighting from his gig which he left in charge of a servant, led the way into the house.

It was immediately apparent that Mr. Yorke was a man of complex contradictions. He shifted effortlessly between broad Yorkshire dialect and pure, educated English; his manner oscillated between bluntness and affability. His station was difficult to gauge by his speech alone, but the appearance of his residence clarified the mystery.

The men were directed to the kitchen, where Yorke promised they would be served "wi' summat to taste." The gentlemen were ushered into a magnificent hall lined with pictures, then conducted to a large parlour dominated by a roaring fire. It was a room of exceptional taste, devoid of ostentatious wealth but rich in the refined sensibilities of a traveler and a scholar. The walls were adorned with Italian views—genuine works of art depicting sunny pastoral scenes, with soft distances and blue air quivering between the hills. A guitar rested on a sofa, alongside beautiful miniatures and Grecian vases. Books were arranged with elegance in two tall cases.

Yorke bade his guests sit and rang for wine. He gave hospitable orders for the refreshment of the men in the kitchen, but the rector remained standing, refusing the wine offered to him.

"E'en as you will," Yorke remarked, his tone lightening. "I reckon you're thinking of Eastern customs, Mr. Helstone, and you'll not eat nor drink under my roof, feared we should be forced to be friends. But I am not so particular or superstitious. You might sup the contents of that decanter, and you might give me a bottle of the best in your own cellar, and I'd hold myself free to oppose you at every turn still—in every vestry-meeting and justice-meeting where we encounter one another."

"It is just what I should expect of you, Mr. Yorke," Helstone replied.

"Does it agree with you now, Mr. Helstone, to be riding out after rioters, of a wet night, at your age?"

"It always agrees with me to be doing my duty; and in this case, my duty is a thorough pleasure. To hunt down vermin is a noble occupation, fit for an archbishop."

"Fit for you, at any rate. But where's the curate? He's gone to visit some poor body in a sick bed, or he's hunting down vermin in another direction."

"He is doing garrison-duty at Hollow's Mill."

"You left him a sup of wine, I hope, Bob," Yorke turned to Moore, "to keep his courage up?"

He did not wait for an answer but continued, addressing Moore who had lazily claimed a chair by the fireside. "Move it, Robert! Get up, my lad! That place is mine. Take the sofa, or three other chairs, if you will, but not this. It belongs to me, and nobody else."

"Why are you so particular to that chair, Mr. Yorke?" Moore asked, vacating the seat in obedience.

"My father was before me, and that's all the answer I shall give thee; and it's as good a reason as Mr. Helstone can give for the main feck of his notions."

"Moore, are you ready to go?" Helstone inquired.

"Nay; Robert's not ready, or rather, I'm not ready to part with him. He's an ill lad, and wants correcting."

"Why, sir? What have I done?"

"Made thyself enemies on every hand."

"What do I care for that? What difference does it make to me whether your Yorkshire louts hate me or like me?"

"Ay, there it is. The lad is a make of an alien among us. His father would never have talked in that way.—Go back to Antwerp, where you were born and bred, mauvaise tête!"

"Mauvaise tête vous-même; je ne fais que mon devoir; quant à vos lourdauds de paysans, je m'en moque!"

"En revanche, mon garçon, nos lourdauds de paysans se moqueront de toi; sois en certain," Yorke replied, speaking with a French accent nearly as pure as Gérard Moore's.

"C'est bon! c'est bon! Et puisque cela m'est égal, que mes amis ne s'en inquiètent pas."

"Tes amis! Où sont-ils, tes amis?"

"Je fais écho, où sont-ils? et je suis fort aise que l'écho seul y réponde. Au diable les amis! Je me souviens encore du moment où mon père et mes oncles Gérard appellèrent autour d'eux leurs amis, et Dieu sait si les amis se sont empressés d'accourir à leur secours! Tenez, M. Yorke, ce mot, ami, m'irrite trop; ne m'en parlez plus."

"Comme tu voudras."

And here Mr. Yorke held his peace. Leaning back in his three-cornered carved oak chair, he watched the scene unfold, a silent observer in a room filled with the tension of clashing worlds.
