Midnight had barely claimed the sky above the Piazza della Signoria when the moon, cresting the rooftops of the Old Palace, failed to cast its customary chiaroscuro upon the cobblestones. Instead of dappled light and shadow, the ground was swallowed by a suffocating density of humanity. No single paving stone was visible, obscured entirely by the heads of a frenzied, struggling multitude. The usual nocturnal symphony of Florence—the rhythmic patter of footsteps, the murmur of voices, the distant strum of a lute, the skittering of night-wanderers—had been obliterated. In its place roared a chaotic cacophony of shouted curses, trampling feet, jostling bodies, and the metallic clash of weapons, creating a sonic wall through which nothing penetrated save a piercing shriek or the heavy, tolling boom of a bell. Every citizen capable of rising was awake, either trapped within the piazza's boundaries or fighting desperately to breach them.

Inside the palace, the Council Chamber remained a fortress of tension. The chief magistracies, the eighty senators, and the select citizens who had debated through the burning daylight and torchlit nights sat in suspended animation. Their deliberation had been a ferocious quarrel regarding the Appeal: whether to grant clemency or execute the prisoners immediately to preempt the dangers of delay. The debate had grown so heated that the clamor leaked through the walls to the mob outside. Only in the final hour had the verdict fallen. The Signoria had fractured; four members had stubbornly voted for the Appeal, defying the pragmatic warning that refusal meant the sack of their homes. Yet, Francesco Valori, in a brief, furious address, had rendered his faction's resolve ominous, declaring that if the Signoria failed to defend the liberties of the Florentine people by executing the five perfidious citizens, others would seize the cause at the peril of all opponents. The Florentine Cato had triumphed. Upon recounting the votes, the four obstinate white beans vanished; the entire panel of nine cast the fatal affirmative black, sentencing the five prisoners to immediate death—a decision that implicitly, though with greater delay, sentenced Francesco Valori as well.

Now, while the Judicial Eight departed for the Bargello to prepare the instruments of execution, the five condemned men were dragged barefoot and shackled through the council chamber. This spectacle was orchestrated by their friends, hoping that the visible humiliation of such cruelty alongside two venerable elders, Bernardo del Nero and Niccolò Ridolfi, and two brilliant young leaders, Tornabuoni and Pucci, would stir the populace. It was futile. Any pity awakened was of a hopeless variety, leading not to rescue but to the slower, darker fuel of revenge.

Upstairs, amidst the chaos, Romola stood motionless against a massive pillar in the palace courtyard, awaiting the arrival of her godfather on his way to execution. Through powerful intervention, she had secured permission to spend the evening with him, remaining until the council's outcome was sealed. Now, she waited beside his confessor, prepared to follow the guard leading him to the Bargello. Her heart was set on clinging to the childless old man until the absolute final moment, mirroring the devotion of her own father; she had silenced all objections. Giovan Battista Ridolfi, a devoted disciple of Savonarola who faced the bitter sight of his elder brother Niccolò's death, had pledged to ensure her safety and stood faithfully by her side. Tito was also present in the palace, though unseen by her. Since the seventeenth, they had deliberately avoided one another. Tito knew only by inference from the friar's neutral report that her pleas had failed. Surrounded by officials and dignitaries, both Florentine and foreign, he maintained the grave, subdued silence of a man torn between public duty and private agony. When alluded to regarding his wife, he implied that her violent mental state, coupled with her status as the spouse of a man holding office under a government condemning her godfather, bred a diseased hostility toward him. Thus, for her own sake, he deemed it prudent not to approach her.

"Ah, the old Bardi blood!" Cennini muttered, shaking his head. "I shall not be surprised if this affair shakes *her* loose from the Frate, as surely as it loosens others I could name."

"It is excusable in a woman," interjected a young French envoy, bowing smoothly to Tito, "who is doubtless beautiful, since she is the wife of Messer Tito. One assumes her affections must supersede the good of the State, and that no one is to be beheaded who is anyone's cousin. But such sentimentality is not to be encouraged in the male population. Your Florentine polity seems significantly weakened by it."

"That is true," Niccolò Machiavelli replied calmly. "Where personal ties are strong, the hostilities they ignite must be accounted for. Many of these half-measures are merely hot-headed blundering. The only safe blows inflicted on men and parties are those too heavy to be avenged."

"Niccolò," Cennini warned, "there is a clever wickedness in thy talk sometimes that makes me mistrust thy pleasant young face as if it were a mask of Satan."

"Not at all, my good Domenico," Machiavelli smiled, placing a hand on the elder's shoulder. "Satan was a blunderer, an introducer of novelty who met with a stupendous failure. Had he succeeded, we would all be worshipping him, and his portrait would be far more flattering."

"Well, well," Cennini conceded. "I say not thy doctrine is not too clever for Satan; I only say it is wicked enough for him."

"I tell you," Machiavelli continued, "my doctrine belongs to all men who seek an end slightly beyond their own noses. Ask our Frate, our prophet, how his universal renovation is to be achieved. He will tell you: first, establish a free and pure government. Since this cannot be accomplished by making all Florentines love one another, it must be done by severing every head that stands obstinately in the way. Only if a man incurs odium by sanctioning a severity insufficient to be final does he commit a blunder. And I suspect the Frate has committed precisely that blunder. On this occasion, he might have won lustre by exerting himself to maintain the Appeal. Instead, he has lost lustre and gained no strength."

Before another word could be spoken, the announcement came that the prisoners were leaving the council chamber. The majority surged toward the door, racing to secure the clearest path to the Bargello behind the prisoner's guard, drawn equally by deep passion and cold curiosity. Tito remained behind. He possessed a natural repugnance to sights of death and pain; five days prior, he had hoped the execution would not occur, settling for exile as the maximum penalty. His own safety required no more. Yet now, he felt a grim assurance: learning that Bernardo del Nero's head had rolled would be a welcome guarantee of his security. The new knowledge and attitude revealed by Romola on the day of his return had instilled in him a fresh dread of the power she held to render his position precarious. If any act of hers succeeded in making him an object of suspicion and hatred, he foresaw not only frustration but frustration under unpleasant circumstances. Her belief in Baldassarre had clearly hardened her wavering feelings against further submission; if her godfather lived, she would easily win him to share her faith. Romola seemed an increasingly unmanageable variable in his destiny. However, if Bernardo del Nero were dead, the difficulties besetting her in opposing her husband would likely prove insurmountable to her shrinking pride. Consequently, Tito felt relieved knowing the Eight had gone to the Bargello to order the instant erection of the scaffold. Four other men—his intimates and confederates—were to die alongside Bernardo. A man's own safety is a god that sometimes makes very grim demands. Tito felt them acutely: even in pursuing what was agreeable, this paradoxical life forced upon him the desire for what was disagreeable. Hearing the shuffle of many feet and the clanking of metal on the stairs through the open doorway, he answered the French envoy's questions with a display of sad resignation to state necessity.

Those sounds struck Romola as if her auditory faculties had been exalted alongside every other sense. She required no arm to support her; she shed no tear. She felt an intensity of life transcending both grief and joy, wherein the mind aligns itself with elder forces that wrought existence before the birth of pleasure and pain. Since her godfather's fate was decided, her internal struggle had yielded to an identification with him in these supreme moments. She asserted inwardly that if he suffered the punishment of treason, he did not deserve the name of traitor; he was the victim of a collision between two forms of faithfulness. It was not given him to die for the noblest cause, yet he died because of his nobility. He might have been a meaner man and found it easier to avoid this guilt. Romola felt the full force of that sympathy with the individual lot that continually opposes the formulas by which actions and parties are judged. She walked the path with her second father to the scaffold, steeling herself to defy ignominy through the consciousness that it was undeserved.

The route was barricaded by three hundred armed men, ordered by Francesco Valori. Among the apparent contradictions of this event, none was more striking than the dual alarms: the fear of popular rage against the conspirators and the fear of a rescue attempt by a hostile crowd.
