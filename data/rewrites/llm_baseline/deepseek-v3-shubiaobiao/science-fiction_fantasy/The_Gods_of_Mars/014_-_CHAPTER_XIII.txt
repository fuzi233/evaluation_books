Here is the rewritten chapter in a more concise and engaging style while preserving the original plot and characters:

Xodar’s face twisted in disbelief as I recounted our defiance of Issus in the arena. “That settles it,” he muttered. “No goddess would endure such insult. She’s nothing but a tyrant clinging to power through fear.”

“A tyrant who still holds the keys to our cage,” I countered. “We leave tonight.”

Xodar barked a laugh. “Escaping the First Born? I’ve yet to see a ‘good time’ for that.”

“Then we’ll make one.” I eyed the dimming light filtering through the prison’s dome. “Can you swim?”

“Like a sea-snake,” he grinned.

“Good. The red youth can’t—his people fear deep water. We’ll need to support him.”

Xodar nodded. “Three swords are better than two. Especially his.” His gaze sharpened. “You fight alike—same stance, same lethal grace. Almost like kin.”

I dismissed the observation, though it pricked my memory. “We’ll need that kinship tonight.”

As the guard made his final rounds, we waited in taut silence. The moment the door clanged shut, I sprang to the window. Below, the fleet lay unguarded, crews sprawled in drugged slumber. Only a lone watchman paced a battleship’s deck—soon even he succumbed to the siren call of Mars’ dreamless sleep.

“There,” Xodar pointed to a sleek five-man flier. “Fastest in the fleet. Reach her, and we’ve a chance.”

Retrieving the red youth required acrobatics. He met my outstretched hand with a warrior’s leap, scaling the wall with startling agility. “You’d make a fine Thark,” I remarked.

His grin held secrets. “Someday I’ll explain.”

By the time we regrouped with Xodar, plans were set: I’d pilot, we’d aim for Helium, and fight as one. The youth stiffened at my mention of home but said nothing.

Our escape unfolded like a shadowplay. Harness straps became ropes; sentry patrols, predictable rhythms. We slithered over walls and crept past guardhouses, hearts pounding—until the glint of swords lured me inside.

The armory nearly proved our undoing. A creaking hinge stirred a guard. I froze, fingers inches from his throat, until his breathing steadied. Swords secured, I turned—and stared into the shocked eyes of the watch officer.

Twin gunshots cracked. His bullet grazed my ear as mine found its mark. Through the window I plunged, hitting the water as alarms erupted across Omean.

Chaosis a poor description. A thousand ships awoke as we thrashed toward the flier, Xodar dragging the gasping youth while I clutched our stolen blades. We hauled aboard just as searchlights speared the waves.

“Rise high!” Xodar urged. “Their artillery can’t risk the dome!”

The flier screamed upward, pursued by swarms of smaller craft. Ahead yawned the shaft—our only exit, now blocked by a cruiser angling to intercept.

Desperation birthed genius. I feigned ascent, then rammed her keel at full throttle. The cruiser flipped, crew spilling like ants into the void as we careened off, nearly flung from our own deck.

The shaft swallowed us, a black maw where instinct replaced sight. I steered by the shrinking coin of light below, hurtling upward at suicidal speed. Two miles of darkness—then, impossibly, open sky.

But no stars greeted us. The polar summer’s clouds veiled the heavens, leaving us blind in a void. Somewhere in that gloom lay freedom—or death.

The flier streaked onward, carrying three fugitives into the unknown.
