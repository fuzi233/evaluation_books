The clock’s hands moved relentlessly, marking the dwindling hours before my return to the *pensionnat*. Each tick was a whisper of time slipping away, and though I clung to these final moments, they dissolved like mist beneath my fingers.

“Lucy must stay another day,” Mrs. Bretton coaxed over breakfast, her voice warm as the tea steaming in my cup.

“I cannot delay any longer,” I replied, my resolve firm despite the ache in my chest. “My trunk is ready. I must go.”

Yet my departure hinged on Graham. Engaged until dusk, he arrived only as evening draped the city in shadow. A brief struggle ensued—pleas from mother and son to remain—but my heart was a clenched fist, desperate to endure the parting and be done with it.

When at last Dr. John handed me down at Madame Beck’s door, the rain fell in a fine, cold drizzle, glazing the pavement beneath the lamplight. It mirrored that night a year ago when I had first stood here, a stranger trembling on the threshold. Graham lingered in the vestibule, his kindness a balm I could not bear.

“Keep up your courage,” he said. “Think of us as true friends.”

“I will.”

My trunk was carried inside. We parted with pressed hands, yet he hesitated, his generosity unsatisfied.

“Shall you be very lonely here?” he asked.

“At first.”

“Then I’ll write—anything cheerful that comes to mind.”

I smiled but shook my head. “Do not trouble yourself.”

He left. The door thudded shut, and the blow I had braced for struck true.

---

Madame Beck received me with polished cordiality, her welcome brief as a rehearsed scene. Dismissed, I joined the evening study, then retreated to the dormitory. Exhausted, I sank onto the bed, my thoughts spiraling.

Reason, stern as a governess, whispered: *Perhaps once. But no more.* She forbade hope, forbade the indulgence of imagining his letters, his words. Yet how cruel her tyranny! I had obeyed her out of fear, not love, and she had starved me of all solace.

But then—soft as starlight—Imagination came. She soothed, she promised, she gilded my dreams. For one night, I slept.

Dawn broke with rain lashing the windows. I rose, drank icy water to steady myself, and descended to the refectory, where the stove’s glow offered scant comfort. There, I wrestled with my thoughts, forging silent rules: *No looking back. No futile longing. Walk the present’s desert, and endure.*

A face appeared at the window—M. Paul’s. Intrusive, unwelcome, his eyes fixed on mine.

“You are sad,” he accused, entering uninvited.

“I have the right to be.”

He studied me, relentless. “You grieve the separation from friends, do you not?”

I would not answer. When at last he left, the tears came—hot, silent, a relief.

---

By breakfast, I had mastered myself. Ginevra Fanshawe, radiant as ever, slid into the seat opposite, her laughter bright.

“Timon! You’ve returned just in time to save me from boredom.”

I indulged her chatter, trading bread for coffee, our old ritual. But when she probed about Graham, I deflected, weaving half-truths to shield his pride—and my own heart.

Later, in the quiet of the first *classe*, I feigned study, though my mind wandered. Ginevra burst in, all mischief, demanding gossip.

“Tell me,” she pressed, “was Dr. Bretton *truly* heartbroken?”

I spun a tale of his despair, embellishing for her amusement, though each word cost me. Let her think him pining. Let her never guess the quiet ruin in my own chest.

For that was the truth of it: I had returned to Villette, but a piece of me remained behind—unacknowledged, unmourned, and entirely lost.
