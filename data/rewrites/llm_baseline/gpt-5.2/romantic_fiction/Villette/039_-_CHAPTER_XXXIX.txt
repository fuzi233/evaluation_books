Fascinated as by a basilisk with three heads, I could not quit that little knot of people under the interlaced trees. The very soil seemed to have taken my feet for its own; the branches made a roof that promised concealment; the night—soft, warm, and close as a pantry after baking—whispered protection. An officious lamp, like a serving-boy with a single hurried errand, flashed one pale beam, showed me an obscure seat that would pass for empty, and then was gone again into the gaiety.

Let me set down, briefly and plainly, what—during this dark fortnight—I had been gathering in silence from Rumour, concerning the origin and object of M. Emanuel’s departure. It was no romance of devotion, no pilgrimage of pure zeal; it began in Mammon and ended in Interest, and between alpha and omega there was nothing but bargaining.

Madame Walravens—hideous as some squat idol set up in a niche and fed with incense—had, nevertheless, the weight of an idol among her worshippers. Once she had been rich—very rich; and though at present she did not command her money, she was likely, one day, to be rich again. In Basseterre, in Guadaloupe, she held a large estate: a dowry brought sixty years ago, sequestered since her husband’s failure; now, they supposed it clear of claims, and, if only tended by a competent agent, capable of being made, in a few years, largely productive. They talked of it as people talk of a fruit-tree long neglected, whose branches—if pruned, grafted, and defended from blight—might yet be made to load the basket: a plantation of canes, perhaps; coffee; whatever in that fierce sun turns labour into sweetness and coin.

Père Silas took an interest in the prospective harvest for the sake of the Church, whereof Magloire Walravens was a devout daughter. Madame Beck—distantly related to the hunchback, and knowing her without family of her own—had long brooded, like a thrifty housewife over winter stores, on contingencies; harshly treated as she was, she never ceased to court her, for interest’ sake. Priest and directress were thus, for money reasons, equally and sincerely attentive to the nursing of that West Indian estate.

But the distance was great, and the climate hazardous. The competent and upright agent wanted—must be a devoted man: a man who could live without applause, and work without being watched; who could bear fever and solitude, and still keep accounts like a conscience. Just such a man had Madame Walravens retained for twenty years in her service, blighting his life, and then living on him like an old fungus; such a man Père Silas had trained, taught, and bound to him by gratitude, habit, and belief. Such a man Madame Beck knew, and could in some measure influence.

“My pupil,” said Père Silas, “if he remains in Europe, runs risk of apostasy, for he has become entangled with a heretic.”

Madame Beck made her private comment also, and cherished in her own breast her secret reason for desiring expatriation. The thing she could not obtain, she desired not another to win: rather would she destroy it.

As to Madame Walravens, she wanted her money and her land, and knew Paul—if he liked—could make the best and faithfullest steward. So the three self-seekers banded and beset the one unselfish. They reasoned, appealed, implored; on his mercy they cast themselves; into his hands they confidingly thrust their interests. They asked but two or three years of devotion—after that, he should live for himself: and one of the number, perhaps, wished that in the meantime he might die.

No living being ever humbly laid advantage at M. Emanuel’s feet, or confidingly put it into his hands, that he spurned the trust or repulsed the repository. What might be his private pain, his inward reluctance to leave Europe—what calculations for his own future—none asked, or knew, or reported. For me, all that was blank. His conferences with his confessor I might guess; the part duty and religion were made to play in the persuasions used, I might conjecture. He was gone, and had made no sign: there my knowledge closed.

With my head bent and my forehead on my hands, I sat amidst grouped trunks and branching brushwood. Whatever talk passed in the circle near me I could have heard, had there been motive. They gossiped about dresses, music, illuminations, the fine night; the very words sounded like the clink of glasses in a room where one is hungry and has no place at table. I listened for one phrase, one crumb dropped from a careless tongue—“It is calm weather for his voyage; the Antigua will sail prosperously.” No such remark fell. Neither the Antigua, nor her course, nor her passenger was named.

Perhaps the light chat scarcely interested old Madame Walravens more than it did me. She appeared restless, turning her head this way and that, looking through the trees and among the crowd, expectant and impatient. “Où sont-ils? Pourquoi ne viennent-ils?” I heard her mutter more than once; and at last, determined to have an answer, she spoke aloud—brief, simple—and it sent a shock through me as if a cold hand had been laid on my neck.

“Messieurs et mesdames,” said she, “où donc est Justine Marie?”

Justine Marie! The dead nun. Where was she? In her grave, Madame Walravens—what can you want with her? You shall go to her, but she shall not come to you. Thus I should have answered, had the response lain with me. But nobody seemed of my mind; nobody started, or frowned, or even looked surprised. The strangest question received the quietest commonplace reply.

“Justine Marie,” said one, “is coming; she is in the kiosk; she will be here presently.”

Out of that question and answer sprang a change in the chat—chat still: desultory, familiar gossip. Hint, allusion, jest, went round; but all so broken, so dependent on persons not named and circumstances not defined, that listen as intently as I would—listen I did now, with a fated interest—I could make out no more than that some scheme was on foot in which this ghostly Justine Marie—dead or alive—was concerned. They seemed grasping at her for some reason; there was talk of a marriage, of a fortune; for whom I could not quite seize—perhaps for Victor Kint, perhaps for Josef Emanuel: both bachelors. Once I thought the raillery fell thickest on a young fair-haired foreigner of the party, called Heinrich Mühler.

Amidst all the badinage, Madame Walravens obtruded from time to time hoarse, cross-grained speeches; her impatience diverted only by an implacable surveillance of Désirée, who could not stir but the old woman menaced her with her staff, as if guarding a dish on a crowded sideboard.

“La voilà!” suddenly cried one of the gentlemen. “Voilà Justine Marie qui arrive!”

That moment was peculiar for me. The painted nun on the panel rose before me; the sad love-story, long whispered in the house like old kitchen legends told over kneading-trough and fire, came back; I saw again the vision of the garret, the apparition of the alley, the strange birth of the berceau. A presentiment of discovery pressed on my heart; a conviction that some disclosure was at hand. And indeed, when imagination once runs riot, where do we stop? The barest winter tree, the humblest hedge-munching animal, a passing cloud, a struggling moonbeam—Fancy will clothe all in spirituality and make a phantom of it.

With solemn force, the expectation of mystery breaking up, I leaned forward and looked.

“She comes!” cried Josef Emanuel.

The circle opened, as if to admit a welcome member. A torch chanced to be carried past; its blaze, aiding the pale moon, lit to perfection the little stage where my dread had been rehearsing its scene. Surely those near me must have felt some portion of my suspense. For me—my life stood still.

It is over. The moment and the nun are come. The crisis and the revelation are passed by.

The flambeau still glares within a yard, held up in a park-keeper’s hand; its long tongue of flame almost licks the figure of the Expected—there—full in my sight. What is she like? What does she wear? How does she look? Who is she?

There are many masks in the park to-night; and as the hour wears late, a feeling of revelry and mystery spreads abroad, so that you would scarcely discredit me, reader, were I to say she is like the nun of the attic, wears black skirts and white head-clothes, looks the resurrection of the flesh, a risen ghost.

All falsities—all figments. We will not deal in that gear. Let us be honest, and cut, as heretofore, from the homely web of truth.

Homely, though, is an ill-chosen word. What I see is not precisely homely. A girl of Villette stands there—a girl fresh from her pensionnat: comely, with the beauty indigenous to this country. She looks well-nourished—fair and fat of flesh, round-cheeked, good-eyed, abundant-haired. She is handsomely dressed. She is not alone; her escort consists of three persons—two elderly, whom she calls “Mon Oncle” and “Ma Tante.” She laughs, chats; good-humoured, buxom, and blooming, she looks at all points the bourgeoise belle.

So much for Justine Marie; so much for ghosts and mystery. Not that the last was solved—this girl certainly was not my nun: what I had seen in garret and garden must have been taller by a span.

We have looked at the city belle; we have cursorily glanced at the respectable old uncle and aunt. Have we a stray glance to give the third member of this company? We ought, reader: he has claims on us; we do not meet him now for the first time.

I clasped my hands very hard; I drew my breath very deep. I held in the cry, devoured the ejaculation, forbade the start. I spoke and stirred no more than a stone; but I knew what I looked on. Through the dimness left in my eyes by many nights’ weeping, I knew him.

They said he was to sail by the Antigua. Madame Beck said so. She lied; or she had uttered what was once truth, and failed to contradict it when it became false. The Antigua was gone—and there stood Paul Emanuel.

Was I glad? A huge load left me, as if some iron pot had been lifted from my breast. Was it a fact to warrant joy? I know not. Ask first what circumstances attended this respite. How far did this delay concern me? Were there not those whom it might touch more nearly?

After all, who might this young girl—this Justine Marie—be? Not a stranger: she was known to me by sight; she visited in the Rue Fossette; she was often at Madame Beck’s Sunday parties. She was a relation of both the Becks and Walravens; she derived her baptismal name from the sainted nun who would have been her aunt had she lived; her patronymic was Sauveur. She was an heiress and an orphan; M. Emanuel her guardian—some said her godfather.

The family junta wished this heiress married to one of their band. Which was it? Vital question—which was it?

I felt very glad now that the drug administered in the sweet draught had filled me with a possession which made bed and chamber intolerable. I have always, through my whole life, liked to penetrate to the real truth; to seek the goddess in her temple and dare the veil. It is not that truth comes without pain—often it strikes like the first knife that opens a too-full loaf, letting out steam and heat that smart the hand; but to see and know the worst is to take from Fear her main advantage.

The Walravens’ party, augmented in numbers, now became very gay. The gentlemen fetched refreshments from the kiosk. All sat down on the turf under the trees; they drank healths and sentiments; they laughed, jested. M. Emanuel underwent some raillery—half good-humoured, half, I thought, malicious—especially on Madame Beck’s part. I soon gathered that his voyage had been deferred of his own will, without the concurrence—nay, against the advice—of his friends. He had let the Antigua go, and had taken his berth in the Paul et Virginie, appointed to sail a fortnight later.

It was his reason for this resolve they teased him to assign, and which he would only vaguely indicate as “the settlement of a little piece of business which he had set his heart upon.” What was this business? Nobody knew. Yes—there was one who seemed at least partly in his confidence: a meaning look passed between him and Justine Marie.

“La petite va m’aider—n’est-ce pas?” said he.

The answer was prompt enough, God knows.

“Mais oui, je vous aiderai de tout mon cœur. Vous ferez de moi tout ce que vous voudrez, mon parrain.”

And this dear “parrain” took her hand and lifted it to his grateful lips.

At that demonstration, I saw the light-complexioned young Teuton, Heinrich Mühler, grow restless, as if the sound of that kiss on fingers were a note that jarred him. He grumbled a few words. M. Emanuel actually laughed in his face; and with the ruthless triumph of the assured conqueror, drew his ward nearer.

M. Emanuel was very joyous that night. He seemed not one whit subdued by the change of scene and action impending. He was the life of the party: a little despotic, perhaps, determined to be chief in mirth as well as in labour, yet proving from moment to moment his right of leadership. His was the wittiest word, the pleasantest anecdote, the frankest laugh. Restlessly active after his manner, he multiplied himself to wait on all; but I saw which was his favourite. I saw at whose feet he lay on the turf; whom he folded carefully from the night air, as one wraps a delicate thing away from damp; whom he tended, watched, and cherished as the apple of his eye.

Still hint and raillery flew thick; and still I gathered that while M. Paul should be absent, working for others, these others—who were not quite ungrateful—would guard for him the treasure he left in Europe. Let him bring them an Indian fortune; they would give him in return a young bride and a rich inheritance. The saintly consecration, the vow of constancy, was forgotten: the blooming and charming Present prevailed over the Past; and at length, his nun was indeed buried.

Thus it must be. The revelation was come. Presentiment had not been mistaken in her impulse; there is a kind of presentiment that never is mistaken. It was I who had for a moment miscalculated: not seeing the true bearing of the oracle, I had thought she muttered of vision, when in truth her prediction touched reality.

I might have paused longer upon what I saw; I might have deliberated ere drawing inferences. Some would have held the premises doubtful, the proofs insufficient; some slow sceptics would have examined incredulously ere accepting the project of a marriage between a poor, unselfish man of forty and his wealthy ward of eighteen. But far from me such shifts and palliatives—far from me such coward fleeing from the swift-footed Fact.

No. I hastened to accept the whole plan. I extended my grasp and took it all in, gathered it to me with a rage of haste, folded it round me as the soldier struck on the field folds his colours about his breast. I invoked Conviction to nail upon me the certainty—abhorred while embraced—to fix it with the strongest spikes. And when the iron had entered well my soul, I stood up, as I thought, renovated.

In my infatuation, I said: “Truth, you are a good mistress to your faithful servants. While a Lie pressed me, how I suffered! Even when Falsehood was sweet—flattering to fancy, warm to feeling—it wasted me with hourly torment. The persuasion that affection was won could not be divorced from dread that, by another turn of the wheel, it might be lost. Truth stripped away Falsehood, Flattery, Expectancy—and here I stand—free.”

Nothing remained but to take my freedom to my chamber, carry it to my bed, and see what I could make of it.

The play was not yet quite played out; I might have waited and watched longer that love-scene under the trees—that sylvan courtship. Had there been nothing of love in the demonstration, my Fancy—generous and creative in this hour—could have modelled for it the sharpest lineaments and given it deepest colour of passion. But I would not look. I had fixed my resolve; I would not violate my nature. And then—something tore me so cruelly under my shawl, something dug into my side like a talon—some vulture, strong in beak and claw—I must be alone to grapple with it. I think I never felt jealousy till now.

It was not like enduring the endearments of Dr. John and Paulina—against which, while I sealed eyes and ears, withdrew my thoughts, my sense of harmony still acknowledged a charm. This was an outrage. The love born of beauty was not mine; I had nothing in common with it: I dared not meddle. But another love—venturing diffidently into life after long acquaintance, furnace-tried by pain, stamped by constancy, consolidated by affection’s pure and durable alloy, submitted by intellect to intellect’s own tests, and finally wrought up, by his own process, to his own unflawed completeness—this Love, that laughed at Passion with his fast frenzies and hot hurried extinction—in this Love I had a vested interest. Whatever tended to its culture or its destruction, I could not view impassibly.

I turned from the group of trees and the merrie companie in its shade. Midnight was long past; the concert was over; crowds thinned. I followed the ebb.

Leaving the radiant park and well-lit Haute-Ville—still well lit, for it seems this was to be a nuit blanche in Villette—I sought the lower quarter. And there, the moonlight—forgotten in the park—flowed in again upon perception. High she rode, calm and stainless. The music and mirth of the fête, the fire and bright hues of lamps, had outdone her for an hour; but now her glory and silence triumphed. The rival lamps were dying. She held her course like a white fate.

How different the soundscape here—the deep peace unvexed by drum and bugle. In the upper town, those brassy mouths had clanged and clamoured as if man could purchase joy by noise; here, the night had its own language: the measured tread of a homeward burgher; the far, soft shutting of a door; the faint wash of one’s own dress against the stones. It seemed to me that this quiet was not mere absence but a kind of health—an acoustic shelter where the mind could hear itself. I thought how easily a city’s true memory is drowned: not by forgetting alone, but by being shouted down; how the native cadence of a street—its old footsteps, its low talk—dies when every festival must be louder than the last, when art is hawked like a fairing and forced to compete with clatter.

These oil-twinkling streets were still. I liked them for their lowliness and peace. Homeward-bound pedestrians passed now and then, making little noise and soon gone. So well I loved Villette under that aspect that I would not willingly have re-entered under a roof—but I was bent on pursuing my strange adventure to a successful close, and regaining my bed in the great dormitory before Madame Beck returned.

Only one street lay between me and the Rue Fossette; as I entered it, for the first time that night, the sound of a carriage tore up the peace. It came very fast. How loud sounded its rattle on the paving—an iron violence that seemed to insult the narrowness and sleep of the place. The street was confined; I kept carefully to the causeway. The carriage thundered past; and what did I see—or fancy I saw—as it rushed by? Surely something white fluttered from that window—surely a hand waved a handkerchief. Was that signal meant for me? Am I known? Who could recognise me? It was not M. de Bassompierre’s carriage, nor Mrs. Bretton’s; and besides, neither the Hôtel Crécy nor the château of La Terrasse lay in that direction. I had no time for conjecture. I must hurry home.

Gaining the Rue Fossette, reaching the pensionnat, all was still. No fiacre had yet arrived with Madame and Désirée. I had left the great door ajar; should I find it thus? Perhaps the wind—or some accident—might have thrown it to with force enough to start the spring-bolt. In that case, hopeless became admission; my adventure must issue in catastrophe.

I lightly pushed the heavy leaf. Would it yield?

Yes. As soundless, as unresisting, as if some propitious genius had waited within upon a sesame-charm. Entering with bated breath, quietly making all fast, mounting the staircase as softly as bare feet in a sickroom, I sought the dormitory and reached my couch.

Ay—I reached it, and drew a free inspiration. The next moment, I almost shrieked—almost, but not quite, thank Heaven.

Throughout the dormitory, throughout the house, reigned the stillness of death. All slept; and in such hush it seemed none dreamed. Stretched on nineteen beds lay nineteen forms, motionless. On mine—the twentieth—nothing ought to have lain: I had left it void, and void should have found it. What then did I see between the half-drawn curtains? What dark usurping shape—supine, long, strange? A robber, crept in through the open street-door, lying there in wait? It looked very black. I think it looked—not human. A wandering dog, perhaps, that had come in from the street and nested there? Would it spring if I approached? Approach I must.

Courage. One step.

My head reeled; for by the faint night-lamp I saw stretched on my bed the old phantom—the NUN.

A cry might have ruined me. Be the spectacle what it might, I could afford neither scream nor swoon. Besides, I was not overcome. Tempered by late incidents, my nerves disdained hysteria. Warm yet from illuminations, music, thronging thousands—lashed up by a new scourge—I defied spectra. Without exclamation I rushed on the haunted couch. Nothing leaped out or stirred; all the movement was mine. So was all the life, the reality, the substance, the force—as my instinct knew.

I tore her up—the incubus. I held her on high—the goblin. I shook her loose—the mystery. Down she fell—down all around me—in shreds and fragments—and I trode upon her.

Here again—behold the branchless tree, the unstabled Rosinante; the film of cloud, the flicker of moonshine. The long nun proved a long bolster dressed in a long black stole, artfully invested with a white veil. The garments, strange as it may seem, were genuine nun’s vestments, disposed by some hand with studied care to deceive the eye in dusk. Whence came these clothes? Who contrived this artifice? These questions still remained.

To the head-bandage was pinned a slip of paper, bearing in pencil these mocking words:

“The nun of the attic bequeaths to Lucy Snowe her wardrobe. She will be seen in the Rue Fossette no more.”

And what—and who—was she that had haunted me? She whom I had actually seen three times? Not a woman of my acquaintance had that stature. She was not of a female height. Not to any man I knew could the machination, for a moment, be attributed.
