The following day, a Sunday marked by the bustle of creation, saw Catherine's household in the Hoog Straet abuzz with activity. The shop stood ready, a testament to weeks of labor, with the names "Cornelis and Sybrandt" freshly painted above the entrance and a sign depicting a white lamb suckling a gilt sheep fluttering gently in the breeze. Eli had arrived laden with fresh stock from his store, ensuring they began with ample provision. Within the home, the atmosphere was electric; the parents' hearts burned with a fervent glow, driven by the resolve to embrace commerce over idleness. They strolled side by side through the garden, their spirits lifted by the prospect of earning rather than wasting time, vowing to adhere to business with the tenacity of wax.

Through an open window, Catherine's mother, sharp-eyed and ever-watchful, caught the drift of their conversation. She called out to Eli, announcing that evening supper would be an inaugural feast, welcoming Margaret and her son. It was to be a gathering where wisdom regarding trade would flow freely from the elders, accompanied by wine raised to the health of those converting from agriculture to commerce—a shift away from wild oats and unremunerative toil. Margaret, eager to shake off a lingering malaise, had joined her mother-in-law. Both women wore complexions flushed as if by the very fires they tended. Soon, Joan entered carrying a crisp salad harvested from Jorian's garden.

"He sliced it especially for you, Margaret," Joan remarked, her tone laced with playful jealousy. "He said you were to feast today. But tell me, lass, what sermons were delivered in the new church? I have never heard preaching before."

"I wish I had been there," Margaret murmured, her voice hollow with longing. "I feel parched, drained of the heavenly dew."

"Why, he preaches again this afternoon," Joan noted. "Perhaps you are needed here, though?"

"Not yet," Catherine interjected warmly. "Go on, if your heart is set."

"But I fear I might dampen the spirits at table," Margaret hesitated. "Unless I return warmed by a fine sermon."

"Then you must move swiftly," Joan advised, her gaze fixed on the street. "Look! The people are heading that way. By my soul, here comes the holy friar!" The energetic woman bounded from the room, crossed the road, and intercepted the monk. Returning moments later, her face alight, she declared, "I was determined to see him close up. 'My daughter,' says he, 'I shall visit him ere sunset, God willing.' His voice was sweet, but look at those thin cheeks, Margaret, and those large eyes—not unlike yours."

"I long to hear him," Margaret confessed, though her brow furrowed. "Yet my cap is not quite clean, and all will be wearing their snow-white veils."

"There, take my handkerchief from the basket," Catherine instructed kindly. "You cannot keep the child; I need him for my poor Kate. She is having one of her difficult days."

Margaret ascended the stairs with the boy, finding Kate lying in bed. "How fares you, darling? I need not ask; you are in sore pain, yet you smile. Look, I have brought you something you cherish."

"Two, by my count," Kate whispered, offering an angelic smile. A sudden spasm seized her, causing the air in the room to seem heavy.

"In your lap?" Margaret asked, interpreting Kate's gesture. "No, he is too heavy, and you are in such distress."

"I love him too dearly to care for his weight," Kate replied softly.

Seizing the moment, Margaret attended to her own toilette. "I am bound for the church," she announced, "to listen to a beautiful preacher." Kate let out a soft sigh. "A minute ago, Kate, I was eager to go. That has been my state for the past month—up and down, like the rolling waves of the Zuyder Zee. I would much rather stay with you. Say the word!"

"Nay," Kate insisted, her eyes filling with tears. "Pray, go. Bring me back every word. Alas, I cannot go myself." These words broke Margaret's hesitation. She leaned forward, kissed Kate tenderly, glanced lovingly at the sleeping infant, and let out a small sigh. "I think I dare not," she whispered. "I could never bear to part with him, even briefly. My father forbade waking a child by day or night. If it pleases thee to rouse him, call upon Aunt Kate for the two new words thou hast learned." With a final, affectionate look over her shoulder, she closed the door soundlessly.

Downstairs, Catherine turned to Joan. "Will you lend a hand peeling these, Joan?"

"With pleasure, mistress." The kitchen fell into a rhythm of silent, vigorous preparation. "Now, Joan, remember: those who assist me in cooking and serving shall share in the eating. That is a rule."

"There are harsher laws in Holland than that," Joan laughed. "Your will is my delight, mistress. For my Luke has already missed his supper, thanks to his digging today by good fortune." Just then, Margaret descended.

"Eh, woman, that is no pleasant trade," Catherine observed, gesturing to Margaret's refreshed appearance. "She has just washed her face and coiffed her hair, and now who is fit to compare? Rotterdam, indeed, for you!" She snapped her fingers playfully. "Give me a kiss, hussy! Mind, Eli will not wait for the Duke to join us. Do not linger once the service ends."

Together, Catherine and Joan watched Margaret depart down the street. Among homely wives stepping out of doors, the departure itself became half an event. Catherine paused, reflecting aloud. "Joan, it feels as though I am sending off my own daughter to church, standing watch behind her door—a scene repeated countless times. Joan, lass, she refuses to hear a word spoken against our Gerard. And since he lives, he has treated her cruelly. This is why my heart aches for the poor wretch. I am older and wiser than she; therefore, I shall wed her to that simple Luke, and thus shall the matter conclude. What is one grandchild, after all?"
