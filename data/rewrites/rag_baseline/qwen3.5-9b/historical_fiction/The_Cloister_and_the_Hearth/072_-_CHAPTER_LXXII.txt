To understand the trajectory of Clement's soul, one must first grasp the shifting tectonic plates of the ecclesiastical world in which he moved. The Dominicans, once the towering giants of European spirituality, were crumbling under the relentless pressure of their rivals, the Franciscans. Even in England, a land steeped in piety where the Black Friars had once ruled supreme, the tide had turned. In response, the order's leadership, driven by a desperate urgency to reclaim their dominion, sought to forge weapons of unparalleled zeal. They identified Clement as such a weapon, drawn to his rare linguistic mastery—a polyglot fluent in Latin, Italian, French, and both High and Low Dutch—and dispatched him to England to preach.

Yet, the path of the saint is rarely a straight line. Jerome, the shadow behind the throne of the Superior, intercepted this destiny. With the cunning of a guardian dog protecting a vulnerable pup, Jerome argued against sending the novice abroad. "Clement," he intoned, his voice dripping with feigned concern, "still carries the milk of the world in his veins. Let not his newborn zeal or his fragile humility tempt us to discard our ancient wisdom. Temper him first. Test him, lest one day we find ourselves leaning on a reed for a staff."

The Prior, recognizing the strategic necessity of the test, acquiesced. "It is well advised," he conceded, handing the burden to Jerome. "Take him in hand yourself."

Thus began the crucible. Jerome led Clement not to the pulpit, but to the muddy periphery of society. He dragged him to fields where youths played, knowing these grounds were haunted by the ghosts of Clement's past friends. Sure enough, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea appeared, casting casual glances at the two monks. They failed to recognize the dead friend in the shaven head of the friar. Clement flinched, a micro-tremor of recognition, before lowering his eyes to recite a *paternoster*.

"Would you not speak with them, brother?" Jerome probed, watching for a spark of attachment.

"No, brother," Clement replied, his voice steady despite the internal storm. "Yet it was good for me to see them. They remind me of the sins I can never repent enough."

Satisfied with the detachment, Jerome filed a cold report in favor of the novice. But the trials escalated from psychological probing to physical purgatory. Jerome subjected Clement to deathbed vigils, forcing him to confront the finality of mortality, and then thrust him into dungeons where the air itself seemed to rot. These were places of appalling darkness and pestilential stench, where men resembling wild beasts coiled in rags and despair. The trial was grueling; Clement's body suffered, yet his soul gathered every ounce of its power to offer comfort to the wretched, shining with a radiance that defied the filth. Jerome reported back: "His spirit is willing, but his flesh is weak."

"Good!" declared Anselm. "His flesh is weak, but his spirit is willing."

However, the ultimate test awaited, one witnessed by the chaotic theater of Rome itself. One morning, the city's principal streets were choked with a sea of heads, the avenues blocked by the anticipation of violence. It was an execution, but not for a common crime. The Governor of Rome had been slaughtered in his sleep at dawn. His hand, severed at the wrist, lay beside him alongside a throat slit clean and precise, his temples bruised by a blunt instrument. The murder was traced to his servant, and justice demanded a retribution as bloody as the act.

The criminal was dragged to the house of the victim and pinned to the wall for thirty minutes, a grotesque preview of the law's vengeance. Then, his left hand was hacked off, mirroring the governor's. A freshly killed bird was slit open and stuffed onto the bleeding stump—a macabre offering of some forgotten medical theory—before the mutilated man was hurried to the scaffold. There, a young friar knelt, praying with an earnest, affectionate fervor, pressing a crucifix close to the condemned man's eyes.

Suddenly, the executioner shoved the friar aside. With a heavy mallet, he struck the culprit, and immediately drove a blade from ear to ear. A cry of horror erupted from the crowd. The young friar swooned, collapsing into unconsciousness. A gigantic monk stepped forward, seizing the fainting youth and carrying him away like a child.

Brother Clement returned to the convent, his spirit crushed by the spectacle. He confessed to the Prior, tears streaming down his face. "Courage, son Clement," the Prior urged. "A Dominican is not forged in a day. You shall have another trial. And I forbid you to approach it fasting."

Clement bowed, accepting the mandate. He had not long to wait. A robber, a monster of wanton cruelty who had slain men for sheer malice, was brought to the scaffold. Clement spent his last night in prison with the man, accompanying him to the platform. He prayed with such intensity that the hardened ruffian wept and embraced him. Clement, though his flesh quivered with revulsion, reciprocated the embrace, holding the crucifix firmly before the dying man's eyes. As the rope tightened and the dark spirit departed, Clement continued to pray aloud until the hangman raised his hatchet. The crowd, indifferent to the living man's suffering, uttered piteous cries at each blow upon the corpse, feeling nothing. Clement shuddered but stood firm, immovable as a rock vibrating in the wind.

Then, Jerome's voice cut through the air. "Brother Clement, mount that cart and preach to the people. Quickly! Strike with all your force upon this iron while it is still hot, and save these souls!"

Clement's complexion drained and flushed in rapid succession; his breath hitched. Yet, he obeyed. With uncertain steps, he climbed the cart and faced the first crowd he would ever address. That sea of upturned faces seemed to dry his throat, hammer his heart, and shake his voice. But gradually, the magnitude of the occasion ignited within him. The sight of the eager multitude, coupled with his own surging zeal, transformed the pale monk. He recounted the robber's history, fresh from the prison cell, using the man's fate to illustrate the deadly rungs of folly and crime, warning them solemnly not to place their foot on the first step.

Alternating between thunderous denunciations of bloodshed and moving appeals to charity, his tremors vanished. He felt himself strung tight like a lute, gifted with an unsuspected power. He mastered the listening crowd, sensing their very pulse, playing sacred melodies upon them as if they were strings on a psaltery. Sobs and groans confirmed his sway over the mob, already stirred by the preceding tragedy. Jerome stared, bewildered, like a man watching a rocket launch unexpectedly. When Clement noticed the lack of approval in Jerome's gaze, he abruptly stopped and descended.

"It was my first endeavor," Clement apologized humbly. "Your command came upon me like a thunderbolt. Did I succeed? Correct me, and aid me with your experience, Brother Jerome."

"Hmph," Jerome murmured dubiously. After a long pause, he added sullenly, "Give the glory to God, Brother Clement. My opinion is that you are an orator born."

He relayed this assessment to headquarters, albeit with reluctance, for he was an honest friar, however disagreeable.

Soon, another trial arose involving Julio Antonelli, accused of sacrilege. Three witnesses placed him at the scene of the theft, while others provided a solid alibi. Caught in this contradiction, Antonelli was granted the trial by water. The clergy, hoping for his acquittal, recommended the hot water ordeal—immersing an arm in boiling water to retrieve a pebble—which was considered less terrifying than the alternative. But Antonelli lacked the nerve and chose the cold trial.

Jerome seized this opportunity to steel Clement further. Antonelli received the sacrament, was stripped naked on the banks of the Tiber, and bound hand and foot to prevent struggling, which would cause him to sink. He was lowered gently into the stream, floating momentarily with only his hair above the surface. A simultaneous roar from the crowds on either bank declared him guilty. But seconds later, the new ropes soaked in water grew heavy, dragging him down. Another roar proclaimed his innocence. Left submerged for the appointed time, he was drawn up gurgling, gasping, and screaming for mercy. After the requisite prayers, he was released, cleared of all charges.

On the bank, Clement had prayed earnestly throughout the experiment. Upon completion, he thanked God in a loud, slightly wavering voice. Later, he turned to Jerome. "Should not the man be compensated?"

"For what?" Jerome asked.

"For the pain, the dread, the suffocation. Poor soul, he lives, but has tasted all the bitterness of death. Yet he committed no ill."

"He is rewarded enough in being cleared of his fault," Jerome countered.

"But being innocent of that fault, he has nonetheless drunk Death's cup, though not to the dregs," Clement persisted. "And his accusers, less innocent than he, suffer naught."

Jerome replied sternly, "It is not in this world that men are truly punished, Brother Clement. Unhappy are those who sin yet escape suffering. Happy are those who endure such ills as earth can inflict; it is counted to them above, yes, a hundredfold."
