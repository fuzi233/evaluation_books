Fifty-five years of breathing the woodsmoke of a thousand transient hearths, and here I find myself taking up a pen to preserve a history before its flavor dissipates entirely. I am no traditional man of letters, but rather a collector of archaic recipes and a student of the agrarian elders who coaxed sustenance from the deep African interior. I trade in ivory, yes, but my true wealth has always been the sensory tapestries of the continent—the scent of wild garlic bruised beneath a wagon wheel, the rich, slow-roasting marrow bones seasoned with untranslatable dialects. 

I write this for a blend of reasons, much like the careful layering of spices in a complex, multi-generational stew. Primarily, Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good asked it of me. Secondly, I am laid up here at Durban. A lion’s teeth—a bite I took months ago—throb in time with the changing seasons. There is a cyclical nature to the poison, a circular rhythm that brings the agony back just as the rains begin, an acoustic memory of the beast’s deafening, chest-rattling roar that shattered the delicate ecology of the savanna that day. It breaks the natural routine of the seasons, and as a meticulous man, I resent the interruption. 

Thirdly, I want my boy Harry, who is currently enduring the sterile, utilitarian corridors of a London hospital, to have something to nourish him. I fear for him there, surrounded by the mechanical shrieks of modern industrial noise pollution, his spirit fed only by the joyless, institutional fuel of hospital gruel. I hope this tale might offer him the rich, simmering broth of true memory. And lastly, I write because this is the strangest story I have ever tasted—one devoid of women, save for Gagool, if that ancient fiend could even be called one.

To begin, I must declare myself. Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal, Gentleman. But what is a "gentleman"? The word is a heavy, colonizing implement, a piece of the dominant lexicon meant to flatten the subjective experiences of the myriad souls with whom I have broken bread. I have known white men, fresh from home with pockets full of commercially sterilized coin, who lack the slightest sensory appreciation for the earth that feeds them; men who are gentlemen in name only. And I have known natives whose languages are dying embers—dialects that hold within their syllables the precise temperature to smoke a guinea fowl, or the exact acoustic crack of a ripening melon splitting—who possess true nobility. When a native language dies, the culinary and cultural memory of a people dies with it. The Almighty gave us our lives to defend, and though I have spilled blood, I have never done so wantonly. I have tried to live in harmony with the world’s quiet rhythms.

Eighteen months ago, my luck had soured beyond Bamangwato. A fever took me, leaving a lingering taste of copper and ash in my mouth. Once well enough, I sold my wagon, my oxen, and my stores of ivory down at the Diamond Fields—a place where the harmonious, sweeping sounds of the natural world have been violently replaced by the clanking, soulless cacophony of commercial mining. Seeking the quiet of the sea, I took the post-cart to Cape Town. I found brief solace in the botanical gardens, charting the indigenous edible flora that local elders once used to flavor their broths, though the newly erected Houses of Parliament seemed a monument only to empty, echoing bureaucracy. Finding the hotel overcharged me for meals bereft of any true gustatory depth, I boarded the *Dunkeld* to return to Natal.

The ship’s engines thrummed—a relentless, jarring mechanical throb that grated against my ear, a stark contrast to the organic, comforting crackle of a mopane-wood fire. But among the passengers transhipping from the *Edinburgh Castle*, two men drew my attention. One, a man of about thirty, was a towering figure with a thick yellow beard and deep-set grey eyes. He carried the aura of an ancient Dane, a man whose ancestors might have presided over mythic, timbered mead-halls, turning spits of salted boar over roaring hearths. I would later learn this was Sir Henry Curtis, a man of actual Danish blood, whose very presence seemed to demand a return to older, more deeply rooted domestic traditions.

Beside him stood Captain John Good, a stout, dark, and immaculately neat naval officer of thirty-one. I have always found a unique purity in navy men; the wide, acoustic expanse of the open ocean and the breath of God’s winds seem to scour the industrial bitterness from their minds, washing them clean. Good had been turned out of Her Majesty’s employ with half-pay and the barren honor of a commander’s rank—another victim of a utilitarian empire discarding its most seasoned hands just as they reach the prime of life. He possessed two pristine sets of false teeth and an eyeglass he wiped religiously, yet beneath that fastidious exterior was a man who understood the hunger of displacement.

As evening closed in, a damp, aggravated mist drove us below deck. The *Dunkeld*, a flat-bottomed punt of a vessel, rolled violently. I stood near the engines for warmth, watching the pendulum swing, though the grinding of the gears was a torment to my acoustic sensibilities. 

"That pendulum’s wrong; it is not properly weighted," a testy voice barked over the mechanical din. It was Captain Good. "If she had really rolled to the degree that thing pointed to, she would never have rolled again. Just like these merchant skippers, so confoundedly careless."

The dinner bell, a sharp, metallic clang, thankfully interrupted his critique. We descended to the dining saloon, taking our seats opposite Sir Henry Curtis. The meal was a typical shipboard affair, entirely lacking the passionate nuance of a slow-braised venison or the olfactory depth of a well-spiced tagine, but we made do, treating it as the necessary ritual of shared sustenance. As we ate, the Captain and I fell into talk of elephant hunting, our voices forming a pleasant counter-rhythm to the clattering of the cutlery.

"Ah, sir," a nearby passenger called out, chewing a rather over-boiled piece of mutton. "You've reached the right man for that; Hunter Quatermain should be able to tell you about elephants if anybody can."

Sir Henry started, his deep voice rumbling like distant thunder—a sound that resonated with a rich acoustic gravity against the superficial chatter of the dining room. "Excuse me, sir, but is your name Allan Quatermain?"

I nodded. The big man made no further remark, but I heard him mutter "fortunate" into his thick beard. 

After the meal, Sir Henry invited us to his deck cabin. He ordered the steward to bring a bottle of whisky. When it arrived, we poured our measures. The liquor was heavy with the scent of peat and smoked oak, a complex olfactory landscape that instantly warmed the palate and settled the stomach after the ship’s relentless rolling. 

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry began, his great arms resting on the table as the lamplight caught his grey eyes. "The year before last, you were, I believe, at a place called Bamangwato."

"I was," I replied, savoring the smoky finish of the whisky on my tongue, the warmth spreading in a slow, circular rhythm. 

"You were trading there, were you not?" Captain Good put in quickly.

"I was. I took up a wagon-load of goods, made a camp outside the settlement, and stopped till I had sold them."

Sir Henry leaned forward, an anxious hunger in his gaze. "Did you happen to meet a man called Neville there?"

"Oh, yes. He outspanned alongside of me for a fortnight to rest his oxen. We shared the space of our fires. I had a letter from a lawyer a few months back asking of him, which I answered."

"Yes," Sir Henry said. "Your letter was forwarded to me. You wrote that Neville left Bamangwato in May, intending to trek to Inyati, sell his wagon, and proceed on foot. You noted that he did sell it, for six months later you saw the wagon with a Portuguese trader, who told you the white man and his Kafir hunter, Jim, had started for the deep interior."

"Yes."

A heavy silence filled the cabin, broken only by the rhythmic, unnatural pounding of the ship’s engines pushing through the waves. 

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said suddenly. "I suppose you know or can guess nothing more of the reasons of my—of Mr. Neville’s journey to the northward, or as to what point that journey was directed?"

"I heard something," I said cautiously. The taste of the whisky suddenly felt sharper. Some secrets, like the ancient, untranslatable recipes of the veld, are not meant to be shared lightly.

Sir Henry and Captain Good exchanged a look. "I am going to tell you a story," Sir Henry said, "and ask your advice. The agent who forwarded your letter assured me of your discretion."

I bowed and drank some whisky and water to mask my hesitation. 

"Mr. Neville was my brother."

The resemblance clicked immediately—the same deep-set eyes, the same underlying, ancestral timber. 

"He was my only and younger brother," Sir Henry continued, his voice dropping into a mournful resonance. "Five years ago, a bitter misfortune befell us. We quarreled over our inheritance. Our father died intestate. Under the dominant, unyielding laws of England—laws that strip away the subjective bonds of blood and replace them with cold, real property statutes—everything came to me as the eldest. George was left without a penny."

Captain Good nodded vigorously, perfectly in time with a heavy roll of the ship. 

"It was my duty to provide for him, to keep our family table whole," Sir Henry said, his voice laced with the ache of a broken domestic harmony. "But in my anger, I waited for him to make advances, and he made none. Without a word, he drew out his paltry savings, took the name Neville, and vanished to South Africa to find a fortune. Time passed, and my letters went unanswered. I found out, Mr. Quatermain, that blood is thicker than water."

"That's true," I said, thinking of my boy Harry, eating his tasteless food amidst the clamor of London.

"I found out that I would give half my fortune to know my brother George was safe, and that I should sit across from him again."

"But you never did, Curtis," Captain Good jerked out.

"So, to cut a long story short, I made up my mind to come out and look for him myself," Sir Henry concluded, "and Captain Good was so kind as to come with me."

"Yes," the Captain added. "Nothing else to do. Turned out by my Lords of the Admiralty to starve on half-pay. And now, perhaps, sir, you will tell us what you know or have heard of the gentleman called Neville."
