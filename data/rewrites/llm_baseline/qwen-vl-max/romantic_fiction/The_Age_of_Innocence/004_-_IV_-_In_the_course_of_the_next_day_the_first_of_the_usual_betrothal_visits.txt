IV. The next day brought the first of the customary betrothal visits, a ritual in New York that followed rules as rigid as they were unspoken. Newland Archer began by accompanying his mother and sister to call on Mrs. Welland, then joined her and May for a visit to old Mrs. Manson Mingott’s home, where they would receive the blessing of the family matriarch—a tradition both solemn and inevitable.

For Newland, visiting Mrs. Mingott was always a peculiar pleasure. Her house stood like a relic of another time, though not quite as ancient as some of the grand residences along University Place or lower Fifth Avenue. Those homes belonged firmly to the 1830s: dark rosewood consoles, fireplaces with black marble mantels, round arches, and towering mahogany bookcases filled with leather-bound volumes. But Mrs. Mingott had built later, and she had long since cast aside the heavy furniture of her youth, replacing it with the lighter, more ornate trappings of the Second Empire—frivolous upholstery, gilded mirrors, and curving lines that seemed almost theatrical.

She herself sat in the window of her ground-floor sitting room, as if waiting patiently for the world to catch up with her. There was no urgency in her gaze, only quiet confidence. She believed—without doubt—that one day the rough streets, wooden greenhouses, and goat-topped rocks surrounding her house would give way to elegant townhouses as stately as her own, perhaps even more so. And the cobblestones, rattled over by clattering omnibuses, would be replaced by smooth Parisian asphalt. Until then, she waited. And because everyone who mattered came to see *her*, she did not feel isolated. Her rooms were always full, her suppers as lavish as those of the Beauforts, without ever needing to add a single course to her menu.

Her body had changed dramatically over the years. Once a plump but active woman with delicate feet and ankles, she had been overwhelmed in middle age by an immense accumulation of flesh—like lava swallowing a city. Now she was vast, almost monumental, a natural force. Yet she accepted this transformation with the same calm resolve she had shown through every trial. In her advanced age, she looked at herself in the mirror and saw a nearly flawless expanse of firm pink and white skin, with just a small face faintly visible beneath—like a fossil awaiting excavation. A cascade of double chins led down to a snowy bosom covered in fine muslin, held in place by a miniature portrait of her late husband. Around her, waves of black silk flowed over the arms of her enormous chair, while two tiny white hands rested atop them, poised like gulls on a sea of fabric.

Long ago, the weight of her frame had made climbing stairs impossible. With characteristic independence, she had moved her reception rooms upstairs and settled herself—defying all social norms—on the ground floor. This meant that anyone seated beside her in the sitting room could glance through an open door and a looped-back damask curtain into her bedroom: a low, wide bed upholstered like a sofa, a vanity with lace trimmings, and a gilt-framed mirror. Visitors were both startled and intrigued by the arrangement—something straight out of French novels, where scandal lived in proximity, where rooms opened onto one another like secrets. It reminded Newland of the love scenes he’d imagined in *Monsieur de Camors*—scenes he now pictured unfolding in this very room. He smiled inwardly, amused by the irony: such a virtuous woman, living in a setting that whispered of forbidden affairs. Still, he thought with respect, if she had wanted a lover, she would have found one. She had the courage for it.

To everyone’s relief, the Countess Olenska was not present when the couple arrived. Mrs. Mingott said she had gone out—an odd choice, given the bright sunlight and the shopping hour. For a woman whose reputation was already fragile, such a public outing seemed indiscreet. But at least it spared the newly engaged pair from any awkwardness, from the shadow their future might cast over Ellen’s past.

The visit unfolded smoothly, as expected. Old Mrs. Mingott was delighted by the engagement—long anticipated, carefully discussed in family councils. When May showed her the ring—a large sapphire set in invisible claws—she admired it warmly. “It’s the new setting,” Mrs. Welland explained gently, glancing at Newland. “Of course it shows the stone beautifully, but it looks a little bare to old-fashioned eyes.”

“Old-fashioned eyes? I hope you don’t mean mine, my dear,” Mrs. Mingott replied, lifting the ring to her clear, glassless eyes. “I like all the novelties.” She returned the jewel. “Very handsome. Very generous. In my day, a cameo set in pearls was enough. But it’s the hand that sets off the ring, isn’t it, Mr. Archer?” She waved one of her tiny hands—nails sharp, wrists encircled by rolls of fat like ivory bracelets. “Mine was modeled in Rome by Ferrigiani. You should have May’s done. No doubt he will. Her hand is large—modern sports spread the joints—but the skin is white.”

Then she turned abruptly to Newland. “When’s the wedding?”

“Oh—” Mrs. Welland murmured, while Newland, smiling at May, answered, “As soon as possible, if you’ll back me up, Mrs. Mingott.”

“We must give them time to get to know each other,” Mrs. Welland interjected, with the proper air of reluctant caution.

“Know each other? Nonsense!” Mrs. Mingott declared. “Everyone in New York has always known everyone. Let the young man have his way. Don’t wait till the bubble’s off the wine. Marry them before Lent. I may catch pneumonia any winter now, and I want to give the wedding breakfast.”

The remarks were met with polite laughter, surprise, and gratitude. The visit was drawing to a close when the door opened—and in walked the Countess Olenska, in bonnet and mantle, followed by the unexpected figure of Julius Beaufort.

A murmur of greeting passed among the women. Mrs. Mingott extended her hand, showing Ferrigiani’s model to the banker. “Ha! Beaufort, this is a rare favor!”

“Thanks,” Beaufort said, his usual arrogance relaxed but present. “I wish it happened more often. I’m generally tied down. But I met the Countess Ellen in Madison Square, and she was kind enough to let me walk home with her.”

“Aha!” Mrs. Mingott exclaimed. “I hope the house will be gayer now that Ellen’s here!” She gestured toward a yellow armchair. “Sit down—sit down, Beaufort. Now I’ve got you, I want a good gossip. I hear your ball was magnificent. And I understand you invited Mrs. Lemuel Struthers?”

“Well, if you and Regina invite her, the thing’s settled,” Mrs. Mingott said, turning her attention fully to Beaufort. “We need new blood, new money—and I hear she’s still very good-looking.”

In the hall, as Mrs. Welland and May put on their furs, Newland noticed Ellen Olenska watching him with a soft, questioning smile.

“Of course you already know,” he said, returning her look with a shy laugh. “About May and me.”

“She scolded me last night at the Opera for not telling you,” he added. “She gave me orders to tell you we were engaged—but I couldn’t, not in that crowd.”

The smile deepened, spreading from her eyes to her lips. She looked younger, more like the bold, brown-eyed Ellen Mingott of his childhood. “Of course I know. Yes. And I’m so glad. But one doesn’t tell such things first in a crowd.”

The ladies were at the door. She reached out her hand. “Good-bye. Come and see me sometime,” she said, still looking at him.

In the carriage, as they rolled down Fifth Avenue, they spoke of Mrs. Mingott—her age, her spirit, her remarkable presence. No one mentioned Ellen. But Newland knew what his mother was thinking: *It’s a mistake for Ellen to be seen, the very day after her return, walking up Fifth Avenue at the busiest hour with Julius Beaufort.* And in his own mind, he added: *And she should know that a man who’s just engaged doesn’t spend his time visiting married women. But then again, maybe in the world she’s come from, that’s how things are done—maybe that’s all they do.*

And despite the cosmopolitan views he prided himself on, he felt a sudden gratitude—to be a New Yorker, to be marrying into his own world, to belong to a society where rules were clear, and honor was measured in silence.
