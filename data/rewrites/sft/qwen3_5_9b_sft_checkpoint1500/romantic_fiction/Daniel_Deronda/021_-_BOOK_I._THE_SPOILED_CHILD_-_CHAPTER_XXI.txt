Knowledge is often called power, yet few have truly grasped the terrifying magnitude of Ignorance. While wisdom slowly constructs worlds through patience and accumulation, Ignorance can dismantle them in a heartbeat. It burns the records of ages to fuel a single meal, and when it arrives drunk on the seventh day of creation, wielding a match and a jar of oil, it shrivels the vibrant tapestry of life into a charred ruin. True knowledge operates with a conscience, understanding the limits of what *must* be and what *may* be; Ignorance, however, is a blind giant. Left unchecked, it would tear down the pillars supporting human goodness and plunge joy into the darkness of a buried Babylon. In the growth of any individual soul, a lack of insight into the bonds between events—the false confidence that one can compel outcomes through sheer will—is like a visual defect that sees distant mountains as steps away. Such blindness precipitates the soul onto a cliff of destruction.

It was past ten o'clock when Gwendolen Harleth arrived at the station for Offendene, her gloomy journey from Leubronn finally complete. No carriage or friend awaited her; in her telegram from Dover, she had requested a later train, unable to bear the lingering wait at the London terminus. Now, she stood alone, deposited as a *femme sole* amidst her large trunks, forced to wait for a vehicle summoned from the "Railway Inn"—a sprawling establishment that felt more like a prison cell than a lodging. The waiting room was a study in dreariness: dirty paint, a dusty decanter of flat water, and large, moralizing texts urging repentance. These were merely the opening notes of her family's tragedy, and Gwendolen hurried to the outer door, seeking the lane and fields. Yet, even the sunshine seemed melancholy. Autumn leaves and grass shivered in the wind, while a pair of old hens, having lost their chicks, cackled uselessly, turning up their feathers in confusion. Nearby, a railway official observed her with a look of innocent condescension, his gaze heavy with the judgment of a man who recognized her poverty. Beside him, a laborer prepared a dirty, ancient barouche. To Gwendolen, these trivial details—the grime, the isolation, the lack of funds—accumulated with crushing force, shaping a mood that felt like a grim theory. Solitary at this ugly, remote spot, stripped of her expected income, she felt the sting of a new consciousness: poverty. What use was charm, intelligence, or resolve if they could not buy a decent carriage? As the vehicle lumbered toward home, she ruminated on her misery, convinced that life itself was a series of humiliations.

Her driving vision had been one of escape: the family moving abroad, finding some sliver of income, living quietly in a dull foreign land while she aged gracefully away from her mother's increasing melancholy. But now, staring at the empty road ahead, the illusion shattered. Weariness and disgust replaced her dreams. She felt a strange, hollow futility in being "anything" at all. Events spun wildly, and men, she realized with a sudden, bitter clarity, were hateful. Yet, beneath this hatred lay a shift in her feelings toward Grandcourt. It was one thing to hate stolen goods; it was another to hate them because they blocked one's path. Anger flared within her—not just at the theft, but at Grandcourt for being the obstacle that prevented her marriage, the architect of her current desolation.

As the barouche crested the hill and approached Offendene, the windows framed the house, and a figure appeared beneath the portico. Gwendolen's heart leaped. It was her mother, Mrs. Davilow, her face lined with fresh, deep sadness. Gwendolen rushed from the carriage and threw her arms around her mother's neck, momentarily drowning her own sorrows in the weight of her mother's grief. Behind them followed the four younger sisters, their faces etched with sorrow, each carrying a small, isolated world that mattered only to herself. Yet, strangely, Gwendolen's presence acted as a balm to their weakness; her quick, authoritative dismissal—"Now go away, girls!"—offered them a semblance of order. Miss Merry, usually so meek, busied herself with the trunks and the coachman's pay, while Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen ascended the stairs, shutting themselves inside the black-and-yellow bedroom.

"My dear, never mind," Gwendolen whispered, pressing her handkerchief to her mother's weeping cheeks. "I will do something. I will be someone. Things will come right."

She spoke with a conviction that stirred a wave of compassionate tenderness, transforming vague hopes into concrete resolve. Her mother, forgetting the depths of their plight, gazed at her daughter with a sort of adoration. "Bless you, my good, good darling! I can be happy, if you can!"

Yet, by afternoon, the tide receded. The comforting illusions vanished, revealing the old, slippery rocks and weedy realities of their situation. The "announcement" of misfortune had become a tyrannical inhabitant. For now, however, Gwendolen clung to the comforts of the house: the roomy silence, the ready-to-hand luxuries of her toilette, and a private tray bearing her favorite foods. She had insisted on solitude, wishing to be alone with her mother.

When Gwendolen descended to the drawing-room, fresh as a swan, she leaned against the cushions beside her mother, ready to confront the facts. "Mamma, what exactly have you thought of doing?"

"Oh, my dear," Mrs. Davilow replied, "the first step is to leave this house. Mr. Haynes is delighted to have it back, and Lord Brackenshaw's agent, Bazley, will arrange the terms."

"But surely, Mamma, Lord Brackenshaw would let you stay rent-free?" Gwendolen asked, her instincts failing her in matters of finance.

"My love, Lord Brackenshaw is in Scotland and knows nothing of us. Nor would your uncle wish to approach him. Besides, how could we manage without servants or money to heat the house? We must move quickly. We have nothing but our clothes."

"And where shall we go?" Gwendolen asked, a sharp edge returning to her voice. Fear, cold and new, coursed through her veins.

"It is decided," Mrs. Davilow said, her voice trembling. "We shall go to Sawyer's Cottage."

Gwendolen went silent, her face paling with a justifiable rage. "That is impossible! Something else must have been arranged. My uncle will not allow it. I will not submit!"

"My sweet child, what else could be done? Your uncle is kind, but he suffers too, and he has his own family to raise. Do you understand? We have absolutely nothing, save what your uncle and aunt provide. They have been wise and active, but we must try to earn something. I and the girls intend to work a table-cloth border for the Ladies' Charity at Winchester, and a communion cloth for Pennicote Church."

Mrs. Davilow spoke timidly, dreading the shock she delivered to her daughter, yet hoping Gwendolen would accept the necessity. Gwendolen, however, refused to listen to deprecation. "Sawyer's Cottage? Where an exciseman lived? Surely some other place exists!"

"No, indeed, dear. Houses are scarce. We must be thankful for privacy. It has two parlors and four bedrooms. You may sit alone whenever you wish."

Gwendolen, her sympathy for her mother exhausted, paid no heed. "I cannot believe all your property has vanished so quickly, Mamma. It hasn't been a week since you wrote!"

"The news came much earlier," Mrs. Davilow admitted, hesitating. "But I wished to spare you the pain until it was unavoidable."

"Oh, how vexatious!" Gwendolen cried, flushing with anger. "If I had known, I could have brought home the money I won! Because I stayed, I lost it. I had nearly two hundred pounds!" She paused, her voice rising impetuously. "Everything goes against me. People come near me only to blight me."

In referring to "people," she included Daniel Deronda. Had he not intervened, she would have returned to the gaming tables with a few napoleons and likely recovered her losses. Instead, fate had intervened.

"We must resign ourselves to the will of Providence, my child," Mrs. Davilow murmured, startled by the mention of gambling but afraid to press further. She suspected "people" referred to Grandcourt, a name her lips dared not utter.

"But I do not resign myself!" Gwendolen snapped. "I will fight it. Why do you call people's wickedness 'Providence'? You wrote it was Mr. Lassman's fault. Did he run away with all the money?"

"No, dear, you misunderstand. There were vast speculations—he intended to profit. It involved mines and risky ventures. He simply risked too much."

"I don't call that Providence! It was his improvidence with *our* money, and he must be punished! Can we not sue to recover our fortune? My uncle must take action!"

"My dear child, the law cannot restore money lost in such games. Your uncle says it is milk spilled upon the ground. Moreover, one needs a fortune to hire lawyers; there is no law for the ruined. Our money has mingled with others'; many suffer alongside us."
