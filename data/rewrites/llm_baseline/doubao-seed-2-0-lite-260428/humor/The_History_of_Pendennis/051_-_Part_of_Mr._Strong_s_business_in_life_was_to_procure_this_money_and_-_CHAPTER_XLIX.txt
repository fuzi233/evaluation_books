Our man Pen spotted Mr. Bows loitering in the porter’s lodge doorway and yelped “How d’ye do, Mr. Bows!” so loud and cheery you’d think he hadn’t been caught red-handed sneaking off to Vauxhall the night before to gallivant with the lodge keeper’s pretty daughter. He slapped on a dashing, too-offhand grin to shake the old man’s hand, but that didn’t stop a bright cherry-red blush from creeping up his ears — which Fanny immediately mirrored, her cheeks flushing so fast you’d swear someone held a match to a ripe strawberry.
The air in the room immediately curdled into the kind of guilty, thick silence you get after you knock over your aunt’s best china and everyone’s pretending they didn’t see who did it. Pen scrambled to fix it, making such a racket knocking over a stack of dog-eared song sheets and a half-full mug of spruce beer that you’d think a whole team of street porters had stumbled through. The noise vanished quick enough, but the gloom only deepened, like you lit a single match in a damp coal cellar and all you got for your trouble was extra smoke.
Pen tried to do a funny impression of Captain Costigan yelling at the Vauxhall ticket-taker the night before, and it was terrible. No mortal man alive could replicate Costigan’s slurred, whiskey-warmed brogue anyway, and this attempt was so far off base Mrs. Bolton couldn’t tell if he was mocking the ticket-taker or the Captain. Fanny forced a giggle that squeaked like a rusted door hinge. Old Bows scowled so hard his eyebrows furrowed deep enough to grow turnips in, looking exactly as glum as he did mid-orchestra gig when some drunk in the pit yells out for a song he’s already played three times. Pen’s joke died a miserable, flickering death, his voice dwindling to a squeak before cutting out entirely. The whole room went so quiet you could count the clink of the passing inn porter’s boot heels on the flagstones under the archway, every single one.
“You said you were paying me a visit, sir,” Bows said, his voice flat as a week-old pancake. “Would you do me the honour of walking up to my chambers? They’re a bit high up, but —”
“Oh, I live in a garret myself!” Pen cut in, huffing. “Shepherd’s Inn is twice as cheery as my grim little Lamb Court digs!”
“Knew you had third-floor rooms,” Bows said, still deadpan. “Was just going to say — no offence intended — the air three flights up is far wholesomer for gentlemen than the air hanging round a porter’s lodge, isn’t it?”
Pen blew up like an offended hot air balloon, already spoiling for a fight the way only guilty men are. “Sir! Am I not allowed to choose my own company without —?”
“You were so kind as to say you’d come pay my humble hovel a call,” Bows said, his sad old voice not shifting an inch. “Shall I lead the way? Mr. Pendennis and I are old friends, Mrs. Bolton — knew each other when he was still in knee breeches. Crossed paths before he was even old enough to buy a penny lollipop.”
The old man pointed to the door with a trembling finger, hat in his other hand, striking a pose so dramatic it could’ve been pulled straight from the third-rate melodrama he’d played a banished duke in 40 years prior. He wasn’t faking it, though, Pen knew that — even if he wanted to roll his eyes at Bows’s over-the-top theatrical airs.
“Fine, fine, you win, let’s go,” Pen said, marching over to say goodbye. “Good day, Mrs Bolton! Goodbye, Miss Fanny — I’ll always remember our night at Vauxhall. Don’t you worry, those theatre tickets are good as yours already.” He squeezed her hand so hard he nearly crushed her fingers, and she squeezed back just as tight, and then he was out the door.
“What a nice young gentleman, isn’t he?” Mrs Bolton cooed, craning round the curtain to watch him trudge up the inn courtyard with Bows.
“D’you really think so, ma?” Fanny mumbled, staring at the floor.
“Reminds me of that city gentleman I knew back at Sadler’s Wells,” Mrs Bolton babbled, wiping mugs and tucking them into the corner cupboard. “Used to drive up in a little tilbury with a white top hat, looked just like him — only the fellow’s whiskers were black, not that pretty red shade Mr. P. has. He was head over heels for Emly Budd, the dancer who played Columbine that one summer, ran off and married her in a secret ceremony at St. Bartholomew’s, and now she keeps her own carriage! Saw her name in the paper last month, patroness of the Washywomen’s Asylum Ball, fancy as anything. And you know Lady Mirabel — Captain Costigan’s daughter — she was professional once too, and look how far she got!”
Fanny barely heard half of her ma’s chattering. Costigan, it turned out, had forgotten all the old details of Pen’s modest income he’d learned back at Chatteris years prior, and had rambled to Mrs Bolton the night before about Fairoaks Park’s endless acres, Pen’s wealthy Major uncle, and all the land and money Arthur supposedly had stacked up in bank accounts. By the time they finished folding the dinner cloth together, Mrs Bolton was already daydreaming of Fanny riding in her own private carriage, no more porter’s lodge chores, no more mopping the step at 6 a.m. She tapped Fanny under the chin and kissed her, and Fanny blushed so hard her whole face looked dipped in strawberry jam — this time not from embarrassment, but from pure, giddy hope.
Mrs Bolton popped on her bonnet and grabbed her market basket, saying she had errands to run, and told Fanny to mind the lodge. The second her ma was out the door, Fanny glued herself to the window that faced Bows’s front step, not tearing her eyes away for a second. Her two little sisters Betsy-Jane and Ameliar-Ann were making up nonsense stories about fire and brimstone from a very boring religious tract they held upside down in the corner, and Fanny didn’t hear a single word they said.
At last she gave a little jolt, her face lighting up: Pen was coming out! But her smile died instantly. Bows was right on his heels, trailing him like a grumpy little shadow. Pen tipped his hat and gave one tiny nod in through the window, but he didn’t stop, he didn’t hang around, he trundled right under the archway and out of sight.
Four minutes later (or four hours, it felt like to Fanny) Bows trundled back alone. “Where’s your ma, dear?” he asked.
“Search me!” Fanny huffed, tossing her head, her needle stabbing at her embroidery like she was poking every last one of Bows’s annoying interfering habits. “I don’t follow her round the street every time she steps out, do I?”
“Am I my mother’s keeper, eh?” Bows said, that familiar sad, dry edge to his voice. “C’mere you two, Betsy-Jane and Ameliar-Ann — got a gingerbread cake for whoever can read their letters best, and another one for the runner-up.” The little girls scampered off cackling with their cakes after their A-B-C test, leaving Fanny and Bows alone in the lodge. Bows sat where he could see the courtyard entrance, but the only person who walked through the door was Mrs Bolton, coming home from market with a basket of turnips and mutton, no sign of Pen anywhere.
Up in their shared third-floor rooms, the visit between Pen and Bows was far from fun — mostly because Captain Costigan was camped out in the middle of the parlour the whole time, having only just stumbled out of his bedroom ten minutes before Pen arrived. The man reeked so strongly of whiskey-scented cologne you’d think he’d dunked his whole head in a still, and his hands shook so violently it was a miracle he hadn’t sliced his own throat shaving that morning.
While Costigan’s half of the room was a disaster zone of crumpled playbills and empty liquor bottles, Bows’s side was neat as a pin: his threadbare wardrobe hung behind a curtain, his books and sheet music stacked perfectly on his shelves, and a tattered old lithograph of Emily Fotheringay in her role as Mrs Haller hung over his bed, her messy, girlish signature scrawled in the corner. Bows had taught Emily to sing and write once, and when she married the rich baronet and left him behind, he’d poured all that quiet old affection into teaching Fanny instead — the equivalent of a sailor clinging to a raft after a shipwreck, or a man who loses his whole dog and adopts three very spoiled tabby cats. He was heartily unimpressed to see Pen back, sniffing around his new little protégée.
Costigan, for his part, was completely oblivious that he was crashing a tense private talk, gabbing Pen’s ear off for an hour about how he was invited to join Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel at Baden-Baden, how the Grand Duke would roll out the red carpet for him, how he’d been a guest of honour at Dublin Castle back in the viceroy’s heyday. For a minute Pen got all soft and nostalgic, remembering the days back in Chatteris when he was a starstruck teen who’d eaten up every one of Costigan’s wild stories, and he shook both their hands warmly when he left, completely forgetting Fanny Bolton existed for ten whole minutes.
Then he spotted Bows hobbling after him down the stairs, determined to follow him all the way out of Shepherd’s Inn to make sure he didn’t sneak back to the lodge. Pen huffed at first, annoyed that the old fellow was acting like his chaperone, and then burst out laughing on the Strand — a guilty, embarrassed little laugh that made passersby glance at him like he’d just stolen a pork pie from the corner bakery. He blushed so hard he had to turn his face away.
He tried every trick in the book to shake that weird, fuzzy guilty feeling: he panted all the way up the steep stairs to the top of the Panorama, but Care huffed right up behind him, no matter how fast he climbed. He went to his club, wrote a snarky, witty long letter home to his mother and Laura, and didn’t breathe a single word about Vauxhall or Fanny, knowing they’d tease him for being a hopeless fool. He tried to read a novel, ducked away from the unbearably dull Jawkins who wanted to rant about sheep farming for three whole hours, and wandered home past a comic theatre plastered with bright red signs screaming NONSTOP ROARS! STUNNING FARCE! GOOD OLD ENGLISH FUN! He ducked into the pit to watch, but the usual gags — the pretty actress in men’s tights, the funny old actor dressed up as a cackling crone — all felt flat and dull, not a single laugh coming out. He remembered how he’d thought that same show was the funniest thing on the whole planet his first week in London, and it felt like that had been a hundred years ago, not a handful of months.
