VII. Descending

Even now, the memory of that night clings to me like a shadow—cold, unrelenting, and impossible to shake. It was not the fleeting dread of a nightmare, nor the kind of fear that fades with morning light. This horror deepened with time, seeping into the very walls of my room, staining the furniture, the silence, the air itself. I could not bear to be alone after dawn broke. The thought of solitude filled me with a dread too vast for words.

I longed to tell my father—but two fears held me back. One, that he would laugh, dismissing my tale as mere fancy, and I could not endure such mockery. The other, that he might suspect I had fallen victim to the same mysterious illness that had swept through our village—a sickness whispered about in hushed tones, one that left no trace but death. I felt no such symptoms myself, yet I feared his frail health might not withstand the shock. So I kept silent.

My companions—Madame Perrodon, kind and gentle, and Mademoiselle Lafontaine, lively and quick-witted—sensed my distress. They noticed how pale I grew, how often I trembled without cause. At last, I confessed everything, the whole terrible truth of what had happened.

Mademoiselle laughed lightly. “Ah, so it’s true! Martin says the lime tree walk behind Carmilla’s window is haunted.”
“Nonsense!” Madame snapped, though her voice lacked conviction. “Who spreads such tales?”
“Martin,” replied Mademoiselle, “who saw the same woman twice before sunrise, walking slowly down the path.”
“Oh, well then,” said Madame, “perhaps it’s just cows being milked in the fields.”
“I suppose,” Mademoiselle shrugged, “but Martin is terrified—and never have I seen a man more easily frightened.”

“I mustn’t mention it to Carmilla,” I interrupted, “she can see the walk from her window, and she’s far more timid than I am.”

That day, Carmilla came down later than usual. As soon as we were alone, she spoke.
“I was so frightened last night,” she whispered, her eyes wide. “I dreamed something black crept around my bed. I woke in terror—sure I saw a dark figure near the fireplace. But I reached under my pillow… and touched the charm. The moment I did, it vanished. I know—I *know*—if I hadn’t had it, something dreadful would have come for me. Just like those poor people they spoke of…”

She looked at me, pleading. “Tell me—did you have it?”

“No,” I admitted. “I’d dropped it into a vase in the drawing room. But tonight, I’ll take it with me. You believe in it so strongly.”

And yet, even now, I cannot explain how I managed to lie alone in my room that night, heart pounding, every nerve taut. I pinned the charm to my pillow, and slept—deeply, soundly, more peacefully than I had in weeks. The next night was the same. Sleep came swiftly, dreamless, restorative.

But when I woke, a strange lassitude settled over me—soft, heavy, almost pleasant. A melancholy, quiet and profound, wrapped around my thoughts like a shroud. I did not resist it. In fact, I welcomed it. Thoughts of death began to drift into my mind—not with fear, but with a strange serenity. I felt myself slipping away, slowly, gently, as if sinking into water. And somehow, I did not wish to rise.

I refused to admit I was ill. I refused to send for the doctor. I refused to tell my father. I told myself I was only tired, perhaps nervous. But inside, something was changing—something beyond comprehension.

Carmilla grew more attentive, more tender. Her affection deepened into something intense, almost unnerving. She would gaze at me for long moments, her eyes fixed, her breath shallow, as if drinking in my presence. It unsettled me—like watching someone on the edge of madness.

Yet I did not protest. I let her stay close. I let her touch me. I let the weakness grow.

For some nights, I slept deeply. But each morning brought the same weariness, the same dull ache in my bones. I was no longer the girl I once was. A new sensation began to haunt my dreams—first faint, then growing stronger: a cold, electric thrill, like stepping into a river current at midnight. Then came the dreams—vague, endless, full of shadows and voices I could not name. I remembered only darkness, and whispers, and a single voice—deep, clear, female—speaking from afar, slow and solemn, sending chills through my soul.

Sometimes, I felt a hand glide softly across my cheek, down my neck. Sometimes, warm lips brushed my skin—lingering, tender, climbing higher until they reached my throat. Then, stillness. My breath caught. My heart pounded. A sob rose in my chest, swelling into a choking pressure. Then—darkness. Unconsciousness.

Three weeks had passed since it began. My face had grown pale, my eyes hollowed, shadowed beneath. My father asked again and again if I was ill. I insisted I was fine. In truth, I had no pain. No fever. No visible sickness. Only this creeping emptiness, this strange peace that felt like surrender.

It could not be the oupire—the vampire sickness the peasants spoke of. That took days, not weeks. And it ended quickly. I was not dying fast. I was fading.

Carmilla, too, complained of dreams—of heat, of feverish visions—but nothing like mine. Hers were restless, uneasy. Mine were terrifying. If I had known what was happening, I would have screamed for help. But I was numb. Drugged by something unseen, something invisible, something ancient.

Then came the dream that changed everything.

This time, instead of the distant voice, I heard one close—sweet, soft, yet chilling:
“Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin.”

And suddenly—light.

I sat up, gasping. There stood Carmilla, at the foot of my bed, in her white nightdress, drenched in blood—from her chin to her feet, a crimson stain spreading across her skin like ink in water.

I shrieked.

I leapt from the bed, stumbling into the hallway, screaming for help. Madame and Mademoiselle rushed out, lanterns in hand. The lamp burned bright in the hall. They saw me—pale, trembling, wild-eyed—and understood at once.

We ran to Carmilla’s door. We knocked. We pounded. We called her name. No answer.

The door was locked.

Panic seized us. We backed away, hearts racing, and rang the bell frantically. But my father’s room was far off—beyond hearing. To reach him meant crossing the house in the dark, and none of us dared.

Servants arrived, drawn by the noise. I had thrown on my dressing gown and slippers. My companions were already dressed. We rushed to the door together, shouting, demanding entry.

They forced the lock.

We stepped inside, lights raised high.

The room was untouched. Exactly as I had left it. The bed neatly made. The chair where she’d sat. The mirror, the dresser—everything in place.

But Carmilla was gone.

Not asleep. Not resting. Gone.

And the blood—where had it come from? Where had it gone?

Only silence answered us.
