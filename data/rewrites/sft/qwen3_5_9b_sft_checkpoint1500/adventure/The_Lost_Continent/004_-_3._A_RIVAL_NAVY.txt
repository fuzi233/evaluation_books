The coast of Atlantis finally rose into view, a vision so stark and real that it seemed to suspend the laws of the gods themselves. Though Tob, guided by his modern instruments, had navigated with a precision that bordered on the miraculous, landing mere leagues from the shore, the final stretch required a tedious return journey up a narrow arm of the sea. This detour led us directly to the harbor where the great city, the capital of Atlantis, lay hidden.

It was the sight of the land, and the sweet, heavy breath of earth and growing herbs carried on the breeze, that saved us. After months of crossing oceans, many of our crews had perished, and those who remained were reduced to husks by scurvy, weakened by the unnatural diet of salted meat and hardtack. Yet, the mere smell of soil and the green horizon acted as a miracle. Men who had been helpless logs, unable to lift a hand even under the lash, suddenly sprang to life. They scrubbed the decks, hauled ropes, and stood ready to fight for their lives and their ships when the need arose.

Since my deposition in Yucatan, despite Tatho's reassurances, a shadow of doubt had lingered in my mind regarding how Atlantis would receive me. Would Empress Phorenice grant me an audience, or would she order my execution the moment I stepped onto the sand? I had tried to dismiss these fears, placing my trust entirely in the Lord Sun, who sat above the Empress. If my head were to fall, it would be by divine decree, and I had spent the voyage studying the higher mysteries with a clear mind, unconcerned by earthly politics.

But when our fleet retraced its path far enough to reveal the two vast headlands marking the entrance to the inland waters, the illusion of peace shattered. Only two days' sail from the capital, we encountered a rival navy waiting specifically to intercept us. Their ships rode at anchor in a protected bay, yet scouts atop the cliffs had already signaled our approach. As we rounded the headland, they surged forward to block our passage.

Our force had dwindled to five storm-battered vessels, the rest lost to storms or decimated by disease. Against us stood three magnificent ships and six galleys, pristine, black, and gleaming. In contrast, our fleet was ragged, weather-beaten, and fouled with seaweed. We flew the colors of Tatho and Deucalion openly, displaying our origins without shame, while the enemy advanced without banners or standards, moving with the silent, shameless aggression of creatures born to war.

A fight seemed inevitable. The seas knew no mercy, and brothers often hunted brothers when the wind blew them apart, especially when one returned laden with plunder. Tob, with his characteristic system, quickly brought our "Bear" into fighting trim, and the other captains did likewise, forming a compact squadron. Neither side wished to surrender now that the journey's end was in sight.

Our Lord Sun shone brilliantly, driving the machinery with relentless speed, as if He favored the clash. The two navies closed rapidly, the galleys holding back to maintain formation. But when we were within a hundred ship-lengths, the enemy halted. One of their galleys broke rank, flying green boughs from her mast—a signal for parley—and sought a truce.

Given our battered state, unnecessary combat was a luxury we could not afford. Hoarse shouts passed between the fleets, and both sides stopped. Tob raised a withered branch to signal readiness for talk and respect for ambassadors. The enemy galley drew alongside, swinging until its stern scraped our shield rail. A young, robust man, dressed in finery and healthy from land provisions, climbed aboard. He sneered at our tattered appearance, looking at us with arrogant confidence before nodding to Tob.

"Old pot-mate," he called out, "your woman waits for you on the quay in Atlantis, with four children at her heels. I saw her less than a month ago."

"You didn't come this distance just to gossip," Tob replied, his voice steady. "I've shared enough drinks with you, Dason, to know your jokes."

"I merely pointed out that your home awaits you, wife and children included."

"I am not a man who forgets," Tob said grimly. "Because they are always in my thoughts, I have sailed over countless pirates, men who would have accepted slavery if I had been alone."

"Oh, I know you're desperate," Dason admitted. "If this turns to blood, we'll lose a few men before we sink your crippled ships comfortably. My navy has orders, and the purpose of my mission is simple: we want to know if you will act sensibly and yield what we demand, allowing you to depart with an uncut skin?"

"You've come to the wrong bird for plucking," Tob laughed heavily. "We took no treasure or goods from Yucatan. We stayed long enough to cure our provisions and refill our water, no longer. We sail back empty-handed. You wouldn't believe me if the roles were reversed, but you may inspect the holds. You'll find only broken sailors suffering from scurvy. All you can steal here, Dason, is blows, and we are happy to offer them cheap."

"I'm glad you value your cargo so lightly," the envoy smirked. "For it is precisely that cargo I must transport if you wish safe passage home."

Tob furrowed his brow. "Speak plainly, I am a common sailor and don't understand your riddles."

"It is obvious," Dason said, "that you were sent to bring Deucalion back to Atlantis as a prop for Phorenice. We find her difficult enough to oppose without extra reinforcements. We want Deucalion in our custody to handle as we see fit."

"And if I refuse, this piece of my freight?" Tob asked.

The envoy glanced at the splintered hull of the *Bear* and the battered fleet around it. "Then, Tob, we shall send you all to the fishes shortly. Instead of Deucalion standing before the Gods alone, he will descend with a ragged, limping company at his heels."

"I doubt it," Tob retorted. "As for handing over my Lord Deucalion, it is impossible. First, if I return without him, Lord Tatho will eventually return, and I shall die slowly in his hands. Second, I have witnessed Lord Deucalion slay a great sea-lizard; he proved himself a true man that day, and I would not betray him willingly, not even to Tatho. Third, you owe me for our share of the wine-drinking ashore, and I'll see you with the Nether Gods before I give you anything until you settle that debt."

"Well, Tob, I hope you drown easily," Dason spat. "As for your wife... I've always fancied her, and I know how to make use of her."

"I'll draw your neck, you son of a European!" Tob roared. "If you don't leave this deck, I'll cut it right here! Go, you father of monkey children! Clear off and let me fight you fairly, or by my honor, I'll rip your innards out and make your silly crew wear them as necklaces!"

With that, Dason retreated to his galley.

Immediately, Tob engaged the machinery of the *Bear*, shouting orders to the other ships. Even weak with scurvy, the crews moved with terrifying speed. Instantly, the five vessels surged forward, converging on a single opponent. Caught off guard by the sudden attack, the enemy ship attempted to turn and flee rather than meeting us bow-on. Consequently, all five of our ships struck her simultaneously on her broadside, tearing her planking with their submerged prows and sinking her before we could disengage.

While we had reduced the enemy count to five, matching our numbers, the advantage did not last. The three agile galleys formed a deadly line. Their boatwains' whips cracked as the slaves bent to their oars, and soon one of our ships was gored and sunk, her crew drowned in the water without hope of rescue.

What followed was a melee of such savage intensity it would have warmed the heart of the greatest warrior alive. Ships and galleys were pressed together, grinding savagely upon the swells as if driven by some sentient malice. Men on board hurled arrows, slashed with axes, thrust with swords, and unleashed throwing fire. Yet, every blow and arrow converged upon the *Bear*. It was her that the enemy hated most fiercely, rallying around her as their other ships caught fire, sank, or were captured.

Battle is an old companion to us of the Priestly Clan, familiar enough to cloy even the most chivalrous appetite. Having carved out territories for new colonies for half a lifetime, I have heard "Deucalion" shouted as a battlecry and witnessed many fierce encounters. But this sea-fight surprised me in its raw ferocity. The bleak, shifting elements surrounding us—the swaying decks, the burning throwing fire that consumed flesh and timber alike, the great man-eating birds circling overhead, and the man-devouring fish swarming from the depths—created circumstances fit to daunt the bravest armies.

Yet, these seasoned shipmen faced it all with indomitable courage. Never a cry of fear rose among them. Life on the seas is so brutal, plagued by beasts and savage dangers, that Death has lost half its terror through sheer familiarity.
