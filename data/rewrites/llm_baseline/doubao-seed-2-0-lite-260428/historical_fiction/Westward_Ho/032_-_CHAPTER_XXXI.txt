“Britannia needs no bulwarks, No towers along the steep, Her march is o'er the mountain wave, Her home is on the deep.”
—CAMPBELL, *Ye Mariners of England*

Now began the epochal twelve-day sea fight that would decide whether popery and despotism, or Protestantism and freedom, would shape half of Europe, and the whole unwritten future of the Americas. It is an epic fit for the thunder-roll of Homer’s verse, not plain prose, but I will tell it as faithfully as I can, leaning whenever possible on the accounts of those who lived through it rather than my own fancy.

The Lord High Admiral of England sent a small pinnace named the *Defiance* ahead to fire its ordnance and formally declare war. He then drew within musket-shot range, his own ship the *Ark Royal* (or *Triumph*) thundering broadsides, and struck what he believed to be the Spanish admiral’s flagship—though it proved to be the vessel of one Alfonso de Leon. Moments later, Drake, Hawkins, and Frobisher hammered the rearmost Spanish squadron, commanded by Recalde, with relentless, well-aimed fire. The Spaniards quickly learned the bitter truth of how much more nimble the English ships were; Recalde’s men, finding they took far more damage than they could give even at their commander’s best efforts, rushed to scramble back into the main body of the fleet.
Medina Sidonia, the Spanish grand admiral, saw his ships scattering fast, and herded them into a tight half-moon formation. The great Armada moved forward with stately, deliberate pomp, like a herd of hulking buffaloes marching across a prairie, disdaining to notice the snarling wolves nipping at their heels. But the English were no wolves: they were cunning, swift-mounted hunters, and as Drake himself put it, they would “shamefully shuffle” that vast, proud herd from the Lizard to Portland, and from Portland all the way to Calais Roads. Even in those first two hours of fighting, no small number of Spaniards had already begun to doubt the much-boasted invincibility of their “invincible” Armada.

One of the four massive Spanish galleasses was already riddled through with shot, its pulpits, chapels, and the friars who served there thrown into chaos. The rest of the Armada had to cluster tight around it to shield it, or Drake and Hawkins would sink it outright. In the chaos of that maneuver, the principal galleon of Seville, carrying Don Pedro de Valdez and a whole company of blue-blooded Spanish nobles, collided with a neighboring ship, snapped off its foremast, and was abandoned by the rest of the fleet in spite of all their boasts of chivalry. The signs did not bode well for a Spanish victory, but the Spaniards told themselves to take heart: Our Lady, the saints, and the *Coena Domini* bull were still on their side, and to doubt that would be blasphemy. But they could not escape the obvious question: if they fared this badly against only a third of the Plymouth fleet, what would become of them when the forty lagging English ships already dotting the blue water between them and the Mewstone arrived to join the fight?

So ended the first day of battle. Not a single English ship was lost, and barely a single English seaman was seriously hurt. That single day shattered forever the English public’s awe of Spain’s supposed unbeatable might. It fully vindicated Lord Howard’s choice, taken on the advice of Raleigh and Drake, to fight a running, hit-and-run battle instead of blindly lashing their ships together to board, a mistake Raleigh swore would have ended in total defeat had Howard not been wiser than the scores of petty, foolish critics who complained he was not fighting “honorably” enough.

Amyas Leigh and the rest of the Bideford contingent spent the whole two hours of that first day hammering at a huge galleon that bore a statue of a maiden with a wheel on its sterncastle: the *Sta. Catharina*. The flag at its bowsprit bore a coat of arms, likely belonging to the captain of its soldiers, but the whole banner had been shot away early in the fight, so Amyas could never confirm if it had belonged to De Soto or not. He knew there would be time enough for his private revenge later, and when the admiral’s signal called him off at last, he tumbled into his hammock and slept soundly.

He had not been asleep an hour when Cary shook him awake to ask for new orders. “We were supposed to follow Drake’s lantern, Amyas, but I can’t see it anywhere. Unless the man was plucked right up into the stars to become a new planet named Drakium Sidus.”
Amyas grumbled as he stumbled back on deck, but there was no sign of the guiding lantern. Only a sudden explosion and a great burst of flame on one of the distant Spanish ships, which the crew managed to put out eventually, while almost the whole English fleet was forced to heave to and drift through the dark.

Dawn found them off Torbay, and a small pinnace pulled up alongside Amyas’ ship carrying a personal letter from Drake. Its spelling was as whimsical as most men’s of the era, but the content ran roughly thus:
> I was wool-gathering all night after five great hulks that the pixies tricked me into mistaking for Spanish galleons, until daylight revealed them to be nothing more than harmless German merchantmen. I let them go with my blessing, and on my way back, to my great joy, stumbled straight across Valdez’ abandoned galleon. It was stuffed full of fine plunder that Valdez’ so-called valiant comrades had left behind for him, even as Lord Howard had sailed right past thinking the crew had already deserted. I’ve sent off to Dartmouth nearly fifty Spanish noblemen and gentlemen as prisoners. When I first sent my pinnace over to Valdez to demand his surrender, the fool insisted on haggling over terms to save his punctilio. I told him I had no time to waste arguing: if he wanted to die, I would happily oblige him, and if he wanted to live, he would get good quarter. He wrote back bragging he was Don Pedro Valdez, that it was beneath his honor and that of the Dons with him to surrender unconditionally. I wrote back to say I was Francis Drake, and my matches were already lit. At that, he decided my name was honor enough to salve his own pride, and came aboard to kiss my fist, spouting all sorts of Spanish lies about how fortunate he was to fall into the hands of the famous lucky Drake, and a lot more nonsense he could have kept to cool his porridge for all I cared.
> I got all sorts of useful intelligence out of him, the old leaky tub, and one piece in particular concerns you: your Don Guzman is serving on the *Sta. Catharina* as commandant of its soldiers. He flies his own coat of arms at the bowsprit, right next to that carved maiden with the wheel on the stern. It’s a tall three-decker packed with ordnance. God keep you from harm, and send you the same good luck I’ve had.
> Your deare Friend and Admirall,
> F. Drake.
> She sails in Recalde’s squadron. The Armada had planned to smoke us out of Plymouth harbor, and thank God they never tried: their orders from home are so tight they fight like a bull tied to a tether, no further than the rope will let them, and they’re finding the devil a hard master as most men do in the end. They can’t wrap their heads around how nimbly we tack and turn, and they all swear we must be witches. We know their tricks of old. Time and light can catch any hare, and they will find the stretch from Start Point to Dunkirk a very long way indeed.

“Our admiral’s in a fine mood to send you such a long, friendly letter, Leigh,” Cary said.
“St. Catherine? That was the galleon we hammered for two whole hours yesterday!” Amyas roared, stamping his boot hard on the deck.
“Of course it was. We’ll find her again, never fear. Cunning old Drake, even managed to line his own pockets with plunder while the rest of the fleet was left waiting for him all night.”
“Well, he certainly left Lord Howard out of the loot,” Amyas grumbled.
“Lord Howard is too high-hearted to stop and chase plunder, even if he is a Papist,” Cary said. Amyas only growled in response: he worshipped Drake, and was never very fair to Catholics.

They did not catch up with Lord Howard’s flagship until nightfall. He and Lord Sheffield had sailed hard after distant Spanish lanterns all the previous night with only two ships between them, and their courage put an end to all slurs cast by fanatics that England’s Roman Catholic nobility might betray the crown. All through the rest of the campaign, the Howards fought with reckless, desperate valor, as if determined to prove their loyalty beyond any doubt. Soon a derelict half-burnt Spanish galleon drifted past them, and Amyas’ men called out to stop and board her, but he refused outright. “We came out here to fight, not loot. The nearest ship to her can have the prize without us grudge.” They sailed on, and his men pulled long faces when they saw that galleon claimed by the next ship in line and towed off to Weymouth: it proved to be the vessel of vice-admiral Miguel d’Oquenda, nearly blown up the night before by a Dutch gunner who, sick of Spanish abuse, had tried to blow the whole ship sky high to pay off his old scores against his tyrants.

So ended the second day of fighting. The next morning found both fleets off the Isle of Portland. The wind had shifted north, blowing sharp and cool off the white chalk downs above Weymouth Bay. The Spaniards turned to face the English, clearly meaning to stand back and forth on the tide until the wind shifted again, so they could make a run for the Needles. But the English were determined they would not get past Purbeck Isle without a very hard fight.

The English sailed west for a little while, only to tack back on the opposite bearing, and for a long stretch both fleets maneuvered furiously to try to gain the windward advantage. Before noon, the English had slipped close-hauled between the Armada and the shore, and were bearing down on the Spaniards directly before the wind.
Then began the fiercest, most brutal battle of the campaign so far. The English fought through the chaos to rescue a squadron of London merchant ships that had been hemmed in by the Spaniards, while the Spanish fought just as hard to pull Recalde out of danger when his ship was trapped. The thunder of thousands of cannon roared nonstop, though most of the Spanish shots flew far over the top of the smaller English vessels without doing harm. Only one English captain, a Devon man named Cock, died fighting honorably in his tiny small ship, deep in the thick of the Spanish ranks. The far smaller, far more agile English ships would dash in, unleash a full broadside, then dart away across the deep water, their shots never missing the sides of the slow, unwieldy floating castles the Spaniards sailed. Though the fight only ended in the capture of one huge Venetian galleon and a handful of small craft, it was the bloodiest clash of the whole run. Lord Howard himself was fighting deep inside the Spanish formation when he spotted one of his captains, George Fenner, lingering far back. “O George, what doest thou?” he roared. “Wilt thou now frustrate all the hope and good opinion I have of thee? Wilt thou forsake me now?” Stung by the shout, Fenner charged straight into the fight and fought like a lion, as every other captain and seaman did that day.

Night fell over the heaving, sulphurous floating battlefield, and dawn found them far past Purbeck, the white chalk peak of Freshwater Head rising dead ahead. Ships poured out of every southern English harbor to join the chase: the flower of English chivalry, Lords Oxford, Northumberland, Cumberland, Pallavicin, Brooke, Carew, Raleigh, Blunt, and dozens more honorable names, sailing in vessels they had fitted out at their own expense, flocking to the fight as if it was a great tournament where immortal fame could be won. Spain had staked all her greatest noble houses on this throw of the dice: every family of rank in Aragon and Castile had sent at least one son or brother to fight, and many would never return.
Lord Howard sent light small craft racing back to Portsmouth to resupply with gunpowder, but the wind died completely that evening before they could return, and both fleets drifted helpless on the tide, shouting idle challenges back and forth across the water with trumpets, fifes, and drums. The glassy, calm sea held them all night long.

It was the 25th of July, St. James’s Day, the patron saint of Spain. The Spaniards told themselves St. James, who they swore had been seen riding a white horse at the head of their armies to slaughter infidels for centuries, would surely grant them a fair wind at least, or ride to their aid himself. Three of the four great galleasses—their crippled sister left limping in the main fleet—thrashed the sea to foam with three hundred oars apiece and charged the English line. They did not see St. James leading them to victory, however: they saw Lord Howard’s *Triumph*, his brother’s *Lion*, Southwell’s *Elizabeth Jonas*, Lord Sheffield’s *Bear*, Barker’s *Victory*, and George Fenner’s *Leicester* towed out to meet them, firing broadsides of chain-shot that smashed oars to splinters and tore rigging to rags. Only a sudden shift of wind toward noon and the arrival of the main Spanish fleet saved the galleasses from being captured, and after that day none of them dared to venture out to fight again.
The battle raged general all afternoon beneath the sheer white chalk cliffs of Freshwater. Thousands of sea birds rose screaming from every ledge, their black wings blotting the bright chalk, and a lone shepherd tending his flock on the high slopes hurried to the cliff edge to peer down, forgetting the small wheatear fluttering trapped in his snare, staring trembling through the great leagues of rolling sulphur smoke to catch glimpses of tall masts and bright silk flags glinting far below.

St. James’s Day ended exactly like the day the priests of Baal cried out on Mount Carmel: their patron saint did not answer, not with fire from heaven, not with victory—only the fire of English cannon. The Armada huddled itself into a tight defensive circle, and refused to give battle any more, turning all sails for Calais, where they hoped the Guises would send a French army to support them, before sailing on to Dunkirk to join the Duke of Parma and his great flotilla of Netherland troops.
A fair steady south-southwest wind carried both fleets onward past Brighton Cliffs, Beachy Head, Hastings, and Dungeness. It felt less like a running battle and more like a victory procession. Lord Howard stopped to reward his bravest captains on the deck of his flagship: after Lord Thomas Howard, Lord Sheffield, Townsend, and Frobisher had all been knighted—an honor far greater than a peerage in those days—old Admiral John Hawkins knelt to receive the accolade, rose up Sir John, and joked that his wife would never know herself again when people started calling her “My Lady.”
All the while, the cliffs behind them were lined with pikemen, musketeers, and every countryman, groom, and farmer who could bear arms, marching eastward under their squires and sheriffs as fast as they could travel toward the Dover shore. Not just soldiers, either: women, children, and aged folk in wagons had trudged from miles inland to add their thin shouts and fierce, unshakable prayers to the roar of mixed faith and fear rising toward heaven from the spectators of Britain’s Salamis.

They had every right to pray. The danger was far from over. Lord Howard had received word from Newhaven that the Guises would not dare to stir against England, and Seymour and Winter had sailed away from their watch post off the Flemish coast to join the main English fleet, bringing its total number of ships up to 140: slightly more than the Spanish fleet, but less than half its total tonnage, and only a third as many men. The Spaniards were battered and dispirited, but still fully intact as a fighting force. They dropped anchor in Calais Roads on the Saturday evening of that most memorable week, and every prudent man in the fleet knew well: the church bells that would ring across all Christendom the next morning, the Sabbath, would be either the triumphal peal for the English, or the death knell of the Reformed faith across the whole of Europe.

That Sabbath was a solemn, trembling day across every English town and village. Many a light-hearted coward who had once scoffed at the very idea the Armada could ever reach England, now fell abjectly to his knees shaking, seeing the threat he had refused to acknowledge laid bare right across the Channel. Many a brave man knelt beside his wife and daughters at church, his heart sinking to the cold stone floor at the thought of what those he loved would suffer at the hands of Spain’s brutal, fanatical soldiery, or the far more deliberate cruelty of the Inquisition. The spectres of the St. Bartholomew’s Day massacres, the Smithfield burnings, the extermination of the Moors and the West Indian peoples, the unspeakable horrors of the Piedmontese persecutions, were not dim, distant tales from centuries past then: they were fresh, gaping wounds, flitting before every Englishman’s eyes and stoking the fire in their breasts. They knew that one single defeat, and the same unspeakable fate that had befallen the people of Magdeburg a generation later would have fallen on every English town from London to Carlisle.

Amyas Leigh, however, barely noticed the grand stakes of the national battle unfolding around him. Day after day, he had hunted the *Sta. Catharina* through the thickest parts of the fight, and had not been able to find her, or even hear word of her. One hour he feared she had sunk overnight, robbing him of his vengeance; the next he feared she had repaired her damage and slipped away from him forever.
