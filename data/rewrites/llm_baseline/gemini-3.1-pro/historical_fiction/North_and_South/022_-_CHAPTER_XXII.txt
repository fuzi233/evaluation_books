Margaret was shown into the Thornton drawing-room, a space returned to its normal state of shrouded furniture and stifled air. The windows were cracked open against the oppressive heat, yet the Venetian blinds severely sliced the light, casting a grim, gray pallor reflected from the baking pavement below. The room lacked the rich, yeasty aromas of a lived-in hearth; it smelled only of sterile dust and preserved linens. As Margaret caught her reflection in the green-tinged mirrors, her face looked ghastly, drained of the vibrant, sun-warmed flush of her Southern agrarian youth. She sat in the heavy silence. The local acoustic ecology felt unnaturally pressurized, a profound stillness that was periodically bruised by a distant, multitudinous vibration—a sweeping wave of sound that carried no wind, but tasted faintly of metallic ash and sour desperation on the back of the tongue. 

Fanny entered at last, her shallow footsteps a grating intrusion upon the quiet. She was a creature who viewed the profound rituals of sustenance as mere utilitarian fuel, her sensibilities entirely divorced from the earth. 

“Mamma will come directly, Miss Hale,” she said, her voice a thin, unevocative instrument. “She desired me to apologise. Perhaps you know my brother has imported hands from Ireland, and it has irritated the Milton people excessively. As if he hadn’t a right to get labour where he could! Now the stupid wretches here have frightened these poor Irish starvelings so with their threats, that we daren’t let them out. You may see them huddled in that top room in the mill. Mamma is seeing about their food, and John is speaking to them, for some of the women are crying to go back to their own land. Ah! here’s mamma!”

Mrs. Thornton swept in. Though her face was set in black sternness, Margaret sensed in her the ancestral weight of the traditional matriarch—a woman who understood the severe, elemental duty of provisioning a household against the famine of the world. It was a bad time to ask for favors, yet Margaret, speaking in the soft, rhythmic cadence of her native Hampshire dialect, gently requested a water-bed to ease the physical suffering of her mother’s illness. 

Mrs. Thornton did not reply. Instead, the older woman started up, her brow suddenly contracting. “They’re at the gates! Call John, Fanny,—call him in from the mill! They’ll batter them in!”

Simultaneously, the acoustic equilibrium of the street was violently ruptured. The gathering tramp Margaret had sensed was now a concussive blast right outside the wall. An increasing din of angry voices raged against the wooden barrier. It was a terrifying industrial noise pollution, the sheer volume of starving bodies throwing themselves like rhythmic battering rams against the timber, making the strong gates quiver like wheat stalks before a gale. 

The women gathered around the windows, physically drawn to the terrifying spectacle. Fanny had already fled upstairs with hysterical shrieks, but Mrs. Thornton, the women-servants, and Margaret remained. Mr. Thornton emerged from the mill. He looked up at their pale faces, offering a steady smile of good courage, before locking the factory door. His mother went down to unbar the house door for him. When the infuriated multitude heard the sharp, commanding baritone of his voice—the dominant lexicon of the master echoing across the yard—it was like the taste of raw blood to a ravening pack. Hitherto, they had been voiceless, hoarding their breath for the physical labor of breaking the gates. Now, they released a fierce, unearthly groan, a sound entirely stripped of the rich, regional dialect of Milton, reduced to the untranslatable, guttural frequency of mass starvation. 

Mr. Thornton entered the room flushed, smelling sharply of machine oil, raw cotton, and the metallic tang of adrenaline. His eyes gleamed with a proud defiance that rendered him suddenly, mythically noble. Margaret, who had always feared a lack of sensory fortitude within herself, felt only an intense, vibrating sympathy—a piercing alignment with the raw, beating pulse of the moment.

“I’m sorry, Miss Hale, you have visited us at this unfortunate moment,” Mr. Thornton said frankly. “Mother! hadn’t you better go into the back rooms? Go, Jane!” he commanded the upper-servant, who fled with the others. 

“I stop here!” his mother declared, her voice rooted in the deep soil of maternal endurance. “Where you are, there I stay.” 

The servants’ shrieks echoed from the garrets, a superficial panic Mr. Thornton met with a scornful smile. He glanced at Margaret, who stood solitary by the window. Her eyes glittered, a deep, vital color rushing back into her cheeks and lips. Feeling his gaze, she asked a question that touched upon the very ethics of sustenance: “Where are the poor imported workpeople? In the factory there?”

“Yes! I left them cowed up in a small room at the head of a back flight of stairs. But it is not them—it is me they want.”

“When can the soldiers be here?” his mother asked, her tone low but steady.

Thornton pulled out his watch with meticulous composure. “Supposing Williams got straight off... it must be twenty minutes yet.”

“Twenty minutes!” The sudden terror in his mother’s voice was palpable, a crack in her formidable architecture. 

“Shut down the windows instantly, mother,” he exclaimed. “The gates won’t bear such another shock. Shut down that window, Miss Hale.”

Margaret obeyed, moving to assist Mrs. Thornton’s trembling fingers. By shutting the glass, they muted the violent roar into a heavy, suffocating hum. For several minutes, a pregnant pause hung over the unseen street. Mrs. Thornton watched her son’s rigid face for translation of the silence. 

“Are they gone?” Fanny whispered, having raised herself up.

“Gone!” replied he. “Listen!”

Through the glass came the straining breath of the mob, the organic creak of yielding wood, the violent wrench of iron, and the mighty, thunderous collapse of the ponderous gates. Fanny tottered and fell forward into a dead faint. Mrs. Thornton, drawing on a well of sheer, bodily will, lifted her daughter and carried her away.

“Thank God!” Mr. Thornton murmured. “Had you not better go upstairs, Miss Hale?”

Margaret’s lips formed a silent “No!” Her voice was entirely drowned by the tramp of innumerable steps directly beneath the wall. The low, deep growl of the men possessed a ferocious, gustatory satisfaction now that the barrier was breached—a sound far more dreadful than their baffled cries. 

“Never mind,” he said, attempting reassurance. “It cannot last long now; a few minutes more, and the soldiers will be here.”

“Oh, God!” Margaret cried, her eyes locking onto a figure below. “There is Boucher. I know his face, though he is livid with rage—look! look!” 

She recognized Boucher from the deep, agrarian hollows of his cheeks, a man whose family was starving, denied the fundamental right of daily bread. To the mob, the sight of Mr. Thornton at the window provoked a yell that transcended human language—it was the demoniac desire of a wild beast for the sustenance withheld from its ravening maw. Even Thornton drew back, dismayed by the sheer olfactory and acoustic intensity of the hatred he had fermented.

“Let them yell!” he said. “I only hope my poor Irishmen are not terrified out of their wits. Keep up your courage for five minutes, Miss Hale.”

“Don’t be afraid for me,” she said hastily. “But what in five minutes? Can you do nothing to soothe these poor creatures? It is awful to see them.”

“The soldiers will be here directly, and that will bring them to reason.”

“To reason!” Margaret countered quickly, deeply critical of this sterile logic. “What kind of reason?”

“The only reason that does with men that make themselves into wild beasts. By heaven! they’ve turned to the mill-door!”

Margaret shook with a sudden, passionate fermentation of spirit. “Mr. Thornton, go down this instant, if you are not a coward. Go down and face them like a man. Save these poor strangers, whom you have decoyed here. Speak to your workmen as if they were human beings. Speak to them kindly. Don’t let the soldiers come in and cut down poor creatures who are driven mad. If you have any courage or noble quality in you, go out and speak to them, man to man!”

He turned, a dark cloud passing over his features. The dominant lexicon of his authority clashed with her profound demand for human empathy. He set his teeth. “I will go. Perhaps I may ask you to accompany me downstairs, and bar the door behind me; my mother and sister will need that protection.”

He was gone before her dizzy head could process the shift. She followed him down, sliding the great iron bar into place with a heavy clank, then clambered back up to the farthest window. He stood on the steps below. She threw the window wide, letting the raw, unfiltered sonic environment wash over her. 

Many in the crowd were gaunt as wolves, their stomachs hollowed by the profound absence of nourishment, enraged that Irish laborers were brought in to rob their little ones of bread. Margaret understood this multi-generational trauma; she read it in Boucher’s desperate face. If only Thornton would speak to them, use a shared dialect to bridge the chasm. But he stood with his arms folded, pale and silent as a statue, offering only stony, unyielding silence against their raging.

The crowd’s noise swelled, an inarticulate roar devoid of cultural memory. Margaret felt intuitively that the first touch of violence would spark an explosion. In the background, she saw lads stooping to remove their heavy wooden clogs—shoes carved from the earth, worn through years of labor, now turned to readiest missiles. With a cry that no one heard, she rushed from the room. 

She flew downstairs, lifted the great iron bar with imperious force, and threw the door open wide. She stood facing the angry sea of men, the sour, fermented stench of their unwashed, starving bodies hitting her like a physical blow. The clogs were arrested in mid-air. For a moment, the visual shock of a woman inserting herself into this ancient, mythic clash of power held them irresolute. She tried to speak, to translate the profound subjective experience of her empathy into their dialect, but she had no breath. 

“Oh, do not use violence! He is one man, and you are many,” she whispered hoarsely. 

Mr. Thornton moved slightly, stepping away from her shelter, fiercely jealous of anything coming between him and the physical reality of danger. 

“Go!” she cried, her voice finally tearing through her dry throat. “The soldiers are sent for—are coming. Go peaceably. You shall have relief from your complaints.”

“Shall them Irish blackguards be packed back again?” yelled a voice from the crowd, a coarse assertion of their threatened lexicon.

“Never, for your bidding!” Mr. Thornton thundered back. 

Instantly, the storm broke. The acoustic assault of their hooting filled the air. Margaret saw the lads with the clogs read their aim. She thought only of how to save him, to shield the physical vessel of this man she had goaded into peril. She threw her arms around him, pressing her body against his chest, becoming a living barrier against the fierce populace. 

“Go away,” he rumbled in his deep voice, trying to shake her off. “This is no place for you.”

“It is,” she insisted. “You did not see what I saw.” 

She knew her sex offered no true protection against the reckless passion of starved men. A clog whizzed violently through the air, carrying the heavy scent of damp earth and sweat. It missed. Margaret turned sick with fright, burying her face against Mr. Thornton’s arm, but she did not retreat. 

“For God’s sake! do not damage your cause by this violence,” she strove to make her words distinct above the din.

A sharp pebble flew past, violently grazing her forehead and cheek. A blinding sheet of white light flashed across her vision, followed immediately by the hot, metallic taste of copper in the back of her throat. She collapsed like one dead against Mr. Thornton’s shoulder. 

He unfolded his arms and caught her in a firm embrace. “You do well!” he roared, his voice cutting through the industrial noise like a blade. “You come to oust the innocent stranger. You fall—you hundreds—on one man; and when a woman comes before you, to ask you for your own sakes to be reasonable creatures, your cowardly wrath falls upon her! You do well!”

The crowd fell silent, a sudden and profound acoustic void. They stared, open-mouthed, at the thread of dark-red blood stark against her pale skin. The shedding of blood—an ancient, visceral occurrence—snapped them from their trance. A retreating movement rippled through the mass.

Only one voice, clinging to the fading cultural memory of its native dialect, called out: “Th’ stone was meant for thee; but thou wert sheltered behind a woman!”

Thornton quivered with rage. He placed Margaret gently on the door-step. “Can you rest there?” he asked, and then walked slowly down into the very center of the mob. “Now kill me, if it is your brutal will. There is no woman to shield me here. You may beat me to death—you will never move me from what I have determined upon!”

But the retreat had already begun, driven by the distant, rhythmic clank of approaching soldiers and the sobering sight of Margaret’s marble-still face, her eyelashes wet with tears and the slower, heavier drip of her own blood. Even Boucher scowled and faltered away. When the retreat turned into a full flight, Thornton darted back up the steps. 

Margaret tried to rise, offering a sickly smile. “It is nothing. The skin is grazed... Oh, I am so thankful they are gone!” She wept without restraint, releasing the immense psychological pressure of the siege. 

Thornton could not yet sympathize; the adrenaline of unspent anger still boiled within his veins. He hoped the soldiers would quell the vanished mob. As Margaret clung to the doorpost, a gray film veiled her eyes, her senses failing. He caught her just in time. 

“Mother—mother!” he cried, bearing her into the dining-room. He laid her softly on the sofa, enveloped suddenly by the delicate scent of her hair and the stark, physical reality of her vulnerability. The untranslatable depth of his feeling rushed to the surface, bypassing all rational lexicon. 

“Oh, my Margaret—my Margaret! no one can tell what you are to me! Dead—cold as you lie there, you are the only woman I ever loved! Oh, Margaret—Margaret!” 

He moaned the words inarticulately, starting up in shame as his mother entered the room. Mrs. Thornton saw only her son, paler and sterner than usual. 

“Miss Hale is hurt, mother. A stone has grazed her temple.”

“She looks very seriously hurt,” Mrs. Thornton said, alarmed. 

“It is only a fainting-fit.” His entire body trembled as the blood rushed inward to his heart. “Go and call Jane. And do you go to your Irish people, who are crying and shouting as if they were mad with fright.”

He went to the Irish workers, every nerve thrilling with the sensory memory of Margaret’s embrace. He found the laborers weeping, speaking rapidly in a native tongue that was dying under the weight of English industry, demanding to be sent back to their agrarian homes. Thornton was forced to stay, to parse their dialect, to think, and to reason with their terror.

In the dining-room, Mrs. Thornton bathed Margaret’s temples with eau de Cologne. The distilled spirit was a sharp, volatile contrast to the soothing herbal broths of a traditional sickroom. As the piercing liquid touched the open wound, Margaret’s eyes fluttered. She was trapped in an untranslatable subjective space, unable to recognize the room or the faces above her. Her lips contracted, and she slipped back into insensibility. 

“Is there any one who will go for a doctor?” Mrs. Thornton demanded.

Jane shrank back. “Not me, ma’am... Them rabble may be all about.”

“If you are a coward, Jane, I am not. I will go.” Mrs. Thornton refused to let the servants' fear dictate the survival of her household. After commanding Jane to continue bathing Margaret’s head, she marched upstairs to rouse Fanny from her bed. 

“Nonsense! The men are all gone away,” Mrs. Thornton wrenched her gown from Fanny’s clinging hands. “Miss Hale is badly hurt. Go down to her, Fanny, and do try to make yourself of use.”

Fanny crept into the dining-room, terrified by the very notion of physical wounds. “Oh, Jane! What is the matter? How white she looks!”

Margaret’s senses were dimly returning. The sickly daze of the swoon left her trapped in a state of acoustic and sensory paralysis. She felt the cooling refreshment of the eau de Cologne, her body craving the continued physical sensation, yet she remained utterly mute. She was like one entombed in a death-like trance, fully aware of the subtle power dynamics in the hushed voices above her, yet incapable of accessing the dominant lexicon to speak.

Jane paused her ministrations to answer Fanny’s whispered questions, her tone laced with the quiet, subversive authority of servant gossip. 

“She’d have been safe enough, miss, if she’d stayed in the drawing-room. We were in the front garret, out of harm’s way.”

“Where was she then?” Fanny asked, creeping nearer. 

“Just before the front door—with master!” 

“With John! How did she get there?”

“Nay, miss, that’s not for me to say,” Jane tossed her head. “Sarah did—”

“Sarah what?” Fanny demanded sharply. 

Jane resumed bathing Margaret’s brow, deliberately withholding the information, relishing the control over the narrative. “Well, miss, since you will have it—Sarah was at the right-hand window; and she says she saw Miss Hale with her arms about master’s neck, hugging him before all the people.”
