I owe every good habit of order and punctuality I possess to my dear parents, long now at rest in heaven, who instilled those virtues into me before I was old enough to understand their value.

In that happy, vanished time, I was taught to keep my hair neat at every hour of the day and night, to fold every article of my clothing with deliberate care, laying them in exactly the same arrangement on the very same chair set at the foot of my bed before I slept. No night passed without first jotting the day’s events in my little diary. The “Evening Hymn”, recited once I was settled under the covers, always came after the folding. And the soft, unbroken sleep of childhood always followed the hymn.

In later life, alas, the hymn has been replaced by sad, bitter meditations, and that sweet child’s sleep exchanged for the fitful, broken rest that haunts the uneasy pillow of the careworn. Yet I still fold my clothes each night, and I still keep my little diary. The first habit ties me back to that happy childhood before my papa was ruined. The second, which I had always thought only useful for helping me discipline the fallen nature we all inherit from Adam, has turned out to be unexpectedly useful to my small fortunes. It has allowed poor little me to oblige the whim of a wealthy relative by marriage: I am fortunate enough to be of use to Mr. Franklin Blake.

I have been cut off from all news of that side of the family for quite some time now. When you are poor and isolated, it is no rare thing to be forgotten. For economy’s sake, I am now living in a small Breton town, among a select circle of serious English friends, where we are blessed with a Protestant clergyman of great piety and markets that charge almost nothing for bread and vegetables. It is my little Patmos, set amid the howling ocean of Roman Catholic idolatry that surrounds us.

It was in this quiet retirement that a letter from England finally reached me. My insignificant existence had suddenly been remembered by Mr. Franklin Blake. My wealthy relative—would that I could add my spiritually wealthy relative!—writes without even the faintest attempt to hide the fact that he wants something from me. The whim has seized him to dig up the deplorable old scandal of the Moonstone, and he wants me to contribute an account of everything I personally witnessed while staying at Aunt Verinder’s London house. He has even offered me pecuniary remuneration, with the characteristic lack of feeling the rich always show. I am to pry open wounds Time has barely had time to close, recall the most searing of painful memories, and in return I am to be “compensated” by Mr. Blake’s cheque, a new laceration to my pride all its own. My nature is weak. It cost me a long, hard struggle before Christian humility conquered sinful pride, and self-denial allowed me to accept the cheque.

Without my diary, I doubt—let me speak as plainly as possible!—that I could ever have honestly earned that money. With my diary, the poor labourer (who forgives Mr. Blake for insulting her) is fully worthy of her hire. Nothing escaped me during my visit to dear Aunt Verinder’s house. Every event, no matter how small, was written down in my diary the very day it happened, thanks to that early training my parents gave me. And every single detail will be told here. My sacred regard for truth is (thank God) far higher than my respect for persons. It will be easy enough for Mr. Blake to cut out any parts of these pages that are not sufficiently flattering to the person most central to this story. He has purchased my time, but not even his great wealth can purchase my conscience too.*

My diary records that I was passing Aunt Verinder’s house in Montagu Square quite by accident on Monday, July 3rd, 1848.

Seeing the shutters open and the blinds drawn up, I thought it a polite and Christian act to knock and inquire after the family. The girl who answered the door told me my aunt and her daughter (I really cannot bring myself to call that young woman my cousin!) had arrived back in London from their country house a week prior, and planned to stay for some time. I immediately sent up a message saying I did not wish to disturb them, only begged to know if I could be of any small use.

The girl who answered the door took my message in insolent silence, and left me standing alone in the hall. She is the daughter of that heathen old man Betteredge, who was tolerated for far too long in my aunt’s household. I sat down to wait for my answer, and, always carrying a few tracts in my bag, selected one that seemed providentially suited to her. The hall was dirty, the chair was hard, but the blessed feeling of returning good for evil lifted me far above such trivial discomforts. The tract was part of a series addressing young women on the sinfulness of frivolous dress, titled A Word With You On Your Cap-Ribbons.

“My lady thanks you, and says please come to lunch tomorrow at two.”

I paid no mind to her rude tone, or the shocking boldness of her stare. I thanked this young castaway, and spoke to her in a tone of gentle Christian interest: “Would you do me the favour of accepting this little tract?”

She glanced at the title. “Was it written by a man or a woman, Miss? If it was a woman, I’d rather not read it. If it was a man, you can tell him he doesn’t know the first thing about cap-ribbons.” She handed the tract back to me, and held the door open for me to leave. We must sow the good seed somehow, no matter the obstacle. I waited till the door had shut behind me, then slipped the tract through the front letterbox. I dropped a second different tract through the area railings for the benefit of the servants below, and felt I had lightened a small part of my responsibility for the salvation of others.

That evening, we held a meeting of the select committee for the Mothers’-Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society. The aim of this excellent charity, as all serious people know, is to rescue unredeemed fathers’ trousers from the pawnbroker, and prevent their irreclaimable owners from ever wearing them again by cutting them down to fit the proportions of their innocent young sons. I was a committee member at the time, and I mention the Society here because my dear, admirable friend Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite was closely involved in this work of moral and material usefulness. I had expected to see him at the boardroom that Monday evening, and had planned to tell him as soon as we met that dear Aunt Verinder was back in London. To my great disappointment, he never arrived. When I expressed surprise at his absence, all the sisters on the committee looked up at once from the pile of trousers we were mending and cutting down—we had a great backlog of work that night—and asked me in amazement if I had not heard the news. I told them I knew nothing, and they told me for the first time of the event that forms the starting point of this whole narrative. On the previous Friday, two gentlemen from entirely different walks of life had been the victims of an outrage that had startled all of London. One was Mr. Septimus Luker, the wealthy gem merchant of Lambeth. The other was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Living as isolated as I do now, I have no way of digging up the original newspaper account of the attack to insert here. I also was not there at the time to hear the events related by Mr. Godfrey’s own fervid, eloquent lips. All I can do is state the facts exactly as they were told to me that Monday evening, following the same principle of neatness I learned in childhood for folding my clothes: every fact in its proper place, nothing out of order. These lines are written by a poor, weak woman. Who could be cruel enough to expect more of me?

Thanks to my dear parents, no dictionary in the world is more careful about dates than I am. The day of the attack was Friday, June 30th, 1848.

Early that memorable day, our gifted Mr. Godfrey happened to be cashing a cheque at a banking house in Lombard Street. The name of the firm is accidentally blotted out in my diary, and my sacred regard for truth forbids me to guess at what it might have been. Fortunately the name does not matter. What matters is what happened when Mr. Godfrey finished his business and went to the door: he ran into a gentleman, a total stranger, who was leaving the bank at exactly the same moment. A tiny, polite tussle ensued over who should go through the door first. The stranger insisted Mr. Godfrey go ahead; Mr. Godfrey spoke a few civil words; they bowed, and parted ways on the street.

Thoughtless, superficial people might say that is a very trivial little incident, described in absurdly minute detail. Oh, my young friends and fellow sinners! Beware of trusting your poor carnal reason! Be morally tidy! Let your faith be as neat as your stockings, and your stockings as spotless as your faith—both always ready to put on at a moment’s notice!

A thousand pardons. I have unthinkingly slipped into my old Sunday School lecturing tone, which is most unsuitable for a record like this. Let me try to speak “worldly” for a moment: trifles, as so often happens, led to terrible consequences here. We only need to note that that polite stranger was Mr. Luker of Lambeth, before we follow Mr. Godfrey back home to his rooms in Kilburn.

When he got to his front door, he found a small, delicately built, poorly dressed little boy waiting for him in the hall. The boy handed him a letter, saying only that he had been given it by an old lady he did not know, who had not told him to wait for a reply. Incidents like this were far from uncommon for Mr. Godfrey, who was a well-known face in charitable circles across London. He let the boy go, and opened the letter.

The handwriting was entirely unfamiliar. It begged him to come to a house in Northumberland Street, Strand, within the hour, to give the manager certain details about the Mothers’-Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society. The information was requested by an elderly lady who planned to make a very large donation to the charity if her questions were answered satisfactorily. She gave her name, and added that she was only visiting London for a very short time, so had not been able to give him more advance notice.

Ordinary people might have hesitated before dropping their own plans to suit a total stranger. But the Christian Hero never hesitates when there is good to be done. Mr. Godfrey immediately turned back, and made his way to the house in Northumberland Street. A very respectable, somewhat stout man answered the door, and on hearing Mr. Godfrey’s name, led him straight to an empty back room on the drawing room floor. He noticed two strange things the moment he stepped inside: one was a faint smell of musk and camphor hanging in the air. The other was an ancient Oriental manuscript, richly illuminated with Indian figures and patterns, laid open on the table for anyone to look at.

He was standing studying the book, his back turned to the closed folding doors that led to the front of the house, when without the slightest warning noise, an arm suddenly wrapped tight around his neck from behind. He only had time to register that the arm was bare, and tawny brown in colour, before his eyes were bound with a bandage, his mouth stuffed full of a gag, and he was thrown helpless to the floor by what he judged to be two men. A third man rifled his pockets, and—if a lady may be permitted to use such a blunt phrase—searched him thoroughly, right down to his skin.

I would dearly love to say a few cheerful, encouraging words about the devout faith that alone could have sustained Mr. Godfrey through such a terrible ordeal. But I fear the position and state of dress of my admirable friend at the height of that outrage falls well outside the bounds of proper female discussion. I will skip over those few moments, and return to Mr. Godfrey as soon as that odious search was over. The entire attack had been carried out in total, dead silence. When it was done, some of the invisible wretches exchanged words in a language he did not understand, their tones thick with disappointment and rage. Suddenly they lifted him from the floor, strapped him hand and foot to a chair, and he felt the draft of open air as they left the room. For a few minutes, he was entirely alone.

Then he heard a soft rustle, like the sound of a woman’s dress, coming up the stairs from below. It drew closer, and stopped. A woman’s scream split the silence. A man’s voice called out “Hullo!” and heavy footsteps began climbing the stairs. Next he felt what he realised were respectable, Christian fingers untying his bandage and pulling the gag out of his mouth. He stared in amazement at two total strangers, and gasped out faintly, “What does this mean?” The two strangers stared right back at him, and said, “That’s exactly what we were about to ask you.”

Of course, the explanation followed. No—let me be perfectly precise. First came sal volatile and water, to calm dear Mr. Godfrey’s frayed nerves. Then the explanation.

The landlord and landlady of the house, who were well-respected in the neighbourhood, said their first and second floor apartments had been rented the day before, for a full week, by that very respectable stout man who had answered the door to Mr. Godfrey. He had paid the full week’s rent and all extra costs up front, saying the rooms were for three Oriental noblemen, friends of his, visiting England for the first time. On the morning of the attack, two of the foreign gentlemen, along with their respectable English friend, moved into the rooms. The third nobleman was expected to join them shortly, and their very large luggage was supposed to arrive later that afternoon once it had cleared Customs. The landlord and landlady downstairs had noticed nothing unusual at all, until five minutes before they came up the stairs: they had seen all three foreigners, and their respectable English friend, leave the house together, walking calmly off in the direction of the Strand. Remembering a visitor had called earlier, and seeing no sign he had left, the landlady had thought it odd to leave a guest all alone upstairs. She and her husband had discussed it briefly, decided to check that nothing was amiss, and found exactly what they found. Their explanation ended there.

They searched the room, and found all of Mr. Godfrey’s property scattered all over the floor. But when they gathered everything up, nothing was missing: his watch, chain, purse, keys, handkerchief, notebook, and every loose paper in his pockets had been closely examined, then left completely untouched. Nothing belonging to the house’s owners had been stolen either. The Oriental noblemen had taken nothing but their own illuminated manuscript, and nothing else.

What did it all mean? From the worldly point of view, it looked as if Mr. Godfrey had been the victim of a terrible mistake, made by unknown men. A dark conspiracy was afoot right in the heart of London, and our beloved, innocent friend had been tangled up in its trap. When the Christian hero of a hundred charitable triumphs falls headfirst into a pitfall dug for someone else, what a warning it is to all of us to be unceasingly on our guard! How easily our own hidden passions can prove to be “Oriental noblemen” who pounce on us unawares!

I could write pages of loving Christian warning on that theme alone, but alas, I am not here to improve anyone, I am condemned to narrate. My wealthy relative’s cheque, that incubus on my existence, reminds me I am not yet done with this record of violence. We must leave Mr. Godfrey to recover in that Northumberland Street lodgings, and follow what happened to Mr. Luker later that same day.

After leaving the bank, Mr. Luker had been out on various business errands across London. When he got back to his house, he found a letter waiting for him, left only a short time earlier by a small boy. Just like with Mr. Godfrey, the handwriting was unfamiliar—but the name signed was that of one of his best, most loyal customers, an enthusiastic collector of Oriental antiquities who had patronised his shop in Lambeth for many years. The letter, written in the third person by what looked like a secretary, said the collector had been unexpectedly called to London, had just settled into lodgings in Alfred Place, Tottenham Court Road, and wanted Mr. Luker to come see him immediately to discuss a new purchase he planned to make. Oh, when will we ever wean ourselves from the worship of Mammon! Mr. Luker called a cab at once, and drove straight to his wealthy patron’s address.

Exactly the same thing that happened to Mr. Godfrey in Northumberland Street now happened to Mr. Luker in Alfred Place. The same respectable stout man answered the door, and led him upstairs to the back drawing room. There the same illuminated manuscript lay open on the table, and Mr. Luker, just as absorbed by its beauty as Mr. Godfrey had been, barely noticed when the tawny bare arm snaked around his throat, the blindfold was tied over his eyes, and the gag was stuffed in his mouth. He too was thrown to the floor and searched down to his skin. More time passed for him than had passed for Mr. Godfrey, but it ended the same way: the landlord and landlady downstairs got suspicious, and came up to investigate. They told Mr. Luker exactly the same story the Northumberland Street landlords had told Mr. Godfrey—they had been tricked in exactly the same way by the plausible, well-dressed stranger who said he was renting the rooms for three foreign friends. There was only one small difference between the two cases: when they gathered up Mr. Luker’s scattered pockets, his watch and purse were perfectly safe, but unlike Mr. Godfrey, one of his loose papers had been stolen.
