In recent times, a deluge of curates has poured down upon the northern counties of England. They blanket the hills, thick as moss, numbering several in almost every parish. Young, vigorous, and seemingly destined for immense spiritual good, they form a striking contrast to the era from which we now retreat. We leave behind the present, with its dust, sunburn, and arid heat, to drift backward into a slumber from which we dream of a cooler dawn.

Do not let this prelude mislead you into expecting a conventional romance. Seek not sentiment, poetry, or reverie; seek not the electric shocks of melodrama. Calm your hopes and lower your standards to a humbling baseline. What awaits you is something real, cool, and solid—a narrative as unromantic as a Monday morning when the working class rises with the grim knowledge that duty calls. It is not promised that you will encounter excitement, perhaps later in the feast, but the first course laid before you is one that a devout Catholic—nay, an Anglo-Catholic—might consume on Good Friday during Passion Week: cold lentils steeped in vinegar, unleavened bread with bitter herbs, and no roast lamb.

Indeed, in those distant years, the "abundant shower" of curates had not yet fallen. In 1811–1812, the priesthood was scarce. There existed no Pastoral Aid, no Additional Curates' Society to bolster worn-out rectors or fund a fresh recruit from Oxford or Cambridge. The successors of the Apostles, disciples of Dr. Pusey and instruments of Propaganda, were then merely being hatched under cradle blankets or regenerated via nursery baptisms in washbasins. One could not have gazed upon any of them and foreseen that the Italian-ironed double frills of their net caps concealed the brows of future saints; nor could one have predicted from the folds of their long night-gowns the white surplices in which they would later exercise the souls of their parishioners or nonplus their old-fashioned vicars by flourishing aloft in the pulpit garments never before raised higher than the reading desk.

Yet, even in those lean times, curates existed. This particular branch of the divine plant was rare, but it could be found. A favored district in the West Riding of Yorkshire could boast three rods of Aaron blossoming within a circle of twenty miles. Behold them, reader. Step into this neat garden-house perched on the skirts of Whinbury, and enter the little parlour. There, seated around the table, they are at dinner. Permit me to introduce them: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield; and Mr. Sweeting, curate of Nunnely. These are Mr. Donne's lodgings, inhabited by John Gale, a modest clothier. Mr. Donne has graciously invited his brothers to share a repast. Join us, observe what is seen, and hear what is spoken. For the moment, however, they are only eating, so let us converse aside.

These gentlemen are in the prime of youth, possessing an energy that their morose, elderly vicars desperately wish to channel into pastoral duties—into the diligent supervision of schools and frequent visits to the sick. Yet the youthful Levites find this tedious. They prefer to lavish their energies on a regimen that, to outside observers, appears heavy with ennui and cursed with monotony, more burdensome than the toil of a weaver at his loom. To them, however, it yields an unfailing supply of enjoyment and occupation.

I refer to their frantic triangulation between their respective lodgings. Not a circle, but a triangle of visits, they maintain throughout the year, indifferent to season or weather. With a zeal that defies explanation, they brave snow, hail, wind, rain, mire, and dust to dine, drink tea, or sup with one another. What attracts them remains difficult to pinpoint. It is certainly not friendship, for whenever they meet, they quarrel. It is not religion—the word is never uttered among them, though they may occasionally debate theology; piety, alas, is absent. Nor is it the love of food and drink: each might enjoy a superior joint and pudding, tea as potent, and toast as succulent at his own dwelling as is served to him at his brother's. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp—their respective landladies—firmly assert that "it is just for naught else but to give folk trouble." By "folk," the virtuous ladies naturally mean themselves, for indeed, they are kept in a perpetual "fry" by this system of mutual invasion.

Mr. Donne and his guests are currently engaged in dinner. Mrs. Gale attends to them, though a spark of the hot kitchen fire burns in her eye. She considers the privilege of inviting a friend to a meal occasionally, without extra charge—a benefit included in the terms of her letting—to have been exercised quite sufficiently of late. The week is but Thursday, yet on Monday Mr. Malone of Briarfield came to breakfast and stayed until dinner; on Tuesday, Mr. Malone and Mr. Sweeting of Nunnely came to tea, lingered till supper, occupied the spare bed, and kept her company to breakfast on Wednesday morning; now, on Thursday, they are both here at dinner, and she is nearly certain they will remain all night. "C'en est trop," she would mutter if she spoke French.

Mr. Sweeting is carefully mincing a slice of roast beef on his plate, complaining that it is excessively tough; Mr. Donne remarks that the beer is flat. Ah, that is the crux of the matter: if they would only be civil, Mrs. Gale would not mind so much; if they would only seem satisfied with what they receive, she would not care. But "these young parsons are so high and so scornful, setting everyone beneath their fit." They treat her with scant courtesy precisely because she does not keep a servant but performs the household labor herself, as her mother did before her. Furthermore, they constantly speak against Yorkshire ways and Yorkshire folk; by that very token, Mrs. Gale refuses to believe one of them to be a genuine gentleman or of gentle kin. "The old parsons are worth the whole lump of college lads," she observes, "they know what belongs to good manners, and are kind to high and low."

"More bread!" cries Mr. Malone, in a tone which, though prolonged to only two syllables, instantly identifies him as a native of the land of shamrocks and potatoes. Mrs. Gale hates Mr. Malone more than either of the other two, yet she fears him as well. He is a tall, powerfully built figure, possessing real Irish legs and arms, and a face that is genuinely national—not the Milesian countenance, nor the style of Daniel O'Connell, but the high-featured, North-American Indian sort of visage belonging to a certain class of the Irish gentry. It possesses a petrified, proud look, better suited to the owner of a plantation of slaves than to the landlord of a free peasantry. Mr. Malone's father called himself a gentleman; he was poor, deeply in debt, and besottedly arrogant, and his son was exactly like him.

Mrs. Gale offers the loaf.

"Cut it, woman," commands her guest. And the "woman" cuts it accordingly. Had she followed her inclinations, she would have cut the parson as well; her Yorkshire soul revolts absolutely from his manner of command.

The curates have hearty appetites. Though the beef is "tough," they devour a great quantity of it. They swallow a tolerable allowance of the "flat beer," while a dish of Yorkshire pudding and two tureens of vegetables disappear like leaves before locusts. The cheese receives distinguished marks of their attention, and a "spice-cake," served by way of dessert, vanishes like a vision, leaving no trace. Its elegy is chanted in the kitchen by Abraham, Mrs. Gale's son and heir, a youth of six summers. Having reckoned upon the reversion thereof, when his mother brings down the empty platter, he lifts up his voice and weeps sore.

Meanwhile, the curates sit and sip their wine, a liquor of unpretending vintage, moderately enjoyed. Mr. Malone, indeed, would much prefer whisky, but Mr. Donne, being an Englishman, keeps no such beverage. As they sip, they argue—not on politics, nor philosophy, nor literature (topics now, as ever, totally devoid of interest)—nor even on theology, practical or doctrinal, but on minute points of ecclesiastical discipline, frivolities that seem empty as bubbles to anyone but themselves. Mr. Malone, contriving to secure two glasses of wine while his brethren content themselves with one, gradually becomes hilarious in his own peculiar fashion; that is, he grows slightly insolent, speaks rude things in a hectoring tone, and laughs clamorously at his own brilliance.

Each companion becomes, in turn, his butt. Malone possesses a stock of jokes which he dispenses regularly on convivial occasions like the present, seldom varying his wit, for which there is no necessity, as he never considers himself monotonous and cares little for others' thoughts. He favors Mr. Donne with hints regarding his extreme meagreness, allusions to his turned-up nose, cutting sarcasms concerning a certain threadbare chocolate surtout which that gentleman sports whenever it rains or threatens to do so, and criticisms of a choice set of Cockney phrases and modes of pronunciation, Mr. Donne's own property and certainly deserving of remark

Night had fallen with a heavy, suffocating weight, the sky a bruised canvas of gray rain-clouds that swamped both stars and moon. Even Malone, usually indifferent to the shifting moods of nature, felt the damp chill seep through his clothes. He walked with a dogged persistence, his hat pushed back on the crown, a distinctively Irish habit, his boots sinking rhythmically into the mud of the cart tracks. *"Tramp, splash, tramp, splash"* echoed his progress along the causeway, where the road itself seemed swallowed by the earth. His destination was Hollow's Mill, but his path first led him past the glowing warmth of the Redhouse Inn. Inside, a fire crackled, glasses clinked, and laughter bubbled over an oak settle. For a fleeting second, Malone, a curate with a taste for the comforts of a tumbler of whisky-and-water, hesitated. But looking around, he saw familiar faces—Mr. Helstone's parishioners, neighbors who knew his business. With a resigned sigh, he turned away from the warmth, stepping back into the cold, wet world.

Leaving the high road, Malone cut across the flat, monotonous fields, jumping over hedges and walls with practiced ease. Beyond them stood a large, irregular building, a mill dominated by a towering stack of chimneys. It was silent, save for the rhythmic dripping of rain from the eaves and the low, wild whistle of the wind tearing through the boughs. Further down, the land dropped sharply into a valley, where a single light glimmered like a beacon through the gloom. Steered by this faint glow, Malone reached a small, white-washed house. A fresh-faced servant admitted him, revealing a narrow staircase carpeted in crimson, flanked by doors of matching velvet.

"Is Mr. Moore at home?" Malone asked.

"Yes, sir, but he is not in," the girl replied. "At the mill—in the counting-house."

A crimson door swung open, and a woman appeared. Her hair was dressed with elaborate curls, a style that somehow managed to look both artificial and imposing. To Malone, she seemed less a goddess and more a formidable obstacle. He shrank back slightly, muttering, "I'll go to him," and hurried down a short lane toward the massive black silhouette of the mill.

Inside the counting-house, the atmosphere shifted from the gloom outside to a stark, utilitarian brightness. The room was simple, furnished with sturdy green chairs and a solid desk cluttered with blueprints and machinery designs. An excellent fire burned in the grate, casting a warm glow that did little to soften the sharp edges of the room's function. Mr. Moore entered, a man who looked as though he had been carved from stone and shadow: thin, dark, with a sallow complexion and hair streaked with premature gray. He moved with a quiet intensity, his features marked by a perpetual anxiety that betrayed a mind constantly working ahead of the present moment.

"Welcome, Mr. Malone," Moore said, his voice carrying a faint, foreign accent. "I regret deeply that Mr. Helstone sent for you on such a night. The new machinery requires careful supervision."

Malone sat by the fire, warming his chilled limbs, and the two men fell into conversation. Moore spoke passionately about his business, the "Orders in Council," and the Luddites—the "frame-breakers" who destroyed his machines. He described the devastation caused by the war and the economic blockade, which had cut off America, his primary market. "These fellows have done to others what they may do to me," Moore declared, his eyes flashing with a mixture of fear and defiance. "But I stand by my trade. I am not like Sykes, who gave up when his shop burned. I will fight."

As the conversation waned, Moore noticed Malone growing restless. The curate kicked his heels together, yawning, clearly bored by the grim details of industrial strife. Sensing his guest's need for comfort, Moore produced a gridiron and a kettle. "Can you cook a mutton chop?" he asked, turning to the task with vigorous enthusiasm. Malone, eager to prove his worth, accepted the challenge. Together, they prepared a simple meal, the smell of cooking meat filling the room, breaking the tension of the evening. Moore poured a glass of punch, offering it to Malone with a gesture of genuine hospitality, though his eyes remained fixed on the door, listening intently for any sign of disturbance.

For some time, Moore watched Malone with a strange, unreadable expression, his gaze piercing and meditative. Suddenly, his demeanor shifted. He raised a hand, signaling silence. *"Chut,"* he whispered in a hushed tone. Rising abruptly, he extinguished the lamp and slipped out into the night, leaving Malone alone in the counting-house.

Outside, the air was still and stagnant, the river running swiftly beneath the bridge. Moore's keen ear caught a sound that cut through the silence: the crunch of heavy wheels on stones, distant but unmistakable. He returned to the millyard, lighting a lantern, and waited. Soon, the great wagons emerged, drawn by enormous horses, their hooves splashing through the mud. Moore hailed them, expecting his workers, Joe Scott and the others.

"Ay, ay, devil! All's raight! We've smashed 'em!" a voice cried out.

Before Moore could react, the drivers vanished. The wagons stood empty, abandoned in the yard. Moore rushed to inspect the vehicles, his heart pounding. The frames, the costly machinery he had risked his entire fortune to acquire, were gone. In their place, pinned to the harness of one of the lead horses, was a crumpled piece of paper—a challenge.

Moore read the scrawled message, his face pale under the lantern light. *"Your hellish machinery is shivered... Take this as a warning..."* Anger surged through him, hot and violent, mixed with a terrifying resolve. He would not let them win. He would hunt them down.

Hurrying back inside, Moore gave urgent instructions to Sarah, the servant. Ring the bell, he commanded, until the whole neighborhood heard. Then, he fetched his saddlebags and mounted his horse. Moments later, the heavy tolling of the mill bell shattered the silence, echoing across the hollow. Neighbors from the Redhouse arrived in a panic, lanterns blazing, drawn by the sound. Among them rode Mr. Helstone, the local rector, accompanied by a young man on a shaggy pony.

Moore met them in the yard, his voice steady despite the chaos. "The wagons have been attacked," he announced, holding up the note. "Joe Scott and his men are waylaid on Stilbro' Moor. The frames are destroyed."

A murmur of outrage rippled through the crowd. The situation demanded immediate action. Moore proposed a rescue mission, urging a group of men to accompany him to Stilbro' Moor to find the trapped workers and recover whatever remained of the equipment. Helstone and the young man on the pony volunteered eagerly, their spirits lifted by the prospect of adventure and justice.

As Moore mounted his horse, Helstone leaned close, asking quietly if he had secured the mutton chops, lest anyone else try to steal them. Moore nodded, a grim smile playing on his lips. "Reserve them for those who earn them," he muttered, though the thought lingered uneasily in his mind. The rescue party set off, riding into the dark, wet night, driven by the urgency of the moment and the fierce determination to protect what was theirs. Behind them, the empty wagons stood as a testament to the violence that had erupted, a stark reminder of the fragile balance between progress and tradition, industry and survival.

Cheerfulness, it would seem, is a matter dependent far more upon the internal state of affairs than upon the external world surrounding us. I venture this observation because I observe that Messrs. Helstone and Moore emerged from the mill-yard gates, leading their diminutive retinue, in spirits of the highest order. As the beam of a lantern—each of the three walkers carried one—fell upon Mr. Moore's countenance, one could discern an unusual, indeed a lively, spark dancing in his eyes, a newfound vivacity blooming across his dark features. Similarly, when the light touched the rector's face, his stern visage was transformed, aglow with a radiant, infectious glee. Yet, consider the circumstances: a drizzling night, a somewhat perilous expedition. One might assume such conditions were calculated to dampen spirits rather than enliven them. If any laborer who had worked on Stilbro' Moor had witnessed this procession, they would have taken great pleasure in taking aim at either of the leaders from behind a wall. And the leaders knew this perfectly. Being men of steely nerves and steady hearts, they were elated precisely by this knowledge.

Now, I am aware, dear reader, and need not be reminded, that it is a dreadful thing for a parson to be warlike. I am aware that he ought to be a man of peace. I possess a vague understanding of a clergyman's mission among mankind, and I recall distinctly whose servant he serves and whose example he should emulate. Nevertheless, if you are a parson-hater, do not expect me to accompany you every step of your dismal, downward-trending, unchristian path. Do not expect me to join in your deep anathemas, which are at once so narrow and so sweeping, in your poisonous rancour, so intense and so absurd, against "the cloth." Nor will I raise my eyes and hands in the manner of Supplehough, nor inflate my lungs with the breath of Barraclough, in horror and denunciation of the diabolical rector of Briarfield.

He was not diabolical at all. The simple truth was that he had missed his vocation. He should have been a soldier, and circumstances had conspired to make him a priest. For the rest, he was a conscientious, hard-headed, hard-handed, brave, stern, implacable, faithful little man—a man almost devoid of sympathy, ungentle, prejudiced, and rigid, yet a man true to principle, honorable, sagacious, and sincere. It seems to me that one cannot always trim a man to fit his profession, nor should one curse a man because his profession sometimes hangs upon him ungracefully. Nor will I curse Helstone, though he was a clerical Cossack. Yet he *was* cursed, and by many of his own parishioners, as by others he was adored—which is the frequent fate of men who display partiality in friendship and bitterness in enmity, who are equally devoted to principles and adherent to prejudices.

Since Helstone and Moore were both in excellent spirits and united for the moment in a single cause, one might expect them to converse amicably as they rode side by side. Alas! These two men, both of hard, bilious natures, rarely came into contact without chafing each other's tempers. Their frequent bone of contention was the war. Helstone was a high Tory (there were Tories in those days), and Moore was a bitter Whig—at least, as far as opposition to the war-party was concerned, for that was the sole question affecting his own interests; on no other topic did he profess any British politics. He delighted in infuriating Helstone by declaring his belief in the invincibility of Bonaparte, by taunting England and Europe with the impotence of their efforts to withstand him, and by coolly advancing the opinion that it was as well to yield to him soon as late, since he must, in the end, crush every antagonist and reign supreme.

Helstone could not bear these sentiments. It required only the consideration that Moore was something of an outcast and alien, possessing but half measure of British blood to temper the foreign gall that corroded his veins, before Helstone could bring himself to listen without indulging the fierce wish to cane the speaker. Furthermore, somewhat allaying his disgust, was a fellow-feeling for the dogged tone with which these opinions were asserted, coupled with a profound respect for the consistency of Moore's crabbed contumacy.

As the party turned onto the Stilbro' road, they met whatever little wind there was; the rain dashed in their faces. Moore, having been fretting his companion previously, now braced up by the raw breeze and perhaps irritated by the sharp drizzle, began to goad him anew.

"Does your Peninsular news please you still?" he asked.

"What do you mean?" came the surly demand of the rector.

"I mean, have you still faith in that Baal of a Lord Wellington?"

"And what do you mean now?"

"Do you still believe that this wooden-faced and pebble-hearted idol of England possesses the power to send fire down from heaven to consume the French holocaust you wish to offer up?"

"I believe Wellington will flog Bonaparte's marshals into the sea the day it pleases him to lift his arm."

"But, my dear sir, you cannot be serious in what you say. Bonaparte's marshals are great men, who act under the guidance of an omnipotent master-spirit. Your Wellington is the most humdrum of commonplace martinets, whose slow, mechanical movements are further cramped by an ignorant home government."

"Wellington is the soul of England. Wellington is the right champion of a good cause, the fit representative of a powerful, a resolute, a sensible, and an honest nation."

"Your good cause, as far as I understand it, is simply the restoration of that filthy, feeble Ferdinand to a throne which he disgraced. Your fit representative of an honest people is a dull-witted drover, acting for a duller-witted farmer; and against these are arrayed victorious supremacy and invincible genius."

"Against legitimacy is arrayed usurpation; against modest, single-minded, righteous, and brave resistance to encroachment is arrayed boastful, double-tongued, selfish, and treacherous ambition to possess. God defend the right!"

"God often defends the powerful."

"What! I suppose the handful of Israelites standing dryshod on the Asiatic side of the Red Sea was more powerful than the host of the Egyptians drawn up on the African side? Were they more numerous? Were they better appointed? Were they more mighty, in a word—eh? Don't speak, or you'll tell a lie, Moore; you know you will. They were a poor, overwrought band of bondsmen. Tyrants had oppressed them through four hundred years; a feeble mixture of women and children diluted their thin ranks; their masters, who roared to follow them through the divided flood, were a set of pampered Ethiops, about as strong and brutal as the lions of Libya. They were armed, horsed, and charioted; the poor Hebrew wanderers were afoot. Few of them, it is likely, had better weapons than their shepherds' crooks or their masons' building-tools; their meek and mighty leader himself had only his rod. But bethink you, Robert Moore, right was with them; the God of battles was on their side. Crime and the lost archangel generalled the ranks of Pharaoh, and which triumphed? We know that well. 'The Lord saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians, and Israel saw the Egyptians dead upon the sea-shore'—yea, 'the depths covered them, they sank to the bottom as a stone.' The right hand of the Lord became glorious in power; the right hand of the Lord dashed in pieces the enemy!"

"You are all right; only you forget the true parallel. France is Israel, and Napoleon is Moses. Europe, with her old overgorged empires and rotten dynasties, is corrupt Egypt; gallant France is the Twelve Tribes, and her fresh and vigorous Usurper the Shepherd of Horeb."

"I scorn to answer you."

Moore accordingly answered himself—or rather, he subjoined to what he had just said an additional observation in a lower voice.

"Oh, in Italy he was as great as any Moses! He was the right thing there, fit to head and organize measures for the regeneration of nations. It puzzles me to this day how the conqueror of Lodi should have condescended to become an emperor, a vulgar, a stupid humbug; and still more how a people who had once called themselves republicans should have sunk again to the grade of mere slaves. I despise France! If England had gone as far on the march of civilization as France did, she would hardly have retreated so shamelessly."

"You don't mean to say that besotted imperial France is any worse than bloody republican France?" demanded Helstone fiercely.

To understand Mr. Yorke, one must first accept that he is a creature of stark contrasts, a man carved from the raw granite of the North rather than the polished marble of the South. At fifty-five, though appearing older due to a crown of silver hair, he possessed a visage that was undeniably, thoroughly English. It lacked the Norman curve, the aristocratic grace, or the classical symmetry that fine folks often prize. Instead, his face was a map of unvarnished reality: broad, fresh, and marked by a harsh, northern severity that mirrored the steel in his voice. Every lineament spoke of a rugged, unclassical originality—a countenance that sensible souls might term "characteristic," while the shrewd would delight in its biting sagacity. Yet, it was also a face of indocility and scorn, etched with a sarcasm that defied leadership and mocked direction. Tall, wiry, and carrying a stately integrity, he bore no trace of the clown, projecting an aura of formidable, solitary power.

If you seek a benevolent sage or a kindly philanthropist in Mr. Yorke, you will find yourself disappointed. While he occasionally spoke with sense and warmth to friends like Mr. Moore, his default setting was far colder. His mind, the labyrinthine core of his being, suffered from three distinct deficiencies. First, he lacked the organ of veneration; to kings, nobles, priests, and parliaments, he offered only disdain. He viewed their institutions as rubbish, believing that if the high places were razed and their occupants crushed, the world would emerge cleaner and freer. This absence of awe dried up the electric delights of admiration, leaving him blind to the sublime in beauty or virtue. Second, his inability to compare stripped him of true sympathy. While he preached mutual toleration, he harbored a bigoted hatred for specific classes—particularly "parsons" and "lords"—speaking of them with a harshness that bordered on insolence. He could not step into their shoes to weigh their burdens against their flaws; instead, he projected his own frustrations onto them, wishing for arbitrary, even cruel, solutions to the problems of inequality. And third, he possessed scant benevolence, rendering him impatient with weakness and unforgiving of faults that grated on his sharp nature. His sarcasm was a weapon wielded without mercy, often wounding repeatedly without a second thought for the pain inflicted.

Yet, let us not paint him entirely in shadows. Beneath this rough exterior lay a man of considerable honor and capability, highly respected even by those who loathed him. To his workmen, he was a father figure, considerate and cordial. When dismissing a worker, he went out of his way to help them find new employment or relocate their families. However, his kindness to the downtrodden coexisted with a terrifying tyranny over those beneath him; like his ancestors, he could not bear control, and he crushed rebellion in its germ with the vigor of a gardener pulling weeds. He was a proud man, content to submit to the natural hierarchy of labor, yet haughty as Beelzebub toward anyone he deemed superior. His religion, if it could be called that, was devoid of the tender awe that moves the soul. He believed in a God and a Heaven, but they were concepts stripped of imagination and emotion, belonging to a man who found comfort in the stark logic of the physical world rather than the ethereal.

Regarding poetry and idealism, Mr. Yorke held a pragmatic, if dismissive, view. He lacked the poetic imagination himself, considering it a superfluous, even dangerous, attribute in others. He could appreciate the result of a painter's brush or a musician's instrument—the beauty itself—but he held little regard for the internal struggle, the "fire" and "force" that fueled the artist. To him, the quiet poet who could not play the game of commerce, the tradesman who failed to thrive in the Piece Hall, was destined for obscurity and scorn. He understood, perhaps instinctively, why the true poet needs a truculent spirit beneath a placid surface, why they must laugh in their sleeves when the world condemns them as useless. But these were reflections for the sensitive soul, not for the utilitarian mind of Hiram Yorke.

Despite his faults, Mr. Yorke was a man of influence, the head of the oldest family in the district. His education had been sound, and his travels on the Continent before the French Revolution had left him fluent in French and Italian. His home was adorned with treasures gathered during a two-year sojourn in Italy, and when he chose, his manners were those of a gentleman of the old school. Yet, he preferred the company of the shrewd workman or the sagacious old cottager to the fashionable, insipid fine gentleman. He valued the "Yorkshire burr" over the Cockney lisp, believing the former sounded like a bull's bellow compared to the latter's squeak.

Among his guests, the dynamic between Mr. Yorke and Mr. Moore was complex. Yorke entertained a faint partiality for the younger man, drawn by the foreign accent of his speech and the distinctly non-Yorkshire lines of his face. These trivialities, perhaps, served as a portal to Yorke's own youth and travels, reviving memories of Parisian cafés and Italian streets. Furthermore, a business connection with Moore's father, complicated by shared financial losses, tethered them, as did Yorke's role as guardian of the estate on which Hollow's Mill stood. Finally, he recognized in Robert Moore a sharp businessman, resolved and acute, qualities Yorke respected.

In stark opposition stood Mr. Helstone. Between the free-thinking industrialist and the formalist clergyman ran a double antipathy: the clash of nature and the weight of circumstances. Rumors swirled that they had once been rival suitors for the same lady. Indeed, Mr. Yorke, in his youth, had preferred spirited, dashing women, until he fell utterly, unexpectedly in love with a girl of "living marble" silence—Mary Cave. She was the antithesis of the lively belles he usually courted, answering in monosyllables and offering no smile, yet she became his obsession. Conversely, Mr. Helstone, then a curate, had loved Mary as well, or so he fancied. Unlike Yorke's consuming passion, Helstone approached her with a calm, almost clinical observation. He saw her clearly, without the illusions that clouded the eyes of other admirers. He accepted her readily, marrying her before the depth of his feelings or hers could fully mature.

Tragically, nature had not designed Mr. Helstone to be a husband to a quiet wife. He operated under a delusion that silence equaled contentment, failing to perceive that a woman who did not complain was simply holding her breath. He regarded women as a separate, inferior order of existence, incapable of being companions or confidantes. After a brief period of marriage, Mary ceased to hold any significance for him. Her eventual death, sudden and mysterious, left behind a still, beautiful mold of clay. While the local housekeeper and servants whispered of a broken heart, driven by harsh treatment, Mr. Helstone remained dry-eyed and stoic, scandalizing the neighborhood with his lack of visible grief. Unbeknownst to him, rumors of his cruelty had taken root, feeding on the truth that he had indeed neglected his wife's emotional needs. Mr. Yorke, hearing these tales, felt a bitter, rooted animosity toward his former rival. He could not forgive the fact that what he had cherished so deeply had been "neglected, perhaps abused" by another man. If Helstone had known the extent of Yorke's lost love, he likely would never have crossed his threshold again.

Back in the parlour, the conversation shifted from personal history to the turbulent state of the country. The recent depredations on mill-properties sparked a fierce debate. Mr. Helstone, representing the establishment, argued that the masters were aggrieved and the workpeople unreasonable. He advocated for vigorous government interference, strict magisterial vigilance, and if necessary, prompt military coercion to quell the spirit of disaffection. He viewed the unrest as a dangerous deviation from inevitable evils that required firm correction.

Mr. Yorke, however, scoffed at the notion of inevitable suffering. He asked if military force would feed the hungry or provide work for the unemployed. To him, public patience was a camel already overloaded; the last straw had been laid, and resistance was now a moral duty. He identified the "corrupt, base, and bloody" government as the true tyrant, blaming figures like Pitt and Castlereagh for a ruinous war and oppressive taxation that hung a millstone around England's neck. Eighteen is the threshold where childhood's fairy tale ends and the real world begins. Until then, life is a storybook filled with heroes and monsters, where forests are darker and skies more radiant than nature allows. The moon is larger, the sun a burning heaven of gods. But at eighteen, the shores of Reality rise ahead, painted in blues and greens that promise spring meadows and living waters, yet hiding floods and sorrows beneath. We do not know this yet. Hope smiles, promising tomorrow, and Love wanders in like a lost angel, welcomed with open arms. We miss the quiver in his wing, unaware that his arrows carry wounds disguised as thrills of new life. We believe passion is purely good, blind to the school of experience that awaits us—a mentor with a frozen face, a heavy rod, and an inexorable hand that drags the novice into the wilds of life, forcing the acquisition of hard truths.

Spring evenings are often cold and raw. Spring advanced, turning the surface of England a pleasant green, yet the heart of the nation remained frozen. While fields bloomed and gardens opened, the country's soul was still tormented by a war that drained her blood and wealth for ends that seemed increasingly inadequate. News of victories in the Peninsula arrived in slow, sparse drips, interrupted by long silences filled only with the arrogant boasts of Bonaparte. For those who bore the brunt of the conflict, this struggle felt endless and despairing; they clamored for peace on any terms whatsoever. Men like Sir John Moore and the countless merchants trembling on the edge of bankruptcy fought for this deliverance with the desperate energy of men holding back a collapsing dam.

They held meetings, delivered speeches, and circulated petitions, caring little for the specific conditions of peace. After all, men taken individually are naturally selfish, and when gathered in bodies, that selfishness intensifies into a roaring tide. The British merchant class illustrated this rule with striking clarity. Too obsessed with the accumulation of gold, they were blind to every national consideration save the expansion of commerce—*their* commerce. Chivalry, honor, and disinterested patriotism had died within them. A nation ruled solely by traders would likely submit ignominiously to a conqueror, not from noble conviction, but from the seduction of Mammon. During the recent wars, English tradesmen would have borne blows to both cheeks, handed their cloaks to Napoleon, and politely offered their coats and waistcoats if pressed. They would pray only to keep their final garment, solely to protect the purse within it. It was only when the Corsican thief's hand closed around that beloved purse that these "British bulldogs" would spring at the robber's throat, hanging there, insatiable and furious, until the treasure was returned. When speaking against war, these merchants always claimed to hate it for its brutality, painting themselves as uniquely civilized and kind. In reality, many were narrow, cold-hearted, and viewed anyone outside trade as a parasite breathing air they didn't deserve. Long may it be before England truly becomes a nation of shopkeepers!

Sir John Moore, despite his lack of sacrificial patriotism and his tendency to prioritize personal interest, fought fiercely when pushed to the brink of ruin. He agitated in the North, instigating others with greater influence than his own. Occasionally, flashes of reason pierced his foggy mind regarding his party's demands on government. When he heard of Europe arming to resist Bonaparte, when he saw Russia rising to defend her frozen soil and serfs from foreign tyranny, he realized that a free realm like England could not delegate its sons to make concessions to an unjust, grasping leader. Yet, amidst the news of Lord Wellington's steady, unhasting, and unresting advances in the Peninsula, Moore confessed a deep-seated admiration. He saw a vigilance and endurance in the British troops that promised ultimate victory. But victory lay in the "end"—a horizon still far off. Meanwhile, as an individual, Moore feared he would be crushed, his hopes ground to dust. His focus shifted entirely to himself, to fulfilling his own destiny.

He fulfilled it so vigorously that a decisive rupture ensued with his old Tory friend, the Rector, Mr. Helstone. They quarreled publicly and exchanged biting letters in the newspapers. Mr. Helstone denounced Moore as a Jacobin, severed all ties, and strictly forbade Caroline's niecehood communications with Hollow's Cottage. He insisted she cease taking French lessons, dismissing the language as frivolous and injurious to the weak female mind. He questioned who first made it fashionable to teach women French, comparing it to feeding a rickety child chalk and water gruel. Caroline, obedient and quiet, agreed immediately. There was no vexed flush or gathering tear; the shadowy thoughtfulnes that had covered her features remained undisturbed. Indeed, the mandate coincided with her own judgment. Going to Hollow's Cottage had become a source of pain, meeting only disappointment. Hope and love had fled that little tenement; Robert seemed to have deserted its precincts. Whenever she asked after him—a rarity, as uttering his name made her face grow hot—the answer was invariably that he was from home or consumed by business. Hortense feared he was killing himself with application.

At church, Caroline rarely looked at him, for it was both too painful and too pleasurable to do so, and she had learned that such emotion was ultimately wasted. On a particularly dark, wet Sunday, when few attended and the observant ladies were absent, she allowed her eye to seek Robert's pew. He sat alone, arms folded, eyes cast down, looking sadder and more abstracted than usual. Depressed, the hue of his face seemed darker, his olive skin devoid of color. By instinct, Caroline knew his thoughts were running in channels far removed from her comprehension, wrapped in interests and responsibilities from which she was excluded. Nothing they had ever discussed was in his mind.

Meditating on this separation, Caroline realized the vast disparity between their conditions. "Different, indeed," she thought. "I think only of him; he has no room, no leisure, to think of me. Love has been the predominant emotion of my heart for two years... Quite other feelings absorb his reflections." When service ended and Robert rose, he did not turn his head toward her. He gave her no look. "Strange that grief should almost choke me," she lamented, "because another human being's eye has failed to greet mine."

That evening, after tea, Caroline retired to her chamber. Mute and solitary, she paced the carpeted floor, her head drooped, hands folded. The study door muffled the voices of Mr. Helstone and Mr. Malone, who were discussing theology. The servants were quiet, engrossed in books Miss Helstone had lent them for "Sunday reading." Caroline, too, had a book open, but she could not read it. Its theology was incomprehensible, and her mind teemed with images of Moore, winter firesides, and the lush landscape of Nunnely Wood. She remembered sharing September treasures of blackberries with him, feeding them to him berry by berry like a bird feeding a fledgling. But these joys, hollow and illusory, were quickly crushed. The pictures faded, the voice failed, and the warm seal of lips on her forehead felt now like a cold, sleety raindrop. Returning from an enchanted region to the real world, she saw her narrow chamber instead of Nunnely Wood and heard the rain on her casement instead of the south wind.

Turning from the pale phantom of her reflection, she sat down, resolving to live. "As far as I know, I have good health," she mused, "half a century may lie before me. How am I to occupy it?" She reflected that she would likely never be married, for Robert did not care for her. She would probably be an old maid, watching Robert marry some wealthy lady, while she remained unmarried. "What was I created for?" she wondered. "Where is my place in the world?"

She saw then the puzzle that plagued old maids: the question of purpose. Others solved it by saying, "Your place is to do good to others." But she sensed a terrible hollowness in that existence, a craving for something of one's own to bestow. Was virtue merely abnegation of self? Did undue humility create tyranny? She suspected not. Every human being had a share of rights, an allotment to be held tenaciously. Life was short, a vapour like a dream, terminating inevitably in the grave, where the body decays and springs anew, while the soul winged its flight upward to gaze upon the vision of the Trinity.

Caroline did not yield easily. Within the quiet chambers of her girlhood, she harbored a strength that was rarely summoned, yet when called upon, it proved formidable. It is a peculiar truth of human nature that men and women fight hardest when they stand alone—without witnesses, counselors, or confidants, unaided, unadvised, and unpitied. Miss Helstone found herself in this solitary arena. Her suffering was the only spur that drove her forward; it was acute and piercing, striking a nerve that woke her spirit to a feverish vigilance. Each evening she returned, pale and worn, yet seemingly devoid of fatigue. As soon as she removed her bonnet and shawl, instead of resting, she began to pace her room. The charity fund flouried. Through Miss Keeldar's inspiring example, the tireless efforts of the three rectors, and the steady, silent support of their spinster aides—Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall—a substantial sum was secured. Managed wisely, these resources immediately alleviated the suffering of the unemployed poor, bringing a palpable calm to the neighborhood. Yet, whenever she encountered Gérard Moore, her tenant, she sensed a discord beneath his quiet listening. She implored him with impatient glances for an explanation, some depth to his thoughts. He would smile, his expression lending a peculiar sweetness to his mouth while his brow remained grave, and speak of his faith in the war's end. "I trust in the finite nature of this conflict," he would say, anchoring his hopes there. "I work Hollow's Mill entirely on speculation. I sell nothing; there is no market. As long as my hands are free to work, I cannot be depressed. One year, nay, six months of this 'olive reign,' and I shall be safe. You are right regarding commerce, but I harbor doubts about the permanence of this tranquility. Eleemosynary relief never truly tranquilizes the working classes; it does not make them grateful. It is not in human nature to be so. Were they in our position, they would feel the same humiliation. Furthermore, why should they be grateful? The disaffected here maintain constant correspondence with the agitators of Nottingham, Manchester, and Birmingham. "Well, Mr. Moore," the conference invariably concluded, "take care of yourself. If you think I have ever done you any good, reward me by promising to take care of yourself."

"I do," Moore replied, his voice steady. "I will take close and watchful care. I wish to live, not to die. The future opens like Eden before me; and yet, when I look deep into the shades of this paradise, I see a vision that I like better than seraph or cherub gliding across remote vistas."

The early days of May had been fine, the middle wet, but as the moon changed, the skies cleared. A fresh wind swept away the heavy, silver-white rainclouds, carrying them eastward until they vanished behind the horizon, revealing a vault of pure blue, ready for the reign of the summer sun. That sun rose broad and golden on Whitsuntide. The schools prepared for their great assembly with splendid weather.

Whit-Tuesday marked the climax. The two large schoolrooms of Briarfield, recently built by the present rector at considerable personal expense, were scrubbed, whitewashed, repainted, and adorned with flowers and evergreens—gathered from the rectory garden, two cartloads from Fieldhead, and a wheelbarrow-full from the stingier domain of De Walden. Twenty tables, each seating twenty guests, were laid out with white cloths, surrounded by benches. Suspended above them were dozens of cages filled with canaries, a fancy cherished by Mr. Helstone's clerk who delighted in their piercing songs. These tables were not for the twelve hundred scholars, whose feast was to be held outdoors, but for the patrons and teachers.

At one o'clock the troops would arrive; by two they would be marshaled; by four they would parade the parish, followed by the feast and a meeting in the church with music and speeches. Why Briarfield was chosen as the rendezvous was not due to population size—Whinbury far exceeded it—or antiquity, for Nunnely's ancient temple predated it all. It was simply because Mr. Helstone willed it so, and his will proved stronger than the objections of either Boultby or Hall. They dared not dispute his authority.

For Caroline Helstone, this anniversary had traditionally been a trial. It forced her into public view, compelling her to face the wealthy and influential without the protection of her mother, aunt, or any chaperon. Obligated to walk at the head of her regiment as the rector's niece and first teacher, and to serve tea to a mixed multitude of ladies and gentlemen, she trembled at the prospect. She was a nervous soul who mortally feared publicity.

But this year, Shirley was with her. Shirley altered the nature of the trial entirely, transforming it into something akin to enjoyment. Miss Keeldar was better company than a host of ordinary friends; she was self-possessed, spirited, and easy, possessing a social confidence that inspired courage in others. The only worry was Shirley's tendency to linger behind schedule. At the precise moment the church clock tolled two, the bells would clash and the march begin. Caroline realized she must ensure Shirley's punctuality, for if her companion failed to appear, Caroline would be left stranded.

Whit-Tuesday dawned almost before sunrise. Caroline, Fanny, and Eliza spent the morning arranging the rectory parlors in first-class order, setting out a collation of cooling refreshments—wine, fruit, and cakes—upon the dining-room sideboard. Caroline then donned her finest attire: a dress of white muslin, perfect for the solemnity and brilliance of the day. Her new sash, a birthday present from Margaret Hall which she suspected Cyril had purchased in return for a set of cambric bands, was tied by Fanny's dexterous fingers. Her simple bonnet was trimmed to match, and her white crape scarf suited her modest dress. When ready, she formed a picture—not bright enough to dazzle, but fair enough to interest; not striking, yet delicately pleasing. Her sweetness of tint, purity of air, and grace of mien compensated for the lack of rich color. Her brown eyes and clear forehead revealed a mind that was modest, gentle, and pensive, harmonious like a lamb or a dove. Yet, as Cyril Hall noted, she was an imperfect human being, lacking the goodness and greatness of the withered Miss Ainley, who was currently donning her best black gown and Quaker drab shawl in her narrow cottage chamber.

Caroline departed, gliding quickly across secluded fields and hidden lanes toward Fieldhead. No dust or moisture soilled her garment or dampened her sandals. After the recent rains, everything was clean and dry under the glowing sun. She walked fearlessly over daisies and turf, through thick plantations, until she reached Fieldhead and entered Miss Keeldar's dressing room.

If she had not arrived, Shirley would have been hopelessly late. Instead of hurrying, Shirley lay stretched on a couch, absorbed in reading. Mrs. Pryor stood nearby, vainly urging her to rise. Caroline wasted no words; she snatched the book from Shirley's hands and began the arduous business of disrobing and rerobing her. Shirley did look the picture of that tiresome quality, though.

There was a striking contrast between them. Shirley possessed style in every fold of her dress and every line of her figure. Shirley possessed a constitution of remarkable resilience. Warm-hearted and sympathetic, she was not prone to nervous collapse. Time seemed to waste itself in vigils like this, which Caroline had kept too often of late. When the mind finds no pleasant food to nourish it, no manna of hope, no honeycomb of joyful memories, it survives on the meagre diet of wishes. Failing to find delight or support in these hollow cravings, the mind feels ready to perish, turning to philosophy, to resolution, to resignation. It cries out to these gods for aid, but the cry goes unheard, unhelped, leaving the sufferer languishing in the dark.

Caroline was a Christian, and in her trouble, she framed prayers according to the Creed, offering them with deep earnestness, begging for patience, strength, and relief. Yet, as the world knows, life is a scene of trial and probation. For any favorable result her petitions had wrought so far, it seemed to her that they fell on deaf ears. Sometimes she believed God had turned His face away from her entirely. At moments, she felt the grip of Calvinism, sinking into a gulf of religious despair where she saw the darkening doom of reprobation hanging over her.

Many have lived through hours like this, when hope hangs on by a thread and the day of fruition is endlessly deferred, causing the heart to truly sicken. It is a terrible hour, yet it is often the darkest point before the dawn—that turning of the year when the icy wind of January sweeps across the land, carrying simultaneously the funeral dirge of departing winter and the prophecy of coming spring. The perishing birds, shivering before the blast, cannot understand it; so little can the suffering soul, in the climax of its affliction, recognize the approaching light of deliverance. Yet, let anyone who still grieves cling fast to love and faith in God. He will never deceive, nor finally desert. "Whom He loveth, He chasteneth." These words are true and must not be forgotten.

Sir William Moore, having demonstrated his fierce resolve in the defense of the mill, turned his gaze with equal ferocity toward those who had instigated the unrest. While he spared the common mob—the misguided followers driven by hunger and false counsel—he pursued the ringleaders with an indefatigable, relentless vigor. To him, punishing the suffering masses would have been the act of a tyrant, not a judge; thus, he let them pass unseen on the roads and streets. But the leaders were different. They were strangers, emissaries from the great cities, mostly "down-draughts"—bankrupts, debt-ridden drunkards, and men with nothing to lose and everything to gain in terms of character and cash. These were the targets Moore hunted with the enthusiasm of a sleuth-hound, finding the chase infinitely more thrilling than the mundane labor of weaving cloth.

His horse seemed to share his master's disdain for these times, enduring a regimen of hard riding that left them both breathless. Moore lived his life on the road, breathing in the crisp, invigorating air of the countryside rather than the choking steam of the dye-houses. The local magistrates, men of timidity and slowness, likely dreaded him. Moore relished the power to frighten them, to force a tremor of fear into their resolve—a simple, primal terror of assassination. Indeed, this shadow hung over every manufacturer and public figure in the district save for the old Cossack himself. Sir William knew the peril; he knew he could be shot. Yet, for a man of his steel nerves, such a death held no terror. If given a choice, it would have been his preferred end.

Conversely, the knowledge that he hunted assassins acted as a spur to his high spirits. Fear was foreign to his proud, hard-natured constitution. Countless nights, he rode alone over the moors, whether moonlit or moonless, feeling more elated and refreshed than when trapped within the stifling walls of his counting-house. There were four leaders to account for. Within two weeks, two were cornered near Stilbro', while the final pair required a journey further west, rumored to haunt the outskirts of Birmingham.

Yet, amidst this storm of stern justice and exhausting business, did Moore ever spare a moment for gentler fires? Did he nurture anything softer than the cold embers of Nemesis? It was difficult to discern. He rarely ventured near Fieldhead, and his visits to the rectory were strictly confined to formal conferences with the rector in his study. His course remained rigid, unmoving. Meanwhile, the wider world remained embroiled in turmoil. The war's hurricane still swept across the continent, bringing no respite, no serene skies, and no gentle rains to soothe the land. Beneath Moore's feet, the specter of Ruin worked tirelessly, sapping the foundations of the region. Whether crossing his hearth or galloping over the sullen moors, he felt the earth vibrate with a hollow echo, shaking beneath his tread.

But how did the season pass for Shirley and Caroline? Let us first turn our attention to the heiress. Was she a love-lorn maiden, pale and pining for a neglectful lover? Did she spend her days bent over sedentary tasks, eyes fixed on books or sewing, silent and withdrawn?

Far from it. Shirley was vibrant and whole. Though a wistfulness lingered in her gaze, her careless smiles remained intact. She kept her dark, ancient manor house aglow with her cheerful presence. The galleries and low-ceiled chambers echoed with her lively voice; the dim entrance hall grew accustomed to the rhythmic rustle of silk dresses as she swept from room to room. Now she carried flowers to the barbarous peach-bloom salon, now entered the dining room to open casements and let in the scents of mignonette and sweet-briar, and anon brought plants from the staircase window to the sun-drenched porch.

Sewing occupied her hands, but fate seemed to doom her to abandon it before five minutes elapsed. Hardly would her thimble be fitted or her needle threaded when a sudden impulse would whisk her upstairs—perhaps to retrieve an unnecessary ivory-backed needle-book or a confused drawer, or merely to peer from a specific window at the view of Briarfield church and rectory nestled in the trees. Before she returned, Tartar's bold scrape and strangled whistle would herald his arrival at the porch. Panting from the heat, he must be ushered to the kitchen to ensure his water-bowl was full.

Through the open kitchen door lay the courtyard, a riot of sunshine and gaiety, teeming with turkeys, poults, peahens, Guinea-fowls, and a dazzling array of pigeons in white, purple, blue, and cinnamon plumage. An irresistible spectacle, Shirley ran to the pantry for a roll and stood on the doorstep scattering crumbs. Soon, her eager, plump, happy feathered vassals thronged around her. John was about the stables, and he demanded conversation; her mare needed admiration. Still petting the animal, she was interrupted by the cows returning to be milked. This was of paramount importance; Shirley stayed to inspect them all. Perhaps there were tiny calves or newly yeaned lambs, maybe twins rejected by their mothers. Beside her sprawled the tawny, lion-like bulk of Tartar, his negro muzzle resting on his forepaws, straight, strong, and shapely like the limbs of an Alpine wolf. "My dear, you had better not have that great dog so near you; he is crushing the border of your dress."

The book, perhaps a good one, had refreshed, refilled, and rewarmed her heart, setting her brain afire with images and ideas. The still parlour, the clean hearth, the window opening onto the twilight sky and revealing its "sweet regent," new-throned and glorious, sufficed to transform earth into an Eden and life into a poem for Shirley. A deep, inborn delight glowed in her young veins, unmingled and untarnished, incapable of being seized or spoiled by human agency, for it was a divine gift, the free dower of Nature bestowed upon her child. This joy granted her a glimpse of a *genii*-life. Buoyant, ascending by green steps and glad hills, all verdance and light, she reached a station scarcely lower than that from which angels looked down upon the dreamer of Bethel. Her eye sought, and her soul possessed, the vision of life as she wished it. Alas, not as she wished it; she had no time to wish. The swift glory spread out, sweeping and kindling, multiplying its splendors faster than Thought could combine them or Aspiration articulate her longings. While the trance held her, Shirley said nothing, quite mute; but if Mrs. Pryor spoke to her now, Shirley would depart quietly, continuing her walk upstairs in the dim gallery.

Had Shirley not been indolent, reckless, and ignorant, she would have seized a pen at such moments, while the memory burned fresh on her spirit. She would have fixed the apparition, told the vision revealed. Had she possessed a greater measure of the organ of acquisitiveness, a stronger love of property, she would have taken a large sheet of paper and written plainly, in her own queer yet clear hand, the story narrated, the song sung to her, thereby possessing what she had created. But indolent she was, reckless she was, and most ignorant; for she did not know that her dreams were rare, her feelings peculiar. She did not know, nor would she know until her dying day, the full value of that spring whose bright, fresh bubblings kept her heart green.

Shirley took life easily.

The heat of a fine summer day had pressed down upon Caroline, sealing her within the solitude of the Rectory. Though surrounded by the blooming gardens of an English home, she felt as isolated as if wandering the wastes of Sahara. She fled the solitude, the sadness, and the nightmare of her daily existence, running down the green lane that sloped toward the Hollow. Sometimes the future whispers a low, trembling warning, like a distant storm gathering in the sky, announcing a blast strong enough to scatter wreckage across the sea. At other times, it bursts forth with sudden violence, as if a tombstone had cracked open to release a sleeper from the grave—a new Lazarus emerging from the dark.

Caroline Helstone returned home from Hollow's Cottage feeling robust, as she believed. Yet, the next morning, a heavy, unfamiliar languor pressed down upon her. Breakfast, and every meal thereafter, tasted of ash and sawdust; she lost all desire to eat.

"Am I ill?" she asked, staring into the mirror. Her eyes burned brightly, her pupils wide, her cheeks flushed with a rosy, unusual fullness. "I look well; why cannot I eat?"

A frantic pulse beat in her temples, and her mind raced with strange, brilliant, broken thoughts. A warm glow suffused her skin. Then came a hot, parched, restless night. Toward dawn, a terrifying nightmare seized her like a tiger. When she awoke, she knew with certainty that she was sick.

She could not recall how the fever had taken root. Perhaps, on her final walk home, a sweet, poisoned breeze—laden with honey-dew and miasma—had slipped into her lungs. Finding there a fever born of mental excitement and the lingering fatigue of long, habitual sadness, it had fanned a spark into a roaring fire.

During this season, the mother and daughter seemed left almost entirely alone in the neighborhood. Almost every family in Briarfield was absent on an excursion. Miss Keeldar and her friends were at the seaside; so was Mrs. Yorke's household. Caroline's youth could now serve her, as could her mother's nurturing. Fortune, often called changeful, sometimes repeats her strokes with remarkable consistency. Miss Keeldar's refusal of Samuel Fawthrop Wynne created a sensation throughout the district. Three additional offers followed, all from eligible gentlemen of ample means and unexceptionable character. Each was pressed upon her by her uncle, and each was rejected. The local gossips, initially perplexed by her insolent fastidiousness, eventually concluded they had discovered the key to her conduct.

Consequently, Mr. Sympson's demeanor underwent a radical transformation. The tension at Fieldhead, previously exacerbated by his aunt's inability to reconcile with her quarrelsome nephew, dissipated. The atmosphere shifted from one of chilling dread to tranquility.

The narrative focus then turned to the village of Nunnely, specifically its ancient priory and its owner, Sir Philip Nunnely, a baronet. Unlike the other estates in the region, Nunnely boasted a man of actual title. During Shirley's stay at Cliffbridge, she had encountered Sir Philip frequently. He was solitary, unpretending, and seemingly content with their company, though he never condescended to them.

Shirley found him kind and modest, delighting in the power to amuse him. A slight drawback, however, marred the friendship: Sir Philip possessed a fervent literary turn. He composed poetry—sonnets, stanzas, ballads—and was prone to reciting them. Shirley, finding his verses lacking in musicality and fire, often winced when he revisited the subject, diligently steering the conversation elsewhere. Nevertheless, he persistently invited her on moonlit walks along the bridge solely to pour forth his ballads, leading her to secluded spots where he would read his sonnets in a voice tremulous with emotion. Unaware that his rhymes were merely mechanical exercises devoid of true poetic spirit, he failed to perceive Shirley's mortification.

Despite her efforts to wean him from this monomania, Sir Philip remained devoted to his muse. Eventually, following Shirley's advice to return to his ancestral halls, he traveled to the Priory in late September. He soon extended visits to Fieldhead, praising the hospitality of the oak-beamed manor. His presence was assiduous, covering Shirley with distinction and earning the admiration of her uncle, who now viewed her not as a "mad girl," but as a "superior person" destined for a splendid future.

As Sir Philip's courtship progressed, strange feelings permeated Fieldhead. Restless hopes and anxious anticipations haunted the household. It became clear that Sir Philip was a man of genuine sensibility and intelligence, free from the vulgarity Shirley had condemned in Samuel Wynne. Although physically slight and boyish in appearance, Shirley perceived a nobility in his heart that outweighed physical perfection.

Meanwhile, a subplot involving Louis Moore, Shirley's tutor, unfolded. Louis Moore was accustomed to a life of profound quietude, a state far better suited to his temperament than the bustling clamor of the wider world. All of Briarfield marveled at his strange absence, and the villages of Whinbury and Nunnely brought forth their own distinct contributions of astonishment. Was the reason for his delay a mystery? He had finished the business that had driven him away months prior. His four ringleaders had been quickly identified, tracked down, tried, sentenced, and safely shipped off for transportation. This fact was common knowledge at Briarfield; the newspapers had reported it, and the *Stilbro' Courier* had provided every detail, amplified. Yet, applause for his perseverance was absent. Just before this, Shirley had gazed from a window down onto the park. She had witnessed the stormy moonlight, perhaps the same beams Professor Louis was contemplating from his oak parlour. She saw the domain's isolated giants—the broad, strong oaks and the high-towering heroic beeches—wrestling with the gale. Turning from sight and sound, she was touched, if not rapt; awakened, if not inspired.

When called upon, she sang. The ballad spoke of faithful love, undeterred by disaster, growing fonder in calamity, clinging tighter in poverty. Set to a fine old air, the words were simple and sweet. Perhaps, on paper, they lacked force, but when sung, they needed nothing. Shirley sang them with mastery. She breathed softness into the feeling, pouring force around the passion. Her voice was fine, its expression dramatically vivid. She impressed everyone, and charmed one in particular.

Old Lady Nunnely eyed her stonily from her great chair by the fireside. Her gaze declared, "This woman is not of my kind, nor of my daughters'. I object to her as my son's wife."

With no other hand to raise, no other voice to offer kindness, no other mind to devise a plan, he was forced to act alone. This absolute dependence of the speechless, bleeding boy on his benevolence ensured that benevolence was exercised with unyielding efficiency. Mr. Yorke, who relished the exercise of power, now held dominion over a fellow creature's life in the palm of his hand. It suited him perfectly.

Equally, the incident appealed to his saturnine better half. While lesser women might have been stricken with terror by a gory man dragged over their threshold to lie in the "howe of the night," Mrs. Yorke found in such drama a source of exhilaration. Where others would have succumbed to hysteria, she remained composed, her spirits buoyed by the crisis. An attempted murder near her door, a half-dead man resting in her best bed, set her cap askew with a dash akin to a turban and filled her with a strange, vigorous energy.

Mrs. Yorke possessed a peculiar constitution; she could render the drudging existence of a simple maid miserable, yet nurse a hospital full of plague victims like a heroic saint. She developed an almost tender regard for Moore. When she found him committed to her charge, left in her arms as dependent as her youngest child, her tough heart yearned for him. If she had seen a domestic or one of her daughters approach him with water or to smooth his pillow, she would have boxed their ears. Consequently, she banished Jessie and Rose from the upper realms of the house and strictly forbade the maids from entering.

Had the accident occurred at the rectory gates and Old Helstone had taken in the martyr, neither Yorke nor his wife would have pitied him; they would have deemed him rightly served for his tyranny and meddling. But as it stood, he became the apple of their eye.

But when Hortense Moore arrived, the situation shifted. The entire family of the Moores seemed to suit Mrs. Yorke better than any other. Hortense and she shared an exhaustless mutual fascination with the corrupt propensities of servants, viewing them with identical suspicion and severity. Dr. MacTurk, to whom Moore's case was entrusted, pronounced the wound dangerous, though hopefully not hopeless. Initially, he desired to assign a nurse of his own selection, but neither Mrs. Yorke nor Hortense would consent. Promising faithful adherence to his directions, he left them in charge.

Undoubtedly, they executed the trust to the best of their ability, yet something went awry. The bandages were displaced or tampered with, resulting in a catastrophic loss of blood. Dr. MacTurk, summoned with a steed foaming at the mouth, proved to be one of those surgeons dangerous to vex; abrupt in his best moods and savage in his worst. Upon witnessing Moore's state, he relieved his feelings with a flowery vocabulary unnecessary to recount here. A bouquet of the choicest blossoms fell upon the unperturbed head of Mr. Graves, a stony young assistant he usually carried with him; another nosegay landed on another young gentleman in his train—an interesting facsimile of himself, indeed his own son—but the full corbeille of blushing blooms fell to the lot of meddling womankind, en masse.

For most of a winter night, the doctor and his satellites labored around Moore. Shuttered alone with him in his chamber, they wrought and wrangled over his exhausted frame. Three stood on one side of the bed, and Death on the other. The conflict was sharp, lasting until daybreak, when the balance between the belligerents appeared so equal that either party might have claimed victory.

At dawn, Graves and the younger MacTurk remained in charge, while the senior departed to secure additional strength in the person of Mrs. Horsfall, the best nurse on his staff. With stern injunctions regarding the heavy responsibility laid upon her shoulders, he handed Moore over to her. She accepted this burden stolidly, taking likewise the easy-chair at the bedhead. That moment, she began her reign.

Mrs. Horsfall possessed one virtue: she obeyed orders received from MacTurk to the letter. In her eyes, the Ten Commandments were less binding than her surgeon's dictum. In every other respect, she was no woman but a dragon. Hortense Moore fell effaced before her; Mrs. Yorke withdrew, crushed. Yet both women were personages of some dignity in their own estimation and considerable bulk in the eyes of others. Perfectly cowed by the breadth, height, bone, and brawn of Mrs. Horsfall, they retreated to the back parlor. She, for her part, sat upstairs when she pleased and downstairs when she preferred it, consuming three drams daily and four pipes of tobacco.

As for Moore, no one dared inquire about him. Mrs. Horsfall had him at dry-nurse; it was she who was to do for him, and the general conjecture ran that she did so accordingly.

Morning and evening, MacTurk visited him. Complicated by this new mischance, his case had become an object of intense interest to the surgeon, who regarded him as a damaged piece of clockwork, crediting his skill with the ability to set it going again. Graves and the young MacTurk—Moore's sole other visitors—contemplated him as they might the occupant of a dissecting table at Stilbro' Infirmary.

Robert Moore enjoyed a peculiar existence—in pain, in danger, too weak to move, almost too weak to speak, with a sort of giantess as his keeper and the three surgeons as his only company. Thus he lay through the diminishing days and lengthening nights of the dreary month of November.

Initially, Moore feebly resisted Mrs. Horsfall. He hated the sight of her rough bulk and dreaded the contact of her hard hands, but she taught him docility in a trice. She made no account whatsoever of his six feet, his manly thews, and sinews; she turned him in his bed as another woman would turn a babe in its cradle. When he was good, she addressed him as "my dear" and "honey"; when he was bad, she sometimes shook him. If he attempted to speak when MacTurk was present, she lifted her hand and bade him "Hush!" like a nurse checking a forward child. If she had not smoked, and if she had not taken gin, it would have been better, he thought. Once, in her absence, he intimated to MacTurk that "that woman was a dram-drinker."

"Pooh! my dear sir, they are all so," replied the surgeon. "But Horsfall has this virtue," he added, "drunk or sober, she always remembers to obey *me*."

Martin Moore's plans were intricate, woven with the skill of a master architect, designed solely for his private diversion. Yet, history teaches us that even the most dexterous schemes often crumble before the sweeping broom of Fate—a domestic figure whose red arm, wielded with irresistible force, knows no master. "Good-bye!" he replied again, adding, "You have been a mother to me; grant your wilful son one embrace."

Like a stranger offering gifts, he presented one cheek, then the other. She kissed them.

"Praise God!" he exclaimed. "I have had enough nursing to last a lifetime."

Let the worst come, I can work, as Joe Scott does, for an honorable living; in such a fate, I see hardship but no degradation. Once, financial ruin equated to personal dishonor in my eyes. He spoke of his future, envisioning a life far from the confines of England, perhaps among the Blackfeet or Flatheads, seeking a partner who could join him in the wilderness. Shirley, initially stunned, rallied. She admitted a "little bit" of love but demanded honesty, refusing to play games. The conversation shifted to a matter of principle: the equality of their souls. She questioned whether they were equals, asking if he would be kind and refrain from tyranny. He, in turn, asked if she would let him breathe freely and not bewilder him. The tension was palpable, the air thick with unspoken promises and dangers.
