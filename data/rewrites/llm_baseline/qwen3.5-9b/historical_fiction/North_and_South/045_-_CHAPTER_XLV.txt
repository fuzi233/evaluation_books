"Where are the sounds that swam along / The buoyant air when I was young; / The last vibration now is o'er, / And they who listened are no more; / Ah! let me close my eyes and dream." —W. S. Landor.

The vision of Helstone had taken root in Mr. Bell's waking consciousness during his talk with Mr. Lennox, and throughout the long hours of the night, it ran riot through his dreams. Once again, he found himself a tutor in the college where he currently held the rank of Fellow; once again, it was a long vacation, and he was staying with his newly married friend—the proud husband and happy Vicar of Helstone. Together, they leaped across babbling brooks, impossible jumps that seemed to suspend them whole days in mid-air. Time and space ceased to exist, though everything else felt undeniably real. Every event was measured not by its physical duration, but by the intensity of emotion, for in this realm, nothing possessed true existence. Yet, the trees blazed with gorgeous autumnal foliage; the warm scents of flowers and herbs drifted sweetly upon the senses; and the young wife moved about the house with that specific blend of irritation regarding her financial station, mixed with deep pride in her handsome and devoted husband—a dynamic Mr. Bell had observed in real life nearly twenty-five years prior.

The dream was so vividly lifelike that upon awakening, his current reality felt like a phantom. Where was he? In a cramped, handsomely furnished room of a London hotel! Where were the voices that had spoken to him, the movements around him, the touches upon his skin, mere moments ago? Gone! Buried! Lost forever, as far as "earth's forevermore" could stretch. He was an old man, so recently exulting in the full vigor of manhood. The utter loneliness of his life became insupportable to contemplate.

He rose hastily, attempting to banish these thoughts by rushing into a hurried dressing for breakfast in Harley Street. He could not fully attend to the lawyer's intricate details. As he watched, Margaret's eyes widened and her lips grew pale; one by one, fate seemed to decree, or so it appeared, that every scrap of evidence capable of exonerating Frederick fell from beneath her grasp and vanished. Even Mr. Lennox's usually regulated professional voice softened into a tender murmur as he approached the extinction of their last hope.

It was not that Margaret had been unaware of the outcome beforehand. Rather, the relentless minutiae of each successive disappointment poured in to extinguish all remaining hope until she finally broke down in tears. Mr. Lennox paused his reading.

"I had better not go on," he said, his voice filled with concern. "It was a foolish proposal of mine. Lieutenant Hale..." Even granting him the title of the service from which he had so harshly been expelled proved soothing to Margaret. "Lieutenant Hale is happy now; more secure in fortune and future prospects than he could ever have been in the navy; and has, doubtless, adopted his wife's country as his own."

"That is it," Margaret replied. "It seems so selfish in me to regret it," she managed a faint smile, "and yet he is lost to me, and I am so lonely."

Mr. Lennox turned over his papers, silently wishing he were as wealthy and prosperous as he believed he someday would be. Mr. Bell blew his nose but remained silent, as did Margaret, who within a minute or two appeared to recover her usual composure. She thanked Mr. Lennox very courteously for his trouble—courteousness heightened precisely because she was acutely conscious that her behavior might lead him to believe he had caused her needless pain. Yet, it was pain she would not have been without.

Mr. Bell stepped forward to bid her farewell.

"Margaret!" he exclaimed, fumbling with his gloves. "I am going down to Helstone tomorrow to inspect the old place. Would you like to come with me? Or would it give you too much pain? Speak out, do not be afraid."

"Oh, Mr. Bell," she began, but could find no further words. Instead, she took his old, gout-afflicted hand and kissed it.

"Come, come; that's enough," he said, flushing with awkwardness. "I suppose your Aunt Shaw will trust you with me. We'll start tomorrow morning and arrive there about two o'clock, I fancy. We'll take a snack and order dinner at the little inn—the Lennard Arms, it used to be—and go for a walk to whet our appetites in the forest. Can you manage it, Margaret? I know it will be a trial for both of us, but it will be a pleasure to me, at least. There we'll dine—we may get doe-venison, if we're lucky—and then I'll take my nap while you go out to visit old friends. I'll bring you back safe and sound, barring any railway accidents, and I'll insure your life for a thousand pounds before we start, which might offer some comfort to your relations; but otherwise, I'll return you to Mrs. Shaw by lunchtime on Friday. So, if you agree, I'll just go upstairs and propose it formally."

"It's no use my trying to tell you how much I shall like it," Margaret whispered through her tears.

"Well, then, prove your gratitude by keeping those fountains of yours dry for the next two days," Mr. Bell insisted, trying to lighten the mood. "If you don't, I shall feel quite queasy about my own lachrymal ducts, and I dislike that."

"I won't cry a drop," Margaret promised, winking her eyes vigorously to shake the moisture from her lashes and forcing a smile.

"There's my good girl. Then we'll go upstairs and settle the arrangements."

Margaret was in a state of almost trembling eagerness as Mr. Bell discussed his plan with her Aunt Shaw. The aunt was initially startled, then doubtful and perplexed, but ultimately yielded to the rough force of Mr. Bell's persuasion rather than her own convictions. To the very end, she could not decide whether it was right or wrong, proper or improper, until Margaret's safe return and the happy fulfillment of the project gave her sufficient peace of mind to declare, "She was sure it had been a very kind thought of Mr. Bell's, and exactly what she herself had been wishing for Margaret, providing her with the very change she required after all the anxious times she had endured."
