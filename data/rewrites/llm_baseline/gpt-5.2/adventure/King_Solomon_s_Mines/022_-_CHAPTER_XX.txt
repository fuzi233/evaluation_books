And now I come to the oddest turning of the wheel in all our odd business—a thing so unlikely that, if I had not seen it with my own eyes and heard it with my own ears, I should have set it down as a traveller’s lie. Yet it happened as quietly as a pot coming to the boil.

We were following the thin stream that leaked from the oasis and went on murmuring until the desert drank it—softly at first, with that kindly, reed-brushed sound water makes when it runs over sand, and then fainter and fainter, as though the world itself were being muffled. I had wandered on a little ahead of Sir Henry and Good, for I like to walk where I can hear my own footfall and the small honest noises of a place—wind worrying the fig leaves, a bird shifting in its sleep, the click of a beetle in dry grass—rather than the incessant clink and scrape of men who have forgotten how to be silent.

The air smelt of sun-warmed dust and green sap, a scent as startling there as fresh-cut herbs on a ship’s deck. Here and there the ground was starred with crushed mint-like growth where some beast had lain. I remember thinking, with that foolish domestic twist one gets even in the wilds, how a handful of those leaves bruised under a knife would have woken a stew—if ever we should have a pot again and leisure to mind it.

Then I stopped dead and rubbed my eyes.

Not twenty yards in front of me, tucked under the shade of a fig-tree—broad-leafed, milky-sapped, a homely tree that looked as if it belonged in a courtyard and not on the lip of a burning waste—stood a snug hut. It was built in the Kafir fashion with grass and withes, but with a full-length door instead of the usual bee-hole, as though whoever made it had insisted on walking upright into his own shelter, even at the end of the earth. The door faced the stream, set so that a man could sit in the shade and listen to water, which is the best music a desert ever gives you.

“What the dickens,” I said to myself, “can a hut be doing here?”

Even as the thought shaped itself, the door opened. Out limped a white man.

He was clothed in skins, sun-blackened, with an enormous black beard that made his face look like something half-buried and newly dug up; and he moved with the stiff, careful hitch of one who has long since learned to negotiate pain as a daily chore. For a second my mind skittered, like a kettle-lid rattling—heat, mirage, a touch of sun. No hunter ever came to such a place as that; certainly no hunter would ever choose to settle in it. Yet there he was, solid enough to cast a shadow, and the fig leaves above him stirred with the small dry applause of wind.

I stared; he stared back; and just then Sir Henry and Good came up.

“Look here, you fellows,” I said, for my voice would come, though it sounded strange in that quiet, “is that a white man, or am I mad?”

Sir Henry looked. Good looked. And all at once the lame man gave a great cry—a sound torn raw out of a throat that had not used its mother tongue much of late—and began hobbling towards us. When he got close, the strength went out of him as suddenly as water out of a cracked gourd. He fell in a sort of faint at Sir Henry’s feet.

With a spring Sir Henry was down beside him, his great hands trembling as they lifted the bearded face.

“Great Powers!” he cried. “It is my brother George!”

At the sound, another figure burst from the hut—also in skins, lean as a whip, a gun in his hand, eyes bright as a jackal’s in the dusk. He ran towards us, and when he saw me he cried out as though the very syllables were food.

“Macumazahn!” he halloed. “Don’t you know me, Baas? I’m Jim the hunter. I lost the note you gave me to give to the Baas, and we have been here nearly two years.”

And down he went at my feet and rolled over and over, weeping for pure gladness. It was an absurd sight, and yet in the hush of that place—only the stream talking to itself and the fig leaves ticking overhead—his sobbing had a kind of truth in it that no fine speech could manage.

“You careless scoundrel!” I said. “You ought to be well sjambocked”—that is to say, given a hiding; for in the wild world a lost scrap of paper can change men’s lives as surely as a bullet.

Meanwhile the man with the black beard—George Curtis—came round again. He rose, swaying, and he and Sir Henry fell to “pump-handling” each other, gripping and shaking as though they meant to wring the marrow out of one another. They seemed for a while to have no words at all, only that hard clasp and the broken breathing of men who have carried a grief too long. Whatever quarrel had lain between them in the old days—I have my guesses, as any man might, but I never asked—was washed clean out there by desert water and need.

“My dear old fellow,” Sir Henry burst out at last, his voice thick, “I thought you were dead. I have been over Solomon’s Mountains to find you. I had given up all hope of ever seeing you again, and now I come across you perched in the desert like an old aasvögel.”

“I tried to cross Solomon’s Mountains nearly two years ago,” George answered. He spoke hesitatingly, like a man who has had little opportunity of using his tongue, as though words had become stiff tools in his mouth. “But when I reached here a boulder fell on my leg and crushed it, and I have been able to go neither forward nor back.”

Then I stepped in.

“How do you do, Mr. Neville?” I said; “do you remember me?”

“Why,” he said, blinking at me as though he feared I might vanish if he looked too hard, “isn’t it Hunter Quatermain, eh, and Good too? Hold on a minute, you fellows—I am getting dizzy again. It is all so very strange, and, when a man has ceased to hope, so very happy!”

We made camp there, for no sensible man starts off across the sands with a cripple at twilight. The oasis, after the long dead stretches, had the feeling of a little household set down in the wilderness: a place where routines—fetching water, tending a fire, portioning out meat—could be done with the same hands that had lately done nothing but crawl and pray.

The night came on with its own soundscape: the thin stream whispering, the occasional plop where something drank, the far cough of a beast moving in the dark, and the steady crackle of our fire, that most companionable of noises. Smoke climbed into the cool air smelling of singed hide and resin, and it put me in mind of kitchens—of fat dripping onto coals, of the sharp sweetness when you throw a crushed herb into heat and it gives itself up at once.

Over that fire George Curtis told us his story, and in its way it was almost as eventful as our own.

A little less than two years before, he had started from Sitanda’s Kraal to try to reach Suliman’s Berg. As for the note I had sent him by Jim, that worthy had lost it; Curtis had never heard of it till that day. But, acting on what he had learned from natives, he did not make for Sheba’s Breasts. Instead he aimed for the ladder-like descent of the mountains down which we had just come—clearly a better route than the one marked in old Dom Silvestra’s plan.

In the desert he and Jim suffered hard—thirst that makes a man’s tongue feel like old leather, heat that sings in the skull, nights when the cold creeps into the bones. At last they won to the oasis. And there, on the very day of their arrival, came the accident that chained them.

George had been sitting by the stream, letting the sound of running water do for his mind what no prayer could, while Jim went scrambling to a bank above to rob a nest of stingless bees—a little desert treasure, honey thick as boiled syrup. In levering at the comb, Jim loosened a great boulder. It broke free, slid, and fell straight onto George Curtis’s right leg, crushing it frightfully.

From that day George was so lame he could not go forward or back, and he chose the chances of dying where there was water over the certainty of perishing out in the sand.

As for food, they managed better than most would have done, for they had ammunition, and the oasis was visited—especially at night—by quantities of game coming in to drink. They shot what they could, and where shooting would have been wasteful they dug pitfalls and took beasts cleanly. Meat they ate when it was fresh; when it was too much for one day, they cut it in strips and dried it in the desert air until it was hard and dark and would keep. Fat they rendered in whatever tins they had, scraping and saving, because fat is life when you are thin as a reed. Even the bones they cracked and boiled for what little strength was in them. When their clothes gave out, they cured hides as best they could—smoke and sun and the patient rubbing of a blade—until they had something to cover themselves with. It was rough living, but it had its own stern housekeeping, and I could not help thinking that a man does not go mad so easily if he can still make a meal with care and listen each evening to the same fire.

“And so,” George ended, “we have lived for nearly two years, like a second Robinson Crusoe and his man Friday, hoping against hope that some natives might come here to help us away, but none have come. Only last night we settled that Jim should leave me and try to reach Sitanda’s Kraal to get assistance. He was to go to-morrow, but I had little hope of ever seeing him back again. And now you—of all people in the world—you, who, as I fancied, had long ago forgotten all about me and were living comfortably in old England—turn up in a promiscuous way and find me where you least expected. It is the most wonderful thing that I have ever heard of, and the most merciful too.”

Then Sir Henry, with that dogged thoroughness of his, set to work and told him the main facts of our adventures. He talked till late into the night, while the fire sank to a red heart and the desert sounds drew nearer—the cautious pad of something hunting, the sudden flutter of wings, the long, steady silence between. Now and again George would repeat a word to himself as if tasting it, like a man remembering his own language after living too long with nothing but his thoughts and a servant’s speech. In that place I felt, more keenly than ever, how a tongue can wither when there is no one to answer it—and how, without words, even memory begins to crumble and be replaced by whatever names the world chooses to give you.

When at last I showed George some of the diamonds, they winked in the firelight like cold drops of water.

“By Jove!” he said. “Well, at least you have got something for your pains, besides my worthless self.”

Sir Henry laughed, and the laugh sounded large in the quiet.

“They belong to Quatermain and Good. It was a part of the bargain that they should divide any spoils there might be.”

That set me thinking. Later, having spoken with Good, I told Sir Henry it was our joint wish that he should take a third share of the diamonds—or, if he would not, that his share should be given to his brother, who had suffered even more than we had on the chance of getting them. At first Sir Henry would not hear of it. In the end we wore him down, as men do with a friend when there is justice in it. George Curtis knew nothing of this till long afterwards.

Here, at this point, I mean to end the history itself.

Our journey back across the desert to Sitanda’s Kraal was most arduous, especially because we had to support George Curtis, whose right leg was very weak indeed and continually threw out splinters of bone. There are pains that have a sound of their own—a little sucked-in breath, a stifled groan—and more than once that sound in the night made the desert feel like an enormous, indifferent machine. But we did accomplish it somehow, and to set down its details would be only to tell again much of what happened to us on the former crossing.

Six months from the date of our re-arrival at Sitanda’s—where we found our guns and other goods quite safe, though the old rascal in charge was much disgusted at our surviving to claim them—saw us all once more safe and sound at my little place on the Berea, near Durban, where I write this now. From there I bade farewell to those who have gone with me through the strangest trip I ever made in the course of a long and varied experience.

P.S.—Just as I had written the last word, a Kafir came up my avenue of orange trees with a letter carried in a cleft stick from the post. The orange blossom was out, and the place smelt of it—white sweetness laid over green rind and warm earth—and the bees made a steady, wholesome music that struck me after the desert silence like the return of an old song in one’s own dialect.

The letter was from Sir Henry. As it speaks for itself, I give it in full.

October 1, 1884.  
Brayley Hall, Yorkshire.

My Dear Quatermain,

I send you a line a few mails back to say that the three of us, George, Good, and myself, fetched up all right in England. We got off the boat at Southampton, and went up to town. You should have seen what a swell Good turned out the very next day, beautifully shaved, frock coat fitting like a glove, brand new eye-glass, etc., etc. I went and walked in the park with him, where I met some people I know, and at once told them the story of his “beautiful white legs.” He is furious, especially as some ill-natured person has printed it in a Society paper.

To come to business, Good and I took the diamonds to Streeter’s to be valued, as we arranged, and really I am afraid to tell you what they put them at, it seems so enormous. They say that of course it is more or less guess-work, as such stones have never to their knowledge been put on the market in anything like such quantities. It appears that (with the exception of one or two of the largest) they are of the finest water, and equal in every way to the best Brazilian stones. I asked them if they would buy them, but they said that it was beyond their power to do so, and recommended us to sell by degrees, over a period of years indeed, for fear lest we should flood the market. They offer, however, a hundred and eighty thousand for a very small portion of them.

You must come home, Quatermain, and see about these things, especially if you insist upon making the magnificent present of the third share, which does not belong to me, to my brother George. As for Good, he is no good. His time is too much occupied in shaving, and other matters connected with the vain adorning of the body. But I think he is still down on his luck about Foulata. He told me that since he had been home he hadn’t seen a woman to touch her, either as regards her figure or the sweetness of her expression.

I want you to come home, my dear old comrade, and to buy a house near here. You have done your day’s work, and have lots of money now, and there is a place for sale quite close which would suit you admirably. Do come; the sooner the better; you can finish writing the story of our adventures on board ship. We have refused to tell the tale till it is written by you, for fear lest we shall not be believed.

If you start on receipt of this you will reach here by Christmas, and I book you to stay with me for that. Good is coming, and George; and so, by the way, is your boy Harry (there’s a bribe for you). I have had him down for a week’s shooting, and like him. He is a cool young hand; he shot me in the leg, cut out the pellets, and then remarked upon the advantages of having a medical student with every shooting party!

Good-bye, old boy; I can’t say any more, but I know that you will come, if it is only to oblige

Your sincere friend,  
HENRY CURTIS.

P.S.—The tusks of the great bull that killed poor Khiva have now been put up in the hall here, over the pair of buffalo horns you gave me, and look magnificent; and the axe with which I chopped off Twala’s head is fixed above my writing-table. I wish that we could have managed to bring away the coats of chain armour. Don’t lose poor Foulata’s basket in which you brought away the diamonds.

H.C.

To-day is Tuesday. There is a steamer going on Friday, and I really think I must take Curtis at his word and sail by her for England—if it is only to see you, Harry, my boy, and to look after the printing of this history, which is a task I do not like to trust to anybody else.

ALLAN QUATERMAIN.
