Ghysbrecht Van Swieten carried the key to Gerard’s prison in his pouch. He waited until ten o’clock before making his visit, muttering to himself, “A little hunger does no harm—it breaks their spirit.” At precisely ten, he crept up the stairs with a loaf and a pitcher, followed by his loyal servant, well armed and silent. Ghysbrecht paused at the door and listened. Not a sound came from within. A cold smile curled his lips. “By now, he must be as broken as Albert Koestein was,” he thought.

He turned the key and pushed the door open.

No Gerard.

Ghysbrecht froze. Though his face was hidden, his body stiffened in a way that spoke of shock—then, slowly, he sank forward, trembling so violently that the servant behind him sensed something was dreadfully wrong. He crept closer, peering over his master’s shoulder.

The cell was empty. The iron bar lay on the floor. And there, hanging loosely from the ceiling, was the rope.

The servant gasped. “Master! What in heaven’s name—?”

But Ghysbrecht ignored him. Instead, he dropped to his knees before the wooden chest that had held the town records, his hands flying over its surface as if he could not believe what he saw. His fingers trembled, probing every seam, every corner, as though afraid his eyes deceived him.

The servant stared, bewildered. “Master, what is it? Why do you stare into that empty box? The boy isn’t here. See—look at this! He pulled out the bar and—”

“GONE! GONE! GONE!” Ghysbrecht shrieked, his voice cracking with panic.

“Gone? What is gone, Holy saints?” the servant cried. “You’re struck mad!”

“STOP THIEF!” roared Ghysbrecht, whirling around and seizing his servant by the collar. “Do you stand there like a fool while your master is robbed? Run! Fly! I’ll give a hundred crowns to whoever brings me back what’s missing!”

He released the man, then fell back against the chest, his strength failing him. “Oh, fool! Fool! To leave it in the same room with him! But none ever found the secret spring before… only he could. It was meant to be. It is meant to be. Lost! Lost!”

His voice trailed off into a whisper, worn down by age and despair. He slumped onto the chest, muttering over and over: “Lost… lost…”

“What is lost, master?” the servant asked gently.

“House… lands… good name…” Ghysbrecht groaned, wringing his hands. “All gone.”

“WHAT?” the servant exclaimed, his voice sharp with sudden interest.

That word—sharp, urgent—struck Ghysbrecht like a spark. He straightened slightly, his cunning returning. “I’ve lost the town records,” he stammered, glancing sideways at his servant like a fox caught near a henhouse.

“Oh, is that all?” the servant said, relieved.

“Is it not enough? What will the burghers say? What will the burgomaster do?” Ghysbrecht snapped, then suddenly raised his voice again. “A hundred crowns to whoever recovers them—every single one! If even one is missing, I give nothing!”

“It’s a bargain, master,” the servant replied, already pulling a purse from his belt. “The hundred crowns are in my pouch. See—you know where Gerard Eliassoen is, and there, I warrant, are those sheepskins you value so highly.”

“That’s true,” Ghysbrecht nodded, catching the drift. “That’s true, good Dierich—faithful Dierich. Every last piece, mind you. All.”

“Master,” Dierich said, “I’ll take the constables to Gerard’s house and arrest him for theft.”

“Theft? Yes, yes—good idea. Very good. It *is* theft. I forgot. So now that he’s a thief, we’ll throw him into the dungeons below—the ones with the toads and rats. Dierich, that boy must never see daylight again. It’s his own fault—he meddled where he shouldn’t. Quick, quick! Before he has time to talk, you understand—time to talk!”

Within half an hour, Dierich Brower and four constables burst into the hosier’s house, demanding young Gerard from the terrified Catherine.

“Alas! What has he done now?” she sobbed. “That boy will break my heart.”

“Nay, dame,” Dierich said smoothly, “only a prank of youth. He’s taken some parchment skins, likely in jest. But the burgomaster is responsible for their safety, so he’s worried. As for the boy, he’ll probably get off with a scolding.”

Catherine believed him instantly. But her daughter, Kate, watched with narrowed eyes. Her suspicion deepened when Dierich’s face darkened at the news that Gerard was not home—not even the night before.

“Come along then,” he growled. “We’re wasting time.”

He added fiercely, “I’ll find him if he’s above ground.”

Affection sharpens perception—and sometimes turns the innocent into the most shrewd opponent. As Dierich stepped toward the door, Kate gave him a signal. He paused, motioning his men onward, and waited outside.

She joined him. “Hush,” she whispered. “Mother doesn’t know. Gerard has left Tergou.”

“How?” Dierich demanded.

“I saw him last night.”

“Where?” he pressed.

“At the foot of the haunted tower.”

“How did he get the rope?”

“I don’t know. But I know this—my brother bade me farewell there. He’s many leagues away by now. You know the town never deserved him. When they imprisoned him, he swore never to return. Let the burgomaster be satisfied. He has jailed him, driven him from his birthplace, from his homeland. What need now to ruin us and rob us of our honor?”

At another time, such words might have made sense. But Dierich was too humiliated by Gerard’s escape and the loss of his reward.

“What need had he to steal?” he retorted bitterly.

“Gerard didn’t steal,” Kate said calmly. “He took them to spite the burgomaster who stole his freedom. But he’ll answer to the Duke for it—he shall. As for those parchments you care so much about, look in the nearest brook or pigsty. You’ll likely find them there.”

“Think you so, mistress?” Dierich hissed, eyes flashing. “Think you so? Perhaps you know it’s true.”

“This I know,” Kate said quietly, “that Gerard is too noble to steal, and too wise to burden himself with rubbish on a journey.”

“Then good day to you,” Dierich snapped, turning sharply. “The sheepskin you despise, I value more than any skin in Tergou.”

And with that, he stormed off—pursuing a false trail.

Kate returned inside and pulled Giles aside.

“Giles,” she whispered, “my heart misgives me. Do not tell a soul what I say. I told Dierich Gerard is beyond Holland—but I fear he’s not a league from Tergou.”

“Where is he, then?” Giles asked.

“Where should he be but with the woman he loves? But if so, he must not linger. These are deep, dark, wicked men hunting him. Giles—I see it in Dierch’s eye. It makes me tremble. Oh, why can’t I fly to Sevenbergen and warn him? Why am I not strong and swift like other girls? God forgive me for fretting at His will—but never have I known what it means to be weak, lame, useless. But you—you are strong, dear Giles,” she added coaxingly.

“Yes, I am strong,” Perpusillus thundered. Then, catching her meaning, he sulked, “but I hate walking.”

“Alas, alas! Who will help me if you won’t?” she pleaded. “Dear Giles, do you not love Gerard?”

“Yes,” he said gruffly, “he’s the best of the lot. I’ll go to Sevenbergen on Peter Buyskens’ mule. Ask him—he won’t lend her to me.”

Kate protested. The whole town would follow. Everyone would know where he was going—and Gerard would be in greater danger than before.

Giles countered: “I’ll ride out the opposite way. I won’t turn the mule toward Sevenbergen until I’ve shaken the curious.”

Satisfied, Kate agreed. She borrowed the mule and gave Giles a short, urgent message. She made him repeat it over and over until he could recite it perfectly.

Giles set off on the mule, his face grim. Kate stood at the window, watching him disappear into the distance. Then, alone, she knelt on the floor and prayed—long, earnestly—for her brother’s safety.
