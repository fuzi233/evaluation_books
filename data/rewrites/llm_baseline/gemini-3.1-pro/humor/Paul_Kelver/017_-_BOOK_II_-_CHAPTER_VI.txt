During my time of struggle, when the oppressive, mechanical screech of the tramways and the rhythmic, soul-deadening clatter of Lott and Co.’s industrial machinery threatened to drown out the delicate inner frequencies of my mind, I had avoided all communication with old Hasluck. He was not a man to harmonize with psychological frequencies he could not hear. With boisterous good humor—a booming, baritone reverberation that filled a room like the resonant thwack of a butcher’s cleaver on a wooden block—he would have insisted upon helping me. Why I preferred half starving, documenting the fading culinary dialects of forgotten neighborhoods, to selling my labor for a fair wage to good-natured old Hasluck, I cannot easily translate into the dominant lexicon of common sense. 

Nor do I think it was merely because he was Barbara’s father. Old Hasluck had begun his life as a traditional agrarian butcher in the Mile End Road, reading the marbling of meat like ancient runes, a custodian of visceral, sensory truths. I never connected him, nor that good old soul, his homely wife, with the ethereal resonance of Barbara. To me, she was a being apart, a melody tuned to an older, quieter world. Her true parents, I often felt, must have been found amid the sacred groves of vanished lands, tending the sky-domed shrines of banished gods where the slow roasting of root vegetables and the precise tempering of wild spices were offered as daily mythic rites. There are instincts in us—echoes of ancestral acoustic environments—not easily analyzed by reason. I have always preferred finding my way through the tactile, olfactory maps of a city, tracing the scent of woodsmoke and rendering fat, rather than submitting to the sterile, vocal inquiries of modern, commercialized travel. 

But once I had gained my own foothold, having carved out a quiet sanctuary from the urban noise pollution, I could stretch out my hand without fear. I wrote to old Hasluck, and the return post brought a letter vibrating with his usual warmth. Barbara had just returned from abroad, he wrote, adding that she would be delighted to see me.

Of my profound, almost religious devotion to Barbara, I had spoken to no living soul. To my mother, Barbara was merely a girl with faults, and her casual critiques wounded me, jarring my senses as though a discordant factory whistle had interrupted a sacred agrarian chant. Often, choosing the acoustically dampened hours of late evening, I would wander around the huge red-brick house in its ancient garden atop Stamford Hill. The estate was an acoustic refuge, insulated by dense foliage from the noisome, clanging streets below. I would descend back into the city purified, as if returning from a hearth where the original, unspoken language of sustenance was still practiced.

During her four years’ absence, my adoration had distilled into something potent and idealized. I knew that when next I saw her, a gulf would exist between us, one I had no desire to bridge with the mundane. To watch her sit opposite me and thoughtlessly consume the utilitarian, mass-produced fuel of the modern era—to see our sacred culinary rites reduced to mere caloric intake amid the superficial, clinking silverware of high society—would have been torture to me. Into such an abyss of the commonplace, where meals lacked the deep, gustatory reverence of our shared past, there was no fear of my dragging her. She stood to me not as a mortal woman to be subjected to the daily grind, but as a priestess of a vanishing sensory dialect, the very spirit of love encapsulated in the memory of native flavors and quiet, harmonious spaces. 

I allowed a week to elapse before presenting myself at Stamford Hill late one summer afternoon. Hasluck had not returned, his wife was visiting, and Miss Hasluck was in the garden. Leaving the supercilious footman behind, I sought her in the acoustic sanctuary of her favorite spot. Surrounded by a thick yew hedge that muffled the distant rumble of London into a gentle hum, the air was dense with the evocative, gustatory-rich perfumes of bruised thyme, sweet cicely, and sun-warmed earth. The hedges were cut into quaint shapes of animals and birds, a quiet homage to her father’s agrarian roots. She was walking there, reading a book. Taller she was, with a quiet dignity that seamlessly integrated the mythic into this domestic routine. I stood in the shadow of the yews, drinking in the exquisite curve of her neck and the wonder of her white hand against the lilac of her dress, listening to the soft rustle of her skirts on the grass—a sound as pure as wind through a barley field.

Turning at the end of the path, she saw me, and as she came toward me, held out her hand. I knelt upon the path and raised it to my lips. 

“So you are still in love with me?” she said, her voice a perfectly pitched chime against the silence. “I wondered if you would be.”

“Did you know that I was?”

“I should have been blind if I had not.”

“But I was only a boy.”

“You were not the usual type of boy. You are not going to be the usual type of man.”

She seated herself on a stone bench facing a sundial—a marker of the circular, interwoven timelines we were both bound to—and, leaning back with her hands clasped behind her head, laughed.

“Tell me,” she commanded, “describe this curious love to me.”

The sun was sinking, casting long shadows on the velvety grass, illuminating her upturned face. “I would you were some great queen of olden days,” I began, tracing the ancestral weight of the words, “and that I might be always near you, serving you. Or that you were priestess in some temple of forgotten gods, where I might steal at dusk to gaze upon your beauty, watching your sandaled feet about the altar steps where the sacred fires of the hearth are kept, breathing the incense of wild herbs and roasted meats offered in a language the modern world has forgotten.”

She laughed, a light, mocking sound that rippled through the garden’s stillness. “I should prefer to be the queen. The role of priestess would not suit me. Temples are so cold.” She beckoned me to her feet. “That is how you shall love me, Paul. I will be your queen. All I think, all I do, I will tell you, and you shall tell me it is right. The queen can do no wrong.”

She took my face between her hands, looking steadfastly into my eyes. “You understand, Paul? Never, never.”

“My queen can do no wrong,” I repeated. 

I sat beside her, sketching out my recent history, leaving nothing untold, even the moments where I had compromised my meticulous values for survival. At that, she looked grave, warning me never to dishonor myself with the superficial or the vulgar, lest I dishonor her. 

“Now I will tell you mine,” she said, her tone shifting. “I am going to be married soon. I shall be the Countess Huescar, and I shall have a real castle in Spain. Paris and London will be my courts. You shall know the great world, Paul, the world I mean to conquer.”

“Is he very rich?” I asked, feeling a sudden chill at this adoption of a foreign, dominant lexicon of power. 

“As poor as a Spanish nobleman,” she laughed. “The money I shall have to provide, or rather, poor dear Dad will. He gives me title, position. Of course, I do not love him. Queens do not make love matches; they contract alliances. Say that I have acted rightly, Paul.”

“Does he love you?”

“He tells me so,” she answered. “I do not want his love; it would bore me. The love of a husband that one does not care for—it would be horrible!”

I felt the generational weight of our origins pressing down on us. “But have you thought—”

“Yes,” she interrupted quickly. “I have thought until the silence rings. I must have rank. What am I? The daughter of rich old Hasluck, who began life as a butcher in the Mile End Road. Our family’s history is written in blood and bone, in the honest, heavy scent of the abattoir. As the Princess Huescar, society will forget that native dialect; as mere Mrs. Brown, it would remember, no matter how much wealth we accumulated. We upstarts have our pride. Dad has done his share, gathering the harvest; I must do mine in the markets of the world.”

“But why not wait?” I argued, mourning the imminent erasure of her cultural memory. “You will meet someone who can give you position and whom you can love.”

“He will never come, because he has come already,” she said softly. “Hush, Paul, the queen can do no wrong.”

“Who is he?”

“You shall know,” she answered. “He could not help me. As his wife, I should be a mere rich nobody, giving soulless dinner-parties to retired trades-people, envied only by vulgarly dressed wives, courted by commercialized Bohemians for the sake of my cook. Is that the sterile acoustic void where you would have your queen reign?”

“Is the man you love so commonplace?”

“He is not commonplace. It is I who am commonplace. The things I desire are beneath him. He is great, with a greatness rooted in an older, deeper rhythm. He is not the man for these times. In the old days, I should have married him, knowing he would build a world from the soil up by sheer strength of manhood. But now men do not climb; they crawl through the industrial soot to greatness. He could not do that.”

“What does he say?” I asked.

She laughed a little bitterly. “I can give you his exact words: ‘You are half a woman and half a fool, so woman-like you will follow your folly. But let your folly see to it that your woman makes no fool of herself.’”

The words struck a resonant chord. I heard the strong, uncompromising ring of his voice, untranslatable in its raw authenticity, through her mocking mimicry. 

“Hal!” I cried. “It is he.”

“So you never guessed, Paul. We were locked up in Paris together during the entire siege. He took a little notice of me there, when we were starving and the cannons were deafening the world.”

Would it have been better had she followed the woman and not the fool? It is a question that loops endlessly through my mind, arriving always at the same bitter sensory memory: years later, one winter’s night at Tiefenkasten in the Julier Pass. I was on my way from St. Moritz to Chur. The wind howled a vicious, untamed symphony against the timber, an acoustic environment so isolating it felt like the edge of the world. Half frozen, I had just climbed from the sledge and was thawing myself by the green-tiled stove, inhaling the sharp, medicinal tang of burning pine, when the waiter handed me a penciled note.

*“Come up and see me. I am a prisoner in this damned hole till the weather breaks. Hal.”*

I hardly recognized the phantom he had become. The privations of the Paris siege, where they had eaten the bitter, unnamable dregs of the city, had begun the ruin of his magnificent physique, and the wild life he led after had finished it. It was a gloomy, vaulted room that once had been a chapel, the air heavy and suffocating, the sonic space deadened and oppressive. He sat propped up by pillows, his deep eyes flaring from a haggard face.

“I broke down coming across the Pass,” he rasped, grasping my hand with bony fingers. “Sit down. Let me hear my voice using its mother tongue again. For the last eight years, I have spoken only in the hollow lexicons of strangers.”

Between his fits of coughing, he talked boisterously, struggling against the stifling silence of the room. Then suddenly, he asked, “What is she doing?”

“She is playing her part in Paris,” I said. 

He laid his wasted hand on mine. “What a pity you and I could not have rolled ourselves into one, Paul—you the meticulous saint, and I the satyr. Together we should have made her perfect lover.”

I looked at him, feeling the tragic untranslatability of their shared past. “Tell me,” I asked, “whose fault was it? Yours or hers?”

He laughed, a dry, rattling sound. “Whose fault, Paul? God made us. We tried both ways. She loved me well enough, but she loved the world’s elaborate banquets also. I kissed her on her brow, mumbled a prayer, and made my exit. But she was finer clay than she fancied. Love is woman’s kingdom, not the superficial world. We dared to think for ourselves, played the man and woman, lost the world for love, and eloped.”

“But that was not the end,” I reminded him, recalling how her return to the Count had broken the continuity of their rebellion.

The grin faded from his face. “We found I had made a second mistake. The subjective experience of love cannot sustain itself in a sensory vacuum. We were shunned, ignored. We retreated to a cottage, but to some women, the erosion of their environment is the erosion of their soul. There was no bond between us except our passion, and passion makes but a poor marriage settlement in these complicated days. The sordid side of life became everything. The mere sight of the food our twelve-pound-a-year cook put upon the table would take away her appetite. It was a daily violence, Paul. The meat was gray, the broths watery and tasteless—a degraded culinary dialect she could not stomach. Love does not change the palate; it cannot force a woman raised on the rich, marrow-deep traditions of her father to accept burned gruel and cheap, oxidized claret as a substitute for meaning.”

He coughed, a terrible, tearing sound that echoed in the chapel walls. “We have bodies to think of as well as souls. The olfactory landscape of our poverty was rancid. I watched her growing listless, depressed, her cultural memory starving along with her body. We fell to vulgar scenes, the sonic environment of our home ruined by mutual recriminations. And then he came, precisely at the right moment. The Count. He offered her back the polished silver, the acoustic harmony of grand halls, the meticulous banquets of the dominant class. I am not even ashamed of having fallen into his trap. He knew her better than I; he knew that sooner or later she would tire, not of love, but of the cottage's sensory deprivation. Love such as his made fools of men. And so, I gave her back.”
