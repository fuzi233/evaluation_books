Rough travel holds a peculiar allure for the English soul, even when the traveler bears no pilgrim's badge of romantic devotion. Yet, the chronicles of such journeys possess a fascination akin to that of a fossil; they preserve the strata of human experience. Nevertheless, since the true axis of this narrative turns upon Holland, it becomes incumbent upon us to return to that soil—to revisit the ordinary personages and mundane incidents that constitute the fabric of life across all eras.

Jorian Ketel arrived at Peter's house intent upon claiming Margaret's pledge. However, Margaret lay confined to her bed, succumbing to illness. Upon learning of Jorian's errand, Peter, inflamed with indignation, affronted him and ordered him from the premises. Those present, emboldened by the crowd's fervor, threatened to dunk him in the water. Both father and daughter were objects of intense local affection, and the entire saga had become public property. The citizens of Sevenbergen, caught in a state of heated, indiscriminate agitation fueled by popular sympathy, demanded justice. Thus, Jorian departed in a fury, regretting his earlier benevolence. Such repentance is not uncommon, possessing the virtue of sincerity.

Meanwhile, Dierich Brower, discovered at "The Three Kings" attempting to intoxicate a talkative individual to extract information regarding Martin Wittenhaagen's whereabouts, was apprehended and cast into a horse pond. He faced threats of further brutality should he ever appear in the borough again. Municipal jealousy, stirred to life, prompted the burgomaster of Sevenbergen to dispatch a formal missive to his counterpart in Tergou. The letter admonished the latter for overstepping legal boundaries and requested that any future complaints, real or imagined, directed against Sevenbergen's townsfolk be lodged directly with Sevenbergen's authorities.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, suppressing his rage at this rebuke, returned a courteous reply. He claimed the man he had tracked to Sevenbergen was merely a Tergovan named Gerard who had stolen the town records. Asserting that Gerard had fled into foreign lands, likely carrying the documents with him, Ghysbrecht declared the matter concluded. In doing so, he made a virtue of necessity. Yet, beneath this veneer of composure lay a storm of frustration. Baffled by the resistance in Sevenbergen, he redirected his gaze elsewhere, dispatching emissaries to the household in Tergou to ascertain Gerard's destination. Their response—"they did not know"—filled him with infinite surprise and deepening unease. He feared Gerard was merely lurking nearby, waiting to make a discovery and exact a terrible vengeance.

From this point forward, Dierich and his associates noted a palpable deterioration in Ghysbrecht's demeanor. He became a moody, irritable figure, haunted by a pervasive dread. His eyes darted furtively, scanning the horizon as if expecting a blow from an unknown quarter. The act of making others wretched had failed to bring him happiness, for rarely does inflicting pain heal the inflictor. The small family at Tergou, which might otherwise have resolved its differences without exiling *spem gregis* (hope of the flock) to a distant land, retained its outward appearance but lost its inner simplicity. The joyful unity that opened this tale had evaporated.

Little Kate understood the role Cornelis and Sybrandt played in banishing Gerard. Though she withheld this knowledge from her mother to prevent further discord, there were moments when she shuddered at their mere presence and blushed at their hypocritical expressions of regret. Catherine, exercising a woman's vigilant subtlety, observed these shifts without comment, quietly pondering the signs and watching for confirmation. The "black sheep," in their attempt to share the prevailing gloom, managed to deceive their father and Giles temporarily. However, the demure satisfaction simmering beneath their surfaces could not evade the keen scrutiny of the feminine gaze. "Noting all, seeming to note nothing," they masked their true feelings. Consequently, mistrust and suspicion settled at the dinner table, serving as poor substitutes for Gerard's intelligent visage, which had once illuminated the entire circle, remaining unnoticed until it vanished.

As for the elderly hosier, his pride had been wounded by his son's disobedience. He maintained a stiff exterior, striving never to utter Gerard's name. Yet, beneath his Spartan cloak, Nature tugged relentlessly at his heartstrings. One anxiety he never feigned to conceal: "If I but knew where the boy is, and that his life and health remain secure, my cares would be minimal," he would declare, followed invariably by a profound sigh. One cannot help but imagine that had Gerard opened the door at that precise moment and stepped inside, the scene would have been filled with tears and embraces rather than reproaches.

Then came a shock to the old couple: publicity. Within a week of Gerard's departure, his adventures dominated every conversation. Worse still, popular sympathy rallied passionately behind the lovers and against Gerard's "cruel" parents, as well as the meddling burgomaster who inserted himself into affairs beyond his jurisdiction.

"Mother," Kate remarked softly, "it is known throughout the town that Margaret is afflicted with a fever—a burning fever—and her father fears greatly for her."

"Margaret? Which Margaret?" Catherine queried, feigning a treacherous calm.

"Oh, Mother! Whom else could I mean? Why, Gerard's Margaret."

"Gerard's Margaret!" Catherine shrieked. "How dare you utter such a word to me? I warn you never to mention that hussy's name within these walls, for she has laid herself open to ruin. She is the destruction of my poor boy, the flower of my entire flock. Because of her, he is not a holy priest among us but roams the world, leaving me a desolate, broken-hearted mother. There, do not weep, my child; I speak harshly because I am distressed. Oh, Kate, you do not comprehend the turmoil within a mother's heart. I endure before you all; I must swallow my fears. Yet, at night, I see him in my dreams, always plagued by some new tribulation. Sometimes he is torn by wild beasts; other times, he falls into the grasp of robbers, their cruel knives raised to strike his pale face—one might think it would move a stone. Oh! When I recall that, while I sit here in comfort, my poor boy may lie dead in some savage land, all because of that girl... her very name is ratsbane to me. I tremble whenever I hear it."

"I shall say nothing, nor do anything to grieve you further, Mother," Kate replied tenderly, though a sigh escaped her lips.

She whose name was so fiercely proscribed within this home was widely discussed and even pitied outside. All of Sevenbergen felt sorrow for her, and young men and maidens cast numerous pitying glances as they passed the small window where the village beauty lay "dying for love." In this familiar phrase, they underestimated her spirit and selflessness. Gerard was alive, and she was too loyal to doubt his constancy. Her father was dear to her and helpless. Had not her physical weakness held her back, all her love for Gerard would not have prevented her from fulfilling her duties, though she might have performed them with a drooping head and a heavy heart.

However, the combination of physical and mental strain had triggered a violent fever. Only her youth and robust constitution saved her. The malady eventually receded, leaving her in a terrible state of debility where life felt like a burdensome weight. It is during such fragility that love and friendship become mortal angels, offering comfort in their voices and healing in their palms. Yet, this poor girl had to find a path back to vigor on her own. For many days, she lay alone as heavy hours rolled over her like leaden waves. In her enfeebled state, existence seemed a burden, and life appeared as something already passed. Unable to strive for recovery, she often lay motionless for hours, tears welling gently from her eyes.

One day, awakening from an uneasy slumber, she found two women in her chamber. One was a servant; the other, distinguished by the deep fur lining her collar and sleeves, was a woman of considerable standing. A narrow band of silvery hair, spared by her coiffure, indicated she had passed the age when sensible women conceal their years. Both women's expressions were kind and friendly. Margaret attempted to rise, but the older lady placed a gentle hand upon her arm.

"Lie still, sweetheart. We come not here to disturb you, but to offer comfort, God willing. Now, cheer up a little and tell us, first, who do you think we are?"

"Nay, madam, I recognize you, though I have never seen you before. You are the demoiselle Van Eyck, and this is Reicht Heynes. Gerard has often spoken of you and your kindness to him. Madam, he has no friend like you near him now," she said, collapsing back onto the pillows as tears instantly welled in her eyes.

The good-natured Reicht Heynes began to weep for companionship, prompting her mistress to chide her lightly. "Well, you are a pretty addition to a sickroom," she remarked, deploying a world of innocent artistry to lift the patient's spirits, achieving modest success. An old woman who has witnessed life's trials is a sovereign blessing beside a sorrowful youth. Knowing precisely what to say and what to omit, how to soothe and engage, she achieved remarkable results within an hour. Margaret's head rested on the elder's shoulder instead of the pillow, her soft eyes fixed upon her with gentle gratitude.
