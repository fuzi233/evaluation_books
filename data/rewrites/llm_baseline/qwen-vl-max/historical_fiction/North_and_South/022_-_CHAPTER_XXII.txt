“But work grew scarce, while bread grew dear,
And wages lessened, too;
For Irish hordes were bidders here,
Our half-paid work to do.”

Margaret was ushered into the drawing-room. It had returned to its usual state—curtains drawn back, furniture restored, but the air heavy with tension. The windows stood half-open against the stifling heat, and the Venetian blinds cast a dull, grey light across the room, reflecting the dusty pavement below. The shadows fell oddly, distorted by the angle of the sun, and mingled with the greenish glow from above to give even Margaret’s own face, as she caught her reflection in the mirror, a ghostly pallor. She sat quietly, waiting. No one came. From time to time, the wind seemed to carry the distant roar of voices closer—yet there was no wind. Then silence would fall, deep and absolute, as if the world itself held its breath.

At last, Fanny entered.

“Mamma will come directly, Miss Hale,” she said, breathless. “She asked me to apologize. You may know my brother has brought in workers from Ireland—Irish labourers—and it’s stirred up the Milton folk terribly. As if he didn’t have the right to hire where he could! These people refused to work for him, and now they’ve frightened the poor Irish so badly with their threats that we dare not let them out. You can see them crowded in the top room of the mill—they’re to sleep there, for safety. And mamma is looking after their food. John is speaking to them—some of the women are begging to go back. Ah! Here’s mamma!”

Mrs. Thornton entered, her face set in a mask of sternness that made Margaret feel instantly that this was not the best moment to make her request. Still, she had promised to ask for anything needed in her mother’s illness. Mrs. Thornton’s brow furrowed, her lips tightened, as Margaret spoke gently of her mother’s restlessness and Dr. Donaldson’s suggestion that a water-bed might bring relief. She paused. Mrs. Thornton did not answer at once. Then she sprang up, her voice sharp with alarm:

“They’re at the gates! Call John, Fanny—call him from the mill! They’re at the gates! They’ll break them down! Call John, I say!”

And at that very moment, the sound she had been listening for—the tramp of countless feet—rose just beyond the wall. A rising din of angry voices surged behind the wooden barrier. The gate trembled under the weight of unseen hands, as if the crowd outside were using their bodies as battering rams. They stepped back only to charge again, united and relentless, until the great iron gates quivered like reeds in a gale.

The women gathered at the windows, transfixed by the scene unfolding before them. Mrs. Thornton, the servants, Margaret—all stood watching. Fanny had fled upstairs, screaming as if hunted, and collapsed in hysterics on the sofa. Mrs. Thornton waited for her son, still inside the mill. He emerged, glanced upward at the pale faces clustered at the window, and offered a brief smile of defiance before locking the factory door. Then he called to one of the maids to open his own door, which Fanny had bolted behind her in panic. Mrs. Thornton went herself. The sound of his firm, commanding voice seemed to ignite the fury outside. Until then, the mob had been silent, breathing hard in their effort to break through. But now, hearing him speak, they unleashed a roar so fierce and unnatural that even Mrs. Thornton turned pale.

He entered the room, slightly flushed, but his eyes gleamed with the fire of danger. There was a proud, defiant look upon his face that made him seem noble, though not conventionally handsome. Margaret, who had always feared she might fail in courage, found herself forgetting herself entirely. In this moment of real terror, she felt nothing but a fierce, painful empathy for those around her.

Mr. Thornton stepped forward without hesitation.

“I’m sorry, Miss Hale, that you’ve come at such a time—when, I fear, you may be caught up in whatever danger we face. Mother, perhaps you’d better retreat to the back rooms? I’m not certain whether they’ve reached the stable yard from Pinner’s Lane—but if not, you’ll be safer there than here. Go, Jane!” he added, turning to the housekeeper.

She obeyed, followed by the others.

“I stay here,” said his mother firmly. “Where you are, there I remain.” And indeed, retreat offered no safety—the crowd had surrounded the outbuildings at the rear, their voices echoing threats from every side. The servants fled to the garrets, shrieking. Mr. Thornton smiled scornfully as he heard them. He glanced at Margaret, standing alone near the factory window. Her eyes sparkled, her cheeks flushed. Sensing his gaze, she turned and asked a question that had long been on her mind:

“Where are the Irish workers? Are they in the factory?”

“Yes,” he replied. “I left them huddled in a small room at the top of a back staircase. I told them to flee down the stairs if they heard any attack on the mill doors. But it’s not them they want—it’s me.”

“When will the soldiers arrive?” his mother asked, her voice low but steady.

He pulled out his watch, calm as ever. After a brief calculation, he said:

“If Williams left immediately and didn’t have to dodge through them, it should be twenty minutes yet.”

“Twenty minutes!” she repeated, her voice finally betraying her fear.

“Shut the windows at once, mother,” he ordered. “The gates won’t hold another shock. Shut that one, Miss Hale.”

Margaret closed her window, then hurried to help Mrs. Thornton’s trembling hands.

For several minutes, the street outside fell silent. Mrs. Thornton watched her son’s face, seeking meaning in the sudden quiet. His expression was rigid—contemptuous, defiant. No trace of hope or fear showed.

Fanny lifted her head.

“Are they gone?” she whispered.

“Gone?” he replied. “Listen.”

They all listened. Then came the sound—a single, deep breath, the groan of wood straining, the wrench of iron, the thunderous crash of the massive gates falling. Fanny staggered upright, took a few steps toward her mother, then collapsed into her arms, unconscious.

Mrs. Thornton lifted her with strength born more of will than muscle, and carried her away.

“Thank God,” Mr. Thornton murmured as he watched her leave. “Had you not better go upstairs, Miss Hale?”

Margaret’s lips formed a “No,” but he couldn’t hear her. Beneath the house wall, the tramp of hundreds of feet resumed, accompanied by a deep, growling murmur—more terrifying than their earlier cries, filled with a savage satisfaction.

“Never mind,” he said, trying to reassure her. “I’m sorry you’ve been caught in this alarm, but it won’t last long. The soldiers will be here soon.”

“Oh, God!” Margaret suddenly exclaimed. “There’s Boucher. I recognize his face—he’s livid with rage, fighting to get to the front. Look! Look!”

“Who is Boucher?” Mr. Thornton asked coolly, stepping close to the window to identify the man.

As soon as they saw him, the crowd erupted in a yell—unhuman, animalistic, like a beast denied its prey. Even Mr. Thornton recoiled, stunned by the depth of hatred he had provoked.

“Let them scream,” he said. “In five minutes—. I only hope my poor Irishmen aren’t terrified out of their minds by such a fiendish noise. Hold on for five minutes, Miss Hale.”

“Don’t worry about me,” she said quickly. “But what will happen in five minutes? Can’t you do something to calm these people? It’s awful to see them.”

“The soldiers will arrive soon. That will restore reason.”

“To reason?” she asked sharply. “What kind of reason?”

“The only kind that works when men turn into beasts. By heaven—they’ve turned to the mill door!”

“Mr. Thornton,” she said, trembling with passion, “go down at once—if you’re not a coward. Face them like a man. Protect these poor strangers you’ve brought here. Speak to your workers like human beings. Speak kindly. Don’t let the soldiers come in and cut down people driven mad. I see one who is already losing control. If you have any courage, any nobility, go out and speak to them—man to man!”

He turned to look at her as she spoke. A shadow passed over his face. He clenched his teeth.

“I will go. Perhaps you’ll accompany me downstairs and bar the door behind me. My mother and sister will need protection.”

“Oh, Mr. Thornton—I don’t know—only—”

But he was already moving. Downstairs he went, unbarred the front door. All she could do was follow swiftly, fasten the door behind him, and climb back up the stairs, heart sick, head dizzy. She returned to the farthest window. He stood on the steps below—she could see that by the direction of a thousand furious eyes. But she could neither see nor hear much beyond the savage, rolling murmur of anger.

She threw open the window. Many in the crowd were boys—cruel, reckless, thoughtless. Others were men, gaunt as wolves, starving for blood. She understood. Like Boucher, they had children at home—starving, helpless—counting on higher wages, enraged to discover Irish laborers had been brought in to steal their livelihood. She saw it all in Boucher’s face: desperate, broken, livid with fury. If Mr. Thornton would only speak—just let them hear his voice—it might be better than this blind rage against stone silence.

Perhaps he was speaking now. A momentary hush fell over the crowd—like animals pausing to listen. She tore off her bonnet, leaned forward. But she could only see. If he had spoken, the moment had passed. The fury rose again, fiercer than before.

He stood with arms folded, motionless as a statue, face pale with suppressed emotion. They were trying to intimidate him—to force him to flinch. Each urged the other forward, ready for violence. Margaret knew instinctively that the next moment would be chaos. One touch, and the explosion would come—among hundreds of inflamed men and reckless boys, even Mr. Thornton’s life would not be safe.

Then she saw it—boys in the background bending to remove their heavy wooden clogs, the nearest thing to weapons they had. The spark struck the powder. With a cry unheard by others, she rushed from the room, down the stairs. She lifted the great iron bar with strength born of desperation, threw open the door—and stood before the raging sea of men, her eyes blazing with reproach.

The clogs froze in mid-air. Faces, moments ago grim and determined, now looked uncertain, confused—asking what this meant. She stood between them and their enemy. She could not speak, but stretched her arms out, waiting for breath.

“Oh, don’t use violence! He is one man, and you are many,” she whispered. But the words died in her throat—no tone, only a hoarse gasp.

Mr. Thornton stepped slightly aside, as if offended by anything between him and danger.

“Go!” she said again—this time, her voice a cry. “The soldiers are coming. Go peacefully. You’ll have relief for your grievances—whatever they are.”

“Will those Irish scum be sent back?” shouted one from the crowd, voice thick with threat.

“Never, by your command!” Mr. Thornton declared.

Instantly, the storm broke. Hoots and roars filled the air. But Margaret did not hear. Her eyes were fixed on the boys with their clogs. She saw their movement—knew their intent. Another moment, and Mr. Thornton might be struck down—the man she had urged to face this danger.

She threw her arms around him, using her body as a shield.

He shook her off.

“Go away,” he said, voice deep and firm. “This is no place for you.”

“It is,” she insisted. “You didn’t see what I saw.”

If she hoped her gender would protect her—if she believed that by turning away, they might pause and vanish—she was wrong. Their fury had gone too far. Only the reckless boys led the riot—those who loved cruelty for its own sake, regardless of bloodshed.

A clog flew through the air. Margaret watched it—missed its mark. She turned sick, but did not move. She buried her face in Mr. Thornton’s arm.

Then she raised her head.

“For God’s sake! Don’t destroy your cause with violence! You don’t know what you’re doing.” She forced her voice to be clear.

A sharp pebble struck her forehead, grazing her cheek. A blinding flash of light exploded before her eyes. She slumped, lifeless, onto Mr. Thornton’s shoulder.

He unfolded his arms, held her briefly in one hand.

“You do well,” he said, voice low and charged. “You come to defend the innocent stranger. You fall—hundreds—on one man. And when a woman stands before you, pleading for reason, your cowardice turns on her! You do well!”

They fell silent. They stared—wide-eyed, mouth agape—at the thin line of dark blood trickling down her temple. Those closest to the gate backed away, ashamed. A ripple moved through the crowd—a retreat.

One voice called out:

“The stone was meant for you—but you were sheltered by a woman!”

Mr. Thornton trembled with rage. The blood loss brought Margaret back—dimly, vaguely aware. He laid her gently on the doorstep, her head resting against the frame.

“Can you rest here?” he asked.

Without waiting, he descended the steps, stepped into the middle of the crowd.

“Now kill me,” he said, “if it is your brutal will. There is no woman to shield me now. Beat me to death—you will never move me from what I have decided—not you!”

He stood among them, arms folded, exactly as he had been on the steps.

But the crowd began to move backward—unreasonably, blindly, as if the tide had turned. Or perhaps it was the thought of the approaching soldiers, and the sight of that pale, still face, eyes closed, tears forming beneath her lashes, blood dripping slowly from her wound.

Even Boucher hesitated, scowled, and finally turned away, muttering curses.

As soon as the flight began—inevitable from its nature—Mr. Thornton darted up the steps to Margaret.

She tried to rise.

“It’s nothing,” she said, forcing a weak smile. “Just a graze. I was stunned. Oh, I’m so glad they’re gone!” And she wept openly.

He could not comfort her. His anger burned hotter now that the danger had passed. The clank of soldiers’ boots echoed in the distance—five minutes too late to impress the mob with authority.

While he thought, Margaret clung to the doorpost. Then a film covered her eyes. He caught her just in time.

“Mother—mother!” he called. “They’re gone. Miss Hale is hurt!”

He carried her into the dining room, laid her softly on the sofa. Looking at her pale face, the truth struck him with sudden force.

“Oh, my Margaret—my Margaret! No one can tell what you are to me! Dead—cold as you lie there—you are the only woman I’ve ever loved! Oh, Margaret—Margaret!”

He knelt beside her, whispering, almost moaning. Then he started, ashamed, as his mother entered. She saw only her son, paler, sterner than usual.

“Miss Hale is hurt, mother. A stone grazed her temple. She’s lost quite a bit of blood.”

“She looks terribly injured—almost dead,” Mrs. Thornton said, alarmed.

“It’s only a fainting fit. She spoke to me just now.” But as he said it, his blood seemed to rush inward, and he trembled.

“Go call Jane—she’ll find what I need. And you—go to the Irish workers. They’re shouting like mad with fright.”

He left, each step weighted as if dragging anchors. He called Jane, called his sister. She must have every care, every tenderness. Yet every pulse in his body throbbed with memory—how she had come down, placed herself in danger—could it be to save him?

At the time, he had pushed her aside, spoken harshly. He had seen only unnecessary risk.

Now, among the frightened Irish, he struggled to understand their words, to calm them. They refused to stay. They demanded to be sent back.

So he had to think, to argue, to reason.

Mrs. Thornton bathed Margaret’s temples with eau de Cologne. When the spirit touched the wound—hidden until then—Margaret opened her eyes. But she did not recognize where she was, or who they were. Her lips quivered, her eyes darkened, and she slipped back into unconsciousness.

“She’s suffered a terrible blow,” Mrs. Thornton said. “Is there anyone who can fetch a doctor?”

“Not me, ma’am,” Jane said, shrinking. “Them rabble may still be around. I don’t think the cut is as deep as it looks.”

“I won’t take the chance. She was hurt in our house. If you’re afraid, Jane, I’m not. I’ll go.”

“Please, ma'am, send one of the police. There are plenty here—soldiers too.”

“And yet you’re afraid to go! I won’t waste their time on our errands. They’ll have enough to do catching some of the mob. You’ll not be afraid to stay here,” she asked coldly, “and keep bathing Miss Hale’s forehead? I’ll be back in ten minutes.”

“Couldn’t Hannah go, ma'am?”

“Why Hannah? Why not you? No, Jane. If you won’t go, I will.”

Mrs. Thornton went first to the room where Fanny lay on the bed.

“Oh, mamma! You frightened me!” Fanny cried. “I thought it was a man who’d gotten into the house.”

“Nonsense! The men are gone. Soldiers are everywhere, searching now that it’s too late. Miss Hale lies on the dining-room sofa, badly hurt. I’m going for the doctor.”

“Oh, don’t, mamma! They’ll murder you!” Fanny clung to her gown.

Mrs. Thornton wrenched it away.

“Find someone else. But that girl must not bleed to death.”

“Bleed? Oh, how horrible! How did she get hurt?”

“I don’t know—I haven’t time. Go down to her, Fanny. Try to be useful. Jane is with her. I trust it looks worse than it is. Jane refuses to leave—cowardly woman! And I won’t allow further refusals from my servants. I’m going myself.”

“Oh, dear, dear!” Fanny sobbed, preparing to descend rather than be left alone with thoughts of wounds and blood in the house.

“Oh, Jane!” she whispered, creeping into the dining room. “What happened? How white she looks! How did she get hurt? Did they throw stones into the drawing-room?”

Margaret did look pale, though her senses were returning. But the daze of the fainting spell kept her weak, helpless. She felt movement around her, the cooling scent of cologne, a longing for the bathing to continue. But when they paused to talk, she could not open her eyes, could not speak—just as a person in death-like trance cannot move or cry out to stop the preparations for burial, even while fully aware of everything happening around them.

Jane paused in her task.

“She’d have been safe enough, miss, if she’d stayed in the drawing-room or come up with us. We were in the front garret, safe from harm.”

“Where was she then?” Fanny asked, inching closer.

“Just before the front door—with master!” Jane said, significantly.

“With John! With my brother! How did she get there?”

“Nay, miss, that’s not for me to say,” Jane replied, lifting her chin slightly. “Sarah did——”

“Sarah what?” Fanny pressed.

Jane resumed her work, as if reluctant to repeat what Sarah had said.

“Sarah what?” Fanny demanded sharply. “Don’t speak in halves or I can’t understand.”

“Well, miss, since you insist—Sarah, you see, was in the best position to see, being at the right-hand window. And she says, and said at the time, that she saw Miss Hale with her arms around master’s neck, hugging him before all the people.”
