Habit, that treacherous servant, guided them through the evening. The reckless chatter of the ballroom bubbled automatically from their lips; the mask of untroubled enjoyment plastered itself instantly onto their faces. No soul within Lennon House that night suspected that a swift, jagged knife of severance lay buried between them. Harry Feversham watched Ethne laugh and spin, appearing as carefree as ever, and he was perpetually startled, forgetting to wear the same disguise himself. When she swept past him, the light, rhythmic thud of her feet nearly convinced him that her heart still beat to the tempo of the dance. It seemed she could even summon color back to her cheeks by sheer force of will. So, they both wore brave fronts, exactly as she had instructed. They even danced together.

Yet, beneath the surface, Ethne knew she was hoisting a mountain of pain and humiliation that would soon crush her bones, and Feversham felt those four feathers burning fiercely against his chest. It was a miracle to him that the entire company remained ignorant of the scandal. Every time he approached a partner, a phantom vision seized him: she turning upon him, spitting out the contemptuous name that now branded him on her tongue. Yet, a strange, hollow calm settled over him. If the word had been spoken aloud, if the truth had erupted in the noise, he would not have flinched. He had already lost Ethne. He scanned the crowd, searching desperately among her guests for a reflection, a mirror in which to compare himself, knowing full well he would find none worthy. There were women, yes—pretty, graceful, undeniably beautiful—but Ethne stood apart. Her beauty possessed a particular, arresting character. The breadth of her forehead, the perfect arch of her brows, the large, steady, clear grey eyes, the full red lips capable of dimpling into tenderness or shutting tight with iron resolution, and the royal grace of her carriage marked her out. To Feversham, watching her in a state of despairing amazement, it seemed impossible that he had ever truly owned her.

Her endurance finally cracked, though only for a fleeting second. She was dancing with Feversham, gazing toward the windows where the daylight began to bleed through the blinds, pale and chilling.

"Look!" she cried, and suddenly Feversham felt her entire weight pressing into his arms. Her complexion drained, growing tired and ashen. Her eyes squeezed shut, trembling, then snapped open. He feared she would faint.

"The morning at last!" she exclaimed, and then, in a voice worn thin as parchment, "I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain."

"Hush!" Feversham whispered, gripping her waist. "Courage! A few minutes more... only a very few!" He stood motionless before her, anchoring her until the blood returned to her cheeks.

"Thank you," she murmured, gratitude breaking through her exhaustion. The bright, spinning wheel of the dance caught them in its gears once more.

It was ironic, somehow, that he was urging her to courage while she thanked him for support, yet neither noticed the twist of their roles. Ethne was simply spent by the strain of the last hours, while Feversham, having read the deep wound in the drawn lines of her face and the agony in her eyes, understood the depth of his betrayal. He no longer spoke of losing her; the concept of loss had evaporated. Instead, he clung to her words, *"I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain."* They echoed in his mind, persistent and haunting, spoken with the sharp accent of her voice. He was certain he would hear them above the din of the grave when he died, serving as his eternal condemnation. For it was not right.

The ball dissolved shortly thereafter. Carriages rolled away, and the guests dispersed to the smoking rooms or retreated upstairs to sleep, segregated by sex. Feversham, however, lingered in the hallway with Ethne. She understood why without a word.

"There is no need," she said, standing with her back to him as she struck a match to light a candle. "I have told my father. I told him everything."

Feversham bowed his head in silent acquiescence.

"Still, I must wait and see him," he said.

Ethne did not object, but she turned, her brow furrowed in a frown of perplexity. Waiting for her father under such circumstances required a courage that unsettled her. Indeed, she felt a flicker of apprehension as she heard the study door creak open and the heavy tread of Dermod Lennon approaching. The old man strode directly up to Harry Feversham, looking, for once, entirely exposed as what he was—a very ancient creature—and stood there staring into Feversham's face with a muddled, bewildered expression. Twice he opened his mouth to speak, but silence swallowed him. Finally, he turned to the table, lighting his own candle and Feversham's, before turning back. Rather quickly, as if sensing Ethne stepping forward to interpose herself, he simply stared at Feversham again, for a long, agonizing stretch of time. Nothing else passed between them. Eventually, he picked up his candle.

"Well—" he started, then stopped. He snuffed the wick with his scissors and began again. "Well—" and stopped again. Clearly, the candle had offered no illumination for suitable words. He stared into the flame now, instead of into Feversham's face, for an equal duration. He could think of nothing, yet he was acutely conscious that something *must* be said. In the end, he uttered a lame, broken sentence:

"If you want any whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand."

And then he walked heavily up the stairs. The old man's forbearance was perhaps not the least part of Harry Feversham's punishment.

It was broad daylight when Ethne was finally alone within her chamber. She pulled up the blinds and threw the windows wide. The cool, fresh air of the morning washed over her like a draft of spring water. She looked out upon a world still unilluminated by color, finding within it an image of her future days. The dark, towering trees looked black; the winding paths were a singular, dead white; the lawns themselves were dull and grey, though the dew lay upon them like a network of frost. It was a noisy world, despite its quiet appearance, for the blackbirds called from the branches and the grass, and below, beneath the overhanging trees, the Lennon flowed in music between its banks. Ethne drew back from the window. She had much to do before sleep claimed her again. With her natural thoroughness, she designed to make an immediate end to all associations with Harry Feversham. She wished that from the moment she woke next, she might never encounter a single thing to recall him to her memory. And with a stubborn persistence, she began the work.

But she changed her mind. In the very act of gathering the gifts he had given her, her resolve shifted. Each item she handled carried its own history, and the days preceding this miserable night, which had darkened her happiness, began to return to her slowly, one by one, as she looked upon them. She determined to keep one thing that had belonged to Harry Feversham—a small trifle of no value. First, she chose a penknife he had once lent her, which she had forgotten to return. But the next instant, she dropped it and hurried away. For she was, after all, an Irish girl, and though she did not believe in superstitions, where matters of superstition were concerned, she preferred safety. She selected his photograph in the end, locking it away safely in a drawer.

She gathered the rest of his presents, packing them carefully into a box, fastening it, addressing it, and carrying it down to the hall so the servants might dispatch it in the morning. Returning to her room, she took his letters, making a small pile of them on the hearth, and set them alight. They took some time to consume, but she waited, sitting upright in her armchair while the flame crept from sheet to sheet, discoloring the paper, blackening the ink like a stream of oil, and leaving behind, in the end, only flakes of ash like feathers, and white flakes like white feathers. The last sparks were barely extinguished when she heard a cautious step on the gravel beneath her window.

It was broad daylight, yet her candle still burned on the table beside her. With a quick, instinctive movement, she reached out and snuffed it out. Then she sat very still and rigid, listening. For a while, she heard nothing but the blackbirds calling from the garden trees and the throbbing music of the river. Afterward, she heard the footsteps again, cautiously retreating. And in spite of her will, in spite of her formal disposal of the letters and the presents, she was mastered all at once—not by pain or humiliation, but by an overpowering sense of loneliness. She seemed to be seated high atop an empty world of ruins. She rose quickly from her chair, and her eyes fell upon a violin case. With a sigh of relief, she opened it, and a little while later, one or two of the guests sleeping in the house chanced to wake and heard floating down the corridors the music of a violin played very lovingly and low.
