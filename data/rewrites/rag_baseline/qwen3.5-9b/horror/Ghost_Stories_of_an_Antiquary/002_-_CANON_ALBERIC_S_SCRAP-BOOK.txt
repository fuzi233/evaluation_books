St. Bertrand de Comminges is a decayed settlement perched on the spurs of the Pyrenees, situated roughly equidistant between Toulouse and Bagnères-de-Luchon. Once the seat of a bishopric prior to the Revolution, it retains a cathedral that draws a modest stream of tourists. In the spring of 1883, an Englishman arrived at this antiquated locale—a place too insignificant to warrant the title of city, housing fewer than a thousand souls. He was a scholar from Cambridge, dispatched specifically from Toulouse to inspect St. Bertrand's Church. He had left two companions in their hotel, men whose archaeological ambitions were far inferior to his own, promising to reunite with them the following morning for the continuation of their journey toward Auch. For his associates, a brief half-hour inspection of the church would suffice; thereafter, the trio could proceed together. However, our protagonist, let us designate him Dennistoun, had arrived prematurely. His objective was singular and exhaustive: to saturate a notebook and exhaust dozens of photographic plates documenting every inch of the magnificent edifice dominating the hill of Comminges. To execute this survey with absolute precision required the exclusive services of the church's verger. Consequently, the somewhat brusque proprietress of the *Chapeau Rouge* tavern summoned the sacristan. When the old man appeared, Dennistoun found himself confronted by an object of unexpected, unsettling interest.

The intrigue did not stem from the sacristan's physical attributes—he was a diminutive, arid, wizened figure indistinguishable from hundreds of similar French church guardians—but rather from a pervasive aura of furtiveness, a hunted and oppressed demeanor that clung to him. He was perpetually casting sidelong glances over his shoulder; the musculature of his back and shoulders remained in a state of constant, involuntary contraction, as if anticipating the imminent grasp of an unseen adversary. Dennistoun struggled to categorize the man: was he tormented by a fixed delusion, crushed by a guilty conscience, or perhaps the victim of an intolerably domineering spouse? While the statistical probability favored the latter hypothesis, the impression conveyed suggested a persecutor far more formidable than any nagging wife.

Nevertheless, Dennistoun was quickly consumed by his notebook and the operation of his camera, granting the sacristan only sporadic attention. Each time he glanced up, the old man was found at a close proximity, either huddled against a pillar or crouching within one of the ornate stalls. After some time, Dennistoun grew restless. Suspicions mingled in his mind: that the old man was being kept from his midday meal, or that he feared Dennistoun might abscond with St. Bertrand's ivory crozier or the taxidermized crocodile suspended above the baptismal font. These anxieties gnawed at him.

"Will you not depart?" Dennistoun asked eventually. "I am entirely capable of completing my notes alone; feel free to lock me in if you wish. I require at least two additional hours here, and it must be chilly for you, is it not?"

"Heavens!" exclaimed the little man, the suggestion throwing him into a state of inexplicable terror. "Such a notion is unthinkable. Leave Monsieur alone in the church? No, no; two hours, three hours, it matters not to me. I have eaten, I am not cold, thank you, Monsieur."

"Very well, my little man," Dennistoun muttered to himself. "You have been warned; you shall bear the consequences."

Within the allotted two hours, the stalls, the massive dilapidated organ, the choir screen of Bishop John de Mauléon, the fragments of stained glass and tapestry, and the artifacts of the treasury had been thoroughly scrutinized. The sacristan trailed Dennistoun's heels, periodically whipping around as though stung by an invisible insect whenever one of the peculiar noises plaguing the vast, empty structure drifted to his ears. These sounds were often bizarre.

"On one occasion," Dennistoun recounted later, "I could have sworn I heard a thin, metallic voice laughing from high within the tower. I darted an inquiring glance at my sacristan. He was pale-lipped. 'It is he—that is—it is no one; the door is locked,' was all he said. We stared at one another for a full minute."

Another incident perplexed Dennistoun significantly. He was examining a large, dark painting hanging behind the altar, part of a series depicting the miracles of St. Bertrand. The composition was nearly indecipherable, yet a Latin inscription below read: *Qualiter S. Bertrandus liberavit hominem quem diabolus diu volebat strangulare* ("How St. Bertrand delivered a man whom the Devil long sought to strangle"). Dennistoun was turning to address the sacristan with a smile and a jest, only to be confounded by the sight of the old man kneeling before the image, gazing at it with the desperate eyes of a supplicant in agony, hands clasped tight, tears streaming down his cheeks. Dennistoun feigned ignorance, yet the question persisted: why should such a crude painting evoke such visceral reaction? He began to suspect the man suffered from a monomania, but the nature of this obsession remained elusive.

By nearly five o'clock, twilight descended, lengthening the shadows within the nave. The curious noises—the muffled footsteps and distant murmuring voices audible throughout the day—appeared to grow more frequent and insistent, amplified by the failing light and the heightened sensitivity of hearing. The sacristan displayed signs of urgency and impatience for the first time. He sighed with relief when the camera and notebook were finally secured, hastily gesturing Dennistoun toward the western door beneath the tower. It was time to ring the Angelus. Several pulls on the reluctant rope activated the great bell, Bertrande, high in the tower. Its voice rang out among the pines and down into the valleys, resonant with the sound of mountain streams, summoning the inhabitants of those lonely heights to repeat the angel's salutation to Her whom he calls Blessed among Women. With that, a profound silence settled upon the little town for the first time that day, and Dennistoun and the sacristan exited the church.

Upon the threshold, conversation resumed.

"Monsieur seemed particularly interested in the ancient choir books stored in the sacristy."

"Indeed. I intended to inquire if there exists a library in the town."

"No, Monsieur; perhaps there once belonged to the Chapter, but now..." Here, a strange hesitation seized him, followed by a sudden plunge into speech. "But if Monsieur is an amateur of old books, I possess something at home that might interest you. It is not a hundred yards away."

Instantly, Dennistoun's cherished dream of discovering priceless manuscripts in the untouched corners of France flared, only to dim moments later. It was likely a mediocre missal printed by Plantin around 1580. What chance existed that a location so proximate to Toulouse had escaped the thorough scouring of collectors? Yet, it would be foolish to decline; he would reproach himself eternally if he refused. Thus, they departed. During the walk, the sacristan's oscillation between curiosity and resolve returned to Dennistoun's notice, prompting a shameful suspicion that he was being lured into a secluded spot to be disposed of as a wealthy Englishman. He therefore initiated conversation, clumsily introducing the fact that he expected two friends to join him early the next morning. To his astonishment, this announcement seemingly relieved the sacristan of a portion of his oppressive anxiety.

"That is well," he said, his tone suddenly bright. "That is very well. Monsieur will travel in the company of his friends; they will remain constantly near him. It is a good thing to travel thus, in company—sometimes."

The final word appeared to be an afterthought, triggering an immediate relapse into gloom for the unfortunate little man.

They soon arrived at the residence, a structure slightly larger than its neighbors, constructed of stone with a shield carved above the door bearing the insignia of Alberic de Mauléon, a collateral descendant of Bishop John de Mauléon. This Alberic served as Canon of Comminges from 1680 to 1701. The upper windows of the mansion were boarded shut, and the entire property bore the aspect of decaying age common to the rest of Comminges.

Upon reaching the doorstep, the sacristan paused.

"Perhaps," he hesitated, "perhaps, after all, Monsieur lacks the time?"

"Not at all—plenty of time—nothing to occupy us until tomorrow. Let us see what you have obtained."

The door opened, revealing a face far younger than the sacristan's, yet retaining a trace of that same distressing countenance. Here, however, the expression seemed less indicative of fear for personal safety and more of acute anxiety regarding another. Clearly, the owner was the sacristan's daughter. Aside from the described expression, she was a handsome young woman. She brightened noticeably upon seeing her father accompanied by a robust stranger. A few exchanges occurred between father and daughter, of which Dennistoun caught only these words from the sacristan: "He was laughing in the church." The response was a look of pure terror from the girl.
