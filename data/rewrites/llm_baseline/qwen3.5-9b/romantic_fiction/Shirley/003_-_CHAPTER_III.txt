Cheerfulness, it turns out, relies just as heavily on what is happening inside us as on what surrounds us outside. I mention this obvious truth because I observed Messrs. Helstone and Moore stepping out from the mill-yard gates, leading a tiny procession, buoyed by spirits that were genuinely high. When the beam of a lantern—each of the three walkers carried one—fell upon Mr. Moore's countenance, a peculiar, lively spark ignited in his eyes, casting a newfound vitality across his dark features. Similarly, when the light touched the rector's face, his usually severe expression softened into a grin, shining with evident delight. One might assume that a drizzling night and a somewhat dangerous journey were circumstances calculated to dampen such enthusiasm, yet here they were, undeterred. Had anyone working on Stilbro' Moor glimpsed this party, they would have taken great pleasure in taking aim at either leader from behind a wall; and the leaders, knowing this perfectly, were elated precisely because of it.

I am well aware, reader, that it is dreadfully improper for a parson to be warlike; I know he should be a man of peace. I have a vague understanding of a clergyman's mission among mankind, and I recall clearly whose servant he is, whose message he bears, and whose example he should emulate. Nevertheless, if you are a parson-hater, do not expect me to walk every step of your dismal, downward path. Do not expect me to join your sweeping anathemas, your narrow and poisonous rancour against "the cloth," nor to raise my voice in horror alongside a Supplehough or a Barraclough against the diabolical rector of Briarfield. He was not diabolical at all. The fault lay simply in that he had missed his vocation. He was born a soldier, but circumstance had made him a priest. Otherwise, he was a conscientious, hard-headed, hard-handed, brave, stern, implacable, and faithful little man—a figure almost devoid of sympathy, ungentle, prejudiced, and rigid, yet utterly true to principle, honorable, sagacious, and sincere.

It seems to me that one cannot always trim men to fit their professions, nor should one curse them solely because their calling hangs upon them awkwardly. Nor will I curse Helstone, though he was indeed a clerical Cossack. Yet he *was* cursed, and by many of his own parishioners, as well as by others; he was also adored. This is the frequent fate of men who display partiality in friendship and bitterness in enmity, who cling equally to principles and prejudices.

Helstone and Moore, both in excellent spirits and united temporarily in a common cause, might seem destined to converse amicably as they rode side by side. Oh, no! These two men, both of hard, bilious constitutions, rarely met without chafing each other's tempers. Their constant point of friction was the war. Helstone was a staunch Tory (Tories existed in those days), while Moore was a bitter Whig—at least, as far as opposing the war-party went, which directly affected his own interests; on that specific issue alone did he claim any British political stance. He delighted in infuriating Helstone by declaring his belief in the invincibility of Bonaparte, taunting England and Europe with the impotence of their resistance, and coolly arguing that it was better to yield sooner rather than later, since the Frenchman must eventually crush every opponent and reign supreme. Helstone could not endure such sentiments. He tolerated them only because Moore was considered an outcast and alien, possessing but half-British blood to temper the foreign gall in his veins. Furthermore, Helstone harbored a grudging respect for the dogged consistency with which Moore asserted these opinions.

As the party turned onto the Stilbro' road, they met the scant wind available; the rain lashed their faces. Moore, already fretting his companion earlier, braced himself against the raw breeze and the sharp drizzle, and began to goad him anew.

"Do your Peninsular news still please you?" he asked.

"What do you mean?" came the surly reply from the rector.

"I mean, do you still have faith in that Baal of a Lord Wellington?"

"And what do you mean now?"

"Do you still believe this wooden-faced, pebble-hearted idol of England possesses the power to send fire from heaven to consume the French holocaust you wish to offer?"

"I believe Wellington will flog Bonaparte's marshals into the sea the day it pleases him to lift his arm."

"But, my dear sir, you cannot be serious. Bonaparte's marshals are great men, acting under the guidance of an omnipotent spirit. Your Wellington is the most humdrum of commonplace martinet, whose slow, mechanical movements are further hamstrung by an ignorant home government."

"Wellington is the soul of England. He is the rightful champion of a good cause, the fitting representative of a powerful, resolute, sensible, and honest nation."

"Your 'good cause,' as I understand it, is merely the restoration of that filthy, feeble Ferdinand to a throne he disgraced. Your 'fit representative' is a dull-witted drover acting for a duller-witted farmer. Against these stand victorious supremacy and invincible genius."

"Against legitimacy stands usurpation; against modest, single-minded, righteous, and brave resistance to encroachment stands boastful, double-tongued, selfish, and treacherous ambition to possess. God defend the right!"

"God often defends the powerful."

"What! Did you suppose the handful of Israelites standing dryshod on the Asiatic side of the Red Sea was more powerful than the host of the Egyptians arrayed on the African side? Were they more numerous? Better equipped? Mightier, in a word? Don't speak, or you'll tell a lie, Moore; you know you will. They were a poor, overwrought band of bondsmen. Tyrants had oppressed them for four hundred years; a weak mixture of women and children diluted their thin ranks. Their masters, who roared to lead them through the divided flood, were a set of pampered Ethiopians, about as strong and brutal as the lions of Libya. They were armed, horsed, and charioted; the poor Hebrew wanderers were on foot. Few possessed better weapons than their shepherds' crooks or masons' tools; their meek and mighty leader himself wielded only his rod. But consider, Robert Moore: right was with them; the God of battles stood on their side. Crime and the lost archangel commanded Pharaoh's ranks, and who triumphed? We know the tale. 'The Lord saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians, and Israel saw the Egyptians dead upon the sea-shore'—yes, 'the depths covered them, they sank to the bottom as a stone.' The right hand of the Lord became glorious in power; the right hand of the Lord shattered the enemy!"

"You are entirely correct; you simply forget the true parallel. France is Israel, and Napoleon is Moses. Europe, with her bloated empires and rotten dynasties, is corrupt Egypt; gallant France is the Twelve Tribes, and her fresh, vigorous Usurper is the Shepherd of Horeb."

"I scorn to answer you."

Accordingly, Moore answered himself—or rather, added an observation in a lower voice. "Oh, in Italy he was as great as any Moses! He was the right thing there, fit to head and organize measures for the regeneration of nations. It puzzles me to this day how the conqueror of Lodi could condescend to become an emperor, a vulgar, stupid humbug; and even more, how a people who once called themselves republicans could sink again to the grade of mere slaves. I despise France! If England had marched as far toward civilization as France did, she would hardly have retreated so shamelessly."

"You don't mean to say that besotted imperial France is any worse than bloody republican France?" demanded Helstone fiercely.

"I mean to say nothing, but I can think what I please, you know, Mr. Helstone, both about France and England; about revolutions, regicides, and restorations in general; about the divine right of kings, which you often preach in your sermons, and the duty of non-resistance, and the sanity of war—and—"

Mr. Moore's sentence was abruptly cut short by the rapid rumbling of a gig, which stopped suddenly in the middle of the road. Both he and the rector had been so engrossed in their debate that they failed to notice its approach until it was close upon them.

"Nah, maister; did th' wagons hit home?" a voice demanded from the vehicle.

"Can that be Joe Scott?"

"Ay, ay!" returned another voice, for the gig contained two occupants, visible by the glimmer of its lamp. The men with lanterns had now fallen to the rear, or rather, the horsemen of the rescue party had outridden the pedestrians.

"Ay, Mr. Moore, it's Joe Scott. I'm bringing him back to you in a bonny pickle. I found him atop the moor yonder, him and three others. What will you give me for restoring him to you?"

"Why, my thanks, I believe; for I could better afford to lose a better man. Is that you, I suppose, Mr. Yorke, by your voice?"
