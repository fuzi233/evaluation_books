The wrong fork at the junction of the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean's Corners, does not lead to a mistake; it leads to a trap. The traveler finds himself descending into a geography that defies the logic of the settled world. Here, the earth rises with a sickening steepness, and the stone walls, choked with thorny briars, press inward until the road feels like a throat being constricted. The forests are not merely dense; they are oppressive, their trees swollen to grotesque proportions, while the undergrowth—a chaotic luxuriance of wild weeds and brambles—suggests a nature that has forgotten how to be tamed. Yet, amidst this overgrown wildness, the cultivated fields are terrifyingly sparse, and the scattered houses stand as uniform monuments to a slow, agonizing decay. They are ancient, squalid, and dilapidated, wearing the same face of neglect that seems to haunt the very air.

One hesitates to seek aid from the figures spotted on crumbling doorsteps or lurking in the rock-strewn meadows. These natives are silent, furtive creatures, moving with a caution that suggests they are hiding from something far worse than the traveler. To approach them feels like an intrusion into a forbidden zone, a violation of a pact one should never break. As the road climbs, revealing the mountains looming above the deep woods, a primal unease settles in the gut. The summits are unnaturally rounded and symmetrical, lacking the rugged randomness of true geology, and often, the sky cuts a sharp silhouette against the tops, revealing the queer, circular crowns of tall stone pillars that pierce the heavens like the ribs of some buried leviathan.

Gorges of impossible depth slice through the landscape, bridged only by crude wooden structures that seem poised to collapse at the slightest tremor. When the road dips, it plunges into stretches of marshland that radiate a cold, instinctive dread, especially at twilight. Here, the unseen whippoorwills chatter with a malicious rhythm, and fireflies dance in abnormal profusion to the raucous, creeping pipes of bullfrogs whose calls sound less like nature and more like a warning. The Miskatonic River, winding close to the feet of these domed hills, looks less like water and more like a thin, shining serpent slithering through a nightmare.

As the hills draw nearer, their dark, precipitous sides loom like the walls of a tomb, offering no escape route. Beyond a covered bridge, a small village huddles between the stream and the vertical slope of Round Mountain. It is a cluster of rotting gambrel roofs, architectural relics from a time long past, standing in stark contrast to the modern world outside. Closer inspection reveals a grim truth: most houses are empty shells, falling into ruin, while the broken steeple of the church now shelters the only mercantile establishment in the hamlet—a slovenly, desperate outpost of commerce. One dreads the tenebrous tunnel of the bridge, yet there is no choice but to enter. Once across, the village street seems to exhale a faint, malign odor, the scent of massed mold and centuries of decay. It is a relief to flee, to follow the narrow road around the base of the hills and back to the Aylesbury Pike, only to learn later, with a chill that lingers for days, that one has passed through Dunwich.

Outsiders visit Dunwich as rarely as possible. Since the season of horror that ended in 1928, every signboard pointing toward the town has been torn down. By any ordinary aesthetic standard, the scenery is breathtakingly beautiful; yet, no artists or tourists dare tread here. Two centuries ago, when superstition held sway, people avoided the area due to whispers of witch-blood and strange forest presences. Today, in our sensible age, the avoidance is driven by a collective amnesia regarding the Dunwich Horror of 1928, a tragedy hushed up by those who claimed to care for the town's welfare. But perhaps the true reason lies deeper: the natives are repellently decadent, having walked a path of retrogression that has turned them into a distinct, degenerate race. Defined by the mental and physical stigmata of inbreeding, their intelligence is woefully low, and their history reeks of overt viciousness, half-hidden murders, incest, and acts of violence so perverse they defy naming.

Even the old gentry, the remnants of the armigerous families from Salem in 1692, cannot escape the tide of decay. While some branches of the Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard and Miskatonic University, those sons seldom return to the moldering gambrel roofs where their ancestors were born and died. No one can definitively explain what is wrong with Dunwich, though old legends persist. They speak of unhallowed rites and Indian conclaves where forbidden shadows were summoned from the great rounded hills, answered by wild orgiastic prayers and the loud crackings of the earth itself. In 1747, Reverend Abijah Hoadley preached a memorable sermon on the close presence of Satan, claiming to hear the cursed voices of Azazel, Buzrael, Beelzebub, and Belial rising from underground. He described a discourse of evil powers in the hill behind his house—a rattling, rolling, groaning, screeching, and hissing that no earthly thing could produce. Hoadley vanished shortly after delivering this sermon, but his text, printed in Springfield, remains.

Noises in the hills continue to puzzle geologists and physiographers to this day. Other traditions speak of foul odors near the hill-crowning stone circles and of rushing airy presences heard faintly from the bottom of the ravines. There is the Devil's Hop Yard, a bleak, blasted hillside where no vegetation can take root. The natives are mortally afraid of the numerous whippoorwills, which grow vocal on warm nights. They are believed to be psychopomps, lying in wait for the souls of the dying, timing their eerie cries to the sufferer's struggling breath. If they catch the fleeing soul, they flutter away in demoniac laughter; if they fail, they sink into a disappointed silence.

These tales, though dismissed by the rational mind as obsolete and ridiculous, stem from a reality that is undeniably old. Dunwich is older than any community within thirty miles. South of the village, one can still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop house, built before 1700, while the ruins of the mill at the falls, constructed in 1806, represent the most modern architecture in sight. Industry never flourished here; the nineteenth-century factory movement proved short-lived. Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns on the hilltops, generally attributed to the Indians rather than the settlers. Deposits of skulls and bones found within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill sustain the popular belief that these were burial places of the Pocumtucks, even though many ethnologists, disregarding the absurd improbability of such a theory, persist in insisting the remains are Caucasian.
