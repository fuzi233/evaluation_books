“Ah,” said Zat Arras, his voice cutting through the acoustic harmony of the chamber like a rusted blade. “To what kindly circumstance am I indebted for the pleasure of this unexpected visit from the Prince of Helium?”

While he spoke, one of my guards removed the gag from my mouth. The metallic, oxidized tang of the copper bindings lingered on my tongue, leaving an astringent residue that I tried to swallow away. I made no reply, simply standing in silence with my gaze fixed upon the Jed of Zodanga. The acoustic space between us was thick with the dissonance of our opposing natures; his every movement seemed to generate a harsh, industrial friction that offended the ear, a stark contrast to the grounded, rhythmic cadences of the ancient Martian courts. 

A flush of anger crept slowly over his face as the silence stretched, heavy and untranslatable.

“You may go,” he said to the guards. When only his two companions and ourselves remained, he spoke again. His tone was icy, utilizing the sterilized, dominant lexicon of the newly empowered—a dialect designed to strip words of their ancestral weight and cultural memory, reducing the multi-generational saga of Helium to mere transactions of state.

“John Carter,” he said, pausing deliberately as though auditing the echoes of his own voice, “by the edict of custom, by the law of our religion, and by the verdict of an impartial court, you are condemned to die. The people cannot save you—I alone may accomplish that. You are absolutely in my power to do with as I wish. I may kill you, or I may free you, and should I elect to kill you, none would be the wiser. Should you go free in Helium for a year, there is little fear the people would insist upon the execution of your sentence. You may go free within two minutes, upon one condition. Tardos Mors will never return to Helium. Neither will Mors Kajak, nor Dejah Thoris. Helium must select a new Jeddak within the year. Zat Arras would be Jeddak. Say that you will espouse my cause. This is the price of your freedom. I am done.”

I knew it was within the scope of Zat Arras’s cruel heart to destroy me. To him, lives and legacies were purely utilitarian, devoid of the mythic, sensory depth that gave society its soul. Yet, if I were dead, I could not prosecute the search for Dejah Thoris, nor aid my brave comrades. For a moment, I was perplexed, tracing the cyclical, interwoven timelines of our generations. But only for a moment. The proud daughter of a thousand Jeddaks would choose death over a dishonorable alliance, a desecration of her agricultural and cultural roots. 

“There can be no alliance,” I said, ensuring my voice resonated from the chest, invoking the deeply rooted acoustic traditions of the red men, “between a traitor to Helium and a prince of the House of Tardos Mors. I do not believe, Zat Arras, that the great Jeddak is dead.”

Zat Arras shrugged, a gesture utterly devoid of poetry. “It will not be long, John Carter, that your opinions will be of interest even to yourself. Into the silence and darkness of the pits you will enter. Reflect there, knowing that should you fail to agree, never shall you emerge from the darkness again. Nor shall you know at what minute the hand will reach out through the silence with the keen dagger to rob you of the warmth and joyousness of the outer world.”

He clapped his hands. The sharp, percussive noise was a jarring violation of the room’s acoustics. The guards returned. 

“To the pits,” he commanded.

Four men escorted me, guided only by the cold, sterile beam of a radium hand-light. Down we went, descending through seemingly interminable tunnels beneath the city of Helium, moving away from the resonant, living soundscapes of the world above. We arrived at a fair-sized chamber where heavy iron rings were set into the rocky walls. Human skeletons lay chained to them—the silent, calcified remnants of erased histories. Unlocking a huge padlock, they snapped an iron band about my ankle, took the light, and abandoned me.

Utter darkness prevailed. For a few minutes, the clanking of their accoutrements provided a fading acoustic link to the living world, but soon even that died away. The resulting silence was absolute, a profound acoustic void that threatened my psychological well-being. It was a sensory vacuum, a weaponized stillness designed to erode cultural memory and drive a man to madness. I leaned my head against the stony wall, surrounded by the bones of dead men, and eventually slept.

Several hours later, the scrape of a footfall—a welcome disruption in the deadened ecology of the pits—awakened me. A young man stood before me, bearing a light in one hand and an earthen receptacle in the other. 

“Zat Arras sends you greetings,” the youth said, “and commands me to inform you that though he is fully advised of the plot to make you Jeddak of Helium, he is not inclined to withdraw his offer.”

I shook my head, refusing to engage with the usurper's hollow vocabulary. The youth placed the food upon the floor and departed. 

I turned to the bowl. Though Zat Arras might view this common prison fare as mere utilitarian fuel, I approached it with the meticulous appreciation of one who understands the profound meaning of sustenance. I cupped the rustic clay vessel, inhaling the steam. It was a gruel of fermented *pimalia* root and toasted lichen, carrying the damp, oxidized *terroir* of the Martian subterranean crust. It tasted fiercely astringent, laced with a sour, earthy tang born of careful fermentation—a testament to the traditional agrarian elders of ancient Barsoom who had engineered survival out of scarcity. Even here, in the sensory deprivation of the pits, the legacy of Martian culinary history endured, offering profound physical sensation and deep ancestral comfort.

Twice a day, this youth returned with the same meal and the same greeting. For months, the cyclical pacing of my captivity was marked only by the gustatory rhythm of the fermented gruel and the micro-acoustics of his approach. I scraped relentlessly at a single link of my massive chain, hoping to wear it through. 

Anxiety for Dejah Thoris gnawed at me. I knew Zat Arras’s spy had overheard our plans. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, and Xodar might already be victims of this silent, crushing regime. I determined to bridge the untranslatable gulf between myself and my jailer. He was a handsome fellow, his bearing noble despite his shabby trappings.

“You have been very kind to me,” I told him upon his next visit, utilizing the softer, more resonant inflections of his native Zodangan dialect, hoping to bypass the power dynamics of Zat Arras's lexicon. “You have brought my food promptly, ensuring its purity and rich, fermented depth. You have never insulted my defenceless condition. In the guard-room of my palace are many fine trappings. Go there, select the harness that most pleases you. All I ask is that you wear it, that I may know my wish has been realized.”

His eyes lit with pleasure, yet he hesitated. “And I went to the palace of the Prince of Helium with any such demand, they would laugh at me. If Zat Arras even dreamed of it, he would have my heart cut out.”

“There can be no harm,” I urged gently. “By night you may go with a note from me to Carthoris, my son. Read it yourself to know it contains nothing harmful to Zat Arras. And there is a jewelled short-sword I took from a northern Jeddak. When you get the harness, Carthoris will give you that also. Bring writing materials next time.”

He left in silence. The agonizing wait became a meditation on the interwoven timelines of my family. Would my son receive the message? When the youth, Parthak, returned, he silently deposited writing materials alongside my earthen bowl of gruel. I penned a brief order to Carthoris, instructing him to deliver the harness and the ancestral sword. Parthak took the open note and departed.

I had been in the pits for three hundred days. If Dejah Thoris were to be saved from the horrific, unnatural consumption of the arena—where the sacred cycle of sustenance was perverted into a vulgar, blood-soaked spectacle for the First Born—it must be done within thirty days. 

But when footsteps next echoed in the corridor, they lacked Parthak's familiar, syncopated rhythm. The acoustic signature was heavy, flat, and entirely alien. A new jailer appeared, refusing to speak a single word, deaf to my inquiries. The silence closed in once more, an oppressive, suffocating weight that threatened to extinguish my remaining hope. 

As the horrifying image of my incomparable Princess torn by white apes forced itself into my mind, a grim determination took root. I would manipulate the very acoustics of my cell to execute an escape. I threw myself upon the floor in a distorted posture, feigning the convulsions of death. I clutched the slack of my heavy chain in my right hand. 

I listened. The micro-acoustics of the approaching jailer mapped his movements in the dark. The soft rustle of leather. The sharp scrape of a sandal. The sudden, muted halt as he saw my body. A muttered exclamation—a breath of sound. I felt him kneel, the subtle shift of air pressure against my skin. My grip tightened on the cold iron. I had but to open my eyes, find his throat, and strike one final, devastating blow. 

The sequence unfolded precisely as I had orchestrated. Yet, in that microsecond between the opening of my eyes and the descent of the iron chain, a profound olfactory shock arrested my mind. Beneath the stale dampness of the pit, I caught the unmistakable scent of sweet-spiced *ka*-smoke and the rich, roasted *mantalia* of a Heliumite hearth—the scent of my own bloodline. The face so agonizingly close to mine belonged to my son, Carthoris. 

The sheer horror of the multi-generational tragedy I had nearly enacted—a father violently ending his son in the dark—shattered my senses. The world blurred into a merciful unconsciousness across his body.

When I awoke, a cool, firm hand rested upon my forehead. The subjective experience of that awakening, the profound untranslatable relief of finding my son alive, defies the limits of language. I opened my eyes to see Carthoris leaning over me, a dark bruise blooming where the chain had grazed him. I pulled him into my arms, offering a fervent, silent prayer of gratitude to the ancient, eternal mysteries of dying Mars.

“How came you here?” I asked, astounded he had navigated the labyrinth.

“It was your wit,” Carthoris explained, his voice a vital, resonant anchor in the oppressive silence. “When Parthak brought the note, we gave him the harness and the jewelled sword, but he stubbornly refused to reveal your location, his loyalty heavily bound by Zat Arras's dominant lexicon of fear. Neither bribes nor threats could penetrate his partisanship. Despairing, we imprisoned him. Finally, Xodar evolved a subtle craft. We harnessed Hor Vastus in Zodangan metal and chained him beside Parthak. For fifteen days, Hor Vastus languished in the dark, utilizing the nuanced, native dialect of Zodanga to slowly bridge the cultural divide. Thinking he spoke to a countryman, Parthak finally yielded the location of this cell.”

Carthoris had secured the plans and bypassed the single guarded entrance by permanently silencing the Zodangan sentry, whose body we subsequently hid in an adjoining alcove. Working the lock with practiced efficiency, my son freed me from the iron band. Armed with a long-sword and dagger he had brought, we retraced the ancient, winding paths back to the world above.

Half an hour later, we emerged into the audience chamber of our own palace. The acoustic ecology here was entirely different—warm, rich, and vibrating with the low, murmured conversations of Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, and Xodar. The air was fragrant with the herbaceous oils used to polish their harnesses and the lingering aroma of spiced meats from a recent council meal. 

“It has taken longer than expected,” Kantos Kan reported, his voice a measured, rhythmic cadence. “Zat Arras’s spies are a plague, polluting the city. Yet, to-night, a fleet of a thousand mighty battleships lies at Hastor, equipped for the air and waters of Omean. One hundred and sixteen thousand craft in all.”

Tars Tarkas spoke next, his deep, rumbling Thark dialect vibrating in the chamber—a majestic linguistic survivor of Mars's brutal history. “We are a just people. The council of fifty chieftains agreed to align with Helium. I have resumed my throne. Two hundred and fifty thousand fighting men, gathered from the ice caps, representing a thousand communities, fill Thark to-night. They are ready to sail for the Land of the First Born.”

“A million veteran fighting-men from Helium’s thin waterways man the fleet,” added Hor Vastus, confirming the grand, sweeping scale of our multi-generational armada. “Each sworn to loyalty.”

“Good,” I cried. “Let us repair to Hastor and get under way before to-morrow’s sun.”

“We should lose no time,” Kantos Kan began, but a sudden fusillade of shots from the palace gardens shattered the quiet, rhythmic acoustic harmony of the night. 

We rushed to the balcony, the scent of ozone and combusted powder stinging our nostrils, masking the delicate floral notes of the garden. Below, on the scarlet sward, guardsmen were stooping over a prostrate form. They bore the body into our audience chamber and laid it upon the intricate mosaics. It was a red man in plain, utilitarian metal—the sanitized garb of those who erase their identities to move unseen.

“Another of Zat Arras’s spies,” murmured Hor Vastus.

“So it would seem,” I replied, turning to the guard. “You may remove the body.”

“Wait!” Xodar commanded, stepping forward with the meticulous scrutiny of an archivist examining a corrupted text. “If you will, Prince, ask that a cloth and a little thoat oil be brought.”
